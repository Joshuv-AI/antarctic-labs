Interface access control graph neural network method for flexible automation production of mine ventilation duct
Authors: Hui Zhang, Jun Qian, Wang Shun, Jiang JianLin, XiaoXi Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterface authorization in flexible automated mine ventilation duct production lines is difficult because heterogeneous users and services access safety-critical resources under time-varying cyber-physical risk that static RBAC/ACL rules cannot capture. This study presents HGRAD, a heterogeneous-graph risk-adaptive access control framework for industrial cyber-physical systems. HGRAD models each access event as a dynamic graph with four node types: user nodes encode operator identity, role, and history; interface nodes represent exposed PLC/MES/service access points and protocol/load states; resource nodes denote commands, records, or work-order objects with different sensitivities; and physics-informed risk nodes provide conservative structural-risk proxies for critical resources. A Temporal-HGT encoder and relation-specific hierarchical attention capture temporal context, structural semantics, and abnormal-path salience, while an intervention-inspired log-based filter, adversarial perturbation, Shapley audit weighting, and MC-Dropout uncertainty estimation support adaptive authorization rather than fixed-threshold decisions. In the TON_IoT benchmark mapping, the physics-informed node is derived only from benchmark-log proxy signals, including access intensity, operation criticality, freshness, and resource criticality; it is not generated by real mine ventilation sensors, plant-side finite-element outputs, or field digital-twin measurements. HGRAD achieves the strongest validation and held-out benchmark performance among compared baselines. The results are therefore benchmark-level proof of concept for access-control design, not evidence of completed industrial deployment or field-validated mine-safety performance.Keywords: Interface access control, Heterogeneous graph neural network, Temporal graph modeling, Industrial cyber-physical systemsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

As the coal mining industry accelerates its transition toward intelligent and flexible operations, the integration of automated control and information systems in underground production continues to deepen. Particularly in critical processes such as longwall mining, tunneling, transportation, and ventilation systems, the widespread adoption of automated equipment, wireless communication networks, and real-time dispatching platforms has driven the development of flexible production lines in coal mines. Research indicates that coal mine automation not only significantly enhances production efficiency but also demonstrates substantial potential in equipment coordination, safety management, and energy efficiency optimization1.

However, current access control mechanisms in production lines predominantly rely on traditional approaches such as Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)2 and Access Control Lists (ACL). These methods enforce static permission rules, which fail to accommodate the dynamic and time-sensitive access requirements of flexible production environments. Consequently, they exhibit coarse security granularity and delayed risk response capabilities.

To address these challenges, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a promising solution for context-aware access control3, leveraging their exceptional structural data modeling capabilities. As a specialized deep learning framework for non-Euclidean data structures, GNNs employ node-to-node feature propagation mechanisms to achieve semantic modeling and information fusion in entity relationship graphs4,5. This approach has demonstrated remarkable performance advantages in diverse applications including social media recommendation6, financial fraud detection7, and industrial security auditing.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) can autonomously learn structural features and behavioral semantics in access paths, overcoming the limitations of traditional models that rely on "hard-coded rules" and "static judgments." This enables access control decisions to consider behavioral history, contextual states, and structural risks. However, most existing GNN methods are designed for homogeneous or static graphs, which still cannot fully address the challenges of joint modeling in industrial scenarios involving heterogeneous entities, temporal evolution, and physical security constraints.

To this end, this paper proposes a new method of interface access control graph neural network (HGRAD) for flexible automated production of mine air ducts. The model constructs a dynamic heterogeneous graph with four types of entities: user, interface, resource, and physical simulation. By integrating temporal modeling and risk assessment, it achieves refined and trustworthy dynamic authorization control.

Related research and improvement measures

Numerous domestic and international researchers have conducted in-depth exploration and improvements on graph neural network models. For instance, Zhao et al. proposed a multivariate time series anomaly detection method integrating GAT and Informer. This approach achieves accurate identification of sensor anomalies by synergistically utilizing short-term and long-term prediction errors9. Subsequently, Zhao et al. introduced the MSDG model in the same journal, which enhances spatiotemporal dependency modeling of sensor data through multi-scale sliding window mechanisms and dynamic graph construction, demonstrating performance improvements across multiple industrial datasets10.

Meanwhile, Wu et al. emphasized a method framework that integrates node-level, contextual, and collective anomaly detection in their review of graph neural networks for industrial internet anomaly detection11. Lyu et al. proposed a graph neural network method called GLIN, which achieves high-precision equipment status prediction by combining local node features with global network representations12. Wang et al. introduced the MTSGAT model, which simultaneously constructs variable graphs and time graphs, feeds them into dual graph attention networks, and performs joint optimization through prediction and reconstruction modules. This approach not only improves the accuracy of multivariate time series anomaly detection but also enhances the localization capability of anomalous variables13. Jin Seo et al. proposed an anomaly detection and diagnosis method integrating graph autoencoders and graph convolutional networks. Based on system topology and enhanced features, this method generates adjacency matrices and trains GAE models to acquire reconstruction capabilities under normal conditions, thereby identifying anomalies through reconstruction errors14. Ali Yilmaz and Resul Das addressed the challenge of traditional methods struggling with large-scale, complex graph network anomaly detection by proposing a hybrid graph neural network model combining GCN and GAT for anomaly detection tasks15. Zhou et al. proposed the RGGLD model, which models IoT communication behaviors as directed graph nodes, defines specific rules to generate edge structures, and combines GAT with MLP to extract structural and traffic features, supporting knowledge distillation at the graph level. The model incorporates a local subgraph retention mechanism and a global information alignment mechanism, significantly improving the accuracy and efficiency of anomaly detection16. Recent multi-level graph-attention research also supports industrial-control anomaly detection baselines17.

Algorithm design

To enable dynamic monitoring and secure control of industrial interface access in flexible automated production lines for mine ventilation ducts, this study proposes a Heterogeneous Graph-based Risk-Adaptive Decision Model (HGRAD). The model establishes dynamic heterogeneous graph representations based on production line components including users, interfaces, resources, and finite element simulation platforms. By integrating graph attention mechanisms with risk modeling, it implements access authorization policies that demonstrate fine-grained control, strong temporal adaptability, and high interpretability.

Overall framework design

The model’s architecture consists of four core components: the heterogeneous graph modeling module, the Temporal-HGT encoder module, the security-enhanced attention mechanism module, and the adaptive authorization decision module, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Architecture of the proposed HGRAD model.

Specifically, the system first constructs a heterogeneous graph structure containing users, interfaces, resources, and simulation nodes based on operational data from the flexible production line of mine ventilation shafts. A sliding window is then used to generate temporal snapshots as model inputs. The Temporal-HGT encoder performs type-aware and time-aware feature aggregation on graph nodes to generate contextual representations for each access behavior. To enhance the robustness and interpretability of access path scoring, the model further introduces a three-stage relationship-specific attention mechanism (R-HAT) that strengthens attention distribution through three dimensions: pre-event causal filtering, in-event adversarial perturbation, and post-event audit interpretability. Building on this foundation, the system estimates the uncertainty distribution of access scores through MC-Dropout multiple sampling, and constructs an adaptive authorization table by combining mean and variance of scores. This enables dynamic, fine-grained, and risk-aware authorization decisions for interface access behaviors.

Isomorphism modeling

To effectively model the complex interactions among users, interfaces, resources, and the physical simulation platform in the flexible automated production line of mine ventilation ducts, this paper designs a heterogeneous graph structure with temporal dynamics and physical constraints as the foundational representation of the access control model. The graph not only supports fine-grained modeling of access paths between multi-source heterogeneous entities but also integrates structural safety margins, historical interface behaviors, and temporal dynamics, thereby enabling context-aware processing and risk inference for graph neural networks.

Specifically, this paper defines the types of nodes and edges in the graph from the perspective of system semantics, combined with the control logic, call path and risk propagation mechanism in the production line, and further designs the weight function to quantify the behavioral importance and physical risk sensitivity of the edges.

System semantic mapping

In the flexible automated production line for mine ventilation ducts, the control process is typically coordinated by three core systems: PLC controller, MES scheduling system, and finite element simulation platform (FEA). Together, they form an OT control network with deep integration of human-machine-object interactions. Each system exposes numerous fine-grained interfaces involving multi-role operations and resource access, posing significant security risks.

The PLC controller primarily handles real-time control operations at the foundational level, including tensioning, feeding, and cutting. Its interface calls are mapped as control-class interface nodes, while their control targets are modeled as resource nodes. The MES system is represented through scheduling-class interface nodes and task-oriented resource nodes. For the physical side, the present benchmark study does not ingest live mine-side finite-element outputs; instead, it introduces a physics-informed risk node that mimics the role of structural-state supervision and can be replaced by real FEA or digital-twin outputs in future deployment Fig. 2.

Relationship-Specific attention mechanism

In the previous section, this paper implemented type-awareness and temporal dynamic modeling for heterogeneous nodes in access paths using the Temporal-HGT encoder, achieving a context-sensitive node representation. However, in real-world industrial interface access control scenarios, relying solely on feature aggregation and temporal modeling still fails to meet the comprehensive requirements of trustworthiness, robustness, and interpretability in access path analysis. Particularly when attackers forge identities or tamper with features to construct illegal paths, traditional attention mechanisms often lack targeted response mechanisms.

To address this, this paper proposes a three-stage relation-specific hierarchical attention mechanism (R-HAT) based on the standard Graph Attention Network (GAT). The mechanism enhances the credibility of attention computation and semantic expression through three stages: causal filtering, adversarial correction, and audit weighting. As an upper-layer enhancement module for the Temporal-HGT encoder, it further improves the model’s precision in responding to security risk behaviors.

Preemptive defense mechanisms

Before formally generating access control scores, the system applies a causal-constrained attention mechanism to downweight access paths that are inconsistent with the observed operational context. In the revised manuscript, we avoid treating this stage as unrestricted causal identification from arbitrary logs. Instead, the stage is implemented as an intervention-inspired filter over a simplified structural graph containing user role, historical risk, interface load, operation type, resource sensitivity, time bucket, and access outcome.

This mechanism estimates the causal impact factor of each edge from observational historical logs using a g-computation style adjustment over the observed confounder set C = {role, prior risk, interface load, operation type, resource sensitivity, time bucket, historical success rate}. In practice, a calibrated classifier is fitted within each relation type and the estimated interventional probability is used as a soft prior rather than as proof of fully identified causality.

The quantity is therefore interpreted as an adjusted intervention-inspired score rather than as a simple association measure. This differs from Pearson correlation or mutual information because the estimate is conditioned on observed confounders and targets an interventional quantity; however, unmeasured confounding may still remain and is acknowledged as a limitation.

HGRAD model pseudocode

The proposed HGRAD model’s workflow and technical implementation are illustrated in Algorithm 1. The algorithm first constructs a heterogeneous graph structure comprising user nodes, interface nodes, resource nodes, and physical simulation nodes based on real-time access behavior sequences in flexible mine ventilation duct automation production lines. This structure uniformly represents the access relationships and structural constraints among humans, machines, and objects.

The model then employs a Temporal-HGT encoder to perform time-sensitive feature aggregation across heterogeneous graph nodes. By incorporating node type-aware projection, multi-head attention mechanisms, and temporal embeddings, it effectively captures the dynamic contextual features of access behaviors, generating semantically rich node representations.

To further enhance the robustness and interpretability of access control processes, the algorithm designs a three-stage relationship-specific attention mechanism (R-HAT) based on the encoder. This mechanism dynamically models and integrates attention weights from three dimensions: causal filtering, adversarial correction, and audit interpretability. It strengthens the model’s responsiveness to illegal paths, attack perturbations, and core access dependencies.

Finally, the system combines the MC-Dropout multiple sampling mechanism to model the uncertainty of the scoring results generated for each access path, and maps the mean and variance of the scores to the adaptive authorization policy table, achieving dynamic, fine-grained, and secure access decision outputs for industrial interface access behaviors.

Experimental design

Data set selection

This study employs the TON_IoT dataset to investigate industrial interface access control and security behavior modeling. As one of the most comprehensive public datasets in IoT and industrial control security, it features rich structures, diverse entities, and complete temporal sequences. Published by the University of New South Wales (UNSW), Australia, the TON_IoT dataset contains multiple subsystem logs that encompass multi-source heterogeneous information including device telemetry data, OS calls, network traffic patterns, and user login activities. These characteristics make it particularly suitable for multi-entity and multi-relational modeling tasks in graph neural network models.

Heterogeneous graph construction scheme

User node

User node

Identify source IP addresses or user IDs in the dataset as user nodes. Static attributes of nodes, such as roles and permissions, are initially categorized by analyzing their historical behavior patterns. Dynamic attributes of nodes, such as historical risk scores and active status, are dynamically calculated based on their behavior sequences in the logs.

(2)Interface node

Interface node

Define the combination of "destination IP + destination port + protocol type" as a unique interface node. Attributes such as interface type, operations, historical failure rate, and load can be analyzed and calculated by examining network traffic logs flowing to the interface.

(3)Resource node

Resource node

To handle stale physical information, each proxy node stores the refresh timestamp . Its contribution to the physical edge is multiplied by a freshness factor . When the refresh age exceeds a preset threshold , the policy layer downgrades the structural signal to advisory status and increases decision uncertainty rather than treating the stale proxy as a hard constraint. This directly addresses the mismatch between relatively slow structural refresh and high-frequency interface calls.

Resources are logical objects that interfaces operate on. Each interface node is linked to one or more resource nodes. Resource types and sensitivity levels are defined according to industrial standard ports—for example, resources associated with Modbus/502 ports are classified as high-sensitivity control command resources, while those linked to HTTP/80 ports are designated as low-sensitivity log information resources.

(4)Physical simulation node

Physical simulation node

For each resource node classified as high or medium sensitivity, a virtual physics-informed risk node is created and linked. In the benchmark study, this node is not produced by an online plant-side finite-element solver. Instead, it is a physics-informed proxy derived from access frequency, operation type, and recency so that cyber events can be coupled with a structural-risk surrogate in the heterogeneous graph. Specifically, for resource r in time window t, the proxy stress is defined as , where ,r and ,r denote write and control invocation counts, ,r is an operation-severity coefficient, and is the physical relaxation constant. The resulting proxy is min-max scaled within each resource class to a bounded engineering interval and paired with a structural margin . This design preserves a physically interpretable monotonic relation while avoiding an unsupported claim that real mine-side FEA measurements were directly available in . Physics-informed proxy node generation and stale-information handling.

To make this construction explicit, the TON_IoT-based physical risk node is built from benchmark-log variables rather than from any hidden mine-side measurement20. The proxy feature set includes (i) source identifier and user-history statistics, (ii) destination IP-port-protocol combinations used to define interface nodes, (iii) operation class inferred from protocol and port mapping (read, write, or control), (iv) recent write/control invocation counts in the sliding window, (v) failed-call ratio and historical success rate, (vi) interface load and inter-arrival or idle time, and (vii) resource criticality assigned from industrial-port semantics such as Modbus/502 and HTTP/80. These variables are used only as proxy signals for access-induced structural-risk salience: more frequent or more critical write/control requests increase the proxy stress, whereas longer idle intervals relax it through temporal decay.

Accordingly, the phrase physics-informed risk does not denote a measured mechanical stress field, finite-element solution, airflow state, or field-validated ventilation-safety state in this benchmark. It denotes a monotone risk prior that makes cyber access decisions more conservative when the benchmark log suggests high access intensity and high resource criticality. In a deployed system, the same node type would need to be replaced or calibrated with actual finite-element/digital-twin output, ventilation sensors, and site-specific safety thresholds before it could support engineering safety claims.

Uncertainty calibration and strategy effectiveness analysis

To comprehensively evaluate the model’s performance in uncertainty modeling and access control, this section conducts experimental validation from two dimensions: the credibility of probabilistic outputs and the rationality of policy allocation. MC-Dropout is used here as an approximate Bayesian uncertainty estimator for access-score calibration and policy gating.

Specifically, MC-Dropout is introduced during the inference phase to obtain multiple sampling results, calculate the mean and variance of predictions, and combine the adaptive strategy table to implement dynamic authorization, thereby enhancing the security and reliability of access control.

To systematically evaluate the role of uncertainty modeling in access control, this section presents three comparative approaches:

w/o MC-Dropout: During the inference phase, only a single forward pass is performed to directly output the prediction probability without incorporating uncertainty estimates.

w/o + Temp: Without employing MC-Dropout, the system performs comprehensive calibration of output probabilities via temperature scaling to mitigate overconfidence issues.

HGRAD (T=10): The MC-Dropout method is applied to perform 10 sampling iterations to compute the mean and variance of the predicted distribution, which are then integrated with the adaptive strategy table for access control decision-making.

Figure 12 presents a comparative analysis of reliability curves for calibration performance across three methods. Results demonstrate that the w/o MC-Dropout method exhibits significant overconfidence across multiple confidence intervals, with predicted probabilities consistently exceeding actual accuracy rates, resulting in an Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 0.224. While the w/o + Temp method partially mitigates this issue through temperature scaling, it still shows deviations in low-to-mid confidence intervals, achieving an ECE of 0.193. In contrast, HGRAD (T=10) achieves near-perfect alignment between predicted probabilities and true accuracy rates across all confidence intervals, with its curve closely matching the ideal line and an ECE approaching zero. This indicates that incorporating MC-Dropout’s uncertainty modeling significantly enhances model calibration, enabling probability outputs that better align with real-world distributions and establishing a robust foundation for subsequent policy implementation.

Reliability diagram for calibration comparison.

Compared with a fixed-threshold policy, MC-Dropout changes the final access-control decision whenever the predictive distribution is wide or unstable. Under a fixed threshold, a request with a point estimate above the cut-off can be allowed even if the model is uncertain. In the proposed policy, the mean score determines the basic risk interval, while the variance downgrades uncertain requests to conditional write, read-only tracking, risk-tagged manual review, or denial according to Table 2. This explains why the no-MC-Dropout ablation in Table 10 retains acceptable classification accuracy but increases the false-authorization rate to 5.7%, whereas full HGRAD reduces it to 2.1%. The operational effect of MC-Dropout is therefore a risk-sensitive decision-migration mechanism, not merely a calibration curve.

Model parameter sensitivity and scalability analysis

To further validate the stability and practicality of the HGRAD model in real-world industrial deployment, this section evaluates three aspects: sensitivity to core hyperparameters, scalability of computational overhead with network expansion, and the relation between model compactness and deployability. Following the reviewers’ suggestion, the discussion now connects these observations with the broader literature on neural-network compression and formally grounded model reduction.

In graph neural network models, the node embedding dimension and the number of attention heads in the multi-head attention mechanism are key hyperparameters that influence the model’s expressive power and complexity. To investigate the HGRAD model’s dependence on these parameters, this study conducted a parameter sensitivity analysis. The experiment fixed other parameters at optimal settings while adjusting the embedding dimension and attention head count, observing changes in the model’s F1-Score on the validation set.

As shown in Fig. 14, the model demonstrates excellent stability when the node embedding dimension varies within the range of [32,64,128,256]. While the F1-score reaches its peak at dimension 128, performance degradation remains minimal (under 2%) across other dimensions, indicating the model’s insensitivity to embedding-dimension selection and eliminating the need for fine-tuning in practical applications. Similarly, Fig. 15 reveals that adjusting the number of attention heads between [2,4,8,16] results in smooth fluctuations in the model’s F1-score. The optimal performance is achieved at 8 attention heads, suggesting that this number effectively captures the rich heterogeneous relationships in access paths without significant additional computational overhead. Overall, HGRAD demonstrates remarkable robustness by showing insensitivity to key hyperparameters.

The interface network in mining production lines may dynamically expand with system scaling, making computational efficiency and scalability critical metrics for evaluating industrial application value. To assess HGRAD’s scalability, we conducted random node sampling on the TON_IoT dataset to construct five progressively larger subgraphs with node counts of [2000,4000,6000,8000,10000]. The average inference time per batch for the model was measured across these five subgraphs.

The experimental results shown in Fig. 16 demonstrate that HGRAD’s inference time exhibits a nearly linear growth trend with increasing graph node counts, without experiencing exponential computational explosion. When the graph scale expands from 2,000 to 10,000 nodes, the inference time increases from approximately 2.8 ms to 14.5 ms, representing a fivefold growth within acceptable limits. This high efficiency is attributed to the message-passing mechanism in the graph neural network framework, which employs neighbor sampling and maintains linear computational complexity with edge quantity. These results conclusively demonstrate HGRAD’s excellent scalability, enabling efficient processing of large-scale industrial access network graphs while meeting real-time performance requirements for practical deployment.

Practical implications and limitations

In a practical mine ventilation duct production line, HGRAD should be viewed as a supervisory authorization layer on top of existing PLC and MES permissions rather than as a replacement for basic safety interlocks. The access score can prioritize high-risk calls, downgrade uncertain requests to read-only mode, or trigger manual review for borderline cases. Real deployment would require direct ingestion of site authentication logs, stable interface identifiers, and genuine structural-monitoring signals from FEA or a digital twin. Future work will explore compression-aware variants of HGRAD, such as structured pruning and formally constrained reduction, to improve edge-deployment efficiency without materially degrading authorization reliability.

The use of TON_IoT as a benchmark proxy has several important limitations. First, TON_IoT records generic IoT/IIoT cyber events rather than interface calls collected from an operating mine ventilation duct production line. Second, the mapping from ports and protocols to PLC/MES resources is an engineering abstraction and may omit site-specific command semantics. Third, the physical-risk node is a log-derived surrogate and cannot validate true duct stress, airflow, or ventilation-safety dynamics. Fourth, the attack labels and access outcomes in TON_IoT represent benchmark cyber-security classes rather than enterprise authorization labels produced by a mine operator. Fifth, maintenance schedules, shift operations, sensor calibration, emergency-control procedures, and operator override rules are not captured. Consequently, the results should be read as a benchmark-level proof of concept for the HGRAD design and must be validated with field logs and real digital-twin/FEA data before safety-critical deployment.

Summary

To address the growing complexity of multi-system interface coordination and access control in flexible automated production lines for mine ventilation ducts, this study proposes HGRAD, a heterogeneous-graph risk-adaptive decision framework that combines temporal modeling, relation-specific risk weighting, and uncertainty-aware authorization. The model organizes users, interfaces, resources, and physics-informed risk nodes in a dynamic heterogeneous graph, uses a Temporal-HGT encoder to capture contextual semantics, and employs the three-stage R-HAT mechanism to improve robustness, auditability, and path-level discrimination.

The revised benchmark experiments on the TON_IoT-based graph mapping indicate that HGRAD provides stronger validation and held-out test performance than the compared baselines under the adopted protocol, while module-level and stage-level ablations support the importance of the R-HAT pipeline, temporal embedding, uncertainty modeling, and physics-informed proxy nodes.
