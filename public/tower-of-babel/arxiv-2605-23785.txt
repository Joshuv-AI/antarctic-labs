FPGA Acceleration of Matrix-Element Calculations for Monte Carlo Event Generation
H. Gutiérrez Arance, F. Carrió, L. Fiorini, S. Folgueras, F. Hervás Álvarez, P. Leguina López, A. Oyanguren, A. Valero, C. Vico Villalba

Abstract
We present an FPGA-based study of matrix-element acceleration for Monte Carlo event generation, using MadGraph5_aMC@NLO as a benchmark framework. Two complementary scenarios are considered. First, we implement the full matrix-element workflow on an AMD Alveo U250 accelerator for the benchmark process e + ​ e − → μ + ​ μ − e^{+}e^{-}\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-} , enabling an end-to-end evaluation of FPGA acceleration for a simple process. Second, for the more complex g ​ g → t ​ t ¯ + X gg\to t\bar{t}+X processes with increasing jet multiplicity, we investigate FPGA acceleration of the color-algebra kernels as a structured and scalable entry point for selective acceleration. In this second case, the reported speedups correspond to the isolated color-reduction kernel operating on precomputed amplitudes, rather than to the full matrix-element evaluation or the complete event-generation workflow. The proposed implementations are developed using High-Level Synthesis and are evaluated in terms of numerical accuracy, performance, energy efficiency, resource utilization, and scalability. Compared with CPU and GPU implementations available within the MG5aMC framework, the FPGA solutions achieve substantial speedups and significantly improved energy efficiency. For the considered benchmarks, the numerical results remain in close agreement with the corresponding CPU reference calculations, while the resource analysis highlights the importance of numerical representation in determining scalability on FPGA devices. These results support the use of FPGAs as a competitive architecture for selected Monte Carlo event-generation workloads in high-energy physics.

Introduction

Due to the intrinsically probabilistic nature of fundamental particle interactions and the composite structure of the proton, accurately modelling proton collisions at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) remains one of the major challenges in high-energy physics (HEP). Physics event generators provide the computational framework required to describe these collisions and to obtain quantitative predictions for a given theoretical model
[
5
,
6
]
.

For a partonic initial state
i
, consisting of two incoming partons
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, evolving into a final state
X
, the hard-scattering contribution is determined by the squared matrix-element. Schematically, the corresponding partonic cross section can be written as

\sigma(i\rightarrow X)\propto\int d\Phi\,|M_{i\rightarrow X}|^{2},

(1)

where
d\Phi
denotes the corresponding phase-space measure.

To compute the cross section for a given process
X
at the LHC, the squared matrix-element must be integrated over the accessible phase-space connecting the initial and final states. In practice, these calculations are performed in perturbation theory up to a fixed order in an expansion in powers of the coupling constant, which quantifies the strength of the interaction. Physics event generators therefore rely on Monte Carlo (MC) integration techniques, which approximate the integral by sampling random points in the accessible phase-space. Modern MC generators provide predictions at leading order (LO), while some also support higher-order calculations such as next-to-leading order (NLO) and next-to-next-to-leading order (NNLO)
[
5
,
10
]
. These corrections are often essential for precision phenomenology, especially given the ever-increasing size of LHC datasets
[
6
]
. However, increasing the perturbative order leads to a rapid and non-linear growth in computational complexity, placing severe demands on computing resources
[
6
]
.

The repeated evaluation of matrix-elements over large samples of independent phase-space points makes Monte Carlo event generation particularly attractive for hardware acceleration. This opportunity has already motivated significant effort towards accelerating matrix-element integration and event generation on vectorized CPU and GPU platforms
[
8
,
22
,
20
,
13
,
21
,
12
]
.

While CPUs and GPUs have already demonstrated the value of heterogeneous computing for event generation
[
8
,
21
,
12
]
, the use of Field-Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) in this domain remains relatively unexplored
[
7
,
11
]
. FPGAs enable custom hardware architectures tailored to specific computations, offering fine-grained parallelism and deeply pipelined execution for arithmetic-intensive workloads. They also provide an attractive energy-efficiency profile, which is particularly relevant for large-scale HEP computing, where power consumption is an increasingly important constraint. Historically, FPGA programming has been challenging for complex algorithms such as those arising in MC event generators, due to the large number of arithmetic operations and the structured control flow associated with Feynman-diagram calculations. Recent advances in high-level synthesis (HLS)
[
3
]
tools have significantly lowered these barriers, making FPGA-based acceleration increasingly accessible
[
16
,
9
]
.

Previous work by the authors demonstrated the feasibility of porting MG5aMC-derived computations to FPGA platforms using high-level synthesis for a simple benchmark process and a floating-point implementation
[
11
]
. In the present work, we extend that proof of concept in two complementary directions: first, by introducing a fixed-point end-to-end FPGA implementation of the full matrix-element workflow for
e^{+}e^{-}\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}
; and second, by investigating selective FPGA acceleration of color-algebra kernels for
gg\to t\bar{t}+X
processes with increasing jet multiplicity. Using
MadGraph5_aMC@NLO
as a benchmark framework and targeting an AMD Alveo U250 accelerator
[
5
]
, we present a comparative study of numerical accuracy, throughput, energy efficiency, resource utilization, and scaling behavior across FPGA, CPU, and GPU platforms.

MadGraph5_aMC@NLO as a benchmark for hardware acceleration

MadGraph5_amc@nlo
(MG5aMC)
[
5
]
is one of the most widely used frameworks for Monte Carlo event generation in high-energy physics. In recent years, it has also incorporated significant acceleration-oriented developments for GPUs and vector CPUs
[

,
20
,
13
,
21
,

]
. In particular, the CUDACPP plugin enables the acceleration of leading-order (LO) processes on these platforms through extensive refactoring for data-parallel execution, with a primary focus on matrix-element calculations, which are traditionally one of the main computational bottlenecks in event-generation workflows
[
21
]
.

These developments have demonstrated substantial performance improvements, although the achieved speedup depends on the process, numerical precision, and hardware platform. GPU acceleration has been shown to yield matrix-element speedups ranging from one to two orders of magnitude relative to single-threaded Fortran implementations, with significant gains also observed at the workflow level
[
8
,
21
,
12
]
. At the same time, once the matrix-element calculation is accelerated, other stages of the event-generation chain may become the limiting factor
[
8
,
12
]
.

The MG5aMC framework is particularly well suited as a benchmark for hardware-acceleration studies because it combines physics flexibility with automatic code generation
[
5
]
. Once the process definition, model parameters, and integration settings are fixed, MG5aMC produces process-specific code for the corresponding matrix-element calculations. This makes it possible to study metrics such as throughput, latency, numerical accuracy, and resource usage starting from a realistic and widely used HEP software stack, and therefore provides a natural benchmark framework for assessing FPGA acceleration.

In this work, MG5aMC is used as a benchmark framework in two complementary ways. First, we consider the benchmark leptonic process
e^{+}e^{-}\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}
, which provides a controlled baseline for implementing the full matrix-element workflow on the FPGA. Second, we consider the more complex hadronic processes
gg\to t\bar{t}+X
, for which the complete event-generation workflow becomes substantially more demanding. In this second case, it is not feasible to generate the full matrix-element workflow for all benchmark processes in the target FPGA platform. Hence we focus on the color-algebra kernels, because they constitute a computationally intensive and structurally regular component of the generated code, and therefore provide a realistic target for FPGA acceleration.

Within MG5aMC, the relevant event-generation workflow can be decomposed into several stages, including random-number generation, phase-space sampling, and matrix-element evaluation
[
5
,
23
,
12
]
. For complex multi-parton processes, the matrix-element calculation remains one of the dominant costs, and, within it, the color algebra becomes increasingly expensive as the final-state multiplicity grows. This observation motivates the two benchmark strategies adopted in this work: a full-workflow FPGA implementation for a simple process, and a kernel-level acceleration study for increasingly complex
gg\to t\bar{t}+X
color-algebra kernels.

3
Methodology

This section presents the methodology used to assess the feasibility of FPGA-based acceleration for Monte Carlo event generation. The study combines two complementary perspectives: a full-workflow implementation for the benchmark process
e^{+}e^{-}\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}
, and a kernel-level acceleration study of the color-algebra kernels for
gg\to t\bar{t}+X
processes with increasing jet multiplicity.

The target FPGA platform is an AMD Alveo U250 accelerator card
designed for data-center and high-performance computing applications. Its numerical accuracy, performance, resource usage, and energy efficiency are evaluated by comparison with CPU and GPU implementations available within the MG5aMC framework.

3.1
Target Hardware Platform

The AMD Alveo U250 accelerator card is based on the Xilinx UltraScale+ XCU250 device
[
2
,
1
]
. Unlike system-on-chip FPGA platforms, the Alveo U250 operates as a PCIe-attached accelerator and does not include embedded general-purpose processors. Instead, it is designed to accelerate compute kernels managed by a host CPU, making it well suited to heterogeneous high-performance computing workflows.

The XCU250 device provides 1,759,631 Look-Up Tables (LUTs), 3,660,140 Flip-Flops (FFs), 12,424 Digital Signal Processor (DSP) slices, and 5,442 Block RAM (BRAM) units. These resources enable the implementation of highly parallel and deeply pipelined hardware architectures, while the DSP blocks provide efficient support for numerically intensive arithmetic operations.

The platform also integrates multiple external DDR4 memory banks and supports PCIe Gen3 communication with the host system, enabling the handling of large data streams and intermediate buffers.

Overall, the combination of programmable logic, dedicated DSP resources, and external memory bandwidth makes the Alveo U250 a suitable platform for accelerating Monte Carlo event-generation kernels, particularly in dataflow-oriented designs aimed at high-throughput execution.

3.2
Event Implementation

In matrix-element generators such as MG5aMC, the evaluation of each event follows a well-defined computational workflow. First, a phase-space point is sampled and a valid external momentum configuration is generated for the particles in the process. Then, for each phase-space point, the corresponding matrix-element is evaluated and used to determine the event weight. Although these steps are traditionally executed sequentially for a single event, they are naturally parallel across large samples of independent phase-space points
[
23
]
.

Figure
1
illustrates the general event-generation workflow in MG5aMC, from process definition and diagram generation to phase-space sampling, matrix-element evaluation, and event output. This workflow provides the conceptual framework for the implementation strategies considered in this work.

In this work, we focus on the stages of this workflow that are most relevant for FPGA acceleration. In particular, matrix-element evaluation constitutes the dominant computational cost, while color algebra becomes an increasingly important component for more complex multi-parton processes. The following subsections describe how these stages are implemented and mapped onto the target FPGA platform.

3.2.1
Phase-space sampling and momentum generation

The generation of phase-space points is the first step in the event-generation workflow. It consists of constructing valid kinematic configurations for the external particles from pseudo-random inputs, subject to energy–momentum conservation. In this work, we employ a RAMBO-based
[
15
]
phase-space generator to produce the corresponding sets of four-momenta.

Each phase-space point defines a complete external momentum configuration and serves as input to matrix-element evaluation. In the present implementation, pseudo-random number generation is performed on the host system, while the phase-space points are constructed directly on the FPGA accelerator. This enables a streaming execution model in which random inputs are transferred from the host and the full kinematic configuration is generated on-device.

Although the computational cost of phase-space generation is much smaller than that of matrix-element evaluation, it remains an essential component of the overall workflow. Integrating this stage within the FPGA pipeline reduces data-movement overheads and allows the event-generation chain to be organized as a continuous streaming process.

3.2.2
Matrix-Element computation

The evaluation of the matrix-element consists of computing the squared scattering amplitude for a given phase-space point, defined by the four-momenta of the external particles. Within the MG5aMC framework, this calculation is performed by evaluating the relevant Feynman-diagram contributions and summing them over the allowed helicity and color configurations
[
5
]
.

From a computational perspective, this procedure involves a structured sequence of arithmetic operations on real and complex quantities, including products, sums, and the recursive evaluation of intermediate wavefunctions. For a fixed process definition, the computation follows a deterministic pattern, which makes it suitable for hardware acceleration.

A key property of matrix-element evaluation is that different phase-space points can be processed independently. This enables the concurrent evaluation of multiple events, while the repeated arithmetic structure of the calculation can be exploited to construct parallel and pipelined execution schemes.

In this work, the matrix-element computation is mapped onto the AMD Alveo U250 using a custom streaming architecture. Phase-space points generated on the FPGA are fed directly into the computation pipeline, where different stages operate concurrently. This approach exploits both event-level and operation-level parallelism, leading to deeply pipelined processing units with predictable latency. For simple benchmark processes, this structure makes end-to-end hardware mapping feasible. For more complex multi-parton processes, however, the overall hardware cost of the full matrix-element calculation grows rapidly, motivating a more selective acceleration strategy.

3.2.3
Color algebra calculation

For the gluon-initiated
gg\to t\bar{t}+X
processes considered in this work, one of the final reduction steps in matrix-element evaluation consists of combining the partial amplitudes associated with different color configurations. In the MG5aMC framework, this is typically formulated through a color-flow decomposition, in which the total amplitude is expressed in terms of color-flow components that are subsequently contracted with a color matrix
[
5
]
.

More specifically, the squared matrix-element can be written as

|\mathcal{M}|^{2}=\sum_{i,j}\mathcal{A}_{i}^{*}\,C_{ij}\,\mathcal{A}_{j}

(2)

where
\mathcal{A}_{i}
denote the color-decomposed amplitudes and
C_{ij}
is the corresponding color matrix. This operation performs the contraction over color indices and constitutes a structured reduction step within the matrix-element calculation.

From a computational perspective, color algebra has a regular and well-defined structure, since it consists of structured matrix–vector operations applied to the set of partial amplitudes. This makes it particularly suitable for hardware acceleration, as it can be mapped efficiently onto parallel and pipelined architectures with regular dataflow and arithmetic patterns. In this sense, the color-reduction stage is especially hardware-friendly when compared with the broader matrix-element pipeline, whose wavefunction and amplitude evaluation involve more complex control flow and a less tractable hardware cost.

2
shows the execution-time breakdown for the representative
gg\to t\bar{t}+2
jets process. As expected, matrix-element evaluation dominates the total runtime, while the internal breakdown of the matrix-element kernel shows that the color-related stages constitute only a relatively small fraction of the full computation for this benchmark. Nevertheless, unlike wavefunction and amplitude evaluation, the color-algebra component has a highly regular structure and a substantially more tractable hardware cost than the full matrix-element pipeline. Moreover, previous work has identified color computation in MG5aMC as a relevant target for dedicated optimization in high-multiplicity QCD processes
[
17
]
. This reinforces the choice of color reduction as a meaningful kernel for selective FPGA acceleration, not only because of its growing relevance in complex processes, but also because of its regular and hardware-friendly structure.

In this work, color algebra therefore plays a central role in the evaluation of
gg\to t\bar{t}+X
processes, where we focus on accelerating this specific component of the computation. This enables the study of selective acceleration strategies, in which the color reduction is executed on the FPGA while the remaining parts of the matrix-element evaluation are kept on the host side. The choice is motivated not only by its increasing relevance for higher-multiplicity QCD processes, but also by the fact that its regular structure makes it a particularly suitable hardware target within a heterogeneous HEP computing workflow.

3.3
FPGA implementation

The computational workflow described in Section
3.2
is mapped onto the FPGA in order to exploit its inherent parallelism.
For the benchmark process
e^{+}e^{-}\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}
, the full event-evaluation chain is implemented on the FPGA, enabling end-to-end acceleration of the workflow. For the more complex
gg\to t\bar{t}+X
processes, a selective acceleration strategy is adopted, focusing on the color algebra as a structured and computationally intensive component of the calculation.

Together, these two approaches make it possible to study how resource utilization, numerical representation, and achievable performance evolve as process complexity increases. All FPGA kernels are developed using a HLS flow and integrated within the Xilinx Vitis toolchain.

3.3.1
Overall architecture

The computational workflow described in Section
3.2
is implemented on a heterogeneous architecture composed of a host CPU and an FPGA accelerator, namely the AMD Alveo U250
[
2
,
1
]
. The host system is responsible for runtime control, pseudo-random input preparation, and result retrieval, while the FPGA executes the core computational stages of the event-evaluation pipeline.

Data transfer between the host and the accelerator is performed through a PCIe Gen3 interface. Input data are first written to global memory (DDR4) accessible by the FPGA, from which they are consumed by the hardware kernels. The FPGA executes the subsequent stages of the computation on-device and writes the resulting observables back to global memory for retrieval by the host.

The FPGA kernel follows a streaming dataflow architecture, as illustrated in Figure
3
. The computation is decomposed into a sequence of processing stages connected through on-chip streaming channels (
hls::stream
), which are executed concurrently through the use of the
#pragma HLS DATAFLOW
directive.

The host CPU manages runtime control, input preparation, and result retrieval, while the AMD Alveo U250 executes the streaming computational pipeline. Depending on the target workflow, the compute stage implements either the full matrix-element evaluation or the color-algebra kernel.

The overall pipeline consists of four main stages:

•

Input loader
, which reads data from global memory and formats them into streaming packets.

•

Phase-space generation
, where particle momenta are constructed using a RAMBO-based algorithm
[

.

•

Compute stage
, which performs either the full matrix-element evaluation or the color-algebra kernel depending on the target workflow.

•

Output writer
, which packs the results and writes them back to global memory.

Each stage is internally pipelined to sustain high-throughput execution, while the streaming interfaces allow continuous data propagation across the pipeline. This architecture provides a unified execution model for both implementation strategies considered in this work: full event evaluation for simple processes, and selective acceleration of the color-algebra stage for more complex ones.

3.3.2
Full matrix-element implementation for
e^{+}e^{-}\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}

For the benchmark process
e^{+}e^{-}\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}
, the full matrix-element computation is implemented on the FPGA, enabling end-to-end acceleration of the event-evaluation pipeline. In this approach, the complete workflow, from random-input consumption to final matrix-element evaluation, is executed on-device.

The implementation is organized into three main processing stages. First, pseudo-random numbers are read from global memory through an AXI interface and unpacked into fixed-size data packets, which are then forwarded through streaming channels to the phase-space generation stage.

In the second stage, phase-space momenta are generated directly on the FPGA using a RAMBO-based algorithm adapted for hardware execution. The implementation operates on fixed-point data types and computes the four-momenta of the external particles while enforcing energy–momentum conservation in the center-of-mass frame. The resulting momenta are streamed to the next stage without intermediate storage in external memory.

The third stage performs the matrix-element evaluation through a hardware implementation of the HELAS formalism
[
18
]
, in which wavefunctions, propagators, and interaction vertices are explicitly computed using fixed-point arithmetic and complex data types. The amplitudes associated with the individual Feynman diagrams are then evaluated and coherently summed to obtain the total scattering amplitude for a given helicity configuration.

The summation over helicity states is fully unrolled, enabling the concurrent evaluation of all helicity contributions. For each configuration, the squared amplitude is computed from the real and imaginary parts of the complex amplitude, and the results are accumulated using a wider accumulator in order to avoid overflow. The final matrix-element is obtained after averaging over the initial-state helicities.

Overall, the design exploits both event-level parallelism, through the continuous processing of independent phase-space points, and operation-level parallelism, through pipelining and loop unrolling within each stage. The full matrix-element implementation relies on a fixed-point numerical representation with explicit scaling of physical quantities, including particle momenta and masses. This strategy reduces resource utilization, while the resulting numerical accuracy for the benchmark process is assessed in Section
4.1
. Its scalability, however, is ultimately limited by the increasing complexity of the matrix-element, particularly for processes involving a larger number of diagrams and non-trivial color structures.

3.3.3
Color-algebra acceleration for
gg\to t\bar{t}+X
processes

For more complex processes, such as
gg\to t\bar{t}+X
, mapping the full matrix-element computation onto the FPGA becomes increasingly constrained by resource usage and arithmetic complexity. For this reason, a selective acceleration strategy is adopted, focusing on the color algebra as a structured and computationally intensive component of the overall workflow.

In this approach, the FPGA does not evaluate the full set of wavefunctions, propagators, and interaction vertices. Instead, the precomputed
jamp
amplitudes associated with the different color configurations are transferred to the FPGA, where the color-reduction kernel is evaluated. The accelerator therefore computes the contraction of these amplitudes with the corresponding color matrix, yielding the color contribution to the squared matrix-element. The corresponding execution flow is illustrated in Figure
4
.

Figure 4
:
Overview of the selective acceleration strategy used for the color-algebra kernels. The host CPU prepares the precomputed
jamp
amplitudes and transfers them to the AMD Alveo U250 through PCIe and global memory. The FPGA executes a streaming dataflow pipeline composed of an input loader, the color-reduction kernel, and an output writer. The resulting color factors are written back to global memory and retrieved by the host.

From a computational perspective, the color algebra corresponds to a matrix–vector contraction followed by a reduction over color indices,

\Delta\mathrm{ME}=\sum_{i}A_{i}^{*}\sum_{j}C_{ij}A_{j}

(3)

where
A_{i}
are the color-flow amplitudes,
C_{ij}
is the color matrix, and
\Delta\mathrm{ME}
denotes the color-reduction contribution evaluated by the FPGA kernel. For the FPGA implementation, this contraction is evaluated using a folded triangular form that is analytically equivalent to the full double sum used in MG5aMC. This reformulation exploits the fact that the color matrix is real and symmetric: diagonal terms are treated explicitly, while off-diagonal contributions are traversed only once through the upper-triangular part of a precomputed normalized matrix, whose coefficients already include the corresponding symmetric factor. This structure is particularly well suited to FPGA implementation, since it can be mapped efficiently onto parallel multiply–accumulate operations with regular data-access patterns.

The complexity of this operation grows rapidly with the number of external partons, as reflected in the size of the color basis. In the
gg\to t\bar{t}+1
jet and
gg\to t\bar{t}+2
jets cases, the color basis contains 6 and 24 amplitudes, respectively, allowing a direct implementation of the matrix–vector contraction on fixed-size blocks of complex amplitudes. For the
gg\to t\bar{t}+3
jets process, the color basis increases to 120 amplitudes, leading to a significantly larger computational workload and memory footprint.

In this case, the kernel is organized as a multi-stage dataflow architecture, in which the computation is decomposed into modules responsible for data loading, unpacking, matrix-product evaluation, and output packing. The color matrix is stored on-chip as a constant ROM array, while the matrix-product kernel exploits loop unrolling and partial array partitioning to increase arithmetic parallelism.

This organization makes it possible to sustain high throughput despite the increased complexity of the color-algebra, while keeping the hardware cost within the available FPGA resources. Accordingly, the reported performance for these kernels should be interpreted as kernel-level acceleration results, rather than as end-to-end matrix-element or event-generation speedups. Overall, this approach shows that the color algebra provides a scalable entry point for FPGA acceleration, allowing increasingly complex processes to be studied without requiring the full matrix-element pipeline to be mapped onto the device.

3.3.4
Numerical representation

The FPGA implementations considered in this work employ different numerical representations depending on the complexity and structure of the target kernel. In all cases, the numerical format is chosen as a compromise between hardware cost and numerical accuracy, with the goal of preserving sufficient precision while keeping resource usage within the limits of the target device.

For the full matrix-element implementation of
e^{+}e^{-}\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}
, a fixed-point numerical representation is adopted throughout the main computational pipeline. This choice is motivated by the reduced hardware cost of fixed-point arithmetic compared to floating-point arithmetic, while the resulting numerical accuracy for the benchmark process is evaluated in Section
4.1
. Multiple fixed-point formats are employed across the different stages of the pipeline: a 24-bit fixed-point format is used for both phase-space generation and the internal matrix-element computation, while wider formats are used at the memory interface and for the accumulation over helicity configurations. This organization reflects the fact that the scaled kinematic variables and internal amplitudes can be represented within a compact range, whereas accumulation and output stages require additional headroom to accommodate growth in magnitude without overflow.

For the color-algebra kernels associated with
gg\to t\bar{t}+X
processes, the numerical representation is adapted to the complexity of each configuration. Unless otherwise stated, floating-point arithmetic refers to single-precision FP32 arithmetic. FP32 is used for the one- and two-jet cases, where the color bases contain 6 and 24 amplitudes, respectively, and the corresponding kernels can be implemented without excessive resource pressure. For the three-jet case, where the color basis increases to 120 amplitudes, a floating-point implementation becomes significantly more demanding in terms of FPGA resources. To address this, a fixed-point representation with explicit scaling is adopted, using compact formats for the input amplitudes together with progressively wider formats for matrix coefficients, intermediate products, and accumulation. This reflects the different numerical requirements of each stage: the input amplitudes can be stored in a reduced range, whereas the matrix–vector products and final reduction require additional integer width to preserve dynamic range and avoid overflow.

The selected formats result from an empirical compromise between synthesis feasibility, dynamic-range requirements, and the numerical behavior observed for the considered benchmarks. More specifically, the integer and fractional widths were selected by combining four criteria: the dynamic range required by the corresponding physical quantities, the need to avoid overflow in intermediate products and accumulations, the numerical agreement with the CPU reference, and the resource/timing constraints of the target FPGA. Compact formats were preferred whenever possible in order to reduce DSP usage and routing pressure, while wider types were introduced in stages involving accumulation, rescaling, or matrix–vector products, where the growth in magnitude is more significant. A key aspect of the full matrix-element implementation is the explicit scaling of particle momenta and mass parameters. The four-momenta are scaled by a constant factor chosen as a power of two,

p_{\mathrm{scaled}}=\frac{p}{S},\qquad S=2^{10},

(4)

which reduces the dynamic range of the kinematic variables while preserving relative precision. This scaling keeps derived quantities, such as invariant masses and propagator denominators, within a numerically stable range compatible with the fixed-point representation. The parameters of the electroweak model are adapted consistently to the same scheme: particle masses and widths are scaled together with the momenta, whereas dimensionless quantities such as coupling constants remain unchanged.

The full matrix-element computation is performed using complex fixed-point arithmetic, in which both real and imaginary components are explicitly represented. The squared matrix-element is then expressed in terms of real-valued combinations of these components, allowing the final reduction to be implemented using real arithmetic. In addition, a wider fixed-point representation is used at the memory-interface level in order to ensure compatibility with the external memory system and to avoid precision loss during data transfers.

For the fixed-point color-algebra implementation, the input color-flow amplitudes are represented using compact 16-bit fixed-point real and imaginary components. The color-matrix coefficients and intermediate products use wider formats, while the final color reduction is accumulated using a dedicated fixed-point accumulator. This mixed-precision organization reduces the hardware pressure associated with the substantially larger three-jet color basis while preserving sufficient dynamic range for the scaled inputs and intermediate quantities.

This adaptive strategy makes it possible to match the numerical representation to the complexity of the target workload: FP32 arithmetic provides a straightforward implementation with good numerical behavior for the simpler kernels, whereas fixed-point arithmetic enables a more resource-efficient implementation for the most demanding case. The numerical impact of these choices is evaluated in Section
4.1
.

3.4
Benchmark setup and evaluation metrics

The FPGA, CPU, and GPU implementations are evaluated in terms of both computational performance and energy efficiency. The primary performance metric is throughput, defined as the number of events processed per unit time. In addition, power consumption is measured in order to derive the energy per event, which provides a normalized metric for comparing different hardware architectures.

The FPGA execution is managed through the Xilinx Runtime (XRT), with kernels invoked from a Python-based host application
[
4

4.1
Numerical validation

We first assess the numerical accuracy of the proposed FPGA implementations against reference results obtained with MG5aMC. For the full matrix-element implementation, the validation includes both phase-space generation and matrix-element evaluation. For the color-algebra studies, the validation is performed independently for each kernel. The reported statistics are based on a sample of
3\times 10^{7}
randomly generated events. The CPU reference is evaluated in double precision, whereas the FPGA implementations use reduced-precision numerical representations. In addition to relative errors, absolute error metrics are also reported, since they are particularly informative in regions where the reference value becomes small and relative differences may become less representative. To provide further context for these absolute deviations, we also consider the least significant bit (LSB) of the main fixed-point formats used in each implementation. For a type
ap_fixed<W,I>
, the LSB is given by
2^{-(W-I)}
, and represents the nominal resolution of the corresponding quantized quantity. In the present implementations, however, the reported absolute deviations reflect the cumulative effect of quantization across multiple pipeline stages, including input representation, intermediate products, casts, accumulation, and final reconstruction. They should therefore not be interpreted as deviations arising from a single quantization step or from the LSB of a single format alone.

Full matrix-element implementation

For the full FPGA implementation of the
e^{+}e^{-}\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}
process, two main sources of numerical discrepancy can be identified: the precision of the generated momenta and the precision of the matrix-element evaluation itself.

The momenta are generated on the FPGA using a fixed-point representation within the RAMBO algorithm. Their comparison with the CPU reference shows a mean relative error of 0.020%, with a standard deviation of 0.027% and a maximum observed relative deviation of 0.138%. This indicates that the kinematic configuration is reproduced with high accuracy.

The matrix-element evaluation exhibits a mean relative error of 0.160%, with a standard deviation of 0.256% and a maximum observed relative deviation of 1.352%, as summarized in Table

Despite the comparison against a double-precision CPU reference, the observed discrepancies remain small, suggesting that, for this benchmark, the reduced-precision FPGA implementation remains in close numerical agreement with the full matrix-element calculation.

For reference, the dominant internal fixed-point format used in the full matrix-element pipeline is
ap_fixed<24,8>

4.3
Throughput efficiency analysis

To better interpret the measured performance, the experimental results are compared with the ideal throughput expected from the target clock frequency and the pipeline initiation interval (II). For a design operating at a clock frequency
f_{\mathrm{clk}}
, the ideal throughput of a single compute unit is given by

\mathrm{Throughput}_{\mathrm{ideal,\,CU}}=\frac{f_{\mathrm{clk}}}{\mathrm{II}},

(5)

so that the corresponding ideal aggregate throughput for a design with
N_{\mathrm{CU}}
compute units is

\mathrm{Throughput}_{\mathrm{ideal}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{CU}}\,f_{\mathrm{clk}}}{\mathrm{II}}.

(6)

The corresponding ideal time per event is therefore

t_{\mathrm{ideal}}=\frac{\mathrm{II}}{N_{\mathrm{CU}}\,f_{\mathrm{clk}}}.

(7)

The efficiency of the measured implementation with respect to this ideal limit is then defined as

\eta=\frac{\mathrm{Throughput}_{\mathrm{measured}}}{\mathrm{Throughput}_{\mathrm{ideal}}}.

(8)

These expressions refer to the ideal steady-state behavior of the pipeline and therefore neglect startup, drain, and synchronization overheads.

The resource-utilization trends for the color kernels reflect both the rapid growth in computational complexity with jet multiplicity and the strong impact of the adopted numerical representation. In particular, the larger color bases in the two-jet and three-jet cases lead to a substantial increase in hardware cost with respect to the one-jet kernel. However, the adopted numerical representation also plays a major role in determining the final resource footprint. For this reason, the two-jet and three-jet implementations should not be interpreted as a like-for-like comparison of hardware cost at fixed numerical precision, but rather as an illustration of how numerical representation reshapes the resource footprint of increasingly complex kernels.

For the floating-point implementations, DSP usage dominates, reflecting the cost of complex arithmetic in the matrix-contraction stage. This effect is especially pronounced in the
t\bar{t}+2
jets case, where the combination of a larger color basis and floating-point arithmetic drives the utilization to 45.1% of LUTs and 74.8% of DSPs, placing the design close to the practical resource limits of the device.

This strong resource pressure motivates the transition to fixed-point arithmetic in the three-jet implementation. Despite the larger problem size, the use of a more compact numerical representation makes it possible to reduce LUT and DSP usage with respect to a floating-point design of comparable complexity, while enabling timing closure. This comes at the expense of a substantial increase in BRAM usage, which reflects the larger storage requirements of the adopted architecture.

These results show that the achievable scalability of the color kernels is determined jointly by the complexity of the color basis and by the numerical representation used to implement it. In particular, the use of fixed-point arithmetic in the three-jet kernel makes it possible to realize an FPGA implementation for a substantially larger problem than would be practical with a purely floating-point design.

Overall, both the complexity of the target physics process and the chosen numerical representation play a central role in determining the scalability of FPGA-based implementations. In practice, resource usage and timing closure jointly constrain the achievable level of parallelism, particularly for the more demanding color-algebra kernels.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented an FPGA-based approach to the acceleration of matrix-element calculations in high-energy physics, considering two complementary scenarios: a full event-level implementation for the benchmark process
e^{+}e^{-}\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}
, and a kernel-level acceleration study of the color algebra for
gg\to t\bar{t}+X
processes with increasing jet multiplicity. The proposed designs rely on streaming architectures and deeply pipelined execution to exploit fine-grained parallelism and sustain high-throughput processing.

For the
e^{+}e^{-}\to\mu^{+}\mu^{-}
process, the complete matrix-element workflow was mapped onto the FPGA, demonstrating that end-to-end acceleration of event evaluation is feasible on this platform. For this benchmark, the FPGA implementation achieves a speedup of up to
95.7\times
over the Intel i7-13700 CPU baseline,
86.7\times
over the RTX 3050,
10.0\times
over the RTX 6000, and
6.15\times
over the H100 in the 8-CU configuration. The numerical comparison against the double-precision CPU reference indicates that the reduced-precision FPGA implementation remains in close numerical agreement with the reference for this benchmark, while providing a substantial gain in performance and energy efficiency.

For the more complex
gg\to t\bar{t}+X
processes, the study focused on the color algebra as a representative structured kernel within the MG5aMC workflow. In this case, the reported speedups correspond to the isolated color-reduction kernel operating on precomputed amplitudes, and should not be interpreted as speedups of the full matrix-element evaluation or of the complete event-generation workflow. Within this scope, the results show that FPGA acceleration is particularly effective for this class of workload, with the observed improvement increasing strongly with process complexity. In particular, for the
t\bar{t}+3
jets kernel, the FPGA implementation outperforms the AMD EPYC 9474F, Intel i7-13700, RTX 6000, and H100 baselines by factors of about
389\times
,
560\times
,
245\times
, and
85\times
, respectively. This supports the use of selective acceleration strategies as a practical and scalable alternative when the full matrix-element computation becomes too demanding to map efficiently onto the device.

A central outcome of this work is the strong impact of numerical representation on performance, scalability, and resource utilization. Floating-point implementations provide a straightforward and accurate solution for moderate problem sizes, but they rapidly become resource intensive, especially in DSP usage. In contrast, the adoption of fixed-point arithmetic in the more demanding kernels enables a substantially more efficient use of hardware resources and makes timing closure possible for larger problem sizes, at the cost of an increase in numerical deviation. For the benchmarks considered here, this impact remains limited enough to preserve reasonable numerical agreement while enabling a much more efficient hardware realization.

At the same time, the results highlight the main limits of the current approach. The scalability of the FPGA implementations is ultimately constrained by hardware resources, in particular DSP availability, as well as by routing complexity, timing-closure pressure, and memory-system effects. These factors prevent ideal linear scaling at the largest parallel configurations and become increasingly relevant as the complexity of the target process grows.

Future work will focus on extending this strategy to more complex physics processes and on exploring more advanced optimization techniques. In particular, mixed-precision arithmetic, improved memory organizations, and multi-FPGA scaling appear as promising directions for further increasing performance and broadening the applicability of FPGA-based acceleration in high-energy physics simulations.

Overall, this work shows that FPGAs constitute a viable and flexible platform for accelerating matrix-element calculations. Taken together, these results indicate that FPGAs are not only a practical target for selected MG5aMC kernels, but also a competitive architecture for future heterogeneous Monte Carlo event-generation workflows in data centers for high-energy physics.
