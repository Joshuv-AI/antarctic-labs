Where diverse populations gather: Transit accessibility and the spatial structure of social mixing
Yuan Liao

Abstract
Urban venues serve as arenas for social mixing, where individuals from different socioeconomic backgrounds share space during daily activities. While residential and activity-space segregation have been extensively studied, less is known about how public transit infrastructure shapes the spatial structure of social mixing at specific locations. This study examines how transit accessibility influences visitor diversity at points of interest (POIs) in nine cities in Sweden and the United States (New York, Washington DC, Atlanta), using mobile phone GPS traces and aggregated foot traffic data in 2024. We employ spatial regression models and geographically weighted regression (GWR) to test whether transit catchment diversity, the socioeconomic heterogeneity of populations reachable by public transit, predicts visitor diversity, and the spatial heterogeneity of this relationship. Transit catchment diversity is a consistent positive predictor of visitor diversity across nearly all cities, with the strongest effects in large metropolitan areas and where transit links demographically distinct neighborhoods. However, the relationship is much more spatially clustered in US cities, where transit-mixing hotspots emerge in peripheral, lower-diversity areas, whereas in Swedish cities diversity is more spatially diffuse and hotspots occur in already-diverse neighborhoods, suggesting that transit compensates for local homogeneity in the former but amplifies existing diversity in the latter. These results suggest that transit infrastructure functions as a spatial bridge, channeling diverse populations to common destinations and reshaping the geography of social mixing in urban space, particularly for venues located farther from city centers and in less-dense areas.

Introduction

Cities host diverse populations within limited geographic space, yet this proximity does not automatically translate into exposure across group boundaries
(
18
)
.
This is partly due to residential segregation, socioeconomic and ethnic groups reside in separate neighborhoods, limiting everyday contact with each other
(
5
;
22
;
24
)
.
Such everyday contact across group boundaries has only recently become measurable at scale thanks to increasingly available big geolocation data from our mobile phones: a growing body of research now reveals that experienced diversity, the composition of people one actually encounters, often differs substantially from what residential composition alone would predict
(
4
;
23
;
25
;
31
;
19
)
.
Points of interest (POIs), such as restaurants, shops, and parks, serve as the physical sites where populations from different neighborhoods converge or fail to converge
(
26
)
.
Recent work has demonstrated that certain locations attract visitors from across the urban socioeconomic spectrum, while others primarily serve local, homogeneous populations
(
25
)
.
Understanding what makes some places more socioeconomically mixed than others is a central question for urban planning aimed at fostering inclusive cities
(
6
)
.

The social mixing diversity at various urban venues depends on two distinct pools of potential visitors: local visitors who walk in from the surrounding neighborhood, and those who arrive from farther areas via transportation infrastructure
(
21
)
.
Visitor diversity at a location thus reflects both the residential composition of its immediate surroundings (residential diversity) and the diversity of populations made accessible by mobility networks.
For example,
23
revealed that 55% of experienced income segregation in US cities is attributable to mobility patterns rather than residential composition alone.
This decomposition motivates an analytical separation between local residential diversity and transit catchment diversity, the composition of populations reachable through public transport.
The key empirical question is the extent to which realized visitor mixing exceeds what the local residential composition alone would predict, and whether that excess is attributable to the populations that transit delivers.

Public transit infrastructure plays a demonstrated role in shaping social mixing.
Experienced racial isolation is lower in US metropolitan areas with greater public transit use
(
4
)
, bus and metro trips are positively associated with place-level social mixing in Hong Kong
(
21
)
, and transit structures shape mode-specific income mixing patterns
(
16
)
.
Conversely, limited transit access constrains the mobility of foreign-born minorities, reinforcing their experienced segregation
(
19
)
.
Yet these findings primarily establish aggregate, city- or individual-level associations between transit provision and segregation outcomes, leaving the spatial mechanisms underspecified.
We propose that transit nodes function as “spatial bridges”: because transit funnels passengers from neighborhoods of varying composition through common stations and along fixed corridors, the catchment area of a well-connected stop aggregates populations from multiple origins, creating a pool of potential visitors more diverse than any single surrounding neighborhood
(
25
)
.
This mechanism operates through different channels, including pedestrian dispersal from transit nodes into surrounding commercial areas
(
15
;
9
)
, the agglomeration of amenities catering to varied populations near transit hubs

(
8
;
7
)
, and trip-chaining behavior that extends the spatial footprint of transit-facilitated visits

(
30
;
12
)
.
All these channels yield a common prediction: visitor diversity should exhibit spatial spillover, with the diversity of one POI depending on that of nearby locations.
Whether such spillover is structured by the transit network or merely reflects general spatial proximity remains an open question.

To examine the spatial structure of social mixing at urban venues, we introduce a cross-national comparison of Swedish and US cities.
The cities in these two countries differ systematically in urban form, transit orientation, and the structure of social inequality.
The major Swedish cities are compact and transit-oriented, with extensive public transportation networks; segregation there operates along both birth-background and economic lines, with foreign-born minorities disproportionately concentrated in peripheral, lower-income housing estates where daily mobility tends to reproduce rather than reduce residential sorting
(
27
;
14
;
19
)
.
The US cities span a gradient from transit-rich (New York) to car-dependent, with segregation shaped largely by income and race amid substantially higher inequality
(
25
;
23
)
.
This variation provides analytical leverage.
Let’s consider transit accessibility as the transit catchment populations within a certain travel time from a venue.
In the Swedish cities, where transit coverage is extensive and widely used, transit catchment populations may be a weaker differentiator of actual social mixing at venues precisely because most locations are transit-accessible.
In the US cities, where transit access varies sharply across space, transit catchment populations should more strongly distinguish socially mixed venues from homogeneous ones.
Examining birth-background diversity alongside income diversity —connected but distinct dimensions of social stratification —allows us to observe how the transit — mixing relationship manifests across different forms of inequality rather than assuming they behave the same way.

Specifically, this study addresses three research questions:

•

To what extent does the socioeconomic diversity of a POI’s transit catchment populations predict the realized diversity of its visitors?

•

Does this relationship exhibit spatial spillover effects, and if so, are spillovers better explained by general geographic proximity or by transit-network proximity specifically?

•

How does the transit–mixing relationship vary spatially within cities?

Our analysis makes three contributions.
First, we introduce a spatially explicit framework for analyzing social mixing at POIs that distinguishes between residential composition, transit catchment diversity, and realized visitor diversity, connecting the “supply” of diversity through transit networks to the mixing actually observed at venues.
Second, we test whether visitor diversity spills over between nearby POIs and, if so, whether this spillover follows the transit network rather than geographic distance alone, providing direct evidence on the mechanism linking transit infrastructure to social mixing.
Third, we embed these analyses in a cross-national comparison of Swedish and US cities, assessing whether the transit–mixing relationship holds across different urban forms, transit systems, and dimensions of social inequality.

2
Results

We analyze approximately 200,000 POIs across three US cities (New York, Washington DC, Atlanta) and 130,000 POIs across nine Swedish cities using mobile phone-derived mobility data from 2024 (see Section
4.1
Study Areas).
For Sweden, anonymized GPS trajectories from 6.5 million devices are processed into POI visits via stop detection and home-location inference; for the US, we use aggregated weekly foot traffic records reporting visitor counts and home census tract distributions (see Sections
4.2
Data Sources and
4.3
Data Preprocessing).

Social mixing at each POI is quantified as normalized entropy of visitors’ home-neighborhood composition (Section
4.4
Quantifying Social Mixing), yielding values from 0 (all visitors from one socioeconomic category) to 1 (visitors evenly distributed across categories).
We compute this metric along two dimensions — birth background (native-born vs. foreign-born in Sweden and the US) and income (national income quartiles) — and apply it to three population bases: actual visitors (
visitor diversity
), the POI’s home census zone (
residential diversity
), and the population reachable within 45 minutes by public transit (
transit catchment diversity
).
We start by mapping and comparing these three diversity measures using bivariate choropleth plots and spatial autocorrelation analysis (see Section
4.5
Describing Spatial Structure of Social Mixing).
The rest of this section then attempts to answer the three research questions.
We estimate OLS and spatial lag regression models to quantify the independent contribution of transit catchment diversity to visitor mixing, testing whether spatial spillovers follow geographic proximity or transit network connectivity.
In addition, we employ geographically weighted regression (GWR) to examine within-city spatial heterogeneity in the transit–mixing relationship and characterize where transit catchment diversity most strongly predicts realized visitor mixing (see Sections
4.7
–
4.8
).
Full methodological details are provided in Materials and Methods (Section
4
).

2.1
Visitor social mixing is linked to residential and transit catchment diversity

We begin by examining the relationship among three measures of social mixing at POIs: residential diversity, which captures the socioeconomic composition of the POI’s home tract; transit catchment diversity, which reflects the composition of areas reachable within 45 minutes by public transit; and empirical visitor diversity, which measures the observed composition of actual visitors.

Focusing on the largest three Swedish cities and the selected US cities, the bivariate maps in Figure
1
a–b reveal the spatial organization of diversity.
Regarding birth background, in US cities, visitor diversity is strongly clustered in space, with distinct high-diversity and low-diversity zones that closely mirror residential and catchment patterns.
In the city center, the pattern is dominated by High–High areas (dark green), where high residential or transit catchment diversity corresponds to high visitor diversity.
Moving toward the periphery of the urban core, the pattern shifts toward light blue, indicating areas with low visitor diversity despite relatively high residential or transit catchment diversity.
In the outer urban fringes, the dominant pattern is Low–Low (gray), where low visitor diversity coincides with low residential or transit diversity.
In contrast, Swedish cities display a more spatially even distribution of visitor diversity and a weaker correspondence with local catchment composition, as reflected by the dominance of intermediate colors across the urban landscape, with fewer gray and dark green points.
While some central areas display a light blue tone, indicating lower visitor diversity despite relatively high residential or transit catchment diversity, these areas are interspersed with scattered light green patches, where high visitor diversity emerges even under similar surrounding conditions.
This mosaic-like pattern is distinctive and contrasts with the more coherent, spatially segregated structure observed in US cities.
At the same time, all three Swedish cities contain concentrated High–High clusters (dark green) in specific areas known for relatively high shares of foreign-born residents.
The boundaries between these dark green clusters and the surrounding areas also appear fairly sharp, making these pockets of high visitor and residential/transit diversity stand out clearly from the broader urban pattern.

Interestingly, when shifting from birth background to income, both Swedish and US cities exhibit weaker spatial clustering, with colors appearing more dispersed and intermixed.
Notably, some areas that show High–High patterns for birth background also overlap spatially with areas of high visitor diversity in the income dimension.
However, in the income case, these same areas often correspond to lower residential or transit catchment diversity while still maintaining relatively high visitor diversity.
This overlap highlights a key distinction: the income dimension operates differently from birth background, with realized mixing less directly aligned with the surrounding socioeconomic context.

It is worth noting that not all POIs have corresponding transit catchment diversity (see Figure
1
c) due to a lack of census tract coverage within the travel time threshold of 45 min by public transit.
Nearly all POIs in Swedish cities fall within a transit catchment, while coverage in Washington DC and Atlanta drops, covering only the core downtown areas.
Therefore, it is important to examine the three social-mixing measures separately for POIs with and without transit catchment diversity (see Figure
1
d).
Swedish cities show higher mean visitor diversity than US cities regarding income, while lower regarding birth background (0.52–0.62 vs. 0.65–0.72).
Residential, visitor, and catchment diversity differ significantly (pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests
p<0.001
), confirming that the three measures capture distinct aspects of social mixing (see Supplementary Tables S1-2).
POIs without a transit catchment are typically located in peripheral, car-oriented areas.
Although visitor diversity is still generally higher than residential diversity, in most cases significantly (
p<0.001
), POIs without transit catchment tend to remain closer to the local residential composition and thus show a smaller divergence than POIs with transit catchment measures, with a few exceptions for birth-background diversity (see Supplementary Table S1).

For all POIs, the patterns of social mixing differ markedly between visitor vs. residential diversity by country (Figures
1
a-b and Supplementary Table S1).
For birth background, both Swedish and US cities show modest gains in visitor diversity relative to residential composition, though Swedish cities exhibit greater heterogeneity—ranging from strong mixing in Stockholm (+0.130) to slightly weaker mixing in Malmö.
The most striking cross-national difference emerges for income diversity: Swedish POIs consistently attract visitors more diverse than their residential surroundings (the deviation ranging from +0.021 to +0.061), whereas US cities show the opposite pattern—visitors are substantially less income-diverse than the residential population (
-

-
0.246).
This reversal suggests that while Swedish venues draw visitors across income groups, US venues—even those with transit access—tend to attract visitors from narrower income bands than the surrounding neighborhood composition would predict.

For those POIs with transit catchment, comparing visitor diversity against transit catchment diversity reveals additional nuances (Figure
1
b and Supplementary Table S2).
For birth background, Swedish metropolitan cores (Stockholm, Gothenburg) attract visitors who are more diverse than their catchment populations, but smaller Swedish cities show the opposite—visitors are consistently less diverse than their transit-accessible population pools would suggest.
US cities follow a similar pattern: New York shows modest gains, while Washington, D.C., and Atlanta fall short.
In terms of income, Swedish cities have POIs that attract more income-diverse visitors than their transit catchment areas.
In the US, New York and Atlanta mirror this pattern, but Washington DC exhibits a pronounced deficit (-0.145): visitors are far less income-diverse than the population reachable by transit, pointing to stronger income-based sorting in venue selection despite transit accessibility.

2.2
Transit catchment diversity predicts visitor mixing with spatial spillovers driven by proximity

Having established the spatial landscape of visitor diversity, we next ask whether transit catchment diversity predicts visitor mixing and whether visitor diversity spills over between neighboring POIs.
We estimate OLS regressions to quantify the effect of transit catchment diversity on visitor diversity, controlling for POI category.
To test for spatial spillovers, we estimate spatial lag models that add a spatially weighted average of neighboring POIs’ visitor diversity,
\rho\sum_{j}w_{ij}V_{j}
, under two alternative weight specifications: W1 connects each POI to its nearest neighbors in a distance-decayed manner, capturing general spatial clustering; W2 connects POIs sharing the same nearest transit stop within 400 meters, capturing transit-mediated connectivity specifically (see Section
4.6.2
for model details and weight construction).
If transit infrastructure channels diversity spillovers between POIs, we would expect W2 to yield a larger spatial autoregressive coefficient
\rho
than W1.
Models are estimated separately for each city and diversity dimension.

The transit catchment diversity coefficient is positive and significant in nearly all cities for both diversity dimensions, confirming that POIs whose transit catchments draw from more diverse populations also tend to attract more diverse visitors (Figure
2
).
However, the magnitude of this relationship varies substantially across cities and between dimensions.
For birth-background diversity (Figure
2
a), the three US cities and Gothenburg exhibit the largest coefficients, with New York standing out at approximately 0.5.
Atlanta and Gothenburg follow closely, around 0.3. Stockholm and Washington DC cluster in the 0.2 range.
The mid-sized Swedish cities, Uppsala, Malmö, Linköping, Helsingborg, Västerås, and Örebro, show smaller but still significant coefficients between 0.1 and 0.2.
For income diversity (Figure
2
b), the overall pattern is similar, but the coefficients are considerably smaller in magnitude.

On birth-background social mixing, we see strong spatial spillover effects of visitor diversity and a systematic difference between Swedish and US cities in both baseline model fit and the nature of spatial dependence.
The spatial lag models with W1, which connects each POI to its nearest neighbors (with distance decay), consistently outperform OLS, with
\rho
positive and significant in nearly every city–dimension combination.
The coefficient
\rho
under W1 ranges from approximately 0.5 (Washington DC) to 0.9 (Malmö), meaning that when nearby POIs attract diverse visitors, a given POI tends to do the same.
However, W2, which connects POIs that share the same nearest transit stop, yields
\rho
values that are either non-significant or negligibly small across all Swedish cities.
In other words, knowing that two venues sit near the same bus or train stop does not help predict whether they share similar levels of visitor diversity, once general geographic proximity is accounted for.
This suggests that in Swedish cities, the spatial pattern of social mixing reflects broader neighborhood characteristics rather than a transit-station-specific spillover mechanism.

The US cities tell a different story in two respects.
First, the basic OLS models already explain far more of the variation in birth-background diversity, with
R^{2}
values of 0.51-0.67, compared with 0.04–0.14 for Swedish cities.
This likely reflects the stronger spatial structure of racial and ethnic segregation in US metropolitan areas, where residential composition and transit geography are more tightly coupled.
Second, W2 produces statistically significant spatial dependence across all three US cities, although the direction and magnitude of this dependence vary.
New York and Washington DC exhibit a positive coefficient, indicating that transit-mediated connectivity matters: venues clustered around the same station tend to share similar diversity levels, even after accounting for general proximity.
However, Atlanta shows a small but significant negative coefficient, indicating a tendency toward dissimilarity among POIs sharing transit connections.
Still, W1 delivers larger improvements in model fit than W2, suggesting that geographic clustering remains the dominant spatial pattern even where transit spillovers are detectable.

Together, we identify that transit catchment diversity is a significant predictor of visitor mixing in most cities, with the strongest effects in large cities like New York and in cities where transit networks bridge demographically distinct neighborhoods, such as Gothenburg and Atlanta.
The relationship is stronger for birth-background than for income diversity.
Spatial lag models confirm that diverse venues cluster near other diverse venues, but this clustering follows general geographic proximity (W1) rather than shared transit stops (W2) in Swedish cities.
Only in the US cities does transit-specific connectivity contribute significant additional explanatory power.
These findings suggest that transit infrastructure shapes visitor diversity primarily by channeling populations into surrounding areas, as captured by the catchment diversity measure, rather than through direct spillovers between venues at the same stop.
The role of transit as a driver of social mixing is thus indirect: it is linked to who is nearby, and neighborhood-level processes then distribute that diversity across local venues.

2.3
Spatial variation in transit effects on birth-background visitor mixing

The preceding analyses estimate global relationships that assume the transit–mixing association is spatially constant within each city.
To examine whether this relationship varies across space, and to identify where transit catchment diversity most strongly translates into realized visitor mixing, we employ geographically weighted regression (GWR).
GWR estimates location-specific coefficients for each POI using an adaptive bisquare kernel, with bandwidth optimized by corrected AIC (see Section
4.7
).
Statistical significance of local coefficients is assessed using the da Silva–Fotheringham correction for multiple testing.
We classify POIs as
transit–mixing hotspots
where the local transit catchment coefficient is both positive and significant, then characterize these hotspots by their distance to the city center, surrounding POI density, and proximity to the nearest transit stop.
A logistic regression jointly models the predictors of hotspot status (see Section
4.8
).

Across cities, the GWR results (all POIs by city and social-mixing dimension) reveal substantial spatial heterogeneity in the relationship between transit catchment diversity and visitor diversity, with clear differences between the birth background and income dimensions (Supplementary Table S5).
For birth background, most cities exhibit predominantly positive local coefficients (typically 58-76%), indicating a generally positive association between transit accessibility and birth-background mixing, although the magnitude and spatial concentration of this effect vary markedly.
Notably, US cities, especially New York, show much stronger and more spatially concentrated effects, reflected in high hotspot shares (e.g., 60.1%) and substantially higher model fit (
R^{2}\approx.80
), whereas Swedish cities display weaker and more diffuse patterns with lower
R^{2}
values.
In contrast, income-based results are less stable and more heterogeneous, with larger coefficient variability, weaker model fit (generally
R^{2}<.15
), and less consistent spatial patterns across cities.
Given these differences, subsequent analyses focus on birth-background diversity in top cities where the GWR achieves relatively sufficient explanatory power (
R^{2}>.15
), as these cases provide more reliable and interpretable evidence of how transit accessibility shapes spatial patterns of social mixing.
This gives us the three Swedish cities, Gothenburg, Malmö, and Uppsala, and the three US cities, New York, Atlanta, and Washington, DC (Figure
3
).

Focusing on the diversity of birth backgrounds, the spatial distribution of local coefficients reveals striking cross-national differences in the transit-mixing relationship.
In US cities, the association between transit catchment diversity and visitor diversity is predominantly positive and spatially consistent (Figure
3
), particularly in New York.
In contrast, Swedish cities exhibit weaker, more spatially heterogeneous patterns.
While positive coefficients still predominate (58–63% of POIs), the mean effects are smaller (0.05–0.20) and exhibit greater local variation (Supplementary Table S5).
Notably, only 1–2% of Swedish POIs qualify as significant hotspots after correcting for multiple testing (Supplementary Table S5), suggesting that the transit–mixing relationship, though present on average, is not statistically distinguishable from zero at most individual locations.
This pattern is consistent with Sweden’s more uniform residential diversity, which limits the potential for transit to generate spatial diversity gradients.
In both Swedish and US cities, POIs in central, transit-dense areas do not exhibit notably higher visitor diversity than those elsewhere, despite the concentration of both transit infrastructure and amenities in these locations.
This pattern suggests that the agglomeration of POIs around transit hubs does not, by itself, amplify the translation of transit catchment diversity into realized visitor mixing.

This spatial heterogeneity in the transit catchment diversity effect raises a natural question: where within cities does transit catchment diversity most strongly translate into realized visitor diversity?
Transit-mixing hotspots — locations where transit catchment diversity significantly predicts visitor diversity — exhibit distinct spatial and contextual characteristics that differ between Swedish and US cities (Figure
4

Hotspot rates vary substantially by venue category and urban context, with US cities showing higher overall rates, particularly for food and dining establishments (Figure

Compared with non-significant POIs, hotspots in the US show a consistent profile: they tend to occur in areas with lower residential diversity (Figure

4

In Sweden, the spatial pattern is similar in terms of centrality, local density, and transit proximity—hotspots also tend to lie farther from city centers, in less dense commercial clusters, and farther from transit stops—but they differ markedly in social context, being located in areas with higher residential and transit diversity.

In both countries, hotspots are located away from city centers and in less-dense commercial clusters (Figure
4
).
But they diverge on residential/transit diversity and transit proximity.
This divergence suggests that transit catchment diversity translates into realized visitor diversity through fundamentally different spatial mechanisms in the two national contexts.
In US cities, hotspots concentrate in lower-diversity, peripheral locations where the surrounding residential population is relatively homogeneous — suggesting that transit functions as a diversity bridge, importing heterogeneous visitor flows into areas that would otherwise attract uniform populations.
The greater distance to transit stops further implies that these are not immediate station-area effects but rather diffuse spillovers along corridors where transit-connected visitors disperse into neighborhoods with few alternative sources of population mixing.
In Swedish cities, by contrast, hotspots emerge in already-diverse neighborhoods closer to transit stops, indicating that transit amplifies pre-existing residential diversity rather than compensating for its absence.
Here, the mechanism appears to operate through reinforcement: diverse local populations combine with diverse transit-delivered flows, and the proximity to stops ensures that this compounding effect is spatially concentrated.
The shared pattern — peripheral locations in less-dense commercial clusters — suggests that, in both contexts, central, high-density commercial areas may already achieve visitor diversity through sheer volume and variety of visitors, regardless of transit catchment composition.
It is at the urban periphery, where visitor pools are thinner and more dependent on specific access channels, that transit catchment composition becomes a decisive predictor.
This aligns with the theoretical expectation that transit’s role in shaping social mixing should be most detectable where alternative pathways to diversity are limited.

In summary, while hotspot formation in both urban systems is negatively associated with dense local commercial environments, it is not concentrated in the most central or immediately transit-adjacent locations.
Instead, hotspots appear to arise in more spatially selective settings, particularly in the US.
These findings are further corroborated by the logistic regression results (Supplementary Table S6).

3
Discussion

This study examined how transit accessibility predicts the spatial structure of social mixing at urban venues across Swedish and US cities.
By distinguishing between residential diversity, transit catchment diversity, and realized visitor diversity, we tested whether the socioeconomic heterogeneity of populations reachable by public transit predicts the diversity of visitors at points of interest, whether this relationship exhibits spatial spillovers, and how the transit-mixing association varies across urban space.
Our findings reveal consistent but context-dependent relationships about the potential and the limits of transit infrastructure as a driver of social mixing.

Our results support the hypothesis that transit catchment diversity independently predicts visitor mixing at POIs, even after controlling for local residential composition.
The finding aligns with prior work demonstrating that mobility patterns account for a substantial share of experienced segregation beyond what residential sorting alone would predict
(
23
;
4
)
, and extends this literature by identifying transit catchment composition as a specific mechanism through which infrastructure shapes encounter probabilities at the venue level.

However, the spatial lag model results complicate a simple “transit as connector” narrative.
We hypothesized that if transit nodes function as spatial bridges, visitor diversity should spill over more strongly between POIs sharing the same transit stop (W2) than between POIs that are merely geographically proximate (W1).
This prediction was not supported in Swedish cities, where W2 contributed no additional explanatory power beyond general proximity.
In US cities, W2 showed significant spatial dependence, but W1 consistently yielded larger improvements in model fit.
This pattern suggests that transit shapes visitor diversity primarily by channeling populations into surrounding areas (as captured by the catchment diversity measure), rather than through direct station-to-station spillovers.
The mechanism is thus indirect: transit delivers diverse populations to neighborhoods, and local processes then distribute that diversity across nearby venues.

A central contribution of this study is the identification of fundamentally different spatial mechanisms linking transit to social mixing across national contexts.
In US cities, transit-mixing hotspots concentrate in lower-diversity, peripheral locations where the surrounding residential population is relatively homogeneous, suggesting that transit functions as a
diversity bridge
, importing heterogeneous visitor flows into areas that would otherwise attract uniform populations.
In Swedish cities, by contrast, hotspots emerge in already-diverse neighborhoods closer to transit stops, indicating that transit
amplifies
pre-existing residential diversity rather than compensating for its absence.
This divergence likely reflects differences in urban form and segregation structure: Swedish cities exhibit more spatially diffuse visitor diversity (Moran’s I = 0.05–0.24) compared with the strong clustering observed in US cities (Moran’s I = 0.35–0.83), limiting the potential for transit to generate diversity gradients as sharp as in US cities.

The shared finding across US and Swedish cities that hotspots occur in peripheral, less commercially dense areas rather than in central transit hubs offers additional insight.
Central areas may already achieve visitor diversity through sheer volume and variety of visitors, regardless of transit catchment composition.
It is at the urban periphery, where visitor pools are thinner and more dependent on specific access channels, that transit catchment composition becomes a decisive predictor.
This aligns with theoretical expectations that transit’s role in shaping social mixing should be most detectable where alternative pathways to diversity are limited
(
25
)
.

Our analysis shows that the transit–mixing relationship is stronger and more spatially structured for birth background than for income.
Birth-background diversity is strongly clustered in US cities and moderately clustered in Swedish cities, whereas income-based mixing is much less spatially organized in both contexts, likely because racial and ethnic segregation is more firmly embedded in neighborhood boundaries while income sorting operates through finer-grained or venue-specific mechanisms.
The clearest cross-national contrast appears for income: Swedish POIs attract visitors who are more income-diverse than their surroundings, whereas US POIs attract visitors who are less income-diverse, suggesting that transit accessibility alone does not guarantee income-diverse mixing.

Our findings carry implications for urban planning and transit policy aimed at fostering inclusive cities.
The consistent positive association between transit catchment diversity and visitor mixing suggests that expanding transit access to demographically diverse catchments can support social mixing at destination venues.
However, the cross-national differences caution against one-size-fits-all prescriptions.
In contexts resembling US cities, where residential segregation is spatially concentrated and transit coverage is uneven, extending transit to peripheral, lower-diversity areas may have the greatest potential to introduce diverse visitor flows where they are otherwise absent.
Transit-oriented development strategies that prioritize connectivity across demographically distinct neighborhoods, rather than simply densifying already-accessible central areas, may be most effective at leveraging transit’s bridging potential.
In contexts resembling Swedish cities, where transit coverage is already extensive and residential diversity more spatially diffuse, the policy lever shifts from expanding access to ensuring that transit-served areas maintain diverse residential populations.
Here, housing policies that prevent socioeconomic homogenization of transit-accessible neighborhoods may be as important as transit investments themselves.
In both contexts, the finding that central, high-density commercial areas do not exhibit stronger transit–mixing effects suggests that the mixing benefits of transit are not automatic consequences of agglomeration.
Planners should consider the full spatial footprint of transit networks, including peripheral nodes and corridor spillovers, rather than focusing solely on central hubs.

This study has several limitations.
First, birth-background measures are not directly comparable across countries, as Sweden distinguishes native- vs. foreign-born (excluding Europe-born) while the US captures racial and ethnic composition; results should be interpreted within rather than across national contexts.
Second, the analysis is observational, and although we control for key factors and account for spatial dependence, unobserved confounders may remain.
Third, mobile phone data may be subject to selection bias despite weighting and large sample sizes.
Fourth, key parameters such as the 45-minute catchment threshold are somewhat arbitrary, though results appear robust.
Finally, we measure co-presence rather than actual social interaction, meaning observed diversity reflects the potential for contact rather than realized social mixing.
Future work could strengthen causal identification, test alternative definitions of accessibility, improve cross-national harmonization of diversity measures, and link co-presence more directly to realized social interaction and longer-term social outcomes.

3.1
Conclusions

In summary, this study provides the first spatially explicit, cross-national analysis of how transit accessibility predicts social mixing at urban venues.
We find that transit catchment diversity consistently predicts visitor diversity, but the spatial structure of this relationship differs markedly between Swedish and US cities (New York, Washington DC, and Atlanta).
In US cities, transit functions as a diversity bridge, importing heterogeneous visitor flows into otherwise homogeneous peripheral areas.
In Swedish cities, transit amplifies pre-existing residential diversity in already-mixed neighborhoods.
Spatial spillovers in visitor diversity are driven primarily by geographic proximity rather than transit-network connectivity, suggesting that transit shapes mixing through the populations it delivers to neighborhoods rather than through direct station-level mechanisms.
These findings advance our understanding of how urban infrastructure mediates social stratification in everyday life.
They also highlight the importance of context: the same infrastructure can operate through different mechanisms depending on the urban form, segregation structure, and transit coverage in which it is embedded.
As cities worldwide invest in public transit to address climate, equity, and livability goals, understanding these contingent relationships is essential for designing systems that foster not only mobility but also the social mixing that proximity makes possible.

4
Materials and Methods

4.1
Study Areas

We examine nine cities in Sweden and three cities in the United States, representing distinct urban mobility regimes.
The Swedish cities include Stockholm, Gothenburg, and Malmö, the country’s three largest metropolitan areas, along with six mid-sized cities (Uppsala, Västerås, Örebro, Linköping, Helsingborg, and Lund).
These cities are characterized by relatively integrated public transit networks and compact urban forms.
The US cities, New York, Washington DC, and Atlanta, span a range of transit provision, from New York’s dense subway network to Washington DC’s radial metro system to Atlanta’s car-dependent metropolitan form.
In addition, the selected US cities are substantially larger in population (6–19 million) than the Swedish cities (0.7–2.4 million), allowing us to examine how the transit-mixing relationship varies across different metropolitan sizes.

4.2
Data Sources

4.2.1
Points of Interest

Venues of social mixing in this study are operationalized using points of interest (POIs).
For Sweden, POI data were obtained from SafeGraph Global Places
(
32
)
.
The US mobility data come with integrated POI information from the same underlying source.
The dataset provides comprehensive coverage of physical locations where people spend time or money, including restaurants, retail stores, recreational facilities, and public services.
Each POI record includes geographic coordinates, address, brand affiliation, and category classification using NAICS codes.

4.2.2
Mobility Data

For Sweden, we use anonymized GPS trajectory data collected from approximately 6.5 million mobile devices throughout 2024.
The raw geolocation records are processed using Infostop
3
, a stop-detection algorithm to identify stationary periods that may represent potential POI visits.
Stays are defined as sequences in which a device remains within a 30-meter radius for at least 15 minutes, yielding approximately 175 million detected stops across the study period
(
19
)
.
The mobility data are further processed to have the same structure as the US foot traffic data.

For the United States, we use Advan Weekly Patterns Plus data
(
1
)
throughout 2024.
This dataset provides weekly foot traffic aggregations for POIs derived from a panel of anonymized mobile device locations.
The data cover three metropolitan areas: New York (14.1 million POI-week records, 290,270 unique POIs), Washington DC (4.0 million records, 84,650 POIs), and Atlanta (4.8 million records, 102,181 POIs).
The dataset spans 53 weeks from January 2024 through early January 2025, capturing approximately 48 billion total visits across the three cities. Each record reports the number of visitors to a POI during a given week, along with the distribution of visitors’ home CBGs, enabling computation of visitor diversity without individual-level tracking.

4.2.3
Socioeconomic Data

For Sweden, socioeconomic characteristics are obtained from Statistics Sweden (SCB) register-based statistics at the DeSO (Demografiska statistikområden) level for the year 2024
(
33
)
.
Birth background data categorize the population into three groups: born in Sweden, born elsewhere in Europe, and born outside Europe.
Income data include the distribution of residents across national income quartiles based on total earned income (sammanräknad förvärvsinkomst), along with median and mean income values.

For the United States, socioeconomic data come from the 2024 5-year American Community Survey (ACS) estimates at the census tract level (median population 4,000)
(
34
)
.
We obtain household income distributions from ACS Table B19001, which reports household counts across 17 income brackets ranging from under $10,000 to $200,000 or more.
We aggregate these brackets into national quartiles using cutoffs of $30,000 (Q1), $60,000 (Q2), and $100,000 (Q3), assigning each tract a population-weighted distribution across the four quartiles.
Birth background data distinguishes native-born from foreign-born residents.
To enable diversity calculations, we compute the proportion of each tract’s population in each socioeconomic category.

4.2.4
Transit Network Data

For Sweden, transit network data were obtained from Samtrafiken AB via its publicly available API in the General Transit Feed Specification (GTFS) static format
(
19
)
.
Public transit networks for the three US cities were obtained from their respective metropolitan transit agencies.
For New York, we combine GTFS feeds from the Metropolitan Transportation Authority (MTA), including the subway system, local and express bus routes, the Long Island Rail Road (LIRR), and Metro-North Railroad, capturing the full multimodal transit network serving the New York metropolitan area.
For Washington DC, we use GTFS data from the Washington Metropolitan Area Transit Authority (WMATA), encompassing the Metrorail heavy rail system and Metrobus network serving the District of Columbia, northern Virginia, and suburban Maryland.
For Atlanta, we obtain GTFS data from the Metropolitan Atlanta Rapid Transit Authority (MARTA), which operates both heavy rail and bus services in the Atlanta metropolitan area.

The GTFS data were up to date and processed to remove invalid route shapes and extract the geolocations of all transit stops.
OpenStreetMap road network data were obtained from Geofabrik to enable multimodal routing
(
13
)
.
The processed GTFS data and road network data serve as the basis for constructing transit-proximity spatial weights and for computing accessibility measures using
r5r

(
29
)
, an open-source multimodal transport routing package.

4.3
Data Preprocessing

4.3.1
Mobility Data Processing (Sweden)

Home locations are identified using the HoWDe (Home and Work Detection) algorithm
(
11
)
, which classifies stops based on temporal visitation patterns.
The algorithm identifies home locations from stops occurring during residential hours (before 6:00 and after 18:00) over a 14-day rolling window, applying density and frequency thresholds to distinguish consistent home patterns from transient visits.
This procedure achieves a 71.4% home detection rate.

To ensure reliable home location quality, we link detected home coordinates to residential buildings from Overture Maps
(
28
)
using a 50-meter buffer to account for GPS positioning uncertainty.
Only devices with both a detected home location and a confirmed residential building match (85.2% match rate) are retained for analysis.
Home locations are mapped to DeSO zones (Demographic Statistical Areas, median population of 1,500).
To correct for sampling bias in the mobile phone panel, we apply inverse probability weighting (IPW) based on the population size of each DeSO zone
(
19
;
20
)
.

Detected stays are matched to SafeGraph POIs using a tiered spatial assignment approach.
Stops falling inside a POI’s building footprint polygon (available for 91% of POIs) receive the highest confidence assignment; stops within 20 meters of a POI centroid are assigned with high confidence, accounting for GPS uncertainty; stops within 50 meters receive medium confidence; and stops within 100 meters receive low confidence assignments.
Stops within 100 meters of a device’s home location are excluded to focus on out-of-home activities.
This tiered approach yields a 46.1% match rate, producing approximately 80 million POI-linked visits that are aggregated to origin-zone-to-POI flows weighted by IPW, producing a dataset structurally comparable to the US foot traffic data.

4.3.2
POI Category Processing and Harmonization

To ensure cross-national comparability, we developed a unified POI categorization scheme that harmonizes venue classifications across Swedish and US datasets.
We mapped SafeGraph sub-category labels to 12 unified categories: food and dining, general retail, specialty retail, personal services, pharmacy and drug stores, entertainment and recreation, accommodation and travel, financial services, professional services, automotive, higher education, and civic and community venues.
We retained only categories achieving at least 50% visitor home census block group coverage in the US foot traffic data, ensuring sufficient geographic information for reliable diversity estimation.
Several category groups were systematically excluded: healthcare providers (due to HIPAA privacy restrictions in US data), K-12 schools, and childcare facilities (available in the data for Sweden but not for the US), transit infrastructure (analyzed separately via GTFS data), and non-public venues such as manufacturing facilities, warehouses, and real estate offices.
To ensure reliable downstream calculations, we further filtered POIs to include only those with at least 50 total visits and visitors originating from at least 5 distinct residential zones, yielding final samples of approximately 200,000 POIs across the three US cities and 130,000 POIs across the Swedish cities.

4.3.3
Transit Catchment Populations

For each POI, we define its transit catchment as the set of residential zones reachable within 45 minutes by a combination of walking and public transit.
Transit accessibility is computed using the
r5r
package
(
29
)
, which implements the R5 routing engine to calculate realistic door-to-door travel times accounting for scheduled service, transfers, and walking segments.
We combine GTFS transit schedules with road network data to enable multimodal routing.

Travel times are computed for a typical weekday morning departure (8:00 AM) with a 15-minute time window to account for schedule variability, using the median travel time across departures within this window.
We set a maximum total trip duration of 45 minutes (including walking and waiting time), with a maximum walk time of 15 minutes at either end of the journey and an assumed walking speed of 4.5 kmh.
Residential zones (census tracts in the US, DeSO zones in Sweden, represented by centroids) with median travel times at or below this threshold are included in the POI’s catchment.
The socioeconomic composition of catchment populations forms the basis for quantifying potential social mixing at each POI.

4.4
Quantifying Social Mixing

For each POI, we compute
visitor diversity
as the entropy of the socioeconomic composition of its visitors’ home neighborhoods.
Let
p_{i}
denote the proportion of visitors originating from neighborhoods in socioeconomic category
i
.
The entropy-based diversity index is:

H=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\ln(p_{i})

(1)

We normalize by maximum entropy (
\ln k
) to obtain values between 0 (all visitors from one category) and 1 (visitors evenly distributed across categories).
We compute separate indices for two dimensions: (1) birth background (native-born vs. foreign-born), and (2) income (quartiles of neighborhood median income).
For Sweden, the original three-category birth-background classification is collapsed into a two-group entropy measure: Sweden-born and born outside Europe, with Europe-born individuals excluded.
This is intended to focus the analysis on forms of migration-related diversity that are more likely to structure social boundaries and unequal exposure in everyday urban life, while avoiding the inclusion of Europe-born populations whose socioeconomic and cultural integration patterns may differ substantially from those of non-European migrants
(
19
)
.

Using the same metric (Equation
1
),
transit catchment diversity
measures the socioeconomic heterogeneity of populations reachable from each POI within 45 minutes by public transit (see Section
4.3.3
), applied to the population-weighted socioeconomic distribution of reachable census zones.

Residential diversity
captures the socioeconomic composition of the POI’s immediate neighborhood, computed as the entropy of the tract/DeSO zone in which the POI is located.
This controls for the baseline expectation that POIs in diverse neighborhoods attract diverse visitors solely because of proximity.

4.5
Describing Spatial Structure of Social Mixing

We assess spatial clustering in visitor diversity using global and local measures of spatial autocorrelation.
For each city, POIs are first projected to the appropriate UTM coordinate system to ensure accurate distance calculations.
We construct a
k
-nearest neighbors (KNN) spatial weights matrix with
k=10
, connecting each POI to its 10 nearest neighbors based on Euclidean distance in projected coordinates.
The weight matrix is row-standardized so that the weights for each POI sum to unity, ensuring that the spatial lag represents a weighted average of neighboring values.

We compute Moran’s I statistic to quantify the overall degree of spatial clustering in visitor diversity:

I=\frac{n}{\sum_{i}\sum_{j}w_{ij}}\frac{\sum_{i}\sum_{j}w_{ij}(y_{i}-\bar{y})(y_{j}-\bar{y})}{\sum_{i}(y_{i}-\bar{y})^{2}}

(2)

where
n
is the number of POIs,
w_{ij}
are elements of the row-standardized spatial weights matrix,
y_{i}
is visitor diversity (normalized entropy) at POI
i
, and
\bar{y}
is the mean visitor diversity.
Values of
I>0
indicate positive spatial autocorrelation (similar values cluster together), while
I<0
indicates negative autocorrelation (dissimilar values are neighbors).

To identify statistically significant local clusters (Local Indicators of Spatial Association, LISA), we compute Local Moran’s I for each POI
(
2
)
:

I_{i}=\frac{(y_{i}-\bar{y})}{\sigma^{2}_{y}}\sum_{j}w_{ij}(y_{j}-\bar{y})

(3)

where
\sigma^{2}_{y}
is the variance of
y
.

Statistical inference is based on a conditional permutation approach: for each POI, we hold its value fixed while randomly permuting the values at all other locations, repeating this process 100 times to construct a reference distribution under the null hypothesis of spatial randomness.
A POI is classified as belonging to a significant cluster if its pseudo
p
-value (the proportion of permuted statistics as extreme as the observed) is below
\alpha=0.05
.

Significant POIs are assigned to one of four quadrants based on the relationship between the focal POI’s value and its neighbors’ values:

•

High-High (HH)
: High-diversity POIs surrounded by high-diversity neighbors (diversity hotspots)

•

Low-Low (LL)
: Low-diversity POIs surrounded by low-diversity neighbors (diversity coldspots)

•

High-Low (HL)
: High-diversity POIs surrounded by low-diversity neighbors (spatial outliers)

•

Low-High (LH)
: Low-diversity POIs surrounded by high-diversity neighbors (spatial outliers)

4.6
Models for Transit Catchment Effects on Visitor Diversity

To examine whether transit catchment diversity predicts visitor diversity at POIs, we employ two complementary regression approaches: ordinary least squares (OLS) as a baseline and spatial lag models (SLM) to account for spatial dependence.

4.6.1
OLS Baseline Model

We first estimate a standard OLS regression for each city and diversity dimension (birth background, income):

V_{i}=\alpha+\beta_{1}R_{i}+\beta_{2}C_{i}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\mathbf{D}_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}

(4)

where
V_{i}
is the normalized visitor diversity at POI
i
,
R_{i}
is the residential diversity of the POI’s home tract,
C_{i}
is the transit catchment diversity (the population-weighted diversity of areas reachable within 45 minutes by public transit),
\mathbf{D}_{i}
is a vector of dummy variables for POI category (with the five most common categories in each city plus an “other” category, one category omitted as reference), and
\varepsilon_{i}
is the error term.
The coefficient
\beta_{2}
captures the independent contribution of transit accessibility to visitor diversity, controlling for local residential composition and venue type.

4.6.2
Spatial Lag Model

OLS estimates may be biased if visitor diversity exhibits spatial dependence, i.e., if diversity at one POI is correlated with that at nearby POIs.
To address this, we estimate a spatial lag model (SLM):

V_{i}=\rho\sum_{j}w_{ij}V_{j}+\alpha+\beta_{1}R_{i}+\beta_{2}C_{i}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\mathbf{D}_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}

(5)

where
\rho
is the spatial autoregressive parameter capturing the degree of spatial spillover,
w_{ij}
are elements of a row-standardized spatial weights matrix
\mathbf{W}
defining the neighborhood structure, and
\mathbf{D}_{i}
represents venue category fixed effects as defined above.
A significant positive
\rho
indicates that visitor diversity at a POI is influenced by visitor diversity at neighboring POIs.
The SLM is estimated using the generalized method of moments (GMM)
(
17
)
.

For valid interpretation, the spatial lag model requires stationarity:
|\rho|<1
.
When this condition holds, the spatial multiplier
(I-\rho W)^{-1}
converges and total effects can be meaningfully decomposed into direct and indirect components.
Results where
|\rho|\geq 1
indicate non-stationarity, suggesting model misspecification (e.g., omitted spatially-structured variables) rather than genuine spillover effects; such cases are flagged and excluded from substantive interpretation.

We construct two alternative spatial weights matrices to test different spillover mechanisms:
W1 indicates the distance-decay weights with a cutoff, where POIs are connected based on Euclidean distance with inverse-distance weighting and a maximum threshold:

w_{ij}^{(1)}=\begin{cases}d_{ij}^{-\alpha}&\text{if }d_{ij}\leq d_{\max}\text{ and }i\neq j\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(6)

where
d_{ij}
is the distance between POIs
i
and
j
in meters,
\alpha=1
is the decay exponent, and
d_{\max}=800
m is the distance cutoff.
This specification captures pedestrian-scale spatial spillover: nearby POIs receive higher weights than distant ones, and POIs beyond typical walking distance receive zero weight.
Unlike
k
-nearest neighbors, distance-decay weights distinguish between neighbors at different distances (e.g., 50m vs. 500m) rather than treating all neighbors equally.

W2 indicates shared transit stop weights, where POIs are connected if they share the same nearest transit stop.
Each POI is first assigned to its nearest transit stop within a 400-meter radius; two POIs are then considered neighbors if and only if they are assigned to the same stop:

w_{ij}^{(2)}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }s^{*}(i)=s^{*}(j)\text{ and }d_{i,s^{*}(i)}\leq 400\text{m}\text{ and }d_{j,s^{*}(j)}\leq 400\text{m}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(7)

where
s^{*}(i)=\arg\min_{s}d_{is}
is the nearest transit stop to POI
i
, and
d_{is}
is the distance from POI
i
to stop
s
.
This specification captures transit-mediated connectivity: POIs clustered around the same transit node are assumed to draw from overlapping visitor pools.
Both matrices are row-standardized such that
\sum_{j}w_{ij}=1
for all
i
with at least one neighbor.

We compare spatial lag models with different weight specifications to test whether transit-specific connectivity (W2) better explains spatial dependence than general proximity (W1).
A larger
\rho
under W2 than W1 would suggest that transit infrastructure specifically channels diversity spillovers between POIs, supporting the hypothesis that transit nodes act as spatial bridges for social mixing.

4.7
Geographically Weighted Regression

While OLS and spatial lag models estimate global relationships, they assume that the effect of transit catchment diversity on visitor diversity is spatially constant.
To examine whether this relationship varies geographically—and to identify locations where transit infrastructure most strongly shapes social mixing—we employ Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR).

GWR extends the global regression framework by allowing coefficients to vary continuously across space.
For each POI
i
, we estimate a local model:

V_{i}=\beta_{0}(u_{i},v_{i})+\beta_{1}(u_{i},v_{i})R_{i}+\beta_{2}(u_{i},v_{i})C_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}

(8)

where
(u_{i},v_{i})
are the projected coordinates of POI
i
,
V_{i}
is visitor diversity,
R_{i}
is residential diversity,
C_{i}
is transit catchment diversity, and
\beta_{k}(u_{i},v_{i})
are location-specific coefficients.
Each local regression is estimated using a spatial kernel that assigns higher weights to nearby observations.

We use an adaptive bisquare kernel with bandwidth defined by the distance to the
k
th nearest neighbor.
The optimal bandwidth is selected by minimizing the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc):

\text{AICc}=2n\ln(\hat{\sigma})+n\ln(2\pi)+n\left(\frac{n+\text{tr}(\mathbf{S})}{n-2-\text{tr}(\mathbf{S})}\right)

(9)

where
n
is the sample size,
\hat{\sigma}
is the residual standard deviation, and
\text{tr}(\mathbf{S})
is the trace of the hat matrix, representing the effective number of parameters.
To ensure numerical stability in local estimation, we impose a minimum bandwidth of
\max(50,0.01n)
neighbors.

Prior to estimation, POI coordinates are projected from WGS84 to the appropriate UTM zone for each city to ensure accurate distance calculations.
To address duplicate or near-duplicate coordinates (e.g., multiple POIs within the same building), we add small random jitter (approximately 1 meter standard deviation).
Independent variables are standardized to improve numerical convergence, and coefficients are subsequently transformed back to their original scale for interpretation.

Statistical inference in GWR requires adjustment for multiple, spatially dependent hypothesis tests.
We apply the da Silva–Fotheringham correction
(
10
)
, which accounts for the effective number of independent tests implied by spatial smoothing.
The effective number of tests is given by

n_{\text{eff}}=\frac{n}{\text{tr}(\mathbf{S})},

(10)

yielding a corrected significance threshold below.

\alpha_{\text{corrected}}=\alpha\cdot\frac{\text{tr}(\mathbf{S})}{n}=\frac{\alpha}{n_{\text{eff}}}.

(11)

Local
t
-statistics are computed as

t_{i}=\frac{\hat{\beta}_{k}(u_{i},v_{i})}{\text{SE}_{k}(u_{i},v_{i})},

(12)

with
p
-values based on a two-tailed
t
-test using effective degrees of freedom
df=n-\text{tr}(\mathbf{S})
.
Coefficients are considered significant when
p_{i}<\alpha_{\text{corrected}}
.
We interpret local significance cautiously and primarily use it to identify broad spatial patterns rather than strict pointwise inference.

To characterize spatial heterogeneity, we summarize the distribution of the local catchment coefficient
\hat{\beta}_{2}(u_{i},v_{i})
using its mean, standard deviation, interquartile range, and coefficient of variation:

\text{CV}=\frac{\text{SD}(\hat{\beta}_{2})}{\left|\overline{\hat{\beta}_{2}}\right|}.

(13)

We further identify
transit mixing hotspots
as locations where the catchment coefficient is both positive and statistically significant under the corrected threshold, and
coldspots
as locations with significant negative effects.
This classification highlights where transit accessibility most strongly—and least strongly—predicts visitor diversity.

GWR models are estimated separately for each combination of city and diversity dimension (birth background and income).
We evaluate both POI-category-stratified GWR models and the GWR models using all POIs.
All POIs meeting data completeness requirements are included, and strata with fewer than 100 observations are excluded due to insufficient sample size for stable local estimation.

4.8
Characterizing Transit-Mixing Hotspots

To understand the spatial heterogeneity of transit-mixing effects identified through GWR, we examine the locational and contextual characteristics that distinguish hotspots—POIs where transit catchment diversity significantly predicts visitor diversity—from POIs with non-significant effects.
This analysis addresses the question:
where
do transit-mediated mixing effects concentrate, and what features characterize these locations?

We construct three additional variables to characterize each POI’s spatial context.

Distance to city center
.
For each POI, we compute the geodesic distance to the city center (in kilometers) using the Haversine formula.
City center coordinates are defined as the central business district location for each city (e.g., Times Square for New York, Central Station for Stockholm).
This variable captures whether transit-mixing effects concentrate in central urban areas or extend to peripheral locations.

POI density
.
We measure local commercial intensity as the count of other POIs within a 500-meter radius of each focal POI.
This is computed efficiently using a KD-tree spatial index on projected coordinates.
Higher POI density indicates clustering of commercial activity, which may facilitate or reflect pedestrian flows from nearby transit infrastructure.

Transit stop proximity
.
We compute the Euclidean distance (in meters) from each POI to its nearest transit stop, using GTFS (General Transit Feed Specification) data for each city.
This variable directly measures physical accessibility to transit infrastructure.

Hotspot status is taken from the pooled GWR specification model that fits all POIs jointly without stratifying by venue category, so the results characterize heterogeneity across the full set of POIs rather than within specific venue types.
We compare the distributions of each spatial variable between hotspots and non-significant POIs using non-parametric tests.
For each city, we compute bootstrapped median values with 95% confidence intervals (1,000 bootstrap replicates) and test for differences using the Mann-Whitney U test.
To visually communicate statistical significance, we display results with full opacity for cities with significant differences (
p<0.05
) and reduced opacity for non-significant comparisons.

4.8.1
Logistic Regression

To jointly model the predictors of hotspot status, we estimate a logistic regression:

\text{logit}\left(P(\text{Hotspot}_{i}=1)\right)=\mathbf{X}_{i}\boldsymbol{\beta}

(14)

where
\text{Hotspot}_{i}
is a binary indicator for whether POI
i
has a significant positive transit catchment coefficient in the GWR model,
\mathbf{X}_{i}
is a vector of standardized predictors, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
are the regression coefficients.

The predictors include the spatial context variables defined above: distance to city center (km, standardized), POI density within 500m (standardized), and distance to nearest transit stop (m, standardized).
We deliberately exclude transit catchment diversity and residential diversity from this model, as these variables are already included in the GWR specification that defines hotspot status.

We estimate separate models for Swedish and US cities to allow for context-specific effects reflecting differences in urban form and transit infrastructure.
Models are estimated using maximum likelihood estimation for binary response models.
We report odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals, computed as
\exp(\hat{\beta}\pm 1.96\times\text{SE})
.
An odds ratio greater than 1 indicates that a one-standard-deviation increase in the predictor is associated with higher odds of being a hotspot.
We assess model performance using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (ROC-AUC), computed from predicted probabilities, along with classification accuracy at a threshold of 0.5.
