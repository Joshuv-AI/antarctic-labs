Asymptotic Limits of Entanglement Distribution
Piotr Masajada, Aby Philip, Alexander Streltsov

Abstract
Reliable distribution of quantum entanglement over long distances is a central challenge in quantum information science, fundamentally limited by decoherence in noisy communication channels. In this work, we investigate the asymptotic limits of entanglement distribution across quantum networks utilizing intermediate repeater stations and local operations and classical communication (LOCC). We establish a strict dichotomy: the asymptotic preservation of entanglement over arbitrarily long distances is possible if and only if the underlying quantum channel admits a correctable subspace. For channels lacking such a subspace, we prove that the transmitted state converges exponentially fast to the set of separable states, rendering standard LOCC filtering insufficient. To counteract this exponential degradation, we analyze networks employing parallel channel uses per link. We derive a fundamental lower bound on the physical resource requirements, proving that for channels without a correctable subspace, the number of parallel channels per link must scale at least logarithmically with the number of intermediate stations to sustain a non-zero amount of entanglement. This theoretical limit serves as a stringent benchmark for quantum repeater architectures and underscores the necessity of advanced quantum error-correcting codes, such as qLDPC codes, which show promise in saturating this optimal resource scaling.



Introduction.
Quantum entanglement
20
is a fundamental resource for a wide array of quantum information processing tasks, including quantum key distribution
4
, quantum teleportation
2
, and distributed quantum computing
22
;
27
. A central challenge in realizing these technologies is the reliable distribution of entanglement between distant parties. In realistic scenarios, quantum states must be transmitted through noisy quantum channels, which inevitably interact with the environment and induce decoherence. This continuous degradation strictly limits the amount of entanglement that can be shared and maintained between remote parties.

In the standard entanglement distribution scenario
32
;
37
;
24
;
41
two distant parties, Alice and Bob, have access to a noisy quantum channel
\Lambda
. Alice first prepares a bipartite quantum state
\rho
in her laboratory, keeping one subsystem and using the channel to transmit the other subsystem to Bob. Because the transmission is imperfect, the channel acts nontrivially on the particle sent to Bob, while the particle retained by Alice remains unchanged. As a result, the initially prepared state
\rho
is transformed into the final bipartite state
\Lambda\otimes\openone[\rho]
. Any entanglement initially present in
\rho
is generally degraded by the action of the channel, and the properties of
\Lambda
determine how much entanglement can ultimately be shared between the two parties.

If the noisy channel
\Lambda
is used sequentially, the resulting state shared by Alice and Bob is given by
(\Lambda\circ\cdots\circ\Lambda)\otimes\openone[\rho]
. This setting is relevant, for example, in long-distance quantum communication through a chain of noisy transmission segments, where the quantum system effectively undergoes several consecutive applications of the same channel before reaching Bob. In general, the detrimental effects of noise accumulate with the number of channel uses, leading to a progressive degradation of the shared entanglement
25
. In many physically relevant situations, this entanglement eventually disappears altogether in the limit of infinitely many channel applications
35
.

To counteract these effects, parties can employ entanglement distillation
5
;
3
;
10
;
11
and filtering protocols
36
. Using local operations and classical communication (LOCC)
7
, they can extract high-quality entangled states from a larger ensemble of degraded ones. Additionally, the use of intermediate repeater stations can significantly enhance the feasibility of establishing entanglement over long distances
1
.

In this work, we investigate the fundamental and asymptotic limits of long-distance entanglement distribution in the presence of repeater stations. The central question we address is whether there exists
any
LOCC-based strategy capable of preserving a nonzero amount of entanglement in the asymptotic limit, that is, over arbitrarily long distances.

In this scenario, after each application of the noisy channel
\Lambda
, the parties—or the stations assisting the transmission—may apply an LOCC protocol
F_{i}
to the shared state before the next channel use. These operations can depend on previous measurement outcomes and therefore need not be identical at every step; see also Fig.
1
. Such intermediate processing stations can be viewed as local checkpoints that attempt to counteract, delay, or partially compensate for the progressive accumulation of noise. In a multipartite transmission architecture involving many channels operating in parallel, these intermediate nodes play the role of quantum repeater stations
1
;
28
. If the channel
\Lambda
is used
n
times during the transmission process, the resulting transformation of an initial state
\rho
can be written as

\Lambda_{n}[\rho]=(\Lambda\otimes\openone)\circ F_{n}\circ\cdots\circ(\Lambda\otimes\openone)\circ F_{1}\bigl[\rho\bigr].

(1)

The central question we address is whether, for a given quantum channel
\Lambda
, there exists any strategy involving such intermediate stations and filtering operations
F_{i}
that can preserve any entanglement in the asymptotic limit or as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

While various entanglement measures have been introduced in the literature, in what follows we focus on the entanglement of formation
18
. For pure bipartite states, this quantity coincides with the entanglement entropy, namely

E_{\mathrm{f}}(\psi^{AB})=S(\psi^{A}),

(2)

where
S(\rho)=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\log_{2}\rho)
denotes the von Neumann entropy.

For a mixed bipartite state
\rho^{AB}
, the entanglement of formation is defined as the minimal average entanglement entropy over all pure-state decompositions of
\rho^{AB}
, that is
40
,

E_{\mathrm{f}}(\rho^{AB})=\min_{\{p_{i},\psi_{i}^{AB}\}}\sum_{i}p_{i}E_{\mathrm{f}}(\psi_{i}^{AB}),

(3)

where the minimum is taken over all ensembles
\rho^{AB}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\ket{\psi_{i}}\!\bra{\psi_{i}}^{AB}
.

Our central question is therefore whether, for an appropriate choice of LOCC filters
F_{i}
, it is possible to retain a nonzero amount of entanglement in the asymptotic limit
n\rightarrow\infty
, that is,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda_{n}[\rho^{AB}])>0.

(4)

Single-qubit channels.
To begin, we investigate entanglement distribution through a single-qubit quantum channel
\Lambda
. We assume Alice holds a single-qubit memory that she aims to entangle with Bob’s system across a sequence of
n
transmission segments, each linked via the quantum channel
\Lambda
. We recall that the Choi state of
\Lambda
is defined as
8

\Gamma=(\Lambda\otimes\openone)(\phi^{+}),

(5)

where
\ket{\phi^{+}}=(\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt{2}
is a Bell state.

We consider the scenario in which the intermediate filtering operations
F_{i}
are allowed to be stochastic local operations and classical communication (SLOCC)
12
rather than deterministic LOCC. This broader framework is important because probabilistic filters can, upon success, increase the entanglement of the post-selected state which is not possible with deterministic LOCC. The key question is thus whether this additional freedom provided by SLOCC can preserve a nonzero amount of entanglement after many sequential applications of the channel by conditioning on successful filtering events. In the following, we show that although SLOCC filters may temporarily improve the quality of the surviving states, this does not overcome the fundamental limitations of the channel: any such apparent entanglement preservation comes at the price of a success probability that necessarily decreases with the number of channel uses. To show this, we use concurrence whose definition we recall in the Supplementary Material. Note that going forward,
P(C\geq c,n)
denotes the probability that the final state
\Lambda_{n}[\rho^{AB}]
has concurrence at least
c
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\Lambda
be an arbitrary single-qubit channel, and let
\{F_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be any sequence of intermediate independent SLOCC filters. Then, for every threshold
c>0
, the probability that the final post-selected state
\Lambda_{n}[\rho^{AB}]
has concurrence at least
c
satisfies

P(C\geq c,n)\leq\frac{C(\Gamma)^{n}}{c},

(6)

where
\Gamma=(\Lambda\otimes\openone)(\phi^{+})
is the Choi state of
\Lambda
.

The proof of the theorem can be found in the Supplemental Materials. If we demand that
P(C\geq c,n)=1
in Theorem
1
, we recover the following statement.

Corollary 2
.

For any single-qubit channel
\Lambda
the amount of concurrence
decreases as

C(\Lambda_{n}[\rho^{AB}])\leq C(\Gamma)^{n}.

(7)

We also note that this corollary has been proven using methods in
23
. We provide a proof in the Supplemental Materials.

General channels.
We now consider the more general setting in which subsystem
A
has an arbitrary finite dimension
d_{A}\geq 2
, and the channel
\Lambda
acts on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
. This extension goes beyond the single-qubit case and requires more refined tools, as two-qubit entanglement monotones such as concurrence are no longer directly applicable in higher dimensions. In the following, we say that a quantum channel
\Lambda
admits a correctable subspace
\mathcal{H}_{c}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
if there exists a recovery channel
\mathcal{R}
such that

\mathcal{R}\circ\Lambda[\psi]=\psi

(8)

for all
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_{c}

6
.

Having established the necessary groundwork, we now prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

For any quantum channel
\Lambda
, there exists a state
\rho^{AB}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda_{n}[\rho^{AB}])>0,

(9)

if and only if
\Lambda
contains a correctable subspace.

Proof.

It is clear to see that if
\Lambda
contains a correctable subspace then Eq. (
9
) holds. We will now deal with the reverse implication.

If Eq. (
9
) holds, then there must exist an entangled state
\rho^{AB}
such that

E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda\otimes\openone[\rho^{AB}])=E_{\mathrm{f}}(\rho^{AB}).

(10)

See Lemma
6
in the Supplemental Material for proof. We now show that this condition implies the existence of a pure entangled state

\ket{\psi^{AB}}
satisfying

E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda\otimes\openone[\psi^{AB}])=E_{\mathrm{f}}(\psi^{AB}).

(11)

For this, let
\{p_{i},\psi_{i}^{AB}\}
be an optimal pure state
decomposition of
\rho^{AB}
, i.e.,

E_{\mathrm{f}}(\rho^{AB})=\sum_{i}p_{i}E_{\mathrm{f}}(\psi_{i}^{AB}).

(12)

Since
E_{\mathrm{f}}
does not increase under local operations,
for each of the states
\ket{\psi_{i}^{AB}}
we have

E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda\otimes\openone[\psi_{i}^{AB}])\leq E_{\mathrm{f}}(\psi_{i}^{AB}).

(13)

By contradiction, assume that for all entangled states
\ket{\psi_{j}^{AB}}

this inequality is strict, i.e.,

E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda\otimes\openone[\psi_{j}^{AB}])<E_{\mathrm{f}}(\psi_{j}^{AB}).

(14)

Using these assumptions and convexity
39
of
E_{\mathrm{f}}
we obtain

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda\otimes\openone[\rho^{AB}])

\displaystyle=E_{\mathrm{f}}\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}\Lambda\otimes\openone[\psi_{i}^{AB}]\right)

(15)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}p_{i}E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda\otimes\openone[\psi_{i}^{AB}])

\displaystyle<\sum_{i}p_{i}E(\psi_{i}^{AB})=E_{\mathrm{f}}(\rho^{AB}).

This contradicts to Eq. (
10
), which implies that Eq. (
11
)
is true for some entangled state
\ket{\psi}^{AB}
.

Let now
\ket{\psi}^{AB}
be an entangled state such that Eq. (
11
) holds.
In the following, let
\{K_{i}\}
be a set of Kraus operators corresponding
to the quantum channel
\Lambda
. We further define the probabilities

q_{i}
and pure quantum states
\phi_{i}^{AB}
as follows:

\displaystyle q_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[K_{i}\otimes\openone\psi^{AB}K_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone\right],

(16)

\displaystyle\phi_{i}^{AB}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q_{i}}K_{i}\otimes\openone\psi^{AB}K_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone.

(17)

Without loss of generality we assume that
q_{i}>0
for all
i
.

Consider now the average entanglement of the ensemble
\{q_{i},\phi_{i}^{AB}\}
:

\sum_{i}q_{i}E_{\mathrm{f}}(\phi_{i}^{AB})=\sum_{i}q_{i}S(\phi_{i}^{B}).

(18)

By the concavity of the von Neumann entropy it holds that

\sum_{i}q_{i}S(\phi_{i}^{B})\leq S\left(\sum_{i}q_{i}\phi_{i}^{B}\right)=S(\psi^{B}).

(19)

Since the von Neumann entropy is strictly concave

, Eq. (
19
) holds with equality if and only if

\phi_{i}^{B}=\psi^{B}\,\,\,\mathrm{for\,all}\,i.

(20)

By convexity of the entanglement of formation we further have

E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda\otimes\openone[\psi^{AB}])\leq\sum_{i}q_{i}E_{\mathrm{f}}(\phi_{i}^{AB})=\sum_{i}q_{i}S(\phi_{i}^{B}).

(21)

Summarizing these arguments, the condition (
11
) implies
that
\phi_{i}^{B}=\psi^{B}
holds for all
i
. A similar argument has also been used in
17
.

In the next step we introduce a reference system
R
.
By the Stinespring dilation theorem
31
, for any quantum channel
\Lambda
there exists a unitary

U_{RA}
on
RA
such that

\Lambda\otimes\openone[\psi^{AB}]=\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[U_{RA}\left(\ket{0}\!\bra{0}^{R}\otimes\psi^{AB}\right)U_{RA}^{\dagger}\right]

(22)

with a pure state
\ket{0}^{R}
. We further define the pure state

\ket{\mu}^{RAB}=U_{RA}\left(\ket{0}^{R}\otimes\ket{\psi}^{AB}\right).

(23)

Note that any rank-1 POVM
\{M_{j}^{R}\}
on the reference system

R
gives rise to a set of Kraus operators
\{L_{j}\}
for the channel

\Lambda
, such that

\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[M_{j}^{R}\mu^{RAB}\right]=\left(L_{j}\otimes\openone\right)\psi^{AB}\left(L_{j}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone\right).

(24)

From the arguments given above we see that Eq. (
11
) implies
that the following equality holds for all rank-1 POVMs
\{M_{j}^{R}\}

and all
j
:

\mathrm{Tr}_{RA}\left[M_{j}^{R}\mu^{RAB}\right]=\mathrm{Tr}_{RAB}\left[M_{j}^{R}\mu^{RAB}\right]\times\psi^{B}.

(25)

We will now take a closer look at the state
\mu^{RB}
. Due to Eq. (
25
),
it holds that

\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[M_{j}^{R}\mu^{RB}\right]=\mathrm{Tr}_{RB}\left[M_{j}^{R}\mu^{RB}\right]\times\psi^{B}

(26)

for all POVMs
\{M_{j}^{R}\}
and all
j
. As we show in Lemma

in the Supplemental Material,
this is only possible if
\mu^{RB}
is a product state, i.e.,

\mu^{RB}=\mu^{R}\otimes\psi^{B}.

(27)

Recalling that the state
\mu^{RAB}
is pure and using Eq. (
27

we see that it is always possible to attach an ancillary system
A^{\prime}

on Alice’s side in a pure state
\ket{0}^{A^{\prime}}

on Alice’s part of the total state

Since

Theorem
3
establishes a strict qualitative dichotomy for asymptotic entanglement based entirely on the existence of a correctable subspace. A natural question that follows is how the system behaves when such a subspace is absent. In this regime, the entanglement must vanish, but understanding the rate at which it disappears is crucial for characterizing the channel’s noise properties. The following theorem provides a quantitative bound, showing that without a correctable subspace, the output state converges exponentially fast to the set of separable states, denoted by
\mathcal{S}
. In the following,
\|X\|_{1}=\mathrm{Tr}\sqrt{X^{\dagger}X}
denotes the trace norm of a matrix
X
.

Theorem 4
.

For any quantum channel
\Lambda
acting on a subsystem
A
of dimension
d_{A}
that possesses no correctable subspace, there exists a constant
\kappa\in(0,1)
such that for any initial bipartite state
\rho^{AB}
, the trace distance to the set of separable states
\mathcal{S}
satisfies

\min_{\sigma^{AB}\in\mathcal{S}}\|\Lambda_{n}[\rho^{AB}]-\sigma^{AB}\|_{1}\leq 4\kappa^{n/2(d_{A}-1)}(\sqrt{d_{A}}-1).

(33)

We refer to the Supplemental Material for the proof.

This exponential decay has severe implications for long-distance quantum communication and entanglement distribution. If we restrict each connection to a single use of a noisy channel lacking a correctable subspace, the state inevitably becomes separable over large distances. To combat this exponential degradation, we will extend the scenario to parallel uses of channels between the links. By utilizing multiple channels simultaneously, we can attempt to offset the noise accumulation. The next theorem illustrates the physical resource cost of this approach, quantifying exactly how many parallel channel uses are required to successfully establish entanglement across a network of a given length.

First, we assume that Alice and Bob are connected via linear network consisting of
n
sequential links. Second, the connection between any two adjacent nodes is modeled by
m
parallel and identical qudit depolarizing channels, each characterized by a depolarizing parameter
p\in(0,1)
, which is a noise model that inherently lacks a correctable subspace. The qudit depolarizing channel can be written as follows:

\mathcal{D}(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+p\frac{\openone}{d}.

(34)

Finally, the operational goal is to distribute a bipartite entangled state across the entire network such that the endpoint state retains a strictly positive amount of entanglement. Under these conditions, the following theorem establishes the minimum physical resource scaling required to perfectly counteract the exponential degradation of entanglement, specifically the number of parallel channels
m
per link.

Theorem 5
.

Consider a quantum communication chain of length
n
, where each link consists of
m
parallel uses of a qudit depolarizing channel
\Lambda
with depolarizing parameter
p\in(0,1)
. In order to establish non-zero entanglement between the endpoints of the network in the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
, the number of channels
m
per link must satisfy

m\geq\frac{\ln n}{\gamma}+O(1),

(35)

where
\gamma=-\ln p
and
O(1)
denotes a constant factor independent of the network length
n
.

The complete proof can be found in the Supplemental Material. We provide a brief proof outline here. We use the fact that, the depolarizing channel has at least one Kraus decomposition containing a rank-one Kraus operator. This allows to non-trivially upper bound the quantity
\kappa
from Theorem
4
. Then using Theorem
4
, we establish a relation between length of the communication chain,
n
and the number of parallel uses,
m
, of the qudit depolarizing channel.

Discussion.
In this work, we have established fundamental asymptotic limits for long-distance entanglement distribution across noisy quantum networks. We demonstrated a strict dichotomy dictating the survival of entanglement: the asymptotic preservation of entanglement over arbitrarily long distances is possible if and only if the underlying quantum channel possesses a correctable subspace. For channels lacking such a subspace, we proved that the shared state inevitably converges to the set of fully separable states exponentially fast with respect to the number of intermediate nodes, regardless of any intermediate local operations and classical communication filtering protocols applied.

To overcome this inherent exponential degradation, we extended our analysis to scenarios employing parallel channel uses between repeater links. Our central result establishes a strict theoretical lower bound on the required physical resources: to successfully distribute a non-zero amount of entanglement across a network of length
n
using depolarizing channel, the number of parallel channels per link must scale at least logarithmically with
n
. Since many full-rank quantum channels can be expressed as convex combinations involving the depolarizing channel, it is natural to expect that related scaling constraints may extend beyond the purely depolarizing case.

This fundamental lower bound provides a crucial benchmark for the evaluation and design of quantum repeater architectures and quantum error correction protocols. It is known that by employing quantum error correction codes, the number of physical resources needed to establish long-distance entanglement scales as
polylog(n)
, where n is the number of repeater stations
29
;
30
. Specifically, using surface codes, it is possible to achieve a scaling of
\mathcal{O}(log^{2}(n))

13
. It is shown by using property of surface codes stating that probability of error decreases exponentially with code distance
9
, while code distance increases as
\sqrt{m},
where
m
is the number of physical qubits
9
. A similar result has been obtained for quantum Low-Density Parity-Check (qLDPC) codes: it was shown in
15
that there exist qLDPC codes for which the error probability decreases exponentially with the code distance. More recent results established the existence of asymptotically good qLDPC codes with linear minimum distance
33
, together with efficient decoding algorithms capable of correcting a constant fraction of errors
26
. Combined with the fundamental limits derived in this work, these advances strongly suggest that the optimal resource overhead for long-distance entanglement distribution scales as
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
. Although a rigorous proof of the achievability of this scaling is still lacking, establishing such a result remains an important and compelling direction for future research.

Concurrence

For a two-qubit state
\rho
the concurrence is defined as
18

C(\rho)=\max\{\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}-\xi_{3}-\xi_{4},0\},

(36)

where
\xi_{i}
are the square roots of the eigenvalues of
\rho\tilde{\rho}
, arranged in decreasing order, and

\tilde{\rho}=(\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y})\rho^{*}(\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y}),

(37)

where
\sigma_{y}
is the Pauli
y
-matrix and
\rho^{*}
denotes complex conjugation in the computational basis. For two-qubit states, the concurrence is directly related to the entanglement of formation via

E_{\mathrm{f}}(\rho)=h\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-C(\rho)^{2}}\right),

(38)

where
h(x)=-x\log_{2}x-(1-x)\log_{2}(1-x)
is the binary entropy function.

Proof of Theorem
1

In the proof, we employ the concurrence factorization law
23
, which states that for any completely positive trace-preserving map
\Lambda
one has

C\bigl((\Lambda\otimes\openone)[\rho^{AB}]\bigr)\leq C\bigl((\Lambda\otimes\openone)[\phi^{+}]\bigr)\,C(\rho^{AB})=C(\Gamma)\,C(\rho^{AB}).

(39)

Assume that we have a state
\rho_{i}
with concurrence
C(\rho_{i}).
Let
F_{i}
be an SLOCC filter acting on
\rho_{i}
, producing an ensemble of
m
possible output states
\{\rho_{is}\}_{s=1}^{m}
with corresponding probabilities
\{p_{is}\}_{s=1}^{m}
. We denote by
\rho_{ij}=F_{i}(\rho_{i})
a particular post-selected outcome occurring with probability
p_{ij}
. Since concurrence is an entanglement monotone under SLOCC, it cannot increase on average
40
, which implies

p_{ij}C(\rho_{ij})\leq\sum_{s=1}^{m}p_{is}C(\rho_{is})\leq C(\rho_{i}).

(40)

Assume that after the action of the filter
F_{i}
, we obtain the state
\rho_{ij}
. Using Eq. (
40
), we obtain an upper bound on the probability of this event:

p_{ij}\leq\frac{C(\rho_{i})}{C(\rho_{ij})}.

(41)

Since concurrence is non-negative, using Eq. (
39
) we can write
C(\Lambda\otimes\openone(\rho))=\alpha C(\Gamma)C(\rho),
for some coefficient
\alpha\in[0,1].

Now consider the
i
-th round of the sequence. We start from the state
\rho_{i-1}
. After applying the channel
\Lambda
, we obtain the intermediate state
\sigma_{i}=\Lambda\otimes\openone(\rho_{i-1}).
Its concurrence is given by:

C(\sigma_{i})=\alpha_{i}C(\Gamma)C(\rho_{i-1}).

(42)

Next we apply an SLOCC filter to
\sigma_{i}.

We can now use the bound derived in Eq. (
41
). Treating
\sigma_{i}
as the input state to the filter, and noting that the probability
p_{i}
of obtaining specifically the state
\rho_{i}
is bounded by Eq. (
41
), we obtain:

p_{i}\leq\frac{\alpha_{i}C(\Gamma)C(\rho_{i-1})}{C(\rho_{i})}.

(43)

Assuming that each filter acts independently, the probability that all filtering steps succeed is given by

\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}C(\Gamma)C(\rho_{i-1})}{C(\rho_{i})}\leq\frac{C(\Gamma)^{n}C(\rho_{0})}{C(\rho_{n})}.

(44)

Since
C(\rho_{n})
is non-zero, we obtain exponential decrease of probability. Now putting
c=C(\rho_{n})
and noting that
C(\rho_{0})\leq 1
we finally obtain:

\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\leq\frac{C(\Gamma)^{n}}{c}.

(45)

2

where
F_{1}
is an LOCC filter. In the second inequality, we used the fact that LOCC operations cannot increase concurrence. We have thus established that Eq. (
46
) holds for
n=1
.

For the induction step, note that

\Lambda_{n+1}[\rho^{AB}]=(\Lambda\otimes\openone)\circ F_{n+1}\circ\Lambda_{n}[\rho^{AB}]=(\Lambda\otimes\openone)\circ F_{n+1}[\sigma^{AB}],

(49)

where we defined the state
\sigma^{AB}=\Lambda_{n}[\rho^{AB}]
. Now assume that Eq. (
46
) holds for some
n
. For
n+1
, we have

\displaystyle C\bigl(\Lambda_{n+1}[\rho^{AB}]\bigr)

\displaystyle=C\bigl((\Lambda\otimes\openone)\circ F_{n+1}[\sigma^{AB}]\bigr)

(50)

\displaystyle\leq C(\Gamma)\,C\bigl(F_{n+1}[\sigma^{AB}]\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq C(\Gamma)\,C(\sigma^{AB}).

By the induction hypothesis,

C(\sigma^{AB})\leq C(\Gamma)^{n}\,C(\rho^{AB}).

(51)

Combining the above inequalities, we obtain

C\bigl(\Lambda_{n+1}[\rho^{AB}]\bigr)\leq C(\Gamma)^{n+1}\,C(\rho^{AB}),

(52)

which completes the induction.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\Lambda
be a quantum channel and
\rho^{AB}
an initial state. If

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda_{n}[\rho^{AB}])>0,

(53)

then there exists an entangled state
\mu^{AB}
such that

E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda\otimes\openone[\mu^{AB}])=E_{\mathrm{f}}(\mu^{AB}).

(54)

Proof.

Let

c=\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{\mathrm{f}}\bigl(\Lambda_{n}[\rho^{AB}]\bigr),

(55)

so that
c>0
by assumption. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
N
such that

\left|E_{\mathrm{f}}\bigl(\Lambda_{k}[\rho^{AB}]\bigr)-c\right|<\varepsilon

(56)

for all
k>N
. In particular, for all
k>N
we also have

\left|E_{\mathrm{f}}\bigl(\Lambda_{k+1}[\rho^{AB}]\bigr)-c\right|<\varepsilon.

(57)

Now observe that

\Lambda_{k+1}[\rho^{AB}]=(\Lambda\otimes\openone)\circ F_{k+1}\circ\Lambda_{k}[\rho^{AB}]=(\Lambda\otimes\openone)[\sigma^{AB}],

(58)

where we define

\sigma^{AB}=F_{k+1}\circ\Lambda_{k}[\rho^{AB}].

(59)

Since the entanglement of formation is non-increasing under LOCC, it follows that

E_{\mathrm{f}}\bigl(\Lambda_{k+1}[\rho^{AB}]\bigr)\leq E_{\mathrm{f}}(\sigma^{AB})\leq E_{\mathrm{f}}\bigl(\Lambda_{k}[\rho^{AB}]\bigr).

(60)

Combining this with Eqs. (
56
) and (
57
), we obtain

c-\varepsilon<E_{\mathrm{f}}(\sigma^{AB})<c+\varepsilon,

(61)

or equivalently,

\left|E_{\mathrm{f}}(\sigma^{AB})-c\right|<\varepsilon.

(62)

Using the triangle inequality for the trace norm together with Eq. (
58
), we therefore obtain

\displaystyle\left|E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda\otimes\openone[\sigma^{AB}])-E_{\mathrm{f}}(\sigma^{AB})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|E_{\mathrm{f}}(\Lambda\otimes\openone[\sigma^{AB}])-c\right|

(63)

\displaystyle+\left|E_{\mathrm{f}}(\sigma^{AB})-c\right|<2\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
can be chosen arbitrarily small, the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 7
.

A quantum state
\rho^{AB}
fulfills

\mathrm{Tr}_{A}\left[M_{i}^{A}\rho^{AB}\right]=\mathrm{Tr}_{AB}\left[M_{i}^{A}\rho^{AB}\right]\times\rho^{B}

(64)

for all POVMs
\{M_{i}^{A}\}
on the subsystem
A
and all
i

if and only if
\rho^{AB}
is of the form

\rho^{AB}=\rho^{A}\otimes\rho^{B}.

(65)

Proof.

The implication
\Leftarrow
is immediate. We now prove the implication
\Rightarrow
. Let us write a state
\rho^{AB}
in its Operator-Schmidt decomposition
21
such that
\rho^{AB}=\sum_{k}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}E_{k}\otimes F_{k},
where
\{E_{k}\}
and
\{F_{k}\}
form orthonormal sets of Hermitian operators acting on systems
A
and
B
, respectively. Without loss of generality, we restrict ourselves to
\lambda_{k}\neq 0.
We also expand the operator
M_{i}^{A}
in the basis
\{E_{\alpha}\}
as
M_{i}^{A}=\sum_{\alpha}m_{\alpha}E_{\alpha},
where
m_{\alpha}
are real coefficients. We have:

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Tr}_{A}\left[M_{i}^{A}\rho^{AB}\right]=\sum_{k,\alpha}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}m_{\alpha}\mathrm{Tr}\left(E_{k}E_{\alpha}\right)F_{k}\\
&=\sum_{k}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}m_{k}F_{k}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[M_{i}^{A}\rho^{A}\right]\sum_{k}b_{k}F_{k},\end{split}

(66)

where in the last equality we used Eq. (
64
) and
\mathrm{Tr}\left[M_{i}^{A}\rho^{AB}\right]=\mathrm{Tr}\left[M_{i}^{A}\rho^{A}\right].
We also decomposed
\rho^{B}
in a basis
F_{k}
obtaining
\rho^{B}=\sum_{k}b_{k}F_{k}
with real coefficients
b_{k}
. Coefficient
m_{k}
satisfies
m_{k}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{i}^{A}E_{k}\right).
Using this, and comparing coefficients in Eq. (
66
) we obtain:

\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{i}^{A}E_{k}\right)=\frac{b_{k}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{i}^{A}\rho^{A}\right).

(67)

It must hold for all
M_{i}^{A},
thus
E_{k}=\frac{b_{k}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}\rho^{A}.
Plugging it into
\rho^{AB}
we obtain:

\rho^{AB}=\sum_{k}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}E_{k}\otimes F_{k}=\sum_{k}\rho^{A}\otimes b_{k}F_{k}=\rho^{A}\otimes\rho^{B}.

(68)

∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
\ket{\psi}^{AB}
be a pure entangled state satisfying

(N\otimes\openone)\bigl[\psi^{AB}\bigr]=\psi^{AB},

(69)

where
N
is a quantum channel and

\ket{\psi}^{AB}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\ket{i}^{A}\ket{i}^{B},

(70)

such that
\ket{\psi}^{AB}
has at least two non-zero Schmidt coefficients. Here,
\{\ket{i}^{A}\}
and
\{\ket{i}^{B}\}
denote the Schmidt bases for subsystems
A
and
B
, respectively.

Then, for any pure state
\ket{\eta}
belonging to the subspace spanned by
\{\ket{i}^{A}\}
, i.e.

\ket{\eta}=\sum_{i}c_{i}\ket{i}^{A},

(71)

with

, it holds that

N[\eta]=\eta.

(72)

Proof.

From Eq. (
69
) and the assumed Schmidt decomposition of
\ket{\psi}^{AB}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}\,N\!\left[\ket{i}\!\bra{j}^{A}\right]\otimes\ket{i}\!\bra{j}^{B}=\sum_{i,j}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}\,\ket{i}\!\bra{j}^{A}\otimes\ket{i}\!\bra{j}^{B}.

(73)

Left multiplying by
\openone\otimes\ket{k}\!\bra{l}
and tracing over subsystem
B
, we obtain

N\!\left[\ket{l}\!\bra{k}^{A}\right]=\ket{l}\!\bra{k}^{A}.

(74)

Since
N
is a quantum channel, it is linear on operators. This completes the proof.

Proof of Theorem

In the following we consider a pure bipartite state

This definition can be extended in a straightforward way also to non-normalized

\mathcal{H}_{AB}

\ket{\phi}_{k}^{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\ket{ii}.

(76)

Lemma 9
.

For any pure state
\ket{\psi}^{AB}

k
and any matrix

on

the following

G_{k}[(M\otimes\openone)\,\psi^{AB}(M^{\dagger}\otimes\openone)]\\

If
k
is greater than Schmidt rank of
\ket{\psi^{AB}},
both sides of the Eq. (
77
) are 0. Assume that Schmidt rank of
\ket{\psi^{AB}}
is

21

\ket{\psi^{AB}}=\sum_{i,j}X_{i,j}\ket{i}_{A}\ket{j}_{B}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\ket{e_{i}}_{A}\ket{f_{i}}_{B},

(78)

where
\ket{i}_{A},\ket{e_{i}}_{A}
are orthonormal bases in space
\mathcal{H}_{A},

\ket{j}_{B},\ket{f_{i}}_{B}
are orthonormal bases in space
\mathcal{H}_{B}
and
\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}
are singular values of

M\otimes\openone\ket{\psi^{AB}}=\sum_{i,j}(MX)_{i,j}\ket{i}_{A}\ket{j}_{B}.
Thus, the Schmidt coefficients of

\begin{split}&\prod_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}(M_{k}X)=\sqrt{\det(X^{\dagger}M_{k}^{\dagger}M_{k}X)}=\\
&=\sqrt{\det(M_{k}^{\dagger}M_{k})}\sqrt{\det(X^{\dagger}X)}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}},\end{split}

(79)

where

Substituting

Plugging it into Eq. (

Proposition 10
.

For a pure state
\ket{\psi}^{AB}
with Schmidt rank at most
k

and a local quantum operation
\Lambda
which has no correctable
subspace the state
\Lambda\otimes\openone[\psi^{AB}]

\Lambda\otimes\openone[\psi^{AB}]=\sum_{i}p_{i}\ket{\phi_{i}}\!\bra{\phi_{i}}^{AB}

(82)

\sum_{i}p_{i}G_{k}(\phi_{i}^{AB})=\kappa G_{k}(\psi^{AB})

(83)

Proof.

Let
\{K_{i}\}
be a set of Kraus operators for
\Lambda
. Using
Lemma
9
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i}G_{k}[(K_{i}\otimes\openone)\psi^{AB}(K_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone)]

(84)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}G_{k}[(K_{i}\otimes\openone)\phi_{k}^{+}(K_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone)]\times G_{k}(\psi^{AB}).

As we prove in Lemma

\Lambda

can always be chosen such that

\sum_{i}G_{k}[(K_{i}\otimes\openone)\phi_{k}^{+}(K_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone)]<1.

(85)

(86)

\sum_{i}G_{k}[(K_{i}\otimes\openone)\psi^{AB}(K_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone)]=\kappa G_{k}(\psi^{AB}).

(87)

The proof of the proposition is then completed by defining probabilities

p_{i}
and pure states

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[K_{i}\otimes\openone\psi^{AB}K_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone\right],

(88)

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{i}}^{AB}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{i}}}K_{i}\otimes\openone\ket{\psi}^{AB},

(89)

and noting that

\sum_{i}G_{k}[(K_{i}\otimes\openone)\psi^{AB}(K_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone)]=\sum_{i}p_{i}G_{k}(\phi_{i}^{AB}).

(90)

Lemma 11
.

Any quantum channel
\Lambda
which has no correctable subspace has a set of Kraus operators
\{K_{i}\}
such that that

\sum_{i}G_{k}[(K_{i}\otimes\openone)\phi_{k}^{+}(K_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone)]<1

(91)

for any
k\geq 2
.

Proof.

We begin by showing that if
\Lambda
does not have a correctable subspace, then
E_{f}(\Lambda\otimes\openone\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}})<E_{f}(\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}})
. We proceed by contradiction. Assume that there is no correctable subspace and that
E_{f}(\Lambda\otimes\openone\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}})=E_{f}(\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}})
for some
k
. Repeating the steps of the proof of Theorem
3
with
\phi_{k}^{+}
in place of
\psi^{AB},
we obtain that a correctable subspace must exist. This contradicts the assumption that no correctable subspace exists. Therefore,
E_{f}(\Lambda\otimes\openone\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}})<E_{f}(\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}}).

Let
\rho=\Lambda\otimes\openone(\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}}).
Let us denote by
\{q_{i},\ket{\psi_{i}}\}
the ensemble decomposition of
\rho
that minimizes
\sum_{i}q_{i}E_{f}(\ket{\psi_{i}}).
There exist Kraus operators
\{L_{i}\}
such that
L_{i}\otimes\openone\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}}\bra{\phi_{k}^{+}}L_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone=q_{i}\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{i}}.
Additionally, we have
\sum_{i}q_{i}E_{f}(\ket{\psi_{i}})<E_{f}(\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}}).
Consequently, for at least one
i
there is inequality
E_{f}(\ket{\psi_{i}})<E_{f}(\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}}).
Both the entanglement of formation and the
G_{k}
-concurrence reach their global maximum for pure states if and only if all their Schmidt coefficients are equal. Therefore, any pure state
\ket{\psi_{i}}
that is not maximally entangled (i.e. satisfying
E_{f}(\ket{\psi_{i}})<E_{f}(\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}})
) must necessarily satisfy
G_{k}(\ket{\psi_{i}})<G_{k}(\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}})
. Consequently, since for at least one
i
there is relation
G_{k}(\ket{\psi_{i}})<G_{k}(\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}}),
the following inequality must hold

\sum_{i}q_{i}G_{k}(\ket{\psi_{i}})<G_{k}(\ket{\phi_{k}^{+}})=1

(92)

This concludes the proof.
∎

Consider now a mixed state

Now, let us define the
k
-Schmidt fidelity of a bipartite pure state
\ket{\psi}^{AB}
as:

F_{k}(\psi^{AB})=\max_{\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}F(\psi^{AB},\phi^{AB}).

(107)

where
\mathcal{S}_{k}
denotes the set of states that can be expressed as convex combinations of bipartite pure states with Schmidt rank at most
k
. Since
\ket{\psi^{AB}}

is pure, the maximum in (

) is attained by a pure state.

Proposition 13
.

For a bipartite pure state
\ket{\psi}^{AB}
, the following holds:

F_{k}(\psi^{AB})=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{i},

(108)

where
\lambda_{i}
are Schmidt coefficients of
\ket{\psi}
in decreasing order. Additionally, the state
\ket{\phi}^{AB}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
that achieves the maximum in Eq. (
107
) is given by

\ket{\phi}^{AB}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{j}}}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\ket{ii}^{AB}

(109)

where
\ket{i}
are the Schmidt vectors of
\ket{\psi}
.

Proof.

Let
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i,j}M_{ij}\ket{i}\ket{j}
and
\ket{\phi}=\sum_{i,j}N_{ij}\ket{i}\ket{j}.
Their inner product can be written as

|\!\braket{\phi|\psi}\!|=\left|\sum_{i,j}N_{ij}^{*}M_{ij}\right|=\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(N^{\dagger}M\right)\right|.

(110)

Using the von Neumann trace inequality
19
, we obtain:

\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(N^{\dagger}M\right)\right|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}s_{i}(N)s_{i}(M)=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}s_{i}(N)\sqrt{\lambda_{i}},

(111)

where
s_{i}(X)
denote the singular values of
X
in non-increasing order. In the last step, we used that the singular values of
M
are the Schmidt coefficients of
\ket{\psi}
.

Since
\ket{\phi}
has Schmidt rank
k
, the matrix
N
has rank at most
k
, and hence only the first
k
singular values are non-zero. Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}s_{i}(N)\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}s_{i}(N)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{i}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{i}}.

(112)

In the last step we used that squares of singular values of
N
must add up to 1. Thus, we have the inequality
|\!\braket{\phi|\psi}\!|\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{i}}.
Taking squares we obtain:

|\!\braket{\phi|\psi}\!|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{i}.

(113)

We have thus established an upper bound on the fidelity. One can verify that the bound is saturated by the state given in Eq. (
109
), which completes the proof.

Analogous to the definition of
k
-Schmidt fidelity for pure states, we define

Proof of Theorem

We consider

\Lambda

\kappa=\min_{K_{i}}\sum_{i}p_{i}G_{k}(\phi_{i}),

(141)

(142)

\phi_{i}=\frac{(K_{i}\otimes\openone)\psi_{k}^{+}(K_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone)}{p_{i}}.

(143)

We now restrict our attention to a single instance of the channel
\Lambda

\Lambda
such that at least one Kraus operator, denoted by
L_{i}
has rank one. It then follows immediately that

\kappa\leq\sum_{i}q_{i}G_{k}(\theta_{i}),

(144)

where
q_{i}=\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{L_{i}}\otimes\openone\psi_{k}^{+}\widetilde{L_{i}}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone)

Since the channel under consideration is

The depolarizing channel

\begin{split}&L_{0}=\sqrt{1-p}\openone\\
&L_{ab}=\sqrt{\frac{p}{d}}\ket{a}\bra{b}.\end{split}

(145)

where

\begin{split}&\sum_{i}L_{i}\rho L_{i}^{\dagger}=(1-p)\rho+\sum_{ab}\frac{p}{d}\ket{a}\bra{b}\rho\ket{b}\bra{a}=\\
&(1-p)\rho+\sum_{a}\frac{p}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\ket{a}\bra{a}=(1-p)\rho+p\frac{\openone}{d},\end{split}

&+\sum_{\widetilde{L_{i}}\in S_{>1}}q_{i}G_{k}\left((\widetilde{L_{i}}\otimes\openone\psi_{k}^{+}\widetilde{L_{i}}^{\dagger}\otimes\openone)/q_{i}\right)\leq\\
&\sum_{\widetilde{L_{i}}\in S_{>1}}q_{i}=1-\sum_{\widetilde{L_{i}}\in S_{1}}q_{i},\end{split}
