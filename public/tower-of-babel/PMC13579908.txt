Association between changes in sleep quality and serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in patients with major depressive disorder following duloxetine treatment
Authors: Yayun Xu, Wenfan Gao, Feng Shan, Qingrong Xia, Jun Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is accompanied by prominent physiological abnormalities, notably sleep disturbances and immune-inflammatory dysregulation. As a serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, antidepressant duloxetine exhibits both anti-inflammatory effects and therapeutic benefits in improving sleep architecture. This study aimed to investigate the effects of duloxetine on objective sleep parameters and serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in patients with MDD, and further to explore the correlation between treatment-related improvements in sleep quality and dynamic changes in inflammatory cytokine profiles.MethodsThis study included 74 patients with MDD who met the DSM-5 criteria, had a baseline 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale score ≥ 20, and no prior use of antidepressants or anti-inflammatory drugs within 3 months. All patients were administered duloxetine at a daily dose of 40–60 mg for 4 consecutive weeks. Objective sleep parameters, including total sleep time (TST), sleep efficiency (SE), wake time after sleep onset (WASO), and rapid eye movement (REM) latency, were evaluated using polysomnography (PSG). Serum levels of 6 pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β), IL‑6, IL‑8, IL‑12, tumor necrosis factor‑α (TNF‑α), and interferon‑γ (IFN‑γ), were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Changes from baseline to post-treatment were analyzed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests based on data distribution. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson or Spearman tests, followed by partial correlation analysis adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsAfter 4 weeks of duloxetine treatment, significant improvements in sleep parameters were observed, characterized by increased TST (t = -6.614, P < 0.001) and SE (t = -6.631, P < 0.001), as well as decreased WASO (t = 6.331, P < 0.001). Moreover, serum concentrations of IL‑1β (t = 4.759, P < 0.001), IL‑8 (t = 6.327, P < 0.001), IL‑12 (t = 6.194, P < 0.001), and IFN‑γ (t = 8.713, P < 0.001) were significantly reduced following treatment. Furthermore, at baseline, TST was negatively correlated with serum IL-12 levels (r = -0.306, P = 0.016) in patients with MDD. Similarly, SE was negatively correlated with serum levels of IL‑1β (r = -0.343, P = 0.006), IL‑8 (r = -0.297, P = 0.019), and IL‑12 (r = -0.279, P = 0.028). Additionally, following treatment, the change in TST was negatively correlated with the change in IL-12 levels (r = -0.285, P = 0.025). Likewise, the change in SE was negatively correlated with changes in IL-8 levels (r = -0.263, P = 0.039) and IL-12 levels (r = -0.294, P = 0.020).ConclusionsFour-week duloxetine treatment was associated with improved sleep disturbances and reduced peripheral pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in patients with MDD. The baseline correlations between sleep parameters and pro-inflammatory cytokines, as well as the concurrent changes in these indicators following duloxetine treatment, reveal a potential relationship between sleep improvement and altered inflammatory profiles during duloxetine intervention. These preliminary findings suggest a possible link between duloxetine-related therapeutic effects on sleep and inflammatory regulation in MDD, which warrants further verification in future studies.Keywords: Sleep quality, Major depressive disorder, Pro-inflammatory cytokine, Duloxetine, Association

Introduction

Depression is a prevalent mental disorder characterized by persistent low mood, cognitive retardation and various somatic symptoms, featuring high recurrence, disability and suicide risks [1, 2]. Notably, patients with depression are prone to alexithymia and suicidal ideation, and greater illness severity is closely linked to heightened suicidal thoughts [3]. According to the latest report from the World Health Organization, approximately 322 million people worldwide are affected by depression, with a global prevalence of 4.4%. It is projected that depression will become the leading cause of global disease burden by 2030, and in China, it has already become the second leading cause of disability [4].

Sleep disturbance is a highly prevalent comorbidity and typical clinical symptom of major depressive disorder (MDD). It not only elevates depression onset risk but also aggravates suicidal tendency among patients with depression [5–8]. Accumulating evidence reveals a bidirectional interaction between depression and sleep disorders, sharing overlapping neurobiological mechanisms. Sleep disturbance even precedes depressive episodes, serving as an important prodromal marker. MDD patients complicated with sleep problems usually show worse clinical outcomes, poorer treatment response and higher relapse rate [9, 10]. Beyond intrinsic pathogenic factors, external stressors such as the COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated psychological distress and social isolation, aggravating disease-related disability and psychosocial vulnerability among susceptible populations, and adversely affecting public mental well-being and quality of life [11]. Hence, exploring the underlying mechanism and effective interventions for MDD combined with sleep disorders is of great clinical value.

Cytokines are immune-regulatory proteins secreted by multiple cell types. Accumulating evidence confirms that pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6, CRP and TNF-α are closely involved in depression pathogenesis [12–15]. Increased peripheral inflammatory factors elevate depression risk, aggravate clinical symptoms and lead to poor antidepressant response, among which TNF-α acts as a vital biomarker for treatment-resistant depression [16–19]. In turn, antidepressant therapy can effectively downregulate systemic inflammatory levels, further validating the intimate correlation between inflammation and depression [20].

Similarly, excessive inflammatory activation is a core driver of sleep disorders. Sleep dysfunction activates HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system, triggering systemic inflammatory response and increasing circulating inflammatory cytokines [21–23]. In turn, sustained inflammation induces brain neural plasticity abnormalities, accelerating the progression of depression [24]. In this context, inflammatory response is considered a critical common pathological bridge connecting depression and sleep disturbance.

Growing studies focus on the anti-inflammatory efficacy of antidepressants, which provides novel strategies for managing comorbid depressive and sleep disorders. Classical antidepressants such as SSRIs and SNRIs exert obvious anti-inflammatory effects both in preclinical experiments and clinical practice [25–27]. As a first-line dual-reuptake inhibitor antidepressant, duloxetine possesses definite antidepressant effects and confirmed anti-inflammatory activities in animal experiments and clinical observations [26, 28–30]. Besides, duloxetine has been proven to regulate sleep structure and alleviate insomnia symptoms in MDD patients, achieving concurrent improvement of depressive mood and sleep quality [31–33].

Nevertheless, the internal association between duloxetine-mediated sleep improvement and inflammatory cytokine changes remains poorly clarified in clinical settings. We therefore hypothesized that duloxetine could simultaneously optimize sleep status and regulate serum pro-inflammatory cytokines in patients with MDD, and the improvement of sleep indicators is closely correlated with the alteration of inflammatory levels. The present study was designed to investigate the influences of duloxetine on sleep parameters and inflammatory cytokines in patients with MDD, and further analyze their correlation after intervention.

Materials and methods

Study design and participant

This study recruited 74 patients with MDD at Anhui Mental Health Center from August 2020 to August 2024. All MDD diagnoses were confirmed by experienced psychiatrists based on the DSM-5 criteria. Eligible participants were aged ≥ 18 years, presented with baseline HAMD-24 scores ≥ 20, received 4-week duloxetine treatment (40–60 mg daily), completed paired baseline and post-treatment polysomnography (PSG) assessments, and had no use of antidepressants, anti-inflammatory drugs or other psychotropic medications within three months prior to enrollment. Patients with neurological diseases, comorbid psychiatric disorders, substance abuse, chronic inflammatory or immune-related diseases were excluded. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital (HFSY-IRB-PJ-XQR-2020001) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study design.

Polysomnography examination

All participants underwent overnight polysomnography (PSG) monitoring using the Embla N7000 system. Core objective sleep parameters were analyzed, including total sleep time (TST), sleep efficiency (SE), wake time after sleep onset (WASO), and REM latency, in accordance with standard sleep scoring criteria.

Blood sample collection and serum cytokine measurement

Fasting venous blood samples were collected from all participants at baseline and post-treatment. After centrifugation, serum samples were stored at -80 °C for subsequent detection. Serum levels of six pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-12, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Shanghai Jianglai Biotech Co., Ltd.) following the manufacturer’s standard protocols. The ELISA kits for IL‑1β (JL13662), IL‑6 (JL14113), IL‑8 (JL19291), IL‑12 (JL18332), TNF‑α (JL10208), and IFN‑γ (JL12152) were purchased from Shanghai Jianglai Biotech Co., Ltd.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 17.0 software. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess data normality. Paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied for normally and non-normally distributed continuous variables, respectively. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) accordingly. Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation analyses were used to explore bivariate correlations. Partial correlation analysis was further performed to adjust for potential confounders and identify the independent association between treatment-induced changes in sleep parameters and serum cytokine levels. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effect of duloxetine on the HAMD-24 scores and sleep quality in patients with MDD

The cohort consisted of 17 male and 57 female participants. The mean age was 44.2 ± 16.3 years, and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 21.3 ± 3.4 kg/m2. As presented in Table 1, the baseline HAMD-24 score was 33.24 ± 9.79. Following 4 weeks of duloxetine treatment, there was a significant reduction in HAMD-24 scores, which decreased to 11.74 ± 3.71 (t = 20.846, P < 0.001). In addition, significant improvements in sleep parameters were observed after 4 weeks of treatment, as reflected by increased TST (t = -6.614, P < 0.001) and SE (t = -6.631, P < 0.001), along with a significant decrease in WASO (t = 6.331, P < 0.001).

Discussion

Four core findings were identified in the present study. First, four-week duloxetine treatment significantly improved objective sleep indicators in MDD patients, including elevated total sleep time and sleep efficiency as well as reduced wake after sleep onset. Second, serum pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL‑1β, IL‑8, IL‑12 and IFN‑γ) were distinctly decreased after duloxetine intervention. Third, baseline analyses showed total sleep time was negatively correlated with serum IL‑12 level, and sleep efficiency was inversely associated with IL‑1β, IL‑8 and IL‑12. Fourth, longitudinal correlation further confirmed that increased total sleep time was related to declined IL‑12, and improved sleep efficiency was linked to reduced IL‑8 and IL‑12 following treatment.

As a SNRI, duloxetine exerts a well-validated, context-dependent bidirectional modulatory effect on sleep. Its sleep-improving properties are predominantly manifested in sleep disturbances comorbid with MDD and chronic pain, mediated primarily through the regulation of sleep architecture and amelioration of concomitant clinical symptoms. In patients with MDD-associated sleep dysfunction, long-term duloxetine treatment has been shown to significantly improve sleep-related rating scale scores and alleviate insomnia symptoms relative to placebo [32]. Moreover, duloxetine normalizes sleep structure in patients with depression by augmenting slow-wave sleep (stage 3) and suppressing REM sleep [31]. With respect to chronic pain-related sleep disorders, a robust correlation has been observed between pain attenuation and sleep enhancement among patients with painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy receiving duloxetine therapy [34]. Consistent with these earlier findings, our present study demonstrated that a 4-week course of duloxetine substantially improved objective sleep parameters in patients with MDD, as reflected by increased TST and SE, alongside reduced WASO. Collectively, these results further validate the context-dependent sleep-regulating effects of duloxetine in MDD, highlighting its dual therapeutic advantages in simultaneously attenuating depressive psychopathology and ameliorating objectively measured sleep disturbances. Further large-scale longitudinal studies are therefore warranted to clarify the long-term trajectory of sleep architectural changes and to determine whether early sleep restoration can serve as a predictive biomarker for subsequent antidepressant outcomes.

Beyond its canonical serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibition, duloxetine has been reported to exert potential anti-inflammatory properties, most of which are primarily supported by abundant preclinical experimental evidence. Preclinical studies have verified that duloxetine can downregulate elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines in brain tissues, inhibit microglial overactivation and regulate multiple immune-related signaling pathways in various inflammatory animal and cell models [29, 35, 36]. Nevertheless, relevant clinical evidence remains relatively limited. Existing clinical observations have indicated that duloxetine intervention can reduce peripheral pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in patients with depression, and such inflammatory alterations tend to coincide with relieved depressive symptoms [30]. In consistent with prior clinical findings, our study also observed decreased serum IL-1β, IL-8, IL-12 and IFN-γ levels after short-term duloxetine treatment among MDD participants. Notably, it cannot be ignored that the decline in low-grade inflammatory status may be secondary to the alleviation of depressive symptoms itself, rather than a direct independent pharmacological anti-inflammatory effect of duloxetine. Therefore, the symptomatic improvement induced by antidepressant treatment serves as an important confounding factor that cannot be excluded in the present study. At this stage, we could only tentatively speculate that duloxetine may exert potential immunomodulatory effects apart from its classic monoamine regulatory mechanism, while further rigorous controlled studies are still required to distinguish the direct anti-inflammatory action of duloxetine from the indirect inflammatory improvement caused by relieved depressive mood.

Accumulating clinical evidence demonstrates a robust bidirectional interplay between peripheral pro‑inflammatory cytokines and sleep homeostasis, particularly among patients with MDD. Chronic sleep disturbance and experimental sleep deprivation consistently elevate circulating levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, exacerbating systemic low‑grade inflammation. Conversely, sustained elevation of these inflammatory mediators disrupts physiological sleep architecture, reduces sleep efficiency, fragments sleep maintenance, and disturbs rapid eye movement regulation in patients with depression [37, 38]. This reciprocal inflammatory-sleep vicious cycle is commonly observed in patients with comorbid depression and insomnia, wherein enhanced immune activation deteriorates sleep quality, while poor sleep further amplifies inflammatory responses, collectively worsening disease progression and clinical outcomes. At the mechanistic level, pro-inflammatory cytokines function as endogenous neuromodulators governing sleep architecture and circadian rhythmicity. These peripheral cytokines access central neural circuits either by crossing the blood-brain barrier or through vagal afferent signaling, thereby modulating hypothalamic sleep centres and brainstem arousal pathways to promote non-rapid eye movement sleep and slow-wave activity under acute inflammatory conditions [39]. In contrast, persistent activation of NF-κB and p38 MAPK signalling cascades triggers excessive microglial reactivity, disrupts sleep homeostatic regulation, and ultimately induces sleep fragmentation, diminished sleep efficiency, and aberrant REM cycling [37]. Accordingly, central neuroinflammation constitutes a critical molecular pathway linking immune dysregulation to impaired sleep physiology. Consistent with these theoretical and clinical frameworks, our baseline correlation analyses further validated this inflammation-sleep association in patients with MDD. Specifically, TST was negatively correlated with serum IL‑12 concentrations, whereas SE exhibited inverse relationships with circulating IL-1β, IL-8, and IL-12 levels, suggesting that heightened peripheral inflammatory status at baseline is closely linked to objective impairments in sleep parameters. Importantly, we acknowledge a key confounding factor that cannot be overlooked in the present study. Sleep disturbances are highly dependent on the severity of depressive symptoms, and the observed improvements in objective sleep parameters after duloxetine treatment may largely occur as a secondary consequence of overall clinical remission, rather than resulting solely from targeted modulation of inflammatory pathways by duloxetine. Given the retrospective observational design and lack of controlled grouping in our study, we cannot completely exclude the confounding effect of improved depressive symptomatology on sleep recovery. Therefore, the observed parallel improvements in inflammation and sleep only indicate a concomitant correlative change, rather than confirming a direct causal regulatory relationship between inflammatory amelioration and sleep restoration. These preliminary findings merely provide tentative hypothesis-generating evidence for the interplay among duloxetine treatment, inflammatory modulation and sleep improvement, and further rigorous controlled studies are required to disentangle the independent effects of drug immunomodulation and depressive symptom relief on sleep recovery in patients with MDD .

Although the anti-inflammatory properties and sleep-regulating effects of duloxetine have been well documented separately, whether the therapeutic improvements in sleep parameters are mechanistically linked to reductions in systemic pro‑inflammatory cytokines during duloxetine intervention remains poorly characterized in MDD populations. In the present study, longitudinal correlation analyses revealed robust interrelationships between cytokine modulation and sleep restoration following treatment. Specifically, treatment-induced increases in TST were negatively correlated with changes in serum IL-12 concentrations, indicating that greater improvements in sleep duration were associated with larger reductions in IL-12 levels. Similarly, improvements in SE were negatively correlated with changes in circulating IL-8 and IL-12 levels such that greater increases in SE corresponded to greater decreases in these cytokines over the therapeutic course. These longitudinal associations indicate that the sleep‑promoting effects of duloxetine are not independent of its immunomodulatory actions, suggesting that suppression of peripheral inflammatory cascades may partially mediate its ability to consolidate sleep architecture and enhance objective sleep quality. Combined with the baseline cross‑sectional relationships observed in our cohort, these findings further support the existence of an integrated inflammation-sleep regulatory axis, whereby duloxetine concurrently attenuates pro‑inflammatory burden and normalizes disrupted sleep physiology in patients with MDD.

Several inherent limitations of this study need to be fully acknowledged. First, this is a single-center clinical study with a limited sample size, which may weaken the representativeness of the enrolled population and restrict the generalizability of our conclusions to broader depressive patient cohorts. Second, we only detected serum inflammatory cytokines at baseline and the 4-week endpoint, without conducting continuous dynamic monitoring throughout treatment, thus failing to clarify the changing trends of inflammatory indicators and the long-term regulatory effects of duloxetine. Third, this study did not measure serum duloxetine levels, so we cannot further explore the dose-effect relationship between drug concentration, inflammatory improvement and sleep recovery. In addition, we did not strictly control potential confounding factors including patients’ daily sleep habits, auxiliary psychotherapy and combined mild medications, which may interfere with the authenticity of research outcomes. Moreover, this study merely focused on peripheral serum inflammatory markers, and lacked further exploration of central inflammatory mechanisms underlying sleep disturbance in major depressive disorder. In view of the above deficiencies, well-designed multi-center trials with larger sample sizes, multi-time-point dynamic detection of inflammatory cytokines, combined therapeutic drug monitoring and strict control of confounding variables are urgently needed in future research, so as to further verify and deepen the research results of this study.

In conclusion, short-term duloxetine treatment can effectively optimize objective sleep structure and downregulate peripheral pro-inflammatory cytokines in patients with MDD. Both baseline cross-sectional correlations and longitudinal dynamic associations confirm that improvements in sleep indicators are closely accompanied by decreased inflammatory levels after treatment. This study further clarifies that apart from classic monoamine regulatory effects, duloxetine also exerts definite immunomodulatory activity, and peripheral inflammation relief acts as a key mediating pathway connecting duloxetine intervention with sleep function recovery. From clinical perspectives, the identified inflammatory biomarkers can be applied to assist efficacy evaluation and dynamic therapeutic monitoring in MDD patients complicated with sleep disorders. In terms of theoretical significance, this finding enriches the inflammatory-sleep interaction mechanism of antidepressants. Clinically, these outcomes are applicable to most inpatients and outpatients with depression accompanied by sleep disturbance, and can provide practical reference for individualized clinical medication and combined intervention strategies. Collectively, the present findings offer novel mechanistic evidence and feasible serological indicators for the clinical management of comorbid depression and sleep disorders.
