Composition Theorems for Multiple Differential Privacy Constraints
Cemre Cadir, Salim Najib, and Yanina Y. Shkel

Abstract
The exact composition of mechanisms for which two differential privacy (DP) constraints hold simultaneously is studied. The resulting privacy region admits an exact representation as a mixture over compositions of mechanisms of heterogeneous DP guarantees, yielding a framework that naturally generalizes to the composition of mechanisms for which any number of DP constraints hold. This result is shown through a structural lemma for mixtures of binary hypothesis tests. Lastly, the developed methodology is applied to approximate f f -DP composition.

A mechanism

I-B

Composition Theorems

In most differentially private applications, the same database is reused for multiple queries as opposed to requesting just a single query. Privacy degrades with composition and it is crucial to quantify the loss closely. The composition theorems characterize this privacy loss. Dwork et al.

II
Preliminaries

II-A

Hypothesis Testing and
f
-DP

Binary hypothesis testing provides a very interesting point of view to understand and study DP. First, recall the binary hypothesis testing problem
\mathcal{H}(P_{0},P_{1})
. Given probability distributions
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
on the same space:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}

\displaystyle:Y\sim P_{0}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}

\displaystyle:Y\sim P_{1}.

The goal is to design a
measurable
and
possibly non-deterministic
decision rule
\hat{\mathcal{H}}:\mathcal{Y}\to\{0,1\}
. For a given
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
, we define two errors:

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{I}}:=\beta_{\mathrm{I}}(\hat{\mathcal{H}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[\hat{\mathcal{H}}=1|\mathcal{H}=0]\mbox{ and}

(2)

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{II}}:=\beta_{\mathrm{II}}(\hat{\mathcal{H}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[\hat{\mathcal{H}}=0|\mathcal{H}=1],

(3)

where (
2
) is type I error or the probability of false alarm, and (
3
) is type II error or the probability of missed detection. In the conventional hypothesis testing problem, the goal is minimizing these errors. However, from the perspective of privacy, we would like the adversary to have high errors when performing certain hypothesis tests.

Given a hypothesis test
\mathcal{H}(P_{0},P_{1})
, we define the trade-off function
f(P_{0},P_{1}):[0,1]\to[0,1]
as

f(P_{0},P_{1})(t)=\inf_{\hat{\mathcal{H}}}\left\{\beta_{\mathrm{II}}|\beta_{\mathrm{I}}\leq t\right\}.

We can interpret DP from the point of view of hypothesis testing
[
25
,
17
]
. Define the trade-off function

f_{\varepsilon,\delta}(t)=\max\left\{0,1-\delta-e^{\varepsilon}t,e^{-\varepsilon}(1-\delta-t)\right\},\ t\in[0,1].

An
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanism
M
satisfies, for all neighboring datasets
D_{0},D_{1}\in\mathcal{D}
, with
M(D_{i})\sim P_{M(D_{i})}
,

f\left(P_{M(D_{0})},P_{M(D_{1})}\right)\geq f_{\varepsilon,\delta}.

(4)

This interpretation inspired a very general variant of DP, called
f
-DP
[
8
]
. Informally, a mechanism
M
satisfies
f=f(P,Q)
-DP if differentiating two neighboring databases
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
through
M(D_{0})
and
M(D_{1})
is at least as difficult as differentiating
P
and
Q
, in a strong sense.
Equation (
4
) indicates that
f
-DP reduces to
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP with the appropriate trade of function, i.e.
f=f_{\varepsilon,\delta}
.

II-B

Privacy Regions

Privacy regions offer an alternative way to express trade-off functions. They are regions including all achievable error pairs and have one to one correspondence to the trade-off functions.

Definition 2

(Privacy Region)
.

Let
f=f(P_{0},P_{1})
be a trade-off function for a hypothesis test
\mathcal{H}(P_{0},P_{1})
. The corresponding privacy region
\mathcal{F}\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
is defined as the set of achievable
(\beta_{\mathrm{I}},\beta_{\mathrm{II}})
error pairs, as follows:

\mathcal{F}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{\mathrm{I}}\\
\beta_{\mathrm{II}}\end{bmatrix}\in[0,1]^{2}\ |\ 1-\beta_{\mathrm{I}}\geq\beta_{\mathrm{II}}\geq f(\beta_{\mathrm{I}})\right\}.

In our work, we state our results as bounds on privacy regions. As in
[
17
]
, we write the privacy region associated with
f_{\varepsilon,\delta}
as
\mathcal{R}\left(\varepsilon,\delta\right)
.

When we talk about multiple
(\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i})
-DP constraints, we use the vector notation, and denote it with bold letters. For example, we let
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
. We denote the privacy region of the intersection of the
n

(\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i})
-DP guarantees by

\mathcal{R}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{R}\left(\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i}\right).

(5)

This intersection region signifies the privacy region of a mechanism that satisfy all
(\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i})
-DP constraints simultaneously.
We study the
k
-fold composition of such mechanisms.

Definition 3

(Composition region for multiple DP constraints)
.

With the same notation as above, we define the privacy region for the
k
-composition of
(\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i})
-DP mechanisms for
i\in\llbracket 1\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mun\rrbracket:=[1,n]\cap\mathbb{Z}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}:=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
to be
\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)
.

We also study the following heterogeneous composition.

Definition 4

(Heterogeneous composition region)
.

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. The composition of
n

\varepsilon_{1}
-DP and
m

\varepsilon_{2}
-DP mechanisms is referred to as a heterogeneous composition, with privacy region
\mathcal{C}_{n,m}\left(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\right)
or
\mathcal{C}_{n,m}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right)
with
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
.

II-C

Mixture of Hypothesis Tests

In this section, we prove a new lemma on the mixture of hypothesis tests
[
24
,
20
]
. This lemma is at the core of our results, linking multiple DP constraint composition and heterogeneous composition. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
P^{i},Q^{i}
be pairs of distributions on
\mathcal{Y}
, for
i\in\llbracket 1\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mun\rrbracket
. We denote the associated hypothesis tests by
\mathcal{H}^{i}=\mathcal{H}(P^{i},Q^{i})
. The mixture distributions are defined as

P(y,i)=\alpha_{i}P_{Y|I}(y|i)=\alpha_{i}P^{i}(y),\ Q(y,i)=\alpha_{i}Q^{i}(y)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in[0,1]^{n}
are fixed and satisfy
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}=1
. Then, the observed-class mixture hypothesis test is
\mathcal{H}_{m}=\mathcal{H}(P,Q)
, where the test
\mathcal{H}^{i}
is selected with probability
\alpha_{i}
. Note that, once randomly selected,
i
is known to the observer.

Lemma 1
.

Let
f_{i}=f(P^{i},Q^{i})
be the trade-off function for
\mathcal{H}^{i}
. The trade-off function
f_{m}=f(P,Q)
of
\mathcal{H}_{m}
satisfies

f_{m}(t)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{i}\in[0,1],\ i\in\llbracket 1\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mun\rrbracket\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}t_{i}=t\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{i}(t_{i}).

(6)

Proof.

Consider that
f_{m}=\inf_{\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{m}}\left\{\beta_{\mathrm{II}}^{m}|\beta_{\mathrm{I}}^{m}\leq t\right\}
. For
\nu\in\left\{\mathrm{I},\mathrm{II}\right\}
,
\beta_{\nu}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{m})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\beta_{\nu}^{i}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{m}(\cdot,i)):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\beta_{\nu}^{i}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{i})
by routine arguments. The reformulated constraint becomes
\beta_{\mathrm{I}}^{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}t_{i}\leq t
,
t_{i}=\beta_{\mathrm{I}}^{i}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{i})
. Then observe that
f_{i}(t_{i})=f_{i}(\beta_{\mathrm{I}}^{i}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{i}))\leq\beta_{\mathrm{II}}^{i}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{i})
, leading to the rewriting of
\beta_{\mathrm{II}}^{m}
as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{i}(t_{i})
. The precise arguments can be found in Appendix
-A
.
∎

The trade-off functions
f_{1.3,0}
and
f_{0.5,0.2}
, and their mixture with weights
\alpha_{i}=0.5

is known in the convex optimization literature as a weighted
infimal convolution

Corollary 1
.

With the same notation as above,

\mathcal{F}_{m}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{\mathrm{I}}^{i}\\
\beta_{\mathrm{II}}^{i}\end{bmatrix}\ |\ \begin{bmatrix}\beta_{\mathrm{I}}^{i}\\
\beta_{\mathrm{II}}^{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}\right\}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}.

(7)

In the cases that will be of interest to us,
f_{i}
s are piecewise affine functions. This makes the computation of
f_{m}
and thus
\mathcal{F}_{m}
possible through properties of
convex conjugates
of piecewise affine functions
[
6
]
. Details are in
Appendix
-B
and Appendix
-H
.

for an example of the mixture of two equally weighted DP regions.

III-A

Heterogeneous Composition

We state the theorem on heterogeneous composition
\mathcal{C}_{x,y}\left(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\right)
(cf. Def.
4
) and the accompanying Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Heterogeneous Composition

1:

Input:

\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},x,y

2:

Find the set
S(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2};x,y)
of all
(a^{*},b^{*})\in\llbracket 0\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mux\rrbracket\times\llbracket 0\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5muy\rrbracket
such that
\varepsilon_{a^{*},b^{*}}^{x,y}=\varepsilon_{1}(x-2a^{*})+\varepsilon_{2}(y-2b^{*})\geq 0
.

3:

(Optional, for efficiency) Remove all but one
(a^{*},b^{*})
that has the same slope
\varepsilon_{a^{*},b^{*}}^{x,y}
from the set
S(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2};x,y)
.

4:

for

(a^{*},b^{*})

in

S(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2};x,y)

do
compute
\delta_{a^{*},b^{*}}^{x,y}
as

\displaystyle\delta_{a^{*},b^{*}}^{x,y}=\ \left(\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon_{1}}+1}\right)^{x}\left(\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon_{2}}+1}\right)^{y}\sum_{b=0}^{y}\sum_{a=a_{0}(b)}^{x}\binom{x}{a}\binom{y}{b}\cdot

\displaystyle\left(e^{a\varepsilon_{1}+b\varepsilon_{2}}-e^{\varepsilon_{1}(2(x-a^{*})-a)+\varepsilon_{2}(2(y-b^{*})-b)}\right)

where

a_{0}(b)=\max\left(0,\lceil(y-b^{*}-b)\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}+(x-a^{*})\rceil\right).

5:

end

for

6:

Intersect the regions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{x,y}\left(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\right)=\bigcap_{(a^{*},b^{*})\in S(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2};x,y)}\mathcal{R}\left(\varepsilon_{a^{*},b^{*}}^{x,y},\delta_{a^{*},b^{*}}^{x,y}\right).

7:

Output:

\mathcal{C}_{x,y}\left(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\right)

Theorem 1
.

Let
\varepsilon_{1}>\varepsilon_{2}>0
, and
x,y\in\mathbb{N}
. The exact privacy region of the composition of
x

(\varepsilon_{1},0)
-DP and
y

(\varepsilon_{2},0)
-DP mechanisms
\mathcal{C}_{x,y}\left(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\right)
is computed by Algorithm
1
.

Proof.

Define distributions
P_{i}^{1}
on
\left\{0,3\right\}
and
P_{i}^{2}
on
\left\{1,2\right\}
:

P_{i}^{1}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{e^{\varepsilon_{1}}}{e^{\varepsilon_{1}}+1}&\text{ if }(i=0,x=0)\text{ or }(i=1,x=3),\\
\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon_{1}}+1}&\text{ if }(i=0,x=3)\text{ or }(i=1,x=0),\end{cases}

P_{i}^{2}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{e^{\varepsilon_{2}}}{e^{\varepsilon_{2}}+1}&\text{ if }(i=0,x=1)\text{ or }(i=1,x=2),\\
\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon_{2}}+1}&\text{ if }(i=0,x=2)\text{ or }(i=1,x=1).\end{cases}

For
j\in\left\{1,2\right\}
, let the mechanism
M^{j}
output
X_{0}^{j}\sim P_{0}^{j}
in the case of the null hypothesis,
X_{1}^{j}\sim P_{1}^{j}
otherwise. Thus
M^{j}
is binary randomized response
[

]
, which achieves the whole of the

-DP region
[

]
. Let

otherwise, where

and

are distributions on

.

∎

III-B

Composition Theorem for Double-DP Constraints

In this section, we study the double-DP composition. We present two theorems that achieve the same privacy region. Theorem
2
depends on the heterogeneous composition regions
\mathcal{C}_{x,y}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right)
, and can be generalized to multiple-DP composition. Theorem
3
provides a closed form expression and does not depend on
\mathcal{C}_{x,y}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right)
.

Assumption 1
.

Throughout this section, we let
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\in[0,1)^{2}
. We assume that
\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}\text{ and }(1-\delta_{1})(1+e^{\varepsilon_{2}})<(1-\delta_{2})(1+e^{\varepsilon_{1}})
, making both
(\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i})
-DP constraints active. We also let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.

Remark 1
.

A trivial upper bound of the double-DP
k
-composition privacy region is the intersection of the privacy regions for each DP guarantee, i.e.
\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1}\right)\cap\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\varepsilon_{2},\delta_{2}\right)
, known thanks to
[
17
]
.

Remark 2
.

Another bound can be derived from
[
13
]
, leveraging the total variation bound induced by DP. Let
\eta=\delta_{2}+(1-\delta_{2})\frac{e^{\varepsilon}_{2}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}_{2}+1}
. Then,
\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{k}\left((\varepsilon_{1},0),(\delta_{1},\eta)\right)\cap\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\varepsilon_{2},\delta_{2}\right)
.

Remarks
1
and
2
are baselines for our main composition result.

Theorem 2
.

The privacy region of the composition of
k
mechanisms which are
(\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1})
and
(\varepsilon_{2},\delta_{2})
-DP is

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)=\tilde{\delta}\mathcal{R}\left(0,1\right)+(1-\tilde{\delta})\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}(1-\alpha)^{i}\alpha^{k-i}\mathcal{C}_{i,k-i}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right)

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
,
\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
,
\tilde{\delta}=(1-(1-\delta_{1})^{k})
and

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{(1-\delta_{1})e^{\varepsilon_{2}}-(1-\delta_{2})e^{\varepsilon_{1}}+(\delta_{2}-\delta_{1})}{(e^{\varepsilon_{2}}-e^{\varepsilon_{1}})(1-\delta_{1})}.

Proof.

We start with the case
\delta_{1}=0
, implying
\tilde{\delta}=0
. Reusing the notation of Theorem
1
’s proof, define the distributions on
\llbracket 0\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mu3\rrbracket
, for
i\in\left\{0,1\right\}

P_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}(1-\alpha)P_{i}^{1}(x)&\text{ if }x\in\left\{0,3\right\},\\
\alpha P_{i}^{2}(x)&\text{ if }x\in\left\{1,2\right\},\end{cases}

Let
M
be the mechanism that outputs
X_{i}\sim P_{i}
under the hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{i}
. It is easy to check that
M
has privacy region
\mathcal{R}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)
. Observe that
M
picks
M^{1}
with probability
1-\alpha
and
M^{2}
with probability
\alpha
. Let
M_{k}
be the
k
-composition of replicas of
M
, thus
M_{k}
picks
M^{1}

i
times with probability
1-\alpha
each time independently, thus with probability
\binom{k}{i}(1-\alpha)^{i}\alpha^{k-i}
overall
M_{k}
picks the mechanism
M^{i,k-i}
. Thus the result follows by applying Corollary
1
and Theorem
1
. We extend to
\delta_{1}>0
in
Appendix
-D
.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumption
1
,

\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta}\right)=\tilde{\delta}\mathcal{R}\left(0,1\right)+(1-\tilde{\delta})\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in\llbracket 0\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5muk\rrbracket\\
u\geq\left\lceil\frac{k\varepsilon_{1}-v(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2})}{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}\right\rceil\end{subarray}}\mathcal{R}\left(\varepsilon_{u,v},\delta_{u,v}\right)

where
\varepsilon_{u,v}=\varepsilon_{1}(u+v-k)+\varepsilon_{2}(u-v)
and

\displaystyle\delta_{u,v}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b,c,d)\\
\in B(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon};u,v)\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{a,b,c,d}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{e^{\varepsilon_{1}}+1}\right)^{a+d}\left(\frac{\alpha}{e^{\varepsilon_{2}}+1}\right)^{b+c}\cdot

\displaystyle\left(e^{a\varepsilon_{1}+b\varepsilon_{2}}-e^{(d+u+v-k)\varepsilon_{1}+(c+u-v)\varepsilon_{2}}\right),

with
(a,b,c,d)\in B(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon};u,v)\subseteq\llbracket 0\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5muk\rrbracket^{4}

\displaystyle=c(c-t^{*})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{f(t^{*})-c}{t^{*}-c}\left(-(t^{*}-c)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=c(c-t^{*})-\frac{1}{2}\frac{f(t^{*})-c}{t^{*}-c}(t^{*}-c)^{2}

\displaystyle=-(t^{*}-c)\left[c+\frac{f(t^{*})-c}{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-(t^{*}-c)\frac{f(t^{*})+c}{2}.

The original problem becomes

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{n}\times[0,1]^{n}\\
f_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta}}\geq f\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{1}f_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\delta}}(t)\di t\equiv

\displaystyle\arg\min_{t^{*}\in[0,c]}J(t^{*})=t^{*}\frac{f(t^{*})+f_{0}}{2}-(t^{*}-c)\frac{f(t^{*})+c}{2}.

Computing derivatives
J^{\prime}
and
J^{\prime\prime}
of
J
, which are well defined, because
f
is twice differentiable:

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(t^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(t^{*})+f_{0}+t^{*}f^{\prime}(t^{*})}{2}-\frac{f(t^{*})+c}{2}-\frac{t^{*}-c}{2}f^{\prime}(t^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{0}-c}{2}+\frac{c}{2}f^{\prime}(t^{*}),

\displaystyle\text{and }J^{\prime\prime}(t^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(t^{*})\geq 0.

J
being convex, its stationary points are global minima. Observe that in the previous section, we have already required
f
to be strictly convex. Hence
J
is strictly convex as well (
J^{\prime\prime}>0
), and has a unique global minimum over
[0,c]
. We seek the stationary points of
J
:

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(t_{\text{stat}})=0

\displaystyle\iff\frac{f_{0}-c}{2}+\frac{c}{2}f^{\prime}(t_{\text{stat}})=0

\displaystyle\iff f^{\prime}(t_{\text{stat}})=\frac{f(c)-f(0)}{c-0}.

Observe that a solution
t_{\text{stat}}\in\left(0,c\right)
to the previous equation exists by the mean value theorem, and is unique by the strict convexity of
J
. Solving that equation then comparing
J(t_{\text{stat}})
to
J(0)
and
J(c)
, we have that

t^{*}:=\arg\min_{t^{*}\in[0,c]}J(t^{*})=\arg\min\left\{J(0),J(c),J(t_{\text{stat}})\right\}.

Observe that

J(0)=J(c)=c\frac{c+f_{0}}{2}.

Hence
J(t_{\text{stat}})<J(0)\iff J(t_{\text{stat}})<J(c)
and to decide between
t_{\text{stat}}
and
\left\{0,c\right\}
, one can compute

h(t_{\text{stat}})=2(J(t_{\text{stat}})-J(0))=t_{\text{stat}}f(0)+c(f(t_{\text{stat}})-f(0)-t_{\text{stat}}),

and we have

t_{\text{stat}}\in\arg\min\left\{J(0),J(c),J(t_{\text{stat}})\right\}\iff h(t_{\text{stat}})\leq 0.

Lastly, we can compute the affine pieces. If
t^{*}\in\left\{0,c\right\}\iff h(t_{\text{stat}})\geq 0
, then one of the two affine pieces collapses and only the second one remains, representing the
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP constraint

\begin{cases}\varepsilon=\ln\left(\frac{f(0)-c}{c}\right),\\
\delta=1-f(0).\end{cases}

When
t^{*}=t_{\text{stat}}\iff h(t_{\text{stat}})\leq 0
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{1}=\ln\left(\frac{f(0)-f(t_{\text{stat}})}{t_{\text{stat}}}\right),\\
\delta_{1}=1-f(0),\\
\end{cases}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{2}=\ln\left(\frac{c-f(t_{\text{stat}})}{t_{\text{stat}}-c}\right),\\
\delta_{2}=1-c-c\frac{c-f(t_{\text{stat}})}{t_{\text{stat}}-c}.\end{cases}

Computation of the mixture of piecewise affine functions

Lemma
1
states that, given trade-off functions
\left\{f_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
with associated weights
\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\subseteq(0,1]
such that
\sum_{i=1}\alpha_{i}=1
, the trade-off function of the mixture of the corresponding
n
hypothesis tests is

f_{m}(t)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{i}\in[0,1],\ i\in\llbracket 1\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mun\rrbracket\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}t_{i}=t\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{i}(t_{i}).

This equation looks very similar to an infimal convolution.

Definition 6

(Infimal convolution)
.

Let
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{\pm\infty\right\}
be functions, for
i=1
to
n
. Their infimal convolution
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{\pm\infty\right\}
is defined by

g(t)=\left(\square_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\right)(t)=\inf_{\left(x_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(x_{i}).

f_{m}
can be written as an infimal convolution of some functions. Extend
f_{i}
by letting
f_{i}(t)=+\infty
when
t\not\in[0,1]
. Define

g_{i}(x)=\alpha_{i}f_{i}\left(\frac{x}{\alpha_{i}}\right),

where
g_{i}(x)=+\infty\iff x\not\in[0,\alpha_{i}]
. Effectively, this is a change of variables
x_{i}=t_{i}\alpha_{i}
. We can write

\displaystyle f_{m}(t)

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{i}\in[0,1],\ i\in\llbracket 1\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mun\rrbracket\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}t_{i}=t\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{i}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{i}\in[0,1],\ i\in\llbracket 1\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mun\rrbracket\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}t_{i}=t\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(\alpha_{i}t_{i})

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha_{i}t_{i}\in[0,\alpha_{i}],\ i\in\llbracket 1\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mun\rrbracket\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}t_{i}=t\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(\alpha_{i}t_{i})

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\in[0,\alpha_{i}],\ i\in\llbracket 1\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mun\rrbracket\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=t\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\ i\in\llbracket 1\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mun\rrbracket\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=t\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle f_{m}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\square_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\right)(t).

The second to last step is due to
g_{i}(x)=+\infty
for
x\notin[0,\alpha_{i}]
.

An infimal convolution can be computed through the
convex conjugate

[
6
]
.

Definition 7

(Convex conjugate)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{\pm\infty\right\}
be a function. Its convex conjugate or Legendre-Fenchel conjugate
f^{*}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{\pm\infty\right\}
is defined by

f^{*}(s)=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}st-f(t).

Known convex conjugate properties
[
6
]
include the sequel.

Proposition 3

(Convex conjugate of an infimal convolution)
.

A few properties of the convex conjugate will be important to us. They are based on the fact that a proper function
f=f^{**}
if and only if
f
is convex and lower semi-continuous.

Let
g_{i}
be proper, convex and lower semicontinuous. Then

1.

the convex conjugate of the infimal convolution of
g_{i}
is the sum of their convex conjugates:

\left(\square_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\right)^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}^{*}.

2.

the convex conjugate of their summation is the infimal convolution of their convex conjugates:

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\right)^{*}=\square_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}^{*}.

Lastly, observe that the infimal convolution of proper, convex and lower semicontinuous functions is convex and lower semicontinuous.

Coupling these statements, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

f_{m}=f_{m}^{**}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}^{*}\right)^{*}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{i}^{*}\right)^{*}.

Proof.

To prove this, it remains to see that
g_{i}^{*}=\alpha_{i}f_{i}^{*}
:

\displaystyle g_{i}^{*}(s)

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}st-g_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}st-\alpha_{i}f_{i}\left(\frac{t}{\alpha_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}s\alpha_{i}\frac{t}{\alpha_{i}}-\alpha_{i}f_{i}\left(\frac{t}{\alpha_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}\sup_{\frac{t}{\alpha_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}}s\frac{t}{\alpha_{i}}-f_{i}\left(\frac{t}{\alpha_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}su-f_{i}\left(u\right)

\displaystyle g_{i}^{*}(s)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}f_{i}^{*}(s).

∎

The formula above is only interesting if
f_{i}^{*}
and the conjugate of their convex combinations can be computed. A particularly important case arises when
f_{i}
is piecewise affine, which is the case when
f_{i}
is the trade-off function of an intersection of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP constraints.

Let
f_{i}(t)=\max\left\{a_{ij}t+b_{ij}\right\}_{j=1}^{m_{i}}
for
t\in[0,1]
, and
f_{i}(t)=+\infty
else. We can compute
f_{i}^{*}
through the following lemma.

Proposition 4

(Convex conjugate of a piecewise affine function with bounded domain)
.

Let
f(t)=\max\left\{a_{j}t+b_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{m}
for
t\in[t_{0},t_{m}]
and
+\infty
else, such that each line constraint
(a_{j},b_{j})
is active at least at one
t
, and
a_{j}<a_{j+1}
for
j\in[1,m-1]
. Then,
\forall s\in\mathbb{R}
,

f^{*}(s)=\max\left\{t_{j}s-f(t_{j})\right\}_{i=0}^{m}

where
t_{j}=-\frac{b_{j+1}-b_{j}}{a_{j+1}-a_{j}}
for
j\in[1,m-1]
.

The computation follows from
[
6
]
. Observe that
f_{i}^{*}
is thus again a piecewise affine function, defined over the unbounded domain
\mathbb{R}
.
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{i}^{*}
is thus itself a piecewise affine proper function defined over
\mathbb{R}
. The next proposition tells us how to compute its convex conjugate, which is similar - also a result from
[
6
]
.

Proposition 5

(Convex conjugate of a piecewise affine function with unbounded domain)
.

Let
f(t)=\max\left\{a_{j}t+b_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{m}
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, such that each line constraint
(a_{j},b_{j})
is active at least at one
t
, and
a_{j}<a_{j+1}
for
j\in[1,m-1]
. Then,
\forall s\in[a_{1},a_{m}]
,

f^{*}(s)=\max\left\{t_{j}s-f(t_{j})\right\}_{i=0}^{m}

where
t_{j}=-\frac{b_{j+1}-b_{j}}{a_{j+1}-a_{j}}
for
j\in[1,m-1]
, and
f^{*}(s)=+\infty
for
s\not\in[a_{1},a_{m}]
.

These two propositions suggest the following algorithm to compute
f_{m}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{i}^{*}\right)^{*}
:

•

Compute
f_{i}^{*}
using Proposition
4
.

•

Find the slopes and offsets of the piecewise affine function
f_{m}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}f_{i}^{*}
.

•

Finally, conjugate back
f_{m}^{*}
to find
f_{m}=f_{m}^{**}
using Proposition
5
.
