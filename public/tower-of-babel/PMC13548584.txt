Instruments for measuring evidence-based practice ability of nursing students: a COSMIN-based systematic review
Authors: Hang Li, Yunqiong Wang, Xuemei Xie, Yue Li, Jiangli Hu, Juan Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThus far, published systematic reviews have centered exclusively on evidence-based practice (EBP) assessment instruments for registered nurses and healthcare professionals. However, there remains a notable absence of comprehensive reviews focusing specifically on nursing students.AimsTo systematically evaluate the psychometric properties of instruments for assessing EBP ability in nursing students.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Consensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. We systematically searched five databases (CNKI, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and CINAHL Complete) for studies on EBP ability assessment instruments for nursing students published up to April 1, 2025. The methodological quality and psychometric properties of the included instruments were evaluated using COSMIN standards, and final recommendations were formulated.ResultsThe content validity of the articles reviewed is questionable. The structural validity of most studies was very good, with most authors reporting measures of internal consistency. A few studies addressed reliability, criterion validity, measurement error, and hypothesis testing; however, the methodological quality of these assessments raised concerns. Crucially, psychometric properties such as cross‑cultural validity and responsiveness were not evaluated.ConclusionThe quality of instrumental psychometric properties for assessing the EBP ability of nursing students needs to be improved. the S-EBPQ and EBP-COQ appear to be the most promising instruments, but the limitations of their content validity and structural validity still need to be considered. In the future, nursing educators should further develop/validate the EBP ability assessment instrument for nursing students accordance with the COSMIN guidelines and combined with the specific situation of nursing students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04808-4.Keywords: Nursing students, EBP, Nursing education, Psychometric properties, Systematic review

Introduction

Patient safety constitutes a core value of nursing [1] and has emerged as a critical priority in global healthcare policy development [2]. As the profession most directly involved in patient care, nursing is widely regarded as a key safeguard for patient safety [3]. In recent years, evidence-based practice (EBP) has been strongly advocated for clinical implementation by numerous researchers due to its pivotal role in ensuring patient safety [4, 5]. Concurrently, EBP is also recognized as an essential core competency in international nursing practice [6]. By promoting high-quality healthcare, eliminating ineffective or potentially harmful medical interventions, and reducing unnecessary costs, EBP demonstrates significant value in clinical settings [7, 8]. Upton [9] argue that effective patient care should rely not on tradition or belief but on the latest scientific evidence-a robust and reliable foundation that underscores the importance of EBP in clinical decision-making. Consequently, establishing clear EBP ability among nurses is essential [6]. However, systematic reviews indicate that the knowledge and skills of nurses regarding EBP often fall short of expected standards [10], potentially due to inadequate education during both academic training and clinical practice [11]. As future nursing professionals, nursing students represent a vital force in advancing high-quality nursing care, thus, it is imperative that they receive structured EBP education during their training [12], enabling them to integrate EBP principles into clinical practice upon becoming qualified nurses. Such integration is crucial for the continued professional development of nursing [13].

While EBP education has been incorporated into undergraduate nursing curricula in some countries [12, 14], adequate attention to EBP implementation remains lacking [15]. Most educational institutions prioritize theoretical academic activities while neglecting clinical education and practical training [16, 17]. As a result, nursing educators face significant challenges in expecting students to demonstrate competent EBP skills prior to clinical placements [4]. A primary challenge lies in the proper assessment of nursing students’ EBP ability. Indeed, evaluating students’ practical EBP abilities has become a global concern, as the complexity of the assessment has challenged educators for decades [18]. Research indicates that nurse educators require scientifically validated instruments to effectively evaluate students’ EBP ability [19], such instruments enable educators to determine whether teaching practices positively influence students’ EBP ability and provide a foundation for enhancing nursing education quality-a critical factor for advancing EBP in nursing curricula [20]. However, the assessment of the EBP ability of nursing students is hampered by the use of unvalidated and unscientific assessment instruments. High‑quality assessment instruments are a prerequisite for the effective evaluation of nursing students’ EBP ability; thus, there is an urgent need for a scientific instrument to facilitate this process [21]. Recent years have seen substantial research focused on developing and validating EBP ability instruments for nursing students, including the EKAN [22], S-EBPQ [23] and EBP-COQ [21]. Although numerous instruments are available, the quality of these instruments has not yet been evaluated, creating uncertainty regarding instrument selection.

In this review, we operationalized nursing students’ EBP ability as a multidimensional construct encompassing knowledge, attitudes, and skills related to EBP. This conceptualization is grounded in established theoretical frameworks, such as Stevens’ ACE Star Model of EBP [24], which emphasize that EBP ability requires not only cognitive understanding but also behavioral capability and positive attitudes toward implementation. Consequently, instruments focusing solely on EBP beliefs or single-dimension constructs were excluded. To date, systematic reviews have focused exclusively on EBP assessment instruments for practicing nurses and healthcare professionals [25, 26], with a notable absence of comprehensive reviews dedicated to instruments for nursing students. Crucially, instruments designed for different populations serve distinct purposes: those developed for registered nurses typically prioritize clinical applicability, incorporating items regarding workload and the integration of EBP into clinical decision‑making. In contrast, instruments targeting nursing students emphasize academic development, specifically their capacity to conduct EBP‑related research and cultivate critical thinking to identify relevant questions during training. Therefore, based on the Consensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) [27] guidelines, this review systematically evaluates the EBP ability assessment instruments for nursing students, examining both their methodological quality and psychometric properties. The goal is to identify high-quality instruments that enable nursing educators to accurately and effectively assess students’ EBP ability. Additionally, this review aims to inform future empirical research and guide the development, validation, and practical application of EBP assessment tools in nursing education.

Methods

Design

This review followed the COSMIN Patient Reported Outcome Measurement (PROMs) approach for systematic review of measurement properties [28, 29]. According to the COSMIN methodology, the target population for systematic reviews or PROMs may include not only patients but also healthy individuals and caregivers. Therefore, although the present review focuses on nursing students, applying this approach is methodologically appropriate. This review also followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [30]. This study was registered at PROSPERO (registration number: ***).

Eligibility and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Population: The target population was explicitly stated as pre-licensure undergraduate nursing students (e.g., Bachelor of Science in Nursing [BSN] or Associate Degree in Nursing [ADN] programs) or RN-BSN. Studies focusing exclusively on graduate nursing students were excluded; (2) The research focused on the development and/or validation of instruments for assessing EBP ability among nursing students; (3) Full‑text articles were available in either English or Chinese.

Exclusion criteria: (1) secondary studies (review, systematic review, etc.); (2) duplicate publications reporting the same data; (3) used the instrument as an outcome measurement instrument (such as intervention studies).

Study search and selection

Studies published in five databases (CNKI, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and CINAHL) on EBP ability assessment instruments for nursing students were systematically searched until April 1, 2025. The literature search combined MeSH terms and free terms. Since only the PubMed filter was formally validated [31], the search filters for the other four databases were formulated by adapting the COSMIN‑recommended PubMed filter, consulting relevant literature, and seeking guidance from experienced librarians. Mesh terms included “EBP”, “Nursing Student”, “Pupil Nurse”, “Surveys”, “Questionnaires”, “assessment”, “evaluation”, “instrument”, “scale”, “measurement”. In order to ensure the comprehensiveness of the literature retrieval, the references included in the literature were retrieved manually. (The comprehensive search strategy can be found in Supplemental Appendix A).

The literature selection process was conducted in Endnote X9. First duplicate records were identified and removed. Next, two trained researchers (** and **) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining records, retaining those potentially meeting the inclusion criteria and excluding those clearly not meeting the criteria. Subsequently, the same researchers (** and **) thoroughly reviewed the full texts of potentially eligible studies. Any disagreements during this process were resolved by a third researcher (**).

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently from the included studies by one investigator (**) using a structured form, verified by another investigator (**), with discrepancies resolved through discussion. The extracted data comprised: (1) study characteristics (Table 1), including instrument name, authors, publication year, country, content/subscales, and Likert scoring; (2) methodological quality and psychometric properties (Table 2); and (3) the certainty of evidence and recommendations for each instrument (Table 3).

Quality appraisal and data synthesis

The Risk of bias (methodological quality) and psychometric properties of the included studies were independently evaluated by two trained researchers (**) using the COSMIN Risk of Bias Checklist and the COSMIN Quality Criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, or by consulting a third researcher (**) when consensus could not be reached.

The evaluation process comprised three steps: (1) assessing the risk of bias (methodological quality) of each study; (2) evaluating the quality of psychometric properties for each instrument; and (3) rating the certainty of evidence using the COSMIN‑modified GRADE approach to form final recommendations [47]. Detailed procedures for each step are described in the following subsections.

Risk of bias (methodological quality) assessment

The COSMIN Risk of Bias checklist was specifically utilized to assess the risk of bias in the included studies, hereinafter referred to as methodological quality. The list consisted of 10 modules and 116 items. They were PROMs development, content validity, structure validity, internal consistency, hypothesis testing, criterion validity, stability, cross-cultural validity, measurement error, and responsiveness. It uses a 4-point scoring system (“Very good (V)”, “Adequate (A)”, “Doubtful (D)”, or “Inadequate (I)”) to evaluate the risk of bias. The lowest rating among the items was taken as the overall rating for the module according to the “worst-score counts” principle.

Quality assessment of psychometric properties

Based on the COSMIN standards, we evaluated nine psychometric properties. The quality of each property was rated as “Sufficient (+)”, “Indeterminate (?)”, or “Insufficient (–)”. If a property was evaluated inconsistently across studies and the inconsistency could not be explained, it was rated as “Inconsistent (±)”.

Grading the quality of evidence

We used the COSMIN‑modified GRADE method to rate the certainty of evidence for each psychometric property [47]. Starting from “high quality,” we downgraded the evidence based on risk of bias, inconsistency, incoherence, and imprecision, resulting in four levels: high, moderate, low, and very low. Based on the quality of psychometric properties and the certainty of evidence, instruments were classified into three recommendation categories: (1) Category A: Content validity rated as “Sufficient (+)” and internal consistency rated as “Sufficient (+)” (with at least low‑quality evidence); (2) Category B: Not belonging to Category A or C, indicating potential but requiring further validation; (3) Category C: High‑quality evidence showing any psychometric property was “Insufficient (–)”.

Results

Search results

A total of 3,053 citations were retrieved from the databases via systematic searching, and 1 additional citation was identified through manual scanning of relevant reference lists. After removing 623 duplicate citations, 2,431 records remained. Of these, 2,371 citations were excluded based on title and abstract screening, leaving 60 articles for full‑text review. Upon full‑text screening, 39 citations were excluded for the following reasons: wrong study type (e.g., qualitative studies or secondary literature) and inability to obtain the full text. Ultimately, 21 citations met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. The citation screening process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The literature screening flow chart

Included study characteristics

Of the 21 studies included, 7 were for instrument development/validation [7, 21–23, 34, 37, 39]; 1 study shortened S-EBPQ entries to 20 [20]; 1 study shortened the EBPQ entries to 24 [43]; EBP-COQ was translated and culturally adapted into Greek, Turkish, Iranian, Polish and Portuguese in 5 studies [33, 35, 38, 40, 44]. S-EBPQ was translated and culturally adapted into Korean, Saudi Arabian, Chinese and Portuguese in 6 studies [32, 36, 41, 42, 45, 46] (Table 1).

Methodological quality and quality of the instruments’ psychometric properties of included studies

The COSMIN guidelines classify the psychometric properties of an instrument into three domains: validity, reliability, and responsiveness. Validity encompasses content validity, structural validity, hypothesis testing, cross‑cultural validity, and criterion validity. Reliability includes internal consistency, stability and measurement error. In this review, the methodological quality and psychometric properties of the included studies were rigorously assessed using the COSMIN checklist. Content validity was rated “Doubtful” due to insufficient reporting on interviewer expertise and verbatim transcription, despite adequate patient involvement. For structural validity, confirmatory factor analysis generally yielded “Very Good” methodological quality, though psychometric ratings varied based on fit indices (CFI/RMSEA). Internal consistency was methodologically sound across 19 studies, with most demonstrating “Sufficient” psychometric properties (Cronbach’s α ≥ 0.7). Reliability was hampered by inadequate reporting of test-retest conditions, resulting in a “Doubtful” methodological rating. Only one study examined measurement error, receiving a “Very Good” methodological rating but an “Uncertain” psychometric rating due to the absence of MIC values. Finally, criterion validity was deemed “Inadequate” for not utilizing a true gold standard, and hypotheses testing was rated “Indeterminate” due to poorly defined comparisons. Table 2 presents the methodological quality and psychometric property ratings for the included studies. Notably, none of the included studies reported on cross‑cultural validity or responsiveness. A structured synthesis of the results is presented in Table 3, which provides a summary of findings for each instrument, including overall rating and the quality of evidence according to COSMIN guidelines.

Content validity

Among the included studies, content validity was evaluated in 12 studies [21, 22, 33, 35, 37–39, 44–46, 48, 49]. Within this subset, four studies involved consultation with expert panels and/or cognitive interviewing with the target population to assess the relevance and comprehensibility of items. Based on these qualitative assessments, items were modified or culturally adapted prior to psychometric testing. According to COSMIN guidelines, when appropriate qualitative methods are employed to establish content validity, the psychometric property can be rated as “Sufficient (+)”, and the methodological quality can be rated as “Adequate (A)”, even in the absence of verbatim transcripts. For the remaining studies, content validity was evaluated solely through survey methods (e.g., CVI scoring) without qualitative inquiry into patient understanding. Due to the lack of robust qualitative evidence and unclear statistical methods, the quality of the psychometric property in these studies was rated as “Indeterminate (?)”, and the methodological quality was rated as “Doubtful (D)”.

Structural validity

Structural validity was verified in 18 of the included studies [8, 20–23, 32–43, 46]. Among them, 7 studies conducted confirmatory factor analysis with sufficient sample size and no methodological defects, so the methodological quality was “very good (V)” [20, 32, 33, 35, 38, 41, 43]. Among them, 3 studies [20, 35, 43] reported the evaluation instrument CFI > 0.95 or RMSEA < 0.06, so the psychometric properties of their evaluation instrument was “sufficient (+)”, and the remaining 4 studies [32, 33, 38, 41] reported the evaluation instrument CFI < 0.95. The psychometric properties of the evaluation instrument was “insufficient (-)”.

Exploratory factor analysis was used in 10 studies [8, 21, 23, 34, 36, 37, 39, 40, 42, 46], among which 1 study [37] methodological quality was considered “inadequate (I)” because the sample size did not meet the standard of 5 times the number of items of evaluation instruments, and the methodological quality of the remaining 9 studies was rated as “adequate (A)”. The relevant psychometric properties of the reporting instruments were not included in the 10 studies using exploratory factor analysis, therefore, the psychometric properties were ‘uncertain (?)’.

The Rasch model was used in 1 study [22] to evaluate the structural validity, methodological quality was “adequate (A)” due to its sample size < 200, however, the report on model fitting was not mentioned, so the psychometric properties was “uncertain (?)”.

Internal consistency

19 studies [8, 20, 21, 23, 32–46] calculated the internal consistency of each dimension of the instrument items, so the methodological quality evaluation of the 19 studies was “very good (V)”, and the Cronbach’s α cosufficient of each dimension of 17 studies was ≥ 0.7, therefore the psychometric properties of internal consistency of the assessment instrument was evaluated as “sufficient (+)”. In 1 study [39], the Cronbach’s α cosufficient of individual dimensions was < 0.7, so the psychometric properties of internal consistency of the assessment instrument was evaluated as “insufficient (-)”0.1 study [44] validated two instruments, EBP-COQ and S-EBPQ, where the Cronbach’s α cosufficient of the individual dimension of EBP-COQ was > 0.7, therefore, the psychometric property of internal consistency of the assessment instrument was evaluated as “sufficient (+)”. The Cronbach’s α cosufficient of the individual dimension of S-EBPQ was < 0.7, therefore the psychometric property of the internal consistency of the assessment instrument was evaluated as “insufficient (-)”.

Reliability

Reliability was verified in 6 of the included studies [8, 33, 35, 37, 43, 44]. Although 4 studies [8, 33, 35, 37] chose the ideal interval of 2 weeks for retest, none of the 6 studies described in detail whether the patients were stable during the retest and whether the conditions were consistent during the retest, so the methodological quality was “doubtful (D)”.

ICC was reported in 5 studies [8, 33, 37, 44, 50], among them, 3 studies [33, 37, 50] reported an ICC of ≥0.7, leading to their psychometric properties being evaluated as “sufficient (+)”, while 1 study [8] reported an ICC of <0.7, resulting in its psychometric properties being evaluated as “insufficient (-)”. 1 study [44] validated two instruments: for S-EBPQ, the ICC was < 0.7, leading to its psychometric properties being evaluated as “insufficient (-)”; for EBP-COQ, the ICC was ≥ 0.7, resulting in its psychometric properties being evaluated as “sufficient (+)”.Another 1 study [35] did not report ICC, so its psychometric properties were evaluated as “uncertain (?)”.

Measurement error

1 study [44] reported on measurement error and calculated the standard error of measurement and the minimum detectable change, therefore its methodological quality was evaluated as “very good (V)”. However, the study did not calculate MIC, so its psychometric properties were evaluated as “uncertain (?)”.

Criterion validity

Among the included studies, 1 study [33] was evaluated for criterion validity. COSMIN guideline standard pointed out that only the original instrument could be used as the “gold standard” for newly developed instruments, while the “gold standard” was not used in this study, so the methodological quality was “inadequate (I)” and the psychometric properties was “indeterminate (-)”.

Hypotheses testing

2 of the included studies [21, 32] used aggregate validity/discriminative validity for hypothesis testing, and the methodological quality was “adequate (A)” because important features of the control measurement instruments and subgroups were not adequately elucidated, and because the review team did not define the assumptions in advance, so the psychometric properties was “indeterminate (-)”.

Quality of evidence

In this study, the psychometric property evaluation results of the included instruments were synthesized, and the certainty of the evidence was downgraded according to the four domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, and indirectness. The results of the certainty assessment are presented in Table 3. Overall, the certainty of evidence for most instruments was rated as low to moderate, with inconsistency being the primary reason for downgrading. For instance, the EBP‑COQ and S‑EBPQ demonstrated inconsistency in their evaluations of content and structural validity; consequently, combined with their risk of bias assessments, the certainty of evidence was downgraded to very low or low.

Recommendations

Although the content validity of SN-EBP was rated as “sufficient” (albeit based on low‑certainty evidence), its internal consistency was rated as “insufficient” (based on high‑certainty evidence), therefore, it received a Class C recommendation. All other instruments were classified as Class B. The recommended grades for the nine instruments are presented in Table 3.

Discussion

“Evidence-based medicine” was first introduced in the medical field during the early 1990s. As its application expanded to other health disciplines, it evolved into what is now termed “evidence-based practice” [51]. Our review identified a growing body of research on the development and/or validation of EBP ability assessment instruments for nursing students, with the majority of studies published between 2018 and 2022. However, this proliferation of instruments also highlights a lack of consensus regarding the most suitable tools for evaluating nursing students’ EBP abilities. The COSMIN guidelines establish clear criteria for assessing the quality (both methodological and psychometric) of measurement instruments [52], we synthesized the psychometric properties of existing instruments designed to evaluate nursing students’ EBP abilities, aiming to provide evidence‑based guidance for educators in selecting assessment instruments. Specifically, we evaluated the instruments’ development processes, content validity, structural validity, internal consistency, reliability, criterion validity, and hypothesis testing. We did not assess cross-cultural validity, measurement error, or responsiveness, as the included studies failed to examine these psychometric properties.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to examine the psychometric properties of instruments designed to assess nursing students’ EBP ability. Based on an analysis of 18 instrument development and/or validation studies, we identified 9 available instruments. None of the instruments fully met COSMIN standards: 8 were classified as weakly recommended and 1 as not recommended. We recommend that nursing educators, in future work, adhere closely to COSMIN guidelines, place strong emphasis on methodological and psychometric quality, and ensure the reliability of assessment instruments.

During our evaluation of the existing instruments, we found that none of them met the COSMIN standards for the development process. The primary issue was the lack of documented participation of nursing students in that process. Rigorous development of PROMs is a challenging and time‑consuming undertaking [53]. Although the included studies suggested that nursing students may have been invited to participate, the development process was inadequately described. Consequently, the methodological quality of the development process in all included studies was deemed ‘Doubtful’.

Content validity is the foremost and most critical psychometric properties to be considered when selecting a PROMs [54]. It encompasses the assessment of each item’s comprehensiveness, relevance, and understandability by both nursing students and professionals. Among the included studies, content validity was evaluated in 12 studies. According to COSMIN guidelines, when appropriate qualitative methods (e.g., expert panels, cognitive interviewing) were employed to establish relevance and comprehensibility, the psychometric property was rated as ‘Sufficient (+)’, and the methodological quality was rated as ‘Adequate (A)’, even in the absence of verbatim transcripts. Furthermore, most studies relied on small samples of nursing students for quantitative surveys to gauge their understanding of the instrument’s content, without conducting qualitative interviews (e.g., with students or professionals). This lack of in-depth engagement was a key reason why the methodological quality of content validity in the included instruments was predominantly rated as “Doubtful”. Therefore, in the future development/verification process of EBP ability assessment instruments for nursing students, researchers should address this limitation by documenting the entire process of group meetings or interviews and providing detailed descriptions in the manuscript to avoid omitting critical information. Researchers should also incorporate qualitative methods, such as focus group interviews, individual in‑depth interviews, and cognitive interviews [55]. These steps will ensure the instrument’s items align more closely with the intended research content and enhance the credibility of the content validity development process.

Structural validity refers to the degree to which an instrument’s scores accurately reflect the underlying theoretical dimensions it aims to measure. Traditionally, factor analysis (FA)-particularly exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) is used to assess structural validity [56], though it does not inherently evaluate the psychometric quality of the identified dimensions. Item response theory (IRT), a modern psychometric approach, addresses many limitations of classical test theory (CTT) and is considered the preferred statistical method for evaluating structural effectiveness [57]. In our review, we were pleased to find that one of the included studies [22] adopted the Rasch model, but since its sample size was less than 200, which falls below the recommended threshold for robust IRT analysis, we rated its methodological quality as “Adequate”. Among the remaining studies, three studies [7, 44, 45] did not conduct any structural validity verification, while all others employed confirmatory or exploratory factor analysis. The COSMIN guideline believes that compared with exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis is considered to be of ‘Very good’ methodological quality. Therefore, the methodological quality of 7 studies was rated “Very good” based on sufficient sample size. However, suboptimal fit indices (e.g., CFI or TLI < 0.95 or RMSEA > 0.06) were observed in four studies, leading to a rating of ‘Insufficient’ for the psychometric property. Future instrument development or validation should consider utilizing IRT, CTT, and other relevant theories [58], while ensuring adequate sample sizes. If factor analysis is employed, CFA is recommended whenever possible, adhering to COSMIN criteria for psychometric properties. This will enhance the structural validity of the developed instrument.

Internal consistency refers to the degree of intercorrelation among items in PROMs [56], which is typically evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. According to COSMIN guidelines, the methodological quality of internal consistency can be rated as “very good” if the Cronbach’s alpha is calculated for every single‑dimensional scale or subscale. Additionally, the psychometric property quality is rated as “Sufficient” when the Cronbach’s alpha value is ≥0.70. In the included studies, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for all studies except two [7, 22], which did not report this statistic. Among the remaining studies, two [39, 44] reported a Cronbach’s alpha < 0.70 for at least one dimension, leading to a rating of “Insufficient” for their psychometric property quality. All other studies met the threshold (α ≥ 0.70) and were rated as “Sufficient”. For future development or translation of instruments assessing EBP ability in nursing students, researchers should ensure the calculated Cronbach’s alpha meets COSMIN guideline standards (i.e., α ≥ 0.70 for all dimensions) to confirm good internal consistency.

The remaining psychometric properties outlined in the COSMIN guidelines include reliability, criterion validity, hypothesis testing, cross‑cultural validity, measurement error, and responsiveness. Reliability refers to the degree of consistency of the results obtained when the same subject is measured again using the same method, including retest reliability, inter-rater reliability, and intra-rater (subject) reliability [59]. Among the included studies, only six [8, 33, 35, 37, 43, 44] validated stability. In terms of time interval, if the time interval is too long, the respondents are easy to change their attitudes under the influence of environment and personal experience; If the interval is too short, it will be affected by the memory of the last survey, so 2 weeks is considered to be a suitable interval for evaluating retest reliability [60]. Four studies described calculations of test‑retest reliability after a two‑week interval, resulting in a rating of ‘Very good’ for methodological quality. Criterion validity refers to the degree to which the result score measured by the instrument adequately reflects the gold standard [61]. Currently, a gold standard for assessing EBP ability in nursing students is lacking; consequently, although 1 study [33] attempted to validate criterion validity, the absence of a gold standard led us to rate its quality as “Insufficient”. Hypothesis testing is used to validate construct validity, which refers to the degree of agreement between the hypothesis of the difference between the score and the relevant group and the score relationship with other instruments [61], and is another important psychometric properties assessed in both instruments [62]. According to COSMIN guidelines, hypothesis testing is considered ‘Very good’ if the construct measured by the comparator instrument is clearly defined, the comparator is administered to a population similar to the study population, and the comparator possesses sufficient measurement properties. However, these criteria were poorly reported in the two studies [21, 32] that reported hypothesis testing. Measurement errors, including systematic and random errors, accounts for variance unrelated to the true score [63]. To assess measurement error, information regarding the Smallest Detectable Change-defined as the smallest change in score that exceeds measurement error-is required [27, 64]. Unfortunately, only one study [44] evaluated measurement error, and its reporting was incomplete.

Cross‑cultural validity and responsiveness of instruments assessing EBP competence in nursing students have not been empirically verified. According to COSMIN guidelines, cross‑cultural validity refers to the degree to which an instrument measures the same construct across different cultural groups, which requires quantitative assessment, such as Differential Item Functioning (DIF) analysis or measurement invariance testing [61]. Although nine studies in this review reported translation or cultural adaptation of existing instruments, these procedures were limited to achieving semantic equivalence and did not involve formal quantitative validation. No study performed statistical testing to confirm cross‑cultural validity, even among instruments adapted for diverse contexts. Consequently, the measurement invariance of the included instruments across different regions and cultural backgrounds remains unclear [65]. Responsiveness is a key indicator of an instrument’s sensitivity to detect meaningful changes over time. It evaluates the instrument’s ability to: detect changes in the same group’s construct scores over time and discriminate between distinct groups [66]. During the development of the instrument, if the instrument has good reliability and validity, but is unable to detect some subtle and clinically significant changes over time, then the instrument is not perfect in design [67], so this is a very relevant measurement feature. Nursing educators need a measuring instrument that can be used to monitor the ability of nursing students to practice evidence-based practices over time.

Limitations

This review has some limitations. First, although the methodology aligns with widely accepted standards, the COSMIN guidelines employ the principle of lowest score-where the overall evaluation of a measurement property is determined by the weakest aspect of its assessment. Consequently, even if a study adhered to COSMIN standards during its research process, a lack of detailed reporting necessitates a downgraded quality rating. Therefore, we acknowledge that this reporting dependency may introduce potential bias into the results. Second, our inclusion criteria were restricted to studies involving nursing students. As a result, studies that employed the same instruments as those included in this review but focused on non-nursing populations (e.g., other healthcare trainees or general student groups) were excluded. This narrow focus could limit the generalizability of our psychometric property assessments to broader contexts beyond nursing education. Third, we limited our review to studies published in English and Chinese. Consequently, potentially relevant studies published in other languages were excluded, which may have introduced language-related bias and reduced the comprehensiveness of our findings.

Conclusion

This review synthesized the psychometric properties of existing instruments assessing EBP ability in nursing students. While the number of studies on instrument development and validation has increased in recent years, most have focused on a limited set of psychometric properties. Critical aspects-including cross-cultural validity, measurement error, and responsiveness-remain underexplored. Furthermore, the methodological and psychometric quality of the development process has been largely suboptimal, with most evidence rated as very low to low certainty. Given these limitations, we refrain from making a firm recommendation. However, based on the currently available evidence, the S-EBPQ and EBP-COQ appear to be the most promising instruments. These instruments have been translated into multiple languages and demonstrate relatively balanced psychometric performance across the evaluated properties compared to other instruments. Nevertheless, the lack of robust evidence regarding cross-cultural validity and the potential influence of cultural background on their applicability must be acknowledged. Therefore, future research should prioritize high-quality validation studies to confirm the psychometric robustness of these instruments, or develop new instruments strictly adhering to COSMIN guidelines. These findings provide valuable insights for nursing educators and serve as a reference for selecting instruments to evaluate nursing students’ EBP ability.
