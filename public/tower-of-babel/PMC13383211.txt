Serious Game for Long Term Oxygen Therapy Education: Initial Results in Undergraduate Medical Students
Authors: Ana Balaña Corberó, Álvaro Clemente-Vivancos, Anna Rodó-Pin, Mireia Admetlló, Anna Herranz, Oscar Garcia Pañella, Juana Martinez-Llorens, Diego Rodríguez-Chiaradía
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT) is a treatment commonly prescribed for patients with chronic respiratory diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease, and pulmonary hypertension, as well as for palliative management. Despite its wide use, both clinicians and patients report insufficient education on LTOT, particularly in non-specialized settings. This study presents the initial results of a developed serious game aimed at teaching core LTOT concepts. The game includes eight interactive clinical scenarios covering indications, initial assessment, device types, daily use, follow-up, equipment maintenance, complications, and special situations. A total of 120 fourth-year medical students participated over two academic years and completed pre- and post-intervention questionnaires. All students completed the game, with high engagement and overall performance. Knowledge significantly improved after the intervention, with a meaningful increase in correct responses. These findings suggest that a serious game may be an effective educational tool for improving LTOT knowledge among preclinical medical students and could be expanded to other health professionals, patients, and caregivers.

Long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT) is a prescribed medical intervention essentially delivered by pulmonologists for a wide range of chronic respiratory diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease, pulmonary hypertension, and for the palliation of refractory dyspnea in advanced or terminal conditions.1 LTOT has been shown to improve survival in chronic respiratory failure, enhances functional status, and reduces acute exacerbations when appropriately indicated and supervised. However, both patients and clinicians express the need about LTOT education,2 especially in non-specialized settings. Teaching LTOT to medical students is challenging because it involves indications, equipment and multiple clinical situations that exceed a single lecture o practice. Gamification has proven effective for complex medical concepts, increasing learner engagement.3 To our knowledge, no formal game-based exercises exist to teach LTOT to medical students. This study aims to present the initial results of a newly designed serious game for teaching core LTOT concepts.

The serious game was developed following a standardized educational gamification model (Hamburger Model),4 which ensures coherent narrative structure, appropriate game mechanics, and a well designed feedback system (Fig. 1). Operationally, the methodology follows a structured, co-creative process that guides the transition from the existing training context to a defined educational objective state. It begins with the systematic collection of inputs, including clinical needs, learning objectives, contextual constraints, and evaluation criteria. This is followed by a co-creation phase involving key stakeholders, such as clinicians and domain experts, through facilitated workshops designed to align expectations, surface challenges, and define the experiential direction. Insights derived from this phase are synthesized into an Experience Design Document, which articulates the “health edugame” that is the gamified educational strategy across narrative, technological, psychological, esthetical, and game design dimensions.5 Subsequent stages include the development of a minimum viable version of the platform, an onboarding and launch phase to introduce participants to the learning universe, and the deployment of the experience as a transmedia system. Also, an engagement loop enables continuous feedback, data collection, and adaptive refinement of the system in response to user performance and needs. The authors created eight clinical LTOT scenarios (indication, initial evaluation, devices types, daily use, follow-up, equipment maintenance, complications and special situations). Each stages contained short texts and interactive questions. Most were single-answer multiple-choice, with some true/false item. Fourth-year medical students were invited to participate in our serious game during the compulsory Integrated Respiratory and Cardiovascular Medicine subject of their pre-clinical curriculum. They completed a pre- and post-intervention survey with 10 LTOT-related questions. For nominal/ordinal variables, relative frequencies were employed for description. For continuous variables, median (IQR 25–75) were computed. IBM SPSS Statistics 27 was used to perform all statistical analyses.

One hundred-twenty students, in two consecutive years (2024–2025), participated in the game sessions and completed the pre-intervention survey (100%), of which 119 completed the post-intervention survey (99%). The experience was open during two weeks. The mean time to complete the experience was 126 (99–189) minutes. All students completed the eight units. All action and question during the game received a specific score, being 900 points the highest score. Twenty participants (16%) received the highest score while six students (5%) rise the lowers score (below 700). The average score of the group was 830 (802–875). Regarding the change from baseline survey questionnaire (corrected answers between post-and pre-intervention), the absolute difference was 9.4 points [95%confidence interval, (7.1–11.8), p = 0.001)]. No differences were observed between students from 2024 and 2025. To our knowledge, this is the first study evaluating the implementation of a serious game specifically designed to teach LTOT. This pedagogical innovation strengthens fundamental concepts in LTOT among medical students during gameplay in potential clinical scenarios. Recent evidence demonstrate that gamification, serious games, and extended reality, based learning environments improve clinical reasoning, knowledge retention, and student satisfaction across multiple health related disciplines.7 These approaches have shown clear advantages over traditional teaching formats, which often rely on passive, lecture based instruction. However, that advantages serious games have relatively low impact in respiratory medicine education in general and LTOT teaching in particular.8 Conversely, there is a growing need for training in LTOT, as it is a complex therapy, applied in various ambulatory contexts9 and not without complications.10 In this way, our serious game addresses this complexity in a practical way within a playful framework. Also, an important advantage of this experience is that its gamification model is scalable and applicable to any educational context without relevant cost of implementation.

Limitations include the small size and lacked a control group. Nevertheless, the study involved a homogeneous cohort of medical students enrolled in a compulsory subject without prior LTOT experience.

In conclusion, implementing a specific serious game within the LTOT curriculum for preclinical and fourth-year medical students may represent an appropriate first step toward expanding LTOT education for health professionals, patients, and caregivers.

Ethical considerations

This study evaluated an educational activity conducted as part of the undergraduate medical curriculum. Only anonymized educational data were analyzed, and no clinical interventions or patient data were involved. According to institutional practice, formal ethical approval was not considered necessary. Participation in the educational activity was voluntary, questionnaires were completed anonymously, and no personally identifiable information was collected.

Use of artificial intelligence

Microsoft Copilot was used for grammar and language revision during the preparation of this manuscript. No AI-generated content was used for data analysis, interpretation, or generation of ideas or conclusions.
