Stiff interlocked nanoclay aerogels for synergistic detection and removal of organic contaminants in water
Authors: Shanying Sui, Jingxing Wang, Yufang Lu, Yufan Yang, Zhenhua Song, Chao Yin, Yixian Zhang, Yi Zhang, Huafeng Quan, Shaoqiang Guo, Zhifang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Removing organic pollutants from water is essential for preserving global ecosystems. However, challenges related to recyclability and material stability hinder effective treatment. Inspired by the mortise-and-tenon structure, a series of Ca2+-enhanced amino acid-intercalated layered double hydroxide aerogels featuring an interlocked framework was developed via the structural memory effect and supercritical drying. The aerogels achieve a balance of compressive strength (40.1 − 45.5 MPa) and toughness, overcoming the brittleness of conventional inorganic aerogels. They can remove a broad range of water pollutants, including tetracycline, doxycycline hydrochloride, methyl orange, Congo red and malachite green, with efficiencies of 95.4 − 99.9%, while retaining 87% of their initial efficiency after five regeneration cycles and enabling pollutant detection with a detection limit of 710 nM. The aerogels also exhibit robust stability under liquid nitrogen and butane flame exposure, along with flame resistance (limiting oxygen index ≈ 100%). These findings highlight their potential for water purification under harsh environmental conditions.

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of organic pollutants (OPs) in water resources raises global public health concerns, such as antibiotic resistance, threatening both human health and aquatic ecological stability1. The pressing need for effective remediation strategies calls for materials capable of sensitive recognition, detection, and high-efficiency removal for OPs. A promising approach combines adsorption and photodegradation, where pre-enrichment reduces mass transfer resistance and solar energy is harnessed for in-situ degradation of OPs2. On this basis, innovative design of fluorescence probes can facilitate the integration of diagnosis and treatment systems for target OPs, thereby enabling more efficient remediation processes. Despite the promising potential of the approaches mentioned above, significant challenges hinder the seamless integration of these two functions in practical applications.

Layered double hydroxides (LDH) are a large class of lamellar anionic synthetic clays, offering a combination of large specific surface area, strong adsorption capacity, thermal stability, and acid-base resistance3. The unique structure of LDH, characterized by strong intralayer chemical bonds and weak interlayer bonds with anions, imparts intrinsic anion exchange capability. This property allows the incorporation of diverse guest species into LDH interlayers to achieve specific physicochemical properties4. In recent years, various LDH-based powders for water remediation have been investigated, but most of them suffer from limitations in practical applications, such as the difficulty in recovery and the risk of pollution re-diffusion. In response, aerogels have gained attention as promising alternatives owing to their high porosity, large specific surface area, and recyclability5. However, previous approaches to preparing LDH aerogels have faced inherent challenges, primarily due to limitations in LDH content. The high loading of LDH typically leads to brittleness and pulverization due to the high crystallinity of inorganic phases, which ultimately undermines the mechanical integrity and practical applications of the aerogels. Therefore, seeking to develop eco-friendly, low-toxicity, and efficient LDH aerogels with enhanced cross-linking strength and robust stability in water remains a critical challenge.

In this work, an effective strategy is presented for fabricating robust and eco-friendly bifunctional Ca2+-enhanced amino acid intercalated LDH (Ca2+-AA-LDH) aerogels. By embedding different amino acids into the LDH structure, we develop five amino acid-intercalated LDH (AA-LDH) hydrogels that, upon Ca2+ strengthening and supercritical drying, are successfully converted into aerogels. The innovative design of these five LDH hybrid aerogels is guided by several discoveries: (ⅰ) The memory effect of LDHs enables their transformation into layered double oxides (LDO) upon calcination, followed by recrystallization in the presence of air and target amino acid solutions3. (ⅱ) The intercalation of amino acids can reshape LDH interlayer spaces, enhancing interactions with adsorbates and modifying intermolecular dynamics, which are crucial for tuning the luminescence properties and reducing the intrinsic wide bandgap of LDHs6. (ⅲ) In the mortise-and-tenon structure, the interlocking mechanism between tenons and mortises enables efficient energy dissipation and stress transfer, thereby achieving superior mechanical performance. Similarly, the intercalation of flexible organic components (amino acids) into rigid crystalline inorganic phase (LDH) forms an interlocked network structure akin to a mortise-and-tenon joint. This design effectively mitigates the brittleness and crystallization-induced fragmentation commonly observed in traditional inorganic materials7,8. (ⅳ) The introduction of metal cations can enhance the cross-linking strength of hydrogels, contributing to the stability of the resulting LDH hybrid aerogels9. (ⅴ) Fluorescence enhancement is observed in non-fluorescent material-intercalated LDH, unlike traditional methods that usually rely on the incorporation of fluorescent materials. The creative design of multifunctional LDH aerogels provides a promising strategy for the control and treatment of residual OPs in wastewater.

Results

Fabrication and characterization of Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels

The fabrication process of the Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels is illustrated in Fig. 1a. Initially, hydrophobic layered Mg/Al LDH was calcined in air to obtain hydrophilic Mg/Al LDO (Fig. S1). X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) analyses confirm the complete decomposition of the original LDH crystal structure in the LDO (Fig. S2, Note 1). SEM images reveal that both LDH and LDO exhibit a typical plate-like morphology, suggesting that the layered structure is largely maintained after heat treatment (indicated by red circles in Fig. S3). However, the TEM images reveal clear morphological differences. After calcination, the surface becomes much rougher and displays worm-like textures, which is consistent with the previous report10. This LDO intermediate was then reacted with aqueous solutions of various amino acids (alanine, glycine, threonine, histidine, sarcosine) under water-bath and hydrothermal conditions. During this process, the amino acids intercalated into the interlayer galleries, resulting in the formation of self-supporting amino acid-intercalated LDH (AA-LDH: Ala-LDH, Gly-LDH, Thr-LDH, His-LDH, Sar-LDH) hydrogels (Figs. 1b and S4). The gelation mechanism is attributed to a synergistic combination of factors: ⅰ) swelling of LDH in zwitterionic amino acid aqueous solutions, ⅱ) electrostatic attraction between the positively charged LDH layer and the α-amino acid anions, and ⅲ) stabilization through hydrogen bonding between amino acid groups and surface −OH groups on the LDH11,12. It is noteworthy that gel formation is not universal for all amino acids tested (Table S1), suggesting a dependence on specific molecular properties, such as structure and aggregation behavior, which are influenced by reaction conditions like pH and temperature. To further enhance the mechanical strength of the as-prepared AA-LDH hydrogels, they were subsequently immersed in a Ca2+ solution, forming Ca2+-AA-LDH hydrogels with enhanced strength. The simple preparation process facilitates the fabrication of Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels with complex architectures, which are shaped from their hydrogel precursors (Fig. S5). By selecting different amino acid precursors, a family of distinct aerogels was successfully synthesized (Ca2+-Ala-LDH, Ca2+-Gly-LDH, Ca2+-Thr-LDH, Ca2+-His-LDH, Ca2+-Sar-LDH, Fig. 1c). Structural analysis confirms the successful reconstruction of the LDH layered structure. The XRD patterns of all aerogels exhibit characteristic LDH diffraction peaks (Fig. 1d)13. Variations in the sharpness and the shift of the (003) plane highlight distinctions in the crystallinity and layer spacing (d003) among different aerogels (Fig. S6). Specifically, the d003 values for Ca2+-AA-LDH are calculated to be 0.757 − 0.7824 nm, all of which exceed that of original Mg/Al LDH (0.7494 nm). Further structural information is provided by FTIR spectra (Fig. 1e), which reveal the reappearance of characteristic LDH bands in the ranges of 400 − 1000, 1359 − 1362, and 3357−3368 cm−114. The bands at 1583 − 1633 cm−1 are assigned to the stretching vibration of −COO− of amino acids15. The absorption bands in the 3357 − 3368 cm−1, attributed to −OH stretching vibration of Al−OH, Mg−OH, and H − OH groups, exhibit a broader width and a red-shift compared to the original Mg/Al LDH, which can be ascribed to the interactions between amino acids, LDH, and Ca2+. In addition, the Raman spectrum proves the interaction between amino acids and LDH (Fig. S7 and Note 2).

Porosity measurements reveal high porosity values of Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels, with Ca2+-Gly-LDH (93%) > Ca2+-His-LDH (92.6%) > Ca2+-Thr-LDH (91.3%) > Ca2+-Ala-LDH (90.8%) > Ca2+-Sar-LDH (89.9%), which contribute to mass transfer efficiency and adsorption capacity during water treatment (Fig. 1f). Furthermore, the Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels exhibit low densities ranging from 145.8 to 207.9 mg cm−3, and volume shrinkage rates between 21.9% and 34.6%. Aerogels with greater shrinkage rates exhibit higher density and correspondingly lower porosity (Fig. S8). Textural properties were further quantified by N2 physisorption. As depicted in Figs. 1g and S9, the adsorption-desorption isotherms exhibit type IV curves with H1 hysteresis loops according to IUPAC classification (Note 3). All five aerogels demonstrate high specific surface areas (80.5 − 126.8 m2 g−1) and hierarchical pore structures, including micropores, mesopores, and macropores, which can provide abundant active sites for adsorption-photodegradation (Table S2).

Microstructural analysis via SEM shows that the aerogels comprise interlocked nanosheets forming a robust, mortise-and-tenon-like architecture with a regular porous network, which is a direct result of the supercritical drying process (Figs. 1h, i and S10). This intricate nanostructure is conducive to strong mechanical performance. Elemental mapping confirms the homogeneous distribution of Mg, Al, O, C, N, and Ca throughout the structure, verifying the uniform intercalation of the amino acids and Ca2+ ions (Fig. S11). TEM analysis reveals the thin, sheet-like morphology of the aerogel cell walls (Fig. 1j). The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern displays continuous rings indexed to the (006), (104), and (018) planes of LDH, consistent with the polycrystalline nature observed by XRD (Fig. 1k).

Mechanical performance of Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels

The mechanical robustness of the Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels, a critical attribute for practical application, was rigorously evaluated. Remarkably, four Ca2+-His-LDH aerogels (1.2 g) can support about 40,000 times their own weight without fracturing or collapsing (Fig. 2a). Uniaxial compression tests reveal that all five aerogels display three characteristic stages: a linear elastic stage, attributed to the slight deformation of the pore structure; a yield stage, characterized by a gradual increase in stress as the pore structure collapses progressively; and a densification stage, during which the stress undergoes a sharp escalation owing to the failure of pore channels (Fig. 2b). All aerogels can be completely compressed and resist fracture, exhibiting high toughness. The compressive strengths of Ca2+-Ala-LDH, Ca2+-Gly-LDH, Ca2+-Thr-LDH, Ca2+-His-LDH, and Ca2+-Sar-LDH aerogels reach 43.6, 42.6, 40.1, 45.5, and 44.8 MPa, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2c, their compressive strengths are higher than those of most previously reported silica composite aerogels16–18, clay composite aerogels19,20, polyorganosiloxane composite aerogels21–25, and other aerogels26–28. The exceptional mechanical properties are attributed to multiple reinforcement strategies: First, the immersion of Ca2+ and subsequent solvent exchange promote the transformation of hydrogels from a soft to an intermediate and ultimately to a relatively rigid state (Figs. 2e and S12). This transition is considered crucial, as the enhanced strength of the hydrogels lays the foundation for fabricating aerogels with superior mechanical performance. Specifically, incorporating inorganic Ca2+ significantly improves the storage moduli of AA-LDH hydrogels by a factor of 248.6 − 2125.6 (Fig. S13). After solvent replacement (Ca2+-His-LDH-EtOH gel), the loss of interlayer water in the Ca2+-AA-LDH hydrogels induces structural contraction and strengthens intermolecular forces, further increasing the storage moduli. Second, the introduction of a rigid-flexible cross-linked network enhances the toughness of the final aerogels. The Ca2+ cross-linking mechanism was elucidated by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Optimized configurations show that, unlike LDH, His-LDH can completely capture Ca2+ within its interlayer without ionization or substitution of Mg/Al atoms (Fig. 2f, g). Furthermore, the considerable overlap between Ca, C, O, H, N, and Mg orbitals in the DOS and PDOS analysis proves the strong interaction among the elestrons of Ca, C, O, H, N, and Mg (Fig. 2h). This interaction facilitates the formation of an organic-inorganic hybrid network, effectively addressing the inherent brittleness of inorganic materials. Third, the high permeability of supercritical fluid ensures uniform penetration and solvent replacement within the hydrogels, which contributes to the formation of aerogels with uniformly distributed microporous structures and interlocked cell walls. This architecture allows for efficient stress distribution across the nanosheets, imparting high resistance to sliding and fracture, which is a distinct advantage over the mechanically weak aerogels produced by freeze-drying (Figs. S14 and S15). This combination of high strength and toughness facilitates efficient recycling and minimizes secondary pollution from material shedding.

The aerogels exhibit high stability under harsh conditions. They can withstand multiple freeze-thaw cycles at −196 °C without fracturing (Fig. 2i and Video S1), and demonstrate exceptional fire resistance, remaining intact and non-combustible upon direct exposure to a butane flame (Figs. 2j and S16, and Video S2). Representative Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel achieved a limiting oxygen index (LOI) approaching 100% (Fig. 2k), surpassing most reported flame-retardant materials29–35. Cone calorimetry tests recorded a very low total heat release (THR) of 3.0 MJ m−2 and total smoke production (TSP) of 6.3×10−2 m2 (Fig. S17a, b), values significantly lower than those of commercial polymer foams36 and most flame-retardant materials (Fig. 2l)37–43. This flame retardancy is governed by synergistic mechanisms: the dilution of oxygen concentration by thermal decomposition products H2O and CO2, the barrier effect of gradually generated MgO, MgAl2O4, and carbon layer on oxygen, and the low thermal conductivity ensured by the structural integrity after ablation (Figs. 2m, n, S17c, d, and S18 and Notes 4 and 5). In conclusion, these Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels can maintain structural integrity from low temperature (−196 °C) to high temperature (~1300 °C).

Adsorption and photodegradation performance of Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels for OPs

The removal behavior of OPs by Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels was further investigated. Throughout the adsorption-photodegradation process of tetracycline (TC), the aerogel maintained excellent mechanical integrity, capable of bearing a load (200 g) approximately 1300 times its own weight (153.8 mg) even after a visible color change from white to yellow (Fig. 3a). Benefiting from the high specific surface area and hydrophilic properties (Fig. S19), all Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels show TC removal capability (Fig. 3b). In a TC solution with an initial concentration (C0) of 50 mg L−1, the representative Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel achieved the highest removal efficiency of 95.4% within 60 min, with 51.3% attributed to adsorption and 44.1% to photocatalytic degradation, surpassing most previously reported materials (Table S3). At the higher initial concentration (C0 = 400 mg L−1), the contribution of adsorption decreased to 26.5%, while the photocatalytic contribution increased to 57.5% (Fig. S20). The effect of different initial concentrations (0.5 − 400 mg L−1) on the removal efficiency was further investigated. The results showed that when C0 < 20 mg L−1, the removal efficiency reached 99.9%, and when C0 < 200 mg L−1, the removal efficiency remained above 92.7% (Figs. 3c and S21). Furthermore, leaching and adsorption-photodegradation experiments demonstrate that Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel exhibits stable and high removal capabilities (87.4% − 96.7%) across a broad pH range of 4 − 13, eliminating the necessity of pH adjustment as a pretreatment step during treatment (Figs. 3d and S22). The practical applicability was further validated using real water samples. The TC removal efficiencies in tap water (93.2%), river water (91.1%), and lake water (90.2%) are only marginally lower than in pure laboratory water (95.4%), demonstrating robustness against complex aqueous matrices (Fig. 3e and Table S4). The Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel also shows excellent removal ability for other OPs, including doxycycline hydrochloride (DOX, 96.3%), methyl orange (MO, 99.9%), Congo red (CR, 97.5%), and malachite green (MG, 98.0%) within 60 min. Even under the influence of 900 °C heat flow, the aerogel still maintains high removal efficiencies (95.0% − 99.8%) for these five OPs (Fig. 3f). These results indicate that the Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogel can efficiently treat complex wastewater systems under high-temperature conditions. To assess scalability and feasibility, a continuous flow-through reactor was designed for cyclic treatment (Figs. 3g and S24). In this system, TC-contaminated water is circulated through the aerogel-filled reactor under simulated sunlight. The device can achieve 98.3% TC removal within 70 min and maintain a high efficiency of 87.0% after five consecutive cycles (Fig. 3h). These findings highlight the robustness, reusability, and versatility of Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels for TC removal under diverse environmental conditions, making them promising candidates for sewage treatment applications.

The adsorption mechanism was systematically analyzed under neutral conditions (C0 = 50 mg L−1, m/V = 2 mg mL−1, T = 25 °C). FTIR and XPS were used to characterize the samples before and after TC adsorption (Ca2+-AA-LDH-TC). Deconvoluted Mg 1s and Ca 2p XPS spectra reveal shifts in binding energies after TC adsorption (Fig. 3i, j). Specifically, the characteristic Mg−O band shifts from 1303.9 to 1303.4 eV, while the original COO−Ca doublet (Ca 2p3/2 at 351.0 eV and Ca 2p1/2 at 352.6 eV) shifts to lower binding energies of 350.7 and 352.4 eV, respectively. Furthermore, a new doublet emerges in the Ca 2p spectrum of Ca2+-AA-LDH-TC aerogel at 351.8 (Ca 2p3/2) and 353.9 eV (Ca 2p1/2), which can be assigned to Ca−O(H) species. In contrast, Al 2p spectrum shows no evident changes, indicating that Al3+ does not participate in TC adsorption. These changes collectively confirm the complexation of Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions with TC44. Additionally, for the XPS spectrum of N 1 s, the band of −NH/ −C−N shifts from 399.9 eV to 398.9 eV, while the band of −C=N shifts from 398.2 eV to 400.1 eV (Fig. S25)45. These shifts may be attributed to the formation of hydrogen bonds during adsorption. FTIR results of Ca2+-His-LDH-TC aerogels further confirm the above results (Fig. 3k). The band corresponding to M−O exhibits distinct shifts compared with that of the Ca2+-His-LDH aerogels, indicating the surface complexation between metal ions and TC46. Moreover, shifts of −COOH and −OH bands indicate hydrogen bonding formed by the surface −COOH/ −OH groups of Ca2+-His-LDH and N− or O− groups of TC47. Collectively, these results indicate that TC adsorption is facilitated by a combination of surface coordination with Mg2+/Ca2+ ions, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions. The XRD result indicates that TC was not intercalated into the interlayer during the adsorption process (Fig. S26).

The adsorption kinetics and isotherm behavior of TC on Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel were quantitatively evaluated. The adsorption process is well fitted by the pseudo-first-order kinetic model (Fig. S27a and Table S5). The fitted intra-particle diffusion model of Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel further reveals two distinct stages with slopes of 1.9 and 0.1, indicating a significant decrease in the diffusion rate (Fig. S27b). This behavior can be attributed to the initial rapid diffusion of TC to the surface and macropores, followed by slower diffusion into mesopores and micropores once the surface sites are occupied. Additionally, the adsorption process of Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel is well described by the Langmuir model (Fig. S28a and Table S6). In terms of adsorption performance, the Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel can achieve a Langmuir maximum adsorption capacity (Qmax) of 975.8 mg g−1, which is comparable to that of two commercially activated carbons (992.3 − 1037.7 mg g−1) (Fig. S28b−c). The Langmuir Qmax values of Ca2+-His-LDH aerogels for DOX, MO, CR, and MG were 330.5, 1036.8, 743.1, and 535.9 mg g−1, respectively (Fig. S29 and Table S7). Furthermore, a fixed-bed column system was used to obtain the dynamic adsorption breakthrough curve of TC on the Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel. The experimental data were fitted using the Thomas and Yoon−Nelson models to quantitatively describe its dynamic adsorption behavior (Fig. S30 and Table S8). The results indicate that this aerogel shows strong potential as a fixed-bed adsorbent in continuous flow systems for TC removal.

The mechanism of TC photodegradation was elucidated through radical trapping experiments and electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy. Methanol (MeOH), benzoquinone (BQ), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt dihydrate (EDTA-2Na·2H2O) were employed as scavengers to quench hydroxyl radicals (·OH), superoxide radicals (·O2−), and photogenerated holes (h+), respectively. The experimental results reveal that MeOH exerts relatively minor inhibitory effects on the degradation process of TC, whereas BQ and EDTA-2Na significantly diminish the removal efficiency (Figs. 4a and S31). These findings confirm that h+, ·OH, and ·O2− are the primary active substances driving TC photodegradation, with the contribution order of h+ > ·O2−> ·OH. As depicted in the ESR spectra (Figs. 4b and S32), characteristic bands of ·OH and ·O2− appear under xenon irradiation but are absent under dark conditions, which is consistent with the above result. The bandgap energies (Eg) of Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels were determined from corresponding Tauc’s plots of ultraviolet-Visible diffuse reflectance spectra (UV-vis DRS) (Fig. S33 and Table S9). The Eg values follow the sequence of Ca2+-His-LDH (2.72 eV) < Ca2+-Gly-LDH (2.73 eV) < Ca2+-Thr-LDH (2.83 eV) < Ca2+-Ala-LDH (2.93 eV) < Ca2+-Sar-LDH (2.94 eV). Based on the valence/conduction band potential (EVB/ECB) (Note 7), a proposed photocatalytic mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 4c. Upon photon excitation, the transition of charge carriers in the excited state occurs, generating h+ and electrons (e−) in the valence band (VB) and conduction band (CB), respectively (Ca2+-AA-LDH + hυ → e− + h+). Because the conduction band potential (ECB) is more negative than the standard reduction potential of O2 ( − 0.33 eV vs NHE) and the valence band potential (EVB) is more positive than the redox potential of H2O/·OH (2.38 eV vs NHE), ·OH and ·O2− are generated via O2 + e− → ·O2− and OH− + h+ → ·OH reactions, respectively5. Such favorable band alignment promotes the generation of reactive oxygen species, thereby significantly enhancing the photocatalytic performance48. The photocurrent response density of Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels was also measured at alternating 30 s intervals of xenon illumination and dark conditions (Figs. 4d and S34). All five samples demonstrated pronounced photocurrent responses, confirming their capability of separating photogenerated carriers. Furthermore, the mineralization degree of TC was evaluated by measuring the total organic carbon (TOC) removal efficiency. As shown in Fig. S35, the TOC removal efficiency (87.3%) is lower than the corresponding degradation efficiency (95.4%), indicating incomplete mineralization and the likely formation of intermediate products. DFT calculations were employed to explore the electron transfer between Ca2+-His-LDH and TC. As shown in Fig. 4e, f, the DOS and PDOS of Ca2+-His-LDH-TC show considerable overlaps between the H, O, C, and N orbitals, implying the strong interactions among H, O, C, and N electrons during TC adsorption. The optimized structures of LDH, His-LDH, and Ca2+-His-LDH are presented in Fig. 4g, with corresponding total energy (Etotal) of −1987.4, −2005.5, and −2016.8 eV, respectively (Table S10). Compared to LDH and His-LDH, Ca2+-His-LDH exhibits the highest adsorption energy (Ead = 1.32 eV) and the narrowest highest occupied molecular orbital-lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (HOMO-LUMO) gap (Table S11). The smaller band gap helps extend light absorption into the visible region, thereby improving the generation efficiency of photoinduced charge carriers49. These findings highlight the critical role of histidine and Ca2+ in enhancing the affinity for TC and catalytic activity by modulating electron distribution and reducing the energy barrier. Additionally, the HOMO of TC ( − 4.75 eV) is lower than the LUMO of Ca2+-His-LDH (1.06 eV), confirming electron transfer from TC’s HOMO to Ca2+-His-LDH’s LUMO50. According to Bader charge analysis, the transfer of 7.1 e from TC to Ca2+-His-LDH is quantified. To better understand electron migration, the charge density difference map visualizes the distribution of electron accumulation (yellow regions) and depletion (blue regions). As can be seen, all atoms except Al exhibit significant electron gain and loss, confirming Al’s minimal involvement in the adsorption process, which aligns perfectly with XPS results (Fig. 4h).

Fluorescence identification and detection of Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels for OPs

The Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels also exhibit intrinsic fluorescence, enabling both OPs removal and detection. All five aerogels display excitation-dependent fluorescence, with emission spectra shifting as the excitation wavelength increases (Figs. 5a and S38). The variations in optimal excitation wavelengths and fluorescence intensities among the samples are attributed to differences in their interlayer microstructure, such as molecular interactions and layer spacing, which collectively influence the energy transition processes upon photoexcitation. The Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel functions as an effective fluorescent probe for TC detection. As shown in Fig. 5b, the fluorescence emission intensity of Ca2+-His-LDH (1 mg mL−1, 1 mL) at about 380 nm progressively decreases with the addition of increasing concentrations of TC (20 μL), demonstrating a turn-off fluorescence response. The intensity as a function of TC concentration exhibits good linearity, with a limit of detection (LOD) of 1.8 μM (Fig. S39a). To further explore the quenching mechanism, the UV-vis absorption spectrum of TC was analyzed, which overlaps well with the excitation spectrum of Ca2+-His-LDH and only partially overlaps with its emission spectrum (Fig. 5c), suggesting possible inner filter effect (IFE) or fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET)51,52. However, the nearly identical fluorescence lifetimes before and after TC addition (3.2 ns vs. 3.5 ns) rule out FRET (Fig. 5d)53. Additionally, the LUMO of Ca2+-His-LDH (1.06 eV) is higher than that of TC ( − 2.71 eV), facilitating electron transfer from Ca2+-His-LDH to TC and enabling fluorescence quenching via photoinduced electron transfer (PET)54. Thus, the fluorescence quenching of Ca2+-His-LDH by TC is attributed to a combination of the IFE and PET effects. This detection system performs robustly in real-world water matrices. As shown in Fig. 5e, significant fluorescence quenching can be observed in tap water, river water, and lake water spiked with TC, with quenching intensities nearly identical to those in laboratory water, indicating its practicality for environmental monitoring. Leveraging the aggregation-induced emission (AIE) property of TC, a complementary turn-on detection method was established. As shown in Fig. 5f, the fluorescence emission at about 525 nm increases progressively with the addition of increasing concentrations of TC (0.4 mL) in Ca2+-His-LDH (1 mg mL−1, 1.2 mL), with a corresponding LOD of 710 nM (Fig. S39b). This detection method is also applicable to four other Ca2+-AA-LDH samples. As the TC concentration increases from 50 to 100 mg L−1, a significant rise in fluorescence intensity can be observed (Fig. 5g). The general capability of representative Ca2+-His-LDH sample for identifying various OPs was further demonstrated. The addition of different OPs (TC, MG, DOX, CR, MO) induces distinct spectral changes: quenching of bands at 345 − 358 nm and 432 − 445 nm, and an enhancement of the band around 507 − 523 nm due to AIE (Fig. 5h). These unique, analyte-dependent fluorescence signatures make these aerogels promising materials for the rapid preliminary screening and identification of pollutants in environmental water monitoring.

Potential applications of Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels

Beyond the removal and detection of OPs, the aerogels developed in this study exhibit significant application potential in two key areas: (1) Antibacterial performance: The Ca2+-AA-LDH-TC aerogels exhibit potent antibacterial activity, reducing Staphylococcus aureus growth by > 99% (Fig. S40). Particularly, the recyclability of the aerogel mitigates environmental pollution caused by the direct use of TC powder for bacterial inhibition, thereby reducing adverse ecological impacts. (2) Thermal insulation performance: The Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels, characterized by low density, high porosity, excellent mechanical strength, and flame-retardant properties, demonstrate significant potential for thermal management applications. As shown in Fig. S41, a 2-cm-high Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel is placed on a constant-temperature heating platform. After 20 min of heating at 150 °C and 200 °C, the upper surface temperatures are only 29.22 °C and 42.61 °C, respectively. The upper surface temperatures stabilize within 3 min, as indicated by the time-dependent temperature curves. This performance, coupled with their cryogenic mechanical robustness, suggests potential applicability in insulating pipelines such as those for liquid nitrogen or liquefied natural gas.

Discussion

In summary, a series of interlocked Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels inspired by the mortise-and-tenon structure was developed. The resulting aerogels exhibit high specific surface area, excellent hydrophilicity, and mechanical strength and toughness. And they function as efficient adsorbents, catalysts, and fluorescence probes for TC, DOX, MO, CR, and MG. Taking TC as a representative pollutant, we conducted a detailed study on the mechanisms of its adsorption, photodegradation, and fluorescence detection by Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel. Specifically, adsorption is driven by surface coordination, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions. Photodegradation is initiated by the generation of reactive species (e−, h+, ·OH, and ·O2−) upon light exposure. TC-induced fluorescence quenching in Ca2+-His-LDH is facilitated through IFE and PET. Fluorescence enhancement of TC induced by Ca2+-His-LDH is attributed to AIE. In addition, the aerogels maintain stable performance under both cryogenic and high-temperature conditions. Importantly, our aerogels surpass most other water remediation materials in key areas such as wide applicability, simplicity of preparation, lack of secondary toxicity, recyclability, environmental friendliness, and low cost. This work not only advances the development of intercalated LDH aerogels but also offers a promising solution for practical and sustainable water treatment technologies.

Methods

Materials

Mg/Al LDH (ALCAMIZER 1-C) was obtained from Kisuma Chemicals (Table S12). Alanine, glycine, threonine, histidine, sarcosine, L-aspartate, L-methionine, L-tyrosine, L-lysine, L-arginine, L-cysteine, L-phenylalanine, CaCl2, NaOH, EDTA-2Na·2H2O, MeOH, TC, doxycycline hydrochloride, methyl orange, Congo red, and malachite green were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. HCl was supplied by Chengdu Chron Chemical Co., Ltd. Ethanol was purchased from Tianjin Zhiyuan Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Benzoquinone was purchased from Energy Chemical Co., Ltd.

Synthesis

Synthesis of LDO

Initially, the original platelet Mg/Al LDH was sintered in a tube furnace at 450 °C for 10 h. After cooling, the obtained Mg/Al LDO powder was removed and transferred to a drying oven to prevent rehydration.

Preparation of AA-LDH hydrogels

Each amino acid (alanine, glycine, threonine, histidine, or sarcosine) was dissolved in laboratory-purified water at a concentration of 40 mg mL−1. LDO powder was then added at the same concentration (40 mg mL−1) to prepare the AA-LDH suspension. For example, to prepare a 100 mL system, 4 g of LDO and 4 g of amino acid were used. The total volume could be scaled up or down proportionally according to experimental needs. The resulting suspension was stirred continuously at 500 rpm in an 80 °C water bath for 2 h to achieve a uniform dispersion. The dispersion was then transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 120 °C for 10 h to promote the formation of AA-LDH hydrogels.

Preparation of Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels

The prepared hydrogel was poured into molds and frozen at −20 °C to shape it. The frozen gel blocks were removed from the molds and immediately immersed in a 0.05 M CaCl2 solution (1:10 volume ratio) for ionic crosslinking at room temperature for 48 h. The CaCl2 solution was replaced every 12 h to ensure sufficient crosslinking. During this process, the hydrogel thawed naturally while Ca2+ crosslinking occurred. Afterward, the samples were solvent-exchanged with absolute ethanol three times (1:10 volume ratio), with each exchange lasting 12 h. Finally, the solvent-exchanged gels were transferred to a supercritical drying system. Liquid CO2 was used as the exchange medium, and the samples were treated under supercritical conditions at 40 °C and 9.5 MPa for 1 h. The pressure was then slowly released to atmospheric pressure at a rate of approximately 0.1 MPa min−1. After cooling, porous Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels were obtained. For comparison, the solvent-exchanged gels were freeze-dried to obtain Ca2+-His-LDHFd aerogel.

Characterization

The microstructures of Ca2+-AA-LDH were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL/JSM-7610FPlus, Japan) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Titan G260-300, USA). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed using an X’Pert PRO diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å, 40 kV × 40 mA). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded using an SP-2 spectrometer (PerkinElmer, USA) to detect the chemical structure. The specific surface area and pore-size distribution were determined by nitrogen adsorption-desorption measurements using a Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analyzer (BSD-PS) and the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method. The porosity was determined by an Automatic True Density Analyzer (TD1-1539). The compression and three-point bending tests were performed by a universal testing machine (TSE503A). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on a simultaneous thermal analyzer (SDT650) under an air atmosphere with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. UV-vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-2450) and Liquid Chromatograph Mass Spectrometer (LC-MS, LC-30AD) were used to determine the concentration of TC solution. The concentration of leached metal ions was measured via an Inductively Coupled Plasma-Atomic Emission Spectrometer (PerkinElmer Avio500 ICP-AES). The total organic carbon (TOC) value was obtained through the TOC-V CPH instrument. The water contact angle (WCA) was measured using a contact angle goniometer (JC2000D1, China). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer with Al Kα radiation. Free radicals (·OH and ·O2−) were determined by a Bruker A300 electron paramagnetic resonance spectrometer. Ultraviolet-Visible diffuse reflectance spectra (UV-vis DRS) were recorded on a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-3600i Plus) with BaSO4 as a reflectance standard. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were obtained using a Fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-4600). Fluorescence lifetime was measured using an Edinburgh FLS1000 spectrometer with a 375 nm excited laser. The forced combustion test was performed in an oxygen-consumption cone calorimeter (FTT0007). The limiting oxygen index (LOI) was measured using an oxygen index meter (FTT0077).

BET specific surface area test

To preserve the original porous structure of the material and ensure accurate test results, the aerogel was cut into small pieces with dimensions of approximately 2 × 2 × 2 mm. The samples were then degassed under vacuum at 60 °C for 3 h. These mild degassing conditions were chosen to effectively remove physically adsorbed moisture and impurities from the sample surface, while avoiding damage to its delicate nanoporous structure caused by excessive temperature. The pretreated sample tubes were subsequently transferred to the analysis station, and nitrogen adsorption-desorption measurements were carried out at an adsorption temperature of 77.3 K.

Adsorption-photodegradation experiments

Adsorption-photodegradation experiments were carried out at room temperature (25 °C). Unless otherwise specified, TC was dissolved in laboratory-purified water (pH = 7). Aerogel samples with dimensions of approximately 2 × 2 × 2 mm were added at the specified amount and allowed to undergo dark adsorption for 60 min under continuous shaking at 80 rpm to reach adsorption equilibrium, followed by photocatalytic treatment under irradiation from a 200 W xenon lamp in the same reactor. During the reaction, 0.5 mL of the solution was sampled at regular time intervals. Each sample was immediately filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. The concentration of TC in the filtrate was determined by UV-vis spectrophotometry and further confirmed using LC-MS. In the test, three repeated measurements were taken using different samples.

Adsorption-photodegradation capacity of five Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogels

A TC solution with an initial concentration of C0 = 50 mg L−1 was prepared. Aerogels were added at a mass-to-volume ratio of m/V = 2 mg mL−1 and pre-adsorbed in darkness for 60 min under shaking at 80 rpm to reach the adsorption equilibrium. Photocatalytic experiments were then conducted under irradiation using a 200 W xenon lamp in the same reactor.

Effect of initial concentration of TC

TC was dissolved in laboratory-purified water to prepare solutions with various initial concentrations (C0 = 0.5, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, and 400 mg L−1). The representative Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel was employed to evaluate TC removal efficiency (m/V = 2 mg mL−1).

Effect of initial pH

TC was dissolved directly in laboratory-purified water with different preset pH values (pH = 4 − 12) to prepare solutions with an initial concentration of 50 mg L−1. The removal performance was then evaluated using Ca2+-His-LDH aerogels at m/V = 2 mg mL−1.

Performance in real water samples

Three real water samples—tap water, Swan Lake water (Central South University), and Xiangjiang River water (Yuelu District, Changsha, China)—were filtered to remove suspended solids and used to prepare TC solutions (C0 = 50 mg L−1). The adsorption-photodegradation of TC was studied using Ca2+-His-LDH aerogels at m/V = 2 mg mL−1. Water quality information is summarized in Table S4.

Removal of other organic pollutants (OPs)

Additional organic pollutants, including DOX, MO, CR, and MG, were dissolved in laboratory-purified water to obtain solutions with C0 = 50 mg L−1. Their removal was investigated using Ca2+-His-LDH aerogels at m/V = 2 mg mL−1 under ambient conditions. Furthermore, the influence of heat flow on the removal efficiency was also examined by employing Ca2+-His-LDH aerogels subjected to thermal treatment.

Cyclic stability test

To investigate the cyclic stability, a flow-through reactor was packed with Ca2+-His-LDH aerogels. TC was dissolved in laboratory-purified water to prepare a solution with C0 = 50 mg L−1, and aerogels were added to the solution at a mass-to-volume ratio of 5 mg per mL of solution. The system was first operated under completely dark conditions until adsorption equilibrium was reached. After that, a 200 W xenon lamp was switched on to start the photocatalytic degradation process for 70 min. For regeneration, the aerogel-based device was directly applied to the next cycle after ethanol washing and vacuum drying. The cycle was repeated four more times, for a total of five cycles. The removal efficiency was monitored to evaluate the cyclic stability of the aerogel.

Leaching experiment

Aerogel samples (200 mg) were immersed in 100 mL of aqueous solutions with pH values ranging from 1 to 7 (m/V = 2 mg mL−1). The suspensions were shaken at 80 rpm in a thermostatic shaking incubator at 25 °C for 1 h, followed by an additional 1 h of treatment under irradiation with a 200 W xenon lamp. After treatment, the suspension was immediately filtered to separate the solid and liquid phases. The concentrations of leached metal ions in the filtrates were quantified by ICP-AES.

Zeta potential and particle size test

The aerogel was ground into a fine powder and subsequently dispersed in laboratory-purified water with pH values ranging from 4 to 12 to prepare suspensions at a concentration of 1 mg mL−1. The suspensions were subjected to ultrasonication using a cell disruptor at a power of 600 W for 1 h to obtain a homogeneous and stable dispersion. The particle size distribution was measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano instrument equipped with a DTS0012 sample cell. The zeta potential was determined using the same instrument fitted with a DTS1070 sample cell.

Adsorption mechanism study

TC was dissolved in laboratory-purified water (pH = 7) to prepare a 50 mg L−1 solution. Subsequently, Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel was added to the solution at a mass-to-volume ratio of 2 mg mL−1. After TC adsorption, a TC-loaded composite aerogel (Ca2+-His-LDH-TC) was obtained. The obtained material was ground into powder and characterized by XRD, XPS, and FTIR to investigate the adsorption mechanism.

Free radical scavenging experiment

100 mg of Ca2+-His-LDH aerogel was added to 50 mL TC solutions (C0 = 50 mg L−1) and stirred in the dark until adsorption equilibrium was reached. Subsequently, to evaluate the respective contributions of different reactive species, specific scavengers were added: 1 mM MeOH to quench ·OH radicals, 1 mM BQ to quench ·O2− radicals, and 1 mM EDTA-2Na to quench photogenerated h+. The obtained suspension was irradiated under a 200 W xenon lamp to evaluate the photocatalytic activity.

TOC and LC-MS analyses

TC solutions with an initial concentration of C0 = 50 mg L−1 were prepared, and Ca2+-His-LDH aerogels were added at a mass-to-volume ratio of m/V = 2 mg mL−1. The system was subjected to both dark adsorption and photocatalytic degradation, each lasting 60 min. To evaluate the mineralization efficiency, solutions collected at specific times were analyzed using a TOC analyzer. To elucidate the degradation pathways of TC in the Ca2+-His-LDH system, samples collected at the same time points were further analyzed by LC-MS. The obtained m/z data of intermediate products were used to propose possible degradation pathways.

Fluorescence detection

The Ca2+-AA-LDH aerogel powder was dispersed in ethanol using a cell disruptor (600 W, 1 h) to obtain a suspension with a concentration of 1 mg mL−1. The average particle size of the dispersed particles was 880.9 ± 46 nm. The optimal effective excitation was determined by changing the excitation wavelength. For exploring the time-dependent fluorescence intensity changes at ~380 nm, 1 mL Ca2+-His-LDH suspension was mixed with 20 μL of TC solutions at different concentrations. The changes in fluorescence intensity were recorded (excitation wavelength set at 310 nm). To evaluate the practical application potential of the Ca2+-His-LDH probe, TC solutions at 50 and 100 mg L−1 were prepared using laboratory-purified water, tap water, Xiangjiang River water, and Swan Lake water, and the fluorescence intensity changes were recorded after adding them to Ca2+-His-LDH dispersion under the above experimental conditions. For exploring the fluorescence behavior at ~525 nm, 1.2 mL Ca2+-AA-LDH suspension was mixed with 0.4 mL TC solutions of various concentrations, and the changes in fluorescence intensity were recorded (excitation wavelength set at 390 nm). Additionally, to determine whether Ca2+-AA-LDH can recognize and detect OPs, we prepared 50 and 100 mg L−1 TC, MG, DOX, CR, and MO aqueous solutions, and recorded the changes in fluorescence properties after adding them to the Ca2+-His-LDH suspension under an excitation wavelength of 300 nm and a PMT voltage of 600 V.

Antibacterial activity

Staphylococcus aureus was selected as the experimental strain and cultured by shaking at 37 °C for 12 h. Aerogel samples (0.3 × 0.3 × 0.3 cm) were immersed in a 1 mL 1×106 CFU mL−1 bacterial solution and then incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. The bacterial suspension was subsequently collected and diluted to 1×103 CFU mL−1. Following this, 15 mL agar at about 45 °C was added to the culture dishes. After solidification, 100 μL of bacterial suspension was added. The antibacterial activity of the composite aerogels was evaluated by agar plate counting. Each sample was tested in triplicate, with the bacterial solution without the aerogel sample serving as the control group.

Dynamic adsorption test

To investigate the dynamic adsorption performance of the aerogel toward TC, fixed-bed column experiments were conducted. Ca2+-His-LDH aerogels were packed into the column (internal diameter1 cm) to obtain bed heights (h) of 5 cm and 10 cm. The void spaces between aerogel particles were filled with quartz sand to ensure uniform fluid distribution. Before operation, the bed was rinsed with laboratory-purified water to ensure a closely packed arrangement of particles with no voids, channels, or cracks. Subsequently, the TC aqueous solution (50 mg L−1, pH = 7) was introduced into the column, and the effluent flow rate was carefully controlled. Effluent samples were collected from the bottom of the column at fixed time intervals, and the TC concentration was measured until the effluent TC concentration approached the influent TC concentration. Breakthrough curves under different operating conditions were then obtained.

DFT calculations

To get insight into the electronic properties of Ca2+ crosslinking process, all calculations are performed with the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) based on the DFT55,56. The exchange-correlation functional is adopted the Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE)57. First, the fully relaxed structure optimization was employed conjugate gradient. The plane-wave cutoff was carried out with a 450 eV. The self-consistent total-energy difference and the convergence criterion for forces on atoms were set to 10−4 eV and 0.01 eV/Å, respectively. A vacuum spacing of ∼15 Å in the vertical direction of the plane was used to avoid the interaction between the periodic images of the sheets. The calculations were carried out using 2 × 5 monolayer supercells of LDH, His-LDH, Ca2+-LDH, Ca2+-His-LDH, and Ca2+-His-LDH-TC (Fig. 2f, g and Fig. 4g). The reciprocal space was sampled by a-centered k mesh of 10 × 4 × 1 in the first Brillouin zone. Based on the static states mentioned above, we calculated the corresponding adsorption energy, charge density difference, Bader charge, density of states, and molecular orbital.
