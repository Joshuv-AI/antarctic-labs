WHAT IS FACIAL PAIN?

Facial pain is defined as pain localised to the region ‘bounded by the eyes and lower mandible, including the oral cavity’.[1] Facial pain is a broad entity seen in myriad conditions, spanning multiple disciplines. The head and neck region is a complex combination of anatomical structures, any of which can result in facial pain. Facial pain can arise from musculoskeletal or soft tissue conditions, neuropathic disorders, vascular disorders, dental issues, and even psychological factors [Box 1].[1234]

Opening Vignette

A 49-year-old man presented with sudden onset of right-sided facial pain localised along the mandible. He described the pain as excruciating and sharp, occurring in intermittent waves that lasted seconds at a time. Mastication and jaw movement from talking triggered the pain. He was asymptomatic in-between episodes of pain, and there were no precipitating traumas or other associated symptoms or neurological deficits. Physical examination revealed intact cranial nerves II–XII, with no facial palsy or facial sensory deficits.

HOW IS THIS RELEVANT TO MY PRACTICE?

Facial pain is a presenting complaint commonly seen in primary care, with prevalence increasing with age. Facial pain can also be distressing, reducing the quality of life. Forssell et al.[5] report a significantly (P < 0.001) lower quality of life in patients with ‘chronic orofacial pain’ as compared to the general population. The key to relieving a patient’s facial pain is determining the right aetiology and treating it.

WHAT CAN I DO IN MY PRACTICE?

Given the many potential causes of facial pain, some of which may be obscure, it may only be possible to narrow the diagnosis to an organ system rather than a specific aetiology. During a short general practitioner consultation, the focus should be on ruling out red flags, identifying aetiologies for which treatment can be initiated in a primary care setting versus those that require a specialist referral, and screening for psychiatric comorbidities such as mood disorders.

The main red flags include giant cell arteritis (GCA), acute angle-closure glaucoma (AACG) and malignancy.[134] Giant cell arteritis should be considered, especially in individuals older than 50 years, as it can cause blindness if not treated promptly. Acute angle-closure glaucoma presents with ipsilateral facial pain, visual deficits, and nausea or vomiting. Malignancy—which can be either primary or secondary metastasis—can present as progressive neuropathic pain.[3]

The aetiology of facial pain can be identified by the characteristic signs and symptoms of each system. Figure 1 summarises the wider differentials of facial pain. Key questions that aid in arriving at a diagnosis include features suggesting a dental or ophthalmological pathology, chronic (more than 3 months)[6] or persistent pain requiring further evaluation, episodic nature of pain and the presence of a neurological deficit.

Giant cell arteritis can present with jaw claudication and nonspecific systemic symptoms such as myalgia, fatigue, malaise, fevers and weight loss. Inspection and palpation of the temporal arteries should be carried out. Tenderness, reduced pulsation, redness and swelling of the temporal arteries are worrisome for GCA.[13] If GCA is suspected, high-dose steroids can be commenced, and an urgent referral to Rheumatology should be made.

Neuralgias can present with sudden, sharp or burning pain triggered or exacerbated by touch, swallowing, chewing or opening of the mouth.[13] Initial analgesia can be initiated for neuralgias in a primary care setting, with a referral to Neurology for further evaluation and treatment.

Primary headaches, such as tension headaches, may present with tightness around the forehead and bitemporal regions. Migraines are associated with prodromes and auras preceding headaches. Cluster headaches usually present with unilateral facial pain associated with rhinorrhoea and/or conjunctival injection. Cervicogenic headaches can present with unilateral headache or facial pain with a posterior origin, radiating to orofacial regions and exacerbated or reproducible by specific neck movements.[1] A referral to Neurology can be made if these headaches persist despite lifestyle changes, analgesia and initiation of prophylactics, or if red flag headache symptoms are present. First-line analgesics for migraines may comprise paracetamol and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, while second-line treatment consists of triptans (e.g. sumatriptan). Prophylactics include amitriptyline, propranolol and topiramate.[7]

Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorders can present with jaw pain that worsens with mastication and clicking of the jaw. Limited range of motion, deviation of the mandible on jaw opening, tenderness at TMJ and abnormal clicking sounds on TMJ movement are also signs of a TMJ disorder pathology.[13] Conservative measures [Table 1] and analgesia for TMJ disorders can be initiated while awaiting referral to Oral–Maxillofacial Surgery for further management.

Vascular or neurological causes, such as carotid or vertebral artery dissection, can present with sudden unilateral pain associated with ipsilateral miosis and ptosis.[1] A same-day urgent referral should be made to the emergency department.

Sinusitis may present with recent upper respiratory tract infection, coryzal symptoms and tenderness around the sinuses. Salivary gland disorders may present with pain before or during a meal, and should be referred to an Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT) specialist for consideration of surgical management.[13] Vesicles in the ear canal are suggestive of Ramsay Hunt syndrome, for which initiation of high-dose steroids (prednisolone at 1 mg/kg for 5 days) and antivirals (valacyclovir 1 g three times a day for 7–10 days) is recommended.[11]

Dental causes can present with pain originating from dental structures and surrounding tissues, possibly worsened by pressure on the affected tooth. Examination of the oral cavity for dental caries, cracked teeth, pulpitis, gingivitis and suspicious oral lesions should be carried out.[13] A dental or Oral–Maxillofacial Surgery referral can be considered.

Ophthalmologic causes, such as ocular ischaemic syndrome and optic neuritis, can present with periorbital pain associated with visual loss or exacerbated by eye movements. Physical examination should evaluate for loss of visual acuity and/or colour vision, conjunctival injection and photophobia.[1] If the abovementioned conditions are suspected, an urgent same-day referral should be made to the emergency department for an ophthalmological review. If AACG is suspected, an initial treatment should be administered to lower intra-ocular pressure while arranging for an urgent ophthalmological review.[4]

Deliberate effort should be made to perform a cranial nerve/cerebellar/upper and lower limb neurological examination, to assess for sensory/motor/cerebellar deficits. Neurological deficits are suggestive of more sinister causes, such as secondary trigeminal neuralgia (i.e. secondary to multiple sclerosis), cerebellopontine angle lesion, vascular (i.e. brainstem haemorrhage or ischaemia), inflammatory (connective tissue disease) or carotid/vertebral artery dissection.[13] An urgent referral should be made to the relevant speciality/emergency department after localisation of the lesion (e.g. Neurosurgery, ENT). Psychological association of facial pain

Facial pain can be closely associated with a patient’s psychological state. The symptom of facial pain may be a somatisation of an underlying mood disorder. Existing mood disorders, such as depression or anxiety, can also increase the severity and risk of chronicity.[12]

Chronic facial pain may have a psychological impact, with psychiatric comorbidity reported in up to 20%–40% of patients with a primary pain condition.[13] It is also possible that symptoms do not fit into any diagnostic criteria, leading to a diagnosis of persistent idiopathic facial pain (also known as atypical facial pain). This should be a diagnosis of exclusion. The cornerstone of treatment for such patients includes the use of anti-depressants (e.g. tricyclic anti-depressants)[3] and cognitive behaviour therapy, which have been shown to be effective.[3]

Patients with chronic facial pain should be screened for comorbid mood disorders such as major depression or anxiety using validated tools such as the Beck Depression Inventory and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale.[3] Patients screened positive should be formally evaluated and escalated to appropriate community or specialist resources.

Treatment

Given the chronicity and psychological associations of facial pain, it is essential to manage the patient holistically. Patients should be educated on their condition and encouraged to participate actively in treatment and establish long-term strategies. This is especially relevant in the primary care setting, where focus is on the integrated and comprehensive care of patients and their comorbidities. The aim of treatment is to identify the aetiology and refer to a specialist if required, manage facial pain through appropriate analgesic measures, and screen for and treat comorbid mood disorders.

Key concern in carbamazepine

Testing for the HLA-B15:02 allele is recommended before initiating carbamazepine therapy in genetically at-risk populations, particularly individuals of East or South Asian descent.[14] The presence of this allele is strongly associated with a significantly increased risk of severe cutaneous adverse reactions, including Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN). In individuals who test positive for the HLA-B15:02 allele, carbamazepine should be avoided.

In Singapore, the Health Sciences Authority (HSA) mandates that all new patients of Asian ancestry undergo HLA-B15:02 genotyping before initiating carbamazepine therapy.[15] This recommendation stems from local pharmacogenomic data showing its high prevalence in Singapore’s multi-ethnic population, particularly among individuals of Chinese, Malay and Indian descent. The HSA also advises that patients who have no adverse reactions after taking carbamazepine for more than 3 months are unlikely to be at risk for SJS or TEN and may not require testing.[15]

Closing Vignette

The patient was referred to a neurologist for suspicion of trigeminal neuralgia. Trigeminal neuralgia was confirmed with magnetic resonance imaging of the brain, which demonstrated neurovascular conflict between the vascular loop of the right superior cerebellar artery and the right trigeminal nerve. He was started on oral analgesics such as gabapentin for pain control, but his condition eventually became refractory to oral analgesia. He was then referred to a neurosurgeon and subsequently underwent microvascular decompression surgery. Postoperatively, the patient is symptom-free.
