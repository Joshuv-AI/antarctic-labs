Getting on the Same Page: Why Australia Needs a National Maternity Early Warning System (MEWS) Chart
Authors: Briony A. Cutts, Lucy Bowyer, Nisha Khot, Sandra Lowe, Stefan C. Kane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObservation charts are used to record and prompt responses for acutely deteriorating patients. Compared to non‐pregnant observation charts, maternity charts reflect physiological changes of pregnancy and the immediate postpartum period. Maternity observation charts differ across Australian states and territories, resulting in a lack of comparable data, standardised care and optimisation of streamlining education and training. To improve maternity care across the country, we propose that the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care, in the upcoming third edition of the National Safety and Quality Health Service Standards, review Standard 8 (recognising and responding to the acutely deteriorating patients) to generate a national Maternity Early Warning System (MEWS) Chart.

Introduction

Across Australia, hospitals are accredited according to their compliance with the eight National Safety and Quality Health Service Standards to ‘protect the public from harm and to improve the quality of health service provision’ [1]. Standard 8 refers to recognising and responding to the acutely deteriorating patient [2]. In most hospitals, deterioration and escalation of care occurs in response to recordings of biophysical parameters on observation charts, including blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature, oxygen saturation levels and mental alertness. Holistic interpretation of these parameters enables detection of the development of serious illness and allows timely intervention to prevent serious harm (usually triggered by a medical emergency team [MET] code) for urgent patient review [3]. In hospitals with maternity services, observation charts reflect the physiological changes of pregnancy and the immediate postpartum period and use adjusted parameters compared with charts used for the non‐pregnant adult population. However, between states and territories, maternity observation charts differ across multiple parameters, making simple recommendations for patient care difficult. This became apparent to our author group when writing a recent position statement on the maternity management of sepsis for the Society of Obstetric Medicine of Australia and New Zealand (SOMANZ) [4].

The rate of maternal deaths from sepsis has remained stagnant across Australia and New Zealand for more than 20 years [5]. Identifying pregnant and postpartum women with potential sepsis remains paramount [6]. The thresholds for abnormal observations that would trigger assessment and recognition of sepsis in a maternity patient, such as an elevated heart rate or respiratory rate, differ across states and territories. There is no clinical basis for this variation. States and territories have been responsible for generating their own maternity charts and so a national collaborative process has not occurred, with multiple chart variations existing as a result. We highlight below the potential dangers of this and the advantages of having a single national Maternity Early Warning System (MEWS) chart, as well as how development of such a chart could occur. In the process of writing this article, we submitted a request asking the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Healthcare to consider reviewing Standard 8 (recognising and responding to acute deterioration) specifically to develop a national MEWS chart.

Differences in Maternity Observation Charts Exist

Vital signs are a critical part of clinical guidelines as they promote the recognition of common pathologies that result in significant maternal and neonatal morbidity, such as maternal sepsis and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. The degree of abnormality between vital signs to trigger a clinical review and escalation to a MET response for abnormal vital signs varies significantly between states and territories. A maternal body temperature classed as fever requiring medical intervention is 38.5°C for New South Wales (NSW) and 38.0°C for South Australia [7, 8]. Similarly, differences exist between charts for clinical response to blood pressure. NSW defines hypertension at a systolic pressure of ≥ 140 mmHg and hypertension triggering a medical emergency response at a systolic pressure of ≥ 160 mmHg. For South Australia, the thresholds are defined as ≥ 160 and ≥ 190 mmHg. SOMANZ published the SOMANZ Hypertension in Pregnancy Guideline 2023 (endorsed by the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC)) that defines blood pressure thresholds to diagnose hypertension and severe hypertension [9]. To roll out national guidance and ensure safe and effective practice, standardised, evidence‐based definitions of routine clinical observations are required.

We cannot benchmark data between health services when such variation exists. Improving outcomes is much more difficult if we are not using the same baseline data from the outset. Having a uniform set of data will allow for the meaningful audit of outcomes between jurisdictions, which will drive improvement in maternity care across Australia. Precedent exists for collecting standardised national data for other aspects of maternity care such as birth and neonatal outcomes [5]. These data have been crucial for improving quality and safety of maternity care.

Why a National Maternity Early Warning System Chart Is Needed

Adoption then of a national standard (similar to measuring other maternal and neonatal outcomes) for maternal observations is likely to be equally valuable. In a systematic review of early warning systems in obstetrics, Umar and colleagues [10] found that the early warning systems had high median sensitivity and specificity for predicting maternal morbidity or intensive care unit admission. A very high accuracy in predicting death in the critically ill maternity population was also noted. Among the studies included, six were derived from high‐income countries [10]. Having a national standardised MEWS means that a maternity patient will have appropriate escalation of care that will be independent of which health service they attend. This will assist in consistency of service delivery within and across health services that care for maternity patients.

If health professionals work across multiple states and territories, which can be common at border towns, having different threshold parameters can lead to confusion and result in inconsistent care. It also means that training needs to be altered across the different states and territories. Optimal and consistent performance by all health care providers comes from familiarity and implementation of clinical processes and protocols. Having a single MEWS means that educating students and new staff is efficient and not duplicated. The quality of care delivered will be more consistent due to the uniformity of the system used. This has been the case in New Zealand where, following the adoption of a national MEWS chart, auditing has found better recognition of the deteriorating maternal patient, simplified education, enhanced confidence in the system with resultant consistency of care [11]. Similarly, in the USA, Sims and colleagues found a statistically significant improvement in the timely treatment of severe hypertension with the use of a standardised MEWS for hypertensive emergencies in their maternity centre [12]. Other high‐income countries that use a national MEWS include Norway, the Netherlands and Ireland. The Canadian‐adapted Modified Early Obstetric Warning System (Can‐MEOWS), which is being recommended to be rolled out Canada‐wide, was found to have a high sensitivity for identifying severe maternal morbidity in obstetric patients [13].

Having previously used multiple different maternity observation charts in different regions, the United Kingdom has designed a systematic and expert‐informed national MEWS, based on prospectively collected data, to determine the level of escalation and monitoring required, as well as using it as a prompt for early multidisciplinary collaboration. This was rolled out in the United Kingdom in March 2026 [14].

Implementation and Monitoring Outcomes

If local stakeholders (such as the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists [RANZCOG] and Australian College of Midwives) agree that the applicability of a MEWS chart is high, Australia could extrapolate from the same data generated from the UK study to design our own Australian MEWS chart. Early warning thresholds are included in the current, colour‐coded patient observation charts, which are a core ingrained component of patient care. Compliance in measuring regular observations is high and these measurement systems are in place: maximising their utility will require unifying parameters and practice across the country.

A national approach using a platform for accreditation standards such as the National Safety and Quality Health Service would enable streamlined rolling out of changes to maternity health care services across Australia. However, national change can also occur when speciality colleges and societies (such as the RANZCOG, Australian College of Midwives and SOMANZ) publish clinical guidelines endorsed by the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) that recommend a national MEWS. To enhance stakeholder engagement and sustainability of a MEWS protocol, we recommend using a human‐centred design approach such as that undertaken by McIntyre and colleagues [15]. Determining the efficacy of a national MEWS can be done both at an individual health service level as well as at a state‐ and nation‐wide level. Auditing maternal and neonatal outcomes, such as admission to intensive care for sepsis or maternal management of hypertension before and after implementation, will help measure success. Nationally, reviewing data to determine if an improvement in maternal mortality occurs for preventable pathology will be the best measure of success.

Conclusion

The lack of a national standard maternity observation chart makes it more challenging to deliver the best outcomes to mothers and their babies. Having a national MEWS will ensure a uniform clinical approach, give staff high confidence in the frameworks within which they work and provide the opportunity to audit and develop national standards that improve maternity care and reduce national maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality.
