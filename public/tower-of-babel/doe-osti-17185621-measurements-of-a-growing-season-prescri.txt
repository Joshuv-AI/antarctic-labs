 Executive Summary
The United States Forest Service at the Savannah River Site (SRS) routinely burns vegetation to reduce the risk of
wildfires and to manage timber compartment health and habitats at SRS. This activity provides a unique opportunity to
study the meteorological parameters surrounding a controlled burn. This report details meteorological measurements
conducted around a prescribed burn at SRS during the growing season on June 4, 2026. The SRS area possesses geologic
features known as Carolina bays which are shallow, natural surface depressions. The burn activity described in this study
took place near Mona Bay, one of the Carolina bays located at SRS. Prescribed burns in this area help manage the forest
to preserve habitats for species native to SRS. Measurements were made from three portable meteorological stations
and drone flights. Measurements included wind speed, wind direction, temperature, relative humidity, pressure, and
precipitation. These measurements taken during the growing season can be compared to those taken around a
prescribed burn on February 24, 2026 during the dormant season (SRNL-STI-2026-00516). This data will provide
important input and validation information for the QUIC-Fire model and other fire spread modeling efforts.

1.0 Introduction
The Savannah River Site (SRS) contains several shallow geologic depressions known as Carolina bays.
Mona Bay, the area of focus for this study, is an important habitat to various species onsite including the
gopher turtle and gopher frog, both of which are ecologically significant and conservation priorities.
Performing prescribed burns around the bay helps prevent forest overgrowth. Due to the plant life
cycle, plants recover more slowly from a growing season burn, which helps meet longer term
management objectives and reduces competition [1]. Other methods such as using heavy machinery to
cut down and haul trees compacts the soil around the bay which can disturb the soil structure, reduce
water storage capability, and reduce available oxygen [2]. Specifically for the gopher turtle and gopher
frog, these activities would compact the soil preventing them from burrowing into the ground to create
housing. Additionally, prescribed burns are necessary across the site to manage timber and prevent
wildfires. The United States Forest Service Savannah River office (USFS-SR) at SRS routinely burns
vegetation to reduce fuel loading; reduce litter layer for wildlife, and remove undergrowth to allow
sunlight penetration for growth [3].
The Atmospheric Technologies Group (ATG) at the Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL)
supplements fixed meteorological towers with three portable meteorological stations which measure
wind speed, wind direction, temperature, relative humidity (RH), pressure, and precipitation.
Additionally, SRNL has an Unmanned Aircraft System (UAS) Program with measurement capabilities for
describing atmospheric responses to prescribed burns. Data collected around the prescribed burns at
SRS is representative of wildfires and can be used to validate wildfire spread models. Previous
measurements were performed for a dormant season burn [4]. The prescribed burn during the growing
season provides varied conditions and will be used for determining model consistency.
In this study, measurements from portable meteorological stations and drone flights were used to
characterize the atmospheric conditions around a growing season prescribed burn. Portable
meteorological stations were placed around outside of the burn area, including upwind and down wind,
to measure wind speed, wind direction, temperature, and RH. Additionally, UAS flights above the burn
area provide elevated measurements of temperature, RH, and pressure. These measurements will be
used for model validation and as inputs to the QUIC-Fire model. The QUIC-Fire model simulates the
effects of fuel structure on local winds and fire behavior [5]. This model improves fire-atmospheric
feedback to aid in comparing, evaluating, and designing burn plans and understanding complex ignition
patterns.

2.0 Prescribed Burn and Field Measurements
The USFS-SR plans, performs, and manages prescribed burns at SRS to reduce the risk of wildfires and
manage the forestland. In collaboration with USFS-SR, a prescribed burn was conducted on June 4, 2026
during the growing season to facilitate comparison with an earlier prescribed burn conducted in the
dormant season [4]. The prescribed burn operations began at 10:16 Eastern Daylight Time (EDT) and
continued through 14:24 EDT. The burn covered approximately 205 acres surrounding Mona Bay (burn
area in red outline) on the eastern edge of the SRS (Figure 2-1). Figure 2-1 also shows the locations for
the three meteorological stations and the drone takeoff/landing pad (Drone Control). Figure 2-2 shows
vegetation burning along the fire line near downwind meteorological station MET2. Since the burn is
designed to remove surface fuel while leaving the general structure of the forest intact it is considered a

2.1 Portable Meteorological Stations
Three portable meteorological stations were placed around a burn in the growing season. One station
was placed upwind (based on the above canopy wind direction) of the prescribed burn (MET3), another
downwind (MET2) and a third away from the burn to the west (MET1) (Figure 2-1 and Table 2-1). The
station locations were selected to characterize the meteorological parameters surrounding the
prescribed burn, especially the up- and down-wind station. Each meteorological station consisted of a 3
m tall tripod outfitted with a Campbell Scientific METSENS-600 instrument. This instrument measures
temperature, RH, wind speed, wind direction, pressure, and precipitation. The instrument provides
measurements which are stored at 5-minute intervals while the sensor itself measures more frequently
than 5-minutes (standard deviations are calculated). Measurements are collected using a Campbell
Scientific Datalogger (model CR-1000). Figure 2-3 shows the portable meteorological station MET3
located upwind from the burn.

2.2 Drone Flights
An iMet-XQ2 sensor was mounted to a Freefly Alta-X drone to measure temperature, RH, and pressure
at a 1 Hz frequency (Figure 2-4). Additionally, the Alta-X records data from onboard peripheral and
sensors including Global Positioning System (GPS) location, acceleration, motor speeds, and wind
estimations. Wind data is estimated using drone controller correction (i.e., how much the drone is
correcting its path to stay at the desired location) and approximate reference area of the drone’s side
profile. While this data has not been validated for accuracy and is not included in this report, it is
available. A future project plans to validate the Alta-X wind data.
Before and after the prescribed burn at the “Drone Control” location vertical flight profiles (soundings)
were completed as a baseline. The vertical profile consisted of a slow ascent and descent (less than 1
m/s) up to 100 m. To supplement the measurements from the three meteorological stations at 3 m
during the burn, the drone path used the upwind (MET3)/downwind (MET2) meteorological stations as
reference points. The drone flew at an altitude of 50 m above ground level (AGL) at 4.5 m/s. Four flights
(Figure 2-5) consisted of transect legs between MET2 and MET3 with the necessary flight legs to and
from the “Drone Control”. Each flight had 2 to 3 legs between MET2 and MET3 before the battery was
exhausted.

A Skydio X2 drone was used to capture thermal and standard video. This drone has a built-in Long-wave
Infrared (LWIR) thermal camera with 320p resolution. LWIR cameras present a relative temperature
gradient, where white/red indicates the hottest areas and black/blue colors represent the coldest areas
instead of displaying absolute temperature values. Thermal imaging was used to track the fire line
movement.

3.0 Results and Discussion
Figure 3-1 shows a comparison of the meteorological parameters measured at portable stations MET2
(downwind) and MET3 (upwind). The same parameters were measured at MET1 but are not included in
this figure. MET1 was further away from the fire and data collection began approximately an hour later
than MET2 and MET3, missing the beginning part of the burn. The temperature, RH, and wind speed
values each have larger jumps at MET2 shortly after the beginning of the burn (~10:45 EDT). At the time,
winds at MET2 were mostly from a northerly direction (varying east and west). The temperature
continued to be elevated at MET2 relative to MET3 until 12:00 EDT at which time the winds at MET2
weaken and turn to come more from the southwest. The wind direction at MET3 is generally from the
northeast during the entire period, turning slightly more easterly later in the day which contrasts with
the downwind station (MET2). The southwest winds at MET2 indicate the return flow of air at the low
levels into the burn contrary to the prevailing winds above the tree canopy. The temperature at MET3
slowly increases during the measurement period during which the RH is slowly decreasing, which is
expected during the day at an upwind site. The wind speed at MET3 varies during the day but does not
have a sharp change in windspeed in contrast to the increase seen at MET2 near the beginning of the
burn.

Figure 3-1. Meteorological parameters measured at portable stations MET2 (red, downwind location) and MET3
(blue, upwind location).

The Alta-X drone measured vertical profiles of temperature and relative humidities up to 100 m AGL at
“Drone Control” (see Figure 2-1). Figure 3-2 shows the vertical profiles measured before and after the
prescribed burn. Before the burn the temperature was consistent with a gradual decrease in
temperature as the height increased (pressure decreased) with a steeper rate just above the surface.
The post-burn temperature also shows a gradual decrease with height but slightly steeper than the pre-
burn temperature decrease. Similarly, there is a steeper rate in temperature decrease just above the
surface, but it is larger than the near surface rate pre-burn. Since the post-burn flights occurred later in
the day, the temperatures compared to the pre-burn temperatures were warmer from diurnal heating.
For the RH profiles, the trends are similar between pre-burn and post-burn measurements. The RH is
consistent during the vertical profile with some minor increases in RH during the pre-burn flights. Both
pre-burn and post-burn measurements have lower RH closer to the surface with the pre-burn flights
having a much lower RH than post-burn.

Figure 3-2. Vertical profiles of temperature (left) and relative humidity (right) from drone flights. Profiles are 2 m
averages of both the ascent and descent. Altitude is calculated from pressure.

Figure 3-3 shows the temperature measurements from MET2, MET3, and the drone at 50 m above
MET2 and MET3. The drone measurement closest to the location of the MET2 and MET3 was averaged
with 5 seconds of data prior to and after that measurement providing an 11-second centered average.
These 50 m AGL measurements were compared to the 5-minute average data from MET2 and MET3 (at
3 m AGL) for the time periods shown. The first data point at MET2, the red (near surface) and pink (at 50
m) markers overlap showing these temperatures were similar due to vertical mixing of warm air from
the burn. By the next measurement (at MET2) there is a difference in temperature at the ground
compared to 50 m with the ground measurements being higher as the burn moves away from MET2.
There is a larger difference between the ground and drone measurements at MET3 than MET2. Since
MET3 is upwind and not directly impacted by the burn, the 50 m temperatures are generally cooler
compared to the MET2 location. By the end of the burn, the 50 m temperatures at MET3 began
increasing. This was when the burn had moved closer to MET3. MET2 surface temperatures were
consistently cooler than MET3 at the surface. While the MET2 location was adjacent to the road, it was
also closer to trees where MET3 was more open. Additionally, MET2 surface temperatures are likely
influenced by the cool air return flow at the surface turning into the burn area.

Figure 3-3. Temperature comparison between portable meteorological stations and drone at MET2 and MET3.

Figure 3-4 shows the lapse rates (vertical change in temperature) measured at MET2 and MET3. On the
day of the burn, the meteorological conditions were mostly sunny with light winds. An expected lapse
rate for these conditions is a temperature decrease with height that would be steeper (more negative)
than a dry adiabatic lapse rate (-1°C/100 m or -0.5°C per 50 m) once the nocturnal inversion is mixed
out. MET2 has a smaller lapse rate (less negative) compared to MET3. This is due to the transport of
warmer air downwind of the prescribed burn above MET2, preventing the steepening of the lapse rate
(more negative). Immediately after the burn started the lapse rate at MET2 is near zero, slightly positive
due to the well mixed warm air from the burn. This was when the burn was closest to MET2 and the
drone passed through the rising thermal plume. As the burn begins moving away from MET2 the lapse
rate shifts to negative values, but these lapse rates are never as steep (more negative) as at MET3 that
was always upwind of the burn.

Figure 3-5 shows the time series of temperature for all four UAS flights (left panel). The spikes in
temperature during the flights indicate where the thermal plume from burning is rising into the 50 m
AGL flight path. Plotting the location of the UAS when these spikes occur provides a map (right panel) of
the burn progression. At 11:39 EDT the burn was on the south end where the Forest Service started
operations. Then the burn (and burn operations) moved north and east, backing into the prevailing wind
direction as a way of controlling the burn [6]. The last temperature spike (northeastern most point)
occurred shortly before the end of the Forest Service burn operations (14:24 EDT). Differences in flight
paths also provided some location beyond the transects between MET2 and MET3. The timing of these
is still consistent with the burn moving northward.

4.0 Conclusions
Three mobile meteorological stations and an instrumented drone were used to perform measurements
around a prescribed growing season burn. The mobile station measurements included two-dimensional
winds, temperature, RH, and pressure and the Alta-X drone measured temperature, RH, and pressure.
Four drone flights at 50 m AGL were performed with multiple transects from a downwind
meteorological station to an upwind meteorological station. The flight pattern allowed for multiple
measurements of the thermal plume from the burn as it progressed across the burn area.
Measurements from the downwind station indicated a jump in temperature at beginning of the burn
which was ignited nearly across the road from the station. After the burn progressed, the low-level
winds turned from the south and with lower temperatures indicating a return flow of cooler air moving
into the burn. Atmospheric lapse rates between the surface stations and the drone were less negative
over the downwind station than the upwind for nearly the entire burn. This was from the heated air
from the burn warming the 50 m temperatures down wind. Using the measurements from the drone,
thermal plumes indicated the location of the active fire within the burn area transects. The thermal
plume positions indicated the burn backing into the prevailing wind directions as planned for the
prescribed burn.
Growing season burn measurements show some similarities when compared to measurements
previously conducted during the dormant season, although several differences are notable [4].
Measurements in both burns demonstrated low-level cool air return flow into the burn areas. Both
burns were preceded by precipitation several days prior to the burns. Due to seasonal changes the
temperatures were cooler in the dormant season burn and warmer in the growing season burn.
Additionally, RH was higher in the growing season and lower in the dormant season. During the growing
season burn, the prevailing wind direction was mostly consistent (coming from northeast) where the
winds during the dormant season were more variable. The variability between these two burns creates
excellent case studies for testing fire spread models under different seasonal conditions.
The measurements taken during this study can be used to validate modeling results from the QUIC-Fire
model. Common output files from QUIC-Fire include wind field (3D wind velocity components), fuel
state (surface fuel density, moisture, height), and atmospheric/thermodynamic (temperature, pressure,
and humidity). The mobile meteorological towers and drone measurements can be used to validate the
outputs in the low-level wind field and atmospheric/thermodynamic files. Specific features of interest
will be the ability of the model to resolve the low-level return flow of cool air into the burn as indicated
by the downwind station or the transport of the thermal plume downwind altering the lapse rates.
Additionally, the drone indicated the timing and position of the burn which could be used to validate the
spread of the fire. This data will provide insight into how representative the QUIC-Fire model outputs
are compared to actual measurements conducted around a prescribed burn.
