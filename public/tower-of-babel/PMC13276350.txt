Low temperature rather than hypoxia drives cholesterol synthesis and oxygen consumption in plateau zokors
Authors: Qingfei Hu, Linna Wei, Zhifang An, Yaxuan Wang, Tongzuo Zhang, Dengbang Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySubterranean burrows of plateau zokors (Eospalax baileyi) on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau become significantly colder and more hypoxic with increasing altitude. Because cholesterol biosynthesis is highly oxygen-intensive, it is typically inhibited by hypoxia. Here, our findings revealed that with increasing altitude, the expression and activation of SREBP2, alongside FDFT1 and SQLE expression, were significantly upregulated in the liver. Furthermore, the incomplete degradation of SQLE produced two distinct isoforms. High-altitude zokors showed an increase in the active trunSQLE-1 and a concomitant decrease in the inactive trunSQLE-2, completely opposite to low-altitude zokors. Additionally, blood cholesterol and oxygen consumption increased significantly, with no significant difference in arterial oxygen saturation. Notably, body weight changes of plateau zokors conformed to Bergmann’s rule. These results suggested that the elevated cholesterol synthesis was not hypoxia-induced. Therefore, the increase in cholesterol synthesis in plateau zokors likely results from adaptation to the cold and humid underground burrow habitat.

Introduction

High-altitude environments impose severe physiological challenges on endothermic animals through hypoxia and low ambient temperatures. To survive in these extreme habitats, endemic plateau species have evolved diverse physiological and metabolic adaptation strategies.1 For instance, in response to high-altitude hypoxia, the yak has evolved a specialized hemoglobin structure with a significantly higher oxygen affinity than that of common cattle, enabling highly efficient oxygen binding and transport.2,3 Furthermore, a thick coat and a substantial subcutaneous fat layer allow the yak to withstand extreme cold. Similarly, the Himalayan marmot has evolved highly efficient oxygen utilization mechanisms and a relatively low metabolic rate to reduce oxygen consumption in hypoxic environments; to cope with extreme cold, it has developed hibernation habits, lowering its metabolism to evade severe winter conditions.4 In low-temperature environments, animals must expend more energy to maintain body temperature. Consequently, the regulation of metabolic systems, particularly lipid and cholesterol metabolism, plays a crucial role in maintaining cell membrane fluidity, meeting elevated energy demands, and ensuring overall physiological homeostasis.5

Plateau zokor (Eospalax baileyi) is a subterranean rodent endemic to the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, with a distribution that extends across altitudes of approximately 2000–4000 m.6,7,8 The burrows of plateau zokors are characterized by severe hypoxic and hypercapnic conditions; the oxygen content is about 20% lower than that of the atmosphere in the same area, while carbon dioxide levels are 7.3- to 48.6-fold higher.6,7,8 Combined with the decreasing temperature at higher altitudes, low oxygen, and coldness define the main characteristics of their habitat. Through long-term natural selection, the plateau zokor has evolved a comprehensive array of physiological adaptations to thrive in this extreme environment. Specifically, they exhibit significantly enhanced blood oxygen-carrying capacity, accompanied by adaptive remodeling in heart structure and function.9,10,11 Their respiratory system is optimized for hypoxia, characterized by specific alterations in pulmonary surfactant composition and acinus structure.12,13 Furthermore, their metabolic strategies—including glycolysis, lactate clearance, and hepatic metabolic profiles—differ distinctively from those of surface-dwelling mammals.14 To ensure adequate oxygen delivery and neuroprotection, their brain tissues show significantly elevated vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression and increased microvessel density.15 At the genetic and molecular levels, the plateau zokor genome displays massive structural variations, with multiple genes directly related to hypoxia adaptation undergoing profound positive selection.16,17,18 Recent chromosome-level genome analysis revealed that 71 genes in the plateau zokor are under species-specific positive selection, including six genes involved in ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolism and five genes involved in the positive regulation of proteolysis.16 Furthermore, transcriptionally active domains (TADs) specific to the plateau zokor contain genes related to apoptosis and autophagy, as well as cholesterol and lipid biosynthesis.16

Cholesterol is an essential mammalian sterol that maintains cell membrane integrity and serves as a vital precursor for bile acids and steroid hormones.19,20 Within the complex network of lipid metabolism, de novo biosynthesis plays a pivotal role in maintaining cholesterol homeostasis. While nearly all cell types can synthesize cholesterol in the endoplasmic reticulum, the liver is the primary site, contributing approximately 50% of the body’s total production.21 Cellular cholesterol biosynthesis is tightly regulated by the transcription factor SREBP2.22 In the Golgi apparatus, SREBP2 is proteolytically processed to release its N-terminal fragment, generating the activated form—also known as nuclear SREBP2 (nSREBP2)—which up-regulates key enzymes to promote lipid production.22 Among these, HMGCR functions as the primary rate-limiting enzyme, while SQLE acts as the second rate-limiting enzyme, catalyzing the conversion of squalene into 2,3-oxidosqualene.19,23 Structurally, SQLE is anchored to the endoplasmic reticulum membrane and consists of an N-terminal lipid-sensing regulatory domain (SQLE-N100) and a C-terminal catalytic center.24,25,26,27,28 To maintain lipid homeostasis, both enzymes are subjected to ubiquitin-mediated degradation.29,30,31 Notably, SQLE, regulated by the E3 ligase MARCH6,32,33,34 can undergo a specific incomplete degradation. This process cleaves only its N-terminal regulatory domain, releasing a truncated isoform (trunSQLE). Because trunSQLE loses the ability to sense intracellular cholesterol but retains its entire C-terminal catalytic center, it evades cholesterol-induced degradation. Consequently, trunSQLE exhibits a significantly prolonged half-life, driving continuous squalene-to-cholesterol conversion.26,35

This molecular mechanism serves as a vital metabolic adaptation under specific physiological challenges, such as chronic hypoxia. Cholesterol synthesis is highly oxygen-intensive, requiring 11 oxygen molecules per cholesterol molecule.21,36 While general hypoxia typically inhibits SREBP2 activation and down-regulates cholesterol synthesis to conserve oxygen,37,38,39 it paradoxically triggers the incomplete degradation of SQLE.35 The resulting accumulation of constitutively active trunSQLE allows cells to bypass standard feedback inhibition. Specifically, in subterranean or high-altitude mammals exposed to severe environmental hypoxia, this trunSQLE-mediated mechanism provides a critical survival strategy, ensuring sustained de novo cholesterol synthesis required for maintaining cell membrane integrity despite extreme oxygen limitation.

Building upon the unique genomic evolutionary features of the plateau zokor regarding lipid biosynthesis, an intriguing physiological phenomenon was revealed by our preliminary studies: As altitude increased, the oxygen content within their subterranean burrows significantly decreased, yet their in vivo cholesterol synthesis and serum cholesterol levels were paradoxically observed to increase. Given that cholesterol biosynthesis is a highly oxygen-intensive process, this up-regulation could not be explained by hypoxia alone. Therefore, the following hypothesis was proposed: low ambient temperature, rather than simple hypoxia, served as the primary ecological driver up-regulating cholesterol synthesis to meet the physiological demands of high-altitude adaptation. To test this hypothesis, plateau zokors from low-altitude habitats (ZL group), high-altitude habitats (ZH group), and a high-altitude temperature-controlled group (ZC group) were systematically compared. By evaluating body weight, blood cholesterol concentrations, systemic oxygen consumption, and hepatic transcriptomic profiles across these groups, the activation status of SREBP2 and the degradation patterns of SQLE were comprehensively analyzed. The present study aimed to uncover the specific molecular mechanisms underlying cholesterol metabolic adaptation in the plateau zokor, providing crucial ecophysiological insights into how subterranean rodents survive in extreme high-altitude environments characterized by intertwined hypoxia and low temperatures.

As altitude increased from 3,000 to 3,700 m, there was a significant increase in the expression levels of srebp2 mRNA and total SERBP2 (P-SREBP2 + nSREBP2) in the liver tissues of plateau zokors (Figures 2E–2G). While P-SREBP2 expression demonstrated no significant difference (Figure 2H), nSREBP2 expression exhibited a substantial increase (Figure 2I), and the ratio of nSREBP2 to total SREBP2 underwent a significant increase (Figure 2J). When the plateau zokors were raised at an altitude of 3,700 m for one week at a temperature of about 15.7°C, the expression levels of srebp2 mRNA and total SREBP2 were decreased markedly (Figures 2E–2G). P-SREBP2 and nSREBP2 expression levels exhibited a significant decrease (Figures 2H and 2I). However, the ratio of nSREBP2 to total SREBP2 was unchanged (Figure 2J).

The above results indicated that, as the altitude increases, the expression of srebp2 and the activation of SREBP2 were increased significantly in the liver of the plateau zokors.

In the high-altitude habitats, the expressions of FDFT1 and SQLE, as well as the incomplete degradation of SQLE, were increased in the liver of the zokors, and cholesterol concentration and oxygen consumption were increased in their blood. Cholesterol synthesis is an oxygen-intensive process; the synthesis of one molecule of cholesterol consumes 11 molecules of oxygen. Squalene is a vital intermediate in cholesterol synthesis,21 and SQLE, the second rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis, catalyzes the conversion of squalene to 2,3-oxidosqualene, which is the first oxygen-consuming metabolic reaction in cholesterol synthesis.25,26,27,28 Arterial blood oxygen saturation levels were measured to be approximately 90% in both ZH and ZL (Figure 3G). In contrast, the venous blood oxygen saturation levels were found to be around 70% in ZL (Figure 3G). However, in ZH, the venous blood oxygen saturation was found to be undetectable (Figure 3G). It is important to note that the detection range of the oximetry chip is from 60% to 100%, indicating that the venous oxygen saturation levels were below 60% in ZH. Given this technical limitation, the derived oxygen consumption values for these animals serve as semi-quantitative estimates, and any cross-group comparisons based on these estimates must be interpreted with caution. Consequently, the calculated oxygen consumption was approximately 20% in ZL and exceeded 30% in ZH (Figure 3H). When the plateau zokors (ZC) were raised at an altitude of 3,700 m for one week at a temperature of about 15.7°C, their arterial blood oxygen saturation levels were maintained at approximately 90%, while venous blood oxygen saturation levels were undetectable (Figure 3H). Despite the fact that the oximetry chip’s detection range (60–100%) precludes a definitive assessment of whether oxygen consumption decreased significantly in the ZC, the findings indicated that SQLE expression in liver tissues of ZC was decreased significantly, blood squalene concentration was increased clearly, and cholesterol concentration was decreased significantly. These findings suggested that oxygen consumption might be decreased significantly in the ZC (Figure 3H).

Western blot analysis showed three bands, designated as MS-1, MS-2, and MS-3, with molecular weights ranging from 45 to 65 kDa in the liver tissues of plateau zokors (Figure 3I). The application of MS sequencing Co-IP led to the identification of MS-1 as SQLE, and MS-2 and MS-3 as the incomplete degradation products trunSQLE-1 and trunSQLE-2, respectively (Figure 3I). As indicated by MS sequencing, the molecular weights of SQLE, trunSQLE-1, and trunSQLE-2 were determined to be 64.13, 54.35, and 47.66 kDa, respectively (Figure 3I). Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that the sqle CDS and amino acid sequence of the plateau zokor exhibited 93.73% and 94.87% similarity with those of the blind mole rat, respectively (Figures 3J and 3K, S2). The prediction of ubiquitin degradation sites in SQLE was conducted using the DeepMVP platform, which revealed 38 candidate sites (Table S5). The sequence alignment revealed that trunSQLE-1 and trunSQLE-2 correspond to cleavage products generated at lysine 82 (K82) and lysine 148 (K148), respectively (Figure 3L).

As altitude increases, the expression of total SQLE (SQLE + trunSQLE-1 + trunSQLE-2) (Figures 3N and 3O), SQLE (Figure 3P), trunSQLE-1 (Figure 3Q) and trunSQLE-2 (Figure 3R), and trunSQLE (Figure 3S) in the liver tissues of plateau zokors was exhibited a significant increase, but the ratio of trunSQLE to total SQLE was decreased significantly in (Figure 3T). Of note, there was a significant increase in the ratio of trunSQLE-1 to total SQLE (Figure 3U), but that of trunSQLE-2 to total SQLE demonstrated a significant decrease (Figure 3V). Conversely, in liver tissues of ZC, the expression of total SQLE (Figures 3N and 3O), SQLE (Figure 3P), trunSQLE-1 (Figure 3Q), trunSQLE-2 (Figure 3R), and trunSQLE (Figure 3S) was decreased significantly. Furthermore, a significant decrease in the ratio of trunSQLE to total SQLE was observed (Figure 3T), while the ratio of trunSQLE-1 to total SQLE was unchanged (Figure 3U), and that of trunSQLE-2 to total SQLE exhibited a significant increase (Figure 3V).

In summary, in the zokors inhabiting high altitudes, the expressions of key enzymes FDFT1 and SQLE in the liver were upregulated; the incomplete degradation product trunSQLE-1 of SQLE was upregulated, while trunSQLE-2 was downregulated; the accumulation of squalene and lanosterol decreased, and cholesterol synthesis and oxygen consumption of the zokors increased markedly. In contrast, in the zokors inhabiting low-altitude and the plateau zokors that were raised in the oxygen-enriched and higher-temperature conditions for one week, the expressions of FDFT1 and SQLE were downregulated; trunSQLE-1 was downregulated, while trunSQLE-2 was upregulated; the accumulation of squalene increased, and cholesterol synthesis and oxygen consumption of the zokors decreased significantly.

The cholesterol synthesis metabolism pathway in the liver tissues of plateau zokors was downregulated significantly by knockdown of sqle

Our above results suggested that the expression levels of SQLE and its incomplete degradation patterns might be a pivotal physiological mechanism underlying the variations in cholesterol levels and oxygen consumption in different altitude environments. To further test this hypothesis, we performed adenovirus pMultiRNAi-sqle-mediated sqle knockdown in high-altitude plateau zokors (Z sh-sqle) and compared them with adenovirus pMultiRNAi-NS-treated control plateau zokors (Z NC), and investigated the effects on cholesterol metabolism and oxygen consumption. The results showed that the mRNA expression level in the Z NC group remained stable and exhibited no significant difference compared with the Z C group (Figure S3). In comparison with Z NC controls, Z sh-sqle plateau zokors demonstrated a silencing efficiency of 68.05% (Figure 4A) and exhibited significant differential expression of 114 genes in liver tissues, of which 56 genes were significantly upregulated, and 58 genes were significantly downregulated (Figure 4B). GO enrichment analysis revealed a significant downregulation of biological processes related to steroid synthesis, including response to steroids and steroid binding (Figure 4D). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed a significant decrease in the expression of cholesterol metabolism and steroid hormone biosynthesis pathways (Figure 4F). GSEA revealed that, while both up- and downregulated genes were present within the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, its overall functional activity exhibited a significant decrease in liver tissues of Z sh-sqle (Figure 4G). Discussion

Low temperature may be the key ecological factor that induces an increase in cholesterol synthesis and accelerates the growth of plateau zokor

Plateau zokor is a subterranean rodent endemic to the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, with a distribution that extends across altitudes of approximately 2000–4000 m.6,7,8 For every 100 m increase in altitude, the atmospheric pressure drops by approximately 1 kPa, and the environmental temperature decreases by about 0.6°C. Therefore, as the altitude rises, the oxygen content and temperature in the burrows of the plateau zokors also decrease.6,7,8 Cholesterol biosynthesis is a highly oxygen-intensive process as the synthesis of one molecule of cholesterol consumes 11 molecules of oxygen, and the biosynthesis of cholesterol is inhibited by hypoxia.21,36 However, this study found that the arterial blood oxygen saturation of both the zokors inhabiting at 3,000 m and 3,700 m was approximately 90%. It is noteworthy that, as the altitude increased, the synthesis pathway of sterols in the liver of the plateau zokors was upregulated significantly, and the cholesterol concentration and the oxygen consumption in their blood increased markedly. Under an average room temperature of 15.7°C, after the high-altitude plateau zokors were raised at the 3700 m altitude sampling spot for one week, their arterial blood oxygen saturation was also approximately 90%, but the cholesterol concentration and the oxygen consumption in their blood were decreased significantly. These results suggested that, although the oxygen content in the habitat of plateau zokors is significantly decreased with altitude increase, the low-oxygen environment did not lead to hypoxia in plateau zokors. Therefore, the increase in cholesterol synthesis and oxygen consumption of the high-altitude zokors had nothing to do with the low oxygen, but was related to low temperatures in the environment. Moreover, the body weight of the high-altitude zokors was significantly greater than that of the low-altitude zokors, which is in line with Bergmann’s rule. Cholesterol is an important component for regulating the function of biological membranes and is also a necessary precursor for converting into bile acids and steroid hormones, playing a crucial role in the growth and development of mammals.19,20 Therefore, the increase in cholesterol synthesis with altitude rise in plateau zokors might be the physiological basis for the adaptation to the cold environments, and low temperature was very likely the key ecological factor that upregulated the cholesterol synthesis in plateau zokors.

Low temperature may upregulate the expression of cholesterol synthase FDFT1 and SQLE by inducing the expression and activation of SREBP2 in plateau zokor

SREBP2 is a transcription factor in mammals that selectively upregulates the expression of genes encoding cholesterol synthesis enzymes. SREBP2 is synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum and binds to SREBP-cleavage-activating protein (SCAP). When lanosterol levels increase in the endoplasmic reticulum, the sterol-sensing domain of SCAP interacts with insulin-induced gene 1 and 2 (INSIG1 and INSIG2). This interaction retains the SCAP-SREBP2 complex on the endoplasmic reticulum membrane and prevents its transport to the Golgi apparatus, thereby inhibiting SREBP2 activation. A decrease in endoplasmic reticulum lanosterol levels has been shown to result in the dissociation of the SCAP-SREBP2 complex from INSIG proteins. This dissociation enables the SCAP-SREBP2 complex to bud off from the endoplasmic reticulum membrane and traffic to the Golgi apparatus. Within the Golgi apparatus, SREBP2 undergoes proteolytic cleavage, releasing its N-terminal fragment. This process generates activated nSREBP2. The activated nSREBP2 enters the nucleus as a homodimer and binds to sterol regulatory elements (SREs) in the promoters of cholesterol synthesis enzyme genes, including HMGCR and SQLE. This binding serves to upregulate their expression and promote cholesterol synthesis.40 According to the findings of previous studies, the apparent molecular weight of full-length SREBP2 is approximately 120–140 kDa. In contrast, the molecular weight of its activated product, nSREBP2, is around 60–70 kDa.22,41,42 The present study found that plateau zokor nSREBP2 corresponds to the cleavage product generated at lysine 473 (K473). Hypoxia slows demethylation of lanosterol and its metabolite 24,25-dihydrolanosterol, causing both sterols to accumulate in cells.23,43 Furthermore, the expressions of insig-1 and insig-2 are upregulated by hypoxia in a response mediated by hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1alpha.23 Consequently, hypoxia demonstrated to inhibit SREBP2 activation.37,38 However, as altitude increased from 3,000 to 3,700 m, a significant increase was observed in srebp2 mRNA and total SREBP2 (P-SREBP2 + nSREBP2) protein expression in the liver tissues of plateau zokors. Additionally, the ratio of nSREBP2 to total SREBP2 also increased significantly. When the plateau zokors were raised at an altitude of 3,700 m for one week at a temperature of about 15.7°C, the expression of srebp2 mRNA, total SREBP2, and nSREBP2 in the liver tissues of plateau zokors was decreased markedly. However, the ratio of nSREBP2 to total SREBP2 remained unchanged. Adenovirus pMultiRNAi-sqle-mediated sqle knockdown resulted in a significant decrease in both srebp2 expression and SREBP2 activation in the liver tissues of plateau zokors. These results suggested that it was not hypoxia but low temperature that induced the expression of SERBPR and its activation in plateau zokors.

Lanosterol, a pivotal intermediate in cholesterol synthesis, functions as a crucial regulator of SREBP2 activation to nSREBP2. Elevated lanosterol concentrations are observed to impede SREBP2 activation, while diminished lanosterol levels are shown to stimulate SREBP2 activation.29,44,45 Our results showed that the expression of nSREBP2 was significantly increased in the liver of the high-altitude plateau zokors, concomitant with a substantial decrease of lanosterol in their blood. Conversely, in the low-altitude group, the high-altitude rearing group, and the sqle silencing group, the expressions of nSREBP2 were decreased, and the concentrations of lanosterol in blood were increased significantly. These results further suggested that low temperatures might cause a decrease in lanosterol levels in the bodies of plateau zokors, thereby promoting the activation of SREBP2. nSREBP2 exerts a selective upregulation effect on the expression of genes involved in the synthesis of cholesterol; the upregulation of those genes by nSREBP2 serves to promote the process of cholesterol synthesis within the body.40 A multitude of studies conducted on various cell lines and murine subjects have demonstrated that hypoxia impedes the expression of cholesterol synthesis enzymes, including HMGCR, FDFT1, and SQLE.37,39,46 In present studies, we found that, with increasing altitude and decreasing temperature, the mRNA levels of fdft1 and sqle and the expressions of FDFT1 and SQLE were increased significantly in liver tissues of plateau zokors. In contrast, in the zokors rearing in the oxygen-enriched and higher-temperature conditions, the expression levels of fdft1 and sqle, as well as FDFT1 and SQLE, were decreased significantly. In summary, low temperatures were likely to upregulate the expression of SREBP2 and its activation, thereby enhancing the expression of cholesterol synthesis enzymes FDFT1 and SQLE, and increasing the synthesis of squalene and its conversion to cholesterol in plateau zokors. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying cold-induced SREBP2 expression and its activation in plateau zokors have not yet been fully elucidated and require further investigation.

The uniqueness of the incomplete degradation pattern of SQLE in plateau zokors

SQLE is the second rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis.47 SQLE is predominantly composed of a C-terminal catalytic center, an SQLE-N100, and an amphipathic helix situated between these domains.47 SQLE is anchored to the endoplasmic reticulum membrane by hydrophobic amino acids located in its catalytic center, amphipathic helix, and SQLE-N100 regulatory domain. The primary function of the catalytic center is the conversion of squalene to 2,3-oxidosqualene. The amphipathic helix region contains structural elements that sense intracellular cholesterol concentration. The pocket, which is recessed into the endoplasmic reticulum membrane within the SQLE-N100 regulatory domain, possesses the ability to sense both cholesterol and squalene concentrations.25,27,28

According to the extant literature, the existence of ubiquitination sites within the SQLE-N100 regulatory domain of animal SQLE has been previously demonstrated. The aforementioned domain is located approximately between amino acid residues 60 and 70, and SQLE exhibits only one incomplete degradation product.32,33,34,48 The present study found that two cleavage products of different molecular sizes were generated by the incomplete degradation of plateau zokor SQLE, namely trunSQLE-1 and trunSQLE-2. trunSQLE-1 and trunSQLE-2 were identified as cleavage products generated at lysine 82 (K82) and lysine 148 (K148), respectively. According to existing literature, only the SQLE-N100 is removed by incomplete SQLE degradation, thereby releasing trunSQLE, a truncated isoform that retains the entire C-terminal catalytic domain but has a reduced length.35 In plateau zokors, trunSQLE-1 retained the full C-terminal catalytic domain and should have the ability to catalyze the conversion of squalene to 2,3-oxidosqualene. However, the cleavage site for trunSQLE-2 was located within the catalytic domain of SQLE,48 suggesting that trunSQLE-2 was likely inactive. The physiological function of trunSQLE-2 in plateau zokors remains to be further determined.

The incomplete degradation patterns of SQLE in plateau zokors might be an important mechanism for adapting to the different temperature environments

Ubiquitination-mediated degradation of SQLE occurred via two pathways: complete degradation and incomplete degradation. Elevated intracellular cholesterol concentrations were demonstrated to accelerate SQLE degradation.35 Intracellular squalene concentration is demonstrated to regulate SQLE stability. Inhibition of FDFT1 is shown to reduce intracellular squalene levels, resulting in decreased SQLE stability. Conversely, exogenous squalene supplementation is shown to stabilize SQLE on the endoplasmic reticulum membrane.35 Hypoxia is demonstrated to reduce full-length SQLE expression at both the transcriptional and post-translational levels. In addition, hypoxia is shown to induce squalene accumulation, decrease cholesterol synthesis, and enhance the incomplete degradation of SQLE.49 These findings suggested that the degradation of SQLE was coordinately regulated by squalene and cholesterol.34,47,50 Our results showed that, in the zokors inhabiting at high altitude, the expressions of key enzymes SQLE and of the incomplete degradation product trunSQLE-1 in the liver were upregulated, while trunSQLE-2 was downregulated; the concentration of squalene was decreased, and that of cholesterol was increased markedly. In contrast, in the zokors inhabiting low altitudes, in the zokors that were raised in the oxygen-enriched and higher-temperature conditions for one week, and in the zokors of sqle silence, the expressions of SQLE and trunSQLE-1 were downregulated, while trunSQLE-2 were upregulated; the concentrations of squalene were decreased, and those of cholesterol were decreased significantly. These results suggested that the increase of cholesterol accompanying the reduction of squalene might synergistically promote the cold-specific pattern of SQLE incomplete degradation, which was characterized by increased catalytically active trunSQLE-1 and decreased catalytically inactive trunSQLE-2. Conversely, the combined reduction of cholesterol and increase of squalene might promote synergistically the warm-specific pattern of SQLE incomplete degradation, which was characterized by decreased catalytically active trunSQLE-1 and increased catalytically inactive trunSQLE-2. A series of previous studies established that trunSQLE retained the C-terminal catalytic domain and possessed the capacity to catalyze the conversion of squalene to monooxidosqualene and dioxidosqualene. However, trunSQLE is devoid of the SQLE-N100 regulatory domain, a deficiency that led to the loss of cholesterol-sensing capability and resistance to cholesterol-induced degradation. Consequently, trunSQLE exhibited a substantially prolonged half-life and perpetually catalyzed the conversion of squalene to cholesterol.26,35 Thus, the cold-specific pattern of SQLE incomplete degradation in the high-altitude plateau zokors accelerated squalene-to-cholesterol conversion, and resulted in reducing squalene accumulation and increasing cholesterol synthesis. In contrast, the warm-specific pattern in the low-altitude plateau zokors delayed squalene-to-cholesterol conversion and resulted in increasing squalene accumulation and decreasing cholesterol synthesis. Therefore, the incomplete degradation patterns of SQLE in plateau zokors might be an important mechanism for adapting to the different temperature environments.

In conclusion, oxygen content and temperature in the burrows of plateau zokors are the most sensitive ecological factors to changes in altitude. They are also the most important ecological factors influencing the physiological functions of the zokors. However, as the altitude increases, although the oxygen content in the burrows of plateau zokors decreases, the hypoxia in the high-altitude environment does not cause hypoxia in the zokors. Instead, the synthesis of cholesterol in the liver of the zokors was not inhibited but upregulated significantly. Therefore, the environmental hypoxia might not be the key ecological factor regulating cholesterol anabolism in plateau zokors. Notably, as the altitude increases, the temperature in the burrows of plateau zokors decreases, and the synthesis of cholesterol in the zokors is increased significantly. Furthermore, as the altitude increases, the body weight of plateau zokors increased (in line with Bergmann’s rule).51 Therefore, the increase of cholesterol synthesis in the zokors might be the physiological basis for the adaptation to the cold and humid underground habitat. Low temperature might be the key ecological factor that upregulates the cholesterol synthesis metabolism of plateau zokors. In the high-altitude and low-temperature habitat, the cholesterol synthesis in the zokors might be increased by increasing the expression and activation of SREBP2, up-regulating the expression of cholesterol synthase FDFT1 and SQLE, and increasing the proportion of trunSQLE-1, an incomplete degradation product of SQLE with enzymatic activity, thereby the oxygen consumption of the zokors was increased However, the molecular regulatory mechanisms by which low temperature regulated the expression and activation of SREBP2, as well as the incomplete degradation mode of SQLE in plateau zokor, remains unclear and requires further clarification.

Limitations of the study

While our experimental design successfully uncoupled the effects of hypoxia and cold to identify temperature as the primary driver of cholesterol synthesis, we acknowledge certain limitations regarding our temperature control. As subterranean rodents, plateau zokors are heavily dependent on their specific burrow microclimates rather than above-ground temperatures. In this study, the high-altitude temperature-controlled group (ZC) was maintained at 15.7°C to create a thermal contrast. However, this temperature deviates significantly from their natural high-altitude burrow environment (approximately 2.5°C). Although this 15.7°C setting was sufficient to demonstrate the metabolic impact of warming compared to the natural cold exposure, it does not perfectly replicate their precise ecological niches. Future investigations should incorporate more refined temperature gradients that strictly mimic seasonal burrow temperatures across different altitudes to comprehensively map the thermal thresholds of these metabolic adaptations.

Finally, we must acknowledge certain methodological limitations in our study that warrant consideration for future research. First, regarding the blood gas analysis, the detection range of the oximetry chip used is restricted to 60%–100%. Consequently, the venous oxygen saturation (SvO2) levels in the ZH and ZC groups fell below this lower detection limit (<60%) and were recorded as undetectable. Because systemic oxygen consumption was calculated using this baseline limit, these specific data represent a semi-quantitative estimate, and related comparisons should be interpreted with caution. In future investigations, employing specialized full-range co-oximeters or alternative invasive hemodynamic monitoring will be necessary to acquire precise SvO2 measurements under extreme hypoxic conditions. Second, due to logistical constraints during field sampling, only two animals per group (n = 2) were utilized for tissue fixation and immunofluorescence evaluation. While this small sample size restricts the statistical power of the histological data, these images primarily serve as qualitative spatial validation. Importantly, our core quantitative conclusions regarding protein expression are robustly supported by western blot analyses with a substantially larger sample size (n = 6 per group), ensuring the overall reliability and validity of our findings. Moving forward, optimizing field preservation protocols to accommodate larger cohorts or conducting parallel experiments under simulated laboratory conditions will help provide more robust quantitative spatial mapping.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals, field sampling, and experimental allocation

A total of 40 plateau zokors were live-trapped in September 2024, non-breeding season to eliminate physiological fluctuations in Qinghai Province, China. High-altitude plateau zokors were captured at the Laji Mountain area (36°21′ N, 101°25′ E; 3700 m a.s.l.). In this sampling area, the atmospheric pressure was 59 kPa, and the average oxygen partial pressure was 11.7 kPa. The ambient temperature in the natural burrows was 2.5°C. The precise oxygen content in the atmosphere of this area was 173.1 ± 2.3g/m3, while the oxygen content within the burrows was approximately 138.2 ± 17.7g/m3, which was about 20% lower than the atmospheric levels. Thirty-two healthy plateau zokors (body weight: 280 ± 10 g) were randomly divided into four groups (n = 8 per group): the high-altitude group (ZH), high-altitude control group (ZC), adenovirus pMultiRNAi-NS group (Z NC), and adenovirus pMultiRNAi-sqle group (Z sh-sqle).Low-altitude plateau zokors (ZL group, n = 8, body weight: 280 ± 10 g) were captured at Hacheng Village (36°46′ N, 101°16′ E; 3000 m a.s.l.), where the atmospheric pressure was 65 kPa, the ambient burrow temperature was 7.5°C, and the burrow oxygen content was similarly about 20% lower than the local atmosphere. Capture sites and corresponding measured environmental factors are shown in Table S6.

To investigate the specific effects of ambient temperature and the role of the sqle gene, the remaining animals captured at 3700 m were randomly allocated into three experimental groups (n = 8 per group): the high-altitude control group (ZC), the negative control adenovirus group (Z NC), and the sqle-knockdown adenovirus group (Z sh-sqle). On the day of capture, animals in the Z NC and Z sh-sqle groups were intraperitoneally injected with 4×109 PFU of pMultiRNAi-NS and pMultiRNAi-sqle adenoviruses, respectively, while the ZC group was administered an equal volume of normal saline.

Subsequently, all three groups (ZC, Z NC, and Z sh-sqle) were housed individually for a duration of one week in sound-attenuated, dark chambers designed to simulate the subterranean environment. The captive environment was maintained at a constant temperature of 15.7°C, significantly higher than the natural burrow temperature (2.5°C), to serve as a temperature-altered high-altitude model. This one-week period was selected to allow for both the recovery from capture-related stress and the sufficient in vivo expression of the viral vectors. During this period, all animals were manually fed their naturally preferred plants (Potentilla anserina and Ajania tenuifolia). No mortality was recorded during the acclimation and treatment phase.

Sample Allocation: To clarify the specific sample sizes used in various downstream assays, the 8 animals in each group were allocated as follows. Blood samples were collected from all animals (n = 8 per group) for blood gas and serum lipid analyses. Liver tissues from two randomly selected zokors per group (n = 2 per group, three samples were embedded per individual.) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for immunofluorescence. The liver tissues from the remaining six zokors per group (n = 6 per group) were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently aliquoted for various molecular biology experiments. Specifically, these frozen liver samples were used for qRT-PCR (n = 6 per group) and Western blot analyses (n = 6 per group). For transcriptome sequencing, a subset of three samples was randomly selected from the frozen liver pool of each group (n = 3 per group). Furthermore, for the Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays, to guarantee sufficient target protein concentration due to its high expression at high altitudes, liver tissues were exclusively selected from the high-altitude group (ZH), utilizing a subset of four samples (n = 4) from its frozen liver pool.

All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Qinghai University and complied with the China Practice for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (GB/T35892-2018).

Method details

Sequence retrieval and bioinformatics analysis

Coding sequences of srebp2 and sqle across 12 mammalian species were retrieved from NCBI. The relevant information is listed in Table S1. For the plateau zokor, specific sequences were identified by screening a local BLAST database constructed from our previously sequenced second- and third-generation transcriptomic data. Ubiquitination and SUMOylation sites of the translated amino acid sequences were predicted utilizing the DeepMVP web server.

AAV vector construction and in vivo delivery

Recombinant AAV vectors targeting plateau zokor sqle were constructed and packaged by ObiO Technology (Shanghai, China). Three distinct shRNA sequences targeting the sqle transcript (5′-GCAGCTACCTCTACATCAACA-3′, 5′-GGATAAAGAGACTGGAGATAT-3′, and 5′-GCTACGGAAAGCTTGCTTTCT-3′) and a scrambled negative control sequence (5′-CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCG-3′) were designed and individually cloned into the pAAV-U6-shRNA/spgRNA v2.0-CMV-MCS-WPRE vector. Viruses were produced in AAV-293 cells and purified from cell lysates using iodixanol density gradient ultracentrifugation. Viral titers were determined by qPCR. For in vivo silencing, purified AAV2/9 vectors were administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 1 × 1013 vector genomes (v.g.) per animal.

Blood gas and serum lipid measurement

Arterial and venous blood samples were analyzed using an OPTI CCA-TS blood gas analyzer to determine PaCO2, PvCO2, PaO2, PvO2, SaO2, and SvO2. Oxygen utilization was calculated as the difference between SaO2 and SvO2. For lipid profiling, 150 μL of serum was saponified with 0.5 M KOH in ethanol (37°C, 4 h). Solid-phase extraction (SPE) cartridges were preconditioned with n-hexane–isopropanol (1:1, v/v), water, and methanol. Samples were loaded, washed with methanol–water (75:25, v/v), and eluted with n-hexane–isopropanol (1:1, v/v). The dried eluate was reconstituted in 120 μL of isopropanol–methanol (1:1, v/v). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Waters Acquity UPLC system using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Gradient elution (mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid in water; B: 0.1% formic acid in methanol) progressed from 10% to 80% B over 16 min. Mass detection was performed on an AB SCIEX 5500 QQQ-MS in positive ESI-MRM mode (curtain gas: 35 psi; collision gas: 7 psi; ion spray voltage: −4200 V; source temperature: 450°C; GS1/GS2: 35 psi).

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and quantified via a NanoDrop 2000. Libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Kit and sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform. Raw reads were filtered by fastp, mapped to the plateau zokor reference genome using HISAT2, and read counts were obtained via HTSeq-count. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with thresholds of Q < 0.05 and fold-change >2. Transcriptome data quality control results are shown in Table S2. For transcriptome mapping, the reference genome of plateau zokor (Taxonomy ID: 146132) was used, with the assembly version plateau_zokor_v2.1 (accession number: GWHABJZ000000000, deposited in the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC), China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB)).

Quantitative real-time PCR and western blotting

Liver RNA was reverse-transcribed using the FastKing RT Kit. qRT-PCR was conducted using custom-designed primers (Table S3). For Western blotting, antigenic peptides (CPPKEGERRTTHNII for SREBP2; CSFLPPSPVNKRGVL for SQLE) were utilized to generate plateau zokor-specific polyclonal antibodies. Liver proteins (40 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against SREBP2, SQLE, FDFT1 (all 1:2000), or β-actin (1:5000). Target bands were visualized using ECL reagents and quantified via ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence and Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Liver sections (5 μm) underwent microwave-assisted antigen retrieval in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and were blocked with 10% donkey serum. Sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies against SREBP2, FDFT1, and SQLE (1:200), followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI. For Co-IP, total liver proteins from the ZH group were incubated overnight with an anti-SQLE antibody complexed with Protein A/G magnetic beads. The complexes were magnetically separated, extensively washed with 1× TBS, eluted in reducing loading buffer at 95°C, and resolved by SDS-PAGE.

In-gel digestion and LC-MS/MS analysis

Target SDS-PAGE gel bands were excised, destained, and dehydrated with acetonitrile. Proteins were reduced with 10 mM DTT (55°C, 30 min), alkylated with 30 mM IAA (dark, 40 min), and digested overnight with trypsin at 37°C. Extracted peptides (200 ng) were loaded onto an Evosep One nano-UPLC coupled to a timsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometer. Data were acquired in DDA-PASEF mode (scan range: 100–700 m/z), with collision energy increasing linearly with ion mobility (20 eV at 1/K0 = 0.6 Vs/cm2; 59 eV at 1/K0 = 1.6 Vs/cm2). Raw data were searched against the custom plateau zokor database using SpectroMine (v4.2). Sequence coverage mapping and cleavage fragment assembly were conducted using MaxQuant and DNAMAN 9.0.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 and SPSS 25.0. Prior to parametric testing, Levene’s test was conducted to confirm the homogeneity of variances. For normally distributed data with equal variances, differences between two groups were assessed using Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were evaluated via one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s post-hoc multiple comparison test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and n values representing biological replicates are defined in the respective figure legends. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, indicated by distinct lowercase letters (a, b, c) in multi-group analyses.
