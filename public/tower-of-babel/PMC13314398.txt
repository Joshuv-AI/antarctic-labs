Physicochemical and Sensory Evaluation of a Novel Lupin-Lentil-Derived Artificial Meat-Based Burger as a Sustainable Alternative to Beef-Based Burger
Authors: Rawya Obaid Alatawi, Basem Mohammed Al-Sawalha, Mohammed Ismael Saleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rising demand for plant-based foods has driven interest in developing them as sustainable alternatives to meat products. Burgers, being globally popular and culturally adaptable, provide an effective vehicle for introducing plant-based meat (PBM) formulations. This study aimed to develop a novel PBM using lupin and lentils as primary ingredients. It also attempted to characterize and compare its nutritional, physicochemical, and sensory properties with a commercial beef (CB)-based burger (CBB). Proximate composition, pH, cooking loss, color parameters (L*, a*, b*), and sensory attributes were evaluated. The results showed that PBM contained significantly higher fiber and carbohydrate levels, while CBB was richer in protein, fat, and energy (P<0.05). CB had a more elevated moisture content, whereas PBM exhibited superior structural stability and markedly lower cooking loss (7.25% vs. 21.92%). Color analysis did not reveal any difference in redness (a*), but PBM demonstrated greater lightness (L*) and yellowness (b*) than CBB. Sensory evaluation (n=32) did not show any significant variations (P>0.05) in overall acceptability, tenderness, juiciness, or appearance, although CBB scored slightly better in flavor. In conclusion, lupin- and lentil-based meat burgers achieved comparable sensory acceptance to CBBs while offering favorable nutritional and physicochemical attributes, reinforcing their potential as sustainable “green” alternatives.

INTRODUCTION

The global demand for plant-based foods has increased substantially over recent years. This trend is driven by several factors, including rising food costs, growing health and environmental sustainability awareness, ethical concerns, and adoption of vegetarian and vegan lifestyles (Graça et al., 2015; Poore and Nemecek, 2018; Willett et al., 2019). Health-related considerations are particularly influential, as high consumption of red and processed meats has been linked to elevated risks of chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and colorectal cancer (Bouvard et al., 2015; Satija et al., 2016; Willett et al., 2019).

Burgers represent one of the most widely consumed fast foods in the world, valued for their convenience, flavor, and cultural adaptability. Traditionally prepared with ground beef or other animal meats, burgers have become a staple in both restaurant menus and home cooking (Edge, 2005). More recently, the burger format has emerged as an effective vehicle for introducing plant-based meat (PBM) alternatives, offering consumers a familiar and highly acceptable form of food through which novel sources of proteins can be added to the diet (Sha and Xiong, 2020; Michel et al., 2021). This advantage makes burgers an ideal candidate for introducing meat reformulated with sustainable, plant-based ingredients, while meeting consumer expectations regarding taste, texture, and satisfaction (Kyriakopoulou et al., 2021).

PBM burgers are specifically designed to mimic the sensory attributes of traditional commercial beef (CB)-based burger (CBB) while being made entirely from plant-derived ingredients (Kumar et al., 2017; Sha and Xiong, 2020). Typical formulations incorporate legumes, grains, vegetables, and plant-based oils, chosen for their nutritional value and functional properties (Joshi and Kumar, 2015; Kyriakopoulou et al., 2021). Legumes are particularly valuable because of their high protein and dietary fiber contents, as well as technological functionality. In this regard, lupin (Lupinus spp.) and lentils (Lens culinaris) are especially promising (Duranti et al., 2008; Lucas et al., 2015). Lupin contains proteins at ≤40%, with low starch levels, and is a rich source of bioactive compounds, antioxidants, and dietary fiber (Sirtori et al., 2015). It also possesses functionally-relevant characteristics such as excellent water-binding and emulsifying capacities, which are critical to burger production (Muranyi et al., 2016). Lentils contribute essential nutrients, including iron, folate, resistant starch, and polyphenols; their ability to improve the texture and structure of food matrices is well recognized (Tosh and Yada, 2010; Bouchenak and Lamri-Senhadji, 2013). A combination of lupin and lentils as a single PBM formulation therefore provides a balanced nutritional profile and desirable textural properties (Lucas et al., 2015; Clark and Tilman, 2017).

Although lupin (Chilón-Llico et al., 2022) and lentils (Summo et al., 2016) have been investigated individually for PBM development, their combined application has not yet been reported. The present study, therefore, aims to develop and characterize a novel PBM incorporating lupin and lentils as primary ingredients. It evaluates nutritional and sensory quality, including formulation development, cooking loss, proximate composition (moisture, proteins, fats, ash, and carbohydrates), pH, color, and sensory attributes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Raw materials

Bitter lupin (Lupinus albus L.), lentils (L. culinaris), mushrooms (Agaricus bisporus), red beans (Phaseolus vulgaris L.), beetroots (Beta vulgaris L.), rolled oats (Avena sativa L.), coarse bulgur (Triticum durum), onions (Allium cepa L.), chia (Salvia hispanica L.) seeds, textured soya protein, and refined deodorized coconut oil were obtained from local markets in Amman, Jordan. Nutritional yeast was from Bob’s Red Mill (https://www.bobsredmill.com/product/nutritional-yeast). The mixture was fortified with thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, folate, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12. All ingredients were stored under appropriate conditions－ambient temperature for dry goods and refrigeration at 4°C for fresh produce－until further processing.

Pre-processing of ingredients

Lentils were boiled in water for 20 min until a mashable texture was achieved; the water was drained, and the seeds were ground to a medium-coarse consistency. Bitter lupin seeds were soaked in distilled water (2:3, w/v) for 30 min at room temperature to facilitate debittering; they boiled for 120 min, drained of water, cooled, manually dehulled, and finely ground. Mushrooms were diced and sautéed with onions, seasonings, and refined deodorized coconut oil over medium heat for 15 min, cooled, and ground. Red beans were soaked for 12 h, boiled for 90 min, drained of water, and ground. Coarse bulgur was cooked until tender, cooled, and ground, while beetroot was peeled and shredded. Chia seeds were soaked in water (1:3, w/v) overnight until a mucilage formed. All pre-processed ingredients were stored in sealed polyethylene bags at −18°C until further use, except hydrated chia seeds, which were incorporated fresh during formulation.

Formulation of a PBM

The PBM was formulated on a wet basis and comprised the following ingredients: 110 g processed lupin, 80 g processed lentils, 50 g processed red beans, 300 g sautéed mushrooms combined with onions, seasonings, and coconut oil, 25 g rolled oats, 55 g cooked coarse bulgur, 400 g hydrated chia seeds, 5 g grated beetroot, 15 g nutritional yeast, 70 g textured soya protein, and 50 g carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) as a binding agent. The seasoning consisted of 18 g salt, 3 g black pepper, 9 g powdered onion, 8 g garlic powder, and 10 g mixed meat spices. In addition, 42 g of refined, odorless coconut oil was incorporated to enhance moisture retention and mouthfeel. All ingredients were blended thoroughly with a stainless-steel food processor (Moulinex) for ∼5 min, performed in short pulses until a homogeneous, dough-like mixture was obtained. This procedure ensured an even distribution of moisture, oil, and binders (CMC and hydrated chia seeds) without overheating. The mixture was shaped into uniform patties and stored in plastic bags at −18°C until further analysis.

Optimization and selection of the lupin-lentil ratio

Several pilot formulations with different proportions of lupin and lentils were prepared to identify the most stable and sensorially acceptable ratio. The formulations included lentil:lupin=3:5, lentil:lupin=3:10, and lentil:lupin=1.84:1, each exhibiting specific limitations. The 3:5 formulation showed noticeable surface charring during cooking, likely due to excessive protein-induced browning. The 3:10 ratio produced a pinkish hue after frying, differing markedly from the brown color typical of cooked beef and reducing consumer appeal. The 1.84:1 ratio suffered from textural instability, lost binding integrity, and partially disintegrated upon heating. The ratio of lupin:lentil=11:8 (w/w, as processed ingredients), however, provided the best overall balance between color, texture, cooking stability, and sensory quality, and was therefore selected for further physicochemical and sensory evaluations.

Cooking procedure

Prior to cooking, frozen PBM patties were thawed to room temperature, while fresh CBB patties were used. Both meat types were shaped into uniform discs, weighing ∼55 g, and shallow-fried in refined, odorless coconut oil using a pre-heated non-stick pan for 3 min per side. Cooking continued until a golden-brown surface was achieved and the internal temperature reached ≥75°C, which was measured with a TES-1310 digital thermometer (http://www.eeprocess.com/TES/1310.htm). After frying the patties, excess surface oil was removed by blotting with absorbent paper before subsequent physicochemical and sensory analyses.

Moisture level determination

The moisture contents of the PBM and CBB samples were determined by employing the oven-drying method and according to the standard gravimetric procedure for determining “Total Solids and Moisture in Flour” (Official Method 925.10; AOAC, 2016). Of each homogenized sample, ∼5 g was weighed into a pre-dried and pre-weighed moisture dish. The samples were dried in a hot air oven at 105°C until a constant weight was achieved. After cooling in a desiccator, the samples were reweighed, and the moisture content was calculated by applying the formula (1):

Moisture(%)=Weight of water(W2−W3)Weight of sample(W2−W1)×100 (1)

where, W1=weight of the empty dish after drying (g), W2=weight of the dish with the sample before drying (g), and W3=weight of the dish with the sample after drying (g).

Ash content determination

Ash contents of the PBM and CBB samples were determined per the “Direct Method for Ash in Flour” (Official Method 923.03; AOAC, 2016). Of each homogenized sample, ∼5 g was weighed into a pre-weighed crucible and incinerated in a muffle furnace (Carbolite) at 550°C for 10 h until all organic matter was completely burned, leaving a white or light-gray ash residue. After cooling in a desiccator, the crucible was reweighed, and the ash content was calculated using the following equation (2):

Ash(%)=Weight of ash(W3−W1)Weight of sample(W2−W1)×100 (2)

where, W1=weight of the empty crucible before ashing (g), W2=weight of the crucible and food before ashing (g), and W3=weight of the crucible and ash (g).

Protein content determination

Protein contents of the PBM and CBB samples were determined utilizing the Kjeldahl method and as described in the Official Method 978.04 (AOAC, 2016). It involves sample digestion to convert nitrogen into ammonium sulfate, followed by distillation and titration to quantify the total nitrogen. Protein content was calculated using the equation (3):

Protein(%)=N×6.25 (3)

where N represents the percentage of nitrogen ascertained by the Kjeldahl method.

Fat content determination

The fat contents of the PBM and CBB samples were determined using a modern Soxhlet extraction apparatus (VELP Scientifica) and per the Official Method 991.36 (AOAC, 2016) with certain modifications. Of the dried and ground sample, ∼5 g was placed in a cellulose extraction thimble, and fat was separated using petroleum ether as the solvent for 6 h. Solvent extraction and evaporation were conducted within the same unit of this apparatus, allowing for automatic solvent recovery and fat residue collection. After extraction, the residual fat was weighed, and the fat level was calculated by applying the following equation (4):

Fat (%) on a dry matter basis=Weight of fat(W3−W2)Weight of food sample(W1)×100 (4)

where, W1=weight of the dried sample (g), W2=weight of the dried empty bottom flask (g), and W3=weight of the dried round bottom flask and the extracted fat (g).

Fiber content determination (crude fiber)

Crude fiber of the PBM and CBB samples was quantified following the “Fiber (Crude) in Animal Feed and Pet Food” protocol (Official Method 978.10; AOAC, 2016). Samples were first dried to a constant weight at 105°C, defatted via the Soxhlet method, and then finely ground. Of the sample, ∼2 g was accurately weighed and acid-digested with 200 mL of 1.25% H2SO4 solution in a fiber extraction apparatus. The mixture was boiled and refluxed gently at 90°C for 40 min with occasional stirring. It was filtered through a pre-weighed crucible, and the residue was washed thoroughly with hot distilled water until neutral pH.

For alkali digestion, the residue was returned to the apparatus, and 200 mL of 1.25% NaOH solution was added. The mixture was boiled, refluxed at 90°C for 40 min, and filtered through the same crucible. The residue was washed with hot distilled water and then acetone. The crucible with the residue was oven-dried at 105°C until constant weight, cooled in a desiccator, and weighed (WeightA).

For ashing, the dried residue was incinerated in a muffle furnace at 500°C－600°C for 2 h. The crucible was cooled in a desiccator and reweighed (WeightB). The fiber content was calculated employing the following equation (5):

Crude fiber (%)=WeightA-WeightBWsample×100 (5)

Carbohydrate content determination

The carbohydrate contents of the PBM and CBB samples were determined based on the difference, following the “Proximate Analysis of Milk-based Infant” protocol (Official Method 986.25; AOAC, 2016). The proportion of carbohydrates was calculated by subtracting the sum of the moisture, crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, and ash contents from 100; the following formula (6) was used:

Carbohydrates(%)=100−(Moisture+Protein+Fat+Fiber+Ash) (6)

Caloric value calculation

The caloric contents of the PBM and CBB samples were estimated based on their proximate composition and using the Atwater factors (FAO, 2003). The energy value (kcal/100 g) was calculated by applying the following formula (7):

Calories(kcal/100 g)=(Protein×4)+(Fat×9)+(Carbohydrates×4) (7)

where “4” is the energy conversion factor of each component. Moisture, ash, and fiber contents were excluded as they do not contribute significantly to metabolism-derived energy.

Cooking loss determination

Cooking loss was evaluated by weighing the patties immediately before frying and again after cooking and cooling for 5 min on an absorbent paper to remove surface oil. The percentage cooking loss was calculated according to the following equation (8):

Cooking loss(%)=Wraw−WcookedWraw×100 (8)

where Wraw is the initial weight before frying, and Wcooked is the final weight after cooling.

pH measurement

PBM and CBB patties were dried in a hot air oven at 105°C until a constant weight was achieved. The dried samples were then finely ground in a coffee grinder to obtain a homogeneous powder. Of the powdered sample, 5 g was accurately weighed, transferred into a beaker containing 100 mL of deionized water, and mixed continuously on a magnetic stirrer for 5 min to obtain a uniform slurry. After briefly allowing it to settle, the pH was measured by directly inserting the electrode of a calibrated digital pH meter into the slurry. The pH value was recorded once a stable reading was obtained.

Color determination

The surface color of PBM and CBB patties was assessed immediately after frying. Color parameters: L* (lightness), a* (redness-greenness), and b* (yellowness-blueness) were ascertained with a CR-400 Chroma Meter (Konica Minolta), pre-calibrated against a standard white tile before each measurement (L*=98.45, a*=−0.10, and b*=−0.13). Readings were taken at three different locations on the surface of each patty, and their average value was calculated.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation involved a panel of 32 semi-trained volunteers aged 21－65 years. They belonged to the Department of Nutrition and Food Technology, The University of Jordan, Amman, Jordan. The panelists participated in a two-week training program (eight sessions, ∼18 h in total) following ISO 8586 and Stone and Sidel (2004) guidelines to gain familiarity with burgers made from natural meat or plant-based alternative meat. Training included (1) orientation to sensory attributes: appearance/color, flavor, tenderness, juiciness, and overall acceptability; (2) instructions on using the 9-point hedonic scale (1=dislike extremely, 9=like extremely); and (3) calibration with reference patties representing low, medium, and high intensities for color, tenderness, and juiciness. Practice sessions were conducted to align scoring behavior and reduce variability among assessors.

The evaluation was performed in a dedicated sensory analysis laboratory of the Department, and was designed in accordance with ISO 8589 standards. The facility consists of individual booths separated by opaque white partitions to prevent oral or visual-cue communication among panelists. Each booth was equipped with a coded sample tray, palate cleansers, and evaluation forms. The testing area was maintained at 22±2°C, had neutral gray walls, and was provided with uniform (∼1,000 lux) fluorescent white lighting to avoid color distortion. Background noise and external odors were minimized through controlled ventilation.

To isolate and identify the intrinsic sensory properties, two coded patties－PBM and CBB－were served as sandwiches in identical plain burger buns, without adding any condiments, sauces, tomatoes, or vegetables. Samples were presented monadically and in a randomized order on identical white plates labeled with random three-digit codes. Each burger patty was cooked immediately before serving and presented warm (60±2°C) to ensure uniform temperature and freshness. The individual panelists remained seated within their respective booths throughout the test. They were allowed to cleanse their palates with water and unsalted crackers in between servings.

The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines and was pre-approved by the Dissertations and Theses Ethics Committee of The University of Jordan (Protocol code: 0633999; Date: 7 December 2024). All participants provided informed consent before participation in the sensory evaluation.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp.). Physicochemical parameters and sensory evaluation scores were statistically analyzed by employing the Student’s t-test. Data was considered statistically significant at P<0.05. The results are presented as the mean±standard deviation.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

pH and cooking loss

Table 1 presents the pH and cooking loss (%) values of the PBM and CBB samples. The pH did not differ significantly (P>0.05), with both showing identical values (5.66 vs. 5.65). This result indicates that despite variations in ingredient composition, PBM and CBB fell within a slightly acidic range (pH 5.5－6.0), which is typical for cooked patties and contributes to microbiological safety and desirable flavor development (Hughes et al., 2014). In contrast, cooking loss varied significantly (P<0.05) between the two types. It was much higher in CBB (21.92%) compared with PBM (7.25%), reflecting a substantially enhanced release of water and fats during frying. This outcome is consistent with the structural behavior of beef muscle proteins, which contract upon heating and expel moisture and lipids (Aaslyng et al., 2003; Tornberg, 2005). In contrast, the reduced cooking loss with PBM can be attributed not only to the intrinsic water-binding capacity of legume seeds but also to the functional role of chia seed mucilage and CMC. Chia mucilage forms a gel-like network with excellent water-holding and thickening properties, reducing drip loss during cooking (Muñoz et al., 2012; Capitani et al., 2012). Similarly, CMC, a hydrocolloid widely used in meat analogs, enhances emulsion stability, helps the protein matrix in retaining water, and improves texture under thermal processing (Jiménez-Colmenero, 2007; Youssef and Barbut, 2011).

Together, these results highlight that while the pH of the two formulations stabilized, the incorporation of hydrocolloids such as chia seeds and CMC provided PBM with a distinct technological advantage by minimizing cooking loss, thereby improving yield and juiciness retention during processing.

Proximate composition

Table 2 presents the proximate composition and energy values of PBM and CBB. All parameters differed significantly (P<0.05) between the two types. The ash content was greater in PBM (11.24%) compared with CBB (4.20%), highlighting that legumes such as lupin and lentils are rich in minerals. Legumes are widely recognized as vital dietary sources of iron, zinc, magnesium, and potassium, which contribute to the enhanced nutritional profile of plant-based products (Duranti et al., 2008; Lucas et al., 2015). In contrast, CBB had a remarkably greater moisture content (55.70%) than PBM (48.47%). Higher moisture levels are typical of fresh beef patties, where enhanced water retention contributes to juiciness but may also predispose products to more prominent cooking losses and reduced stability during storage (Keeton, 1994). The relatively lower moisture content of PBM may help better in maintaining structural integrity during processing, consistent with the reduced cooking loss observed in this study.

Concerning macronutrients, lipid and protein contents were significantly (P<0.05) higher in CBB (19.08% and 20.73%, respectively) compared with PBM (6.38% and 12.15%, respectively). These values reflect the inherent composition of beef, which is naturally protein- and fat-rich, and explains its greater caloric density. By contrast, PBM exhibited markedly higher fiber (8.89%) and carbohydrate (12.88%) levels compared with CBB (0.019% and 0.27%, respectively). These findings are consistent with an earlier study showing that PBM typically provides more dietary fiber and complex carbohydrates but fewer proteins and lipids than CBBs (Graça et al., 2015). The elevated fiber content of PBM derives from lupin and lentils, which supply soluble and insoluble fractions (Bouchenak and Lamri-Senhadji, 2013; Lucas et al., 2015). Such enrichment has been linked to improved satiety, glycemic regulation, and gut health, offering benefits not presented by conventional meat (Kyriakopoulou et al., 2021). Carbohydrates, meanwhile, contribute to energy supply and play functional roles in structure and binding, reinforcing the physicochemical suitability of legumes for PBM formulations.

Overall, these compositional variations were reflected in energy values, with CBB (255.71 kcal) showing a remarkably higher calorie contribution compared with PBM (157.51 kcal). While CBB is rich in proteins, fats, and energy, PBM is enriched with fiber, carbohydrates, and ash, aligning with global dietary recommendations that promote legume-derived proteins as sustainable and health-oriented alternatives (Willett et al., 2019). It should be noted that proximate composition was determined on the cooked patties, and therefore, the values reported reflect post-cooking composition. The substantial cooking loss observed with CBB (21.92%) indicates considerable water and fat release during frying, which likely reduces its actual calorie content on a ready-to-eat basis. In contrast, the markedly lower cooking loss with PBM (7.25%) suggests better moisture and lipid retention, thereby minimizing nutrient and energy decline following cooking.

Color parameters

Table 3 presents the color parameters－L*, a*, and b*－of PBM and CBB. Significant (P<0.05) variations in lightness (L*) and yellowness (b*) between the two types were observed, unlike redness (a*). PBM exhibited significantly (P<0.05) elevated L* values (43.05) than CBB (42.07), indicating a much lighter surface appearance. Such a difference can be attributed to the inclusion of ingredients derived from plants, such as lupin and lentils, which yield a paler matrix compared with the denser muscle structure and enhanced myoglobin content of beef (Mancini and Hunt, 2005; Bohrer, 2019).

Similarly, b* values were significantly (P<0.05) greater in PBM (12.75) compared with CBB (11.75), reflecting increased yellowness. This phenomenon may be associated with the presence of carotenoids derived from lentils and other legumes (Tosh and Yada, 2010; Bouzari et al., 2015), which impart yellow-orange tones. In contrast, beef patties generally exhibited lower b* values due to the absence of such pigments and apronounced protein-lipid browning induced during cooking.

Redness (a*) values were comparable between the two types (3.75 vs. 3.65) and did not vary significantly (P>0.05), suggesting that the PBM effectively mimicked the characteristic red tones of meat. In such a case, redness in PBM was largely contributed by betalains－betacyanins and betaxanthins－derived from beetroot, which produce a vivid red-purple color (Clifford et al., 2015). By contrast, the redness in beef originates from myoglobin and its heat-induced derivatives－oxymyoglobin, metmyoglobin, and hemichromes－that determine the appearance of cooked meat (King and Whyte, 2006).

Overall, these results indicate that PBM formulations can achieve native meat-like coloration through a combination of natural pigments (carotenoids, betalains) and processing strategies, enabling them to replicate consumer-expected visual attributes of burgers.

Sensory evaluation

Fig. 1 presents the PBM and CBB sensory evaluation scores. No significant (P>0.05) differences were identified between the two types across all the attributes tested, including overall acceptability, appearance and color, flavor, tenderness, and juiciness. These findings demonstrate that the PBM formulation was well accepted by panelists and performed similarly to CBB across key sensory dimensions.

Appearance and color scores were similar between PBM (6.84) and CBB (7.09). These outcomes corresponded with the instrument-based color measurements (Table 3), which showed significantly (P<0.05) elevated lightness (L*) and yellowness (b*) in PBM, while redness (a*) did not vary between the two formulations. The comparable a* values (3.75 vs. 3.65) are particularly critical, as redness is a primary indicator of “meat-like” visual appeal (Mancini and Hunt, 2005; King and Whyte, 2006). The slightly elevated L* and b* values in PBM likely reflected the influence of legume- and beetroot-derived pigments, contributing to a lighter and more yellow surface that was acceptable to the panelists.

Flavor scores were marginally higher with CBB (6.88) compared with PBM (6.25), although the variation was statistically insignificant (P>0.05). This trend can be explained by compositional differences: CBB had a higher lipid content (19.08%) (Table 1), which contributed to the release of volatile compounds during cooking, enhancing flavor perception (Elmore et al., 2004). PBM, while demonstrating lower fat contents (6.38%), ingredients such as mushrooms, legumes, and seasonings provided umami notes and balanced flavor. Flavor remains one of the most challenging attributes for PBM analogs to match those of native meat, primarily due to the lack of meat-derived volatiles (Curtain and Grafenauer, 2019).

Tenderness ratings were comparable between PBM (7.03) and CBB (6.84), suggesting that the panelists perceived the two types as similarly soft and palatable. These results can be explained by cooking losses and composition-based properties. CBB exhibited a higher cooking loss (21.92%) than PBM (7.25%) (Table 2), which is typically associated with a much firmer texture due to enhanced protein shrinkage and water release in beef (Tornberg, 2005; Hughes et al., 2014). In contrast, PBM benefitted from the functional contributions of textured soy protein, lupin, and lentils, which form a cohesive matrix producing good bite-related characteristics. The inclusion of hydrocolloids such as chia mucilage and CMC further supported structural integrity and reduced toughness, thereby aligning tenderness perception more similarly with that of beef (Youssef and Barbut, 2011).

Juiciness scores also did not differ significantly (P>0.05) between PBM (5.63) and CBB (6.28). This finding again correlates with the cooking loss results: PBM’s markedly lower cooking loss meant elevated water retention during frying, which probably contributed to its acceptable juiciness (Aaslyng et al., 2003). While CBB may be slightly more juicy due to fat melting and flavor-component release, PBM compensated for this effect via its high moisture-binding capacity derived from legume fibers and hydrated chia seeds (Capitani et al., 2012). This observation highlights the ability of PBM formulations to approximate succulence even with lower fat levels.

Overall, the acceptability scores were similar between PBM (6.44) and CBB (6.75). Given that appearance, tenderness, and juiciness evaluation results were all comparable, it is unsurprising that the generalized acceptability of both products did not vary markedly. The slightly lower flavor score with PBM did not translate into a decline in acceptability, suggesting that consumers may equally prioritize texture and appearance with flavor when judging burgers (Sha and Xiong, 2020).

In conclusion, this study developed a novel PBM using lupin and lentils and compared its properties with a commercial CBB. Compositional differences were obvious: CBB was richer in proteins, fats, and energy, while PBM provided more fiber, carbohydrates, and ash contents, supporting nutritional profiles consistent with sustainable dietary recommendations.

Technique-wise, PBM exhibited significantly (P<0.05) lower cooking loss than CBB, largely due to the water-binding and gelling capacities of legumes, chia mucilage, and CMC, which enhanced moisture retention and yield. Color analysis showed that PBM matched CBB in redness (a*), a vital marker of “meat-like” appearance, while displaying higher lightness (L*) and yellowness (b*) attributable to plant-derived pigments. These results were consistent with sensory evaluations, where appearance and color scores were comparable across types.

Sensory attributes, including tenderness, juiciness, and overall acceptability, did not vary markedly between PBM and CBB. Although CBB exhibited marginally elevated flavor scores, this trend was insignificant and highlights an area that requires further optimization for application in PBM formulations. Overall, the integration of lupin, lentils, and hydrocolloids produced a PBM with competitive nutritional and sensory quality, reinforcing its potential as a sustainable alternative to conventional beef products.
