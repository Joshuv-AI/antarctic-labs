Follicular Lesions in Pediatric Dermatology: A Comprehensive Review and Approach Based on Clinical, Dermoscopic, and Histopathological Evaluation and Treatment
Authors: Shreya K. Gowda, Rahul Mahajan, Maitreyee Panda, Biswanath Behera, Madhusmita Sethy, Pavithra Ayyanar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pediatric dermatoses presenting as follicular papules are common entities encountered in clinical practice. These are a heterogeneous group of disorders of infectious or noninfectious origin that commonly present as regularly spaced, small papules with or without perifollicular inflammation. Inflammatory causes include follicular eczema, pityriasis rubra pilaris, lichen nitidus, follicular seborrheic dermatitis, keratosis pilaris and its variants, keratosis circumscripta, follicular psoriasis, lichen spinulosus, follicular lichen planus, follicular mucinosis, perforating folliculitis, follicular porokeratosis, and follicular dermographism. These conditions may arise due to filaggrin mutations, keratinization defects, autoimmunity, or microbial triggers. Nutritional causes include phrynoderma and scurvy, while connective tissue diseases such as dermatomyositis and chronic cutaneous lupus erythematosus are autoimmune. Additional categories include infectious, genetic, hormonal, environmental, frictional, malignant, nevus-related, iatrogenic, and idiopathic entities. An early and accurate diagnosis is essential for proper management and reducing disease-associated apprehension in children and/or their parents. This review attempts to describe the role of history taking and clinical examination, including morphology, location, pattern of distribution, and associated features, while dealing with a child with follicular-based papules. In addition, it delineates the role of dermoscopy and pathological examination in reaching a correct diagnosis. Furthermore, it describes the available treatment options and disease course of various follicle-based dermatoses.

In the pediatric age group, folliculocentric dermatoses are a common clinical presentation. In many instances, varied disorders may present with similar clinical manifestations. Therefore, an accurate diagnosis is of utmost importance from the treatment point of view. Data on the approach to follicular papules are scarce, and hence this review aims to outline the approach based on a detailed history, clinical examination, dermoscopy, and histopathological evaluation. A detailed literature search using “PubMed” and “Google Scholar” was performed. The search terms used were (‘follicular papules’ OR ‘digitate keratosis’ OR ‘spiny keratosis’ OR ‘follicular spicules’ OR ‘acneiform lesions’ OR ‘follicular pustules’) AND (‘pediatric’ OR ‘children’ OR ‘infants’ OR ‘neonates’). Articles were screened; duplicate records, articles not in English, and data that could not be retrieved were excluded from the analysis.

Etiology

A detailed history regarding possible disease etiology and associations aids in reaching a diagnosis [Supplementary Table 1]. In pediatric patients with atopic predisposition, conditions like follicular eczema, keratosis pilaris (KP), and lichen spinulosus should be considered in the differential diagnosis, particularly since follicular eczema and KP often exacerbate during winter months.[345]

Nutritional deficiencies, especially of vitamins A, B, C, and E, essential fatty acids, or protein-energy malnutrition, may manifest as phrynoderma (follicular keratotic papules) or scurvy.[7] In post-transplant patients, graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) should be considered when acral or central follicular papules appear.[8] In a patient with a history suggestive of tuberculosis, the suspicion of lichen scrofulosorum should be heightened in the presence of follicular and perifollicular papules.[9]

Genetic disorders like ichthyosis follicularis atrichia with photophobia (IFAP) syndrome (X-linked, membrane-bound transcriptor factor peptidase site 2 mutation), follicular Dowling-Degos disease (autosomal dominant, keratin 5, protein O-fucosyltransferase 1, presenilin enhancer, gamma-secretase subunit mutation, and protein O glucosyltransferase 1), atrichia with papular lesions (APL)(hairless gene mutation), and monilethrix (desmoglein 4/keratin81–86 gene mutations) present with distinctfollicular patterns.[121314] Follicular porokeratosis has been associated with abnormalities in keratinization due to defects in the cholesterol pathway.[15]

Connective tissue disorders (CTDs) such as dermatomyositis (DM) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) can also present as follicular papules, with a predominance of telangiectasia observed in the former case.[16] Follicular mucinosis can manifest as primary (idiopathic) or secondary to atopic dermatitis, SLE, drugs, mycosis fungoides (MF), and others.[4] Other notable causes include follicular urticarial dermographism (traction-induced wheal), perforatingfolliculitis (linked to diabetes/renal disease), and contact dermatitis (triggered by cosmetics or textiles).[171819]

Viral etiologies presenting as follicular lesions include measles, follicular pityriasis rosea (PR), trichodysplasia spinulosa, and follicular spicules linked to human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).[20212223] Fungal causes include eosinophilic pustular folliculitis (EPF), pityrosporum folliculitis, and Majocchi granuloma.[242526] Cutibaterium acnes-induced follicular spicules and Pseudomonas folliculitis are caused by bacterial infection.[2728] Parasitic etiologies include follicular larva migrans[29] and facial spinulate demodicosis.[2930] Among bacterial etiologies, Pseudomonas folliculitis has been linked with whirlpool and hot tub usage.[28] Necrotizing infundibular crystalline folliculitis has been associated with both fungal and bacterial etiologies.[31] Meanwhile, pseudofolliculitis is due to traumatic hair removal practices.[32] Following radiation therapy at the sites of irradiation, post-irradiation folliculitis has been described.[33]

Morphology and special characteristics

The morphology of lesions can be subdivided into keratotic follicular papules, digitate follicular papules, follicular lichenoid papules, erythematous follicular papules with/without scaling, comedonal papules, urticated follicular papules, and follicular pustules. In addition, these disorders can be further segregated by the following features: arrangement of the papules (grouped or discrete), associated perifollicular pigmentary alteration (perifollicular hyperpigmentation or perifollicular hypopigmentation), hair shaft abnormalities, perifollicular erythema, and perifollicular petechiae. The flowchart [Supplementary Flowchart 2] depicts the diagnostic approach based on the clinical morphology. A few follicular conditions are associated with nonfollicular lesions, including lichen scrofulosorum, multiple minute digitate hyperkeratosis, lichen nitidus, and granular parakeratosis.[33] Follicular nevus lipomatosus cutaneous superficialis presents as follicular papules and is extremely rare.[4243] Follicular dermographism is a transient eruption. KP and its variants, such as ulerythema ophryogenes, usually heal with atrophic scarring, while monilethrix improves with age.

Bedside Tests

In cases of folliculotropic MF, mucinorrhea, that is, fluid extrusion from follicular openings, may be observed.[4] Potassium hydroxide (KOH) preparation, which can rapidly identify fungal elements, spores, and hyphae, confirms pityrosporum folliculitis, while hyaline hyphae suggest Majocchi granuloma, and Demodex mites point to demodicosis.[2630] Gram staining further refines the diagnosis by revealing eosinophils in eosinophilic pustular folliculitis, Gram-negative bacilli in Pseudomonas folliculitis, or Gram-positive bacilli in Cutibacterium acnes infections. Next, Wood’s lamp examination can provide additional clues, with yellow-green fluorescence indicating pityrosporum folliculitis, green fluorescence suggesting Pseudomonas infection, and orange-red fluorescence (from coproporphyrin) pointing to Cutibacterium acnes.[2425262728] For suspected follicular contact dermatitis, patch testing can confirm allergen triggers. The ink test helps visualize the characteristic groove in porokeratosis, while a dermographometer can elicit follicular urticarial dermographism with firm stroking.[1718] Finally, a biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing ambiguous cases.

Histopathology

Histopathology is the gold standard diagnostic tool in many of these follicle-based dermatoses; however, overlapping features can be observed. Therefore, careful analysis of various pathological features and clinicopathological correlation are the cornerstones in making the correct diagnosis. The first step is to recognize the reaction pattern, which can be psoriasiform, lichenoid, spongiotic, granulomatous, transepidermal elimination, coronoid lamella, epidermolytic hyperkeratosis, and acantholytic dyskeratosis. At times, mixed reaction patterns can be observed. The second step is to look for associated pathological features such as parakeratosis, acanthosis, follicular dilation and plugging, type of exocytosis, type of granuloma, perifollicular fibrosis, perifollicular hemorrhage, flame figures, and perifollicular and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates [Figure 5a-e]. Lastly, additional pathological clues should be looked for to support a particular diagnosis: atrophy of the sebaceous gland in phrynoderma and keratosis pilaris, claw and ball appearance in follicular lichen nitidus, eosinophilic crystalline deposits in necrotizing infundibular crystalline folliculitis, perifollicular xanthomatosis in Fox- Fordyce disease, mucin in follicular mucinosis, and atypical lymphocytes in follicular MF. Furthermore, special stains such as periodic acid Schiff, Gomori methenamine silver stain, acid-fast, Gram stain, reticulin, Alcian blue, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) markers, as in MF, need to be performed. Evaluation of systemic features

A detailed general and systemic examination, including required investigations, is paramount to support or refute a particular diagnosis and treat it appropriately. The associated features of ocular involvement (keratoconjunctivitis sicca, nocturnal blindness, and fundoscopy) and signs of malnutrition support the diagnosis of phrynoderma.[6] In patients suspected of having scurvy, spongy and hypertrophic gums with bleeding, painful, swollen knees, scorbutic rosary, epistaxis, and radiological features support the diagnosis.[7] An extensive malignancy screening, such as complete blood count, serum creatinine, liver function tests, serum electrophoresis and free light chains, cancer antigen 15-3 and 19-9, carcinoembryonic antigen 125, chest X-ray, and ultrasonography of abdomen and pelvis, is required in patients suspected of having multiple minute digitate hyperkeratosis.[33] Screening for immunosuppression, such as malignancy, drug history, serology for HIV, and hepatitis B and C, is required for follicular spicules.[23] Workup for tuberculosis (Mantoux test, interferon-gamma release assay, chest X-ray, ultrasound of abdomen and pelvis) is required in patients suspected of lichen scrofulosorum.[9] Serum angiotensin-converting enzyme level, Mantoux test, serum calcium level, and ocular, gastrointestinal, and cardiac evaluations are needed in patients with suspected follicular sarcoidosis.[45] Severe IFAP is associated with ocular, neurological, renal, cardiac, and gastrointestinal involvement, such as microcephaly, seizures, intellectual disability, frontal bossing, hypotonia, Hirschsprung disease, and megacolon.[10] Folliculotropic MF may have associated lymphadenopathy or metastasize to involve internal organs.[4] In cases of follicular papules secondary to CTDs, a detailed evaluation of all systems according to symptoms, along with antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and ANA immunoblot, is needed.[16]

Treatment and course of the diseases

Treatment of a child with follicle-based dermatoses requires making the parent understand various treatment options and their possible side effects, along with educating them about the disease course and prognosis. Disseminated recurrent infundibulofolliculitisis considered a benign, self-limiting condition, but supplementation with vitamins A and E usually show faster resolution of lesions.[36] Classical juvenile PRP typically resolves within 1–2 years, whereas the atypical juvenile form follows a chronic, prolonged course that may persist for 15–20 years or even lifelong. Acitretin can accelerate disease clearance. In contrast, circumscribed PRP shows a more variable and unpredictable disease course.[34] Lichen nitidus has an unpredictable course, and there are reports of spontaneous resolution within 1 year.[35] Pityrosporumfolliculitis has a waxing and waning course, depending on the patient’s activities, time of the year, and other factors. Pityrosporum folliculitis will often worsen with traditional acne therapy and dramatically responds to antifungal therapy.[25] KP rarely heals with scarring due to manipulation; improvement is noted with topical keratolytics.[5] Resolution occurs with nutritional supplementation in phrynoderma.[6] Treatment of multiple minute digitate hyperkeratosis is often unsuccessful; however, various topical keratolytics and topical and oral retinoids have been reported to show some improvement.[33] Trichodysplasia spinulosa is asymptomatic, rarely pruritic, and treatment includes reduction of immunosuppression if feasible, and it responds to topical cidofovir and oral antivirals (valganciclovir).[22] Darier disease persists for life and has a relapsing and remitting course, with more severe disease requiring oral retinoids.[14] Hyperkeratotic spicules linked to myeloma resolve when chemotherapy is initiated. Among tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α inhibitors, etanercept is FDA-approved for the treatment of pediatric psoriasis in children 4 years and older. Secukinumab and ustekinumab are approved for pediatric psoriasis beginning at 6 years of age. For atopic dermatitis, FDA-approved Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors include topical ruxolitinib for children older than 2 years, and oral abrocitinib and upadacitinib for those 12 years and above. The tetracycline class of antibiotics is generally considered safe for use in children older than 8 years for folliculitis.[14233435363738394041424344454647484950515253545556575859]

Conclusion

Diagnosing a pediatric patient presenting with follicle-based papules can be challenging due to diverse etiology and, at times, similar presentation. An early and accurate diagnosis is warranted for appropriate treatment and counseling of parents about the disease course and prognosis. This review comprehensively describes the approach to a case with follicular papules. It emphasizes the role of detailed history-taking and in-depth clinical examination in narrowing down the differentials and further highlights the role of dermoscopy and pathological examination in reaching a diagnosis. In addition, it briefly describesthe treatment options and prognosis of the various follicle-based diseases.
