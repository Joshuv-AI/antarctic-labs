Abstract

Impacts-relevant Earth system data refers to observational and ESM data that are downscaled,
debiased, validated, and provisioned for use by decision-makers. Impacts-relevant Earth system
data is essential for mitigation and adaptation planning across a variety of regions and sectors. A
vast number of these data products have emerged in recent years, which has led to confusion
among stakeholders and scientists as to the best product to use. With no standard evaluation
protocol available for these products, the decision on which product to use was sometimes made
because it was pragmatic rather than the best product to use. This project sought to develop
foundational capabilities around impacts-relevant data products that would support more
informed selection and application of these products. This work has been immensely successful,
driving several academic publications and supported the development of a community of practice
around impacts-relevant data products.

Background

Impacts-relevant Earth system data (also known as decision-relevant Earth system data) include
a variety of data products that are available at high-resolution and provide variables that assist in
adaptation planning and a broader understanding of the statistics of weather. Most of these
products are generated through the use of statistical downscaling (SD), which prescribes
empirically developed relationships between large-scale variables, or dynamical downscaling
(DD), which uses a regional climate model to simulate the meteorology at local scales. Newer
techniques have emerged that overcome issues with stationarity, such as hybrid-downscaling
products, which train empirical methods on dynamically downscaled climate data. Further,
several new artificial intelligence / machine learning (AI/ML) methods have recently emerged
that promise more realizations of possible weather with far less computing power and time
needed. In each of these cases, the basic approach is depicted in Figure 1.

Numerous academic groups, national labs, and commercial entities have produced impacts-
relevant Earth system products intended for wider use (e.g., EPRI, 2026). Unfortunately, with no
common standards for evaluating these products, and no broadly accepted authoritative source
on the best product for each need, end-users must instead rely on pragmatism to choose which
product to use, rather than choosing the best product for their need. This project aimed to
develop both novel capabilities and a community of support in the broader community that could
address this unmet need. Put succinctly, the goal of this project was to quantify and explain the
ability of climate models and associated impacts-relevant Earth system data products to represent
high-impact extreme weather events over the contiguous United States.

Figure 1: Impacts-relevant data production methods that are used in practice. * includes low-
resolution, high-resolution, ultra-high-resolution, and regionally refined simulations. ** artificial
intelligence and machine learning methods could be used for these methods as well.

Over the project’s three years we have addressed six tasks: First, the development of standard
evaluation metrics for impacts-relevant climate data; second, the development of a novel suite of
atmospheric river metrics; third, the development of novel metrics for precipitation feature
analysis; fourth, the development of novel metrics for assessing co-variances between
temperature and precipitation; fifth, the development of a dashboard for interactive examination
of impacts-relevant climate data; and sixth, the establishment of a community of practice around
impacts-relevant climate data that will continue beyond the conclusion of this project.

Efforts to Standardize Evaluation

The first task addressed in the project was to develop a standard suite of evaluation metrics, and
subsequently apply those metrics to selected impacts-relevant data products. Two of the most
readily available and cutting-edge products at the time were the LOCalized Analogs (LOCA2)
and Seasonal Trends and Analysis of Residuals empirical statistical downscaling model (STAR-
ESDM). We considered a variety of metrics and consulted with personnel at the National
Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Technical Support Unit (TSU) to select
metrics that were most important to end-users. We subsequently developed software, now
implemented in the PCMDI Metrics Package (PMP; Lee et al., 2024), that could readily evaluate
those products. This code was cross-validated against an analogous implementation in the
TempestExtremes software package (Ullrich et al., 2021). Results from this study were
subsequently published in a U.S. Department of Energy Report (Ullrich, 2023). A snapshot of
the tabulated metrics is depicted in Figure 2.

Figure 2: Tabulated decision-relevant metrics for temperature and precipitation evaluated across
four regions of the contiguous United States. A score of zero indicates that the model product
matches with the observation data. Dark shading indicates that the interquartile range of the
metric within that region doesn’t include zero, suggesting significant deviation from
observations.

A Suite of Atmospheric River (AR) Evaluation Metrics

The second task addressed the development of metrics for tracked atmospheric features. Since
earlier efforts had already developed metrics for tropical cyclones (e.g., Zarzycki et al., 2021),
we instead focused on developing a new metrics package for atmospheric river events.
Atmospheric rivers (ARs) are extreme wintertime storms that bring significant precipitation to
many areas of the midlatitudes, including California. Our novel evaluation protocol featured
evaluation metrics for both ARs over the ocean and landfalling ARs. The software could also be
applied using any of the many available tools for tracking of atmospheric river objects. Figure 3,
which is reproduced from Dong et al. (2025), depicts the workflow adopted by this evaluation
package. The evaluation package has been implemented in the PMP.

Figure 3: The AR metric tool processes tagged ARs from user-selected detection methods and
optional Earth system data. It subsets data into user-defined regions (ocean basins and landfall
areas) and applies statistical tools to analyze AR characteristics. (from Dong et al., 2025).

Evaluation of Precipitating Feature Character

Most SD products only provide precipitation and temperature at daily frequency, with no other
meteorological quantities that could be used to assess whether or not the model produces the
right answer for the right reasons; namely, that its outputs are consistent with physical laws.
While basic gridpoint statistics provide one means of evaluating model performance, these
statistics contain no information about spatial coherence, which is another important physical
property. To address this gap, along with collaborators, we proposed and developed new metrics
that evaluated the size and structure of precipitation objects obtained from basic feature tracking
methods. This work is recently published in Chen et al. (2026). We showed that many SD
methods produced precipitation objects that were unphysically large, and closer to the resolution
of the driving model. However, the LOCA2 method generally performed well because of its
analog approach that leverages spatially consistent fields. Compared with SD methods, DD
methods were more consistent with observed features and showed consistent improvement with
resolution. Some results from this study are depicted in Figure 4.

Figure 4: A comprehensive view of DD and SD performance on precipitating features. The four
corners of each diamond indicate four features: (left) total precipitation, (top) average feature
area, (right) peak precipitation intensity, and (bottom) spatial concentration of precipitation
intensity. Perkins score is used to assess the match between these impacts-relevant data products
and the PRISM precipitation product. The center of each diamond denotes a Perkins score of 0
(perfect mismatch), while the four corners denote a Perkins score of 1 (perfect match). Model
resolution is depicted along the x axis, and driving reanalysis / model set is given along the y
axis. (from Chen et al., 2026)

Evaluation of Hydroclimatic Co-variances

Statistical downscaling methods often downscale temperature and precipitation independently of
one another, which raises the concern of inconsistencies arising between these two fields.
However, the relationship between temperature and precipitation is complicated and cannot be
teased apart by looking at simple relationships, such as correlation. Consequently, we developed
a novel evaluation approach that was based on work by Zhang and Boos (2023); specifically, this
paper noted that temperatures will generally rise until convection is triggered, leading to an
increase in the likelihood of precipitation following an extreme heat event. This suggests that the
hottest day of the year should be associated with drier conditions before the temperature
maximum and wetter conditions thereafter. We used this behavior to develop a metric to evaluate
the ability of SD methods to respect co-variances, and extended it to examine expected behavior
from passage of cold frontal systems. We found despite no explicitly enforced requirement for
co-variance, both SD and DD models tended to exhibit physically consistent behavior, and
further generally showed consistent enhancement into the future. Some results from this study
are depicted in Figure 5.

Figure 5: A depiction of temperature and precipitation on the days leading up to and following
the warmest day of each year from several model simulations. The assessed models generally
show consistency under this metric. (from Baek et al., 2024)

Long Term Impact: Establishing a Community of Practice

Perhaps one of the most significant deliverables from this project relates to the ongoing effort to
establish a community of practice around impacts-relevant data products that would allow this
work to be carried on after this project’s end. Our work to establish this community is described
in Ullrich et al. (2026), along with a landscape analysis around impacts-relevant data products
and the large set of remaining challenges to ensure their appropriate use. This work brought
together players from all of the major U.S. agencies and combined their perspectives to concisely
summarize the major challenges of the coming decade. Additionally, because of the efforts
undertaken in this project, we have established a monthly telecon entitled the “Downscaling
Workstream” which features speakers and discussion on topics related to the production of
impacts-relevant data products. Since this telecon series is a grassroots effort, we expect it to
continue for the foreseeable future.

Mission Impact

This project supports LLNL’s mission to build U.S. energy system resilience by developing
novel impacts-relevant methods for evaluating Earth system data products, and quantifying our
confidence in their representation of disruptive weather events. This work further supported our
national security, the DOE mission and other agencies (including DOW and DOI) by identifying
risks and vulnerabilities to national infrastructure from climate and weather hazards, and
characterizing the uncertainty around those hazards.
