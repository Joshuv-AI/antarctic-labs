The Impact of Baseline Negative Emotions on Postoperative Quality of Life in Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis Patients: A 2‐Year Follow‐Up Study
Authors: Ying Yang, Haoran Zhang, Xue Tian, Yiwei Zhao, Xiaohan Ye, Shengru Wang, Jianguo Zhang, Yaping Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveAdolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a three‐dimensional spinal deformity that develops during puberty without a clear etiology. Beyond physical manifestations, AIS severely impacts adolescents' psychological and social well‐being, leading to anxiety, depression, and low self‐esteem. While advancements in surgical techniques have enhanced objective outcomes, existing studies on AIS have primarily focused on objective indices, with limited attention to the long‐term impact of preoperative negative emotions on patient‐reported subjective quality of life.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study. A total of 112 eligible AIS patients who underwent posterior spinal correction surgery between April and August 2023 were enrolled. Inclusion criteria included confirmed AIS, completion of 2‐year follow‐up, and informed consent; exclusion criteria included missing imaging/questionnaire data, comorbid psychiatric/neurological diseases, or prior spinal surgery. Patients were grouped using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) administered on admission. Quality of life was assessed preoperatively and 2 years postoperatively using the Scoliosis Research Society‐22 (SRS‐22, evaluating self‐image, mental health, pain, function, treatment satisfaction) and Short Form 36 Health Survey (SF‐36, assessing 8 physical and mental health dimensions). Statistical analysis was performed via SPSS, using independent t‐tests, paired t‐tests, Mann–Whitney U test, and chi‐square test. p < 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsThere were no significant differences in baseline characteristics (age, gender, BMI, surgical parameters, scoliosis type, preoperative/postoperative Cobb angles) between the two groups (all p > 0.05). Preoperatively, SRS‐22 and SF‐36 scores showed no inter‐group differences (all p > 0.05). Postoperatively, the Negative Emotion Group had significantly lower scores in SRS‐22 mental health (3.9 ± 0.3 vs. 4.5 ± 0.2) and treatment satisfaction (4.0 ± 0.3 vs. 4.6 ± 0.7), as well as SF‐36 general health (68.6 ± 6.4 vs. 79.7 ± 13.3), role‐emotional (61.3 ± 9.3 vs. 70.8 ± 9.7), and mental health (61.8 ± 14.3 vs. 68.9 ± 10.7) (all p < 0.05); no inter‐group differences were observed in physical function‐related dimensions. Both groups showed significant improvements in physical function‐related dimensions postoperatively. The Non‐Negative Emotion Group also exhibited significant improvements in SRS‐22 self‐image/pain and SF‐36 bodily pain (all p < 0.05), while the Negative Emotion Group showed no significant improvements in these dimensions.ConclusionsPreoperative anxiety and depression do not affect the recovery of physical function in AIS patients after spinal correction surgery but significantly impede improvements in subjective quality of life dimensions, including mental health and treatment satisfaction. These findings highlight the need to integrate psychological assessment and targeted interventions into the perioperative management of AIS. Such a patient‐centered approach will help optimize both physical and psychological outcomes, ultimately achieving comprehensive rehabilitation for AIS adolescents.

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS), defined as a three‐dimensional spinal deformity with a Cobb angle ≥ 10° that develops during puberty without a clear underlying cause, is one of the most common musculoskeletal conditions affecting adolescents worldwide [1]. Epidemiological studies have shown that the incidence of AIS in adolescents is approximately 1%–3% [2, 3, 4]. Beyond the obvious physical manifestations, such as shoulder asymmetry, trunk deviation, or rib humping, AIS exerts a profound impact on the psychological and social well‐being of affected adolescents. During a critical period of identity formation, body image development, and social integration, the visible spinal deformity often leads to self‐consciousness, low self‐esteem, social withdrawal, and even avoidance of physical activities. These factors increase the risk of developing emotional distress, including anxiety and depression [5, 6].

Surgical correction remains the gold standard treatment for moderate‐to‐severe AIS or progressive deformities that cannot be controlled with non‐surgical measures such as bracing. The primary goal of surgery is to halt deformity progression, restore spinal alignment, and improve physical function, with the aim of enhancing the long‐term quality of life [7, 8]. Over the past few decades, advancements in surgical techniques, perioperative care, and spinal cord monitoring have significantly improved the safety and efficacy of surgery, leading to excellent radiological outcomes and reduced complication rates [9, 10]. However, while most clinical studies on AIS have focused on objective outcomes (e.g., radiographic deformity correction, improvement in pulmonary function, or complication rates), less attention has been paid to subjective patient‐reported outcomes, particularly the impact of preoperative psychological factors on postoperative quality of life.

A growing body of evidence in other spinal conditions (lumbar spinal stenosis, cervical radiculopathy, degenerative disc disease) has highlighted the critical role of preoperative mental health in determining postoperative outcomes. For instance, studies have shown that preoperative depression and anxiety are associated with slower functional recovery, increased postoperative pain perception, lower patient satisfaction, and even higher long‐term reoperation rates [11, 12, 13]. In the context of AIS, however, research exploring the relationship between preoperative emotional status and postoperative quality of life remains surprisingly limited. Existing studies on AIS and mental health have primarily focused on the prevalence of anxiety and depression in the preoperative period rather than their impact on surgical outcomes [14]. This gap in knowledge is particularly concerning given that adolescents with AIS are a unique population: they are not only recovering from a major surgical procedure but also navigating the psychosocial challenges of adolescence, making them potentially more vulnerable to the long‐term effects of preoperative emotional distress.

Furthermore, the few existing studies that have touched on this topic have been limited by small sample sizes, short follow‐up periods, or the use of non‐validated assessment tools for measuring anxiety, depression, or quality of life [15, 16]. For example, some studies have used generic mental health scales or non‐specialized quality of life questionnaires, which may not fully capture the unique concerns of AIS patients [17]. In contrast, the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) is a validated and widely used tool specifically designed to screen for anxiety and depression in healthcare settings, which avoids the confounding of physical symptoms (e.g., fatigue, sleep disturbances) that may overlap with spinal disorders and emotional distress, making it ideal for assessing the emotional status of patients with AIS [18]. In terms of assessing quality of life, the Scoliosis Research Society‐22 (SRS‐22) questionnaire is a disease‐specific tool tailored for patients with AIS that assesses five key areas of quality of life: self‐image, mental health, pain, function, and treatment satisfaction, thus providing a comprehensive measure of subjective surgical outcomes [19]. In addition, the short form 36 health survey questionnaire (SF‐36) is a health‐related quality of life assessment tool widely used in medicine, public health, and clinical research. It comprehensively measures the individual status in multiple dimensions of physical health and mental health through patient self‐assessment, and is suitable for healthy people and patients with chronic diseases.

Against this backdrop, the present study aimed to address this critical knowledge gap by systematically evaluating the effect of preoperative anxiety and depression on postoperative quality of life in AIS patients. By using the HADS to assess preoperative emotional status and the SRS‐22 and the SF‐36 to measure quality of life both preoperatively and 2 years postoperatively (a key time point for functional recovery and adaptation to surgical changes), we sought to answer two primary questions: (1) Does preoperative anxiety or depression affect the magnitude of improvement in quality of life following AIS surgery? (2) Are patients with preoperative anxiety or depression more likely to have poorer postoperative quality of life compared to those without these emotional disorders? The findings of this study are intended to provide valuable insights for clinical practice, emphasizing the need to integrate psychological assessment and targeted interventions into the perioperative management of AIS patients, ultimately leading to more comprehensive, patient‐centered care that optimizes physical and psychological outcomes.

Methods

Study Population

Inclusion criteria: (1) Diagnosis of AIS confirmed by clinical and radiological examinations; (2) Undergoing posterior spinal deformity correction and fusion surgery; (3) Complete follow‐up data for at least 2 years; (4) Voluntary participation with informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) Missing preoperative or postoperative imaging data; (2) Incomplete quality of life assessment questionnaires; (3) Comorbidity with other psychiatric disorders or neurological diseases; (4) History of previous spinal surgery.

We conducted a preliminary study that recruited 20 patients with AIS. The mean SRS‐22 of the two groups was 4.2 and 4.6, respectively, with an overall standard deviation of 0.4. Based on the results, we used PASS 11.0 software to calculate the sample size. With the level of significance set at 0.05 and the power at 0.95, the calculated sample size was 61. The number of patients in subsequent studies needs to meet this standard.

A total of 112 eligible patients were recruited from our hospital and underwent surgical treatment between April and August 2023. The study was approved by the Peking Union Medical College Hospital Institutional Review Board (K2326), and all patients and their guardians signed informed consent forms.

Emotional Assessment and Grouping

The HADS was used to assess anxiety and depression on the admission day. The scale consists of 14 items, with 7 items for anxiety and 7 for depression. Each item is scored from 0 to 3, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 21 for each subscale. A score ≥ 8 was defined as the presence of clinically significant negative emotions, while a score < 8 indicated no anxiety or depression [20]. Based on the HADS scores, patients were divided into Negative Emotion Group (n = 20) and Non‐Negative Emotion Group (n = 92).

Surgical Procedure

All patients underwent the same surgical protocol performed by the same team. This surgical team specializes in spinal deformity surgery and is qualified to perform the relevant procedures. Patients were placed in the prone position on a spinal surgery table, with spinal cord monitoring electrodes implanted. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered intravenously before surgery. After standard disinfection and draping, a midline incision was made, and paravertebral muscles were dissected subperiosteally to expose the spinous processes, laminae, facet joints, and transverse processes. Pedicle screws were inserted at predetermined levels under fluoroscopic guidance. Osteotomy and release were performed around the apex vertebra. Pre‐bent rods were placed to restore thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis, followed by derotation, rod rotation, and shoulder balance adjustment. Final fluoroscopic confirmation was performed to ensure satisfactory deformity correction and trunk balance before wound closure with absorbable sutures.

Follow‐Up and Outcome Measures

All patients were followed up 2 years postoperatively. The outcome measure was quality of life assessed by the SRS‐22 and SF‐36 questionnaires. SRS‐22 questionnaire includes five domains: self‐image, mental health, pain, function, and treatment satisfaction. Each item is scored from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating better status. SF‐36 questionnaire includes eight dimensions: physical functioning, role‐physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role‐emotional, and mental health. The score range for each dimension is 0 to 100 points (the higher the score, the better the health status). All questionnaires were distributed directly to patients, and data were collected and processed by data collection personnel after completion. The preoperative questionnaire was administered on the first day of hospital admission, while the postoperative questionnaire was completed during outpatient follow‐up visits.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Independent t‐tests were used for comparisons between groups, and paired t‐tests were used for comparisons within groups. Non‐normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range) and analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi‐square test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study systematically explored the impact of preoperative anxiety and depression on postoperative 2‐year quality of life in 112 AIS patients through standardized assessment tools and long‐term follow‐up. The core findings indicate that preoperative negative emotions do not affect the improvement of physical function‐related indicators after surgery, but significantly hinder the recovery of mental health, treatment satisfaction, and other subjective quality of life dimensions. This result fills the gap in existing research on the association between preoperative psychological status and postoperative outcomes in AIS patients and provides important empirical support for optimizing perioperative management of this specific population.

Interpretation of Core Results

The study found that both groups achieved significant improvements in physical function‐related dimensions such as SRS‐22 function score and SF‐36 physical functioning score after surgery, with no significant difference between the two groups. This is consistent with the primary goal of AIS corrective surgery: effectively correcting spinal deformity and restoring physical function. Advances in surgical techniques, standardized perioperative care, and spinal cord monitoring ensure that even patients with preoperative negative emotions can obtain satisfactory anatomical correction and physical function recovery [21, 22, 23]. However, in terms of subjective experience, the Negative Emotion Group showed obvious disadvantages: postoperative mental health and treatment satisfaction scores in SRS‐22 were significantly lower than those in the Non‐Negative Emotion Group, and general health, role‐emotional, and mental health scores in SF‐36 were also worse than those in the Non‐Negative Emotion Group. Moreover, the Non‐Negative Emotion Group achieved comprehensive improvements in self‐image, pain, mental health, and other dimensions, while the Negative Emotion Group showed no significant improvement in these key subjective indicators.

This difference is closely linked to AIS‐specific stressors and the unique psychological vulnerability of adolescents. First, AIS patients have strong preoperative expectations for postoperative physical appearance recovery (e.g., trunk symmetry, shoulder balance, and elimination of rib hump), and preoperative negative emotions amplify the sensitivity to appearance expectation gaps (even with satisfactory radiographical correction), patients with anxiety and depression tend to overfocus on minor residual deformities, leading to poor self‐image and low treatment satisfaction. Second, the pain and discomfort of postoperative rehabilitation training (e.g., orthosis wearing, spinal mobility exercise, and posture correction) act as persistent stressors; patients with preoperative negative emotions have lower pain tolerance and poorer coping ability, which prevents them from actively participating in rehabilitation and further weakens subjective health perception. Third, adolescence is a critical period for body image construction and social integration; the superposition of spinal deformity‐related social distress and surgical stress makes patients with preoperative negative emotions more prone to social avoidance and emotional internalization, which directly impairs the recovery of mental health and role‐emotional function. In contrast, patients without preoperative negative emotions have a more positive psychological foundation, can better adapt to AIS‐specific stressors, and thus achieve comprehensive improvement in quality of life [24, 25].

Connection With Existing Literature

Previous research on AIS has mostly focused on the prevalence of preoperative mental health problems, with limited attention to their impact on postoperative outcomes. A narrative review pointed out that AIS patients have a higher risk of anxiety and depression, but did not clarify the long‐term impact of these emotions on surgical recovery [5]. A cross‐sectional survey only reported the mental health status of AIS patients and their parents, lacking follow‐up data on surgical outcomes [16]. Our study supplements this research gap by confirming that preoperative negative emotions in AIS patients are an independent risk factor for poor postoperative subjective quality of life, which is consistent with the research conclusions in other spinal disease fields. For example, Johnson et al. found that preoperative depression and anxiety hindered functional recovery after rotator cuff repair [11], and Cao et al. reported that postoperative emotional disorders affected the long‐term outcomes of laparoscopic ventral rectopexy [12]. This suggests that the negative impact of preoperative mental health problems on surgical outcomes may be a common rule across different spinal and orthopedic surgeries, while the uniqueness of AIS patients lies in the superposition of adolescent psychological vulnerability and surgical stress, which makes this impact more prominent in subjective experience dimensions.

The advantage of this study is the use of disease‐specific and generalized validated assessment tools. The HADS scale avoids confounding effects of physical symptoms such as fatigue and sleep disturbance, making the assessment of anxiety and depression more accurate for AIS patients with spinal deformity. The SRS‐22 questionnaire targets the core concerns of AIS patients such as self‐image and treatment satisfaction, while the SF‐36 comprehensively reflects physical and mental health status. The combination of the two ensures the comprehensiveness and specificity of quality of life assessment. In addition, the study controlled for confounding factors such as surgical team, surgical protocol, and preoperative Cobb angle, and achieved a 2‐year complete follow‐up, which enhances the reliability and validity of the results.

Study Limitations and Future Research Directions

Despite the above contributions, this study still has certain limitations. First, it is a single‐center study with a sample size of 112, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size limited the subgroup analysis (different types of deformities and different negative emotions), affecting the robustness of the conclusions. Multi‐center, large‐sample studies are needed in the future to verify the conclusions in different regions and populations. Second, the follow‐up period was set at 2 years, which is a key time point for functional recovery, but the long‐term impact of preoperative negative emotions (such as 5 years or more) on quality of life and mental health needs further observation. Third, the study only assessed the presence of preoperative negative emotions, but did not explore the severity of anxiety and depression, nor did it track changes in emotional status during the postoperative recovery period. It is unclear whether postoperative improvement of negative emotions can reverse the adverse impact on quality of life. Fourth, the study did not evaluate the effect of perioperative psychological intervention measures. It is necessary to verify whether targeted interventions such as preoperative psychological counseling, cognitive‐behavioral therapy, and family support can improve the postoperative quality of life of AIS patients with negative emotions. Finally, we only analyzed the functional status before and after surgery, and did not show the difference in the magnitude of change between the two groups.

Future research can focus on the following directions: First, carry out prospective intervention studies to compare the postoperative outcomes of AIS patients with preoperative negative emotions under psychological intervention and conventional care, so as to provide evidence‐based medical evidence for effective intervention measures. Second, explore the biological and psychological mechanisms underlying the impact of preoperative negative emotions on postoperative recovery, such as the role of stress hormones, inflammatory factors, and coping styles in the process. Third, expand the follow‐up time to explore the long‐term trajectory of quality of life in AIS patients with different preoperative emotional statuses, and clarify whether the impact of preoperative negative emotions is persistent. Fourth, develop a personalized psychological assessment and intervention system for AIS patients, combining the characteristics of adolescent psychological development to improve the acceptability and effectiveness of interventions.

Clinical Implications

The findings of this study have important guiding significance for clinical practice, and specific operable intervention strategies can be implemented as follows: the HADS should be integrated into the routine admission evaluation of AIS patients, and for those with negative emotions, standardized psychological counseling should be conducted once a week from admission to 1 day before surgery, focusing on appearance expectation management, rehabilitation pain education, and cognitive behavioral therapy to alleviate preoperative anxiety and depression; a fixed multidisciplinary team (MDT) collaboration model should be established, consisting of spinal surgeons, clinical psychologists, rehabilitation therapists, and head nurses, with joint ward rounds conducted once a week during the perioperative period, where surgeons clarify surgical effects and appearance recovery prospects, psychologists provide real‐time emotional intervention, rehabilitation therapists formulate painless and acceptable training plans, and nurses track daily emotional changes to form a closed‐loop management of physical and psychological recovery; standardized postoperative psychological follow‐up nodes should be set at 1 week (in‐hospital), 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years postoperatively, with each follow‐up combining the mental health dimension of the SRS‐22 and simplified emotional assessment to intervene timely when emotional abnormalities are detected; targeted family support education should be carried out, with one preoperative family education session and one monthly postoperative family guidance session for guardians of AIS patients, teaching parents to identify adolescent emotional changes, provide positive emotional feedback, avoid overemphasis on physical deformity, and build a supportive family environment to relieve postoperative psychological pressure; in the evaluation of surgical outcomes, Cobb angle correction should be taken as the objective index, and the mental health and treatment satisfaction of SRS‐22 as well as the emotional function of SF‐36 as the core subjective indexes, so as to achieve a comprehensive evaluation of physical and psychological recovery rather than focusing only on anatomical correction.

Conclusions

In summary, preoperative anxiety and depression may affect the improvement magnitude of quality of life after AIS surgery, as they do not change the improvement of physical function‐related indicators but markedly reduce the improvement range of subjective quality of life dimensions including mental health, treatment satisfaction, general health, and role‐emotional function. AIS patients with preoperative anxiety and depression have significantly poorer postoperative quality of life, since at the 2‐year follow‐up, their scores in mental health and treatment satisfaction (SRS‐22) as well as general health, role‐emotional, and mental health (SF‐36) are significantly lower than those of patients without preoperative negative emotions.

Preoperative anxiety and depression exert a sustained negative impact on postoperative subjective quality of life in AIS patients, which is likely closely related to AIS‐specific stressors such as appearance recovery expectation gap and rehabilitation pain, and AIS treatment should not only focus on spinal deformity correction but also implement graded psychological assessment, fixed‐node follow‐up, MDT collaboration, and targeted family support throughout the perioperative period; future multi‐center intervention studies and long‐term follow‐up are needed to verify the effectiveness of standardized psychological interventions, so as to achieve patient‐centered comprehensive physical and psychological rehabilitation for AIS adolescents.
