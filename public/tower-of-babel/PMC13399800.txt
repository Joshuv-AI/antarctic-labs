Dual‐Scaffold Technique: Autologous Minced Cartilage and Bone Marrow Aspirate Scaffold Combined With Synthetic Collagen Membrane for Cartilage Repair
Authors: Leonardo Ataíde, Moises Cohen, Pedro Debieux, Camila Cohen Kaleka, José Martins Juliano Eustáquio, Lucas Barbosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTreating cartilage injuries has long been a complex challenge; however, recent progress has greatly increased the variety of effective therapeutic approaches. The minced autologous matrix‐induced chondrogenesis plus procedure for full‐thickness knee cartilage lesions uses a collagen matrix combined with shaver‐minced cartilage graft and bone marrow aspirate. It merges a source of bone marrow‐derived stem cells and a biological autologous cartilage scaffold, leveraging their chondrogenic differentiation capacity and paracrine activity. This technique aims to enhance outcomes and provides an associated single‐stage knee cartilage repair option with biological augmentation.

Cartilage injuries have been increasingly the subject of study in recent years, with increased therapeutic diversity. 1 , 2 , 3 Regarding focal lesions, exclusive arthroscopic repair options with proven safety and efficacy, such as minced cartilage and osteochondral allograft transplantation, are also being explored. 4 , 5 , 6 Benthien and Behrens introduced the autologous matrix‐induced chondrogenesis technique, which implants a type I and III collagen matrix (Chondro‐Gide, Geistlich Pharma AG) to cover the area of the chondral defect treated by microfracture and stabilize the bone marrow clot “superclot” during organization, while permitting the mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to develop into chondrocytes. 7

Bone marrow stem cells may produce progeny belonging to several differentiation lineages, but their therapeutic potential is based on their immunomodulatory functions and paracrine regulatory activity in situ. MSCs provide a promising option for enhancing cartilage repair.

In this article, we describe the technique for repairing focal, full‐thickness knee cartilage lesions using the bone marrow aspirate (BMA) + minced cartilage + membrane—a dual‐scaffold autologous matrix‐induced chondrogenesis approach combined with minced cartilage graft and BMA.

Evaluation and Planning

A thorough clinical evaluation provides essential clinical data for therapeutic planning. Understanding the chondral lesion enables the surgeon to perform the most precise cartilage repair.

Diagnostic investigation in cases of suspected symptomatic chondral lesions should include imaging examinations such as panoramic radiography of the lower limbs, orthogonal weight‐bearing radiographs, and magnetic resonance imaging of the knee. These methods are fundamental for analyzing limb alignment, delineating the chondral lesion, and ruling out associated conditions such as ligamentous insufficiencies or ruptures, as well as uncorrected joint misalignments that may compromise treatment effectiveness.

If coronal misalignment greater than 2° to 3° is detected, along with overload in the affected compartment, alignment correction should be considered as a key element of the treatment strategy.

Preparation of Minced Cartilage

In the process of minced cartilage preparation, autologous fragments from a nonload‐bearing donor site, free cartilaginous bodies, or the edge of the lesion are collected and prepared manually with the help of cutting forceps to obtain sufficient minced cartilage to treat the lesion to be repaired. 6

Preparation of the Scaffold

At the anterior superior iliac crest, a BMA trocar is inserted to aspirate 5 mL of bone marrow. The minced cartilage and 3 mL of BMA are mixed in a sterile dish until a clot forms (approximately 7 minutes). After this period, the scaffold can be manipulated, shaped, and even cut to match the contour of the lesion. The clot is then manually placed into the lesion site, carefully pressed against the base of the defect, ensuring that it remains flush with the surrounding cartilage without any prominence.

Cartilage Repair

Arthroscopic inspection, treatment of concomitant lesions, and cartilage bed preparation are performed concurrently with the collection of BMA and scaffold preparation. Limb axis realignment and treatment of associated injuries should ideally be completed in the same surgical session.

Exposure of the chondral defect is achieved via arthrotomy (Figure 1). The lesion edge is defined using a scalpel blade, a fine straight periosteal elevator, and curettes, aiming to create perpendicular margins with healthy surrounding cartilage. It is critical to avoid breaching the subchondral bone; punctate bleeding indicates an appropriate depth of debridement. The defect is then filled with the scaffold (minced cartilage + BMA), followed by placement of a collagen membrane.

The patient is placed in the supine position on the left side. An anterior approach is performed, followed by a medial parapatellar arthrotomy. A 3.0 × 300 mm Steinmann pin is positioned along the medial border of the patella to facilitate patellar eversion. Next, the chondral lesion is debrided to create stable margins, ensuring proper execution of the procedure.

A laminated sheet from the Chondro‐Gide kit (Geistlich Pharma AG, Switzerland) is used to create a custom mold of the lesion, ensuring a precise fit without overlaps or uncovered areas (Figure 2). The membrane's smooth surface is marked “Up” using a Codman pen to ensure proper orientation, with the rough surface facing the lesion bed to optimize graft integration.

The membrane template is positioned over the left‐sided lesion, ensuring that it does not extend beyond the lesion margins.

The membrane is trimmed slightly smaller than the mold to account for expected expansion (approximately 10%‐15%) (Figure 3). Subsequently, 2 mL of BMA is instilled onto the collagen I/III membrane inside the sterile package, allowing full absorption before implantation.

With the appropriate template, the membrane is cut to match the defect and prepared for implantation. It is important to note that, at this stage, the BMA has already been harvested from the iliac crest and is being kept ready for use in the procedure. (BMA, bone marrow aspirate.)

Any membrane edges extending beyond the defect border are carefully trimmed to achieve a tension‐free fit. Excessive tension, particularly in elongated femoral condylar lesions due to natural curvature, may compromise graft accommodation and fixation strength, while reducing central tissue maturation capacity. 8

The membrane is allowed to rest for 5 minutes to ensure full saturation with graft material. The chondral defect is then filled with the minced cartilage‐BMA mixture, and the membrane is carefully placed over the defect, ensuring complete containment (Figure 4), without overfilling, overflow, or undue tension.

Left‐sided lesion completely filled with BMA and minced cartilage and covered with a membrane secured using fibrin glue and sutures placed in an opposing direction to optimize membrane fixation. (BMA, bone marrow aspirate.)

Fixation is performed with interrupted absorbable 6‐0 PDS (Ethicon) sutures, anchored to the cartilage rim at 3 to 5 mm intervals. Knots are secured over the membrane, flush with the cartilage surface, to avoid prominence and ensure effective sealing. Additional fixation with fibrin glue is applied externally to prevent graft displacement. The knee is cycled through 5 flexion‐extension repetitions (0°‐90°) to confirm construct stability, completing the autologous matrix‐induced chondrogenesis (AMIC) + minced cartilage chondral repair procedure.

Postoperative Protocol

Rehabilitation begins immediately after surgery, focusing on quadriceps activation and strengthening. During the first 14 days, the patient should flex the knee up to a maximum of 30° to 45°, gradually increasing knee flexion as tolerated. The use of a continuous passive motion device is recommended, and the patient is instructed to perform exercises regularly at home.

If the lesion is located on the femur or tibia, during the first 4 weeks, the patient should maintain toe‐touch weight‐bearing with crutches. From weeks 4 to 6, weight‐bearing should be progressively increased to partial weight‐bearing, reaching approximately 50% to 60% of body weight. By the end of the 6th week, the patient is encouraged to apply full weight‐bearing on the operated limb. Rehabilitation continues with the initiation of work or sport‐specific training after 8 weeks. If the lesion is located in the trochlea or patella, partial weight‐bearing with crutches may be allowed immediately.

The procedure incorporates the use of the Chondro‐Gide membrane (Chondro‐Gide, Geistlich Pharma AG, Switzerland), minced cartilage, and BMA, with the objective of establishing a standardized surgical and postoperative protocol for use in future prospective studies focused on clinical and radiological outcomes.

This approach aligns with a growing body of literature emphasizing the importance of biological and cellular augmentation in cartilage repair. A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis by Steinwachs et al. evaluated the safety and mid‐ to long‐term outcomes of the AMIC technique, confirming sustained clinical improvement in the treatment of grade III to IV cartilage lesions. 9 In a comparative systematic review between AMIC and matrix‐induced autologous chondrocyte implantation, Migliorini et al. reported significantly better outcomes and lower complication rates in the AMIC group. 10

Improved patient‐reported outcome measures and enhanced radiological findings have also been observed with the combination of microfracture (Mfx) and adipose‐derived MSCs, compared with isolated microfracture in focal or diffuse/osteoarthritic lesions.

We describe a modification to the AMIC technique involving the direct grafting of autologous minced cartilage combined with BMA into the chondral defect, filling the entire lesion to provide a higher concentration of adipose‐derived MSCs. Proper application of the Chondro‐Gide membrane ensures graft containment, and the resulting “superclot” promotes a favorable microenvironment, serving as a scaffold for cellular differentiation and maturation. The mini‐open parapatellar arthrotomy approach allows for precise defect preparation, graft placement, and membrane fixation. Additionally, there is the advantage of not violating the subchondral bone during the execution of the technique.

Focal cartilage repair strategies combining orthobiologics and scaffold‐based approaches have shown satisfactory clinical outcomes and favorable safety profiles. The AMIC technique combined with autologous cartilage matrix‐induced chondrogenesis and BMA emerges as a reliable and promising option, safe and feasible to perform as a single‐stage procedure.

Despite these advantages, certain technical considerations must be emphasized. Ensuring perpendicular and stable lesion borders is critical for graft containment. Furthermore, tension‐free fixation and accurate membrane sizing are essential to avoid complications such as graft delamination or hypertrophy. Proper patient selection remains crucial, particularly in cases of significant malalignment or joint instability, in which concomitant procedures should be addressed during the same surgical intervention.

Further studies are warranted to evaluate the long‐term outcomes of the minced AMIC+ technique and to compare its efficacy with other 1‐ or 2‐stage cartilage repair methods. Nevertheless, the encouraging preliminary results and biologically robust nature of this technique make it a promising addition to the evolving armamentarium for cartilage restoration.
