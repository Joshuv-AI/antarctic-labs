Point mutation in zinc sensor causes a threefold zinc level increase in Sorghum bicolor
Authors: Feixue Liao, Anko Blaakmeer, Liliana S. Silva, Søren Knudsen, Ana G. L. Assunção
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Point mutation in sensor triples zinc levels in Sorghum bicolor.

Introduction

Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench) is one of the most important cereal crops in the world, being the dietary staple for over 500 million people. It is a versatile and fast‐growing annual crop that thrives in warm and dry climates, including arid and semiarid regions. It is widely cultivated in Asia, Africa and America, mostly for human consumption and animal forage and fodder, as well as bioethanol and biofuel production. The sorghum grain is gluten‐free, suitable for a celiac‐safe diet, and its consumption and cultivation are expected to increase (Ananda et al., 2020; Khoddami et al., 2023). Sorghum is a primarily self‐pollinating grass (Poaceae) with C4 photosynthesis and a relatively small diploid genome (c. 730 Mb), which makes it an interesting crop for breeding varieties with improved nutrition value and climate resilience (Mason et al., 2024).

Deficiency of plant‐available zinc (Zn) in soils is common globally, making Zn deficiency in crops a prevalent concern (Alloway, 2008). The risk of Zn malnutrition affects approximately two billion people, particularly those living in developing regions with cereal‐based diets. Deficiency of Zn impacts human growth and development, cognitive and immune functions, especially in children (Hussain et al., 2022). This global problem could be eased with breeding strategies to improve Zn use efficiency and the Zn nutritional value (biofortification) of crops (White & Broadley, 2009).

Plants maintain an adequate level of micronutrient Zn in their organs and cells through tightly regulated mechanisms of Zn homeostasis, which comprise Zn uptake from the soil, its transport and distribution throughout the plant (Clemens, 2001). In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis), the transcription factors basic region leucine zipper19 (bZIP19) and bZIP23 were identified as the key regulators of Zn homeostasis and deficiency response. The double knockout mutant bzip19 bzip23 was hypersensitive to Zn deficiency, while not showing a visible phenotype with sufficient Zn supply (Assunção et al., 2010). These transcription factors belong to the family of bZIP transcription factors, which contain the characteristic bZIP domain, comprising a basic region for DNA binding and a leucine zipper for protein dimerization (Dröge‐Laser et al., 2018). bZIP19 and bZIP23 belong to the F group within the bZIP family (F‐bZIP) and regulate the response to Zn deficiency through transcriptional activation of Zn homeostasis‐related genes (Fig. 1a). Target genes include members of the ZIP (ZRT/IRT‐like Protein) family of Zn transporters involved in cellular Zn uptake, and genes encoding the enzyme nicotianamine synthase (NAS), which catalyzes the synthesis of NA, a Zn ligand involved in the translocation and distribution of Zn in plants (Sinclair & Krämer, 2012).

The bZIP19 and bZIP23 transcription factors also act as sensors of intracellular Zn status through binding of Zn ions to their Zn sensor motif (ZSM) (Lilay et al., 2021). The ZSM is rich in cysteine (Cys) and histidine (His) amino acids, which typically coordinate with Zn ions in Zn metalloproteins (Assunção et al., 2013). Under Zn‐sufficient conditions, cellular Zn ions bind the ZSM in nuclear‐localized bZIP19/23, halting the activation of target gene expression (Fig. 1b). Interestingly, if the ZSM is deleted or mutated with Cys or His amino acid substitutions, the Zn‐regulated activity of the transcription factor is affected (Fig. 1c). It becomes Zn‐insensitive, leading to a constitutive expression of the target genes and causing a significant increase of Zn concentration in Arabidopsis leaves and seeds (Lilay et al., 2021).

A phylogenetic analysis of F‐bZIP homologs across land plants showed a high level of sequence conservation between F‐bZIP proteins and indicated conservation of the Zn deficiency response (Castro et al., 2017). F‐bZIP proteins were already characterized as functional homologs of Arabidopsis bZIP19 and bZIP23 in diverse land plants (Poaceae: rice (Lilay et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2024; Qing et al., 2024), wheat (Evens et al., 2017), barley (Nazri et al., 2017) and Fabaceae: Medicago truncatula (Liao et al., 2022)). The evolutionary conservation of the Zn deficiency response provides the opportunity for a translational approach from Arabidopsis to crops, targeting the ZSM of F‐bZIP as a ‘molecular switch’ for Zn biofortification (Assunção, 2022).

For Zn biofortification to be effective, it is necessary to show that ZSM variants can substantially increase Zn accumulation in crops without compromising their performance. This demonstration has not yet been done. Sorghum is an ideal crop to evaluate the efficacy of ZSM variants in biofortification, because its genome has only one F‐bZIP homolog to the Arabidopsis bZIP19/23 transcription factors. This S. bicolor F‐bZIP member (Sobic.010G267500), now referred to as SbFbZIP1, was identified in phylogenetic analysis and predicted to be the bZIP19/23 functional homolog (Castro et al., 2017; Lilay et al., 2020). The sorghum SbFbZIP1 protein contains the bZIP domain, characteristic of bZIP transcription factors (Dröge‐Laser et al., 2018), and the Cys‐ and His‐rich ZSM at the protein N terminus, which is characteristic of F‐bZIP members (Castro et al., 2017; Fig. 2a). A few gene members from the sorghum ZIP family were reported to be induced at Zn deficiency (Maharajan et al., 2023). This includes SbZIP7 (Sobic.009G073100), which was identified as the homolog of Arabidopsis ZIP4 and also contains the zinc deficiency response element (ZDRE) in its promoter (Castro et al., 2017). Arabidopsis ZIP4 is a target gene of bZIP19/23, and its promoter contains ZDRE elements to which the bZIP19/23 transcription factors bind (Assunção et al., 2010). This indicates conservation of the F‐bZIP‐regulated Zn deficiency response in sorghum (Fig. 1).

Previously, we showed in Arabidopsis that if the ZSM is deleted or mutated with Cys or His amino acid substitutions, the Zn‐regulated activity of bZIP19 becomes Zn‐insensitive (Lilay et al., 2021). Here, we used a large‐scale ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS)‐induced sorghum mutant library (Mason et al., 2024), coupled with fast identification of nucleotide variants by droplet DigITal PCR (FIND‐IT) (Knudsen et al., 2022), with the objective of searching for two genetic variants in SbFbZIP1: one with a substitution of the first Cys of the ZSM, because this was one of the mutations shown in Arabidopsis to cause Zn‐insensitive activity of bZIP19 (Lilay et al., 2021); and another with a premature stop codon intended to produce a knockout mutant (Fig. 2a).

The EMS‐induced mutagenesis targets primarily guanine (G) and cytosine (C) bases in the treated genome causing single base‐pair substitutions from G to adenine (A) and C to thymine (T) (Sega, 1984). The EMS treatment in the large FIND‐IT populations is fine‐tuned, on the one hand, to induce single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) evenly distributed across genomes, and on the other hand, to be of low mutation rate to facilitate the recovery of mutant variants with minimal background genetic changes (Knudsen et al., 2022; Mason et al., 2024). It was estimated that all G/C bases in the genome are covered in this sorghum mutant library, meaning that all possible G/C mutations within the BTx623 genome are likely represented (Mason et al., 2025). This allowed screening for point mutations in SbFbZIP1 that substitute the amino acid Cys (TGC) at Position 45 with tyrosine (Tyr, TAC) and glutamine (Gln, CAG) at Position 136 with a stop codon (TAG). The identified genetic variants were analyzed for Zn biofortification.

Results and Discussion

Sorghum library screening identifies SbFbZIP1 nucleotide variants

We identified two mutant plants for variant A, with the Cys45 to Tyr45 point mutation in the ZSM. These two M2 plants were found in the same mutant pool and therefore likely originate from the same M1 plant. We named them variants A1 and A2. For variant B, the Gln136 to stop codon point mutation, we identified one candidate. The M2 seeds of variants A1, A2 and B were germinated and allowed to grow. Sequencing analysis on M2 plants validated the expected single base‐pair substitutions and, additionally, showed that variant A1 is homozygous, whereas variants A2 and B are heterozygous for their respective single base‐pair substitutions (Fig. 2b).

We allowed M2 plants to self and produce M3 seeds, and we used M3 plants for trait evaluation analysis. Plants from M3 progeny were genotyped and the following were obtained: five variant A1 homozygous plants (Tyr/Tyr), five variant A2 homozygous plants (Tyr/Tyr), two wild‐type (WT) plants from variant A2 (Cys/Cys), two variant A2 heterozygous plants (Cys/Tyr), five variant B homozygous plants (stop codon) and five nonmutagenized WT plants with the original BTx623 genome.

A mutated ZSM controls Zn biofortification

To evaluate the Zn concentration trait in leaves and seeds, we grew the M3 plants in soil pots in a glasshouse for 12 months. Quantitative element analysis with inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP‐OES) was performed in leaves (older and younger leaves separately) and tiller from 3‐month‐old plants, and in harvested mature seeds. Results showed that homozygous plants from variant B (B HM) did not differ in leaf and seed Zn concentration compared with WT plants (Fig. 3b). The premature stop codon in variant B likely represents a sbfbzip1 knockout mutant, and this is supported by the hypersensitive phenotype of these plants when grown at Zn deficiency (Fig. 4a). In plants grown with sufficient Zn supply, the F‐bZIP‐based Zn deficiency response is not activated (Fig. 1b), wich may explain the comparable Zn levels between B HM and WT plants, as previously shown in Arabidopsis bzip19/23 mutant (Huizinga et al., 2025) (Fig. 1b). Nonetheless, the 12‐month‐old B variant plants showed more chlorosis than the WT (Supporting Information Fig. S1), indicating the importance of an active F‐bZIP regulator to cope with fluctuating Zn availability in long‐term soil‐grown plants.

By contrast, the homozygous A variants (A1 HM and A2 HM) and the heterozygous A variant (A2 HZ) all had at least twofold higher Zn concentrations in leaves, tiller and seeds than in WT plants (Fig. 3b). In the homozygous variants (A1 HM and A2 HM), this increase appears even higher, with plants showing at least a threefold higher Zn concentration in leaves, tiller and seeds than in the WT (Fig. 3b). Analysis of other micronutrient concentrations (i.e. Fe, Cu, Mn) do not show such a clear effect on the variant A plants (Fig. S2), compared to that observed for Zn (Fig. 3b). Nonetheless, the concentration of Fe in seeds is significantly higher in the homozygous A variants than in the WT (Fig. S2a). The images from the different sorghum genotypes (Figs 3a, S1) do not reveal striking differences between them in plant growth, despite variant A and B plants carrying additional EMS‐induced mutations. Future analysis, including back‐crossing, will assess whether there are any trait penalties associated with the point mutation in variant A plants.

The increased Zn concentration observed not only in the homozygous (A1 HM and A2 HM) but also in the heterozygous (A2 HZ) mutant plants indicates that mutation in a ZSM allele overrules to some extent the native F‐bZIP activity, pointing at a gain‐of‐function mutation (Huizinga et al., 2025). The sorghum plants that segregated with a WT genotype for the A variant (A2 WT) showed a Zn content similar to that of nonmutant plants (WT) (Fig. 3b). Overall, these results show that modulating the ZSM from SbFbZIP1, with a single Cys substitution, causes a large‐effect Zn biofortification of sorghum plant and seeds.

SbFbZIP1 is a functional homolog of AtbZIP19/23

The Zn deficiency response of the sorghum A and B variants was analyzed in seedlings grown in hydroponics with control (+Zn) and deficient (−Zn) Zn supply (Fig. 4). Expression of two sorghum ZIP genes was analyzed in the WT, and in the homozygous A2 and B variants (A2 HM and B HM). SbZIP7 is the homolog of Arabidopsis ZIP4, a target gene of AtbZIP19/23 (Castro et al., 2017). SbZIP2 (Sobic.001G339600) is the homolog of Arabidopsis ZIP11, a nontarget gene of AtbZIP19/23 (Assunção et al., 2010), and was used here as a control. The B HM plants showed a Zn deficiency hypersensitive phenotype (Fig. 4a) and low expression of SbZIP7, irrespective of Zn supply (Fig. 4b). These results show that the premature stop codon in SbFbZIP1 in variant B (Fig. 2) constitutes a knockout mutant and that SbFbZIP1 is the key regulator of the Zn deficiency response in sorghum. The expression of SbZIP7 was induced at Zn deficiency in roots and shoots of WT plants, consistent with previous findings by Maharajan et al. (2023), while in the A2 HM plants, the expression in roots and shoots was comparably induced but Zn‐insensitive (Fig. 4b). The expression of SbZIP2 in roots and shoots did not show significant variation in response to Zn supply (Fig. 4b), also in line with that reported by Maharajan et al. (2023). These results show that the ZSM point mutation in SbFbZIP1 in variant A (Fig. 2) alters its transcriptional regulatory activity and its Zn‐sensing capacity (Fig. 1). Overall, it confirms that the sorghum SbFbZIP1 is the functional homolog of Arabidopsis bZIP19/23, that SbZIP7 is most likely a ZIP target gene, and it reinforces the evolutionary conservation of the F‐bZIP‐regulated Zn deficiency response (Assunção, 2022).

Implications for Zn biofortification

This work provides the first proof of concept that modulating the ZSM from F‐bZIP transcription factors with amino acid substitutions is an efficient ‘molecular switch’ for Zn biofortification in crops, as previously hypothesized (Assunção, 2022). Qing et al. (2024) reported preliminary results showing increased Zn concentration in two rice lines with a gene‐edited deletion of one or 19 ZSM amino acids from OsbZIP4, together with a gene‐edited knockout of the other rice functional homolog of AtbZIP19/23, OsbZIP50 (Lilay et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2024). However, these rice lines had severe plant developmental penalties. Here, by contrast, we show robust evidence of a Zn biofortification effect without obvious developmental penalties, resulting from a single nucleotide mutation at the sorghum F‐bZIP ZSM. Our evidence is strengthened by the analysis of the segregating heterozygous A2 variant line, in which the progeny plants segregating back to WT allele (Cys/Cys) lost the Zn biofortification trait (Fig. 3).

Micronutrient content varies with environmental and growth conditions, but in general, the concentration of Zn in sorghum seeds is c. 20 mg kg−1 dw (Gaddameedi et al., 2022), in line with our results in BTx623 WT plants (Fig. 3b). Currently, the target level for Zn biofortification in sorghum seeds is 32 mg kg−1 dw (HarvestPlus, 2014; Guild & Stangoulis, 2021), which is achieved with the Parbhani Shakti (14001), a released Zn biofortified sorghum variety (Gaddameedi et al., 2022). Our results with the homozygous A variants point to much higher Zn concentrations in sorghum seeds, in the range of 50–60 mg kg−1 dw (Fig. 3b). Having found a strong Zn biofortification effect in the A variant, we anticipate a successful integration of the ZSM point mutation into elite plant germplasm to obtain sorghum plants biofortified with Zn and with increased use efficiency in Zn‐deficient soils. Future studies on Zn localization and speciation in the grain and its bioavailability during human digestion are necessary.

Given the magnitude of Zn deficiency problems in agricultural soils and human nutrition (HarvestPlus, 2014), the importance of sorghum as a cereal crop and the expected increase in its cultivation due to its resilience in a changing climate (Khoddami et al., 2023), the ability to develop Zn‐biofortified sorghum will impact nutrition security in regions with impoverished soils. Our findings unfold a direct link between a SNP and an important agricultural trait, highlighting the key contribution of plant functional biology and molecular breeding for finding novel strategies for sustainable agriculture.

Materials and Methods

Nucleotide substitution screening using FIND‐IT technology

A large‐scale sorghum (Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench) M1 mutant library (150 000 plants) coupled with FIND‐IT technology (Mason et al., 2024) was used to search for the nucleotide substitutions Cys45 (TGC) to Tyr (TAC) (variant A) and Gln136 (CAG) to a stop codon (TAG) (variant B) in the SbFbZIP1 gene (Sobic.010G267500). A detailed description of the FIND‐IT protocol can be found in Mason et al. (2024), but in summary, pooled DNA samples originating from seeds of 100 plants are screened until a positive pool is found (phase I). Seeds from the corresponding pool are drilled, and flour is collected in batches of 10. DNA is isolated from the flour and screened with Droplet Digital PCR (ddPCR) (phase II). Seeds from the positive pools are grown and genotyped to identify the individual mutant plants (phase III). The primers used are as follows (5′ to 3′): A_forward (TCCATGGACAGCTACTTC), A_reverse (GTTGCAGGTGTGGGT), B_forward (GAAGGCCCACACCG), B_reverse (GCTCTGGAGCTTCTTCA). The probes used are as follows (5′ to 3′): A_HEX_WT (CACGCGTGCACGCA), A_FAM_MUT (ACGCGTACACGCAC), B_HEX_WT (AGCTGCTGGTTGAGC), B_FAM_MUT (AGCTGCTAGTTGAGCG).

Sequence validation

The identified M2 seeds were germinated, grown on soil and allowed to set M3 seeds.

Leaf samples from the identified M2 plants were harvested for DNA extraction using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol followed by PCR amplification of SbFbZIP1. The primers used are as follows (5′ to 3′): forward (CTGATACCACTACAATTTTCC) and reverse (CTAGAAGATAGACAGGAGTT). The amplified fragments were isolated and purified from agarose gel and were sequenced. The analysis of the sequencing chromatogram allowed the genotyping and sequence validation of the M2 genetic variants to confirm the mutations found with ddPCR. The same procedure was used to genotype M3 seedlings that were progeny of heterozygous M2 plants.

Plant growth

Sorghum seeds from the genotypes BTx623 WT, and variants B homozygous, A1 homozygous, A2 homozygous, A2 WT and A2 heterozygous were germinated on a Petri dish with wet filter paper in the dark at 28°C for 2–3 d. Germinated seedlings were planted on a soil with 60% peat and 40% vermiculite in 7.5‐l pots, one seedling per pot. Plants grew for 12 months in a glasshouse under a 23°C : 18°C, light : dark cycle, with ventilation (windows) open at +2°C, with 16 h of LED light (blue 15%, green 35%, red 49% and far red 1%) at 250 μmol m−2 s−1, with the onset of curtains at 700 μmol m−2 s−1 and a relative humidity set at 65%. Plants were watered regularly with tap water, and once a week with a water‐soluble fertilizer (Pioner Basis Brown, Azelis, Denmark A/S) with 13% total N, 2% P, 23% K, 3% Mg, 4% S + micronutrients (0.18% Fe (DTPA), 0.06% Mn (EDTA), 0.035% B (EDTA), 0.03% Zn (EDTA), 0.015% Cu (EDTA) and 0.006% Mo), with pH adjusted to 5.9 and Ec. 2.2. The experiment was conducted during one growing season, with all plants and genotypes grown and watered simultaneously, randomized in a glasshouse table. Tissue samples were harvested from each 3‐month‐old plant: one older and one younger leaf blade, cut from the collar, and one to two young tillers. Before flowering of the sorghum plants, the heads were bagged with microperforated pollination bags to ensure self‐pollination. Upon maturation, seeds were harvested from each plant (Fig. S1).

Tissue element analysis

After harvest, samples (leaves and tillers) were placed separately in paper bags, dried in an oven at 60°C for 4 d followed by grinding with zirconium beads in a high‐speed shaking ball mill. The grinded samples and the seeds (10 seeds per plant) were digested in a solution of HNO3 (65% [v/v]) and H2O2 (30% [v/v]) in a 3 : 1 ratio. Following overnight incubation at room temperature, the samples were incubated at 85°C in HotBlock (Environmental Express) for 12–24 h. Samples were then diluted five times by adding deionized Milli‐Q water before the measurement. Elemental analysis was performed using ICP‐OES (ICP‐OES 5800; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) using calibration standard solutions provided by the manufacturer. The elemental concentration of each sample derives from three technical replicates. The ICP‐OES is calibrated in wavelength each time the machine is switched on, using a 5‐ppm calibration solution supplied by the manufacturer (ICP‐OES&MP‐AES wave cal 6610030100; Agilent Technologies), and performance tests are proceeded (optics, sensitivity and resolution). Quality control was performed with certified reference material (CRM) strawberry leaf powder (LGC7162), which corresponded perfectly to the type of matrices usually handled. Atomic emission wavelengths are selected using the precision obtained from CRM measurements within ±10% of the theoretical value. The ICP‐OES data are available in the Dataset S1.

Hydroponic assay

Germinated sorghum seeds were placed in plastic holders fitting an 18‐hole lid from a 5 l hydroponic tray containing half‐strength Hoagland nutrient solution with the following composition: 2 mM Ca(NO3)2, 1 mM MgSO4.7H2O, 3 mM KNO3, 1 mM KH2PO4, 25 μM Fe‐Na‐EDTA, 25 μM H3BO3, 3 μM MnSO4.H2O, 0.1 μM CuSO4.5H2O, 0.5 μM (NH4)6Mo7O24, 50 μM KCl, with 1 mM MES at pH 5.7, with either 2 μM ZnSO4 (control, +Zn) or with no ZnSO4 added (Zn deficiency, −Zn). The plastic containers, for the control and (−)Zn treatments, were rinsed with 0.1 N HCl solution followed by rinsing with ultrapure water before use, and the nutrient solutions were prepared with ultrapure water. The nutrient solutions were replaced once in the first week and twice in the weeks thereafter. Three genotypes, that is BTx623 WT and variants B homozygous and A2 homozygous, were grown in each tray, with three trays/replicas for each Zn supply condition, that is three trays with control/(+)Zn and three trays with (−)Zn. Plants grown on the hydroponics setup were kept in a growth chamber under a 16 h : 8 h, light : dark cycle, with c. 125 μmol m−2 s−1 white light, 25°C : 20°C, light : dark temperature and 60% relative humidity.

RT‐qPCR analysis

Roots and shoots from 2‐wk‐old sorghum plants grown under control (+Zn) or Zn‐deficient (−Zn) hydroponic conditions were harvested, separated and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Samples were ground to a fine powder using a mortar and pestle under liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using both a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and standard agarose‐gel electrophoretic analysis. The RNA samples were treated with Recombinant DNase I (ThermoFisher Scientific), and cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg total RNA using iScript™ cDNA synthesis kit (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed on an Applied Biosystems™ QuantStudio™ 5 Real‐Time PCR System (ThermoFisher Scientific) using NZYSpeedy qPCR Green Master Mix (NZYtech, Lda, Lisbon, Portugal). Two technical replicate PCR amplifications were performed per biological replicate, with six or three biological replicates per line and Zn treatment (corresponding to two or one plant(s) per tray, respectively). The amplifications were performed for each primer pair and sample combination, using 5 ng of cDNA and 200 nM of each primer. The qPCR conditions consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of amplification (5 s at 95°C and 20 s at 60°C), with a single fluorescence measurement per cycle. A melting curve analysis (60–95°C, with 0.5°C increments) was performed to confirm amplification specificity. The relative expression of SbZIP2 (Sobic.001G339600) and SbZIP7 (Sobic.009G073100) was calculated using gene‐specific primers reported in Maharajan et al. (2023). Gene expression levels were normalized to SbPP2C (accession no.: XM_002468507) as the reference gene (Reddy et al., 2016). The primers used are as follows (5′ to 3′): SbZIP2 forward (CATCTCCTACGTCGTCTCACG), SbZIP2 reverse (AGGTGGTGTGGGATCAGAG), SbZIP7 forward (ACATGTCCGCTCCATCGT), SbZIP7 reverse (AGAACTGGTGGAATGCAAGC), SbPP2C forward (GTAACCCTTCCCGCGAAATCC), and SbPP2C reverse (GCGCTACACTTTGCTGCTTTT). The primer efficiencies for SbZIP2, SbZIP7 and SbPP2C were calculated and were within 1.9–2.1. Cycle threshold (Ct) values were determined using the Diomni™ Design and Analysis (RUO) v.3.0.1 software (ThermoFisher Scientific). The relative transcript levels were expressed against the WT plants grown under control conditions, and calculated according to the Livak 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak & Schmittgen, 2001). The RT‐qPCR data are available in the Dataset S1.
