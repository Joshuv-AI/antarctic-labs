Protocol to maximize single-nuclei multiome library complexity for limited primary tissues via optimized sample preparation and library synthesis
Authors: Faizan Uddin, Awadhesh Pandit, Akshay Kudpaje, Dimple Notani, Radhakrishnan Sabarinathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySingle-nuclei multiomic profiling of clinical biopsies is often hindered by limited tumor mass and high debris content. Here, we present a protocol for the dissociation of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) and matched-normal tissues using a mechanical-enzymatic workflow. We describe steps for minimizing ambient nucleic acid background by red blood cell (RBC) lysis and DNase-I treatment and for removing cellular debris using density gradient centrifugation. We then detail procedures for isolating intact nuclei and modified single-nuclei assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (sn-ATAC) and 3′-GEX (gene expression) indexing PCR and size selection to maximize library complexity.Subject areas: Cancer, Genomics, Molecular Biology, Gene Expression

Before you begin

Background

High-quality sample preparation is essential for robust data generation in single-cell genomic assays, particularly with the growing clinical interest in profiling tumor microenvironments to inform cancer management strategies. While several protocols exist for dissociating large tumor biopsies for single-cell RNA sequencing1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8 these approaches are not directly applicable for small samples like HNSCC tumor specimens and matched-normal tissues, largely due to distinct extracellular matrix characteristics and commercial kit limitations. Furthermore, many steps that are critical for isolating intact nuclei from the viable single cells need to be optimized for each kind of tissue samples to ensure high quality data generation from them while utilizing expensive and highly sensitive single-nuclei multiome assay.

Our initial attempts to apply generalized tissue dissociation workflows, specifically those utilizing commercial human tumor dissociation kits and automated mechanical disruption to patient-derived HNSCC tumor and matched-normal tissues revealed critical failure points. Suboptimal dissociation efficiency was observed in matched-normal tissues, where the standard HRA enzyme cocktail (comprising the proprietary Enzymes H, R, and A from the Miltenyi Biotec Tumor Dissociation Kit, human) proved insufficient for the dense extracellular matrix (ECM) characteristic of these specimens. Furthermore, standard debris removal protocols led to significant column clogging during viable cell enrichment and inaccurate cell counts due to persistent Red Blood Cell (RBC) contamination. Beyond mechanical yield, we identified two primary sources of biochemical background noise that compromised the library integrity. First, the ambient RNA released from non-nucleated RBCs during the lysis step. Second, the ambient DNA released via NETosis from activated granulocytes, which are highly prevalent in the HNSCC microenvironment. Furthermore, standard, harsh lysis conditions were found to be unsuitable for these primary cells, often leading to leaky nuclei and fragmented chromatin (Figures 1A and 1B). The cumulative effect of these shortcomings was a total loss of molecular complexity: the resulting cDNA profiles were of low quality, and the single-nuclei multiome assay detected only ∼5% of targeted cell numbers, with high background noise preventing any meaningful data output (Figures 1C and 1D). Because the 3′-GEX and sn-ATAC libraries are derived from the same transposition and partitioning events, the poor transcriptomic quality observed in Figure 1D served as a definitive indicator of isolation failure.

Innovation

This protocol enables single-nuclei multiomic profiling of primary HNSCC specimens. Unlike generalized dissociation workflows that often fail with dense, fibrotic (HNSCC) specimens, this protocol employs a bifurcated dissociation strategy tailored to the distinct extracellular matrix (ECM) compositions of tumor and matched-normal samples. While several existing protocols focus on dissociating large tumor biopsies, they often lack direct applicability to small primary samples and matched-normal tissues.4,5 Our protocol utilizes a tailored enzyme cocktail comprising STEMxyme-I and Liberase-TM specifically optimized for HNSCC matched-normal tissues, and a gradual dissociation strategy which maximizes cell yield and cellular heterogeneity. Standard protocols often subject biopsies to high-stress enzymatic events that deplete fragile infiltrating immune cells. Our multi-stage approach first liberates and pools loosely attached interstitial cells (soft fraction) during initial mincing. Use of progressively stronger dissociation enzymes ensures that more recalcitrant cells are recovered without over-processing the easily liberated fractions.

We address specific technical challenges often ignored or oversimplified in recently published methods,9,10,11,12 including the presence of ambient nucleic acids, inefficient debris removal and problematic cell types. Our optimizations achieve superior enrichment of debris-free nucleated viable cells, and purges ambient nucleic acids via early RBC lysis step and strategic DNase-I treatment. We implemented a controlled kinetic cell lysis using 2× strength buffer for a strictly defined 2-min window, preventing the nuclear leakiness and transcript loss typical of standard 3–5-min protocols.9,12,13

Matched-normal tissue integration remains a gap in current HNSCC research, which is essential for a comprehensive understanding of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Many existing protocols have not utilized matched-normal specimens for single-nuclei multiome assays.3,4,5,10 Our protocol fills this gap by providing a validated, side-by-side workflow for both tissue types from the same patient. Furthermore, it addresses the low-input limitation of primary clinical biopsies by refining downstream library preparation steps, such as concentrating elution to 80 μl (instead of 160 μl) and reducing indexing PCR cycles to maximize unique molecular identifier (UMI) capture and ensure high library complexity even from low starting cell numbers.

Preparation of complete lysis buffer (per 50 μL reaction)

The cell lysis buffer is prepared at 2× lysis strength which is later mixed with equal volume of cell suspension to achieve 1× lysis strength during the cell lysis step. This allows for the rapid mixing of detergents and aids in uniform exposure to all the cells. Nonidet P40 serves as the primary lysis reagent and must not be substituted with Tergitol type NP40, with latter being more potent and can swiftly solubilize both the nuclear and plasma membranes. Digitonin selectively acts on cholesterol-rich cell membranes of the tumor cells and does not lyse the nuclear membrane. Tween-20 helps to prevent the sticking of mitochondria to the nuclear membrane.

Step-by-step method details

Collection, transport, and transient storage of primary tissue samples

Timing: 1 h and 30 min

Here, we describe steps for collecting primary tissue punches from the clinical site, submerging them in protective medium, and transporting them to the laboratory while preserving cellular viability.

Note: The human tumor and matched-normal tissues from HNSCC patients were surgically resected using 8mm biopsy punches by the qualified surgeons at Cytecare Hospitals, Bengaluru, India. The primary tissue samples were immediately submerged into MACS® Tissue Storage Solution and were transferred from hospitals to the culture room. Alternatively, CO2-independent media like Leibovitz's L-15 Medium, traditionally used for temporary storage and transport of biopsy samples, can also be used for transient sample transportation. Ideally, tumor and matched-normal samples should be processed immediately upon receipt. When immediate processing is not feasible, tumor samples should be prioritized, while matched-normal specimens can be reliably preserved in MACS® Tissue Storage Solution for up to 24 h.

Dissociation of HNSCC tumor tissue samples

Timing: 1 h and 30 min

Here, we describe steps for the gradual dissociation of HNSCC tumor biopsies using a mechanical-enzymatic workflow on the automated gentleMACS® system to preserve fragile cell populations and cellular heterogeneity.

Note: To preserve cellular heterogeneity and minimize stress-induced artifacts, loosely attached cells were first liberated during initial mincing and collected as a soft fraction. The remaining tissue underwent enzymatic dissociation with the proprietary cocktail reconstituted in Gibco Hibernate-A media. Unlike CO2-dependent media (e.g., DMEM or RPMI-1640), Hibernate-A is CO2-independent and maintains physiological pH during benchtop processing.14 This prevents the alkaline shift above pH 7.8 typically observed in ambient conditions, which otherwise compromises cell viability and total yield.15 Recalcitrant fragments were subsequently treated with TrypLE to further maximize the recovery of adherent populations without over-digesting the initial fractions.

Removal of RBCs from dissociated tissue samples

Timing: 12 min

Here, we describe steps for the selective osmotic lysis of contaminating red blood cells (RBCs) to prevent ambient transcript carryover into single-nuclei multiomic partitions.

Note: RBCs often comprise a substantial portion of the cells extracted from primary tissue samples, and although they are non-nucleated, they contain significant RNA content that can negatively impact the data quality in single-nuclei 3′-GEX libraries.22 Allowing RBCs to persist until the cell lysis step for nuclei isolation is not advisable, as lysis releases ambient RNA that may be carried into single-nucleus partitioned droplets, thus increasing background noise in downstream analyses. This ambient RNA can be reverse transcribed in empty Gel Beads-in-emulsion (GEMs), resulting in heightened signal attributed to non-cell droplets and eventually decrease the fraction of reads in cells. Therefore, it is essential to effectively remove RBCs prior to isolating nuclei for 10X Genomics single-nuclei multiome assays to ensure optimal data quality.

Nuclei isolation from viable cells

Timing: 1 h

Here, we describe steps for isolating high-purity, intact nuclei from a single-cell suspension of primary HNSCC and matched-normal tissue using a strictly timed detergent exposure strategy.

Note: Precise calibration of lysis kinetics is critical for maintaining nuclear compartmentalization and minimizing ambient background in primary HNSCC tissues. We established a controlled lysis strategy utilizing a 2× strength lysis buffer mixed 1:1 with the cell suspension for a strictly defined 2-min kinetic window. This approach ensures rapid, uniform detergent exposure, effectively isolating >90% intact nuclei with minimal blebbing and high-quality nuclear RNA (Figure S4D). Given the low-input limitation of viable cells, nuclei isolation was performed using swing-out rotors, low-binding tubes, and low-retention tips to maximize recovery and maintain integrity.

Dual size selection of single-nuclei ATAC-seq libraries

Timing: 45 min

Here, we describe steps for a modified, successive paramagnetic bead-based size selection workflow designed to narrow the fragment size distribution of single-nuclei ATAC-Seq libraries.

Note: After sn-ATAC sample index PCR, the amplified library contains both primer dimers (<150 bp) and large fragments (>1 kb). Standard dual size selection with 0.6× and 1.55× beads, following 10X-KIM, aims to enrich 150–1,000 bp fragments. However, sn-ATAC libraries often retain excess >1 kb fragments (Figure S5A), which do not affect sequencing clustering per se, but compromise accurate library quantification. Repeating the standard dual size selection removes large fragments but also risks losing desired fragments. To overcome this, we implemented a successive 0.6× pellet-and-rebind strategy. In this approach, we successively remove large fragments twice with 0.6× beads, and then use a single 1.55× bead step for adapter and primer dimers omission (Figure 7). This streamlined approach maximizes recovery of 170–700 bp fragments and effectively removes fragments >1 kb (Figure S5B).

Expected outcomes

We optimized several technical steps that compromise data quality in the 10X Genomics single-nuclei multiome assay, particularly when using limited primary biopsies. To maximize cellular recovery and maintain the integrity of the HNSCC tumor microenvironment (TME), we implemented a gradual dissociation strategy: initially pooling soft fraction cells released during mincing to minimize stress, followed by a targeted enzymatic-mechanical dissociation and a sequential TrypLE treatment to maximize total yield and cellular heterogeneity. This staggered approach ensures that fragile cell populations are not lost to over-digestion, while tougher structural components are fully dissociated, ultimately resulting in a more representative and viable single-cell suspension than achieved with standard, single-stage dissociation protocols. The high cellular heterogeneity observed in the TME clusters (Figures 8D and 8E) is attributed to our gradual dissociation strategy, which preserves fragile infiltrating populations that are typically depleted in standard, high-stress dissociation workflows.

Beyond establishing a specialized dissociation workflow for the tumor and matched-normal HNSCC tissues, our protocol incorporates strategic deviations in lysis kinetics and library preparation steps that increased data complexity. By utilizing a kinetically controlled 2-min cell lysis, we successfully isolated intact nuclei devoid of the ambient, smeary leaking chromatin often associated with previously published standard 3 to 5-min lysis protocols9,12 (Figures 8A and 8B). This preservation of nuclear integrity directly resulted in superior cDNA quality, characterized by a significant enrichment of high-molecular-weight fragments (>1 kb) (Figure 8C). Furthermore, our implementation of dual DNase-I treatment of dissociated tissues, optimized debris removal and modified bead selection produced sn-ATAC-seq libraries with clear nucleosomal periodicity (Figure 8D) and high signal-to-noise ratios as depicted by Transcription Start Site (TSS) enrichment scores (Figure 8D; Figure S2B). This was quantitatively validated by the TSS enrichment scores, which increased from 2.18 to 6.73 in our optimized HNSCC sn-ATAC-seq libraries. The 3-fold improvement in TSS enrichment score serves as a quantitative proxy for the physical integrity of the isolated nuclei, confirming that the 2-min kinetic lysis duration successfully preserved the nuclear envelope while the dual DNase-I treatment steps effectively reduced the noise associated with ambient chromatin background.

Concentrating the elution of barcoded pre-amplified DNA fragments and reducing PCR cycles improved library complexity. In a direct comparison using shallow-depth sequencing of 3′GEX libraries, sample processed with our optimized strategy achieved a median of 380 genes per cell at only 2,557 mean reads per cell (Figure S7). The barcode rank plot for primary HNSCC (Figure S7A) exhibits a transitional slope characteristic of heterogeneous clinical samples containing both high-RNA tumor cells and low-RNA infiltrating immune cells. The improved quality of this library is validated by a 69.6% Fraction of Reads in Cells, indicating minimal ambient RNA contamination and successful preservation of diverse cell populations within the TME. In contrast, sample processed via the standard manufacturer’s protocol (utilizing 160 μl elution and 50% template input) yielded only 230 median genes per cell despite a significantly higher sequencing depth of 10,050 mean reads per cell (Figure 1D). Our modified protocol yielded 65% more genes per cell despite using 75% fewer sequencing reads in the shallow depth sequencing, indicating that these optimizations are essential for high-quality multiomic data from primary tissues. Furthermore, our protocol consistently achieved a Fraction of Reads in Cells of 70% or higher in 3′GEX libraries. This indicates that despite the high debris typical of HNSCC biopsies, our multi-stage cleaning process consisting of density gradient, Flowmi™ filtration and nuclei washings successfully restricted the majority of sequencing reads to intact nuclei rather than ambient RNA.

These refinements allowed for the emergence of distinct, high-resolution cell clusters from both gene expression and chromatin accessibility data (Figures 8F–8H), which were consistently corroborated by matched-normal tissue analyses (Figure S6). This protocol enables high-complexity multiomic data generation from primary tissues, ensuring that limited clinical specimens yield robust data required for comprehensive investigations.

Limitations

The protocol described here is optimized specifically for processing primary tissue samples from HNSCC patients using buffers and reagents intended for immediate processing in the 10X Genomics single-nuclei multiome assay. If the objective is to culture tumor-derived cells, such as for organoids or patient-derived cell line establishment, buffers and reagent formulations must be modified to ensure sterility and prevent microbial contamination during subsequent culture.

Furthermore, although fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) is a standard for cell purification,27 it was found to be unsuitable for our protocol. Given the fibrotic nature of HNSCC tissue and the limited mass of clinical biopsies (0.1–0.3 gram), we prioritized a high-recovery purification strategy. The significant cell loss associated with FACS (often exceeding 50% in low-input settings), and its potential for hydrodynamic shear-induced transcriptional and epigenetic artifacts at mechanotransduction and cell cycle genes28,29,30,31 led us to prioritize a gravity-based MACS and density gradient fractionation strategy. Since the sn-multiome assay captures a frozen snapshot of the transcriptional and chromatin state immediately following isolation, these flow-induced artifacts would confound the analysis of the native tumor microenvironment. Also, MACS-sorted populations, such as T-cells, have been shown to preserve significantly higher functional and proliferative potential compared to flow-based sorting methods30 However, when sample size and experiment design permits, FACS can be employed to accelerate the removal of debris, dead cells and ambient nuclei-acid contamination.

In addition, our workflow is specifically optimized for small primary biopsies rather than large surgical resections. While automated platforms like autoMACS can improve reproducibility in high-volume settings, they introduce tubing dead-volume that is often incompatible with low-yield HNSCC specimens. However, for studies utilizing larger tissue inputs, the use of automated magnetic separation is recommended. While the matched-normal tissue dissociation protocol works well for human oral tissues, researchers should perform pilot experiments when adapting it to other primary tumor types. Enzymatic incubation times may vary depending on ECM composition and tissue stiffness in different tumors. Furthermore, cell lysis duration for nuclei isolation should be optimized for tumor types beyond oral tissue.

Though our current protocol has markedly improved the quality metrics suitable for complex TME analysis, further refinements could push the signal-to-noise ratio even higher. From a wet-lab perspective, the quality of barcoded cDNA could be further shielded by the targeted addition of RNase Inhibitors during the initial tissue dissociation phase. This would serve to neutralize the enzymatic activity of RNases released from ruptured RBCs and activated granulocytes, ensuring the preservation of low-abundance transcripts. Ambient RNA contamination, originating from ruptured or necrotic cells during tissue dissociation, is a known factor that can contaminate endogenous gene expression profiles and confound cell-type annotations. While our experimental workflow utilizes early RBC lysis and modified density gradient separation to minimize cellular debris containing ambient RNA, we acknowledge that physical removal of ambient RNA is rarely exhaustive in primary tissue specimens. Therefore, we recommend that users of this protocol implement a two-step computational strategy. Following initial filtering by Cell Ranger, users should apply community-developed tools such as SoupX, CellBender or DecontX to refine the cell-specific signal.32,33 The necessity of these tools should be evaluated on a per-sample basis by inspecting Barcode Rank plots for a steep cliff and monitoring for the enrichment of mitochondrial genes or non-specific marker co-expression across clusters.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low cell recovery and incomplete tissue dissociation.

Potential solution

To maximize cell yield, tissue samples should be thoroughly minced into smaller pieces before enzymatic digestion. Prolonged incubation and use of ordinary plastic tips or collection tubes that have affinity for cells can reduce yield. It is essential to optimize enzyme concentration and incubation time for each tissue type. Additionally, use low protein-binding tips and tubes during critical steps, especially when handling small biopsy samples, to prevent cell loss.

Problem 2

Low cell viability after complete tissue dissociation.

Potential solution

Less or compromised cell viability can be attributed to several factors such as using impure dissociation enzymes with high levels of endotoxins or dissociation media pH becoming alkaline. Crude collagenase preparations have been reported to have high endotoxin contents and lower cell viability during tissue dissociation.34 Also, it is best to prepare the dissociation enzyme cocktail in CO2 independent media such as Hibernate A or Leibovitz’s L-15 media with enhanced buffering capacity that can protect the cells from change in pH during the dissociation process.

Problem 3

Slow flow-through of viable cells from MS column in step 70.

Potential solution

Residual debris in the single-cell suspension can clog the MS column, drastically slowing the flow-through and reducing the recovery and viability of viable cells due to increased retention time. To prevent this, always use a debris-free cell suspension for enrichment. When using Miltenyi Biotec Debris Removal Solution, discard the supernatant down to the 1.5ml mark, as outlined in step 63, to effectively remove debris and optimize cell yield.

Problem 4

Low nuclei recovery from viable cells.

Potential solution

Low nuclei recovery can stem from inaccurate counting of nucleated cells or loss during centrifugation washes. Accurate counts require viable, RBC-free cell suspensions, and manual counting using a haemocytometer. RBC contamination inflates viable cell counts but leads to fewer nuclei after lysis, as mature RBCs lack nuclei. To minimize nuclei loss during centrifugation, use a swing-out bucket rotor to pellet nuclei centrally, and perform isolation in Eppendorf Protein Lo-bind 2ml tubes at steps 77, 82 and 87. Further, adding BSA to the nuclei wash buffer enhances pellet visibility and reduces accidental loss during aspiration.

Problem 5

Nuclei lack distinct morphology and appear leaky during microscopic examination.

Potential solution

Diffuse or leaky nuclei typically result from prolonged lysis of the single-cell suspension. As primary tissue contains diverse cell types, careful optimization of lysis time is essential to isolate intact nuclei with well-preserved nuclear RNA fraction and chromatin. Presence of up to 5% viable cells in the nuclei suspension suggests well-controlled lysis, whereas samples with only nuclei and no viable cells make it difficult to distinguish between controlled and excessive lysis. For particularly sensitive tumor-derived cells, reducing the concentration of Nonidet P-40 in the lysis buffer may also be beneficial. Failure to optimize lysis conditions can lead to compromised nuclear integrity and poor downstream data quality.

Problem 6

Prominent mononucleosome peak in sn-ATAC library.

Potential solution

A pronounced mononucleosome peak in sn-ATAC libraries, as noted in technical documentation from 10X Genomics (article 360046631331), is typically due to decondensed chromatin released by dead or activated granulocytes, which is readily tagmented by Tn5 transposase.23 Activated granulocytes, especially neutrophils, can undergo NETosis,24 producing extracellular chromatin that degrades sn-ATAC data quality (Figure S2A). Although negative selection based on high side scatter can help exclude granulocytes from primary tissue suspensions, practical challenges include FACS-induced stress, extended processing time, limited input material, and potential cell loss during sorting. For small biopsy samples, brief DNase-I treatment of single-cell suspensions prior to nuclei isolation is recommended to remove ambient chromatin and improve library quality. Importantly, microbead-based removal of granulocytes via anti-CD66abce should be avoided in tumor samples, as CD66 markers are expressed on both granulocytes and tumor epithelial cells, including cancer stem cells,35 thus making this strategy unsuitable for processing primary tumor tissues or leukaemia samples.

Problem 7

Reduced separation of droplets ascribed to cells vs non-cells in sequenced data.

Potential solution

Increased noise from empty droplets or low signal from droplets containing nuclei can hinder accurate cell identification using CellRanger cell calling algorithms and can decrease the separation between the cells versus non-cells (Figure S2D). Ambient DNA and RNA contribute to this background and can be minimized by DNase-I treatment and effective RBC lysis prior to intact nuclei isolation. If droplets containing nuclei show low signal, it is likely due to compromised or leaky nuclei being used for the multiome assay.

Problem 8

Determining the required depth of sequencing.

Potential solution

Tumor samples contain heterogeneous cell populations with varying RNA and DNA content, so sequencing depth should be tailored to the target cell types.22 Larger epithelial cells have more RNA and may not reach sequencing saturation at 50,000 mean reads per cell, continuing to detect more genes with increased depth. Smaller cells saturate sooner with higher median gene counts at 50,000 reads per cell, assuming high-quality RNA and cDNA. Additionally, the fraction of reads mapped to cells during shallow sequencing must be considered when determining the final sequencing depth for 3′GEX libraries. While shallow spike-in sequencing can be used to estimate the required reads per cell for single-nuclei 3′-GEX libraries, this approach cannot be applied to sn-ATAC libraries. This limitation arises because sn-ATAC libraries require specific sequencing run parameters that prevent their pooling with other libraries for shallow sequencing runs. Therefore, it is recommended to sequence sn-ATAC libraries only after confirming high-quality transcriptome data in 3′-GEX libraries from the multiome samples.

Problem 9

High percentage of PCR duplicates in sequenced libraries.

Potential solution

The complexity of sn-ATAC and 3′-GEX libraries can be improved by increasing the amount or concentration of input template DNA at various stages of library preparation. Higher input DNA at step 98 and 104 allows reducing PCR amplification cycles by 2–3 cycles from manufacturer recommendations. Assuming 100% PCR efficiency, each additional cycle theoretically doubles the number of PCR duplicates, significantly impacting library complexity.
