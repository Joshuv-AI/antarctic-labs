Expression of PIEZO1 in lung adenocarcinoma correlates with PD-L1 expression, cell migration, and poor prognosis: an exploratory study
Authors: Hongyan Xu, Pengcheng Zou, Yang Gao, Weichao Bai, Lei Hou, Na Yuan, Xueyan Li, Shanzhi Gu, Xinhan Zhao, Shan Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) treatment is challenging process. and the function of PIEZO1, a mechanically sensitive ion channel has not been systematically determined. In this study we aimed to explore the expression, potential associations, and clinical significance of PIEZO1 in LUAD.Methods and aimsA comprehensive bioinformatics analysis was performed using data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database and other databases. Experimental validation was performed to confirm the expression patterns and preliminarily examine the associations of PIEZO1 in LUAD cell lines.ResultsPIEZO1 expression was significantly lower in LUAD tissues than in normal lung tissues (P < 0.05). Its expression was correlated with advanced pathological stage, lymph node involvement, and distant metastasis. High PIEZO1 expression was associated with a distinct immune-related tumor microenvironment, characterized by correlations with the expression levels of multiple immune checkpoint molecules, and was identified as an independent factor associated with poor overall survival (HR = 1.49; 95% CI 1.11–2; P < 0.007). In vitro experiments confirmed the downregulated PIEZO1 expression in LUAD cell lines, and functional knockdown experiments revealed its association with cell migration and PD-L1 expression.ConclusionThis exploratory study revealed that PIEZO1 expression in LUAD cell lines correlated with the expression of immune-related features and EMT-related genes, as well as poor prognosis. In vitro, PIEZO1 knockdown is associated with reduced cell migration and decreased PD-L1 expression. These findings provide a basis for future investigations into the potential role of PIEZO1 in LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-026-05482-3.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains among the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Adenocarcinoma is the main subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), accounting for nearly 40% of all cases. Notably, this subtype is the most common form of NSCLC among nonsmokers and young people, and is typically driven by mutations in genes, such as EGFR and ALK [1]. Although the treatment of lung cancer has gradually shifted from traditional chemotherapy to precision medicine, the biological complexity and heterogeneity of the disease remain the main clinical obstacles affecting the clinical efficacy of these treatments [2].

In recent years, the molecular mechanisms underlying the progression of lung adenocarcinoma have been widely. Emerging evidence suggests that complex regulatory networks involving transcription factors, noncoding RNAs, and signaling pathways play critical roles in tumor proliferation, metastasis, and immune regulation in patients with LUAD [3–6]. In addition, the remodeling of the tumor microenvironment and immune-related molecular signatures have been increasingly recognized as important determinants of disease progression and patient prognosis [7–10]. For example, the KRAS G12C mutation activates both the MAPK signaling pathway and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, promoting the occurrence and development of tumors. Small molecule inhibitors targeting this mutation, such as sotorasib and adagrasib, have shown promising efficacy in clinical trials [2]. Furthermore, the combination of immune checkpoint inhibitors with targeted therapies represents as a promising strategy for improving patient outcomes [11, 12]. However, despite these advances, the molecular mechanisms underlying the progression of LUAD and corresponding metastasis remain incompletely understood. Therefore, a systematic investigation of the key molecular drivers and potentially targetable pathways involved in the progression of LUAD is essential for the development of more effective therapeutic strategies.

Piezoelectric mechanical-sensitive ion channel element 1(PIEZO1) is a mechanically sensitive ion channel whose role in various physiological processes, including tactile perception, hearing, and vascular tension regulation, has been extensively studied [13–16]. The significant involvement of PIEZO1 in tumor development through multiple mechanisms has been elucidated [13, 14]. PIEZO1 detects mechanical stimuli in the tumor microenvironment, such as matrix stiffness and fluid shear forces, triggering calcium influx; in turn, the increase in calcium results in the activation of multiple oncogenic signaling pathways, thereby potentially contributing to the proliferation, invasion and metastasis of tumor cells [15].

Moreover, PIEZO1 has been reported to enhance increase the immunosuppressive activity of regulatory T cells (Tregs) by inhibiting the cytotoxic functions of effector T cells and inducing the polarization of M2 macrophages. These actions significantly shape the tumor immune microenvironment and collectively facilitate immune escape [16]. Notably, the inhibition of PIEZO1 can increase both the mechanical traction force and the cytotoxicity of T cells while increasing their tumor infiltration capacity, ultimately leading to increased responses to immunotherapy [17, 18]. We speculate that PIEZO1 may serve as a potential target to improve outcomes in patients with lung adenocarcinoma; however, its expression pattern and mechanism of action in lung adenocarcinoma remain unclear.

To explore the expression of PIEZO1 in patients with LUAD and evaluate the prognostic value of PIEZO1 on the basis of different clinicopathological factors, we conducted multiple bioinformatics analyses. Moreover, the relationships of PIEZO1 expression with the tumor immune microenvironment and tumor metastasis were studied. Finally, in vitro experiments were conducted to confirm the results of the biological analyses.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

RNA-seq data and clinical information from patients with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. In addition, the GSE72094, GSE68465, GSE50081, and GSE37745 datasets were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Batch effects were removed before these GEO datasets were merged to construct the validation cohort.

Analysis of gene expression

To study the expression of PAM38A (also known as PIEZO1) in multiple tumor types, the information on gene expression levels was obtained from the TIMER2.0 online database (https://compbio.cn/timer2/). Normal tissues were compared with primary tumor samples, and data were compared across different subgroups defined by the tumor grade, pathological cancer stage and other relevant clinicopathological features [19].

Furthermore, R (version 4.1.2) was used to compare the expression of PIEZO1 between lung adenocarcinoma tissues and normal tissues in the TCGA-LUAD dataset. Differences in PIEZO1 expression between tumor and normal tissues were assessed using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann-Whitney U test) with the ggpubr package. For clinicopathological features, binary comparisons (M0 vs. M1, N0 vs. N + , T1–2 vs. T3–4, early stage [I–II] vs. advanced stage [III–IV]) were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For comparisions the overall stage (I, II, III, and IV), the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. All box plots were generated with ggplot2, and statistical significance is denoted as follows:*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and***P < 0.001.

Prognosis and survival analysis

The pROC package was used to generate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate the diagnostic performance of PIEZO1 expression. Survival analysis was performed using the survival (version 3.5–5) and survminer (version 0.4.9) packages. The survfit function was used to fit Kaplan–Meier curves, and coxph for was used for Cox regression analysis. The models were adjusted for key clinical variables such as the AJCC stage, lymph node metastasis status, age and sex. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards analyses were conducted to determine whether PIEZO1 expression was an independent prognostic factor. The results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Analysis of the tumor immune microenvironment

The CIBERSORT algorithm was applied using the LM22 signature matrix and 1000 permutations, with quantile normalization disabled as recommended for RNA-seq data, to estimate the proportions of 22 tumor-infiltrating immune cell types in the TCGA-LUAD cohort (n = 541) [20, 21]. Furthermore, the relationship between PIEZO1 expression and immune cell infiltration was investigated. The ESTIMATE algorithm was implemented via the estimate package (version 1.0.13) using the estimateScore function to derive immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores [22]. Correlations between PIEZO1 expression and the expression of key immune checkpoint molecules (PDCD1, CD274, CTLA4, LAG3, TIGIT, and HAVCR2) were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) in R (version 4.1.2, default settings of the stats package) [23].

Gene set enrichment analysis

To further explore the biological processes and pathways associated with PIEZO1 in patients with LUAD, functional enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.0) in R. Specifically, biological processes and pathways were analyzed using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. Pathways and biological processes were deemed enriched if the Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value (p.adjust) was less than 0.05.

For the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), hallmark gene sets from MSigDB version 7.5.1 were applied, including subsets C2: CP (chemical and genetic perturbations), C5: GO (Gene Ontology biological processes) and C7 (immune signatures). GSEA was performed using the fgsea package (version 1.20.0), and a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 0.05 was implemented to ensure statistical significance [24].

Cell culture and RNA isolation

Both the PC9 and A549 cell lines, which are human lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cell lines, were purchased from the Wuhan Purrense Company, and their authenticity was verified through an STR analysis. PC9 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin,and A549 cells were cultured in high-glucose EMEM. All the cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% carbon dioxide.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the cultured cells (such as PC9 or A549 cells) using TRIzol reagent (NCM Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration and purity of the RNA were determined using a NanoDrop pectrophotometer. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Bio, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was then performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Polymer Medicine, China), with gene-specific primers (Table 1) on a CF96 real-time PCR detection system. The reaction conditions included initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 5-s cycles at 95 °C and 30-s cycles at 60 °C. Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from the cells using RIPA lysis buffer (NCM Biotech, China)supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (NCM Biotech, China). The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (NCM Biotech, China). Equal amounts of protein (30–50 μg) were separated on 10% SDS‒PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Vazyme, China). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST at room temperature for 1.5 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies used included anti-PIEZO1 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-beta-actin (1:1000; Proteintech), and anti-PD-L1 (1:1000; Abcam). After being washed, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Xi'an Zhuangzhi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) at room temperature for 1 h. The protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (MedChemExpress, USA), and quantification was performed using ImageJ (version 1.54p) software.

Transwell cell migration assay

To assess the invasion and migration abilities of the cells, migration tests were conducted. Transwell chambers were used to simulate the process by which tumor cells penetrate the basement membrane. In the experiments, cells in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts (Corning) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The lower chambers contained complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After incubation, the nonpenetrating cells on the upper part of the membrane were gently removed with a cotton swab. The cells that migrated through the membrane were subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Psaitech) for 15 min at room temperature, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Psaitech) for 20 min at room temperature. Finally, the stained cells were imaged and counted under an inverted microscope (10 ×) to quantify cellular migration. The cells were divided into negative and positive control groups, and the experiments were repeated at least three times to ensure the reproducibility and reliability of the results. Finally, the data were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.54p).

Immunohistochemistry

A total of 40 patients with lung adenocarcinoma who were surgically treated at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University were included. All diagnoses were pathologically confirmed following surgery. After ethical approval and the signing of confidentiality agreements, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues were sectioned by individuals in pathology department at the hospital. A total of 40 tumor sections and 40 adjacent normal sections were obtained. Immunohistochemical staining was performed by staff at Bai’ao Biotech Changyan Pathology Company using an anti-PIEZO1 primary antibody (1:400; San Ying). PIEZO1 staining was quantified using the IHC Toolbox plugin in ImageJ (version 1.54p). Specifically, the color deconvolution plugin (H DAB vector) was used to separate the applied to DAB-positive staining from hematoxylin counterstaining. A consistent threshold range (0–220) was then applied to set on the DAB image to define positively stained pixels, and the percentage of positively stained area (%area) relative to the total tissue area was calculated as the primary metric for statistical comparison, representing the protein expression level of PIEZO1 in the tissue.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2). Continuous variables following a normal distribution are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, whereas nonnormally distributed data are expressed as the median and interquartile range. The Shapiro‒Wilk test was used to assess normality. For normally distributed data, Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA was used; otherwise, the Mann‒Whitney U test or Kruskal‒Wallis test was applied. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance in all analyses unless otherwise specified, and all the statistical tests were two-sided. Corrections for multiple testing were implemented where appropriate using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method. Data were visualized using the ggplot2 (version 4.1.2), pheatmap (version 4.1.2), and survival (version 3.5.0) packages.

After identifying LUAD as cancer with significantly altered PIEZO1 expression, we next validated this finding through multiplatform genomic analysis. In the TCGA-LUAD data (59 normal and 541 primary tumor samples), the mRNA expression of PIEZO1 was significantly lower in LUAD tissues than in normal tissues (P < 0.05; Fig. 1B). This result was also observed in the GEO dataset (Fig. 1C) and at the protein level in the CPTAC-LUAD dataset (Fig. 1D). To further validate these findings at the cellular level, we compared PIEZO1 expression between LUAD cell lines and normal bronchial epithelial cells. qPCR analysis confirmed that the mRNA expression of PIEZO1 was significantly lower in PC9 and A549 cells than in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 1E–F; S1). Western blot analysis further demonstrated decreased PIEZO1 protein expression in PC9 cells (Fig. 1G–H; original blots in Supplementary WB1). Together, these multiplatform and experimental analyses confirm that PIEZO1 expression is downregulated in LUAD tissues at both the transcriptional and translational levels.

To explore the potential mechanisms underlying the downregulation of PIEZO1 expression, we analyzed the genetic alterations and promoter methylation levels of PIEZO1 in LUAD. The results revealed that PIEZO1 expression was altered in 1.81% of the tissues, with deep deletions being the most frequent alteration type among 1880 patients with LUAD(1.44%, 27 patients) (Supplementary Fig. S1A). In addition, compared with that in normal tissues (n = 32), the promoter methylation levels of PIEZO1 were significantly lower in primary tumor tissues (n = 473) (unpaired two-sample t test, P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. S1B). These observations provide preliminary evidence of mechanisims underlying the dysregulation PIEZO1expression in LUAD tissues.

Associations between PIEZO1 expression and clinicopathological features

To further explore the clinical relevance of PIEZO1 dysregulation, we evaluated the associations between PIEZO1 expression and different clinicopathological features using the TCGA-LUAD dataset. The results revealed that compared with patients without metastasis, PIEZO1 expression was significantly elevated in patients with distant metastasis (M1 stage) (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A). Similarly, higher PIEZO1 expression was observed in patients with larger tumors (T3–T4) than in those with smaller tumors (T1–T2) (Fig. 2B). Futhermore, increased PIEZO1 expression was observed in patients with lymph node metastasis, although this difference was not statistically significant(Fig. 2C). Consistent with these findings, PIEZO1 expression progressively increased with advancing tumor stage, with a statistically significant overall difference among the different stages (P < 0.001; Fig. 2D). Prognostic significance of PIEZO1 in LUAD

To evaluate the prognostic significance of PIEZO1 in LUAD, survival analysis was performed to compare OS between patients stratified by PIEZO1 expression levels. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that patients with high PIEZO1 expression (median split) had significantly shorter overall survival (OS) than patients with low PIEZO1 expression did (HR = 1.49; 95% CI: 1.11–2; P < 0.007; Fig. 3A). These result were validated in the GEO dataset (Fig. 3B). Multivariate Cox regression models adjusting for age, sex, smoking history, and stage, confirmed that high PIEZO1 expression was an independent predictor of shorter OS (HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.01–1.02, P = 0.001), along with advanced T stage (HR = 2.12, P < 0.001) and positive lymph node status (HR = 1.92, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3C).Fig. 3Prognostic significance of PIEZO1 expression in lung adenocarcinoma. To assess the predictive performance of PIEZO1 expression for OS, a time-dependent ROC curve analysis was performed. The AUC values for PIEZO1 expression were 0.55 at 1 year, 0.601 at 3 years, and 0.545 at 5 years (Fig. 3D), indicating its modest predictive accuracy as a standalone marker.

To evaluate the robustness of the prognostic association, we performed sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds. When patients were stratified by tertiles, those in the highest tertile had significantly worse OS than those in the lowest tertile did (HR = 1.61; 95% CI 1.27–2.04; P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. S2A). Similarly, the quartile-based analysis confirmed that patients in the highest quartile (Q4) had poorer prognosis compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (HR = 1.74; 95% CI 1.33–2.27; P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. S2B). When analyzed as a continuous variable, PIEZO1 expression remained significantly associated with OS (HR = 1.02 per unit increase; 95% CI 1.01–1.02; P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. S2C). Furthermore, restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between PIEZO1 expression and mortality risk (Supplementary Fig. S2D), suggesting a threshold-dependent association between thses factors. Collectively, these sensitivity analyses indicate that the prognostic association between PIEZO1 expression and OS is robust and does not depend on the cutoff threshold chosen.

In summary, high PIEZO1 expression is associated with poor prognosis in patients with LUAD. However, the AUC values suggest that PIEZO1 expression alone is unlikely to be clinically useful in predicting prognosis and that this factor may be more valuable when included in multigene panels.

To investigate the cell type-specific expression of PIEZO1, we analyzed two independent single-cell transcriptomic datasets (Fig. 4B). In the GSE139555 dataset, PIEZO1 was most highly expressed in monocytes and macrophages. Consistent results were observed with the GSE99254 dataset. In which, the highest PIEZO1 expression was also observed in monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells.

We next performed an ESTIMATE analysis to evaluate immune and stromal infiltration. Compared with tumors with low PIEZO1 expression, those with high PIEZO1 expression had significantly higher immune scores (1740.50 vs. 1474.4; P = 3.09 × 10⁻5), stromal scores (567.46 vs. 293.20; P = 1.24 × 10⁻⁶), and composite ESTIMATE scores (2307.97 vs. 1767.61; P = 1.36 × 10⁻⁶) (Fig. 4C).

We then evaluated the correlation between PIEZO1 expression and the expression of eight immune checkpoint genes. Spearman correlation analysis revealed that PIEZO1 expression was significantly positively correlated with the expression of all eight genes (all P < 0.01). The strongest positive correlation was observed with the expression of CD276 (B7-H3) (ρ = 0.346; P = 2.61 × 10⁻1⁸); Significant positive correlations were also observed between PIEZO1 expression and the expression of CD274 (PD-L1), PDCD1LG2, PDCD1, HAVCR2, LAG3, TIGIT, and CTLA4 (Fig. 4D).

Preliminary clinical validation of PIEZO1 expression and its association with PD-L1 expression in LUAD tissues

To obtain preliminary clinical validation of our findings, tissues from 40 patients with LUAD were collected from the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University. The surgically removed tissue of all patients underwent complete pathological analysis. We then performed immunohistochemical staining. The results revealed that PIEZO1 expression was significantly lower in tumor tissues than in 40 normal adjacent tissues (Fig. 8A–C; S3). Moreover, the expression levels of PD- L1 in samples from these 40 patients were analyzed. Patients with a TPS ≥ 1% were included in the TPS (+) group, and other patients were included in the TPS (-) group. The expression level of PIEZO1 was lower in the TPS (-) group than in the TPS (+) group (Fig. 8D–F). Discussion

PIEZO1 expression in LUAD: downregulation with paradoxical prognostic associations

Pan-cancer analysis revealed that PIEZO1 expression varies across tumor types. It is highly expressed in gastrointestinal tumors such as gastric adenocarcinoma and colon cancer; however, its expression is significantly downregulated in lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma. This tissue-specific pattern may reflect differences in the physical tumor microenvironment, given that PIEZO1 functions as a mechanosensitive ion channel. Previous studies have reported that PIEZO1 expression levels differ by acocording to the tumor type and microenvironmental context [25, 26].

In this study, we confirmed that PIEZO1 expression is downregulated in LUAD tissues at both the mRNA and protein levels. The mechanisms underlying this downregulation remain unclear. Previous studies have suggested that miR-942-5p may directly target PIEZO1 and reduce its expression [27]. In addition, deep gene deletions have been reported for PIEZO1 in non-small cell lung cancer tissue [28], which is consistent with the results of our genetic alteration analysis.

Our results indicate an apparent paradox. Compared with that in normal lung tissue, PIEZO1 expression is downregulated in LUAD tissue, yet patients with higher PIEZO1 expression have shorter overall survival rate. The potential explanations from the literature include intratumoral heterogeneity, context-dependent activation by mechanical forces, and the dual roles of PIEZO1 in early-stage suppression and late-stage promotion [29–31]. Our clinicopathological analysis revealed that high PIEZO1 expression is associated with metastasis and advanced disease stage, which aligns with these possibilities. However, our data do not provide sufficient direct evidence to determine the appropriate explanations. The observed associations remain correlational, and the reasons underlying this paradoxical finding must be investigated further in dedicated studies.

Associations between PIEZO1 expression and immune-related features

Our findings indicate that PIEZO1 expression is correlated with immune cell infiltration patterns and the expression of multiple immune checkpoint molecules. The positive correlation between PIEZO1 expression and PD-L1 expression, supported by the reduction in the protein expression of PD-L1 upon PIEZO1 knockdown, aligns with prior evidence that PD-L1 plays a key role in immune evasion [32]. However, the immune-related analyses were performed on the basis of bulk RNA-seq and computational deconvolution; no functional immune cell assays or spatial validations were performed. As highlighted by previous work [33], computational immune profiling warrants cautious interpretation. Therefore, future studies are warranted to further verify the relationship between PIEZO1 and the immune microenvironment.

External reports provide context for these associations. For instance, PIEZO1 is highly expressed in macrophages and helps these cells sense mechanical signals such as tissue stiffness and fluid flow. Knockdown of PIEZO1 reduces the number of macrophage [34]. In addition, PIEZO1 expression levels may influence macrophage polarization. Low PIEZO1 expression has been linked to the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, whereas high PIEZO1 expression may enable macrophages to be maintained in an unpolarized M0 state or promote the antitumor M1 phenotype [35]. These observations are consistent with our finding that PIEZO1 expression is positively correlated with the number of M0 macrophages. Zhang et al. reported that PIEZO1 activation upregulates PD-L1 expression in non-small cell lung cancer [36], which is directionally consistent with the reduction in PD-L1 expression we observed upon PIEZO1 knockdown. Importantly, none of these external parallels establish a regulatory mechanism in our dataset; they simply provide a plausible biological context for future studies.

Associations between PIEZO1 expression and EMT-related features

The epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a process by which epithelial cells lose their polarity and adhesion ability while they gain mesenchymal properties. The EMT is considered a key step in tumor cell dissemination and invasion [37]. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly MMP1 and MMP2, play crucial roles in degrading extracellular matrix components and facilitating tumor cell invasion and metastasis [38–41]. In addition, the TGF-β signaling pathway is widely recognized as a central regulator of the epithelial–mesenchymal transition and metastatic progression in lung cancer [42, 43].

In this study, PIEZO1 expression was positively correlated with the expression of EMT-related genes, including transcription factors (SNAI1, SNAI2, and ZEB1), mesenchymal markers (VIM and FN1), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMP2 and MMP9). Furthermore, the pathway analysis revealed enriched PIEZO1 expression in pathways associated with focal adhesion, integrin signaling, and cytoskeletal organization. However, these markers have not been validated at the protein level, or have rescue or overexpression experiments been performed. Therefore, the present data establish only correlations at the gene expression level.

Although our results revealed an association between PIEZO1 expression and the expression of EMT-related genes, and indicated that PIEZO1 knockdown reduces cell migration, these findings do not establish a causal role for PIEZO1 in the EMT. Further functional studies are needed to elucidate these results.

Limitations and future directions

In this study, bioinformatics analysis, in vitro experiments, and clinical sample validation were combinated to expression of investigate PIEZO1 in LUAD tissues. However, this was an exploratory study, and several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, our mechanistic insights remain largely correlational. We did not identify the upstream regulators underlying the downregulation of PIEZO1 in LUAD tissue, nor did we map the downstream signaling pathways through which PIEZO1 may act.

Additionally, the biomarker performance results should be evaluated cautiously. With an AUC of only 0.55–0.60, PIEZO1 alone is unlikely to serve as a clinically useful prognostic marker. In future work, multigene panels that incorporate PIEZO1 in addition to other immune-related or EMT-related markers should be developed.

Our immune-related analyses also have limitations. The findings were derived from bulk RNA-seq data, and computational tools such as CIBERSORT and ESTIMATE offer only estimated immune cell fractions and correlational inferences rather than direct evidence of regulation or function. In terms of immune checkpoints, we focused primarily on PD-L1. Although we observed correlations between PIEZO1 expression and the expression of with TIM-3, LAG-3, TIGIT, and CTLA4, these associations were not experimentally validated.

Additionally, functional validation should be considered, as our in vitro experiments relied on siRNA-mediated knockdown. Overexpression experiments are needed to confirm that increased the expression of PIEZO1 increase migration and PD-L1 expression. In addition, we did not use in vivo animal organoids models, and immune-competent mouse models would be particularly important for evaluating the role of PIEZO1 in tumor immunity.

Finally, the clinical validation cohort comprised only 40 patients from a single center, This sample size is small and may introduce selection bias, and we did not collect peripheral blood samples to assess whether circulating PIEZO1 has potential as a liquid biopsy biomarker.

In summary, this study serves as a preliminary exploration of the biological functions and clinical significance of PIEZO1 in lung adenocarcinoma, providing hypotheses and directions for subsequent research. Future studies focus on advance along mechanistic depth, functional completeness, and translational breadth to further evaluate the potential of PIEZO1 as a therapeutic target or biomarker in LUAD.

Conclusion

This exploratory study demonstrated that PIEZO1 expression is downregulated in LUAD, yet higher PIEZO1 expression correlated with a poor prognosis, the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, and the expression of EMT-related genes. In vitro, PIEZO1 knockdown is associated with reduced cell migration and decreased protein expression of PD-L1. These findings are correlational and do not establish causality. The modest predictive performance of PIEZO1 expression as a standalone marker (AUC of ~ 0.55–0.60) further indicates that it is unlikely to have independent clinical utility at present. These results provide a foundation for future hypothesis-driven investigations into the potential significance of PIEZO1 in LUAD, but underlying mechanism remains to be determined.
