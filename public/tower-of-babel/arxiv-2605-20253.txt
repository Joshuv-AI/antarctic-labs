Distributions of Inversions and Descents over Integer Compositions
Eder G. Santos

Abstract
We derive a generating function for the number of integer compositions of n n into k k parts (i.e., k k -compositions of n n ) with a given number of inversions, and obtain similar results for k k -compositions of n n with a given number of descents. Our approach relies on a known bijection that associates each integer composition σ \sigma with a pair ( π , λ ) (\pi,\lambda) , where π \pi is a permutation and λ \lambda is an integer partition. We show that the distribution of inversions and the distribution of descents over k k -compositions are related, respectively, to the distribution of (maj,inv) and to the distribution of (inv,des) over permutations of { 1 , 2 , … , k } \{1,2,\ldots,k\} , where maj, inv, and des denote the classical permutation statistics major index, inversion number, and descent number, respectively.

Revisiting two joint statistics on permutations

2.1
The distribution of
(\maj,\inv)
over
\mathbf{S}_{k}

In the literature, the distribution of
(\maj,\inv)
over
\mathbf{S}_{k}
, which we denote by
H_{k}(p,q)
, is often related to the expansion of the infinite product

\displaystyle\prod_{m,n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-p^{n}q^{m}t}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{k}(p,q)t^{k}}{(p)_{k}(q)_{k}}.

(1)

Carlitz
[
2
]
was the first to demonstrate that
H_{k}(p,q)
defined by (
1
) is a polynomial in
p
and
q
with integral coefficients. He also derived recurrences to calculate the respective expressions.

Theorem 1

(Carlitz
[
2
]
)
.

Let
H_{k}(p,q)
be defined as in (
1
). Then,
H_{k}(p,q)
is a polynomial of degree
\binom{k}{2}
in both variables with integral coefficients. Additionally, the following recurrence holds:

\displaystyle H_{k}(p,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}p^{j}\frac{(p)_{k-1}}{(p)_{j}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{j}_{q}H_{j}(p,q),

(2)

with initial condition
H_{0}(p,q)=1
.

The connection with permutation statistics was only established later, by Roselle
[
13
]
, who provided a simple combinatorial interpretation for
H_{k}(p,q)
, that can be stated as follows
[
9
, Sol. Ex. 27, Sec. 5.1.1]
:

Theorem 2

(Roselle
[
13
]
)
.

Let
H_{k}(p,q)
be defined as in (
1
). Then,

\displaystyle H_{k}(p,q)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}}p^{\imaj(\pi)}q^{\maj(\pi)}.

Then, Foata and Schützenberger
[
3
]
proved an equivalence between three pairs of permutation statistics, establishing the interpretation of
H_{k}(p,q)
as the distribution of
(\maj,\inv)
over
\mathbf{S}_{k}
.

Theorem 3

(Foata and Schützenberger
[
3
]
)
.

The joint statistics
(\imaj,\maj)
,
(\inv,\imaj)
, and
(\maj,\inv)
have the same symmetric distribution over
\mathbf{S}_{k}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}}p^{\imaj(\pi)}q^{\maj(\pi)}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}}p^{\inv(\pi)}q^{\imaj(\pi)}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}}p^{\maj(\pi)}q^{\inv(\pi)}.

In the study of symmetric functions, the polynomial
H_{k}(p,q)
appears as a special case of a result known in the literature as “Cauchy identity”. The reader may consult MacDonald
[
10
, Sec. I.4]
or Stanley
[
15
, Thm. 7.12.1]
for additional references.

Theorem 4

(Cauchy identity)
.

Let
\{x_{i}\mid i\geq 1\}
and
\{y_{j}\mid j\geq 1\}
be infinite sets of variables, and let
s_{\lambda}
be the Schur function indexed by the integer partition
\lambda
. Then,

\displaystyle\prod_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-x_{i}y_{j}}=\sum_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)s_{\lambda}(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots),

(3)

where
\lambda
runs through all integer partitions.

Using Theorem
4
, Stanley
[
15
, Cor. 7.23.9]
derived an explicit expression for
H_{k}(p,q)
:

Theorem 5

(Stanley
[
15
]
)
.

We have

\displaystyle H_{k}(p,q)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}f^{\lambda}(p)f^{\lambda}(q).

(4)

Proof.

We set
x_{i}=p^{i-1}
and
y_{j}=q^{j-1}t
in (
3
) and use the homogeneity of
s_{\lambda}
to write

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{k}(p,q)t^{k}}{(p)_{k}(q)_{k}}=\prod_{m,n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-p^{n}q^{m}t}=\sum_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(1,p,p^{2},\ldots)s_{\lambda}(1,q,q^{2},\ldots)t^{|\lambda|}.

Grouping the sum by
|\lambda|=k
and comparing the coefficients of
t^{k}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{H_{k}(p,q)}{(p)_{k}(q)_{k}}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}s_{\lambda}(1,p,p^{2},\ldots)s_{\lambda}(1,q,q^{2},\ldots).

Now, using the principal specialization of
s_{\lambda}

[
15
, Cor. 7.21.3]
, given by

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}(1,q,q^{2},\ldots)=\frac{q^{b(\lambda)}}{\prod_{u\in\lambda}(1-q^{h(u)})},

we get

\displaystyle H_{k}(p,q)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}\frac{p^{b(\lambda)}(p)_{k}q^{b(\lambda)}(q)_{k}}{\prod_{u\in\lambda}(1-p^{h(u)})(1-q^{h(u)})},

and (
4
) follows after trivial simplifications.
∎

It is straightforward to verify that (
4
) is a
(p,q)
-analog of the well-known identity

\displaystyle k!=\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}(f^{\lambda})^{2}.

The polynomial
f^{\lambda}(q)
is a
q
-analog of

, given by the classical hook-length formula of Frame, Robinson, and Thrall
[

15
, Cor. 7.21.6]
. Additionally, Stanley
[
15
, Cor. 7.21.5]
obtained a combinatorial interpretation for the coefficients of

by extending the concepts of descent and major index to standard Young tableaux.

\displaystyle=1+(p+p^{2})q+(p+p^{2})q^{2}+p^{3}q^{3},

\displaystyle=1+(p+p^{2}+p^{3})q+(p+2p^{2}+p^{3}+p^{4})q^{2}+(p+p^{2}+2p^{3}+p^{4}+p^{5})q^{3}+

\displaystyle+(p^{2}+p^{3}+2p^{4}+p^{5})q^{4}+(p^{3}+p^{4}+p^{5})q^{5}+p^{6}q^{6}.

2.2
The distribution of
(\inv,\des)

In the literature, the distribution of
(\inv,\des)
over
\mathbf{S}_{k}
, which we denote by
A_{k}(q,t)
, is known as the
q
-Eulerian polynomial, which is a

[

]
proved that the

-Eulerian polynomials satisfy the following identity:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A_{k}(q,t)\frac{z^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}=\frac{1-t}{\qExp_{q}(z(t-1))-t},

(5)

where
\qExp_{q}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}q^{\binom{k}{2}}\frac{z^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}
. We expand this generating function to obtain an explicit expression for
A_{k}(q,t)
in terms of a partition indexed sum, as done in Theorem
5
.

Theorem 6
.

We have
A_{0}(q,t)=1
and, for
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle A_{k}(q,t)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}t^{l(\lambda)-1}(1-t)^{k-l(\lambda)}g^{\lambda}(q).

(6)

Proof.

Let
\qexp_{q}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{z^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}
. Using (
5
) and the well-known identity
\qexp_{q}(-z)\qExp_{q}(z)=1
, it is possible to obtain

\displaystyle 1+t\sum_{k\geq 1}A_{k}(q,t)\frac{z^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}=\left(1-t\sum_{k\geq 1}(1-t)^{k-1}\frac{z^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}\right)^{-1}.

(7)

Expanding the right-hand side as a geometric series and simplifying common terms, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}A_{k}(q,t)\frac{z^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 1}t^{m-1}\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}(1-t)^{k-1}\frac{z^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}\right)^{m}=

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 1}t^{m-1}\sum_{k\geq 1}(1-t)^{k-m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+k_{2}+\cdots+k_{m}=k\\
k_{i}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{[k]_{q}!}{[k_{1}]_{q}![k_{2}]_{q}!\ldots[k_{m}]_{q}!}\frac{z^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}

Now, we rearrange summation indices and note that contributions from all
m
-compositions of
k
corresponding to a fixed
m
-partition are identical, therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}A_{k}(q,t)\frac{z^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{m=1}^{k}t^{m-1}(1-t)^{k-m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash k\\
l(\lambda)=m\end{subarray}}\frac{m![k]_{q}!}{\prod_{i}m_{i}^{\lambda}!([i]_{q}!)^{m_{i}^{\lambda}}}\frac{z^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}=

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}t^{l(\lambda)-1}(1-t)^{k-l(\lambda)}g^{\lambda}(q)\frac{z^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}.

Comparing coefficients of
\frac{z^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}
on both sides yields the result.
∎

Statistics on integer compositions

The main results of this Section are consequences of Theorem

[

[

. But first, we state the following auxiliary result:

Lemma 7
.

Let
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{k})
be a
k
-composition. Let
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\ldots\pi_{k}
be the unique permutation that sorts
\sigma
in weakly decreasing order,
\sigma_{\pi_{1}}\geq\sigma_{\pi_{2}}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{\pi_{k}}
, with repeated entries sorted by increasing order of their indices, i.e.,
\sigma_{\pi_{j}}=\sigma_{\pi_{j+1}}
implies
\pi_{j}<\pi_{j+1}
. Then,

\displaystyle\inv(\pi)

\displaystyle=\inv(\sigma^{R}),

(8a)

\displaystyle\imaj(\pi)

\displaystyle=\comaj(\sigma^{R}),

(8b)

\displaystyle\icomaj(\pi)

\displaystyle=\maj(\sigma^{R}),

(8c)

\displaystyle\ides(\pi)

\displaystyle=\des(\sigma^{R}),

(8d)

where
\sigma^{R}=(\sigma_{k},\sigma_{k-1},\ldots,\sigma_{1})
is the reverse of
\sigma
.

Proof.

We prove that, for a given
d>0
,
\pi^{-1}_{i}>\pi^{-1}_{i+d}
if and only if
\sigma^{R}_{k-i+1}<\sigma^{R}_{k-i-d+1}
. Recall that
\pi^{-1}_{i}
is the position of
i
in
\pi
. Since
\pi
is the permutation that sorts
\sigma
in weakly decreasing order, we have three possibilities: (1)
\sigma_{i}>\sigma_{i+d}
, then
i
appears before
i+d
in
\pi
, therefore
\pi^{-1}_{i}<\pi^{-1}_{i+d}
; (2)
\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i+d}
, then
i
appears before
i+d
in
\pi
, due to the defined tie breaking rule, therefore
\pi^{-1}_{i}<\pi^{-1}_{i+d}
; (3)
\sigma_{i}<\sigma_{i+d}
, then
i
appears after
i+d
in
\pi
, therefore
\pi^{-1}_{i}>\pi^{-1}_{i+d}
. Hence,
\pi^{-1}_{i}>\pi^{-1}_{i+d}
if and only if
\sigma_{i}<\sigma_{i+d}
, which is equivalent to
\sigma^{R}_{k-i+1}<\sigma^{R}_{k-i-d+1}
. If

, we conclude that
i
is a descent of
\pi^{-1}
if and only if

is a descent of
\sigma^{R}
, proving (

), (

), and (

). If we let

, we conclude that
(i,j)
is an inversion of
\pi^{-1}
if and only if
(k-j+1,k-i+1)
is an inversion of
\sigma^{R}
. Then, since
\inv(\pi^{-1})=\inv(\pi)
, Equation (
8a
) follows.

Theorem 8

(MacMahon
[
11
]
)
.

Let
\sigma
be a
k
-composition, let
\lambda
be a
k
-partition, and let
\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}
. Then, there exists a bijective map
\sigma\mapsto(\pi,\lambda)
such that
\pi
is the unique permutation defined in Lemma
7
and
|\lambda|+\maj(\pi)=|\sigma|.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{k})
be a
k
-composition and let
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\ldots\pi_{k}
be the respective unique permutation defined in Lemma
7
. We build a
k
-partition
\lambda
as follows. First, let
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{k})=(\sigma_{\pi_{1}},\sigma_{\pi_{2}},\ldots,\sigma_{\pi_{k}})
. Then, for

, subtract from each
\mu_{j}
the number of descents of
\pi
appearing to the right of or at position

in the one-line notation of
\pi
. We still have
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{k}\geq 1
because
\mu_{j}
is strictly greater than
\mu_{j+1}
whenever
\pi_{j}>\pi_{j+1}
. After this procedure, the total reduction in
\mu
is
\maj(\pi)
, resulting in
\mu^{\prime}
, and taking
\lambda=\mu^{\prime}
yields
|\lambda|+\maj(\pi)=|\sigma|
.
Conversely, let
\lambda
be a
k
-partition and
\pi
be a permutation of
[\mathbf{k}]
. We can proceed backwards computing the integers
\mu_{i}
from
\lambda
and
\pi
, and then reconstruct the composition
\sigma
by applying
\pi^{-1}
to
\mu
. It is straightforward to see that the resulting
\sigma
will be a
k
-composition satisfying
|\sigma|=|\lambda|+\maj(\pi)
.
∎

Example 9
.

We apply the bijection described in the proof of Theorem
8
to the composition
\sigma=(4,2,1,2,1,5,3)
. In this case,
|\sigma|=18
,
\pi=6172435
,
D(\pi)=\{1,3,5\}
,
\maj(\pi)=9
, and
\mu=(5,4,3,2,2,1,1)
. Then,
\lambda=(2,2,1,1,1,1,1)
and
|\lambda|=9
. Conversely, if we start with
\pi=6172435
and
\lambda=(2,2,1,1,1,1,1)
, we get
\mu=(5,4,3,2,2,1,1)
. Applying
\pi^{-1}=2465713
to
\mu
, we retrieve
\sigma=(4,2,1,2,1,5,3)
.

MacMahon used Theorem
8
to prove that the distribution of
\maj
over
\mathbf{S}_{k}
is equal to
[k]_{q}!
, the same distribution of
\inv
, giving a bijective proof of the following generating function identity:

\displaystyle\frac{q^{k}}{(1-q)^{k}}=[k]_{q}!\frac{q^{k}}{(q)_{k}}.

(9)

The right-hand side of (
9
) is the generating function for the number of
k
-compositions of
n
, while the left-hand side is the product of the distribution of
\maj
over
\mathbf{S}_{k}
and the generating function for the number of
k
-partitions of
n
.

We are now ready to state the main results.

Theorem 10
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbf{C}_{k}}p^{|\sigma|}q^{\inv(\sigma)}t^{\comaj(\sigma)}u^{\maj(\sigma)}v^{\des(\sigma)}=\frac{p^{k}}{(p)_{k}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}}p^{\maj(\pi)}q^{\inv(\pi)}t^{\imaj(\pi)}u^{\icomaj(\pi)}v^{\ides(\pi)}.

(10)

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be a
k
-composition of
n
, let
\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}
, and let
\lambda
be a
k
-partition of
n
. Since the map
\sigma\mapsto\sigma^{R}
is an involution on
\mathbf{C}_{k}
, the distribution of
(|\cdot|,\inv,\comaj,\maj,\des)
over
\mathbf{C}_{k}
is the same whether we sum over all
\sigma
or over all
\sigma^{R}
. By Theorem
8
and Lemma
7
, this distribution can be obtained by counting pairs
(\pi,\lambda)
that have specified values of

\inv(\pi)
,
\imaj(\pi)
,
\icomaj(\pi)
, and
\ides(\pi)
, and, simultaneously, satisfy
\maj(\pi)+|\lambda|=n
. Thus, in terms of generating functions, Equation (
10
) holds.
∎

It is straightforward to see that Theorem
10
generalizes (
9
) and extends naturally to every composition statistic that can be expressed in terms of a corresponding statistic on the unique permutation defined in Lemma
7
.

Corollary 11
.

Let
IC_{q}(p,k)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{r\geq 0}ic_{r}(n,k)p^{n}q^{r}
and
IC_{q}(p)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{r\geq 0}ic_{r}(n)p^{n}q^{r}
be the ordinary bivariate generating functions of
ic_{r}(n,k)
and
ic_{r}(n)
, respectively. Then,

\displaystyle IC_{q}(p,k)=\frac{p^{k}}{(p)_{k}}\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}f^{\lambda}(p)f^{\lambda}(q),

(11)

\displaystyle IC_{q}(p)=\sum_{\mu}\frac{p^{|\mu|}}{(p)_{|\mu|}}f^{\mu}(p)f^{\mu}(q),

(12)

where
\mu
runs through all integer partitions. Additionally, the following recurrence holds:

\displaystyle IC_{q}(p,k)=\frac{p^{k}}{1-p^{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{j}_{q}IC_{q}(p,j),

(13)

with initial condition
IC_{q}(p,0)=1
.

Proof.

By Theorem
10
,

\displaystyle IC_{q}(p,k)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbf{C}_{k}}p^{|\sigma|}q^{\inv(\sigma)}=\frac{p^{k}}{(p)_{k}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}}p^{\maj(\pi)}q^{\inv(\pi)}=\frac{p^{k}}{(p)_{k}}H_{k}(p,q).

(14)

Replacing (
4
) in (
14
) yields (
11
). Summing (
11
) over all possible
k
results in (
12
). Equation (
13
) follows after trivial manipulations in (
2
).
∎

It is well-known that
(|\cdot|,\maj)
and
(|\cdot|,\inv)
have the same distribution over
\mathbf{C}_{k}
, which is, therefore, given by (
11
). It is not difficult to see that
(|\cdot|,\comaj)
also shares the same distribution. Therefore, from the list of composition statistics given in Lemma
7
, it remains to analyze the distribution of
(|\cdot|,\des)
over
\mathbf{C}_{k}
.

Corollary 12
.

Let
DC_{t}(q,k)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{r\geq 0}dc_{r}(n,k)q^{n}t^{r}
and
DC_{t}(q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{r\geq 0}dc_{r}(n)q^{n}t^{r}
be the ordinary bivariate generating functions of
dc_{r}(n,k)
and
dc_{r}(n)
, respectively. Then,
DC_{t}(q,0)=1
and, for
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle DC_{t}(q,k)=\frac{q^{k}}{(q)_{k}}\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}t^{l(\lambda)-1}(1-t)^{k-l(\lambda)}g^{\lambda}(q),

(15)

\displaystyle DC_{t}(q)=1+\sum_{\mu}\frac{q^{|\mu|}}{(q)_{|\mu|}}t^{l(\mu)-1}(1-t)^{|\mu|-l(\mu)}g^{\mu}(q)=\frac{1-t}{\sum_{j\geq 0}q^{\binom{j+1}{2}}\frac{(t-1)^{j}}{(q)_{j}}-t},

(16)

where
\mu
runs through all integer partitions, except the empty partition.

Proof.

By Theorem
10
,

\displaystyle DC_{t}(q,k)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbf{C}_{k}}q^{|\sigma|}t^{\des(\sigma)}=\frac{q^{k}}{(q)_{k}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}}q^{\maj(\pi)}t^{\ides(\pi)}.

(17)

It is well-known that the Foata bijection
[
3
]

\phi:\mathbf{S}_{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{S}_{k}
satisfies, for all
\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}
,
\maj(\pi)=\inv(\phi(\pi))
and
D(\pi^{-1})=D((\phi(\pi))^{-1})
. Therefore, Equation (
17
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle DC_{t}(q,k)=\frac{q^{k}}{(q)_{k}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}}q^{\inv(\pi)}t^{\des(\pi)}=\frac{q^{k}}{(q)_{k}}A_{k}(q,t).

(18)

Replacing (
6
) in (
18
) yields (
15
). Summing (
15
) over all possible
k
is equivalent to set
z=\frac{q}{1-q}
in (
5
), resulting in (
16
).
∎

The last expression in Equation (
16
) is not new, as it is stated in a more general form by Heubach and Mansour
[
7
, Thm. 4.3]
.

To compute the coefficients of
p^{n}q^{r}
in the generating function (
12
), for instance, one must sum the expression in (
11
) over all partitions
\lambda
satisfying
|\lambda|\leq n
, and then extract the desired coefficient. Obviously, after this procedure, the coefficients of
p^{i}q^{j}
for all
j
and for all
i<n
will also be correct. Since the number of partitions of
n
grows exponentially, this computation becomes increasingly difficult as
n
increases, but it is still more efficient than running through all
2^{n-1}
compositions of
n
and computing the distribution of
\inv
directly. The same reasoning applies to (
16
).

Remark 13
.

Among the several other distributions of joint statistics on
\mathbf{C}_{k}
that follow from Theorem
10
, we highlight the distribution of
(|\cdot|,\comaj,\des)
, denoted by
B_{k}(p,q,t)
. It is a known result, due to Gessel
[
5
, Thm. 8.4]
, that

\displaystyle A_{k}(p,q,t)\coloneqq\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}}p^{\inv(\pi)}q^{\maj(\pi)}t^{\des(\pi)}

(19)

has the following generating function:

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{A_{k}(p,q,t)u^{k}}{(t;q)_{k+1}[k]_{p}!}=\sum_{k\geq 0}t^{k}\qexp_{p}(u)\qexp_{p}(qu)\cdots\qexp_{p}(q^{k}u),

(20)

where
(t;q)_{k+1}=(1-t)(1-tq)\cdots(1-tq^{k})
and
\qexp_{p}(u)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{u^{k}}{[k]_{p}!}
. Now, if we set
\pi\rightarrow\pi^{-1}
and use inverse Foata bijection
[
3
]
, recalling that it preserves the descent set of
\pi^{-1}
, we can rewrite (
19
) as

\displaystyle A_{k}(p,q,t)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathbf{S}_{k}}p^{\maj(\pi)}q^{\imaj(\pi)}t^{\ides(\pi)}.

(21)

Then, by Theorem
10
,

\displaystyle B_{k}(p,q,t)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbf{C}_{k}}p^{|\sigma|}q^{\comaj(\sigma)}t^{\des(\sigma)}=\frac{p^{k}}{(p)_{k}}A_{k}(p,q,t).

Setting
u=\frac{p}{1-p}
in (
20
) yields an expression involving
B_{k}(p,q,t)
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{B_{k}(p,q,t)}{(t;q)_{k+1}}=\sum_{k\geq 0}t^{k}\qexp_{p}\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\qexp_{p}\left(\frac{qp}{1-p}\right)\cdots\qexp_{p}\left(\frac{q^{k}p}{1-p}\right).

(22)
