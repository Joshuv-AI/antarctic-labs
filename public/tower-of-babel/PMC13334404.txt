Targeted gene editing of PCCA pseudoexon using CRISPR-Cas12a for potential therapy in propionic acidemia
Authors: Mar Álvarez, José V del Álamo, Eva Richard, Lourdes R Desviat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep-intronic variants activating pseudoexons (PEs) are a common cause for monogenic diseases. Removal of the PE region predictably corrects the splicing defect, offering a potential therapeutic strategy. Previous studies, including our own, have identified an 84-bp PE in intron 14 of the gene PCCA that is included in the mature mRNA at relatively high basal levels across all tissues. When activated by the c.1285-1416A>G variant, this PE becomes fully included, ultimately causing the potentially lethal neurometabolic disorder propionic acidemia due to the deficiency of propionyl-CoA carboxylase (PCC) enzyme. In this study, we explored, through a CRISPR-Cas12-assisted non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)-mediated approach, whether PE removal or abrogation of the splice enhancer strengthened by the variant could efficiently restore normal transcript and functional PCCA protein. Both in wild-type hepatoma cells and in an edited cellular model homozygous for the activating variant, we show that the CRISPR-Cas12a approach increases PCC activity, with the highest values obtained with a guide RNA (gRNA) targeting the enhancer region in the PEs. The results provide proof-of-concept of its therapeutic potential for patients with PE activation and those with hypomorphic missense variants in whom residual activity levels may be effectively raised.Keywords: MT: RNA/DNA editing, propionic acidemia, pseudoexon, Cas12a, gene editing, splicing, deep-intronic

Introduction

Genetic variants affecting splicing significantly contribute to the mutational spectrum in many monogenic diseases.1 Overall, variants affecting consensus splice sites at the exon-intron junctions (consensus 3′ and 5′ splice sites [ss], polypyrimidine tract, and branchpoint) account for 10%–30% of the pathogenic alleles in different genes.2 However, recent estimates indicate a higher proportion of splicing variants, as the implementation of transcriptome and whole-genome sequencing in clinical practice has led to the increasing identification of splicing defects, arising both from exonic and intronic variants.3,4 Deep-intronic variants can activate pseudoexons (PEs), non-functional intronic sequences with cryptic splice sites, thus resembling exons, but normally not included in the mature transcript. In many cases, PEs are naturally included in basal levels, resulting in variable amounts of unproductive splicing, which may serve to regulate gene expression. The resulting aberrant transcripts often contain premature termination codons (PTCs), which target the mRNA for degradation via nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD).5,6 PEs are recognized as high-risk sites in the human genome as they can be easily activated with variants creating/strengthening splice sites or splice enhancers, thus causing genetic disease.7

Pre-mRNA splicing can be therapeutically modulated using splice switching antisense oligonucleotides (SSOs),8 and these compounds have proven to be safe, having successfully reached the clinic.9 PEs are ideal candidates for SSO therapy as the natural splice sites remain intact. Proof-of-concept of therapeutic splicing correction by blocking PE inclusion with SSO has been shown in patient-derived cells in a wide range of disorders.10,11,12 One of the best examples is that of an 84-bp PE (13:100305751-100305834; GRCh38) in intron 14 of the PCCA gene (GenBank: NM_000282) that codes for one of the two subunits of the propionyl-CoA carboxylase enzyme (PCC, Expasy: E.C.6.4.1.3). PCC deficiency, caused by mutations in PCCA or PCCB, results in propionic acidemia (PA, OMIM: 606054, Orphanet: ORPHA35), a life-threatening organic acidemia, in which the accumulation of toxic metabolites and absence of downstream ones result in a multisystemic disease with neurological alterations, frequent pancreatitis, and cardiac complications.13,14 Basal inclusion levels of the PCCA PE in wild-type (WT) cell lines were first described by the group of Dr. Gravel,15 representing low levels of unproductive splicing. This was further analyzed and validated by bioinformatic analysis of RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data.6 Inclusion of the PCCA PE introduces PTCs, triggering NMD or leading to translation of a truncated protein. The PCCA PE is activated by the deep-intronic sequence variant c.1285-1416A>G (13:100305776; GRCh38), identified in a PA patient with a severe phenotype, in accordance with the predicted loss of function of the aberrant transcript, which abolishes PCC activity16 This variant disrupts an heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A1 (hnRNP A1)-binding splice silencer and simultaneously strengthens a splice enhancer (ESE), which explains the complete activation of PE inclusion.11 SSO-mediated blocking of PCCA PE results in the recovery of normal splicing, PCCA and PCCB proteins, and PCC up to WT levels.11,16 Interestingly, SSO blocking of the non-activated WT PE results in increased activity of PCC in patient fibroblasts with pathogenic PCCA or PCCB hypomorphic missense variants,11 a representative example of what has been named as targeted augmentation of nuclear gene output (TANGO).6 This refers to SSO-mediated blocking of a naturally occurring non-productive alternative splicing event, with great potential to increase functional gene expression for haploinsufficiency disorders, as in Dravet syndrome, or those caused by hypomorphic alleles.6,17

Despite their potential, the main limitation of SSO-based therapies and mRNA replacement therapy, currently in clinical trials for PA,18 is the requirement for recurring and lifelong administration. This has spurred interest in clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) gene editing therapies, which would possibly enable permanent splicing correction. In the case of deep-intronic PE activation variants, the deletion of the PE region or the ablation of its splicing signals by means of genome editing strategies has been shown to rescue the normal splicing in different gene defects.19,20,21,22,23

In this study, we evaluated different gene editing approaches targeting the PCCA PE, using CRISPR-Cas12a in HepG2 cellular models, to provide proof-of-concept of its potential application to correct the disease-associated molecular pathology. We designed pairs of Cas12a guide RNAs (gRNAs) to create targeted double-stranded breaks (DSBs) in PCCA intron 14 at either side of the PE or used a single gRNA targeting the splice regulatory sequence activated by the c.1285-1416A>G variant. We performed in-depth characterization of the genomic modifications at the on-target sites and screened potential off-targets. To explore the potential clinical translation of the approach, we also evaluated the use of the best-performing gRNA in combination with Cas12a delivered as mRNA. The results underscore the potential of CRISPR-Cas12a as a viable therapeutic option for addressing the underlying genetic defects in a subset of PA patients.

Results

Design and testing of gRNAs in HepG2 cells show high editing efficiency within intron 14 of PCCA

The inspection of PCCA intron 14 regions surrounding the PE did not reveal any NGG protospacer adjacent motifs (PAMs) recognized by SpCas9. Instead, the presence of several TTTV motifs allowed the design of predicted high-score gRNAs to target the genomic region with Cas12a nuclease. Two gRNAs (designated 1 and 3) upstream the PE, one downstream (gRNA 22), and one within the PE, targeting the ESE activated by variant c.1285-1416A>G (gRNA 15), were combined in pairs with Cas12a to evaluate five different dual DSB (Figure 1A). The dual gRNA-Cas12a approach aimed to delete a segment of genomic DNA of ∼100–300 bp encompassing the entire PE. In addition, gRNA15 was used alone with Cas12a, with the aim of generating small indels in the targeted region, altering or eliminating the ESE that activates the PE. Both approaches are expected to reinstall correct splicing of the corresponding PCCA transcript.

Gel analysis of PCR products spanning the target region from the transfected bulk cells was performed 120 h post-transfection. Combinations of gRNAs 1 + 22 and 3 + 22 were the most efficient (53%–70% deletion), as estimated by gel densitometry. In contrast, transfection of gRNAs 1 + 15 or 3 + 15 resulted in low overall editing efficiencies (3%–9% deletion) (Figure 1B).

Targeted editing of the intronic region rescues PCCA splicing defect

To assess whether the targeted deletion of the PE by the different gRNA pairs or by gRNA15 ESE cleavage rescued PCCA splicing, we performed reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) analysis in the edited samples, both in WT and c.1285-1416A>G HepG2 cells. We compared the results in cells treated with or without puromycin, which is used to inhibit NMD. In puromycin-treated WT cells, the PE-containing band was even more prominent than the correctly spliced band (which represented only 43% of the total transcripts by PCR) (Figure 2A), confirming that the PE-containing band is subjected to NMD, as previously shown.11 After CRISPR-Cas12a editing, we observed a decrease in the intensity of the PE-containing band with all gRNA pairs tested; however, the most pronounced effect corresponded to the use of single gRNA15 that predictably abolished the activated ESE. In this case, the level of correctly spliced transcripts reached 84.5% in puromycin-treated cells (Figure 2A). In c.1285-1416A>G HepG2 cells, only ∼5% of correctly spliced transcripts were detectable, which increased to 35% after CRISPR-Cas12a treatment with gRNA15 and puromycin. All the gRNA pairs used in c.1285-1416A>G HepG2 cells increased the correctly spliced band, up to 14%–24% total transcript levels (Figure 2B). In every editing experiment, RT-PCR analysis did not show any unexpected transcripts in the region, indicating that the deletions induced by the dual gRNA approach did not generate novel splice sites. To more accurately quantify the extent of PE exclusion (correct splicing), quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) assays were performed. The results obtained corroborate previous findings, showing that gRNA15 alone results in the highest levels of PE exclusion, in both WT and mutant cells (Figures 2C and 2D).

CRISPR-Cas12a editing increases both PCCA and PCCB levels and rescues PCC activity

The targeted deletion of PE region or the ESE abrogation with gRNA15 and the corresponding splicing rescue should result in the recovery of PCCA expression, as well as in the rescue of PCCB, which is unstable in the absence of PCCA.24 We, therefore, performed western blot analysis in CRISPR-Cas12a transfected cells. We observed no significant difference in PCCA or PCCB levels in WT HepG2 cells with any of the treatments, probably due to the limited sensitivity of the technique (Figure 3A).

In untreated c.1285-1416A>G HepG2 cells, PCCA and PCCB were barely detectable (Figure 3B). The use of different dual gRNA combinations with Cas12a resulted in PCCA and PCCB recovery up to 15% relative to WT levels, while with gRNA15, the levels reached up to 20% (Figure 3C). By increasing PCCA and PCCB levels, PCC activity was thereby increased, which could be detected in WT cells with some gRNA combinations, not reaching statistical significance (Figure 3D). In the case of c.1285-1416A>G cells, PCC activity increased above detection levels in all cases, reaching up to 30% WT levels with gRNA15 (Figure 3E).

There were no significant differences in splicing correction using the WT or variant-specific gRNA15 in c.1285-1416A>G mutant cells (Figure S1).

On- and off-target analyses reveal a variety of DNA repair outcomes and no changes at top predicted off-targets

To characterize gRNA15-mediated Cas12a nuclease cleavage and subsequent DNA repair outcomes, we analyzed by next generation sequencing (NGS) the resulting PCR product of the PE-containing region in transfected c.1285-1416A>G cells. The analysis detected a spectrum of end-joining repair variants. Overall, 46.8% of the alleles were edited, with most of the repair events presenting 6- to 7-bp deletions around the predicted cut sites (Figure 4). No insertions were detected in sequences with >0.1% of the reads. Close inspection of the sequence revealed that all the observed deletions abrogated an ESE motif in the cut site, while the ESE activated by the c.1285-1416A>G variant was disrupted in approximately 40% of the alleles.

Unlike Cas9 that creates blunt DSB, Cas12a cuts the target strand (with the PAM) around 18 nucleotides away from the PAM’s 3′ end and the non-target strand typically 23 nucleotides from the PAM, producing a staggered cut with a 5′ overhang. In the deletion sequences, we observed different modifications surrounding the joint sequence. The results are summarized in Table 1. When comparing PAM orientations, we observed the best efficiency in PE region deletion with PAM-out/PAM-in combinations (gRNAs 1 + 22 and 3 + 22), as also observed by gel electrophoresis (Figure 1B) and corresponding to a deletion size of ∼230–270 bp. The worst efficiency was observed for PAM-out/PAM-out combinations (gRNAs 1 + 15 and 3 + 15) (Table 1; Figure 1B).

In addition to assessing on-target editing, we aimed to investigate the specificity of the different CRISPR-Cas12a combinations by Sanger sequencing of potential off-target regions in every experiment in which the gRNA had been used, alone (in the case of gRNA15) or in combination. No off-target events were detected in top predicted off-target sites, all of which presented 3–4 mismatches to the corresponding gRNA (Figures S2, S3, S4, and S5).

Cas12a delivered as mRNA with gRNA15 results in therapeutic levels of splice correction

From a translational perspective, gene editing outcomes rely on efficient delivery to target tissues/organs, with a plethora of viral and non-viral systems being currently explored.25 In many cases, the gene editor is delivered as mRNA along with the corresponding gRNA, encapsulated in lipid nanoparticles, following the success of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines.25 Therefore, we sought to explore the efficiency of Cas12a delivered as mRNA along with gRNA15 that had shown the best results in protein and activity recovery. In addition, this strategy offers the advantage of relying on a single DSB contrary to the use of dual gRNA combinations. After mRNA/gRNA15 transfection, the splicing profile of c.1285-1416A>G HepG2 cells treated with puromycin (Figure 5A) was comparable to that observed in previous experiments using Cas12a delivered as an RNP complex (Figure 2B), showing approximately 30% correctly spliced PE-exclusion product. Western blot analysis showed recovery of PCCA and PCCB up to 10%–15% of WT levels (Figure 5C). PCC activity showed increases from non-detectable to 10%–15% levels relative to WT HepG2 cells (Figure 5D).

CRISPR-Cas12a gene editing approach results in a level of functional rescue comparable to SSO-based treatment

For a more comprehensive evaluation of the designed gene editing strategy, we compared the effect of CRISPR-Cas12 editing with a previously validated hit SSO targeting the PCCA PE (Table S6, SSO2 described previously11). We performed splicing analysis by RT-PCR and western blotting to assess protein levels and enzymatic activity assays to compare SSO with gene editing strategies, using both RNP gRNA15 or Cas12a mRNA-gRNA15.

Hit SSO transfection in c.1285 + 1416A>G HepG2 cells resulted in approximately 77% of correctly spliced transcripts upon puromycin treatment (Figure S6). Western blot analysis showed a substantial recovery of PCCA and PCCB levels relative to those observed in WT cells (Figure S6). These values were higher than those obtained with Cas12a gene editing, although a marked recovery of both proteins was observed with both strategies. PCC activity in HepG2 c.1285-1416A>G cells was nearly non-detectable (7 ± 3 pmol/min/mg) and increased to near 50% relative to WT cells after SSO treatment (1082 ± 71 versus 2142 ± 633 pmol/min/mg in WT cells), comparable to the value obtained with Cas12a-gRNA15 RNP (720 ± 153 pmol/min/mg), albeit higher than that obtained with Cas12a mRNA-gRNA15, (183 ± 41 pmol/min/mg).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of CRISPR-Cas12a for mediating PE disruption in PCCA. PCCA intron 14 PE is activated by the c.1285-1416A>G variant identified in a PA patient.16 However, the PE shows relatively high levels of inclusion also in the WT context; so, decreasing or abrogating its inclusion can be exploited as a gene regulatory switch, effectively upregulating PCCA and PCCB levels and thus increasing enzyme activity. We recently demonstrated this using patient fibroblasts harboring hypomorphic PCCA and PCCB missense variants transfected with an SSO targeting the PE,11 which, for clinical translation, presents the disadvantage of requiring lifelong re-administration. Hence, this PE is a therapeutically relevant target for a sustainable CRISPR-based one-time gene therapy approach.

Transfection of dual gRNA-Cas12a RNP in HepG2 cells resulted in efficient excision of the PCCA PE genomic region. DNA repair of two DSBs via non-homologous end joining repair (NHEJ) and/or microhomology-mediated end-joining (MMEJ), which are the main pathways for repair of DSBs, has been shown to reproducibly result in the deletion of the genomic sequence between the two target sites.26 This strategy has been applied using Cas9 for exon and PE skipping approaches with a therapeutic aim,19,22,27,28 as well as for disease modeling.29 In the latter study, using K562 cells electroporated with plasmids containing gRNA and Cas9 sequences, PAM orientation of the gRNAs was reported to affect the editing results. The best efficiencies in the deletion of the sequences between both cut sites were obtained with gRNAs with PAM-in/PAM-in orientations.29 In our study, best efficiencies were observed for gRNA pairs with PAM-out/PAM-in orientations. As every CRISPR-Cas approach differs regarding genetic locus, potential off-targets, cell- or tissue-specific context, and precise CRISPR tools utilized, our work adds to previous knowledge of the NHEJ DNA repair process after CRIPSR-Cas12a editing. We show, for the first time, the in vitro efficacy and safety of correcting aberrant splicing caused by PE inclusion, in this case using Cas12a, both delivered as protein in an RNP complex and as mRNA, and transfection in a hepatoma model of a rare inherited metabolic disease.

CRISPR-Cas12a targeted excision of the PCCA PE region reached up to 60%–70% levels, depending on the pair of gRNAs used (Figure 1). As a result, for some gRNA pairs, normal splicing was restored and functional protein was produced at levels (15%–20%) considered therapeutically relevant for this recessive disease (Figure 3). gDNA editing efficiency and splicing rescue were not always comparable, as observed for the use of gRNA1 + 22 and 3 + 22 in HepG2 c.1285-1416A>G cells (see Figures 1 and 2). This may be explained by alterations in splicing regulatory signals caused by the resulting large deletions, by allele specific expression, or by the presence of mRNA molecules transcribed before editing. A similar observation of disparate efficiencies at the DNA and transcript levels after gene editing has been reported previously.23

We postulated that NHEJ- and MMEJ-induced indels near the pathogenic deep-intronic c.1285-1416A>G variant would cause the disruption of the splice enhancers that favor PE inclusion, thereby reinstalling correct splicing. Indeed, with gRNA15 with a predicted cut site near the variant, we observed the highest splicing correction levels and PCC activity recovery up to 30% compared with WT levels. We confirmed previous reports indicating that a mismatch in the gRNA is tolerated,28 as there were no significant differences in using the WT or variant-specific gRNA15 in HepG2 c.1285-1416A>G mutant cells. Of all the strategies tested, the simplicity of a single gRNA approach makes it the best candidate for future translational development. It does not require a donor template and involves a single DSB, not inducing the deletion of a large genomic fragment, thus reducing the number of potential off-targets and the chance of chromosomal aberrations. Off-target assessment, limited to top predicted sites with 3–4 mismatches to the gRNA sequence, showed specificity of all the used gRNAs, although more sensitive unbiased whole-genome sequencing methods would be required for future research stages.

To our knowledge, none of published studies have reported genome editing therapy for PA. Other research efforts have centered on PE skipping, specifically targeting the conserved splice sites or the activated ESE by use of SSO.11,16 In our study, we benchmarked best-performing gRNA15 with Cas12a delivered as protein or as mRNA with previously optimized SSO treatment.11 While SSO proved to be more effective in restoring correct splicing in the conditions used, functional rescue at the level of PCCA and PCCB and the PCC activity were comparable. Moreover, gene editing approaches potentially provide a one-time treatment and permanent correction, while SSO requires lifelong re-administration.

Dual mRNA therapy coding for the PCCA and PCCB subunits, which have to be expressed in a concerted manner, is currently being tested in clinical trials.18 While therapeutically promising, its transient effect is a main challenge, as for SSO. In recent years, the focus of gene therapies for monogenic diseases has shifted to gene editing strategies. The ultimate goal of gene editing is to enable in vivo delivery, while maintaining high efficacy and a favorable safety profile. In PA, liver is the major organ of propionate metabolism, acting as a metabolic sink for accumulated toxic metabolites. Our results represent the proof-of-concept of the feasibility of the gene editing approach, albeit being limited to the use of a hepatoma cell model. We observed a modest recovery of enzyme activity, which must be further optimized preclinically before in vivo translation. In a previous work, we showed that 30% PCC activity is enough to restore normal metabolite levels in a cellular model11 and, in vivo, it was shown that the symptoms of PA can be alleviated with just a small increase (10%–20%) in liver PCC activity.30 Currently, there is no in vivo model for the PE inclusion defect. Intronic sequences are not conserved between mouse and humans; so, a humanized model with the whole-human genomic sequence would have to be generated, with confirmation of the splicing defect and subsequent testing of Cas12a mRNA and hit gRNA using a liver-directed delivery system. For successful application of the therapeutic approach described here, it should be noted that patient-specific dose adjustments are also possible, as shown in the recent n-of-1 gene editing trial targeting a liver inherited metabolic disease.31 Following the clinical protocol, a second dose of the gene editor was given after three weeks, given the incomplete biochemical correction in the patient.31

All current therapeutic gene editing strategies, including NHEJ, homology-directed repair (HDR), base editing, and prime editing, have been evaluated for several inherited metabolic diseases (phenylketonuria, tyrosinemia type 1, and ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency) with liver as target organ.25 Four liver-directed gene editing therapies—for familial hypercholesterolemia, OTC deficiency, transthyretin amyloidosis, and hereditary angioedema—have entered clinical trials.32,33,34 A seminal study reported the life-saving liver-directed gene editing therapy developed in 6 months for a single patient known as “KJ,” with a severe urea cycle disorder.31 CRISPR-Cas12a is being employed in multiple clinical trials. In Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), its application has demonstrated restoration of muscle function to near WT levels (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06594094). Additionally, clinical studies involving this system are underway for sickle cell disease and transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia, currently in phase 1/2. Furthermore, an early phase 1 study investigating CRISPR-Cas12a in primary hyperoxaluria type 1 (PH1) has reported promising preliminary results.35

Overall, our work validates the use of CRISPR-Cas12a system as an approach for correcting splicing defects caused by aberrant PE inclusion in PA, using hepatoma cell models of the disease, and it demonstrates the higher efficacy of the use of a single gRNA targeting ESE as compared with a dual gRNA PE-excision strategy. The approach may be applicable for patients with PE-activating variants or with hypomorphic missense PCCA or PCCB variants, given the high levels of WT PE inclusion that result in unproductive splicing. These findings pave the way to develop potential treatments for PA and can also be applied to other diseases in which deep-intronic variants have been identified.

Methods

Cell culture

Human hepatoma HepG2 cells, WT or homozygous for the PCCA c.1285-1416A>G variant,11 were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% glutamine, and 0.1% antibiotic mix (penicillin/streptomycin) at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 95% relative humidity.

Splicing analysis by RT-PCR and RT-qPCR

Cells were treated with puromycin (200 μg/mL) (Sigma Aldrich) 5 h before harvesting to inhibit NMD. Cell lysis was performed by adding TRIzol reagent (Ambion). After homogenization of cell lysates, total RNA was extracted by phase separation with chloroform and precipitation in isopropanol. cDNA synthesis was performed using the NZY First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (NZYtech) with 1 μg of total RNA. Splicing analysis was carried out by PCR amplification with FastStart Taq Polymerase (Roche), using specific primers (PCCA exon 13F and PCCA exon 16R) (Table S1). PCR products were analyzed by 3% agarose gel electrophoresis, and PE inclusion was estimated by gel densitometry using FIJI/ImageJ software.

cDNA synthesis for RT-qPCR was performed using 500 ng of total RNA. Quantitative real-time PCR (real-time qPCR) was carried out using a CFX384 system (Bio-Rad) in combination with PerfeCta SYBR Green FastMix (Quanta) and 0.3 μM of specific primers: PCCA exon 13F and PCCA exon 15R for the detection of all isoforms (with and without PE), and PCCA exon 13F and PCCA exon 14–15R for the isoform lacking the PE (Table S1). mRNA levels were first normalized to GAPDH expression and subsequently to total RNA levels in non-transfected cells.

CRISPR-Cas12a gene editing and off-target analysis

The online tools Breaking-Cas (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/breakingcas/)36 and CRISPOR (https://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu)37 were used to design gRNAs (provided by Integrated DNA Technologies, IDT) for Cas12a nuclease and for off-target prediction (Tables S2, S3, S4, and S5). Two gRNAs upstream of the PE (gRNAs 1 and 3), one downstream of the PE (gRNA22), and another within the PE (gRNA15) were selected. The sequence and position of each gRNA in the gene are shown in Figure 1A. Cas12a and the corresponding gRNAs were delivered into cells as an RNP complex, formed by the combination of 24 pmol of A.s.Cas12a (Cpf1) (IDT), 24 pmol of gRNAs (using dual gRNA strategy or single guide), and 9.6 μL Cas9 Plus Reagent (IDT). A total of 6 × 105 HepG2 cells were reverse transfected with the RNP complex by using 13 μL of Lipofectamine CRISPRMAX transfection reagent (IDT) in a 6-well plate in antibiotic-free medium. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the cells were subcultured and expanded for further analysis. The cells were harvested 120 h after transfection, and cell pellets were stored at −70°C until further use.

To evaluate Cas12a mRNA, 1 × 105 HepG2 cells were reverse transfected in a 12-well plate with 4 μg of L.b.Cas12a (Cpf1) mRNA V2 (Genscript) and 0.8 μg of gRNA15, using 4 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Forty-eight hours after transfection, the cells were subcultured and expanded for further analysis. The cells were harvested 144 h after transfection, and cell pellets were stored at −70°C.

The top predicted off-targets for each gRNA were amplified using specific primers (Tables S2, S3, S4, and S5) from bulk transfected cells and Sanger sequenced.

SSO transfection

To analyze the effect of SSO, 6 × 105 HepG2 cells were reverse transfected in 6-well plates, using 4 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) and 20 nM RNA SSO with 2′-O-methyl modifications and phosphorothioate backbones (LGC Biosearch Technologies, Risskov, Denmark). A scrambled non-targeting SSO was used as control along with a previously validated hit SSO11 (Table S6). The cells were harvested at 48 and 96 h post-transfection for total RNA isolation and protein analysis, respectively.

Genome editing efficiency and deep sequencing analysis

Genomic DNA from the cells was extracted using the QIAampⓇ DNA Mini kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The intron 14 region was amplified by PCR using FastStart Taq polymerase (Roche) with specific primers (PCCA intron 14F and PCCA intron 14R) (Figure 1A; Table S1). Cleavage efficiencies were estimated for the dual gRNA approach based on the intensities of unedited (full-length) and edited (PE-excision) bands of the PCR amplicons on 2% agarose gel, using Fiji/ImageJ software. The characterization of DNA repair outcomes at each cut site was performed using the genomic DNA amplified by PCR with specific primers (PCCA intron 14 NGSF and PCCA intron 14 NGSR) (Figure 1A; Table S1). After validation of the quality of PCR products by gel electrophoresis, DNAs were isolated using the E.Z.N.AⓇ Cycle Pure Kit (Omega BIO-TEK) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The samples were sequenced using Illumina-based NGS by GENEWIZ. The results were analyzed using CRISPResso2.38

Protein analysis

Cells were disrupted by freeze-thaw cycles in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS) containing protease inhibitors and centrifuged for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was determined using Bradford method (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Samples containing 20–50 μg protein were prepared using NuPAGE LDS sample buffer with 10% dithiothreitol (DTT) and loaded onto a 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (iBlot 2 2NC Mini Stacks, Invitrogen) using an iBlot 2 Gel Transfer Device (Invitrogen). Immunodetection was performed using commercially available antibodies against PCCA (1:2000, Origene) and PCCB (1:500, Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Secondary antibody used was anti-mouse IgG (1:2000, Cell Signaling). GAPDH was used as a loading control (1:5000, Abcam). Antibody binding was detected by enhanced chemiluminescent reagent(ECL,Cytiva) or Super Signal West Femto (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein quantification was performed using a GS-900 Calibrated Densitometer (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and quantified with ImageLab software (Bio-Rad).

PCC activity

PCC activity was assayed by measuring the enzyme-dependent incorporation of [14C]-bicarbonate into non-volatile Krebs cycle products, as previously described.39 Briefly, the cells were resuspended in 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 buffer containing 0.81 mM glutathione and disrupted by freeze-thaw cycles. The enzymatic reaction was initiated by adding homogenate, maintained at 30°C during 20 min, and stopped with 30% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). The samples were centrifuged at 16,000 ×g for 15 min, and the supernatant was transferred to a scintillation vial and evaporated at room temperature for 24–48 h. The non-volatile products were measured using a Tri-Carb 2810 TR Liquid Scintillation Counter (PerkinElmer) after 48 h. PCC activity is expressed as pmol of incorporated 14C min−1 mg−1 of total protein. Protein concentration in cellular extracts was determined by the Bradford method (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism version 10.2.3. Data are represented as the mean ± SEM. Differences between non-transfected and edited cells were assessed using Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA with Dunnet correction. Critical values for significance of ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, or ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001 were used throughout the study.
