Necessary teaching or preventable disruption? Reconsidering resident trainee-related workflow interruptions in academic operating rooms
Authors: Amirali Alizadeh
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the observational study in the Journal by Kalantari et al., who quantified interruptions and delays during 251 operations across five academic teaching hospitals [1]. Their full-case observations documented 50,675 min of operating room activity and identified an important teaching-related signal: 45 of 319 identified causes (14.1%) were attributed to the educational nature of the hospitals and involved residents or trainees [1]. This finding deserves further attention. However, we suggest that the central question is not simply how often trainee-related events occur, but what function those events serve.

First, the reported 14.1% is a proportion of identified causes, not of the 281 interruption or delay events. Because some events had multiple causes, cause attribution and event frequency are not interchangeable. More importantly, “trainee-related” is unlikely to represent a single, clinically coherent patient-safety construct. The same observed loss of time may reflect fundamentally different phenomena: inadequate preparation that should have been prevented; slower performance during supervised skill acquisition; a senior surgeon stopping a trainee to correct a potentially unsafe maneuver; contextually necessary intraoperative teaching; or a system failure in role allocation, case selection, task–competence matching, or supervisory availability. The label may therefore obscure both downstream function and upstream cause.

This distinction matters because interruptions are not uniformly harmful. Healthcare observations have shown that their consequences depend on source and function: coordination-related interruptions may support care, whereas irrelevant or poorly timed interruptions may degrade workflow [2]. Other work has emphasized classifying interruptions by function and avoidability rather than occurrence alone [3]. In intraoperative education, patient safety is the overriding principle by which supervisors regulate resident autonomy, continuously balancing learning opportunities against the need to intervene [4]. Thus, a corrective interruption may increase measured “lost time” while simultaneously preventing an error. Conversely, longer operative time associated with resident involvement does not necessarily imply worse postoperative outcomes [5]. This argument does not diminish the established association between surgical flow disruptions and errors; it refines which events should be targeted for elimination [6].

We therefore propose a function-based classification of trainee-related workflow events: (1) preventable preparedness-related disruption, such as inadequate case preparation or failure to complete prerequisite tasks; (2) skill-related procedural prolongation, reflecting the expected time cost of supervised learning; (3) corrective safety interruption, in which supervision stops or redirects a potentially unsafe action; (4) contextually necessary intraoperative teaching, required to proceed safely or appropriately in a specific case; and (5) system-induced training disruption, arising from unclear roles, inappropriate task–competence matching, unsuitable case allocation, or unavailable supervision. These categories demand different responses. The first and fifth call for prevention and system redesign; the second requires realistic scheduling, case selection, and graded autonomy; the third should be recognized as a safety mechanism rather than targeted for elimination; and the fourth should be optimized rather than indiscriminately eliminated.

Future studies should record, for each event, the surgical phase, initiator, interrupted task, duration, trainee level, degree of autonomy, supervisory context, preventability, educational necessity, and immediate safety consequence. Preventability and safety function should be independently rated by at least two reviewers, with disagreement adjudicated by a multidisciplinary panel including surgical educators, perioperative professionals, and human factors or patient-safety experts. Analyses should distinguish events from causes and report not only frequency but also minutes lost by category, rates per operating room hour, and associations with near misses, errors, and adverse outcomes. Because trainee involvement is strongly shaped by case complexity and context, multilevel analyses should account for procedure type, complexity, urgency, staffing, trainee experience, and clustering within teams and hospitals.

Academic operating rooms must be both efficient and educational. This is not an argument to exempt teaching from efficiency scrutiny. Rather, it is a call to ensure that efficiency metrics do not treat every minute devoted to supervised learning or safety-preserving correction as waste. The most informative future question is therefore not whether trainees interrupt workflow, but whether a given trainee-related event represents preventable disruption, necessary learning, or active protection of the patient.
