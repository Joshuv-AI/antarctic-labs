Genome-wide DNA methylation profiling during metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis-related hepatocarcinogenesis in patients in Japan and the United States
Authors: Junko Kuramoto, Eri Arai, Mao Fujimoto, Hiroki Muramoto, Hidenori Ojima, Yosuke Seki, Kazunori Kasama, Nobuaki Funahashi, Haruhide Udagawa, Takao Nammo, Kazuki Yasuda, Nobuyoshi Hiraoka, Teruhiko Yoshida, Kimberley Jane Evason, Yae Kanai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare ethnicity-related differences in DNA methylation profiles during metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)-related hepatocarcinogenesis in patients from Japan and the United States (US). Genome-wide DNA methylation analysis using the Infinium assay was performed in 36, 148 and 36 samples of normal liver tissue (NLT), non-cancerous liver tissue showing MASH, and MASH-related hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), respectively (220 samples in total), from the Japan and US cohorts. Principal component analysis revealed that MASH had a distinct DNA methylation profile differing from that of NLT, and that the MASH profiles in the two cohorts differed from each other. DNA methylation alterations of cancer-related genes in MASH were inherited by or strengthened in MASH-related HCC itself, resulting in expression alterations. DNA methylation alterations of FGFR2, FUT4, B3GNT5 and MOSC1 in the precancerous MASH stage were shared by the two cohorts, suggesting that such genes are commonly associated with MASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis. On the other hand, it was suggested that DNA methylation alterations of ZNF611 and SAMD10, and those of SHC1, are involved specifically in MASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis in the Japan and the US cohorts, respectively. These findings suggest that DNA methylation alterations, which may reflect race and lifestyle, are associated with MASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57253-3.

Introduction

The global increase in obesity and diabetes, largely driven by high-fat dietary habits, has led to a marked rise in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cases associated with metabolic dysfunction- associated steatohepatitis (MASH), formerly known as non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), now recognized as a precancerous condition1. While hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections leading to chronic hepatitis and cirrhosis have traditionally been the major causes of HCC, accumulating evidence indicates that MASH is emerging as another important precursor to HCC2,3.

It is well established that both genomic and epigenomic alterations contribute to carcinogenesis across various human organs4,5. Among these alterations, DNA methylation abnormalities are key epigenetic changes that promote chromosomal instability and dysregulated expression of tumor-related genes6–9. In the context of MASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis, our previous genome-wide DNA methylation analysis using the Infinium assay revealed that MASH-specific DNA methylation changes, which were different from such changes seen in viral hepatitis, are retained or further enhanced in MASH-related HCCs10. DNA methylation alterations could contribute to multistage hepatocarcinogenesis by affecting the expression of specific genes, such as TRIM4, PRC1, and TUBA1B11. DNA hypomethylation of SPHK1 and LTB, leading to their overexpression in poorly differentiated HCCs, may underlie their aggressive phenotypes by promoting tumor cell proliferation and/or migration12. Furthermore, WHSC1 showing MASH-specific DNA methylation alterations may serve as a potential therapeutic target in MASH-related HCC10.

Although the global burden of MASH-related HCC is rising, its proportion among all HCC cases remains lower in Asian countries, including Japan, than in Western countries13. It has been speculated that this variation to reflects differences in epigenetic alterations driven by genetic predisposition and environmental factors across populations. However, since only a limited number of previous studies have directly compared epigenome profiles of samples from different populations using the same analytical platform, these interethnic differences have not been fully clarified.

Accordingly, in the present study using the Infinium assay, we simultaneously performed genome-wide DNA methylation analysis of 179 samples from a Japan cohort and 41 samples from a United States (US) cohort to directly compare their DNA methylation profiles during MASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis.

Materials and methods

Patients and tissue samples

For the Japan cohort, we used 26 paired samples of non-cancerous liver tissue (N) and the corresponding cancerous tissue (T), obtained by partial hepatectomy from 26 HCC patients whose N samples exhibited histological features consistent with MASH. All 26 patients tested negative for both hepatitis B surface antigen and anti-HCV antibody and did not receive any preoperative treatment. The histological stage was evaluated microscopically using the histological scoring system for NASH14 and the Brunt classification15, both of which are histological evaluation methods for NASH and continue to be recommended as histological assessment methods for the newly defined MASH16. HCC diagnosis was based on histological criteria according to the World Health Organization classification17 and the Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) classification system18. These 26 N and 26 T samples are hereafter referred to as “N showing MASH from patients with HCC in Japan (J-MASH-W)” and “MASH-related T from patients in Japan (J-T)”, respectively.

Additionally, liver biopsy specimens showing histological features of MASH were obtained from 91 morbidly obese patients undergoing bariatric surgery under general anesthesia at Yotsuya Medical Cube. Prior to inclusion, these patients were informed of the risks of obesity-associated MASH19, the clinical benefits of early diagnosis, and the invasiveness of the biopsy procedure. None of these patients had evidence of HBV or HCV infection, nor a history of HCC. These 91 N samples are hereafter referred to as “N showing MASH from patients without HCC in Japan (J-MASH-O)”.

As normal controls, 36 normal liver tissue (NLT) samples were obtained via partial hepatectomy from patients with liver metastases of primary colorectal cancer. These patients had no history of HBV or HCV infection, chronic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, or HCC and did not receive any preoperative treatment. All cancer patients underwent hepatectomy at the National Cancer Center Hospital. Accordingly, the Japan cohort comprised 36 NLT, 91 J-MASH-O, 26 J-MASH-W, and 26 J-T samples, totaling 179 specimens.

For the US cohort, we used 11 paired samples of N and the corresponding T, collected via partial hepatectomy or total hepatectomy performed for liver transplant from 11 HCC patients whose N showed MASH-compatible histology. Among the 11 T specimens, one was excluded from the analysis due to necrotic tissue accounting for more than 90% of the tumor area. All were negative for HBV and HCV. MASH staging and HCC diagnosis followed the same criteria as those used in the Japan cohort14–18. These 11 N and 10 T samples are hereafter referred to as “N showing MASH from patients with HCC in the US (US-MASH-W)” and “MASH-related T from patients in the US (US-T)”, respectively.

In addition, 20 N samples were obtained from patients who underwent total hepatectomy for liver transplantation due to hepatic failure secondary to progressive MASH. These 20 N samples are hereafter referred to as “N showing MASH from patients without HCC in the US (US-MASH-O)”. These patients underwent surgery at the University of Utah Hospital. Thus, the US cohort consisted of 20 US-MASH-O, 11 US-MASH-W, and 10 US-T samples, for a total of 41 specimens.

The demographic and clinicopathological characteristics of all 179 Japanese samples and 41 US samples are summarized in Table 1. For the Japan cohort, tissue samples were immediately frozen after surgical removal or biopsy and stored in accordance with the Guidelines for the Handling of Pathological Tissue Samples for Genomic Research issued by the Japanese Society of Pathology20. This study was approved by the ethics committees of Keio University, the National Cancer Center, the National Center for Global Health and Medicine (currently renamed the Japan Institute for Health Security), and Yotsuya Medical Cube, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. For the US cohort, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples were obtained from the University of Utah Pathology Archives. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Utah. As the data analyzed were deidentified and collected in accordance with the institution’s standard of care, the study met criteria for exemption, and informed consent from individual patients was not required. For the US cohort, procedures for preparing formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens were fully consistent with those described in the same Guidelines20. Tissue preparation and bisulfite modification

Genomic DNA was obtained from fresh frozen tissue samples in the Japan cohort using phenol–chloroform extraction followed by dialysis. In the US cohort, FFPE tissue sections were manually dissected, and genomic DNA was extracted using the GeneRead FFPE Kit (QIAGEN GmbH, Hilden, Germany).

For bisulfite modification, 500 ng of DNA from fresh frozen tissues was treated using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). For FFPE-derived DNA (50–300 ng), bisulfite conversion was followed by DNA restoration using the Illumina HD FFPE Restoration Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), which includes DNA polymerase, a repair enzyme, and a ligase, as described previously. In our previous study, we had confirmed that such restoration yielded reliable data even when FFPE-derived DNA was used: genome-wide DNA methylation profiles obtained from FFPE tissues showed strong concordance with those obtained from the gold-standard frozen tissues from the same cases21.

Genome-wide DNA methylation screening by Infinium assay

Genome-wide DNA methylation status at 485,764 CpG sites was assessed using the Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip array (Illumina)22. After hybridization, site-specific labeling by single-base extension and detection were performed using an iScan reader (Illumina), following the manufacturer’s protocol. To reduce batch effects, all samples of both cohorts were hybridized by the same researcher in the same laboratory by applying almost equal numbers of both cohort samples to each array.

The data were processed using GenomeStudio Methylation software (Illumina). At each CpG locus, the methylation level was quantified as a β-value (ranging from 0.00 to 1.00), calculated as the ratio of the fluorescence intensity of the methylated probe to the total signal from both methylated and unmethylated probes. As the standard Infinium methylation analysis pipeline, background correction and normalization including correction of Infinium type I/II probe bias were performed within GenomeStudio. Probe genomic annotations were based on the hg19 (GRCh37) human genome build.

Infinium data for a portion of the Japan cohort were previously reported in a study comparing DNA methylation profiles between MASH-related and viral hepatitis-related HCC10,23, and are available in the GEO database (Accession numbers: GSE89852 and GSE183468). Infinium data for the US cohort have also been deposited in the GEO database (Accession number: GSE304513).

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription (RT)-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) from 26 paired samples of frozen liver tissue (J-MASH-W and J-T) from HCC patients with histologically confirmed MASH, and from 28 NLT samples for which sufficient tissue remained after the Infinium assay. For the 91 J-MASH-O samples, frozen liver tissue was insufficient after the Infinium assay, and therefore total RNA could not be extracted. Additionally, total RNA was extracted from FFPE samples using the RNeasy FFPE Kit (QIAGEN) for 13 US-MASH-O, 6 US -MASH-W, and 5 US-T samples.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using random primers and SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Expression levels of the FGFR2, FUT4, B3GNT5, MOSC1, ZNF611, SAMD10, SHC1 and STAT5A genes were quantified using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems), using the relative standard curve method. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the average cycle threshold (CT) value was used for quantification. CT values were normalized to GAPDH mRNA levels in the same sample.

Cell lines

The human HCC cell line Hep3B was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA) in February 2020. The human HCC cell lines PLC/PRF/5, JHH-7, and HLF were purchased from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB, Osaka, Japan) in January 2020. Hep3B, PLC/PRF/5, JHH-7 and HLF were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling by the JCRB Cell Bank in August 2022 (certification numbers: KBN0850-01, KBN0850-02, KBN0850-03, and KBN0850-0, respectively). Hep3B was maintained in Minimum Essential Medium Eagle (M4655, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), supplemented with sodium pyruvate solution (S8683, Sigma-Aldrich) and non-essential amino acid solution (M7145, Sigma-Aldrich). PLC/PRF/5 and HLF were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium–low glucose (D6046, Sigma-Aldrich). JHH-7 was maintained in William’s E Medium (12551032, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, and cells were incubated at 37 °C under 95% air and 5% CO2.

5-Aza-2'-deoxycytidine (5-aza-dC) treatment of cell lines

Cells were seeded at a density of 3.0 × 104 cells/mL and incubated for 24 h to allow adhesion. Subsequently, 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine (5-aza-dC; Sigma-Aldrich) was added at a final concentration of 1 μM. After 72 h of treatment, genomic DNA and total RNA were extracted from each cell line for Infinium assay and real-time RT-PCR analysis, respectively.

Statistical analysis

In the Infinium assay, the call proportions (P-value < 0.01 for detection of signals above the background) for 2,476 probes in all 220 tissue samples in the Japan and US cohorts (NLT, J-MASH-O, J-MASH-W, J-T, US-MASH-O, US-MASH-W and US-T samples) were less than 90%. Since such a low proportion may have been attributable to polymorphism at the probes, these 2476 probes were excluded from the present assay, as described previously24,25. To eliminate potential sex-specific methylation effects, 11,648 probes located on the X and Y chromosomes were also removed. In addition, 2558 probes with missing β-values in more than 10% of the samples were excluded. Furthermore, according to the HumanMethylation450 Manifest File, 3156 quality control probes including non-CpG probes and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) measurement probes were excluded.

DNA methylation profiles were analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA). Infinium probes with statistically significant differences in DNA methylation or mRNA expression levels between sample groups were identified using Welch’s t-test, followed by Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. DNA methylation changes across stages of multistep carcinogenesis were assessed using the Jonckheere-Terpstra (Jo-T) trend test, also adjusted with Bonferroni correction. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using β-values from the Infinium assay. Correlations between DNA methylation levels and clinicopathological parameters were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation test. Statistical significance was defined as a P-value of < 0.05. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Reactome database (https://reactome.org), and pathways with a false discovery rate (FDR) of < 0.2 were considered significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (RStudio Inc., Boston, MA; https://www.rstudio.com) and R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing; https://www.r-project.org).

Since our previous study had revealed that the DNA methylation levels of genes participating in MASH-related multistage carcinogenesis showed stepwise alteration from NLT to MASH-O, MASH-W, and then to T10, Jo-T trend test adjusted by Bonferroni correction (P < 0.05) was performed using the 1,001 probes. DNA methylation alterations during MASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis in the US cohort

In the US cohort, using a method similar to that for the Japan cohort, we identified candidate genes for which DNA methylation alterations are associated with multistage hepatocarcinogenesis even from the precancerous MASH stage and for which levels of mRNA expression could be potentially regulated by DNA methylation levels (Supplementary Fig. S3).

First, we identified 7189 probes showing significant differences in DNA methylation levels between the US-MASH-W and NLT samples (Welch’s t test adjusted by Bonferroni correction, P < 0.05, and a ΔβUS-MASH-W-NLT value of more than 0.1 or less than − 0.1) (Fig. 2b). ROC curves were then constructed for the 7189 probes and the corresponding AUC values were calculated (Fig. 2b). Among the 6650 probes showing AUC values larger than 0.95, 4568 probes showed ordered differences in DNA methylation levels from NLT to US-MASH-O, US-MASH-W and then to US-T (Jo-T trend test adjusted by Bonferroni correction, P < 0.05): 1362 and 3206 probes showed DNA hyper- and hypomethylation during multistage hepatocarcinogenesis, respectively (Fig. 2b).

Among 4568 probes, 1058 were located in CpG islands, island shores and island shelves around the TSS (TSS1500, TSS200, 5'UTR, first intron, or first exon). From these 1058 probes, 208 were mapped to 160 genes exhibiting significant inverse correlations between levels of DNA methylation and mRNA expression (Pearson’s r < – 0.3, P < 0.05 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction) in the TCGA dataset (Supplementary Fig. S4). These 160 genes whose levels of mRNA expression are potentially regulated by DNA methylation status are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Comparison of DNA methylation profiles during MASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis between the Japan and US cohorts

We next investigated whether or not DNA methylation abnormalities during MASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis were shared or differed between the cohorts from Japan and the US, where environmental factors such as diet and lifestyle differ. From the 22 genes included in Table 2, 12 genes, DDR1 (cg07187855 in Table 2; cg07187855 and cg16707952 in Supplementary Table S2), CCDC102A (cg08096786 in Table 2; cg08096786 in Supplementary Table S2), PYCARD (cg05214748, cg05907835 and cg12100791 in Table 2; cg15468095 in Supplementary Table S2), FGFR2 (cg02210151 in Table 2; cg11430259 and cg18566515 in Supplementary Table S2), FUT4 (cg02709181 in Table 2; cg00796728 and cg02709181 in Supplementary Table S2), KIAA1949 (cg03953626, cg05098566, cg08887400 in Table 2; cg00221185 in Supplementary Table S2), B3GNT5 (cg26215406 in Table 2; cg01720186, cg15510249, and cg17701886 in Supplementary Table S2), NQO1 (cg10708675, cg13969273 in Table 2; cg10708675 in Supplementary Table S2), AATK (cg00555905 and cg09142578 in Table 2; cg09142578 in Supplementary Table S2), TMEM125 (cg04355435 in Table 2; cg04355435 in Supplementary Table S2), MOSC1 (cg04396721 in Table 2 and Supplementary Table S2) and CYBA (cg05744675 in Table 2; cg05744675 in Supplementary Table S2) were shared by the Japan and US cohorts, suggesting that DNA methylation alterations of these 12 genes are commonly associated with MASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis in both cohorts through the alterations in their expression. Among the 12 genes, DNA methylation levels of FGFR2 (cg18566515, cg11430259, cg02210151), FUT4 (cg02709181, cg00796728), and B3GNT5 (cg15510249, cg26215406, cg01720186, cg17701886) were inversely correlated with the fibrosis stages considered to reflect carcinogenetic risk, when analyzed on the MASH-W samples from both the Japan and US cohorts combined into one sample group (Supplementary Table S3).

After exclusion of these 12 genes, the DNA methylation alterations of the remaining 10 genes—ZNF611, LIME1, MFSD7, NRSN2, ZNF107, SAMD10, RAB34, PROCR, HK1 and BCL9L—in Table 2 were considered to be specific to the Japan cohort. Among the 10 genes, DNA methylation levels of ZNF611 (cg04293891) and SAMD10 (cg05929574) were again inversely correlated with the fibrosis stages of the MASH-W samples from the Japan cohort (Supplementary Table S3).

Discussion

PCA demonstrated that the genome-wide DNA methylation profiles of MASH samples (J-MASH-O, J-MASH-W, US-MASH-O, and US-MASH-W) were clearly different from those of NLT samples, indicating that DNA methylation alterations are involved in multistage hepatocarcinogenesis even from the precancerous MASH stage (Fig. 1), consistent with our previous findings9–11. In addition, HCC samples (J-T and US-T) showed broader heterogeneity of DNA methylation profiles on PCA plots than did N and MASH samples (Fig. 1), potentially reflecting the clinicopathological diversity of HCCs. This observation is again in line with our previous reports12,43. Furthermore, the DNA methylation profiles of both MASH samples (J-MASH-O and J-MASH-W vs US-MASH-O and US-MASH-W) and HCC samples (J-T and US-T) differed between the Japan and the US cohorts (Fig. 1). The observed differences between the Japan and US cohorts could be explained by environmental factors such as diet and lifestyle, rather than by genetic background, since the differences were revealed using a genetically heterogeneous US cohort. Since metabolic disorders are influenced by diet and lifestyle, it is understandable that MASH-related carcinogenesis would be more largely determined by environmental factors than by genetic predisposition. However, whether genetic predisposition or environmental factors play a larger role cannot be determined from the present findings alone.

We then identified cancer-related genes whose DNA methylation levels were already altered at the precancerous MASH stage relative to NLT, and for which such alterations were inherited by or strengthened in MASH-related HCC itself and whose levels of mRNA expression were potentially regulated by DNA methylation status in the individual Japan and US cohorts. Twenty-two such genes were identified in the Japan cohort, whereas 160 were identified in the US cohort. The larger number of cancer-related genes identified in the US cohort may reflect a broader diversity of epigenetic alterations, which could be attributable to the cohort’s inherent genetic and environmental heterogeneity44–46 rather than to any biological difference between the two cohorts. As shown in Table 1, the US cohort included individuals from multiple racial and ethnic backgrounds, such as White Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, and Native American, each of which may carry distinct genomic variants. In addition, differences in lifestyle and diet across these populations may also contribute to variations in environmental exposure. The combined impact of interethnic genetic polymorphism and environmental diversity may have affected the heterogeneity of epigenetic alterations in the US cohort.

FGFR2, FUT4, B3GNT5 and MOSC1 were identified as cancer-related genes shared between the two cohorts. Progressive DNA hypomethylation of FGFR2, FUT4 and B3GNT5 was associated with increased gene expression across disease stages, from NLT, to O, W and then to T when the Japan and US samples were analyzed together, while MOSC1 exhibited progressive DNA hypermethylation and a corresponding decrease in gene expression (Fig. 3a). Moreover, it is understandable that the DNA methylation levels of the FGFR2, FUT4 and B3GNT5 genes were significantly correlated with the degree of fibrosis, which is considered to reflect carcinogenetic risk (Supplementary Table S3).

FGFR2 is a receptor tyrosine kinase that mediates signaling involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival26. Aberrations in FGFR2, including gene amplification, mutations, and translocations, have been implicated in various cancers such as breast cancer, gastric cancer, and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma26–28. The present study is the first to report upregulation of FGFR2 due to DNA hypomethylation in MASH-related HCC, suggesting a potential oncogenic role under epigenetic regulation. Given the availability of FGFR inhibitors in clinical use, FGFR2 might serve as a therapeutic target for a subset of MASH-related HCCs, although clinical evidence for the usefulness of FGFR inhibitors in HCC is still limited. In addition, the proportion of MASH patients who would be eligible for such targeted therapy is still unclear.

FUT4 (fucosyltransferase 4) is an enzyme that catalyzes the addition of fucose to N-acetyllactosamine-containing glycans. Fucosylation is a common glycan modification on the surface of cancer cells and is involved in tumor progression, invasion, and metastasis29. Upregulation of FUT4 has been documented in colorectal cancer30, and previous in vitro studies have demonstrated increased FUT4 expression in HCC cell lines31. Our present study is the first to demonstrate increased FUT4 expression in HCC using clinical samples of liver tissue, with DNA hypomethylation likely contributing to this upregulation.

B3GNT5 encodes β1,3-N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase, a glycosyltransferase involved in the biosynthesis of lactotriaosylceramide (Lc3Cer) by transferring N-acetylglucosamine to galactose on glycan chains32. Overexpression of Lc3Cer has been reported in multiple cancers and is associated with poorer prognosis33,34. B3GNT5 has also been identified as a marker associated with stem-like phenotypes and poor outcomes in gliomas35, although its role in HCC has not been reported. Our findings suggest, for the first time, that upregulation of B3GNT5 driven by DNA hypomethylation may be associated with MASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis.

It is understandable that genes related to growth factor signaling and abnormal glycosylation, which are well known to contribute to various organ cancers, were shared by both cohorts as mentioned above. On the other hand, MOSC1 encodes a mitochondrial outer membrane amidoxime-reducing component and has been implicated in the suppression of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease development36,37. Moreover, the present study has revealed that MOSC1 silencing due to DNA hypermethylation continues to be further associated with the development of MASH-related HCC.

Focusing on ZNF611 and SAMD10, cancer-related genes specific to the Japan cohort, analyses of our clinical liver tissue samples revealed that progressive DNA hypermethylation from NLT to J-MASH-O, to J-MASH-W, and finally to J-T resulted in a corresponding increase of gene expression (Fig. 3b). It is again understandable that the DNA methylation levels of the ZNF611 and SAMD10 genes in J-MASH-W samples were significantly correlated with the degree of fibrosis which is considered to reflect carcinogenetic risk.

ZNF611 encodes a zinc finger protein belonging to one of the largest families of transcription factors in eukaryotes38. In colorectal cancer, ZNF611 has been identified as a hub gene in co-expression networks associated with subtypes showing high microsatellite instability39. Our study is the first to suggest that ZNF611 may be involved in MASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis through hypomethylation-induced overexpression, potentially affecting the transcriptional regulation of tumor-related genes.

SAMD10 encodes a protein containing a sterile alpha motif (SAM) domain. SAM domains are structurally conserved and are known to mediate diverse protein–protein and RNA–protein interactions implicated in both transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation40–42. Although the biological functions of SAMD10 remain largely uncharacterized, its structure suggests potential involvement in RNA regulation and signal transduction40–42. Increased expression of SAMD10 due to DNA hypomethylation may play a role in the transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation of genes involved in MASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis within the Japan cohort.

Supplementary Table S4 shows that SHC1, encoding an adaptor protein involved in both inflammation and cell proliferation, was specifically hypomethylated in the US cohort. This gene participates in the transduction of signals from receptor tyrosine kinases, including FGFR and IGF1R, to the downstream Ras/MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways, both of which are frequently dysregulated in cancer47. Previous studies have demonstrated that SHC1 is overexpressed in several malignancies and is associated with enhanced proliferation, invasion and poorer prognosis47,48. Although its role in MASH-related HCC has not been fully characterized, our findings suggest that DNA hypomethylation-driven upregulation of SHC1 may be associated with the progression of such HCC.

In conclusion, FGFR2, FUT4, B3GNT5 and MOSC1 were identified as epigenetically dysregulated genes common to both the Japan and US cohorts, possibly participating in carcinogenesis, while ZNF611 and SAMD10 were unique to the Japan cohort, and SHC1 was specific to the US cohort. Functional analysis of these genes is needed to further clarify their significance during hepatocarcinogenesis. Our previous research has already shown that DNA methylation markers are useful for stratification of carcinogenetic risk (23). In addition, this study showed that epigenetically regulated genes may serve as potential therapeutic targets for MASH-related HCC in each population, after further clinical validation. Although clarification of the racial and lifestyle factors responsible for DNA methylation abnormalities in genes specific to the Japan and US cohorts remains a future challenge, the present direct comparison of DNA methylation profiles of tissue samples from different cohorts may provide a starting point for devising optimal therapeutic strategies for each population.
