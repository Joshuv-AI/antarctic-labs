Cryoextraction for Airway Bleeding After Valve-in-Valve Procedure
Authors: Teresa Bretones del Pino, Vicente F Alcalde Martínez, Joaquín Sánchez Gila, Rocío García Orta, Alberto Caballero Vázquez, José Luis Zunzunegui Martínez, Eduardo Molina Navarro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHemoptysis after percutaneous valve implantation is a rare but life-threatening complication.Case SummaryAn 84-year-old woman with prior mechanical aortic and biological tricuspid valve replacements presented with tricuspid stenosis and regurgitation. A transcatheter valve-in-valve procedure using a 24.5-mm Myval was performed successfully. However, she developed severe hemoptysis, hypoxia, and ventilation difficulties shortly after the procedure. Flexible bronchoscopy with cryoprobe-assisted clot extraction was performed, removing large obstructive clots from the bronchi. A second cryoextraction was necessary 72 hours later. The patient improved rapidly, was extubated, and was discharged in stable condition after 7 days.DiscussionCryoextraction is commonly used for tumor-related airway bleeding and foreign body removal, but its application in postvalve implantation hemoptysis is novel. This case highlights its lifesaving role.Take-Home MessageCryoextraction can be a safe and effective alternative when conventional bleeding control methods fail after valve implantation.Key words: clot extraction, cryoprobe, hemoptysis, hypoxemia, pulmonary bleeding, valve replacement, tricuspid valve-in-valve

History of Presentation

An 84-year-old woman with a history of mechanical aortic valve replacement and a surgically implanted biological tricuspid valve (23-mm Edwards PM) presented with symptomatic severe tricuspid stenosis and regurgitation (Figure 1, Video 1). She underwent a planned percutaneous valve-in-valve implantation using a 24.5-mm Myval valve (Meril Lifesciences) through a 24-F DrySeal sheath (Gore Medical; selected because of severe tortuosity and extensive calcification of the iliofemoral access, which made advancement of the prosthetic valve technically challenging) using a Lunderquist guidewire. The procedure was initially successful, with good hemodynamic and echocardiographic results (Figure 2, Videos 2 to 4). However, shortly after valve deployment, the patient developed sudden and severe hemoptysis through the endotracheal tube, accompanied by oxygen desaturation and difficulty in ventilation.

At that point, distal lobar artery perforation was suspected as the underlying cause (Figures 3B and 3C). Angiographic studies were performed but failed to identify the exact bleeding site (Video 5). Therefore, standard percutaneous hemostatic maneuvers were attempted. Initially, a prolonged inflation was performed with a 6-mm balloon proximal to the lobar artery where the stiff guidewire had been positioned (Figure 3E), without success. Subsequently, a more proximal prolonged inflation was carried out in the left lower lobar trunk using a 12-mm balloon (Figure 3F), and anticoagulation was reversed with protamine, acknowledging the risk of thrombosis of the mechanical aortic prosthesis implanted in the patient.

Although several maneuvers were performed to treat a presumed arterial branch bleeding—without angiographic evidence—the patient showed persistent hemodynamic instability. This prompted evaluation for alternative bleeding sources, and fluoroscopy began to reveal mobile high-density material within the bronchial tree, raising suspicion of active airway bleeding, possibly exacerbated by high-dose procedural heparinization (Video 6).

The anesthesiologists and intensivists performed a fiberoptic bronchoscopy to try to aspirate the blood clots, but they occupied the entire bronchial lumen (Figure 3G).

A bronchography was then performed to try to delimit the clots, their location and size (injection of 30% contrast through the endotracheal tube), and we tried to break the clots up with a balloon to clear the bronchial lumen (Figure 3H).

Despite all these maneuvers, the patient's ventilation continued to deteriorate, with respiratory acidosis and desaturation, so a pulmonary specialist was urgently consulted in the catheterization laboratory, and flexible bronchoscopy was performed, revealing large blood clots obstructing both bronchi.

Given the suspicion of ongoing airway bleeding and the presence of obstructive clots, a flexible bronchoscopy was performed using a cryoprobe, a device capable of reaching temperatures below −75 °C. This technique, more commonly used for endobronchial lesion ablation or foreign body extraction,1,2 was employed in this context to perform cryoextraction of the endobronchial clots.3 Upon application of the cryoprobe, the clots adhered to the probe tip through rapid freezing and were removed en bloc from both bronchi (Figure 4). This immediate intervention led to significant clinical improvement: The patient's gas exchange parameters normalized, and ventilatory dynamics stabilized.

Cryoextraction of Obstructive Clot From the Left Main Bronchus

(A) Complete occlusion of the left main bronchial lumen by a large clot. (B) The 2.4-mm cryoprobe used during the procedure and the extracted clot. (C) Recanalization of the left main bronchus after successful cryoextraction.

Given the initial suspicion of pulmonary arterial bleeding, the 12-mm balloon was intentionally maintained inflated at the level of the left lobar artery branch despite the absence of angiographic or fluoroscopic evidence of active bleeding. After clinical stabilization, the balloon was removed without evidence of bleeding, and the procedure was concluded.

The patient was subsequently admitted to the intensive care unit, where she remained under close observation for 72 hours. During her stay in the intensive care unit, a follow-up chest computed tomography scan revealed residual clots in the airways (Figure 5). A second bronchoscopy with cryoextraction was performed, successfully removing the remaining material (Figure 6). After this second intervention, the patient continued to improve and was extubated without complications. She was discharged from the hospital in stable condition just 7 days after the procedure, after confirmation of a normally functioning tricuspid prosthesis by transthoracic echocardiography (Video 7).

Discussion

This case illustrates a rare but serious complication of transcatheter valve-in-valve implantation. Postprocedure hemoptysis is often managed with balloon tamponade or embolization. However, in this case, these measures were unsuccessful, and the patient presented with airway bleeding and hemodynamic compromise. In such situations, cryoextraction of airway clots using a flexible cryoprobe proved effective and lifesaving while the primary bleeding was being addressed. Cryotherapy is typically reserved for tumor ablation, biopsies, or foreign body removal in pulmonary procedures. Its novel use here for clot extraction due to lobar artery injury is outside current guideline-based practices, making it a noteworthy case for consideration in future procedural complication management.

Conclusions

Flexible bronchoscopy with cryoextraction should be considered as a safe, rapid, and effective option for severe airway bleeding after structural cardiac interventions when other methods are not feasible or unsuccessful.
