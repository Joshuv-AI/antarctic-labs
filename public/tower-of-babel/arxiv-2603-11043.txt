An asymptotically optimal bound for the concentration function of a sum of independent integer random variables
Valentas Kurauskas

Abstract
For a random variable X X define 𝒬 ⁡ ( X ) = sup x ∈ ℝ ℙ ⁡ ( X = x ) \mathcal{Q}(X)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(X=x) . Let X 1 , … , X n X_{1},\dots,X_{n} be independent integer random variables. Suppose 𝒬 ⁡ ( X i ) ≤ α i ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \mathcal{Q}(X_{i})\leq\alpha_{i}\in(0,1] for each i ∈ { 1 , … , n } i\in\{1,\dots,n\} . Juškevičius (2023) conjectured that 𝒬 ⁡ ( X 1 + ⋯ + X n ) ≤ 𝒬 ⁡ ( Y 1 + ⋯ + Y n ) \mathcal{Q}(X_{1}+\dots+X_{n})\leq\mathcal{Q}(Y_{1}+\dots+Y_{n}) where Y 1 , … , Y n Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n} are independent and Y i Y_{i} is a random integer variable with 𝒬 ⁡ ( Y i ) = α i \mathcal{Q}(Y_{i})=\alpha_{i} that has the smallest variance, i.e. the distribution of Y i Y_{i} has probabilities α i , … , α i , β i \alpha_{i},\dots,\alpha_{i},\beta_{i} or probabilities β i , α i , … , α i \beta_{i},\alpha_{i},\dots,\alpha_{i} on some interval of integers, where 0 ≤ β i < α i 0\leq\beta_{i}<\alpha_{i} . We prove this conjecture asymptotically: i.e., we show that for each δ > 0 \delta>0 there is V 0 = V 0 ​ ( δ ) V_{0}=V_{0}(\delta) such that if Var ⁡ ( ∑ Y i ) ≥ V 0 {\mathrm{Var}}\,(\sum Y_{i})\geq V_{0} then 𝒬 ⁡ ( ∑ X i ) ≤ ( 1 + δ ) ​ 𝒬 ​ ( ∑ Y i ) \mathcal{Q}(\sum X_{i})\leq(1+\delta)\mathcal{Q}(\sum Y_{i}) . Our long and technical argument relies on several non-trivial previous results including an inverse Littlewood–Offord theorem and an approximation in total variation distance of sums of multivariate lattice random vectors by a discretized Gaussian distribution.



1
Introduction

The
concentration function

of
a random variable
X
at value
t>0
will be defined as
\mathcal{Q}(X,t)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(X\in(x,x+t))
.
We will denote the maximum concentration at a point by
\mathcal{Q}(X)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(X=x)
.
If

X
is an integer random variable, then
simply

\mathcal{Q}(X,1)=\mathcal{Q}(X)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{P}(X=x)
.

For any random variable and a positive integer
k
we define
\mathcal{Q}_{k}(X)=\sup_{A:|A|=k}\mathbb{P}(X\in A)
, that is
\mathcal{Q}_{k}(X)
is the sum of
k
largest probability masses of (the law of)
X
.
If the law of
X
is
\mu
, we will use the symbols
X
and
\mu
interchangeably, so
\mathcal{Q}(\mu)
is the same as
\mathcal{Q}(X)
and similarly for other functionals in this paper.

Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in(0,1]
. What is the maximum possible
value of
\mathcal{Q}(S_{n})
(or
\mathcal{Q}(S_{n},L)
) if
S_{n}=X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}
and
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}

are independent random variables such that for each
i
we have
\mathcal{Q}(X_{i})\leq\alpha_{i}
(or
\mathcal{Q}(X_{i},\lambda_{i})\leq\alpha_{i})
)?
These questions and their variants have been the subject of quite a lot of research
over the past 90 years.
They originated in the works of Lévy, Doeblin and Kolmogorov, also in those of Littlewood and Offord, and were further studied by Esseen, Kesten, Rogozin and others, see, for example, the paper
[
24
]
and the references therein.
Nevertheless, for a general sequence of constraints,
inequalities obtained using classical characteristic function methods, e.g.
[
24
,
30
]

can be
suboptimal by at least a constant factor.

A subtler type of results are related to determining sequences
of random variables that satisfy the same conditions as
(X_{i})

and maximize the concentration function value of the sum.
If
the extremizing sequence has a simple structure,
we can easily get sharp quantitative bounds.
For the interval version
(in the case when
\lambda_{1}=\dots\lambda_{n}=L
)
optimal results of this kind have been obtained in
[
12
]
(single-dimensional case)
and
[
15
]
(asymptotically, high-dimensional case).
The question of optimal bounds for the pointwise
version of the problem has remained open.
While the two versions
of the concentration function problem
look
similar, they are not equivalent and the extremizers are different in general: they are symmetric in the interval case, see
[
12
,
15
]
,
and non-symmetric in the pointwise case, see
[
14
]
and the definition of
\nu_{\alpha}
below.
This makes the pointwise problem more technically challenging and
we need to use
completely different ideas
in this work.

It is known, see Ushakov
[
30
]
, that the general problem of
maximizing
the pointwise concentration
\mathcal{Q}(S_{n})
given pointwise bounds for the summands can be reduced to
the case of
integer
random variables, see Corollary
1.2
below.
To our knowledge, the best previous results for the concentration function problem for integer random variables, or its equivalent formulation in the language of sums, have been obtained in
[
11
,
19
,
21
]
.
For example, building on Gabriel’s inequality, see Hardy, Littlewood and Pólya
[
11
]
, Lev
[
19
]
showed that if each
X_{i}
is a
uniform random variable
on a set of size
k_{i}
then
\mathcal{Q}(S_{n})\leq\mathcal{Q}(Y_{1}+\dots+Y_{n})

where
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are independent and
Y_{i}
is distributed uniformly on the interval
\{0,\dots,k_{i}-1\}
. In other words, in the uniform case, removing “gaps” in the supports of all the random summands can only increase the
maximum probability of the sum. Madiman, Wang and Woo
[
21
]
obtained a similar result that works for a more general family of distributions of integers that includes all two-point distributions, see Theorem
2.27
below.

Given
\alpha\in(0,1]
let
\nu_{\alpha}
be the probability measure
which satisfies

\nu_{\alpha}(\{i\})=\alpha\mbox{ for }i\in\{0,\dots,\lfloor\alpha^{-1}\rfloor-1\}

and

\nu_{\alpha}(\{\lfloor\alpha^{-1}\rfloor\})=1-\alpha\lfloor\alpha^{-1}\rfloor.

For example, when
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
,
\nu_{\alpha}
is
the Bernoulli distribution with parameter
1-\alpha
and when
\alpha=\frac{1}{k}
for an integer
k
, it is the uniform distribution on
\{0,\dots,k-1\}
.

It will be important that
\nu_{\alpha}
has minimum
variance among probability distributions

\mu
on integers with
\mathcal{Q}(\mu)\leq\alpha
, this can be seen using a standard argument and the main result of
[
2
]
.

In this work we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
\delta>0
there exists a number
V_{0}(\delta)
such that the following holds.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be a sequence of independent integer random variables such that
\mathcal{Q}(X_{i})\leq\alpha_{i}
,

\alpha_{i}\in(0,1]
and let

S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be independent,
Y_{i}\sim\nu_{\alpha_{i}}
.

If

{\mathrm{Var}}\,(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i})\geq V_{0}(\delta)

(1)

then

\mathcal{Q}(S_{n})\leq\mathcal{Q}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Y_{i})(1+\delta)

(2)

for some
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\{-1,1\}
.

As the sequence
(a_{i}Y_{i})
itself satisfies the conditions of the theorem, (
2
) is optimal up to the factor
(1+\delta)
.

For a random element
X
in a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
we
can extend the notation by defining
Q(X)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{P}(X=x)
.
The following corollary follows immediately by applying
the reduction of
[
30
]
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Theorem
1.1
extends verbatim to the case
when
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are independent random elements
from a separable Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
over the reals.
In particular, the constant
V_{0}(\delta)
does not depend on
\mathbb{H}
.

Since we can embed the random variables
Y_{i}

into
\mathbb{H}
by multiplying each by a fixed vector
from
\mathbb{H}
, the corollary is also optimal up to
the constant factor
(1+\delta)
.

Prior to this work,
similar asymptotically tight
upper bounds were available in the “uniform” case

\alpha_{1}=\dots=\alpha_{n}

[
14
,
30
]
,
and exact ones could be deduced,
via the reduction of
[
30
]
, in the case
\alpha_{i}^{-1}\in[1,2]\cup\mathbb{Z}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
from
the results of
[
19
,
21
]
mentioned above.
The latter results could also be used to show bounds similar to (
2
) for more general sequences
(\alpha_{i})
,
however, with an extra constant factor of up to
\frac{2\sqrt{6}}{3}
.
Alternatively, they can be derived from a very general framework of Madiman, Melbourne and Xu
[
20
]
.

This work has been motivated by a conjecture that an exact version of (
2
) holds, see also
[
6
,
14
,
21
]
:

Conjecture 1.3
.

(Juškevičius
[
13
]
)
Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be as in Theorem
1.1
.
Then there are
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\{-1,1\}

such that
\mathcal{Q}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i})\leq\mathcal{Q}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}Y_{i})
.

Let us explain
the need of a
general sequence
(a_{i})\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
in the statements of both the theorem and the conjecture.
Consider the special case
\alpha_{1}=\dots=\alpha_{n}=\alpha
. When
n
is even, the balanced choice, for example
a_{i}=(-1)^{i},i\in\{1,\dots,n\}

maximizes
\mathcal{Q}(\sum a_{i}Y_{i})

[
14
,
28
]
.
However, when
n
is odd and, for example,
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, the optimal number of -1s and 1s can depend
on
\alpha
in a non-trivial way
[
28
]
. On the other hand, it seems plausible that

asymptotically
, in the general setting of Theorem
1.1
, the choice of these signs has only a second order effect.

Theorem
1.1
shows that
Conjecture
1.3

is
close to being true in the limit; however, the constant
V_{0}(\delta)
that arises
in our proof is huge and not explicit.
If
the conjecture
is indeed true, its proof
appears to be beyond the
reach of the methods
we are
currently
aware of.

The rest of the paper consists of two
major parts: Part I (Section
2
) and
Part II (Section
3
).
The final proof of Theorem
1.1
is given in Section
2.5
of Part I.
The most difficult result needed for the proof,
Lemma
2.26
, is stated in Part I,
but the content of the whole Part II is devoted to its proof.

A reader who wishes to focus on the less trivial Part II may
proceed straight to
Section
3
after reading just the definitions in Section
2.1
and
Section
2.3
, and the statement
of Lemma
2.26
.

Intuitively, Lemma
2.26
that we prove in Part II can be seen as a special case of
Theorem
1.1
where for
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
the random variables
X_{i}
are distributed according to some “rearrangement” of the distribution
\nu_{\alpha_{i}}
(i.e. each
X_{i}
has
\lfloor\alpha_{i}^{-1}\rfloor
atoms with probability mass
\alpha_{i}
and, if there is any, an atom with the remaining mass), for
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
the values
\alpha_{i}
are bounded away from zero, “quantized” and “balanced” (Definition

),
while the last random variable

is a uniform random variable on an arbitrary finite set of integers.

2
Part I: Main proof

In this part we state the necessary lemmas and prove the main result, Theorem
1.1
.

2.1
Definitions

A measure
\mu
on integers is called
unimodal
if there is
m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\mu(\{m\})\geq\mu(\{m+1\})\geq\dots
and
\mu(\{m\})\geq\mu(\{m-1\})\geq\dots
. Any such
m
is called a
mode
of
\mu
.

In their work on rearrangement inequalities, Hardy, Littlewood and Pólya
[
11
]
introduced the notation
\mu^{+}
,
{}^{+}\mu
and
\mu^{*}

which will be defined
as follows.
Let
\mu
be a finite measure on
integers. We call a measure
\mu^{\prime}
on
\mathbb{Z}

a rearrangement of
\mu
if there exists a bijection
f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}

such that
\mu^{\prime}(\{x\})=\mu(\{f(x)\})
for each
x\in\mathbb{Z}
.

\mu^{+}
is the rearrangement of
\mu
that satisfies

\mu^{+}(\{0\})\geq\mu^{+}(\{1\})\geq\mu^{+}(\{-1\})\geq\mu^{+}(\{2\})\geq\mu^{+}(\{-2\})\geq\dots
. Similarly
{}^{+}\mu
is the rearrangement
of
\mu
that satisfies
{}^{+}\mu(\{0\})\geq{}^{+}\mu(\{-1\})\geq{}^{+}\mu(\{1\})\geq{}^{+}\mu(\{-2\})\geq{}^{+}\mu(\{2\})\geq\dots
. If
\mu^{+}={}^{+}\mu
then we say that
\mu
has a
symmetric decreasing
rearrangement and denote this symmetric measure by
\mu^{*}
.
Note that for a symmetric unimodal measure on integers
{}^{+}\mu=\mu^{+}=\mu^{*}=\mu
. When the law of an integer random variable
X
is
\mu
, we let
X^{+}
,
{}^{+}X
and
X^{*}
denote random variables distributed
according to
\mu^{+}
,
{}^{+}\mu
and
\mu^{*}
respectively, whenever these distributions exist.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Let
k=\lfloor\alpha^{-1}\rfloor
.
We call a (probability) measure
\mu

extremal
for
\alpha
if there is a set
S\subset\mathbb{Z}
,

|S|=k
and a number
b\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus S
such that

\mu(i)=\alpha
for all
i\in S
and
\mu(b)=1-k\alpha
.

Recall that an extremal measure for
\alpha
obtained by taking
S=\{0,\dots,k-1\}
and
b=k
is denoted by
\nu_{\alpha}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
We call a (probability) measure
\mu

standard extremal
for
\alpha
if it is the distribution of
X+i
where
i
is an integer and

X\sim\nu_{\alpha}
or
-X\sim\nu_{\alpha}
,
i. e. the support of
\mu
is an interval and the smallest atom, whenever it is unique, is on one of the endpoints of the interval. We denote the set of standard extremal measures for
\alpha
by
\mathrm{SE}(\alpha)
.
Any measure which is standard extremal for some
\alpha
will be called

standard extremal
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in(0,1]^{n}
.
A sequence of
standard extremal measures
(\mu_{i},i\in\{1,\dots,n\})
will be called a

standard extremal sequence (of measures) for
\alpha

if
for each
i

\mu_{i}
is
standard extremal for
\alpha_{i}
.
It will be called
optimal

if its convolution maximizes the concentration over the standard extremal sequences for
\alpha
:

(\mu_{i})\in\arg\max_{(\mu_{i}):\forall i\,\mu_{i}\in\mathrm{SE}(\alpha_{i})}\mathcal{Q}(*_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}).

We denote the corresponding maximum concentration function value by
{t_{SE}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
.

We will use the definitions above and the next one interchangeably both for sequences
(\mu_{i})
of measures and for sequences
(X_{i})

of corresponding independent random variables.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in(0,1]^{n}
. A standard extremal sequence
(X_{i})
for
(\alpha_{i})
will be
called

balanced

if each index
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
can be mapped
bijectively to

j_{i}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
so that
X_{j_{i}}\sim-X_{i}
.

We call
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in(0,1]^{n}

balanced
if
a balanced standard extremal sequence exists for
(\alpha_{i})
, equivalently, if for each
\alpha\in(0,1]
either
\alpha^{-1}
is an odd integer or
|\{i:\alpha_{i}=\alpha\}|
is even. We call
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})

strongly balanced
if
|\{i:\alpha_{i}=\alpha\}|
is even for each
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

\mathbb{P}(\sum X_{i}=0)
equals
\mathcal{Q}(\sum X_{i})
and is the same for each standard extremal balanced sequence
(X_{i})
for
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
.
We denote this probability by
{t_{SE}^{bal}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
.

Finally, the ultimate quantity of our interest will be the maximum concentration
a sum of random variables with given concentration bounds can achieve:

Definition 2.5
.

Given
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in(0,1]^{n}
define

t(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}):=\sup_{(\mu_{i}):\forall i\,\mathcal{Q}(\mu_{i})\leq\alpha_{i}}\mathcal{Q}(*_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}).

A probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{Z}
is called
strongly unimodal
if its convolution with any unimodal probability measure
on
\mathbb{Z}
is unimodal. It is called log-concave if for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}

\mu(\{i\})^{2}\geq\mu(\{i-1\})\mu(\{i+1\}).

Keilson and Gerber
[
16
]
proved that (a) a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{Z}
is log-concave if and only if it is strongly unimodal (their Theorem 3) and (b) the class of strongly unimodal measures is closed under convolution (their Theorem 2).

As our measures
\nu_{\alpha}
are log-concave, they are strongly unimodal and
the convolution of each standard extremal sequence of measures is also log-concave and strongly unimodal.
This property will be of fundamental importance in our proofs.

2.2
Useful lemmas

In this section we present a few simple but essential lemmas.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
Y_{\alpha}\sim\nu_{\alpha}
. Then

{\mathrm{Var}}\,Y_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{12}\left(-3\alpha^{2}k^{2}(k+1)^{2}+2\alpha k(k+1)(2k+1)\right),\quad k=\lfloor\alpha^{-1}\rfloor.

In particular, if
\alpha=\frac{1}{k}
for a positive integer
k
then

{\mathrm{Var}}\,Y_{\alpha}=\frac{k^{2}-1}{12}.

If
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
then

{\mathrm{Var}}\,Y_{\alpha}=\alpha(1-\alpha).

Furthermore, let
f
be defined by
f(\alpha)={\mathrm{Var}}\,Y_{\alpha}
.
Then
f
is continuous and non-increasing for
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
If
k
is a positive integer, then in the interval
(\frac{1}{k+1},\frac{1}{k})

f
is differentiable and for
\alpha
in this interval
f^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)<0
and

-\frac{1}{6}k(k+1)(k+2)\leq f^{\prime}(\alpha)\leq-\frac{1}{6}k(k^{2}-1)\leq 0.

Finally, if
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k\geq 2
then

\partial_{+}f(\frac{1}{k+1})=\sup_{\alpha\in(\frac{1}{k+1},\frac{1}{k})}f^{\prime}(\alpha)=-\frac{1}{6}k(k^{2}-1)=\inf_{\alpha\in(\frac{1}{k},\frac{1}{k-1})}f^{\prime}(\alpha)=\partial_{-}f(\frac{1}{k-1}),

where
\partial_{+}f(\alpha)
,
\partial_{-}f(\alpha)
denote the right and left derivatives of
f
at
\alpha
respectively.

Thus at points
\frac{1}{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
f
is described by a convex function,
however in-between those points
f
is concave. Also its derivative within the intervals
(\frac{1}{k+1},\frac{1}{k})
it is decreasing, however the ranges of
f^{\prime}
in the consequtive intervals have an opposite, “increasing” order.

Proof

The second and the third equalities are just the variance of a uniform random variable on
k
points
an the variance of a Bernoulli random variable.
To prove the first equality, let
p=p(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha-\frac{1}{k+1}}{\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k+1}}=1-(k+1)(1-k\alpha)
.
Then

\nu_{\alpha}=p\nu_{\frac{1}{k}}+(1-p)\nu_{\frac{1}{k+1}}.

By the law of total variance and the case
\alpha=\frac{1}{k}
of this lemma and by a simple calculation:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\,Y_{\alpha}=\frac{p(k^{2}-1)}{12}+\frac{(1-p)((k+1)^{2}-1)}{12}+p(1-p)/4

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\left(-3\alpha^{2}k^{2}(k+1)^{2}+2\alpha k(k+1)(2k+1)\right).

It is easy to check from this that
\lim_{x\to 1-}f(x)=0
and
\lim_{x\to\frac{1}{k}-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to\frac{1}{k}+}f(x)
for
k
integer,
k\geq 2
.

For
k
a positive integer and
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{k+1},\frac{1}{k})

we have
f^{\prime}(\alpha)=12^{-1}(-6\alpha k^{2}(k+1)^{2}+2k(k+1)(2k+1))
,
so
f^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)<0
in this interval and

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{6}k(k+1)(k+2)=-\frac{1}{12}(-6k(k+1)^{2}+2k(k+1)(2k+1))

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to\frac{1}{k}-}f^{\prime}(x)\leq f^{\prime}(\alpha)\leq\lim_{x\to\frac{1}{k+1}+}f^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}(-6k^{2}(k+1)+2k(k+1)(2k+1))=-\frac{1}{6}k(k^{2}-1)\leq 0.

Since for a fixed integer
k
,

12^{-1}(-6xk^{2}(k+1)^{2}+2k(k+1)(2k+1))
is differentiable for all
x
,

\lim_{x\to\frac{1}{k}-}f^{\prime}(x)=\partial_{-}f(x)
and

\lim_{x\to\frac{1}{k}+}f^{\prime}(x)=\partial_{+}f(x)
.

\Box

It will be important for us that
for a log-concave random variable
the value of the
concentration function
is determined by its variance up to a constant factor.
The next lemma follows from Theorem 1.1 of Bobkov, Marsiglietti and Melbourne
[
6
]
and
an improvement of the upper bound by Aravinda
[
1
]
.

Lemma 2.7

(
[
1
,
6
]
)
.

Let
X
be an integer-valued log-concave random variable
Then

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+12{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}}\leq\mathcal{Q}(X)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}}.

Our next lemma says that the distribution of a log-concave integer random variable
X
must be
almost flat in an interval of length around
o(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}}\,X)
that includes its mode.
It will be used multiple times in this paper.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
X
be a random integer variable with a log-concave distribution.
Assume
\mathbb{P}(X=x_{0})=\max_{x}\mathbb{P}(X=x)
. Let
i
be a positive integer.
If, for
\gamma\in[0,1)
we have

{\mathrm{Var}}\,X\geq\frac{2(\gamma+1)i^{3}\mathbb{P}(X=x_{0})}{(1-\gamma)^{3}}

then

\max(\mathbb{P}(X=x_{0}-i),\mathbb{P}(X=x_{0}+i))\geq\mathbb{P}(X=x_{0})\gamma.

(3)

In particular, (
3
) always holds for

\gamma=1-\left(\frac{4\mathbb{P}(X=x_{0})}{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}i\geq 1-\frac{2^{\frac{2}{3}}i}{\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}}.

Proof

Write
p_{0}:=\mathbb{P}(X=x_{0})
.
Suppose that (
3
) does not hold. Then

\mathbb{P}(X=x_{0}+i)<p_{0}\gamma\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(X=x_{0}-i)<p_{0}\gamma.

Since
X
is log-concave we get for non-zero integers
k
:

\mathbb{P}(X=x_{0}+ki)<p_{0}\gamma^{k}.

Using unimodality:

\mathbb{E}(X-x_{0})^{2}\mathbb{I}_{X-x_{0}\geq 0}<p_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}i(ki)^{2}\gamma^{k-1}=\frac{p_{0}i^{3}(\gamma+1)}{(1-\gamma)^{3}}.

and similarly for
\mathbb{E}(X-x_{0})^{2}\mathbb{I}_{X-x_{0}<0}
.
Thus

\mathbb{E}(X-x_{0})^{2}<\frac{2(\gamma+1)i^{3}p_{0}}{(1-\gamma)^{3}}.

So

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\,X=\mathbb{E}(X-x_{0}+(x_{0}-\mathbb{E}X))^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(X-x_{0})^{2}-(x_{0}-\mathbb{E}X)^{2}<\frac{2(\gamma+1)i^{3}p_{0}}{(1-\gamma)^{3}},

which is a contradiction.

Using
the already proved part we have
that (
3
) holds if

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\,X\geq\frac{4i^{3}p_{0}}{(1-\gamma)^{3}}\geq\frac{2(\gamma+1)i^{3}p_{0}}{(1-\gamma)^{3}},

or

\gamma\leq 1-\left(\frac{4p_{0}}{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}i.

Finally, the last inequality of (
3
) follows by using the upper bound from Lemma
2.7
.

\Box

A simple corollary of the last lemma shows that the terms of a sum of independent log-concave random variables which
don’t contribute much to the variance of the sum do not affect the concentration much.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\epsilon>0
. Let
X
be a log-concave integer random variable,
and let
Y
be any integer random variable.
If
{\mathrm{Var}}\,Y\leq\epsilon{\mathrm{Var}}\,X
then

\mathcal{Q}(X+Y)\geq(1-3\cdot 2^{\frac{4}{9}}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}})\mathcal{Q}(X).

Proof

By Chebyshev’s theorem for any
a>0

\mathbb{P}(|Y-\mathbb{E}Y|\geq\frac{a}{2}\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X})\leq\frac{4{\mathrm{Var}}\,Y}{a^{2}{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}\leq\frac{4\epsilon}{a^{2}}.

Let
m
be a mode of
X
. By Lemma
2.8

either for all integers
i
with
m\leq i\leq m+a\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}

or for all integers
i
with
m-a\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}\leq i\leq m

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X=i)\geq\mathbb{P}(X=m)\left(1-\frac{2^{\frac{2}{3}}a\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}}{\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}}\right)=\mathbb{P}(X=m)(1-2^{\frac{2}{3}}a).

Without loss of generality we can assume that the inequality holds for all integer
i\in I_{1}
,
I_{1}=[m,m+a\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}]
.
A closed interval of length
l
contains either
\lfloor l\rfloor
or

\lfloor l\rfloor+1
integer points, it contains exactly
\lfloor l\rfloor+1
integer points when it starts with an integer.
Thus the interval
I_{1}

contains at least as many integer points as the interval
I_{2}=[\mathbb{E}Y-\frac{a}{2}\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X},\mathbb{E}Y+\frac{a}{2}\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}]
.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(X+Y)\geq\mathbb{P}(X+Y=m+\lfloor\mathbb{E}Y+\frac{a}{2}\sqrt{{\mathrm{Var}}\,X}\rfloor)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(Y\in I_{2})\min_{i\in I_{1}}\mathbb{P}(X=i)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(X=m)(1-\frac{4\epsilon}{a^{2}})(1-2^{\frac{2}{3}}a)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(X=m)(1-\frac{4\epsilon}{a^{2}}-2^{\frac{2}{3}}a).

As the minimum of
f(x)=\frac{b}{x^{2}}+cx
for
b,c>0
is
3\cdot 2^{-\frac{2}{3}}b^{\frac{1}{3}}c^{\frac{2}{3}}
,
maximizing the right side of the last inequality over
a

we get that

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(X+Y)\geq\mathbb{P}(X=m)\left(1-3\cdot 2^{-\frac{2}{3}}(4\epsilon)^{\frac{1}{3}}(2^{\frac{2}{3}})^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(X=m)(1-3\cdot 2^{\frac{4}{9}}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}).

\Box

Lemma 2.11
.

t(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})

Proof

We omit the details as the proof, based on Krein-Milman theorem, see, e.g., proof of Theorem 1 in

The proof of monotonicity of the optimal value for
standard extremal sequences is also simple, but with a different argument.

Lemma 2.12
.

{t_{SE}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})

Fix

{t_{SE}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha^{\prime})\geq{t_{SE}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha)

Consider a standard extremal sequence of independent random variables

for
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha)

Write
\mu_{n-1}
and
\mu
for the laws

Let
I
be a set of

Furthermore we can assume that a set

of

largest atoms of
\mu_{n-1}

It follows that

is maximized over the “extended set” of standard extremal measures
\mu
for

when

Now consider replacing

.

So

\displaystyle(\mu_{n-1}*\mu)(\{0\})=p(\mu_{n-1}*\nu_{-I})(\{0\})+(1-p)(\mu_{n-1}*\nu_{-J})(\{0\})

\displaystyle=p^{\prime}(\mu_{n-1}*\nu_{-I})(\{0\})+(1-p^{\prime})(\mu_{n-1}*\nu_{-J})(\{0\})+(p-p^{\prime})\Delta

\displaystyle=(\mu_{n-1}*\mu^{\prime})(\{0\})+(p-p^{\prime})\Delta

\mu_{n-1}
and the average of

largest atoms of
\mu_{n-1}

\displaystyle{t_{SE}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha^{\prime})\geq(\mu_{n-1}*\mu^{\prime})(\{0\})

\displaystyle\geq(\mu_{n-1}*\mu)(\{0\})={t_{SE}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha).

We now proceed to give a few lemmas about the continuity of the optimal concentration function

We start with a simple lemma for the balanced case that is valid for large

Lemma 2.13
.

If
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-2})
is a balanced sequence,

{t_{SE}^{bal}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})\leq(1+8\alpha\epsilon){t_{SE}^{bal}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha,\alpha).

Proof

Let
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
be the laws of the sum of balanced standard extremal sequences for
(\alpha,\alpha)
and
(\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
respectively.
Then
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
are symmetric and unimodal with support on
\{-1,0,1\}

The last line follows since

\Box

The next couple of lemmas are a bit more technical.

into a grid with step size

]

3.1
Approximation by discretized multivariate normal distribution

Recall that the total variation distance

In this section we state a result which
says that the distribution of a sum of bounded iid random vectors with values in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}

tends in
total variation
to what one would expect from the Central Limit Theorem: an appropriate
discretized multivariate normal random distribution.

Even though this type of CLT seems to be quite a fundamental property of
lattice random vectors and the topic has more than a solid body of
literature from various schools, an accessible theorem is surprisingly
difficult to locate.

Our first attempt to do this was using the book of Bhattacharya and Ranga Rao
[
4
]
, see Corollary 22.3 on p. 237 which is loosely based on a multivariate local limit theorem by Bikelis
[
5
]
proved using
the so-called
Edgeworth expansion method. While these results give extremely precise asymptotics,
their statement is a bit technical and the edge case that concerns convergence in total variation with rate
o(1)
is included only in
[
4
]
without a full proof.

Therefore we will use the following much more recent result proved using Stein’s method,
which we restate here in a bit more self-contained form and a slightly adapted notation.

(Theorem 4.2 of Barbour, Luczak and Xia
[

3.3
Applying inverse Littlewood–Offord

In this section we introduce a theorem from the
inverse Littlewood–Offord theory
and use it to derive one of the key lemmas necessary for the proof of Lemma

.

We adapt the next definition from
[
29
]
. Let
G
be an additive group. A symmetric generalized arithmetic progression in
G
, or a symmetric GAP, is a quadruplet
{\bf A}=(A,M,g,r)
, where the
rank

\operatorname{rank}({\bf A})=r
is a non-negative integer, the
dimensions

M=(M_{1},\dots,M_{r})
are an
r
-tuple of positive reals, the
generators
(steps)
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{r})
are an
r
-tuple of elements of
G
, and
A\subseteq G
is the set

A=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}j_{i}g_{i}:j_{i}\in[-M_{i},\dots,M_{i}]\cap\mathbb{Z}\quad\forall i\in\{1,\dots,r\}\right\},

Like
[
25
,
29
]
we may abuse notation and write
A
for
{\bf A}
.
For any
t>0
, also following
[
29
]
,
we define the
dilate

t{\bf A}
of
{\bf A}
to be the GAP
{\bf A}_{t}:=(A_{t},tM,g,r)
formed by dilating all the dimensions by
t
.
We say that
\bf{A}
is
proper
if all the elements
j_{1}g_{1}+\dots+j_{r}g_{r}
,
j_{i}\in\{-\lfloor M_{i}\rfloor,\dots,\lfloor M_{i}\rfloor\}

\forall i
, are distinct.
We define the
volume
of
\bf A
to be
\operatorname{vol}({\bf A}):=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(2\lfloor M_{i}\rfloor+1)
. Note that
|A|\leq\operatorname{vol}({\bf A})
, with equality if and only if
\bf A
is proper.

The next claim follows almost directly from the definition.
For two sets
A
and
B
from an additive group
G
we denote their

by
A+B=\{x+y:x\in A,y\in B\}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
be two symmetric GAPs
of rank
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
respectively and volume
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
respectively.
Then their sumset
A_{1}+A_{2}
can be represented by a symmetric GAP of rank
r_{1}+r_{2}
and volume
v_{1}v_{2}
.

Proof

The sumset is exactly the symmetric GAP with generators obtained by concatenating the generator tuples of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}

and dimensions obtained by concatenating the dimension tuples of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
.

\Box

We will need the following particular version of the inverse Littlewood–Offord theorem:

Theorem 3.4

(Theorem 2.1 of Nguyen and Vu
[
25
]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon<1
and

be positive constants. Let

be a multiset from a torsion-free group

. Let

be independent Rademacher random variables

Assume that

Then, there exists a proper symmetric GAP

of rank

that contains all but at most

elements of
V
(counting multiplicity), where

|A|=O_{C,\varepsilon}(\rho(V)^{-1}n^{-\frac{r}{2}}).

In this paper we only use

. We
will apply the theorem even in a simpler case where

,
however the generators of the resulting GAP may still be in

The next lemma is our main application.

be a fixed positive integer,

of rank at most

such that all but at most

of random variables

have their support contained in

We obtain the decomposition using the idea from Lemma 3.1 of Juškevičius

\displaystyle\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{m^{\prime}}w_{i}^{\prime}\mu_{i}^{\prime}\,\mbox{ for some }m^{\prime}\leq\frac{N}{2},\,w_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{2a_{i}^{\prime}}{N}\mbox{ and }a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime}\in\{1,2,\dots\}
