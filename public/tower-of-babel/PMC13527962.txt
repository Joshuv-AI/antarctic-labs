Aging trends, challenges, and policy framework within the Ghanaian context: a systematic review
Authors: Deborah A. Dadson, Alfred Boakye, Henrietta A. Bennett, Ebenezer Martey, Amma S. Aboagye Kyei, Josephine A. Boateng, Adam Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPopulation aging has received considerable attention across economies presenting significant social, economic, and health challenges. While research has focused on demographics and health, limited attention has been given to the context, processes and outcomes of active aging in Ghanaian society. An exploration into this is essential to understanding the underlying dynamics between social stability, socioeconomic growth, health promotion and quality of life of older adults in Ghana.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review to compile and report on existing empirical evidence on population trends, age-related health issues, available support systems, and needs of older adults in Ghana to inform age-friendly services and policies. In accordance with PRISMA guidelines, we screened peer-reviewed articles published between 2015 and 2025 using search terms related to aging in Ghana.ResultsThrough thematic synthesis, four themes were identified – health utilization, health status, social support, and policy implications. Older adults who had better emotional, relational, and instrumental support from family and the community demonstrated higher levels of wellbeing, greater use of healthcare services, and reduced feelings of social isolation. Individuals reported unmet needs in food insecurity, financial hardship, lack of assistance with daily activities, and limited access to healthcare. Furthermore, older adults who reported multiple chronic conditions reported high levels of functional disabilities and physical difficulties.ConclusionsThis review underscores an immediate, coordinated policy intervention and research action to prepare Ghana for its next demographic phase. Priority action should focus on enhancing healthcare access, fostering social inclusion and promoting community engagement among older adults in Ghana to improve their quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28040-6.

Background

Several empirical evidence suggest that population growth of a country is linked to interactive dynamics between social stability, socioeconomic growth, health promotion, and quality of life. Understanding the growth changes and age structures of populations in sub-Saharan Africa, especially Ghana is important to achieving the 2030 Agenda for Healthy People and Sustainable Development Goals [1]. This review is particularly relevant to SDG 3 - Good Health and Well-Being, through its focus on healthy aging, healthcare access, chronic disease management, and quality of life among older adults in Ghana. Findings from our systematic review also contribute to SDG 1 (No Poverty), SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities), and SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), given the identified challenges related to financial hardship, social isolation, and the need for age-friendly policies and community support systems in the Ghanaian communities. The aim of this systematic review is to theoretically synthesize both qualitative and quantitative findings on population aging in Ghana, across major regions to identify key gaps and rely on estimates to expand current literature, policies and practices.

A population is typically considered an aging population if the proportion of its older adults aged 65 and older is over 7% [2]. Currently, population growth is one of the major demographic trends shaping the world [3]. With an average annual increase of approximately 83 million people in the global population, the world population was estimated at 7.6 billion in 2017 and is projected to reach 8.6 billion by 2030. Over the same period, the population of persons aged 60 years and older was estimated at approximately 0.6–0.7 billion in 2017 and is expected to approach 1.0 billion by 2030 and 1.4 billion by 2050 [4]. The changing demographic trend has been attributed to several factors such as increasing levels of life expectancy and decreasing levels of fertility, advanced healthcare practices, improved medication and lifestyle changes [5, 6]. Understanding the trends and patterns of population aging and associated challenges over time will offer valuable insights into how socioeconomic and health forces could be used to plan interventions and policies that are context-specific to meet the needs of older people across the world.

Although every country across the world is experiencing growth in the number of older people, the trends are not the same across countries. Research shows that roughly two-thirds of older people reside in developing regions, including sub-Saharan Africa. In 2017, 5.5% of older people resided in Ghana and these estimates are projected to increase to 8.3% by 2050 [7]. The aging trend of Ghana’s population is influenced by factors such as high fertility and low death rates among infants. Although Ghana’s population is aging at a relatively slow pace compared to other developing sub-Saharan African countries, it has maintained an annual average growth rate of older adults from 4.6% since 1960 to 7.2% in 2000 [8].

Population aging has generated both opportunities and challenges for individuals, social and healthcare systems, and society. Several researchers have drawn attention to specific challenges related to the growing burden of chronic diseases and disabilities. Specifically, in the context of Ghana, marked by healthcare access and utilization barriers, research reveals elevated levels of morbidity and functional impairment [9–11], a rise in both communicable and non-communicable diseases [12], increased risk of falls and fall-related injuries [13, 14] and mental issues [15]. Despite these challenges, population aging has opened additional opportunities for economic growth especially when accompanied by better health, a phenomenon known as “compression of morbidity” [16]. Additionally, a considerable number of older adults remain a critical social support pillar where they take up caregiving roles as grandparents and engage in volunteering activities in later life [17, 18].

Despite Ghana’s long history of aging research, many prior studies have primarily focused on six areas: demographic profiles of aging; physical and mental health status of older adults; care and support; roles and responsibilities; social representations of aging (ageism and stereotypes); socio-economic status and financial protection for older adults [19]. However, little to no studies have explored areas of technology and aging, institutional support systems, transportation, home modification and availability of geriatric services for older adults in Ghana. Identifying these gaps are important priorities for future research and policy development as they may provide opportunities to improve healthcare access, social connectedness, and service delivery for older adults. Furthermore, investigating these gaps in more detail contributes to understanding the context, processes and outcomes of active aging in Ghanaian society [20].

In 2004, the United Nations through the National Institute on Aging organized an eight-member panel workshop for more empirical evidence on aging to understand the status and needs of older adults in sub-Saharan Africa [21]. While the meeting provided an avenue for improving age-related needs, there was limited research to support discussions. To the best of our knowledge, this paper represents the first systematic review to provide up-to-date summaries of research, identify gaps, and provide specific recommendations for stakeholders, older adults, family caregivers, and healthcare professionals. Specifically, our review aimed to answer the following question: What does the existing literature reveal about the health status, healthcare utilization, social support systems, policy implications, and needs of older adults in Ghana, and what gaps remain in aging-related research, services, and interventions?

Methods

A structured and comprehensive process was followed in this review to identify, evaluate and synthesize peer-reviewed articles related to aging in Ghana. The aim of this systematic review was to synthesize existing data on needs, policies, and support systems that impact the health and independence of older adults to inform decisions and interventions that are beneficial to them. Methodological procedures including search strategy, article screening, and data extraction were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [22].

Eligibility criteria

The applied criteria for eligibility included articles that (i) focused on older adults or the elderly, (ii) were conducted in Ghana, (iii) addressed issues that were related to needs, health, care, support and policy, and (iv) were published in peer-reviewed journals between 2015 and 2025. To ensure that our review was comprehensive enough, studies that used either qualitative, quantitative, or mixed methods were considered for analysis. Articles were excluded if (a) they were conducted in other sub-Saharan African countries aside Ghana (b) did not address issues that were related to aging and the older population.

Search strategy

A thorough literature search for peer-reviewed articles was conducted using several databases including Embase, PubMed, Google Scholar, PsycINFO, and Ovid. PubMed was included due to its comprehensive coverage in public health research on aging, while Google Scholar was selected to extract broader multidisciplinary ‘gray literature’ that is not indexed by other databases [23]. An initial intensive search of the databases was conducted to assess the scope and volume of available literature and identify key articles that were relevant to this review. The search was restricted to English-language publications and included keywords and terms that were related to aging, Ghana, older adults, needs, illness, care, support and policy. A combination of keywords (support + needs+ policies + aging + Ghana) and BOOLEAN operators were used to ensure all relevant articles were identified and captured. The search and review processes began in November 2024 and were completed in July 2025. Included articles were derived primarily from PubMed and Google Scholar as searches in Embase; Ovid and PsycINFO did not identify studies that met the eligibility criteria. Due to the limitations of Google Scholar in relation to reproducibility, measures were applied to ensure the data search followed a transparent process. The search was conducted by one reviewer using predefined search terms that were relevant to the key objective of the review question. The search strategy and initial results were verified by a second reviewer to ensure consistency before screening. Results were subsequently sorted by relevance while applying the eligibility criteria against potentially eligible studies. Although searches in PubMed followed a similar process used in Google Scholar, there were publication date limits that were set in relation to the eligibility criteria. Detailed search strings for PubMed and Google Scholar can be found in the supplemental file.

Data selection/extraction process

After the initial screening of the titles and abstracts, a secondary screening was conducted by independent reviewers on the full-text articles to ensure that the included articles met all the eligibility criteria. Any articles that met the inclusion criteria but were not properly referenced were manually included. The selected studies were categorized based on identified themes and imported into Rayyan [24] where three of the authors continued with screening and evaluation.

Three of the authors (DD, AB, HB) extracted data from the different journal database sources mentioned above. AA and EM double-checked all the data to ensure there were no errors. All the search results were stored in Microsoft Excel and Google Drive, which are our main platforms for keeping track of the review. Information regarding the study title, authors, publication year, study objectives, methodology, study results and conclusions were extracted for all included articles, after ineligible articles were removed. For included articles that used multi-country datasets, like the SAGE, only Ghana-specific results were extracted and included in the review. Quality assessment

The quality of included articles was assessed by two of the authors and reviewed by a third author using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist [25] based on study designs (see Table 1). For example, qualitative articles were assessed using the JBI checklist for qualitative studies with 10 items while cross-sectional studies were appraised using the corresponding checklist with 8 items. Qualitative studies were rated as high quality (8–10), medium quality (5–7) and low quality (1–4) whereas cross-sectional studies were rated as high quality (7–8), medium quality (5–6) and low quality (1–4). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consultation with the third reviewer. All qualitative studies included were of high quality. For the cross-sectional studies, 23 studies were rated as high quality and 4 were rated as medium quality. One study was a mixed method which was appraised based on both the qualitative and quantitative checklists. The quantitative part of the article was rated as medium, while the qualitative design was high quality. This study was included since it met our inclusion criteria for medium and high-quality papers. One article, which was of low quality, was excluded based on the appraisal results. Data analysis and synthesis

Following the guidelines for meta-integration synthesis approach for systematic reviews, the extracted data were analyzed using the six steps proposed by Frantzen and Fetters [61]. This approach is consistent with systematic reviews that seek to integrate findings across quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods into a comprehensive and meaningful synthesis [62]. First, the included studies were categorized by methodological design. Quantitative findings were “qualitized” into textual descriptive to enable cross-method comparisons. Prior to data integration and assessment for convergence, divergence, and complementarity, free-line coding was conducted to generate an initial descriptive theme for each type of study. Codes were transformed into themes and sub-themes which emerged inductively from the data. Findings that showed strong convergence were considered robust, while divergent findings were maintained for analytic value. Interpretations made from final conclusions that emerged from data integration moved beyond any single methodological strand.

The iterative synthesis process, which required repeated engagement with the data at every stage, ensured rigor and comprehensiveness of the analysis while accounting for nuances, context, and methodological variations. While quantitative studies provided trends and associations, qualitative studies provided insights and lived experiences of older adults in Ghana. Two authors (AB & ASAK) independently conducted coding in steps 3 and 4 – intra-method synthesis and inter-method synthesis and integration – and resolved discrepancies through discussions and consensus with the review team. The research team met regularly throughout the data extraction, coding, and synthesis process and kept an audit trail to support transparency and rigor. The Rayyan software was used for data screening and Microsoft Excel for data management.

Results

We conducted a systematic review that yielded 42,604 from two databases (Google Scholar and PubMed) which were retrieved from our initial database search. 22 references were removed manually and 346 duplicates identified by Rayyan software [63]. A total of 42,236 studies were moved to the screening stage after removing duplicates. First, we performed an evaluation of the titles and abstracts and excluded articles that failed to satisfy eligibility. At the full-text stage, we excluded 19 studies that were either misaligned with wrong study design (n = 5), due to low quality (n = 1), wrong population (n = 2), and full text not found (n = 11). We selected articles that were published between 2015 and 2025 (see Table 2). The final screening yielded 37 studies that were included in the final analysis. Out of this number, 26 studies were quantitative, 10 were qualitative, and 1 was mixed method. All the studies included in the review originated from Ghana.

Theme – health, social support needs, and policy frameworks of older adults in Ghana

Study findings

Theme 1: healthcare utilization

Barriers

Four studies [37, 40, 48, 59] expressed the role that income and geographical location play in influencing older adults’ ability to access healthcare. Evidence shows that older adults who receive pension benefits [37] and individuals in the wealthy quintile are likely to have voluntary insurance cover. These securities enable them to receive proper healthcare and therefore, are likely to report good health [30]. Contrary to these assertions, there is only a handful that benefits from such available arrangements. Most of the older adults in Ghana are not enrolled in the social security scheme, which makes accessing quality healthcare almost impossible [30, 37]. Although policies by the government of Ghana such as National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), Livelihood Empowerment Against Poverty (LEAP) and Social Security and National Insurance Trust (SSNIT) provide some relief, they are not adequate on their own to ensure proper healthcare is given to older adults.

Closely related to income is the neighborhood and environments where people live. Most older adults who are considered generally capable of accessing healthcare live in urban centers [37] and can seek both inpatient and outpatient care from health facilities [48]. This points to the fact that the greater percentage of older adults who find it challenging to seek care from primary health facilities are usually rural residents.

Additionally, evidence from the reviewed studies emphasized age-unfriendly healthcare facilities and experiences of disrespect and non-compassionate care from healthcare providers [44, 55, 59]. These experiences made participants feel discriminated against, while attributing this to limited gerontological education among caregivers and providers. Similarly, Atakro and his colleagues highlighted additional challenges like queuing, insufficient information from healthcare professionals, and financial difficulty which hindered the quality of care received by older adults [51].

Access

The National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), Livelihood Empowerment Against Poverty (LEAP) and Social Security and National Insurance Trust (SSNIT) are among some of the policies instituted by the Government of Ghana to tackle challenges older adults are likely to face [26, 30, 37]. These schemes, which were introduced by the Government of Ghana to lessen the financial burden of citizens when seeking healthcare, have primarily encouraged health seeking behavior among older adults. Retirement benefits and other voluntary health insurance cover the cost of accessing good healthcare among older adults [37].

Theme 2: health status

Psychological wellbeing

Overall, five studies reported outcomes related to the psychological health of older adults [27, 33, 38, 39, 66]. Mental distress accounted for the most reported clinical condition, recording a mean of 11.71 [39] compared to earlier findings which showed a mean of 7.5 [46]. Across all the psychological wellbeing studies, depression prevalence was measured mainly through self-reports or a symptom-based approach [27, 33, 35, 38, 39, 66]. One study examined associations between psychological wellbeing and selected health and social factors. Gyasi et al. [34] reported statistically significant negative relationships between happiness and pain, social isolation, functional limitations, and physical inactivity, while age and income were not significantly associated with happiness [34]. Increased physical inactivity and pain interference were also associated with higher levels of emotional problems in this sample.

Two studies reported findings related to social support and psychological wellbeing. Gyasi et al. [27] found that older adults receiving structural support reported higher levels of general satisfaction and wellbeing [27]. Similarly, Boakye and Armah [52] documented evidence of emotional bonds and social support provided through religious institutions, particularly during organized events such as appreciation days [52].

Physical and mental wellbeing

Eight studies accounted for physical, mental and functional difficulties associated with health [34, 39, 42, 44]. For instance, a cross-sectional study utilizing data from SAGE Ghana Wave 2 tracks 80% of participants with insufficient intake of food being functionally disabled [32]. However, as older adults deliberately engage in exercising, the damage that can be caused by insufficient nourishment significantly declines [32, 47, 50]. Similarly, sleep problems accounted for pain interference and poor self-rated health [34, 39] with 37% of females experiencing poor self-rated health and sleep because of pain interference.

Records of some physical health challenges faced by older adults include oral health problems, angina, stroke, hypertension, diabetes, cataract,and bodily pain, with hypertension and diabetes being the most common cardiometabolic disease among older adults [32, 42]. As data reflects, an increase in age led to the likelihood of having moderate to severe disabilities and multimorbidity increased [42]. For instance, findings from Nutakor et al. [49] show that more than half (54.88%) of older adults have poor mental or cognitive function [49]. For gender disparities, more women (34%) than men (23%) reported functional decline and comorbidities/multimorbidity [41, 43].

Theme 3: social support & family networks

Twelve studies reviewed emphasized various forms of social support as integral components of caregiving within the Ghanaian context. These forms of support included financial support [26, 27, 29, 51, 54, 58, 59], emotional support [27, 28, 30, 52, 55, 56], informational support [26, 28, 51, 52], instrumental support [28, 31, 52, 54, 58], and spiritual/religious support [52, 53].

Financial support

Financial support seems to be the most sought-after primarily due to the inadequacy of existing social welfare systems in addressing the evolving needs of older adults in Ghana. The predominance of informal sector employment, which typically lacks pension coverage after retirement, coupled with escalating living expenses, has exacerbated the socioeconomic vulnerabilities of older adults. For example, older adults who were engaged in informal businesses reported that limited digital skills led to low economic resilience overall [57]. Consequently, many people depend on adult children or extended family members for financial assistance. Empirical evidence indicates that older adults who received financial assistance or remittances from close or distant children were more likely to utilize healthcare services [26, 30, 51, 59], meet their nutritional needs, housing needs, preferential healthcare and social needs [54]. These individuals also reported enhanced psychological well-being [27]. Although findings are mixed, extended families have been shown to provide financial support, particularly in the context of urbanization and migration of adult children. For instance, a study by Adam et al. [29] found that older adults who received remittances through familial networks reported improved financial well-being [29]. Furthermore, women, individuals with lower educational attainment, the unemployed, and those with limited resources were more likely to receive remittances and utilize healthcare services [26, 27]. The financial precarity of older adults has prompted policy discussions regarding the expansion of state-led initiatives such as the NHIS [51], and the LEAP program [26], aimed at mitigating financial burdens and enhancing health and functional outcomes.

Emotional support

In addition to financial support, emotional support was highlighted in six studies. The formation of social connections and companionship was deeply embedded within social networks, with women reportedly receiving more emotional support than men. Gyasi et al. [27] found that 62% of women who established emotional bonds with family or friends reported improved psychological well-being. Similarly, 74.4% of older adults who received emotional support demonstrated better functional health literacy, reported improved health status [28] and exhibited increased health care utilization [30]. Community-based programs, when effectively scaled, possess the potential to alleviate loneliness and poor health among older adults. Religious organizations also play a significant role in providing emotional support, contributing to increased happiness, reduced stress and loneliness, and enhanced quality of life [52, 56]. However, such support was notably absent in rural healthcare settings, where older adults frequently encountered discrimination and verbal abuse from healthcare professionals, reflecting a lack of compassionate and respectful care [55].

Informational support

Informational support, as documented in four studies, is essential for older adults. Most participants exhibited low levels of formal education, which impeded their ability to navigate social systems and access relevant services. Often, older adults lack critical information related to health literacy and disease prevention. Informational support-comprising suggestions, feedback, or advice on health-related or aging-related matters-proved beneficial [52]. Conversely, the absence of geriatric training among healthcare professionals frequently resulted in inadequate communication, leading to confusion and dissatisfaction [51]. When informational support was provided by friends and family members, older adults were more likely to utilize healthcare services [26], experience reduced anxiety and stress, and report improved physical and mental well-being [52]. Quantitative findings suggest a positive correlation between high levels of informational support and improved functional health and overall health status [28].

Instrumental support

The literature also highlights instrumental support, which includes tangible assistance that enables older adults to live independently despite weak formal welfare systems. Common forms of instrumental support include the provision of safe, affordable, age-friendly, and flood-resilient housing [31, 54], as well as improved sanitation, access to clean drinking water, appropriate housing construction materials, and safe cooking conditions [31, 58]. Additional needs encompass nutritional support [52, 54] and access to medications. As people live longer and become increasingly vulnerable to chronic conditions and comorbidities, the absence of instrumental support can lead to a greater demand for long-term care. This demand is often intensified by poverty, declining reciprocity, and weakened intergenerational solidarity. For example, a study by Amoah [28] found that more than half of older adults reported a need for instrumental support, although this need was not associated with self-reported health outcomes, nor did it moderate the effect of functional health literacy on overall health status but was associated with the need for long-term care in future [28].

Spiritual support

Only two studies mentioned spiritual support [52, 53]. Older adults in the study shared how spirituality is deeply embedded in their daily lives, serving as a vital resource for coping with emotional and physical challenges and fostering resilience. Older adults often participate in corporate prayers or attend special prayer meetings. Notably, some preferred seeking spiritual guidance from pastors over pursuing medical assistance [52]. Older adults’ engagement in spiritual practices was critical to them considering the centrality of religion and spirituality within the Ghanaian culture. Older adults reported that spiritual support not only enhances their health and well-being but also represents one of the most accessible forms of care in resource-limited or underserved communities. In all, older adults shared how spirituality or religiosity increased religious engagement and support [53], particularly for those who are more susceptible to isolation, loneliness, or depression.

Theme 4: Policy frameworks & gaps

Geriatric education & care

Two studies [44, 45] reported gaps in geriatric education and care within the Ghanaian caregiving context. The studies highlighted that the growing needs of older adults in Ghana were inconsistent with the number of trained gerontologists and geriatricians. Additionally, geriatric and gerontology education is underdeveloped, coupled with the absence of a comprehensive National Aging Policy to address the unique needs of older adults. For example, Karikari et al. [45] examined current geriatric-focused training programs and explored the factors that influence clinical medical and nursing students’ geriatrics career intention [45]. The study highlighted the factors that can increase and strengthen the future geriatric workforce. Medical students were more likely to express intentions to specialize in geriatrics. Again, medical students who showed positive attitudes towards older adults were more likely to express an intention to specialize in geriatrics. Other factors including experiences with older adults, amount of geriatric mentoring, age, gender, even though not significant, were found to impact geriatric career intentions. The study highlighted several challenges that impede the implementation of geriatric-focused training and practice in Ghana. These include a lack of action-oriented approaches such as enrollment of a comprehensive geriatric education system, the absence of formalized geriatric training in higher education, and no knowledge and skills transfer into practical support for older adults who utilize healthcare services.

Furthermore, there is a need to dismantle systemic barriers [44], such as infrastructural gaps, funding and incentive shortfalls, minimal institutional prioritization, and unclear national education policy mandates, all of which undermine the quality of elder care. Existing data suggest that Ghana is not keeping pace with its rapidly aging population and the accompanying care demands. These findings underscore the importance of increasing and strengthening the geriatric workforce specialized in older adult care [45]. Bridging the gaps in geriatric education and practice can lead to more positive caregiving attitudes, optimal care provision, increased support, and better health outcomes for older adults.

Discussion

This systematic review explored and synthesized evidence on aging in Ghana, focusing on health-related areas, age-friendly services and policies. To our knowledge, this is the first review that has examined previous studies conducted in Ghana that address broad areas of importance to the health and well-being of older adults. The findings of the review revealed four themes, which include healthcare utilization, health status, social support and family network, and policy frameworks affecting the aging population in Ghana. Overall, there was an increasing trend of poor health outcomes for older adults and diminishing social support and networks as people age. The findings also showed limited and fragmented policies designed to support older people in Ghana.

Healthcare utilization

Older adults have complex medical needs and often require frequent and sustained access to healthcare, which is necessary to maintain their well-being [67]. However, the ability to meet these health needs is not equally distributed among older adults as demonstrated in this review. Although the socioeconomic status of individuals has been well established as a determinant of healthcare utilization, its effect is particularly prevalent in low- and middle-income countries where income-earning capacity and opportunities decline often by age 60, with limited formal social protection services [68]. This pattern of socioeconomic status contributing to healthcare utilization is not unique to Ghana, as studies from other Sub-Saharan African countries have consistently reported socioeconomic effects on healthcare access among older adults and the younger generation. Furthermore, high income countries such as the United States and Canada, have reported similar disparities in use of healthcare services related to differences in socioeconomic status [69–71]. These differences reflect the compounding effects of economic disadvantage on health system engagement in later life [72, 73].

The socioeconomic barriers to healthcare utilization among older adults are structurally embedded. For instance, income alone does not fully explain differences in healthcare access and utilization as other enabling resources, including insurance coverage, geographic proximity to services, and health literacy, remain unevenly distributed. Whereas policies and interventions such as the NHIS, LEAP, and SSNIT are available to support older adults economically and facilitate easy access to healthcare, the current review and other studies have found them inadequate in covering the healthcare costs of older adults [70, 74, 75]. In other studies, inadequate knowledge and awareness of these policies and interventions among older adults contribute to barriers in healthcare access. For instance, Van der Wielen et al. found NHIS enrollment higher among wealthier older adults compared to older adults living in poverty [70]. This suggests the need for dual efforts targeted at enhancing the effectiveness of existing policies and increasing older adults’ awareness of these policies and the ways they can help facilitate access to healthcare.

Healthcare provider–patient relationships are another important factor that influences healthcare utilization. Trusting relationships that involve respect and compassion among healthcare workers serves as a positive factor to consistent access and use of healthcare by older adults [76, 77]. In contrast, the absence of compassionate care and poor healthcare provider-patient relationships, as found in this review and previous studies, discourage use of health services among older adults in Ghana [67, 78]. These highlight gaps in person-centered and geriatric-specific care in Ghana’s healthcare setting. Insights from age-friendly health care initiatives such as the 5Ms (What Matters Most, Medication, Mentation, Mobility and Multi-complexity) [79] need to be prioritized as they have shown great promise in improving geriatric care across healthcare settings.

Health status

The health status findings from this review reflect how both biological vulnerability and social determinants influence the health status of older adults. While advanced age has been widely reported in existing literature as a risk factor for chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension [75], the burden of these conditions on older adults in low and middle-income countries, where health systems have limited capacity for early detection, effective management, and continuity of care, may be exacerbated. This disproportionate burden of chronic conditions is not merely a clinical issue, but also reflects broader structural inequities, given that most of the older people living with chronic conditions live in low and middle-income countries where diagnostic infrastructure, specialist availability, and long-term care options are constrained [80, 81]. The high prevalence of chronic conditions among older adults in Ghana, as found in this review, therefore reflects not only the physiological vulnerability of old age but also the limited resources and opportunities to respond to these conditions appropriately.

The role of social factors, such as loneliness and social connection on older adults’ health status, cannot be underestimated, as previous evidence highlighted their compounding effects on physiological risks [82, 83]. In low- and middle-income countries where traditional intergenerational living arrangements were widely practiced, older adults were mostly surrounded by family members, which buffered them against social isolation and loneliness [84, 85]. However, increasing urbanization and migration of adult children to urban areas for economic opportunities and education have contributed to the fragmentation of intergenerational living arrangements, increasing older adults’ risks of loneliness and its associated negative health outcomes. While the living arrangement in high-income countries also contributes to social isolation and loneliness among older adults [86, 87], these settings often have social and community-based programs such as senior centers and adult day care centers that promote social connectedness and engagement among older adults. Many of these programs, however, remain limited or underdeveloped in low- and middle-income countries [88].

Social support

Social support describes the feeling or experience of being part of a social network and cared for by others [89]. Our findings indicate that family members were more likely to be the primary social networks of older adults. Built on collectivist values and social integration, younger generations were generally expected to take care of older generations [56, 90]. Within this context, children of older adults are mostly at the center of informal caregiving, an expectation which is both moral and reciprocal. In this review, financial support was a salient form of assistance older adults received from kin ties. Existing evidence on older adults in Ghana corroborate our findings that financial assistance is significantly associated with healthcare utilization compared to other domains such as instrumental, informational and spiritual domains [91]. This finding underscores the extent to which structural inequalities such as economic constraints limit access to care and well-being in low-income sociocultural contexts.

Featuring prominently in the experiences of older adults were emotional and informational domains of social support. Informational support has been found to moderate the relationship between functional health literacy and health status [28]. Like Western economies, frail older adults [92] and those with limited formal education often rely on social networks and healthcare professionals for health and disease-related information and decision-making. Greater reliance on social networks for accessing information highlights the critical role of guidance and advice as critical protective resources in later life. In the Ghanaian context, religious organizations and community-based support groups were found to be the source of emotional support for older adults. The breakdown of family systems due to migration or emigration can have a significant impact on the mental health and well-being of older adults. For instance, those living alone or without proximity to families and friends raise concerns for social isolation and loneliness, which increases older adults’ risk of age-related conditions such as dementia [93, 94]. Together, informational and emotional domains of social support create both cognitive and affective mechanisms that encourage health-seeking behaviors while fostering psychological resilience [28].

Consistent with our findings, receipt of instrumental support has been reported in previous research to be associated with improved well-being among older adults [95]. Although evidence from our review corroborate existing findings that indicate no association between instrumental support and healthcare utilization [91], social networks retain high contextual relevance in providing material and financial assistance while navigating the healthcare system. This suggests that instrumental support may be more pronounced in context where older adults experience greater functional decline and increased chronicity.

Spiritual support represents a distinctive and culturally embedded aspect of social support within the Ghanaian context. As a coping strategy during late adulthood, a stage often associated with age-related decline and cumulative losses; religion and spirituality play a central role in everyday life. Engaging in spiritual practices such as prayer and meditation can play an important role in coping and resilience. Thus, religion and spirituality as evidenced in our review have a positive association with positive affect and overall life satisfaction which acts as a buffer against psychological distress even in later life [96]. Taken together, financial, emotional, instrumental, informational, and spiritual/religious domains of social support reflect the complex nature of support systems accessible to Ghanaians with each serving distinct functions in promoting the health and well-being of older adults in Ghana.

Policy frameworks & gaps

The population of older adults globally has increased rapidly beyond policies and institutional systems designed to support older adults. In both developed and developing countries, policies and initiatives have been introduced to support the care of the aging population [97, 98]. However, there continue to be policy gaps in developing the capacity of the geriatric workforce, financing mechanisms for geriatric care initiatives, and integrated policy implementation. Findings from the review emphasize substantial deficiencies in policy frameworks and educational systems that support geriatric care in Ghana, reflecting a broader neglect of aging-related issues within national development agendas [99, 100]. Importantly, gaps in frameworks reinforce one another in ways that constrain the development of a comprehensive geriatric care system. There are critical shortages in trained gerontologists and geriatricians, reflecting an underdeveloped education and training landscape for aging-related disciplines [101, 102]. This limitation is compounded by inadequate institutional prioritization and the lack of a comprehensive National Aging Policy tailored to address the specific needs of older adults [103]. While some public universities have initiated geriatric-focused programs, these remain fragmented, underfunded, and insufficiently integrated into broader healthcare and education systems, limiting their ability to produce the scale of trained professionals that an aging population requires.

What makes these aging care policy gaps analytically significant is not their independent existence but how they reinforce one another. Structural barriers such as inadequate infrastructure, weak policy mandates, and poor funding mechanisms further constrain progress in this area [104]. For instance, an aging healthcare workforce that lacks specialized aging training will continue to deliver suboptimal care regardless of insurance coverage expansions, and insurance expansions will yield limited returns if the facilities and providers available to older adults remain inadequate. Consequently, Ghana’s policy environment is not only progressing slowly in tandem with its rapidly aging population, but it also appears to be lagging structurally in ways that require coordinated, cross-sectoral interventions rather than isolated reforms.

Implications

Findings from the systematic review offer valuable insights and important implications for future practice, policy, and research efforts. The review shows inconsistencies in healthcare utilization among older adults. The Ministry of Health (MoH) and Ghana Health Services (GHS) can start by implementing person-centered and geriatric-focused healthcare delivery models that prioritize the 5Ms [79] across the healthcare continuum in service of underserved older adults. Increasing culturally competent training and awareness among clinical practitioners is an opportunity to bridge access and information sharing. Policy makers may focus on ensuring that existing social protection programs leverage community structures, prioritize social dimensions of aging, and raise public awareness of aging issues to address knowledge gaps. As the findings suggest, implementing a comprehensive National Aging Policy with greater institutional commitment, funding, and targeted community-level interventions has implications for healthcare access, social protection, and well-being of older adults. Future research should consider longitudinal designs and randomized controlled trials to capture dynamic relationships between social dimensions of aging and health outcomes among older Ghanaians. Research funders should ensure that future research addresses knowledge gaps to inform evidence-based and person-centered care interventions. There has been relatively little research on the lived experiences of older adults. As a valuable focus of future research efforts, researchers should center the voices of older adults on how to effectively implement changes in service delivery to meet their needs.

Study limitations and strengths

The current review has some limitations. First, there are limitations regarding studies that were included in the study. Only studies published in scientific journals and selected databases were included in the systematic review, which may have excluded potentially relevant and unpublished studies. The included studies were not evenly distributed across the country potentially leading to a biased representation of the overall aging research in Ghana. Also, studies published before 2015 were not included in the final review. Many of the studies varied considerably in terms of old age definitions. Therefore, caution should be taken when interpreting findings to the entire older population in Ghana, given that there exist regional disparities in health care access/utilization, family and social support systems and aging experiences.

Despite the above limitations, our systematic review provides a comprehensive synthesis of aging research in Ghana by including both qualitative and quantitative studies. By focusing exclusively on Ghana, the review offers contextually grounded literature into the health status of older Ghanaian, their use of healthcare facilities, social support, and several policy frameworks that are often obscured in broader sub-Sahara aging research. The use of the JBI tool for quality appraisal by two independent authors, the rigorous systematic review procedures, guided by PRISMA standards, a transparent search strategy and a clearly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria all enhanced the methodological rigor of the findings.

Conclusions

This review captures a comprehensive synthesis on the existing literature on aging in Ghana. It emphasizes critical issues like health utilization, health status, social support, and policies that are necessary for healthy aging in older adults. Specifically, evidence from this review suggests that older adults in Ghana have unmet needs that are shaped by social and economic challenges which affect their wellbeing. The rise in non-communicable diseases, financial difficulties, and decrease in social support are barriers that contribute significantly to their poor quality of life. It is time to pay attention to the needs and service delivery models of older adults. Our top priority should be to emphasize immediate, coordinated policy intervention and research action that will help bridge the gaps in health and social dimensions of aging outcomes. To ensure healthcare access, social inclusion, community engagement and improved quality of life among Ghanaian older adults, there is the need to implement practical measures through coordinated action across practice, policy and research.
