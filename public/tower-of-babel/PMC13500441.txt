Unpacking the Ethiopian paradox of career durability in elite endurance runners via a mixed methods study
Authors: Arefayne Mesfen Dessye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEthiopia's dominance in distance running is well-documented, yet the systemic factors leading to high rates of athlete burnout and injury remain unexamined. This study addresses the "Ethiopian Paradox": the coexistence of unparalleled success and significant athlete attrition within the same high-performance system. Guided by the Holistic Athlete Development Model, this research investigated the interplay of training, psychological, and socio-cultural factors.MethodsA retrospective, mixed-methods cohort study was conducted with 60 retired elite Ethiopian endurance runners. Two cohorts were compared: a "Durable & Thriving" group (n = 30; careers ≥ 10 years) and a "Burnout & Injury" group (n = 30; careers ≤ 7 years). Quantitative data from historical training logs (used to calculate a Training Load Volatility Index) and psychological surveys (ACSI-28; MPS) were analyzed. Qualitative data from semi-structured interviews underwent thematic analysis. Findings were integrated to develop a predictive "Durability Profile."ResultsQuantitative analysis revealed the Durable cohort exhibited significantly lower training load volatility (p < .01, d = 0.85), higher scores on coping with adversity (p < .05, d = 0.65), and lower scores on maladaptive perfectionism (p < .01, d = 0.90). Qualitative thematic analysis identified three core themes differentiating the cohorts: "Guided Autonomy" in coaching, "Integrated Athletic Identity," and "The Buffer of Community." Integration of these findings produced a three-pillar "Durability Profile" model.ConclusionCareer longevity in Ethiopian elite runners is not solely a product of physiological talent. Instead, it is fostered by a synergistic combination of intelligent training-load management, specific psychological resilience traits, and robust socio-cultural support structures. The "Durability Profile" provides an evidence-based framework for coaches to cultivate sustainable, long-term success in athlete development.Keywords: Athletic longevity, Burnout, Endurance running, Ethiopia, Mixed-methods, Sports psychology, Training load

Introduction

Ethiopia stands as a global superpower in distance running, consistently producing Olympic and World Championship medalists [1]. Explanations for this success often cite high-altitude living, genetic predisposition, and a deep-seated running culture. However, these narratives overlook a critical issue: behind the celebrated champions lies a vast population of athletes who train under similar conditions but fall short, often succumbing to career-ending injury, illness, or burnout. The global endurance community faces alarmingly high rates of injury (19–79% annually), overtraining syndrome (OTS), and Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S), leading to premature retirement and long-term health consequences [2, 3].

This study investigates the "Ethiopian Paradox"—the coexistence of incredible athletic success and a high attrition rate within the same system. While existing research has explored the physiological and anthropological factors of East African runners, a critical gap remains in understanding the holistic, long-term factors that underpin career durability within the Ethiopian system itself [4]. Specifically, "career durability" is defined here as the ability to sustain elite-level performance for a decade or more, while "short-career trajectories" refer to exits from elite sport within seven years due to maladaptive outcomes.

To comprehensively investigate this multifaceted issue, this study is grounded in the Holistic Athlete Development Model [5]. This model posits that an athlete's development occurs across multiple interacting levels (athletic, psychological, psychosocial, academic/vocational) and stages. It emphasizes that long-term success depends not only on physical training but on the development of the whole person. This framework allows us to systematically explore how factors at the psychological level (e.g., coping skills, identity), the psychosocial level (e.g., coach-athlete relationships), and the athletic level (e.g., training load management) interact.

No prior study has systematically investigated the protective psycho-social and training-load management factors that differentiate the nation's durable, world-class athletes from the many who do not achieve their potential. The primary aim was to identify the key factors that foster career longevity and well-being, and to develop a practical tool for coaches. The study was guided by three research questions:

What are the key differences in training-load management strategies between durable and short-career Ethiopian runners?

Methods

Research design

A retrospective, mixed-methods cohort study was employed, combining the statistical power of quantitative analysis with the rich, contextual understanding of qualitative inquiry.

Participants

Sixty retired elite Ethiopian endurance runners (30 male, 30 female) were recruited via purposive sampling through the Ethiopian Athletics Federation. Inclusion criteria required having competed at a national championship or international level in events ranging from 5000 m to the Marathon. A priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1), assuming a medium effect size (d = 0.50), α = 0.05, and power (1 − β) = 0.80, indicated a minimum sample size of 52 was required. We recruited 60 to ensure adequate power. Exclusion criteria included retirement due to non-sport-related accidents or incomplete training data. Participants were stratified into two cohorts:

Cohort 1 (Durable & Thriving): n = 30. Athletes with careers of 10 + years at the elite level.

Cohort 1 (Durable & Thriving): n = 30. Athletes with careers of 10 + years at the elite level.

Cohort 2 (Burnout & Injury): n = 30. Athletes whose elite careers were cut short (< 7 years) due to injury, OTS, or burnout.

Cohort 2 (Burnout & Injury): n = 30. Athletes whose elite careers were cut short (< 7 years) due to injury, OTS, or burnout.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected between January and August 2023.

Quantitative data

Historical training logs: Participants retrieved personal training diaries and official coach-mandated logs that were maintained contemporaneously during their peak competitive years (spanning 5–15 years prior). In the Ethiopian high-performance system, daily logging of training volume (km) and intensity (session RPE) is a standard requirement. Data extraction focused on these weekly metrics to calculate the Acute: Chronic Workload Ratio (ACWR). To ensure data integrity, only weeks with complete diary entries were included in the analysis. The Training Load Volatility Index (TLVI) was operationalized as the standard deviation of the weekly ACWR over the observation period.

Psychological surveys: Participants completed the Athletic Coping Skills Inventory (ACSI-28), which measures coping with adversity and goal setting, and the Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale (MPS), assessing maladaptive versus adaptive perfectionism. Both instruments have demonstrated cross-cultural validity in sports contexts.

Qualitative data

Semi-Structured Interviews: Each participant engaged in a confidential interview (approx. 75 min) exploring career journeys, coaching relationships, and support systems.

Data analysis plan

Quantitative: Independent samples t-tests and MANOVA were conducted using SPSS (Version 26). Normality was assessed via Shapiro–Wilk tests. Effect sizes (Cohen's d) were calculated for all t-tests, and partial eta squared (ηp2) for the MANOVA. To control for Type I error across the multiple dependent variables in the MANOVA, a Bonferroni correction was applied to the follow-up univariate ANOVAs, with the alpha level set at p < 0.007.

Qualitative: Interview transcripts were analyzed using inductive Thematic Analysis [6]. To ensure rigor, the author independently coded an initial subset of 20% of the transcripts. After a 2-week interval, the author recoded a random selection of these transcripts to verify intra-rater reliability, ensuring consistency and trustworthiness of the analysis.

Quantitative findings

Training load analysis

The Durable cohort demonstrated significantly lower training load volatility (TLVI) compared to the Burnout cohort (p < 0.01, d = 0.85). While total volume was similar, the Burnout cohort exhibited frequent "spikes" in ACWR, entering the "danger zone" (> 1.5) associated with injury risk (Fig. 1).

While both groups possessed high levels of personal standards, the Durable cohort scored significantly higher on Coping with Adversity (10.2 vs. 8.7) and critically lower on Concern over Mistakes (22.1 vs. 28.5). Training load volatility

This line graph visualizes the "Training Load Volatility" concept. It contrasts the stable, guided management of the Durable cohort against the erratic, high-spiking pattern of the Burnout cohort, highlighting the entry into the "Danger Zone" (> 1.5).

As visualized in Fig. 3, the quantitative analysis of training logs revealed that total volume was similar between groups, but the pattern of loading was fundamentally different. The Burnout cohort was characterized by high Training Load Volatility, with frequent, unmanaged spikes in ACWR that crossed the critical 1.5 threshold. This erratic loading contrasts sharply with the stable, guided load management observed in the Durable cohort, providing a physiological explanation for the higher rates of injury and overtraining syndrome in the former group.

Qualitative findings

Thematic analysis of the 60 interviews revealed three overarching themes that distinguished the Durable cohort from the Burnout cohort.

Theme 1: guided autonomy in coaching

Durable athletes described collaborative relationships where coaches acted as facilitators.

"My coach gave me the plan, but he always asked me, 'How do you feel today?' If I was tired, we adjusted. He was not a dictator. He taught me to be the expert on myself."—Male, Durable Cohort.

In contrast, the Burnout cohort often described rigid, top-down coaching systems.

"The coach had the schedule. You did it. If you were tired, that was a sign of weakness. You pushed through until you broke."—Female, Burnout Cohort.

Theme 2: integrated athletic identity

Durable athletes maintained identities outside of running, providing a buffer against setbacks.

"Running was what I did, not who I was. I had my family, my small shop. When I had a long injury, my world did not end. I was still a husband and a businessman."—Male, Durable Cohort.

The Burnout cohort frequently described "athletic identity foreclosure."

"When I could no longer run fast, I was nothing. I had no other skills. I felt like a ghost."—Female, Burnout Cohort.

Theme 3: the buffer of community

Durable athletes cited strong communal support.

"We trained together, we ate together, and we worried together. When one of us struggled, the others lifted them up. It was not just about being the fastest; it was about all of us succeeding."—Female, Durable Cohort.

The Burnout cohort often reported feelings of isolation and intense rivalry.

"It was every runner for them. If you got injured, you were left behind."—Male, Burnout Cohort.

Discussion

This study provides novel evidence that career durability in Ethiopian runners is multi-factorial. The finding that Training Load Volatility was a stronger predictor of career length than volume aligns with global injury prevention models [7], yet this is the first confirmation within an East African context. The qualitative theme of "Guided Autonomy" explains this statistical finding; collaborative coaching likely facilitates better load management and self-regulation, preventing the dangerous spikes associated with injury [8, 9].

The psychological profile of high coping skills and low maladaptive perfectionism supports the Holistic Athlete Development Model, suggesting that psychological resilience buffers the physical demands of training. The "Integrated Athletic Identity" finding aligns with recent literature suggesting that exclusive athletic identity is a risk factor for burnout [10, 11].

We synthesized these findings into the "Durability Profile" (Fig. 2), a three-pillar model:

Limitations and future research

This study utilizes a retrospective design, which inherently carries a risk of recall bias regarding psychological states and specific training details. However, we mitigated this limitation significantly regarding training load data by utilizing archival, contemporaneous training diaries rather than relying solely on retrospective athlete recall. Given that daily training logging is a mandatory practice within the Ethiopian athletic federation system, the data used to calculate the Training Load Volatility Index (TLVI) reflects actual recorded sessions from the time of competition, rather than reconstructed memories.

Nevertheless, while the "spiking" patterns of volatility are clearly evident in these records, minor absolute variations in volume cannot be entirely ruled out. Additionally, the psychological survey scores reflect the participants' current state reflecting on the past, rather than a real-time measurement during their peak.

Conclusion

Career longevity in Ethiopian elite runners is fostered by intelligent training-load management, psychological resilience, and socio-cultural support. The "Durability Profile" offers a practical, evidence-based framework for coaches to shift focus from short-term performance to long-term athlete sustainability.
