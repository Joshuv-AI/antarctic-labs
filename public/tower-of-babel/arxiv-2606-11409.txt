Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation of Adversarial Robustness in Language Models
Malikeh Ehghaghi, Boglárka Ecsedi, Marsha Chechik, Colin Raffel, University of Toronto

Abstract
Adversarial robustness evaluations of large language models (LLMs) typically report attack success rate (ASR) under fixed query budgets, implicitly treating all attacks as equally costly. In practice, the computational expense of different attack strategies can vary by orders of magnitude. Consequently, ASR at a fixed budget can obscure the true effort required to “jailbreak” a model, thereby making it hard to determine whether an attack’s cost justifies its payoff to the attacker. We propose a compute-aware evaluation framework based on computational pressure, measured in cumulative floating-point operations ( FLOPs ), as a proxy for adversarial effort. We introduce risk-compute curves, which map compute budgets to attack risk, and derive two metrics that summarize the average pressure required for a given attack to succeed. Across ten models spanning three families and four different stages in language model training and alignment, evaluated with three attack strategies (gradient-based, iterative refinement, and template-based) on two jailbreak robustness benchmarks, we find: (1) alignment training has non-monotonic effects on compute-space robustness; (2) scaling model size reduces gradient-based attack effectiveness but has limited impact on cheaper template-based attacks; (3) gradient-based attacks optimized on a surrogate model can transfer to a separate target model, providing a way to reduce attacker costs; (4) compute cost varies by up to ≈ 5 × {\approx}5{\times} across harm categories within a single model; and (5) safety-aligned RL increases aggregate cost while leaving some categories disproportionately accessible. We release our framework to enable compute-aware risk assessment and evaluation.

Introduction

Misuse of LLMs can facilitate fraud
(
42
)
, spread
misinformation
(
11
)
, or enable cyberattacks
(
5
)
,
leading to providers investing heavily in safety tuning
(
8
;
30
;
3
)
. Yet adversarial jailbreaks, prompts designed to circumvent
safety guardrails, remain a persistent threat
(
51
;
41
;
4
;
31
)
. This leads to an important security question: How hard is it to jailbreak a language model? The community’s standard answer “attack
X
achieves
Y
% success rate after
Z
queries” answers
how often
, but not
at what cost
. Imagine two models asked to write a defamatory article about a public figure: one complies immediately, while the other resists nine attempts before complying on the tenth. Single-budget attack success rate (ASR) scores both as equally unsafe (100% ASR), collapsing the
10\times
difference in adversarial effort into a binary outcome and obscuring that one model may be much more expensive to exploit in practice.

This limitation reflects a broader gap between LLM safety evaluation and classical security, where a system’s robustness is quantified by its
work factor
: the computational effort an adversary must expend to breach it
(
27
;
35
)
. Computer systems are evaluated not merely by whether breaking them is
possible
, but by whether it is
feasible
given realistic adversary budgets. Yet LLM safety benchmarks typically report outcomes under a fixed query budget, without a comparable measure of adversarial effort, making it difficult to translate ASR into operational risk or to compare models under equal attacker spend.

Recent defenses report near-zero ASR against static attack datasets
(
18
;
32
)
or computationally weak optimization methods applied without adaptation to the defense
(
21
)
. As
29
demonstrate, these evaluations can systematically overestimate robustness: adaptive attacks that explicitly counter a defense’s design bypass 12 recent defenses with
>90\%
ASR, despite original reports of near-zero failure rates. We argue that the core issue is
incomplete cost accounting
, where all queries are treated as equally expensive, thereby obscuring the true adversarial investment required.

Real adversaries would operate under finite compute budgets and choose attacks based on return on investment. A vulnerability is only operationally relevant if exploitable at a cost justified by its value. From the defender’s perspective, forcing attacks to be computationally expensive could therefore be as effective as lowering their success rate. In other words, a defender’s goal should not be perfect robustness but rather raising the adversarial cost floor high enough to deter realistic threat actors.

To address shortcomings in existing evaluation practices, we introduce a
compute-aware evaluation framework
based on
computational pressure
, which is a proxy for adversarial effort, measuring pressure in cumulative floating-point operations (
FLOPs
) within a fixed query budget. We define
risk–pressure curves
to characterize how attack risk evolves with increasing compute, and derive two summary metrics:
C_{@\tau}
, the compute required such that attacks succeed a certain proportion (
\tau
) of the time, and
AE
, the risk gained per unit
FLOP
. By placing diverse attack strategies (including gradient-based optimization, model-guided refinement, and template-based prompting) on a shared compute axis, we enable more unified, budget-aware comparisons across models and attacks.

We instantiate this framework in a systematic study across different model families, training stages, model sizes, attack strategies, and jailbreak robustness benchmarks. Compute-aware analysis uncovers patterns that step-count ASR alone hides: alignment training affects compute-space robustness non-monotonically, with intermediate stages (SFT) sometimes outperforming later ones (DPO, RLVR); model size benefits gradient-based attacks far more than cheap template attacks; gradient-based attacks optimized on a surrogate can transfer to a target model at a fraction of cost; compute cost to breach a model varies by up to
{\approx}5{\times}
depending on the harm category; and dedicated safety-RL raises aggregate adversarial cost while leaving certain harm categories disproportionately accessible. We release our pipeline as an open-source framework for reproducible, compute-aware LM safety evaluation.

2
Framework

2.1
Budgeted Iterative Refinement and Computational Pressure

We model adversarial attacks as
iterative refinement
under a fixed query budget
\lambda
: at each step
t
, an attack policy
\pi
proposes a candidate prompt
p^{(t)}
, the target model
M
produces a response
y^{(t)}
, and, for the purposes of evaluation, a safety judge
\mathcal{E}
records a binary outcome
z^{(t)}
. Figure
1
illustrates this pipeline; Algorithm
1
in Appendix
A
formalizes it. We apply it uniformly across attack families with
p^{(1)}
initialized from prompts sourced from safety benchmarks (see Section
3.3
).

Each step of an attack policy can incur compute from various sources: (i) forward passes through the target model
M
for response generation, candidate evaluation, or loss computation; (ii) backward passes through
M
when the attack requires gradients (e.g., for white-box optimization); (iii) forward passes through an optional auxiliary attacker model
M_{A}
that proposes the next prompt (e.g., black-box attacker LLM); and (iv) forward passes through the safety judge
\mathcal{E}
that evaluates each response, typically under an early-stopping criterion as in Algorithm
1
. All of the above can be reduced to a cost in
FLOPs
(standard transformer
FLOPs
approximation as defined in
(
40
;
19
;
15
)
):

C_{\text{fwd}}\approx 2NL,

(1)

where
N
is the parameter count and
L
the sequence length in tokens; backward passes are charged at approximately
2\,C_{\text{fwd}}

(
14
)
, with attack-specific overheads accounted for in §
3.1
. We define
computational pressure
as the cumulative
FLOPs
incurred over
\lambda
refinement steps, averaged across prompts. Section
3.1
instantiates this accounting for each attack in our study. We define quantities in
FLOPs
and report numerical results in
TFLOPs
(
1\,\text{TFLOP}=10^{12}\,\text{FLOPs}
) in figures and tables for readability.

Why
FLOPs
?

FLOPs
are an fundamental property of an attack’s cost, invariant to GPU, kernel, and batching choices that swing wall-clock time by orders of magnitude without changing the attack’s intrinsic difficulty, following the same reason
FLOPs
are the canonical comparison axis in transformer scaling-law analysis
(
19
;
15
)
. FLOPs are comparable across heterogeneous attack components (gradient steps, auxiliary attacker calls, target/judge queries), letting otherwise-incommensurable attacks share a common axis (§
3.1
).

FLOPs
are also the invariant predecessor of the operational quantities a defender ultimately tracks (e.g., energy, wall-clock time, GPU-hours, USD per breach), each obtained from
FLOPs
by a hardware- or contract-specific factor, so
FLOPs
-based rankings translate to rankings on these proxies up to that factor.

2.2
Risk-Compute Curves

Given
N
trial records for model
M
under attack
\pi
, we define the empirical risk at
query budget
\lambda
:

\hat{R}(M,\pi,\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}[\text{trial}_{i}\text{ succeeds within }\lambda\text{ steps}].

(2)

Compute parameterization

For each query budget
\lambda
, we measure the
average cumulative
FLOPs
per
prompt
consumed up to that budget:

\bar{C}(M,\pi,\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{\min(\lambda,\,t_{i}^{*})}c_{\pi}(M,t),

(3)

where
t_{i}^{*}
is the first-success step for trial
i
(or
\lambda
if no success), and

c_{\pi}(M,t)
is the
FLOPs
cost of step
t
under attack
\pi
on model
M

(defined in §
3.1
). The risk-compute curve plots
(x,y)=(\bar{C}(M,\pi,\lambda),R(M,\pi,\lambda))
as
\lambda
varies from
1
to
\lambda_{\max}
.

2.3
Summary Metrics

Risk-compute curves offer rich visual detail on attack scaling behavior but are difficult to compare systematically. We extract two scalar summaries, including the compute required to reach a certain risk threshold and risk gained per compute unit, enabling consistent cross-model and cross-attack comparisons.

Compute to
\tau
%-risk (
C_{@\tau}
)
measures
the average cumulative
FLOPs
required for an attack to first reach
\tau
% risk, where we chose
\tau=0.5
to show above half, but this threshold can be changed according to use case or deployment constraints:

C_{@\tau}(M,\pi)=\min_{\lambda}\left\{\bar{C}(M,\pi,\lambda)\;:\;\hat{R}(M,\pi,\lambda)\geq\tau\right\}.

(4)

Higher
C_{@\tau}
indicates a model that demands more adversarial compute to compromise.
When
\hat{R}
never reaches
\tau
within budget, we set
C_{@\tau}=\infty
.

Area under the risk-compute curve (
\mathrm{CAURC}
)
is computed via a trapezoidal approximation of

\mathrm{CAURC}(M,\pi,\lambda_{\max})=\int_{\lambda=1}^{\lambda_{\max}}R(M,\pi,\lambda)\,d\bar{C}(M,\pi,\lambda)

(5)

Average efficiency (AE)
,

\mathrm{AE}=\mathrm{CAURC}/\bar{C}_{\max}
, is the normalized expected risk per
FLOP
, measuring how
much risk an attacker gains on average for each unit of compute spent.
High AE means an attack extracts substantial risk even under tight compute constraints.
All metrics are aggregated across 10 different seeds using
t
-distribution 95% confidence intervals
(
\mathrm{df}=n_{\text{seeds}}-1
, where
n_{\text{seeds}}
is the number of random seeds).

3
Experimental Setup

3.1
Attacks and Per-Step Cost

Our empirical study covers three diverse and widely used attack strategies. For each, we describe the policy and instantiate the per-step cost
c_{\pi}(M,t)
from Eq. (
1
).

JailBroken
(
41
)
, a template-based attack that randomly selects from eight obfuscation strategies applied to the base adversarial prompt
p
: prefix injection, refusal suppression, style injection, role-play framing, AIM persona adoption (“Always Intelligent and Machiavellian”, a role-play jailbreak that reassigns assistant identity and suppresses refusals), developer-mode framing, Base64 encoding, and AIM+Base64. At each step
t
, a new template is applied independently to
p
, leading to a per-step cost of

c_{\text{JB}}(M)=\underbrace{2N_{M}L_{\text{gen}}}_{\text{target fwd}}+\underbrace{2N_{J}L_{J}}_{\text{judge fwd}},

(6)

where
N_{M}
and
N_{J}
are the parameter counts of the target and safety judge,
L_{\text{gen}}
is the target’s response length, and
L_{J}
is the judge’s input length.

Prompt Automatic Iterative Refinement (PAIR)
(
7
)
utilizes
black-box iterative refinement using an attacker LLM that rewrites a jailbreak prompt based on the target’s prior response. We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
(
48
)
as the attacker, initializing with the initial adversarial prompt
p=p^{(0)}
. At each subsequent step
t
, the attacker receives the current prompt
p^{(t-1)}
, the target’s response
y^{(t-1)}
, and the judge’s binary verdict
z^{(t-1)}\in\{0,1\}
, and generates an improved prompt
p^{(t)}
. If the previous step succeeded (
z=1
), the prompt is returned unchanged. Generation uses temperature 1.0 and returns only the refined prompt; the full system prompt is provided in Appendix
C
. PAIR adds one forward pass through the attacker LLM, resulting in a cost of

c_{\text{PAIR}}(M)=c_{\text{JB}}(M)+\underbrace{2N_{A}L_{A}}_{\text{attacker fwd}},

(7)

where
N_{A}
is the attacker LLM’s parameter count and
L_{A}
its input/output length.

Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG)
(
51
)
, a
white-box attack that appends a short adversarial suffix to a harmful query and greedily updates it token-by-token. The suffix is initialized as 20 “!” tokens. At each step, (i) one backward pass computes gradients with respect to one-hot token embeddings, identifying the top-256 candidate substitutions per position; (ii) 128 candidates are sampled from this pool and evaluated via forward passes; (iii) the candidate minimizing cross-entropy toward a fixed affirmative prefix (
“Sure, here is”
) is selected, steering the model away from refusal behavior. This results in a cost of

c_{\text{GCG}}(M)=\underbrace{(128+\beta_{\text{bwd}})\cdot 2N_{M}L_{\text{opt}}}_{\text{candidates + gradient}}+\underbrace{2N_{M}L_{\text{gen}}}_{\text{generation}}+\underbrace{2N_{J}L_{J}}_{\text{judge fwd}},

(8)

where
L_{\text{opt}}
is the optimization sequence length (harmful prompt plus adversarial suffix). We set
\beta_{\text{bwd}}=3
, corresponding to the standard
2{:}1
backward-to-forward
FLOP
ratio
(
14
)
plus a
50\%
overhead for GCG’s discrete operations (gradients through one-hot embeddings across vocabularies of size
V\approx 30{,}000
–
150{,}000
, top-
k
selection at each position, and coordinate aggregation
(
51
)
). This conservative choice avoids undercounting the cost of gradient-based attacks. Varying
\beta_{\text{bwd}}\in\{2,3,4\}
changes total GCG step cost by
\pm
1% for
large models (where the 128-candidate cost dominates) and
\pm
5% for 0.5B models.
Qualitative findings are robust to this choice.

All sequence lengths are measured from exact tokenizer counts per trial; in our experiments, mean values are
L_{\text{gen}}\approx 180
–
320
,
L_{J}\approx 1{,}800
–
2{,}200
, and
L_{\text{opt}}\approx 80
–
120
tokens. Example case studies of each attack are included in Appendix
B
.

Using our computational pressure framework, we address five research questions: how training stage and model scale shape adversarial compute costs; how risk transfers from small open-weight surrogates to closed-weight targets; whether safety-RL meaningfully raises breach costs; and whether harm categories demand unequal adversarial effort. Our motivation is to show that translating adversarial queries into a unified compute axis surfaces distinctions that attack success rate alone cannot capture.

Impact of Training Stage on Adversarial Compute Cost

Models typically undergo multiple post-training stages, each optimizing different objectives. The final checkpoint is deployed by default, yet it is unclear whether this final stage yields maximum robustness against jailbreaks. The alignment pipeline of Tulu 3 illustrates this sharply: robustness follows a non-monotone trajectory that standard ASR captures only in part.
Tulu3-SFT attains the highest robustness (Table
1
, Figure
2
): GCG and PAIR never breach the 50% risk threshold within budget, with ASR held to
3.2\times
and
2.4\times
lower than base, respectively. Tulu3-DPO erodes this protection: GCG’s computational pressure to 50% risk collapses to 521.2
TFLOPs
and PAIR’s to 79.9
TFLOPs

Tulu3-RLVR deepens the regression. JailBroken’s adversarial compute cost falls further, and its ASR surges to 0.90, a 23% increase over DPO and 40% above SFT.
AE sharpens the picture: JailBroken’s per-

1.8\times

ASR follows the same trend, while the two compute-cost metrics provide a complementary view of the adversarial effort required to break models across training stages. RLVR exceeds both SFT and DPO, yet the compute-cost collapse reveals the full depth of the degradation. Consistent with findings from the Tulu 3 report
(

and RLVR’s binary rewards can inadvertently deprioritize calibrated refusals

strong protection against compute-intensive attacks like GCG, while leaving the model nearly as vulnerable to low-cost attacks like JailBroken. These observations are in line with prior work highlighting that increasing model size alone does not consistently improve robustness under standard evaluation, but scaling attack compute significantly and predictably improves attack performance
(

)
.

Related Work

Jailbreak Attacks, Red-teaming, and Benchmark-based Safety Evaluation

A growing literature studies how safety-aligned LLMs can be induced to produce policy-violating outputs via jailbreak prompts and iterative attacks, spanning template-based transformations and multi-turn refinement to optimization- and search-based methods (e.g.,
41
;
51
;
24
;
7
;
26
;
34
;
33
). In parallel, community benchmarks and red-teaming protocols have standardized
what
is evaluated and
how
success is judged, notably through curated harmful behavior sets and automated judging pipelines
(
25
;
6
;
10
)
.
However, most benchmark reporting still centers on attack success under fixed query/step caps, which is well-suited for comparing prompt sets but can blur substantial heterogeneity in the underlying effort across attack classes and access regimes.
Recent work also highlights that static evaluations can systematically overstate robustness when attackers adapt to the defense mechanism
(
2
;
39
;
9
;
29
)
, reinforcing that robust evaluation depends not only on
which
attacks are run, but also on a clear accounting of the adversary’s effective resources.

Security Aspect and Work Factor

Classical security evaluates systems by the computational effort required to break them (the
work factor
) under the assumption that attackers know the design (Kerckhoffs’ principle)
(
20
;
35
;
27
)
.
In that tradition, vulnerability is operationally meaningful only relative to realistic attacker budgets and incentives; economic perspectives emphasize that attackers (and users) respond to cost–benefit trade-offs rather than worst-case possibility
(
1
;
12
)
.
AI risk guidance similarly stresses operational threat modeling and measurable risk management processes
(
37
;
28
)
.
Within LLM safety,
29
sharpen this gap by demonstrating that many defenses that look robust under non-adaptive testing can fail catastrophically under stronger adaptive attacks, underscoring that evaluation claims should be interpreted through a security lens that distinguishes
possibility
from
feasibility
.
Our work adopts this security framing but focuses on a complementary axis: not merely whether an adaptive attacker exists, but what
computational effort
different jailbreak strategies effectively require when compared on equal attacker spend.

A Resource-Quantitative View of Adversarial Outcomes (Compute vs. Risk)

Scaling-law analyses in language modeling motivate hardware-agnostic resource accounting (e.g.,
FLOPs
) as a common axis for comparing systems and regimes
(
19
;
16
;
13
)
.
In LLM robustness and safety, recent studies have begun to probe how scale and training choices interact with robustness outcomes
(
17
)
, and benchmarking work has emphasized that jailbreak performance depends strongly on attack configuration and search effort even when the evaluation budget is nominally fixed
(
46
)
.
A key limitation is that query- or step-based budgets implicitly treat heterogeneous attack steps as comparable units of effort, even though they can differ by orders of magnitude in computation and therefore correspond to different real-world feasibility regimes.
Our contribution is to make this resource dependence explicit by expressing jailbreak outcomes as
risk as a function of compute
, enabling comparisons across attack families, model scales, and alignment stages under a shared compute budget; in this sense, we sit at the intersection of benchmark-based jailbreak evaluation and security-style work-factor analysis.

6
Conclusion

Standard safety evaluations report attack success rates over fixed query budgets, obscuring massive disparities in the computational cost required to breach different defenses. We introduce a compute-aware framework that measures adversarial robustness through computational pressure (cumulative FLOPs), defining risk-compute curves and summary metrics that enable unified comparison across diverse attack strategies. Evaluating across training stages, model families, and sizes, we find that traditional metrics systematically obscure critical robustness trade-offs. Adversarial cost varies non-monotonically across alignment stages, while model scaling creates an asymmetry, strongly deterring expensive gradient attacks while offering minimal protection against cheap templates. This gap is exploited by surrogate-optimized attacks that can transfer to larger targets with reduced cost. Within individual models, compute requirements vary up to
{\approx}5{\times}
across harm categories, revealing that safety tuning raises aggregate costs while leaving certain risks disproportionately accessible. Models appearing robust under query-count metrics can fail catastrophically under compute-aware evaluation, demonstrating the need for cost-conscious adversarial assessment.

7
Future Work & Limitations

We use standard transformer FLOPs as a theoretical proxy for attacker effort, which may not fully reflect real-world constraints (memory bandwidth, latency, parallelizability, etc.). Our results are limited by automated judge reliability (false positives/negatives, category bias)
(
36
;
49
)
and potential output truncation artifacts due to computational resource constraints. We evaluate three attack families (GCG, PAIR, JailBroken) without adaptive attacks that jointly optimize against defenses, which would likely tighten robustness bounds. Our binary success metric treats all jailbreaks equally; future work should weight by harm severity and utility gain, shifting the question from
whether
an attack succeeds to
how much the outcome is worth the adversarial investment
. Extensions to larger scales, higher budgets, closed-weight frontier models, and multimodal settings remain open empirical directions.

Where the benchmarks disagree

Rank disagreements are minimal and involve only adjacent mid-tier models with nearly identical AE values.

Category-level vulnerability patterns do diverge: HarmBench transfer attacks concentrate on cybercrime/intrusion and misinformation/disinformation, while JailbreakBench transfer concentrates on information-seeking categories, including Expert Advice and Disinformation.
