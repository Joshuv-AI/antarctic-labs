Introduction

In April of 2021, Pellucere Technologies, Inc., was awarded voucher funding to conduct research
and analysis at the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) per the American-Made
Solar Prize, Round 4. Pellucere produces silica-based coatings that can be applied to the glass
surface of photovoltaic (PV) modules with the intent of improving the anti-reflective and anti-
soiling properties of the PV module. The objective of the Pellucere voucher funding was
established as follows:

Leverage NREL’s existing testing and data analysis capability to evaluate solar panel anti-soiling
coating in both laboratory testing conditions and from actual solar array field data.

The intended scope of the voucher work was prescribed as follows:

1. Evaluate anti-soiling coating on cover glass integrated into minimodules with silicon cells in
an existing NREL environmental test chamber for anti-soiling testing. Specifical
environmentally test conditions of 55% relative humidity and 10-20% soiling losses will be
included as NREL has examined soiling loss data from southern California where these
conditions can be common [1].

 2

2. Evaluate data from an actual solar array field to determine rates of soiling on coated solar
panels relative to soiling rates of soiling on uncoated modules in the same array.

This report is organized as follows: 1) the Methods section provides full details of the equipment
used, 2) the Testing and Test Results section provides an itemization chamber cycles, chamber
validation and of each unique chamber or field test result, 3) The Discussion provides a brief
explanation of the results in context of the greater PV soiling problem, and 3) the Conclusion
provides an overall summary of the findings.

Methods

Equipment

Pellucere purchased ten Topsolar solar silicon solar modules (33.3 cm by 20.3 cm) which consist
of typical layered package of (starting from sun-side)

 1) uncoated tempered low-iron glass

 2) ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA) film, two monocrystalline silicon solar cells

 3) EVA film

 4) Tedlar/Polyester/Tedlar (TPT) backsheet

 5) protected by an aluminum frame.

The Topsolar modules are rated at 10 W with a short-circuit current (Isc) of 0.60 amps.
Pellucere used the meniscus drag method to coat five of the Topsolar modules with their
MoreSun plus Talus coating and cured the coating at 55 °C, while no changes were made to the
remaining five modules. The modules are designated P1-P5 for those with coatings and UC1-
UC5 for the uncoated modules.

A dust chamber was constructed by NREL as shown from a top view in Figure 1. The chamber
was designed to control blown dust dispersal, airborne particulate levels, wind speed, relative
humidity, and module temperature. The chamber shown in Figure 1 does not include a
plexiglass sheet that covers the module area, completing the enclosure that enables maintaining
humidity and dust levels within the chamber. The plexiglass cover provides access for cleaning
the modules between differing tests and also provides the ability to illuminate the modules to
record in situ Isc measurements.

Testing and Test Results

Test Conditions and Parameters

The data logger provides the ability to design various accelerated soiling cycles to simulate
different outdoor climates and soiling conditions. The primary focus of this work is to simulate
conditions similar to windy, low humidity desert PV soiling environments in southern California.
While conditions vary from site-to-site, across geographies, and across seasons; NREL has
examined a number of sites in southern California and found that during long soiling periods it is
common for the relative humidity (RH) to be 55% or less, dew cycles are not typical, winds in
excess of 5 m/s increase airborne particulates, and periods of three or more months without rain
can easily result in accumulated soiling losses of 10-20% [1]. With this under consideration the
following soiling cycles were tested.

Cycle 1: RH < 50%, module temperature (TM) set to 45 ± 1 °C (daytime nominal on-sun
temperature), cycle length = 10 minutes, airborne particulate matter (PM) minimum thresholds
set to achieve ~ 10% soiling loss over 24 hours (144 cycles) on a control module. The cycle is
conducted as follows:

 1) 0-10 seconds LED lights are turned on and Isc measurements are recorded.

 2) 16-300 seconds: the main chamber fans are on at full duty cycle and PM is maintained at
 the minimum threshold. If PM drops below the threshold, dust release is triggered, and

 6

 the main fans reduce to a 30% duty cycle. Dust release continues until PM is sufficiently
 above the threshold. The dust release period is typically achieved in 5-10 seconds.

 3) Between 300-500 seconds the main fan duty cycle is reduced to 50% and the PM
 minimum threshold is reduced 20%.

 4) Between 500-600 seconds the main chamber fans are turned off and PM levels are no
 longer maintained (dust is allowed to settle).

 5) Test room RH is < 50% and RH is allowed to fluctuate with the ambient RH.

 6) Module temperature is automatically maintained throughout the cycle.

Cycle 2: RHset = 55% ± 2.5%, all other specifications are the same as Cycle 1.

 1) Any time the RH drops 2.5% below RHset, a flag is set to inject humidity into the
 chamber. Humidity is injected only after the current dust cycle is completed. The
 chamber fans are turned off while humidity is injected. As seen in Figure 1, humidity is
 injected on the opposite side of the chamber from the modules to prevent any direct
 precipitation of water vapor particles on the modules. Humidity is injected until RH set
 +2.5% is achieved. Due to the location of RH sensor near the modules and not the
 humidity injection point, test data shows the RH will at times increase higher than RH set
 +2.5% after humidity injection has turned off.

Cycle 3: RHset = 65% ± 2.5%, all other specifications are the same as Cycle 1.

Cycle 4: RHset = 75% ± 2.5%, all other specifications are the same as Cycle 1.

Chamber Validation

Two uncoated modules were mounted in the NREL dust chamber and allowed to soil per Cycle
1. The process was repeated three times and representative results are shown in Figure 3.
Within the three validation tests the soiling ratio between the two modules deviates no more than
0.5%, module temperature is maintained within 2 °C of the target, and the soiling rate varies
from 0.33%/hr to 0.45%/hr. The variation in soiling rate shows that caution should be taken
when one run is compared to another run. This variation is expected for two reasons: 1) the
Plantower PM sensor is generally considered a low-cost, low-accuracy sensor, 2) the chamber is
not cleaned between runs, which allows dust to build up on the floor and other surfaces of the
chamber; this deposited dust can then reenter the air stream as the fans are turned to full velocity.
While caution should be taken in comparing runs, more importantly the side-by-side modules
demonstrate near-identical soiling in a given run, allowing side-by-side coated and uncoated
soiling to be compared.

Soiling Rate Testing at Ambient Relative Humidity

After validation that two side-by-side modules (UC1 and UC2) soiled at the same rate (see
Figure 3), module UC1 and P1 were mounted side-by-side in the NREL chamber and tested per
Cycle 1 (RH is allowed to fluctuate per the larger room humidity). This test was repeated three
times (R1, R2, and R3), where R1 and R2 were allowed to soil for 23 hours and R3 was extended
for 41 hours. The average RH for all three tests was 44.3%. In between each run, P1 and UC1
were cleaned per the following method:

 1) Dust was blown off the modules with compressed air.

 2) The modules were misted with deionized water and wiped free of moisture with a clean
 white microfiber cloth. This cleaning was repeated at least 3 times and until no
 discoloration could be seen on the microfiber cloth.

 3) Waited 30 minutes after the last wiping before beginning a new test run.

The results show that while P1 achieves an average loss of 0.8% in all three runs, there is no
statistically significant soiling rate over time. Alternatively, the uncoated module achieves
statistically significant linear soiling in all three tests with ~7% loss after 23 hours and 10.5%
loss after 41 hours.

modules would show a larger loss for frame-edge soiling as compared to full-size modules.

Soiling Rate Testing at 55% Relative Humidity

After completing three test runs with UC1 and P1 at ambient RH conditions (mean RH of
44.3%) per Cycle 1, these same test modules were subject to three test runs (Ra, Rb, Rc) per
Cycle 2 (RHset = 55% ± 2.5%). The results of RHset = 55% are provided in Figure 6. In all runs
the Pellucere module (P1) shows a nonlinear and decreasing soiling trend over the first 9-12
hours of testing and thereafter shows no further soiling with time. The uncoated module (UC1)
shows a statistically significant linear soiling trend in all runs for the full test period. After 19
hours of testing at 55% humidity, the Pellucere module results in losses of 2.6%, 2.7%, and
2.5%, while the uncoated module has approximately twice those losses at 7.2%, 6.0%, and
5.6%. Due to the six soiling and cleaning cycles P1 was subjected to, an additional test at 55%
RH was conducted on fresh or untested coated modules, P2 (the results are labeled ‘Raa’ in
Figure 6). In Run Raa, the uncoated module has a 5.6% soiling loss after 22 hours while P2 has
only 1.6%.

Soiling Rate Testing at 65% Relative Humidity

Pellucere coated module P2 was subjected to one test run at RH set = 55% and then was cleaned
by compressed air only. The Isc of the module confirmed complete cleaning within the
uncertainty of the Isc measurement. Following the single 55% RH test, P2 was subjected to five
additional runs at RHset = 65% and only compressed air cleaning. In the first three runs at 65%,
P2 showed similar results to P1 testing at 55% humidity (i.e., the first 12 hours of testing showed
nonlinear decreasing soiling trend followed by little to no additional soiling with time). In all
three of these runs P2 stabilized to a ~2% soiling loss after 24 hours of testing while the uncoated
linearly soiled as expected. In contrast, P2 began to show linear soiling in runs R5 and R6 at
65% RH (as shown in Figure 8). It is outside the scope of the current work to investigate what
mechanisms might explain the increase soiling after multiple tests runs at 65% humidity. Note
that there is existing research that demonstrates that at humidity levels above 65 - 75% with all
else fixed, soiling rates can increase on typical PV glass [2, 3]. Regardless, the results of R5 and
R6 still demonstrate that after repeated testing at 65% humidity, the coated module has
significantly less soiling than the uncoated module. Specifically, per least squared linear fits of
the soiling rates, the soiling rate of the uncoated module is 2.3 times higher than the coated
module.

Soiling Rate Testing: with Consecutive Simulated Rain Cycles

Pellucere coated module P3 was mounted in the chamber side-by-side with UC1 with the intent
of simulating consecutive soiling periods followed by rain like cleanings. 20% was set as the
target soiling loss for UC1 to represent a 3-4 month soiling period as has been seen in some
locations in southern California [1]. A rainfall cleaning of both UC1 and P3 was simulated by
spraying DI water on the modules per a hand pumped garden sprayer. The DI water was sprayed
on both modules until dust or particles were no longer visible on the module surface. After the
water spray, the modules were allowed 30 minutes to dry and return to 45 °C. For three
consecutive times, the soiling chamber was held at 55 ±2.5% RH and UC1 was subjected to a
20% soiling loss followed by a water spray “rain” cleaning. After the three soil/rain cycles at 55
% RH, three more soil/rain cycles were completed but the chamber was held at 75 ±2.5% RH
and UC1 was subjected to a 20% soiling loss. Chamber controls for 55% RH were similar to
Cycle 2 and 75% RH were similar to Cycle 4 but airborne particulates levels were increased to
target a 20% soiling loss over approximately 36 hours. The results for three soiling/rain cycles at
55% RH are provided in Figure 9 while the soiling/rain cycles at 75% RH are provided in Figure
10.

Field Data

Pellucere provided NREL with 10-minute incremented PV inverter data from September 24th,
2018, through November 27th, 2020, for two inverters from a utility-scale solar field near
Avenal, California. All modules on one of the two inverters were coated per the meniscus drag
method with MoreSun plus Talus coating between August 26th, 2019, through September 12th,
2019, and modules for the second inverter were not coated. This provides ~11 months of data
for baselining the comparative performance of the two inverters before coating and greater than
14 months for evaluating changes in performance due to the coating application. Each inverter is
rated at one MW and therefore any data points above 1 MW were excluded to ensure that no
inverter clipping was included in the analysis. This filtering avoids unfairly comparing data
where one inverter is clipped while the other is not. Additionally, if one inverter was reporting a
near zero or nonreal value, both inverters were excluded for the given time stamp. Data were
summed daily for all comparisons. Rain data for the associated time period were acquired from
the PRISM climate group at Oregon State University [4]. The daily performance difference
between inverters was calculated as follows:

The data in Figure 11 is partitioned into various performance trends for better understanding the
impact of applying the Pellucere coating to one of the two inverters:

PrePeriod A (prior to November 21, 2018): This period shows a rainfall at the beginning of the
dataset but is then dry thereafter. Per the downward trend in the data, it appears the modules that
will be coated are increasingly underperforming the baseline or uncoated modules. Per the period
being dry, it is possible that precoated modules are soiling at faster rate than the baseline
modules. Variation in soiling across utility-scale plants is well documented due to prevailing
winds, proximity to dust sources like gravel roads, adjacent agricultural fields, or other factors
[5, 6].

 16

Period A (11/21/2018 - 8/25/2019): This period begins with rainfall (i.e., a cleaning event for all
modules) and regular rainfall occurs into the summer of 2018. Although rain stops in June, the
two inverters perform relatively similarly for all of period A. We evaluated all the data in period
A and found that the precoated inverter is underperforming the baseline of the uncoated inverter
by a mean of -0.11%. The data in period A do not show any clear seasonal variation, and
therefore a corrective factor of 0.11% is applied to the coated inverter for all periods after the
coating is applied.

Period B (9/13/2019 – 11/27/2019): This period is marked by no rainfall and a strong soiling
trend in the data, and linearly increasing performance for the coated inverter compared to the
uncoated inverter. We applied a comparison of the performance of the first 10 days of this
period (post-cleaning and -coating application) with the baseline period and find a mean initial
performance improvement of 3.9%. It is assumed that is gain is due to the antireflection (AR)
properties of the Pellucere coating. We note caution in this result as it is only based on 10 days.
AR gains vary with angle of incidence between solar irradiance and module orientation (which
varies with time of day, season, and the clear sky index), and therefore 10 days is a short period
to capture the full range of angle of incidence effects. For all of period B, the inverter with
uncoated modules reduces in output 0.086%/day more than the coated modules based on a least
squares linear fit with an R2 of 0.85.

Period C (11/27/2019 – 4/21/2020): This period is characterized by more regular rainfall and the
modules associate with both inverters are expected to be relatively clean. The coated inverter
shows a mean improvement of 3.0% over the uncoated inverter. If the assumption of both
inverters having clean modules is correct, a 3.0% AR gain for the Pellucere coating could be
approximated from this longer time period.

Period D (4/21/2020 – 8/5/2020): This period is characterized by the cessation of rainfall but no
clear indication of soiling. This is not inconsistent with other California PV sites that NREL has
investigated where soiling is delayed as green vegetation slowly dies and the soil dries,
eventually leading to a strong soiling trend in the fall. The coated inverter shows a mean
improvement of 2.7% over the uncoated inverter for period D.

Period E (8/5/2020 -- 11/7/2020): This period is marked by no rainfall and a strong soiling trend
in the data, and linearly increasing performance for the coated inverter compared to the uncoated
inverter. The inverter with uncoated modules reduces in output 0.066%/day more than the
coated modules based on a least squares linear fit with an R2 of 0.75.

Period F (11/7/2020 - 11/27/2020): This period begins with rain cleaning the PV plant, and the
performance difference between the two inverters drops to ~3%, which is maintained for the last
20 days of the dataset.

Entire Post coating period: For the entire post-coated period (>14 months) with the baseline

Discussion

Both chamber testing and field data demonstrated that the MoreSun plus Talus coating
outperforms uncoated modules in dry soiling environments. Per the results, the performance
gains depend on the soiling and environmental conditions. While the above results show strong
positive benefits of the MoreSun plus Talus coating over uncoated PV panels, caution should be
taken in applying these results to different environmental conditions. Specifically, this work
demonstrates results from chamber testing with AZ road dust at 75% RH or less and field testing
per Avenal, California (where the detailed environmental conditions have not been analyzed in
this report). Soiling is well known to be site-specific due to pollution sources, environmental
conditions, PV orientation and various other factors [5, 6]. This work examined the MoreSun
plus Talus coating in two specific conditions, but no investigation has been made towards
predicting performance gains in alternate environments. It should also be noted that the longest
test period in this work is ~14 months, and no investigation or claims about long-term (multiple
years) performance of the MoreSun plus Talus coating has been made.

Conclusions

The focus of this work was to conduct a performance comparison between uncoated solar panels
and solar panels with Pellucere’s MoreSun plus Talus coating. The performance comparison
was conducted both on minimodules within a soiling chamber depositing Arizona test dust and
on inverter field data from a utility-scale plant based in Avenal, California. The following key
results were found in these studies:

 1) The MoreSun plus Talus coating reduces soiling rates to near zero per chamber testing
 with AZ road dust at a mean relative humidity of 44%.

 2) The MoreSun plus Talus coating on average limits soiling losses to 2.6% per chamber
 testing with AZ road dust at a mean RH of 55%. Alternatively, in the same test
 conditions, the uncoated module continued to linearly increase soiling losses with time
 and had already reached an average of 6.3% soiling loss when the coated module had
 capped losses at 2.6%

 3) Initial chamber testing at 65% RH shows that linear soiling rates can occur on MoreSun
 plus Talus coating, but in these conditions, the uncoated module soils at a linear rate 2.3
 times higher than MoreSun plus Talus coating.

 4) In repeated cycles with soiling up to 20% for the uncoated module followed by a water
 spray cleaning, the Pellucere MoreSun plus Talus coating showed only 1% soiling losses
 for both runs at 55% and 75% RH.

 5) Inverter field data shows the MoreSun plus Talus coating provides an initial gain of 3.9%
 in clean conditions. Longer periods of data in conditions with regular rainfall show a
 2.7% - 3.0% gain for the MoreSun plus Talus coating.

 6) The inverted field data demonstrated two strong soiling periods and in each of these
 periods the inverter strings with MoreSun plus Talus coating showed linear output gains
      of 0.086%/day and 0.066%/day, respectively.

 18

 7) For the entire post coating data set (excluding inverter clipping and near zero data),
 inverter strings with MoreSun plus Talus coating showed an overall energy production
      gain of 3.8%.

All the above results consistently show performance gains for PV modules with the MoreSun
plus Talus coating, but caution should be noted in the following areas:

 1) Soiling is well known to be site-specific due to pollution sources, environmental
 conditions, PV orientation, and a number of other factors. This work examined the
 MoreSun plus Talus coating in two specific conditions, and no investigation has been
 made on extrapolation or predicting performance gains in differing conditions.

 2) The longest test period in the work is ~14 months. We have made no investigation or
 claim about long term (multiple years) performance of the MoreSun plus Talus coating.

 3) The investigation here has included relatively dry environments and has not tested results
 for humidity above 75% that would be more representative of climates that, for example,
 occur in the eastern United States.
