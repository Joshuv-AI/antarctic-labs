Structural, dielectric, and radiation shielding properties of erbium doped phosphate glasses
Authors: Gharam A. Alharshan, W. M. Morsi, Shaaban M. Shaaban, A. A. Demewez, Shimaa Ali Said
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Novel phosphate glasses augmented with different amounts of the erbium (Er2O3) oxide were created in this work using the melt-quench process. The amorphous nature was verified by X-ray diffraction (XRD) investigation. The density for the resultant glass sample is increased when P2O5 is substituted with Er2O3. The structure of current glasses was investigated using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. Increased local ordering and the creation of Er-linked cross-bridges (Er–O–P interactions), which lower NBO concentration, are two structural changes brought about by Er2O3 doping up to 0.5 mol%. The dielectric properties were assessed throughout a wide frequency spectrum. Two distinct parts show the frequency dependence of the dielectric constant, ε′: a falling portion during small frequencies and a plateau portion over large frequencies. It is clear that (ε′) declines at 0.25 as well as 0.5 mol% Er2O3 and continuously increases at higher concentrations. Additionally, (σac) exhibits a similar trend, declining at Er2O3 values of 0.25 and 0.5 mol% and gradually increasing at higher concentrations. The computability of glass through cross linked at low Er doping is the main reason for reduction in ε′, and σac. The Er-0.5 specimen with a minimum (ε′) and ac conductivity is the ideal option for packing material because to its maximal propagation velocity. Phy-X/PSD software was used to calculate the mean free path (GMFP), equivalent atomic number (GZeq), and fast neutron removal cross-sections (GFNRCS). Furthermore, build up factors (GBF) were calculated for photon energies from 0.015 to 15 MeV and penetration depths from 0.5 to 40 mfp by using the G-P fitting technique. Er-1.0 sample has provided greater gamma-ray shielding than other samples, according to the results. Additionally, these findings highlight the potential of these glasses for radiation shielding in medical and industrial applications.

Introduction

A lot of research has been done on phosphate glasses because of their special properties and versatile applications across multiple fields. These glasses typically comprise network formers, modifiers, and intermediate oxides. Formers such SiO2, B2O3, and P2O5 give them structural integrity1–3. Such formers give distinct and better capabilities through polymerization by linking oxygen atoms to form a stable network. However, modifiers like alkali or alkaline metals introduce non-bridging oxygen (NBO), that alters the linkages and characteristics of the network4,5. Such modifications can lower melting points or enable the integration of additional chemical bonds and components into the framework of glass6,7. Alkali metal-enriched glasses have a high electrical conductivity, making them fast-ion-conducting materials appropriate for cathodes or electrolytes in batteries6, and their remarkable physical behavior is largely determined by their large free volume8. Such features make them suitable for use in systems that demand high ion mobility and good electrical conductivity in various industrial and research-related fields.

When compared to other kinds of glass, phosphate glasses stand out for their exceptional physical characteristics. These glasses exhibit outstanding chemical robustness in basic and acidic environments, minimal melting, and significant warmth expansion coefficients9. Despite certain limitations in optical clarity, they also have very good ultraviolet (UV) transmission and strong electrical conductivity, which increase their usefulness in a variety of optical applications. Phosphate glass systems can exhibit strong reactivity, which is an advantage over silicate and borate-based glasses10. Nonetheless, their hygroscopic characteristics and reduced transparency may limit their use in a number of domains11,12. Despite their drawbacks, phosphate glasses are appreciated for their flexible structure, which makes it easier to incorporate a variety of cations and anions. The direct creation of cross-links among PO4 tetrahedrons upon the addition of modifiers like Na2O, ZnO, and Al2O3 develops the physical characteristics of a structure of phosphate-based glasses10. This feature makes them especially well-suited for packing material, glass-to-metal sealing, and biocompatible materials13–15. Glasses are being used more to protect against radiation because of their non-toxicity, low cost, and ease of production. Glass can also be used as an optical barrier and screen against hazardous radiation in apparatus and environments due to its wide spectrum of transparency16–18. Glass composition has a significant impact on its radiation attenuation as well as additional properties. The insertion of additives, including alkaline earth or transition- metal ions, is a primary strategy used to overcome the problems phosphate glasses confront19,20. The purpose of these modifications is to improve the glass’s binding structure, hence increasing its mechanical and chemical resilience. Glass intermediates serve two purposes, serving as formers or modifiers based on the particular composition and system conditions21–23.

Zinc ions’ (Zn2+) with unique properties and interactions inside the glass matrix have a major impact on glass compositions24–26. The electrical arrangement and ionic radii of Zn2+ ions contribute to their well-known modest polarizability.

Zn2+ ions can cause structural changes when added to glass compositions. These modifications may have an impact on the glass network’s physical, mechanical, and chemical resilience as well as its connectivity. In particular, ZnO improves parent glass’s chemical stability as well as aqueous durability27. In recent years, extensive research has been conducted on optical absorption as well as luminescence characteristics of rare earth ions (REI) doped glasses. Surprisingly, a number of optical devices are commonly made using REIs such as Sm3+, Er3+, and Pr3+28,29.

There has been an increase in interest in rare-earth-doped glasses in recent years due to their potential to significantly enhance the optical, dielectric property, and radiation-shielding qualities of glass materials. The light absorption, emission, and fluorescence characteristics of rare-earth ions, such Er3+ and Eu3+, make them useful in photonic applications like lasers, optical fibers, and LEDs. Furthermore, these elements’ large atomic numbers improve the glass’s shielding against high-energy radiation, including gamma and neutron radiation, which makes them appealing for use in nuclear power plants, medical equipment, and space technology30. Due to these characteristics, rare earth-doped glasses are becoming more popular in both industrial and scientific domains. According to S. Yalcin et al.31, doping obsidian glass with Er2O3 improves radiation shielding effectiveness. Similarly, M. El Baiomy et al.32 investigated the structural, optical, and radiation shielding properties of zinc silicate glasses doped with erbium oxide, demonstrating that erbium incorporation enhances both optical performance and radiation shielding capability, highlighting their potential for optoelectronic and radiation protection applications. Building on this concept, Berna Oto et al.33 introduced CeO2–Er2O3 co-doping as a novel approach for radiation shielding ceramics. The high atomic numbers of cerium and erbium enable this dual modification to enhance both gamma-ray and neutron attenuation while providing an environmentally safe and structurally robust alternative to conventional shielding materials. Supporting these findings, Zekiye Madak et al.34 examined the gamma-ray and neutron attenuation characteristics of ceramics doped with cerium and erbium, showing that the doping significantly improves their protective performance against both types of radiation. Dosing PbO-SiO2 glass substance with Nd2O3 enhances its structure and optical characteristics, based on R. K. Guntu et al.35. The thermo luminescence and photoluminescence characteristics of PbO-SiO2 glasses modified with Al3+ and Sb3+ were also examined by P. Ashok et al.36. Additionally, CoO inclusion at a dose of 0.4 mol% in phosphate glasses improves structural, thermal stability, and optical quality for optoelectronic applications, as demonstrated by R. K. Guntu37. Collectively, these studies highlight the effectiveness of rare-earth doping, particularly with erbium and cerium, in enhancing the structural, optical, and radiation shielding properties of glass and ceramic.

Research Gap

While earlier studies have mainly focused on the structural, optical, or shielding characteristics of similar glass systems1,10,19,22,23, the present work provides new insight into the structural, shielding, electrical conduction, and dielectric behavior. The combined investigation of structure, shielding characteristics, and dielectric response establishes a correlation between rare earth (Er3+) incorporation and dielectric performance, highlighting the multifunctional potential of these glasses for applications in packing material, glass-to-metal sealing, and radiation shielding.

In this study, investigating the impact of gradually substituting Er2O3 for P2O5 on the characteristics of the glass composite (60-x) P2O5−20Li2O-15ZnO- 5Bi2O3- x Er2O3, developed by melt quenching in a laboratory, is the aim of the study. The glass system’s characteristics include its physical characteristics, dielectric, and shielding properties.

Experimental

Phosphate glasses preparation

A set of phosphate glasses were created for this investigation and their composition was as follows: (60-x) P2O5−20Li2O-15ZnO- 5Bi2O3- x Er2O3, where x has values of 0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1 mol%. The glasses were created by traditional melt-quenching method and were given the following sequential labels: Er-0.0, Er-0.25, Er-0.5, Er-0.75, and Er-1.0 as illustrated in Table 1. A glass batch weighing around 15 g was made by combining powders of the reagents Er2O3, Li2O, ZnO, Bi2O3, and P2O5. All the raw materials, which were more than 99.9% pure, were weighed carefully, ground into a very fine powder, and then put into a 100 mL porcelain crucible. To ensure a consistent melt, the mixture was then melted for an hour at temperature 1150 °C in an electric furnace. When the glass attained a homogeneous melt, it was rapidly cooled by placing it in a metal mold. The rapid cooling encouraged the formation of amorphous glass structure by preventing crystallization. To remove internal stress, the made glasses were then placed in an oven and annealed for nine hours at a temperature of 300 °C which close to (Tg). After that, they slowly brought down to ambient temperature. Both surfaces of the annealed glass were careful polished in order to get it ready for further testing and performance assessments. Thermal Profile

A programmed electric furnace was used to heat the homogenous powder mixes after they were placed in a porcelain crucible. The thermal profile comprised melting at 1150 ± 10 °C for 40 min after preheating at 350 °C for three hours to eliminate moisture and carbonates. To ensure homogeneity, the mixture was periodically swirled when it melted. A brass mold that had been heated was used to quickly quench the viscous melt. In order to get rid of internal mechanical tensions, the created glasses were immediately moved to an annealing furnace that was kept at 300 °C for nine hours. After that, they were allowed to cool gradually to room temperature inside the furnace.

Characterizations

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted utilizing a Bruker D8 Diffractometer coupled with a Cu-Kα X-ray emitter (λCu = 1.5406 Å) to confirm that the glasses were amorphous. Each glass was immersed in distilled water to determine its density using the Archimedes principle. FTIR measurements were performed in ATR mode using a JASCO, FT/IR-430 A spectrometer (Japan) spanning 2000 and 400 cm− 1 to investigate any structural alterations in the synthesized glasses. Glass transition temperature (Tg) was determined by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) under nitrogen, heating rate 10 °C/min; Tg is reported as the onset midpoint of the endothermic step in the DSC trace.

Dielectric spectroscopy has been investigated utilizing the LCR criterion using an HIOKI 3532-50 Hi-tester device with a power source of 1 V and normal duration frequencies ranging from 50 Hz to 5 MHz. For this, specially shaped and refined pieces of glass with a diameter larger than one centimeter are utilized. Only a small amount of silver pigment is added to those two flat surfaces to act as an electrode. After that, a spring action is utilized to establish precise electrical contact and apply consistent mechanical force between the glass specimen and the dual electrodes of the test cell. The documented electrical capacitance (C) plus resistance (R) data over all frequencies were used to calculate the electric conductivity (σac) as well as dielectric constant (ε′)38–40. Result and discussion

Physical characteristics

Figure 1 illustrates the distinctive broad hump in the Er-1.0 glass XRD curve, which stretches from 2θ = 18.7o to 33.1o. This finding demonstrates a lack of structural regularity and long-range order inside the lattice, supporting the amorphous nature of the samples. When a small amount of P2O5 has been partly substituted by the same molarity ratio of Er2O3, the density of the phosphate glass specimens in the setup rises from 2.9223 g/cm³ (Er-0.0) to 3.006 g/cm³ (Er-1.00), as illustrated in Table 1. This increase indicates that the inclusion of Er₂O₃ results in a denser glass network because Er2O3 has a larger molar mass value (382.56 g/mol) than P2O5 (141. 9 g/mol)41,42. This increase is explained by the compact nature and higher atomic weight of Er-containing units, which probably replace lower-density glass formers and result in a denser packing of the glass network.

XRD of Er-1.0 glass sample.

The DSC thermograms of the (60 − x)P2O5–20Li2O–15ZnO–5Bi2O3–xEr2O3 glasses (x = 0.0–1 mol%) reveal a slight increase in the glass transition temperature (Tg) from ~ 270 °C to ~ 280 °C upon Er₂O₃ incorporation as shown in Fig. 2. This increase is attributed to the role of Er³⁺ ions as network modifiers, which enhance the rigidity of the phosphate glass network and reduce the mobility of structural units. The crystallization temperature (Tc) also shifts towards higher values (~ 355 °C to ~ 365 °C), with a less pronounced exothermic peak, indicating that the doped glasses are more resistant to devitrification. The melting temperature (Tm) remains almost unchanged (~ 490–492 °C), confirming the structural stability of the system. Consequently, the thermal stability parameter (ΔT = Tc – Tg) increases, suggesting improved stability of the doped glass. These observations are consistent with earlier reports on Er₂O₃-doped phosphate-based glasses, where Tg and ΔT were found to increase, improving resistance against crystallization and structural relaxation43–45.

Previous FTIR analyses of glasses based on phosphate were used to identify the absorption bands45–51. In this work, the baseline-corrected spectrum of the base glass exhibits well-defined bands at 525–580 cm− 1, 750–780 cm− 1, 890–950 cm− 1, and a broad composite envelope between 1100 and 1300 cm⁻1.

The low-frequency region (≈ 525–580 cm− 1) corresponds to P–O–P bending modes of metaphosphate chains52,53, while the 750–780 cm− 1 component arises from symmetric stretching of bridging P–O–P linkages55.

Higher-frequency bands around 900–950 cm− 1 represent asymmetric P–O–P stretching of Q² tetrahedra54, and the broad 1100–1300 cm− 1 feature contains overlapping P = O and non-bridging P–O⁻ vibrations of Q¹ units57.

Quantitatively, the deconvolution of Er-0.0 gives large FWHM values (90–206 cm− 1) and moderate peak heights (0.09–0.31), confirming a relatively disordered, partially depolymerized metaphosphate network. The highest area % (≈ 20%) occurs within the 1100–1240 cm− 1 band, emphasizing the dominance of non-bridging oxygens (NBOs)44,57.

After adding 0.25 mol % Er₂O₃ and re-fitting with a uniform zero baseline, two distinct components were introduced in the 600–800 cm− 1 region to capture both the 572 cm− 1 and ~ 740 cm− 1 shoulders. This adjustment reduced residuals and yielded physically reasonable widths (FWHM ≈ 45–80 cm− 1) instead of unrealistically broad single-band fits. The 875–947 cm− 1 features display increased area % (6–14%) and higher heights (0.07–0.14) compared with the undoped glass, in agreement with the raw spectral intensities. This enhancement, together with reduced FWHM, indicates improved local ordering and the formation of Er-linked cross-bridges (Er–O–P interactions) that decrease NBO concentration58.

The Er-0.5 sample required multiple components within each major envelope to avoid over-broadening. The bands near 704, 772, and 880 cm− 1 show area % up to 14–15% with narrowed FWHM (30–95 cm− 1), indicating a denser, more homogeneous structure. The peak heights for the 940–1147 cm− 1 modes reach ~ 13 a.u., confirming stronger dipole coupling as P–O⁻ groups are replaced by P–O–Er linkages61.

Importantly, the broad 1100–1300 cm− 1 envelope is decomposed into three sub-bands (≈ 1100, 1220, and 1300 cm− 1). Each band exhibits FWHM ≤ 220 cm− 1, consistent with overlapping P = O and asymmetric P–O–P modes rather than a single vibration. The cumulative area % (~ 15%) of these sub-bands signifies a higher fraction of polymerized Q² units58. Thus, at 0.5 mol % Er₂O₃ the glass achieves an optimal balance of compactness and connectivity.

At higher Er contents (Er-0.75), a band centered at ~ 1600 cm− 1 (weakly bound OH/H₂O bending) and a second at ~ 1830 cm− 1 (combination/overtone) reproduce the asymmetric shoulder, each with FWHM ≈ 120 cm− 1.

For Er-1.0, the primary component lies near 1669 cm− 1, complemented by a smaller ~ 1818 cm− 1 band, providing physically plausible widths (100–180 cm− 1).

In the main phosphate region, the 870–940 cm⁻¹ peaks become dominant with area % 12–27% and height 6–10 a.u., while the sub-bands within 1100–1300 cm⁻¹ collectively reach area % ≈ 25–27%. Such growth demonstrates continued conversion of Q¹→Q² units and increasing network polymerization58,60.

Nevertheless, slight broadening of individual FWHM values at 1.0 mol % Er₂O₃ suggests minor structural strain or Er-cluster formation, a common phenomenon at heavy rare-earth loading60.

Although the Zn–O stretching modes lie below the recorded window (< 500 cm− 1), Zn²⁺ acts as a modifier that strengthens cross-linking by polarizing neighboring oxygen atoms, indirectly shifting P–O–P vibrations to higher wavenumbers61.

Also, Bi₂O₃ contributes broad Bi–O and Bi–O–P modes near 500–700 cm− 1 and increases the overall polarizability of the network62. These Bi–O contributions are acknowledged as overlapping with phosphate bands and as potential causes of the observed intensity broadening in that region.

Overall, Er³⁺ behaves primarily as an intermediate/modifier, not a full network former. Its direct Er–O vibrations are expected at 400–600 cm− 1, consistent with the small low-frequency components detected in the fits, while the strong evolution around 1100–1300 cm− 1 stems indirectly from Er’s field strength reducing NBOs and enhancing P–O–P connectivity. At higher loadings, partial Er–O–P linkages may form locally, but the ion remains structurally intermediate between modifier and former51,58–60. Hence, the observed increase in polymerization with Er₂O₃ addition reflects indirect structural tightening rather than the appearance of new Er–O–P stretching modes at 1300 cm− 1.

These correlated trends confirm progressive network densification and the evolution from a loosely connected metaphosphate structure to a compact ultraphosphate network. Such structural strengthening, supported by consistent quantitative parameters (area %, FWHM, height), underpins the improved optical durability and rare-earth solubility of these glasses60.

Dielectric spectroscopy

For every composition under investigation, Fig. 5 displays the dielectric constant (ε′) change with frequency [(605-x) P2O5−20 Li2O-15ZnO-5Bi2O3-x Er2O3]. The spectra reveal two distinct regions indicating the frequency dependence of ε′: a decreasing region throughout small frequencies and constant region at large frequencies. The same pattern for reliance of (ε′) on provided frequencies has been found in studies of several other structures, such as glass and polymer65–67. Various polarization mechanisms, including electronic, ionic, orientation, and space charge, influence the ε′ of glasses. In this investigation, heavy, medium, and light dipoles aligned with the polarity of the applied electric field, contributing to the total polarization were observed in the analyzed samples at low frequencies. At these lower frequencies, adequate time facilitates charge accumulation at interfaces, resulting in surface charge polarization and an increase in (ε′), a phenomenon referred to as Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars polarization. However, the discovery of a relatively tiny magnitude ε′ within high frequencies would suggest that the field being applied frequency has difficulty keeping in line with the charge carrier’s oscillation frequency. At higher frequencies, charge accumulation diminishes, leading to a reduction in (ε′).

Figure 6 displays (ε′) fluctuation with erbium enrichment at certain frequencies of 3 kHz as well as 30 kHz. With Er2O3 contents of 0.25 as well as 0.5 mol%, it is clear that (ε′) drops, and at higher concentrations, it continuously increases. In fact, the degree of interconnectedness created among all glass structure elements has a substantial impact on ε′66–68. The decrease in ε′ may be caused by the increase in glass lattice solidity that results from formation of additional Er–O–P bond when an intermediate quantity of Er2O3 is incorporated to the phosphate zinc lithium glasses. (ε’) has decreased because erbium at a concentration of 0.5 mol% is strategically located to support glass building a dense substance structure of networks, significant convergence, and to allow the production of cross-links with the remaining structural components. More precisely, the main cause of space charge polarization is the mobility of unbound carrier charges, which is inhibited by cross-linkage formation. However, when a significant amount of Er2O3 is added to the phosphate zinc lithium glasses, rising in ε′ is attributed to the elevation in NBO and increases structural disorder which raise the charge carriers.

Radiation shielding

A combination of high and low Z-materials has been demonstrated to be the most effective radiation barrier against X-ray, neutron, and gamma radiation. Numerous businesses, such as industrial processing, and medical fields including diagnostic radiology and radiation therapy, use high intensity and penetrating radiation. Although radiation has many benefits, it must be used with precautions as long-term exposure can have serious health consequences70,72. Consequently, substantial research efforts have been directed toward developing effective strategies and materials to mitigate radiation hazards and enhance human protection. Therefore, the glass samples utilized in this work were produced by doping the (60-x) P2O5−20 Li2O-15ZnO-5Bi2O3 with various quantities of Erbium oxide, a rare earth metal oxide with a high density (8.64 g cm− 3) and atomic number (68). These glasses’ ability to protect against radiation was examined at photon energies ranging 0.015 and 15 MeV. γ-ray attenuation parameters of produced samples were calculated theoretically by Phy-X/PSD tool. A number of shielding and attenuation-related properties are calculated using the web-based program (Phy-X) for the substance compositions and dosimetry under study. Densities, elemental composition, and energy ranges were added as Phy-X parameter inputs75,76. To determine how well shielding materials protect against photon radiation, several physical characteristics must be computed and interpreted. These parameters measure the probability of photons interacting with matter and attenuating such as the mass, linear attenuation coefficients, mean free path (GMFP), equivalent atomic number (GZeq), build up factors (GBF), and fast neutron removal cross-section (GFNRCS). It has been investigated how increasing the molar ratio of erbium from 0 to 1.00 mol% may improve the shielding qualities of the glasses. GMFP is the reciprocal of the linear attenuation coefficient and is used to express the average distance at which a photon interacts with the shielding material. It provides information on the material’s capacity to absorb gamma radiation74.

Figure 8 displays the GMFP patterns of the glasses under study in relation to energy and Er2O3 concentration. Because the linear attenuation coefficient and photon GMFP vary inversely, glasses with higher shielding competency should have lower GMFP. The GMFP results indicate that the sample with the greatest GMFP value was Er-0.0, while the sample with the lowest GMFP value was Er-1.00. This suggests that sample Er-1.00 is superior to other samples for radiation shielding. As seen in Fig. 8, the GMFP of examined glasses is compared with ordinary concrete (OC)76. The Er-1.00 sample exhibits a somewhat reduced GMFP over the whole energy domain, as figure illustrates. This suggests that in terms of photon absorption, the Er-1.00 glass exceeds these common shielding materials. GZeq is a parameter, just like the atomic number by a single element, but it depends on photon energy. GZeq was calculated by comparing the glass samples’ (GMAC) Compton to (GMAC) Total ratio at a certain energy with the element’s corresponding rate at the same energy.

The Buildup factors (GBF) are modification variables used in shielding calculations that account for the impacts from extra stray radiation in the medium. They were described using equation of (GZeq). Gamma-ray interaction is the basis for the glasses’ GZeq. The energies of photons and the elemental makeup of the glass affect the GZeq values. According to the incoming photon energy and matter interactions, (GZeq) changes in three distinct areas, as was given in Fig. 9. The highest GZeq levels in the middle-energy area are caused by Compton’s scattering effect, and the lowest GZeq values in both low- and high-energy intervals are caused by photoelectric and electron-pair production interactions. Furthermore, GZeq increases when the proportion of erbium condensation in glass grows as obtained in Fig. 9 and Tables (4–6). The G–P (GEBF) and (GEABF) fitting coefficients (b, c, a, Xk, and d) for the Er-0.0, Er-0.5, and Er-1.0 samples are shown in Tables (4–6). Figure 10 also shows the accumulation factors (GEABF and GEBF) as a function of energy from depths of 0.5, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 20, 30, and 40 mean free paths throughout energy zone of 0.015 to 15 MeV. It has been noted that when the mean free path (mfp) increases, more scattered photons occur, which causes GEABF and GEBF increases as well. Instead of being absorbed, photons are dispersed with energy loss, which extends their time in the medium. For all glasses, GEABF and GEBF have the lowest values corresponding with low gamma rays in this energy range because of photoelectric interactions, whereas paired interactions offer the highest values, resulting in complete absorption of photon energies at peaks. Furthermore, photon energy is reduced but not completely absorbed due to Compton scattering, which brings the energy to the medium extremely near to a maximum70,78. The GEABF and GEBF values were shown to rise at high energies (> 1 MeV) and decrease at low and medium energies with the addition of erbium oxide. In this work, photon energy is partially absorbed by the glass samples’ GEABF and GEBF, which results in a drop in energy and an alteration in the photons’ direction. These results of the several scattering mechanisms are thus related to the photons within the medium as accumulating factors. Consequently, adding Er2O3 to the glass reduces the photon accumulation factor, indicating that the photons stay in the sample for an extended period because they are distributed due to energy loss rather than being absorbed79. So, Er-1.0 is a good choice in situations where radiation shielding is essential because, as its lower GEBF values demonstrate, it is a more effective radiation attenuator81. In nuclear physics and reactor engineering, fast neutron removal cross-section (GFNRCS) is a critical parameter that quantifies the likelihood of fast neutrons being attenuated. This parameter plays a vital role in numerous nuclear applications, including reactor core design, nuclear fuel evaluation, and the development of effective radiation shielding materials. Because of rare-earth ions, glass technology has greatly progressed for a variety of applications [84]. According to Table 7, the GFNRC for the Er-0.0 and Er-1.0 samples was 0.091 cm− 1 and 0.092 cm− 1, respectively. The theoretical value of GFNRC steadily increases with an increase in erbium oxide concentration. The higher value of the GFNRC is caused by the high density of erbium oxide (8.64 g cm− 3) as well as impact of large-Z value (68). This implies that combinations of high-Z constituents and low-Z concentrations are necessary for fast neutron shielding to be effective.
