Beyond IGO-Flow: Toward Convergence Analysis of IGO in Continuous Spaces
Ryosuke Kimura, Youhei Akimoto

Abstract
Information-Geometric Optimization (IGO) provides a unified framework for black-box optimization by interpreting the adaptation of a search distribution as a natural gradient update. Despite its conceptual importance, the convergence theory of IGO remains limited: most existing results concern continuous-time idealizations such as the IGO flow, rather than discrete-time updates with non-infinitesimal learning rates. In this paper, we study discrete-time IGO in continuous spaces, formulated as natural gradient updates in the expectation-parameter coordinates of an exponential family. In particular, we analyze IGO over the multivariate Gaussian family on strongly convex quadratic objective functions. Our analysis covers a setting that simultaneously incorporates full covariance adaptation, a fixed positive learning rate, and quantile-based weights. In this setting, we prove that the covariance matrix converges to the zero matrix. We further show that the mean vector converges to the global optimum, provided that the condition number of the appropriately scaled covariance matrix is bounded at sufficiently frequent iterations. These results advance the convergence theory of IGO and help bridge the gap between the mathematical theory of IGO and practical covariance-adaptive search methods such as CMA-ES.

Introduction

Many real-world optimization problems are black-box in the sense that only function evaluations are available and gradient information cannot be accessed
[
11
]
.
Evolution strategies (ESs), especially CMA-ES
[
10
]
, have demonstrated strong empirical performance across a wide range of black-box optimization (BBO) problems in continuous domains
[
16
,
14
,
12
,
7
]
.
However, despite its practical success, the theoretical understanding of these methods remains limited.

A natural way to study ESs theoretically is through Information-Geometric Optimization (IGO), which provides a unified view of a broad class of evolutionary algorithms
[
15
]
.
IGO formulates optimization as the evolution of a parametric search distribution updated along the natural gradient.
This perspective is particularly appealing for rank-based stochastic search methods, including algorithms closely related to CMA-ES.
Despite its conceptual importance, the convergence theory of IGO is still far from complete.
Establishing theoretical guarantees for IGO as a unifying framework for evolutionary computation is of fundamental importance for improving its reliability, promoting its use in real-world optimization problems, and advancing research in evolutionary computation.

In this paper, we study discrete-time IGO in continuous domains, formulated in the expectation-parameter coordinates of an exponential family.
We focus on IGO over the multivariate Gaussian family on strongly convex quadratic objective functions, while retaining three key features of practical covariance-adaptive search methods: full covariance adaptation, a fixed positive learning rate, and quantile-based weights.
Although this setting captures essential aspects of practical covariance-adaptive search methods, it also makes the analysis substantially more difficult than in continuous-time idealizations or isotropic models.

Our main contributions are as follows.
First, we prove that the covariance matrix generated by discrete-time IGO converges to the zero matrix under full covariance adaptation.
Second, we show that the mean vector converges to the global optimum, provided that the condition number of the appropriately scaled covariance matrix is bounded at sufficiently frequent iterations.
The present analysis does not yet give a fully unconditional convergence guarantee for the mean vector.
Nevertheless, it isolates the remaining obstacle in terms of the conditioning of the scaled covariance matrix and thereby provides a concrete route toward a complete convergence theory.
In this sense, our results help narrow the gap between the mathematical theory of IGO and practical covariance-adaptive search methods such as CMA-ES.

2
Related Work

Information-Geometric Optimization (IGO) is a unified framework for black-box optimization based on natural gradient updates of a search distribution
[
15
]
.
Existing analyses of IGO can be broadly divided into two lines: (1) analyses of discrete-time updates with a finite learning rate, and (2) analyses of idealized continuous-time trajectories obtained in the infinitesimal-step-size limit, such as the IGO flow.

For discrete-time IGO, existing analyses mainly establish monotone improvement guarantees, including recent divergence-based refinements
[
4
,
9
]
.
There are also convergence analyses with finite learning rates for related algorithms
[
1
,
3
]
, including an IGO-like natural gradient method with a simple objective transformation
[
1
]
and an ODE-based analysis proving geometric convergence for a simplified isotropic ES with step-size adaptation
[
3
]
.
However, these results do not cover discrete-time IGO with both quantile-based weights and full covariance adaptation, which is the setting considered here.

Most convergence analyses of IGO have been developed for the continuous-time IGO flow
[
8
,
2
,
6
]
.
While these studies are mathematically important, they analyze idealized trajectories rather than finite-step updates.
In contrast, we study a less idealized and more practically relevant setting: discrete-time IGO in continuous spaces with multivariate Gaussian distributions, full covariance adaptation, a finite learning rate, and quantile-based weights.

3
Information-Geometric Optimization (IGO)

In this section, we present the IGO formulation studied in this paper.
We first summarize the central idea of IGO and then introduce the specific formulation used in our analysis.
In particular, we consider IGO on an exponential family of probability distributions, parameterized by expectation parameters, together with a quantile-based truncation weight.

3.1
Overview

Let
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be an objective function on the search space
\mathcal{X}
, and consider the minimization of
f
.
IGO is based on a parametric family of probability distributions
\{P_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
on
\mathcal{X}
.
Rather than optimizing
f
directly over
\mathcal{X}
, IGO iteratively updates the distribution parameter
\theta
by natural gradient ascent on the surrogate objective

\displaystyle J_{t}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\theta}}\bigl[W_{t}^{f}(x)\bigr].

(1)

Here,
W_{t}^{f}(x)
is a rank-based weight defined with respect to the current distribution
P_{t}=P_{\theta_{t}}
.
Typically,
W_{t}^{f}(x)
is defined using a nonincreasing function
w:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

\displaystyle W_{t}^{f}(x):=w\Big(\Pr_{x^{\prime}\sim P_{t}}\bigl(f(x^{\prime})\leq f(x)\bigr)\Big).

(2)

Thus,
W_{t}^{f}(x)
depends only on the rank (quantile level) of
f(x)
under
P_{t}
.

The natural gradient with respect to the Fisher metric is defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{\theta}J_{t}(\theta)=F(\theta)^{-1}\nabla_{\theta}J_{t}(\theta),

(3)

where
F(\theta)
denotes the Fisher information matrix
[
5
]
.
The IGO update is then given by the natural gradient ascent

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\tau\widetilde{\nabla}_{\theta}J_{t}(\theta_{t}),

(4)

with learning rate
\tau\in(0,1]
.
Throughout this paper, we analyze the infinite-population model, i.e., the deterministic limit in which the expectations in the update are evaluated exactly rather than approximated from finite samples.

3.2
Quantile-based Truncation Weight

Fix a quantile level
q\in(0,1)
, and let
\kappa_{t}^{q}
denote the
q
-quantile of
f(x)
under
P_{t}
, defined by

\kappa_{t}^{q}=\sup\Bigl\{u\in\mathbb{R}:\Pr_{x\sim P_{t}}\!\bigl(f(x)\leqslant u\bigr)\geq q\;\text{and}\;\Pr_{x\sim P_{t}}\!\bigl(f(x)\geqslant u\bigr)\leq 1-q\Bigr\}.

(5)

In this paper, we use the quantile-based truncation weight

\displaystyle W_{t}^{f}(x):=\frac{1}{q}\,\mathbb{I}\{f(x)\leq\kappa_{t}^{q}\}.

(6)

For simplicity, we write
I_{t}^{\leq}(x):=\mathbb{I}\{f(x)\leqslant\kappa_{t}^{q}\}
.
This weight corresponds to the case where
w(z)=q^{-1}\mathbb{I}\{z\leqslant q\}
in (
2
).

When
f(x)
has no atom at the threshold
\kappa_{t}^{q}
under
P_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{x\sim P_{t}}\bigl(f(x)=\kappa_{t}^{q}\bigr)=0,\qquad\Pr_{x\sim P_{t}}\bigl(f(x)\leq\kappa_{t}^{q}\bigr)=q,

(7)

and therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}}\bigl[W_{t}^{f}(x)\bigr]=1.

(8)

In
Section

3.3
, we formulate the IGO assuming normalized weights, as in (
8
).
This assumption is satisfied in the analytical setting used in this study.

3.3
IGO in Expectation Parameterization

Assume that
\{P_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
is an exponential family with respect to a reference measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
, with density of the form

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x)=\exp\bigl(\beta^{\top}T(x)-\psi(\beta)\bigr)h(x),

(9)

with natural parameter
\beta
, sufficient statistic
T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and log-partition function

\displaystyle\psi(\beta)=\log\int\exp\bigl(\beta^{\top}T(x)\bigr)h(x)\nu(dx).

(10)

We use the expectation parameter

\displaystyle\eta_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}}[T(x)]=\nabla_{\beta}\psi(\beta).

(11)

For exponential families, the natural gradient of
J_{t}
with respect to the expectation parameter
\eta
has the simple form
[
15
]

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{\eta}J_{t}(\theta_{t})=\nabla_{\beta}J_{t}(\theta_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}}\big[W_{t}^{f}(x)(T(x)-\eta_{t})\big].

(12)

Hence, with learning rate
\tau\in(0,1]
, the parameter update reads

\displaystyle\eta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}+\tau\widetilde{\nabla}_{\eta}J_{t}(\theta_{t})

(13a)

\displaystyle=(1-\tau)\eta_{t}+\tau\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}}\big[W_{t}^{f}(x)T(x)\big].

(13b)

In the Gaussian case
P_{t}=\mathcal{N}(m_{t},C_{t})
, define the weighted mean vector
m_{t}^{\star}
and the weighted covariance matrix
C_{t}^{\star}
by

\displaystyle m_{t}^{\star}:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}}\big[W_{t}^{f}(x)x\big],\kern 5.0ptC_{t}^{\star}:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}}\big[W_{t}^{f}(x)(x-m_{t}^{\star})(x-m_{t}^{\star})^{\top}\big].

(14)

Then the parameter update (
13b
) reduces to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}m_{t+1}=(1-\tau)m_{t}+\tau m_{t}^{\star},\\
C_{t+1}=(1-\tau)C_{t}+\tau C_{t}^{\star}+\tau(1-\tau)(m_{t}^{\star}-m_{t})(m_{t}^{\star}-m_{t})^{\top}.\end{cases}

(15)

Hereafter, we assume that
P_{t}=\mathcal{N}(m_{t},C_{t})
and focus on the update rule (
15
).

4
Convergence Analysis of IGO in Continuous Spaces

We consider the convergence of the IGO update (
15
) on a
d
-dimensional strongly convex quadratic objective function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form

\displaystyle f(x)=x^{\top}Ax\qquad(A\succ 0).

(16)

Since
P_{t}=\mathcal{N}(m_{t},C_{t})
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f
is continuous, the random variable
f(x)
under
x\sim P_{t}
has a continuous distribution.
In particular,

\displaystyle\Pr_{x\sim P_{t}}\bigl(f(x)=\kappa_{t}^{q}\bigr)=0,

(17)

so (
7
) and (
8
) hold in this setting.

In
Section

4.1
, we establish a bound on the one-step progress of the expected objective value, which in particular implies its monotone improvement.
This result is used in
Section

4.2
to establish the convergence of the covariance matrix.
In
Section

4.3
, we combine the fact
\lim_{t\to\infty}C_{t}=O
with Assumption
4.1
and prove by contradiction that the mean vector converges to the optimal solution, i.e.,
\lim_{t\to\infty}m_{t}=0
.

4.1
Monotone Improvement

Define
\alpha:=(0,A)
.
The sufficient statistic for the Gaussian distribution is
T(x)=(x,xx^{\top})
.
Hence, the objective can be written as

\displaystyle f(x)=\langle\alpha,T(x)\rangle=\langle A,xx^{\top}\rangle=x^{\top}Ax.

(18)

Let
V(P_{t}):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}}[f(x)]
denote the expected objective value under
P_{t}
.
Since
V(P_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}}[\langle\alpha,T(x)\rangle]=\langle\alpha,\eta_{t}\rangle
,
premultiplying both sides of (
13b
) by
\alpha
yields

\displaystyle\langle\alpha,\eta_{t+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-\tau)\langle\alpha,\eta_{t}\rangle+\tau\big\langle\alpha,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}}\big[W_{t}^{f}(x)T(x)\big]\big\rangle

(19)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow V(P_{t+1})

\displaystyle=(1-\tau)V(P_{t})+\tau\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}}\big[W_{t}^{f}(x)f(x)\big].

(20)

Therefore, since
W_{t}^{f}
is defined by (
6
), the evolution of
V(P_{t})
is determined by the average objective value over the selected
q
-quantile region.

Proposition 1

Consider the sequence
\{V(P_{t})\}_{t\geqslant 0}
generated by the IGO update (
15
) applied to the strongly convex quadratic objective function (
16
).
Define
\mu_{t,(i)}:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{t}}\big[(f(x)-V(P_{t}))^{i}\big]
for
i=2
and
4
.
Then, for all
t\geqslant 0
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle V(P_{t+1})\leqslant V(P_{t})-\tau(1-q)\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{\mu_{t,(4)}/\mu_{t,(2)}^{2}+3}}\sqrt{\mathstrut\mu_{t,(2)}}

(21)

That is,
V(P_{t})
is nonincreasing, i.e.,

V(P_{t+1})\leqslant V(P_{t})
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

Proof

We first show

\displaystyle V(P_{t+1})\leqslant V(P_{t})-\frac{\tau}{2q}\mathbb{E}_{x,y\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{t}}\big[|I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)-I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)|\big]\sqrt{\tfrac{\mu_{t,(2)}^{3}}{\mu_{t,(4)}+3\mu_{t,(2)}^{2}}}.

(22)

Let
x,y\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{t}
.
Since
W_{t}^{f}(x)=q^{-1}I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)
, (
20
) becomes

\displaystyle V(P_{t+1})=(1-\tau)V(P_{t})+\frac{\tau}{q}\mathbb{E}\big[I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)f(x)\big].

(23)

Since
P_{t}
is Gaussian and
A\succ 0
, the scalar random variable

f(x)=x^{\top}Ax
has a continuous distribution under
P_{t}
.
Therefore, we have
\Pr\big(f(x)=f(y)\big)=0
,
and
I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)
is an almost surely nonincreasing function of
f(x)
.
In particular, for
(P_{t}\otimes P_{t})
-almost everywhere,

\displaystyle\bigl(I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)-I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)\bigr)\bigl(-f(x)+f(y)\bigr)\geqslant 0.

(24)

Thus, the assumptions of the improved Chebyshev sum inequality
[
3
, Theorem 21]
are satisfied by
I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)
and
-f(x)
.
Then, applying
[
3
, Theorem 21 with
K=2
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\big[I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)f(x)\big]

\displaystyle\geqslant-\mathbb{E}\big[I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)\big]\mathbb{E}\big[f(x)\big]+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)-I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)|\big]\mathbb{E}\big[|f(x)-f(y)|\big].

(25)

Since
\mathbb{E}[I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)]=q
and
\mathbb{E}[f(x)]=V(P_{t})
, this yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)f(x)\big]\leqslant qV(P_{t})-\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)-I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)|\big]\mathbb{E}\big[|f(x)-f(y)|\big].

(26)

Moreover, since
f(x)
is a quadratic form of a Gaussian random vector, it has a finite fourth moment.
Hence
[
3
, Proposition 22]
applies and gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|f(x)-f(y)|\big]\geqslant 2\sqrt{\tfrac{\mu_{t,(2)}^{3}}{\mu_{t,(4)}+3\mu_{t,(2)}^{2}}}.

(27)

Substituting this into (
26
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)f(x)\big]\leqslant qV(P_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)-I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)|\big]\sqrt{\tfrac{\mu_{t,(2)}^{3}}{\mu_{t,(4)}+3\mu_{t,(2)}^{2}}}.

(28)

Finally, plugging this bound into (
23
) yields (
22
).

Now we prove (
21
) by explicitly evaluating the term

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,y\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{t}}\big[|I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)-I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)|\big]

(29)

appearing in (
22
).
This term is evaluated as follows.
Since
\Pr\big(f(x)=\kappa_{t}^{q}\big)=0
, we have
I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)\in\{0,1\}
almost surely for
x\sim P_{t}
.
Hence,
|I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)-I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)|=\mathbb{I}\{I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)\neq I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)\}
.
Therefore,
\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)-I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)|\big]=\Pr\big(I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)\neq I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)\big)
.
Since
x,y\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{t}
,

\displaystyle\Pr\big(I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)=1,\ I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)=0\big)

\displaystyle=\Pr\big(I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)=1\big)\Pr\big(I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)=0\big)=q(1-q).

(30)

Similarly, since
\Pr\big(I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)=0,\ I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)=1\big)=(1-q)q
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|I_{t}^{\leqslant}(x)-I_{t}^{\leqslant}(y)|\big]=q(1-q)+(1-q)q=2q(1-q).

(31)

By substituting (
31
) into (
22
), we obtain (
21
).
This completes the proof. ∎

4.2
Convergence of the Covariance Matrix

Now we establish the convergence of the covariance matrix
C_{t}
.

Proposition

2
shows that, once
\mu_{t,(4)}
is controlled by
\mu_{t,(2)}^{2}
, the variance of
f
converges, and the convergence of the covariance matrix
C_{t}
then follows immediately.

Proposition 2

Consider IGO updates (
15
) applied to the strongly convex quadratic objective function (
16
).
There exists a constant
L\geqslant 1
such that

\displaystyle\mu_{t,(4)}\leqslant L\mu_{t,(2)}^{2}\qquad\text{for all $t\geqslant 0$}.

(32)

In fact, we may take
L=15
.
Then, we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu_{t,(2)}=0
, implying

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}AC_{t}=O.

(33)

Proof

The existence of a constant
L=15
can be derived in a straightforward manner from classical results on Gaussian quadratic forms; details are given in Appendix
0.A
.

First, we show that the second centered moment
\mu_{t,(2)}
(i.e., the variance of
f
) converges to
0
.
By inequality (
21
), we obtain

\displaystyle V(P_{t+1})\leqslant V(P_{t})-\tau(1-q)\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{L+3}}\sqrt{\mathstrut\mu_{t,(2)}}.

(34)

Let
\delta:=\tau(1-q)(L+3)^{-1/2}\in(0,\tau)
.
Rearranging (
34
), we obtain

\displaystyle\delta\sqrt{\mathstrut\mu_{t,(2)}}\leq V(P_{t})-V(P_{t+1}).

(35)

Summing from
t=0
to
n
yields

\displaystyle\delta\sum_{t=0}^{n}\sqrt{\mathstrut\mu_{t,(2)}}\leqslant\sum_{t=0}^{n}\big(V(P_{t})-V(P_{t+1})\big)=V(P_{0})-V(P_{n+1})\leqslant V(P_{0}).

(36)

Since
V(P_{0})<\infty
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\mathstrut\mu_{t,(2)}}\leqslant\frac{V(P_{0})}{\delta}<\infty.

(37)

Since
\mu_{t,(2)}\geqslant 0
for all
t\geqslant 0
, it follows that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\sqrt{\mathstrut\mu_{t,(2)}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu_{t,(2)}=0
.

Second, we show that
AC_{t}\to O
.
Since

\displaystyle\mu_{t,(2)}=\Var_{x\sim P_{t}}[f(x)]=2\Tr(AC_{t}AC_{t})+4m_{t}^{\top}AC_{t}Am_{t},

(38)

we have
\mu_{t,(2)}\geqslant 2\Tr(AC_{t}AC_{t})
.
Hence
\mu_{t,(2)}\to 0
implies
\Tr(AC_{t}AC_{t})\to 0
.
Noting that
\Tr(AC_{t}AC_{t})\geqslant\lambda_{\max}^{2}(AC_{t})
, we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}\lambda_{\max}^{2}(AC_{t})=0
, implying
\lim_{t\to\infty}AC_{t}=O
.
This completes the proof.∎

We note that the proof of
\mu_{t,(2)}\to 0
does not use any Gaussian-specific property.
Under (
7
), for IGO updates (
15
), this part of the argument only requires the moment bound (
32
).

4.3
Convergence of the Mean Vector

We prove the convergence of the mean vector to the optimal solution as follows.
First,
Lemma

1
shows that
m_{t}^{\top}Am_{t}
has a limit

V^{\prime}\geqslant 0
.
Therefore, it suffices to exclude the case
V^{\prime}>0
.
We prove it by contradiction.

Lemma

3
, together with Assumption
4.1
, implies that
\Tr(AC_{t})
must grow asymptotically.
This contradicts
Proposition

2
, and the resulting contradiction argument is formalized in
Proposition

3
.

To establish the convergence of the mean vector, we need an additional control on the shape of the covariance matrix, summarized in Assumption
4.1
.
While the previous subsection showed that
C_{t}\to O
, this does not prevent the covariance from becoming extremely anisotropic during the collapse.
A trivial way to avoid this difficulty is to assume uniform boundedness of the condition number of the covariance matrix during the whole optimization process, which is, however, not valid.
Instead, we construct a more realistic assumption on the shape of the covariance matrix.

For this purpose, consider the change in the trace
\Tr(AC_{t})
at each iteration.
From the IGO update (
15
) for the covariance matrix, let
\theta=(m,C)\in\Theta
and define

\displaystyle\Delta_{C}(\theta):=\tau C^{\star}+\tau(1-\tau)(m^{\star}-m)(m^{\star}-m)^{\top}-\tau C.

(39)

Then
\Delta_{C}(\theta)\succeq-\tau C
, and we obtain
\Tr(AC_{t+1})=\Tr(AC_{t})+\Tr(A\Delta_{C}(\theta_{t}))
,
which is lower-bounded by
(1-\tau)\Tr(AC_{t})
.
Based on this observation, we define

\gamma:=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(1+\frac{\Tr(A\Delta_{C}(\theta))}{\Tr(AC)}\right).

(40)

It is guaranteed that
\gamma\geqslant 1-\tau
.
With this quantity, we pose the following assumption on the condition number
\Cond(AC)
.

Assumption 4.1

Let
\{C_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
be the sequence of covariance matrices generated by the IGO updates (
15
) applied to the strongly convex quadratic objective function (
16
).
Assume that there exists
R<\infty
such that

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{I}\{\Cond(AC_{t})\leqslant R\}>\frac{-\log(\gamma)}{\log(\rho)-\log(\gamma)},

(41)

where
\rho>1
denotes the constant appearing in
Lemma

3
.

The assumption states that
\Cond(AC_{t})
need not be bounded during optimization, but is bounded by a constant
R
with higher frequency than the right-hand side of (
41
) would suggest.
This is a more reasonable assumption than the boundedness of the condition number over the optimization process, as the bound can be violated infinitely many times.

Experiments are performed on the quadratic objective
f(x)=x^{\top}Ax

,
A=\diag(10^{0},10^{4/9},\ldots,10^{4})
, and the quantile level

.
We evaluate two initializations where the ratio of
\|\sqrt{A}m_{0}\|^{2}
to
\Tr(AC_{0})
is approximately

and

, respectively.

To clarify the scope of Assumption
4.1
,
we visualize the algorithmic behavior under two distinct initialization conditions in
Figure

.
Here,
\sqrt{A}
is defined by
\sqrt{A}=Q\Lambda^{1/2}Q^{\top}
, where
A=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}
is the eigendecomposition of
A
and
\Lambda^{1/2}
is the diagonal matrix of the square roots of the eigenvalues.
In the regime where
\|\sqrt{A}m_{0}\|^{2}\ll\Tr(\sqrt{A}C_{0}\sqrt{A})
,

\Cond(\sqrt{A}C_{t}\sqrt{A})
rapidly approaches
1
,
demonstrating that the covariance matrix converges to a scalar multiple of
A^{-1}
.
Consequently, the assumption is empirically validated under this condition.
In contrast, when
\|\sqrt{A}m_{0}\|^{2}\gg\Tr(\sqrt{A}C_{0}\sqrt{A})
,
serious anisotropy can emerge prior to mean convergence if a large learning rate is employed.
However, given a sufficiently small learning rate, the evolution of the shape of the covariance is better regulated, and the condition number eventually approaches approximately
1
.
Therefore, this assumption serves to exclude overly aggressive initial conditions for a given learning rate, where the collapse of the covariance is much faster and more anisotropic than mean convergence.

Lemma 1

Consider the sequences

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied discrete-time Information-Geometric Optimization in continuous spaces.
Focusing on IGO over the multivariate Gaussian family on strongly convex quadratic objective functions, we analyzed a practically relevant setting that combines full covariance adaptation, quantile-based weights, and natural-gradient updates in the expectation-parameter coordinates of an exponential family with a fixed positive learning rate.

In this setting, our analysis established the convergence of the covariance matrix to the zero matrix.
In addition, under Assumption
4.1
, we proved that the mean vector converges to the global optimum.
These results constitute a mathematically meaningful first step toward a complete convergence theory of IGO in continuous domains.

Our results also clarify the main remaining obstacle: controlling the evolving shape of the covariance matrix, rather than only its overall collapse.
In the current analysis, this issue is addressed through Assumption
4.1
, thereby highlighting the gap to a fully unconditional convergence guarantee.

An important direction for future work is therefore to remove this assumption and establish a fully unconditional convergence guarantee for the mean vector.
We hope that the present analysis provides a useful foundation for further progress in this direction.

(116)

and let

\displaystyle H_{t}:=\{Y_{t}\leqslant\alpha_{q}\}.

(117)

By Step 2, we have
\Pr\big(\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}\neq\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}\big)\to 0
.
We first consider the truncated first moment.
Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}Z_{t}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}Z_{t}\right]=\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}-\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}\big)Z_{t}\right],

(118)

the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}Z_{t}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}Z_{t}\right]\right\|

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\big\{\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}\neq\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}\big\}\|Z_{t}\|\right]

(119a)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\frac{1}{q}\big(\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{t}\|^{2}]\big)^{1/2}\Pr\big(\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}\neq\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}\big)^{1/2}.

(119b)

Since
Z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{t}\|^{2}]<\infty
, and therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}Z_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}Z_{t}\right]+o(1).

(120)

Similarly, for the truncated second moment,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\right]=\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}-\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}\big)Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\right].

(121)

Hence,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\right]\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\big\{\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}\neq\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}\big\}\|Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\|_{F}\right]

(122a)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\frac{1}{q}\big(\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}]\big)^{1/2}\Pr\big(\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}\neq\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}\big)^{1/2}.

(122b)

Since
\|Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\|_{F}=\|Z_{t}\|^{2}
and
Z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{t}\|^{4}]<\infty
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\right]+o(1).

(123)

To compute the truncated moments under the asymptotic half-space, define

\displaystyle U_{t}:=(I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top})Z_{t},

(124)

so that

\displaystyle Z_{t}=Y_{t}e_{t}+U_{t},\qquad Y_{t}=e_{t}^{\top}Z_{t}.

(125)

Since
e_{t}
is a deterministic unit vector and
Z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, both
Y_{t}
and
U_{t}
are linear transformations of the Gaussian vector
Z_{t}
.
Hence
(Y_{t},U_{t})
is jointly Gaussian.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[U_{t}]=(I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top})\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}]=0,

(126)

\displaystyle\Cov(U_{t})=(I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top})\Cov(Z_{t})(I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top})^{\top}=(I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top})^{2}=I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}.

(127)

Finally, the cross-covariance between
Y_{t}
and
U_{t}
is

\displaystyle\Cov(Y_{t},U_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{t}U_{t}^{\top}\big]

(128a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[e_{t}^{\top}Z_{t}\,Z_{t}^{\top}(I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top})\big]

(128b)

\displaystyle=e_{t}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}](I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top})

(128c)

\displaystyle=e_{t}^{\top}(I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top})=0.

(128d)

Since
(Y_{t},U_{t})
is jointly Gaussian and uncorrelated,
Y_{t}
and
U_{t}
are independent.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}Z_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{Y_{t}\leqslant\alpha_{q}\}(Y_{t}e_{t}+U_{t})\right]

(129a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{Y_{t}\leqslant\alpha_{q}\}Y_{t}\right]e_{t}+\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{Y_{t}\leq\alpha_{q}\}U_{t}\right].

(129b)

By independence of
Y_{t}
and
U_{t}
, and since
\mathbb{E}[U_{t}]=0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{Y_{t}\leq\alpha_{q}\}U_{t}\right]=\Pr(Y_{t}\leqslant\alpha_{q})\mathbb{E}[U_{t}]=0.

(130)

Hence, by using the standard truncated normal identity

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}|Y_{t}\leqslant\alpha_{q}]=-\frac{\phi(\alpha_{q})}{\Phi(\alpha_{q})},

(131)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}Z_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}\mid Y_{t}\leqslant\alpha_{q}]\,e_{t}=-\frac{\phi(\alpha_{q})}{\Phi(\alpha_{q})}\,e_{t}=-\lambda_{q}e_{t},

(132)

where
\lambda_{q}:=\phi(\alpha_{q})/\Phi(\alpha_{q})=\phi(\alpha_{q})/q
.

Next,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{Y_{t}\leqslant\alpha_{q}\}(Y_{t}e_{t}+U_{t})(Y_{t}e_{t}+U_{t})^{\top}\right].

(133)

Expanding the product gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{Y_{t}\leqslant\alpha_{q}\}Y_{t}^{2}\right]e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}+\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{Y_{t}\leqslant\alpha_{q}\}Y_{t}U_{t}\right]e_{t}^{\top}

(134a)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\frac{1}{q}e_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{Y_{t}\leqslant\alpha_{q}\}Y_{t}U_{t}^{\top}\right]+\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{Y_{t}\leqslant\alpha_{q}\}U_{t}U_{t}^{\top}\right].

(134b)

Again by independence of
Y_{t}
and
U_{t}
, together with
\mathbb{E}[U_{t}]=0
, the two cross terms vanish.
Also,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{Y_{t}\leq\alpha_{q}\}U_{t}U_{t}^{\top}\right]=\mathbb{E}[U_{t}U_{t}^{\top}]=I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}.

(135)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{2}\mid Y_{t}\leq\alpha_{q}]\,e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}+(I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}).

(136)

Hence, by using the standard truncated normal identity

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{2}\mid Y_{t}\leq\alpha_{q}]=1-\frac{\alpha_{q}\phi(\alpha_{q})}{\Phi(\alpha_{q})},

(137)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{H_{t}\}Z_{t}Z_{t}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{2}\mid Y_{t}\leq\alpha_{q}]\,e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}+(I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top})

(138a)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{q}\phi(\alpha_{q})}{\Phi(\alpha_{q})}\right)e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}+(I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top})

(138b)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{q}\lambda_{q})e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}+(I-e_{t}e_{t}^{\top})

(138c)

\displaystyle=I-\alpha_{q}\lambda_{q}\,e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}.

(138d)

Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle\psi_{t}=-\lambda_{q}e_{t}+o(1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi_{t}=I-\alpha_{q}\lambda_{q}\,e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}+o(1).

(139)

4. Expansion of
m_{t+1}
and
C_{t+1}
.

Finally, we combine the asymptotic expansions of the truncated first and second moments obtained in Step 3 with the IGO update formulas, and derive the claimed expansions.

Using the IGO update formulas, we have

\displaystyle m_{t}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}X_{t}\right]=m_{t}+\sqrt{C_{t}}\psi_{t}

(140)

\displaystyle C_{t}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{I}\{S_{t}\}(X_{t}-m_{t}^{\star})(X_{t}-m_{t}^{\star})^{\top}\right]=\sqrt{C_{t}}(\Psi_{t}-\psi_{t}\psi_{t}^{\top})\sqrt{C_{t}}.

(141)

By (
139
),

\displaystyle m_{t}^{\star}-m_{t}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{q}\sqrt{C_{t}}e_{t}+o\big(\lambda_{\max}^{1/2}(C_{t})\big),

(142)

where we used that
\sqrt{C_{t}}o(1)=o\big(\lambda_{\max}^{1/2}(C_{t})\big)
.
Since

\displaystyle e_{t}=\frac{v_{t}}{\|v_{t}\|}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sqrt{A}\sqrt{C_{t}}v_{t}=\sqrt{A}C_{t}Am_{t}=u_{t},

(143)

we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{A}\sqrt{C_{t}}e_{t}=\frac{u_{t}}{\|v_{t}\|}.

(144)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sqrt{A}(m_{t}^{\star}-m_{t})=-\lambda_{q}\frac{u_{t}}{\|v_{t}\|}+o\big(\lambda_{\max}^{1/2}(AC_{t})\big).

(145)

Next, by (
139
),

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}-\psi_{t}\psi_{t}^{\top}

\displaystyle=I-(\alpha_{q}\lambda_{q}+\lambda_{q}^{2})e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}+o(1),

(146)

and therefore

\displaystyle\sqrt{A}C_{t}^{\star}\sqrt{A}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{A}C_{t}\sqrt{A}-(\alpha_{q}\lambda_{q}+\lambda_{q}^{2})\sqrt{A}\sqrt{C_{t}}e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}\sqrt{C_{t}}\sqrt{A}+o\big(\lambda_{\max}(AC_{t})\big).

(147)

Again using
\sqrt{A}\sqrt{C_{t}}e_{t}=u_{t}/\|v_{t}\|
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{A}C_{t}^{\star}\sqrt{A}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{A}C_{t}\sqrt{A}-(\alpha_{q}\lambda_{q}+\lambda_{q}^{2})\,\frac{u_{t}u_{t}^{\top}}{\|v_{t}\|^{2}}+o\big(\lambda_{\max}(AC_{t})\big).

(148)

Moreover, since

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{u_{t}}{\|v_{t}\|}\right\|=\frac{\|\sqrt{A}C_{t}Am_{t}\|}{\|\sqrt{C_{t}}Am_{t}\|}\leqslant\|\sqrt{A}\sqrt{C_{t}}\|=\lambda_{\max}^{1/2}(AC_{t}),

(149)

we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{A}(m_{t}^{\star}-m_{t})(m_{t}^{\star}-m_{t})^{\top}\sqrt{A}=\lambda_{q}^{2}\,\frac{u_{t}u_{t}^{\top}}{\|v_{t}\|^{2}}+o\big(\lambda_{\max}(AC_{t})\big).

(150)

Therefore, substituting the above expansions into the IGO update (
15
) for the covariance matrix in the
\sqrt{A}
-transformed coordinates yields

\displaystyle\sqrt{A}C_{t+1}\sqrt{A}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{A}C_{t}\sqrt{A}-\tau(\alpha_{q}\lambda_{q}+\lambda_{q}^{2})\frac{u_{t}u_{t}^{\top}}{\|v_{t}\|^{2}}+\tau(1-\tau)\lambda_{q}^{2}\frac{u_{t}u_{t}^{\top}}{\|v_{t}\|^{2}}+o\big(\lambda_{\max}(AC_{t})\big)

(151a)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{A}C_{t}\sqrt{A}-(\tau\alpha_{q}\lambda_{q}+\tau^{2}\lambda_{q}^{2})\,\frac{u_{t}u_{t}^{\top}}{\|v_{t}\|^{2}}+o\big(\lambda_{\max}(AC_{t})\big).

(151b)

This completes the proof.∎
