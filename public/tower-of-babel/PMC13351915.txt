Retrospective cohort study of recovery trajectories following anterior versus posterior fusion surgery for cervical degenerative pathology
Authors: Katie Lee, Arpitha Pamula, Alex Tang, Tan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) and posterior decompression and instrumented fusion (PDIF) are two common spinal procedures for treating cervical degenerative pathology. While previous studies have compared outcomes and complication profiles between ACDF and PDIF, there is limited outcomes data investigating the rates and patterns of clinical improvement of the two techniques. The purpose of this study was to (I) compare trends in patient reported neck disability index (NDI) scores perioperatively between ACDF and PDIF; (II) identify demographic and perioperative characteristics that may influence rate of improvement; and (III) compare surgical intraoperative complications and reoperation rates.MethodsA retrospective review from 2019–2023 was performed identifying 309 consecutive patients undergoing primary or revision ACDF (n=271) or PDIF (n=38) for degenerative disease. A minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 11 was assumed based on previous literature.ResultsAverage follow-up was 2.2 years. ACDF patients were younger (56.1 vs. 60.4 years, P=0.02), had more primary surgeries (99% vs. 74%, P<0.001) for radiculopathy (67% vs. 35%, P<0.001), less blood loss (34.9 vs. 128.2 mL, P<0.001), and shorter hospital stays (2.0 vs. 2.9 days, P=0.001). There was no difference in intraoperative complication or revision rates. ACDF patients had lower NDI scores at all timepoints (P<0.001) and greater delta improvement at final follow-up (−24.1±16.9 vs. −15.9±17.7, P=0.006). MCID achievement rates for ACDF vs. PDIF were: 3 months (72% vs. 46%, P=0.004), 6 months (76% vs. 50%, P=0.005), and 1 year (78% vs. 61%, P=0.04). There was similar MCID improvement between radiculopathy, myelopathy, and myeloradiculopathy diagnoses and single vs. multilevel fusion at all timepoints (P>0.05). No demographic or perioperative factors influenced NDI improvement.ConclusionsBoth ACDF and PDIF achieve long-term improvement exceeding MCID with similar rates. ACDF patients demonstrated faster recovery and higher MCID achievement rates at 1-year. Clinical improvement was comparable across diagnoses and number of fusion levels. These findings provide guidance in preoperative counseling, establishing realistic recovery timelines, and managing postoperative expectations.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Both anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) and posterior decompression and instrumented fusion (PDIF) produced significant long-term improvement in neck disability for patients with cervical degenerative disease with similar intraoperative complication and revision rates. However, patients undergoing ACDF demonstrated faster recovery and higher rates of achieving minimal clinically important difference (MCID) in neck disability scores at 12 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year compared with PDIF. Clinical improvement was similar across diagnoses (radiculopathy, myelopathy, myeloradiculopathy) as well as between single and multi-level fusions.

What is known and what is new?

• Both anterior and posterior cervical fusion techniques are established treatments that provide significant symptom improvement in cervical degenerative disease. However, limited literature has described the recovery and rates of postoperative recovery between anterior and posterior fusion approaches.

• This study demonstrates that while both procedures achieve meaningful long-term improvements, ACDF is associated with a faster recovery, while PDIF patients, with typically more complex pathology, demonstrate meaningful improvement over longer periods of time.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Since ACDF and PDIF are selected for patients with different clinical presentations, differences in recovery rate should be interpreted in the context of baseline pathology and surgical goals. In this cohort, ACDF patients demonstrated earlier MCID achievement, while PDIF patients also achieved clinically meaningful improvement over time. These data can help clinicians counsel patients regarding expected recovery timelines and support shared decision making.

Introduction

Background

Cervical radiculopathy commonly occurs secondary to degenerative disease. While many cases resolve with conservative treatment, surgery is indicated for patients who do not improve with non-surgical options (1). Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) and posterior decompression and instrumented fusion (PDIF) are two surgical approaches for the treatment of cervical radiculopathy (1,2). Patients who undergo ACDF were seen to have significantly improved short- and long-term outcomes in their pain and disability (3,4). Similarly, PDIF resulted in significant clinical improvements such as pain relief and function along with low rates of revision and complications (5). When comparing the two techniques in a systematic review, there is some evidence that describes comparable outcomes of both, as well as the ACDF cohort having better clinical outcomes (1). Compared to the ACDF, the PDIF technique had lower rates of reoperation while patients undergoing ACDF had better postoperative pain, bleeding, and hospital stay (6). Both procedures have been shown to be equally effective in treating cervical radiculopathy due to foraminal stenosis with no significant differences in neck disability index (NDI) scores or patient satisfaction (7,8).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Yet to date, there are a limited number of studies that describe or compare the rate of improvement, or recovery trajectory, between the two surgical techniques. Patel et al. described the concept of maximal medical improvement (MMI) which can be defined as the time point in which 90% of patients achieved a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) based on change in NDI scores (9). In their series, they found that the majority of patients undergoing ACDF achieved MCID at 3 months follow-up. However, this data has only been described for primary, single-level ACDFs. There is no data describing MMI for PDIF or multi-level surgeries. More importantly, there is a lack of analyses describing the rates of improvements of anterior and posterior surgical decompression and fusion for degenerative disease.

Objective

Therefore, the purposes of this study were to (I) compare trends in patient reported NDI scores perioperatively of ACDF and PDIF patients; (II) identify demographic and perioperative characteristics that may influence rate of improvement; and (III) analyze surgical complication and reoperation rates. The secondary study objectives were to describe the trends in NDI scores between patients being treated for either radiculopathy, myelopathy, or myeloradiculopathy as well as between patients undergoing single versus multiple level fusion. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0073/rc).

Methods

Study design

A retrospective analysis was performed between October 2019 and October 2023 identifying patients who underwent primary or revision ACDF or PDIF for cervical degenerative disease with a minimum follow up period of 1 year. Patients were retrospectively identified through review of electronic medical records across the Geisinger Health System, from which the study dataset was generated. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was determined by Geisinger Institutional Review Board to be waived for ethical approval and patient informed consent (FWA # 00000063 IRB# 00008345) as defined by the US Department of Health and Human Services Regulation for the Protection of Human Subjects. Exclusion criteria included patients who were less than 18 years old, had a primary diagnosis of trauma, tumors, or infection, had any previous corpectomies, or had a minimum follow up less than 1 year.

Data collected included patient demographics [age, gender, body mass index (BMI), race], perioperative data (levels fused, primary or revision surgery, estimated blood loss, length of stay, opioid use, tobacco use, ethanol use), diagnosis, surgical intraoperative complications and reoperations, patient-reported outcomes measure information system (PROMIS) scores, and longitudinal NDI scores. Firstly, patients were separated into two cohorts for comparative analyses based on the index surgical technique, anterior or posterior. PROMIS scores were recorded via questionnaires preoperatively and compared to scores completed at the final follow-up to calculate a delta (∆) 1-year post-op value. Secondly, these two cohorts were analyzed to determine the average time to achieve MCID of NDI scores. The ∆NDI was calculated as the difference between the latest post-op NDI score from the preoperative NDI at each timepoint. An MCID of 11 was assumed based on previous literature (9). Lastly, all patients regardless of surgical technique were separated by their diagnoses (radiculopathy, myelopathy, or radiculomyelopathy) and single vs. multi-level fusion procedures for subgroup analyses. Analyses comparing the proportion of MCID of NDI achieved were performed for these cohorts and compared at different time points at 6 weeks, 12 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year, postoperatively with sample sizes varying slightly between timepoints.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as means ± standard deviation for continuous data and count (%) for categorical data. Independent t-tests were used to compare means between continuous variables, while Chi-squared or Fisher exact tests were used to compare proportions for categorical data. Multivariate linear regression analysis was used to identify risk factors that may influence ∆NDI scores. Statistical significance was determined as a P value less than or equal to 0.05 with 95% confidence intervals. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v25 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 309 patients (ACDF n=271; PDIF n=38) were included. Patients who underwent traumatic injury, and those who did not undergo ACDF or PDIF, were excluded. Average follow-up time was 2.2±0.9 years (range, 1–4 years). Missing data were primarily observed due to variation in follow-up availability across timepoints (Figure 1). At 6 weeks, NDI data was available for 93 ACDF and 15 PDIF patients, increasing to 261 and 37 patients at 12 weeks, 247 and 36 patients at 6 months, and 271 and 38 patients at 1 year, respectively. Demographic and perioperative analysis revealed that the ACDF cohort was younger (56.1±10.5 vs. 60.4±8.8 years, P=0.02), had a greater proportion of patients with a primary diagnosis of radiculopathy (67% vs. 35%), had a greater proportion of primary surgeries compared to revisions (99% vs. 74%, P<0.001), lower estimated blood loss (34.9±57.1 vs. 128.2±97.6 mL, P<0.001), shorter length of stay (2.0±0.9 vs. 2.9±1.6 days, P=0.001), and a lower proportion of patients using an assistive device postoperatively (8% vs. 23%, P=0.03) (Table 1). No differences were found between the number of fusion levels between cohorts (P=0.45). No statistical significance was found for tobacco use prior to the procedure between anterior and posterior cohorts (12% vs. 0%, P=0.09).

Discussion

Key findings

With an aging population, the number of cervical spine surgeries is predicted to rise considerably with an estimated 13.3% increase in ACDFs and 19.3% of PDIFs in the United States from 2020–2040 (11). This increase in primary procedures is paralleled by numbers of revision procedures that have been associated with longer lengths of hospital stay, increased hospital charges, and complications such as surgical site infections (10). In light of the increased demand within an aging population, it is essential to understand the long-term outcomes of the most common types of cervical spine procedures, which include ACDFs and PDIFs. The purpose of this study was to describe trends in patient reported NDI scores perioperatively between ACDF and PDIF, identify demographic and perioperative characteristics that may influence rate of improvement, and compare surgical intraoperative complication and reoperation rates.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. As a retrospective analysis, the study is subject to limitations such as selection bias and potential for missing data. There was also a significant imbalance in sample sizes between the ACDF (n=271) and PDIF (n=38) cohorts, which may have influenced our study findings. Although, a previous study noted that anterior procedures are more commonly performed than posterior in the United States, with an estimated 153,288 ACDFs and 29,620 posterior cervical decompression and fusion (PCDF) procedures in 2020, which may help explain the uneven sample size seen in our study over a 4-year study period (11,12). While previous literature describes a point of MMI of 90%, our analysis compared several MCID time points between anterior and posterior cohorts and showed a maximum of 78% MCID improvement at 1 year for ACDF. This difference is possibly explained by patient data lost to follow up or incomplete patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) data that were not captured in our hospital’s electronic medical record. Moreover, our study includes both primary and revision cases, which introduces variability in our data, although we would argue that this variability reflects real life clinical practice and enhances the generalizability of our findings. Despite this, our results demonstrated a statistically significant difference between cohorts, which may provide valuable, clinically meaningful insight on projected outcomes for patients undergoing ACDFs and PDIFs.

Last, the primary outcome measure, NDI, while widely used and validated, is a subjective patient-reported outcome that may not capture all aspects of clinical improvement. To address this, we supplemented NDI data with PROMIS questionnaires as a secondary outcome measure, providing a more comprehensive assessment of patient-reported outcomes. Follow-up availability varied and had missing NDI data at 6 weeks. Findings regarding early postoperative recovery, particularly at 6 weeks, should be interpreted cautiously. Later timepoints had more complete follow-up and may provide more reliable estimates of postoperative improvement. Our results did show a similar trend with PROMIS scores favoring the anterior cohort which corroborates our NDI data. There was also a statistically significant delta improvement of PROMIS physical scores, although this may not be clinically meaningful. Future studies addressing these limitations could provide more robust evidence to guide clinical decision-making in cervical spine surgery.

Comparison with similar research

Patients undergoing ACDF demonstrated lower NDI scores at all postoperative timepoints and higher rates of MCID achievement at 12 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year compared with patients undergoing PDIF. However, the ACDF and PDIF cohorts differed substantially at baseline, reflecting the clinical reality that these procedures are often selected for different patterns and severities of cervical degenerative disease. In our study, ACDF patients were generally younger, had a higher proportion of patients with a primary diagnosis of radiculopathy undergoing primary surgeries, lower estimated blood loss, shorter hospital stays, and had less frequent use of assistive devices postoperatively compared to the PDIF group. The racial demographics of our sample population reflects the limited demographic diversity of the geographic region from which the patients received their care. Our demographic findings are similar to a previous study that compared ACDF and PCDF outcomes in which the authors noted their ACDF cohort to be younger with fewer comorbidities (13). In another study, patients undergoing anterior surgery for cervical spondylotic myelopathy were found to be younger and have less severe myelopathy (14). These demographic differences may reflect the spine surgeon’s inherent patient selection bias towards anterior approaches (15,16). The ACDF cohort may serve as a reference pattern for recovery in a less complex degenerative cervical fusion population, while the PDIF cohort provides insight into recovery among patients with more complex pathology. Within this framework, the finding that both cohorts achieved clinically meaningful improvement supports the effectiveness of both procedures, while the more gradual MCID achievement observed after PDIF may help inform postoperative counseling and expectation-setting.

In our analysis, ACDF patients experienced a more rapid postoperative recovery compared to PDIF patients, with a significantly greater proportion achieving MCID in NDI scores at 12 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have shown ACDF procedures to be less invasive and associated with quicker recovery times compared to posterior approaches (1,17). It is also important to note the clinically distinct patient populations of both groups where patients undergoing PDIF typically have more complex pathology. The anterior approach allows for direct decompression of the spinal cord and nerve roots without manipulation of the posterior musculature, potentially reducing postoperative pain and facilitating earlier mobilization (18,19). Monk et al. found that the gain in quality-adjusted life-years was lower for their posterior cervical foraminotomy (PCF) cohort compared to the ACDF cohort (20). Lambrechts et al. reported similar findings in that the ACDF cohort had significantly more improvement in NDI while the PCDF cohort was independently associated with a worse improvement by NDI (13). Based on their findings, the authors concluded that patients undergoing an anterior and posterior approach for multilevel degenerative cervical disease have similar outcomes after surgery. Though PCDF may be different from PDIF, their results are the closest to support our findings of differing recovery trajectories given the lack of evidence reported in the literature detailing the difference in recovery between the two techniques. A majority of ACDF patients in our study reached MCID by 6 weeks post-surgery, while PDIF patients showed a more gradual improvement, with half achieving MCID at 6 months. Although the baseline values for NDI differ between the anterior and posterior cohorts, this is likely explained by the aforementioned patient selection for each approach. ACDF patients demonstrated earlier achievement of MCID which should be interpreted in context of differing patient populations. In PDIF patients, there is a slight decrease in PROMIS Global Overall from preop (26.8) to 6 weeks (26.7), which may suggest transient early postoperative worsening due to many factors, such as postoperative pain and more extensive soft tissue involvement, that may contribute to the already more complex patient population compared to the anterior approach. Even so, PDIF patients have higher proportions of myelopathy and complex disease. Therefore, the difference in recovery rates may reflect surgical approach as well as the underlying cervical degenerative disease of the patient populations. These findings have important implications for surgical approach decision-making and patient counseling, particularly for those who may be candidates for either approach.

As a secondary outcome measure, a subgroup analysis comparing recovery rates of all patients who underwent ACDF and PDIF procedures between patients with radiculopathy, myelopathy, or myeloradiculopathy revealed no statistically significant differences between cohorts at any of the assessed timepoints. To our knowledge, no previous studies have specifically investigated the rate of clinical improvement among different cervical spine diagnoses (myelopathy, radiculopathy, and myeloradiculopathy), specifically comparing anterior versus posterior surgical approaches. Each of these conditions presents distinct clinical challenges. Radiculopathy typically manifests as nerve root compression causing radiating arm pain and neurological symptoms, myelopathy involves spinal cord compression leading to balance and fine motor difficulties, while myeloradiculopathy presents with a combination of both pathologies. Traditionally, ACDF has been favored for focal anterior compression and radiculopathy due to its direct approach to the pathology, while PDIF has been preferred for multilevel disease and significant posterior compression. This may provide an explanation for the greater number of radiculopathy diagnoses seen in our study’s anterior cohort, and greater myelopathy and myeloradiculopathy diagnoses in the posterior cohort. Our subgroup analysis comparing recovery rates between these diagnoses revealed no statistically significant differences between cohorts at any of the timepoints, suggesting that both procedures address the pathology regardless of the presenting diagnosis. Although sample sizes slightly varied between postoperative timepoints, this likely reflects loss to follow up and would not significantly impact the associations observed. In several meta-analyses comparing postoperative outcomes of ACDF for radiculopathy and myelopathy, it was found that both conditions had similar rates of improvement in pain and disability (21-23). The severity of symptoms remains a crucial factor in determining surgical intervention, with severe or rapidly progressive conditions often necessitating earlier intervention, which is a factor that has been previously identified as a predictor of improved patient-reported outcomes (21). Further research is needed to better understand the impact of specific radiographic parameters, timing of intervention, and patient-specific factors that might favor one approach over another. Additionally, prospective studies comparing these approaches with longer follow-up periods would help establish more definitive guidelines for specific diagnoses.

Another subgroup analysis was performed comparing recovery rates between single and multilevel fusions which revealed no statistically significant differences. However, a trend toward faster MCID achievement was observed in multi-level fusion patients compared to single-level fusions at 6 and 12 months. These findings suggest that although both single and multilevel fusions can lead to similar overall recovery rates, multilevel fusions may offer a potential advantage in terms of faster symptom improvement for some patients. While both single and multilevel cervical spine surgeries can improve NDI scores, studies have shown that multilevel surgeries can lead to greater improvements, especially in patients with more extensive disease (24). In a study performed by Luo et al., multilevel ACDFs in patients with degenerative myelopathy were associated with less postoperative neck pain even when comparing elderly to young populations (25). Multilevel surgeries can address more extensive areas of spinal cord compression but are also associated with higher rates of surgical site infection, longer operative times, and greater risk of pseudoarthrosis (18,26). Despite some data supporting this finding, our analysis ultimately revealed no statistically significant difference. The decision between single and multi-level fusion should therefore be based on the severity of pathology, patient-specific factors such as comorbidities, and careful consideration of the risk-benefit for each patient. There is no current literature that explores patient rates of recovery when comparing anterior and posterior approaches for cervical spine pathologies. Further studies would help provide guidance on potential surgical intervention.

Explanation of findings

Our study demonstrated that both ACDF and PDIF lead to meaningful improvements in patient-reported disability following surgery for cervical degenerative disease, with most patients in both groups achieving the MCID in NDI scores at 1 year follow-up. However, patients undergoing ACDF experienced a faster rate of recovery, with significantly higher proportions achieving MCID at 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year compared with patients undergoing PDIF. Several factors may explain this difference. Differences in patient selection may account for these findings, as ACDF patients in this cohort were younger and more frequently treated for radiculopathy, whereas PDIF patients more often had myelopathic pathology or revision procedures. Posterior approaches often involve greater soft tissue dissection and muscle detachment, which can contribute to postoperative discomfort and a more gradual recovery process. Despite these differences, multivariate analysis did not identify demographic or perioperative factors that significantly influenced NDI improvement. The ACDF cohort may serve as a reference group for recovery in a generally less complex degenerative cervical fusion population, while the PDIF cohort provides insight into recovery among patients with more complex pathology.

Implications and actions needed

These findings have several implications for clinical decision making and patient counseling in cervical spine surgery. The results highlight the importance of setting realistic postoperative expectations for patients undergoing PDIF, who may experience a more gradual improvement despite ultimately achieving similar long-term outcomes. Surgeons can incorporate these differences in recovery rate into preoperative discussions, emphasizing that both procedures are effective but may differ in the timing of symptomatic improvement. Future research should focus on prospective studies with larger and more balanced cohorts to further clarify factors influencing recovery trajectories and to better define patient populations that may benefit most from each surgical approach.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that both ACDF and PDIF procedures lead to significant clinical improvement in patients with cervical degenerative pathologies, with similar long-term outcomes. However, ACDF patients exhibited a more rapid postoperative recovery, with a significantly greater proportion achieving MCID in NDI scores at 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year compared to PDIF patients. Clinical improvement was similar regardless of the specific diagnosis (radiculopathy, myelopathy, or myeloradiculopathy) and regardless of single or multi-level procedures that were performed. These findings provide valuable guidance for preoperative counseling, establishing realistic recovery timelines, and managing postoperative expectations. Future prospective studies with larger, balanced cohorts and longer follow-up periods may further identify the long-term outcomes and factors influencing recovery trajectories in cervical spine surgery.
