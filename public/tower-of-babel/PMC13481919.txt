Ameloblastoma of the zygomatic region with infratemporal and orbital extension: a case report
Authors: Alaa Senjab, Ahmad Al-Bitar, Mahdi Sandouk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmeloblastoma is a slow-growing but locally invasive odontogenic tumor with a recognized tendency for recurrence despite its histologically benign appearance. Although most cases arise in the mandible, midfacial presentations with orbital or infratemporal extension are uncommon and may create substantial diagnostic, surgical, and reconstructive challenges. We report the case of a 63-year-old man who presented with a progressively enlarging right midfacial swelling centered over the zygomatic region, clinically apparent over the preceding 2–3 months. Computed tomography demonstrated a destructive mass centered in the right zygomatic bone, measuring approximately 6.5 × 4.5 × 6.0 cm, with extension into the zygomatic–orbital region and infratemporal fossa, while the maxilla appeared relatively preserved. Incisional biopsy favored ameloblastoma over adenomatoid odontogenic tumor, and definitive characterization was established after resection. The patient underwent en bloc tumor excision through a transfacial Weber–Ferguson approach, selected to provide adequate superior, lateral, and posterior exposure for margin-oriented resection of the orbital and infratemporal components. Reconstruction was performed using titanium mesh to restore the zygomatic–orbital contour, with buccal fat pad flap coverage. Final histopathology confirmed ameloblastoma with focal cystic change and tumor-free margins. Postoperative FDG PET/CT performed 1 month after surgery showed no residual local disease or distant metastasis. At 6-month clinical and radiologic follow-up, there was no evidence of recurrence, and the patient had no nasal obstruction, relevant phonation difficulty, diplopia, visual deterioration, or wound-related complications. This case illustrates an unusual zygomatic–orbital presentation of ameloblastoma with infratemporal extension and emphasizes the importance of detailed imaging, adequate surgical exposure, individualized reconstruction, and long-term surveillance.Keywords: Ameloblastoma, Zygomatic bone, Orbital extension, Infratemporal fossa, Weber–Ferguson approach, Craniofacial tumor, Case report

Introduction

Ameloblastoma typically presents as a slow-growing but locally invasive odontogenic tumor, often appearing on imaging as an expansile jaw or craniofacial lesion with cortical destruction and potential extension into adjacent structures, while retaining a marked tendency for recurrence despite its histologically benign appearance [1]. It is widely regarded as the most common odontogenic tumor, occurring predominantly in the mandible, whereas only a minority of cases arise outside the gnathic region [2]. This biologic and anatomic behavior is particularly relevant in the midface, where ameloblastoma may extend into the sinonasal tract, orbit, skull base, or other deep craniofacial compartments [1, 3]. Orbital involvement is distinctly uncommon but clinically important because it has been associated with substantial ocular morbidity and mortality, and published reviews emphasize that maxillary ameloblastoma, while relatively rare compared to mandibular involvement, may behave more aggressively because the thin cancellous maxillary bone and proximity to the maxillary sinus, nasal cavity, orbit, infratemporal fossa, and skull base can permit relatively silent extension and delayed diagnosis [1, 3]. Likewise, spread into the infratemporal fossa is rare, but it poses additional diagnostic and surgical challenges because this is a deep, anatomically complex space where disease may not be clinically evident until relatively advanced stages [4]. Because of these patterns of extension, cross-sectional imaging plays a central role in defining tumor extent, detecting cortical perforation and soft-tissue invasion, and guiding the operative plan; CT is particularly emphasized, while MRI is recommended when extensive soft-tissue spread is suspected [5]. Current literature also supports definitive surgical excision with adequate margins, especially for maxillary lesions, together with prolonged follow-up because recurrence may occur many years after primary treatment [1, 2]. Against this background, the present case is notable for its unusual zygomatic–orbital location with extension toward the infratemporal region, highlighting the rare craniofacial spectrum of ameloblastoma and the importance of aggressive imaging assessment, appropriate surgical exposure, and long-term surveillance [1, 3, 5].

Case report

A 63-year-old Arab male presented to the Otolaryngology clinic with a progressively enlarging right midfacial swelling centered over the zygomatic region, resulting in marked facial asymmetry. The swelling had become clinically apparent over the preceding 2–3 months and had gradually increased in size during that period. There were no clinical features suggestive of superimposed infection, such as fever, erythema, local warmth, skin ulceration, or purulent discharge. On clinical examination, the lesion was firm and painless, with intact overlying skin and no associated paresthesia. The patient reported no nasal obstruction, epistaxis, or other sinonasal symptoms. No clinically palpable cervical lymphadenopathy was noted [Figure 1].

Preoperative clinical photograph showing marked right midfacial swelling centered over the zygomatic region, resulting in obvious facial asymmetry

Computed tomography demonstrated a large locally destructive isodense mass centered in the right zygomatic bone, measuring approximately 6.5 × 4.5 × 6.0 cm in anteroposterior, mediolateral, and craniocaudal dimensions, respectively, with extension into the zygomatic–orbital region and infratemporal fossa. The right maxilla appeared radiologically preserved, supporting that the lesion was centered outside the usual maxillary location [Figure 2]. Given the aggressive radiologic appearance and extent of bony involvement, an incisional biopsy was performed. Histopathologic examination showed an infiltrative epithelial neoplasm with trabecular and cribriform areas, focal duct-like structures, nuclear palisading, and focal squamous metaplasia. Immunohistochemistry showed positivity for CK7, CK19, and p40; CD117 and S-100 were negative, calretinin was focally weak positive, and CD56 was non-conclusive. Overall, the initial biopsy favored ameloblastoma over adenomatoid odontogenic tumor, but definitive characterization was established on examination of the resection specimen.

Axial CT image demonstrating a locally destructive expansile soft-tissue mass centered in the right zygomatic–orbital region, with associated bony destruction and marked right midfacial soft-tissue expansion

Definitive surgical treatment was performed under general anesthesia using a transfacial Weber–Ferguson approach, achieving en bloc excision of the tumor. A transfacial approach was selected because the lesion involved the zygomatic–orbital region and extended posteriorly toward the infratemporal fossa, requiring wide superior, lateral, and posterior exposure for controlled en bloc excision and margin-oriented resection. A purely intraoral approach was considered insufficient because it would not provide adequate access to the orbital and infratemporal components of the lesion. Intraoperative findings documented a large tumor bulk in the zygomatic–orbital region and a substantial post-resection defect. Reconstruction was undertaken using titanium mesh to restore the bony contour of the zygomatic–orbital region. The titanium mesh was contoured to the zygomatic–orbital defect and stabilized with screw fixation to the remaining bony framework. Soft-tissue coverage was achieved using a buccal fat pad flap, and the maxillary sinus was packed as part of operative field management [Figs. 3a and b, 4 and 5].

On final histopathological examination of the resection specimen, the diagnosis of ameloblastoma was established based on the more representative excision specimen, which demonstrated an infiltrative ameloblastomatous epithelial proliferation arranged in nests and cords, with peripheral palisading, focal cystic change, and a fibrous stromal background. Although the incisional biopsy showed focal duct-like and cribriform areas that raised adenomatoid odontogenic tumor as a differential diagnostic consideration, these features were focal and insufficient to support a final diagnosis of adenomatoid odontogenic tumor. In contrast, the infiltrative growth pattern, ameloblastomatous epithelial architecture, peripheral palisading, and cystic change observed in the excision specimen favored ameloblastoma. No definite necrosis, increased mitotic activity, or prominent cytologic atypia was identified, and the surgical margins were tumor-free. [Fig. 6a and b].

A Low-power hematoxylin and eosin–stained section showing an infiltrative ameloblastomatous epithelial proliferation with cystic change within the lesional architecture. B Higher-power hematoxylin and eosin–stained section demonstrating ameloblastomatous epithelial nests and cords with peripheral palisading and focal duct-like/cystic spaces within a fibrous stroma

Postoperatively, a staging fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET/CT performed approximately one month after surgery demonstrated normal postsurgical changes at the right facial operative bed involving the right maxillary sinus region, right cheek, and right infratemporal fossa, with mild non–FDG-avid fat stranding. Importantly, there was no evidence of FDG-avid local residual disease and no evidence of distant metastasis [Figure 7]. No pathological lymphadenopathy was identified; subcentimeter submental and bilateral cervical nodes (levels Ib, II, and V) and bilateral axillary nodes were non–FDG-avid and interpreted as reactive, while mildly FDG-avid subcentimeter bilateral hilar nodes were also considered reactive. At 6-month follow-up, clinical and radiologic assessment showed no evidence of local recurrence. At follow-up, the patient had no nasal obstruction, no relevant phonation difficulty, no diplopia or visual deterioration, and no wound-related complications. Facial contour was clinically acceptable after reconstruction.

Discussion

Ameloblastoma is a histologically benign but biologically locally aggressive odontogenic epithelial tumor characterized by infiltrative growth, progressive bone destruction, and a well-recognized propensity for recurrence when inadequately treated [1, 6]. Although mandibular involvement is far more common, maxillary and midfacial lesions are often considered more clinically hazardous because the thin cortical boundaries and cancellous architecture of the maxilla may permit substantial subclinical spread before diagnosis, thereby facilitating extension into adjacent structures such as the paranasal sinuses, orbit, pterygopalatine region, and skull base [7]. This anatomical behavior helps explain why apparently benign ameloblastoma in the midface may demand oncologic-style resection and complex reconstruction rather than conservative local excision [6].

The present case is notable because it arose in an unusual zygomatic–orbital region with extension toward the infratemporal compartment, a pattern that lies outside the classic mandibular presentation and places the lesion within the rarer spectrum of craniofacial and extra-gnathic ameloblastoma [7, 8]. Reports from the sinonasal tract show that ameloblastoma can present in nontraditional craniofacial sites and mimic more common sinonasal or maxillofacial disease, often delaying recognition until the lesion becomes locally extensive [8, 9]. In the largest clinicopathologic sinonasal series available in the provided literature, recurrence occurred in 21% of cases, with most recurrences developing within 1–2 years but one occurring as late as 13 years after treatment, underscoring that long-term control depends not only on initial clearance but also on prolonged surveillance [7].

Orbital extension of ameloblastoma is rare but clinically important because it usually reflects secondary spread from adjacent maxillary or gnathic sites rather than a primary orbital tumor and may be associated with substantial ocular morbidity [1, 3]. In the systematic review by Abtahi et al., 37 cases of orbital invasion were identified, with a mean age of 52.79 years, male predominance, and a mean interval of 12.7 years from initial disease to orbital involvement, indicating that orbit-threatening extension may occur late in the disease course or at recurrence [1]. Reported manifestations included decreased vision, proptosis, globe displacement, diplopia, and limitation of ocular motility, underscoring that orbital spread is not merely radiologic but potentially vision-threatening [3]. Milman et al. further showed that maxillary ameloblastoma with orbital involvement carries high rates of recurrence and clinically significant morbidity, while a recent case also demonstrated concomitant invasion of orbital and zygomatic structures, a pattern anatomically relevant to the present case [3, 10].

Extension into the infratemporal fossa is another key feature that strengthens the rarity and surgical significance of this case. Previous reports have documented recurrent or locally invasive ameloblastoma involving the infratemporal fossa, temporal fossa, and adjacent deep craniofacial compartments, indicating that this tumor can spread beyond expected bony confines into anatomically concealed spaces when diagnosis is delayed or treatment is insufficient [4, 5, 11, 12]. Reports of temporal and infratemporal recurrence repeatedly associate these advanced recurrences with inadequate primary treatment or insufficient management of soft-tissue extension, reinforcing the principle that anatomically advanced disease should be approached with margin-oriented surgery rather than limited curettage or piecemeal excision [4, 6, 11].

The surgical strategy in the present case is therefore well supported by the available literature. In extensive maxillary disease, trans-facial approaches such as the Weber–Ferguson incision has been used to provide adequate exposure for resection of lesions approaching the orbit, maxillary sinus, and adjacent midfacial structures [2]. The same report described partial maxillectomy with orbital floor resection using safety margins and immediate reconstruction with titanium mesh and a buccal fat pad flap, illustrating that combined ablative and reconstructive planning is often essential in advanced craniofacial ameloblastoma (2). This is directly relevant to the current case, in which adequate access to the zygomatic–orbital and infratemporal region would not have been feasible through a limited approach, and reconstruction was necessary not only for contour but also for orbital support and compartmental restoration after tumor extirpation [6].

The reconstructive strategy in the present case was guided by the anatomic requirements of the post-resection defect rather than by the diagnosis alone.

Midface and maxillary reconstruction is inherently challenging because this region has a complex three-dimensional architecture with simultaneous aesthetic and functional roles, including orbital support, nasal airway maintenance, oronasal separation, speech, mastication, and restoration of symmetric facial contour [13].

In defects involving the zygomatic–orbital framework, reconstruction must therefore provide structural support rather than merely fill dead space, because loss of orbital or midfacial support may result in globe malposition, diplopia, impaired ocular motility, facial asymmetry, and loss of malar projection [14]. Titanium mesh was selected in this case because it is rigid enough to support the orbital and zygomatic framework, yet sufficiently moldable to reproduce the complex contour of the orbital floor, lower orbital rim, zygomatic prominence, and midfacial projection [15].

This rationale is supported by reports of orbital floor reconstruction after extensive maxillectomy showing that individualized titanium mesh can preserve postoperative globe projection and orbital volume, with low rates of diplopia, visual impairment, ocular motility disturbance, and mesh exposure when adequately covered by soft tissue [14]. Similarly, Dediol et al. described prefabricated titanium mesh reconstruction of Brown class III maxillectomy defects, using the mesh to reconstruct the orbital floor, lower orbital rim, zygomatic prominence, anterior maxillary wall, and alveolar contour, with satisfactory midface projection, globe position, ocular movements, vision, and facial appearance in most patients [15]. More recent long-term data also support titanium mesh as a stable reconstructive option for maxilla–orbital defects, with successful restoration of ophthalmic function in most patients and no mesh deformation or fracture on clinical and CT evaluation [17]. In the present case, a prosthetic obturator was not considered the optimal reconstructive option because the principal defect was centered in the zygomatic–orbital region rather than being primarily a palatal defect requiring prosthetic separation of the oral and nasal cavities [13]. This distinction is important because obturators are generally best suited for smaller palatal or palatomaxillary defects that do not involve the orbital floor, cranial base, cheek, or facial skin, whereas orbitomaxillary and higher midfacial defects usually require structural reconstruction [16].

Although obturators may offer advantages such as lower cost, avoidance of more extensive surgery, and easier visual inspection of the defect, their limitations include discomfort, daily maintenance, difficulty with retention in large defects or poor dentition, and frequent need for prosthodontic readjustment [13].

Microvascular osseous free-flap reconstruction, such as fibula, iliac crest, scapular, or osteocutaneous radial forearm flaps, represents another important option when large composite defects require restoration of maxillary height, dental rehabilitation, or substantial bony stock for osseointegrated implants [16]. However, in this patient, the maxilla was relatively preserved and the main reconstructive objective was restoration of the zygomatic–orbital contour and support rather than palatal-alveolar or dental rehabilitation, making titanium mesh with local vascularized soft-tissue coverage a proportionate reconstructive choice [15, 17]. The buccal fat pad flap was used to provide vascularized soft-tissue coverage and to help obliterate the post-resection dead space, which is particularly relevant because inadequate soft-tissue coverage is repeatedly identified as a major contributor to titanium mesh exposure [14]. Sun et al. specifically emphasized that titanium mesh should be combined with adequate soft-tissue coverage to cover the mesh, close the wound, fill dead space, and reduce the risk of exposure [17].

Contemporary reconstruction of complex zygomatic–orbital defects may benefit from digital surgical planning and patient-specific solutions, including three-dimensional planning, virtual mirroring, and individualized titanium mesh or implants [18]. These approaches may improve restoration of orbital volume, globe position, malar projection, and facial symmetry in selected cases. Although conventional contoured titanium mesh was used in the present patient, patient-specific reconstruction remains an important option for similarly complex defects when available.

Preoperative and postoperative imaging also remain central to management. The literature recommends CT once ameloblastoma is suspected because it defines osseous extent, cortical perforation, and regional spread, while MRI may further clarify soft-tissue invasion when disease becomes extensive [2, 5]. Detailed imaging is especially valuable in midfacial and infratemporal disease, where deep extension may be clinically occult and where complete surgical clearance depends on understanding the three-dimensional relation of tumor to the orbit, sinus walls, and surrounding soft tissues [1, 5]. Large multidisciplinary experience similarly supports radical excision with immediate reconstruction as the most reliable strategy for disease control in anatomically advanced cases [6].

Finally, the long-term behavior of ameloblastoma justifies continued surveillance even after apparently complete resection. The provided literature documents late recurrence not only in sinonasal disease but also in temporal and infratemporal presentations, including extremely delayed recurrence decades after initial treatment [4, 7]. This prolonged biologic potential, together with the documented association between recurrence and inadequate primary treatment, supports long-term follow-up protocols even in patients with negative margins and reassuring postoperative imaging findings [1, 6, 11].

Limitations

The main limitation of this report is the short follow-up period. Although no clinical or radiologic recurrence was detected at 6 months, this interval is insufficient to confirm long-term disease control in ameloblastoma, as recurrence may occur several years after apparently complete excision. Accordingly, the 6-month assessment should be interpreted only as an early postoperative outcome, and the patient will require prolonged clinical and radiologic surveillance of the zygomatic–orbital region, infratemporal fossa, maxillary sinus, and surgical margins. Additional limitations include the single-case design and the absence of validated patient-reported quality-of-life assessment after reconstruction. Another limitation is that molecular testing, including assessment for BRAF V600E or SMO-related alterations, was not performed; therefore, no molecularly guided diagnostic, prognostic, or therapeutic inferences can be drawn from this case.

Conclusion

In summary, this case illustrates an unusual zygomatic–orbital presentation of ameloblastoma with infratemporal extension and relative preservation of the maxilla. It highlights the importance of integrating clinical assessment with detailed cross-sectional imaging to define tumor extent, guide surgical access, and plan reconstruction in anatomically complex midfacial disease. In such cases, complete excision through adequate exposure, individualized reconstruction of the zygomatic–orbital framework, and prolonged clinical and radiologic surveillance are essential, as early postoperative disease control does not exclude the possibility of late recurrence.
