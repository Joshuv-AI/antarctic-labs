Federated learning for privacy-preserving opportunistic screening of lumbar-spine DXA-defined osteoporotic-level bone mineral density using chest radiographs: A multi-center independent validation study
Authors: Bo-Ru Lin, Ming-Yueh Huang, Wei-Chung Sun, Po-Wei Wu, Paul Chang, Ethan Tu, Wei-Chieh Hung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the performance of an artificial intelligence (AI) system trained via federated learning for opportunistic screening of lumbar-spine DXA-defined osteoporotic-level bone mineral density (T-score ≤ −2.5) using routine chest X-rays (CXRs) in a multi-center independent validation setting.MethodsA deep learning model was developed via privacy-preserving federated learning on 5943 paired CXR and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) examinations and validated on an independent dual-center cohort of 3007 adults undergoing same-day CXR and DXA. Ground truth was a lumbar spine DXA T-score ≤ −2.5. The primary metric was AUROC, alongside sensitivity and specificity. Image-only and clinical-only ablation models were evaluated on the same cohort to disentangle imaging from demographic contributions.ResultsAmong 3007 validation cases, 200 (6.7%) had a T-score ≤ −2.5. The AI achieved an AUROC of 0.942 (95% CI, 0.923–0.962), with sensitivity of 91.0% and specificity of 82.7%, and performance was preserved across study sites, sexes, and the clinically relevant older stratum (≥60 years). In ablation analysis, the image-only model achieved AUROC 0.931 (95% CI 0.912–0.950) and the clinical-only baseline 0.780 (0.732–0.827); non-overlapping 95% CIs (image-only higher) support an independent imaging contribution beyond demographics.ConclusionsThis federated learning-based AI demonstrates high discrimination for identifying lumbar-spine DXA-defined osteoporotic-level bone mineral density (T-score ≤ −2.5) from standard CXRs and shows temporal transportability across two institutions without centralizing raw imaging data. Positioned as a triage adjunct to confirmatory DXA, it offers a scalable, privacy-preserving opportunistic screening solution.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Artificial intelligence, Opportunistic screening, Chest X-ray, Federated learning

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by compromised bone strength and microarchitectural deterioration, predisposing individuals to an increased risk of fragility fractures [1]. Often termed a “silent disease,” it remains asymptomatic until a catastrophic event—such as a hip or vertebral fracture—occurs. The burden is particularly acute in aging societies; in Taiwan, data from the National Nutrition and Health Survey indicate a prevalence of 23.9% in men and 38.3% in women over the age of 50 [2]. Despite the availability of effective pharmacological interventions, a significant “treatment gap” persists: a vast majority of high-risk individuals are neither screened nor treated prior to their first fracture, largely due to the asymptomatic nature of the condition and the lack of proactive screening strategies [3].

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) remains the gold standard for diagnosing osteoporosis [4]. However, its utility as a mass screening tool is constrained by several barriers, including the limited availability of scanners, relatively high costs, and strict reimbursement criteria that often exclude younger, yet at-risk, populations [5]. Consequently, DXA is typically utilized as a confirmatory tool rather than for primary screening. In contrast, chest X-rays (CXRs) are among the most frequently performed radiological examinations globally for indications ranging from preoperative assessment to respiratory complaints. These routine images harbor incidental yet rich information regarding vertebral integrity and thoracic bone quality, which is currently underutilized in routine clinical practice [6].

The advent of deep learning (DL) has spurred interest in “opportunistic screening,” where AI algorithms repurpose routine CXRs to predict bone mineral density (BMD) [7,8]. While numerous studies have reported strong diagnostic performance, a critical barrier prevents their widespread clinical adoption: data privacy and cross-institutional transportability. Most existing models are trained on centralized datasets from single institutions. This “centralized” approach faces two major hurdles: first, data privacy regulations and institutional governance requirements (such as the United States Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) [9], European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) [10], and Taiwan's Personal Data Protection Act [11]) can complicate the aggregation of large multi-hospital datasets for training [12]; second, models trained on data from a single site often fail to generalize when deployed to hospitals with different X-ray equipment or patient demographics (the “domain shift” problem) [13].

To overcome these barriers, we propose a paradigm shift using Federated Learning (FL). Unlike traditional approaches that require data centralization, FL allows the AI model to be trained across multiple institutions by keeping the raw patient data within local firewalls and sharing only the model updates [14]. In this study, we developed the deep learning-based TAIMedImg OsteoPredict system using an FL architecture to ensure strict data privacy and enhance model robustness. The primary objective of this study was to rigorously evaluate the diagnostic performance of this privacy-preserving AI system using a large, temporally separated independent validation cohort derived from two distinct tertiary medical centers in Taiwan, assessing its potential as a scalable and reliable tool for the opportunistic screening of osteoporotic-level BMD.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective, multi-center independent validation study was conducted at two tertiary medical centers in Taiwan: E-DA Hospital (EDAH) and Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital (VGHKS). To evaluate temporal transportability and patient-level independence, we collected an independent validation cohort consisting of adult patients (aged ≥20 years) who underwent both a posteroanterior (PA) view CXR and a DXA scan within 1 day of each other. To ensure strict independence, patients included in the validation cohort were distinct from those used in the federated training process. The data collection periods for the validation cohort were from November 14, 2023, to February 27, 2025, for EDAH, and from December 2, 2021, to September 14, 2022, for VGHKS. Patients were excluded if their CXR images were produced by mobile X-ray units or contained significant artifacts affecting image quality. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRB) of E-DA Hospital (IRB No. EMRP-111-150 and IRB No. EMRP-114-012) and Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital (IRB No. KSVGH23-CT11-03), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Inclusion required paired CXR and DXA acquisition within one day. This stringent same-day requirement enrolled exclusively health-examination program participants at both sites (comprehensive health examinations and executive health programs), rather than acutely ill, hospitalized, pre-procedural, or homebound individuals. Consequently, the validation cohort represents an opportunistic-screening-eligible population skewed toward younger and relatively healthier adults, and may under-represent the very elderly and those with advanced comorbidity.

Data acquisition and ground truth

Routine PA view chest radiographs were acquired using digital radiography systems (Toshiba KXO-50R at EDAH; Carestream Health DRX-Evolution at VGHKS). BMD was measured at the lumbar spine (L1–L4) using site-specific DXA densitometers (Hologic Horizon-W at EDAH; GE Healthcare Lunar iDXA at VGHKS). T-scores were derived using the NHANES III young-adult reference database at both sites. Per International Society for Clinical Densitometry (ISCD) recommendations [15], T-score derivation requires at least two evaluable lumbar vertebrae; vertebrae rendered unevaluable by overt fracture, focal degenerative change with apparent BMD artifact, prior surgical instrumentation, or focal sclerotic or lytic lesions are typically excluded from the calculation by the interpreting radiologist. Standard DXA acquisition at both sites covers the lumbar spine and the hip; when fewer than two lumbar vertebrae are evaluable at the time of imaging, the lumbar acquisition is omitted and the examination is reported as a hip-only DXA, so no lumbar-spine T-score is issued. Because our study's inclusion criterion explicitly requires a lumbar-spine DXA T-score, patients without such a record are not candidates for selection and do not enter the source pool from which the validation cohort was drawn; the cohort is therefore intrinsically restricted to patients meeting the ISCD ≥2 evaluable lumbar vertebrae criterion, with no separate post-hoc exclusion step. The ground truth was operationally defined using the diagnostic threshold established by the World Health Organization (WHO) [16]. Patients with a lumbar spine DXA T-score of ≤ −2.5 were classified as the positive group (osteoporotic-level BMD), while those with a T-score > −2.5 were classified as the negative group.

We emphasize that the operational outcome of this study is detection of lumbar-spine DXA-defined osteoporotic-level BMD (T-score ≤ −2.5), not the comprehensive clinical diagnosis of osteoporosis. The latter, per ISCD and WHO frameworks [15,16], additionally requires assessment of femoral neck and total hip BMD, age- and sex-appropriate use of T-versus Z-scores, evaluation for secondary causes in younger adults, and consideration of fragility fracture history. The AI system reported here is therefore positioned as a triage adjunct to flag candidates for confirmatory DXA, not as a substitute for guideline-concordant osteoporosis diagnosis.

ISCD recommends [15] Z-score-based classification rather than T-score-based classification for premenopausal women and men under 50 years of age, with assessment for secondary causes. In this study, we applied a unified T-score ≤ −2.5 threshold across the entire adult cohort as the operational machine-learning label, providing a consistent, age-invariant target reflecting severely reduced lumbar BMD that the model could learn from imaging features. This does not constitute a clinical diagnosis of osteoporosis in younger adults; predictions in the < 50 years stratum should be interpreted as detection of imaging features associated with severely reduced lumbar-spine BMD rather than osteoporosis diagnosis.

AI model development (TAIMedImg OsteoPredict)

The AI system, TAIMedImg OsteoPredict (Taiwan Medical Imaging Co., Ltd., Kaohsiung, Taiwan), is a deep learning-based software designed to predict the likelihood of lumbar-spine DXA-defined osteoporotic-level BMD. It was developed using a multicenter training dataset comprising 5943 paired CXR and DXA examinations collected from EDAH (April 17, 2018 – November 13, 2023) and VGHKS (January 4, 2021 – December 1, 2021). To ensure consistency across heterogeneous datasets acquired from different X-ray acquisition systems, a standardized image preprocessing pipeline was implemented. Raw chest X-ray images were converted to the RGB color space, resized to a uniform spatial resolution of 256 x 256 pixels, and subsequently normalized using Z-score statistical parameters derived from the ImageNet dataset [17]. To enhance the model's robustness and prevent overfitting, data augmentation techniques—specifically random horizontal flipping with a probability of 0.5—were employed during the training phase to mimic natural anatomical variations.

The model processes multi-modal inputs, integrating PA view CXR images with patient demographics (age, sex, height, and weight). Its architecture (Fig. 1) comprises five core modules: a ConvNeXt backbone network for image feature extraction [18], a global average pooling (GAP) layer, a metadata embedding layer, a dense connection layer, and a linear regression output layer. Image features extracted by the ConvNeXt backbone are compressed via the GAP layer, while demographic variables are simultaneously processed through the embedding layer to align their dimensions. These multi-modal features are then fused and propagated through the dense layer to regress a continuous predicted T-score.

Schematic overview of the TAIMedImg OsteoPredict deep learning architecture.

The model consists of two parallel streams: (1) a visual stream utilizing a ConvNeXt backbone to extract features from posteroanterior chest X-ray (CXR) images, and (2) a demographic stream that processes clinical metadata (age, sex, height, and weight) through an embedding layer. The resulting image feature vector and demographic feature vector are fused and passed through a fully connected dense layer to predict a continuous T-score. Finally, a pre-specified operating threshold selected during internal validation classifies patients as screen-positive or screen-negative for lumbar-spine DXA-defined osteoporotic-level BMD.

To address patient data privacy and facilitate multi-institutional collaboration without data centralization, we deployed a Federated Learning (FL) architecture orchestrated via a client-server model based on the PyTorch Lightning framework (Fig. 2). In this decentralized setup, raw chest radiographs and source clinical records remained within the local institutional firewalls of the participating institutions. During training, only model gradients were transmitted to the federated aggregation server. During evaluation, each participating site's raw data remained within its institutional firewall, and the federated evaluation platform computed model-level AUROC and 95% CI from predicted probabilities and returned summary performance metrics. The training cohort was partitioned into ten folds for 10-fold cross-validation; for each iteration, nine folds (∼90%) were used for model fitting and the held-out fold (∼10%) served as the internal validation subset. Model training was conducted in iterative rounds over 200 epochs using the AdamW optimizer [19] (learning rate of 0.0001, batch size of 16), utilizing Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss. During each synchronized communication round, the central server distributed global model parameters to local clients; institutions performed local training, and only the updated model gradients (without patient-identifiable information) were securely transmitted back. These updates were aggregated using a Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm [14]. Out-of-fold predictions from the ten internal validation folds were pooled to select the binary operating threshold by maximizing the Youden index [20]. The model checkpoint exhibiting the minimum validation loss across all ten folds was selected as the final deployed model. The selected operating threshold was then fixed before any independent validation analysis and applied to the independent validation cohort to categorize patients as screen-positive versus screen-negative for lumbar-spine DXA-defined osteoporotic-level BMD, ensuring no data leakage occurred.

Operational flowchart of the proposed federated learning framework.

The pipeline comprises five key stages: (1) Local Data Preprocessing, involving image standardization and metadata encoding; (2) Sampling, including dataset splitting and class balancing with a batch size of 16; (3) Federated Learning, an iterative process of distributing the global ConvNeXt model, performing local training using 10-fold cross-validation and the AdamW optimizer, and aggregating model weights on the central server; (4) Independent Validation, where the finalized model and fixed operating threshold are evaluated on the independent validation cohort; and (5) Post-validation deployment, where the cleared multimodal configuration is implemented as an API inference service. The post-validation API inference service represents product implementation after model finalization and was not used for threshold selection or independent validation.

All required inputs — the PA-view CXR plus age, sex, height, and weight — are needed for inference of the multimodal model. In the deployed implementation, missing or out-of-range demographic values trigger a controlled inference failure rather than imputation, preventing silent performance degradation. The image-only ablation model presented below provides initial evidence for the technical feasibility of an image-only inference pathway in scenarios where demographic data are unavailable; clinical validation and regulatory consideration of such a fallback mode would be required prior to deployment in routine practice.

Ablation models for disentangling imaging and clinical contributions

To rigorously address whether the multimodal model's performance was driven primarily by imaging features or by demographic variables, we trained two additional models on the identical federated training cohort: (1) an image-only model using PA-view CXR alone (the ConvNeXt backbone with the metadata embedding layer removed); and (2) a clinical-only model using age, sex, height, and weight via logistic regression. Both ablation models were evaluated on the identical independent validation cohort (N = 3007). For each model, predicted probabilities were used within the federated evaluation platform to compute AUROC and threshold-dependent performance metrics. The platform returned model-level performance summaries for comparison across the multimodal, image-only, and clinical-only configurations. Formal paired case-level testing of AUROC differences was not available in the current platform implementation. Raw imaging data and clinical records remained within the local institutional firewalls of the participating institutions throughout, preserving the federated privacy-by-design framework.

For each ablation model, the binary classification threshold was determined by maximizing the Youden index on the pooled out-of-fold internal-validation predictions from the same 10-fold cross-validation framework used for the primary multimodal model. The threshold was fixed before evaluation on the independent validation cohort.

The clinical-only baseline was fitted as a logistic regression on de-identified demographic variables (age, sex, height, and weight) from the federated training cohort. A BMI-encoded sensitivity analysis is reported in (Table S1) [Supplementary materials].

Model configurations and regulatory status

The multimodal TAIMedImg OsteoPredict system is the configuration covered by Taiwan FDA clearance (December 2025). The image-only and clinical-only ablation models reported in this study are research tools used solely to address methodological questions raised during peer review and have not undergone separate regulatory validation. Operating-point analyses at alternative cut-offs are likewise reported as research characterization; the deployed multimodal system operates at the Youden-derived threshold described above, and deployment at alternative operating points would require separate regulatory consideration.

Statistical analysis

In alignment with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance [21] for the clinical performance assessment of computer-assisted detection (CADe) devices in radiology, the primary performance metric of the study was the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC). AUROC was selected because it evaluates the system's overall discriminative ability across all possible decision thresholds and remains robust against the class imbalance inherent in real-world opportunistic screening cohorts (where the prevalence of osteoporotic-level BMD was 6.7% in our dataset).

Furthermore, to evaluate threshold-dependent screening performance at the pre-determined operational threshold, sensitivity and specificity were analyzed as secondary metrics. These indices characterize the system's behavior as a triage adjunct: high sensitivity supports detection of screen-positive candidates for confirmatory DXA, whereas specificity helps limit unnecessary downstream referrals. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for AUROC were calculated using the nonparametric method [22], while CIs for sensitivity and specificity were estimated using the exact binomial method [23]. To evaluate the robustness of the model, subgroup analyses were performed stratified by study site (EDAH vs VGHKS), sex, and age groups (20–39, 40–59, 60–79, and ≥80 years), plus a clinically-relevant 40–79 stratum. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.0.2 [24]. A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Formal paired pairwise testing of AUROC differences (eg, paired DeLong) was not performed because the federated evaluation platform does not implement paired case-level covariance estimation across model outputs. We therefore compared the multimodal, image-only, and clinical-only models using AUROC point estimates and 95% confidence intervals, treating non-overlapping CIs as conservative evidence of model differentiation; this approach is more conservative than a paired test and may understate differences with overlapping CIs.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the validation cohort

A total of 3007 adult patients who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in the independent validation cohort. The study population was derived from two separate medical centers: E-DA Hospital (EDAH, N = 1677, 55.8%) and Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital (VGHKS, N = 1330, 44.2%). Based on the lumbar spine DXA reference standard, 200 patients (6.7%) were classified into the positive group (osteoporotic-level BMD, T-score ≤ −2.5). This relatively low prevalence reflects the screening-eligible, ambulatory nature of the cohort, which intrinsically includes a substantial proportion of younger adults undergoing comprehensive or executive health examinations. As detailed in Table 1, the prevalence of osteoporotic-level BMD was expectedly higher in females (8.8%) compared to males (4.8%), and demonstrated a clear age-dependent increase, rising from 1.3% in the 30–39 age group to 14.8% and 16.2% in the 60–69 and 70–79 age groups, respectively.

Diagnostic performance of the AI system

The primary metric, the AUROC, demonstrated strong discrimination of the TAIMedImg OsteoPredict system. In the overall validation cohort (N = 3007), the system achieved an AUROC of 0.942 (95% CI, 0.923–0.962). Using the pre-determined binary classification threshold established during the internal validation phase, the system successfully identified osteoporotic-level BMD with a sensitivity of 91.0% (95% CI, 86.2%–94.6%), a specificity of 82.7% (95% CI, 81.3%–84.1%), a positive predictive value (PPV) of 27.3% (95% CI, 23.9%–30.8%), and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 99.2% (95% CI, 98.8%–99.5%) (Table 2).

Ablation analysis comparing image-only, clinical-only, and multimodal models

As detailed in Table 3, the image-only model achieved an AUROC of 0.931 (95% CI 0.912–0.950), the clinical-only model (age, sex, height, and weight; logistic regression) achieved an AUROC of 0.780 (95% CI 0.732–0.827), and the multimodal model (primary) achieved 0.942 (95% CI 0.923–0.962). The 95% confidence intervals of the image-only model and the clinical-only baseline did not overlap, with the image-only model substantially higher, supporting an independent contribution of CXR-derived imaging features beyond demographic and anthropometric variables. Comparison of the multimodal model against the image-only model showed AUROC values within each other's 95% confidence intervals (multimodal 0.942 vs image-only 0.931), indicating that demographic variables in the multimodal configuration did not measurably improve discrimination beyond the imaging features alone at this validation cohort.

Subgroup analyses and temporal transportability

To assess performance consistency across sites and demographic subgroups, diagnostic performance was evaluated across different study sites and demographic subgroups (Table 2). Stratified by study site, the model maintained consistent discrimination across both institutions, yielding an AUROC of 0.947 in the EDAH cohort and 0.936 in the VGHKS cohort. The VGHKS cohort exhibited a sensitivity of 97.26%. This consistent performance across two independent medical centers — using DXA densitometers (Hologic at EDAH; GE Lunar at VGHKS) and chest radiography systems (Toshiba at EDAH; Carestream at VGHKS) from different manufacturers — is consistent with stable cross-vendor and cross-institutional behavior. Regarding demographic variations, performance was preserved across both sexes (AUROC 0.948 for females and 0.928 for males). When stratified by age, the system maintained clinically relevant performance in older age strata. In the 60–79 years age group, despite the inherently higher prevalence and complexity of age-related skeletal changes, the model maintained an AUROC of 0.897 and a sensitivity of 93.98%. This sensitivity in the older subgroup supports the tool's ability to maintain screening performance in the population most relevant to opportunistic osteoporosis screening.

Patients aged ≥80 years constituted only 0.3% (N = 10) of the validation cohort. This low representation reflects strict image-quality inclusion criteria: CXRs acquired with portable or mobile X-ray units — the predominant modality for bedridden, frail, or acutely hospitalized very-elderly patients — were excluded; in addition, many octogenarians cannot maintain the upright posture required for standard PA-view acquisition. Performance metrics in the ≥80 years subgroup are therefore presented for descriptive transparency only and are not used to support efficacy claims; the apparent AUROC of 1.000 in this stratum is an artifact of small sample size rather than a reliable estimate of model behavior.

Performance in the clinically most relevant 40–79 years stratum

The 40–79 years stratum (N = 2396, prevalence 7.8%), spanning the principal target population for opportunistic osteoporosis screening, demonstrated an AUROC of 0.932 (95% CI 0.911–0.955), sensitivity of 91.0%, specificity of 79.9%, PPV of 27.8%, and NPV of 99.1%. This stratum-restricted analysis confirms the model's applicability to the clinically-relevant screening demographic. Consistent performance was observed in the ≥50 years stratum (N = 1506, prevalence 10.6%; AUROC 0.915, 95% CI 0.885–0.943; (Table S2) [Supplementary Materials], the population in which guideline-based osteoporosis screening is most strongly recommended.

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and number-needed-to-DXA-per-detected-case are reported for each. The cleared multimodal configuration operates at the Youden-index point; the other reference points are presented for research transparency and are outside the current regulatory clearance scope. Across the three reference operating points, the number-needed-to-DXA-per-detected-case (NND-DXA) ranged from 4.5 at the high-sensitivity reference point (Sn 95.0%, Sp 76.6%) to 2.7 at the high-specificity reference point (Sn 71.5%, Sp 91.4%), spanning the threshold-dependent trade-off between detection completeness and confirmatory-DXA referral efficiency around the deployed Youden-index point (NND-DXA 3.7; Sn 91.0%, Sp 82.7%).

Discussion

Summary of main findings

In this multicenter independent validation study, the deep learning-based TAIMedImg OsteoPredict system, developed using a federated learning architecture, achieved strong screening performance for identifying lumbar-spine DXA-defined osteoporotic-level BMD from routine chest X-rays (overall AUROC 0.942, Sn 91.0%, Sp 82.7%) in an independent validation cohort. These findings support standard CXRs as a viable opportunistic screening tool to identify individuals with lumbar-spine DXA-defined osteoporotic-level BMD (T-score ≤ −2.5) who may otherwise be overlooked, as a triage adjunct complementary to definitive DXA assessment.

Comparison with previous studies and the role of federated learning

Several recent studies have explored deep learning for osteoporosis screening from plain radiographs [7,8], but most are trained on centralized single-institution datasets, raising concerns regarding data privacy and cross-institutional generalizability. Our federated learning architecture trained the model across institutions without transferring sensitive patient data [12]. The system was tested on an independent, temporally separated dual-center validation cohort; the consistent performance across the two sites (AUROC 0.947 and 0.936) — across two different chest radiography vendors (Toshiba at EDAH; Carestream at VGHKS) — is consistent with stable model behavior across heterogeneous CXR acquisition hardware. The use of two different DXA densitometers (Hologic at EDAH; GE Healthcare Lunar at VGHKS) for ground truth provides additional reassurance that the observed performance is not artifactually tied to a single BMD measurement system.

Privacy-preserving architecture and interpretability

The FL approach preserves data sovereignty: raw chest radiographs and source clinical records remain within local institutional firewalls, with only model gradients transmitted to the federated aggregation server. While this privacy-by-design framework mitigates governance barriers to multi-institutional data sharing, it inherently limits direct inspection of the training corpus for fine-grained error analysis. Interpretability therefore becomes paramount. Previous saliency-map studies (eg, Grad-CAM) [25] for osteoporosis screening consistently report that models focus on clavicles, ribs, and the thoracic spine — structures where cortical thinning and trabecular changes indicate systemic bone loss [26]. The AI provides a probabilistic risk assessment as a second opinion to prompt definitive DXA referral; this human-in-the-loop approach mitigates FL limitations while maximizing scalability.

Independent contribution of imaging features

A key concern in multimodal screening models is whether predictive performance is driven by imaging features or by easily-obtained clinical variables already known to predict osteoporosis (age, sex, BMI). Our ablation analyses (Table 3) directly address this: the image-only model achieved AUROC 0.931 (95% CI 0.912–0.950), while the clinical-only logistic-regression baseline using age, sex, height, and weight achieved AUROC 0.780 (95% CI 0.732–0.827). The non-overlapping 95% CIs (image-only higher) support an independent contribution of CXR-derived imaging features beyond demographic and anthropometric variables. The image-only AUROC fell within the multimodal model's 95% CI (0.942, 0.923–0.962), suggesting that demographic variables did not materially increase AUROC beyond the image-derived features in this validation cohort, although they may still affect threshold-dependent operating characteristics.

Notably, despite achieving comparable AUROC, the image-only model exhibited lower specificity (66.2%) and PPV (15.5%) at its Youden-optimal threshold compared to the multimodal model (Sp 82.7%, PPV 27.3%). This discrepancy between threshold-independent discrimination (AUROC) and threshold-dependent operating characteristics suggests that while CXR-derived imaging features provide the primary discriminative signal, the integration of demographic variables improves the separation of predicted-probability distributions near the decision boundary, yielding a more favorable sensitivity–specificity trade-off at the operational threshold. In clinical deployment, this translates to a meaningful reduction in unnecessary DXA referrals — a practically important advantage for a triage tool.

Biological mechanism and anatomical basis of predictions

A common skepticism regarding AI-based osteoporosis screening from CXRs is the biological plausibility. How can a modality designed for cardiopulmonary assessment quantify BMD? Our results align with evidence suggesting the thoracic cage contains rich osteological information. The cortical thickness of the clavicle is an established surrogate marker for systemic bone mass [27], with previous radiogrammetry studies correlating clavicular indices with lumbar spine BMD [28]. Similarly, the trabecular pattern of the proximal humerus and rib cortical integrity provide visual cues. While human radiologists may overlook these subtle textural changes, deep learning architectures excel at extracting these high-dimensional, non-linear features. Supported by the image-only ablation analysis, these findings are consistent with the model performing an automated radiogrammetric assessment aggregating information across multiple thoracic skeletal sites.

Clinical implications for opportunistic screening

Current guidelines typically recommend DXA for women ≥65 years and men ≥70 years [29], yet adherence remains suboptimal. Our AI system leverages existing CXRs taken for other indications, creating an opportunistic screening pathway without additional imaging. Across older age strata (≥60 years), sensitivity remained at clinically relevant levels. The lower osteoporotic-level BMD prevalence at VGHKS (5.5%) compared to EDAH (7.6%) is not explained by age or sex composition: mean age was nearly identical (EDAH 49.5 vs VGHKS 48.8 years) and VGHKS had a slightly higher female proportion (49.8% vs 43.4% at EDAH), which—given the higher female prevalence in our cohort overall (8.8% vs 4.8% in males)—would, if anything, predict a higher prevalence at VGHKS. The observed prevalence asymmetry therefore likely reflects differences in the referral patterns governing concurrent DXA ordering within each site's health-examination program (eg, site-specific clinical-risk-factor thresholds for same-day DXA referral). Despite this prevalence asymmetry, the model maintained consistent AUROC across sites (0.947 vs 0.936), supporting stable discriminative performance across heterogeneous screening populations. The divergent sensitivity–specificity profiles (VGHKS: higher sensitivity, lower specificity) likely reflect the interaction of this prevalence difference with the transportability of a single fixed operating threshold; site-specific threshold calibration may further harmonize cross-institutional operating characteristics. The system has obtained Taiwan FDA clearance (December 2025), supporting potential integration into routine clinical workflows, subject to institutional implementation, workflow validation, and local governance requirements.

Cost-effectiveness and health economic implications

Osteoporosis-related fractures impose a catastrophic economic burden, making preventive identification imperative, yet mass DXA screening is often cost-prohibitive. A 2025 study by Reginster et al. [30] demonstrated AI-assisted opportunistic CXR-based screening in Japan to be highly cost-effective at base-case sensitivity 90% and specificity 80%; our system exceeded both parameters (91.0% and 82.7%; formal Taiwan-specific cost-effectiveness analysis was beyond this study's scope). The reported PPV of 27.3% should be interpreted in opportunistic-screening context: at the cohort prevalence of 6.7%, it represents a 4.1-fold enrichment over the untriaged base rate, reducing confirmatory DXA referrals per detected osteoporotic-level case from approximately 15 to 3.7. PPV is intrinsically prevalence-dependent; deployment in higher-prevalence demographics (eg, post-menopausal women ≥65 years) would yield substantially higher PPV at the same threshold. Table 4 reports operating-point sensitivity analysis (research transparency only); the cleared multimodal configuration uses the Youden-derived threshold, and deployment at alternative operating points would require separate regulatory consideration.

Analysis of discordance: when AI and DXA disagree

While DXA T-scores serve as the ground truth, DXA has inherent limitations. Degenerative changes such as osteophytes [31] and abdominal aortic calcification (AAC) [32] can artificially elevate measured lumbar BMD, leading to “false-negative” DXA findings. When the AI classifies a patient as screen-positive but lumbar DXA is negative (a statistical false-positive against the lumbar-spine DXA endpoint), the AI may be detecting subtle trabecular or cortical changes in the thoracic cage — a region less prone to degenerative artifacts — not captured by lumbar DXA. This interpretation remains hypothesis-generating; future studies incorporating quantitative computed tomography (QCT) and longitudinal fracture endpoints are warranted to evaluate discordant cases.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strengths are the dual-center independent validation and the federated learning architecture. Several limitations exist. First, this retrospective study requires prospective validation to assess actual fracture-prevention impact. Second, the operational ground truth was lumbar-spine (L1–4) DXA only. Comprehensive osteoporosis diagnosis under ISCD/WHO frameworks [15,16] additionally incorporates femoral neck and total hip BMD, and fragility fractures in severe disease; our findings should be interpreted as detection of lumbar-spine DXA-defined osteoporotic-level BMD (T-score ≤ −2.5) rather than diagnostic performance for clinical osteoporosis in its entirety. Femoral neck and total hip BMD were not separately collected at retrospective dataset assembly; extension to multi-site BMD and ultimately fragility-fracture endpoints is the subject of an ongoing prospective follow-up study. Third, ISCD-recommended Z-score-based classification was not applied for individuals <50 years and premenopausal women; the unified T-score ≤−2.5 label was used across all ages as a machine-learning convenience, and reported performance in the <50 years stratum should be interpreted as detection of severely reduced lumbar-spine BMD rather than osteoporosis diagnosis. Fourth, both cohorts were drawn from the same two institutions (EDAH and VGHKS); the study therefore evaluates temporal and patient-level generalizability rather than fully external institutional generalizability. The low cohort prevalence (6.7%) yields a high NPV (99.2%) but a modest PPV (27.3%). Fifth, the same-day CXR–DXA inclusion criterion enrolled exclusively health-examination program participants and under-represented frail, hospitalized, pre-procedural, or homebound patients; generalizability to inpatient or long-term-care settings has not been established.

Future directions: beyond T-scores

Future iterations will aim to: (1) extend the reference standard to multi-site BMD (femoral neck, total hip) and ultimately to fragility fracture endpoints (a follow-up study incorporating multi-site DXA measurements is underway); (2) incorporate age- and sex-appropriate Z-score-based labeling for younger adults; (3) integrate the image-based AI score with Electronic Health Record (EHR) data and FRAX clinical risk factors [33] to create a holistic multimodal risk assessment tool; (4) develop age-stratified or risk-stratified operating points to optimize PPV and DXA referral efficiency across deployment contexts; and (5) validate the system in additional institutions, geographic regions, and X-ray equipment manufacturers to establish fully external institutional generalizability.

Conclusions

The deep learning-based TAIMedImg OsteoPredict system, developed via a privacy-preserving federated learning architecture, provides strong screening performance for lumbar-spine DXA-defined osteoporotic-level BMD. By avoiding centralized aggregation of raw patient imaging data, this study demonstrates that high-discrimination AI models can be developed across institutions while preserving data sovereignty. Validated on a temporally separated, dual-center independent cohort, the system supports opportunistic screening from routine chest X-rays as a triage adjunct to confirmatory DXA, including individuals undergoing CXRs for unrelated indications who may not otherwise receive DXA assessment. Prospective evaluation and external institutional validation are warranted to further establish clinical utility.
