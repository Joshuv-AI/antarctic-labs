Empathy and xenophobic attitudes toward migrants among midwifery students in Türkiye: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Dilek Hacıvelioğlu, Ayşenur Turan Tercan, Nazan Tuna Oran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined xenophobia and empathic tendency among 724 midwifery students from different regions of Turkey. Data were collected online using the Student Information Form, the Empathic Tendency Scale, and the Xenophobia Scale, and analyzed with non-parametric tests. Empathy tendencies differed in terms of the university attended, citizenship status, income level, intercultural friendships, and providing care to a foreign individual during clinical practice. Xenophobia levels, on the other hand, were significantly associated with the university attended, perceived income level, type of place of residence where they grew up, family type, and having taken a course on intercultural midwifery. Introduction

In recent years, migration movements have been increasing worldwide due to factors such as educational opportunities, security-related concerns, economic expectations, and environmental degradation. While this phenomenon contributes to greater cultural diversity within societies, it simultaneously brings to the forefront not only the concepts of cultural adaptation and cultural competence but also exclusionary attitudes, including racism and xenophobia. Xenophobia, a significant social problem frequently faced by immigrant individuals in many countries that receive immigrants, is defined as the development of fear, perceived threat, and prejudiced attitudes towards national or cultural groups that do not belong to one's own [1, 2]. The emergence of these attitudes can be influenced by individuals' psychological, social, cultural, and economic characteristics, perceptions of cultural identity, and societal competition dynamics. It is suggested that competition for access to employment, housing, and social services, as well as perceptions that immigrants deplete local resources or reduce job opportunities, can trigger xenophobia [3, 4]. Furthermore, concerns about changes in existing social structures create a perception of threat in individuals, and social identity processes based on the "us" and "them" distinction can reinforce xenophobic behaviors by strengthening individuals' tendency to protect and consider their own group superior [5, 6].

Multicultural societal structures resulting from forced migration pose significant challenges, particularly in the delivery of healthcare services. The literature reports that immigrant individuals face prejudices regarding the transmission of infectious diseases, perceived deficiencies in reproductive health services, and the idea that they burden the healthcare system; language barriers and cultural differences also complicate communication between healthcare professionals and patients. Prejudice and discriminatory attitudes can negatively impact immigrants' access to healthcare services, deepening health inequalities [7–9].

It is emphasized that xenophobia is not limited to social exclusion but also creates multifaceted negative effects on individuals' health. Xenophobia, which has significant effects on mental health, is associated with anxiety, depression, increased stress levels, and a decline in overall psychological well-being. Furthermore, another important consequence of xenophobia is the creation of barriers to access for immigrants' access to healthcare services. This situation can lead to the worsening of existing health problems and the loss of opportunities for early diagnosis and treatment, particularly for individuals belonging to already vulnerable groups [2]. Women’s health services are among the areas most affected by migration and cultural differences. In particular, refugee and migrant women are at increased risk in terms of maternal and infant health due to structural and individual barriers to accessing care [10]. Midwives play an active role in public health services and provide care to immigrant families in this process. It has been reported that midwives’ attitudes toward these groups may be shaped by institutional structures, systemic inequalities, and individual prejudices, and that such factors are determinant in the quality of care provided [10, 11]. In this context, developing a midwifery workforce that represents cultural diversity and is sensitive to transcultural care is of critical importance.

Another topic addressed in this research is empathy. Empathy is an emotional response that encompasses attention and experience toward perceiving another person's feelings or situation [12]. Empathic tendency, on the other hand, reflects an individual's emotional predisposition to empathy and directly influences professional caregiving behaviors [13]. Empathy, considered fundamental in human relations, is also a crucial component of the relationship between healthcare providers and caregivers. Through empathy, it is possible to provide effective and high-quality care by better understanding the individual's feelings and circumstances [14]. An empathetic approach in healthcare delivery benefits both patients and healthcare professionals [15]. High levels of empathy in healthcare are associated with better clinical outcomes, more accurate diagnoses, and higher patient satisfaction. It has also been noted that high levels of empathy protect healthcare workers from burnout [16].

Empathy stands out not only as an individual trait but also as a psychosocial mechanism and a public health determinant affecting health equity. Empathic approaches in healthcare are associated with reducing discrimination and improving access to care, particularly among vulnerable and immigrant populations. Empathy can contribute to reducing health inequalities by reducing prejudice and improving culturally sensitive care [17, 18].

It is suggested that the predisposition to empathy is inherent in human nature and can be stimulated throughout life. In the midwifery profession, where emotional processes are intensely experienced, the development and internalization of empathy skills should begin from the student years [19]. Considering the decisive role of the educational process in shaping professional attitudes and values, it is important to evaluate the empathic tendencies and xenophobia-related attitudes of midwifery students, who are expected to assume an active role in healthcare services in the near future. Delivering effective care in multicultural settings requires the development of educational approaches grounded in intercultural awareness and empathy from the student years onward. This study aimed to determine the levels of empathy and xenophobia among midwifery students, to examine the relationship between these variables, and to evaluate whether empathic tendency predicts xenophobia.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed a descriptive, correlational, and analytical cross-sectional design. The planning, implementation, and reporting of the study adhered to the guidelines outlined in the STROBE statement [20].

Study settings and sample

The research was conducted between March and June of the 2024–2025 academic year with midwifery students from three different universities in Turkey. These universities were located in the Marmara Region, the Aegean Region, and the Southeastern Anatolia Region of Turkey.

Students aged 18 years and older who were actively enrolled during the data collection period, were able to read and comprehend Turkish, and completed the study questionnaires were included. Students younger than 18 years, those who were unable to read and comprehend Turkish, those who did not participate in the study, and those who returned incomplete questionnaires were excluded. The total number of midwifery students at these universities was 584 at the first university located in the Marmara Region, 476 at the second university located in the Aegean Region, and 300 at the third university located in the Southeastern Anatolia Region. The sample size was calculated using the G*Power 3.1 software for a one-way ANOVA (k = 3, f = 0.25, α = 0.05, power = 0.95), and the minimum required sample size was determined as n = 252. Within the scope of the study, a total of 724 students were reached, which was substantially above the calculated minimum sample size.

The selection of universities was based on their potential to represent regions of Turkey with different sociocultural characteristics, as well as the accessibility of the researchers.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using the Student Information Form, the Empathic Tendency Scale, and the Xenophobia Scale.

Student information form

This form was developed by the researchers based on the relevant literature to determine students’ sociodemographic and educational characteristics and consists of 18 items [13, 21–23].

Empathic tendency scale

The Empathic Tendency Scale, developed by Dökmen [24], measures individuals’ tendency to empathize in daily life. The scale consists of 20 items. Mean empathic tendency scores are interpreted as low between 20–46, moderate between 47–73, and high between 74–100. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the original scale was reported as 0.82 [24]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the Empathy Tendency Scale was found to be 0.68. Although this value is slightly below the commonly recommended threshold of 0.70, it was considered to indicate an acceptable level of internal consistency for research purposes in measuring psychosocial constructs. The scale is in Turkish,however, an English translation of the scale items is provided in the Supplementary Files for international readers.

Xenophobia Scale

Originally developed by Van der Veer et al. [25], the scale was adapted into Turkish by Özmete et al. [26]. The scale, which assesses individuals' attitudes towards xenophobia, is a 6-point Likert-type scale and consists of 11 questions. The minimum possible score is 11 and the maximum possible score is 66. Higher scores indicate a higher level of xenophobia. No cutoff value was determined in the adaptation study of the scale,therefore, the obtained scores were considered as continuous variables and used accordingly in the analyses. In the Turkish validity and reliability study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was reported as 0.87 [26]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was found to be 0.93.

Data collection method

Data was collected through an online survey administered to midwifery students enrolled at three universities located in different regions of Türkiye. The survey link was shared with eligible students via online platforms. Participation was voluntary, and students completed the questionnaire individually. The data collection instruments included the Student Information Form, the Empathic Tendency Scale, and the Xenophobia Scale. Responses were recorded electronically and transferred to the statistical software for analysis.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), median, interquartile range (IQR), and minimum–maximum values, whereas categorical variables were presented as number (n) and percentage (%). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Since continuous variables did not show a normal distribution, non-parametric tests were applied in univariable analyses. Differences between two independent groups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, while comparisons among more than two groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. When the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated a significant difference, pairwise post hoc comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction to identify the groups responsible for the difference. Associations between scale scores were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. In addition, a multivariable general linear model was used to identify factors independently associated with Xenophobia Scale scores. Variables that were significant in univariable analyses, together with the Empathic Tendency Scale score, were included in the model. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant; for post hoc pairwise comparisons, Bonferroni-adjusted significance levels were applied.

Results

Table 1 presents the distribution of the sociodemographic and educational characteristics of the midwifery students according to universities. The mean age of the participants was 20.5 ± 2.0 years (median: 20; min–max: 18–34). Overall, 49.3% of the students were in their first year of study, 27.9% were in their second year, 14.9% were in their third year, and 7.9% were in their fourth year. Most participants were Turkish citizens (85.5%) and reported that they had chosen the midwifery program willingly (80.7%). In terms of perceived income level, 80.7% reported moderate income, 10.5% poor income, and 8.8% good income. Regarding the type of settlement where they were raised, 33.8% reported metropolitan cities, 21.3% provinces, 29.6% districts, and 15.3% villages. Most students were from nuclear families (85.5%). Nearly half of the participants were living in dormitories (49.0%), while 45.4% were living at home with their families and 5.5% were living at home with friends. A total of 73.5% reported having friends from different cultures, and 57.6% stated that they were considering working in another country. Regarding communication with people from other countries via social media, 51.9% reported occasional communication, 42.3% reported never communicating, and 5.8% reported frequent communication. Previous experience of being in another country was reported by 15.2% of the students. In addition, 81.6% reported the presence of foreigners or migrants in their living environment, 27.6% had taken a transcultural midwifery course, 57.3% had provided care to a foreign or migrant individual during clinical practice, and 97.2% reported awareness of the concept of empathy.

The Xenophobia Scale scores showed statistically significant differences according to perceived income level (p = 0.029), type of settlement where students were raised (p = 0.001), family type (p = 0.001), having previously stayed in another country (p = 0.001), having taken a transcultural midwifery course (p = 0.022), and being familiar with the concept of empathy (p = 0.002). For perceived income level, Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons (adjusted significance level p < 0.0167) indicated that students who perceived their income level as poor had significantly higher xenophobia scores than those reporting moderate income (p = 0.009). Higher xenophobia scores were also observed among students from nuclear families, those who had not taken a transcultural midwifery course, and those who reported being familiar with the concept of empathy. Regarding the type of settlement where students were raised, Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons (adjusted significance level p < 0.0083) showed that students raised in metropolitan areas had significantly higher xenophobia scores than those raised in districts (p = 0.003) and villages (p = 0.001). In contrast, students who had previously lived in another country for a certain period of time had lower xenophobia scores (Table 4).

When the relationship between the Empathic Tendency Scale and the Xenophobia Scale was examined, no statistically significant correlation was found between the two scales (Spearman’s rho = 0.065, p = 0.079, n = 724).

The overall model was statistically significant, F(10, 713) = 7.126, p < 0.001, explaining 9.1% of the variance in xenophobia scores (R2 = 0.091; adjusted R2 = 0.078). After adjustment, university, perceived income level, type of settlement where students were raised, family type, and having taken a transcultural midwifery course remained significantly associated with xenophobia. Compared with students from Second University, students from Third University (B = −3.29, 95% CI: −6.24 to −0.35, p = 0.029) and First University (B = −3.58, 95% CI: −5.80 to −1.36, p = 0.002) had lower xenophobia scores. In addition, students raised in metropolitan areas (B = 6.53, 95% CI: 3.47 to 9.59, p < 0.001) and provinces (B = 4.41, 95% CI: 1.10 to 7.72, p = 0.009) had higher xenophobia scores than those raised in villages. Students from extended families had lower xenophobia scores than those from nuclear families (B = −6.21, 95% CI: −9.01 to −3.40, p < 0.001). Having taken a transcultural midwifery course was also associated with lower xenophobia scores (B = −2.28, 95% CI: −4.48 to −0.09, p = 0.042). Empathic Tendency Scale score was not independently associated with xenophobia after adjustment (B = 0.11, 95% CI: −0.02 to 0.24, p = 0.090) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study investigates the complex interplay between midwifery students’ empathic predispositions and their attitudes toward foreigners, evaluating these constructs within the framework of social determinants of health and professional midwifery ethics.

The moderate empathic tendency observed among participants suggests a foundational psychological baseline, indicating that these future midwives possess the necessary interpersonal readiness to engage with diverse and vulnerable populations [27, 28]. While this result aligns with recurring trends in Turkey, the variability noted in other national [13, 29] and international studies [15, 19, 30, 31] underscores that empathy is a dynamic construct shaped by cultural norms, educational content, and psychometric variations. Furthermore, as midwifery education in Turkey is currently limited to female students, the sample reflects a gendered professional identity that may inherently reinforce empathetic predispositions [12]. From a public health standpoint, however, these moderate scores emphasize the importance of evolving beyond individual sensitivity toward fostering structural competency and public health ethics to effectively address health inequities in migrant-sensitive care [2, 17].

In the present study, higher empathic tendency levels were observed among students who were not citizens of the Republic of Turkey, those who reported having friends from different cultures, and those who provided care to foreign individuals during clinical practice. This finding is consistent with perspectives suggesting that intercultural contact may enhance individuals’ perspective-taking and communication skills [12, 19]. However, since participants’ ethnic background was not assessed in this study, the findings cannot be interpreted in terms of ethnic identity.

International students may be more likely to encounter experiences such as exclusion, misunderstanding, or adaptation difficulties due to living in a different country, and such processes may contribute to the development of empathic awareness. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional design of the study, these interpretations do not imply causality and should be considered as possible explanations only.

The finding that students who provided care to foreign individuals during clinical practice had higher empathic tendency levels may be related to close interpersonal communication established in clinical settings, the need for more attentive communication due to cultural differences, and a heightened sense of professional responsibility. Moreover, it has been reported that empathic skills may be supported through mentoring and role-model influences in clinical practice environments [15].

In the present study, students who perceived their income level as good had higher empathic tendency scores. Although several studies have similarly reported an association between income level and empathy [29, 32], other research has indicated that this relationship is not statistically significant [13, 27, 28]. This inconsistency suggests that the processes influencing empathy cannot be explained solely by economic conditions and that multidimensional determinants should be taken into consideration.

As migration movements continue to reshape global healthcare landscapes, midwives have emerged as pivotal frontline practitioners navigating the complex linguistic and cultural nuances of women’s health [33–35]. The central distribution of xenophobia scores observed in this study suggests that midwifery students’ attitudes are situated at a complex intersection between professional ethical frameworks and pervasive societal prejudices. While these moderate levels appear less extreme than the exclusionary attitudes reported in some health-related vocational settings [36], they remain notably higher than those found in specific cohorts of midwifery students elsewhere in Türkiye [22]. Such discrepancies, including higher levels reported during the pandemic in the Black Sea region [37], underscore that xenophobic attitudes are not static but are heavily contingent upon the prevailing socio-cultural and economic climate. The findings imply that while professional socialization and foundational empathy may foster anti-discriminatory tendencies, these predispositions are often counterbalanced by dominant public discourses regarding economic burdens and altered social dynamics resulting from the Syrian civil war [33]. This tension indicates that xenophobia functions as a structural barrier to care, even when individual intent is professionally grounded [2, 9]. Consequently, these results should be interpreted as a critical necessity for developing structural competency and a robust public health ethics framework within midwifery curricula. Moving beyond basic 'cultural sensitivity' is essential to dismantle the systemic inequalities and negative social discourses that inevitably shape student perspectives on migrant health and health equity [10, 17].

Our findings demonstrated that midwifery students reporting low-income status and those raised in metropolitan areas exhibited significantly higher levels of xenophobia; these associations remained statistically significant in the multivariable general linear model. From the perspective of the social determinants of health, these results suggest that xenophobia is not merely an individual prejudice but a systemic consequence of societal inequalities and economic insecurity [2]. This is consistent with resource competition theory, which posits that individuals in host communities—especially those in precarious economic positions—may perceive refugees as rivals for access to limited resources such as employment, housing, and social services [3, 4]. For students who define their economic status as 'poor,' the perception of refugees as an economic burden [1] likely triggers xenophobic attitudes as a defense mechanism against perceived structural and economic threats.

In the present study, a linear relationship was identified between the size of the settlement where students were raised and their xenophobia levels, with those raised in metropolitan areas exhibiting the highest levels of xenophobia. This elevated xenophobia can be associated with the social threat perception generated by intense migration into large cities [34]. Challenging living conditions in urban centers—including rising costs, increased rental prices, and public concerns regarding access to social and healthcare rights—foster a climate of fear concerning the deterioration of the existing social structure [34]. Such environments sharpen the 'us' and 'them' distinction within social identity processes, reinforcing exclusionary behaviors to protect the group’s perceived security [5, 6]. Qualitative research conducted with midwifery students in the United Kingdom similarly indicates that student perceptions are shaped by negative and dominant public discourses, aligning with our current findings [34]. Ultimately, xenophobia deepens health inequalities for migrant populations by creating structural barriers to healthcare access [9]. Within this framework, midwives must be positioned as fundamental public health actors who go beyond mere 'cultural sensitivity' to actively champion health equity and dismantle the structural obstacles that hinder migrant health [10, 17].

Family type, which was identified as a sociodemographic variable with a statistically significant difference in xenophobia levels in the initial analyses, remained a significant predictor in the general linear model. Midwifery students from nuclear families were found to have higher xenophobia levels. In Turkish society, extended family structures remain relatively common (13.3%) [38]. In a study conducted with nursing students in Turkey, living in an extended family was suggested to be associated with xenophobia levels; this was explained by the greater interaction with differences within extended family environments and the development of more inclusive values [1]. However, in the study by Durat and Tarsuslu [39], family type was examined but no significant association was reported.

Although evidence regarding this variable among midwifery students remains limited, addressing the relationship between family structure and xenophobia may contribute to a better understanding of potential sociocultural determinants.

In our study, midwifery students who had previously been in a different country were found to have significantly lower xenophobia levels in the initial analyses; however, this association was not statistically significant in the general linear model. On the other hand, it is reported that contact with different cultures and overseas experience can increase intercultural sensitivity and influence individuals' perspectives [1].

Cultural differences constitute an important factor that may complicate community midwifery practices, particularly in care processes involving asylum seekers and refugees [40]. Our study found that students who did not take an intercultural midwifery course had significantly higher levels of xenophobia, and this relationship remained significant in the general linear model analysis. Similarly, higher xenophobia levels have been reported among midwives and nurses who have not received education regarding migrants’ healthcare needs [33].

However, a study conducted with midwifery students reported higher xenophobia levels among those who had received education related to transcultural midwifery and professional ethics, and it was discussed that this result might be associated with students’ lack of willingness in choosing the profession [22]. In a study carried out in the Netherlands, some midwives were reported to have limited knowledge regarding pregnancy- and childbirth-related traditions in different cultures, and it was emphasized that this may hinder care processes [40]. Moreover, it has been noted that access to and standards of maternity care for pregnant asylum seekers may be concerning,therefore, attention has been drawn to the importance of supporting midwifery students through education in order to provide culturally sensitive care [34].

Taken together, these findings suggest that strengthening transcultural content within midwifery education is an important necessity. In the universities where the research was conducted, intercultural midwifery is offered as an elective course. Considering the distribution of participants taking this course (27.6%) and its reflection on xenophobia levels, the importance of including it in the mandatory midwifery curriculum is evident.

Our study showed that students who stated they knew the concept of empathy initially had higher levels of xenophobia; however, after general linear model analysis, this relationship was found to be statistically insignificant. Because refugee and asylum-seeking women may be exposed to multifaceted risks such as malnutrition, infections, adverse pregnancy outcomes, and gender-based violence, it is critical for midwives to develop an empathetic approach [34]. Indeed, it is suggested that increased intercultural sensitivity can reduce xenophobic attitudes [37].

Although overall scale scores did not show a significant difference based on voluntary choice, a deeper analysis of university-specific distributions suggests that the motivation for entering the profession is a critical determinant of professional identity and empathic development. At the First University, where the vast majority of students chose the department willingly, empathy scores were significantly higher. This finding implies that a voluntary commitment to the midwifery profession may foster the internalization of an empathetic approach, which is essential for a robust professional identity. Indeed, the literature supports that a positive attitude toward the midwifery profession is closely linked to heightened levels of empathy [27]. Furthermore, the high prevalence of students at this university living with their families suggests that local stability and family support may reinforce this voluntary professional commitment, thereby enhancing their empathic engagement.

Conversely, the Second University, which recorded the lowest proportion of students entering the program voluntarily, exhibited significantly higher levels of xenophobia. This suggests that a lack of intrinsic motivation in professional choice may impede the development of a compassionate and inclusive professional identity, potentially making students more susceptible to exclusionary societal discourses. This observation aligns with previous research indicating that xenophobia levels are notably lower among individuals who enter the midwifery profession by choice [37]. Consequently, the initial motivations for choosing the midwifery path—whether driven by financial concerns, family preference, or a genuine love for the profession—play a pivotal role in shaping the empathic foundations and ethical attitudes of future midwives [22, 27].

In the present study, no statistically significant relationship was found between midwifery students’ empathic tendencies and xenophobia levels. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the Empathic Tendency Scale was calculated as 0.68, which is on the borderline of acceptable consistency and lower than the 0.82 reported in the original scale development study [24]. From a psychometric perspective, alpha values below 0.70 can indicate an increase in measurement error, which may lead to an underestimation of the true relationships between variables and systematically weaken correlation coefficients [41–45]. Therefore, this lower reliability coefficient should be considered a potential methodological explanation for the lack of a significant correlation in our findings. While the obtained alpha value does not completely invalidate the study’s findings, it is suggested that caution should be exercised, particularly in interpreting the relationships between empathy and other variables. In future studies, detailing item analyses and re-examining the construct validity of the scale through confirmatory factor analysis may be beneficial.

Furthermore, this result warrants a deeper analytical interpretation that goes beyond psychometric limitations, suggesting that individual empathy alone may not be a sufficient safeguard against xenophobia, which is fundamentally a structural and societal problem [2]. A student may possess high individual empathy yet still exhibit xenophobic attitudes shaped by dominant societal discourses, such as economic anxieties and social threat perceptions prevalent in metropolitan life [1, 34]. This disconnect highlights a critical necessity in midwifery education: shifting the focus from merely fostering "empathic sensitivity" toward developing "structural competency" and a robust "public health ethics" framework. Rather than relying on individual psychological predispositions, educational programs should equip students to recognize and dismantle the social determinants of health and systemic inequalities that drive xenophobia [2]. By positioning midwives as advocates for health equity, the curriculum can move beyond individual-level interventions to address the structural barriers that hinder migrant health [10, 17, 18].

In the context of increasing cultural diversity and migration, transcultural sensitivity and equity-based approaches in midwifery care have become increasingly important. Accordingly, it may be recommended to monitor empathic tendency and xenophobia levels among midwifery students, identify associated factors, and strengthen transcultural competence components within educational programs. Further studies are also needed to evaluate the relationship between empathy and xenophobia more comprehensively.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. First, due to its cross-sectional design, the findings cannot be interpreted within a causal framework. The restriction of the sample to specific universities and the inability to assess certain variables related to students’ cultural backgrounds in detail limited the extent to which differences potentially influencing empathic tendency and xenophobia levels could be fully explained. In addition, since the data were collected using self-report scales, social desirability bias may have occurred, particularly regarding sensitive topics such as xenophobia. Furthermore, because all midwifery students in the sample were female, evaluation in terms of gender differences was not possible.

The study also has notable strengths. Conducting the research across universities located in three different regions of Turkey and achieving a large sample size are among its major strengths. Moreover, the limited number of studies directly measuring xenophobia levels among midwifery students, and the lack of research addressing empathic tendency and xenophobia together, enhance the originality and contribution of this study to the field. The findings are expected to contribute to understanding factors potentially associated with empathic tendency and xenophobia among midwifery students and to support the development of the relevant literature.
