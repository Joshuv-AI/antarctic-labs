Lactation Interrupted: PFAS Impact on Capacity to Breastfeed Ignored
Authors: Katherine E Pelch, Megan E Romano, Anna Reade, Suzanne E Fenton, Amalie Timmermann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of ReviewIn this commentary, we focus on the association between exposure to per-and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) and effects on breastfeeding duration and mammary gland function. We argue that the ability to breastfeed is vulnerable to PFAS exposure and question whether recent regulatory and clinical decision-making contexts have adequately acknowledged these important public health impacts.Recent FindingsAcross geographically and sociodemographically distinct human populations, shorter duration of breastfeeding is apparent among those with the highest levels of circulating PFAS during pregnancy. Further, toxicological studies indicate that the mammary gland is one of the most sensitive tissues to disruption from PFAS exposure. However, recent analyses, such as the human health toxicity assessment supporting the national primary drinking water regulations and clinical monitoring recommendations fail to adequately incorporate these data into regulatory and clinical decisions that should also support new mothers and their infants.SummaryTo be more protective of public health, we recommend that mammary gland functional effects, including breastfeeding duration, be incorporated in future risk assessment, regulatory, and clinical decision-making contexts. Future toxicological research, including routine toxicological testing, should use contemporary measures of mammary gland development and function to more fully evaluate the impacts on mammary gland function after exposure to a wider variety of PFAS beyond perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA). In addition, clinical recommendations regarding breastfeeding should acknowledge and address the unique concerns expressed by PFAS-exposed individuals and communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40572-026-00546-6.Keywords: Breastfeeding duration, Mammary gland, Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, Risk assessment, Clinical guidance, Population health

Introduction

It is a common misconception that all new mothers can breastfeed their newborn. Breastfeeding is often viewed as a choice, and a large body of research has focused on factors related to breastfeeding motivation. However, many women report difficulty in lactation initiation and undesired early weaning due to inadequate milk supply [1–7], and little is known about causes [8].

Successful lactation depends on infant demand as well as normal mammary tissue development during puberty and pregnancy [8, 9]. As with other biological functions, there is large variation in milk production and thus breastfeeding capability. Breast development and breastfeeding is regulated by complex hormonal processes, and is thus vulnerable to endocrine disrupting chemicals (EDCs) [8]. However, the role of environmental chemicals has been given little attention in the breastfeeding literature, and proper breast development and lactation is also often overlooked as an outcome in risk assessments of environmental chemicals [10].

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), also referred to as “forever chemicals,” are an environmentally persistent family of compounds that due to their extensive industrial use, particularly in the production of consumer products, have been widely detected in the environment and general population [11–13]. When it comes to PFAS and breastfeeding, much attention is paid to how much PFAS is passed from mother to baby through breastmilk [14–19] and to how the mother’s body burden may be lowered by breastfeeding [20, 21], which are important, but are not the only issues to consider.

Across geographically and sociodemographically distinct human populations, shorter duration of breastfeeding is apparent among those with the highest levels of PFAS during pregnancy [22–28]. Toxicological studies also support that PFAS disrupt mammary gland differentiation [29–31], alter expression of placental hormones necessary for the initiation of lactation, and interfere with expression of genes encoding milk proteins [30, 32, 33]. Despite this clarity of evidence across human and animal studies, consideration of adverse effects on the mammary gland and lactation are rarely acknowledged in formal risk assessments or clinical guidance. With this paper, we focus on the association between exposure to PFAS and effects on breastfeeding, and we argue that the ability to breastfeed is vulnerable to PFAS exposure and likely also other EDCs.

State of the Science Regarding PFAS Associated Lactation Effects

The benefits of human milk for children are well described and include higher intelligence and a reduced risk of infection and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, with health benefits extending into adulthood [34–39]. Potential health benefits of lactation for the mother are often described with the “reset” hypothesis, whereby the adverse cardiometabolic changes during gestation (insulin resistance, hyperlipidemia, and visceral fat of pregnancy) are ameliorated by breastfeeding. In contrast, without breastfeeding, these metabolic changes persist [3]. Meta-analyses with over 200,000 women confirm that breastfeeding for 12 months confers protection against common adverse cardiometabolic health outcomes, including a 30% risk reduction for diabetes and a 13% risk reduction for hypertension [40]. Currently, the World Health Organization, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), and other organizations suggest breastfeeding for at least two years or longer [41]. However, only about 40% of infants in the United States continue to be fed human milk through their first year of life [42]. This relatively low rate is often attributed to various barriers, including sociocultural, medical, nutritional, and infant-related issues, such as difficulties with milk supply and latching, as well as challenges balancing work and family responsibilities [43]. However, exposure to PFAS and other endocrine active substances is likely to contribute to low rates of breastfeeding as well.

The association between exposure to multiple different PFAS and subsequent breastfeeding duration have been examined in nine epidemiological studies to date (Supplemental Table 1). In a 2023 systematic review including six studies, five studies showed shorter total duration of breastfeeding with higher PFAS exposure [44]. Notably the associations were found across countries and different study designs [44]. The review included two studies from Denmark [22, 28], one from the United States [25], one from the Faroe Islands [27], one from Norway [45], and one from Sweden [23]. The Norwegian study did not find an association between PFAS exposure and reduced breastfeeding duration, while the other studies did. Associations were most consistent for perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), and perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA) [44].

Since the systematic review was conducted, three additional studies on PFAS and breastfeeding duration have been published [24, 26, 46]. All three studies were conducted among women in the United States. One study found no association between serum PFAS concentration and breastfeeding cessation by 3 and 6 months among primiparous women [46], while in the two other studies, higher serum PFAS concentrations were associated with terminating any breastfeeding in the first nine months [24] and terminating exclusive breastfeeding before 6 months [26]. The latter study, however, did not observe associations between PFAS exposure and stopping any breastfeeding before 12 months [26].

Some have suggested that the association between PFAS exposure and breastfeeding duration is due to confounding, since women who have given birth before and previously breastfed (for a longer period) will have lowered their serum PFAS concentration and be more likely to breastfeed (for a longer period) again [22]. However, several studies found associations between PFAS exposure and breastfeeding duration while taking breastfeeding history and/or parity into account [24, 26, 44, 46], thus minimizing the risk of confounding from prior breastfeeding. The associations also cannot be explained by women with higher exposures potentially choosing to terminate breastfeeding early in order to protect their children from PFAS in breastmilk, as the women in the studies were unaware of their exposure levels while breastfeeding.

Taken together, the majority of studies have shown associations between PFAS exposure and shorter subsequent breastfeeding duration, and the consistency of results across studies with different bias structures reduces the likelihood that the observed associations are caused by confounding or bias [44]. For example, the Swedish study relied on home addresses as a proxy for PFAS exposure in an area where some home addresses had heavily PFAS-contaminated drinking water [23], while all other studies measured PFAS in maternal serum [44]. Using a proxy for the exposure changes the study bias structure, as confounding from previous breastfeeding could not affect the home address data in the Swedish study. Instead, using address data as a proxy of exposure likely increases imprecision, and thus the risk of bias towards null.

Only two studies have examined associations between PFAS exposure and breastfeeding initiation, with one finding no association [45] and one finding increased risk of not initiating breastfeeding with higher PFAS [23]. There is thus a need for more studies examining how PFAS (and other endocrine disrupting chemicals) affect breastfeeding initiation.

In general, the mechanisms by which PFAS can affect breastfeeding, are still not fully understood, though there is some epidemiological evidence to suggest that PFAS disrupt the metabolic processes regulating the content and quality of milk production [47] and animal evidence shows that PFAS disrupts mammary gland development (reviewed in [10]). PFAS are known endocrine disruptors [48], and breast development and breastfeeding is dependent on a balance between hormones - including estradiol, progesterone, and prolactin [9]. One epidemiological study examined the association between maternal pregnancy PFAS and prolactin concentrations, but did not find clear associations [28].

Given the complexity of mammary gland development and lactation initiation and maintenance, it is helpful to look to animal toxicological studies to provide further data on the potential mechanisms by which PFAS disrupt lactation. Controlled laboratory studies of rodents exposed during gestational development [29, 30, 49, 50] or during puberty [31] support the epidemiological findings. Lactation dysfunction as a result of PFOA exposure was first reported in mice in 2007 [30]. This initial study evaluated mammary gland development at the time of peak lactation and at the time when mouse pups are typically weaned. The pregnant mothers had been exposed to 5 mg/kg/day PFOA for three lengths of time: throughout gestation, the second half of gestation, or the last third of gestation. The authors found that mothers that were exposed to PFOA for any length of time had underdeveloped and delayed mammary glands, though the effect was stronger in mothers that had been exposed for longer. The data suggested a substantial delay of possibly up to 10 days in development of the mammary gland during pregnancy [30].

Importantly, the study authors also observed that pups born to exposed mothers were smaller than pups born to untreated mothers, with the effect on body weight most pronounced five days after birth. To begin to understand if the effects on offspring body weight were due to changes in the mother’s ability to lactate or in changes to the offsprings’ ability to suckle, the authors also evaluated mammary glands from animals on gestation day 18, just before birth. As can be seen in Fig. 1, mammary glands from control animals were saturated with milk-filled alveoli, while those from PFOA exposed animals were not. Taken together, these data suggested that PFOA has a functional effect on the mother’s ability to lactate [30]. These findings were replicated in [49] in which the mammary glands were evaluated one day later, on the day of birth.

Moreover, the authors found that PFOA also stunted mammary gland development in the developmentally exposed offspring, suggesting multi-generational effects [30]. Cross-fostering experiments demonstrated that any PFOA exposure during early life (only gestational or only lactational) can impair mammary gland development [49]. Delays in mammary gland development have been noted in multiple mouse species and begin as early as day 21 and persist into adulthood, at least to day 63, and perhaps as long as 18 months [29, 31, 49]. Follow-up studies have determined that the mammary gland may be one of the most sensitive tissues to PFOA disruption as delays in mammary gland development occurred from gestational exposure to doses as low as 0.01 mg/kg/day, lower than doses at which liver impacts were observed [51] (Fig. 2). Mammary gland sensitivity is not unique to PFAS as scientists have observed changes in mammary gland development after exposure to chemicals of concern including bisphenol A, dichloro-diphenyl-trichloroethane (DDT), or genistein at doses lower than other reproductive effects [52]. As far as we are aware, there have been no comparisons of the sensitivity of the lactation effect in humans with other PFAS associated health effects. However, the presence of this effect in multiple epidemiological studies indicates that this effect is occurring at human relevant exposure levels.

The developing mammary gland is one of the most sensitive tissues to PFOA disruption. Comparison of the external PFOA dose at which mammary gland developmental changes have been observed [51] to the external doses for the health effects that were considered for calculating candidate reference doses in the U.S. EPA’s 2024 Human Health Toxicity Assessment for Perfluorooctanoic Acid (PFOA) and Related Salts [53]: IgM response [54], delayed eye opening [55], decreased pup survival at postnatal day 21 [56], liver focal necrosis and individual cell necrosis [57]

In 2011 White et al., again treated two generations (F0 and F1) of mice with PFOA [50]. Despite noting morphological delays in the mammary glands of mice gestationally exposed to PFOA, the offspring (the F2 generation) did not have reduced body weights. To examine if there were functional impacts on lactation, the authors conducted a lactational challenge. In this study, F2 litters were removed from the F1 mothers for three hours and then returned and allowed to nurse for 30 min. The amount of weight the litter gained in this time was recorded as a measure of milk consumption and the time it took to initiate nursing was also recorded. The authors noted that litters from PFOA exposed mothers only gained one third of the weight of control litters during the challenge and were on average 84 s slower to begin suckling, though there was high variability and thus these impacts were not statistically significant [50]. The overall lack of changes in F2 body weight and the inconclusive findings from the lactational challenge led the authors to conclude that there was not clear evidence that PFOA exposure diminished nutritional support. However, the authors also noted there could have been other compensatory behaviors that could have influenced pup body weight that were not evaluated in this study, including an increased number of nursing events per day or longer nursing per event. These behaviors should be reexamined and systematically evaluated with contemporary neurobehavioral systems that are able to better quantify these behaviors over the life course of an animal.

Though most of the toxicological research to-date has evaluated the impact of PFOA exposure on mammary gland development, recent studies on the PFOA replacement, hexafluoropropylene oxide-dimer acid (HFPO-DA, also known as GenX), have shown similar outcomes, with reduced pup weight observed at postnatal day 5 [58] and from postnatal days 1–21 following prenatal exposure [59]. However, the impact of GenX on the differentiation of the mammary gland in lactating dams has not yet been reported. Given the growing body of human epidemiological evidence demonstrating that exposure to several PFAS is associated with decreased lactation duration, it is important for future toxicological research to evaluate the impact on the development and function of the mammary gland for more than just PFOA.

Lack of Testing Requirements for Mammary Gland Effects Hinders Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment

Overall, there is very little evaluation of mammary gland development or function required for new chemicals in the U.S. or Europe. Kay et al., (2022) and Makris (2011) have thoroughly reviewed and described the overall lack of testing requirements for chemicals in guidance documents developed by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) or the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) [10, 60]. Here we briefly summarize the main findings of these reviews, which are relevant to environmental chemicals in general.

Strikingly, none of the U.S. EPA Series 870 Health Effects Test Guidelines, which are intended to meet testing requirements for human health impacts of pesticides (regulated under Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act (FIFRA)) and chemicals (regulated under the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA)), mention the word “mammary,” indicating this tissue is not at all required to be evaluated [61]. The harmonized guidelines from ICH for the detection of reproductive and developmental toxicity of human pharmaceuticals, outlined in S5(R3) also do not include evaluation of mammary glands [62].

In contrast, some guideline studies from OECD do suggest that mammary gland tissue ought to be collected, but the guidelines do not require that they be collected from all dose groups and the specific endpoints that should be evaluated are not indicated. In 2011, there was a request by concerned international scientists to require mammary gland evaluation to the OECD’s Extended One-Generation Reproductive Toxicity Study (EOGRTS; Test No. 443), but the U.S. EPA did not support the effort, so the request languished. Today, the OECD’s EOGRTS, indicates that mammary glands should be collected from male and female animals of the parental and offspring generations, but provides no guidance on which specific mammary glands should be collected, how they should be preserved for histopathological or whole mount analysis, or the types of analyses that should be conducted [63]. Of note, the impacts on lactation described above are best visualized and detected by examining mammary gland whole mounts for lobuloalveolar development and involution or by using longitudinal histological sections, impacts that are not easily identified from the transverse cross-sections that contain the skin and are typically prepared [10]. An additional issue with OECD’s EOGRTS is that it suggests that mammary glands be collected post-weaning, a time point that is not informative for understanding functional impacts of chemical exposures on lactation [63].

In the absence of direct testing of mammary gland development or function, guideline studies do have recommendations to monitor offspring health, which could provide some indication of lactational defects. For example, offspring survival, weight, development, and the presence of a “milk spot” or “milk band” in neonates can indicate if nursing is occurring [60]. Additionally, noticeable dehydration or morbidity might indicate a problem with nursing. If pup weight and development are to be used as surrogate or indirect markers of lactation, Kay et al. (2022) recommended additional collection of these endpoints beyond just the day of birth and the fourth day of life when they are typically recorded [10]. However, OECD’s EOGRTS does not require daily weight recording nor other observations that could indirectly inform whether nursing is occurring. Importantly, these indirect measures are incapable of eliciting whether there is a deficit in the mother’s ability to support lactation or if there is a toxic effect in the offspring that is preventing efficient nursing [10].

Omission of Mammary Gland Effects Undermines Risk Management Decisions

Despite the lack of testing requirements there is still a growing body of evidence demonstrating the mammary gland is very sensitive to PFAS exposure. A 2009 workshop of experts in mammary gland biology and risk assessment came to the consensus that changes in mammary gland growth and differentiation, including changes in developmental timing, are a relevant human health concern. Altered mammary gland development may lead to difficulty in breastfeeding and/or an increase in susceptibility to breast cancer later in life. A synthesis of the workshop was later published in 2011, with workshop participants concluding that “assessment of mammary gland development should be incorporated in chemical test guidelines and risk assessment.” [52]. However, there has historically been minimal attention paid to effects on mammary gland development or function in risk management analysis and decisions (select documents discussed below are listed in Fig. 3), including for PFAS where the human epidemiological findings of harm support findings from experimental animal studies.

Recently, the U.S. EPA failed to adequately review and consider shortened lactation duration in setting its Maximum Contaminant Level Goal (MCLG) for PFOA in Drinking Water. In its toxicity analysis supporting the regulation, the EPA reviewed the animal evidence for impacts on mammary gland development and function but did not evaluate the corresponding epidemiological evidence despite citing two of six human studies that were available at the time [64]. In comments submitted to the U.S EPA, it was suggested that the agency should investigate shortened duration of breastfeeding as an important human health endpoint, associated with significant financial and social costs [65]. However, this oversight was not remedied in the finalization of the document [53, 66].

Similarly, recent draft toxicity assessments of PFDA and PFHxS by the EPA’s Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS) initially failed to include a discussion of lactation duration as a health endpoint, again despite the availability of both human and animal evidence [67, 68]. After public commenters and peer reviewers raised concerns, this endpoint was ultimately incorporated in the final analysis of PFDA and PFHxS and in the draft PFNA assessment [69–74].

Perhaps the failure to adequately consider mammary gland and lactational effects in the above toxicity assessments is, in part, a result of mammary gland impacts being improperly diminished in earlier documents, such as the 2016 U.S. EPA Drinking Water Health Advisory for Perfluorooctanoic Acid (PFOA) [75]. In this document the U.S. EPA states “Data from White et al. (2011) showed no significant effects on body weight gain in pups nursing from dams treated with 1 mg/kg/day, despite these dams having less fully developed mammary glands compared to controls. Similarly, no differences in response to a lactational challenge were seen in PFOA exposed dams with morphologically delayed mammary gland development (White et al. 2011).” When discussing the same data, the United States Centers for Disease Control (CDC), Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) stated in 2021 “… the mammary gland effect did not result in an adverse effect on lactational support… Given that milk production was adequate to support growth, the biological significance of the delayed development of the mammary gland observed at very low doses is uncertain and was not considered a suitable basis for the MRL.” [76] Discussion of this data by the European Food Safety Authority in 2018 was similar [77]. Over the years these agencies continued to repeat the same conclusions, despite the fact that the authors’ own conclusions did not support a dismissal of the impacts. All of this, despite the fact that in 2014 existing nonhuman evidence was systematically curated and a meta-analysis demonstrated that for each 1-unit increase in dose of PFOA (mg/kg-d), there was an associated decrease in mean pup weight of 0.023 g [78].

That is, these summaries rely on indirect measurements and simplify the complex behaviors that are observed during lactation between a mother (dam) and offspring (pup). The lactation challenge in White et al. 2011 only evaluated the amount of milk passed from the dam to the pups in a single nursing event; it did not account for compensatory behaviors that may have been present in the pups [50]. The study authors added this additional context, stating “[t]hese data suggest that nursing behavior of the neonates may have changed (i.e., increased number of nursing events per day or longer nursing per event) to compensate for the decreased potential in milk production by the F1 dam, but we did not evaluate these end points in this study.” [50]. Additionally, by this point there was a growing body of epidemiological evidence indicating lactational effects due to PFOA exposure. Thus, the conclusions reached by the EPA, ATSDR and EFSA on a lack of biological significance are not well supported by the existing evidence.

In the United States, some states have acknowledged the increased risk of harm from mammary gland effects through the application of uncertainty factors in their risk assessments for PFOA. The Michigan Science Advisory Workgroup (MI SAW; 2019) stated that mammary gland effects may not be considered adverse, but that they could be representative of endocrine (hormone) effects at doses below the selected point of departure. The MI SAW therefore applied an additional uncertainty factor for database limitations regarding endocrine effects [79]. The New Jersey Drinking Water Quality Institute (NJ DWQI; 2017) stated that the mammary gland effect is “the most sensitive systemic endpoint for PFOA with data appropriate for dose-response modeling. It is a well-established toxicological effect of PFOA that is considered to be adverse and relevant to humans for the purposes of risk assessment.” [80]. However, the NJ DWQI did not select it as the critical effect because altered mammary gland development had yet to be used as the basis for risk assessment. Instead, the NJ DWQI applied an additional uncertainty factor to protect for this more sensitive effect.

We argue that there is a historical trend in risk assessment of ignoring impacts on mammary gland development and function. As detailed in this section, it is not simply one problem that has contributed to a dismissal of mammary gland and lactational impacts - in some cases, regulatory agencies left out important publications, in other cases they misinterpreted the findings or conducted analyses based only on older studies. This is unfortunate given that it is well known that many chemicals cause changes in mammary gland development and function [81, 82]. Hopefully, the inclusion of mammary gland and lactation effects in the most recent PFAS health and toxicity assessments conducted by the U.S. EPA’s IRIS signals a change in approach. However, generation of data on which these decisions are made will likely be hindered by the U.S. National Institutes of Health’s new guidance to reduce the use of animals in research [83].

One Health and Public Health Implications

In 2021 the NASEM formed an ad hoc committee that released guidance for clinicians on PFAS testing and health outcomes [84]. This committee was tasked with making “recommendations to the CDC on who, when, how, and what to test, as well as the risks of testing.” The committee relied primarily on existing authoritative reviews and assessed the strength of evidence for health outcomes associated with PFAS exposure. New epidemiological studies were only incorporated for endpoints that had been previously reviewed by authoritative bodies. With regard to female reproductive-related outcomes, the committee determined there was “insufficient human evidence of an association of PFAS with female reproductive effects, including breastfeeding duration.” [84]. To reach this conclusion the NASEM committee reviewed the authoritative reviews and epidemiological evidence available at this time, including the study by Rosen et al., (2018) and the ATSDR’s 2021 Toxicological Profile for PFAS (which referenced Fei et al., 2010, Romano et al., 2016, and Timmermann et al., 2017, and which, as indicated above, failed to adequately consider mammary gland and lactational effects). Unfortunately, because the NASEM committee primarily focused on epidemiological research to draw conclusions, it does not appear that the animal toxicological data demonstrating PFAS changes to mammary gland development were considered when this conclusion was reached. The NASEM guidance for clinicians does, however, discuss the transfer of PFAS into breastmilk, how this affects a mother’s PFAS body burden, and potential impacts on children’s exposure and health. It is plausible that if NASEM’s conclusions on lactational duration effects had been different, they may have been able to provide clinical advice to better support mothers. For example, if it is known or suspected that a mother had elevated PFAS exposures, the clinician might encourage the mother to seek additional lactational support services early.

A lack of ability to breastfeed or maintain breastfeeding duration has important social and economic costs. Breastfeeding, in its simplest form, is at no-(financial) cost to the mother and family and can be practiced on demand as needed. In contrast, the alternative to breastfeeding is to prepare infant formula and milk, which is often sold as a powder and reconstituted in water. While this is a wonderful resource to have available, the costs associated with providing infant formula and milk are substantial. A 2017 report evaluated 31 breast milk substitutes in the U.S. from the world’s four largest manufacturers and found that cost per infant at that time ran between $148-$264 per month [85]. In 2025 the NASEM reported that suboptimal breastfeeding in the U.S. currently costs $17.2 billion to over $100 billion annually [86]. Lower end estimates focus primarily on childhood and maternal infections and mortality due to a lack of breastfeeding, whereas higher end estimates also take into consideration costs due to cognitive losses [84, 87–89]. Additionally, there is no guarantee that formula, the water it is reconstituted with, or other breast milk replacements such as cow’s milk are free of PFAS. The presence of PFAS has been reported in some infant formulas, in bottled water that may be used to reconstitute formula, and in dairy milk, none of which are routinely monitored for PFAS [90–96].

Just as mammary gland effects are often omitted from toxicology assessments, effects of PFAS and other endocrine disrupting chemicals are seldom considered in reports on breastfeeding. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM) recently released the report “Breastfeeding in the United States: Strategies to Support Families and Achieve National Goals” without mentioning that PFAS might impair breastfeeding nor that PFAS and other persistent chemicals are transferred with breastmilk [86]. To ensure progress in public health, we need to bridge knowledge across different public health and scientific areas.

The presence of PFAS in human milk complicates decisions about feeding modalities for new parents [97–100]. Existing research indicates that breastfeeding is associated with higher levels of serum PFAS in infants than formula feeding, yet there are currently no established minimum risk levels (MRLs) for infants or tolerable PFAS intake values for human milk [101–106]. Of note however, estimated concentrations of some PFAS (PFNA, PFOA, and PFOS) in breastmilk do exceed drinking water screening values for children derived from ATSDR’s health based guidance values [97], as well as the more recent U.S. EPA’s national drinking water standards for PFAS. In 2020, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) determined a tolerable weekly intake (TWI) for mothers for the sum of PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS, and PFNA of 4.4 ng/kg bodyweight [107, 108]. The TWI is based on the maternal serum concentrations that are unlikely to result in adverse effects in 1 year-old breastfed children; however, this is not easily extrapolated for purposes of understanding PFAS concentration present in human milk. This lack of guidance values augments decisional conflict for mothers living in PFAS-contaminated communities considering breastfeeding and worried about possible long term health implications for their children [97]. The CDC maintains that benefits of breastfeeding outweigh the risk of PFAS exposure through breastmilk and recommends continued breastfeeding also in highly exposed populations [109]. Given the many benefits of breastfeeding, this is likely the right recommendation, but to our knowledge there is currently no research that directly weighs the benefits of breastfeeding against the harms of transferring PFAS to the infant.

There are other societal costs beyond those to human health. For example, in town halls held to support the development of the NASEM clinical guidance community members raised concerns related to agricultural impacts [84]. A fourth-generation dairy farmer described the health effects he observed over time in his affected cattle, including decreased lactation. Another individual shared data from a regional dairy affected by high levels of PFAS in its groundwater that showed a decrease in lactation in dairy cows over time [84]. It is logical to assume that other mammals’ ability to lactate could be affected by exposure to PFAS, however, the agricultural and ecological harms of PFAS contamination are not well studied.

Recommendations

This commentary highlights the importance of protecting against mammary gland effects in chemical management decisions. Proper mammary gland development is important to lactational function and breast health. Yet, impacts on mammary gland development and function have been largely ignored in various decision-making contexts. This commentary described several instances in which effects on mammary gland development or function were not fully considered or acted on. This is despite evidence across geographically distinct and socioeconomically diverse populations of adverse effects of PFAS on breastfeeding duration in human populations that is further supported by toxicological evidence.

The impact on the development and function of the mammary gland is largely unknown for PFAS beyond PFOA. Given the toxicological similarity among PFAS, these effects are likely associated with other PFAS and should be investigated in future studies. For example, mammary gland development and functional endpoints could be incorporated into guideline study requirements, including all two-generation study designs. Further, potential compensatory nursing behaviors should be examined in toxicological studies using more contemporary neurobehavioral systems that are able to better quantify these behaviors over the life course of an animal.

Information about breastfeeding is often available in birth cohort studies, and breastfeeding is included as a covariate in many epidemiological studies on infant health. However, when evaluating the effects of maternal chemical exposure on infant health, the effects of the chemicals on breastfeeding are seldom assessed. As many studies have available information on both maternal pregnancy chemical exposures and breastfeeding duration, there is a large unused data potential. We suggest that existing data from cohort studies should be used in future research assessing the effects of PFAS and other EDCs on breastfeeding. Further, there is an important missed opportunity to integrate exposure information about PFAS and other EDCs into lactation consultation and clinical guidance. For example, despite the growing scientific evidence, EDCs were not considered in a recent consensus statement from NASEM addressing promotion and support of lactation in the United States [110].

Moving forward, it is critical to acknowledge that mammary gland changes are important health effects for risk assessment. When data on mammary gland and/or lactational effects are available, they should be included in risk management decisions. When data is lacking, the application of a database uncertainty factor should be employed, given the shared toxicological profile of many PFAS.

Likewise, it is important that effects of PFAS and other environmental chemicals are considered when providing best practice advice for clinicians, health care systems, and new mothers regarding breastfeeding. It is a huge oversight that NASEM and ATSDR did not develop concrete guidance for pregnant and lactating women. Looking forward, NASEM should update its Guidance on PFAS Exposure, Testing, and Clinical Follow-Up and its more recent document, Breastfeeding in the United States: Strategies to Support Families and Achieve National Goals, to better support pregnant and lactating women who are concerned about their PFAS exposure [84, 86]. It would be helpful to include experts in this area on the panel that could ensure all relevant evidence is considered and guide the recommendations and strategies for improved clinician education, including for lactation specialists. Related guidance of interest to communities includes ways to reduce PFAS exposures prior to and during pregnancy.
