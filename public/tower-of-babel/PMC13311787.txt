Not all invisible wounds cause moral injury
Authors: Britta S von Ungern-Sternberg, Aine Sommerfield, Karin Becke-Jakob
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMoral injury, and stakes-dependent invisibility commonly arise from system issues and can lead to profound emotional distress, including feelings of shame and betrayal, but both are often disregarded by institutions. Stakes-dependent invisibility highlights the lack of power, the lack of perceived recognition of one’s contribution, and the lack of a voice, resulting in the failure to meet the individual’s needs. The mechanism of harm behind moral injury, however, is distinctively different: moral injury goes much deeper, focusing on the internal psychological and ethical damage caused by powerlessness or by actions taken or experienced, which are in direct conflict with the person’s own moral compass, violating their own conscience.Keywords: academic medicine, burnout, clinical ethics, clinical research, ethics, invisibility, moral injury

We would like to thank A/Prof Yanaral for their interest in our article on moral injury, a psychological state, which, in line with the definition of the British Medical Association, “arises where sustained moral distress leads to impaired function or longer-term psychological harm”, with moral distress referring to “the psychological unease generated where professionals identify an ethically correct action to take but are constrained in their ability to take that action”.1, 2, 3 While we are not aware of a formal definition in the literature, “stakes-dependent invisibility”, introduced in the response letter,1 seems to be a self-explanatory term. We agree, from our personal understanding of the term, that stakes-dependent invisibility is indeed a common problem in perioperative medicine; the anaesthetist often receives less recognition compared with their surgical colleagues. This is in direct contrast to their central role, often serving as the link that integrates the various perioperative patient and logistical needs.4

Being invisible does not automatically equal being of low importance; it also depends on whether one feels visible to one’s clinical colleagues but invisible to patients, or whether one feels invisible to both clinical colleagues and patients. Being invisible to patients, with good recognition from the rest of the team, might be enough for some. For other people, being invisible to patients might make them feel overall unrecognised and consequently dissatisfied.

We agree that moral injury and stakes-dependent invisibility share some key aspects: they both often cause invisible wounds, which are defined as injuries or conditions that are not physically apparent to others; while they cannot be seen on the body surface, they do cause real and significant suffering.5 Both are often disregarded by institutions. Both concepts commonly arise from system issues, such as resource shortages, rather than solely from individual failures. Both stakes-dependent invisibility and moral injury can lead to profound emotional distress, including feelings of shame and betrayal.

However, there are some important key differences. Stakes-dependent invisibility highlights the lack of power, the lack of perceived recognition of one’s contribution, and the lack of a voice (e.g. the greater recognition from patients towards the surgeon compared with the anaesthetist). However, moral injury focuses on the internal psychological and ethical damage caused by powerlessness or by the actions taken or experienced, which are in direct conflict with the person’s own moral compass (e.g. restrictions on diagnosis or treatment options due to the level of insurance coverage a patient may have). The mechanisms of harm are distinctively different in our view. Stakes-dependent invisibility may cause harm because the individual's needs are not met, leading to a perceived lack of recognition and unfair treatment. Moral injury goes much deeper; it causes harm because the individual is forced to act in ways or to witness actions that directly violate their own conscience.

We would therefore argue that while stakes-dependent invisibility is not a direct cause of moral injury, it can heavily impact job satisfaction, which can play a role in staff retention and burnout. We do agree that changes should be introduced to lessen stakes-dependent invisibility; however, we suggest not only focusing on the debrief but also starting even earlier, before the procedure. During the preoperative assessment, it is important that the anaesthetist explains their management plan and their role in detail to the patient as part of the shared decision-making and informed consent pathway with every patient and, in the case of minors, their parents or carers. Clearly demonstrated mutual respect and support of each other’s roles between anaesthetists and surgeons (and ideally between all team members) in front of the patient and their family goes a long way in defining the culture towards our patients—the team or the ego approach.

It is imperative that we work together with all stakeholders involved in the perioperative pathway, including clinical and non-clinical staff of all specialities, patients and their families.6 We should all learn more about each other’s work (e.g. during medical school and junior doctor training) to better understand each other’s role, place and importance in every team. This will increase not only our understanding but likely also the value we place into each other’s work. Furthermore, it will aid our learning journey to work together as one cohesive team—if we put our egos aside, the “team” will be visible and not just single players. In medicine, the only person of singular importance is the patient; our care has to centre around our patients. Care should be given by the team, not singular egos. This will lead to more respect for every person involved, making work more enjoyable for all. Collaborations and communications will be more constructive, and the team will strive together towards new heights. Good leadership is required to aid this change.7 As a team, we should celebrate our highs and support each other during our lows. Recognition as a team inspires everyone and creates a pathway to true connections and best clinical practice.
