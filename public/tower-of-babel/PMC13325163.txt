Adsorption Behavior and Storage Capacity Prediction of CO2 Injection into High-Rank Coal
Authors: Jinxiao Yang, Chen Guo, Lingling Lu, Yi Dong, Junzhe Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CO2 geological sequestration (CCUS) serves as a critical technological pathway to achieve the strategic goal of the “dual-carbon” initiative. Coal seams characterized by dual porosity represent a favorable geological structure for storing carbon dioxide. The adsorption characteristics and storage capacity of CO2 in the coal seam are the core indexes to evaluate the feasibility of CO2 injection and storage. Anthracite samples from the south of the Qinshui basin were selected for multi-physical field-coupled CO2 isothermal adsorption experiments. This study investigates the adsorption behavior of high-rank coal samples toward CO2, compares the applicability of various theoretical adsorption models, selects the optimal one to build a storage capacity prediction model, and evaluates the CO2 storage potential of high-rank coal seams. The results indicate that pressure has a positive effect on the adsorption of CO2 (before reaching the supercritical state), while temperature inhibits the adsorption of CO2 by coal samples. With the increase in water saturation, the adsorption capacity of coal samples for CO2 gradually decreases. The theoretical model of micropore filling (the D–A model) has good applicability for predicting the adsorption of CO2 in coal samples. On the basis of experimental results, estimation models for CO2 content under different occurrence states and a predictive model for total storage capacity were established. The content of each CO2 occurrence state is influenced by factors such as formation temperature, pressure, and water saturation. CO2 is mainly stored in coal reservoirs in the form of adsorption. With increasing burial depth, the proportion of free-state CO2 shows an increasing trend, while the content of dissolved-state CO2 exhibits a decreasing trend. The synergistic evolution of three states determines the vertical zoning of the CO2 storage capacity. These findings advance the understanding of storage mechanisms following CO2 injection into high-rank coal reservoirs and provide a quantitative method for assessing the feasibility and storage potential of such injections.

Introduction

CO2 geological sequestration and its resource utilization, serving as a critical pathway for carbon neutrality strategies and an inevitable trend and choice for humanity’s green development, have become a frontier research direction in the energy and environmental fields. − Coal seams are porous media characterized by dual-porosity features and exhibit a strong adsorption capacity for CO2. Their primary micropores (pore size< 2 nm) and secondary fracture networks (macroscopic fractures >50 μm) constitute heterogeneous storage spaces and migration pathways for CO2, making coal seams favorable geological formations for CO2 sequestration. , Against the backdrop of the national “dual-carbon” goals, injecting CO2 into coal seams to enhance coalbed methane recovery (CO2-ECBM) and for geological sequestration (CCUS) has become a significant development direction. China possesses vast geological sequestration potential, estimated at approximately (2.42–4.13) × 10^12t of CO2.

Since Fulton proposed the concept of injecting CO2 into coal seams, researchers both domestically and internationally have achieved substantial results regarding the feasibility, economy, long-term stability, and safety of injecting CO2. The feasibility of CO2 injection is closely linked to the adsorption and migration processes of CO2 within the coal seams. The pores and fractures inside the coal provide storage space for CO2. The adsorption of CO2 is affected not only by the development of pores/fractures and the metamorphic grade of the coal , but also by factors such as water content and reservoir conditions. Furthermore, coal seams suitable for CO2 injection are generally located in deep formations, characterized by high geo-temperature and high in situ stress. , Concurrently, due to the influence of temperature and effective stress, the pore volume within the coal decreases, which leads to decreased permeability, thus affecting the migration of CO2 in coal seams.

Based on the similar storage mechanisms of CO2 and CH4, various scholars have established models to calculate the storage capacity of CO2 in injected coal seams. , Injected CO2 is stored in coal seams in three forms: adsorbed state (adsorbed on the surface of the coal matrix), free state (occupying the pore space within the coal), and dissolved state (dissolved in coal seam water). Adsorbed CO2 constitutes the highest proportion, and its content is typically estimated by adsorption theoretical models. Satya Harpalani compared the accuracy of various adsorption models in fitting CO2 adsorption data for coal samples and found that the D–A model provided the best fitting. Second, free-state CO2 is stored within the pore channels of the coal, and theoretical models of its content is usually based on the gas-state equation and Mariotte’s law. , Additionally, a part of CO2 is dissolved in water and stored in coal seam in a dissolved state. The content of dissolved CO2 is determined according to the reservoir pressure, temperature, and in situ water saturation. , However, due to the relatively small total pore volume and low in situ water saturation of coal, the contents of free-state and dissolved-state CO2 are comparatively minor. , Nevertheless, they still contribute to the overall CO2 storage capacity of the coal reservoir. Therefore, the contents of free-state and dissolved-state CO2 should not be overlooked.

To sum up, significant progress has been made in the feasibility study of injecting CO2 into coal seams. However, the following issues remain to be addressed: regarding isothermal adsorption experiments, studies often focus on the influence of a single factor, such as temperature or injection pressure, and the CO2 adsorption characteristics of coal. The research on the adsorption process of CO2 under the conditions of (water-bearing) coal is relatively limited, and the coupling effects of multiple physical fields including temperature, pressure, and water saturation are lacking. Furthermore, the applicability of the theoretical adsorption models requires further verification. Therefore, it is necessary to carry out isothermal adsorption experiments of CO2 at different temperatures, pressures, and water saturations to analyze the CO2 adsorption characteristics of coal samples under reservoir conditions. Under the controlling effects of temperature, pressure, and water saturation, the optimal adsorption model of coal–CO2 interaction should be selected. It is necessary to determine the key parameters of the prediction model for estimating the adsorbed, free, and dissolved CO2 contents in order to estimate the CO2 storage in coal seams. This will deepen the understanding of the mechanisms of CO2 injection into coal seams and provide theoretical support for evaluating the feasibility of the injection of CO2 into coal seams.

Coal Sample Preparation and Experimental Methodology

Coal Sample Preparation

The coal samples were collected from the Sihe Coal Mine (sample ID: SH) in the southern Qinshui Basin. It originates from the No.3 coal seam of the Permian Shanxi Formation (P1s), which is characterized by stable distribution. The coal is divided into bright coal and semi-bright coal (Figure ), exhibiting a banded structure and a layered structure. Locally, thin lamellae of fusain with linear fabric are observed. The coal contains a large number of endogenous fractures and has no significant structural deformation, which belongs to primary structured coal.

SH coal sample.

Experimental Methodology

Basic Physicochemical Property Tests

Powdered samples with three grain size fractions (20–60 mesh, 60–80 mesh, and >200 mesh) were prepared. Subsequently, maceral composition/maximum vitrinite reflectance measurements, proximate analysis, and low-temperature nitrogen adsorption experiments were conducted.

Multi-Physical Field-Coupled CO2 Adsorption Experiments

The multi-physical field-coupled CO2 adsorption experiments were conducted using an ultrasound-stimulated coal isothermal adsorption apparatus (Figure ). Multi-physical field-coupled isothermal adsorption experiments were performed on coal samples with varying water saturations (0%, 25%, 50%, and 75%), temperatures (20 °C, 30 °C, 40 °C, and 50 °C), and pressures (0–10 MPa). The adsorbate used was high-purity CO2 (99.99%), with equilibrium pressure points set at 1 MPa intervals.

Data Processing

Calculation of CO2 Adsorption Capacity

The CO2 adsorption capacity at a given pressure was calculated using the volumetric method: 1 pV=nZRT where p is the gas pressure (MPa); V is the gas volume (cm3); n is the amount of gas substance (mol); Z is the gas compressibility factor; R is the molar gas constant (J·mol–1·K–1), with a value of 8.314 J·mol–1·K–1; and T is the temperature (K).

The adsorption quantity derived from the above equation is the excess adsorption amount, which does not reflect the true CO2 adsorption capacity of the coal sample. Therefore, the excessive adsorption requires to be converted. Assuming the coal volume remains constant during the adsorption process: 2 Vex=Vab(1−ρgρa) where V ex is the excess adsorption capacity (cm3/g); V ab is the absolute adsorption capacity (cm3/g); ρ g is the density of the free-phase CO2 (g/cm3); and ρ a is the density of the adsorbed-phase CO2 (g/cm3). Based on previous research, ρ a is taken as 1.18 g/cm3.

Adsorption Models

This study employed the Langmuir, D–R, and D–A adsorption models − to fit the experimental adsorption results. The fitting accuracy of different models was compared and the optimal adsorption was selected.

Langmuir adsorption model: 3 V=VLppL+p where V is the adsorption capacity at pressure p (cm3/g); p is the adsorption equilibrium pressure (MPa); V L is the Langmuir volume (cm3/g); and p L is the Langmuir pressure (MPa).

D–R adsorption model: 4 Vex=V0(1−ρgρa)exp[−D(lnρaρg)2] where V 0 is the theoretical limiting adsorption capacity (cm3/g) and D is a constant specific to the adsorbent–adsorbate system. D = (RT/βE) n, where E is the characteristic energy of the adsorption system and β is the affinity coefficient of the adsorbate.

D–A adsorption model: 5 Vex=V0(1−ρgρa)exp[−D(lnρaρg)n] where n is the structural heterogeneity parameter, a constant ranging from 1 to 4.

This is consistent with the high concentration and oriented molecular structure of the anthracite.

Mechanisms Influencing CO2 Adsorption

Pressure

At low temperatures (20–30 °C), the adsorption capacity of CO2 by the coal sample increases with rising pressure. When the pressure exceeds 5 MPa, the increase rate of adsorption begins to slow down (Figure a). Under conditions of 40–50 °C (Figure a), the CO2 adsorption increases with the increase of pressure. After CO2 reached the supercritical state, the adsorption capacity begins to increase and then decreases rapidly with further increase of pressure.

Temperature

As shown in Figure a, the adsorption capacity of CO2 by the coal sample decreases with an increase in temperature. Higher temperatures enhance the activity of CO2 gas molecules, reduce gas viscosity, and increase the kinetic energy of the molecules, making it easier for them to desorb from the coal surface.

With the increase of temperature, the maximum adsorption capacities of all three models decrease (Figure a), indicating that the CO2 adsorption capacity of the SH coal sample decreases linearly. In the D–R model, constant D initially increases and then decreases slightly, while in the D–A model, constant D decreases rapidly at first (Figure b) and then gradually decreases. Parameter p L shows a linear increase with temperature (Figure c), indicating that higher temperatures increase the desorption pressure of CO2 in the coal seam, making CO2 easier to store in the coal seam. Since reservoir pressure and temperature are closely related to the coal seam burial depth, burial depth is a critical factor in evaluating CO2 injection and storage. Thus, there exists an “optimal burial depth range” for the injection of CO2 into high-rank coal seams.

(a) Fitting results of CO2 adsorption experiments; (b) effect of temperature on V 0; (c) variation of constant D with temperature; and (d) variation of p L with temperature.

Water Saturation

With the increase of water saturation, the adsorption capacity of CO2 decreases (Figure b). This is because micropores in the coal sample are occupied by H2O molecules, which reduces the available adsorption sites of CO2, thereby reducing the ability of coal seams to adsorb CO2. In addition, there is a competitive adsorption between CO2 and H2O. , The combined effect of these two factors leads to a significant reduction in the coal’s CO2 adsorption capacity with increasing water saturation.

With increasing water saturation, the maximum adsorption capacity of the coal sample for CO2 initially decreases rapidly and then declines more gradually (Figure a). Parameter p L gradually increases with higher water saturation (Figure b), indicating that it is more difficult to desorb CO2 from the coal sample, which is beneficial to stable CO2 storage. However, due to the increase of water saturation, the CO2 adsorption capacity of coal samples is significantly reduced, which also limits the ability of the coal seam to sequester CO2. Therefore, when evaluating the feasibility of injecting CO2 into high-rank coal seams, it is necessary to evaluate the water content of the coal seam.

Comparison of CO2 Adsorption Model Fittings

The mean relative error (Emr) and standard deviation (S) were employed to conduct a comparative analysis of the fitting accuracy and precision of each model under varying conditions, as well as to examine the influence of temperature and water saturation on different adsorption model parameters.

The calculated results of Emr and S are shown in Figure . As the increase of temperature, the Emr and S values of D–A and Langmuir models first increase and then decrease (Figure a,c), and the peak value appears at 30 °C. On the contrary, the Emr and S values of the D–R model increase with the increase of temperature, indicating that its applicability is poor under high-temperature conditions. Generally speaking, the D–A model exhibits significantly lower errors compared to the D–R and Langmuir models, which makes it more suitable for predicting the CO2 adsorption capacity of coal samples across different temperatures.

CO2 Storage Capacity Prediction

A predictive model for the CO2 storage capacity under different occurrence states is established. On the basis of this model, the content and proportion of each CO2 occurrence state controlled by burial depth are calculated to evaluate the feasibility of CO2 sequestration in high-rank coal through injection.

Theoretical Models for CO2 Content in Different Occurrence States within Injected High-Rank Coal

Theoretical Model for Adsorbed CO2 Content

The D–A model demonstrates the strongest applicability in predicting the amount of CO2 adsorption under various conditions. Based on the D–A model, the content of CO2 adsorbed under reservoir conditions is predicted and expressed by the absolute adsorption capacity: 8 Vab=V0exp[−D(ln1.18ρg)n] where V ab is the absolute adsorption capacity (cm3/g); V 0 is the theoretical limiting adsorption capacity (cm3/g); and ρ g is the density of free-phase CO2 (g/cm3).

According to the fitting results of the D–A model, parameters such as V 0, D, and n under different temperatures (T) and water saturations (S w) were obtained. Under different water saturation conditions, the theoretical model of adsorbed-state content was established as follows: 9 Vab=V0(T)exp[−D(T)(ln1.18ρg)n(T)]

Theoretical Model for Free-State CO2 Content

Free-state CO2 is stored within the pores and micro-fractures of the coal seam, primarily controlled by porosity, water saturation, and reservoir conditions. Using the geological setting of the coal sample area as the background and relevant test parameters as the baseline conditions, the theoretical predictive model for free-state CO2 content is established as follows: 10 Vf=ϕ(1−Sw)ρb·Bg where V f is the free-state CO2 content, cm3/g; ϕ is the porosity, %; S w is the water saturation of the coal sample, %; ρ b is the apparent density of coal, g/cm3; and B g is the CO2 gas volume factor, dimensionless.

Among these, the formula for calculating porosity ϕ is as follows: 11 ϕ=ρc−ρbρc×100% where ρ c is the true density of coal, g/cm3.

The formula for calculating the CO2 gas volume factor Bg is as follows: 12 Bg=VgVsc where V g is the volume of 1 mol of CO2 under reservoir conditions, cm3, and V sc is the volume of 1 mol of CO2 under standard conditions, cm3.

The results show that as the burial depth increases, the adsorbed-state, free-state, and dissolved-state CO2 contents of the SH coal sample continuously rise (Figure a). With increasing depth, the phase state of CO2 transitions from gaseous to supercritical. According to the temperature and pressure of the CO2 supercritical point and the geological conditions of the coal sample area, the depths of phase state transformation of CO2 in the SH coal sample are between 800 m and 950 m. Above 800 m, the adsorbed-state CO2 content initially increases rapidly and then slows. Within the range of 800–950 m, the content has risen sharply. After more than 1000 m, the content of the adsorbed state began to decline. With the increase of burial depth, the micro-storage modes of CO2 in coal seams can be classified into three types: subcritical monolayer adsorption, supercritical gas-like multilayer adsorption, and supercritical liquid-like multilayer adsorption. In deep coal seams, the phase transition of CO2 to the supercritical state controls the interactions between fluid molecules at the microscopic level, thus influencing the content of the adsorbed state and overall storage capacity.

The content of free-state CO2 initially increases slowly, then rises rapidly, and subsequently slows again. This pattern is related to the segmented characteristic of the free-state CO2 density (ρg) with burial depth: ρg increases rapidly in shallow layers, and then the increasing speed slows down with the increase of the burial depth. This, in turn, leads to a distinct vertical zonation in the total CO2 storage capacity: in the shallow interval (<800 m), the dominant controlling factor is the rapid increase in adsorbed-state content; Within the 800–950 m range, it is jointly regulated by adsorbed- and free-state components. When the depth exceeds 950 m, due to the decrease of the maximum adsorption space and the increase of the supercritical CO2 density of the free state, the storage amount of free-state CO2 in the smaller pores of the coal seam increases. Therefore, the contribution of content in the free state to the total storage capacity continues to show an upward trend. The dissolved-state CO2 content increases linearly with burial depth, which is primarily controlled by coal seam water saturation.

Based on the above prediction of CO2 storage capacity, the variation curves of the proportions of each storage state with the burial depth were obtained (Figure b). The adsorbed state is the dominant storage form. With the increases of burial depth, the proportion of the adsorbed state rises rapidly at first, reaching a maximum value of about 76%, then declines sharply within a depth range of 800–950 m, and finally decreases gradually below 1000 m. The proportion of the free-state increases linearly at depths shallower than 800 m, then rises rapidly within the 800–950 m range, and finally shows a slow increasing trend below 1000 m. The growing proportion of free-state CO2 with depth provides favorable conditions for CO2 injection into deep coal seams. The proportion of dissolved-state CO2 exhibits a phased variation with depth: it decreases rapidly at shallow depths (above 400 m), then declines gradually and steadily in the intermediate depth range, and finally transitions to a slow increasing trend below 1000 m.

Conclusions

(1)Pressure exerts a positive effect on CO2 adsorption (before reaching the supercritical state), while temperature inhibits CO2 adsorption of the coal sample. With the increase of water saturation, the CO2 adsorption capacity began to decrease rapidly and then decreased gradually.(2)Langmuir and D–R models are not good at fitting the data on CO2 adsorption. In contrast, the D–A model demonstrates high reliability under both dry and water-bearing conditions. On the basis of the D–A model, the prediction model of adsorbed CO2 content was established. In addition, on the basis of the adsorption experimental results and the basic physical parameters of the SH coal sample, the content prediction models of different occurrence states of CO2 injection into high-rank coal seams were established.(3)The content of each CO2 occurrence state is influenced by factors such as formation temperature, pressure, and water saturation. The adsorbed state is the dominant storage form, reaching up to 76% at shallow depths (450 m) and then decreasing with depth. The free state increases from 0% to 29% (1500 m) monotonically, while the dissolved state exhibits a weak linear increase (1000–1500 m), which is mainly governed by coal seam water saturation. The synergistic evolution of the three states determines the vertical zoning of the CO2 storage capacity.
