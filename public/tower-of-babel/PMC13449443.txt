Combined Anti-CD20 and/or Anti-CD38 Therapy in Recurrent FSGS: Results Before Reasons
Authors: Paolo Cravedi, Umberto Maggiore, Andrea Angeletti
Published: 2026

See Clinical Research on Article 106668

Recurrent focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (rFSGS) remains one of the most feared complications after kidney transplantation. Recurrence occurs in approximately one-third of patients with primary FSGS and is associated with substantial morbidity, prolonged dependence on apheresis-based therapies, and a markedly increased risk of graft loss.1 Despite decades of investigation, the pathophysiology of rFSGS remains incompletely understood.S1 The rapid recurrence after transplantation, the remission of proteinuria when an affected graft was retransplanted into a non-FSGS recipient, and the induction of proteinuria following infusion of patient plasma into experimental models all strongly support the existence of circulating pathogenic factor(s).2 However, none of the proposed candidates has convincingly fulfilled Koch’s postulates, or modern criteria for molecular causality, leaving the identity and mechanism of the disease-driving factor(s) uncertain.

Recent years have brought renewed attention to humoral immune mechanisms as potential contributors to podocyte injury in rFSGS. Among the most compelling candidates are antibodies directed against nephrin and other slit diaphragm proteins, which have been associated with disease activity.3,S2 Yet important limitations remain—antinephrin antibodies are detected only in a minority of patients with rFSGS, and their presence does not invariably predict recurrence.4,S3 These observations raise uncertainty as to whether they represent primary disease initiators, amplifiers of injury, or biomarkers of an ongoing podocytopathy. Other studies have implicated a role of IgM antibodies against cardiolipin and other endothelial-associated antigens, which, through complement activation, may accelerate disease progression. Consistently, both IgM and complement deposition in the glomeruli are negative prognostic factors for primary FSGS cases.S4,S5 Whether these antibody responses are causal drivers or secondary epiphenomena remains unresolved, but together they have strengthened the biological rationale for therapies targeting antibody-producing cells, including B cells and plasma cells.S6

Rituximab and other anti-CD20 agents have shown partial efficacy in selected patients with rFSGS.1,5 However, responses remain inconsistent because many patients fail to respond, relapse after treatment, or require ongoing plasma exchange or immunoadsorption to maintain proteinuria control.1 Because long-lived plasma cells, a major source of circulating antibodies, are not effectively depleted by anti-CD20 therapy, combined anti-CD20 and/or anti-CD38 therapy has emerged as a biologically plausible strategy to target both circulating B cells and plasma cells.S7 Our initial studies first demonstrated the efficacy of this approach in patients with multidrug-resistant or multidrug-dependent nephrotic syndrome, showing robust clinical responses and a reassuring safety profile.6 Encouraged by these findings, we subsequently extended the strategy to post-transplant rFSGS, where we again observed substantial clinical benefit.7 Independent groups later reported similar responses in additional cases of recurrent disease,S8 and a recent multicenter international retrospective study further strengthened the evidence supporting this approach in patients who were resistant to virtually any other available treatment.8 Together, these reports have generated considerable enthusiasm for combined anti-CD20 and/or anti-CD38 therapy as a promising treatment strategy for a disease long characterized by therapeutic frustration and poor outcomes.

The article by Leon et al.9 reports the largest multicenter cohort to date evaluating combined anti-CD20 and/or anti-CD38 therapy in kidney transplant recipients with refractory rFSGS. Seventeen patients treated across 12 French transplant centers received the dual anti-CD20 and/or anti-CD38 strategy after failure of conventional approaches including plasma exchange, immunoadsorption, corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitor optimization, and previous anti-CD20 therapy alone. Median time from recurrence to initiation of combined therapy was 5 months, although treatment timing and dosing schedules varied substantially across centers. Following therapy, 41% of patients achieved complete remission and an additional 24% achieved partial remission, yielding an overall response rate of 65%. Importantly, all responders were able to discontinue apheresis, often immediately after treatment initiation, substantially reducing treatment burden and improving quality of life. Median time to response was only 24 days, although some patients experienced delayed improvement. Three responders subsequently relapsed but regained remission following retreatment with daratumumab, suggesting that repeated plasma-cell depletion may remain effective even after disease recurrence.

Therefore, the study by Leon et al.9 confirms and expands previous observations supporting the efficacy of combined plasma-cell and B-cell depletion in rFSGS. Importantly, the clinical benefit observed in this cohort is consistent with the growing body of published experience supporting combined anti-CD20 and/or anti-CD38 therapy in patients with recurrent disease who are resistant to conventional approaches. At the same time, it highlights an increasingly familiar paradox in nephrology—the treatment appears highly effective, yet we still do not fully understand why it works.

Indeed, despite the remarkable efficacy and reassuring short-term safety profile of this approach, the mechanisms underlying therapeutic response remain incompletely understood. The rationale for combined B-cell and plasma-cell targeting was initially based on the hypothesis that pathogenic autoantibodies contribute to rFSGS. In this study, antinephrin antibodies declined after therapy in 2 antibody-positive patients, in parallel with clinical improvement.9 However, the profound Ig depletion induced by anti-CD38 therapy precludes definitive causal inference between changes in specific antibody titers and disease activity.

Consistent with this uncertainty, in our previous studies antinephrin antibodies were detectable only in a minority of patients.8 By contrast, we observed associations between the depth of IgM depletion and sustained remission, raising the possibility that IgM-mediated complement activation contributes to disease persistence in at least a subset of cases.8 Additional exploratory observations linked circulating CD38-positive immune cell populations with disease activity, suggesting that the effects of anti-CD38 therapy may extend beyond antibody-producing cells.S9 CD38 is widely expressed across immune cell subsets, and therapeutic benefit may involve modulation of non–antibody-producing immune populations.S10 Finally, rFSGS may not represent a single disease entity, but rather a heterogeneous spectrum of immune-mediated podocytopathies converging on a common clinical phenotype. This heterogeneity may explain why a subset of patients fails to respond to combined anti-CD20 and/or anti-CD38 therapy.

These studies may also provide a unique opportunity to better understand disease biology. In this report,9 treatment resistance was associated with more advanced histologic lesions or a fulminant disease presentation. Similarly, in our previous experience, earlier introduction of combined anti-CD20 and/or anti-CD38 therapy seemed to be associated with better outcomes, suggesting that progressive and irreversible podocyte injury may limit therapeutic reversibility over time.8 Although this hypothesis requires prospective validation, it is biologically plausible that delayed treatment reduces the likelihood of complete remission by allowing ongoing podocyte loss, segmental sclerosis, and chronic allograft injury. These observations support the concept that prompt targeting of B cells and plasma cells may be critical to maximize therapeutic efficacy before irreversible structural damage develops.

Current therapeutic algorithms still rely on a sequential escalation strategy in which plasma exchange and anti-CD20 therapy are initiated first, whereas anti-CD38 therapy is reserved for refractory or plasma exchange–dependent disease.5 However, recurrent podocytopathies often develop within hours to days after transplantation, supporting the presence of rapidly acting circulating humoral factors. In this setting, delaying effective plasma-cell depletion may inadvertently permit progressive podocyte loss and irreversible glomerular injury. Based on the available clinical experience and the biological rationale discussed above, we propose that combined anti-CD20 and/or anti-CD38 therapy should be considered early in the course of recurrence, potentially irrespective of the initial response to plasma exchange or anti-CD20 therapy alone (Figure 1).

Proposed early combined anti-CD20 and/or anti-CD38 therapeutic approach for recurrent focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (rFSGS) after kidney transplantation. Schematic representation of an early combined anti-CD20 and/or anti-CD38 therapy rFSGS. Initial treatment consists of plasma exchange (PLEX) combined with anti-CD20 therapy (rituximab or obinutuzumab), followed by early introduction of anti-CD38 therapy (daratumumab), irrespective of the initial response to anti-CD20 treatment. Biologic agents should be administered after PLEX to optimize drug exposure. Treatment response is assessed according to proteinuria evolution. In responders, PLEX is progressively tapered and discontinued, whereas in nonresponders daratumumab may be repeated.

Several limitations temper the interpretation of the study by Leon et al.9 and the broader available evidence. Published studies remain retrospective, uncontrolled, and heterogeneous with respect to treatment timing, number of daratumumab doses, concomitant therapies, and response definitions. Moreover, because published reports are likely enriched for successful or noteworthy experiences, the true response rate in unselected practice may be lower. Because anti-CD20 therapy almost invariably preceded anti-CD38 administration, the specific contribution of plasma-cell depletion remains difficult to isolate. Finally, although reported experiences have generally shown a reassuring short-term safety profile, infectious complications and hypogammaglobulinemia remain important concerns in heavily immunosuppressed transplant recipients.

Nevertheless, the consistency of efficacy signals across independent cohorts argues against isolated anecdotal success. The emerging benefit of combined B-cell and plasma-cell depletion supports the notion that humoral immune mechanisms contribute to disease recurrence in at least a subset of patients. Whether these mechanisms are mediated by antinephrin antibodies, other slit diaphragm autoantibodies, complement-activating IgM, endothelial-reactive antibodies, or yet-unidentified circulating permeability factors remains unresolved. Future studies should move beyond retrospective efficacy assessment and prospectively define treatment timing, dosing, retreatment criteria, and safety monitoring, while embedding biomarker analyses of antinephrin antibodies, IgM/complement activation, CD38-positive immune populations, and podocyte injury markers. Such studies will be essential to identify patients most likely to benefit from early combined therapy and those who may be spared unnecessary immunosuppression.

For now, the field may need to accept that in rFSGS, clinical results have arrived before definitive mechanistic explanations. The challenge is to ensure that these results now drive prospective trials, mechanistic studies, and biomarker-guided treatment selection.
