Primary keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma of the renal pelvis with pancreatic tail invasion and five-year disease-free survival: A case report
Authors: Shynggys Nuraly, Dias Bolatov, Kairat Shakeyev, Akylbek Zhumakayev, Evgenia Kukushkina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the renal pelvis (<1% of upper urinary tract tumors) was diagnosed in a 58-year-old woman with anemia. MRI showed infiltrative renal pelvis mass with hydronephrosis and pancreatic tail invasion (pT4N0M0). Radical nephroureterectomy with bladder cuff, lymph node dissection, and en bloc pancreatic resection achieved complete resection despite morbid obesity. Well-differentiated keratinizing SCC with perineural invasion was confirmed externally. No adjuvant therapy was given. Five-year PET/CT showed no recurrence, demonstrating excellent long-term survival after multivisceral resection in advanced renal pelvic SCC.Keywords: Renal pelvis, Squamous cell carcinoma, Keratinizing, Hydronephrosis, Nephroureterectomy, Multivisceral resection, Pancreatic invasion, pT4, Disease-free survival, Case report

Introduction

Primary squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the renal pelvis is a rare malignancy, accounting for less than 1% of upper urinary tract malignancies 1, 2, 3. It is strongly associated with chronic irritation and obstruction, including nephrolithiasis and chronic infection, which promote squamous metaplasia and subsequent malignant transformation. Radiologic and histopathological correlation has been reported as particularly important in renal pelvic SCC associated with renal stones.4 Atypical presentations of this rare malignancy have also been described, further complicating preoperative diagnosis.5 Additional case reports and case series have emphasized that renal pelvic SCC may present with nonspecific symptoms, hydronephrosis, inflammatory changes, or incidental findings, and that diagnosis is frequently established only after surgical resection and histopathological examination 6, 7, 8, 9. Differential diagnosis from urothelial carcinoma with squamous differentiation and other histologic variants is essential.10 Tumor classification and staging should follow established WHO and AJCC criteria.11,12 Population-based studies have confirmed the rarity of renal pelvic and ureteral tumors, including SCC, and have described their incidence and clinicopathological characteristics.13,14 Large clinical series have shown that upper tract tumors with advanced stage or variant histology are associated with unfavorable outcomes 15, 16, 17. Current guideline-based management, environmental risk assessment, and preoperative prognostic evaluation remain important in upper urinary tract malignancies 18, 19, 20.

In this report, we present a rare case of renal pelvic keratinizing SCC with multiorgan invasion and, more importantly, durable five-year disease-free survival after radical surgery without neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy in pT4 disease.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old woman was evaluated for persistent anemia, during which a left renal mass was detected. Her medical history was significant for arterial hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and morbid obesity (body weight: 150 kg), increasing perioperative risk. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Initial abdominal ultrasound demonstrated left kidney enlargement with distortion of the collecting system and suspected central mass formation. Further assessment with contrast-enhanced MRI confirmed severe hydronephrosis and identified a centrally infiltrative lesion involving the renal pelvis and sinus region. Axial sections showed irregular margins, heterogeneous internal structure with probable necrotic areas, and distortion of the proximal ureter, consistent with obstructive malignant growth (Fig. 1a). Coronal images confirmed heterogeneous tumor infiltration with collecting system deformation and reduced functional parenchyma (Fig. 1b). Sagittal MRI demonstrated a large renal pelvic mass with pronounced calyceal dilatation (Fig. 1c). Cystoscopic examination revealed inflammatory changes of the mucosa in the vicinity of the left ureteral orifice; however, no visible bladder tumor was identified. Cytological analysis demonstrated epithelial dysplasia, which constituted an indication for radical surgical treatment.

MRI of the left kidney (T2-weighted sequences showing infiltrative renal pelvis mass with hydronephrosis, consistent with obstructive malignancy). (a) axial view showing infiltrative mass with hydronephrosis and perirenal fat invasion; (b) coronal view with heterogeneous tumor and parenchyma loss; (c) sagittal view confirming calyceal dilatation.

Radical surgery was performed via open midline laparotomy. Intraoperative exploration revealed a markedly enlarged left kidney with dense perirenal inflammatory infiltration and enlarged regional lymph nodes. After mobilization of the descending colon and retroperitoneal exposure, the renal artery and vein were individually ligated and divided. The kidney was mobilized from the adrenal region and lateral attachments. Direct tumor invasion into the pancreatic tail was identified, and partial pancreatic tail resection was performed en bloc.

The ureter showed diffuse tumor infiltration and was removed together with a bladder cuff. The bladder defect was closed with interrupted absorbable sutures. Para-aortic and left pelvic lymph node dissection was performed. Drains were placed in the nephrectomy bed and paravesical region.

Histological examination showed near-total replacement of renal parenchyma by well-differentiated keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma (G1) with keratin pearl formation and concentric keratinization (Fig. 2). The tumor demonstrated deep invasive growth with extension into renal sinus fat, penetration of the fibrous capsule, and invasion of perinephric adipose tissue, corresponding to stage pT4N0M0. Marked peritumoral inflammatory infiltration and perineural invasion were present. Lymphovascular invasion was not definitively identified. The diagnosis was independently confirmed by an external pathology center.

Histopathology (H&E, ×200) showing well-differentiated keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma with prominent keratin pearl formation.

Immunohistochemical examination was performed on representative tumor sections. Tumor cells showed diffuse strong nuclear positivity for p63 and p40, and diffuse cytoplasmic positivity for CK5/6. Immunostaining for GATA3 and uroplakin II was negative in tumor cells. The proliferative index assessed by Ki-67 was approximately 25%. The immunophenotype, together with the characteristic morphology demonstrating extensive keratinization and keratin pearl formation, supports the diagnosis of keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma. No immunohistochemical evidence of conventional urothelial differentiation was identified.

Postoperatively, infected renal bed collection required drainage and wound debridement; the patient was discharged stable after conservative management. No neoadjuvant/adjuvant therapy was administered. Five-year follow-up PET/CT (Jan 2026) showed no recurrence or metastases; the patient remains disease-free.

Discussion

Primary SCC of the renal pelvis is a distinctly uncommon malignancy associated with chronic inflammatory and obstructive conditions, particularly nephrolithiasis and infection. Long-standing irritation promotes squamous metaplasia and eventual malignant transformation. The association between chronic nephrolithiasis, chronic infection, and renal pelvic carcinoma has been reported in the literature 1, 2, 3, 4,6,9,19.

Early diagnosis is difficult because clinical and imaging findings are often nonspecific. Radiologic patterns may mimic inflammatory or obstructive conditions, including xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis, resulting in delayed recognition. Many tumors are identified only after nephrectomy and histologic analysis 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9. In the presented clinical case, magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a destructive centrally infiltrative lesion accompanied by severe hydronephrosis, findings that were more consistent with malignant involvement of the renal pelvic system than with uncomplicated obstruction or infection.

Despite the substantial evidence base supporting imaging modalities, morphological examination remains the principal diagnostic criterion. True primary SCC must be distinguished from urothelial carcinoma with squamous differentiation and metastatic squamous tumors10. In the present case, the diagnosis was supportednot only by the characteristic morphology, including extensive keratinization and keratin pearl formation, but also by immunohistochemistry demonstrating positivity for p63, p40, and CK5/6, with negative staining for GATA3 and uroplakin II, arguing against urothelial differentiation. These findings were consistent with a keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma according to WHO classification standards11. Tumor invasion into renal sinus fat, fibrous capsule penetration, perinephric adipose tissue involvement, and adjacent organ invasion corresponded to pT4N0M0 staging according to AJCC criteria.12 External expert pathology confirmation further increased diagnostic confidence.

Diagnostic ureteroscopy with biopsy was not performed because cross-sectional imaging demonstrated a large locally advanced infiltrative renal pelvic tumor, and definitive surgical management was considered indicated irrespective of endoscopic biopsy findings.

Renal pelvic SCC typically presents at an advanced stage and is associated with poor survival. Population-based and multicenter series of upper tract tumors demonstrate worse outcomes in advanced-stage disease and in tumors with variant histology 13, 14, 15, 16, 17,20. Importantly, histologic grade does not reliably predict biologic aggressiveness; even well-differentiated tumors may show extensive local invasion, as observed in the present case, where perineural invasion was present despite the absence of definitive lymphovascular invasion.

Radical nephroureterectomy with bladder cuff excision is the standard treatment for high-risk upper urinary tract tumors.18 Reports of multivisceral resection in renal pelvic SCC are limited, but the defining feature of the present case is not the multivisceral resection itself, which has been reported previously, but the durable five-year DFS without adjuvant therapy in pT4 disease. In this case, direct pancreatic tail invasion required en bloc partial pancreatic resection. Although multiorgan extension is usually considered a poor prognostic factor, complete radical resection may provide durable oncologic control in selected patients.

Declarations

There is no well-established systemic therapy standard for primary renal pelvic SCC, and evidence for adjuvant treatment remains limited. Our patient received no neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy, yet achieved five-year disease-free survival, an outcome more favorable than commonly reported. Radiologic-pathologic correlation was particularly strong in this case: the centrally infiltrative renal pelvic tumor with hydronephrosis on MRI corresponded to histologic renal sinus invasion, capsular penetration, perinephric adipose tissue involvement, and adjacent organ invasion.

Conclusion

Primary keratinizing SCC of the renal pelvis is a rare and aggressive malignancy that is usually diagnosed at an advanced stage. Imaging findings may be nonspecific, and histopathologic confirmation using WHO and AJCC criteria is essential.11,12 The key message of this case is not multivisceral resection alone, but five-year disease-free survival in pT4 disease without neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy, which is distinctly uncommon in the published literature 13, 14, 15, 16, 17,20. Despite the generally poor prognosis reported in the literature 13, 14, 15, 16, 17,20, radical surgery achieved durable disease-free survival in this patient. Radical surgery should be considered in selected patients with locally advanced disease, particularly when complete resection is feasible.
