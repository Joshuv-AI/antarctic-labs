Neuroimaging evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammation in psychiatric disorders: a review
Authors: Jing Li, Xian Ma, Juan Liu, Wanze Xu, Xiaoyi Zhang, Feifei Liang, Yu Dou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation are two fundamental pathological processes underlying the development of psychiatric disorders. These processes are interconnected and may jointly contribute to disease progression. This article systematically reviews the progress of multimodal imaging approaches for assessing mitochondrial function, including magnetic resonance spectroscopy, PET tracers targeting mitochondrial complexes, MRI-based cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen, and emerging techniques such as T1ρ imaging and manganese-enhanced MRI, as well as neuroinflammation, including translocator protein (TSPO) PET, monoamine oxidase B PET, novel tracers targeting C3aR/IL-1β, and diffusion imaging techniques reflecting white matter microstructural alterations. These approaches have been applied to various psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, autism spectrum disorder, sleep disorders, and substance use disorders. Based on imaging evidence across different psychiatric disorders and cohorts, this review summarizes three transdiagnostic imaging features: impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism, alterations in glial cell immune status, and white matter microstructural alterations associated with neuroinflammation. However, although individual imaging techniques have independently identified mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation, few studies have applied dual-target imaging within the same research cohort. The direct relationship between these processes remains unverified by in vivo studies. This review outlines the shared imaging features of various psychiatric disorders and examines the utility of multimodal imaging techniques in investigating mitochondrial and neuroinflammatory pathologies. It aims to provide new insights into the diagnosis and treatment of psychiatric disorders.

Introduction

The clinical diagnosis and treatment of psychiatric disorders, such as schizophrenia (SZ), major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder (BD), post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), sleep disorders, and substance use disorders (SUD), have long been challenged by unclear pathological mechanisms, reliance on subjective symptom assessment, and significant heterogeneity in treatment response (Insel and Cuthbert, 2015). Traditional approaches based on symptom descriptions and singular pathological hypotheses, such as genetics, neurodevelopmental abnormalities, or neurotransmitter imbalances, have failed to provide sufficient explanations. Thus, there is an urgent need to investigate these disorders at more fundamental cellular and pathological levels (Geschwind and Levitt, 2007; Fernandes et al., 2017). Against this backdrop, the central roles of mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation in disease pathogenesis have increasingly become the focus of research (Manji et al., 2012; Maes et al., 2025).

Mitochondria, as the brain’s “powerhouses,” are essential for maintaining neuronal activity and metabolic homeostasis by ensuring proper cellular function (Kann and Kovács, 2007), while neuroinflammation, as an immune response in the central nervous system, can directly damage neural circuits when abnormally activated (Yirmiya and Goshen, 2011). Preclinical studies suggest that mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation do not occur independently but are interrelated and may jointly influence disease progression. Mitochondrial damage contributes to oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, whereas neuroinflammation, in turn, exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction. Abnormalities in this pathological axis have been reported across multiple psychiatric disorders; however, its direct imaging correlates in patients’ brains remain to be verified (Morris and Berk, 2015).

Current in vivo imaging studies have independently reported mitochondrial dysfunction and abnormal neuroinflammation in psychiatric disorders. These studies primarily employ techniques for assessing mitochondrial function, including magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) for energy metabolites, PET tracers targeting mitochondrial complexes, and MRI-based cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen (CMRO₂). In addition, novel techniques, including T1ρ imaging and manganese-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MEMRI), are also under exploration. For neuroinflammation, the application of translocator protein (TSPO) PET is the most widely used. PET tracers for astrocyte markers, such as monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B), and novel tracers targeting C3aR or IL-1β are also currently under clinical translation. Diffusion imaging can indirectly reflect white matter microstructural damage and alterations in fluid dynamics resulting from neuroinflammation. However, a comprehensive review integrating findings across various psychiatric diagnoses is still lacking.

This review first outlines the potential vicious cycle in which mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation may drive each other. It then systematically reviews the principles and levels of evidence of imaging techniques used to assess these two pathological processes. Based on this foundation, it summarizes three transdiagnostic imaging features and constructs a hypothesis-generating framework. Finally, it discusses the limitations of current evidence and future research directions, aiming to provide methodological insights for mechanistic research and clinical translation in psychiatric disorders.

Mitochondrial dysfunction in psychiatric disorders

Mitochondrial dysfunction in psychiatric disorders is driven by the synergistic effects of genetic and environmental factors and can be classified into five major categories: mitochondrial DNA damage, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, abnormal mitochondrial dynamics, membrane dysfunction, and defective mitochondrial quality control (Manji et al., 2012). Mitochondrial dysfunction exhibits distinct patterns across different diseases, primarily in terms of the brain regions affected, the predominant types of dysfunction, and their associations with core clinical symptoms (Table 1).

Although the causative factors of mitochondrial dysfunction—such as genetics, stress, and environmental toxins—and its presentation patterns vary among diseases, the core impairment demonstrates cross-diagnostic commonality. Specifically, this impairment is characterized by disruption of oxidative phosphorylation integrity due to reduced respiratory chain complex activity (Holt et al., 1988; Prabakaran et al., 2004). This functional defect leads to two major pathological consequences. First, insufficient ATP synthesis results in energy deficits in neurons, directly impairing processes such as axonal transport and synaptic remodeling, ultimately causing synaptic dysfunction (Rangaraju et al., 2014). Second, increased electron leakage in the electron transport chain promotes excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS; Murphy, 2009). Excessive ROS further exacerbate mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage and lead to the collapse of mitochondrial membrane potential (Yakes and Van Houten, 1997). Defects in the respiratory chain, accumulation of ROS, and mtDNA damage can form a stepwise amplifying positive feedback loop, which simultaneously disrupts intracellular calcium homeostasis and induces mitochondrial membrane permeability transition (Wei et al., 1998; Görlach et al., 2015).

Neuroinflammation

Neuroinflammation refers to the immune response of the central nervous system to pathological stimuli and, in psychiatric disorders, is primarily characterized by a chronic, low-grade, and dynamically fluctuating disturbance of immune homeostasis. The core pathological process involves abnormalities in the functional state of central innate immune cells and disruption of blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity (Kölliker-Frers et al., 2021). Genetic susceptibility, chronic stress, sleep deprivation, and pathogen infection may each trigger or exacerbate immune responses in the central nervous system (Li et al., 2025b; Zhao et al., 2025).

Microglia, the primary immune cells of the brain, are involved in synaptic pruning and environmental surveillance during their resting state. This homeostatic state is maintained through interactions between regulatory signals secreted by neurons and receptors expressed on the surface of glial cells (Fig. 1) (Yang et al., 2025). Upon stimulation by pathogens, damage-associated molecular patterns released from injured cells, or peripheral immune signals, microglia undergo a transition in functional state (West et al., 2011). The classical polarization model categorizes microglia into two phenotypes: the classically activated (M1-like) phenotype and the alternatively activated (M2-like) phenotype (Hu et al., 2015). M1-like microglia perform defensive functions by releasing proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, as well as reactive oxygen and nitrogen species. However, excessive or prolonged activation can directly damage neurons and impair synaptic plasticity. M2-like microglia contribute to tissue repair and the resolution of inflammation by releasing anti-inflammatory factors, such as IL-10, IL-13, and TGF-β (Orihuela et al., 2016).

However, recent advances in single-cell transcriptomics using neurodegenerative disease models have revealed that microglia can simultaneously express proinflammatory and phagocytosis-associated genes, suggesting that their functional states cannot be adequately characterized by the M1/M2 dichotomy (Keren-Shaul et al., 2017; Ochocka and Kaminska, 2021). Additionally, under conditions such as chronic stress and prolonged drug exposure, microglia may exhibit functional suppression. This dysfunctional state is closely associated with abnormal synaptic pruning, impaired neurogenesis, and cognitive decline, and may represent a pathological feature observed in the late stages of certain psychiatric disorders (Kreisel et al., 2014; Tay et al., 2017; Paolicelli et al., 2022).

Astrocytes also play a critical role in neuroinflammation. Under physiological conditions, they support neuronal function by regulating extracellular ion homeostasis, absorbing glutamate, supplying metabolic substrates, and maintaining the integrity of the BBB (Verkhratsky and Nedergaard, 2018). When stimulated by inflammatory mediators released from activated microglia, such as IL-1α, TNF-α, and C1q, astrocytes can be induced to adopt the neurotoxic A1 phenotype. Reactive astrocytes display considerable heterogeneity. A1 astrocytes upregulate complement genes and proinflammatory factors while releasing neurotoxic substances, whereas A2 astrocytes upregulate neurotrophic and anti-inflammatory factors, thereby promoting tissue repair and neuronal survival (Liddelow et al., 2017). The functional transformation and relative abundance of these two reactive astrocyte types contribute to the overall impact of neuroinflammation on neural circuits.

The BBB strictly regulates the exchange of substances via tight junctions formed by endothelial cells. Chronic stress and neuroinflammation can compromise BBB integrity, resulting in downregulation of tight junction proteins, such as ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5, and increased activity of matrix metalloproteinases (Dudek et al., 2020). Following BBB disruption, peripheral immune cells and inflammatory mediators infiltrate the brain parenchyma, interacting with CNS-resident immune cells to establish a “peripheral-central immune dialogue.” This process is particularly prominent in stress-related and affective disorders such as MDD and PTSD (Doney et al., 2022).

Interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation

Mitochondrial dysfunction can drive neuroinflammation through several key molecular pathways, forming a self-reinforcing “mitochondrial–inflammatory axis.”

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) leakage and activation of the cGAS–STING pathway constitute the central link between mitochondrial injury and systemic inflammation. When mitochondrial membrane integrity is compromised, mtDNA from the matrix leaks into the cytoplasm and is recognized by the cytoplasmic DNA sensor cGAS. Activation of cGAS generates the second messenger cGAMP, which subsequently activates the stimulator of interferon genes (STING), triggering downstream signaling cascades involving TBK1–IRF3 and NF-κB. This process leads to the release of type I interferons and proinflammatory cytokines. Upregulation of this pathway has been observed in numerous psychiatric disorders (West et al., 2011; West and Shadel, 2017; Kim et al., 2023).

The ROS/NLRP3 inflammasome axis provides a direct link between oxidative stress and inflammation. Excessive mitochondrial ROS can induce lipid oxidation, such as that of mitochondrial cardiolipin, and the resulting oxidation products directly bind to NLRP3 inflammasomes, promoting their oligomerization and activation. Activated NLRP3 inflammasomes, in turn, activate caspase-1, which cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their mature forms, facilitating their secretion. This pathway directly connects mitochondrial oxidative stress to the release of IL-1β and other inflammatory cytokines (Holley and Schroder, 2020; Peng et al., 2025).

Disorders of mitochondrial mitophagy and the accumulation of inflammatory mediators exacerbate inflammation from the perspective of “quality control failure.” Dysfunction of the mitochondrial autophagy pathway mediated by the PINK1/Parkin pathway leads to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria within cells, resulting in continuous production of ROS and leakage of mtDNA. These events serve as chronic stimuli for the cGAS–STING and NLRP3 pathways (Zhu et al., 2024). Patients with schizophrenia and BD exhibit abnormal expression of genes related to mitochondrial autophagy (Dwivedi, 2020; Statharakos, 2025). In addition, mitochondrial damage-associated components, such as mtDNA and ATP, released extracellularly can be recognized by Toll-like receptors on immune cells, including microglia (Brooks et al., 2026). These pathways interact to initiate and amplify downstream inflammatory cascades.

Neuroinflammation is not only driven by mitochondrial dysfunction but can also act on mitochondria, further aggravating energy metabolism disorders. Proinflammatory cytokines released by activated microglia and astrocytes, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, can impair mitochondrial function through several mechanisms. First, they inhibit respiratory chain complex activity and directly interfere with oxidative phosphorylation (Samavati et al., 2008). Second, they induce excessive production of nitric oxide and ROS, leading to mitochondrial DNA damage and collapse of the mitochondrial membrane potential (Saha and Pahan, 2006; Ghasemi et al., 2018). Third, they downregulate the expression of PGC-1α and other key regulators of mitochondrial biogenesis via NF-κB and other transcriptional pathways, thereby inhibiting mitochondrial regeneration (Rius-Pérez et al., 2020). In addition, metabolic reprogramming induced by chronic inflammation can shift cells toward glycolytic metabolism, further weakening the oxidative phosphorylation capacity of mitochondria (Kim et al., 2025).

Imaging assessment of mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation in psychiatric disorders

In recent years, an increasing number of studies have utilized neuroimaging techniques to noninvasively and quantitatively characterize in vivo pathological processes, including mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation. The evidence regarding “mitochondrial dysfunction” and “neuroinflammation” discussed in this review differs in strength and can be categorized into the following levels. The basic principles and limitations of each imaging technique are discussed in detail in the Imaging technology section.

Regarding mitochondrial dysfunction: (a) Direct evidence: PET imaging targeting mitochondrial complex I, ³¹P-MRS for high-energy phosphate compounds and intracellular pH, and CMRO₂ measured using pCASL and sqBOLD allow direct assessment of mitochondrial function. (b) Indirect evidence: ¹H-MRS measurement of metabolites, including lactate (Lac) and glutathione (GSH); T1ρ imaging; and MEMRI assessment of axonal transport rate (ATR) allow indirect evaluation of mitochondrial energy metabolism abnormalities. (c) Structural consequences: Alterations in brain microstructure assessed by structural MRI, corroborated by transcriptome analysis, may represent downstream effects of mitochondrial dysfunction.

Regarding neuroinflammation: (a) Direct evidence: TSPO-PET and MAO-B PET reflect glial cell activation and are currently the most commonly used tools for in vivo assessment of neuroinflammation. (b) Indirect evidence: ¹H-MRS measurement of metabolites, including myo-inositol (mIns) and choline (Cho), and diffusion MRI metrics for microstructural integrity, such as radial diffusivity (RD), fractional anisotropy (FA), and mean diffusivity (MD), provide indirect evidence of neuroinflammation. (c) Structural consequences: Neuroinflammation can lead to alterations in brain structure and abnormal functional networks. Corroborated by PET, transcriptome analysis, and peripheral blood markers, these changes may represent downstream effects of neuroinflammation.

This review adopts a narrative format. We conducted a search of PubMed and Web of Science for original studies, including human and animal experiments, published between March 2021 and March 2026, using search terms encompassing psychiatric disorders, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and related imaging techniques. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) original studies; (2) use of techniques such as MRS, targeted PET tracers for mitochondria and inflammation, or diffusion imaging; (3) reporting indicators related to mitochondrial dysfunction or neuroinflammation. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-original studies (e.g. reviews, comments); (2) non-imaging studies.

Additionally, reference lists of the included studies were manually examined to supplement the search results. Based on this search strategy, the main research findings were summarized, as presented in Supplementary Table 1. It should be noted that most evidence included in this review originates from single-target imaging of separate cohorts, with independent evaluations conducted for either mitochondrial function or neuroinflammation.

Schizophrenia

In SZ, mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation are considered core features contributing to disease onset and progression. SZ is increasingly recognized as a syndrome characterized by neuroimmunometabolic dysregulation, with clinical manifestations encompassing positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and cognitive deficits. These manifestations are closely associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and regional imbalances in energy metabolism (Müller, 2018; Purcell et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025).

Mizuno et al. employed ¹⁸F-DPA-714 TSPO-PET imaging to show that the distribution volume ratio of TSPO (a marker of glial cell activation) was significantly elevated in both global and temporal lobe gray matter in first-episode psychosis (FEP) patients compared with healthy controls, indicating neuroimmune activation in the early stages of SZ. However, TSPO levels did not correlate with symptom severity, and treatment with α4β1 integrin monoclonal antibodies did not alter PET signals, suggesting that neuroinflammation in SZ may involve complex cellular origins or regulatory mechanisms (Mizuno et al., 2026). Jeon et al. employed 7T functional magnetic resonance spectroscopy (fMRS) to show that, in first-episode schizophrenia (FES) patients, abnormal coupling between prefrontal cortical glutamate dynamics and glutathione levels during cognitive task performance, together with impaired glutamine recovery, may reflect disruption of mitochondrial-mediated redox homeostasis (Jeon et al., 2025). However, metabolite measurements obtained via MRS provide an indirect proxy for mitochondrial energy metabolism, rather than a direct assessment of mitochondrial function.

Structural and diffusion magnetic resonance imaging further provide indirect evidence of brain tissue alterations associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation. Zhang et al. reported that drug-naïve first-episode schizophrenia (FEDN) patients exhibited a higher incidence of white matter hyperintensities, with lesion volumes correlating with elevated levels of IL-2 and ROS (Zhang et al., 2025c). Mamah et al. employed diffusion basis spectrum imaging (DBSI) to identify an increased cellular fraction and a reduced fiber fraction in the white matter of SZ patients, findings indicative of neuroinflammation and axonal injury or loss. In contrast, BD patients exhibited milder white matter abnormalities, primarily manifested as increased radial diffusion in the corpus callosum and elevated extra-axonal water content (Mamah et al., 2025).

Major depressive disorder

MDD patients exhibit clinical manifestations including emotional numbness, cognitive deficits, psychomotor slowing, and impaired social functioning. The pathogenesis of MDD is associated with hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired neuroplasticity, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation (Allen et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a).

Early neuroimaging has established a framework for the pathophysiological model of MDD. Regarding neuroinflammation, Setiawan et al. directly observed increased glial cell activation throughout the brain in MDD patients using TSPO-PET (Setiawan et al., 2018). Regarding mitochondrial function, Su et al. performed a meta-analysis of 10 fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET studies, revealing significantly reduced glucose metabolism in the bilateral insular cortex, right cingulate gyrus, and bilateral putamen in MDD patients (Su et al., 2014). These findings indirectly suggest the presence of mitochondrial dysfunction in these regions in MDD. In addition, multiple MRS studies have reported that N-acetylaspartate (NAA) concentrations in the prefrontal cortex, occipital lobe, and thalamus of MDD patients are reduced, reflecting impaired neuronal integrity or decreased metabolic activity (Saccaro et al., 2024).

In a recent study, Li et al. reported that the surface area, volume, and thickness of the right anterior cingulate cortex in adolescent MDD patients were abnormally increased. Esketamine acetate treatment was able to reverse these structural changes, with its efficacy directly associated with alterations in the expression of mitochondrial metabolism-related genes. These findings indirectly suggest at the genetic level that the mitochondrial metabolism pathway may underlie the rapid antidepressant effects of the drug (Li et al., 2025a). More directly, Zhou et al. employed a novel ¹⁸F-PB200 HDAC6-PET imaging technique to demonstrate, for the first time in vivo, that histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) expression is elevated in the brains of MDD model animals and is positively correlated with TSPO signaling (Zhou et al., 2026). This finding indicates that epigenetic regulation plays a critical role in the initiation of neuroinflammation. Building on this foundation, Lai et al. further elucidated the mechanisms through which activated neuroinflammation induces structural damage. In a chronic stress mouse model, tanshinone I effectively suppressed excessive glial cell-mediated synaptic phagocytosis by targeting complement C3 and inhibiting the C3–CR3–STAT3 signaling axis, thereby restoring abnormal brain functional connectivity. This study demonstrated the pivotal role of the complement system in MDD (Lai et al., 2025).

Bipolar disorder

BD is characterized by state-dependent metabolic disturbances, with manic episodes associated with increased energy expenditure and oxidative stress, whereas depressive episodes are characterized by a low-energy state (Morris et al., 2017). Recurrent neuronal energy deficits and glial cell-mediated chronic inflammation exert a dual detrimental effect on neural plasticity.

Recent studies have further confirmed mitochondrial dysfunction in the brains of BD patients. Magnotta et al. employed imaging methods, including T1ρ and ³¹P-MRS, to directly demonstrate that type I BD patients exhibited impaired oxidative phosphorylation in the cerebellar vermis and putamen, indicated by decreased intracellular pH, reduced α-ATP, and prolonged T1ρ. Concurrently, GSH levels in these regions were elevated, suggesting a compensatory response to oxidative stress (Magnotta et al., 2022). Wigstrom et al. directly observed reduced activity of mitochondrial complex I in specific brain regions of BD patients using ¹⁸F-BCPP-EF PET imaging (Wigstrom et al., 2024).

At the level of indirect evidence, Bravi et al. reported that the glutamate + glutamine (Glx)/NAA ratio in the prefrontal cortex and mIns levels were significantly elevated in BD patients. Both alterations were associated with compromised white matter microstructural integrity. These findings suggest that, during the depressive phase of BD, abnormal glutamatergic metabolism and glial activation may synergistically contribute to white matter microstructural damage (Bravi et al., 2022). Interestingly, inflammatory heterogeneity may underlie differences in prognosis. In a study by Chen et al., using soluble interleukin-6 receptor (sIL-6R)-related functional connectivity clustering, the subgroup of BD patients with mildly elevated sIL-6R levels exhibited a more favorable prognosis. This subgroup was characterized by reduced functional connectivity between the medial frontal network (MFN) and motor network, alongside enhanced connectivity between the MFN and the default mode network. Notably, this pattern represents the inverse of the classic connectivity abnormalities observed in BD patients, suggesting that mild inflammation may exert a protective effect through network regulation (Chen et al., 2025).

Post-traumatic stress disorder

Research on neuroinflammation in PTSD has yielded conflicting results. Specifically, three recent studies investigating TSPO-PET have reported inconsistent findings. In 2020, Bhatt et al. reported reduced TSPO-PET binding in the frontolimbic circuit of PTSD patients and first proposed the “neuroimmunosuppression” hypothesis (Bhatt et al., 2020). Watling et al. were unable to replicate this finding in occupation-related PTSD. Instead, they observed a nominal increase in TSPO-PET binding in the amygdala, with a significant elevation in the subgroup of regular cannabis users (Watling et al., 2023). Unlike the above static measurements, Bonomi et al. reported no significant differences in baseline TSPO-PET binding between PTSD patients and healthy controls. Following lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation, the response amplitude in PTSD patients was lower than that in the control group. Moreover, greater severity of anhedonia symptoms was associated with a more attenuated neuroimmune response (Bonomi et al., 2024). The MAO-B PET study conducted by the same team provided complementary evidence across different glial cell types, supporting the complexity described above. Gill et al. reported that the availability of the astrocyte marker MAO-B in the cortical-limbic regions of PTSD patients was marginally decreased, with a decline of 28–33% observed in the subgroup with comorbid MDD (Gill et al., 2022).

In contrast to neuroinflammation imaging studies, in vivo investigations of mitochondrial dysfunction in PTSD are virtually nonexistent. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that trauma exposure induces mitochondrial impairments in specific brain regions, including decreased activity of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes, reduced ATP production, and oxidative damage in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex (Kaplan et al., 2023). To date, no studies have employed mitochondria-specific in vivo imaging techniques to directly assess PTSD patients.

Autism spectrum disorder

The neuroimmune status of ASD patients exhibits marked heterogeneity and sex-related network imbalances, encompassing glial dysfunction, unstable and dysregulated proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses, and disruptions in peripheral–central immune interactions mediated by the gut–brain axis (Lai et al., 2015; Heine and Dooves, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025d). In addition, neurodevelopment in ASD is closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, which is characterized by impaired mitochondrial complex activity and abnormal energy metabolism, arising from the combined influence of genetic and environmental factors (Frye et al., 2024).

Recent studies have identified mitochondrial complex I and Lac as imaging biomarkers of energy metabolism abnormalities in ASD. Kato et al. utilized ¹⁸F-BCPP-EF PET to directly demonstrate that mitochondrial complex I availability in the anterior cingulate cortex of untreated high-functioning ASD males was significantly lower than in healthy controls and negatively correlated with the ADOS-2 communication subscore (Kato et al., 2023). As indirect evidence, Maier et al. reported using ¹H-MRS that Lac+ concentration in the posterior cingulate cortex of ASD patients was elevated, with 9.4% of the subgroup showing increased lactate levels (Maier et al., 2023).

TSPO-PET studies have indicated that sex and comorbidity modulate neuroinflammation in ASD. In male participants, Zurcher et al. observed reduced TSPO-PET binding across multiple brain regions, which was negatively correlated with symptom severity (Zurcher et al., 2021). Simpson et al. found no significant differences in TSPO-PET distribution in the overall mixed-gender ASD sample. However, after excluding two individuals with MDD, they observed a reduction in TSPO-PET distribution volume in brain regions, including the prefrontal cortex and temporal lobe (Simpson et al., 2022). In female participants, Tseng et al. observed an opposite pattern, with elevated TSPO-PET binding in the middle cingulate gyrus and the splenium of the corpus callosum, and TSPO-PET levels in the left inferior longitudinal fasciculus positively correlated with ADOS-2 total scores (Tseng et al., 2024). Furthermore, Tseng et al. extended TSPO-PET imaging to the cranium and observed elevated TSPO-PET binding in the frontal and temporal bones of adults with ASD. Peripheral C-reactive protein levels were positively correlated with whole-cranial TSPO-PET, providing a novel perspective on peripheral–central immune communication (Tseng et al., 2025).

White matter microstructure provides indirect evidence for neuroinflammation. Andica et al., using the neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) technique, found that in adult ASD patients, the isotropic volume fraction (ISOVF) of the left infratemporal-occipital fasciculus was elevated, while the neurite density index (NDI) was decreased, suggesting spatial co-localization of neuroinflammation and axonal loss in this region (Andica et al., 2021). Nagai et al. observed higher axial kurtosis (AK) values in the right anterior radiate, corpus callosum, and right superior longitudinal fasciculus in a diffusion kurtosis imaging–resting-state fMRI (DKI–rs-fMRI) study compared to healthy controls. Additionally, cerebellar–occipital functional connectivity was negatively correlated with mean kurtosis values in these regions, suggesting that inflammatory changes in white matter microstructure may underlie abnormal long-range functional connectivity (Nagai et al., 2024).

Sleep disorders and SUD

Sleep disorders and SUD are frequently considered comorbidities or risk factors for psychiatric disorders. However, recent imaging studies have demonstrated that both conditions can independently induce structural and functional brain alterations through mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammatory pathways. These findings suggest that studies of psychiatric disorders should consider sleep and substance use history as covariates in statistical analyses.

Sleep disorders exhibit a dose–response relationship with neuroimmune activation. Balter et al. found that, in healthy middle-aged and older adults, shorter sleep duration, daytime fatigue, and subjective sleep deprivation were associated with elevated TSPO-PET binding in the medial frontal cortex. Moreover, greater deviation from an 8-h sleep duration over a 5-year period was associated with higher current TSPO-PET binding across most brain regions (Balter et al., 2025). Mueller et al. reported that Cho and mIns in the bilateral frontal lobes correlated with slower processing speed and reduced sleep efficiency, indirectly suggesting that glial cell activation may serve as an intermediate mechanism underlying cognitive decline induced by sleep deprivation (Mueller et al., 2024). Rahayel et al. found that, in patients with idiopathic rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (iRBD), gene expression in regions of cortical thinning was enriched for mitochondrial complex I assembly and autophagy pathways, whereas increased cortical surface area was associated with inflammatory responses and glial cell markers (Rahayel et al., 2023).

Astrocytes in the brains of SUD patients exhibit abnormal metabolic changes. Best et al. found that MAO-B levels in AUD patients were negatively correlated with disease severity and withdrawal symptoms, with the severe AUD subgroup exhibiting lower MAO-B levels than the mild-to-moderate subgroup (Best et al., 2025). Luo et al. reported that long-term heroin-addicted rats exhibited decreased ATR in the olfactory bulb and in both the proximal and distal olfactory tracts, along with downregulated expression of cytochrome c oxidase IV (COX IV) and ATP synthase β subunit (ATPB). These findings suggest that substance addiction may induce axonal dysfunction through the collapse of mitochondrial energy supply (Luo et al., 2022).

Integration of transdiagnostic imaging features: a framework for hypothesis generation

Genetic, environmental, stress-related, and sleep deprivation risk factors may drive mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation through pathways such as oxidative stress and glial cell activation, with the two processes interacting reciprocally. These pathological processes can be evaluated in vivo using both direct and indirect imaging techniques. By integrating imaging evidence from different psychiatric disorders and cohorts, the present review summarizes three transdiagnostic imaging features of mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation in psychiatric disorders, thereby providing a framework for hypothesis generation (Fig. 2).

Mitochondrial energy metabolism collapse: Reduced activity of mitochondrial complex I or downregulation of related genes has been reported in BD, ASD, and iRBD. Indirect evidence, including reduced glucose metabolism and decreased NAA concentration, has also been observed in MDD. Additionally, axonal mitochondrial energy metabolism enzymes (COX IV, ATPB) are downregulated in heroin-addicted animals.

Conceptual framework linking risk factors, pathophysiological mechanisms, imaging techniques, and transdiagnostic imaging features in psychiatric disorders. Stress, genetics, environment, and sleep loss may contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation through pathways involving oxidative stress (ROS, mtDNA-cGAS-STING, NLRP3 inflammasome) and glial activation (microglial cytokine release, NF-κB signaling). These two processes form a bidirectional vicious cycle. These mechanisms can be assessed in vivo using direct imaging techniques (³¹P-MRS, CMRO₂ MRI, complex I PET for mitochondrial function; TSPO-PET, MAO-B PET for neuroinflammation) and indirect techniques (¹H-MRS, T1ρ, MEMRI, diffusion MRI). By integrating imaging evidence from different psychiatric disorders and cohorts, the present review summarizes three transdiagnostic imaging features, thereby providing a hypothesis-generating framework for future studies. Potential sources of neuroinflammation heterogeneity

Although these three features share transdiagnostic commonalities, TSPO-PET findings have revealed heterogeneity in specific psychiatric disorders. While TSPO is expressed in various cell types, its signal changes under neuroinflammatory conditions primarily reflect the activation state of microglia. Therefore, the following explanations focus on microglia.

The heterogeneity of TSPO signals in PTSD may reflect differences in trauma type and stress pattern and may also be influenced by comorbid conditions, substance use, and methodological differences among tracers. The studies conducted by Bhatt et al. and Bonomi et al. both focused on chronic PTSD patients. The former found a decrease in baseline TSPO levels, whereas the latter discovered a blunted response following LPS stimulation. Both findings collectively suggest microglial exhaustion or immune tolerance. Preclinical studies have shown that chronic stress may reduce microglial activation, whereas subchronic stress may increase it (Kreisel et al., 2014). This observation aligns with the findings of Watling et al., in which PTSD followed a subchronic stress pattern and trauma occurred in adulthood rather than childhood. This may explain the nominal increase in TSPO levels. Furthermore, this study also found that TSPO levels in the amygdala of regular cannabis users were higher. This may be because regular cannabis use downregulates the CB1 receptor, induces an inflammatory phenotype in microglia, and thereby increases TSPO binding (Cutando et al., 2013). Comorbid conditions also play a modulatory role: Gill et al. found that PTSD patients with comorbid MDD exhibited a greater decrease in MAO-B levels, suggesting that comorbidity may exacerbate neuroimmune abnormalities. At the methodological level, the tracers used in the three studies differed (Bhatt and Bonomi used ¹¹C-PBR28, whereas Watling used ¹⁸F-FEPPA), which may affect detection sensitivity and the comparability of results.

In ASD patients, the TSPO signal exhibits a sex-dependent pattern: it is lower in males and higher in females. This difference may be primarily attributed to the inherent sex dimorphism of microglia. Studies have shown that both baseline TSPO expression and inflammatory response capacity are higher in female microglia than in male microglia (Biechele et al., 2020). Secondly, sex hormone pathways may play a modulatory role in this process. Briones-Valdivieso et al. conducted a network analysis of ASD-related genetic risk genes and found that a subset of androgen- and estrogen-related genes exhibited a significant “sex-dependent” association within the ASD gene network (Briones-Valdivieso et al., 2025). Furthermore, comorbid MDD may confound the observed results, as MDD itself is associated with elevated TSPO levels. This finding suggests that comorbid conditions represent a confounding factor that cannot be overlooked in studies of neuroinflammation in ASD patients.

These factors suggest that the neuroinflammatory phenotype results from the interaction of multiple factors rather than a simple “activation” or “inhibition” state.

Current in vivo imaging technologies show great promise as core tools for investigating the dynamic changes in mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation associated with psychiatric disorders. However, no single imaging technique can comprehensively capture the pathological features of both processes, and the strength of evidence varies across different techniques. This section systematically summarizes various imaging techniques for assessing mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation, along with their characteristics (Table 2), and focuses on the applicable scope and inherent limitations of each technique, aiming to provide methodological references for mechanistic research and clinical translation in psychiatric disorders.

Direct assessment of mitochondrial dysfunction

31P-MRS

Currently, ³¹P-MRS is the only noninvasive magnetic resonance technique capable of directly measuring high-energy phosphate compounds in the living brain (Du et al., 2007). Unlike ¹H-MRS or T1ρ imaging, which indirectly reflects energy metabolism, ³¹P-MRS directly examines the specific biological processes involved in mitochondrial adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis. ³¹P-MRS targets the phosphorus nucleus, enabling the detection of signals from high-energy phosphates in the brain, including ATP, phosphocreatine (PCr), inorganic phosphate (Pi), and beta-nucleoside triphosphate (β-NTP). A decreased ATP/PCr ratio and an increased Pi/PCr ratio are indicative of impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism, whereas reduced intracellular pH may suggest enhanced anaerobic metabolism (Liu et al., 2017). Its advantages lie in its independence from exogenous tracers, direct assessment of mitochondrial energy metabolism, and compatibility with conventional MRI, enabling multi-parameter assessment of both brain structure and metabolism within the same subject. However, the spatial resolution of ³¹P-MRS is limited (typical voxel volume > 5–10 cm³), making it difficult to precisely localize subcortical nuclei or perform laminar assessment of the cortex. Moreover, ³¹P has a naturally low abundance, which results in long scan times and requires high subject cooperation (Alcicek et al., 2025). Over the past two decades, ³¹P-MRS has been reported to reveal abnormal energy metabolism in various psychiatric disorders. These findings include a decreased PCr/ATP ratio in the basal ganglia of first-episode drug-naïve schizophrenia (FEDN) patients; a correlation between brain β-NTP levels and antidepressant treatment response in MDD patients; and a downward trend in prefrontal PCr levels in ASD patients (Gangadhar et al., 2004; Iosifescu et al., 2008; Golomb et al., 2014). Among the studies included in this review, Magnotta et al. further reported that, in BD patients, pH levels in the cerebellum and putamen decreased, along with a decline in α-ATP levels (Magnotta et al., 2022).

Mitochondria-specific PET imaging

PET enables in vivo quantitative assessment of mitochondrial function by using specific tracers that bind to proteins associated with mitochondrial activity. PET tracers targeting mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I have been a rapidly developing area of research in recent years. The core advantage of these tracers lies in their ability to directly measure complex I activity, making them the most direct in vivo imaging method for assessing mitochondrial function. Complex I, as a key rate-limiting enzyme in oxidative phosphorylation, exhibits reduced activity that is a common feature of mitochondrial dysfunction in most psychiatric disorders. The most mature tracer, ¹⁸F-BCPP-EF, has a binding potential that can directly reflect the expression and activity of complex I. Through dynamic PET combined with kinetic modeling, absolute quantification of complex I activity in different brain regions can be achieved (Harada et al., 2013). However, this technique also has several limitations. The synthesis of the tracer is complex, and currently only a few research centers can independently produce it, resulting in limited accessibility. The gold standard for kinetic modeling requires arterial blood sampling (invasive); although alternative methods such as Logan graphical analysis can lower the threshold, metabolic correction remains necessary (Tsukada et al., 2016; Mansur et al., 2020). Third, brain uptake of the tracer is affected by blood flow perfusion, necessitating simultaneous acquisition of arterial spin labeling for correction (Tsukada et al., 2014b). Fourth, due to the resolution limitations of PET equipment, precise localization of subnuclei remains challenging. The application of ¹⁸F-BCPP-EF PET in psychiatric disorders is still in its early stages. Existing evidence includes reduced complex I activity in the anterior cingulate cortex in ASD patients and reduced activity in multiple brain regions in BD patients with comorbid AUD (Kato et al., 2023; Wigstrom et al., 2024). However, research in other psychiatric disorders, such as SZ and MDD, remains extremely limited.

Additionally, several novel mitochondrial-targeted PET tracers are currently being validated in preclinical studies. For example, the structural analog ¹⁸F-BCPP-BF, which also targets complex I but exhibits higher brain uptake, has demonstrated imaging feasibility in animal models (Tsukada et al., 2014a). ¹⁸F-fluorobenzyltriphenylphosphonium (¹⁸F-FBnTP), which targets mitochondrial membrane potential, has been used to assess mitochondrial function in myocardial and tumor tissues (Zeng et al., 2016). At present, PET tracers targeting mitochondrial dynamic proteins such as dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) and mitofusin 2 (Mfn2) remain in the early stages of development, and no mature tracers are currently available for in vivo imaging (Sharma et al., 2025).

MRI-based cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen

The CMRO₂ represents the amount of oxygen consumed by brain tissue per unit time and directly reflects the overall rate of mitochondrial oxygen consumption in brain tissue. Oxygen, as the final acceptor in the electron transport chain, is primarily consumed at complex IV. Therefore, CMRO₂ serves as a direct in vivo indicator for evaluating mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation function (Cherukara et al., 2019). Pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling (pCASL) combined with quantitative blood oxygen level-dependent (qBOLD) imaging is a noninvasive, radiation-free magnetic resonance technique that calculates CMRO₂ by integrating cerebral blood flow and oxygen extraction fraction based on the Fick principle. This method relies on endogenous contrast and can be repeated for measurement within the same subject, making it suitable for longitudinal studies. It can also be combined with task-based fMRI to dynamically monitor regional mitochondrial metabolic responses induced by cognitive or emotional activities. However, CMRO₂ calculation relies on multiple parameter assumptions, and quantitative accuracy is influenced by signal-to-noise ratio and model simplifications. Different calculation methods, such as cBOLD (based on qBOLD signal), sqBOLD, and QSM + qBOLD (based on combined quantitative susceptibility mapping), differ in their underlying assumptions and application scopes and are highly sensitive to noise, which restricts their clinical translation (Biondetti et al., 2023). Currently, MRI techniques for measuring CMRO₂ have been applied to assess brain oxygen metabolism in conditions such as carotid artery stenosis and glioblastoma, and the normal range of CMRO₂ has been established in studies of healthy individuals (Göttler et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2024). Among the studies included in this review, Russo et al. applied this technique to BD patients (Russo et al., 2024). However, applications in other common psychiatric disorders remain scarce and require further investigation.

Indirect assessment of mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondria-related metabolites by ¹H-MRS

¹H-MRS quantitatively measures the concentrations and ratios of metabolites closely related to mitochondrial function in the brain, thereby indirectly reflecting mitochondrial energy metabolism disorders, oxidative stress levels, and neuronal integrity. Compared with ³¹P-MRS, ¹H-MRS uses hydrogen nuclei as the detection target. Owing to its straightforward operation and high signal sensitivity, it has a broader range of applications. The mitochondria-related metabolites detectable by ¹H-MRS mainly include the following: Lac, the end product of anaerobic metabolism, which may indicate impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation function and compensatory enhancement of glycolysis (Lin et al., 2003); and GSH, the most important antioxidant in the brain, whose level changes reflect the oxidative stress state originating from mitochondria (Chen et al., 2024). In addition, NAA, which is synthesized by neuronal mitochondria, serves as another detectable metabolite. A decrease in NAA concentration may indicate neuronal mitochondrial dysfunction or neuronal loss following neuroinflammation (Ariyannur et al., 2008). Therefore, interpretation of NAA abnormalities requires comprehensive assessment in conjunction with the specific disease context, multimodal imaging (such as DWI or ³¹P-MRS), or laboratory tests.

A limitation of ¹H-MRS is that it measures metabolite concentrations, which are influenced by multiple factors. Moreover, as low-concentration metabolites, GSH and Lac have insufficient detection sensitivity at conventional 3T field strength, requiring high field strength or spectral editing techniques for reliable quantification. Additionally, traditional single-voxel MRS cannot simultaneously cover multiple brain regions, whereas multi-voxel MRS achieves spatial coverage but with reduced data reliability due to magnetic field inhomogeneity and signal contamination between voxels (Mescher et al., 1998; Tkác et al., 2001; Dager et al., 2008; Posse et al., 2013; Sanaei Nezhad et al., 2017). The ¹H-MRS data included in this review indicate abnormal coupling of glutamate and glutathione in the prefrontal cortex of SZ patients, whereas elevated lactate levels in the posterior cingulate gyrus have been observed in ASD patients (Maier et al., 2023; Jeon et al., 2025). Furthermore, lithium has been shown to reduce lactate levels in the anterior cingulate gyrus of BD patients, suggesting that ¹H-MRS can monitor treatment responses (Machado-Vieira et al., 2017).

T1ρ imaging and MEMRI

T1ρ (spin-lattice relaxation time in the rotating frame) imaging is an MRI technique sensitive to proton chemical exchange, enabling the detection of microenvironmental changes such as intracellular pH, metabolite concentration, and cell density (Heo et al., 2014). Studies have shown that T1ρ values may increase in the context of cellular acidosis (Magnotta et al., 2012). Since acidosis is often associated with impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and enhanced anaerobic metabolism, T1ρ may serve as a potential marker of mitochondrial dysfunction. However, an increase in T1ρ values may also be caused by changes in cerebral blood volume, protein content, or cell density. Therefore, an increase in T1ρ values cannot be directly equated with acidosis or mitochondrial dysfunction (Heo et al., 2014; Bustin et al., 2023). A better strategy is to conduct multi-parameter validation using ³¹P-MRS. If both methods detect consistent acidification and abnormal energy metabolism in the same region, they can serve as complementary evidence for each other. Magnotta et al. confirmed in BD-I patients that the increase in T1ρ values in the cerebellum was consistent with the decreased pH and decreased α-ATP levels detected by ³¹P-MRS, suggesting mitochondrial dysfunction (Magnotta et al., 2022).

MEMRI detects the directional transport of Mn²⁺ within neurons based on the paramagnetic properties of Mn²⁺. As a calcium ion analog, Mn²⁺ can enter neurons through voltage-gated calcium channels and be transported along microtubules in the anterograde direction. Based on a comparison between MEMRI and video microscopy, Bertrand et al. proposed that the manganese transport rate is similar to the mitochondrial transport rate and is dependent on the microtubule motor protein kinesin-1. Therefore, MEMRI signals may indirectly reflect mitochondrial transport abnormalities (Bertrand et al., 2011; Medina et al., 2017). However, a current limitation is that MEMRI requires the use of Mn²⁺ as an exogenous tracer, which poses a potential neurotoxic risk, and is therefore mainly limited to preclinical studies. Second, the Mn²⁺ transport detected by MEMRI reflects overall neuronal transport (including axons and dendrites) rather than specifically reflecting mitochondrial transport. Therefore, MEMRI is best suited for combination with ex vivo techniques such as immunohistochemistry or electron microscopy to verify whether changes in Mn²⁺ signals truly reflect mitochondrial transport abnormalities. Among the studies included in this review, Luo et al. utilized MEMRI and immunohistochemistry to observe that, in heroin-addicted rats, the reduction in ATR was positively correlated with the expression levels of mitochondrial energy metabolism enzymes (Luo et al., 2022).

Direct assessment of neuroinflammation

TSPO-PET

TSPO-PET targets the 18 kDa translocator protein located on the outer mitochondrial membrane of activated glial cells for neuroinflammation imaging. The binding potential of the tracer can quantify the degree of neuroinflammation in the brain (Scarf and Kassiou, 2011). Compared with the first-generation tracer ¹¹C-PK11195, second-generation tracers such as ¹¹C-DPA-713, ¹¹C-PBR28, and ¹⁸F-FEPPA exhibit higher brain uptake rates and sensitivity and are currently the mainstream tools for neuroinflammation imaging (Jain et al., 2020). The advantage of this technique lies in its ability to directly measure glial cell activation levels throughout the brain, and it has been validated through applications in various psychiatric disorders. However, it should be noted that although TSPO is regarded as a representative marker of microglial activation, it is also expressed in various other cell types, such as astrocytes and vascular endothelial cells. Therefore, its signal cannot distinguish the cellular source of glial activation (Nutma et al., 2019). Second, the rs6971 polymorphism of the TSPO gene affects tracer binding affinity. Therefore, genotype-based grouping and correction are necessary in TSPO-PET studies (Owen et al., 2015). Furthermore, an increase in TSPO signal does not directly equate to “proinflammatory” neuroinflammation; it may instead reflect any state of immune response. This review incorporates multiple TSPO-PET imaging studies covering psychiatric disorders such as SZ, PTSD, ASD, and sleep disorders. By integrating clinical symptom assessment, peripheral inflammatory markers, and behavioral parameters, these studies have revealed that TSPO signals exhibit bidirectional changes that are specific to both disease and brain region, suggesting heterogeneity in the phenotypic expression of neuroinflammation.

MAO-B PET and other novel neuroinflammation tracers

Apart from TSPO-PET, MAO-B PET is another mature tool for neuroinflammation imaging. MAO-B is highly expressed on the outer mitochondrial membrane of astrocytes. The uptake levels of its mature tracers, such as ¹¹C-SL25.1188 and ¹⁸F-DED, can reflect the metabolic state of astrocytes. This technique serves as an important complement to TSPO-PET and enables specific assessment of astrocytes rather than glial cells in general (Rusjan et al., 2014; Ballweg et al., 2023). However, MAO-B mainly reflects the metabolic state of astrocytes rather than directly measuring their activation level, and its expression patterns vary across different brain regions and pathological conditions. The MAO-B PET studies included in this review cover PTSD and SUD patients. In addition, novel tracers targeting glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a marker of astrocyte activation, are emerging as a research hotspot. These tracers are mainly divided into antibody fragments and small molecule probes and are currently in the preclinical validation stage (Bargagna-Mohan et al., 2010; Hol and Pekny, 2015; Meier et al., 2022).

Targeted imaging of inflammatory molecules and the complement system provides a complementary approach for understanding the neuroimmune microenvironment. Naik et al. were the first to report a PET tracer targeting the complement C3a receptor, [¹⁸F]1. In a mouse model of neuroinflammation, brain uptake of this tracer was 2.2 times higher than that in the control group (Naik et al., 2025). Meanwhile, studies on PET tracers targeting pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6 have also been reported. Dmochowska et al. used ⁸⁹Zr-labeled anti-IL-1β antibodies and found that tracer uptake in colitis mouse models was approximately three times higher than that in the control group (Dmochowska et al., 2019). In conclusion, PET imaging targeting immune effector molecules demonstrates significant potential for clinical application.

Indirect assessment of neuroinflammation

Glia-related metabolites by ¹H-MRS

¹H-MRS can also detect metabolites closely related to glial cell activation, thereby indirectly reflecting cell proliferation and metabolic changes associated with neuroinflammation. Among these metabolites, mIns is mainly localized in astrocytes, and an increase in its concentration is typically regarded as a marker of astrocyte proliferation. Cho is involved in cell membrane phospholipid metabolism and is present at relatively high concentrations in glial cells; an increase in its level may reflect glial cell activation or accelerated membrane turnover (Chang et al., 2013). Jung et al. further discovered that Cho levels were positively correlated with the expression of the astrocyte marker GFAP (Jung et al., 2020). As indirect evidence, changes in mIns and Cho are not specific markers of neuroinflammation. An increase in mIns may also be observed in osmoregulatory disorders, whereas an increase in Cho may result from demyelination, cell proliferation, or abnormal membrane metabolism. When these indicators are interpreted in conjunction with TSPO-PET, diffusion MRI, or other clinical measures, the specificity of their attribution to neuroinflammation can be enhanced. Among the studies included in this review, Mueller et al. found that, in patients with sleep disorders, increases in Cho and mIns in the frontal lobe were associated with slower processing speed and lower sleep efficiency. Bravi et al. found an increase in mIns in BD patients, which was negatively correlated with white matter integrity (Bravi et al., 2022; Mueller et al., 2024).

White matter microstructural alterations by diffusion MRI

Diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging indirectly reflects microstructural alterations resulting from neuroinflammation by detecting changes in the diffusion properties of water molecules in brain tissue. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) is currently the most widely used diffusion technique and can quantify the anisotropy of water molecule diffusion across multiple directions. More than a decade ago, DTI was already being used in neuroinflammation research on diseases such as multiple sclerosis and SZ, enabling the detection of white matter microstructural alterations that appear normal on conventional MRI (Cercignani et al., 2001; Ellison-Wright and Bullmore, 2009). Nevertheless, DTI has limited resolution in regions with crossing fibers, and its parameters are insufficient to distinguish between inflammatory cell infiltration, demyelination, and axonal loss. Subsequent diffusion models have made progress in overcoming these limitations. DBSI can simultaneously assess white matter integrity and inflammatory cell infiltration by separating fiber and cellular signals. Its restricted fraction is highly correlated with glial cell density (Kéri, 2025). NODDI employs a multi-shell diffusion model to distinguish among intra-axonal, extra-axonal, and cerebrospinal fluid compartments. It quantifies fiber orientation dispersion and neurite density, providing superior resolution in regions with crossing fibers compared to DTI (Zhang et al., 2012; Schneider et al., 2017).

Furthermore, diffusion imaging can also capture inflammation-related hydrodynamic changes. Multi-b-value diffusion acquisition based on the intravoxel incoherent motion model separates intrinsic water diffusion from microcirculatory perfusion components. An elevated perfusion fraction may indirectly reflect increased microvascular density and altered blood flow perfusion in inflammatory regions (Le Bihan et al., 1988; Podgórska et al., 2021). Diffusion imaging of the perivascular space provides a new approach for evaluating inflammation-related impairment of fluid clearance (Brown et al., 2018; Mogensen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025b).

In summary, changes in diffusion parameters can sensitively reflect white matter damage caused by neuroinflammation, but it remains difficult to distinguish the specific contributions of distinct pathological processes such as cell infiltration, demyelination, axonal loss, and edema. Although advanced diffusion models (e.g. DBSI and NODDI) can improve certain aspects of pathological specificity, they require longer acquisition times, which limits their clinical application. The diffusion MRI studies included in this review have consistently revealed white matter microstructural damage related to neuroinflammation in SZ, ASD, and BD patients.

Integrative analysis of structural/functional imaging and transcriptomics

Structural and functional MRI have been widely applied in brain imaging studies of psychiatric disorders. Building on this, imaging transcriptomics enables spatial correlation analysis between imaging phenotypes (such as gray matter volume, cortical thickness, and functional connectivity strength) and brain region-specific gene expression profiles. This approach can identify genes and biological pathways enriched for mitochondrial dysfunction or neuroinflammation, thereby providing microscopic molecular explanations for macroscopic imaging findings. This method is primarily based on the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA). By correlating the spatial distribution of imaging phenotypes with AHBA gene expression profiles, it can identify biological pathways and cell types associated with disease-related changes in specific brain regions. The application of imaging transcriptomics in psychiatric disorder research is gradually expanding (Liu et al., 2026). Among the studies included in this review, Bhatt et al. combined TSPO-PET with postmortem brain tissue transcriptome analysis in PTSD patients to validate the in vivo PET findings (Bhatt et al., 2020). Rahayel et al. and Zhou et al. also adopted an imaging-transcriptome integration strategy, revealing associations between structural changes and genes related to mitochondrial function and neuroinflammation in iRBD and MDD patients (Rahayel et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2026).

However, the AHBA contains brain tissue data from only six individuals, making it difficult to assess individual differences. Furthermore, the gene expression data are derived from postmortem adult brain tissues and cannot reflect dynamic changes in disease processes. Additionally, the bulk tissue technique cannot distinguish the contributions of distinct cell types, requiring validation through integration with single-cell data. Nevertheless, imaging transcriptomics remains a crucial bridge connecting macroscopic imaging phenotypes with underlying microscopic molecular mechanisms.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Current imaging studies have shown that mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation exhibit transdiagnostic commonalities across various psychiatric disorders, including brain energy metabolism disorders, alterations in glial cell immune status, and inflammation-related white matter microstructural damage. These findings provide evidence for understanding the underlying disease mechanisms. However, most current evidence comes from single-target imaging studies conducted in different research cohorts. No study has yet simultaneously completed dual-target, specific imaging of both mitochondrial function and neuroinflammation within the same cohort of psychiatric disorder patients. The causal relationship between the two processes has not yet been elucidated. Therefore, the above conceptualization should be regarded as a hypothesis-generating framework rather than an established transdiagnostic pathological axis. Meanwhile, certain contradictions exist between different diseases or across study findings. In PTSD, TSPO signals have shown various patterns, including decreased levels, no change, or a blunted response following LPS stimulation. In ASD, a sex-dependent pattern has been observed, with decreased TSPO signals in males and increased signals in females. These observations suggest that TSPO findings may be influenced by the interaction of multiple factors, including sample heterogeneity, disease subtype, disease stage, sex, and comorbidity status.

Based on the above gaps, future research should focus on the following. First, simultaneous dual-target imaging of mitochondrial function and neuroinflammation should be conducted within the same cohort of patients with a given psychiatric disorder to elucidate the dynamic relationship between the two processes. For example, Rejc et al. simultaneously applied ¹⁸F-DPA-714 and ¹⁸F-FSPG for PET imaging in a mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease and found that oxidative stress may precede neuroinflammation, thereby providing a research paradigm for the design of dual-target approaches (Rejc et al., 2022). Second, study designs should systematically incorporate variables such as sex, disease subtype, disease stage, comorbidity status, and substance use history to analyze the sources of heterogeneity. Third, direct imaging of mitochondrial function is urgently needed in PTSD patients, whereas PET studies of mitochondrial complexes in other psychiatric disorders remain scarce and require further expansion. Fourth, combined with mechanistic experiments and intervention studies, the plasticity of the mitochondria–inflammation axis in response to pharmacological or lifestyle interventions should be validated. Through these efforts, we can expect to advance research on the mechanisms of psychiatric disorders and their clinical applications, thereby providing a scientific basis for precise diagnosis and targeted treatment.
