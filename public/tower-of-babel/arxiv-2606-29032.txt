Representations of compatible Lie algebras
Xabier García-Martínez, Manuel Ladra, Bernardo Leite da Cunha, Samuel A. Lopes

Abstract
We study compatible Lie algebras from algebraic and representation-theoretic points of view, obtaining counterexamples to some fundamental theorems from classical Lie algebra theory, namely the theorems of Lie, Weyl and Levi. We also classify the two-dimensional compatible Lie algebras up to isomorphism and explore their representation theory, presenting families of indecomposable non-semisimple representations, showing that the solvable two-dimensional compatible Lie algebras have wild representation type, and classifying all irreducible finite-dimensional line representations. Finally, we prove a Clebsch–Gordan decomposition for tensor products of finite-dimensional irreducible line representations.



1
Introduction

Given a vector space
\mathfrak{g}
endowed with two Lie products
\left[-,-\right]
and
\left\{-,-\right\}
, we say that they are
compatible
if each linear combination
\lambda\left[-,-\right]+\mu\left\{-,-\right\}
is still a Lie product, or equivalently, if the following identity holds for any
x,y,z\in\mathfrak{g}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\{\left[x,y\right],z\right\}+\left\{\left[y,z\right],x\right\}+\left\{\left[z,x\right],y\right\}\\
&+\left[\left\{x,y\right\},z\right]+\left[\left\{y,z\right\},x\right]+\left[\left\{z,x\right\},y\right]=0.\end{split}

(1.1)

A vector space with two compatible Lie products is called a
compatible Lie algebra
. These structures arise in several areas of mathematics and mathematical physics, including the study of the classical Yang–Baxter equation
[
6
]
, integrable equations of the principal chiral model type
[
4
]
, elliptic theta functions
[
13
]
, and loop algebras over Lie algebras
[
5
]
.

When setting
\lambda=1
instead of considering general linear combinations
\lambda\left[-,-\right]+\mu\left\{-,-\right\}
, the resulting product can be seen as an infinitesimal deformation of
\left[-,-\right]
. This follows by observing that
Equation

1.1
is simply the cocycle identity for the adjoint module. Thus, the study of compatible Lie algebras is also connected with deformation theory.

A related notion is that of bi-Hamiltonian structures, that is, pairs of compatible Poisson brackets defined on the same manifold. These play an essential role in the theory of integrable systems in mathematical physics. Operads of compatible Lie algebras and bi-Hamiltonian algebras have been studied by Dotsenko and Khoroshkin in
[
2
]
.

A broader notion is that of compatible algebraic structures. Two algebraic structures of the same type (i.e. both associative algebras, Lie algebras, Leibniz algebras, etc.)
(V,\circ)
and
(V,\ast)
with the same underlying vector space are said to be compatible if
(V,\lambda\cdot\circ+\mu\cdot\ast)
has the same type of algebraic structure as
(V,\circ)
and
(V,\ast)
for any scalars
\lambda,\mu
. Some non-Lie examples in the literature include compatible associative algebras in
[
12
]
and
[
14
]
, compatible associative bialgebras in
[
11
]
, compatible Lie bialgebras in
[
17
]
and compatible Leibniz algebras in
[
10
]
and
[
18
]
. General compatible structures have been studied from an operadic point of view in
[
16
]
.

The classification of compatible Lie algebras is an increasingly active area of research. The algebraic classification of low-dimensional nilpotent compatible Lie algebras was obtained in
[
7
]
, and the geometric classification in
[
1
]
.
A method of constructing solvable compatible Lie algebras from nilpotent ones was presented by Ouaridi, Navarro, Omirov, and Solijanova in
[
15
]
.

This article is structured as follows. In
Section

2
we recall the definitions of compatible Lie algebras and their representations, and establish basic structural properties.
In
Section

3
, we show that three classical results of Lie theory, namely Lie’s Theorem, Weyl’s complete reducibility Theorem and the Levi decomposition, fail in the compatible setting, highlighting the interest and more subtle nature of compatible Lie algebra representations.

In
Section

4
, we classify the two-dimensional compatible Lie algebras and explore their representation theory. For the solvable ones, we show that the representation type is wild. Focusing next on the unique simple compatible Lie algebra of dimension two, we obtain a family of finite-dimensional irreducible representations, which we name finite-dimensional irreducible line representations.

We end with
Section

5
, wherein we study tensor products of finite-dimensional irreducible line representations, establishing a Clebsch–Gordan decomposition formula.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Compatible Lie algebras

In this section, we start by defining
compatible Lie algebras
and recalling their basic properties. We let
\mathbb{K}
be an arbitrary field of characteristic different from
2
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}=(\mathfrak{g},\left[-,-\right])
and
\undertilde{\mathfrak{g}}=(\mathfrak{g},\left\{-,-\right\})
be two Lie algebras over the same vector space
\mathfrak{g}
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

1.

(\mathfrak{g},\left\llbracket-,-\right\rrbracket)
is a Lie algebra, where
\left\llbracket x,y\right\rrbracket=\left[x,y\right]+\left\{x,y\right\}
for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
;

2.

(\mathfrak{g},\left\llbracket-,-\right\rrbracket_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}})
is a Lie algebra for all
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathbb{K}
, where
\left\llbracket x,y\right\rrbracket_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}=\lambda\left[x,y\right]+\lambda^{\prime}\left\{x,y\right\}
for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
;

3.

The following identity (named the
mixed Jacobi identity
) holds for all
x,y,z\in\mathfrak{g}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\{\left[x,y\right],z\right\}+\left\{\left[y,z\right],x\right\}+\left\{\left[z,x\right],y\right\}\\
&+\left[\left\{x,y\right\},z\right]+\left[\left\{y,z\right\},x\right]+\left[\left\{z,x\right\},y\right]=0.\end{split}

(2.1)

With this, we have the definition of compatible Lie algebras.

Definition 2.2
.

A
compatible Lie algebra
is a triple
(\mathfrak{g},\left[-,-\right],\left\{-,-\right\})
, where
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}=(\mathfrak{g},\left[-,-\right])
and
\undertilde{\mathfrak{g}}=(\mathfrak{g},\left\{-,-\right\})
are Lie algebras satisfying any of the three equivalent conditions in
2.1
.

We refer the reader to
[
7
, Section 2]
for some basic concrete examples of compatible Lie algebras.

Definition 2.3
.

A
compatible Lie algebra homomorphism
between two compatible Lie algebras
(\mathfrak{g},\left[-,-\right]_{\mathfrak{g}},\left\{-,-\right\}_{\mathfrak{g}})
and
(\mathfrak{h},\left[-,-\right]_{\mathfrak{h}},\left\{-,-\right\}_{\mathfrak{h}})
is a linear map
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{h}
which is a Lie algebra homomorphism between
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}
and
\underline{\mathfrak{h}}
, and a Lie algebra homomorphism between
\undertilde{\mathfrak{g}}
and
\undertilde{\mathfrak{h}}
. As usual, an invertible homomorphism is an
isomorphism
.

The classical definitions carry over to the compatible case:

Definition 2.4
.

•

A
subalgebra
of a compatible Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is a vector subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
which is closed for both products.

•

An
ideal

\mathfrak{i}
of a compatible Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is a vector subspace such that

\left[\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{g}\right],\left\{\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{g}\right\}\subseteq\mathfrak{i}.

•

A compatible Lie algebra is said to be
abelian
if both its products are trivial, or equivalently, if both of its component Lie algebras are abelian Lie algebras.

•

A
simple
compatible Lie algebra is one that is non-abelian and contains no non-trivial ideals.

•

The
centre
of a compatible Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, denoted by
Z(\mathfrak{g})
, is the ideal defined by

Z(\mathfrak{g})=\left\{x\in\mathfrak{g}\mid\left[x,\mathfrak{g}\right]=0=\left\{x,\mathfrak{g}\right\}\right\}=Z(\underline{\mathfrak{g}})\cap Z(\undertilde{\mathfrak{g}}).

As expected, the kernel of a homomorphism is an ideal of the domain and the image of a homomorphism is a subalgebra of the codomain. The notion of quotient is well defined and the usual isomorphism theorems hold.

2.2
Representations of compatible Lie algebras

As in the classical case, there is a notion of representation for compatible Lie algebras. In this section, we give the basic definitions in the compatible context.

Definition 2.5

(
[
17
]
)
.

A
representation
of a compatible Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is a triple
(V,\rho,\mu)
, where
(V,\rho)
is a representation of
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}
,
(V,\mu)
is a representation of
\undertilde{\mathfrak{g}}
, and
(V,\rho+\mu)
is a representation of
(\mathfrak{g},\left\llbracket-,-\right\rrbracket)
.

Remark 2.6
.

The previous definition is equivalent to requiring that the following identities hold for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
.

\displaystyle\rho(\left[x,y\right])

\displaystyle=\rho(x)\rho(y)-\rho(y)\rho(x);

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mu(\left\{x,y\right\})

\displaystyle=\mu(x)\mu(y)-\mu(y)\mu(x);

(2.3)

\displaystyle\rho(\left\{x,y\right\})+\mu(\left[x,y\right])

\displaystyle=\rho(x)\mu(y)-\mu(y)\rho(x)+\mu(x)\rho(y)-\rho(y)\mu(x).

(2.4)

Example 2.7

(Adjoint representation)
.

Let
\underline{\mathsf{ad}}
denote the adjoint representation of
(\mathfrak{g},\left[-,-\right])
and
\undertilde{\mathsf{ad}}
denote the adjoint representation of
(\mathfrak{g},\left\{-,-\right\})
. Then the triple
(\mathfrak{g},\underline{\mathsf{ad}},\undertilde{\mathsf{ad}})
is a representation of
\mathfrak{g}
called the
adjoint representation
. The compatibility condition between
\underline{\mathsf{ad}}
and
\undertilde{\mathsf{ad}}
is precisely the mixed Jacobi identity.

The definitions of
subrepresentation
,
irreducible representation
and
indecomposable representation
are the obvious generalisations of the classical counterparts.

2.3
Solvable and semisimple compatible Lie algebras

Note that the results of this section hold, mutatis mutandis, for compatible algebras of any type for which a notion of commutator of subalgebras is available. In fact, the proofs are very similar to the classical case, so we do not include them in the text.

Recall the commutator of subalgebras

\left\llbracket\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t}\right\rrbracket=\left\langle\left[s,t\right],\left\{s,t\right\}\mid s\in\mathfrak{s},t\in\mathfrak{t}\right\rangle.

Using this commutator, we may define the following series

\mathfrak{g}^{(0)}\supseteq\mathfrak{g}^{(1)}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\mathfrak{g}^{(i)}\supseteq\cdots,

where

\mathfrak{g}^{(0)}:=\mathfrak{g}\text{ and }\mathfrak{g}^{(i+1)}=\left\llbracket\mathfrak{g}^{(i)},\mathfrak{g}^{(i)}\right\rrbracket.

Each term of this series is an ideal of the previous one, and each quotient is abelian. A straightforward induction proof shows that

(\mathfrak{g}^{(i)})^{(j)}=\mathfrak{g}^{(i+j)}.

Definition 2.8
.

A compatible Lie algebra is said to be
solvable
if
\mathfrak{g}^{(i)}=0
for some
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a compatible Lie algebra. Then

1.

if
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{h}
is a surjective homomorphism, then
\varphi(\mathfrak{g}^{(i)})=\mathfrak{h}^{(i)}
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
;

2.

if
\mathfrak{h}
is a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, then
\mathfrak{h}^{(i)}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{(i)}
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
;

3.

if
\mathfrak{i}
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
, then
\mathfrak{g}
is solvable if and only if both
\mathfrak{i}
and
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{i}
are solvable.

With the aid of this lemma, we are now able to give the following definition and to prove the next corollary.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional compatible Lie algebra. The set of all solvable ideals of
\mathfrak{g}
is nonempty and has a unique maximal element called its
radical
, which we will denote by
\mathsf{rad}(\mathfrak{g})
. We say that a compatible Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is
semisimple
if
\mathsf{rad}(\mathfrak{g})=\left\{0\right\}
.

Corollary 2.11
.

1.

For any compatible Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, the compatible Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}/\mathsf{rad}(\mathfrak{g})
is semisimple.

2.

A direct sum of simple compatible Lie algebras is semisimple.

3
Counterexamples to the theorems of Lie, Weyl and Levi

Three of the most well-known theorems from the theory of Lie algebras are Lie’s Theorem, Weyl’s Theorem and Levi’s Theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(Lie’s Theorem)
.

Suppose
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed and of characteristic
0
. The finite-dimensional irreducible representations of a finite-dimensional solvable Lie algebra have dimension
1
.

Theorem 3.2

(Weyl’s Theorem)
.

Suppose
\mathbb{K}
has characteristic
0
. Any finite-dimensional representation of a finite-dimensional simple Lie algebra is completely reducible.

Theorem 3.3

(Levi’s Theorem)
.

Suppose
\mathbb{K}
has characteristic
0
. A finite-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
has a subalgebra isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}/\mathsf{rad}(\mathfrak{g})
so that
\mathfrak{g}
admits a semidirect product decomposition
\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{s}\ltimes\mathsf{rad}(\mathfrak{g})
where
\mathfrak{s}
is a semisimple subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
. Such a subalgebra
\mathfrak{s}
is called a
Levi subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
.

In the case of compatible Lie algebras, these theorems fail to hold. To construct counterexamples, we make use of the following compatible Lie algebras (see
Theorem

4.1
).

Definition 3.4
.

Let
CL_{2,1}
be the two-dimensional compatible Lie algebra with basis
{x,y}
and nonzero products between basis elements

\left[x,y\right]=x.

Let
CL_{2,4}
be the two-dimensional compatible Lie algebra with basis
{x,y}
and nonzero products given by

\left[x,y\right]=x,\quad\left\{x,y\right\}=y.

The compatible Lie algebras
CL_{2,1}
and
CL_{2,4}
are solvable and simple, respectively, and they will provide a counterexample to Lie’s Theorem and to Weyl’s and Levi’s Theorems, respectively.

3.1
The failure of Lie’s and Weyl’s theorems

Proposition 3.5
.

The compatible Lie algebra
CL_{2,1}
admits irreducible representations of each finite dimension greater than
1
. Therefore, Lie’s Theorem fails to hold in the class of compatible Lie algebras.

Proof.

Some families of such representations will be constructed in
Section

4.3
.
∎

We note that we show a stronger result, that all solvable two-dimensional compatible Lie algebras have wild representation type.

Proposition 3.6
.

The compatible Lie algebra
CL_{2,4}
admits non-simple and indecomposable representations of each finite dimension greater than
1
. Therefore, Weyl’s Theorem fails to hold in the class of compatible Lie algebras.

Proof.

Some families of such representations will be constructed in
Section

4.4
.
∎

3.2
Counterexample to Levi’s theorem

Let us note that to find a counterexample to Levi’s Theorem it suffices to find a compatible Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
that is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}/\mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{g})\oplus\mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{g})
as a vector space but admits no subalgebra isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}/\mathrm{rad}(\mathfrak{g})
.

The simplest possible case is a one-dimensional abelian extension, so let
V=\mathbb{C}z
. A direct computation shows that the only possible one-dimensional representations of
CL_{2,4}
are the following:

\rho(x)z=0,\quad\rho(y)z=-\lambda z,\quad\mu(x)z=\lambda z,\quad\mu(y)z=0.

For simplicity, we take
\lambda=1
.

To build an extension, we use a cocycle of
\mathfrak{g}
with values in
(V,\rho,\mu)
, i.e. a pair of bilinear alternating maps
\omega=(\underline{\omega},\undertilde{\omega})\colon\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow~V
that satisfy the cocycle identities:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\>\underline{\omega}(\left[x,y\right],z)+\underline{\omega}(\left[z,x\right],y)+\underline{\omega}(\left[y,z\right],x),

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\>\undertilde{\omega}(\left\{x,y\right\},z)+\undertilde{\omega}(\left\{z,x\right\},y)+\undertilde{\omega}(\left\{y,z\right\},x),

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\>\underline{\omega}(\left\{x,y\right\},z)+\underline{\omega}(\left\{z,x\right\},y)+\underline{\omega}(\left\{y,z\right\},x)+\>\undertilde{\omega}(\left[x,y\right],z)+\undertilde{\omega}(\left[z,x\right],y)+\undertilde{\omega}(\left[y,z\right],x),

for all
x,y,z\in\mathfrak{g}
.

We refer the reader to Liu, Sheng, and Bai
[
8
]
for the general theory of compatible cohomology.

Since
CL_{2,4}
is two-dimensional, any pair of bilinear alternating maps satisfies these identities, and since
V
is one-dimensional, the only such maps are those of the form

\underline{\omega}(x,y)=pz,\quad\undertilde{\omega}(x,y)=qz,\quad\text{for some }p,q\in\mathbb{C}.

We let
p=-1
,
q=0
, so that
\underline{\omega}(x,y)=-z,~\undertilde{\omega}(x,y)=0
.

The general definition of abelian extension given in
[
8
, Section 4.2.]
appears to contain a sign error; with the corrected formula, the products on
\mathfrak{g}=\left\langle x,y,z\right\rangle=CL_{2,4}\oplus\mathbb{C}z
are as follows:

\displaystyle\left[(a,u),(b,v)\right]_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle=(\left[a,b\right]_{CL_{2,4}},\rho(a)v-\rho(b)u-\underline{\omega}(a,b))

\displaystyle\left\{(a,u),(b,v)\right\}_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle=(\left\{a,b\right\}_{CL_{2,4}},\mu(a)v-\mu(b)u-\undertilde{\omega}(a,b)),\text{ for }a,b\in CL_{2,4},u,v\in\mathbb{C}z.

With our particular choices of representation and cocycle, we end up with the following non-zero products on the basis elements:

\displaystyle\left[x,y\right]

\displaystyle=x+z,\quad

\displaystyle\left[y,z\right]

\displaystyle=-z,

\displaystyle\left\{x,y\right\}

\displaystyle=y,

\displaystyle\left\{x,z\right\}

\displaystyle=z.

We now proceed to show that this algebra is indeed a counterexample to Levi’s Theorem, by proving the following:

Proposition 3.7
.

The compatible Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with basis
{x,y,z}
and relations

\displaystyle\left[x,y\right]=x+z,\quad\left[y,z\right]=-z,\qquad\left\{x,y\right\}=y,\quad\left\{x,z\right\}=z,

has no subalgebra isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}/\mathsf{rad}(\mathfrak{g})
. Thus, Levi’s Theorem fails to hold in the class of compatible Lie algebras.

Proof.

It can be directly checked that
\left\langle z\right\rangle
is a solvable ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
. Since
\mathfrak{g}/\left\langle z\right\rangle\cong CL_{2,4}
is semisimple, it follows that
\mathsf{rad}(\mathfrak{g})=\left\langle z\right\rangle
.

Now, assume that we have a subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\left\langle X=ax+by+cz,Y=a^{\prime}x+b^{\prime}y+c^{\prime}z\right\rangle
of
\mathfrak{g}
such that
\mathfrak{g}\cong\left\langle z\right\rangle\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{0}\cong CL_{2,4}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\left[X,Y\right]=X
and
\left\{X,Y\right\}=Y
.

Computing the expression
\left[X,Y\right]
we obtain

\displaystyle\left[X,Y\right]

\displaystyle=\left[ax+by+cz,a^{\prime}x+b^{\prime}y+c^{\prime}z\right]=ab^{\prime}\left[x,y\right]+ba^{\prime}\left[y,x\right]+bc^{\prime}\left[y,z\right]+cb^{\prime}\left[z,y\right]

\displaystyle=(ab^{\prime}-ba^{\prime})(x+z)+(cb^{\prime}-c^{\prime}b)z.

Setting this equal to
X=ax+by+cz
and comparing coefficients yields the following linear system of equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a=ab^{\prime}-ba^{\prime}\\
b=0\\
c=ab^{\prime}-ba^{\prime}+cb^{\prime}-bc^{\prime}\end{cases}\Leftrightarrow\begin{cases}a=ab^{\prime}\\
b=0\\
c=ab^{\prime}+cb^{\prime}\end{cases},

which yields either
b^{\prime}=1
or
a=c=0
. Since the set
\left\{z,X,Y\right\}
is required to be linearly independent, we must have
b^{\prime}=1
and thus
c=a+c
, so
a=0
and
X=cz
, a contradiction.
∎

4
Two-dimensional compatible Lie algebras and their representations

In this section, we classify all two-dimensional compatible Lie algebras and discuss their representations. In the case of two-dimensional solvable compatible Lie algebras, we show that they have wild representation type. We next focus on the representations of the only simple algebra of dimension
2
, obtaining a family of counterexamples to Weyl’s Theorem and classifying a family of irreducible representations that resemble the finite-dimensional representations of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.

4.1
Classification of two-dimensional compatible Lie algebras

We begin by providing a complete classification of two-dimensional compatible Lie algebras over an arbitrary field
\mathbb{K}
of characteristic not
2
.

Theorem 4.1
.

In dimension
2
, there are the following isomorphism classes of compatible Lie algebras:

•

CL_{2,0}
, abelian;

•

CL_{2,1}
, with nonzero brackets
\left[e_{1},e_{2}\right]=e_{1}
;

•

CL_{2,2}
, with nonzero brackets
\left\{e_{1},e_{2}\right\}=e_{1}
;

•

CL_{2,3}^{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
, with nonzero brackets
\left[e_{1},e_{2}\right]=e_{1}
,
\left\{e_{1},e_{2}\right\}=\alpha e_{1}
;

•

CL_{2,4}
, with nonzero brackets
\left[e_{1},e_{2}\right]=e_{1}
,
\left\{e_{1},e_{2}\right\}=e_{2}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that the mixed Jacobi identity (
Equation

2.1
) holds trivially for any pair of Lie brackets in a two-dimensional vector space. Thus, the algebras listed in
Theorem

4.1
are indeed compatible Lie algebras.

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a compatible Lie algebra of dimension
2
.

Suppose first that
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}
is not abelian. Then
\left[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}\right]
has dimension
1
, say
\left[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}\right]=\mathbb{K}e_{1}
for some nonzero element
e_{1}\in\mathfrak{g}
. Choose any
e_{2}\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
e_{1},e_{2}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{g}
. In particular, since
\left[e_{1},e_{2}\right]=\lambda e_{1}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
, we can replace
e_{2}
with
\lambda^{-1}e_{2}
and thus assume that
\left[e_{1},e_{2}\right]=e_{1}
.

Case 1:

\left\{\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{K}e_{1}
.

Then
\left\{e_{1},e_{2}\right\}=\alpha e_{1}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{K}
. If
\alpha=0
then
\mathfrak{g}\cong CL_{2,1}
, and if
\alpha\neq 0
then
\mathfrak{g}\cong CL_{2,3}^{\alpha}
.

Case 2:

\left\{\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}\right\}\not\subseteq\mathbb{K}e_{1}
.

Then, as
\dim\left\{\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}\right\}=1
, it follows that
\mathfrak{g}=\mathbb{K}e_{1}\oplus\mathbb{K}e_{2}
where
\left\{\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}\right\}=\mathbb{K}e_{2}
. So
\left\{e_{1},e_{2}\right\}=\lambda e_{2}
, for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
. If we replace
e_{1}
with
\lambda^{-1}e_{1}
we get
\left[e_{1},e_{2}\right]=e_{1}\text{ and }\left\{e_{1},e_{2}\right\}=e_{2},

so
\mathfrak{g}\cong CL_{2,4}
.

Now suppose that
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}
is abelian. If
\undertilde{\mathfrak{g}}
is not abelian, then by the argument at the beginning of the proof, we have
\mathfrak{g}\cong CL_{2,2}
. If both products are abelian,
\mathfrak{g}\cong CL_{2,0}
.

In terms of isomorphism classes, suppose that
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{h}
is an isomorphism of compatible Lie algebras. Then
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}
is abelian if and only if
\underline{\mathfrak{h}}
is abelian, and similarly for
\undertilde{\mathfrak{g}}
and
\undertilde{\mathfrak{h}}
. Thus, the algebras
CL_{2,0}
,
CL_{2,1}
and
CL_{2,2}
are in distinct isomorphism classes; moreover,
CL_{2,3}^{\alpha}
and
CL_{2,4}
are not isomorphic to any of the former three algebras. Hence, it remains to show that
CL_{2,3}^{\alpha}\cong CL_{2,3}^{\beta}
if and only if
\alpha=\beta
, and that
CL_{2,4}\not\cong CL_{2,3}^{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
.

Suppose that
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{h}
is an isomorphism of compatible Lie algebras, where
\mathfrak{g}=CL_{2,3}^{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{h}=CL_{2,3}^{\beta}
or
\mathfrak{h}=CL_{2,4}
.
Then,

\displaystyle=\varphi(e_{1})=\varphi(\left[e_{1},e_{2}\right]_{\mathfrak{g}})=\left[\varphi(e_{1}),\varphi(e_{2})\right]_{\mathfrak{h}}=\lambda\left[e_{1},\gamma e_{1}+\delta e_{2}\right]=\lambda\delta e_{1},

4.2
Representations of two-dimensional compatible Lie algebras

We start this section by stating and proving a lemma that will be useful.

Lemma 4.4
.

Given a compatible Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\left[-,-\right],\left\{-,-\right\})
, then, for any
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{K}
, the triple
(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime},\left[-,-\right]^{\prime},\left\{-,-\right\}^{\prime})
is a compatible Lie algebra, where
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{g}
as vector spaces, and the products of
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
are given by
\left[-,-\right]^{\prime}=\left[-,-\right]
and
\left\{-,-\right\}^{\prime}=\lambda_{1}\left[-,-\right]+\lambda_{2}\left\{-,-\right\}
.

Moreover, if
(V,\rho,\mu)
is a compatible representation of
\mathfrak{g}
, then
(V,\rho^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
is a representation of
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
, where
\rho^{\prime}=\rho
and
\mu^{\prime}=\lambda_{1}\rho+\lambda_{2}\mu
. Furthermore, the subrepresentations of
(V,\rho,\mu)
are also subrepresentations of
(V,\rho^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
, and in case
\lambda_{2}\neq 0
, the converse is also true.

Proof.

The first part of the statement is trivial, as any linear combination of
\left[-,-\right]^{\prime}
and
\left\{-,-\right\}^{\prime}
is also a linear combination of
\left[-,-\right]
and
\left\{-,-\right\}
, and thus a Lie product.

Now, to prove the second part of the statement, we just verify that
\rho^{\prime}
and
\mu^{\prime}
satisfy
Equations

2.2
to
2.4
.

Since
\left[-,-\right]^{\prime}=\left[-,-\right]
and
\rho^{\prime}=\rho
, it is immediate that
Equation

2.2
holds.

Now, let
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}(=\mathfrak{g})
. Consider the left-hand side of
Equation

2.3
for
\mu^{\prime}
and
\left\{-,-\right\}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}(\left\{x,y\right\}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mu^{\prime}(\lambda_{1}\left[x,y\right]+\lambda_{2}\left\{x,y\right\})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\rho(\lambda_{1}\left[x,y\right]+\lambda_{2}\left\{x,y\right\})+\lambda_{2}\mu(\lambda_{1}\left[x,y\right]+\lambda_{2}\left\{x,y\right\})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}^{2}\rho(\left[x,y\right])\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\big(\rho(\left\{x,y\right\})+\mu(\left[x,y\right])\big)+\lambda_{2}^{2}\mu(\left\{x,y\right\}).

Focus now on the right-hand side:

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}(x)\mu^{\prime}(y)-\mu^{\prime}(y)\mu^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda_{1}\rho(x)+\lambda_{2}\mu(x)\right)\left(\lambda_{1}\rho(y)+\lambda_{2}\mu(y)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\left(\lambda_{1}\rho(y)+\lambda_{2}\mu(y)\right)\left(\lambda_{1}\rho(x)+\lambda_{2}\mu(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}^{2}\rho(x)\rho(y)+\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\rho(x)\mu(y)+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{1}\mu(x)\rho(y)+\lambda_{2}^{2}\mu(x)\mu(y)

\displaystyle\qquad-\lambda_{1}^{2}\rho(y)\rho(x)-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\rho(y)\mu(x)-\lambda_{2}\lambda_{1}\mu(y)\rho(x)-\lambda_{2}^{2}\mu(y)\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}^{2}\rho(\left[x,y\right])+\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\big(\rho(\left\{x,y\right\})+\mu(\left[x,y\right])\big)+\lambda_{2}^{2}\mu(\left\{x,y\right\}),

where in the last equality, we group like-terms together and use

2.2
,
2.3
and
2.4
with
\rho
and
\mu
. Since both sides are equal, the identity is satisfied.

We proceed in the same way for the third identity, and although the computations are longer, the result still holds.

Finally, the last part of the statement can be deduced by the fact that if
W
is a subrepresentation of
(V,\rho,\mu)
, meaning it is invariant under

and
\mu(x)
for all

, then it must also be invariant under any linear combination of those maps. In particular, it is invariant under
\mu^{\prime}=\lambda_{1}\rho+\lambda_{2}\mu
, and thus it is a subrepresentation of
(V,\rho^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
. Conversely, if
\lambda_{2}\neq 0
, then
(\mathfrak{g},\left[-,-\right],\left\{-,-\right\})
and

are obtained from
(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime},\left[-,-\right]^{\prime},\left\{-,-\right\}^{\prime})
and

by the process above, as

and
\left\{-,-\right\}=-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}^{-1}\left[-,-\right]^{\prime}+\lambda_{2}^{-1}\left\{-,-\right\}^{\prime}

∎

Remark 4.24
.

•

We can see that these representations are irreducible, thus justifying their name: for any nonzero element

successively, so that we end up with a multiple of

and

•

We have previously considered another family of line representations where

•

Since

•

We do not claim that these are the only finite-dimensional irreducible representations of

; that remains an open question.

In an irreducible finite-dimensional line representation of dimension

.

Let

Proposition 4.27
.

An irreducible finite-dimensional line representation satisfies

Applying

\displaystyle\rho(x)\mu(y)v_{i}-\mu(y)\rho(x)v_{i}=\rho(x)(\delta_{i}v_{i+1})-\mu(y)(\alpha_{i}v_{i-1})=\delta_{i}\alpha_{i+1}v_{i}-\alpha_{i}\delta_{i-1}v_{i},

\displaystyle\delta_{i}\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}\delta_{i-1}=\gamma_{i}+\beta_{i}.

Remark 4.29

This immediately shows that

We prove our claim that

In terms of the
w_{ij}
this is equivalent to

\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\zeta_{i}\Big((\ell-i+1)w_{i,\ell-i+1}-(i+1)w_{i+1,\ell-i}\Big)=0.

(5.2)

The coefficient of
w_{i,\ell-i+1}
in this expression is

\zeta_{i}(\ell-i+1)-i\zeta_{i-1}.

(5.3)

Since the sum (
5.2
) evaluates to
0
, each of these coefficients is zero, and we readily show by induction that this implies that each
\zeta_{i}
is also zero, for
0\leq i\leq\ell
, proving our claim.

We will now proceed by proving that
C
is a subrepresentation of
V(m,\beta)\otimes V(n,\beta^{\prime})
isomorphic to
V(m-1,\beta+1)\otimes V(n-1,\beta^{\prime})
by directly computing the actions
\rho(x)
,
\rho(y)
,
\mu(x)
,
\mu(y)
on the basis elements
c_{ij}
. Whenever indices appear which are out of bounds, we assume the corresponding element is
0
(namely,
i<0
,
i\geq m
, and
j\geq n
when indexing elements of type
c_{ij}
or
i<0
,
i>m
, and
j>n
when indexing elements of type
w_{ij}
).

\displaystyle\rho(x)c_{ij}

\displaystyle=\rho(x)\left((j+1)w_{i,j+1}-(i+1)w_{i+1,j}\right)

\displaystyle=(j+1)w_{i-1,j+1}+(j+1)w_{i,j}-(i+1)w_{i,j}-(i+1)w_{i+1,j-1}

\displaystyle=(j+1)w_{i-1,j+1}-iw_{i,j}+jw_{i,j}-(i-1)w_{i+1,j-1}

\displaystyle=c_{i-1,j}+c_{i,j-1};

\displaystyle\rho(y)c_{ij}

\displaystyle=\rho(y)\left((j+1)w_{i,j+1}-(i+1)w_{i+1,j}\right)

\displaystyle=(i+j+1-m-n-\beta-\beta^{\prime})(j+1)w_{i,j+1}-(i+1+j-m-n-\beta-\beta^{\prime})w_{i+1,j}

\displaystyle=(i+j-(m-1)-(n-1)-(\beta+1)-\beta^{\prime})c_{ij};

\displaystyle\mu(x)c_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mu(x)\left((j+1)w_{i,j+1}-(i+1)w_{i+1,j}\right)

\displaystyle=(\beta+\beta^{\prime}+i+j+1)(j+1)w_{i,j+1}-(\beta+\beta^{\prime}+i+1+j)(i+1)w_{i+1,j}

\displaystyle=((\beta+1)+\beta^{\prime}+i+j)c_{ij};

\displaystyle\mu(y)c_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mu(y)\left((j+1)w_{i,j+1}-(i+1)w_{i+1,j}\right)

\displaystyle=(j+1)\Big((i-m)(i+1)w_{i+1,j+1}+(j+1-n)(j+2)w_{i,j+2}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+(i+1)\Big((i+1-m)(i+2)w_{i+2,j}+(j-n)(j+1)w_{i+1,j+1}\Big)

\displaystyle=(j+1)(j+1-n)(j+2)w_{i,j+2}-(i+1)(i+1-m)(i+2)w_{i+2,j}

\displaystyle\qquad+(i+1)(j+1)(i-m+j-n)w_{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle=(i-m+1)(i+1)\Big((j+1)w_{i+1,j+1}-(i+2)w_{i+2,j}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+(j-n+1)(j+1)\Big((j+2)w_{i,j+2}-(i+1)w_{i+1,j+1}\Big)

\displaystyle=(i+1)(i-(m-1))c_{i+1,j}+(j+1)(j-(n-1))c_{i,j+1}.

Looking at the coefficients in the above expressions and comparing with (
5.1
), we see that
C
is a subrepresentation isomorphic to
V(m-1,\beta+1)\otimes V(n-1,\beta^{\prime})
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

5.8
.

A dimension count shows that if
C
and
D
have trivial intersection, they satisfy
V(m,\beta)\otimes V(n,\beta^{\prime})=D\oplus C
, as
\dim V(m,\beta)\otimes V(n,\beta^{\prime})=(m+1)(n+1)=\dim D+\dim C=(m+n+1)+mn
.

We are left to show that
C
and
D
have trivial intersection, and since
D
is irreducible, it suffices to show that
D\not\subseteq C
. But from the definitions of
C
and
D
, this is immediate, as
w_{mn}=d_{m+n}\in D
but
w_{mn}\not\in C
.
∎
