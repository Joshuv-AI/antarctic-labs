Mesoscopic Rates of Convergence for Complex Wishart Matrices at the Leftmost Spectrum Edge
Mengchun Cai

Abstract
This paper establishes mesoscopic rates of convergence in the L 1 L^{1} -Wasserstein distance for eigenvalue determinantal point processes (DPPs) derived from the Laguerre Unitary Ensemble (LUE) to the corresponding limiting point processes as the dimension goes to infinity. Specifically, we prove convergence rates at the leftmost edge of the LUE spectrum, which corresponds to the least eigenvalue. These results are termed mesoscopic because they allow for a direct comparison of point counts between the convergent DPPs and their limits over a range of scales. Our approach relies on controlling the trace class norm of the integral operators defined by the DPP kernels. As an important byproduct, the mesoscopic analysis also yields the convergence rate of the least eigenvalue to the Tracy–Widom distribution T ​ W 2 TW_{2} for the complex Wishart ensemble (the same as the LUE when the dimension satisfies certain conditions).

Random matrix theory (RMT) is an active subfield of mathematics, statistics and physics. It was initiated in statistics and introduced to physics by Wigner and Dyson in the 1950s-1960s. Given its mathematical richness, RMT has become very popular since the end of 1970s through the development of quantum physics, statistical mechanics, probability theory, geometric functional analysis, theoretical computer science and qualitative finances. 1.1
Determinantal Point Process (DPPs)

The basic idea in this paper is to analyze the eigenvalues of a random matrix with large dimension. The eigenvalues can be regarded as a random point process.

Definition 1.2
.

A point process (PP)
\mathfrak{X}
in a locally compact Polish space
\Lambda
is a random discrete subset of
\Lambda
. For any
A\subset\Lambda
,
\mathcal{N}_{\mathfrak{X}}(A)
denotes the number of points of
\mathfrak{X}
in
A
. The integer-valued random function:
A\rightarrow\mathcal{N}_{\mathfrak{X}}(A)

is called the
counting function
of
\mathfrak{X}
.

In this paper, we mainly focus on point processes in
\Lambda=\mathbb{R}
. Given a Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}
and a random matrix
H
, we use the notation
\mathcal{N}_{H}(A)
to represent the number of eigenvalues of
H
contained in
A
. Moreover, we refer to points in a point process as eigenvalues even when they are not true eigenvalues.

Fix a point process
\mathfrak{X}
on
\mathbb{R}
. If they exist, the
correlation functions
(also called joint intensities) for
\mathfrak{X}
are a sequence of locally integrable functions
\{\rho_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
satisfying the following condition: for all mutually disjoint measurable subsets
\{D_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
of
\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathcal{N}_{\mathfrak{X}}(D_{j})\right]=\int_{\prod_{j}D_{j}}\rho_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})dx_{1}\cdots dx_{k}.

Analogous to the classic moment problem from probability, under many circumstances correlation functions uniquely specify a point process (see
[
24
]
, where it is shown that this is the case for DPPs).

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a PP in
\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that there exists a function
K:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that, for almost every
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\rho_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\det\left(K(x_{j},x_{l})_{j,l=1}^{k}\right),

holds for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\mathfrak{X}
is called a
determinantal point process
, and
K
is called the
kernel
of
\mathfrak{X}
.

As a result, for a DPP, the kernel determines the correlation functions, which themselves determine the PP. Hence, studying the corresponding kernel leads to results for a DPP.

Besides the random matrix ensembles that will be introduced later, let’s briefly review two common DPPs we will encounter when considering the leftmost edge of the LUE

Definition 1.4
.

An
Airy point process

\mathfrak{X}_{Ai}
is a DPP given by the kernel:

K_{Ai}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rr}\frac{Ai(x)Ai^{\prime}(y)-Ai(y)Ai^{\prime}(x)}{x-y},\penalty\ \penalty\ x\neq y\\
\\
\left(Ai^{\prime}(x)\right)^{2}-xAi^{2}(x),\penalty\ \penalty\ x=y\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where

Ai(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos\left(\frac{t^{2}}{3}+xt\right)dt

is the
Airy function
.

Similarly, a
Bessel point process

\mathfrak{X}_{Bes,a}
with parameter
a\geq 0
is a DPP with the kernel:

K_{Bes,a}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\sqrt{y}J^{\prime}_{a}(\sqrt{y})J_{a}(\sqrt{x})-\sqrt{x}J^{\prime}_{a}(\sqrt{x})J_{a}(\sqrt{y})}{2(x-y)},\penalty\ \penalty\ x\neq y\\
\\
\frac{x-a^{2}}{4x}\left(J_{a}(\sqrt{x})\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left(J^{\prime}_{a}(\sqrt{x})\right)^{2},\penalty\ \penalty\ x=y\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

for
x,\penalty\ y\geq 0
, where

J_{a}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{\Gamma(m+1)\Gamma(m+a+1)}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2m+a}

is the
Bessel function
with parameter
a
.

1.2
The LUE and the M–P Law

The eigenvalue distribution addressed in this paper is also referred to as the following important ensemble in RMT.

Proposition 1.5
.

[
10
, Proposition
3.2.2
]

Suppose
a\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
X
be an
(N+a)\times N
matrix with i.i.d. complex standard Gaussian entries, then the joint distribution of the eigenvalues of
L_{a,N}:=X^{*}X
has the following probability density function:

p_{L_{a,N},2}(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})=\frac{1}{C_{L_{a,N},2}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\chi_{\{x_{j}\geq 0\}}|x_{j}-x_{k}|^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{a}\exp(-x_{j})

(1.3)

for
C_{L_{a,N},2}
appropriate normalization constant.

In addition, an
N
-dimensional nonnegative random vector
(x_{1},x_{2},..,x_{N})
with the joint probability density function as (
1.3
) is called a
Laguerre Unitary Ensemble (LUE)
with parameter
a
.
For convention, we say the Wishart matrix
L_{a,N}
belongs to the LUE.

Although the general definition for the LUE makes sense whenever
a>-1
, we only focus on the case where
a\in\mathbb{N}
because of the corresponding matrix model in this setting.

One well-known fact is that the LUE with parameter
a\in\mathbb{N}
forms a DPP with the kernel:

K_{L_{a},N}(x,y)=\frac{-\Gamma(N+1)}{\Gamma(N+a)}\frac{\psi_{L_{a},N}(x)\psi_{L_{a},N-1}(y)-\psi_{L_{a},N}(y)\psi_{L_{a},N-1}(x)}{x-y}

(1.4)

where

\begin{split}\psi_{L_{a},j}(x):=x^{\frac{a}{2}}e^{-\frac{x}{2}}\chi_{\{x\geq 0\}}L_{j}^{a}(x)=x^{\frac{a}{2}}e^{-\frac{x}{2}}\chi_{\{x\geq 0\}}(-1)^{j}j!\sum_{k=0}^{j}\left(\begin{matrix}j+a\\
j-k\end{matrix}\right)\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}x^{k}\end{split}

is the
j
th
Laguerre function
with parameter
a
and
L_{j}^{a}(x)

is the
j
th
Laguerre polynomial
with parameter
a
(see
[
10
, Equations
(5.46)\penalty\ to\penalty\ (5.48)
]
).

Given a Wishart matrix
W_{m,n}
, the following probability measure is closely related to its
Empirical Spectrum Measure
(ESM).

Definition 1.6
.

Given an Hermitian matrix
H\in M^{n}(\mathbb{C})
, the ESM of
H
is defined as:

\mu_{H}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{\lambda_{j}}

where
\lambda_{j}
’s are eigenvalues of
H
.

Definition 1.7
.

Given two parameters
\lambda>0
and
\sigma>0
, define

g_{\lambda,\sigma}(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\frac{\sqrt{(\lambda^{+}-x)(x-\lambda^{-})}}{\lambda x}\chi_{[\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+}]}

where
\lambda^{\pm}:=\sigma^{2}(1\pm\sqrt{\lambda})^{2}
. We say a probability measure
\rho_{\lambda,\sigma}
follows the
Marchenko–Pastur distribution
with parameter
\lambda
and
\sigma
if and only if for any measurable
A\subset\mathbb{R}
,

\rho_{\lambda,\sigma}(A)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\chi_{\{0\in A\}}(1-\lambda^{-1})+\mu_{\lambda,\sigma}(A),\penalty\ \lambda>1\\
\\
\mu_{\lambda,\sigma}(A),\penalty\ \lambda\in(0,1]\end{array}\right.

where
\mu_{\lambda,\sigma}
has the density
g_{\lambda,\sigma}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

For a Wishart matrix
W_{n,m}
, the following
Marchenko–Pastur law
, which was first proved by Marchenko and Pastur in
[
16
]
, holds for its ESM.

Proposition 1.8
.

[
5
, Theorem
3.6\&3.7
]
Let
W_{n,m}
be Wishart matrices (real or complex) with
\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n}=\gamma\in(0,\infty)
. Then with probability one, the ESMs of
W^{\prime}_{n,m}:=\frac{1}{n}W_{n,m}
converge to
\rho_{\gamma,1}
in distribution.

For a sequence of Wishart matrices
W_{n,m}=X^{*}X
such that
\frac{m}{n}\rightarrow\gamma>1
, we see it must have a bunch of
0
’s, according to its rank, as the eigenvalues when
n
is large. These
0
eigenvalues are considered trivial as they provide us with little interesting information but a mass around
0
as in the M–P distribution. Noticing that
X^{*}X
and
XX^{*}
have the same nontrivial eigenvalues, we always assume
\gamma\in(0,1]
in this paper and only draw attention to
L_{a,N}
as similar discussion applies to
XX^{*}
when
\gamma>1
.

Given an LUE
L_{a,N}
, the density
g_{1,\gamma}
already identifies
(1-\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}
and
(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}
as two spectrum edges. At the right edge—the expected position of the largest eigenvalue—points converge to the Airy point process after proper scaling as the dimension goes to infinity (see
[
6
]
,
[
8
]
and
[
14
]
). Since there is no bound that absolutely limits the size of the largest eigenvalue (as argued in
[
4
]
and
[
11
]
, although it is possible for the largest eigenvalue to exceed its typical location, the probability of this event is very low), the right edge is often referred to as the
soft edge
.

The behavior at the left edge, which is the main focus of this paper, is limited by the fact that the least eigenvalue of a positive semidefinite matrix must be absolutely bounded below by
0
. Consequently, both the location and nature vary according to the value of limiting ratio
\lim\limits_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{N}{N+a}:=\gamma\in(0,1]
. In fact, the authors in
[
13
]
established a comprehensive classification of the edges of the support of the ESM for complex Wishart matrices (LUE). Simply following their framework, we restrict our attention to two regular regimes for the left edge of LUE. First,
\lim\limits_{N\rightarrow\infty}a(N)=a
, or equivalently,
a(N)
is a fixed integer for
N
large. In this setting, the left edge of
L_{a,N}
touches
0
, which is a natural bound for the eigenvalues as
L_{a,N}
is a positive semidefinite matrix. Under this assumption,
0
is called the
regular hard edge
of
L_{a,N}
. After proper rescaling, around the regular hard edge, authors in
[
6
]
established the ROC of the LUE towards the Bessel point process with parameter
a
. Second, the ratio
\gamma
is in
(0,1)
. In this case, the left edge is called a
regular soft edge
as it is bounded away from
0
. Near this edge, the main purpose of this paper is to show that when the dimension gets large, the LUE converges to the
symmetric Airy point process
after proper scaling.

Given a Polish metric space
(\Omega,d)
, two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu

\Omega

, the
L^{p}
-Wasserstein distance

W_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\\
\inf_{(x,y)\in\pi(\mu,\nu)}\left(\mathbb{E}d^{p}(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

where

contains all couplings of
\mu

\nu
. The choice of

[
29

6

L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance

Theorem 1.9

(
[

\lim\limits_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{N}{n}=\gamma\in(0,1]

I\subset[s,+\infty)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mu^{R}_{k,m}:=\left(\sqrt{k+\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{m+\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2},\\

1.3
Outline of Proof

For a kernel function
K(x,y)
on some domain
D^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we can define a corresponding integral operator
\mathbf{K}
acting on
L^{2}(D)
by the formula:

\mathbf{K}(f)(x)=\int_{D}K(x,y)f(y)dy

for any
f\in L^{2}(D)
. Recall further that there is a hierarchy of classes of such (compact) operators (again analogous to
L^{p}
spaces) based on the rate of decay of the singular values of the operator known as the
Schatten
p
-Norms
. Given a compact operator
\mathbf{T}
, it belongs to the Schatten
p
-class if

\lVert\mathbf{T}\rVert_{S_{p}}:=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}s_{n}(\mathbf{T})^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty

where
s_{n}(\mathbf{T})
’s are singular values of
\mathbf{T}
. For convenience, for a given suitable operator
\mathbf{L}
, we simply denote its Schatten
p
-norm as
\|\mathbf{L}\|_{p}
in this paper.

As expected, the two most important cases are
p=1
and
p=2
. The
p=1
case are known as trace-class operators and the
p=2
case are called Hilbert–Schmidt operators.

Soshnikov proved in
[
24
]
that the kernel of a self-adjoint, locally trace-class operator defines a determinantal point process if and only if all the eigenvalues of the operator are between
0
and
1
. Consequently, it is possible to translate the question regarding random point counts to the analysis of the corresponding integral operators.

The following lemmas in
[
6
]
provide us with tangible bounds for
W_{1}
distance between random point counts.

Lemma 1.10
.

[
6
, Lemma
1.1
]

Consider two DPPs
\mathfrak{X}
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{X}}
with Hermitian kernels
K(x,y)
and
\widetilde{K}(x,y)
and associated integral operators
\mathbf{K}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}
respectively. Assume the integral operators are trace class. Then,

d_{TV}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{N}_{\tilde{\mathcal{X}}})\leq W_{1}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{N}_{\tilde{\mathcal{X}}})\leq\lVert\mathbf{K}-\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}\rVert_{1}

where
d_{TV}
is the total variation distance.

Lemma 1.11
.

[
6
, Lemma
3.1
]

Suppose
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
are Hilbert–Schmidt operators on a Hilbert space. Then the following inequality holds:

2\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}-\sqrt{2}\mathbf{C}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}+\sqrt{2}\mathbf{C}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|^{2}_{2}.

Moreover, for any Hilbert–Schmidt operator
\mathbf{K}
with the integral kernel
K
, it follows that
\|\mathbf{K}\|_{2}=\|K\|_{L^{2}}
.

Lemma
1.11
is useful since the exact computation of the trace-class norm is complicated, whereas estimating the
L^{2}
norm of the kernel is more tractable.
Therefore, the work in the proofs boils down to two things: decomposing the operators appropriately so that the inequality in Lemma
1.11
can be used, and getting non-asymptotic approximation towards the Airy function (since it serves as the limiting kernel in our setting) to finish the estimates.

The following operator decomposition, first introduced in
[
26
]
and then extended in
[
14
]
, plays an important role in our reasoning.

Proposition 1.12
.

Let
L_{a,N}
be an LUE with parameter
a\in\mathbb{N}
and kernel
K_{L_{a,N}}(x,y)
. Then for
a\geq 2
, the following decomposition holds for the corresponding integral operator
\mathbf{K}_{L_{a,N}}
:

\mathbf{K}_{L_{a,N}}=\mathbf{G}_{a,N}\mathbf{H}_{a,N}+\mathbf{H}_{a,N}\mathbf{G}_{a,N}.

(1.6)

Thereinto, the operators
\mathbf{G}_{a,N}
and
\mathbf{H}_{a,N}
have kernels
\xi_{a,N}(x+y)
and
\eta_{a,N}(x+y)
for

\begin{array}[]{ll}\xi_{a,N}(x):=\frac{(-1)^{N}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(N(N+a)\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(N+1)}{\Gamma(N+a)}}x^{\frac{a}{2}-1}e^{-\frac{x}{2}}L_{N}^{a-1}(x)\chi_{(0,\infty)}(x),\\
\\
\eta_{a,N}(x):=\frac{(-1)^{N-1}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(N(N+a)\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(N)}{\Gamma(N+a+1)}}x^{\frac{a}{2}}e^{-\frac{x}{2}}L_{N-1}^{a+1}(x)\chi_{(0,\infty)}(x)\end{array}

(1.7)

respectively.

Let
\mathbf{K}_{Ai}
be the integral operator with the Airy kernel defined in (
1.1
), then

\mathbf{K}_{Ai}=\mathbf{Ai}\mathbf{Ai}

(1.8)

where
\mathbf{Ai}
has the kernel
Ai(x+y)
.

Moreover, all operators above are Hilbert-Schmidt on
L^{2}(0,+\infty)
.

Statement of Results

The following is the main result proved in this paper.

Theorem 2.1
.

(left soft edge) Consider an LUE
L_{a,N}
such that
\lim\limits_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{N}{n}=\gamma\in(0,1)
where
n:=N+a
. Let
I\subset[s,+\infty)
for
s>-\infty
. Define parameters:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mu^{L}_{k,m}:=\left(\sqrt{k+\frac{1}{2}}-\sqrt{m+\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2},\\
\sigma^{L}_{k,m}:=\left(\sqrt{k+\frac{1}{2}}-\sqrt{m+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left(\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}-\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}},\end{array}

\mu_{L}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{n-1,N}^{L}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{n,N-1}^{L}}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\mu^{L}_{n-1,N}\sqrt{\sigma_{n-1,N}^{L}}}+\frac{1}{\mu^{L}_{n,N-1}\sqrt{\sigma_{n,N-1}^{L}}}\right)^{-1}

and

\sigma_{L}=\left(\frac{\sqrt{\sigma_{n-1,N}^{L}}}{\mu^{L}_{n-1,N}}+\frac{\sqrt{\sigma_{n,N-1}^{L}}}{\mu^{L}_{n,N-1}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\mu^{L}_{n-1,N}\sqrt{\sigma_{n-1,N}^{L}}}+\frac{1}{\mu^{L}_{n,N-1}\sqrt{\sigma_{n,N-1}^{L}}}\right)^{-1}.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{L_{a,N}}^{LS}
denote the left-soft-edge-scaled point process for
L_{a,N}
with the kernel
K_{L_{a,N}}^{LS}(x,y):=\sigma_{L}K_{L_{a,N}}(t_{L_{N}}(x),t_{L_{N}}(y))
where

, there exists

such that for any

W_{1}(\mathcal{N}_{L_{a,N}}^{LS},\mathcal{N}_{Ai})\leq\frac{g(s,\gamma)e^{-s}}{N^{\frac{2}{3}}}

(2.1)

Remark.
The scaling we used in Theorem
2.1

1.9

[

in Theorem

. This difference arises because, in our estimation of the least eigenvalue, we are unable to construct a uniform bound with respect to
\gamma
in the same way as we can for the largest eigenvalue in Theorem
1.9
. A more detailed explanation will be provided later.

Theorem
2.1
also provides us with a convergence rate for the least eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}
of an LUE towards the Tracy–Widom distribution as following.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
L_{a,N}
be an LUE with parameters satisfying
\lim\limits_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{N}{N+a}=\gamma\in(0,1)
. Suppose
\lambda_{min}^{N}
is the least eigenvalue of the
L_{a,N}
and
TW_{2}(s)
is the distribution function of the least eigenvalue of an Airy point process. Let
\tilde{\lambda}^{N}_{min}:=\frac{\lambda_{min}^{N}+\mu_{L}}{\sigma_{L}}
be the left-soft-edge-scaled least eigenvalue of
L_{a,N}
. Then for large
N
,

|\mathbb{P}\{-\tilde{\lambda}_{min}^{N}\leq s\}-TW_{2}(s)|\leq\frac{g(s,\gamma)e^{-s}}{N^{\frac{2}{3}}}.

where
g
is the same function as in Theorem
2.1
.

Proof.

Notice
-\lambda_{min}^{N}=\mu_{L}-\sigma_{L}\tilde{\lambda}_{min}^{N}
, which matches the explicit scaling in Theorem
2.1
, then (
2.1
) applies. The same as the previous proof, it follows that

\begin{split}|\mathbb{P}\{-\tilde{\lambda}_{min}^{N}\leq s\}-TW_{2}(s)|&=|\mathbb{P}\{\mathcal{N}_{L_{a,N}}^{LS}((s,\infty))=0\}-\mathbb{P}\{\mathcal{N}_{Airy}((s,\infty))=0\}|\\
&\leq d_{TV}(\mathcal{N}_{L_{a,N}}^{LS}((s,\infty)),\mathcal{N}_{Airy}((s,\infty)))\\
&\leq W_{1}(\mathcal{N}_{L_{a,N}}^{LS}((s,\infty)),\mathcal{N}_{Airy}((s,\infty)))\\
&\leq\frac{g(s,\gamma)e^{-s}}{N^{\frac{2}{3}}}\end{split}

where
g
is the same function as in Theorem
2.1
.
∎

The distribution
TW_{2}(s)
, with an explicit form given in
[
26
]
, is the famous
unitary Tracy–Widom distribution
. The result in Corollary
2.2
actually covers the ROC, with a better spacial function
g(s,\gamma)
, derived by Ma in
[
15
, Theorem
2
]
. Moreover, the negative sign in this corollary indicates the fact that after proper scaling, the point process derived by the eigenvalues of
L_{a,N}
around the left soft edge converges to the symmetric Airy point process, which slightly differs from the right soft one. Similar asymptotics can be seen in
[
15
]
in the same setting and
[
21
]
for
\gamma=0
.

2.1
Comparison with Other Results

Unlike a microscopic analysis, which is confined to a fixed, local scale, our approach permits the scaling parameter
s
to change, subject to asymptotic conditions that ensure we remain within the mesoscopic regime. This dynamic scaling covers some the rigid setting of microscopic limits, for example, Forrester’s discussion for the convergence of the kernel functions in
[
10
]
, and provides a powerful tool for probing the spatial structure of the point process across different scales. Indeed, aside from extreme values of the parameter (as the small
s
for the left hard edge and large
s
for the left soft edge for the LUE), we cannot expect our results to be optimal in the spatial parameter
s
, as the estimates used in the proof are not sharp. However, for any fixed
s
, we conjecture that our results are optimal with respect to the dimension
N
.

The method we use relies heavily on the auxiliary variable introduced by the Liouville–Green transform. Following the analysis in the proof, it appears unrealistic to expect a rate higher than
N^{\frac{2}{3}}
, as the power
\frac{2}{3}
is intrinsic in our L–G transform around the least eigenvalue (in fact, it is also necessary around the largest one as in
[
8
]
). As a result, it seems very likely that the order
N^{-\frac{2}{3}}
is optimal in this case.

As indicated in Corollary
2.2
, the mesoscopic results around the left edge of the LUE imply a the ROC for the distribution of its least eigenvalue. However, in contrast to the right edge behavior studied in
[
8
]
, the behavior of the left edge varies a lot according to the limiting value of the ratio
\frac{N}{N+a}
.

3.1
Liouville–Green’s Method

We denote
N+a
as
n
in the following. Given an
LUE

L_{a,N}
, introduce parameters
\lambda_{N}:=\frac{a}{2}
,
\kappa_{N}:=N+\frac{a+1}{2}
and
\omega_{N}:=\frac{2\lambda_{N}}{\kappa_{N}}
, then the function
w_{N}(y)=y^{\frac{a+1}{2}}e^{-\frac{y}{2}}L^{a}_{N}(y)=y^{\frac{1}{2}}f_{a,N}(y)
, which serves as the primary term in our
\xi
and

), satisfies:

\frac{d^{2}w_{N}(y)}{dy^{2}}=\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\kappa_{N}}{y}+\frac{\lambda_{N}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}}{y^{2}}\right)w_{N}(y)

(3.1)

according to the Laguerre recurrences in
[
25
, Chapter
5.1
]
. After changing the variable:
z:=\frac{y}{\kappa_{N}}
, (
3.1
) can be written as:

\frac{d^{2}w_{N}(z)}{dz^{2}}=\left(\kappa_{N}^{2}f(z)+g(z)\right)w_{N}(z)

(3.2)

with

f(z)=\frac{(z-z_{1})(z-z_{2})}{4z^{2}},\penalty\ \penalty\ g(z)=-\frac{1}{4z^{2}}.

Here
z_{1}=2-\sqrt{4-\omega_{N}^{2}}
and
z_{2}=2+\sqrt{4-\omega_{N}^{2}}
are called
turning points
of
f
. As
\omega_{N}\rightarrow 2\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\right)
, the parameter
\omega_{N}
is in
[\delta,4-\delta]
for some
\delta>0
when
N
is large.

Restrict attention to the equation:

\frac{d^{2}w(z)}{dz^{2}}=\left(\kappa_{N}^{2}f(z)+g(z)\right)w(z)

(3.3)

with
f
and
g
given before. It follows that
w_{N}(\kappa_{N}z)
is a solution of (
3.3
) from the previous scaling.

The philosophy of the L–G method is to introduce a new variable
\zeta
in order to further rewrite (
3.3
) as a classical differential equation for
\zeta
. To this end, we aim for
\zeta
to satisfy
\zeta(\zeta^{\prime})^{2}=f
, where the prime denotes differentiation with respect to
z
. This condition ensures that the term involving
f
in (
3.3
) is killed. The following proposition provides a construction of such a
\zeta
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Given the differential equation (
3.3
), let
I_{1}=(a_{1},b_{1})
,
I_{2}=(a_{2},b_{2})
be two intervals containing exactly one turning point of
f
, say,
z_{1}\in I_{1}
and
z_{2}\in I_{2}
. Define
\zeta
by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{2}{3}\zeta^{\frac{3}{2}}(z)=\int_{z}^{z_{1}}f^{\frac{1}{2}}(t)dt,\penalty\ z\leq z_{1}\\
\\
\frac{2}{3}(-\zeta)^{\frac{3}{2}}(z)=\int_{z_{1}}^{z}(-f)^{\frac{1}{2}}(t)dt,\penalty\ z>z_{1}\end{array}\right.

(3.4)

on
I_{1}
, and similarly,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{2}{3}\zeta^{\frac{3}{2}}(z)=\int_{z_{2}}^{z}f^{\frac{1}{2}}(t)dt,\penalty\ z>z_{2}\\
\\
\frac{2}{3}(-\zeta)^{\frac{3}{2}}(z)=\int_{z}^{z_{2}}(-f)^{\frac{1}{2}}(t)dt,\penalty\ z\leq z_{2}\end{array}\right.

(3.5)

on
I_{2}
. Then for any
z\in I_{j}
where
j\in\{1,2\}
,
\zeta^{\prime}:=\frac{d\zeta}{dz}
is positive when
z>z_{j}
and negative when
z<z_{j}
. Moreover, for any
z\in I_{j}
, define
W:=(\zeta^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}w
when
z>z_{j}
and
W:=(-\zeta^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}w
when
z\leq z_{j}
. Further define
\tilde{f}:=\frac{f}{\zeta}
, which equals
(\zeta^{\prime})^{2}
. Then the previous equation (
3.3
) can be expressed as:

\frac{d^{2}W(\zeta)}{d\zeta^{2}}=\left(\kappa_{N}^{2}\zeta+\Psi(\zeta)\right)W(\zeta)

(3.6)

where

\Psi(\zeta)=\tilde{f}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\frac{d^{2}}{d\zeta^{2}}\left(\tilde{f}^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)+\frac{g}{\tilde{f}}.

This
\zeta
is called the
Liouville–Green transformation
of
f
.

Proof.

The sign of
\zeta^{\prime}
and the identity
\zeta(\zeta^{\prime})^{2}=f
are straightforward by the chain rule according to (
3.4
) and (
3.5
). With respect to
\zeta
, since
\frac{dw_{(}z)}{d\zeta}=\frac{dw_{(}z)}{dz}\frac{dz}{d\zeta}=\frac{w^{\prime}}{\zeta^{\prime}}
, it follows that

\frac{d^{2}w}{d\zeta^{2}}=\frac{d}{d\zeta}\left(\frac{{w}^{\prime}}{\zeta^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{{w}^{\prime}}{\zeta^{\prime}}\right)\frac{dz}{d\zeta}=\frac{w^{\prime\prime}\zeta^{\prime}-w^{\prime}\zeta^{\prime\prime}}{(\zeta^{\prime})^{3}}=\frac{(\kappa_{N}^{2}f+g)w}{(\zeta^{\prime})^{2}}-\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime}}{(\zeta^{\prime})^{2}}\frac{dw}{d\zeta}

(3.7)

by (
3.3
). Now consider the equation for
W
. We only prove the case where
z\leq z_{1}
as the method applies to other regions by simply substituting
-\zeta^{\prime}
by
\zeta^{\prime}
if necessary. Since

\frac{dW}{d\zeta}=\frac{d\left(\sqrt{-\zeta^{\prime}}\right)}{dz}\frac{dz}{d\zeta}w+\sqrt{-\zeta^{\prime}}\frac{dw}{d\zeta}=\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime}}{2(\sqrt{-\zeta^{\prime}})^{3}}w+\sqrt{-\zeta^{\prime}}\frac{dw}{d\zeta},

the equation (
3.7
) yields:

\begin{split}\frac{d^{2}W}{d\zeta^{2}}&=\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime}}{2(-\zeta^{\prime})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)\frac{dz}{d\zeta}w+\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime}}{2(-\zeta^{\prime})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\frac{dw}{d\zeta}+\frac{d\sqrt{-\zeta^{\prime}}}{dz}\frac{dz}{d\zeta}\frac{dw}{d\zeta}+\sqrt{-\zeta^{\prime}}\frac{d^{2}w}{d\zeta^{2}}\\
&=\left(\frac{-\zeta^{\prime\prime\prime}}{2(-\zeta^{\prime})^{\frac{5}{2}}}-\frac{3(\zeta^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{4(-\zeta^{\prime})^{\frac{7}{2}}}\right)w+\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime}}{(-\zeta^{\prime})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\frac{dw}{d\zeta}+\frac{(\kappa^{2}_{N}f+g)w}{(-\zeta^{\prime})^{\frac{3}{2}}}-\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime}}{(-\zeta^{\prime})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\frac{dw}{d\zeta}\\
&=\left(\kappa_{N}^{2}\zeta+\frac{g}{\tilde{f}}+\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime\prime}}{2(\zeta^{\prime})^{3}}-\frac{3(\zeta^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{4(\zeta^{\prime})^{4}}\right)W\end{split}

(3.8)

where the fact
\frac{f}{(\zeta^{\prime})^{2}}=\zeta
has been used in the third line. Noting that
\tilde{f}^{\frac{1}{4}}=\sqrt{-\zeta^{\prime}}
, from the computations in (
3.8
), it is easy to see

\begin{split}\tilde{f}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\frac{d^{2}}{d\zeta^{2}}\left(\tilde{f}^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\zeta^{\prime}}}\frac{d^{2}(\sqrt{-\zeta^{\prime}})}{d\zeta^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\zeta^{\prime}}}\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime}}{2(-\zeta^{\prime})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)\frac{1}{\zeta^{\prime}}=\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime\prime}}{2(\zeta^{\prime})^{3}}-\frac{3(\zeta^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{4(\zeta^{\prime})^{4}}\end{split}

and hence, (
3.8
) is exactly the same as (
3.6
).
∎

\lambda_{0}:=\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\pi|x|^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{M}^{2}(x)\right\}<\infty
.

Proof.

Recalling that
W_{2}=Ai\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)+\epsilon_{2}(\zeta)
is a solution of (
3.6
), then
\epsilon_{2}
satisfies

\frac{d^{2}\epsilon_{2}(\zeta)}{d\zeta^{2}}-\kappa_{N}^{2}\zeta\epsilon_{2}(\zeta)=\Psi(\zeta)\left(Ai\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)+\epsilon_{2}(\zeta)\right)

(4.45)

since
Ai\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)
is a solution of
\frac{d^{2}W(\zeta)}{d\zeta^{2}}=\kappa_{N}^{2}\zeta W(\zeta)
.

To give a concrete expression of
\epsilon_{2}
, let’s recall some basic results on second order differential equations. Consider a second order linear differential equation with the standard form:

y^{\prime\prime}(x)+p(x)y^{\prime}(x)+q(x)y(x)=g(x).

(4.46)

Suppose
p,\penalty\ q
and
g
are all continuous functions. Then the solution
y
of (
4.46
), if it exists, is unique given some initial conditions:
y(x_{0})=y_{0}
and
y^{\prime}(x_{0})=y_{1}
. Now let’s check the initial condition of
\epsilon_{2}
. In fact, according to (
3.10
),
\tilde{f}^{\frac{1}{4}}(z)w_{N}(\kappa_{N}z)=c_{N}Ai\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)+c_{N}\epsilon_{2}(\zeta)
. Since the leading term of the left side is of order
\frac{z^{\frac{a+1}{2}}}{z^{\frac{1}{2}}\log^{\frac{1}{6}}\log\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)}
from (
4.41
) for small
z
, which vanishes as
z\rightarrow 0
, we see
\epsilon_{2}(\zeta)
also converges to
0
because
Ai\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)\rightarrow 0
when
z\rightarrow 0
. Further condition can be obtained by taking the derivative:

\frac{1}{4\tilde{f}^{\frac{3}{4}}}\tilde{f}^{\prime}w_{N}(\kappa_{N}z)+\tilde{f}^{\frac{1}{4}}w_{N}^{\prime}(\kappa_{N}z)\kappa_{N}-c_{N}Ai^{\prime}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta^{\prime}=c_{N}\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}(\zeta)\zeta^{\prime}

and hence,

\frac{1}{4\tilde{f}^{\frac{3}{4}}}\tilde{f}^{\prime}w_{N}(\kappa_{N}z)\frac{1}{\zeta^{\prime}}+\tilde{f}^{\frac{1}{4}}w_{N}^{\prime}(\kappa_{N}z)\frac{\kappa_{N}}{\zeta^{\prime}}-c_{N}Ai^{\prime}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}=c_{N}\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}(\zeta).

Similarly, according to (
4.41
), (
4.42
), the leading orders of the first two terms on the left side are
z^{\frac{a}{2}}
and
z^{\frac{a}{2}}\log^{-\frac{5}{6}}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)
respectively since
\zeta^{\prime}\sim\frac{1}{z\log\frac{1}{z}}
. As a result, they both vanish as
z\rightarrow 0
. Combining the fact that
Ai^{\prime}(x)
vanishes when
x\rightarrow\infty
, it follows that
\epsilon^{\prime}_{2}(\zeta)\rightarrow 0
as
z\rightarrow 0
. Consequently,
\epsilon_{2}
is the unique solution of (
4.45
) satisfying the initial condition:
\epsilon_{2}(\zeta)|_{z=0}=\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}(\zeta)|_{z=0}=0
.

Assume
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
are two linearly independent homogeneous solutions of (
4.46
) (solutions with respect to
g=0
). Then by the Wronskian method, a particular solution of (
4.46
) is given as:

y_{p}(x)=y_{2}(x)\int\frac{y_{1}(x)g(x)}{\mathcal{W}_{y_{1},y_{2}}(x)}dx-y_{1}(x)\int\frac{y_{2}(x)g(x)}{\mathcal{W}_{y_{1},y_{2}}(x)}dx

(4.47)

where
\mathcal{W}_{y_{1},y_{2}}(x):=y_{1}(x)y^{\prime}_{2}(x)-y_{2}(x)y_{1}^{\prime}(x)
is the Wronskian of
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
. Furthermore, since
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
are solutions of the homogeneous equation, it is easy to verify
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}_{y_{1},y_{2}}(x)+p(x)\mathcal{W}_{y_{1},y_{2}}(x)=0
and hence,
\mathcal{W}_{y_{1},y_{2}}(x)=C\exp\left(-\int p(x)dx\right)
for
C\in\mathbb{R}
. Regarding
(
4.45
)
as a standard form with
p(\zeta)=0
,
q(\zeta)=\kappa_{N}^{2}\zeta
and
g(\zeta)=\Psi(\zeta)\left(Ai\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)+\epsilon_{2}(\zeta)\right)
, then clearly
Ai\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)
and
Bi\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)
are two linearly independent homogeneous solutions from our previous reasoning. Moreover, since
p(\zeta)=0
in (
4.45
), the Wronskian
\mathcal{W}_{Ai,Bi}(x)
must be a constant function. According to special values of
Ai
and
Bi
at
0
(see
[
19
, Equation
11.1.03\&11.1.11
]
),

\mathcal{W}_{Ai,Bi}(x)=Ai(0)Bi^{\prime}(0)-Ai^{\prime}(0)Bi(0)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sin\frac{\pi}{3}}{\pi}=\frac{1}{\pi}

(4.48)

where Euler’s reflection formula:
\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(z\pi)}
for
z\notin\mathbb{Z}
has been used. According to (
4.47
), as a particular solution of (
4.45
) vanishes at
\infty
,
\epsilon_{2}
can be taken as:

\begin{split}\epsilon_{2}(\zeta)=\int^{\infty}_{\zeta}K(\zeta,t)\tilde{\Psi}(t)\left(Ai\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}t\right)+\epsilon_{2}(t)\right)dt\end{split}

(4.49)

where

\begin{split}&K(\zeta,t)=\pi\kappa_{N}^{-\frac{2}{3}}|t|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(Ai\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)Bi\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}t\right)-Bi\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)Ai\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}t\right)\right):=\pi\kappa_{N}^{-\frac{2}{3}}|t|^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{K}(\zeta,t)\end{split}

and
\tilde{\Psi}(t)=\Psi(t)|t|^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. The term
|t|^{\frac{1}{2}}
has been pulled out to guarantee the finiteness of controlling constants as in Lemma
4.4
. Using the phase function in (
3.21
), for any
t\geq\zeta
, let
u:=\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta
and
v:=\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}t
, then

\begin{split}|\tilde{K}(\zeta,t)|&=\mathbf{M}\left(u\right)\mathbf{M}\left(v\right)\frac{\mathbf{E}\left(v\right)}{\mathbf{E}\left(u\right)}\left|\sin\theta_{u}\cos\theta_{v}-\frac{\mathbf{E}^{2}\left(u\right)}{\mathbf{E}^{2}\left(v\right)}\sin\theta_{v}\cos_{u}\right|\\
&\leq\left(\mathbf{M}\left(u\right)\mathbf{M}\left(v\right)\frac{\mathbf{E}\left(v\right)}{\mathbf{E}\left(u\right)}\right)\left|\frac{\mathbf{E}^{2}(u)}{\mathbf{E}^{2}(v)}\sin\left(\theta_{u}-\theta_{v}\right)+\left(1-\frac{\mathbf{E}^{2}(u)}{\mathbf{E}^{2}(v)}\right)\sin\theta_{u}\cos\theta_{v}\right|\\
&\leq\mathbf{M}\left(u\right)\mathbf{M}\left(v\right)\frac{\mathbf{E}\left(v\right)}{\mathbf{E}\left(u\right)}\end{split}

(4.50)

since
\frac{\mathbf{E}^{2}(u)}{\mathbf{E}^{2}(v)}\in(0,1]
by the monotonicity of
\mathbf{E}
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Similarly, the phase function in (
3.22
) yields:

\begin{split}|\partial_{1}\tilde{K}(\zeta,t)|&=\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\left|Ai^{\prime}(u)Bi(v)-Ai(v)Bi^{\prime}(u)\right|\\
&=\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\mathbf{N}(u)\mathbf{M}(v)\frac{\mathbf{E}(v)}{\mathbf{E}(u)}\left|\sin\omega_{u}\cos\theta_{v}-\frac{\mathbf{E}^{2}(u)}{\mathbf{E}^{2}(v)}\sin\theta_{v}\cos\omega_{u}\right|\\
&\leq\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\mathbf{N}(u)\mathbf{M}(v)\frac{\mathbf{E}(v)}{\mathbf{E}(u)}.\end{split}

(4.51)

Define
Q(t):=\pi\kappa_{N}^{-\frac{2}{3}}|t|^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{M}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}t\right)\mathbf{E}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}t\right)
,
P_{0}(\zeta):=\frac{\mathbf{M}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)}{\mathbf{E}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)}
and
P_{1}(\zeta):=\frac{\mathbf{N}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)}{\mathbf{E}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)}
, it follows that

|K(\zeta,t)|\leq P_{0}(\zeta)Q(t)\penalty\ \penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ |\partial_{1}K(\zeta,t)|\leq P_{1}(\zeta)Q(t)

from (
4.50
) and (
4.51
). Moreover, according to
[
19
, Equation
(11.2.15)
]
,
\lambda_{0}:=\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\pi\left|x\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{M}^{2}(x)\right\}
is a finite number which is slightly greater than
1
and
\lambda_{1}:=\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\pi\mathbf{E}(x)\mathbf{M}(x)\left|x\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}|Ai(x)|\right\}=1
. Now going back to the controlling constants as in (
4.38
), since the function
J(t)
is taken as
Ai\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}t\right)
in our setting,

\begin{array}[]{cc}\kappa:=\sup\limits_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|Q(t)J(t)|=\sup\limits_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\pi\kappa_{N}^{-\frac{2}{3}}|t|^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{M}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}t\right)\mathbf{E}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}t\right)Ai\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}t\right)\right|=\frac{1}{\kappa_{N}},\\
\\
\kappa_{0}:=\sup\limits_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|Q(t)P_{0}(t)|=\sup\limits_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\pi\kappa_{N}^{-\frac{2}{3}}|t|^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{M}^{2}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}t\right)\right|=\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\kappa_{N}}.\end{array}

The convergence of
\Phi(z)
, or equally,
\mathcal{V}(\zeta)
, is straightforward by the expansion of
\Psi(\zeta)
in Proposition
4.6
, which will be discussed later. Consequently, Lemma
4.4
applies and hence,

|\epsilon_{2}(\kappa_{N},z)|\leq P_{0}(\zeta)\frac{\kappa}{\kappa_{0}}\left(e^{\kappa_{0}\Phi(z)}-1\right)\leq\frac{\mathbf{M}(\kappa_{N}^{2/3}\zeta)}{\mathbf{E}(\kappa_{N}^{2/3}\zeta)}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\lambda_{0}\mathcal{V}(\zeta)}{\kappa_{N}}\right)-1\right]

since
\lambda_{0}>1
.
∎

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\epsilon_{2}(\kappa_{N},z)
be the error term and
\mathcal{V}(\omega_{N},\zeta)
be the controlling function introduced before. Then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have
\mathcal{V}(\omega,\zeta)
is bounded for any
\omega\in[\delta,2-\delta]
and
s\in\left[s_{0},\frac{2\mu_{n,N}}{3\sigma_{n,N}}\right]
when
N
is large. Hence, the error is controlled by

|\epsilon_{2}(\kappa_{N},z)|\leq C(\delta)\frac{\mathbf{M}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)}{\kappa_{N}\mathbf{E}\left(\kappa_{N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta\right)}

(4.52)

for some constant
C(\delta)
determined by
\delta
when
N
is large under our assumption.

Proof.

Adapting the method in
[
8
]
of the discussion around
z_{2}
, the bound around
z_{1}
can be obtained similarly. Since
z=\frac{x_{N}(s)}{\kappa_{N}}
, we see
0<z<2
for any
s\in\left[s_{0},\frac{2\mu_{n,N}}{3\sigma_{n,N}}\right]
when
N
is large. Hence,
[
19
, Lemma
11.3.1
]
applies if we replace
(a,b)
by
(0,2]
and
x_{0}
by
z_{1}
. Recalling
\zeta\rightarrow\infty
as
z\rightarrow 0
, we first split
(0,2]
into two pieces for the convenience of argument. In fact, according to Proposition
4.6
,
\Psi(\zeta)\sim\frac{5}{16\zeta^{2}}
when
\zeta
is large and hence, there exists
\zeta_{0}
large enough such that
\Psi(\zeta)\leq\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}}
for any
\zeta>\zeta_{0}
. Let
z_{0}
be the corresponding value such that
\zeta(z_{0})=\zeta_{0}
, then split
(0,2]
into
(0,z_{0})\cup[z_{0},2]
. For the second piece, recalling that

\frac{2}{3}\zeta^{\frac{3}{2}}(z)=\int_{z}^{z_{1}}f^{1/2}(\omega_{N},t)dt

for

and any fixed
\omega_{N}
, we have

\tilde{f}(z)=p^{2}(\omega_{N},z)\left(\frac{3}{2}q(\omega_{N},z)\right)^{-\frac{2}{3}}

where

p(\omega_{N},z)=\frac{(z_{2}-z)^{1/2}}{2z},\penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ q(\omega_{N},z)=\frac{1}{(z_{1}-z)^{3/2}}\int_{z}^{z_{1}}(z_{1}-t)^{1/2}p(\omega_{N},t)dt.

As indicated by Olver in
[
19
]
,
q(\omega_{N},z)\rightarrow\frac{2}{3}p(\omega_{N},z_{1})>0
as

, we see both

and
q(\omega,z)
are bounded away from

on
[\delta,2-\delta]\times[z_{0},2]
. This fact makes both
\tilde{f}(\omega,z)
and
\tilde{f}^{-1}(\omega,z)
well-behaved functions on
[\delta,2-\delta]\times[z_{0},2]
and hence,

\Psi(\zeta)=\tilde{f}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\frac{d^{2}\left(\tilde{f}^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)}{d\zeta^{2}}+\frac{g}{\tilde{f}}

a continuous function on the same domain. According the the explicit L–G transform in (
3.4
), it is easy to see
\zeta
is a nonincreasing function with respect to
z
. So for any
\omega\in[\delta,2-\delta]
, we conclude
\zeta_{2}(\omega)\leq\zeta(\omega):=\zeta(\omega,z)\leq\zeta_{0}(\omega)
where
\zeta_{2}
equals the value of
\zeta
corresponding to

.
Combining the explicit expression of
\mathcal{V}
defined in Proposition
4.7
, for any
\omega\in[\delta,2-\delta]
and

large enough,

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}(\omega,\zeta)&=\int_{\zeta(\omega)}^{\infty}|y|^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\Psi(\omega,y)|dy\leq\int_{\{z\in[z_{0},2]\}}|y|^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\Psi(\omega,y)|dy+\int_{\{z\in(0,z_{0})\}}|y|^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\Psi(\omega,y)|dy\\
&\leq\int_{\zeta_{2}(\omega)}^{0}|y|^{-1/2}|\Psi(\omega,y)|dy+\int_{0}^{\zeta_{0}(\omega)}|y|^{-1/2}|\Psi(\omega,y)|dy+\int_{\zeta_{0}(\omega)}^{\infty}|y|^{-1/2}|\Psi(\omega,y)|dy\\

where

,
S_{r}:=\sup_{\omega\in[\delta,2-\delta]}\zeta_{r}(\omega)
and

is some constant determined by
\delta
only. Here we used the fact
z_{0}
can be small such that
\zeta_{0}
and

are both very large and hence, the convergence of the last integral in the fifth line always holds.
Given the uniform boundedness of
\mathcal{V}
for
\omega\in[\delta,2-\delta]
, (
4.52
) directly follows from the bound in Proposition

.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
R_{n-1,N}(s)
and
R_{n,N-1}(s)
be the integral remainders defined in (
4.30
). For
s\in\left[s_{0},N^{\frac{1}{6}}\right]
, we have

N^{\frac{2}{3}}R_{n-1,N}(s),\penalty\ N^{\frac{2}{3}}R_{n,N-1}(s)\leq C(s_{0},\gamma)e^{-\frac{s}{2}}

when
N
is large for some constant
C(s_{0},\gamma)
.

Proof.

Split
\left[s_{0},N^{\frac{1}{6}}\right]
into
[s_{0},2s_{0}^{\prime}]
and
\left[2s_{0}^{\prime},N^{\frac{1}{6}}\right]
as in Lemma
3.11
. For any
s\in[s_{0},2s_{0}^{\prime}]
, we see
-2|s_{0}|\leq\kappa_{n-1,N}^{\frac{2}{3}}\zeta(\tilde{x}_{N}(s))=s+d_{n-1,N}(s)\leq 4s_{0}^{\prime}
when
N
is large. Therefore, for any
(s,t)\in[s_{0},2s_{0}^{\prime}]\times[0,d_{n-1,N}(s)]
,
|(s+t)Ai(s+t)|\leq M(s_{0})
for some constant
M
determined by
s_{0}
and hence,

\begin{split}R_{n-1,N}(s)&=\left|\int_{0}^{d_{n-1,N}(s)}(d_{n-1,N}(s)-t)(s+t)Ai(s+t)dt\right|\\
&\leq M(s_{0})\left|\int_{0}^{d_{n-1,N}(s)}(d_{n-1,N}(s)-t)dt\right|\\
&\leq C(s_{0})(d_{n-1,N}(s))^{2}\end{split}

(4.53)

for
N
large enough. As
d_{n-1,N}(s)=\Theta\left(N^{-\frac{1}{3}}\right)
according to (
4.31
) in this regime, the inequality:

N^{\frac{2}{3}}R_{n-1,N}(s)\leq\left(\sup_{s_{0}\leq s\leq 2s_{0}^{\prime}}C(s_{0},\gamma)e^{\frac{s}{2}}\right)e^{-\frac{s}{2}}=C(s_{0},\gamma)e^{-\frac{s}{2}}

(4.54)

follows immediately from (
4.53
).

For
s\in\left[2s_{0}^{\prime},N^{\frac{1}{6}}\right]
, the fact that
\min\{s+d_{n-1,N}(s),s\}\geq\frac{s}{2}\geq 1
when is
N
is large, has already been proven in our previous discussion for

. Combining the property that the Airy function is nonincreasing and positive on this interval, for
N
large, we get

\begin{split}R_{n-1,N}(s)&=\left|\int_{0}^{d_{n-1,N}(s)}(d_{n-1,N}(s)-t)(s+t)Ai(s+t)dt\right|\\
&\leq Ai\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)\left|\int_{0}^{d_{n-1,N}(s)}(d_{n-1,N}(s)-t)(s+t)dt\right|\\
&=Ai\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)\frac{\left|d_{n-1,N}^{3}(s)+2d^{2}_{n-1,N}(s)s\right|}{6}\leq Ai\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)d_{n-1,N}^{2}(s)\left(|d_{n-1,N}(s)|+2|s|\right)\\
&\leq C(s_{0},\gamma)Ai\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)d_{n-1,N}^{2}(s)\left(|s|^{3}+2|s|\right)\leq C(s_{0},\gamma)|s|^{3}Ai\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)d_{n-1,N}^{2}(s)\\
&\leq C(s_{0},\gamma)|s|^{9}Ai\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)N^{-\frac{2}{3}}\end{split}

(4.55)

where we used that fact that
d_{n-1,N}(s)=O\left(s^{3}N^{-\frac{1}{3}}\right)
from (
4.31
) and
s\geq 2
. Using the bound (
3.19
) for the Airy function and the fact that
s\geq 2
when
N
is large, we further get

N^{\frac{2}{3}}R_{n-1,N}\leq C(s_{0},\gamma)e^{-\frac{s}{2}}.

(4.56)

The estimations (
4.54
) and (
4.56
) together give us the bound for

. The promised result for
R_{n,N-1}
can be obtained by the same argument.
∎

Proposition 4.10
.

N^{\frac{2}{3}}|R^{\prime}_{n-1,N}(s)||Ai^{\prime}(s)|,\penalty\ N^{\frac{2}{3}}|R^{\prime}_{n,N-1}(s)||Ai^{\prime}(s)|\leq C(s_{0},\gamma)e^{-\frac{s}{2}}
