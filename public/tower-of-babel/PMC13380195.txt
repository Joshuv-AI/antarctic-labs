High-intensity Training and Asthma
Authors: Raquel García Hernáez, Alicia Padilla Galo, Francisco-Javier González-Barcala
Published: 2026

Physical inactivity is a global health problem, as approximately one third of the adult population does not meet the ≥150 min/week of aerobic activity recommended by the World Health Organization. In this context, exercise is positioned as a fundamental therapeutic tool due to its capacity to modulate the immune response.1 However, current evidence suggests that this relationship is not linear, but rather dependent on training load, following a “J-shaped” curve in which strenuous exercise may be associated with a transient state of immunosuppression and increased susceptibility to infections.2

In elite sport, this phenomenon acquires a relevant clinical dimension. Asthma and, particularly, exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB) are more frequent in athletes than in general population, especially in elite athletes and in endurance or winter sports disciplines, where EIB may reach prevalences of up to 30–70%.3, 4 These data should not be interpreted as incidental findings, but rather as frequent manifestations of airway dysfunction in this context.

The pathophysiology of this condition is multifactorial and depends on exercise intensity, minute ventilation, environmental conditions, and individual susceptibility. Sustained hyperventilation, particularly when minute ventilation exceeds certain thresholds, leads to impaired conditioning of inspired air. Thermal, osmotic, and epithelial microtrauma theories explain how this repeated stress triggers inflammatory cascades that may progress from transient bronchoconstriction to airway remodelling in athletes undergoing prolonged training.5 Eucapnic voluntary hyperpnoea is currently considered one of the reference diagnostic tools for its evaluation.6

From a clinical perspective, the main issue lies not only in its physiological complexity but also in its under-recognition. Asthma in athletes presents a heterogeneous phenotypic expression influenced by sport type, environment, and training intensity.5 In competitive swimming, for example, a mixed inflammatory pattern has been described associated with chronic exposure to chloramines, with a predominance of neutrophilic mediators such as leukotriene B4.7, 8, 9 Importantly, symptom reversibility after cessation of exposure reinforces its acquired and environmental nature.10

This concept is further expanded when considering that airway damage is not solely inflammatory but also structural. The presence of epithelial desquamation and elevated biomarkers such as IL-8 suggest persistent injury with potential for bronchial remodelling.11, 12 In winter sport athletes, thermal stress adds an additional component, with evidence of extracellular matrix remodelling and neutrophilic inflammation associated with cold environments.13, 14 In endurance disciplines, epithelial barrier disruption has also been described, with increased levels of markers such as CC-16, reflecting exercise-induced damage.15

Beyond these mechanisms, evidence compels a re-evaluation of the clinical impact of high-intensity exercise on respiratory health in athletes. Asthma and EIB should not be considered marginal findings, but rather frequent clinical entities that may go unrecognised in routine practice. Their underdiagnosis is facilitated by the normalisation of respiratory symptoms in the sporting context, delaying appropriate recognition and management. In this regard, the current challenge is not only diagnostic, but also the systematic identification of these conditions in elite sports medicine practice. Early recognition is essential not only to preserve lung function, but also to optimise athletic performance and safety.
