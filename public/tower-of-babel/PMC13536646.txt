Beyond the offense: clinical correlates of repeated incarceration among justice-involved male adolescents with substance use disorder
Authors: Samet Can Demi̇rci̇, Ahmet Özaslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examined whether repeated incarceration among justice-involved male adolescents with substance use disorder (SUD) reflects not only a legal consequence but also a marker of a more severe clinical risk profile. It also evaluated the roles of addiction severity, family criminal history, psychological resilience, and meaning in life in repeated incarceration.MethodsThe sample comprised 203 male adolescents with a history of substance use who were incarcerated in a closed correctional facility. Participants were classified as first-time incarcerated or repeatedly incarcerated. Addiction severity was assessed with the Addiction Profile Index for Adolescents, psychological resilience with the Brief Resilience Scale, and meaning in life with the Meaning in Life Questionnaire. Group differences were examined using chi-square tests and independent-samples t-tests, and independent predictors of repeated incarceration were analyzed with binary logistic regression.ResultsOverall, 32.5% of participants had a history of repeated incarceration. Compared with first-time incarcerated adolescents, this group showed greater addiction severity and lower psychological resilience and meaning in life, whereas age and offense type did not differ significantly. In binary logistic regression, the model was significant and explained 34.2% of the variance in repeated incarceration. Family history of crime was the strongest predictor, increasing the risk 5.20-fold. Higher addiction severity increased the likelihood of repeated incarceration, whereas meaning in life was protective. Age and psychological resilience were not independently significant in the multivariable model.ConclusionsRepeated incarceration may represent not merely a legal outcome, but a clinical indicator of greater addiction burden, adverse family context, and limited psychological protective resources. Rehabilitation should therefore extend beyond containment and address clinical severity, family functioning, and purpose-related psychological resources simultaneously.

Background

Adolescence is a critical developmental stage during which substance use can quickly progress to problematic use and substance use disorder (SUD); relapse–recovery fluctuations can become established early on [1, 2]. Therefore, adolescent SUD should often be approached not as a “single acute episode” but as a clinical process requiring regular monitoring and long-term support. Particularly since the risk of relapse and functional impairment is high after treatment/discharge, continuing care and seamless transitions between levels of care are considered fundamental components of recovery in adolescent SUD [3, 4].

Evidence-based adolescent SUD treatments are primarily psychosocial in nature. They involve combinations of components such as family-focused approaches (e.g., multidimensional family therapy), cognitive behavioral therapy, and motivational interviewing-based interventions [5–7]. In this context, “rehabilitation” is not merely a temporary restriction of access to substances; it is a structured clinical recovery process that targets the triggers determining the severity of substance use disorder, restores functionality, and reduces the risk of relapse.

Among adolescents who come into contact with the justice system, those with a history of SUD are one of the subgroups with the highest clinical need [8, 9]. It has been reported that SUD and accompanying psychiatric problems are seen at high rates among adolescents in institutional/prison settings; SUD is among the most frequently encountered diagnostic clusters in this population [8, 10]. From a clinical perspective, this picture shows that a significant proportion of these young people should be addressed not only within a penal-judicial framework, but also in terms of the effects of SUD on clinical severity and functionality. Consequently, it can be said that rehabilitation goals for adolescents in prison should also be largely structured around the axes of SUD severity, functional improvement, and continuity of care [8–11].

However, the transition from forensic care to SUD rehabilitation does not always occur automatically. Best practices require a “continuum of care” in which the steps of screening, assessment, referral to appropriate services, initiation of treatment, and retention in treatment are linked together [12, 13]. In practice, however, it is reported that there are gaps between these steps and that significant difficulties are encountered, particularly in linking individuals to care after discharge from the institution [9]. The clinical significance of these discontinuations becomes even more apparent when considering findings that indicate that the risk of relapse into substance use after discharge from the institution is often accompanied by an increased risk of re-offending [14, 15]. For this reason, preventing relapse into substance use can be considered closely related to reducing the risk of re-engagement with the justice system/re-offending. From this perspective, it is important to determine which clinical dynamics rehabilitation should include for this group with a history of substance use disorder [9, 16].

Within this clinical framework, repeated incarceration can be considered a practical indicator that SUD rehabilitation is unsustainable, rather than a judicial “crime” criterion [9, 17]. In our study, incarceration history is defined in concrete and clinically observable terms as those who have been incarcerated once and those who have been incarcerated multiple times (repeated incarceration experience). Repeated incarceration experience may reflect not only repeated institutional exposure, but also the accumulation of clinical processes that may accompany worsening SUD, along with disruptions in continuity of care and separation from protective bonds such as family and school [17, 18]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that in closed environments where high-risk adolescents congregate, risky norms can be reinforced through peer influence, and “deviant peer contagion” processes can come into play [19, 20]. Such mechanisms reinforce the idea that SUD rehabilitation should be conducted not only through “restriction” but also through structured treatment and psychosocial interventions that support recovery [5, 7, 9].

In terms of the clinical goals of rehabilitation, it is necessary to evaluate not only risk factors but also the psychological resources that promote recovery. Psychological resilience refers to the adolescent’s capacity to adapt despite stressors and is based on “normal” protective processes that can be developed [21]. The adolescent resilience literature provides a framework for clinical interventions by identifying protective/buffering mechanisms that enable healthy development under risk conditions [22]. Resilience in the context of SUD is related to clinical processes such as coping with triggers, regulating emotions, and being able to readjust despite negative consequences associated with substance use [23–25]. Similarly, meaning in life is related to the adolescent’s perception of their life as purposeful, consistent, and valuable, and is an important axis that fuels motivation for recovery. The presence of meaning in life and the search for meaning have been defined as a construct that can be validly measured in clinical studies [26]. It has been reported that a higher sense of meaning in life among adolescents is associated with psychological well-being and may play a protective role against certain risk behaviors [27]. Clinically, this suggests that future orientation and the reinforcement of goals and values in SUD rehabilitation may be important for treatment participation and maintenance [28, 29].

However, in juvenile SUD rehabilitation, limiting interventions solely to individual-focused approaches may be insufficient to reduce the risk of repeated incarceration. In juvenile SUD, the family system is one of the fundamental ecological contexts that shapes the course of the disorder and the risk patterns associated with the judicial trajectory [30, 31]. Indicators such as family criminal history; chronic environmental disadvantage, weakened parental control, and cumulative stress burden can disrupt the rehabilitation process. Indeed, meta-analytic evidence confirms that parents’ antisocial behaviors significantly increase children’s risk of being drawn into crime [32].

The originality of this study lies in its integrated clinical approach to repeated incarceration among male adolescents with substance use disorder and a history of detention, by examining addiction severity, psychological recovery resources, and family criminal history within the same framework. Elucidating these relationships may offer practical implications by suggesting that rehabilitation is not achieved through restraint alone, but requires the joint targeting of continuity of care, clinical severity, and psychological resources.

The present study is conceptually consistent with the Risk–Need–Responsivity framework, which emphasizes matching intervention intensity to risk level, targeting clinically relevant needs, and considering individual responsivity factors in justice-involved populations [33]. In adolescents with SUD, addiction severity and family criminal history may represent key risk/need domains, whereas resilience and meaning in life may reflect protective or responsivity-related psychological resources [34, 35]. This perspective also aligns with the behavioral health services cascade, in which screening, referral, treatment initiation, and continuity of care are critical for justice-involved youth with substance use problems [9, 15]. By examining these risk and protective domains together, the present study aims to clarify whether repeated incarceration marks a clinically more vulnerable subgroup rather than merely an offense-based category.

Accordingly, this study aimed to examine whether repeated incarceration (one vs. multiple incarcerations) is associated with greater addiction severity and whether family criminal history, resilience, and meaning in life are related to this risk among male adolescents aged 15–18 with substance use disorder. We hypothesized that repeated incarceration would be associated with higher clinical severity, lower psychological resources, and an additional risk burden related to family criminal history.

Methods

Participants

This study was conducted with male adolescents aged 15–18 (n = 203) who were detained (pre-trial detainees) at the Juvenile Detention Center between March 15 and May 15, 2025. The study included adolescents with a history of substance use, who had the cognitive capacity to independently complete the scales, and who had been in the institution for at least 1 month and no more than 3 months. Those with acute psychotic symptoms/severe mood exacerbation, those with severe neurological/cognitive impairment, and those who did not consent were excluded from the study.

Juvenile justice context in Turkey

In Turkey, juvenile criminal responsibility is age-graded. Children under 12 years of age have no criminal liability. Children aged 12 to under 15 may be held criminally responsible only if they are considered capable of understanding the legal meaning and consequences of the act and controlling their behavior. Adolescents aged 15 to under 18 are criminally responsible, although age-related sentence reductions apply. Under Turkish child protection legislation, detention is prohibited for children under 15 only for offenses carrying a maximum prison sentence of five years or less. Juvenile closed correctional institutions are institutions for child detainees and certain child convicts, and children aged 12–18 may be housed in these institutions separately according to age, sex, and physical development.

The 1–3 month hospitalization requirement in the inclusion criteria; initial adjustment difficulties and possible withdrawal symptoms [36, 37] and the potential confounding of measurements by institutionalization processes [38, 39] that may emerge in longer hospitalizations. Similarly, the repeat incarceration history group was limited to adolescents entering a closed institution for the second time; thus, attempts were made to reduce potential confounding factors associated with cumulative institutional exposure that might be observed in chronic multi-recidivists with three or more incarceration histories [40, 41].

Measures

Sociodemographic and clinical information form

It was prepared to describe the participants’ basic living conditions (age, education, family structure, etc.) and their clinical-forensic background. Three key variables have been standardized to reflect the clinical focus of the study: (1) Incarceration History: Incarceration history was coded as first placement versus second placement in a juvenile closed correctional/remand institution. This variable did not refer to the number of final convictions or completed prison sentences. A second placement may occur because of a separate pending judicial file, a new alleged offense after release, re-detention during an ongoing proceeding, or violation of judicial control obligations. Thus, repeated incarceration was operationalized as second juvenile closed-institution placement. (2) Offense Type: The index offense leading to the current incarceration was classified into four categories for descriptive analysis. Violent/interpersonal offenses (violence/assault, homicide/attempted homicide), property/financial offenses (theft, property damage, fraud), drug-related offenses (drug possession/possession for use, sale/procurement, trafficking, transportation/smuggling, brokerage/distribution), and sexual offenses (sexual offenses). In cases where multiple acts were reported, coding was based on the index/most serious offense leading to the current incarceration. (3) Family History of Crime: In order to specifically measure the adolescent’s core family criminological environment, distant relatives were excluded, and only the presence of a criminal history among first-degree relatives (mother, father, sibling) was coded as a binary (present/absent) contextual risk predictor.

Addiction Profile Index–Adolescent Form (API-A)

API-A assesses multidimensional addiction severity in adolescents aged 15–18 and was developed from the adult Addiction Profile Index (API) [42]. The Turkish adolescent version includes 25 items and yields five subscale scores: substance use characteristics, diagnosis, impact on life, craving, and motivation [42, 43].The total score is computed using the established weighted formula: (substance use characteristics/2) + (diagnosis/5) + (impact on life/6) + (craving/4) + (motivation/3) [42, 43]. API-A has self-report and clinician-report formats; the self-report format was used in this study. Internal consistency for the total scale was reported as α = 0.87 in the validation studies and was α = 0.93 in the present sample.

Brief Resilience Scale (BRS)

Resilience was measured using the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS), a 6-item, 5-point Likert self-report instrument assessing the ability to “bounce back” from stress [44]. The scale captures perceived recovery capacity after stressful experiences, with items reflecting the extent to which individuals recover quickly or have difficulty returning to normal after adversity [44]. The Turkish adaptation and psychometric evaluation were conducted by Doğan (2015) [45]. Three items are reverse-coded; total scores range from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater resilience. Reported internal consistency is α = 0.79 [45]; in the present sample, internal consistency was α = 0.83.

Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ)

Meaning in life was assessed with the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ), a 10-item, 7-point Likert self-report measure comprising two subscales: Presence of Meaning and Search for Meaning [26]. The Presence subscale assesses the extent to which individuals perceive their lives as meaningful and purposeful, whereas the Search subscale assesses active efforts to find or deepen a sense of meaning [26]. Subscale scores were analyzed separately. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by [46]; in the present sample, internal consistency was α = 0.89.

Procedure

The study was conducted with the approval of the relevant Ethics Committee and the permissions of the Ministry of Justice’s Department of Prisons and Detention Houses, under the coordination of the institution’s management and psychosocial services. Participants (and minors in accordance with institutional procedures) were informed that the research was based on voluntary participation, would not affect their judicial processes, and that confidentiality principles were explained, and their informed consent/approval was obtained. The scales were administered face-to-face in interview rooms within the institution, where confidentiality was ensured, accompanied by researcher explanations when necessary, and the sessions lasted an average of 25–30 min.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 28.0. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies (n), percentages (%), means (M), and standard deviations (SD), were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and scale scores. Normality assumptions were evaluated using skewness and kurtosis coefficients, with values between − 1.5 and + 1.5 considered acceptable. Associations between categorical variables (e.g., family history of crime, index offense category) and incarceration history (first-time vs. repeated incarceration) were examined using Pearson’s Chi-square test. Independent samples t-tests were used to compare age, API-A, Resilience, and Meaning of Life scores between the first-time incarcerated and repeatedly incarcerated groups. Finally, binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify risk and protective factors associated with repeated incarceration. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and explanatory power was reported with Nagelkerke R². Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Use of generative AI

A large language model (ChatGPT, OpenAI) was used to assist with English language polishing and reference formatting. The authors verified the accuracy and integrity of the content, take full responsibility for the work, and confirm that no AI system met authorship criteria or had access to non-public data.

Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the independent predictors of repeated incarceration (Table 3). The model was statistically significant (Omnibus X² = 57.10, df = 5, p < 0.001) and explained 34.2% of the variance in repeated incarceration (Nagelkerke R² = 0.342). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated adequate model fit (X² = 12.17, p = 0.143). Family history of crime was the strongest predictor, increasing the odds of repeated incarceration by 5.20 times (OR = 5.20, 95% CI: 2.55–10.60, p < 0.001). Higher API-A scores were also associated with greater odds of repeated incarceration (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.01–1.19, p = 0.015). In contrast, Meaning of Life emerged as a protective factor, with each one-unit increase associated with lower odds of repeated incarceration (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99, p = 0.005). Age and Resilience were not statistically significant in the multivariable model (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study aimed to examine whether repeated incarceration among justice-involved male adolescents with substance use disorder was associated with greater addiction severity, lower psychological resources, and increased familial risk. Our findings indicate that adolescents with repeated incarceration experience higher levels of addiction severity and lower levels of psychological resilience and meaning in life. In the multivariate model, a family history of crime emerged as the strongest predictor, addiction severity as an independent risk factor, and meaning in life as an independent protective factor. In contrast, the fact that the type of crime did not differ significantly between the first-time and repeat incarceration groups suggests that re-entry into prison may be related not so much to the nature of the crime committed, but rather to the clinical severity of SUD accompanying similar crime patterns, risks in the family context, and insufficient protective psychological resources [47–49]. The fact that age and psychological resilience did not remain significant in the multivariate model also supports the notion that, in this sample, the clinical severity of SUD and family ecology were more decisive than legal labels. These findings suggest that repeated incarceration experience may be an indicator associated with more severe clinical needs in this sample [8, 50].

Our findings indicate that adolescents with repeated incarceration experience have significantly higher API-A scores compared to first-time incarcerated adolescents, and that increasing API-A scores independently predict the risk of repeated incarceration. This finding suggests that the institutional cycle among adolescents in contact with the justice system may be more closely related to the clinical severity of SUD than to the nature of the offense committed. Studies on justice-involved youth have shown that substance use and the risk of re-offending persist when in-institution interventions are not sufficiently supported by linkage to care after discharge [9, 14, 17]. Additionally, high-risk adolescents’ intensive interaction with peers exhibiting similar problem behaviors in closed institutions can reinforce substance use and antisocial behavior patterns through deviant peer contagion and deviancy training processes [19, 20, 51, 52]. Therefore, high API-A scores can be interpreted as a finding indicating a more severe clinical risk profile in the group with repeated incarceration experience.

In our logistic regression model, the strongest predictor of repeated incarceration history was family history of crime, which increased this likelihood by approximately 5.20 times. This finding suggests that the course of SUD in adolescents should be assessed within a broader ecological context shaped not only by the severity of individual symptoms but also by the criminological history and control capacity of the family system. This interpretation is also supported by recent evidence showing that the psychosocial burden of polysubstance use disorder is embedded in the family system, where resilience, stigma, and caregiving burden are closely associated with quality of life [53]. The intergenerational transmission dimension of the relationship between parental criminality and antisocial behavior in children and adolescents has been strongly defined in the literature [32, 47, 54]. Indeed, Besemer et al. (2017) demonstrated at the meta-analytic level that children of parents with a criminal history have a significantly increased risk of criminal behavior [32]. In our sample, the fact that a family history of crime remained a strong predictor independent of other clinical variables suggests that familial risk patterns may be important in terms of recidivism. This supports the importance of family-based interventions aimed at strengthening parental control, setting boundaries, and promoting prosocial role models [30, 55]. Therefore, our findings suggest that approaches focusing solely on the individual may not be sufficient in adolescent SUD; evidence-based family-based interventions such as Multidimensional Family Therapy (MDFT) that directly target family functioning, parental monitoring and boundary-setting skills, and crime-related family patterns may be particularly important from a clinical perspective [6, 30, 56].

In terms of psychological protectors, our findings have revealed that adolescents with repeated incarceration experiences have significantly lower levels of both psychological resilience and meaning in life. Psychological resilience is defined as a dynamic capacity that supports positive adjustment under risk; in young people who come into contact with the judicial system, it is associated with lower psychological distress and fewer concurrent mental/substance use problems [21, 22, 24, 25]. However, the lack of significance of psychological resilience as an independent predictor in our multivariate model suggests that the effect of this variable may have been extinguished within the shared variance with other strong factors such as family criminal history, addiction severity, and meaning in life. On the other hand, the fact that meaning in life remains an independent protective factor suggests that the risk of repeated imprisonment may be more closely related not only to the capacity to cope with stress but also to the adolescent’s ability to organize their life around purpose, direction, and values. Indeed, it has been shown that in adolescents, meaning in life serves a protective function against risky behavior patterns, including substance use, and negative psychological outcomes; moreover, higher future orientation in young people with a serious criminal history has been shown to limit the increase in substance use over time [27, 28]. This finding suggests that interventions supporting adolescents in organizing their lives around purpose and direction may be clinically significant. This interpretation is consistent with evidence from Turkey indicating that hope and social support are important correlates of flourishing in addiction recovery, underscoring the potential value of strengthening psychosocial protective resources in rehabilitation processes [57].

The fact that resilience did not remain an independent predictor may suggest that general stress-recovery capacity is partly overshadowed by more proximal or structurally embedded risks, particularly addiction severity and family criminal history. Family criminal history may capture broader ecological influences such as exposure to antisocial norms, weakened supervision, and reduced support for treatment engagement [35, 58]. In contrast, meaning in life may reflect a more motivational and future-oriented protective resource, which may be especially relevant for adolescents whose legal involvement and substance use are accompanied by weakened future orientation [28, 59]. Clinically, this finding suggests that interventions for justice-involved adolescents with SUD may benefit from strengthening purpose, future orientation, and personally meaningful goals alongside addiction-focused and family-informed approaches [9, 60].

These findings are consistent with the current literature indicating that adolescent SUD is a recurrent clinical process requiring assessment, treatment, and aftercare rather than a single acute episode [60, 61]. In this context, it is particularly important to strengthen treatment with services that support post-discharge follow-up care and adherence to treatment [15, 60]. The high incidence of relapse after treatment in adolescents and the difficulties encountered in maintaining continuity of care particularly highlight the persistent nature of this clinical course. The current literature consistently demonstrates that the prevalence of SUD among incarcerated youth is extremely high, that there are significant difficulties in accessing treatment and maintaining continuity of care, and that this psychiatric burden can persist over time [15, 62]. Additionally, it has been reported that substance use and delinquency often overlap during adolescence, and therefore, services that effectively target substance use can also have positive effects on delinquent behavior [56, 63, 64]. Therefore, repeated incarceration can be considered a risk indicator that requires closer clinical monitoring.

These findings suggest that clinical assessment in adolescents with SUD who come into contact with the judicial system should not be limited to current offending behavior or acute substance use symptoms. In the assessment process, considering familial risk patterns, factors that may hinder treatment continuation, and the adolescent’s future orientation together may provide a more appropriate clinical framework [9, 64, 65]. Similarly, it may be beneficial to enhance interventions not only with short-term symptom control but also with developmentally appropriate rehabilitative practices that strengthen family involvement, support continuing care after institutional release, and promote linkage to care [15, 60, 63, 66]. In this context, repeated incarceration history can be considered an indicator that may be associated with the need for more comprehensive and continuous clinical follow-up in this group.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, since the research has a cross-sectional design, it is not possible to make definitive conclusions about the direction and causal nature of the relationships between repeated incarceration experience, severity of addiction, psychological resilience, and meaning in life. Therefore, the findings indicate relationships between the variables in question; however, they do not provide direct evidence regarding the temporal sequence or causal mechanisms of these relationships. Second, the study was conducted only with male adolescents who had contact with the justice system and a history of substance use; therefore, the generalizability of the results to female adolescents, clinical samples without a history of contact with the justice system, or different sociocultural contexts is limited. Third, since a significant portion of the data was obtained using self-report scales, measurement limitations such as social desirability, recall bias, and under- or over-reporting of symptoms may be present. Future longitudinal and multi-center studies may more clearly reveal the course of these relationships over time. Fourth, the study did not include detailed indicators of treatment access, treatment engagement, service referral, or continuity of care after release; therefore, the role of service-level factors in repeated incarceration could not be examined. Although focusing on male adolescents is clinically meaningful because boys constitute a substantial proportion of justice-involved youth, the findings may not generalize to female adolescents or mixed-gender samples.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study suggests that repeated incarceration experiences among male adolescents who have come into contact with the justice system and have a history of substance use may be considered a clinically meaningful indicator associated not only with a criminal history but also with a more severe addiction profile, a more adverse familial risk context, and more limited psychological protective resources. In particular, the emergence of a family history of crime as a strong risk indicator, the association of addiction severity with repeated incarceration, and the emergence of meaning in life as a protective variable point to the need to address this group not only at the symptom level but within a broader ecological and developmental framework. These findings suggest that structuring assessment and intervention processes for adolescents in contact with the justice system to include the family context, treatment continuity, and protective psychological resources may be clinically important.
