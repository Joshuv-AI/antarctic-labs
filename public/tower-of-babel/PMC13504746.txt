Micro-functional protein complexes mining in biological intelligent computing: a weighted network approach
Authors: Tie Hua Zhou, Tian Yu Jin, Ling Wang, Xi Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdentifying protein complexes is of great significance for drug target discovery and understanding disease mechanisms. Recognizing the variations of different protein complexes in individual organisms aids in the development of personalized treatment strategies. In order to identify potential protein complexes with distinct modularity and density, as well as overlapping protein complexes, we propose a method called Micro-Clusters Overlap Reconstruction (MCOR) to mine micro-functional protein complexes by reducing the complexity of protein complexes. The method, by incorporating both network topology and protein biological information, can significantly reduce the impact of false-positive interactions in the protein network. Firstly, we create a weighted network based on functional annotation terms and shared neighbors. Secondly, we define a protein selection mechanism to form initial clusters. Thirdly, we define a complex evaluation function to identify complexes in the network with varying modularity and density. Fourthly, we design a seed expansion algorithm that utilizes the complex evaluation function to expand clusters and form complexes. On real data sets from multiple species, we compared MCOR with the currently most advanced seven algorithms. Our findings suggest that MCOR surpasses these algorithms in terms of F-measure and accuracy criteria across networks generated from different species’ data. Moreover, the identified complexes have a low average p-value, thereby confirming the authenticity of the complexes identified by MCOR.Keywords: Bioinformatics, Computational biology, Graph theory, Machine learning, Protein complex identification, Protein interaction network

Introduction

Protein complexes, intricate macromolecular assemblies resulting from multiple protein interactions, exhibit distinct biological functions [1, 2]. Abnormalities in these complexes are intimately linked to the onset and progression of various diseases [3, 4]. For instance, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy induces alterations in the composition and structure of the cardiac troponin regulatory complex, culminating in cellular dysfunction and disease manifestation [5–8]. Consequently, investigating the composition, structure, and function of protein complexes is paramount for elucidating cellular mechanisms and regulatory processes. Moreover, this area of research offers insights into the development of novel therapeutics by uncovering the mechanisms governing biological molecule interactions, thereby informing targeted drug design [9]. Current findings indicate the presence of highly interconnected disease genes within disease networks, with subgraphs formed by these genes potentially representing disease-associated protein complexes. Exploring the causal relationships between these complexes in disease networks can enhance biologists’ comprehension of disease mechanisms, laying the groundwork for innovative treatment strategies [10].

In recent years, advances in high-throughput technologies have given rise to numerous public databases, leading to an increased availability of Protein-protein Interaction Networks (PINs) [11, 12]. The precise identification of protein complexes within organisms presents a major challenge in both biology and bioinformatics. Although laboratory techniques, including two-hybrid system, co-immunoprecipitation, and chemical cross-linking, offer a more precise determination of protein complexes, they often present limitations such as high costs, specialized expertise requirements, incomplete detection, and time-consuming procedures. As a result, researchers have shifted their focus towards leveraging computational biology algorithms to efficiently and accurately identify protein complexes within PINs [13].

Network depictions of PINs typically involve nodes to depict proteins and edges to indicate their interactions. Computational biology algorithms employ various graph clustering techniques to treat the resulting biological subgraphs or clusters as the identified protein complexes. Existing computational biology algorithms can be primarily classified into two categories based on the nature of the information utilized in protein complex identification. The first category encompasses algorithms reliant on topological structure, which exclusively employ topological information from the PIN network for protein complex identification. The second category comprises algorithms that integrate topological structure with biological information, as proteins constituting the same complex generally exhibit similar biological information. Consequently, these algorithms consider not only topological data but also functional annotation terms. Such annotation information encompasses Gene Ontology (GO) annotations [14], pathway annotations [15, 16], and functional domain annotations [17, 18], which substantially enhance the accuracy and validity of the detected complexes.

An overlapping complex consists of multiple protein complexes that share common members while performing distinct biological functions. These overlapping complexes are essential to cellular processes, and understanding their structure can provide valuable insights into cellular functions and mechanisms. However, existing protein complex identification algorithms often struggle to accurately identify overlapping protein complexes, leading to potential loss of information. This difficulty arises mainly because most of these algorithms are based on clustering approaches that assume a rigid partition of proteins into non-overlapping complexes, which fails to capture the inherent complexity and shared components of overlapping protein complexes in biological systems.

Related work

As of now, there are a considerable number of protein complex identification algorithms available. Here, we present two different types of approaches: seed expansion approaches and dynamic protein interaction networks approaches. Next, we will briefly introduce the characteristics of these two types of algorithms and the directions in which they can be improved in the future.

Seed expansion approaches

The identification of protein complexes frequently employs seed expansion approaches. These approaches, such as Heuristic Graph Clustering Algorithm (HGCA) [19], Identification of Protein Complex Algorithm (IPCA) [20], Identification of Protein Complex using Mutual Important Interacting Partner relation (idenPC-MIIP) [21] and Multiple network Embedding for coMplex detectiOn (MEMO) [22], follow two primary steps: seed selection and seed expansion. HGCA, a heuristic graph clustering algorithm, fully considers the rich topological properties of PINs and the intrinsic organizational composition of complexes. IPCA, on the other hand, detects complexes from PINs based on subgraph density, subgraph diameter, and node weight degree, but its accuracy is low and its time complexity is high. The iden-PCMIIP algorithm first identifies the MIIP relationship by searching for all proteins and then forms protein complexes using seed expansion strategies based on the MIIP relationship. This algorithm can identify not only small but also larger protein complexes. Recently, MEMO has gained popularity with the integration of direct homologous information in PINs between different species and the rise of graph representation learning methods. MEMO integrates PIN data sets from different species into one PIN and represents each protein as a set of vectors using graph representation learning methods. The odds of two proteins being present in the same complex is then evaluated using cosine similarity, and protein complexes are mined using seed expansion methods. In addition, some identification algorithms that assign weights to interaction edges, such as Protein Ranking Plus (ProRank+) [23] and PEWCC [24], have been proposed. They first assess the reliability of edges in PINs and filter out interaction edges below a predetermined threshold to obtain more accurate PINs. Then, protein complexes are identified through the use of improved graph clustering algorithms. The effectiveness of seed expansion approaches primarily relies on the choice and expansion techniques of seeds. Therefore, it is crucial for researchers to focus on the rationality of seed identification and expansion methods when developing such algorithms.

Dynamic protein interaction networks approaches

There have been studies indicating that some complexes are gradually formed during spatial and temporal changes [25], and static PINs cannot reflect the dynamic characteristics of protein interactions [26]. Therefore, it is an inevitable trend to shift from identifying protein complexes from static PINs to dynamic PINs. Lei et al. [27] proposed the Topology Potential Weighted Dynamic Protein-protein Interaction Network (TP-WDPIN) framework algorithm as an approach to identify complexes through the combination of various biological information sources, such as subcellular localization data, functional annotation terms, and gene expression. This approach has gained attention as one of the most representative algorithms for this purpose. It first constructs a dynamic PIN from gene expression data and static PINs, and then assigns weights to interaction edges using various biological and topological information. Finally, using a seed expansion algorithm and a core-attachment structure, the method identifies dynamic protein complexes from the dynamic PIN. Yang et al. [28] proposed a classic model algorithm Time Smooth Overlapping Complex Detection (TS-OCD), which can identify dynamic protein complexes from dynamic PINs. The advantage of this type of algorithm is that it can fully utilize gene expression data to establish a dynamic PIN and mine dynamic protein complexes from it, but the identification accuracy and efficiency of this type of algorithm are affected by the accuracy and completeness of biological information. Zhang et al. [29] suggested a core-attachment structure approach for identifying dynamic complexes from dynamic PINs. The performance of dynamic protein interaction networks approaches mainly depends on the method of constructing dynamic PINs and the reliability assessment of protein interaction edges. At the same time, because the size of instantaneous PINs is usually small, applying identification algorithms to such small-scale subnetworks will identify a large number of small subgraphs, many of which may be false positive protein complexes. This is a key focus of this type of method.

Motivation

The shared neighbors between two interacting proteins generally suggest their involvement in the same protein complex and the execution of related functions. Nonetheless, high-throughput experimental approaches frequently introduce noise into PINs. Presently, protein complex identification algorithms have yet to effectively implement weighting methods or efficiently utilize protein functional annotation data. Both module-based and density-based methods have proven to be superior in performance compared to other identification algorithm categories. However, these methods exclusively concentrate on protein complexes with high modularity and high density, neglecting those that exhibit diverse modularity and densities. Additionally, seed expansion-based identification algorithms may inadvertently select globally overlapping proteins as seed proteins, leading to the detection of functional modules rather than protein complexes. This ultimately compromises the accuracy of such algorithms.

Constructing a weighted protein-protein interaction network

Based on functional annotation terms

When two proteins, t and y, interact and have more shared functional annotation information, their functions are more alike, and they are more likely to form the same protein complex. We have formulated a method for determining the similarity between interacting proteins based on Functional Annotation Term (FAT), derived from Gene Ontology (GO) annotations, specifically focusing on the Cellular Component (CC) branch, as presented in Eq. (1). Notably, if two proteins connected by an interaction edge (t, y) have no shared functional annotation information, the edge is deemed to be noise and is therefore discarded.

Here, |FAT(t)| and |FAT(y)| denote the number of entries of functional annotation terms for protein t and y, respectively. The value of |FAT(t) ∩ FAT(y)| indicates the number of functional annotation term entries that are shared between t and y. The more entries of the same functional annotation terms, the stronger the functional connection between the two proteins. Using max(|FAT(t)|, |FAT(y)|) to assess the functional similarity between two proteins allows for a more comprehensive assessment of the functional overlap between the two proteins. This approach considers the possibility that a protein can have numerous functions and engage in multiple biological processes, potentially resulting in more functional annotation terms than its interaction partner. By considering the maximum number of functional annotation terms between two interacting proteins, the approach can capture a broader range of potential functional connections and enhance the precision of identifying protein complexes that share similar functions. To adjust for interaction edges with fewer functional annotation terms, we use Avg(FAT) × max(|FAT(t)|,|FAT(y)|). In Eq. (2), Avg(FAT) denotes the average number of functional annotation terms across all proteins that have such annotations in the network. Additionally, represents the entire quantity of proteins in the network that have functional terms. By incorporating functional annotation information, MCOR can effectively identify protein complexes that share similar functions and aid in comprehending the roles of proteins within the network.

The gene co-expressions’ role is implicitly integral to the FAT calculation, as proteins with higher functional annotation similarity (determined by shared GO terms) are likely to be co-expressed or participate in similar biological processes. This similarity influences the initial formation of protein clusters before expansion, indicating that co-expression is a foundational concept within the FAT approach, contributing to the precision of complex identification by emphasizing biologically relevant interactions.

Based on shared neighbors

The proposed method, called the Shared Neighbor Similarity Measure (SNSM), determines the weight of each edge by considering the number of shared neighbors that are shared by the interacting proteins. As the number of shared neighbors between two proteins increases, so does the strength of their connection, resulting in a higher weight assigned to their interaction edge. The weight of an interaction edge (t, y) between two proteins t and y is calculated using Eq. (3). This method effectively overcomes the limitations of the edge clustering coefficient in PINs and improves the accuracy of identifying protein complexes.

Here, |NP(t)| and |NP(y)| denote the number of proteins included in the neighbor protein sets of proteins t and y, respectively. The shared neighbor protein count between proteins t and y is represented by |NP(t) ∩ NP(y)|. Based on these values, the algorithm determines the similarity between the two proteins by calculating the quantity of shared neighbors they possess, and then converts this similarity into a weight to assign to the edges in the network, thus reflecting the interaction between proteins more accurately. This method of using shared neighbors to determine protein similarity and assigning weights to edges can enhance the precision of identifying protein complexes.

The utilization of cubes and cube roots in Eq. (3), which concerns the SNSM, serves to non-linearly scale the weight of an edge based on the number of shared neighbors. This mathematical operation amplifies the importance of having a higher number of shared neighbors, reflecting the biological principle that proteins sharing more neighbors are more likely to be functionally related and thus part of the same complex. This non-linear scaling is crucial for differentiating between loosely and tightly connected protein pairs, enhancing the method’s ability to discern true interactions from noise in the data set.

Integrating two weighting methods

The proposed MCOR algorithm incorporates the use of functional annotation terms and shared neighbors to assign weights to interaction edges in the network, with the aim of more accurately evaluating the reliability of protein-protein interactions. To achieve this, the MCOR algorithm calculates the arithmetic mean and geometric mean of the two weighting methods, thereby balancing their influence on the reliability of PIN edges. As expressed in Eq. (4), the weight of each edge (t, y) falls within a range of 0.00 and 1.00. In addition, the study considers interaction edges with a weight of 0.00 as false positives and removes them from the PINs.

Functional enrichment analysis

Since the current set of known complexes is incomplete, it is difficult to accurately assess the biological significance of the complexes identified by algorithms that do not match standard complexes. To overcome this challenge, this study utilizes p-values obtained through annotation terms for Cellular Component (CC) to evaluate the biological implications of these complexes. Each detected complex is linked to a p-value and a corresponding set of functional annotation terms, which serve as indicators of its biological relevance. If the p-value associated with a complex is less than 1.00E-2, it’s considered to have significant biological implications and is likely to be a genuine protein complex, as widely accepted by scholars.

To objectively evaluate the degree of functional enrichment of complexes and thus more accurately discuss and analyze the biological implications they contain, we plan to focus on the following two aspects, as detailed below:

Statistics calculation and deep analysis

A reason for MCOR exhibiting a lower p-value

This section presents some statistical analysis aimed at explaining why the average p-value of complexes detected by MCOR is lower compared to other algorithms. This relationship is based on the fact that the number of neighboring proteins in the PINs follows a power-law distribution [43], which means that the majority of proteins have few neighbors, while a small subset of proteins interact with a large number of other proteins. This implies that the size of real protein complexes is often not large.

In Fig. 4, the horizontal axis represents the proportion of protein complexes of different sizes identified by each algorithm, while the vertical axis represents the different identification algorithms. Meanwhile, Fig. 5 provides the proportions of protein complexes of different sizes in the human standard protein complex data set CORUM as a reference. The majority of protein complexes in CORUM have sizes between 3 and 5, accounting for 79.15%. Additionally, the complexes average size in CORUM is around 6.70, while the average size of complexes detected by MCOR is around 7.20. However, TADW-SC, PC2P, and NABCAM identify protein complexes with larger average sizes, at 9.69, 10.41, and 13.86, respectively. It is apparent that the average size of complexes detected by MCOR is closer to the complexes average size in the standard protein complex set CORUM. TADW-SC, PC2P, and NABCAM also identify many complexes with larger sizes, which increases the likelihood of false positives and explains why MCOR identifies protein complexes with a lower average p-value compared to other algorithms.

Conclusions

On PINs of different species, we successively conducted comprehensive comparisons and analyses of the proposed MCOR and other seven complex identification algorithms using various standard complex data sets. The findings from the experiments indicate that MCOR, which incorporates both functional annotation terms and topological properties, outperforms the other seven advanced complex identification algorithms in terms of overall performance in identifying complexes. Additionally, this paper lists 24 protein complexes identified by MCOR and conducts functional enrichment analysis on them. The results of the experiments suggest that MCOR is capable of identifying protein complexes with biological significance. Furthermore, this paper has conducted relevant statistical analysis to explain why the average p-value of complexes identified by MCOR is significantly lower than other identification algorithms.

As one of the current advanced computational biology methods for identifying protein complexes, MCOR has a wide range of application potential, not only limited to biological networks but also excelling in areas such as drug development and disease research. In the field of drug development, MCOR can help researchers better understand the mechanisms of action and potential side effects of drugs by analyzing the interactions between protein complexes and drugs. This analysis can provide critical information for the design and optimization of new drugs, thereby reducing the time and cost of drug development. In addition, MCOR can help researchers identify potential targets in the early stages by recognizing candidate drugs with therapeutic potential. Within the realm of disease research, MCOR can elucidate the part played by protein complexes in the beginning and advancement of diseases. This is important for discovering potential therapeutic targets and understanding the molecular mechanisms of diseases. Furthermore, MCOR can also be applied to study the gene expression regulatory networks of diseases, thereby gaining a deeper understanding of the associations between genes, proteins, and phenotypes, providing strong support for precision medicine and individualized treatment.
