Personalized feedback about immunity corrects risk misestimation and motivates vaccination
Authors: Alyssa H Sinclair, Morgan K Taylor, Stephen J Beckett, Aroon T Chande, Joshua S Weitz, Gregory R Samanez-Larkin, Paul Van Lange
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBeliefs about immunity may have important consequences for health behaviors. For instance, people who overestimate their protection against a disease may neglect routine vaccinations, preventative measures, or diagnostic testing. Currently, few individuals are up-to-date on COVID-19 vaccines, leading to widespread gaps in protection. To address this challenge, we tested psychological interventions that targeted beliefs about immunity, providing personalized feedback about likely protection against COVID-19. In an online sample of participants (n = 882, stratified by age and gender), this intervention effectively changed immunity beliefs and increased vaccination intentions. Furthermore, the personalized intervention effectively realigned perceived risk (Cohen's d = 0.35), whereas an existing, widely used intervention produced by a national public health organization was ineffective (d = −0.03). The personalized intervention was particularly effective for older adults, who are at greater risk of experiencing severe illness if not sufficiently protected by vaccination. Two months later, belief changes endured, and self-reported vaccine uptake was 2–14× greater than the national rate in the United States during the same time period. Lastly, we scaled our intervention to an interactive public website and replicated our findings in an independent sample that was stratified to approximate the demographic makeup of the United States (n = 553). Overall, our psychological intervention changed immunity beliefs and motivated vaccination, of relevance for COVID-19, influenza, and future pandemic threats.Keywords: vaccination, risk perception, health communication, belief updating, behavior change

Beliefs about immunity can influence how people make choices about important health issues, like getting vaccinated, taking diagnostic tests, or seeking medical care. Few people are staying up-to-date on vaccinations for diseases like COVID-19 and influenza. Low uptake can lead to widespread gaps in coverage, increasing the risk of outbreaks and severe illness for vulnerable individuals. We developed a new psychological intervention that effectively corrected misconceptions about immunity and increased COVID-19 vaccination intentions. Effects endured after 2 months, and self-reported vaccine uptake was greater than population-level norms. We scaled this intervention to a public website, demonstrating an innovative approach to accessible and personalized health communication. Our findings reveal a strategy for promoting vaccination: changing beliefs about immunity with personalized feedback.

Introduction

Beliefs about immunity have important implications for health behaviors. If an individual is aware that they are vulnerable to a disease, they might stay up-to-date on vaccines, take a diagnostic test if they experience symptoms, or take precautions to protect themselves and others. Routine vaccines, such as for COVID-19 or influenza, can safely restore and update immunity to account for evolving viruses. However, uptake remains low—between 2023 and 2025, vaccination coverage plateaued ∼21% for COVID-19 vaccines and ∼47% for influenza vaccines (1, 2). In the present studies, we tested interventions designed to correct misconceptions about waning immunity and motivate uptake of COVID-19 vaccines.

Immunity conferred by COVID-19 infections or vaccinations wanes over time, potentially at different rates (3–8). “Hybrid immunity” acquired through both vaccination and infection also wanes over time, but at a slower rate than immunity developed through vaccination or infection alone (7, 9–11). Regardless of whether individuals have been infected, vaccinated, or both, protection against infection declines more rapidly (4, 5, 12), whereas protection against severe illness declines at a slower rate (6, 7, 13). Updated COVID-19 vaccines, reformulated for evolving viral variants, help restore and maintain protection (12–15). Restoring protection is particularly important for older adults and individuals who are at higher risk of severe illness (13, 16, 17).

However, few Americans are up-to-date on COVID-19 vaccines (1). The CDC currently recommends that nearly all individuals at least 6 months of age should receive an updated COVID-19 vaccine (18). Previously, we found that many individuals chose not to receive a booster vaccine because they believed they still had strong protection against infection and/or severe illness (19). Given evidence of waning immunity, these individuals likely overestimated their protection—72% had not been infected or vaccinated in the previous 6 months, and 51% reported no prior documented infections.

Vaccine uptake is influenced by multiple demographic, social, psychological, and structural factors (20). For instance, one framework characterizes the psychological antecedents of vaccination behavior, emphasizing the role of confidence (perceived safety and effectiveness of a vaccine), complacency (perceived risk of a disease), constraints (structural and psychological barriers), calculation (information seeking and analysis), and collective responsibility (willingness to protect others) (21). Some prior studies have specifically investigated strategies for increasing COVID-19 vaccine uptake (22–27). Existing recommendations for motivating vaccination emphasize dialogues with trusted messengers, such as healthcare providers and community leaders (28). This approach can help address vaccine hesitancy but can be difficult to scale. Text-message reminders (focusing on vaccine availability or ownership) have been shown to increase vaccination when delivered soon after vaccine rollout (29) or before a scheduled doctor's appointment (30) but are less effective for vaccine-hesitant individuals (31).

Prior work has extensively characterized antivaccination arguments and attitudes (32), but COVID-19 booster vaccines occupy a distinct niche—eligible individuals have been previously vaccinated against COVID-19, and report reasons for declining booster doses that are distinct from primary vaccine hesitancy (19, 23, 33). To our knowledge, prior interventions have not aimed to address the unique challenges associated with motivating uptake of updated COVID-19 vaccines (e.g. misconceptions about waning immunity, viral variants, safety, or effectiveness (19)). For instance, an individual who mistakenly believes that they still have strong immunity may conclude that the expected value of a booster vaccine is outweighed by the expected costs (e.g. mild side effects, inconvenience, or rare adverse events) (34). We propose that changing beliefs about immunity is an important understudied mechanism for motivating vaccination.

This hypothesis aligns with theoretical perspectives on health beliefs and behavior change. Beliefs about immunity are conceptually related to beliefs about risk. Numerous studies have investigated risk perception during COVID-19 (35, 36); perceived risk of COVID-19 is related to vaccine uptake, mask wearing, social distancing, and risk-taking (37–42). Furthermore, political attitudes are related to both perceived risk of COVID-19 and behavioral intentions (43–46). Prior studies have shown that COVID-19 risk perception is inaccurate, such as when participants attempt to estimate the risk of viral exposure in social distancing scenarios (47) or in public gatherings (42, 48). Importantly, improving the accuracy of risk estimation—as opposed to unilaterally increasing perceived risk—is important for balancing public health goals with mental health and societal well-being (49–51).

How might beliefs about immunity and risk influence health behaviors? A leading model of health behavior change, the health belief model, considers the role of threat (e.g. perceived susceptibility to a health risk, and perceived severity of the outcome) as well as response effectiveness (e.g. perceived benefits of engaging in a behavior, and perceived barriers that might limit one's ability to change a behavior) (52, 53). Communicating information about waning immunity could motivate vaccination behavior by influencing beliefs related to threat (e.g. “I am susceptible to infection or severe disease due to COVID-19”) as well as response effectiveness (e.g. “Getting a booster vaccine is valuable because it will meaningfully increase my protection”). The health belief model also accounts for other demographic and psychological variables that can influence these beliefs [e.g. age, socioeconomic status (SES), personality, social norms], as well as general health motivation. A complementary theory of behavior change, the Theory of Planned Behavior, proposes that attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control shape intentions and behavior (54). Changing beliefs about immunity—such as by informing people that immunity wanes over time—could increase positive attitudes toward vaccination by increasing the perceived value of boosting one's immunity (55, 56).

Other theoretical frameworks offer predictions about which types of intervention approaches might be most effective, and for whom. Eliciting prediction error, or surprise (e.g. by soliciting predictions and providing corrective feedback), has been shown to drive learning, belief updating, and behavior change (57, 58). For instance, we previously demonstrated that a quiz intervention that provided corrective feedback about local COVID-19 exposure risk effectively recalibrated beliefs and intentions regarding everyday risky behaviors (42, 48). Furthermore, appealing to self-relevance and social-relevance increases engagement with information about COVID-19 and general health topics (59). Importantly, emphasizing personal relevance may be particularly effective for supporting health-related information seeking and decision-making among older adults (60, 61). Lastly, fuzzy-trace theory proposes that “gist” representations, which capture the general meaning and emotional context of information, endure in memory and guide decision-making (62–64). A prediction that arises from this framework is that clearly conveying a gist concept, such as by using a meter to illustrate categories of risk or protection, may be more effective than providing detailed information about risk statistics or vaccination guidelines.

Taken together, prior studies indicate that beliefs about immunity and risk influence vaccine uptake, but these beliefs are often inaccurate. Informational interventions can correct risk misestimation and potentially motivate vaccination, such as by changing beliefs about susceptibility to a disease and benefits of vaccination. Personalized interventions may be particularly effective for older adults, who are at increased risk of severe illness due to COVID-19, as well as other preventable diseases. Here, we tested new interventions to motivate uptake of updated COVID-19 vaccines, targeting beliefs about immunity as a psychological mechanism.

Study 1: results

Intervention approach

We predicted that providing personalized information about immunity would correct risk misestimation and motivate vaccination. In study 1 (session 1), we tested online interventions with 882 US residents, using quota sampling to recruit participants across the adult lifespan (ages 18–93). After providing information about all prior COVID-19 vaccine doses and documented infections, participants were randomly assigned to one of three interventions (Fig. 1). Participants in the Immunity Estimator condition (n = 295) received personalized feedback about estimated protection against COVID-19 based on each individual's history of vaccines and infections. We compared the immunity estimator intervention with two alternative interventions. In the CDC-Flyer condition (n = 292), participants read a CDC-produced informational flyer that promoted the updated COVID-19 vaccine and described eligibility criteria. The purpose of this condition was to test whether the immunity estimator condition was more effective than the existing standard treatment, a flyer that was already widely used. In the Alternative-Flyer (Alt-Flyer) condition (n = 295), we modified the CDC-Flyer to add information about waning immunity. The purpose of this condition was to test whether a minor modification to existing materials would be sufficient to correct misconceptions about immunity.

Overview of study 1 procedure. Participants were randomly assigned to view one of three informational interventions (Immunity Estimator, CDC-Flyer, or Alt-Flyer). Content shown in the CDC Flyer section was developed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Informational flyers developed by the CDC are in the public domain; these materials were publicly available on the CDC website at the time that the study was conducted. Reference to these materials or CDC webpages does not constitute endorsement or recommendation by the CDC, U.S. Government, or Department of Health and Human Services.

Before and after completing one of these interventions, participants answered survey questions about perceived risk of COVID-19 infection and severe illness, beliefs about safety and effectiveness of the updated COVID-19 vaccine, intentions to get a vaccine dose, awareness of the updated vaccine and eligibility criteria, and interest in learning more about COVID-19 risks. After a 2-month delay (session 2), we recontacted participants to assess these beliefs again and investigate postintervention vaccine uptake.

We predicted that the immunity estimator condition would be most effective at correcting risk perception and motivating vaccination. We expected that the Alt-Flyer would be modestly effective and the CDC-Flyer would be least effective, because it did not address beliefs about immunity. Importantly, we assessed the effectiveness of each intervention by measuring within-subjects changes in beliefs and intentions (comparing pre- and postintervention measures); the purpose of comparing the three intervention conditions was to determine whether the immunity estimator condition was effective above and beyond the potential benefits of existing, simpler communication strategies. Drawing on findings from our prior COVID-19 risk communication studies (61), we also tested the prediction that the personalized immunity estimator self-relevant intervention would be more effective for older adults. Lastly, we explored whether political ideology moderated intervention effectiveness.

Drawing on our prior experience with scaling lab-validated risk communication interventions to reach broader audiences and evaluate effectiveness (48, 65), we developed a public website featuring the personalized immunity estimator intervention (https://covid19immunity.com/). In study 2, soon after the release of new updated COVID-19 vaccines, we replicated the effects of the immunity estimator intervention on perceived risk, beliefs, and vaccination intentions in a new sample of 553 US participants (stratified to approximate the demographic makeup of the United States) who interacted with the website version of the intervention.

Associations between perceived and actual risk

We estimated actual risk for each participant based on each participant's history of COVID-19 infections and vaccinations (see Supplementary Methods). To test whether perceived risk was aligned with actual risk at baseline, we correlated these estimates of actual risk with preintervention measures of perceived risk of infection and perceived risk of severe disease. Positive associations thus indicate alignment (i.e. if one's susceptibility to a disease is greater, perceived risk should be higher). Actual risk was negatively associated with baseline perceived risk of infection (r = −0.12, 95% CI [−0.18, −0.05], t = −3.45, P = 0.0006), suggesting that individuals with lower immunity (e.g. due to few vaccine doses) tended to report lower perceived risk of infection. Actual risk was not significantly associated with perceived risk of severe disease (r = 0.05, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.12], t = 1.57, P = 0.117). Overall, at baseline, perceived risk measures were not well aligned with estimates of actual risk. Notably, there were small differences in baseline perceived-actual risk alignment across conditions (Fig. S1); perceived risk of infection was negatively associated with actual risk in the immunity estimator and Alt-Flyer conditions, but not in the CDC-Flyer condition.

We then examined postintervention alignment between perceived risk and actual risk, and whether this alignment differed among conditions (Fig. S1). Using multiple linear regression, we predicted postintervention perceived risk of infection from the variables condition (immunity estimator, CDC-Flyer, or Alt-Flyer), actual risk, and the interaction term. The model included a covariate to control for preintervention (baseline) perceived risk of infection. Results for continuous variables are reported with standardized slope estimates. For analyses pertaining to the condition variable, we report omnibus F-tests, accompanied by follow-up tests [corrected for multiple comparisons with Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD)] reporting slopes across conditions when omnibus tests indicated significant differences.

There were significant main effects of preintervention perceived risk (β = 0.78, 95% CI [0.74, 0.82], t = 36.38, P < 0.0001) and actual risk (β = 0.06, 95% CI [0.02, 0.10], t = 2.79, P = 0.005), but no significant main effect of condition (F(2,875) = 2.88, P = 0.057, ηp2 = 0.01). There was a significant interaction between immunity scores and condition predicting postintervention perceived risk of infection (F(2,875) = 14.99, P < 0.0001, ηp2 = 0.03). Simple slopes indicated that actual risk was significantly associated with postintervention perceived risk of infection in the immunity estimator condition (β = 0.22, 95% CI [0.15, 0.29], t = 6.14, P < 0.0001) but not in the Alt-Flyer condition (β = −0.02, 95% CI [−0.09, 0.05], t = −0.50, P = 0.616) or CDC-Flyer condition (β = −0.03, 95% CI [−0.10, 0.05], t = −0.67, P = 0.504). Follow-up tests indicated that there was a stronger negative association between actual risk and perceived risk of infection in the immunity estimator condition relative to the CDC-Flyer condition (β = 0.25, 95% CI [0.12, 0.37], t = 4.70, P < 0.0001) and the Alt-Flyer condition (β = 0.24, 95% CI [0.12, 0.36], t = 4.73, P = 0.0001). There was no significant difference between the Alt-Flyer and CDC-Flyer conditions (β = 0.01, 95% CI [−0.12, 0.13], t = 0.14, P = 0.989).

We next repeated this analysis to test postintervention perceived risk of severe disease as the dependent variable. There were significant main effects of preintervention perceived risk of severe disease (β = 0.84, 95% CI [0.80, 0.87], t = 46.40, P < 0.0001), actual risk (β = 0.09, 95% CI [0.05, 0.12], t = 4.81, P < 0.0001), and condition (F(2,875) = 5.85, P = 0.003, ηp2 = 0.01). There was a significant interaction between immunity scores and condition predicting perceived risk of severe disease (F(2,875) = 9.51, P = 0.0001, ηp2 = 0.02). Simple slopes indicated that immunity scores were significantly associated with postintervention perceived risk of severe disease in the immunity estimator condition (β = 0.19, 95% CI [0.13, 0.25], t = 6.39, P < 0.0001) but not in the Alt-Flyer condition (β = 0.05, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.11], t = 1.64, P = 0.101) or the CDC-Flyer condition (β = 0.01, 95% CI [−0.05, 0.08], t = 0.45, P = 0.653). Follow-up pairwise comparisons indicated a stronger association between immunity scores and perceived risk of severe disease in the immunity estimator condition relative to the CDC-Flyer condition (β = 0.18, 95% CI [0.08, 0.29], t = 4.05, P = 0.0002) and the Alt-Flyer condition (β = 0.15, 95% CI [0.04, 0.25], t = 3.38, P = 0.002). There was no significant difference between the Alt-Flyer and CDC-Flyer conditions (β = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.14], t = 0.79, P = 0.709).

Overall, we found that at baseline, perceived risk was poorly aligned with our estimates of actual risk based on each individual's history of vaccines and infections. Postintervention, perceived risk measures were aligned with actual risk for participants in the immunity estimator condition, supporting the idea that providing personalized feedback about immunity helped to realign perceived risk with reality.

Changes in risk perception

Having demonstrated group-level associations between actual risk and perceived risk postintervention, we next investigated how baseline risk (mis)estimation was related to within-person changes in perceived risk. Examining within-person changes enables us to characterize whether the interventions corrected both underestimation and overestimation of risk, and tests for movement in the desired direction even when perceived risk is not fully realigned with reality. Ideally, a risk literacy intervention should bidirectionally recalibrate perceived risk, with the degree of change determined by the magnitude and direction of misestimation (i.e. prediction error (42, 66, 67)). After an intervention, individuals who are underestimating risk should report increases in perceived risk, whereas individuals who are overestimating risk should report decreases in perceived risk. We aimed to test whether the magnitude of change in perceived risk—in both directions—differed among the three conditions. We predicted that the immunity estimator intervention would lead to greater bidirectional changes in perceived risk.

We calculated a risk error score for each participant (see Materials and methods, Risk error analyses), defined as the discrepancy between baseline perceived risk (averaged across infection and severe disease measures) and actual risk (estimated on the basis of each participant's immunity history). Positive risk error scores indicate overestimation, negative scores indicate underestimation, and zero indicates accurate estimation. Using multiple linear regression, we predicted change in perceived risk of infection from risk error (continuous variable), condition (immunity estimator, CDC-Flyer, or Alt-Flyer), and the interaction term. We also explored whether vaccination status moderated these effects, as we expected that vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals may respond differently to information about vaccines and immunity. We included a binary variable for COVID-19 vaccination status (0 = no prior vaccine doses, 1 = at least one prior vaccine dose) and tested for interactions with condition and risk error.

There was a main effect of risk error on perceived risk of infection (β = 0.21, 95% CI [0.11, 0.30], t = 4.35, P < 0.0001), indicating bidirectional recalibration of risk perception. There was also an interaction between risk error and condition (F(2,870) = 7.45, P = 0.0006, ηp2 = 0.02) (Fig. 2A). Risk error was associated with change in perceived risk in the immunity estimator (β = 0.48, 95% CI [0.29, 0.66], t = 5.14, P < 0.0001) and Alt-Flyer conditions (β = 0.14, 95% CI [0.01, 0.28], t = 2.13, P = 0.034) but not in the CDC-Flyer condition (β = 0.00, 95% CI [−0.17, 0.17], t = 0.01, P = 0.990). The effect of risk error was significantly stronger in the immunity estimator condition relative to the CDC-Flyer (β = 0.48, 95% CI [0.18, 0.77], t = 3.77, P = 0.0005) and Alt-Flyer conditions (β = 0.33, 95% CI [0.06, 0.60], t = 2.89, P = 0.011); there was no significant difference between the two Flyer conditions (β = 0.14, 95% CI [−0.11, 0.40], t = 1.31, P = 0.389). There was also an interaction between risk error and vaccination status (F(1,870) = 8.18, P = 0.004, ηp2 = 0.01); the effect of risk error on change in perceived risk was stronger for vaccinated individuals. There were no other significant effects; all parameter estimates are reported in Table S1.

We then repeated the analysis described above, but predicted change in perceived risk of severe illness. Overall, results were very similar to the analysis of perceived risk of infection (Fig. 2B). However, in this model, there was also a three-way interaction among risk error, condition, and vaccination status (F(2,870) = 3.61, P = 0.028, ηp2 = 0.01); the effect of risk error on change in perceived risk (in the immunity estimator and Alt-Flyer conditions) was stronger for vaccinated individuals. Detailed results are reported in Table S2.

In the analyses above, we reported the relationship between risk error and change in perceived risk across conditions. An advantage of this approach is that it is intuitive to understand and visualize—zero error in risk estimation should ideally correspond to zero change in perceived risk, and positive slopes indicate effective recalibration. This approach also aligns with a common conceptual framing in the literature: feedback drives adjustments in beliefs according to an error signal (42, 66, 67). However, a limitation of this approach is that the change scores can obscure baseline differences or lingering misestimation. We thus conducted a supplementary set of analyses that predicted postintervention perceived risk (instead of change scores), while controlling for preintervention perceived risk (Tables S3 and S4, Fig. S2). All results were consistent with the change-score results reported above.

Overall, results indicated that baseline beliefs about risk were not aligned with immunity estimates. The immunity estimator condition was the most effective strategy for recalibrating perceived risk of infection and severe illness. The Alt-Flyer condition was modestly effective, and the CDC-Flyer condition did not change perceived risk.

Individual differences: age and politics

Next, we investigated two demographic variables that we expected might influence change in perceived risk: age and political ideology. In prior interventions that focused on communicating COVID-19 viral exposure risk, we found that older adults were most likely to report changes in perceived risk following a personalized, self-relevant intervention (42, 61). In contrast, younger adults were equally responsive to interventions that were personalized or not personalized. Drawing on these prior findings, we predicted that for older adults, the personalized intervention (immunity estimator) would be most effective, whereas the relative benefit of personalization may be smaller for younger adults. In addition to this a priori prediction about age differences, we also explored potential effects of political ideology. On the basis of prior findings relating COVID-19 risk perception to political ideology, we expected that intervention effects would be weaker for politically conservative individuals (43–46), though we did not have specific predictions about how the relationship between political ideology and perceived risk might differ among intervention conditions. As we previously found that the interventions did not significantly change perceived risk for unvaccinated individuals, we conducted these secondary analyses of individual differences within the subset of participants who had received at least one COVID-19 vaccine dose (n = 677). We note that the decision to focus on vaccinated individuals was a post hoc decision informed by the primary analyses reported above.

Using multiple linear regression, we predicted change in perceived risk of infection from the variables condition, risk error, and the interaction term. We also tested whether age (continuous variable) or political attitudes (continuous variable) moderated these effects. There was an interaction among age, risk error, and condition (F(2,659) = 4.81, P = 0.008, ηp2 = 0.01; Fig. S5A). Follow-up tests sampled levels of the continuous age variable to compare younger (age = 20), middle aged (age = 40), and older adults (age = 60). Older adults showed recalibration of perceived risk in the immunity estimator condition (β = 0.73, 95% CI [0.56, 0.90], t = 8.31, P < 0.0001) but not in the Alt-Flyer (β = 0.13, 95% CI [−0.04, 0.30], t = 1.51, P = 0.132) or CDC-Flyer conditions (β = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.11, 0.20], t = 0.56, P = 0.574). In contrast, younger and middle-aged adults responded to both the immunity estimator (younger: β = 0.30, 95% CI [0.07, 0.54], t = 2.53, P = 0.012; middle aged: β = 0.52, 95% CI [0.39, 0.65], t = 7.74, P < 0.0001) and Alt-Flyer (younger: β = 0.39, 95% CI [0.16, 0.63], t = 3.33, P = 0.001; middle aged: β = 0.26, 95% CI [0.12, 0.40], t = 3.74, P = 0.0002) conditions.

There was also an interaction among political attitudes, risk error, and condition (F(2,659) = 3.74, P = 0.024, ηp2 = 0.01; Fig. S5B). Follow-up tests sampled levels of the continuous political attitudes variable to compare moderately liberal (2/5 on political attitudes scale) and moderately conservative (4/5) participants. In the immunity estimator condition, liberals showed a stronger effect of risk error on change in perceived risk than conservatives (β = 0.36, 95% CI [0.15, 0.58], t = 3.36, P = 0.0008). The effect of risk error did not differ by political attitudes in the CDC-Flyer (β = 0.14, 95% CI [−0.08, 0.35], t = 1.26, P = 0.207) or Alt-Flyer conditions (β = −0.08, 95% CI [−0.33, 0.16], t = −0.67, P = 0.504). Notably, risk error still predicted change in perceived risk among moderately conservative participants in the immunity estimator (β = 0.28, 95% CI [0.06, 0.50], t = 2.46, P = 0.014) and Alt-Flyer conditions (β = 0.29, 95% CI [0.05, 0.53], t = 2.37, P = 0.018).

Other results from this model (testing the subset of vaccinated individuals) were consistent with the previous model that included the full sample of participants without moderators. Additional results are reported in Table S5. The effects of age and political attitudes were specific to perceived risk of infection; there were no significant interactions for perceived risk of severe illness (Table S6).

Overall, analysis of individual differences revealed that only the immunity estimator intervention effectively changed perceived risk for older adults, whereas both the immunity estimator and Alt-Flyer interventions had effects on younger and middle-aged adults. These results are consistent with our prior findings that personalized interventions were more effective at changing perceived risk for older adults (61). Although conservatives generally showed weaker changes in risk perception than liberals, we still observed recalibration of perceived risk for moderately conservative participants in the immunity estimator condition.

Intentions, knowledge, and beliefs

Next, we investigated whether the interventions changed intentions, beliefs, or knowledge regarding COVID-19 vaccines. For each measure, we conducted one-sample t tests on the overall change scores (postintervention − preintervention). We also used ANOVAs to compare change scores across conditions; with the exception of interest in learning about COIVD-19 risks, there were no significant differences among conditions. Descriptive statistics and ANOVA results are reported in the Supplementary material (Table S7, Beliefs, attitudes, and knowledge about COVID-19 booster vaccines).

Among the subset of participants who were eligible but had not yet received an updated COVID-19 vaccine (n = 387), 34.6% already intended to receive the dose at baseline. Postintervention, there was a small increase in vaccination intentions (3.1% increase; t(386) = 3.25, P = 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.17, 95% CI [0.06, 0.27]). This subset of participants also reported increased knowledge of eligibility criteria (t(377) = 3.61, P = 0.0004, Cohen's d = 0.19, 95% CI [0.08, 0.29]).

Among all participants, we observed increased awareness of the updated vaccine (t(881) = 2.50, P = 0.013, Cohen's d = 0.08, 95% CI [0.02, 0.15]) but no changes in perceived vaccine safety (t(881) = 1.65, P = 0.100, Cohen's d = 0.06, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.12]) or effectiveness (t(881) = 1.45, P = 0.146, Cohen's d = 0.05, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.11]). These effects did not differ across conditions, suggesting that providing information about waning immunity did not decrease confidence in vaccine safety or effectiveness. Participants also reported decreased interest in learning about COVID-19 risks (t(881) = −3.48, P = 0.0005, Cohen's d = −0.12, 95% CI [−0.18, −0.05]); comparing conditions revealed that this effect was driven by the CDC-Flyer (F(2,879) = 3.01, P = 0.050, η2 = 0.01).

Session 2: follow-up survey

After 2 months, we recontacted participants who (at baseline) were eligible but had not yet received an updated vaccine; 291 participants returned (immunity estimator n = 98, CDC-Flyer n = 99, Alt-Flyer n = 94). We aimed to investigate vaccine uptake and test whether perceived risk remained realigned with actual risk.

Updated booster vaccine uptake

During the 2 months after session 1, updated COVID-19 vaccine coverage among adults in the United States increased by 0.9% (1). We tested whether the proportion of participants who received an updated vaccine was greater than this national increase. During the study period between February and April 2023, in the immunity estimator condition, 7.1% (95% CI: 2–12%) of participants reported having received an updated COVID-19 vaccine, significantly more than the national increase (t(97) = 2.4, P = 0.019, Cohen's d = 0.24, 95% CI [0.04, 0.44]). Similarly, in the Alt-Flyer condition, 7.4% (95% CI: 2–13%) of participants reported receiving a vaccine (t(93) = 2.4, P = 0.018, Cohen's d = 0.25, 95% CI [0.04, 0.46]). In the CDC-Flyer condition, 3.0% (95% CI: 0–6%) of participants reported receiving a vaccine; this increase did not significantly differ from the national benchmark (t(98) = 1.2, P = 0.222, Cohen's d = 0.12, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.32]). Overall, rates of updated vaccine uptake during the postintervention period in the immunity estimator and Alt-Flyer conditions—the two interventions that informed about waning immunity—were approximately 8× greater (95% CI: 2–14×) than the rate among all US adults (Fig. 3A). However, when directly comparing the rate of vaccine uptake across conditions, there were no significant differences (χ2(2,n = 291) = 2.40, P = 0.301, η2 = 0.01).

Overall, during the 2 months postintervention, participants who completed an intervention condition that involved providing information about waning immunity were considerably more likely to self-report receiving a vaccine dose, relative to the rate of booster vaccine uptake among all US adults during the same time period. However, we did not observe significant differences among the intervention conditions; the sample was underpowered to detect such differences among conditions due to attrition and low base rates of vaccine uptake. We also caution that causal inference is limited because vaccine uptake was measured 2 months after the intervention, and there were no significant differences among the conditions to which participants were randomly assigned. Overall, these results suggest that communicating information about waning immunity may motivate vaccination but should be interpreted with caution and verified by future replication studies.

Risk perception 2 months later

Lastly, we assessed the alignment between perceived risk and actual risk 2 months after the intervention. In separate linear regression models, we tested perceived risk of infection and perceived risk of severe disease as the dependent variables. The independent variables included condition, actual risk (inverse of protection category scores, as used for session 1 analyses), risk error (baseline risk misestimation), and all relevant interaction terms. These models included both actual risk and risk error terms to test the alignment of perceived and actual risk at the follow-up survey while also accounting for prior misperceptions of risk (which we previously found were related to change in perceived risk).

We expected that the interventions, particularly the immunity estimator, would lead to enduring alignment between perceived and actual risk. In session 2, actual risk was positively associated with perceived risk of infection (β = 0.43, 95% CI [0.28, 0.59], t = 5.45, P < 0.0001) and perceived risk of severe disease (β = 0.43, 95% CI [0.28, 0.59], t = 5.45, P < 0.0001). The strength of these associations differed across conditions (Fig. 3B); there was an interaction between condition and actual risk predicting perceived risk of infection (F(2,282) = 4.00, P = 0.019, ηp2 = 0.03) and perceived risk of severe disease (F(2,282) = 3.82, P = 0.023, ηp2 = 0.03). Follow-up tests (corrected for multiple comparisons with Tukey's HSD) indicated stronger alignment between perceived and actual risk in the immunity estimator condition, relative to the CDC-Flyer condition (perceived risk of infection: β = 0.53, 95% CI [0.16, 0.90], t = 2.79, P = 0.015); perceived risk of severe disease: (β = 0.48, 95% CI [0.13, 0.82], t = 2.74, P = 0.018). There were no significant differences in actual-perceived risk alignment between the Alt-Flyer and CDC conditions (infection: P = 0.57, severe disease: P = 0.21) nor between the immunity estimator and Alt-Flyer conditions (infection: P = 0.21, severe disease: P = 0.63). Risk error also predicted perceived risk of infection (β = −0.70, 95% CI [−0.86, −0.54], t = −8.73, P < 0.0001) and severe disease (β = −0.93, 95% CI [−0.1.08, −0.79], t = −13.04, P < 0.0001), indicating that baseline misestimation was not fully corrected at session 2. There were no main effects of condition on perceived risk of infection (F(2,282) = 0.51, P = 0.600, ηp2 = 0.004) or severe disease (F(2,282) = 2.61, P = 0.075, ηp2 = 0.02), nor an interaction between condition and risk error predicting perceived risk of infection (F(2,282) = 1.07, P = 0.344, ηp2 = 0.01) or severe disease (F(2,282) = 2.01, P = 0.136, ηp2 = 0.01). Overall, perceived risk was positively associated with actual risk 2 months postintervention, with the strongest alignment observed in the immunity estimator condition.

Study 2: results

Following study 1, we scaled our immunity estimator intervention by developing a free and accessible public website, https://covid19immunity.com (Fig. S8). In September 2023, updated COVID-19 vaccines were released. In December 2023, we recruited an online sample (stratified by age, sex, and race to approximate the demographics of the United States) of 606 participants to complete surveys before and after interacting with the immunity estimator website. After exclusions (see Materials and methods), the sample included survey data from 553 participants and matched immunity estimator website data from 290 participants. Descriptive statistics are provided in Table S8.

Intentions, knowledge, and beliefs

To assess the effects of the immunity estimator website on beliefs and intentions, we calculated change scores (postintervention − preintervention). Among participants who had not yet received an updated COVID-19 vaccine, the intervention significantly increased intentions to get an updated vaccine (t(370) = 3.84, P = 0.0001, d = 0.20, 95% CI [0.10, 0.30]). Among all participants, the intervention increased perceived risk of infection (t(552) = 6.78, P < 0.0001, d = 0.29, 95% CI [0.20, 0.37]), perceived knowledge about the updated COVID-19 vaccines (t(552) = 5.81, P < 0.0001, d = 0.25, 95% CI [0.16, 0.33]), perceived vaccine effectiveness (t(552) = 4.09, P < 0.0001, d = 0.17, 95% CI [0.09, 0.26]), and perceived vaccine safety (t(552) = 2.51, P = 0.012, d = 0.11, 95% CI [0.02, 0.19]). The intervention did not change interest in learning about COVID-19 risks (t(552) = −0.92, P = 0.356, d = −0.04, 95% CI [−0.12, 0.04]) or perceived risk of severe illness (t(552) = −0.98, P = 0.325, d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.04, 0.13]).

Correcting risk misestimation

Next, we tested whether the immunity estimator website corrected risk misestimation. As in study 1, in study 2, we calculated a risk error score for each participant (see Materials and Methods, Risk error analyses), comparing baseline perceived risk with inversed immunity scores to identify the direction and magnitude of risk misestimation. Using linear regression, we predicted change in perceived risk of infection from risk error, vaccination status (0 doses vs. 1 + doses), and the interaction term. Note that in study 1, we found that age and political ideology were related to differences in intervention effectiveness across conditions. As there was only one intervention condition tested in study 2, we included age and political ideology as covariates, but were not able to test for interactions with condition as in study 1.

Replicating our prior findings, risk error predicted change in perceived risk of infection (β = 0.48, 95% CI [0.66, 0.30], t = 5.19, P < 0.0001; Fig. S9A and C). Participants who underestimated risk reported increased perceived risk, whereas those who overestimated risk reported decreased perceived risk. In addition, there were effects of vaccination status (β = −0.26, 95% CI [−0.45, −0.08], t = −2.78, P = 0.006) and age (β = −0.11, 95% CI [−0.22, −0.01], t = −2.78, P = 0.034), such that vaccinated individuals and younger adults reported greater increases in perceived risk. There were no other significant effects; all parameter estimates are reported in Table S9. We also tested a separate model with change in perceived risk of severe illness as the dependent variable. Again, risk error was robustly associated with change in perceived risk (β = 0.40, 95% CI [0.20, 0.60], t = 3.90, P = 0.0001; Fig. S9B and D). There were no other significant effects; all parameter estimates are reported in Table S10.

Discussion

Protection against many viral infections, including COVID-19, weakens as time passes after an infection and/or vaccine dose (3, 4, 7, 9). Communicating waning immunity may be important for motivating preventative health behaviors, including vaccination. In study 1, we showed that our immunity estimator intervention—which provided personalized feedback about estimated protection against COVID-19 infection and severe illness—effectively corrected both underestimation and overestimation of COVID-19 risk. Two months later, corrections to risk perception endured, and the increase in booster vaccine uptake was greater than the national population-level increase. Crucially, this approach was more effective than other informational interventions (a CDC-produced flyer about COVID-19 vaccines or a modified flyer informing about waning immunity), especially for older adults. In study 2, we scaled this immunity estimator intervention by developing a public website and replicated our findings. Overall, we found that providing personalized information about protection against COVID-19 corrected risk misestimation, increased vaccination intentions, and had enduring effects on risk perception.

Realigning perceived and actual risk

In study 1, baseline beliefs about risk of infection and severe illness were not aligned with our immunity estimates. This misalignment coheres with our prior findings that perceived risk of virus exposure is not associated with actual risk estimates (42), and many individuals who are not up-to-date on vaccines erroneously believe they still have strong protection (19). Prior studies have investigated risk perception during the COVID-19 pandemic (35, 42, 43, 48), but have not sought to change risk perception by communicating information about waning immunity.

In the immunity estimator intervention, we provided personalized feedback about estimated protection against viral infection and severe illness, paired with personalized guidance about booster vaccines and eligibility. The intervention effectively recalibrated perceived risk: participants who had underestimated risk reported increased perceived risk, whereas those who had overestimated risk reported decreased perceived risk. Two months later, perceived risk remained aligned with actual risk. Although participants were not able to review their immunity feedback during the 2-month delay, the intervention may have motivated broader information seeking about COVID-19 risk and immunity; in prior work, we showed that postintervention information seeking supported durable effects (61). In study 2, we replicated the effect of the immunity estimator intervention correcting risk perception. These findings parallel other studies on learning from feedback, which have shown that prediction error (i.e. surprise elicited by feedback that challenges one's prior beliefs) drives belief updating depending on the direction and magnitude of the error (42, 66–68).

Bidirectionally correcting risk perception is important; risk underestimation may increase viral transmission, but risk overestimation can harm mental health (49, 69). Prior studies assessing health communication strategies during the COVID-19 pandemic found that unilaterally encouraging risk aversion can increase anxiety without furthering public health goals (50). In the context of vaccination, public health communication campaigns aim to encourage behavior that aligns with current recommendations, such as receiving an annual vaccine dose (18); risk overestimation could lead to overcorrection that does not align with public health guidance (e.g. receiving additional vaccine doses more frequently than needed, straining supply and yielding diminishing benefits for individuals and society). Calibrating risk perception empowers individuals to make informed decisions about risk-taking and preventative health behaviors in daily life.

Comparing informational interventions

We tested whether the immunity estimator intervention was more effective than existing, simpler informational interventions. In the CDC-Flyer condition, we showed participants a CDC-produced informational flyer that promoted the updated COVID-19 vaccines. In the Alt-Flyer condition, we modified the CDC-Flyer to add information about waning immunity, testing whether a minor change to existing materials would be sufficient to correct beliefs. Although the specific information provided differed across conditions, we aimed to demonstrate that the immunity estimator intervention was more effective than the existing standard of treatment. The immunity estimator intervention was most effective, Alt-Flyer was modestly effective, and the CDC-Flyer was not effective at correcting risk misestimation.

Our findings cohere with leading models of health belief and behavior change. The immunity estimator intervention targeted beliefs about perceived susceptibility and severity of a disease, key determinants of health behavior identified in the health belief model (52, 53). Further, changing beliefs about immunity might also increase the perceived value of a vaccine by demonstrating how protection can be gained and restored. Prior studies have shown that perceived susceptibility, severity, and benefits of vaccination are associated with vaccine uptake. However, public health communication campaigns, such as the CDC-Flyer tested in our study, primarily focus on communicating availability, eligibility criteria, and vaccine safety and effectiveness (18). Similarly, messaging campaigns designed to increase vaccine uptake (e.g. via text-message reminders) have emphasized availability or ownership of vaccines, which may influence beliefs about perceived behavioral control, a key determinant of behavior change. Our findings highlight the potential of an alternate target for messaging campaigns: beliefs about immunity.

The effectiveness and durability of the immunity estimator intervention—above and beyond the benefits of communicating waning immunity observed in the Alt-Flyer condition—implicate other psychological features that may enhance risk communication interventions. Emphasizing self-relevance, such as by providing personalized feedback about immunity, has been shown to increase engagement with health information (59). Our findings relate to prior evidence that personalizing communications (22) and emphasizing self-relevance (e.g. “a vaccine is reserved for you”) (29) can motivate vaccination. Personalized approaches to vaccine communication could enhance message effectiveness, particularly for underserved populations (70, 71). The immunity estimator intervention also conveyed “gist” by illustrating levels of protection with a meter graphic; fuzzy-trace theory proposes that gist-like, categorical thinking supports information retention and guides risk-related decision-making, potentially explaining why the immunity estimator intervention had stronger immediate and lasting effects on risk perception than the Flyer conditions (62–64).

Vaccine beliefs, intentions, and self-reported uptake

We also investigated vaccine-related beliefs, intentions, and uptake. In study 1, across all three intervention conditions, participants reported modest increases in vaccination intentions, perceived knowledge about COVID-19 vaccines, and awareness of eligibility criteria. Beliefs about the safety and effectiveness of the vaccines did not change or differ across conditions, suggesting that communicating about waning immunity did not undermine confidence in COVID-19 vaccines. In study 2, participants who used our immunity estimator website again reported increased vaccination intentions, as well as increases in perceived awareness, safety, and effectiveness of the updated vaccines.

We also investigated self-reported vaccine uptake 2 months postintervention. The rate of booster vaccination in the immunity estimator and Alt-Flyer conditions was 8× higher (95% CI: 2–14×) than the nationwide increase among US adults during the same time period. Although these results suggest that communicating waning immunity may be an effective strategy to motivate routine vaccination, these findings should be interpreted with caution—due to attrition and low rates of vaccine uptake, the sample was not sufficiently powered to detect differences in vaccine uptake among the intervention conditions.

Taken together, our findings expand on the existing literature on COVID-19 vaccination interventions in several ways (22, 23, 26, 33). We investigated updated COVID-19 vaccines, which are associated with particularly low uptake and unique challenges (19), and implicated immunity beliefs as a key intervention target. Targeting immunity beliefs may also be effective for increasing routine vaccination for other illnesses, such as influenza.

Age, political ideology, and vaccination status moderated effectiveness

Our findings also revealed individual differences that are relevant to public health communication regarding vaccines. Consistent with our predictions based on prior evidence that older adults may be particularly responsive to self-relevant information (60, 61), we found that the personalized immunity estimator intervention was particularly effective for older adults. The Alt-Flyer condition was effective for younger and middle-aged adults, but older adults selectively corrected risk perception when receiving personalized feedback. The effectiveness of the immunity estimator intervention for older adults conceptually replicates our prior work, in which we demonstrated that a personalized intervention (imagining a scenario that involves yourself and close others) was the most effective strategy for changing risk perception among older adults, whereas younger adults were equally responsive to the personalized and nonpersonalized interventions (61). Communicating risk to older adults is particularly important because older adults are at considerably higher risk of severe illness and death due to many viruses, including COVID-19 (16) and influenza (72).

Exploring other individual differences, we also found that our interventions were less effective for unvaccinated participants and those who identified as politically conservative. These findings align with prior evidence that political partisanship is related to risk perception, beliefs about COVID-19, and vaccine uptake (43–46, 73–75). Importantly, however, the immunity estimator and Alt-Flyer conditions still corrected risk misestimation among moderately conservative participants. These results parallel our prior findings that although conservatives were less likely to engage with COVID-19 risk information, those who did engage with our intervention responded similarly to liberals (48).

Limitations and future directions

The present study is subject to several limitations. Although we used quota sampling to recruit participants across the adult lifespan (study 1) and approximate the demographics of the United States (study 2), the studies used convenience samples rather than nationally representative probability samples. During analysis, we did not weight observations depending on demographic variables to approximate population-level effects. This sampling approach limits the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, the studies were not powered to investigate race or ethnicity as moderators; future studies that oversample underrepresented groups are needed to investigate other factors that may influence beliefs about immunity and vaccines (76). Our immunity estimates are also imperfect. Our estimates were informed by diverse evidence from studies of antibody levels and vaccine effectiveness, but prior studies have yielded different estimates for the rate at which immunity wanes (3–5, 8, 12–15, 17, 77, 78). Protection also depends on viral evolution and reformulation of vaccines. Furthermore, as stated to our participants, our immunity estimates did not account for personal risk factors like age and preexisting conditions, which influence the risk of severe illness.

Other limitations pertain to sample size and effect size. In study 1, although the rate of vaccine uptake was greater than the national average, relatively few participants received a booster vaccine within the 2 months postintervention. Further research is needed to replicate our vaccine uptake findings in a longitudinal sample that is adequately powered to detect small effects. Direct measures of vaccine uptake (e.g. leveraging health system data) would also strengthen evidence, as the present study relied on self-reported vaccinations. Lastly, effect sizes observed for some outcomes were small. Absolute percentages of vaccine uptake were low (∼7% in the immunity estimator and Alt-Flyer conditions), but greater than the national rate of uptake (0.9%) and comparable to effects observed in other studies that have identified light-touch interventions that motivate vaccination (e.g. average increase of 6.1% for influenza (79) and 4.9% for COVID-19 (30)). However, intervention effects on perceived risk were large, and could lead to multiple downstream benefits. Beliefs about risk and immunity may influence other behavioral intentions in daily life, such as willingness to take a COVID-19 test, wear a mask, or avoid situations with high exposure risk (36–39, 45). Future studies could explore whether beliefs about immunity predict other health behaviors.

In future research, measuring perceived social norms and vaccine-related knowledge could provide insights into individual differences and intervention tailoring opportunities. These measures would cover other aspects of the theory of planned behavior that were not prioritized in the present study, enabling exploration of the relative contributions of immunity beliefs and other factors. Collecting qualitative data about subjective perception or interpretation of immunity scores could also yield insights. Subjective interpretation of these immunity scores could also differ depending on prior knowledge or demographic variables.

Conclusion

Overall, we demonstrated that an “immunity estimator” intervention—providing personalized information about protection against viral infection and severe illness—effectively changed risk perception and motivated vaccine uptake. This intervention corrected risk underestimation and overestimation, immediately and 2 months later. Likewise, our personalized immunity estimator intervention increased vaccination intentions immediately postintervention, and was associated with greater vaccine uptake (relative to national norms) during the 2-month period postintervention. This personalized approach to risk communication was particularly effective for older adults, who are at greater risk of severe illness. We then scaled our intervention to an accessible public website and replicated our findings in an independent sample. Overall, we demonstrate that personalized guidance about immunity can change health beliefs and behaviors. We implicate immunity beliefs as a promising target for interventions to increase routine vaccinations, such as for COVID-19 and influenza.

Materials and methods

For both studies, we recruited participants from Prolific, an online platform for paid study participation. All participants provided digital informed consent. The studies were reviewed and determined to be exempt by the Duke University Institutional Review Board and the Georgia Institute of Technology Institutional Review Board.

Study 1: procedure

Data collection took place in February 2023. We used quota sampling to recruit a gender- and age-balanced sample. Detailed information about power analyses, recruitment, exclusions, ethics, and demographics are provided in the Supplementary material (Participant recruitment). The final sample included 882 participants. The attrition rate was 4.4% (40 participants returned the task or timed out without completing). All participants who dropped out did so before being randomized to a condition; therefore, differences among conditions cannot be explained by differential attrition rates. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three intervention conditions (immunity estimator, CDC-Flyer, or Alt-Flyer), described in detail below.

Preintervention questionnaire

We first asked participants to provide information about their prior experiences with COVID-19 infections and vaccinations (see Supplementary material, Vaccine and infection history). In brief, participants reported the date of each prior diagnosed COVID-19 infection, and the date and brand of each prior COVID-19 vaccine dose. Based on the date of updated booster vaccine release nationwide (2022 September 2), we determined whether or not each participant had received an updated vaccine dose.

Participants then completed a questionnaire in which they rated their perceived risk of getting infected with COVID-19 (if exposed to the virus), perceived risk of getting severely ill with COVID-19 (if infected), interest in learning more about COVID-19 risks, prior knowledge about the updated vaccines, perceived eligibility to receive an updated vaccine, perceived safety and effectiveness of the updated vaccines, and intentions to receive the updated vaccine (if they had not yet done so). All survey materials are provided in the Supplementary material (Appendix).

Immunity estimator intervention

Participants first read an overview message describing the immunity estimator tool and noting other factors (e.g. age and preexisting conditions) that can increase risk of infection and severe disease (see Supplementary material, Immunity estimator text). We calculated an “immunity score” for each participant based on each individual's history of COVID-19 infections and vaccines, accounting for the number and recency of immune-modifying events. Details about calculation immunity score calculation are provided in the Supplementary material (Calculation of immunity scores). Due to feasibility and privacy concerns, we did not ask participants to report their medical histories to account for other risk factors, such as preexisting conditions. Participants were not directly shown their immunity scores. Instead, we used the immunity scores to classify participants into five levels of protection, ranging from “no protection” to “strong protection.” Participants viewed a personalized feedback page for at least 5 s before being permitted to proceed (mean = 24.4 s). We displayed a meter graphic indicating the participant's protection level, paired with additional tailored information about waning immunity and vaccine eligibility (see Supplementary material, Immunity estimator text).

Informational flyer interventions

We compared the immunity estimator intervention with two other interventions that provided similar information without personalization. In the CDC-Flyer condition, we presented a digital copy of a CDC-approved informational flyer that was intended to motivate uptake of updated booster vaccines (e.g. in clinics and pharmacies). The Alt-Flyer was a modified version of the CDC-Flyer; we added text about waning immunity to emphasize that protection must be restored and maintained over time. In both conditions, participants were required to view the flyer for at least 5 s before they were permitted to proceed with the task (CDC-Flyer mean = 32.5 s, Alt-Flyer mean = 30.1 s).

Postintervention questionnaire

The postintervention questionnaire repeated all questions from the preintervention questionnaire. We also measured COVID skepticism with two items previously used in other studies of COVID-19 attitudes and vaccine hesitancy (80). For participants who were eligible to receive an updated booster dose, we provided a link to a government appointment-finder tool for COVID-19 vaccines. Lastly, participants completed a demographics survey.

Follow-up survey

Two months later, we recontacted participants who had been eligible to receive an updated booster vaccine but had not yet done so (at the time of the initial study). Of the 384 participants who were recontacted, 291 (76%) completed the follow-up survey. In the follow-up survey, participants reported any recent infections or vaccine doses that had occurred in the past 2 months, then responded to the same set of questions from the postintervention questionnaire. We also collected other measures (e.g. future vaccination intentions and timeline, perceived personal and societal benefits of COVID-19 vaccines) that are not analyzed within this report.

Study 2: procedure

In December 2023, we recruited a sample of Prolific participants stratified by age, sex, and race to approximate the demographic makeup of the United States. Detailed information about power analyses, recruitment, exclusions, and demographics are provided in the Supplementary material (Participant recruitment). The final survey sample included 553 participants who completed the task. The attrition rate was 3.7% (22 participants returned the task or timed out without completing it). However, due to technical issues (likely caused by ad-blocking software that can prevent event logging with Google Analytics), we only logged detailed website usage data from 290 participants for use in risk estimation analyses.

Before interacting with the immunity estimator website (https://covid19immunity.com/), we informed participants that COVID-19 vaccines had been recently updated to increase protection against new variants of the virus. Participants were asked to report whether they had received a dose of a COVID-19 vaccine on or after 2023 September 14, and if so, which brand they had received. Participants then completed a preintervention questionnaire about COVID-19-related beliefs and attitudes, consistent with the surveys used in study 1.

Participants were then instructed to click a link that directed them to our immunity estimator website (in a new tab). Participants were free to read and interact with the immunity estimator website in the same way that other unpaid users would interact with the website. When participants submitted vaccine and infection history via the immunity estimator tool, we saved these responses and used URL parameters to link website data with survey data. After interacting the immunity estimator website, participants returned to the survey and responded to the same questions about COVID-19–related beliefs and attitudes. Although we were not able to directly record time spent on the immunity estimator website, participants were not permitted to advance past the survey page that linked out to the immunity estimator tool until at least 90 seconds had elapsed (mean = 105 s).

Immunity estimator website

The immunity estimator website provided a user-friendly interface for inputting one's vaccine and infection history and receiving personalized guidance about protection and vaccines (Fig. S8). Users were prompted to add “events” to a table, specifying the month and year of all prior COVID-19 vaccines and infections. Users could add, delete, and edit events as needed. Upon completing their personal history, users clicked a button to calculate and reveal feedback. The website then displayed a feedback page that included two protection meter graphics (protection against infection and severe illness), general information about waning immunity, personalized guidance about immunity and vaccine eligibility, and a statement about how COVID-19 vaccines safely strengthen immunity (even for individuals who acquired natural immunity through infection). In addition to providing separate meters for protection against infection and severe disease, we also updated our immunity score calculation approach to reflect new empirical evidence (see Supplementary material, Calculation of immunity scores and immunity estimator text).

On a separate page titled “About our Estimator,” we provided information about how we estimated protection, other factors that influence protection and risk (e.g. age and comorbidities), our institutional affiliations and funding sources, and a link to the CDC's webpage on COVID-19 vaccine guidance (https://www.cdc.gov/covid/vaccines/stay-up-to-date.html).

Statistical analysis

Data were preprocessed and analyzed with R (v4.3.2), implemented in R Studio (v2023.12.1). All follow-up tests for significant interaction terms were corrected with Tukey's HSD to account for multiple comparisons. All continuous variables used in statistical models were z-scored to enable reporting of standardized effect sizes. However, plots depict unstandardized variables for ease of interpretation. ANOVA models use type III sum of squares to account for interactions. Inspection of model diagnostic plots supported linearity, normality of residuals, and homogeneity of variance assumptions of linear regression. As some models included independent variables there were correlated or conceptually related (e.g. political ideology and risk error), we assessed generalized variance inflation factors (GVIFs) for all models to ensure there were no issues related to multicollinearity. All GVIFs were within the acceptable range from 1 to 5.

We measured political attitudes by averaging responses to two questions on the demographics survey; participants rated their social and economic political attitudes on a 5-point scale (1 = very liberal … 3 = centrist … 5 = very conservative). The variable for political attitudes was continuous in statistical models, but visualizations and follow-up tests compare moderately liberal participants (2/5) and moderately conservative (4/5) participants. Likewise, age was included in statistical models as a continuous variable, but visualizations and follow-up tests show the effect of age at three levels: younger adults (age = 20), middle-aged adults (age = 40), and older adults (age = 60).

Risk error analyses

To assess risk misestimation, we calculated a risk error score for each participant. On the basis of their immunity scores (calculated on the basis of prior infections and vaccines), participants were classified into five protection categories (ranging from 1 = no protection to 5 = strong protection); these categories determined the feedback provided in the immunity estimator condition. We reverse-scored participants’ protection category scores to get a “risk category” measure that was directly comparable to the 5-point scale used for perceived risk ratings. We then calculated risk error scores by subtracting each participant's preintervention perceived risk ratings from their risk category scores. A risk error score of zero indicates accurate estimation of risk, negative scores indicate underestimation, and positive scores indicate overestimation. In study 2, we calculated separate immunity scores and displayed separate feedback meters for protection against infection vs. protection against severe illness; therefore, we calculated separate risk error scores for these two protection types.

We investigated the relationship between risk error and change in perceived risk on the basis of prior studies, which have demonstrated that corrective feedback drives changes in beliefs that correspond to the direction and magnitude of an error signal (42, 66–68). We note that although change scores have been historically criticized as statistically unreliable, current guidelines and evidence support the use of change scores when appropriate for the research question and study design (i.e. in randomized experiments where baseline scores cannot influence group assignment) (81, 82). For further consideration of the analysis relating risk error to change in perceived risk, refer to the Supplementary material (Risk error calculation and change in perceived risk).
