Growing binary trees
Olivier Bodini, Antoine Genitrini Khaydar Nurligareev

Abstract
This paper introduces a new combinatorial framework for modeling the growth of binary trees through a discrete evolution process that incorporates a growing rule and an extinction rule. Building upon the theory of increasingly labeled structures and the analysis of polynomial iterates, we extend previous models of increasing trees with label repetitions by allowing growth branches to terminate. This mechanism enables a direct connection between dynamic evolutionary processes and classical unlabeled binary trees. We provide a combinatorial outlook for this model, linking our new approach to essential but traditionally complex parameters such as tree height, the maximum number of leaves at the deepest level (for a given tree size), and the overall tree profile. Our approach reveals structural links with Mandelbrot polynomials and coding theory. Furthermore, we leverage these structural insights to develop an efficient, iterative uniform random sampler for binary trees with a prescribed profile, achieving optimal complexity in both time and space and in random bit consumption.



1
Introduction

The combinatorial modeling of tree evolution serves as a fundamental framework for understanding the growth of discrete structures, bridging the gap between local recursive rules and global structural properties. Historically, a significant portion of the literature has been dedicated to labeled increasing trees, where nodes are integrated into a hierarchy according to specific temporal constraints. Early studies, such as the seminal work of Meir and Moon on recursive trees
[
17
]
, focused on the typical shape of structures generated by sequential addition. This field was later formalized within the framework of analytic combinatorics by Bergeron, Flajolet, and Salvy
[
2
]
, who characterized a wide range of increasing tree families (such as recursive trees, plane-oriented recursive trees, and binary increasing trees) through symbolic functional equations and differential equations. More recently, this research has been extended to include more complex labeling rules and structural synchronizations, notably through the study of increasing Schröder trees and strict monotonic trees
[
6
,
5
]
. These models are particularly relevant in phylogenetics, where node labels encode the chronology of evolutionary branching, and exhibit deep connections to random graphs, such as the one-to-one correspondence between certain increasing Schröder trees and classical labeled graphs
[
7
]
.

The mathematical genesis of these evolutionary models lies in the study of recursive processes where each new node is attached to an existing one according to a predefined rule. A seminal contribution to this area is the work of Flajolet and Odlyzko on iterates of polynomials
[
14
]
itself extending the paper
[
1
]
. Their analysis provided a robust framework for understanding the height and size of trees generated by iterative growth. This approach was then significantly extended in our previous work, On the number of increasing trees with label repetitions
[
4
]
, which explored discrete growth processes where multiple nodes can share the same label. These models, which involve synchronized branching events, typically lead to purely formal power series where the underlying increasing labeling is intrinsically tied to the structural growth. Such systems often require advanced analytic tools (including the approximate Borel transform and Mittag-Leffler transforms) to extract asymptotic behaviors from their coefficients.

In these previous frameworks, the growth process was essentially monotonic: the structures could only expand, and every leaf remained a potential site for further development. The present paper introduces a paradigm shift by adding an extinction (or death) rule to the evolution process. By allowing branches to terminate, we reconnect these dynamic growth models with classical unlabeled structures, specifically unlabeled binary trees. In this perspective, nodes are categorized into three types: internal nodes, active leaves (anchors), and dead leaves. This distinction allows us to consider essential but traditionally difficult-to-access parameters from a new perspective. By leveraging some symbolic substitution and the iterative dynamics of the resulting polynomials, we provide a combinatorial interpretation of the tree height, the maximum number of the deepest leaves (for a fixed tree size), and the tree profile (the number of leaves at each level). Finally, we leverage these structural insights to design a uniform random sampler for binary tree respecting a given profile, that is optimal in terms of time and space complexity, and random bits consumption.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we formally define the growth process under consideration and describe the specific tree families and parameters that arise from this framework. Section
3
focuses on the “bushy” trees (those with a maximum number of leaves at the maximum depth for a given tree size), analyzing their behavior as the overall tree size increases. In this context, we establish structural links to meta-Fibonacci sequences and coding theory.
In Section
4
, we provide a comprehensive outlook on the growth dynamics of binary trees with respect to their height. Finally, leveraging our study of leaf distribution, Section
5
explores the algorithmic implications of this model, specifically focusing on the uniform random sampling of binary trees satisfying a prescribed profile.

2.3
Cumulative anchor counting

To enumerate the total number of leaves in active growing binary trees, we use the series
\tilde{T}(1,z)
, where

\tilde{T}(x,z):=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}T(x,z)=1+\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}2kt_{n,2k}x^{2k-1}z^{n}\,.

By differentiating Relation (
1
) with respect to
x
, we obtain the following relation for
\tilde{T}(x,z)
:

\tilde{T}(x,z)=1+2xz\tilde{T}(1+zx^{2},z)\,.

(3)

This relation allows us to express the series
\tilde{T}(x,z)
in terms of polynomials
p_{h}(x,z)
that count all growing binary trees that can be obtained after the first
h
steps of the growing process.
In fact, the polynomials
p_{h}(x,z)
are determined by the following recurrence relation:

p_{0}(x,z)=x\,,\qquad\quad p_{h+1}(x,z)=p_{h}(1+zx^{2},z)\quad\,\,\mbox{for }h\geqslant 0\,.

(4)

Therefore, taking into account that
1+zx^{2}=p_{1}(x,z)
, we can iterate Relation (
3
) according to (
4
).
By passing to the limit, this gives us the following expression:

\tilde{T}(x,z)=1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}(2z)^{i}\prod\limits_{j=0}^{i-1}p_{j}(x,z)\,.

Note that the polynomials
p_{h}(x,z)
are related to the so-called (shifted)
Mandelbrot polynomials

[
8
]
:

M_{h}(z)=z\,q_{h}(1,z)\,.

From a combinatorial point of view, Mandelbrot polynomials count growing binary trees with an additional internal node attached to the root; see Figure
1
b).
By substituting
x=1
into the relation for
\tilde{T}(x,z)
, we obtain an expression for
\tilde{T}(1,z)
in terms of Mandelbrot polynomials:

\tilde{T}(1,z)=1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}2^{i}\prod\limits_{j=0}^{i-1}M_{j}(z)\,.

Remark 2.1
.

The formal link to Mandelbrot polynomials highlights a phase transition in the tree growth. The discrete dynamic of the iterates
p_{h}(1,z)
has fixed points satisfying
x=1+zx^{2}
, which results in a critical bifurcation at
z_{c}=1/4
. For
|z|<1/4
, the process contracts towards the Catalan generating function
C(z)
(almost-sure extinction), whereas for
z>1/4
, it diverges (explosive growth). Consequently, the analyticity domain of the process is intrinsically bounded by the main cardioid of the Mandelbrot set.

5.1
Combinatorial context

We are focusing on binary trees, where the atoms under consideration are the leaves.
An analogous approach can be applied to binary trees according to their number of internal nodes.

Definition 5.1
.

The
level
of a node in a binary tree is the distance (in terms of traversed edges) from the root to that node. The root is at level
0
.

Definition 5.2
.

The profile of a binary tree is the sequence
(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{h})
of the
nonnegative numbers of leaves at each level.
The index
h
corresponds to the
height
of the tree. The
size
of the tree
(
i.e.
the total number of leaves it contains) is equal to the sum
\sum_{i}\ell_{i}
.

For a tree reduced to a single leaf (the root), we get the profile
(1)
.
For any other binary tree with height
h>0
, we have
\ell_{0}=0
(the root is not a leaf).

Fact 5.3

(Kraft-McMillan equality)
.

There exists a binary tree with profile
(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{h})

if and only if

\sum_{i=0}^{h}\frac{\ell_{i}}{2^{i}}=1.

This is a classical result in the context of code theory; see, for instance,
[

]

Proposition 5.4
.

Let

5.2
Uniform random sampling of trees with a given profile

A natural way to sample binary trees with a given profile is the following. Start with the root of the tree;
if the latter is a leaf, then stop; otherwise, we have to decide
what are the valid profiles of the two children. In the classical recursive method
[
19
,
15
]
,
even for sampling trees of a given size, the necessary computations are heavy.
In our case, the combinatorial complexity is much higher, since we have to consider the profiles of the two subtrees,
which are not independent (like in the case of size) but are also constrained to be valid profiles.
In the context of binary decision diagram sampling
[
9
]
, such a top-down approach has been addressed
to generate structures with a given profile, but it has finally been overtaken by an iterative
approach
[
10
]
, level by level, only focusing on a given level at a time without the need to consider the rest of the profile.

Here in the context of binary trees, such an iterative approach can be developed as well
(and will be more efficient than the one for decision diagrams), but an even more efficient approach is possible,
looking to the tree from the bottom to the top (and for a given level, from left to right).

We can sample a binary tree with a given profile by starting from the last level,
and then iteratively going up to the root, level by level, and for each level.
In fact, in Proposition
5.6
, we can look to the product formula counting the trees with a given profile
from the last factor to the first ones,
i.e.
from the deepest levels of the trees to the root level.

allows for a precise characterization of the limit distribution of the deepest leaves. This approach provides a new way to analyze the tree’s boundary and could be generalized to other substitution rules, offering a unified combinatorial view of extinction-based growth processes.

Algorithmically, the iterative bottom-up sampling introduced in Section
5
can be broadly extended. Natural next steps include the uniform generation of binary forests and the sampling of trees with partial profiles (e.g., profiles with ”holes” representing intermediate levels of arbitrary sizes). Furthermore, the layer-by-layer independence of our combinatorial choices directly paves the way for an optimal unranking algorithm for binary trees with prescribed profiles.

Let

Both bounds are sharp: the lower bound is attained by a path-like tree (with

k=\hat{a}_{n-h+2}
for
n\in[h,2^{h}-1]
.
To find the asymptotic behavior of
\hat{a}_{m}
, we recall that
\hat{a}_{m}
is the inverse of the prefix sum
S_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{b}_{i}
. The ruler function
\hat{b}_{i}
satisfies
\hat{b}_{i}=v_{2}(i)+1
, where
v_{2}(i)
is the 2-adic valuation of
i
.
The sum of the first
k
values is known to be

S_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big(v_{2}(i)+1\big)=2k-O(\log k)\,.

Inverting this asymptotic relation for
S_{k}\approx m
yields
k=\hat{a}_{m}=\frac{m}{2}+O(\log m)
.
Substituting
m=n-h+2
, we get
k=\frac{n}{2}-\frac{h-2}{2}+O(\log n)
.
Dividing by
2^{h-1}
yields:

Y=\frac{X}{2}-\frac{h-2}{2^{h}}+O\left(\frac{\log n}{2^{h-1}}\right)\,.

Since
n<2^{h}
, we have
\log n=O(h)
. Therefore, the error term completely vanishes as
h\to\infty
. The equation converges to the line
Y=X/2
. Since
n\in[h,2^{h}-1]
, the normalized coordinate
X
asymptotically spans the interval
[0,2]
. This gives the line segment connecting
(0,0)
to
(2,1)
.

Bottom boundary:

By definition of active trees, the minimum number of anchors is
2k\geqslant 2
, which means that
k\geqslant 1
. Thus, the bottom boundary satisfies
Y\geqslant 1/2^{h-1}
, which converges to
Y=0
as
h\to\infty
.
For
k=1
, the minimal number of internal nodes is
n=h
(a linear tree) and the maximal is
n=2^{h-1}
(from
\Gamma_{h}
). Normalized,
X\in[h/2^{h-1},1]
, which converges to the interval
[0,1]
. This forms the line segment connecting
(0,0)
to
(1,0)
.

Conclusion:

The scaling limit of the domain
S_{h}
is enclosed by the three asymptotic lines:
Y=X-1
,
Y=X/2
, and
Y=0
. The intersections of these lines form exactly the triangle with vertices
(0,0)
,
(1,0)
, and
(2,1)
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.4
.

Let
T
be a binary tree with profile
(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{h})
where
h\geqslant 1
.
Since the tree has a positive height, its root is an internal node, thus
i_{0}=1
.
For any level
k\in\{1,\dots,h\}
, the total number of nodes at this level is exactly the number of children of the internal nodes from the previous level
k-1
. Since each internal node has exactly
2
children, there are
2i_{k-1}
nodes at level
k
.
These nodes are partitioned into two categories: internal nodes (counted by
i_{k}
) and leaves (counted by
\ell_{k}
). Thus, we have the relation
i_{k}+\ell_{k}=2i_{k-1}
, which immediately yields:

i_{k}=2i_{k-1}-\ell_{k}.\qed

To get the proof of Proposition
5.6
we first derive the following lemma:

Lemma C.1
.

Let
(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{h})
, with
h\geqslant 1
, be a valid profile.
Let
k\in\{0,\dots,h-1\}
,
then the profile
(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k},i_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1})
is valid;
the trees satisfying this profile have exactly height
k+1
.

Note that the profiles under consideration are the profiles in terms of leaf numbers. The key idea of the proof is based on the substitution of each internal node in level
k+1
by a leaf.

Proof of Lemma
C.1
.

Let
P=(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{h})
, with
h\geqslant 1
, be a valid profile.
Let
T
be a binary tree having profile
P
. On its
(k+1)
-th level, it contains
i_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1}
nodes (the sum of internal nodes and leaves). By replacing each internal node of this level with a new leaf, no internal nodes remain at level
k+1
. Therefore, the tree cannot grow deeper, making
k+1
the new maximum depth. We thus obtain a valid binary tree of exactly height
k+1
with profile
(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k},i_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1})
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.6
.

We proceed by induction on the height
h
of the binary tree.

Base case:

Let
h=1
. The single valid profile of height
1
is
(\ell_{0},\ell_{1})=(0,2)
, since the root must be an internal node to reach height
1
, and its children must be leaves.
In this case,
i_{0}=1
. The formula gives:

\prod_{k=0}^{1-1}\binom{2i_{k}}{\ell_{k+1}}=\binom{2i_{0}}{\ell_{1}}=\binom{2}{2}=1.

There is indeed only one such binary tree. The base case holds.

Inductive step:

Assume that the proposition holds for all valid profiles of height
h
.

Let
P=(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{h},\ell_{h+1})
be a valid profile of height
h+1
.
According to Lemma
C.1
, the profile
P^{\prime}=(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell^{\prime}_{h})
where
\ell^{\prime}_{h}=i_{h}+\ell_{h}
is a valid profile of height
h
. Note that the sequence of internal nodes
(i_{k})_{k=0\dots h-1}
is identical for both
P
and
P^{\prime}
.
By the inductive hypothesis, the number of binary trees
T^{\prime}
having the profile
P^{\prime}
is:

N(P^{\prime})=\prod_{k=0}^{h-2}\binom{2i_{k}}{\ell_{k+1}}\cdot\binom{2i_{h-1}}{\ell^{\prime}_{h}}.

Since
P^{\prime}
has height
h
, level
h
is its last level, meaning all nodes at this level are leaves. Thus, the total number of nodes at level
h
is exactly the number of children from level
h-1
, giving
\ell^{\prime}_{h}=2i_{h-1}
. Therefore, the last factor of the product is simply
\binom{2i_{h-1}}{2i_{h-1}}=1
.

To obtain a tree
T
with profile
P
from a tree
T^{\prime}
with profile
P^{\prime}
, we must:

1.

At level
h
, choose which
\ell_{h}
nodes (among the
2i_{h-1}
available) will remain leaves. The remaining
i_{h}=2i_{h-1}-\ell_{h}
nodes become internal nodes. The number of ways to do this is
\binom{2i_{h-1}}{\ell_{h}}
.

2.

At level
h+1
, we have
2i_{h}
available positions created by the
i_{h}
internal nodes of level
h
. We must choose
\ell_{h+1}
leaves among them. The number of ways is
\binom{2i_{h}}{\ell_{h+1}}
.

Multiplying the number of ways to form the first
h-1
levels by these new choices, we get:

N(P)=\prod_{k=0}^{h-2}\binom{2i_{k}}{\ell_{k+1}}\cdot\binom{2i_{h-1}}{\ell_{h}}\cdot\binom{2i_{h}}{\ell_{h+1}}=\prod_{k=0}^{h}\binom{2i_{k}}{\ell_{k+1}}.

These choices are independent and uniquely determine the tree
T
, ensuring no overcounting. This corresponds to the desired product. The property holds for
h+1
, which completes the induction.
∎

Proposition C.2
.

Let
T
be a binary tree with profile
(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{h})
, with
h\geqslant 1
.
And denote its internal profile as
(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{h-1})
. We also have

\begin{cases}i_{h-1}=\frac{\ell_{h}}{2}\\
i_{k}=\frac{i_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1}}{2},\quad\text{for }1\leqslant k\leqslant h-2.\end{cases}

Proof.

A direct combinatorial interpretation yields the result. On level
k+1
, there are exactly
i_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1}
nodes (the sum of internal nodes and leaves). Since each of these nodes is one of the two children of an internal node from level
k
, we have:

2i_{k}=i_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1}.

Dividing by
2
immediately gives
i_{k}=\frac{i_{k+1}+\ell_{k+1}}{2}
.
For the last level
h
, there are no internal nodes (
i_{h}=0
), which yields
i_{h-1}=\frac{\ell_{h}}{2}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition 5.6.

Let
N
be the total number of nodes (both internal nodes and leaves) in the generated binary tree.
We analyze the performance of Algorithm
1
with respect to time, space, and random bit consumption.

Time Complexity:

The algorithm constructs the tree iteratively from the bottom up, level by level. At any step
i
(from
h-1
down to
0
), the algorithm performs a shuffling operation between the sequence
T
(representing the
i_{i}
internal nodes of level
i
formed in the previous step) and a sequence of
\ell_{i}
leaves.
The total number of elements in the resulting sequence
T^{\prime}
is
i_{i}+\ell_{i}
. By Proposition
5.4
, this is exactly
2i_{i-1}
, which is the total number of children belonging to the internal nodes at level
i-1
.
Using the optimal shuffling algorithm from
[
3
]
, the time required to shuffle two sequences of total length
K
is
O(K)
. Thus, the time spent at level
i
is
O(i_{i}+\ell_{i})
.
Summing this cost over all levels yields a total time complexity of:

O\left(\sum_{i=1}^{h}(i_{i}+\ell_{i})\right)=O(N).

Since generating a tree of size
N
requires at least
\Omega(N)
operations simply to instantiate the nodes, our algorithm achieves the optimal time complexity of
O(N)
.

Space Complexity:

The algorithm only maintains the sequence
T
(pointers to the subtrees built so far) and allocates new nodes strictly as they are integrated into the final tree structure. It does not require any heavy auxiliary data structures (such as large precomputed tables typically used in the recursive top-down approach
[
15
]
). The memory footprint is therefore directly proportional to the size of the generated tree, giving an optimal space complexity of
O(N)
.

Random Bits Consumption:

From an information-theoretic perspective, selecting an object uniformly at random from a set of size
S
requires at least
\log_{2}(S)
random bits.
According to Proposition
5.6
, the total number of binary trees with profile
L
is:

N(L)=\prod_{k=0}^{h-1}\binom{2i_{k}}{\ell_{k+1}}.

The theoretically optimal number of random bits to sample one tree is therefore
\log_{2}(N(L))
.
In Algorithm
1
, at each step
i
(corresponding to level
i+1
of the tree), the algorithm chooses the placement of
\ell_{i+1}
leaves among
2i_{i}
available positions. This choice is independent of the other levels.
The algorithm in
[
3
]
performs exactly this combination sampling using an expected optimal number of random bits (matching the entropy of the binomial coefficient).
Since the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms, the total expected random bit consumption of our algorithm is:

\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\log_{2}\binom{2i_{k}}{\ell_{k+1}}=\log_{2}\left(\prod_{k=0}^{h-1}\binom{2i_{k}}{\ell_{k+1}}\right)=\log_{2}(N(L)).

Thus, the algorithm achieves strict optimality in random bit consumption.
∎
