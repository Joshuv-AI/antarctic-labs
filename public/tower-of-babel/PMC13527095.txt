Catalyst-free single-molecule cascade photoreactions for spatiotemporally programmed encryption
Authors: Xiaoling Zuo, Rong Li, Chuan Liu, Yonglang Liu, Dan Mao, Chong Wu, Tao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe escalating crisis of information leakage necessitates a shift from static identifiers to intelligent, dynamically secure systems. Current approaches, even up-to-date time-dependent ones, remain constrained by non-progressive or multicomponent designs that lack programmable spatiotemporal control, underscoring the need for a minimal, integrated molecular platform. Herein, we report a single-molecule system (4BT-Py-PTC) that achieves time-gated, graded multicolor fluorescence via sequential, catalyst-free photoreactions. This designed pathway involves thiocarbonate hydrolysis, alkene cleavage, and aldehyde photooxidation, progressively transforming the initial yellow emitter into red- (3BT-Py, 5 min), yellow- (2BT-CHO, 20 min), and blue-emissive (5BT-COOH, 80 min) products. This intrinsic programmability enables a higher-order of spatiotemporal command. Leveraging this output, we demonstrate two encryption modes: time-gated 3D codes and organogel matrices for multistage (reveal–conceal–erase) control with temporal keys. This work establishes an approach centered on catalyst-free, single-molecule cascade photoreactions, redefining secure materials through spatiotemporal encryption and benchmarking dynamic anti-counterfeiting and data protection.Subject terms: Single-molecule fluorescence, Excited states, Gels and hydrogels

Introduction

The escalating crisis of information leakage and data manipulation poses critical challenges to global security, exacerbated by the inadequacy of static encryption materials (such as conventional fluorescent inks, holographic anti-counterfeiting labels or 1D/2D codes) against increasingly sophisticated counterfeiting1.

Fluorescent polymer gels have emerged as promising encryption materials, leveraging their vivid, stimulus-responsive color changes and soft, programmable 3D networks for dynamic and customizable security2–5. The field has evolved rapidly from simple on-off switches6 to sophisticated systems featuring multistate visualization7,8, 3D anti-counterfeiting platforms9–11, 3D codes12,13, self-erasing properties14,15, multidimensional gel actuators16–18, and even time-dependent color tuning19–21, underscoring time as an indispensable security dimension that moves encryption beyond static features. However, current time-dependent systems remain fundamentally limited. They often exhibit non-progressive optical responses, or rely on multicomponent formulations, or require complex external stimuli, which severely restrict their information density and practical utility. More critically, they lack the ability to execute genuine spatiotemporally programmed encryption, where information access is governed by a pre-defined chemical pathway that autonomously orchestrates changes across both time and space within a single molecular system. To overcome these limitations, there is a pressing demand for a progressive, multi-level and time-gated encryption system capable of true spatiotemporal programming, specifically a system that integrates temporal and spatial control into a single molecular-scale platform under operationally simple conditions.

The design of time-gated encryption materials based on photofluorochromism primarily relies on photoreactions such as cleavage, oxidation, and dimerization, which are enabled by specific photoresponsive skeletons. Among these, thiocarbonates are valuable in organic synthesis, enabling the conversion of vicinal diols to alkenes and facilitating the condensation of carboxylic acids with amines22,23. Thiocarbonates also offer a promising route to alcohols via hydrolysis, yet this process typically requires metal ions or catalysts, limiting its application24,25. Consequently, developing metal- and catalyst-free hydrolysis methods for thiocarbonates is highly desirable yet remains scarcely explored. To construct a multi-responsive encryption system, the integration of additional photoreactions is essential. A particularly attractive candidate is the oxidative cleavage of alkenes, a key functional group in conjugated materials, also an efficient method to produce ketones and aldehydes. However, the conventional means to achieve this transformation remain problematic. Conventional reagents (e.g., KMnO₄, OsO₄) are often toxic and costly26,27, and while improved alternatives exist, they often rely on transition metal or photocatalysis. Notably, Tang et al.28 pioneered a catalyst-free, UV-induced photoreaction for alkene oxidative cleavage. In their specially designed aggregation-induced emission (AIE) system (TPE-2MO2NT), this cleavage reaction proceeded with an intriguing additional feature: it spontaneously generated time-dependent multicolor fluorescence (orange to blue) as a direct consequence of π-conjugation disruption and altered electron delocalization. Thus, beyond achieving the desired chemical transformation, their work provided a valuable demonstration that catalyst-free alkene cleavage can be coupled with tunable photoluminescence for dynamic optical encoding.

The oxidation of aldehydes to carboxylic acids is also a widely used yet fundamentally problematic photochemical transformation. Traditional stoichiometric oxidants (e.g., chromates, permanganates) pose environmental and atom-efficiency concerns, which has spurred the development of catalytic alternatives29–32. While molecular oxygen (O₂) is an ideal oxidant in principle, offering atom economy and producing only water as a byproduct, its practical use is hampered by reliance on transition metal or organocatalysts. These catalysts often demand pure O₂, which introduces explosion risks and energy costs, or require complex reaction setups when using air33–36. In this context, the sunlight-induced autoxidation of aldehydes offers a crucial insight: aldehyde oxidation can proceed under air, without any catalyst, using only light37. This natural process inspires a catalyst-free photooxidation strategy that is both simple and attractive. Despite this, the sequential integration of these three catalyst-free reactions into a single photofluorochromic system using only one type of light source, such as UV irradiation, remains unexplored. Therefore, achieving graded multicolor fluorescence through these sequential processes holds significant promise for advancing fluorescent gel-based information encryption materials.

This work presents a single molecular system (4BT-Py-PTC) that integrates excited-state intramolecular proton transfer (ESIPT) with sequential, catalyst-free photoreactions under UV irradiation in air. The transformation proceeds in a time-gated manner via three distinct pathways: (i) thiocarbonate hydrolysis yields the red-emitting 3BT at 5 min; (ii) alkene oxidative cleavage generates the yellow-emitting 2BT-CHO at 20 min; and (iii) aldehyde photooxidation produces the new blue-emitting luminogen 5BT-COOH at 80 min, thereby achieving graded multicolor fluorescence (Fig. 1a). This photofluorochromic system offers a highly appealing route to create time-gated information storage materials through multicolor fluorescence induced by continuous, catalyst-free reactions under aerial UV irradiation. Building on the solvent-dependent multicolor fluorescence of 4BT-Py-PTC and the distinct photofluorochromic sequences observed in different 4BT-Py-PTC-loaded organogels, we achieve versatile spatiotemporal control for advanced encryption. Accordingly, we demonstrate its potential through two distinct platforms: a solvent-based 3D code array for time-gated information display, and organogel-based cryptographic matrices enabling integrated time-gated encryption, dynamic readout, and self-erasure (Fig. 1b). This work consolidates selective encryption, multi-channel programming, and time-gated multifluorescence behavior into a single system, thereby establishing a paradigm for secure information storage that is inherently resistant to cracking and duplication.

Results

Synthesis and photophysical properties

The structure of 4BT-Py-PTC and its intermediates, synthesized as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, were fully characterized (Supplementary Figs. 2–9). Its photophysical properties were first investigated via absorption and photoluminescence spectra (Supplementary Figs. 10–14). In DMF and DMSO solutions, 4BT-Py-PTC exhibited a sharp π–π* transition peak near 330 nm and a broad intramolecular charge transfer band centered at 610–615 nm. In other solvents (ACN, EtOH, THF), the sharp peak appeared between 328 and 335 nm, with variation attributed to differences in absorption coefficients38. Moreover, the fluorescent spectra of 4BT-Py-PTC in different solvents showed emission maxima at 524 nm in DMF, 424 nm in DMSO, 590 nm in ACN, 570 nm in EtOH, and 545 nm in THF, indicating pronounced solvent effects39.

Time-resolved fluorescence measurements revealed a fluorescence lifetime (τ) of 4.13 ns for 4BT-Py-PTC. For 3BT-Py, 2BT-CHO, and 5BT-COOH, the corresponding τ values were 4.36 ns, 1.53 ns, and 0.54 ns, respectively (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 15). Their fluorescence quantum yields (Φf) of the four emissive species increase progressively along the cascade: 0.51% for 4BT-Py-PTC, 0.58% for 3BT-Py, 0.91% for 2BT-CHO, and 0.90% for 5BT-COOH (Supplementary Table 2). Importantly, these four compounds exhibited excellent stability under various conditions. Specifically, heating the solid sample of 4BT-Py-PTC at 60 °C for 60 min did not alter its fluorescence profile, and the same high thermal stability was confirmed for 3BT-Py, 2BT-CHO, and 5BT-COOH (Supplementary Fig. 16). For 4BT-Py-PTC in the solid state, its fluorescence spectrum remained nearly unchanged after continuous UV irradiation at 365 nm for 120 min, demonstrating high photostability (Supplementary Fig. 17a). Similarly, its fluorescence spectrum showed no significant variation after storage in the dark under dry conditions for 180 days (Supplementary Fig. 17b). Notably, among them, only 3BT-Py showed AIE behavior, while the other three did not (Supplementary Fig. 18). Regarding concentration-dependent behavior, none of the four compounds exhibited inner-filter effects under our experimental conditions (Supplementary Fig. 19).

Solvent- and time-gated multicolor fluorescence properties

The fascinating light response of 4BT-Py-PTC prompted an in-depth study of its photochemical properties. Upon UV 365 nm irradiation, its absorption in DMF and THF revealed complex reaction pathways. In DMF, the original peaks (330 and 610 nm) decreased, while a new band at 380–420 nm emerged at 5 min, indicating photooxidation-generated metastable intermediates40,41. This band vanished after 20 min, confirming intermediate consumption42,43. In THF, a new, persistent band at 365 nm emerged within the irradiation time window of 5–50 min (Supplementary Figs. 20, 24). By contrast, a straightforward decrease in absorption was observed in DMSO, ACN, and EtOH, suggesting pronounced photolysis in these solvents (Supplementary Figs. 21-23).

Upon 365 nm UV irradiation, 4BT-Py-PTC underwent distinct fluorescence evolution in various solvents (Fig. 2). In DMF, the emission exhibited a blue-shifted peak at 420 nm and a red-shifted peak at 662 nm after 5 min. With prolonged exposure, the blue emission intensified, while the red emission at 10 min first rose then diminished. This spectral change corresponded to a successive shift in the CIE chromaticity diagram from grass-green (0.353, 0.491) at 0 min, to cerise-pink (0.329, 0.277) at 5 min, and finally to blue (0.202, 0.226) at 80 min, consistent with the digital photos in Fig. 2a. Under 254 nm UV irradiation, however, 4BT-Py-PTC in DMF displayed a distinct fluorescence evolution without the initial red-shift observed under 365 nm irradiation; instead, the emission peak gradually blue-shifted to 429 nm after 50 min and finally stabilized at 427 nm after 80 min. Correspondingly, the CIE coordinates shifted from (0.326, 0.410) (grass-green) at 0 min to (0.273, 0.250) (blue) at 50 min, and finally to (0.144, 0.171) (blue) at 80 min (Supplementary Fig. 25). These findings confirm that the photoresponse behavior of 4BT-Py-PTC is excitation-wavelength-specific, which motivated our choice of 365 nm UV light as the irradiation source for the cascade. Next, we investigated how other experimental parameters affect the reaction kinetics of 4BT-Py-PTC in DMF. Under 365 nm UV irradiation at a standard distance of 0 cm, increasing the light photon flux from 2.76 × 1019 to 6.62 × 1019 and then to 1.21 × 1020 photons/s progressively shortened the half-rise time (t₅₀%, defined as the time required to reach 50% of the maximum fluorescence intensity at 426 nm) of the overall photoconversion (4BT-Py-PTC → 5BT-COOH) from approximately 24.7 min to 12.2 min and further to 8.3 min (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 26a), confirming that the reaction rate is photon-flux-dependent. Conversely, at a fixed photon flux of 1.21 × 1020 photons/s, extending the irradiation distance from 0 to 10 cm and then to 20 cm prolonged the half-rise time from 8.3 min to 19.1 min and 25.0 min, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 26b), consistent with the inverse-square law of light intensity44. In contrast to 365 nm excitation, irradiation at 254 nm under otherwise identical conditions (1.21 × 1020 photons/s, 0 cm) resulted in a slower fluorescence evolution, with a half-rise time exceeding 36.2 min for the blue-emissive product formation (Supplementary Fig. 26c), further underscoring the wavelength specificity of the photoresponse. Collectively, these quantitative kinetic data establish that the temporal progression of the photochemical cascade can be finely tuned by adjusting the photon flux and distance, while the excitation wavelength dictates the overall transformation pathway.

In DMSO (Fig. 2b), when exposed to 365 nm UV light, the blue emission intensity increased over time. New peaks emerged at 657 nm (5 min) and 523 nm (50 min), both vanishing upon further irradiation. The CIE coordinates shifted accordingly from (0.189, 0.139) at 0 min to (0.278, 0.238) at 5 min, then to (0.247, 0.310) at 50 min, and back to (0.187, 0.184) at 80 min, matching the fluorescence transition from blue to peachblow to cyan and back to blue. For ACN (Fig. 2c), a red-shifted peak appeared at 682 nm after 5 min. Subsequent irradiation produced two new peaks at 467 and 600 nm: the former increased while the latter decreased with time. The CIE coordinates transitioned from (0.465, 0.488) at 0 min, to (0.482, 0.427) at 5 min, and to (0.220, 0.251) at 80 min, accompanied by a visible color change from yellowish-orange to red-orange to blue. In EtOH (Fig. 2d), a new peak at 682 nm emerged after 5 min but progressively diminished with prolonged UV exposure, while the original peak at 570 nm intensified. The CIE coordinates shifted from (0.409, 0.468) at 0 min, to (0.423, 0.442) at 5 min, and to (0.379, 0.460) at 80 min, consistent with the fluorescence transition from yellow to yellowish-orange back to yellow. In THF (Supplementary Fig. 27), 4BT-Py-PTC exhibited dynamic fluorescence under UV irradiation: the original peak intensified, accompanied by two new peaks at 663 nm (after 5 min) and 495 nm (after 50 min). The 663 nm peak rose initially, then declined, while the 495 nm peak grew steadily. These changes shifted the CIE coordinates from grass-green (0.358, 0.406) at 0 min to yellow (0.375, 0.413) at 5 min, and back to grass-green (0.341, 0.464) at 80 min.

According to previous studies45,46, the solvent environment critically governs the ESIPT efficiency through a balance of its polarity and specific hydrogen-bonding interactions. Typically, solvent polarity stabilizes the charge-separated keto-tautomer. However, a striking observation in 4BT-Py-PTC is the distinct blue emission in DMSO, contrasting with the grass-green or yellowish-orange emission in other polar aprotic solvents (e.g., DMF, ACN). This anomaly stems from the superior hydrogen-bond accepting ability of DMSO, which competitively inhibits the ESIPT process and locks the fluorophore in its higher-energy enol form, overriding the general trend expected from solvent polarity alone47,48. Protic EtOH facilitates ESIPT through its hydrogen-bond network, resulting in yellow emission, while the weak interactions in low-polarity THF similarly permit ESIPT, producing a grass-green hue. Additionally, the divergence in color evolution sequences can be attributed to the solvent environment’s regulation of the photochemical cascade kinetics, which governs the intensity of the red-shift stage and the onset timing of the blue-shift stage, ultimately programming distinct spatiotemporal color pathways49,50. Together, this precise solvent control over both the static emission color and the temporally resolved fluorescence evolution establishes a robust platform for spatiotemporal information encryption and advanced anti-counterfeiting.

Organogel- and time-gated multicolor fluorescence properties

Inspired by the vibrant multicolor fluorescence of 4BT-Py-PTC in the solution phase, we next investigated its multicolor fluorescence properties in the solid phase (Fig. 3). Based on the solubility of acrylamide (AAm) and N,N-dimethylacrylamide (DMAA), DMF, DMSO, and THF were selected to prepare encryption materials via vat photopolymerization (VP) 3D printing (Fig. 3a). Real-time Fourier transform infrared (RT-FTIR) spectroscopy analysis of the printing inks showed double-bond conversions of 60.8-63.2% under LED irradiation, confirming highly efficient visible-light-induced photopolymerization (Fig. 3b)51,52. ATR-IR spectra further verified the successful incorporation of both monomers into the organogel, with characteristic peaks of AAm (C=O at 1609 cm⁻¹, N-H at 3170 and 3339 cm⁻¹) and DMAA (C-N at 1256 cm⁻¹, C=O at 1606 cm⁻¹) clearly present (Fig. 3c).

The time-gated multicolor fluorescence of the organogels containing 4BT-Py-PTC was systematically investigated (Fig. 3d-f). Under UV irradiation, the DMF-based organogel (DMF-OG) exhibited a red-shifted emission at 678 nm after 1 min, which gradually diminished. Two new peaks subsequently emerged at 472 nm and 553 nm after 5 min. The former intensified while the latter first increased and then decreased. These changes drove a sequential CIE coordinate shift from yellow (0.367, 0.403) at 0 min, to red (0.635, 0.337) at 1 min, back to yellow (0.388, 0.382) at 10 min, and finally to blue (0.218, 0.238) at 30 min, as visually confirmed by the mushroom-shaped organogels in Fig. 3d. Similarly, the DMSO-based organogel (DMSO-OG) displayed a distinct fluorescence evolution. UV exposure triggered the appearance of two new peaks at 454 nm and 696 nm after 3 min, alongside the original 567 nm emission. The 454 nm peak intensified over time, while the 696 nm peak weakened. A further peak emerged at 545 nm at 10 min before gradually fading. This spectral progression corresponded to a color transition from yellow to red, to yellow, and finally to blue, reflected in the CIE coordinate shift from (0.411, 0.469) at 0 min, to (0.612, 0.313) at 3 min, (0.361, 0.409) at 10 min, and ultimately (0.245, 0.261) at 60 min, as observed in the animal-shaped organogels in Fig. 3e.

Under UV irradiation, the THF-based organogel (THF-OG) exhibited a red-shifted peak at 674 nm after 20 s. Subsequent irradiation for 1 min produced two new peaks at 454 nm (increasing) and 554 nm (decreasing), accompanied by a color transition from yellow to red, back to yellow, and finally to blue, as visualized in the pigeon-shaped organogels (Fig. 3f). The corresponding CIE coordinates shifted from (0.378, 0.453) at 0 s to (0.443, 0.372) at 20 s, then to (0.602, 0.347) at 1 min, and finally to (0.244, 0.249) after 20 min. Notably, these organogel systems not only retain the tunable multicolor emission of the luminogen in solution but also enable more precise and versatile optical output. This enhancement stems from the polymer network’s confinement effect and microenvironmental regulation, which optimize the ESIPT process and create diverse ESIPT conditions unattainable in solution53,54. Specifically, the distinct microenvironment provided by each organogel matrix significantly modulates the hydrolysis and photooxidation kinetics of 4BT-Py-PTC, thereby dictating the temporal sequence for the emergence of each distinct fluorescence color. The THF-based organogel, with its relatively low polarity and specific polymer-solvent interactions, facilitates the fastest hydrolysis and photooxidations, followed by the DMF-based and DMSO-based organogels55. The slowest fluorescence evolution in the DMSO-based organogel, compared to its DMF counterpart, is attributable to DMSO’s stronger suppression of ESIPT kinetics via its superior hydrogen-bond accepting ability56. This solvent-dependent reaction rate within the confined gel network directly translates into a programmable temporal encryption key, a phenomenon where the solid matrix does not merely host but actively dictates the photochemical dynamics. Notably, in DMF organogels, 4BT-Py-PTC exhibits a substantially higher Φf of 25.34% than in DMF solution (0.51%), attributed to the rigidifying effect of the gel network. This value further increases to 65.99% for the final product, 5BT-COOH, which is consistent with the solution-phase Φf trend where the final product also shows a higher value than the starting material. The solvent-dependent Φf variation in different organogels (25.34% in DMF, 21.43% in THF, but only 8.61% in DMSO) is consistent with the ESIPT inhibition by DMSO discussed above (Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 28a-c).

To elucidate in detail the stepwise photooxidation of alkene and aldehyde groups, we investigated the refined time-gated multicolor fluorescence of 4BT-Py-PTC in organogels (taking DMF-OG as an example, Supplementary Fig. 29). Precise control over UV irradiation time yielded a sequence of distinct colors: orange after 5 min, followed by yellow-green, green, and cyan emissions at 15, 20, and 25 min, respectively. These color changes, quantified by the corresponding CIE coordinate shifts from (0.451, 0.368) to (0.328, 0.392) to (0.272, 0.484) and then to (0.258, 0.370), and visualized through the evolving horse-shaped organogels, demonstrate the possibilities of precise spatiotemporal programming of fluorescence. We propose that these intermediate colors arise not from a single emissive species, but likely from two concomitant mechanisms: (i) the superposition of emission spectra from coexisting precursors and products during the transition periods, and (ii) the precise modulation of the ESIPT properties and emission spectra of transient oxidative intermediates by the confined gel microenvironment57,58.

Furthermore, the time-gated fluorescence of DMF-OGs loaded with the synthetic precursors 2BT-CHO and 5BT-COOH is shown in Supplementary Fig. 30. Under UV irradiation, the emission from the 2BT-CHO organogel underwent a progressive blue shift: its color transformed from yellow (0 min) to yellow-green (10 min) to cyan (20 min) and finally to blue (30 min), as visualized in the circle-shaped organogels. Correspondingly, the CIE coordinates shifted from (0.383, 0.431) to (0.302, 0.368) to (0.251, 0.321) and then to (0.223, 0.254). In contrast, the 5BT-COOH organogel maintained stable blue emission throughout the 30-min irradiation, consistent with the minimal change in its CIE coordinates and the maple leaf-shaped organogel photographs. Moreover, for DMF-OGs containing 2BT-CHO and 5BT-COOH, the Φf values were 21.69% and 65.99%, respectively (Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 28d, e). Apparently, the precise temporal control of fluorescence color underscores the potential of these organogels as advanced materials for sophisticated information encryption.

Mechanism of UV-induced photofluorochromism

To gain insight into the precursor conversion mechanism, electrospray mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) analysis was employed to monitor the photochemical reaction processes (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 5). Initially, an ion peak was observed at a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of 495.1191, corresponding to [4BT-Py-PTC]+ (Fig. 4a). Subsequently, the DMF solution of 4BT-Py-PTC was irradiated with 365 nm UV light at room temperature. After 5 min of irradiation, ESI-MS analysis was performed on the solution, revealing a new ion peak at m/z 359.1216, which was consistent with [3BT-Py]+ (Fig. 4b). This confirms that light exposure induced the removal of phenyl carbonothioate groups and the formation of 3BT-Py. Upon continued irradiation, another ESI-MS analysis was conducted after 20 min, identifying a new ion peak at m/z 270.0582, attributed to [2BT-CHO]+ (Fig. 4c). This is likely due to the photo-induced oxidation of olefins to aldehyde groups, resulting in the formation of compound 2BT-CHO. With further irradiation, a subsequent ESI-MS analysis at 80 min detected a new ion peak at m/z 286.0531, assigned to [5BT-COOH]+ (Fig. 4d). This arises from the further photo-oxidation of aldehyde groups to carboxyl groups, leading to the formation of compound 5BT-COOH. Based on the above ESI-MS results, it can be concluded that 4BT-Py-PTC undergoes a sequential chemical reaction under illumination, as illustrated in Fig. 1a.

To identify the final product of the photochemical transformation, ethanol was added to the irradiated DMF solution of 4BT-Py-PTC to induce recrystallization, affording needle-shaped crystals suitable for single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis. Structural characterization unambiguously confirmed 5BT-COOH as the final photoproduct (Supplementary Figs. 31–33). To further elucidate the dynamic evolution of the photochemical process, time-dependent 1H NMR spectroscopy was employed to monitor the structural variations of the reactant and intermediates during irradiation. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 34, the proton signals (Hj at 9.19 ppm and Hi at 8.58 ppm) assigned to the pyridine moiety of pristine 4BT-Py-PTC gradually shifted upfield and decreased in intensity upon continuous irradiation. Analogous spectral variations were observed for the ethylene proton signals (Hh at 7.92 ppm and Hg at 8.14 ppm) and benzothiazole aromatic proton signals (Ha-d at 8.24, 7.62, 7.70, and 8.36 ppm) of 4BT-Py-PTC, demonstrating the generation of 3BT-Py intermediate. Notably, a characteristic aldehyde proton resonance emerged at 10.48 ppm after 20 min of irradiation, indicating the in-situ generation of the 2BT-CHO intermediate. Upon prolonging the irradiation time to 80 min, new proton resonances assigned to the benzene ring (Hf”‘ at 8.56 ppm and He”‘ at 7.94 ppm) and benzothiazole ring (Ha–d”‘ at 8.13, 7.51, 7.61, and 8.21 ppm) became clearly discernible. These signals were in excellent agreement with the standard 1H NMR spectrum of 5BT-COOH, whose structure was independently verified by single-crystal X-ray diffraction. Therefore, the time-resolved 1H NMR results further provide compelling evidence for the stepwise photochemical mechanism proposed in Fig. 1a.

In addition, the photochemical reaction process was monitored by HPLC using a C18 column with a methanol/water mobile phase. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 35, 4BT-Py-PTC exhibited a retention time of 26.30 min. After 5 min of irradiation, the formation of 3BT-Py was clearly detected at a retention time of 26.03 min. Upon prolonged irradiation for 20 min, the peak intensity of 4BT-Py-PTC markedly decreased, accompanied by the generation of a large amount of 2BT-CHO (retention time: 32.83 min) and a minor amount of 5BT-COOH (retention time: 29.49 min). Further extending the irradiation time to 80 min resulted in the complete consumption of 4BT-Py-PTC and the predominant formation of 5BT-COOH, with an overall conversion yield of approximately 80%. These comprehensive analyses, together with the distinct fluorescence spectra of the red- (3BT-Py), yellow- (2BT-CHO), and blue-emissive (5BT-COOH) precursors (Supplementary Fig. 36), collectively confirm the multi-step photoreaction pathway and consistently explain the observed spectral evolution and color variations in Fig. 3.

To gain more insight into this photoreaction, a theoretical calculation was implemented to get a deeper interpretation of the photoreaction. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 37, the HOMO of 4BT-Py-PTC and 3BT-Py were mainly localized on the benzothiazole moiety, whereas the LUMO were primarily centered on benzene-pyridine conjugates. The obvious separation of HOMO-LUMO was beneficial to decrease the energy gap between the singlet and triplet states to increase the intersystem crossing efficiency. The HOMO–LUMO energy gap (ΔE) follows the order 5BT-COOH (3.99 eV) > 2BT-CHO (3.76 eV) > 4BT-Py-PTC (2.12 eV) > 3BT-Py (1.87 eV), which accounts for the transformation of fluorescence spectra of 4BT-Py-PTC after persistent irradiation, as a narrower gap corresponds to lower-energy electronic transitions and longer emission wavelengths. These findings coincided with the experimental results and validated the suggested reaction mechanism.

Four orthogonal experiments (Supplementary Table 6 and Supplementary Fig. 38) quantify the roles of water and oxygen in the cascade using two kinetic metrics: the time to reach 10% of the maximum intensity (t₁₀%) as a sensitive indicator for the thiocarbonate hydrolysis step, and the half-rise time (t₅₀%) for the overall three-step progression59,60. The corresponding dynamic fluorescence spectra monitored under all four conditions are shown in Supplementary Fig. 39. Comparing conditions with water present versus absent, the t₁₀% values increased in the absence of water (Supplementary Table 6), confirming that water is indispensable for the hydrolysis step. Specifically, the t₁₀% increased from 0.64 min (air + normal DMF) to 0.88 min (air + dry DMF). Notably, the t₅₀% values followed the same trend, further supporting this conclusion: they increased from 8.33 min (air + normal DMF) and 25.87 min (N₂ + normal DMF) to 21.70 min (air + dry DMF) and 31.23 min (N₂ + dry DMF). The prolonged lag phase under anaerobic conditions is evident from the fluorescence spectra in Supplementary Fig. 39. We next compared aerobic versus anaerobic conditions. Under aerobic conditions, the t₁₀% values were measurable, whereas under anaerobic conditions they could not be detected. This is not due to inhibition of the hydrolysis step (which is oxygen-independent), but rather because the absence of oxygen prolongs the lag phase of the downstream oxidative steps, delaying the initial rise of the 426 nm fluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 39c, d). In addition, the t₅₀% values were markedly shorter under aerobic conditions than under anaerobic conditions (Supplementary Table 6). In contrast to the aerobic wet condition (air + normal DMF), the other three orthogonal conditions exhibited negligible fluorescence color evolution: the emission spectra remained largely unchanged throughout the irradiation period, with no detectable emergence of new bands corresponding to the intermediates (3BT-Py, 2BT-CHO) or the final product (5BT-COOH) (Supplementary Fig. 39). Consistently, the CIE coordinates showed only minor fluctuations around the initial values, confirming the absence of the time-gated multicolor progression.

This observation prompted us to investigate whether reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated during the photoreactions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 40a, b, the fluorescence intensity of 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluo-rescein (DCFH) increased significantly upon UV irradiation in the presence of 4BT-Py-PTC under aerobic conditions, indicating the generation of ROS such as singlet oxygen (¹O₂) and/or superoxide radical anion (O₂•⁻). This phenomenon is in good agreement with the work published by Tang et al.28. In contrast, no change was observed under anaerobic conditions (Supplementary Fig. 40c,d). This provides direct evidence that photoexcited 4BT-Py-PTC activates molecular oxygen to form ROS, which then participate in alkene cleavage and aldehyde photooxidation steps. Collectively, these results establish that both water and oxygen are strictly required for the cascade photoreactions to proceed. In detail, water gates the hydrolysis step, while oxygen is activated by photoexcited 4BT-Py-PTC to generate ROS that drive the subsequent oxidative photoreactions.

Time-gated 3D code-generating array

As a proof of concept, the time-gated graded multicolor fluorescence of 4BT-Py-PTC encouraged us to explore its potential for advanced encryption systems with spatiotemporally resolved capabilities. A 3D code-generating array was constructed by patterning 4BT-Py-PTC dissolved in five different solvents (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Movie 1), each exhibiting particular fluorescence evolution under timed UV illumination. The original pattern, displaying four emission colors, was defined as Code 1: grass-green (from DMF and THF) and yellow (from EtOH) and blue and yellowish-orange (from DMSO and ACN, respectively). Owing to the different hydrolysis and photooxidation rates of 4BT-Py-PTC in these solvents, the array dynamically transformed into Code 2, Code 3,…Code N with extended UV irradiation. The encoded information could be read using a smartphone under UV light. Specifically, due to its fast hydrolysis rate, the array showed noticeable color changes after 5 min of irradiation, transforming from Code 1 to Code 2, accompanied by red-to-orange emission (DMF, DMSO, ACN), yellow (THF), and yellowish-orange (EtOH). Upon 50 min of UV exposure, the photooxidation of olefins converted 4BT-Py-PTC in DMF, DMSO, and ACN into blue, cyan, and blue emitters, respectively. Concurrently, systems in EtOH and THF showed yellow and grass-green, together forming Code 3. Further irradiation to 80 min triggered the photooxidation of aldehyde groups, transforming the array into a pattern dominated by blue, yellow, and grass-green fluorescence, thereby generating Code 4. This system successfully realizes a time-gated 3D code-generating array, dynamically and sequentially transforming from Code 1 to Code 4 with increasing UV exposure. By designating only Code 2 as the true information and the others as false, the correct information is accessible solely within a specific irradiation window, while misleading codes are displayed at other times. As a result, the encoded information GZMU was exclusively readable at the specific irradiation time of 5 min. In contrast, the 3D arrays generated at other time points effectively concealed and protected the true information, serving as a role for information self-erasure. The precise spatiotemporal control of 4BT-Py-PTC enables a high-security paradigm where information is conditional. This grants a tailored advantage, as access is restricted to authorized individuals knowing the specific irradiation time required.

Time-gated cryptographic matrices

Building on the solution-based studies, we next developed solid-phase information encryption carriers to further explore the practical application of 4BT-Py-PTC in data encryption, aiming for more stable and versatile encryption materials. To this end, an intriguing polymer gel-based cryptographic matrix was constructed. As shown in Fig. 6 and Supplementary Movie 2, a matrix 8888 was initially assembled from printed DMF-based organogel strips. A subset of these strips was DMF-OGs incorporating 4BT-Py-PTC, whereas the others contained the synthetic precursors 2BT-CHO and 5BT-COOH, respectively, resulting in an initial pattern that exhibited yellow and blue fluorescence under 365 nm UV light. Meanwhile, a matrix 7096 exhibiting uniform yellow fluorescence could also be observed. Importantly, access to the concealed information is time-gated: upon 1 min of UV irradiation, the real information 1046 emerged distinctly as selected strips emitted red light, originating from the UV-induced catalyst-free hydrolysis of 4BT-Py-PTC, while the other strips maintained their original yellow and blue fluorescence, respectively. After 10 min of irradiation, UV-induced catalyst-free alkene photooxidation caused the real information to appear as yellow fluorescence. Therefore, a clearly visible matrix 7096 of misleading information emerged. It was composed of yellow fluorescence (induced by catalyst-free alkene photooxidation) and yellow-green fluorescence (from 2BT-CHO DMF-OGs), set against the blue emission of 5BT-COOH DMF-OGs. Following this, further irradiation (20 min) caused this matrix 7096 to display green fluorescence, thereby more effectively concealing the true information. After a total irradiation of 30 min, the matrix 8888 stabilized into an all-blue fluorescent pattern, resulting from the complete photooxidation of aldehyde groups in both 4BT-Py-PTC and 2BT-CHO within DMF-OGs. The system’s operation progresses through a defined sequence: transient information revelation, irreversible reset to the initial appearance, and final permanent locking. This multi-step process establishes a high-security, spatiotemporal encryption mechanism.

Schematic illustration of the matrix composed of DMF-OGs containing 4BT-Py-PTC, 2BT-CHO, and 5BT-COOH, respectively, and representative fluorescent photographs of the time-gated cryptographic matrix under 365 nm UV illumination. Scale bar: 1 cm.

Integrating precise fluorescence control via timed UV exposure, we next developed a novel organogel-based cryptographic matrix that provides a robust platform for selective encryption and dynamic multi-channel information display. The cryptographic matrix was composed of the following elements: a THF-OG (number 1), a DMSO-OG (number 3), and a DMF-OG (number 5), all containing 4BT-Py-PTC; two DMF-OGs incorporating 2BT-CHO (numbers 7 and 9); and four DMF-OGs loaded with 5BT-COOH (numbers 2, 4, 6 and 8) (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Movie 3). Initially, the cryptographic matrix exhibited yellow fluorescence in the numbers 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 and blue fluorescence in the numbers 2, 4, 6, 8. Due to the different hydrolysis rates of 4BT-Py-PTC in various organogels, UV-triggered hydrolysis produced red fluorescence in a distinct temporal sequence. We harnessed this differential kinetics to design the temporal sequence of the red emission as a genuine password carrier, which functioned as a precise time-gated decryption key. Consequently, when all organogel units were exposed to UV light concurrently, the encoded information was decoded sequentially, strictly governed by this built-in chemical clock. Concretely, number 1 first displayed red fluorescence after 20 s. Number 5 followed suit within the next 1 min. Finally, number 3 turned red after 3 min of irradiation, thereby revealing the complete and correct information: 1 → 5 → 3. Meanwhile, the remaining organogel-based numbers retained their original fluorescence. After 10 min of further irradiation, numbers 1, 3 and 5 turned yellow, thereby restoring the initial pattern of yellow/yellow-green numbers (1, 3, 5, 7, 9) alongside the blue-fluorescing numbers (2, 4, 6, 8). As this restored pattern was indistinguishable from the original, it effectively concealed the confidential information. With the extension of UV irradiation, the DMF-OGs containing 2BT-CHO, along with the other organogels containing 4BT-Py-PTC, progressively transitioned from yellow to blue fluorescence, ultimately yielding a final cryptographic matrix composed entirely of blue-emitting numbers. This dynamic decryption process, governed by sequential hydrolysis and photooxidations of 4BT-Py-PTC where each step distinctly modulates its ESIPT behavior, demonstrates a robust time-gated encryption mechanism. It showcases the capability of our organogel-based matrix to integrate multi-channel programming and selective information revelation within a stable, multi-fluorescence background, thereby driving high-security encryption systems with built-in spatiotemporal resolution for information storage and anti-counterfeiting.

Discussion

In summary, we developed a novel photofluorochromic system based on 4BT-Py-PTC, which exhibited time-gated, graded multicolor fluorescence under programmed UV irradiation. This dynamic behavior arose from three sequential, catalyst-free photoreactions in air: thiocarbonate hydrolysis, alkene oxidative cleavage, and aldehyde photooxidation. Specifically, UV irradiation progressively transformed the yellow-emitting 4BT-Py-PTC into distinct red-fluorescent 3BT-Py within 5 min, then to the yellow-emitting 2BT-CHO by 20 min, and finally to the blue-emissive 5BT-COOH after 80 min of UV irradiation. Remarkably, when integrated into organogels, the fluorescence evolution showed enhanced spatiotemporal programmability due to network confinement—as demonstrated by a DMF (high-polarity)-based organogel that sequentially emitted red, yellow, and blue light at controlled intervals of 1, 10, and 30 min, respectively. In contrast, when a low-polarity solvent such as THF was used, the corresponding red, yellow, and blue fluorescence transitions occurred significantly earlier, at just 20 s, 1 min, and 20 min, respectively. Leveraging this controllable output, we demonstrated two advanced encryption modes: (i) solvent-based, time-gated 3D codes, and (ii) organogel-based cryptography featuring multi-level control (reveal-conceal-erase) and temporally keyed selective decryption. This system functioned as a high-security temporal cipher, granting access based solely on illumination time without any catalyst or harsh conditions. Overall, this catalyst-free, time-gated strategy demonstrates a proof-of-concept for a material-design paradigm where spatiotemporally programmed encryption can serve as a foundational strategy for next-generation protection.

Methods

Detailed procedures for the fabrication of the encryption platforms are described below.

Preparation of time-gated 3D code-generating array

4BT-Py-PTC (100 mg) was dissolved in 25 mL of each of five different solvents: DMF, DMSO, ACN, EtOH, and THF. The resulting solutions were sonicated in the dark for approximately 2 h to ensure complete dissolution and homogeneity. The 3D code array was then assembled by dispensing each of the five precursor solutions into separate, clear glass vials. The entire array was placed under a 365 nm UV lamp at a fixed distance to ensure uniform irradiation.

Fabrication of time-gated cryptographic matrices

The cryptographic matrices were assembled using various 3D-printed organogel units. Each unit was loaded with specific fluorogens, including 4BT-Py-PTC, 2BT-CHO, or 5BT-COOH, to generate the desired initial fluorescent pattern (e.g., yellow or blue). These organogel units were spatially arranged into a pre-programmed matrix structure, and the assembled matrix was positioned under a 365 nm UV light source at a strictly fixed vertical distance.

Computational details

The DFT calculations were performed using the Gaussian 16 software package. The B3LYP exchange-correlation functional was employed, and the 6–311 G(d,p) basis set was used for all atoms. The HOMO and LUMO orbitals were visualized using GaussView 6.0.16.
