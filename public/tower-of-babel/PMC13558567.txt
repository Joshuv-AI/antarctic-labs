EMG-based hand gesture recognition using multi-scale deep residual network with SE-module
Authors: Ramkumar Sivasakthivel, Rajendran Thavasimuthu, Manikandan Rajagopal, P. Subhashini, V. Jeyakrishnan, Salman Arafath Mohammed, Mohamed Abbas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the past few years, Hand Gesture Recognition (HGR) utilizing EMG data has gained significant attention for improving human-machine interaction. However, effectively modeling multi-scale temporal variations and discriminative channel-wise features in EMG signals remains challenging. Although recent deep learning-based HGR methods have shown promising performance, many struggle to jointly capture multi-scale temporal dependencies and channel-wise feature relevance inherent in EMG signals. Deep learning (DL) methods offer several advantages over Machine Learning (ML) methods, including promising classification performance. In this research, a DL-based Multi-Scale Deep Residual Network (DRN) with an SE model that utilizes EMG signals to recognize hand gestures is proposed. The proposed architecture integrates multi-scale residual learning with squeeze-and-excitation–based channel recalibration, enabling the network to learn both temporal patterns at different scales and gesture-specific feature importance. Initially, the EMG-EPN-612 dataset is utilized to train and evaluate the research model. The research model includes data collection, preprocessing, feature extraction process and classification tasks. Standard EMG preprocessing, including moving average filtering, min–max normalization, and sliding-window segmentation, is applied, followed by gesture classification using a Multi-Scale DRN integrated with an SE module to capture temporal patterns and channel-wise dependencies. The dataset is divided into two parts, one of which contained 75% of the data for training and the other 25% for testing. The improved performance is attributed to the proposed model’s ability to effectively capture discriminative multi-scale temporal features and adaptively emphasize informative EMG channels. The proposed model not only achieved 99.24% accuracy, 99.15% precision, 99.17% f1-score, 99.10% specificity, and 99.20% recall, but also overcomes compared models.

Introduction

HGR systems have emerged as a significant research focus in recent years due to their wide applicability in human-machine interaction. Nonverbal communication is essential in our daily existence, since it transmits approximately 65% of messages, whereas verbal communication accounts for less than 35% of interaction. Gestures can be categorized into 3 main types: hand & arm gestures, which include hand poses, sign language recognition, and entertainment applications; head and facial gestures, such as nodding, head shaking, eye gaze direction, mouth movements for speech, and winking; and body gestures, which involve full-body movements1. Efficient HGR techniques facilitate strong human-computer interaction, providing alternatives to conventional devices such as mouse and keyboards. Hand gestures play an important role in everyday activities, and automatic HGR is critical for authentic nonverbal communication.

The HGR system can be categorized as vision-based, sensor-based, and hybrid-based systems according to the method of capturing movement of the hands, as seen in Fig. 1. Vision-based HGR systems provide a simpler interaction experience since users are not required to put on any challenging devices. These devices have broader uses in outdoor environments due to their user-friendliness2. However, the sensor-based method needs the utilization of sensory glove devices, inertial measurement units, Wi-Fi signal data, and EMG electrodes to assess hand, finger, and arm movements for precise assessment of finger flexion, hand orientation, and motion. The technique employing a data glove attains enhanced precision, quick responsiveness, and higher mobility. The hybrid technology, which combines vision with glove-based methods, uses sensor data to enhance visual data quality through its sensor data integration. The technology has not been successfully implemented because its complete system requires both high expenses and substantial processing power3. The vision-based and hybrid HGR systems provide usability benefits and improved visual data quality, but applications that need precise hand movement detection in real time should use sensor-based systems.

The HGR systems which use ultrasonic IMU or EMG-based methods work best with various applications. Sensor-based systems, particularly EMG-based methods, provide higher accuracy and responsiveness in tracking hand and finger movements. The system achieves better performance because it does not depend on vision-based systems which struggle with dim lighting and blocked views and complicated backgrounds to identify intricate hand movements. EMG sensors function effectively in real-time systems because they need to detect hand movements with high accuracy. EMG signals remain the predominant method for the recognition of static hand motions. The ability of EMGs to define muscle activation for HGR application made them the preferred standard for hand motion recognition. The sensors development based on EMG signals has led to products such as G-ForcePro and Myo armband4. The body generates EMG as a bioelectrical signal during both upper and lower limb movements. Specific features of the EMG signal, such as the Root Mean Square (RMS), Mean Absolute Value (MAV), Zero Crossings (ZC), and Waveform Length (WL), exhibit measurable correlations with the degree of finger flexion and muscle contraction. The relationship between EMG signal features and finger movements leads to the conclusion that the EMG signal can be used to detect hand movements. The finger movements produce separate EMG signals through which each movement pattern generates distinct EMG signals that contain different characteristics. The recorded EMG signal must be analyzed and assessed to uncover the exact pattern of movement data5.

The unpredictable nature of EMG signals together with their random behavior creates difficulties for researchers in recording steady muscle contraction signals. Surface EMG signal recording, apart from being over the skin, involves the electrical activity of thousands of muscle fibers, which contributes to a high variation in the signal. The amplitude of the EMG signal shows a direct connection to muscle contraction strength during static assessments of both lower and upper body movements. The relationship between these two variables possesses a non-linear character. The researchers employed ML and DL methods to successfully differentiate between different movement patterns which they extracted from EMG signals to solve the existing conflicts.

The method of recognizing hand gestures by means of EMG signals typically comprises data acquisition, pre-processing, feature extraction, and classification, as depicted in Fig. 2.

An EMG-based HGR system typically runs through a series of phases where each stage plays a critical role. The first stage, data acquisition, is the one that records the EMG signals through the sensors. Data preprocessing then takes place, which consists of filtering and normalizing the signals. Data segmentation splits the signal into significant time periods, and feature extraction locates the most important properties for classification. In the end, the classification model predicts hand gestures and performance evaluation measures the system’s reliability and efficiency.

Various ML classification methods have been specifically designed to tackle the problem of EMG variations, and they have achieved different levels of limited success. Adaptive algorithms have been proposed to support the existing HGR systems or even to make them more efficient8. In the last few years, signal processing has benefited enormously from DL. Recent studies have shown that DL techniques are highly effective for analyzing EMG signals in hand gesture recognition. DL enables extraction of complex patterns from EMG data, improving gesture classification accuracy and robustness. While DL has also succeeded in fields like computer vision and audio classification9, its application in EMG-based HGR is particularly valuable for precise hand and finger movement recognition.

The main goal of the real-time HGR model is to perform gesture classification and recognition. Even though there has been significant progress in HGR based on EMG signals, difficulties like signal variability, non-linearity, and the demand for solid feature extraction methods still exist. Conventional ML techniques demand considerable feature engineering and still have problems with generalization between different users. Although there is an increasing interest in using EMG signals for hand gesture recognition, the current methods often have difficulties in dealing with signals that are noisy, non-stationary, and highly varying. Numerous methods are based on thorough feature engineering or do not account for multi-scale temporal and spatial patterns, which restricts their robustness and generality. Real-time issues like electrode movement, muscle exhaustion, and differences in conditions between sessions are factors that have a negative impact on classification accuracy and point out the necessity of models capable of successfully and reliably extracting characteristic features from various conditions.

DL, especially sophisticated architectures such as DRNs, can enhance both classification accuracy and system robustness10,11. The main novelty of this study is the fusion of a Multi-Scale DRN with an SE module for EMG-based hand gesture recognition. EMG signals possess the characteristics of being non-stationary, noisy, and extremely variable in different gestures and subjects. The application of a multi-scale DRN results in an efficient extraction of both fine-grained and global temporal-spatial features of EMG signals, allowing the detection of very subtle gesture-specific patterns. The SE module also contributes to the model’s ability to distinguish by recalibrating the channel features adaptively, thus highlighting the signals that carry information and at the same time minimizing the channels carrying irrelevant or noisy signals. This combination of the proposed model leads to the extraction of significant EMG patterns and therefore the classification performance can be better than the existing methods.

The key aim of this study is to develop an effective DL method for HGR that not only enhances the accuracy of the system but also resolves the difficulties addressed. The proposed model effectively uses a multiscale DRN with an SE mechanism that relies on EMG signals to increase recognition accuracy, make it robust across different users, increase classification accuracy, and minimize the need for manual feature extraction. This study is combining multi-scale DRN with SE layers for EMG-based HGR, explicitly handling sensor noise, multi-user variability, and real-time performance, while reducing the need for manual feature extraction. Related works

This review showcases a literature analysis of various current HGR models utilizing EMG signals. Numerous ML and DL techniques, such as CNN, RNN, LSTM, and hybrid models, have been investigated in this review. The comparative analysis of these techniques in Table 1 highlights their strengths and weaknesses. In12, a hybrid RNN architecture that integrated LSTM and Dense layers was implemented. The model was applied to EMG signals collected over a period of 20 s with a specific arm EMG device, one dataset corresponding to each gesture. The preprocessing procedure used a moving average filter to lower the level of random noise and at the same time maintain the original signal. The accuracy of 87 ± 7% was reached by the model in validation performed in real time.

In13, the transfer learning model was applied for hand gesture signal classification using EMG signals. The Myo Armband dataset was created from the NinaPro database and comprised 10 disabled subjects. The DL networks employing 3 distinct modalities as inputs, namely spectrograms, raw EMG, and CWT, were evaluated on the datasets. BN was utilized to quicken training and enhance model stability by preserving a normalized distribution of hidden layer activations. The CWT-based ConvNet attained an offline accuracy of 98.31% for seven gestures among 17 participants.

The regular stability of wrist EMG signals for HGR was examined in14. Different classification methods, among them the novel MIDA, were applied to assess and lessen the spontaneous EMG changes. The EMG data were collected from 12 healthy subjects. The raw EMG recordings were filtered using a third-order Butterworth high-pass filter. The classifiers based on wrist EMG showed mean classification accuracies of 93.8% to 95.7%.

In15, an approach for gesture recognition was presented based on sEMG and an RNN model. The Myo wristband was utilized to record the sEMG signals of 21 different hand gestures performed by 13 subjects over a span of 15 s each. A specific design for the RNN was made such that it could output the probability distribution for the different classes at each time step right after the gesture was detected. The results of the experiments showed that the predicted accuracy was around 89.6%. However, the non-availability of the Myo armband and the model’s high dependency on the individual wearing positions would limit its use in real-time scenarios.

In16, a classification system based on CNN was presented, which classified hand gestures from raw EMG signals. The manual feature extraction was not required as the raw EMG data were used directly. The convolutional layer with 100 filters was enough, and the Adam optimizer was able to reach the best accuracy with fewer training epochs. The research also incorporated a simplified pre-processing phase of the classifier, which made the feature extraction procedure unnecessary. The mean accuracy across all motion ranges is between 0.77 and 0.93.

EMG-based classifiers for hand and finger gestures, utilizing fixed electrode placement and ML techniques, were created in17. Eighteen characteristics were utilized to construct individualized classifiers for ten gestures using an ANN, an SVM, an RF, and an LR. EMG signals were recorded from 3 channels, and 6 time-domain features were extracted from every channel. Data segmentation was achieved through the use of an overlapped sliding window. The ANN-based classifier attains 0.940 of average accuracy in gesture classification.

A hybrid Bi-LSTM and CNN architecture for hand movement classification utilizing sEMG signals was presented in18. Initially, the CNN layers collect deep features from sEMG signals, after which these feature maps were analyzed by the Bi-LSTM to capture sequential information in both directions. The model underwent training and evaluation using five benchmark datasets. The UCI Gesture dataset demonstrated a similar result, with an accuracy of 93.48%. A constraint of the model was the necessity for more innovation to improve performance on the BioPatRec DB2 dataset.

A study on Surface EMG-based HGR utilizing Temporal Convolutional Networks for classification was given in19. The model was assessed using data from the Ninapro database. The architecture employed temporal causal convolutions, with class labels determined through either an attention mechanism or an average over time. In a real-time testing experiment, the classification accuracy was significantly low when a model was provided with 200 ms segments.

A CNN utilizing Transfer Learning for HGR based on Surface EMG was introduced in20. A CNN-based source network was developed and trained to function as a universal feature extraction model for gesture EMG signals. Two target networks, CNN+LSTM and CNN-only, were built using the same CNN framework as the feature extraction network. Experiments were performed on three distinct target gesture datasets utilizing both Transfer Learning (TL) and Non-TL methodologies. The CNN+LSTM network attained a transfer learning accuracy between 93.73% and 97.34%.

The decoding of hand gestures utilizing sEMG signals obtained from a Myo-armband sensor, with the application of ML techniques, was executed in21. The collection has 6,822 segments, representing 7 categories of hand movements. Band-pass filters were employed during the preprocessing phase to eliminate noise and standardize the data. The classification approach employed ML algorithms, specifically RF, SVM with logistic and linear regression, k-nearest Neighbors and RBF kernels. The RF classifier attained an accuracy of over 95% in comparison to alternative methods.

In22, a deep network known as DeepTPA-Net, which utilized the multi-channel sEMG signal for HGR, was introduced. The DeepTPA-Net utilized a ResNet50 architecture for automatic feature extraction and a novel triple attention block was added. The triple attention block employed temporal, spatial, and channel attention modules together to extract the important features for HGR. The model was assessed on CapgMyo DB-a, NinaPro DB2, NinaPro DB1, Csl-hdemg, and SeNic. The model attained a 98.67% recognition accuracy.

In23, three DNN models were utilized to discriminate the EMG data obtained from seven different motions. For classification, three models were made: a 4-layer DNN, an 8-layer DNN, and a 5-layer CNN. Besides this, five optimizers were tested for every model: Adam, Nadam, Adamax, Adagrad, and AdaDelta. To evaluate the performance of models, an existing EMG dataset was used. The performance of the four-layer DNN was evaluated with 95% accuracy.

A unique DNN utilizing the SFDN dataset for the categorization of sEMG signals in hand movement recognition was introduced in24. During the preprocessing stage, denoising and normalization were performed. The effectiveness of the method was demonstrated by comparing it to conventional learning algorithms, including the Decision Tree, KNN, SVM, Multinomial LR, RF, Naïve Bayes, XGBoost, and Multi-Layer Perceptron. The model achieved exceptional classification accuracies of 99.63% (UCI), 95.56% (Ninapro DB6), and 96.12 (Mendeley datasets), respectively.

A DNN utilizing the Hilbert Huang transform for a hand gesture classification system based on EMG signals was created in25. Wavelet-based noise reduction algorithms are employed to isolate the significant signal from background noise during the preprocessing stage. The extraction of features based on Empirical Mode Decomposition was conducted. The model attained 98.5% of the average classification rate. EMG signals were obtained from both the FM dataset and the HG dataset for testing and training the model’s performance.

The CNN&LSTM model for the real-time detection of five hand gestures utilizing EMG signals was presented in26. An EMG-EPN-612 dataset, comprising EMG signal measurements from 612 participants, was utilized for training and testing purposes. The feature extraction approach employed many blocks of max-pooling and parallel convolutions. A post-processing approach was integrated to improve the precision of HGR models and increase their adaptability to fluctuations in EMG signals. The model with post-processing attained 90.55% of a mean recognition accuracy. Table 1 highlights the strengths and limitations of existing models.

Despite the promising performance of existing EMG-based HGR models such as CNN–LSTM, RNN–LSTM, DeepTPA-Net, and transfer learning-based CNNs, several limitations remain. Many approaches focus either on spatial feature extraction or single-scale temporal modeling, without explicitly learning multi-scale spatiotemporal dependencies across EMG channels. Additionally, real-time applicability is often affected by sensor drift, muscle fatigue, and electrode placement variations. The attention-based models DeepTPA-Net face limitations in their ability to weight features because they fail to replicate both multi-scale temporal patterns and spatial relationships between different EMG channels. The research establishes a Multi-Scale Deep Residual Network which uses Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) attention to solve existing research problems by enabling simultaneous analysis of time-dependent changes and inter-channel relationships. The researchers use moving average filtering together with min-max normalization and sliding window segmentation to strengthen your system’s capability to handle noise while boosting its ability to recognize gestures in real-time.

Materials and methods

The proposed research method provides a DL-based HGR model through the use of EMG signals, which is intended to make human-machine interaction better. The workflow of a proposed methodology is illustrated in Fig. 3. The novelty of the proposed Multi-Scale DRN–SE architecture lies in its task-specific integration of parallel multi-scale residual convolutional paths with SE-based channel attention for EMG signals. Unlike existing DRN- or SE-based methods, the proposed design simultaneously models short- and long-term temporal dependencies while adaptively emphasizing informative EMG channels, enabling robust gesture recognition without manual feature engineering. The proposed model utilizes the EMG-EPN-612 dataset that includes various hand gesture signals obtained through EMG sensors. In order to get high-quality input data, the preprocessing stage is implemented, which includes filtering, normalization, and segmentation. During the filtering process, a moving average filter is applied to minimize noise and artifacts while keeping the important features of the signal intact. Subsequently, min-max Normalization is utilized to adjust the EMG signals within a uniform range between 0 and 1, which guarantees the same scale for various gestures. A sliding window approach is adopted to classify the data in an efficient manner, which means the continuous EMG signals are split into smaller time windows that are still large enough to keep the temporal dependencies necessary for accuracy in classification.

The processed signals are then input to the Multi-Scale DRN, combined with an SE module for gesture classification. The Multi-Scale DRN structure adopts dilated convolutions that increase the receptive field but keep the power of computation low thus the architecture could detect both short-term and long-term dependencies in the EMG signals. The gradient vanishing issue is eliminated and the training gets more stable. Thus, deep features are extracted due to the residual connections used in the network. Moreover, the multi-scale feature extraction method helps the model to differentiate among various gesture patterns in different frequency bands. The SE module has the effect of making classification better by adaptively readjusting the importance of the various feature channels that are feeding in, thus bringing the relevant information and at the same time suppressing the less significant data. The feature representation established by the model turns out to be very discriminative, and gesture recognition, therefore, is more accurate.

The dataset for performance assessment is split into 75% for training and 25% for testing, thus making it possible to evaluate the model’s ability to generalize in a balanced way. The classification performance is assessed with the use of important metrics like accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and F1-score. The superior performance of the system can be attributed to the powerful multi-scale feature extraction, effective noise reduction techniques, and the adaptive feature improvement via the SE module. The advancements in these elements not only make the proposed system effective but also reliable for HGR applications in the real world making it possible for better human-machine interaction in fields like prosthetics, rehabilitation, and virtual control systems.

Data preprocessing

After the collection of the EMG data, the signals underwent preprocessing in order to function as inputs for the suggested model. Preprocessing is an essential step in the signal analysis of EMG, as raw EMG signals were irregular and challenging to analyze. The preprocessing allows for the extraction of valuable information from a raw EMG signal.

To minimize signal noise and extract its envelope the moving average filter is initially employed, which is essential for neural network models. It is the predominant filter in the processing of digital signals owing to its simplicity of implementation. Considering its simplistic design, the filter is optimum for minimizing noise while maintaining the integrity of the signal, making it an excellent filter for signals that are encoded in the time domain. This makes it possible to extract significant attributes from the EMG signals in the temporal domain, at the same time keeping the computational cost low12.

After the filtering and normalization process the second preprocessing stage is where the measured variables on different scales are adjusted to a uniform range. The min-max normalization technique is applied in this research24. The sliding window approach was employed to divide EMG data into segments, thus allowing an efficient analysis of the time-related features. A duration of 300 ms was selected to correspond to the entire muscle activation cycles for the gestures and at the same time, to keep the temporal resolution intact. A stride of 40 ms (overlap of approximately 87%) was used to keep making connections between the adjacent segments, to raise the number of training samples and to lower the chance of missing transitional features.

An energy level of 18% of the maximum EMG energy was used as a criterion to eliminate low-activity or noisy segments. Windows that exceeded this threshold were categorized, while their lower-energy counterparts were simply replaced with zeros. These parameters were found through preliminary tests with the aim of maximizing instance recognition and classification performance. The model makes sure only the segments of the signals that are useful for extracting features, thereby boosting the robustness and total effectiveness of the classification model28.

EMG signals are characterized by nonlinearity, high noise sensitivity, inter-subject variability, and complex temporal dynamics arising from muscle activation patterns. Gesture-related information in EMG signals exists at multiple temporal scales, ranging from short-duration muscle firing patterns to longer-term activation sequences. Conventional single-scale CNN or sequential models often struggle to simultaneously capture these characteristics while maintaining robustness in real-time applications. Therefore, an architecture capable of modeling multi-scale temporal dependencies, ensuring stable deep feature learning, and adaptively emphasizing informative EMG channels is required for accurate hand gesture recognition.

Multi-ScaleDRN with SE module-based classification

This approach, based on DL not only performs classification but also extracts features, thus minimizing the necessity for separate techniques of features extraction. The SE mechanism in the DL model enhances important signal characteristics and effectively captures features at multiple scales. The combination of these two steps leads to higher model efficiency, better recognition accuracy, and more reliable classification of EMG signals for hand gesture recognition.

A Multi-Scale DRN is a DL model that is capable of capturing the features at different scales, as well as solving the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks. It combines multi-scale feature extraction with residual connections, so the model can learn both fine and coarse details in an efficient way. Gradient flow is supported very much by residual connections, which in turn make training of deep networks more stable and effective. The SE model is a mechanism for attention that operates at the channel level and enhances feature representations by learning the significance of each channel29.

The method operates by initially compressing spatial details via global average pooling, subsequently revealing the significant channels with the help of a small neural network that uses attention weights and alters the feature maps correspondingly. The network’s capacity to emphasize significant characteristics while reducing interference is enhanced by this mechanism. The SE block aims to redistribute the weights of the channel features and alleviate the vanishing gradients problem that occurs when the network layers become deeper. It computes the interconnections among channels and adjusts the allocation of weights between them30.

The model initiates feature extraction from EMG signals utilizing the 2-D Conv layer with 25 × 1 Conv kernel, succeeded by 2-D Convolutional (ConvB) employing a 15 × 1 Conv kernel. A subsequent layer of parallel 2-D ConvB with varying kernel sizes is employed for extracting multi-scale characteristics from diverse leads. Every parallel feature derived from the 2-D ConvB is transformed into a 1-D feature, and a 1-D ConvB with an identical kernel size is employed for additional feature extraction. An average pooling layer is subsequently employed following the 1-D ConvB. In the end, information from various scales is concatenated, and a fully connected layer functions as the classifier to provide the output. As the feature-length diminishes, the convolution kernel size is decreased to capture fine-grained information. This model employs three distinct sizes of 2D Conv kernels and 1 parallel 1D Conv kernel for classification purposes. The comprehensive model structure is depicted in Fig. 4.

Computational complexity and real-time feasibility

The proposed Multi-Scale DRN + SE model is designed to be computationally efficient while maintaining high accuracy for EMG-based hand gesture recognition. The model has approximately 2.3 million parameters and requires ~ 4.8 GFLOPs for a single forward pass. On the experimental platform (Core i7 CPU, 12 GB RAM), the average inference time per sample was ~ 12 ms, which is suitable for real-time applications. The lightweight architecture, combined with multi-scale feature extraction and SE attention, ensures that the model can be deployed on wearable devices or embedded systems with moderate computational resources. Memory usage and inference speed were found to be compatible with real-time HGR tasks, including prosthetics control, rehabilitation systems, and human-computer interaction, without significant latency.

However, this study has a few limitations. At first, the size of the data set is restricted, and just a limited number of hand gestures were used. Secondly, the present assessment does not include the real-life variations in EMG signals that occur due to the movement of electrodes, muscle tiredness, or differences between sessions, which could possibly weaken the model’s robustness.

The future research will be directed towards broadening the assessment to incorporate larger, varied, and cross-disciplinary datasets while including noisy and real-world conditions to evaluate generalizability and improve practical applicability. Moreover, the variation in the gestures offered and the implementation of adaptive strategies for managing electrode movement and muscle fatigue will be the next focus towards further enhancing the performance and robustness of the model.

Conclusions

This research proposed a DL-based HGR model that leveraged a Multi-Scale DRN with an SE mechanism for accurate HGR using EMG signals. The EMG-EPN-612 dataset was collected to train and evaluate the proposed research model. The EMG signals were preprocessed through filtering, normalization, and segmentation using a moving average filter, min-max normalization, and the Sliding Window approach, respectively. In the classification process, a DL-based Multi-Scale DRN was combined with an SE model to classify the gestures accurately. The dataset was split into training and testing sets, with 75% used for training and 25% for testing. The proposed model was found to achieve an exceptional accuracy of 99.24%, precision of 99.15%, F1-score of 99.17%, specificity of 99.10%, and recall of 99.20%. These results were compared and validated against other models discussed in the literature review, and the research model was observed to outperform all other models. In the future, the dataset size can be expanded to enhance model robustness and generalization. Additionally, the number of hand gestures can be increased to cover a wider range of movements.
