Noninvasive Gas Exchange Determinants of Exercise Hemodynamics in Non‐Severe Pulmonary Hypertension Associated With Interstitial Lung Disease
Authors: Chebly Dagher, Niala Moallem, Garrett Fiscus, Brett Carollo, Kristen Swanson, Harrison W. Farber, Raj Parikh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLevel 3 cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) can unmask the dynamic burden of pulmonary vascular disease (PVD), while submaximal exercise testing offers a simpler, noninvasive alternative. Emerging markers such as a gas‐exchange derived estimate of pulmonary vascular capacitance (GXCAP) and ventilatory efficiency (VE/VCO2) may reflect PVD and correlate with invasive hemodynamics. Level 3 CPET has been used to stratify PH‐ILD patients into those with exercise‐induced increases in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVRD) and those with static PVR (PVRS). This study evaluated whether GXCAP and VE/VCO2 measured during submaximal testing could identify non‐severe PH‐ILD patients with exercise‐induced increases in PVR. GXCAP and VE/VCO2 were retrospectively analyzed in 16 non‐severe PH‐ILD patients who underwent both submaximal exercise testing and invasive Level 3 CPET. Patients were previously categorized as having dynamic (PVRD) or stable (PVRS) PVR responses. Between‐group differences were assessed using unpaired t‐tests, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis evaluated the ability of each variable to identify the PVRD phenotype. Among the 16 patients included in the analysis, 6 were classified as PVRS and 10 as PVRD. Baseline characteristics, including peak VO2, were similar between groups. Mean GXCAP was significantly higher in PVRS (471 ± 85 mL·mmHg) than in PVRD (311 ± 38 mL·mmHg, p < 0.01), while VE/VCO2 was lower in PVRS (33 ± 2 vs 37 ± 2.5, p < 0.01). ROC analysis demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance for GXCAP (AUC 0.98) and good discrimination for VE/VCO2 (AUC 0.90). The GXCAP/VE/VCO2 ratio performed similarly (AUC 0.98). These findings suggest that GXCAP and VE/VCO2 obtained during submaximal testing may serve as practical, noninvasive tools for identifying exercise‐induced pulmonary vascular impairment and aiding risk stratification in patients with non‐severe PH‐ILD. However, validation in larger prospective cohorts is needed.

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) encompasses several hemodynamic subtypes. In this study, we specifically focus on precapillary pulmonary hypertension, defined by mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg, pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) ≤ 15 mmHg, and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) > 2 Wood units (WU), measured by right heart catheterization (RHC) [1]. Severe PH‐ILD, defined by PVR > 5 WU, is associated with poor outcomes [2]. Treatment options for this population remain limited, with inhaled treprostinil being the only currently approved therapy, based on results of the INCREASE trial [3]. However, a pre‐specified analysis from the trial found that patients with baseline PVR < 4 WU did not achieve a statistically significant improvement in 6‐min walk distance (6MWD), the trial's primary outcome [4]. This highlights the treatment challenge in non‐severe PH‐ILD, particularly for those with PVR < 4 WU, in whom the benefit of therapy remains uncertain.

Level 3 Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing (CPET) is increasingly utilized as a diagnostic and management tool for PH, as it can unmask the dynamic burden of pulmonary vascular disease (PVD) [5, 6, 7] and provide insights into disease severity and functional impairment [7]. Level 3 CPET is an invasive protocol that incorporates simultaneous right heart catheterization during exercise, enabling direct measurement of pulmonary pressures, vascular resistance, and right ventricular response under physiologic stress [5, 6, 7]. Notably, Parikh et al. used Level 3 CPET to stratify patients with non‐severe PH‐ILD into two groups: those whose pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) increased during exercise (PVRD) and those whose PVR remained unchanged (PVRS) [8]. They demonstrated that the PVRD group experienced significant functional and hemodynamic improvement with inhaled treprostinil, whereas the PVRS group remained clinically stable off therapy, defining a potential role of Level 3 CPET in identifying non‐severe PH‐ILD patients who may respond to targeted treatment [8].

Alternatively, submaximal exercise testing provides a simpler, noninvasive approach that might still offer meaningful physiological information compared to Level 3 CPET. One such parameter is GXCAP, a gas exchange–derived estimate of pulmonary vascular capacitance (PVCAP). While PVCAP is calculated invasively using stroke volume (SV) and pulmonary arterial pressure (Ppa), GXCAP has been shown to correlate strongly with invasively measured PVCAP, supporting its potential as a reliable noninvasive tool for detecting PH, particularly in ILD [9, 10]. Another informative variable, the minute ventilation to carbon dioxide production ratio (VE/VCO2), reflects breathing efficiency and is closely associated with PVR, serving as a surrogate marker of pulmonary vascular disease burden [11]. Despite the promise of these measures, effective noninvasive methods to accurately assess the pulmonary vascular response to exercise remain limited.

In this study, we evaluated whether GXCAP and VE/VCO2, assessed during sub‐maximal exercise testing, could reliably identify non‐severe PH‐ILD patients who develop exercise‐induced increases in PVR.

Methods

We conducted a secondary analysis of the same 16 patients with World Health Organization (WHO) Group 3 PH‐ILD previously described by Parikh et al., all of whom had a PVR ≤ 5 WU by right heart catheterization (RHC) and were, thus, classified as non‐severe PH‐ILD [8]. Diagnosis of ILD was identified by diffuse parenchymal lung disease on computed tomography (CT) and pulmonary function tests (PFTs).

All patients underwent Level 3 CPET, defined in our Introduction as CPET with simultaneous RHC, using an upright seated cycle ergometer with breath‐by‐breath gas‐exchange measurements. Continuous monitoring included ECG, arterial oxygen saturation, expired gas analysis, invasive blood pressures, and pulmonary pressures. Exercise workload was determined using a standardized ramp protocol individualized to each subject's predicted maximal capacity (age, sex, height, and spirometry), targeting a symptom‐limited duration of 8–12 min in accordance with CPET guidelines. Testing was conducted with the patients breathing ambient air.

Based on hemodynamic response, patients were stratified into two groups: those with a dynamic increase in exercise PVR (PVRD), and those with a static exercise PVR (PVRS). Submaximal CPET variables were then evaluated to determine whether noninvasive markers could estimate physiological burden. Additional exercise parameters, including resting oxygen saturation (SpO2), nadir exercise SpO2, and peak oxygen consumption (VO2), were collected when available and compared between groups. GXCAP was treated as a gas‐exchange–derived surrogate of PVCAP and calculated as oxygen pulse × end‐tidal CO2 (PETCO2), consistent with prior validation studies [12]. VE/VCO2 was measured as the slope from rest to anaerobic threshold, identified using the V‐slope method.

Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, ILD subtype, use of supplemental oxygen, and anti‐fibrotic therapy, were recorded. PFT parameters, such as predicted forced vital capacity (FVC) and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO), were also collected. Key hemodynamic and clinical parameters, resting and exercise mPAP and PVR, 6MWD, and estimated right ventricular systolic pressure (eRVSP) were also assessed.

Statistical analyzes were performed using independent Student's t‐tests for normally distributed variables and Wilcoxon Rank Sum tests for non‐normally distributed data. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was used. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the discriminative performance of GXCAP and VE/VCO2 in identifying PVRD. Values are presented as mean ± standard deviation. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board.

Results

Level 3 CPET stratified the 16 patients into two groups. The PVRS group (n = 6) had a resting PVR of 3.12 ± 0.12 and an exercise PVR of 3.54 ± 0.35. The PVRD group (n = 10) had a resting PVR of 2.80 ± 0.18, which increased to 5.54 ± 0.51 during exercise [8] (Table 2).

Baseline clinical, functional, and hemodynamic characteristics evaluated included age, sex distribution, oxygen use, antifibrotic therapy, ILD subtype distribution, 6MWD, WHO functional class, pulmonary function (FVC and DLCO), eRVSP, and resting and exercise mPAP and PVR (Tables 1 and 2).

When comparing baseline characteristics between the PVRS and PVRD groups, age (68.5 ± 6 years in PVRS vs. 67 ± 7 years in PVRD, p > 0.05) and sex distribution (67% vs. 70% male, p > 0.05) were similar. All patients were using supplemental oxygen therapy, and antifibrotic use was comparable (33% vs. 30%, p > 0.05). There were no significant differences in functional capacity (6MWD 344 ± 44 m vs. 356 ± 50 m), PFTs (FVC and DLCO), or eRVSP (42 ± 5 mmHg vs. 43 ± 4 mmHg, p > 0.05) (Table 1). Resting oxygen saturation was similar between groups (87.7 ± 1.4% in PVRS vs. 87.1 ± 1.7% in PVRD, p > 0.05). During exercise, the PVRD group demonstrated a lower nadir SpO2 compared with the PVRS group (82.5 ± 1.4% vs. 85.0 ± 0.9%, respectively). Peak VO2 was similar between groups (18.8 ± 3.5 mL/kg/min in PVRS vs. 17.1 ± 3.9 mL/kg/min in PVRD, p = 0.38). PAWP at rest and during exercise, as well as the change in PAWP with exercise, were similar between groups (Table 2). Although cardiac output (CO) increased in both groups, exercise CO was higher in the PVRS group. These findings indicate that left‐sided filling pressures did not account for the observed divergence in exercise PVR.

Just as exercise invasive hemodynamics differed significantly between the two groups, noninvasive gas exchange parameters also demonstrated clear separation. Mean GXCAP was significantly higher in the PVRS group (471 ± 85 mL·mmHg) compared with the PVRD group (311 ± 38 mL·mmHg, p < 0.01) (Figure 1). Similarly, mean VE/VCO2 was significantly lower in the PVRS group (33 ± 2) compared with the PVRD group (37 ± 2.5, p < 0.01) (Figure 2).

ROC analysis demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance for GXCAP (AUC 0.983), with an optimal cutoff of ≤ 349 identifying PVRD with 90% sensitivity and 100% specificity. VE/VCO2 also demonstrated good discrimination (AUC 0.900), with an optimal cutoff of ≥ 37. Notably, the GXCAP/VE/VCO2 ratio performed equivalently to GXCAP alone (AUC 0.983), with a threshold of ≤ 10.3 achieving 90% sensitivity and 100% specificity, underscoring its potential as a simple, intuitive composite marker (Figure 3). Although resting mPAP and PVR were statistically different between groups, these differences were not clinically meaningful, as all resting PVR values remained within a narrow range (3–4 WU; mean 3.12 in PVRS vs. 3.54 in PVRD).

Discussion

Level 3 CPET remains the gold standard for assessing pulmonary hemodynamics during exercise, providing valuable insight into disease severity and functional impairment [7]; however, it is invasive and carries inherent limitations [13]. In contrast, several key parameters obtained noninvasively during submaximal exercise have demonstrated strong correlations with invasive measures such as PVR and PVCAP, offering a practical and less burdensome alternative for evaluating pulmonary vascular function [14].

In a recently published cohort of patients with non‐severe PH‐ILD, Parikh et al [8] found that Level 3 CPET stratified patients into two groups: those with an increase in PVR during exercise (PVRD) and those without (PVRS). Despite having similar baseline noninvasive characteristics and no clinically meaningful differences in resting hemodynamics, the PVRD group demonstrated worse exercise hemodynamics, potentially representing a sicker phenotype. Notably, several patients in both groups were classified as WHO functional class I at baseline. Despite similar symptom profiles and resting clinical characteristics, Level 3 CPET revealed marked differences in pulmonary vascular responses to exercise. This observation highlights the potential value of exercise‐based assessment in uncovering pulmonary vascular abnormalities that may not be evident from resting evaluation alone. In this secondary analysis, we found that GXCAP and VE/VCO2, two noninvasive submaximal exercise parameters, correlated with the exercise‐induced hemodynamic impairment. Importantly, peak VO2 was similar between groups, suggesting that GXCAP and VE/VCO2 may provide physiological information beyond overall exercise capacity alone.

Interestingly, a subgroup of patients with elevated resting PVR demonstrated stable or even slightly reduced PVR during exercise. Although counterintuitive in the setting of abnormal vascular resistance, similar observations have been reported in exercise hemodynamic studies, in which capillary recruitment and vascular distention may still occur despite baseline pulmonary vascular impairment [15]. Relief of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction during exercise, driven by increased tidal volume and improved ventilation–perfusion matching, may further transiently lower vascular tone in early or heterogeneous ILD [16]. Finally, the divergence between PVR‐stabilizing and PVR‐increasing patterns likely reflects differences in vascular remodeling severity rather than two distinct physiological phenotypes. Patients with less fixed remodeling may retain limited recruitable vascular reserve, whereas those with more advanced obliteration lack this capacity, resulting in disproportionate pressure response to flow. These mechanisms remain hypotheses and warrant further investigation using more complete exercise hemodynamic profiling.

In this context, GXCAP, which is a noninvasive estimate of PVCAP, and represents the ability of the pulmonary vasculature to accommodate blood volume under a given pressure has been linked to prognosis and survival in PH populations [12, 17]. GXCAP may also help differentiate between heart failure (HF) patients with and without PH [12]. Moreover, Joseph et al. demonstrated that GXCAP might serve as a noninvasive diagnostic variable capable of distinguishing between ILD patients with PH from those without [10]. However, their findings were significant at a threshold of ≤ 416 mL x mmHg, which, when compared to our cohort, appears to reflect the PVRD group rather than the PVRS group. Notably, almost all patients in the PVRS group had a higher GXCAP (470 ± 85 mL x mmHg), reflecting that GXCAP as a diagnostic tool might be less specific for detecting non‐severe PH‐ILD, particularly in cases with static exercise hemodynamics. Our analysis further supports the use of GXCAP, as the t‐test demonstrated a statistically significant difference in GXCAP between the PVRS and PVRD groups (p < 0.01), confirming that GXCAP values vary significantly between these subgroups.

VE/VCO2 reflects a mismatch between ventilation and perfusion and may indicate impaired gas exchange [18]. Recognized as a prognostic marker [19, 20, 21], it demonstrated a statistically significant difference (p < 0.01) in our analysis of patients with non‐severe PH‐ILD, supporting its utility as a reliable noninvasive tool for patient stratification and a surrogate for invasive exercise hemodynamics. Previous studies have shown that VE/VCO2 correlates closely with PVR and may serve as an indicator of PVD burden, particularly in the context of PH‐ILD [10, 22, 23]. However, these variables have not been previously evaluated as stratification tools for differentiating subgroups within the PH‐ILD population based on physiological severity.

Moreover, GXCAP and VE/VCO2 may aid in identifying patients who are more likely to benefit from therapies such as inhaled treprostinil. Prior studies have demonstrated no significant improvement in 6MWD among treated versus untreated patients with non‐severe resting hemodynamics [3, 24]. However, patients with more pronounced abnormalities, particularly elevated PVR, appear to derive greater benefit from therapy [24]. In a recent study by Parikh et al., patients with PVRD demonstrated significant improvement across multiple parameters, including 6MWD, after 16 weeks of treatment with inhaled treprostinil. In contrast, patients in the PVRS group, who were not treated due to lack of prior evidence supporting benefit in this population, remained clinically stable over the study period [8]. In this context, the ability of GXCAP and VE/VCO2 to noninvasively estimate exercise‐induced changes in PVR in patients with non‐severe PH‐ILD may help identify those with a more advanced physiological burden. This may serve as a useful noninvasive adjunct to invasive assessment for identifying patients with greater physiological burden and informing treatment discussions. Importantly, these parameters may also inform treatment discussions with patients by helping identify those more likely to benefit from treatment.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a small retrospective secondary analysis of a previously described cohort of 16 carefully phenotyped patients with non‐severe PH‐ILD who underwent invasive Level 3 CPET. As such, the study population represents a selected group and may not be representative of the broader PH‐ILD population encountered in routine clinical practice. Second, the ROC‐derived cutoff values were generated and evaluated within the same dataset and therefore should be considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than validated thresholds. Finally, although GXCAP and VE/VCO2 demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance in this cohort, larger prospective studies are needed to externally validate these findings and determine their applicability across a wider spectrum of ILD and pulmonary vascular disease severity.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the potential role of noninvasive exercise parameters in detecting hemodynamic impairment among patients with non‐severe PH‐ILD, particularly those demonstrating exercise‐induced increases in PVR. Given the limitations of invasive assessments, GXCAP and VE/VCO2 may serve as practical and reliable markers for identifying PVD progression and supporting patient stratification. Moreover, these measures may help guide treatment decisions in this complex population, in which management remains uncertain. Future studies are warranted to validate their utility in clinical decision‐making and risk assessment.
