On the Complexity of the Circuit Width Problem
Zhengfeng Ji, Yinchen Liu, Zhe’ou Zhou

Abstract
Polynomial representations of quantum circuits provide a clean algebraic framework that connects quantum computation with classical counting complexity [ 12 , 29 , 2 ] . In this representation, amplitudes of quantum circuits can be expressed as normalized gaps of low-degree polynomials over 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} where the normalization is determined by a parameter w ⁡ ( f ) w(f) called the circuit width of polynomial f f first introduced by Montanaro [ 29 ] . In other words, the width of a polynomial directly governs the precision with which a quantum computer can approximate the gap, and thus quantifies the minimal quantum resources measured in terms of the number of qubits needed to carry out such approximations. Understanding the complexity of computing or approximating circuit width is therefore essential to assessing whether this counting-based approach can yield a meaningful approximate-counting-based combinatorial characterization of quantum computation and 𝖡𝖰𝖯 \mathsf{BQP} . We establish five complexity and algorithmic results on determining the circuit width, resolving the conjecture posed in [ 29 ] : • Exact complexity. We prove that the decision problem CircuitWidth ​ ( f , k ) \textsc{CircuitWidth}{(f,k)} , which asks whether w ⁡ ( f ) ≤ k w(f)\leq k on input ( f , k ) (f,k) , is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -complete. The reduction encodes a 3-SAT instance Φ \Phi to a polynomial f Φ f_{\Phi} whose width satisfies w ⁡ ( f Φ ) ≤ k w(f_{\Phi})\leq k if and only if Φ \Phi is satisfiable. • Hardness of approximation. We prove that CircuitWidth ​ ( f , k ) \textsc{CircuitWidth}{(f,k)} remains 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard to approximate within any multiplicative factor ( 49 48 − ϵ ) (\tfrac{49}{48}-\epsilon) for any constant 0 < ϵ < 1 / 48 0<\epsilon<1/48 , ruling out quantum-efficient additive O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) -approximations for the width function unless 𝖭𝖯 ⊆ 𝖡𝖰𝖯 \mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP} . • Hardness for degree-2 polynomials. We show that the exact and approximate hardness results also extend to degree-2 polynomials, demonstrating that the hardness result holds for more general gate sets and for circuit width defined on graphs rather than polynomials. • Nondeterministic search algorithm. We give a nondeterministic polynomial-time algorithm for the search version of CircuitWidth ​ ( f , k ) \textsc{CircuitWidth}{(f,k)} that uses only O ⁡ ( log 2 ⁡ ( n k ) ) = O ⁡ ( k ​ log ⁡ ( e ​ n / k ) ) O(\log_{2}\binom{n}{k})=O(k\log(en/k)) bits of witness. • Fixed-parameter tractability. We show that CircuitWidth ​ ( f , k ) \textsc{CircuitWidth}{(f,k)} is fixed-parameter tractable with runtime k O ⁡ ( k ) ⋅ n k^{O(k)}\cdot n when parameterized with the bound k k on the circuit width. Taken together, our results indicate that there is a fundamental barrier to the approach of Montanaro for obtaining a combinatorial characterization of 𝖡𝖰𝖯 \mathsf{BQP} from the approximate counting perspective. Our results place circuit width firmly within classical complexity theory and indicate that it behaves as a structural parameter—analogous to graph width measures—rather than as a direct measure of intrinsic quantum computational power.

1
Introduction

Quantum computation is widely believed to outperform classical computation on
important tasks such as simulating quantum systems
[
15
]
and
factoring large integers
[
32
]
.
Despite more than four decades of research, understanding the power and
limitations of quantum computers remains a central challenge in theoretical
computer science.
A primary approach to addressing this challenge involves studying combinatorial
and algorithmic problems related to quantum circuits, which have become the
central formalism for quantum computation ever since Yao’s proof of the
equivalence theorem between the quantum circuit model and the quantum Turing
machine model in 1993
[
34
]
.

One fruitful area of research in this direction is to relate quantum circuits to
classical counting problems.
It has long been known that quantum circuits can be simulated using counting
oracles, yielding the inclusion

\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}\subseteq\mathsf{P}^{\#\mathsf{P}}

[
3
]
.
Later refinements showed that quantum algorithms can be captured even more
tightly within counting-based complexity classes such as

AWPP

[
16
]
.
Similar counting-based ideas are utilized in Aaronson’s proof of the hardness of
the permanent via linear optics
[
2
]
, Kuperberg’s
results on approximating the Jones polynomial
[
26
]
, and
Fujii-Morimae’s work on Ising partition functions
[
17
]
.

This counting perspective becomes more explicit through an algebraic
representation of quantum circuits, motivated by a discrete analogue of the
Feynman path-integral formulation applied to circuits.
Dawson et al.
[
12
]
showed that amplitudes of quantum circuits composed
of Toffoli and Hadamard gates can be expressed as normalized counts of solutions
to systems of polynomial equations over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
Montanaro
[
29
]
refined this framework by adopting the gate set

\mathcal{Z}=\{H,Z,\mathrm{CZ},\mathrm{CCZ}\}
, showing that any quantum
circuit
\mathcal{C}
of
w
qubits can be associated with a single degree-3
polynomial
f_{\mathcal{C}}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
without a constant term.
In this representation, the amplitude
\braket{0^{w}|\mathcal{C}|0^{w}}
is
proportional to the gap denoted by
\operatorname{gap}(f_{\mathcal{C}})
, which is the
difference between the number of solutions and non-solutions of the polynomial

f_{\mathcal{C}}
.
This connection between an entry of a unitary circuit
\mathcal{C}
, which is

\mathsf{BQP}
-complete, and the gap of
f_{\mathcal{C}}
, which reflects massive
cancellation effects analogous to quantum interference, is therefore believed to
underlie both the power of quantum computation and the classical hardness of
simulating quantum systems.
Closely related ideas also underpin hardness results for quantum sampling
problems on instantaneous quantum polynomial-time (IQP) circuits or
boson-sampling circuits
[
7
,
1
]
.
In
[
11
]
, fine-grained complexity assumptions regarding the
counting problem of the gap of degree-3 polynomials are employed to estimate the
number of qubits needed for quantum sampling supremacy for IQP and boson
sampling.

Since quantum computers can estimate the value

\braket{0^{w}|\mathcal{C}|0^{w}}
, we can also approximate the gap of a polynomial
f
to a
certain precision.
Given a circuit
\mathcal{C}
over the gate set
\mathcal{Z}
and its associated
degree-3 polynomial
f_{\mathcal{C}}
, a first natural question is: what precision can
a quantum computer achieve in the approximate counting of the gap?
To address this question, Montanaro
[
29
]
defined the width
w(f)
of a
polynomial
f
as the minimum number of qubits required by any quantum circuit
whose associated polynomial equals
f
.
The smaller the width, the higher the precision that a quantum computer can
achieve.
Montanaro showed that, given a circuit of width
w(f)
, a quantum computer can
approximate
\operatorname{gap}(f)
to absolute error
2^{(n+w(f))/2}/3
where
n
is the
number of variables of
f
.
This observation shows that the complexity of width optimization is directly
tied to the complexity of approximate counting problems that characterize
quantum computation.
The next natural question is then how hard it is to compute or approximate the
width of a polynomial.
If width could be efficiently minimized, even approximately, then one could
combine width optimization with standard amplitude-estimation procedures to
obtain a new and conceptually simple combinatorial characterization of
\mathsf{BQP}

based entirely on approximate counting with known precision
[
29
]
.

In this paper, we undertake a systematic study of the quantum circuit width
problem, formally denoted as
\textsc{CircuitWidth}{(f,k)}
, which asks whether the width

w(f)
is less than or equal to
k
.
We show that
\textsc{CircuitWidth}{(f,k)}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete, and that even approximating the
circuit width within a small constant factor remains
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.
Furthermore, we extend our hardness results to degree-2 polynomials,
demonstrating that both the circuit width problem and its approximate version
remain
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.
This extension has broad implications because many quantum gate sets yield
degree-2 polynomials: for instance, circuits using
\{H,\mathrm{CS}\}
or Clifford
+T
gates produce polynomials with at most quadratic
terms modulo
4
or
8
depending on the gate set
[
33
]
.
Thus, our approximation hardness result establishes that the same computational
intractability is universal across all these diverse gate sets.
Moreover, since degree-2 polynomials without linear terms correspond to graphs,
circuit width in this setting can be interpreted as a graph parameter related to
treewidth or pathwidth, but carrying a quantum information-theoretic
interpretation concerning the arrangement of Hadamard gates and diagonal
two-qubit gates.
Consequently, our results rule out efficient width optimization across all these
practically relevant quantum computing scenarios on classical or quantum
computers unless unlikely complexity-theoretic collapses occur.
We further complement these results with algorithmic upper bounds, including a
nondeterministic search algorithm with succinct witnesses and an efficient fixed-parameter
tractable algorithm parameterized by the circuit width.

The implications of our results are threefold.
First, we answer the open question posed in Montanaro’s paper concerning the
complexity of the circuit width problem
[
29
]
.
Our
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness results show that there is a fundamental barrier to obtaining a
combinatorial characterization of
\mathsf{BQP}
via this approximate counting approach.
Second, circuit width provides a natural framework for minimizing qubit usage in
quantum computation, with potential relevance to practical implementations and
compilation of quantum algorithms.
Our hardness results indicate that, in the worst case, even quantum computers
cannot solve this problem efficiently or even approximately.
Any further progress in this direction must therefore rely on heuristic methods
and exploit the structure of circuits for specific algorithms.
Third, our hardness results, combined with the fixed-parameter tractability
algorithm, suggest that circuit width behaves more like a structural
parameter—such as treewidth—than a parameter that captures intrinsic quantum
computational power.
Every given polynomial
f
can be realized by an IQP circuit of
n
qubits,
implementing all quadratic terms in
f
using
\mathrm{CZ}
gates; and if there
is a circuit of width
w
for
f
using
h
internal Hadamard gates for the
quadratic terms, we have
n=w+h
and the total number of Hadamard gates is

2w+h=n+w
, where the
2w
term arises from the Hadamard gates at the
beginning and end of
\mathcal{C}
as required in the definition of

f_{\mathcal{C}}
.
Thus, minimizing the circuit width is equivalent to minimizing the use of the
interference-creating gate
H
when creating the amplitude

\braket{0^{w}|\mathcal{C}|0^{w}}
.
To put it in perspective, treewidth governs the complexity of classical
simulation methods based on tensor networks
[
28
]
, while linear
rank-width characterizes the complexity of simulation based on decision
diagrams
[
8
]
.
The circuit width considered in this work naturally captures the quantum
resources, as measured by the number of qubits required for creating certain
amplitudes.
Our hardness results, however, suggest that circuit width minimization problem
primarily reflects combinatorial structure because of its
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness rather
than the quantum computational power typically characterized by
\mathsf{BQP}
.

Our Results

In this section, we summarize our main results on the circuit width problem

.
The results are twofold: hardness results and algorithmic results.

Hardness Results

For the hardness of circuit width, we rule out the possibility of efficiently

with no constant term by
proving that the problem of deciding

(denoted by
\textsc{CircuitWidth}{(f,k)}
) is

\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

Theorem 1.1

(See also
Section

3.3
)
.

CircuitWidth
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

In fact, we are able to prove an inapproximability result:

is even

\mathsf{NP}
-hard to approximate within a multiplicative factor of about
1.02
(even
restricted to instances with

), and hence it is impossible to
approximate the width efficiently (in
\mathsf{BQP}
power) within additive

O(\log n)
error unless
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}
.

Theorem 1.2

(Informal, see also
Theorem

4.2
)
.

For any constant
0<\epsilon<1/48
, it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to distinguish degree-3
polynomials

with no constant term and

from those with

w(f)\geq(\tfrac{49}{48}-\epsilon)k
.

The previous hardness results crucially rely on cubic terms (CCZ gates) to
enforce complex geometric constraints.
However, many quantum computational models naturally correspond to degree-2
polynomials.
For instance, circuits composed of Hadamard and diagonal gates such as

\{H,\mathrm{CS}\}
(controlled-S gate), or even Clifford
+T
gates

\{H,\mathrm{CZ},T\}
yield polynomials with at most quadratic terms modulo

4
, or
8
depending on the gate set.
In these cases, it is also possible that many different circuits may correspond

\mathrm{CZ}
and Hadamard gate

contribute to

[
33
]
).
A natural question thus arises: does the approximation hardness persist when we

We answer this affirmatively by showing that approximation hardness persists
with the same factor:

Theorem 1.3

(Informal, see
Theorem

4.8
)
.

The approximation hardness of
CircuitWidth
holds for degree-2
polynomials: for any constant

, it is

-hard to distinguish

Finally, from the viewpoint of conditional lower bounds, our reduction from

(See also

Algorithmic results

On the positive side, we provide a nondeterministic polynomial-time algorithm

1.3.1
\mathsf{NP}
-completeness

We reduce
3-SAT
to
CircuitWidth
by mapping a formula
\Phi
with
m

clauses to a degree-3 polynomial
f_{\Phi}
and a width budget
k=1+6m
such that

\Phi
is satisfiable if and only if
w(f_{\Phi})\leq k
. The reduction encodes
Boolean information in the
geometry
of a minimum-width realization: truth
values are represented by which segment overlaps which, and the width bound
forces a rigid layout that can only occur when all clauses are satisfied.

Circuit-to-polynomial intuition.

By the circuit–polynomial correspondence, each segment between consecutive
H
gates becomes
a symbol, and monomials arise precisely from overlaps induced by
Z
,
H
, CZ, and
CCZ gates. Hence, by prescribing a polynomial we can constrain which segments
must overlap in any realizing circuit. Specifically, a cubic term
uvw
forces the three segments
u,v,w
to overlap at some time, while a quadratic term
uv
may either arise from a CZ gate between overlapping segments
u,v
or from an
H
gate link between adjacent segments
u,v
on the same wire. A direct computation shows that a quantum circuit
\mathcal{C}
on
k
wires with
h
internal
H
gates has exactly
k+h
segments, and hence the number of symbols in the polynomial is
|\mathcal{S}|=k+h
. Therefore, intuitively, to minimize the number of wires
k
for a given polynomial
f_{\Phi}
, one should try to maximize the number of internal
H
gates
h
in the realizing circuit. Note that maximizing
h
is equivalent to minimizing the number of CZ gates, since their sum is fixed by the number of quadratic terms in
f_{\Phi}
.

Our construction uses this intuition by constructing
f_{\Phi}
using
1+18m
symbols and
12m
quadratic terms (recall
m
is the number of clauses in
\Phi
). Therefore, by the above argument, any realizing circuit must have at most
h\leq 12m
internal
H
gates, so that if the circuit realizes
f_{\Phi}
, the number of wires
k\geq|\mathcal{S}|-h\geq 1+6m
, giving a lower bound on the width. Furthermore, a circuit realizes
f_{\Phi}
with
k=1+6m
wires if and only if the above lower bound achieves equality, i.e., if and only if the circuit uses no CZ gates, constraining which symbols are on the same wires. Additional cubic terms in
f_{\Phi}
then enforce that the only way to achieve wire-optimality (and hence no CZ gates) is to have a geometric layout corresponding to a satisfying assignment of
\Phi
. The construction is detailed below.

Notation: variables vs. literals.

We write
x,y,z
for Boolean variables and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{x}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{z}}

Gadgets and roles.

Each qubit wire is cut by at most two
H
gates into three segments. The gadgets
are organized as follows:

1.

A
base wire

b

2.

For each clause
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}

For each literal occurrence, a

1.3.2
Approximation Hardness

Beyond exact computation, we show that
CircuitWidth
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to approximate within any factor better than
\tfrac{49}{48}-\epsilon
for every fixed
0<\epsilon<1/48
. We first observe that the previous reduction in
Section

1.3.1
is “almost” completely local: each clause in
\Phi
contributes a constant number of symbols to
f_{\Phi}
, denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
(with some quadratic and cubic terms inside them):

\underbrace{\{t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\hat{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\tilde{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\}}_{\text{Truth marker}}\cup\underbrace{\{x_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\hat{x}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\bar{x}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\}}_{\text{Var }x}\cup\underbrace{\{y_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\bar{y}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\}}_{\text{Var }y}\cup\underbrace{\{z_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\hat{z}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\bar{z}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\}}_{\text{Var }z}\cup\underbrace{\{\overleftarrow{p_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}},p_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\overrightarrow{p_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}}\}}_{\text{OR }p_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}}\cup\underbrace{\{\overleftarrow{o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}},o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\overrightarrow{o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}}\}}_{\text{OR }o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}},

plus some additional
cubic
terms to force consistency of positions of clauses and variable assignments across clauses. Specifically, no quadratic term exists between
b
and symbols from
\mathcal{S}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
for different clauses
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}\neq{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c^{\prime}}}
. Thus, any realizing circuit must place
b
and
\mathcal{S}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
on separate qubit wires.

Moreover, when a clause
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}
is placed on exactly 6 wires (called a
good clause
), the variable segments
encode
a truth assignment: whether it overlaps the truth marker segment
t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
(encoding
true
), or does not overlap (encoding
false
), the same as in the exact case. The
OR gadgets
formed by cubic terms on
\mathcal{S}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
then
enforce
that at least one of the three literals in
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}
is
true
under this assignment. The
variable gadgets
across clauses ensure that all occurrences of the same variable across different clauses are consistent.

Therefore, when we restrict to good clauses, the construction behaves like in the exact case and gives a variable assignment (partial at first, but we can arbitrarily assign remaining variables). If a clause
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}
is unsatisfied, then the corresponding clause gadget in
f_{\Phi}
forces
\mathcal{S}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
to use at least one additional wire in any realizing circuit (and in fact, one additional wire suffices). As a result, we can create a gap between the “Yes” and “No” instances by leveraging the hardness of approximating
Max-3-SAT
, whose task is to determine the maximum number of clauses that can be simultaneously satisfied by any assignment:

Theorem 1.8

(Reduction from
Max-3-SAT
, see also
Theorem

4.1
)
.

Let
\Phi
be a
3-SAT
instance with
m
clauses and let

be an integer.
Construct

in polynomial time as in

1.3.1
.
Then

if and only if
\Phi
has an assignment satisfying at least
t

It is known from Håstad’s
7/8
theorem
[

]
that
Max-3-SAT
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to distinguish satisfiable instances from those in which at most a
7/8+\delta
fraction of clauses can be satisfied, for any constant
\delta>0
, see
Theorem

2.2
. Now, the reduction works as follows: if
\Phi
is satisfiable, the constructed polynomial
f_{\Phi}
has width
w(f_{\Phi})\leq 1+6m
; if every assignment satisfies at most
(7/8+\delta)m
clauses, then the reduction guarantees

. Choosing

, the additive

from

1.3.3
Hardness for Degree-2 Polynomials

We further extend our inapproximability result to degree-2 polynomials, showing that
CircuitWidth
remains
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to approximate within any factor better than
\tfrac{49}{48}-\epsilon
even when the input polynomial has degree 2, for every fixed
0<\epsilon<1/48
, see
Theorem

4.8
.

Due to technical losses in proving lower bounds on the number of wires needed, we cannot obtain a “tight” reduction from
Max-3-SAT
as in
Theorem

1.8
. Instead, we directly use the inapproximability result from Håstad’s
7/8
theorem
[
18
]
. Given a
3-SAT
instance
\Phi
with
m
clauses, we first construct the degree-3 polynomial
f_{\Phi}
as in
Section

1.3.1
, and then transform it into a degree-2 polynomial
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
using a
twin duplication
strategy (
Definition

4.3
).

The twin construction.

The core technique replaces each symbol
s
in the original polynomial
f_{\Phi}
with many copies
s^{(1)},s^{(2)},\ldots,s^{(M)}
, connected by a complete graph of CZ gates (a “
clique constraint
”) implemented via quadratic terms:

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq M}s^{(i)}s^{(j)}.

The base symbol
b
receives even more copies (
Nm
copies), where
N
and
M
are two large parameters to be set with
N\ll M
. Each quadratic term
uv
is replaced by a “
matching
” connecting only copies with the same index. Each cubic term
b\,u\,v
in
f_{\Phi}
is then replaced by a “
tripartite complete graph
” connecting all pairs of copies across the three symbols. The resulting “
twin polynomial
”
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
clearly has degree 2. It is easy to see that, if
\Phi
is satisfiable, then by repeating the original circuit (with
1+6m
wires)
M
times (and using additional
Nm-M
wires for copies of
b
), we can realize
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
using a circuit of width at most
(6M+N)m
.

For soundness, with Håstad gap parameter
\delta>0
, we need to show that if at most
t=(7/8+\delta)m
clauses of
\Phi
can be satisfied, then any circuit realizing
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
must have width at least
(7M+N)m-(7/8+\delta)Mm-750m
(recall
N\ll M
and
M
is chosen large enough compared with the constant loss).
The key geometric insight is the following clique survival property:

Lemma 1.9

(see
Section

4.2.1
)
.

Let
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\}
be a set of symbol copies with a clique constraint
\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq r}s_{i}s_{j}
appearing in the twin polynomial
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
. Then in any circuit realizing
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
,
at most two copies from this set can share a wire
with another copy from the same set.

The proof of the lemma is clear: no three symbols can lie on the same wire, and no two groups of two symbols can share two wires respectively, establishing that the only possibilities are
r
symbols on distinct wires, or possibly one pair sharing a wire.

This constraint ensures that the vast majority of copies “survive” as isolated symbols. Specifically, using
Section

1.3.3
and a careful analysis for cubic terms in original
f_{\Phi}
, we can show that: in any circuit realizing
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
, for any symbol
s\neq b
in
f_{\Phi}
, at least
M-O(1)
of its copies do not share wires with
any other copy
of
s
and
any other copy
of symbol
u
where
su
appear in a
cubic
term in
f_{\Phi}
. We will call these copies “
survive globally
”. Similarly, at least
Nm-O(m)
copies of the base symbol
b
survive globally.

Enforcing the original structure.

Another key insight is that these globally surviving copies effectively recreate the geometric constraints of the original cubic terms. Consider a cubic term
b\,u\,v
in
f_{\Phi}
. If three copies
b^{(i)},u^{(j)},v^{(k)}
all survive globally, then by definition they lie on distinct wires from any other copy of
b
,
u
, or
v
. The tripartite complete graph connecting all pairs of these copies (arising from the twin construction) then enforces that their supports must pairwise intersect, the same as required by the original CCZ gate in
f_{\Phi}
using 1-dimensional Helly’s theorem (
Theorem

2.1
).

As a result, we can use the same proof strategy as in
Section

1.3.2
by restricting attention to globally surviving groups of symbols in
\mathcal{S}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}^{(j)}
(the groups
\mathcal{S}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}^{(j)},j\in\{1,2,\dots,M\}
so that all symbol copies in it are globally surviving). A union bound argument shows that for each clause
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}
, at least
M-O(1)
such globally surviving groups exist.

We can call such a group a
good clause copy
if it uses exactly 6 wires. Then, by the same arguments as before, each good clause copy encodes a variable assignment (consistency ensured by global survival) and enforces clause satisfaction. For each clause
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}
, there are at least
M-O(1)

globally surviving clause copies
—each copy uses 6 wires if the clause is satisfied under the encoded assignment, and at least 7 wires otherwise. The lower bound argument then proceeds identically to the degree-3 case, up to the constant additive loss from the survival union bound:
w(f^{\prime}_{\Phi})\geq(7M+N)m-(7/8+\delta)Mm-750m
when at most a
(7/8+\delta)
fraction of clauses is satisfiable. Taking
\delta=\epsilon
yields the same
\tfrac{49}{48}
hardness factor when
N\ll M=\Omega(1/\epsilon)
.

1.3.5
Efficient Fixed-Parameter Tractable Algorithm

In this section, we present an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm with runtime
k^{O(k)}\cdot n
, see
Theorem

5.7
.

The key insight is that if a valid
k
-qubit circuit exists, then the connection graph
G_{f}
(whose vertices are symbols in
\mathcal{S}
and edges correspond to pairs appearing in monomials of
f
) has small treewidth—specifically, pathwidth at most
k
. This follows from the correctness of the
greedy extension
procedure in
Section

1.3.4
: simulating the procedure, while retaining completed tails in the auxiliary head sets, yields a path decomposition of
G_{f}
whose bags are the initial head set and the consecutive auxiliary head-set unions
S_{t-1}\cup S_{t}
, each of size at most
k+1
(see
Section

5.2
).

With the pathwidth (and hence treewidth) upper bound at hand, establishing that
CircuitWidth
is
FPT
becomes a direct consequence of Courcelle’s Theorem
[
9
]
. The existence of a valid partition into
k
wires can be expressed in Monadic Second-Order logic (
\textnormal{MSO}_{2}
) over the natural labelled incidence structure of the polynomial: we check for
k
disjoint ordered paths whose direct adjacencies correspond to quadratic monomials of
f
, such that the constraint graph
\mathcal{G}_{H}
induced by all quadratic and cubic terms (as defined in
Section

5
) remains acyclic. Courcelle’s Theorem then implies this property can be checked in linear time with a constant depending on
k
(see
Section

5.2
for details). This shows that
CircuitWidth
is
FPT
.

However, since the form of
f(k)
in Courcelle’s Theorem can be multi-exponential, we provide a more concrete dynamic programming algorithm with runtime
k^{O(k)}\cdot n
for practical purposes.

The algorithm employs dynamic programming on a tree decomposition of
G_{f}
. To obtain such a decomposition, we use a recent 2-approximation algorithm by Korhonen
[
25
]
to obtain a tree decomposition of
G_{f}
with width
w\leq 2k+1=O(k)
in time
2^{O(k)}n
. If the algorithm determines that the treewidth exceeds
k
, we can immediately conclude no solution exists. We then convert this to a nice tree decomposition with four node types (leaf, introduce, forget, join) and
O(kn)
nodes.

The dynamic programming follows the standard tree decomposition paradigm. For each node
x
in the tree (processed bottom-up from leaves to root), we maintain a table
\mathcal{D}_{x}
of
realizable states
. Each state in
\mathcal{D}_{x}
encodes a partial wire assignment and topological ordering for the symbols on the boundary bag
B_{x}
of node
x
. Let
V_{x}
denote all symbols appearing in the subtree rooted at
x
. Then, a state is
realizable
and included in
\mathcal{D}_{x}
if there exists a
compatible extension
to a “valid circuit configuration” using all symbols in
V_{x}
. Intuitively, the
valid circuit configuration
for
V_{x}
consists of:

•

Wire assignments:
A partition of symbols in
V_{x}
into
k
ordered lists
L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}
;

•

Edge type:
An edge type assignment
\rho
that specifies, for each pair of consecutive symbols on the same list, their connectivity type (adjacent via a Hadamard gate on the wire, or disconnected);

such that they satisfy the acyclicity constraint of graph
\mathcal{G}_{H}[V_{x}(\rho)]
(the directed constraint graph in
Section

1.3.4
which only reflects temporal ordering of Hadamard gates incident to symbols in
V_{x}
under edge type
\rho
).

This definition captures exactly the structure needed to realize a circuit for the symbols in
V_{x}
: the lists determining wire assignments, the edge types encoding local connectivity, and the acyclicity of
\mathcal{G}_{H}[V_{x}(\rho)]
ensuring that Hadamard gates can be consistently ordered in time. At the root node
r
where
V_{r}=\mathcal{S}
(all symbols), a valid circuit configuration directly corresponds to a complete quantum circuit on
k
qubits with polynomial
f
.

Now, a state in
\mathcal{D}_{x}
has two components
(\mathcal{W}_{x},\mathcal{O}_{x})
which encode partial information about boundary symbols in
B_{x}
:

1.

\mathcal{W}_{x}=(W_{1},\ldots,W_{k},\tau)
: A partition of boundary symbols in
B_{x}
into

, each with endpoints

, along with an edge type

on consecutive entries of each

: real symbols

•

\tau(u,v)=2
: the two entries are connected through forgotten real symbols in

(each consecutive real-symbol pair on the path is connected by a Hadamard gate);

•

\tau(u,v)=0
: the two entries are not connected inside the current subtree (no Hadamard gate or forgotten symbols between them).

2.

\mathcal{O}_{x}
: A topological ordering on the boundary virtual vertices

, after the identifications induced by direct adjacencies, with

first and

last.

As mentioned, a state

is hence

—and included in

—if it can be

to a
valid circuit configuration
for

: there exist lists

partitioning

and edge types

on consecutive entries in each

such that (i)

with order preserved, (ii)
\tau

(type 0 or 1 edges remain unchanged; type 2 edges become paths of type 1 edges via forgotten real symbols), (iii) the partial constraint graph

is acyclic and
\mathcal{O}_{x}
is the restriction of one of its topological orderings to the boundary virtual vertices (including
S,E
).

For each of the four node types in the nice tree decomposition, we provide explicit transition rules to compute
\mathcal{D}_{x}
from the tables of its children. The crux of the correctness proof is showing that these local transitions preserve realizability: a state is in
\mathcal{D}_{x}
if and only if it can be compatibly extended to a valid circuit configuration for
V_{x}
.

Finally, we extract the solution from the root. At the root node

(which has an empty bag
B_{r}=\emptyset
), we check whether
\mathcal{D}_{r}
is non-empty. If so, any state in
\mathcal{D}_{r}
corresponds to a complete valid circuit on

\mathcal{D}_{r}=\emptyset

The algorithm runs in time
k^{O(k)}\cdot n
, dominated by the DP step: the number of possible states per node is bounded by
k^{O(k)}
(from counting ordered boundary wire structures, edge types, and boundary topological orderings), each node transition contributes only a

factor, and there are
O(kn)
nodes in the nice tree decomposition.

The obtained
FPT
algorithm in fact has a time bound of the form

, see
Theorem

5.7

O(\binom{n}{k}^{2}\textnormal{poly}(n))\leq O((en/k)^{2k}\textnormal{poly}(n))
when

, making it more efficient and practical for polynomially small

.

2.1
From circuits to associated polynomials

The main steps in the transformation from quantum circuits to associated polynomials are as follows:

Normalization step.

Given any quantum circuit

whose gate set is

Symbol assignment.

On every wire place a fresh Boolean

Thus the

Gate-to-monomial map.

Let

be the associated polynomial obtained by assigning one monomial to each non-Hadamard gate
and
to each internal Hadamard gate. More precisely:

f_{\mathcal{C}}\;=\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{each }\textsc{Z},\,\textsc{CZ},\,\textsc{CCZ}\\
\text{gate in }\mathcal{C}\end{subarray}}\bigl(\text{product of symbols on which the gate acts}\bigr)\;+\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{each internal }H\text{ gate}\\
\text{between symbols }x_{i}\text{ and }x_{i+1}\end{subarray}}\bigl(x_{i}\,x_{i+1}\bigr).

In other words:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\textsc{Z}\text{ acting on segment }x&\;\Longrightarrow\;x,\\[6.0pt]
\textsc{CZ}\text{ acting on segments }(x,y)&\;\Longrightarrow\;x\,y,\\[6.0pt]
\textsc{CCZ}\text{ acting on segments }(x,y,z)&\;\Longrightarrow\;x\,y\,z,\\[6.0pt]
\text{interior }H\text{ gate between symbols }x_{i}\text{ and }x_{i+1}&\;\Longrightarrow\;x_{i}\,x_{i+1}.\end{array}

By construction, each non-terminal

2.3.2
Parameterized Complexity

Beyond classical complexity, we also analyze

from the perspective of parameterized complexity
[

Definition 2.7

))
.

A parameterized problem with parameter

that is not

is believed to require time

for some unbounded function

[

,
10
]
.

3.1.1

Our reduction maps a 3-SAT instance

Notation: variables vs. literals.

We write

for a literal (either

The polynomial representation of an arbitrary quantum circuit is hard to control. Our idea is to carefully design the polynomial and the qubit budget

to show that any size-optimal quantum circuit realizing

must satisfy some additional constraints. Under these conditions, any quantum circuit for

1.

Various qubit wire roles.

We require various qubit wires in the circuit to assume distinct functional roles:

•

A single qubit wire

no

H

•

Exactly two

,
\hat{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
, and
\tilde{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}

•

Variable qubit wires.

For each literal occurrence, a variable wire contains
x_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
,
\hat{x}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
, and
\bar{x}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
(in either order).

•

Clause gadgets via nested ORs.

Each clause
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{x}}\lor{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}}\lor{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{z}})
is evaluated with two extra
qubit wires: the first has middle segment
p_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
, representing the
intermediate Boolean value
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{x}}\lor{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}}
, and the second has middle
segment
o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
, representing the final Boolean value

p_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\lor{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{z}}
of the whole clause. Both qubit wires bear two
H
gates and three
segments, arranged as
\{\overleftarrow{q},q,\overrightarrow{q}\}
or
\{\overrightarrow{q},q,\overleftarrow{q}\}
,
q\in\{p_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\}
.
The decorations
\overrightarrow{\bullet}
and
\overleftarrow{\bullet}
are only notational placeholders to squeeze
q
into the correct geometric position.
We construct clause gadgets (see
Section

3.1.4
) with the following soundness/completeness behavior: if
q
overlaps with
t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
in a
k
-qubit circuit, then at least one input literal is
true
(i.e., its segment overlaps
t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
); conversely, if at least one input literal is
true
, then the gadget can be realized with
q
overlapping
t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
.
Finally, we add a cubic monomial

b\cdot t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\cdot o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
for every clause, forcing the segment

o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
to overlap with
t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
and therefore enforcing global
satisfiability.

2.

Size-optimality guarantee.

In the construction, we guarantee that every qubit wire in any realizing quantum circuit for
f
has at most two
H
gates, and
b
is
H
-free. Then, the circuit must use at least
k
qubits; see also
Section

3.2
. Moreover, by further exploiting the structure of the polynomial, we are able to prove that size equality is attained
if and only if
each qubit wire has exactly the configuration above. This yields a bijection between witnesses of the
3-SAT
instance and size-optimal quantum circuits for the polynomial
f_{\Phi}
.

After discussing the overview strategy, we introduce the construction of polynomials formally in the following sections. Throughout the construction, sums over pairs of clauses or literal occurrences are over unordered pairs. When a clause contains repeated literals, the corresponding occurrence symbols should be understood as carrying suppressed occurrence indices, so that distinct occurrences give distinct algebraic symbols.

3.1.2
Truth-Marker Qubit Wires

In the global polynomial, the only quadratic terms that mention the
symbols
t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
,
\hat{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
, or
\tilde{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
are (TM stands for

):

G_{\mathrm{TM}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}}\;=\,\hat{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\ t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\;+\;\hat{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\,\tilde{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}.

Consequently, no other symbol can link any of

t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\hat{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\tilde{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
through Hadamard gates. If these three symbols were placed on two or more
qubit wires, at least one additional qubit wire would be consumed. Hence, every
k
-qubit circuit must host them
on a
single
qubit wire intersected by exactly two Hadamard gates.

To ensure each truth-marker qubit wire encodes the same Boolean value, we include the following terms in our polynomial
f_{\Phi}
:

G_{\mathrm{TM}}=b\sum_{\{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c_{2}}}\}\in\binom{C}{2}}\left(t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c_{1}}}}t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c_{2}}}}+\hat{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c_{1}}}}\hat{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c_{2}}}}+\tilde{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c_{1}}}}\tilde{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c_{2}}}}\right).

Since we can always reverse the circuit and variable orderings without changing the represented polynomial, without loss of generality we fix the left-to-right temporal order

t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\;\;\hat{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\;\;\tilde{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},

and we refer to these qubit wires as the
truth-marker qubit wires
. All later
gadgets assume this canonical ordering.

3.1.4
Clause Gadget

A three-literal clause

3.2
Structural Lemmas and Correctness Proofs

To establish the validity of the reduction, we must prove that the
polynomial
f_{\Phi}
constructed above satisfies two complementary properties:

Soundness.

If the original
3-SAT
instance
(X,C)
is
unsatisfiable
,
then
every
quantum circuit realizing
f_{\Phi}
requires
strictly more than
k=1+6|C|
qubits.

Completeness.

If
(X,C)

is satisfiable
, there exists a quantum circuit with
exactly
k=1+6|C|
qubits that realizes
f_{\Phi}
.

Recall that in the global construction, all
18m+1
symbols can be naturally partitioned into a single
\{b\}
and the associated
18
symbols for each clause
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{x}}\lor{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}}\lor{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{z}})
, denoted
\mathcal{S}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
:

\underbrace{\{t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\hat{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\tilde{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\}}_{\text{Truth marker}}\cup\underbrace{\{x_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\hat{x}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\bar{x}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\}}_{\text{Var }x}\cup\underbrace{\{y_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\bar{y}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\}}_{\text{Var }y}\cup\underbrace{\{z_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\hat{z}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\bar{z}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\}}_{\text{Var }z}\cup\underbrace{\{\overleftarrow{p_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}},p_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\overrightarrow{p_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}}\}}_{\text{OR }p_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}}\cup\underbrace{\{\overleftarrow{o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}},o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}},\overrightarrow{o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}}\}}_{\text{OR }o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}}.

The key observation is that
\mathcal{S}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
naturally requires exactly
6
qubit wires, and any
6
-wire realization uniquely encodes a truth assignment. We establish this through the following structural lemmas.

Lemma 3.1

(Base-qubit and distinct clause isolation)
.

Every circuit that realizes
f_{\Phi}
places the symbol
b
on its own qubit containing
no internal
Hadamard gates, i.e.,
\mathsf{supp}(b)=I
. Moreover, such a circuit places
\mathcal{S}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
on separate qubit wires for different clauses
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}\in C
, i.e., there is no qubit wire containing symbols from two different clauses.

Proof.

b
appears only in cubic monomials of
f_{\Phi}
. If an internal Hadamard gate appears on the qubit wire containing the segment representing
b
, it generates a quadratic term of
the form
bs
for some
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Since the two adjacent segments separated by that Hadamard gate have disjoint open supports, no diagonal gate can produce the same monomial
bs
, so this term cannot be canceled. This contradicts the construction of
f_{\Phi}
. For the same reason, because there are no quadratic terms between symbols from different clauses, consecutive symbols on the same qubit wire cannot belong to different clauses. Any wire containing symbols from two different clauses has such a transition, so the symbols from different clauses must be placed on separate qubit wires.
∎

4
Approximation Hardness and Restricted Degree

4.1
Approximation Hardness

In this section, we prove that even the
\alpha
-approximation version of the problem
CircuitWidth
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard. By observing that the previous reduction from
3-SAT
to
CircuitWidth
in
Section

3.1
is actually a reduction for
Max-3-SAT
, we can invoke the
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness of approximating
Max-3-SAT
due to Håstad (see
Theorem

2.2
). We first review some basic definitions and then prove the main theorem.

Definition 4.1

(
\alpha
-approximation version of
CircuitWidth
)
.

Given a real parameter
\alpha>1
, the promise problem
CircuitWidth
-
\alpha
-Approx is to distinguish between instances in the following
(\mathsf{Yes},\mathsf{No})
languages:

\mathsf{Yes}
:

(f\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[\mathcal{S}],k\in\mathbb{N})
, where
f
is a degree-3 polynomial with no constant term and
w(f)\leq k
.

\mathsf{No}
:

(f\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[\mathcal{S}],k\in\mathbb{N})
, where
f
is a degree-3 polynomial with no constant term and
w(f)\geq\alpha k
.

Definition 4.2

(
Max-3-SAT
)
.

An instance of
Max-3-SAT
is a
3-SAT
formula
\Phi
with
m
clauses. The task is to determine the maximum number of clauses that can be simultaneously satisfied by any assignment.

Theorem 4.1

(Reduction from
Max-3-SAT
)
.

Let
\Phi
be a
3-SAT
instance with
m
clauses and let
t
be an integer.
Construct
f_{\Phi}
in polynomial time as in
Section

3.1
and set

K\;=\;1+6m+(m-t).

Then
w(f_{\Phi})\leq K
if and only if
\Phi
has an assignment satisfying at least
t
clauses.

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{OPT}(\Phi)
be the maximum number of clauses satisfiable by any assignment.
By
Section

3.2
(a), any clause gadget realized on exactly
6
wires must encode a
satisfied clause. Conversely, by
Section

3.2
, any clause can be realized on
7

wires with the segment
o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
overlapping
t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
regardless of the literals.

Now, if
\Phi
has an assignment satisfying at least
t
clauses, then we can set the truth-marker wires honestly and variable wires accordingly, and realize each satisfied clause gadget on
6
wires and each unsatisfied clause gadget on
7
wires. For clauses realized with the extra wire, choose the split-off
t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
with full support
I
, and choose
\hat{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
and
\tilde{t}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
with the same coarse positions as in the good clauses; hence all global truth-marker synchronization terms involving
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}
remain realizable. The variable-synchronization terms are realized by the common assignment as in
Section

3.2
(b). This yields a circuit realizing
f_{\Phi}
on a number of wires at most

1+6m+(m-\mathsf{OPT}(\Phi))\leq 1+6m+(m-t)=K.

Conversely, suppose there is a circuit realizing
f_{\Phi}
on at most
K
wires.
Let
g
be the number of clauses whose symbols are realized on exactly
6
wires.
By
Sections

3.2
and
3.2
, each such clause is a
good clause
.
Every clause needs at least
6
wires, so the remaining
m-g
clauses use at least
7
wires each.
Hence the total number of wires is at least

1+6g+7(m-g)\;=\;1+7m-g.

Since the circuit uses at most
K=1+7m-t
wires, the pigeonhole principle gives
g\geq t
.

Now extract a Boolean assignment from the good clauses.
For any variable
x
that appears in at least one good clause, define
w(x)
to be the truth value
encoded by
x_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
in that good clause (via
Definition

3.1
).
This is well-defined by
Section

3.2
(a), which synchronizes all occurrences of
x
across good clauses.
For variables that do not appear in any good clause, assign an arbitrary truth value.

For each good clause
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}
, the cubic term
b\,t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}\,o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
forces
o_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
to overlap
t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
.
By
Section

3.2
(a), this implies that at least one of the literals in
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}
overlaps
t_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{c}}}
,
hence is
true
under the encoding. Therefore all
g
good clauses are satisfied by
w
,
and in particular at least
t
clauses are satisfied. This completes the reduction.
∎

We now apply Håstad’s theorem (
Theorem

2.2
) to establish the inapproximability of our problem.

Theorem 4.2

(
CircuitWidth
-
\alpha
-Approx Hardness)
.

For any constant
0<\epsilon<1/48
,
CircuitWidth
- (
\tfrac{49}{48}-\epsilon
) -Approx
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

Proof.

Fix a constant
0<\epsilon<1/48
, and apply
Theorem

2.2
with the gap parameter
\delta=\epsilon
. We use the same reduction
\Phi\mapsto f_{\Phi}
from
Section

3.1
, together with
Theorem

4.1
. If
\Phi
is satisfiable, then
w(f_{\Phi})\leq 1+6m
by setting
t=m
. If every assignment satisfies at most
(\tfrac{7}{8}+\delta)m
clauses, then the counting argument in
Theorem

4.1
gives

w(f_{\Phi})\;\geq\;1+6m+\left(\frac{1}{8}-\delta\right)m.

Set
k=1+6m
. Since
\delta=\epsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\left(1+6m+\left(\frac{1}{8}-\delta\right)m\right)-\left(\frac{49}{48}-\epsilon\right)(1+6m)=\epsilon(5m+1)-\frac{1}{48}.

This quantity is positive for all sufficiently large
m
. By
Theorem

2.2
, distinguishing the satisfiable case from the case in which every assignment satisfies at most
(\tfrac{7}{8}+\delta)m
clauses is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard. Hence
CircuitWidth
- (
\tfrac{49}{48}-\epsilon
) -Approx is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.
∎

4.2
Restriction to Degree-2 Polynomials

We now prove that approximation hardness persists even when the instance is restricted to degree-2 polynomials, i.e., cubic terms are forbidden. The key idea is to replace each symbol with multiple “copies” and connect copies via “cliques” of CZ gates. Since at most two copies of any symbol can share a single wire, sufficiently many surviving copies enforce the same geometric constraints as the original construction.

4.2.1
Twin Construction

Given a
Max-3-SAT
instance
\Phi
with
m
clauses and
n
variables, we construct a degree-2 polynomial
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
using the following twin duplication strategy.

Definition 4.3

(Twin Polynomial)
.

Let
f_{\Phi}
be the polynomial from
Section

3.1
, and fix sufficiently large integers
M\leq Nm
. We construct the
twin polynomial

f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
over an expanded symbol set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
as follows. Equivalently, we specify the quadratic support of
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
: if the same quadratic monomial is produced more than once by the rules below, it appears only once in
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
.

1.

Replace the base symbol
b
with
N\cdot m
copies:
\{b^{(1)},b^{(2)},\dots,b^{(Nm)}\}
.

2.

Replace every other symbol
s\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\{b\}
with
M
copies:
\{s^{(1)},s^{(2)},\dots,s^{(M)}\}
.

3.

For each set of copies, add a “
clique constraint
”:

\text{For }b:\quad\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq Nm}b^{(i)}b^{(j)},\qquad\text{For }s\neq b:\quad\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq M}s^{(i)}s^{(j)}.

4.

Replace each
quadratic
term
uv
in
f_{\Phi}
(
u,v\neq b
) with a “
matching
” only:

uv\;\mapsto\;\sum_{1\leq i\leq M}u^{(i)}v^{(i)}.

5.

Replace each
cubic
term
b\,u\,v
in
f_{\Phi}
with a “
tripartite complete graph
”:

b\,u\,v\;\mapsto\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq Nm\\
1\leq j\leq M\end{subarray}}b^{(i)}u^{(j)}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq Nm\\
1\leq k\leq M\end{subarray}}b^{(i)}v^{(k)}+\sum_{1\leq j,k\leq M}u^{(j)}v^{(k)}.

The resulting polynomial
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
has degree 2.

The crucial geometric insight is that “clique constraints” prevent more than two copies from sharing a wire, hence ensure the vast majority of copies “survive” as isolated symbols among symbols in the clique.

Lemma 4.3

(Clique Survival)
.

Let
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{r}\}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
be a set of expanded symbols so that a “clique constraint”
\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq r}s_{i}s_{j}
appears in
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
.
Then in any circuit realizing
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
, at most
2
of
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{r}\}
can share a qubit wire with another symbol in
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{r}\}
.

Proof.

First suppose three symbols
s_{i},s_{j},s_{k}
all lie on the same wire.
Assume the order of them along the wire is
s_{i}-s_{j}-s_{k}
(possibly connected with Hadamard gates).
Then, the term
s_{i}s_{k}
cannot be realized:
s_{i}
and
s_{k}
are non-adjacent on the wire, and CZ gates can only connect symbols on
distinct
wires.

Next, suppose four symbols
s_{i_{1}},s_{i_{2}}
lie on the same wire, while
s_{j_{1}},s_{j_{2}}
lie on another wire (with order from left to right). Then clearly the terms
s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}
and
s_{j_{1}}s_{j_{2}}
force adjacency realization of both pairs. Without loss of generality, assume in the circuit realizing
f^{\prime}_{\Phi}
, the Hadamard gate between
s_{i_{1}}
and
s_{i_{2}}
is applied before the Hadamard gate between
s_{j_{1}}
and
s_{j_{2}}
. Then clearly
\mathsf{supp}(s_{i_{1}})
and
\mathsf{supp}(s_{j_{2}})
are disjoint, so the term
s_{i_{1}}s_{j_{2}}
cannot be realized.

Hence, we conclude that at most two symbols from the clique constraint can share a wire with another symbol from the same set.
∎

(Surviving a Single Cubic Term)

Consider a cubic term

in the original polynomial

4.2.2
Completeness and Soundness

We now establish the wire count bounds for

.

Lemma 4.6

(Twin Polynomial Completeness)
.

If
\Phi
has a satisfiable assignment, then there exists a circuit realizing

using at most
(6M+N)m
qubit wires.

Proof.

Construct
M
parallel copies of the circuit from
Theorem

3.8
, but first add only the Hadamard gates in the temporal order of the original circuit (the same Hadamard gates in different copies are placed close to one another with small perturbations), one for each twin index

. Hence, the Hadamards already realize all quadratic terms arising from the original quadratic terms in

(note that
Theorem

3.8
deals with perfect completeness). We place each remaining base symbol copy

, for

, on its own additional wire.

Next, we add CZ gates across copies: for any cubic term
b\,u\,v
in
f_{\Phi}
, we add CZ gates between all copies of

across all indices to realize the tripartite complete graphs in

. As the circuit from
Theorem

3.8
can implement the CCZ gate for

, and we parallelize
M
copies, all current supports of

intersect for all

. Thus the CZ gates can be added as desired.

Finally, for the “clique constraints”, we can add CZ gates between all pairs of copies of each symbol on their respective wires using the same reasoning.

Total wires:
M\times(6m+1)+Nm-M=(6M+N)m
.
∎

Lemma 4.7

(Twin Polynomial Soundness)
.

and

be sufficiently large. If every assignment to
\Phi
satisfies at most

Step 1: Surviving copies enforce the original structure.

M-96
indices

such that all 18 symbols in

have their

-th copy surviving

The key observation is that, given a clause

3.2

We now apply the counting argument from
Theorem

4.1
to the surviving clause copies.

By the definition of global surviving, surviving copies of the same symbol cannot share wires. Furthermore, for different

.

∎

with bags of size at most
k+1
, and hence width at most
k
, where
k
is the number of wires. We formalize this observation in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

If there exists a valid
k
-qubit circuit realizing the associated polynomial
f
, then the connection graph
G_{f}
(vertices are
\mathcal{S}
and edges correspond to pairs appearing in some monomial in
f
) has pathwidth at most
k
.

Proof.

We can explicitly construct a path decomposition of
G_{f}
by simulating the greedy extension procedure described in
Section

5.1
. Since there exists a valid
k
-qubit circuit realizing the associated polynomial
f
, the condition in
Section

5.1
is satisfied: the witness sets
S_{\textnormal{head}}
and
S_{\textnormal{tail}}
correspond to the symbols at the start and end of each wire in this circuit.

Let
S_{0},S_{1},\dots,S_{n-k}
be the sequence of the set
\mathsf{CurrentHeads}
throughout the execution of the algorithm, where
S_{0}=S_{\textnormal{head}}
. Note that when a wire is completed (i.e., its head symbol is in
S_{\textnormal{tail}}
), the corresponding symbol is removed from
\mathsf{CurrentHeads}
and not replaced. However, we still include it in the sets
S_{t}
for the purpose of constructing the path decomposition. Thus, each
S_{t}
has size exactly
k
for all
0\leq t\leq n-k
.

In each step
t\in\{1,\dots,n-k\}
, the algorithm selects a vertex
v\in S_{t-1}
and replaces it with its neighbor
u
on the same wire, resulting in
S_{t}=(S_{t-1}\setminus\{v\})\cup\{u\}
.

We define the bags of the path decomposition by setting
B_{0}=S_{0}
and, for
1\leq t\leq n-k
,
B_{t}=S_{t-1}\cup S_{t}=S_{t-1}\cup\{u\}
, where
u
is the symbol introduced at step
t
. Since
|S_{0}|=k
and
|S_{t-1}|=k
, every bag has size at most
k+1
. Thus, the width of this decomposition is at most
k
.

We now verify the properties of path decomposition:

•

Vertex Cover:
Every symbol in
\mathcal{S}
is eventually assigned to a wire. It starts as a member of
S_{\textnormal{head}}
or enters
\mathsf{CurrentHeads}
at some step. Since every symbol is in some
S_{t}
, it belongs to at least one bag (using
B_{0}=S_{0}
for the initial symbols).

•

Edge Cover:

Consider any edge in
G_{f}
. If it connects
v
and
u
where
u
follows
v
on the same wire, then this edge is covered in the step
t
where
v
is replaced by
u
, as
\{v,u\}\subseteq B_{t}
.
If the edge connects
x
and
y
on different wires, consider the first step
t
at which at least one of
x,y
belongs to
\mathsf{CurrentHeads}
. If both are already in
S_{t}
, the edge is covered by
B_{t}
(with
B_{0}=S_{0}
when
t=0
). Otherwise, assume without loss of generality that
x\in S_{t}
and
y
has not yet entered
\mathsf{CurrentHeads}
. If
x
is already in
S_{\textnormal{tail}}
, then it remains in all subsequent
S_{j}
for
j\geq t
. If not, then
x
also remains in subsequent
S_{j}
until
y
enters
\mathsf{CurrentHeads}
; otherwise
x
would have at least two neighbors among untouched symbols, namely
y
and the next symbol on
x
’s wire, contradicting the greedy extension condition. Eventually all symbols enter
\mathsf{CurrentHeads}
, so there is a step
t^{\prime}\geq t
where both
x
and
y
are in
S_{t^{\prime}}
, and the edge is covered by
B_{t^{\prime}}
.

•

Running Intersection:
For any symbol
w
, the set of steps where
w\in S_{t}
forms a contiguous interval (it enters once and then either leaves once or remains as a retained completed tail). Since
B_{t}=S_{t-1}\cup S_{t}
, the set of bags containing
w
also forms a contiguous subsegment of the path.

Therefore,
(B_{0},B_{1},\dots,B_{n-k})
is a valid path decomposition of
G_{f}
with width at most
k
.
∎

With the pathwidth (and hence treewidth) upper bound at hand, establishing that
CircuitWidth
is
FPT
becomes a direct consequence of Courcelle’s Theorem
[
9
]
. The existence of a valid partition into
k
wires can be expressed in Monadic Second-Order logic (
\textnormal{MSO}_{2}
) over the natural edge-labelled incidence structure of the polynomial. Specifically, we check for the existence of
k
disjoint ordered paths whose direct wire adjacencies correspond to quadratic monomials of
f
, and such that when the temporal constraints induced by all quadratic and cubic terms of
f
are added, the resulting graph
\mathcal{G}_{H}
(as defined in
Section

5
) contains no directed cycles. Then, Courcelle’s Theorem implies that this property can be checked in linear time, where the parameter is the treewidth of the graph and also the size of the
\textnormal{MSO}_{2}
formula. Note that the size of the formula depends only on
k
(the number of wires). Moreover, one can first use a fixed-parameter tractable algorithm to determine whether the treewidth of
G_{f}
is at most
k
(for example, use the exact treewidth algorithm in
[
5
]
), return “No” if the treewidth exceeds
k
. Otherwise, apply Courcelle’s Theorem to decide the problem in linear time, where the hidden constant depends on
k
. This shows that
CircuitWidth
is
FPT
.

This already answers the question affirmatively. However, since the hidden constant in Courcelle’s Theorem can be multi-exponential, we would like to provide a more concrete dynamic programming algorithm that solves the problem with a more practical dependence on
k
, specifically
k^{O(k)}
. The first step is to compute a tree decomposition of
G_{f}
with width promised to be bounded by
k
.

As mentioned, there exist exact algorithms for treewidth that are fixed-parameter tractable. Bodlaender
[
5
]
provided an exact algorithm for computing treewidth in time
2^{O(k^{3})}n
. Korhonen et al.
[
24
]
improve this runtime to
2^{O(k^{2})}n^{4}
.

For our purposes, faster approximation algorithms suffice and are better for the dependence on
k
. Bodlaender et al.
[
4
]
provided a
5
-approximation algorithm for treewidth that runs in time
2^{O(k)}n
. More recently, Korhonen
[
25
]
presented a single-exponential time
2
-approximation algorithm for treewidth. We state the result below.

Theorem 5.6

(Theorem 1.1 in
[
25
]
)
.

There is an algorithm that, given an
n
-vertex graph
G
and an integer
k

either outputs a tree decomposition of

of width at most
2k+1
or determines that the
treewidth of

is larger than
k
.

By using the
2
-approximation algorithm from
Theorem

5.6
with parameter
k

with width

in time

2.4.1
, we can assume a nice tree decomposition of

with width

is provided.

Now, we can solve

Theorem 5.7

(Main Algorithm)
.

Given a degree-3 polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[\mathcal{S}]
with no constant term on
n

, we can solve the search version of
\textsc{CircuitWidth}{(f,k)}
in time
k^{6k+o(k)}\cdot n+O(m)
.

Proof.

The full algorithm proceeds in the following steps:

1.

Clean up polynomial
: Check
k\leq n
, delete all symbols not appearing in
f
, and remove all quadratic subterms
uv
that appear in a cubic term
uvw
. This takes time
O(m)
.

2.

Construct graph
G_{f}
: From the input polynomial
f
, construct the undirected connection graph
G_{f}
in adjacency list form, where vertices are symbols in
\mathcal{S}
and an edge
(u,v)
included if and only if the product
uv
appears in a monomial of
f
(i.e., as
uv
or
uvw
for some
w
). This takes time
O(m)
.

3.

Compute and Convert to nice tree decomposition
: Use the algorithm from
Theorem

5.6
to compute a tree decomposition of

with width

in time
2^{O(k)}n
. If the algorithm determines that the treewidth is larger than
k
, directly return “No solution” using
Section

5.2
. Then, using
Section

2.4.1
, convert the tree decomposition to a nice tree decomposition with width
w
and
O(wn)=O(kn)
nodes in time
O(w^{2}n)=O(k^{2}n)
.

4.

Dynamic programming
: Run the DP algorithm on the nice tree decomposition. At each node
x
:

•

Let
b_{x}=|B_{x}|\leq w+1
. The number of possible ordered partitions of the
b_{x}
boundary symbols into
k
ordered wire lists is at most
b_{x}!\binom{b_{x}+k}{k}=k^{2k+o(k)}
, using
b_{x}\leq 2k+2
.

•

The number of possible edge types
\tau
between consecutive symbols is at most
3^{b_{x}+k}=2^{O(k)}
.

•

The number of possible topological orderings
\mathcal{O}_{x}
on at most
2b_{x}+2\leq 4k+6
virtual vertices (with global minimum

and maximum

) is at most
(2b_{x}+2)!=k^{4k+o(k)}
.

•

Therefore,
|\mathcal{D}_{x}|\leq k^{6k+o(k)}
.

For each node type:

•

Leaf node
: Initialize in constant time.

•

Introduce node
: For each state in
\mathcal{D}_{y}
, try
O(k)
positions to insert the new symbol, enumerate
O(1)
edge type combinations, and check topological ordering consistency in time
O(k^{2})
. Total:
|\mathcal{D}_{y}|\cdot O(k^{3})
time per node.

•

Forget node
: For each state in
\mathcal{D}_{y}
, update in time
O(k)
. Total:
|\mathcal{D}_{y}|\cdot O(k)
time per node.

•

Join node
: Index states of each child by their ordered boundary lists and topological ordering. For each state from
\mathcal{D}_{y}
, the compatible choices in
\mathcal{D}_{z}
differ only in the edge-type vector, so they can be checked in
2^{O(k)}\cdot k^{2}
time. Total:
|\mathcal{D}_{y}|\cdot 2^{O(k)}\cdot k^{2}
time per node.

Since
|\mathcal{D}_{x}|=k^{6k+o(k)}
for all nodes

, and there are
O(kn)
nodes, the total DP time is
k^{6k+o(k)}\cdot 2^{O(k)}\cdot O(k^{2}\cdot kn)=k^{6k+o(k)}\cdot n
.

5.

Verify and construct circuit
: At the root node
r
, the bag
B_{r}
is empty (by the nice tree decomposition property). Check if
\mathcal{D}_{r}
is non-empty. If
\mathcal{D}_{r}=\emptyset
, return “No solution”.

If
\mathcal{D}_{r}\neq\emptyset
, then there exists a realizable state
(\mathcal{W}_{r},\mathcal{O}_{r})
for the entire

. Since

, the boundary wire structure
\mathcal{W}_{r}
has all

(s,e)
; the type on such an endpoint pair may be

if that wire contains forgotten symbols. The topological ordering
\mathcal{O}_{r}
only orders the virtual vertices

. The realizability of
(\mathcal{W}_{r},\mathcal{O}_{r})
guarantees that there exists a topological ordering on the constraint graph

, which by

implies the existence of a quantum circuit on

.

To extract the circuit, we trace back through the DP table from the root to the leaves:

•

At the root

, pick any state
(\mathcal{W}_{r},\mathcal{O}_{r})\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
.

•

For each node

with children

(and possibly

(and

•

At introduce nodes where symbol

•

The traceback reconstructs the full ordered wire lists. We then run the check/construction procedure from

This extraction takes

The total time complexity is dominated by the DP step, giving the desired

6
Conclusion and Future directions

In this paper, we have conducted a comprehensive complexity-theoretic study of
the quantum circuit width problem.
Our results establish both strong computational hardness barriers and efficient
algorithmic solutions under an appropriate and natural parameterization.

On the hardness side, we proved that determining whether the width
w(f)
of a
degree-3 polynomial
f
is at most
k
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete, settling Montanaro’s
open question in the negative
[
29
]
.
Furthermore, we showed that even approximating the width within a constant
factor of
\tfrac{49}{48}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard, ruling out efficient approximation
algorithms.
Notably, this approximation hardness persists even when restricted to degree-2
polynomials, establishing hardness for a broad class of quantum gate sets
including
\{H,\mathrm{CS}\}
and Clifford
+T
.
Under the Exponential Time Hypothesis, we obtained a conditional lower bound of

2^{\Omega(n)}
for any algorithm solving
CircuitWidth
.

On the algorithmic side, we developed a nondeterministic polynomial-time algorithm with witness complexity
O(\log_{2}\binom{n}{k})=O(k\log(en/k))
bits, yielding an XP algorithm with runtime
\binom{n}{k}^{2}\operatorname{poly}(n)
via enumeration. When
k=\Theta(n)
, we obtain a deterministic algorithm by enumerating witnesses that achieves runtime
2^{O(n)}
, matching the ETH lower bound up to constant factors in the exponent. More significantly, we proved that
CircuitWidth
is fixed-parameter tractable with respect to parameter
k
, presenting an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm with runtime
k^{6k+o(k)}\cdot n
. This algorithm exploits the bounded pathwidth structure of the connection graph and employs dynamic programming over tree decompositions.

Together, these results situate the width problem firmly in classical complexity theory, demonstrating that width optimization is computationally intractable in the worst case, yet tractable when the target width is sufficiently small. This suggests that width behaves more like a structural parameter (akin to treewidth) rather than a measure of intrinsic quantum computational power.

Future directions.

Several interesting questions remain open:

1.

Approximation hardness for larger constants and restricted degree-2 instances.
Our inapproximability result establishes a hardness factor of
\tfrac{49}{48}\approx 1.02
. Can this be improved to a larger constant? A fundamental limitation of our approach is that the reduction encodes Boolean satisfiability using
geometric constraints
on segment overlaps. This geometric encoding strategy is inherently limited to Boolean variables and cannot naturally extend to constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs) over larger alphabets. Consequently, standard PCP-based hardness-of-approximation techniques for CSPs
[
18
,
13
]
do not directly apply to our setting. Obtaining inapproximability with arbitrarily large constant factors may require fundamentally different encoding mechanisms or new connections between quantum circuit geometry and combinatorial optimization. Closely related to this is the question of better characterizing the family of degree-2 polynomials used in our hardness reduction. Our degree-2 result is obtained through a specific twin-construction that simulates cubic overlap constraints using quadratic interactions. It would be very interesting to understand whether the same hardness persists for cleaner or more natural subclasses of quadratic polynomials, or equivalently, whether one can impose meaningful restrictions on the associated family of quantum circuits while preserving hardness.

2.

Optimality of the FPT runtime.
Our
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm achieves runtime
k^{6k+o(k)}\cdot n
. Can this be improved to single-exponential time
2^{O(k)}\cdot n
, or is there a matching lower bound? This question is particularly intriguing in light of a similar work on the
Directed Feedback Vertex Set
problem parameterized by treewidth
[
27
]
. That work presents an algorithm with similar structure to ours—maintaining compatible topological orderings on tree decomposition bags—yet proves a lower bound showing that no algorithm with runtime
t^{o(t)}
exists (where
t
is the treewidth) under the Exponential Time Hypothesis, via a reduction from
k
-CNF satisfiability with
k
-permutation constraints. It remains unclear whether their reduction technique can be adapted to
CircuitWidth
, or whether our problem admits a faster single-exponential algorithm. Resolving this question would provide valuable insights into the fine-grained complexity landscape of parameterized quantum circuit problems.

3.

Tractable special cases and the boundary of hardness.
While our results suggest that minimum circuit width is intractable in the worst case (unless
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}
), they do not rule out broad structured subclasses of polynomials for which the problem may remain tractable. Montanaro already identified several special cases in which the minimum circuit width can be found efficiently, even classically
[
29
]
. It would therefore be valuable to better understand the boundary between these easy cases and the hard instances constructed here. In particular, one may ask whether the techniques developed in this paper point to additional tractable families—for example, classes of polynomials whose overlap structure is sufficiently rigid that a minimum-width realization can be recovered directly, or classes of associated circuits constrained to particularly simple architectures. Any such characterization would complement our worst-case hardness results and could be especially relevant for practical compilation problems.

and
v
. Since
v\notin Y
, the node
z
is not in the subtree
T_{y_{i}}
. On the other hand, since
u\in Y
, there is a node
z^{\prime}\in V(T_{y_{i}})
such that
u\in B_{z^{\prime}}
. The path in
T
from
z
to
z^{\prime}
must pass through
x
, because
T_{y_{i}}
is connected to the rest of the rooted tree through the edge
xy_{i}
.

The bags containing
u
form a connected subtree of
T
by the running-intersection property. This connected subtree contains both
z
and
z^{\prime}
, since
u\in B_{z}\cap B_{z^{\prime}}
. Therefore it contains every node on the path from
z
to
z^{\prime}
, in particular
x
. Hence
u\in B_{x}
, contradicting
u\in Y\setminus B_{x}
. Thus no such edge exists.

It remains to prove the consequence for two distinct children. Let
i\neq j
. First note that

(V_{y_{i}}\setminus B_{x})\cap(V_{y_{j}}\setminus B_{x})=\emptyset.

Indeed, if a vertex
w
belonged to both
V_{y_{i}}
and
V_{y_{j}}
, then there would be bags containing
w
in both child subtrees. The path between these two bags passes through
x
, so the running-intersection property would imply
w\in B_{x}
. Hence no vertex outside
B_{x}
can belong to both child subtrees.

Therefore

V_{y_{j}}\setminus B_{x}\subseteq V(G)\setminus V_{y_{i}}.

Applying the first part with
Y=V_{y_{i}}
shows that there is no edge between
V_{y_{i}}\setminus B_{x}
and
V_{y_{j}}\setminus B_{x}
.
∎

A.2
Acyclicity of Graph Union with Consistent Orderings

Lemma A.2
.

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be directed acyclic graphs with vertex sets
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, and let
V_{0}=V_{1}\cap V_{2}
. If there is a total order
\mathcal{O}_{0}
on
V_{0}
that is consistent with both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
in the sense of
Definition

2.11
, then
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
is acyclic and admits a topological ordering extending
\mathcal{O}_{0}
. Conversely, if
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
is acyclic, then the restriction of any topological ordering of
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
to
V_{0}
is consistent with both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.

Proof.

First assume that the same total order
\mathcal{O}_{0}
on
V_{0}
is consistent with both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. Suppose for contradiction that
G=G_{1}\cup G_{2}
contains a directed cycle. Since
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are individually acyclic, this cycle must use edges from both graphs. Decompose the cycle into maximal nonempty directed subpaths that lie entirely in
G_{1}
or entirely in
G_{2}
. The endpoints of every such maximal subpath lie in the common boundary
V_{0}
, because a switch from one graph to the other can occur only at a vertex that belongs to both vertex sets.

If such a subpath goes from
a\in V_{0}
to
b\in V_{0}
, then
a\neq b
since the corresponding graph is acyclic. By consistency of
\mathcal{O}_{0}
with that graph, we have

a<_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}b.

Traversing all maximal subpaths around the directed cycle therefore gives a strict chain

a_{1}<_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}a_{2}<_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}\cdots<_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}a_{r}<_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}a_{1},

which is impossible. Hence
G
is acyclic.

It remains to show that
G
has a topological ordering extending
\mathcal{O}_{0}
. Write the boundary order as

v_{1}<_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}v_{2}<_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}\cdots<_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}v_{s}.

Add the directed edges
v_{i}\to v_{i+1}
for
i=1,\dots,s-1
, and call the resulting graph
G^{+}
. We claim that
G^{+}
is acyclic. Indeed, if
G^{+}
contained a directed cycle, then since
G
is already acyclic, the cycle would use at least one added boundary edge. Between consecutive added boundary edges, the cycle follows directed paths in
G
with endpoints in
V_{0}
. Decomposing these paths into maximal subpaths inside
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
and using the same consistency argument as above, every such path is increasing with respect to
\mathcal{O}_{0}
. The added boundary edges are also increasing with respect to
\mathcal{O}_{0}
. Thus the cycle would again give a strict increasing cycle in the total order
\mathcal{O}_{0}
, impossible. Therefore
G^{+}
is acyclic.

Any topological ordering of
G^{+}
is a topological ordering of
G
and extends
\mathcal{O}_{0}
, since the added edges force

v_{1}<v_{2}<\cdots<v_{s}.

Conversely, suppose
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
is acyclic. Then any topological ordering of the union restricts to an order on
V_{0}
. Every directed path in either
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
is also a directed path in the union, so the restricted order is consistent with both graphs.
∎
