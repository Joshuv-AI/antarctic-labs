An Accelerometer‐Equipped Running Watch Provides a Valid Estimate of Peak Vertical Ground Reaction Force and Cumulative Weighted Damage: A Cross‐Sectional Laboratory Study
Authors: Richard W. Willy, Isabel S. Moore, John Leske, Tiffany Polson, Brent C. Ruby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe validated a wearable accelerometer and running watch for estimating peak vertical ground reaction force (vGRF) and cumulative weighted damage (CWD). First, 18 well‐trained runners of equal sex distribution (3.3 ± 0.3 m/s habitual training pace) ran a range of speeds (2.7–4.0 m/s, plus training pace) on an instrumented treadmill as reference standard vGRF and wearable data were collected. Wearable data were inputted into a published equation to estimate peak vGRF. The wearable and equation systematically underestimated peak vGRF compared with the instrumented treadmill (mean bias: −0.22 BWs with LOA: −0.44 to 0.01; 9.4–10.1%RMSE). A linear regression model was used to derive a corrected equation to improve the estimate of peak vGRF. In the validation stage, the wearable and corrected equation were validated in 10 runners in an independent sample of equal sex distribution (3.2 ± 0.3 m/s habitual training pace) yielding excellent relationships and trivial errors for peak vGRF versus the reference standard (ICC3,1 = 0.87–0.97; p < 0.001; mean bias −0.008 BWs with LOA: −0.15 to 0.14 BWs; 1.5%–4.0% RMSE). To illustrate how these methods can be applied to runners at risk for a bone stress injury, CWD per km was estimated, resulting in trivial errors by the wearable versus the instrumented treadmill (ICC3,1 = 0.85–0.97; p < 0.001; 1.5–4.1%RMSE). In summary, a commercial wearable and corrected equation were valid estimates of peak vGRF and CWD. These methods have potential for monitoring training loads in runners at risk for bone stress injuries. An online calculator is provided to estimate peak vGRF and CWD from the running watch and wearable.

Introduction

Running carries a high risk of overuse injuries, with an overall incidence of 40% (Kakouris et al. 2021). Applied biomechanical loads during training are thought to contribute to the multifactorial nature of running related injuries (Paquette et al. 2020; Kalkhoven et al. 2021; Kiernan et al. 2018). Recent advances in wearable devices and technology, including inertial measurement units, global positioning systems (GPS), accelerometers, and pressure insoles, enable continuous real‐world monitoring of running biomechanics (Kiernan et al. 2018; Napier et al. 2021; Moore and Willy 2019; Mason et al. 2023). These devices have potential applications in large‐scale epidemiological studies on running‐related injuries, data to inform personalized training prescription, and rehabilitation programs for runners.

Among the biomechanical metrics that can be assessed with wearable devices, peak vertical ground reaction force (vGRF) has been shown to relate to musculoskeletal injuries in runners (Kiernan et al. 2018). For instance, a high peak vGRF prospectively identifies cross country runners at risk for bone stress injury (Martin et al. 2022; Kliethermes et al. 2021) and NCAA track athletes who develop running related injuries (Kiernan et al. 2018). During level running, peak vGRF is strongly related to peak internal tibial bone forces (r = 0.97) (Matijevich et al. 2019), as well as patellofemoral and tibiofemoral joint loads (Saxby et al. 2016; Lenhart et al. 2013). Notably, the tibia and knee are among the most common anatomical sites for running‐related injuries (Kakouris et al. 2021). A wearable capable of estimating peak vGRF during could potentially be used to better understand training‐related loads associated with injuries to these sites. Because wearable devices measure biomechanical loads during real‐world running, they are also capable of estimating cumulative biomechanical loads (CL), operationally defined as applied biomechanical loads over a running distance (e.g., cumulative peak vGRF/km) (Kiernan et al. 2018; Edwards 2018; Backes et al. 2020). For instance, the quantification of CL in runners during routine training may provide insight into the epidemiology of running‐related injuries (Kalkhoven et al. 2021; Warden et al. 2021a; Hoenig et al. 2022). In rehabilitation settings, tracking CL would be valuable to individualize return to run programs for injured runners.

Wearable devices capable of estimating peak vGRF with acceptable validity are available, but adoption at scale remains limited, as these systems are typically research‐grade and require substantial technical expertise or other limitations. For instance, research‐grade pressure‐sensing insoles provide valid estimates of peak vGRF (Burns et al. 2019; Honert et al. 2022), But, the real‐world use of pressure‐sensing insoles is not without issue. Various amounts of cushioning across running shoe models can lead to an underestimation of vGRF by pressure insoles due to shoe deformation during loading (Lambrich et al. 2023). Signal drift, slippage of pressure insoles inside the shoe, and loss of available shoe volume by the added insole also impair their accuracy and comfort over longer runs, potentially altering running mechanics or affect foot‐ground proprioceptive feedback (Cramer et al. 2022; DeBerardinis et al. 2018). Research‐grade single (Kiernan et al. 2018; Gurchiek et al. 2017) or multiple inertial measurement units (Scheltinga et al. 2023) also provide valid estimates of peak vGRF, but their setup and analysis typically exceed the technical expertise of most runners and clinicians.

Commercially available wearable devices offer many advantages over research‐grade wearables, such as lower cost, ease of use and enhanced wearing comfort for the runner, and scalability for clinical practice and epidemiological studies (Mason et al. 2023). For example, running watches can integrate GPS information with biomechanical data from a paired biomechanical sensor, enabling precise, real‐world monitoring of running biomechanics at scale (Moore and Willy 2019). To date, only one study assessed the validity of a commercial wearable's CL estimate of cumulative peak vGRF/km, resulting in 44% of unexplained variance compared with the reference standard measure of CL (Backes et al. 2020). More accurate devices and methods are needed to provide actionable data for researchers, coaches, and athletes.

Ideally, a wearable would not only estimate CL with greater validity but also account for the non‐linear relationship between load and tissue fatigue using a cumulative weighted damage (CWD) model (Kalkhoven et al. 2021; Edwards 2018). CWD models account for the non‐linear relationship between loading magnitude and tissue fatigue and microdamage, Specifically, increases in loading magnitude have a greater influence than the total number of loading cycles (i.e., steps) on tissue fatigue and microdamage (Edwards 2018). Applied practically, faster running increases load magnitude per step, resulting in disproportionately greater tissue fatigue and microdamage despite requiring fewer steps to run the same distance at a slower pace (Firminger et al. 2020). This non‐linear response can be accounted by using a tissue‐specific weighting factor that is, specific to bone or tendon (Edwards 2018; Miner 1945). In a prior study that used a research‐grade, waist‐mounted accelerometer to estimate CWD, collegiate runners who would go on to develop an injury accumulated greater CWD than uninjured teammates, despite similar running distances (Kiernan et al. 2018). To date, no studies have validated a commercially available wearable device for the estimate of peak vGRF and CWD during running.

This study validated a commercially available wearable accelerometer and running watch for estimating peak vGRF, CL, and CWD. We hypothesized that a chest‐mounted, wearable accelerometer and running watch would provide valid estimates of peak vGRF, CL, and CWD compared with laboratory reference measures. We operationally defined validity as a normalized root mean square error (%RMSE) of ≤ 5%. This threshold was informed by prior wearable‐device validation studies (Burns et al. 2019; Honert et al. 2022; Scheltinga et al. 2023) reporting approximately 5%–8% errors in estimates of peak vGRF from various wearable devices when compared with reference‐standard force plates.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The study was approved by the Institutional Human Subjects Research Board (IRB #77‐18 and IRB#151‐23). Participants provided written and verbal consent prior to study enrollment. To qualify for this study, participants were required to be between the ages of 18–35, consistently running at least 16‐km per week, and no history of lower limb or lumbar surgery. All participants were required to be injury‐free for at least the past 90‐day, with an injury defined as three consecutive days or greater disruption in routine run training due to musculoskeletal pain. Participants were also required to able to run 4.0 m/sec for at least 2 min, with ≥ 8/10 score on a visual analog scale that assessed treadmill comfort (Willy et al. 2016).

Pilot testing demonstrated that inputs from the running watch and chest‐mounted accelerometer, when applied to published equations (Dalleau et al. 2004; Kram and Dawson 1998), systematically underestimated peak vGRF. Thus, this investigation consisted of two phases: Phase 1 derived a corrected equation to address the systematic underestimation of peak vGRF, followed by Phase 2 which validated the corrected equation in an independent sample. Phase 1 enrolled 18 participants (9 males and 9 females) and Phase 2 enrolled an independent sample of 10 participants (5 males and 5 females). Data Collection

Participants wore standardized running shoes (Nike Pegasus, Nike Corporation, Beaverton, OR) and a chest‐mounted wearable (Garmin HRM‐Pro; Figure 1; Olathe, KS, USA). The Garmin HRM‐Pro (mass: 59 g) is a heart rate monitor chest strap equipped with an embedded tri‐axial accelerometer which samples temporospatial data using a proprietary‐protected sampling rate. Data from the HRM‐Pro are transmitted via ANT + to a Garmin running watch (Garmin Fenix 6) equipped with Garmin Running Dynamics compatibility, and eventually uploaded to Garmin Connect for analysis. The HRM‐Pro was worn on the bare skin of the runners' torsos with the sensor positioned in the center of the chest at approximately the level of the xiphoid process. The HRM‐Pro was tightened to ensure a secure fit while avoiding impeding the runner's respiration. The HRM‐Pro does not require a calibration procedure prior to use.

Running trials were conducted on an instrumented treadmill (Bertec USA, Worthington, OH). After a 7‐min progressive warm up on the treadmill, data trials commenced. Running biomechanics were collected as participants ran at their self‐selected running pace (Phase 1: mean 3.3 m/sec ± 0.3; Phase 2: mean 3.2 m/sec ± 0.3), to coincide with each runner's self‐described habitual training pace. Next separate trials were collected at 2.6, 3.0, 3.3, 3.6 and 4.0 m/sec in a randomized order. We did not collect 3.0 m/sec data in Phase 1 because pilot testing suggested that 3.0 m/sec was a typical self‐selected pace. To address this omission, we added a 3.0 m/sec condition as a standardized speed in Phase 2. For each running speed condition, participants ran for 2 min, consisting of a 1‐min accommodation period and a 1‐min data trial. During each trial, ground reaction force data were sampled at 1000‐Hz by the instrumented treadmill (data sampled by MotionMonitor; Innovative Sports Training Inc., Chicago, IL). To minimize transient effects associated with trial initiation, participants reached and maintained the target running speed prior to the start of data recording, which was initiated during steady‐state running at each respective test speed rather than from rest. Garmin HRM‐Pro data were collected simultaneously during each trial via a Garmin watch operated by a member of the research team. Although efforts were made to initiate data acquisition on the motion analysis computer concurrently with the start of recording on the running watch, a temporal offset of approximately 1–2 strides between devices may have occurred. Participants were provided with 2‐min of active rest (1.3 m/sec treadmill walking) between each condition.

Calculation of Reference Standard Biomechanics

Ground reaction force data were exported and processed in a custom LabVIEW program (National Instruments Corporation, Austin, TX). Ground reaction forces were filtered via a low‐pass, recursive, fourth order, Butterworth filter with a 50‐Hz cutoff frequency. Stance was determined across the 60‐s trials using a 20‐N threshold of the vGRF and all gait cycles were retained for analysis (range: 78‐96 strides). Peak vGRF was identified and normalized to body weight (BWs) from the vGRF curve. Step frequency (SPM), flight time (msec), and ground contact time (msec) were determined from the vGRF signal. Step length (m) was determined from step frequency and the treadmill speed.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 29.0 (IBM, Houston, TX, USA). All data were complete with no missing values. Independent t‐tests (α = 0.05) assessed any group differences between the independent samples used in Phase 1 and Phase 2. For each participant, mean values from their 1‐min trials were used in the analyses. Variables of interest were step frequency, ground contact time, flight time, peak vGRF, CL, and CWD. Agreement for peak vGRF, CL, and CWD between the reference standard instrumented treadmill and surrogate values from the HRM‐Pro device and various equations were assessed with Bland‐Altman plots in Microsoft Excel (Redmond, WA, USA) and 95% limits of agreement (LOA) were calculated. The relationships between the reference standard (i.e., instrumented treadmill) and the surrogate measures (i.e., wearable device) were evaluated using a two‐way mixed‐effects, single‐measure intraclass correlation coefficient for absolute agreement (ICC3 , 1) with an α = 0.05. Additionally, RMSEs were determined. Normalized RMSEs (%RMSE) were then calculated as the quotient of RMSE and the reference standard value.

A linear regression‐derived correction was developed to adjust for Equation (5)'s systematic underestimation of peak vGRF in Phase 1. The corrected equation was then applied to the peak vGRF estimated by Equation (4) during Phase 2, followed by the estimate of CL and CWD. In a post hoc analysis, we assessed whether the Phase 1 equation (Equation 5) and the corrected equation (Phase 2) were generalizable to other devices providing valid temporospatial measurements. Both equations were applied to temporospatial data derived from force‐plate measurements collected using the instrumented treadmill to determine whether the initial error originated from the Garmin HRM‐Pro or from the original source equation.

Discussion

This investigation sought to validate a commercially available chest‐mounted accelerometer and GPS‐running watch for the estimate of peak vGRF, CL, and CWD. In Phase 1 of this investigation, we found a systematic underestimation of peak VGRF by the chest‐mounted accelerometer and equations, with corresponding underestimates of the cumulative measures of CL and CWD. We developed a corrected equation to address this error. In Phase 2, the corrected equation resulted in trivial errors by the commercial wearable for estimating peak vGRF in an independent sample (mean peak vGRF error = −0.008 BWs; %RMSE = 1.5–4.0%), with correspondingly small errors for CL and CWD, which met our criteria for validity of ≤ 5%RMSE. These results establish validity of the chest‐mounted accelerometer and GPS‐running watch and the corrected equation for the estimation of peak vGRF, CL, and CWD in runners.

In our final model after Phase 2, the chest‐mounted accelerometer and GPS‐running watch showed high consistency, low RMSEs, and low %RMSEs compared with the instrumented treadmill across all variables. The corrected equation reduced relative RMSE from ∼10% in the derivation phase (Phase 1) to 1.5%–4.4% for peak vGRF and CL, values equal to or lower than those reported for more complex models or other wearable systems. Notably, there is no established %RMSE criterion to establish validity for a wearable device, but the small errors for peak vGRF in our final model met or were less than those in the literature for other wearable devices (Burns et al. 2019; Honert et al. 2022; Scheltinga et al. 2023). RMSE values were low when examining data from all speeds together, with 0.09 BWs RMSE for peak vGRF and 42.1 BWs/km RMSE for CL. In contrast, Backes et al. used a more complex model (including BMI, vertical oscillation, and speed) with the HRM‐Run (an earlier version of the HRM‐Pro wearable used in the current study) but explained only 66% of the variance in CL during overground running, though they did not report mean bias or relative RMSE, limiting direct comparison (Backes et al. 2020). Perhaps contributing to the higher error in Backes et al. was the use of just two run speeds in the development of their model, versus the six running speeds in the current investigation. Research‐grade pressure insoles, with or without the use of recurrent neural networks, also provide valid estimates of peak vGRF but with higher (14%) (Burns et al. 2019) or equivalent errors (3%–8%) (Honert et al. 2022) observed here (1.5%–4.0%). Similarly, a cluster of three research‐grade IMUs yielded relative RMSEs of 6.6%–7.3% versus an instrumented treadmill (Scheltinga et al. 2023), which is slightly higher than the errors achieved in the present study using a commercial wearable and simpler methods.

We also evaluated the device's ability to estimate CWD relative to the instrumented treadmill. In the final model (Phase 2), relative RMSEs were low (1.5%–3.7%), indicating that the chest‐mounted accelerometer and GPS‐running watch provided valid CWD estimates. Relying on CL to assess injury risk can be misleading, as it weights loading magnitude and cycles equally (Edwards 2018). Estimated CWD, however, can vary substantially depending on the load magnitude per step and number of loading cycles, even when total CLs are similar between runs (Kalkhoven et al. 2021; Edwards 2018). For instance, running with a 10% higher step frequency increases the number of loading cycles per km, but reduces the probability of a tibial bone stress injury (based on CWD) by 64.8% due to the 12.2% lower tibial strain per step (Sundaramurthy et al. 2023). Previously, running faster was counterintuitively suggested to reduce injury risk since faster running requires a lower number of loading cycles (i.e., steps) to travel a given distance, resulting in lower CL (Petersen et al. 2015). Yet, (Firminger et al. 2020) and Edwards (Edwards 2018) found that while CL decreases with greater running speed, CWD increases. These results align with our findings, which also found that CL decreased linearly as running speed increased (Table 3: 2.7 m/s: 1214.97 BWs/km; 4.0 m/s: 938.67 BWs/km), whereas CWD increased (2.7 m/s: 6.25 BW6.6/km1/6.6; 4.0 m/s: 6.38 BW6.6/km1/6.6).

Considering the range of running speeds that are used by runners, a wearable device that estimates CWD may be critical in causal injury predictions (Kalkhoven et al. 2021) and during rehabilitation from an injury. Since the focus of this project was to develop a wearable device for use in runners at risk for bone stress injuries across a range of speeds, we used a weighting factor (i.e., 6.6) appropriate for cortical bone (Edwards 2018; Madden and Hawkins 2024; Carter and Caler 1985). However, specific weighting factors can also be used for Achilles tendon (Edwards 2018; Firminger et al. 2020; Wren et al. 2003) and the patellar tendon (Firminger and Edwards 2021). Our corrected equation and the chest‐mounted accelerometer and GPS‐running watch showed high agreement for CWD across a range of running speeds, supporting its use in future epidemiological studies with causal models. For example, a causal model assessing risk of bone stress injuries in runners could combine CWD from a validated wearable with measures of energy intake and energy expenditure to assess risk of relative energy deficiency in sport (REDs), psychosocial measures, and imaging to account for bone density. The corrected equation used with the chest‐mounted accelerometer and GPS‐running watch may also be useful in guiding return to running programs after a bone stress injury, particularly as the runner progresses to faster running speeds and longer distances (Warden et al. 2021b).

The equations used in this study to estimate peak vGRF rely exclusively on valid temporospatial measures, indicating that any accurate wearable device capable of providing these inputs could be applied in a similar manner, as demonstrated by our post‐hoc analysis (Supporting Information S3: Appendix 3). When Equation (5) was applied to temporospatial data derived from instrumented treadmill force plates, the initial error observed with the Garmin HRM‐Pro was also found. This suggests that the initial underestimation in peak vGRF in Phase 1 resulted from the original spring‐mass equation (Dalleau et al. 2004; Kram and Dawson 1998), not due to errors from the temporospatial inputs derived from the wearable. Consistent with this interpretation, applying Equation (7) to temporospatial data derived from either the instrumented treadmill or the HRM‐Pro resulted in comparable peak vGRF estimation errors, with similarly low %RMSE values (HRM‐Pro: 1.5%–4.0%; instrumented treadmill: 1.7%–3.4%). We found the chest‐mounted accelerometer and GPS‐running watch provided valid temporospatial data consistent with prior studies of similar devices. For example, an earlier HRM‐Run version demonstrated acceptable‐to‐high ICCs for step frequency (ICC3,1: 0.931) and ground contact time (ICC3,1: 0.749) (Adams et al. 2016). Other commercially available wearables, such as footpods, have reasonable validity for measuring step frequency and contact time (Garcia‐Pinillos et al. 2021), suggesting they could be used with our peak vGRF equation, though separate validation is needed.

This investigation has several strengths and limitations that should be considered. First, we developed a corrected equation for predicting peak vGRF and validated it in an independent sample, across a range of typical running speeds. We also enrolled an equal number of males and females and did not restrict eligibility due to habitual foot strike patterns (i.e., non‐rearfoot strikers were included), providing the basis to use these findings in a wide scope of runners. Still, the relatively low BMI (∼22 kg/m2) of our samples facilitated a secure attachment of the HRM‐Pro strap to each runner's torso, but these methods should be replicated in runners with higher BMIs. We chose to use a standardized running shoe to control for the effect of running shoes. Further validation of the HRM‐Pro wearable device and corrected equation in runners using a variety of shoe models is needed. All testing was conducted on a level instrumented treadmill to ensure steady‐state running but represents a limitation of this study. However, peak vGRF does not differ between treadmill and overground running (Van Hooren et al. 2020). Caution is nevertheless warranted when applying the HRM‐Pro–derived estimates of peak vGRF, CL, and CWD to non‐level overground running, particularly trail running, which would require additional validation. While peak vGRF is highly correlated (r = 0.97) with peak tibial bone force during level running (Matijevich et al. 2019), the relationships between peak VGRF and internal measures, particularly tibial bone forces, are poor during non‐level running (Matijevich et al. 2019). Level running is the foundation of return to run programs for athletes recovering from injuries as well as the key training method of middle and long distance track athletes, suggesting that the derived equations and the chest‐mounted accelerometer may have the greatest utility in these applications. Our calculations also assumed an error‐free quantification of running speed by using a constant treadmill speed. While GPS running watches are valid estimates of outdoor linear running speed and distances (Scott et al. 2015), CL and CWD errors may occur in the real world due to disruption of GPS signal quality. Data acquisition on the motion analysis computer was initiated at approximately the same time as the start of recording on the running watch but may have resulted in a temporal offset of approximately 1‐2 strides between devices. Given that 78–96 total strides were captured per trial and that analyses were based on mean values across trials, any minor temporal mismatch between the motion capture and watch data is unlikely to have meaningfully influenced the results. Although we adopted a ≤ 5% RMSE criterion to define acceptable error, this threshold does not establish the clinical utility of the device or associated formulas. Future research is needed to determine whether the approach evaluated in this investigation improves clinical outcomes in runners recovering from injury or meaningfully informs epidemiological studies of training load in running populations.

Conclusion

A commercially available GPS running watch and paired chest accelerometer provided high level of agreement for the estimates of peak VGRF, CL, and CWD during running across a range of running speeds. Our proof‐of‐concept analysis suggests these methods may have utility in monitoring training loads in runners at‐risk for bone stress injury. The methods and wearable device in this investigation may be useful in assessing risk of running‐related injuries in large epidemiological studies or in guiding return to run programs in athletes recovering from injury.
