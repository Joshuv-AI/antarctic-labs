Understanding Contextual Recall in Transformers: How Finetuning Enables In-Context Reasoning over Pretraining Knowledge
Bhavya Vasudeva, Puneesh Deora, Alberto Bietti, Vatsal Sharan, Christos Thrampoulidis

Abstract
Transformer-based language models excel at in-context learning (ICL), where they can adapt to new tasks based on contextual examples, without parameter updates. In a specific form of ICL, which we refer to as contextual recall , models pretrained on open-ended text leverage pairwise examples to recall specific facts in novel prompt formats. We investigate whether contextual recall emerges from pretraining alone, what finetuning is required, and what mechanisms drive the necessary representations. For this, we introduce a controlled synthetic framework where pretraining sequences consist of subject-grammar-attribute tuples, with attribute types tied to grammar statistics. We demonstrate that while such pretraining successfully yields factual knowledge, it is insufficient for contextual recall: models fail to implicitly infer attribute types when the grammar statistics are removed in ICL prompts. However, we show that finetuning on tasks requiring implicit inference, distinct from the ICL evaluation, using a subset of subjects, triggers the emergence of contextual recall across all subjects. This transition is accompanied by the formation of low-dimensional latent encodings of the shared attribute type. For mechanistic insight, we derive a construction for an attention-only transformer that replicates the transition from factual to contextual recall, corroborated by empirical validation.



1
Introduction

Transformer-based large language models (LLMs) exhibit remarkable abilities to extrapolate far beyond tasks seen during training. A notable instance of this extrapolation is in-context learning (ICL)
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
, where models can adapt to new tasks based on contextual examples, without parameter updates.

In this paper, we investigate a specific form of ICL, which we refer to as
contextual recall
. Here, a model trained on open-ended text acquires factual knowledge and is later able to recall specific facts when prompted with example pairs in an unseen format. To illustrate, pretraining data might include descriptions of various landmarks, from which the model learns multiple attributes for each—such as the country where it is located, the year it was built, or its architectural style. At test time, the model receives a prompt of the form
[Niagara Falls, Canada. Colosseum, Italy. Parthenon, ]
and must generate
[Greece]
. The prompt contains no explicit indication that the relevant attribute is “country” rather than, say, “year built”; the model must infer the attribute type from the in-context examples and recall the corresponding fact for the query subject.

The ability to succeed at contextual recall therefore requires both the acquisition of factual knowledge and adaptability to novel prompt formats, since the model may never have seen these facts presented as implicit subject-attribute pairs during pretraining. In this work, we investigate the origins of this capability:

Does the ability to do contextual recall emerge naturally from pretraining, or does it necessitate specific finetuning? Furthermore, what mechanisms within the Transformer architecture enable the emergence of this ability?

Prior work has leveraged controlled, synthetic settings to understand both ICL and factual recall in transformers trained from scratch. For ICL, various studies have considered well-defined tasks, such as linear regression
(
Garg et al. 2022
;
Akyürek et al. 2023
;
von Oswald et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Fu et al. 2024
)
, discrete functions
(
Bhattamishra et al. 2024
)
, and Markov processes
(
Bietti et al. 2023
;
Edelman et al. 2024
;
Rajaraman et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2025
;
Deora et al. 2025
)
that involve function induction, but do not require recalling learned factual information. Importantly, and in contrast to our contextual recall task, the train and test data share the sequence formats in these settings. On the other hand, in studies focused on question-answering and recall tasks
(
Allen-Zhu & Li 2023
;
Behnia et al. 2025
;
Nichani et al. 2024b
;
Zucchet et al. 2025
)
, the context contains explicit information (e.g., specific words, or linguistic patterns) required to recall specific learned facts, and does not require the model to perform any implicit inference (e.g., what fact is relevant based on the in-context examples) that is characteristic of ICL. (See
Appendix

A
for a detailed discussion on related work.)

Contributions.

We summarize our contributions below.

First, in
Section

2
, we introduce a controlled synthetic framework to study the emergence of contextual recall in transformers. The pretraining (
PT
​​) sequences, used to instill factual knowledge in the model, contain multiple attributes of a single subject, interspersed with grammar tokens that encode information about each attribute type. The
ICL
sequences, used to evaluate contextual recall ability, consist of subject-attribute pairs sharing a common attribute type across different subjects, without any grammar (see
Fig.

1
for an illustration of the data generation process).

In
Section

3.1
, we show that transformers trained with
PT
sequences succeed on factual recall, but fail to generalize to
ICL
sequences, which require the model to implicitly infer the attribute type from the in-context examples. However, we find that finetuning the model on sequences that are distinct from
ICL
sequences, but require implicit inference, using a subset of subjects, enables out-of-distribution generalization on
ICL
sequences on the held-out subjects (
Section

3.2
). We also probe the effect of some key dataset parameters on the model’s performance on
PT
and
ICL
sequences in
Section

3.3
.

In
Section

3.4
, we analyze model representations and find that finetuning induces the formation of low-dimensional encodings of the shared attribute type (task vectors) based on the in-context examples. These representations become more disentangled as the number of in-context demonstrations increases.

Finally, in
Section

4
, for mechanistic insight, we consider a simpler synthetic task and present constructions for an attention-only model that succeeds on factual recall after pretraining and contextual recall after finetuning, and corroborate them with an analysis of the training dynamics for finetuning as well as empirical validation.

2
Data Generation Process

To study contextual recall in transformers, we define three different types of sequences for pretraining, finetuning, and/or evaluation. The first,
PT
​​, is designed to instill factual knowledge, while the other two,
ICL
and
ICL+Grm
​​, are used to evaluate or facilitate how the model leverages such knowledge for in-context reasoning tasks.

We first introduce some useful notation. Let
\mathcal{S}=\{s_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
denote the set of
M
subjects (e.g., landmarks such as
Parthenon
(
j\!=\!1
),
Colosseum
(
j\!=\!2
)). Let the set of unique attributes be denoted as
\mathcal{U}\!=\!\cup_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathcal{U}_{\ell}
, where
\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\!=\!\{u_{i}^{\ell}\}_{i=1}^{M_{\ell}}
, and
\ell
indexes the attribute type (e.g.,
\ell\!=\!1
for “country”,
\ell\!=\!2
for “year built”), and
M_{\ell}
denotes the number of unique values for index
\ell
(e.g., {
Greece
,
Italy
,
Canada
, …} for “country”). Next, let

\mathcal{A}=\cup_{j=1}^{M}\{a_{j}^{1},\dots,a_{j}^{L}\}
denote the set of attributes assigned to the subjects, where
a_{j}^{\ell}
is the type-
\ell
attribute of subject
j
(e.g.,
a_{2}^{1}\!=\!\texttt{Italy}
). Each subject
s_{j}
has one attribute per type, giving
L
attributes per subject. Let
\mathcal{G}\!=\!\{\tilde{g}_{1},\dots,\tilde{g}_{G}\}
denote a set of
G
grammar tokens, and
[\text{sep}]
denote the separator token. The full vocabulary is
\mathcal{V}\!=\!\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{U}\cup\mathcal{G}\cup\{[\text{sep}]\}
, with size
V\!=\!M\!+\!\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}M_{\ell}\!+\!G\!+\!1
. Let
\operatorname{Unif}(\cdot)
denote the uniform distribution, and
||
denote concatenation.

With this notation established, we now describe the data generation process. The key building block is a
subject
-grammar-attribute subsequence
. For subject
s_{j}
, attribute type
\ell
, and grammar sequence length
S
, this subsequence is denoted as
X_{j}^{\ell}=\bigl[s_{j},g_{1},\dots,g_{S},a^{\ell}_{j}\bigr]
(e.g.,
[\texttt{Parthenon},\textit{one},\textit{of},\textit{the},\textit{wonders},\dots,\textit{is in},\texttt{Greece}]
).
The grammar sequence
g_{1:S}
is generated using a first-order Markov chain specific to attribute type
\ell
. That is,
p(g_{t}=\tilde{g}|g_{t-1},\dots,g_{1})=p(g_{t}=\tilde{g}|g_{t-1})
for all
\tilde{g}\in\mathcal{G}
, with the first token drawn uniformly at random. For each attribute type
\ell\in[L]
, we sample a row-stochastic transition matrix
\text{TM}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{G\times G}
, where each row is drawn independently from a Dirichlet prior with parameter
\bm{\alpha}
. Note that since each attribute type has its own Markov chain, the bigram statistics of the grammar sequence
g_{1:S}
implicitly encode information about the attribute type
\ell
.

We now define the three types of sequences used for pretraining, finetuning, and/or evaluation; see
Fig.

1
for an illustration.

PT
Sequences (Pretraining).

To instill factual knowledge in the model, we use
PT
sequences which contain information about a specific
subject
and its associated attributes (analogous to a short encyclopedia entry for a given landmark). Let
N_{\text{tr}}
denote the number of subsequences (subject-grammar-attribute tuples) in each sequence. To generate a
PT
sequence, we first sample a subject
j\!\sim\!\operatorname{Unif}([M])
, then draw
N_{\text{tr}}
attribute types
\ell_{1:N_{\text{tr}}}\!\sim\!\operatorname{Unif}([L])^{N_{\text{tr}}}
, and sample each grammar subsequence
g^{i}_{1:S}
from the corresponding Markov chain
\text{TM}_{\ell_{i}}
. The resulting sequence is
\tilde{X}\!=\!X_{j}^{\ell_{1}}||[\text{sep}]||X_{j}^{\ell_{2}}\dots X_{j}^{\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}}
, with sequence length
T\!=\!(S+3)N_{\text{tr}}\!-\!1
.
To model generic text that does not convey factual information, we also include grammar-only subsequences: with probability
p_{G}
, each subject-grammar-attribute subsequence is replaced by a grammar-only subsequence
g_{1:S+2}
, generated from a separate fixed Markov chain (with transition matrix sampled from a Dirichlet prior). This gives the final sequence
X
.

ICL
Sequences (Finetuning and Evaluation).

To test for contextual recall, we use
ICL
sequences that contain
subject
-attribute pairs with a shared attribute type across different subjects—mirroring the evaluation format from the introduction (e.g., [
Niagara Falls
,
Canada
, [
sep
],
Colosseum
,
Italy
, [
sep
],
Parthenon
, ]). Let
N
denote the number of in-context demonstrations. First, we sample an attribute type
\ell\sim\operatorname{Unif}([L])
, and
N+1
subjects
j_{1:N+1}\sim\operatorname{Unif}([M])^{N+1}
. The resulting
ICL
sequence is
X=[s_{j_{1}},a_{j_{1}}^{\ell},[\text{sep}],\dots,s_{j_{N+1}},a_{j_{N+1}}^{\ell}]
. At evaluation, the model observes
N
subject-attribute pairs and must predict the attribute
a_{j_{N+1}}^{\ell}
for the final subject
s_{j_{N+1}}
, without any grammar cues indicating the attribute type.

ICL+Grm
Sequences (Finetuning).

We also use
ICL+Grm
sequences for finetuning. As the name suggests, these sequences are similar to
ICL
sequences (subject-attribute pairs sharing a common attribute type across different subjects), but they also contain grammar tokens between the subject and attribute. (As we will see in
Section

3.2
, these sequences help instill the implicit inference ability required to succeed at contextual recall on
ICL
sequences.) Let
S_{\text{ft}}
denote the grammar sequence length used in each subsequence, and
N_{\text{ft}}
denote the number of subsequences. We sample attribute type
\ell\sim\operatorname{Unif}([L])
and
subject
s
j_{1:N_{\text{ft}}+1}\sim\operatorname{Unif}([M])^{N_{\text{ft}}+1}
, then generate grammar subsequences
g^{i}_{1:S_{\text{ft}}}
from the corresponding Markov chain
\text{TM}_{\ell}
. The resulting
ICL+Grm
sequence is
X=X_{j_{1}}^{\ell}||[\text{sep}]||X_{j_{2}}^{\ell}\dots X_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}}^{\ell}
. Note that
ICL
sequences are a special case of
ICL+Grm
with
S_{\text{ft}}=0
.

Here, we note that several prior works leverage controlled/synthetic settings to study factual recall or ICL in transformers. In particular, closest to our work are
Nichani et al. 2024b
;
Behnia et al. 2025
, which adopt abstracted setups with subject-grammar-attribute triplets to study factual recall.
Nichani et al. 2024b
uses a setup where the relation (or attribute type) is a single, dedicated token, while other tokens in the grammar sequence are sampled randomly (in
Section

4
, we use a similar setup for mechanistic analysis of contextual recall for an attention-only model). In contrast,
Behnia et al. 2025
propose a setup to more faithfully capture the statistical structure in the grammar sequences, by modeling each template as a randomly sampled Markov chain, but omit the relation token. Our synthetic framework bridges these approaches: inspired by
Behnia et al. 2025
, we model templates using Markov chains, but we retain the relation or attribute type information by associating the Markov chains with specific attribute types. Crucially, as discussed in
Section

1
, both
Nichani et al. 2024b
and

Behnia et al. 2025
focus on
factual recall
,
where the context contains explicit cues about the relevant
attribute type, whereas our focus is on
contextual recall
, which requires the model to infer the attribute type implicitly from in-context examples, that is not captured in prior frameworks. (See App.
A
for a detailed discussion on related work.)

We evaluate the model on two distinct held-out sets: i)

3.2
Finetuning Experiments

Since pretraining on
PT

Specifically, we ask: can a model originally trained to rely on explicit grammar-based cues be adapted to perform implicit inference from in-context examples? To answer this, we finetune the pretrained model using the standard next-token prediction objective on a new data distribution (detailed below). Crucially, to test for generalization, we finetune on only a subset of subjects, reserving the remaining subjects as a held-out set. We then evaluate the model on
ICL

Section

2
) where the query subject belongs to this held-out set. Unless otherwise stated, we set the number of demonstrations
N_{\text{ft}}=16
, and use

Mechanism of Transfer.

Why does finetuning on a subset of subjects enable generalization to the rest? We posit that pretraining and finetuning play distinct but complementary roles. During pretraining, the model
acquires factual knowledge
: given a
PT
subsequence containing
s_{i}
, the model learns to decode the attribute type
\ell
from the grammar statistics and predict the corresponding attribute
a_{i}^{\ell}
. During finetuning, the model does not learn new subject-attribute associations; rather, it learns a new
access mechanism:
inferring the attribute type implicitly from the attribute tokens in the context, rather than from explicit grammar cues. Because there is a shared structure between every
s_{i}
and its type-
\ell
attribute
a_{i}^{\ell}
(across
i\in[M]
), the model can be finetuned on a subset of subjects to learn this implicit inference mechanism and generalize to held-out subjects. We present additional evidence for this mechanism in
Section

4
using a simpler setting.

3.4
Representational Analysis

We now analyze the model’s internal representations to gain insight into the mechanism underlying contextual recall. Specifically, we investigate whether finetuning on
ICL+Grm
sequences induces the formation of a low-dimensional representation that encodes the shared attribute type
\ell
from the in-context examples.

Let
X_{\ell,t}:=[s_{j_{0}},a^{\ell}_{j_{0}},[\text{sep}],s_{j_{1}},a^{\ell}_{j_{1}},[\text{sep}],\dots,s_{j_{t+1}}]
denote an
ICL
sequence for an attribute type
\ell
, with
t\in[N]
demonstrations. For each
\ell\in[L]
, we sample
K
such sequences, denoted
X^{k}_{\ell}
for
k\in[K]
. Let
f_{i}(\cdot)
denote the model’s representation at layer
i
at the last token position
s_{j_{t+1}}
. For fixed
i
and
t
,
we measure the cosine similarity for inter- and intra-task representations, averaged across the
K
contexts for each pair
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in[L]
:

\displaystyle\bar{C}^{t}_{i}(\ell,\ell^{\prime})=\tfrac{1}{K^{2}}\Sigma_{k,k^{\prime}}\cos\!\big(f_{i}(X^{k}_{\ell,t}),\,f_{i}(X^{k^{\prime}}_{\ell^{\prime},t})\big).\vskip-2.84526pt

If the model perfectly disentangles attribute types in its representations, then, for some layer
j
and number of demonstrations
t
, we would have
\bar{C}^{t}_{i}(\ell,\ell^{\prime})\!=\!\mathds{1}\left[\ell\!=\!\ell^{\prime}\right]
. Additionally, we quantify the representation clustering strength,
\bar{S}^{t}_{i}\!\in\![-1,1]
, in terms of a clustering metric using
1-\cos(\cdot,\cdot)
as the distance and attribute-type
\ell
as the cluster label (see App.
B
for details). A high
\bar{S}^{t}
indicates that representations of sequences with shared attribute type are tightly clustered and well-separated from those of different attribute types.

Here, we note that prior work has proposed two main mechanisms for ICL, namely induction heads and task or function vectors, which are low-dimensional representations that encode information about the input-output relations in a given context (see App.
A
for a discussion on related work on task and function vectors for ICL). These results suggest that the formation of these task vectors is the mechanism by which the model succeeds on the contextual recall task in this setting.

Interestingly, while accuracy is comparable in the two settings, higher
Div
during pretraining leads to stronger representation clustering. This suggests that the separation between the Markov chains (determined by
Div
) that encode attribute type information during pretraining, is reflected in the task vector separability and strength after finetuning.

To corroborate this, we use these representations to steer model outputs. Specifically, let
{\bm{v}}_{\ell}:=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{k}f(X^{k}_{\ell,\mathrm{T}})
denote a task vector for attribute type
\ell

Finding 5
: The finetuned model encodes attribute type information from in-context examples in layer-2 attention representations. Clustering strength increases with both the number of in-context examples and the separation between the attribute-specific Markov chains during pretraining.

4
Mechanistic Analysis in a Simpler Setting

To gain mechanistic insight into how pretraining and finetuning contribute to contextual recall, we study an analytically tractable setting that preserves the qualitative behavior observed in Findings 1-2 of
Section

3.1
. Specifically, we simplify in two ways:
i) we encode attribute type information in explicit “relation” tokens rather than grammar sequence statistics, and ii) we use a one-layer attention-only model.
Below, we first describe our setup, then present constructions for accurately predicting the final attribute token on both the
PT
and
ICL
sequences, then analyze the training dynamics for FT, followed by experimental validation for the constructions.

Data Setting.

Let
\mathcal{R}:=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{L}\}
denote a set of relation tokens, one per attribute type. The
PT
sequences are generated in a similar manner to
Section

2
, with a modification to the grammar subsequences. For each subsequence
g_{1:S}
, we first sample a position
i\sim\operatorname{Unif}([S])
and a relation token
r\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{R})
, and set
g_{i}=r
. Then, we sample the remaining entries independently as
g_{i^{\prime}}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{G})
for all
i^{\prime}\neq i
. The
ICL
sequences are generated as in
Section

2
.

Model.

We consider a single-layer attention-only model with fixed relative positional encoding added to the key inputs. The output at position
t
for head
h\in[H]
and the final model output are defined as follows:

\displaystyle g_{h}^{t}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle\!=\!{\bm{X}}_{1:t}^{\top}\bm{\varphi}(({\bm{X}}_{1:t}\!+\!{\bm{P}}_{\mathrm{T}-t+1:\mathrm{T}})\bm{W}_{KQ}^{h}{\bm{x}}_{t})

\displaystyle f^{t}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle\!=\!\sum_{h=1}^{H}\underbrace{\bm{W}_{OV}^{h}g_{h}^{t}({\bm{X}})}_{f_{h}^{t}({\bm{X}})}.

(1)

where
{\bm{X}}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{T}\times d}
denotes the input sequence,
{\bm{x}}_{t}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes its
t^{\text{th}}
row,
{\bm{P}}\!=\![\bm{h}(p_{-\mathrm{T}+1}),\dots,\bm{h}(p_{0})]
denotes the positional encodings, where
p_{-\mathrm{T}+1},\dots,p_{0}
denote the position indices, and
\bm{h}(\cdot)\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the embedding for a vocabulary token or position index. We consider one-hot embeddings and positional encodings and set
d\!=\!V\!+\!\mathrm{T}
, so that these subspaces are orthogonal.
\bm{\varphi}(\cdot)
denotes the softmax. For convenience, we define
\bm{W}_{OV}^{h}\!=\!(\bm{W}_{O}^{h})^{\top}\bm{W}_{V}^{h}
,
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{h}\!=\!(\bm{W}_{K}^{h})^{\top}\bm{W}_{Q}^{h}
, for head
h
using output,value, key, query weight matrices
\bm{W}_{O}^{h},\bm{W}_{V}^{h},\bm{W}_{K}^{h},\bm{W}_{Q}^{h}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}\times d}
. We use the shorthand
f(\cdot)\!=\!f^{\mathrm{T}}(\cdot)
and
g_{h}(\cdot)\!=\!g_{h}^{\mathrm{T}}(\cdot)
to denote the outputs at the last token position.
The model prediction is

\displaystyle v^{*}=\arg\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\bm{h}(v)^{\top}f({\bm{X}}),

where we use fixed embeddings and tied unembeddings.

Construction for
PT
Sequences.

We first investigate whether an attention-only model is expressive enough to perform factual recall on
PT
sequences of the form

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}

\displaystyle={\bm{X}}_{j_{\star}}^{\ell_{1}}||[\bm{h}(a_{j_{\star}}^{\ell_{1}}\!)]||{\bm{X}}_{j_{\star}}^{\ell_{2}}\dots{\bm{X}}_{j_{\star}}^{\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}},\text{where}

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}_{j_{\star}}^{\ell_{1}}\!\!

\displaystyle=\![\bm{h}(s_{j_{\star}}),\bm{h}(g_{1}\!),...,\bm{h}(r_{\ell_{1}}\!),...,\bm{h}(g_{S}\!),\bm{h}([\text{sep}])].

(2)

Here,
{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{T}}=\bm{h}([\text{sep}])
,
j_{\star}\!\sim\!\operatorname{Unif}([M])
denotes the subject index, and the correct last-token prediction is
\bm{h}(a_{j_{\star}}^{\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}})
. We first show that there exists an attention-only model capable of perfectly predicting the next token for such sequences. For simplicity, we focus on last-token prediction here; the construction extends to predictions at any position (see App.
C
).

Proposition 1

(Informal)
.

Consider the input
{\bm{X}}
in
Eq.

2
. There exists a single-layer attention-only model such that when weights
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{h}
are scaled by a sufficiently large
\beta
, it correctly predicts the last token, returning the attribute
a_{j_{\star}}^{\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}}
corresponding to the sequence’s subject
s_{j_{\star}}
and attribute type
\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}
.

Proof Sketch.
We present a construction with a
3
-head model. At a high level, two heads, which we call the
relation
and
subject
heads, are responsible for the prediction at the target position (following
\bm{h}([\text{sep}])
), and the third
grammar
head for other cases. For simplicity, we present the construction here assuming that the output of the grammar head is zero (
f_{\text{grm}}({\bm{X}})\!=\!0
), since the outputs from this head do not affect the conclusions in this case, as shown in the full proof in App.
C
.

First, the
relation head
attends to the most recent relation token
r_{\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}}
and maps it to the sum of
all
attributes of type
\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}
. Specifically,

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}

\displaystyle=\beta\Big(\sum_{\ell}\bm{h}(r_{\ell})+\bm{p}\Big)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\Big(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(r_{\ell})^{\top},

where
\bm{p}:=\sum_{i=1}^{S+2}\bm{h}(p_{-i+1})
With
\beta\to\infty
, the head’s outputs become
g_{\text{rel}}({\bm{X}})=\bm{h}(r_{\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}})
, i.e., the most recent relation token in the sequence, and
f_{\text{rel}}({\bm{X}})=\sum_{j}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}})
,
i.e.
, all attributes of type
\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}
.

On the other hand, the
subject head
attends to the subject token
s_{j_{\star}}
and filters out irrelevant attributes (those not associated with
s_{j_{\star}}
). Specifically,

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}

\displaystyle=\beta\Big(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(s_{j})\Big)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{subj}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j}\Big(\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j,\ell}\bm{h}(a_{j^{\prime}}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(s_{j})^{\top}.

Then, with
\beta\to\infty
, this head outputs
g_{\text{subj}}({\bm{X}})=\bm{h}(s_{j_{\star}})
, the subject token in the sequence, and
f_{\text{subj}}({\bm{X}})=-\Big(\sum_{u}\bm{h}(u)-\sum_{\ell}\bm{h}(a_{j_{\star}}^{\ell})\Big)
,
i.e.
, the negative of all attributes that are not associated with subject
s_{j_{\star}}
.

Combining these outputs, the model isolates the specific attribute
a_{j_{\star}}^{\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}}
, i.e., the attribute reinforced by both heads:
f({\bm{X}})=\sum_{j}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}})+\sum_{\ell}\bm{h}(a_{j_{\star}}^{\ell})-\sum_{u}\bm{h}(u)
, yielding the correct prediction
v^{*}=a_{j_{\star}}^{\ell_{N_{\text{tr}}}}
.

Construction for
ICL
Sequences.

Next, consider
ICL
sequences of the form

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}\!\!=\![\bm{h}(s_{j_{1}}\!),\bm{h}([\text{sep}]),\bm{h}(a^{\ell_{\star}}_{j_{1}}\!),\!...,\bm{h}(s_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}}\!),\bm{h}([\text{sep}])],

(3)

with correct last token prediction
\bm{h}(a^{\ell_{\star}}_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}})
, where
\ell_{\star}\sim\operatorname{Unif}([L])
denotes the shared attribute type. We show that there exists an attention-only model that perfectly predicts the last token for such sequences.

Proposition 2

(Informal)
.

Consider the input
{\bm{X}}
in
Eq.

3
. There exists single-layer attention-only model such that when weights
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{h}
are scaled by a sufficiently large
\beta

s_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}}
and the shared attribute type
\ell_{\star}

Proof Sketch.
We adapt the construction from
Proposition

1
with minimal changes, but with one crucial modification that reflects the role of finetuning.
The
subject
head operates similarly, attending to the query subject and filtering out attributes not associated with it. The key difference lies in the
relation
head: since
ICL
sequences contain no explicit relation tokens, this head must now
infer the attribute type implicitly
by attending to the attribute tokens in the context and mapping them to all attributes of the same type
\ell_{\star}
.

Specifically, for the
subject
head, we set

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}

\displaystyle=\beta\Big(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(s_{j})+{\color[rgb]{0.1758,0.3125,0.6289}\bm{h}(p_{-1})}\Big)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},

(4)

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{subj}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j}\Big(\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j,\ell}\bm{h}(a_{j^{\prime}}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(s_{j})^{\top}.

Note that as compared to the construction for
PT
sequences (
Proposition

1
),
\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{subj}}
, which contains subject-attribute information, is unchanged. The only difference here is that
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}
now contains
\bm{h}(p_{-1})
, so that when
\beta\to\infty

g_{\text{subj}}({\bm{X}})=\bm{h}(s_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}})

i.e.

We now discuss the

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{S}
denote a subset of subjects. Next, for each attribute type
\ell

denote the set of unique attributes seen during finetuning. With this notation established, we set

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}

\displaystyle=\beta\sum_{\ell}\Big(\bm{h}(r_{\ell})+{\color[rgb]{0.1758,0.3125,0.6289}\bm{u}_{\ell}^{\prime}}\Big)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},\quad\bm{u}_{\ell}^{\prime}:=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{\ell}}\bm{h}(u),

(5)

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{\ell}\!\Big(\Big(\!\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\ell}}\bm{h}(u)\Big)\bm{h}(r_{\ell})^{\top}\!+\!{\color[rgb]{0.1758,0.3125,0.6289}\bm{u}_{\ell}^{\prime}\bm{u}_{\ell}^{\prime\top}}\!\Big).\vskip-5.69054pt

In contrast to the construction for
PT

\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}
and
\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}
rely on the relation tokens

i.e.

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{\ell}}\bm{h}(u)
.
In this case, when

,
i.e.

f_{\text{rel}}({\bm{X}})=\sum_{j}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell_{\star}})
, retrieving the sum of all attributes of the shared type
\ell_{\star}

Finally, by combining the outputs from the two heads, the model isolates the specific attribute
a_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}}^{\ell_{\star}}
that is encouraged by both,
i.e.

f({\bm{X}})=\sum_{j}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell_{\star}})+\sum_{\ell}\bm{h}(a_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}}^{\ell})-\sum_{u}\bm{h}(u)

5
Discussion and Conclusion

We studied contextual recall, a form of ICL that requires models pretrained to acquire knowledge about various subjects and associated attributes, to recall a specific attribute for a query subject by implicitly inferring the relevant attribute type based on in-context examples. Using a controlled synthetic framework, we showed that while pretraining only enables factual recall, targeted finetuning on tasks that require implicit inference, but are distinct from the evaluation ICL format, using a subset of subjects, leads to emergence of contextual recall capability on all subjects. Our results give insights into the complementary roles of pretraining and finetuning in enabling in-context reasoning involving learned knowledge.

We believe that our synthetic data setup can be a useful testbed to study several questions. This includes examining the effects of using a mixture of pretraining and finetuning data instead of using them in a stage-wise manner, curriculum learning, non-uniform distributions over the subjects instead of uniform as we use, increasing the number of attribute types, and so on. A key question is to better understand when and how can the representations learned during pretraining be improved to achieve better generalization after finetuning. Another important direction is investigating how incorporating new knowledge via further finetuning might impact the learned contextual recall capability.

Related Work

In this section, we discuss related work on studying ICL and factual recall in transformers using controlled/synthetic settings, as well as on task vectors for ICL.

Factual Recall.

Recent studies have utilized controlled synthetic setups to systematically explore how transformers acquire and recall factual associations.
Allen-Zhu & Li 2023
and
Zucchet et al. 2025
employ synthetic biography datasets, where each entry contains information about an individual or subject in the form of multi-sentence paragraphs. Each sentence associates some fact or attribute with an individual or subject, via a sentence template that contains information about the relation or attribute type, such as a person’s birthplace, in the linguistic structure. They consider a fixed set of templates for each attribute type.
Zucchet et al. 2025
evaluate factual knowledge (
i.e.
, correct attribute token prediction) using similar biography entries that are generated with templates from a held-out set, and identify a stage-wise learning dynamic where the model first learns to predict some attribute token, and then the correct attribute. We also observe similar stage-wise learning in our setup during pretraining in
Section

3.1
. In
Allen-Zhu & Li 2023
, the model is evaluated on question-answering (QA) formats, which constitutes a distribution shift from the biography-style format. Similar to our results in
Section

3.2
, they find that while pretraining is insufficient, finetuning (on QA) with a subset of subjects enables generalization on held-out subjects. Crucially, however, their QA prompts still contain explicit relation or attribute type information (e.g., "What is [subject]’s city of birth?"), whereas our contextual recall task constitutes a more substantial distribution shift as the context does not contain explicit relation cues. Investigating whether we see similar results as in
Section

3.2
for our synthetic setup with synthetic biography data used in these studies would be an interesting direction for future work.

In addition to these works, other studies have adopted abstracted setups with subject-grammar-attribute triplets to study factual recall
(
Nichani et al. 2024b
;
Behnia et al. 2025
)
.
Nichani et al. 2024b
uses a data setup where the relation (or attribute type) is a single, dedicated token, while the rest of the tokens in the grammar sequence are sampled randomly. Using this setup, they show that a one-layer transformer model (with a self-attention layer followed by an MLP) succeeds on factual recall whenever either the total number of self-attention parameters or MLP parameters scales (up to log factors) linearly with the number of facts. In contrast,
Behnia et al. 2025
propose a
setup to more faithfully capture the statistical structure in the grammar sequences,
i.e.
, templates in the controlled language datasets in
Zucchet et al. 2025
;
Allen-Zhu & Li 2023
. They model each template as a randomly sampled Markov chain, and omit the relation token (the facts are independent of the grammar), to study how sequence statistics affect generalization. Interestingly, they show that several phenomena observed with the controlled language datasets in
Zucchet et al. 2025
;
Allen-Zhu & Li 2023
can be reproduced in this abstracted setup.

A key differentiator across these frameworks is how "relation" (or attribute type) information is represented. While in
Nichani et al. 2024b
the relation is a specific token,
Behnia et al. 2025
omits the relation token entirely. Our synthetic framework bridges these approaches: similar to
Behnia et al. 2025
, we model templates using Markov chains, but in contrast to their work, and similar to the other prior studies, we retain the relation information by associating the Markov chains with specific attribute types.

For mechanistic analysis, we adopt an abstracted setup similar to
Nichani et al. 2024b
and compare our attention-only construction for factual recall (
Proposition

1
) with theirs. In both constructions, the relation heads perform a similar role: they attend to the (most recent) relation token in the context to output the sum of all attributes of the relevant type. However, the subject heads behave differently. While the construction in
Nichani et al. 2024b
uses subject heads to boost the attributes associated with the subject present in the context, our construction utilizes subject heads to suppress all attributes not associated with the subject. While we present experimental validation for our construction, we note that changing the initialization seed can lead to the model using a different mechanism, similar to their construction.

As discussed in
Section

1
, while prior works focus on factual recall using explicit cues about the attribute type from the context, our work investigates contextual recall, a more challenging task that requires the model to infer the attribute type implicitly from in-context examples.

In-Context Learning.

Several recent studies have leveraged controlled synthetic environments to analyze how Transformers learn in-context when trained from scratch. A common approach involves training models on well-defined function classes, most notably linear regression
(
Garg et al. 2022
;
Raventos et al. 2023
;
Akyürek et al. 2023
;
von Oswald et al. 2022
;
Ahn et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2023
)
and Markov chains
(
Edelman et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2025
;
Rajaraman et al. 2024
;
Deora et al. 2025
)
. These works often explore whether Transformers implement specific algorithms or functionalities—such as gradient descent
(
von Oswald et al. 2022
;
Ahn et al. 2023
;
Bai et al. 2023
)
or higher-order-algorithms
(
Fu et al. 2024
)
for linear regression, and induction heads for copying tasks
(
Olsson et al. 2022
)
, Markov chain-based setups
(
Edelman et al. 2024
;
Rajaraman et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024b
;
D’Angelo et al. 2025
)
, settings that combine these
(
Bietti et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Kawata et al. 2025
)
, as well as other controlled synthetic settings
(
Singh et al. 2024
)
. Further research
(
Raventos et al. 2023
;
Park et al. 2025
;
Lin & Lee 2024
;
Singh et al. 2025
)
has investigated task diversity, comparing
task retrieval
and
task learning
modes of ICL
(
Pan et al. 2023
)
. Additionally, research has explored transient dynamics to understand how these two modes evolve over the course of training
(
Singh et al. 2023
;
Carroll et al. 2025
;
Singh et al. 2025
)
. Finally, a growing body of work also explores the training dynamics of in-context learning by examining the optimization dynamics of linear regression
(
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
in both one-layer linear attention and softmax attention models
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
, as well as for learning causal structures
Nichani et al. 2024a
.

A defining characteristic of the aforementioned studies is that the training and inference formats are identical; the model is evaluated on the same sequence structures it encountered during training. In contrast, our work on contextual recall introduces a significant prompt-distribution shift. While pretraining focuses on instilling factual knowledge in the model via explicit grammar-based cues, the ICL evaluation requires the model to transition a novel format necessitating inference of the relevant attribute type implicitly from the in-context demonstrations. Therefore, unlike standard ICL setups that focus on function induction, our framework requires the model to bridge the gap between structured knowledge acquisition and implicit in-context reasoning, as mentioned in
Section

1
.

In contrast to our work,
Xie et al. 2022
investigate a distribution shift that is primarily compositional rather than structural. In their framework, the shift occurs when a model pretrained on long, continuous documents is prompted with a sequence of independent, i.i.d. examples. While they frame ICL as a statistical process of implicit Bayesian inference, our work provides a mechanistic perspective discussed in
Section

3.4
and
Section

4
.

Task and Function Vectors in ICL.

Recent work studying ICL mechanisms in pretrained LLMs shows that certain directions in activation space encode the input–output relationship of the ICL task
(
Hendel et al. 2023
;
Todd et al. 2024
;
Yin & Steinhardt 2025
)
. These activation directions, referred to as task or function vectors, seem to encode abstract task information that is largely independent of the specific examples in the prompt, and enable causal interventions that modify model behavior
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
. Other works use controlled synthetic setups to study the emergence of task vectors for ICL of some function classes
(
Yang et al. 2025
;
Dong et al. 2026
)
, how task vector encoding is correlated with task-level decoding
(
Han et al. 2025
)
, as well as task vector disentanglement and composition for two-hop reasoning tasks, and geometry for tasks with numerical-valued continuous latent parameters
(
Hong et al. 2026
)
. Consistent with prior work, our analysis in
Section

3.4
suggests that finetuning drives the emergence of low-dimensional task vectors for the shared attribute type, which enables the model to do well on the contextual recall task. Beyond observing their existence, we make two additional contributions. First, we show that the separability and functional utility of these task vectors (measured through representation clustering and output steering) is directly governed by properties of the pretraining data, specifically the separation between attribute-specific Markov chains. Second, in
Section

4
, we provide a mechanistic explanation for how task vectors emerge through finetuning: the relation head’s OV matrix
learns attribute-to-attribute associations for shared attribute type, which induces the clustering of representations by attribute type.

B.3
Details of Representational Analysis

Consider
ICL
sequences, which are of the form

\displaystyle X_{\ell}=[s_{j_{0}},a^{\ell}_{j_{0}},[\text{sep}],s_{j_{1}},a^{\ell}_{j_{1}},[\text{sep}],\dots,s_{j_{N+1}}],

for a fixed attribute type
\ell
. We sample
K
such sequences for each
\ell\in[L]
, denoted by
X^{k}_{\ell}
. Also, define
X_{\ell,t}:=[s_{j_{0}},a^{\ell}_{j_{0}},[\text{sep}],s_{j_{1}},a^{\ell}_{j_{1}},[\text{sep}],\dots,s_{j_{t+1}}]
, where
t\in[N]
denotes the number of demonstrations.

Let
f_{i}(\cdot)
denote the model’s representation at layer
i
. Consider fixed
i,t
, and for
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in[L]
, define

\displaystyle\bar{C}^{t}_{i}(\ell,\ell^{\prime})=\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\cos\!\big(f_{i}(X^{k}_{\ell,t}),\,f_{i}(X^{k^{\prime}}_{\ell^{\prime},t})\big).

Next, the clustering strength is quantified as follows. For fixed
i,t
, we subsume these and define
v^{k}_{\ell}:=f^{t}_{i}(X^{k}_{\ell})
. Each representation is assigned cluster label based on the attribute type
\ell
. Define the cosine distance
d(v,w):=1-\cos(v,w)
.
For each point
v^{k}_{\ell}
, define the intra-cluster dissimilarity

a(k,\ell)=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{k^{\prime}\neq k}d(v^{k}_{\ell},v^{k^{\prime}}_{\ell}),

and the nearest other-cluster dissimilarity

b(k,\ell)=\min_{\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell}\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k^{\prime}}d(v^{k}_{\ell},v^{k^{\prime}}_{\ell^{\prime}})\right).

The silhouette value for a sample is

s(k,\ell)=\frac{b(k,\ell)-a(k,\ell)}{\max\{\,a(k,\ell),\,b(k,\ell)\,\}}.

Finally, the silhouette score for layer
i
after
t
demonstrations are seen is

\displaystyle\bar{S}^{t}_{i}=\frac{1}{KL}\sum_{\ell}\sum_{k}s(k,\ell).

\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{\ell}=\mathcal{U}
, this model always gives the correct prediction for the last token position.

Proof.

We present a construction for a
3
-head model, with minimal changes to the construction in the proof of
Proposition

1
. Specifically, the
grammar
head is unchanged. At a high level, the
subject
head attends to the last/query subject and maps it to the negative of the sum of attributes not associated with it, while the
relation
head attends to the attributes in the context, and maps them to all attributes of the same type
\ell_{\star}
.

Consider the
subject
head. Using the aforementioned weights, when
\beta\to\infty
,
g_{\text{subj}}({\bm{X}})=\bm{h}(s_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}})
,
i.e.
, the query subject, and
f_{\text{subj}}({\bm{X}})=-\Big(\sum_{u}\bm{h}(u)-\sum_{\ell}\bm{h}(a_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}}^{\ell})\Big)
.

Consider the
relation
head. Using the aforementioned weights, when
\beta\to\infty
,
g_{\text{rel}}({\bm{X}})=\tfrac{1}{N_{\text{ft}}}\sum_{i}\bm{h}(a_{j_{i}}^{\ell_{\star}})
,
i.e.
, the average of the attributes that appear in the context, and
f_{\text{rel}}({\bm{X}})=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\ell_{\star}}}\bm{h}(u)
.

Combining the outputs of the individual heads, the model output is

\displaystyle f({\bm{X}})=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\ell_{\star}}}\bm{h}(u)-\Big(\sum_{u}\bm{h}(u)-\sum_{\ell}\bm{h}(a_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}}^{\ell})\Big)+\tfrac{1}{\mathrm{T}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}}\bm{h}(s_{i})+\tfrac{N_{\text{ft}}}{\mathrm{T}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{R}}\bm{h}(v),

where
\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq[M]
. Then, the final prediction
v^{*}=a_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}}^{\ell_{\star}}
.
∎

C.2
Optimization Dynamics for Finetuning

We state the formal version of
Theorem

1
below followed by its proof.

Theorem 2
.

Consider the two-head attention-only model defined in
Section

4
, initialized with the weights from the construction for
PT
sequences in
Proposition

1
:

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}=\beta\Big(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(s_{j})\Big)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{subj}}=-\sum_{j}\Big(\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j,\ell}\bm{h}(a_{j^{\prime}}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(s_{j})^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}=\beta\Big(\sum_{\ell}\bm{h}(r_{\ell})+\bm{p}\Big)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}=\sum_{\ell}\Big(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(r_{\ell})^{\top}.

Assume that
\mathcal{U}_{\ell}^{\prime}=\mathcal{U}_{\ell}\,\forall\ell\in[L]
,
M\gg mL,M>M_{\text{ft}}\gg U_{L}
,
\mathrm{T}\leq S+2
. Consider
last-token prediction on ICL sequences of the form

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}=[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{T}}]=[\bm{h}(s_{j_{1}}\!),\bm{h}([\text{sep}]),\bm{h}(a^{\ell_{\star}}_{j_{1}}\!),\!...,\bm{h}(s_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}}\!),\bm{h}([\text{sep}])],\qquad{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{T}}=\bm{h}([\text{sep}]),\qquad y=a^{\ell_{\star}}_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}},

(10)

on the population, where the attribute index and each subject in the context are sampled i.i.d. with replacement from a randomly selected set of
M_{\text{ft}}
subjects.
Then, keeping the weight
\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{subj}}
fixed, and updating the other weights with
one step of GD on
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}
with step size
\eta_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}
, one step of projected GD (right projection with
{\bm{I}}-\bm{h}([\text{sep}])\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}
) on
\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}
with step size
\eta_{OV}^{\text{rel}}
, and two steps of GD on
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}
with step size
\eta_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}
, the updated weights satisfy

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}

\displaystyle\approx\beta\left(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(s_{j})+\alpha_{1}\bm{h}(p_{-1})\right)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}

\displaystyle\approx\beta\left(\sum_{\ell}\bm{h}(r_{\ell})+\alpha_{3}\left(\frac{2}{3}\frac{1}{LU_{L}}\sum_{u}\bm{h}(u)+\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\bm{p}_{3i+2}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}}\bm{p}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{s}\bm{h}(s)\right)\right)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\ell}\Big(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(r_{\ell})^{\top}+\alpha_{2}\left((m-1)\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{U_{L}}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{U_{L}}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)^{\top}+\frac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\text{ft}}}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\bm{h}(a_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(s_{j})^{\top}\right),

where
\bm{p}_{i}=\bm{h}(p_{i-\mathrm{T}+1})
,
\alpha_{1}:=\eta_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}m^{-1}\left(1-U_{L}^{-1}\right)
,
\alpha_{2}=\eta_{OV}^{\text{rel}}(L\mathrm{T}U_{L}^{2})^{-1}
, and
\alpha_{3}:=\eta_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}(m-1)^{2}\alpha_{2}
. Further,
when
\beta\to\infty
, this model gets perfect accuracy on
ICL
sequences with seen or held-out query subjects.

Proof.

Note that for a given
ICL
sequence
{\bm{X}}
, the query subject
s_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}}
appears at the fixed relative position. The last-token negative log-likelihood is

\mathcal{L}({\bm{X}},y)=-\bm{h}(y)^{\top}f({\bm{X}})+\log\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\exp\big(\bm{h}(v)^{\top}f({\bm{X}})\big),

with gradient

\bm{u}:=\nabla_{f}\mathcal{L}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{v}\,\bm{h}(v)-\bm{h}(y),\qquad\pi_{v}=\frac{e^{\bm{h}(v)^{\top}f}}{\sum_{u}e^{\bm{h}(u)^{\top}f}}.

Recall that
f({\bm{X}})=\sum_{k=1}^{H}\bm{W}_{OV}^{k}g_{k}({\bm{X}})
, where
g_{k}({\bm{X}})=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{T}}\alpha_{i}^{k}{\bm{x}}_{i}
,
\alpha_{i}^{k}:=\bm{\varphi}(\lambda_{i}^{k})
,
\lambda_{i}^{k}:=({\bm{x}}_{i}+\bm{p}_{i})^{\top}\bm{W}_{KQ}^{k}\bm{h}([\text{sep}])
.

Next, we compute the gradients with respect to
\bm{W}_{OV}^{k}
and
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{k}
.

We have that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}_{OV}^{k}}\mathcal{L}=\bm{u}g_{k}({\bm{X}})^{\top}=\bm{u}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{T}}\alpha_{i}^{k}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}.

Further, for each
i,k
,

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\alpha^{k}_{i}}=\left\langle\nabla_{g_{k}}\mathcal{L},\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial\alpha^{k}_{i}}\right\rangle=\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{W}_{OV}^{k}{\bm{x}}_{i}=:r^{k}_{i},

where we use
\nabla_{g_{k}}\mathcal{L}=(\bm{W}_{OV}^{k})^{\top}\nabla_{f}\mathcal{L}=(\bm{W}_{OV}^{k})^{\top}\bm{u}
. Next, we have that

\frac{\partial\alpha^{k}_{i}}{\partial\lambda^{k}_{j}}=\alpha^{k}_{i}(\mathds{1}\left[i=j\right]-\alpha^{k}_{j}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\lambda_{j}^{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{T}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\alpha^{k}_{i}}\frac{\partial\alpha^{k}_{i}}{\partial\lambda^{k}_{j}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{T}}r^{k}_{i}\alpha^{k}_{i}(\mathds{1}\left[i=j\right]-\alpha^{k}_{j})=r^{k}_{j}\alpha^{k}_{j}-\alpha^{k}_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{T}}\alpha^{k}_{i}r^{k}_{i}=\alpha_{j}^{k}\left(r^{k}_{j}-\bar{r}^{k}\right),

where we define
\bar{r}^{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{T}}\alpha^{k}_{i}r^{k}_{i}
. Finally, we have that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{k}}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathrm{T}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\lambda^{k}_{j}}\,\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{k}}\lambda^{k}_{j}=\beta\sum_{j=1}^{\mathrm{T}}\alpha_{j}^{k}(r^{k}_{j}-\bar{r}^{k})\,({\bm{x}}_{j}+\bm{p}_{j})\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}.

(11)

We next analyze the gradient updates for two iterations of finetuning. We keep
\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{subj}}
fixed throughout.

First Iteration.

For the first iteration, we analyze the updates for
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}
,
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}
, and
\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}
.

Subject Head (
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}
).

We first focus on the
subject head
.
Recall from the construction for
PT
sequences that
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}=\beta\Big(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\bm{h}(s_{j})\Big)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}
.
Let
\mathcal{I}_{s}:=\{i:\exists s\in\mathcal{S},{\bm{x}}_{i}=\bm{h}(s)\}
denote the set of subject positions, with

|\mathcal{I}_{s}|=N_{\text{ft}}+1=:m
. For
i\in\mathcal{I}_{s}
, let
J(i)
denote the subject index
j_{i}
in the context, such that
j_{i}\in[M]
,
s_{j_{i}}\in\mathcal{S}
. For
\beta\to\infty
, the resulting attention weights are

\alpha_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{m},&i\in\mathcal{I}_{s},\\
0,&i\notin\mathcal{I}_{s},\end{cases}

where we omit the superscript
k
for brevity. Substituting into
Eq.

11
, we obtain

\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\beta}{m}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}(r_{i}-\bar{r})\,({\bm{x}}_{i}+\bm{p}_{i})\,\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},\qquad\bar{r}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}r_{i}.

Let
\hat{r}_{i}:=r_{i}-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{v}\bm{h}(v)^{\top}\bm{W}_{OV}{\bm{x}}_{i}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{j}
denote the set of all attributes associated with subject
s_{j}
. We have that

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{v}\bm{h}(v)^{\top}\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{subj}}{\bm{x}}_{i}=\mathds{1}\left[i\in\mathcal{I}_{s}\right]\sum_{u\in\mathcal{A}_{J(i)}}\pi_{u}.

From
Lemma

C.1
,

\pi_{u}\leq\frac{1}{V+LU_{L}(C\exp^{-1}-1)}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle|\hat{r}_{i}-r_{i}|\leq\frac{L}{V+LU_{L}(C\exp^{-1}-1)}\ll\frac{1}{m},

since
V\geq M+U_{L}L\gg mL
. We also define

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}:=\frac{\beta}{m}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}(\hat{r}_{i}-\bar{\hat{r}})({\bm{x}}_{i}+\bm{p}_{i})\,\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},\qquad\bar{\hat{r}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}\hat{r}_{i}.

Then, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\lVert\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}-\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}}{\mathcal{L}}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\left(\lVert\sum_{i}(r_{i}-\hat{r}_{i})({\bm{x}}_{i}+\bm{p}_{i})\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}\rVert+\lVert(\bar{r}-\bar{\hat{r}})\sum_{i}({\bm{x}}_{i}+\bm{p}_{i})\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}\rVert\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{m}\left(m\max_{i}|r_{i}-\hat{r}_{i}|+m\max_{i}|r_{i}-\hat{r}_{i}|\right)\ll\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{m}.

Therefore, in the next part, we analyze
\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}
(as we will see,
\beta^{-1}\lVert\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\rVert
is
\Theta(1/m)
,
i.e.
it dominates the norm of the difference).
We have that

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{i}=-\begin{cases}\mathds{1}\left[a_{J(i)}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right],&i\in\mathcal{I}_{s},\\
0,&\text{o.w.}\end{cases}

Let
q=3N_{\text{ft}}
denote the index of the query subject. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{i}-\bar{\hat{r}}=-\begin{cases}1-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}\mathds{1}\left[a_{J(k)}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right],&i=q,\\
\mathds{1}\left[a_{J(i)}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}\mathds{1}\left[a_{J(k)}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right],&i\in\mathcal{I}_{s}\setminus\{q\},\\
0,&\text{o.w.}\end{cases}

Let
C_{y}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{1}\left[a_{j_{i}}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]
. Then,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}=-\frac{\beta}{m}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\mathds{1}\left[a_{j_{k}}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]-m^{-1}C_{y})\bm{h}(s_{j_{k}})+\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}(\mathds{1}\left[a_{j_{k}}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]-m^{-1}C_{y})\bm{p}_{3k}+(1-m^{-1}C_{y})\bm{h}(p_{-1})\right)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}.

(12)

We first calculate some expectations. Under i.i.d. context sampling with replacement, conditioned on
y
, we have
\Pr(a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\mid y)=\frac{1}{U_{L}}
, and hence
\mathbb{E}[C_{y}\mid y]=\frac{m}{U_{L}}
. Further, averaging over
y
gives
\mathbb{E}[C_{y}]=\frac{m}{U_{L}}
.

Let
\mu_{s}(y):=\mathbb{E}[\bm{h}(s)\mid a^{\ell_{\star}}=y]
. Under i.i.d. context sampling with replacement,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i}\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]\bm{h}(s_{i})\middle|y\right]=\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]s_{i}\mid y]=m\frac{1}{U_{L}}\mu_{s}(y),

since
\Pr(a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\mid y)=1/U_{L}
.
Hence,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i}\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]\bm{h}(s_{i})\right]=\frac{m}{U_{L}}\mathbb{E}[\mu_{s}(y)].

Since each value appears on
M/U_{L}
subjects,
\mathbb{E}[\mu_{s}(y)]=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\bm{h}(s_{j})
,
and therefore

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i}\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]\bm{h}(s_{i})\right]=\frac{m}{U_{L}M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\bm{h}(s_{j}).

Taking expectation in
Eq.

12
over the context, and substituting these to simplify, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\big]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta}{m}\left(\left(\frac{m}{U_{L}M}-\frac{1}{U_{L}M}m\right)\sum_{j}\bm{h}(s_{j})+\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\left(\frac{1}{U_{L}}-\frac{1}{U_{L}}\right)\bm{p}_{3k}+(1-U_{L}^{-1})\bm{h}(p_{-1})\right)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta}{m}\left(1-\frac{1}{U_{L}}\right)\bm{h}(p_{-1})\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}.

Since
\alpha_{1}=\eta_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}m^{-1}\left(1-U_{L}^{-1}\right)
, the updated weight matrix is

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}\approx\beta\left(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(s_{j})+\alpha_{1}\bm{h}(p_{-1})\right)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}.

Relation Head.

Next, we consider the
relation
head. Recall from the construction for
PT
sequences that
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}=\beta\left(\sum_{\ell}\bm{h}(r_{\ell})+\bm{p}\right)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}
. Since
S+2\geq\mathrm{T}
, first consider the update for the KQ part.

We have that

\displaystyle r_{i}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}}\sum_{i}\bm{u}^{\top}\Big(\sum_{\ell}\bm{h}(u_{\ell})\Big)\mathds{1}\left[{\bm{x}}_{i}=\bm{h}(r_{\ell})\right]=0,

since the context does not contain a relation token. This means that the gradient is zero and therefore,
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}
is not updated in this iteration.

\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}
.

Next, consider the update for the OV part. We have that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}_{OV}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{T}}\Big(\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{v}\bm{h}(v)-\bm{h}(y)\Big){\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}.

Define

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}_{OV}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}}\bm{h}(y)\left(\sum_{i}\bm{h}(a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}})+\sum_{i}\bm{h}(s_{i})+m\bm{h}([\text{sep}])\right)^{\top}.

Then, using
Lemma

C.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla_{\bm{W}_{OV}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}-\nabla_{\bm{W}_{OV}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\mathcal{L}\rVert\leq\sum_{v}\pi_{v}\leq\frac{V}{V+(Ce^{-1}-1)LU_{L}}\ll 1,

since
C\gg e
. Therefore, we analyze
\nabla_{\bm{W}_{OV}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}
hereon (as we will see,
\lVert\nabla_{\bm{W}_{OV}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\rVert=\Theta(\sqrt{U_{L}L})>1
,

i.e.
it dominates the norm of the difference). We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{W}_{OV}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{m-1}{L\mathrm{T}U_{L}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{U_{L}}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{U_{L}}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)^{\top}-\frac{m-1}{L\mathrm{T}U_{L}M_{\text{ft}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{U_{L}}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\text{ft}}}\bm{h}(s_{j})\Big)^{\top}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{L\mathrm{T}M_{\text{ft}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\text{ft}}}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\bm{h}(a_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(s_{j})^{\top}-\frac{m}{L\mathrm{T}U_{L}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{U_{L}}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}.

We right project this gradient onto the subspace orthogonal to
\bm{h}([\text{sep}])
, and since
M_{\text{ft}}\gg U_{L}
,
\lambda:=(L\mathrm{T}U_{L}^{2})^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{W}_{OV}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}]({\bm{I}}-\bm{h}([\text{sep}])\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top})\approx\lambda\left((m-1)\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{U_{L}}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{U_{L}}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)^{\top}+\frac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\text{ft}}}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\bm{h}(a_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(s_{j})^{\top}\right).

Since
\alpha_{2}=\eta_{OV}^{\text{rel}}(L\mathrm{T}U_{L}^{2})^{-1}
, the updated weight matrix is

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}\approx\sum_{\ell}\Big(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(r_{\ell})^{\top}+\alpha_{2}\left((m-1)\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{U_{L}}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{U_{L}}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)^{\top}+\frac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\text{ft}}}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\bm{h}(a_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(s_{j})^{\top}\right).

Second Iteration.

In this case, we only update
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}
, keeping the other weights fixed.

\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}
.

We use the updated
\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}
to compute the gradient wrt
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}
. Since
\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}
remains unchanged in the first iteration,
\alpha_{i}^{\text{rel}}=\tfrac{1}{\mathrm{T}}
, and we have

\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\beta}{\mathrm{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{T}}(r_{i}-\bar{r})\,({\bm{x}}_{i}+\bm{p}_{i})\,\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},\qquad r_{i}:=\left(\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{v}\,\bm{h}(v)-\bm{h}(y)\right)^{\top}\bm{W}_{OV}^{\mathrm{rel}}{\bm{x}}_{i},\quad\quad\bar{r}:=\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}}\sum_{i}r_{i}.

Also define

\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{\beta}{\mathrm{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{T}}(\hat{r}_{i}-\bar{\hat{r}})({\bm{x}}_{i}+\bm{p}_{i})\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},\qquad\hat{r}_{i}:=-\bm{h}(y)^{\top}\bm{W}_{OV}^{\mathrm{rel}}{\bm{x}}_{i},\quad\quad\bar{\hat{r}}:=\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}}\sum_{i}\hat{r}_{i}.

Following similar steps as in the update for the subject head, using
Lemma

C.2
, we have that

\displaystyle(\beta\alpha_{2})^{-1}\lVert\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}-\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\mathrm{rel}}}{\mathcal{L}}\rVert\leq 2\sqrt{2}\alpha_{2}^{-1}\max_{i}|r_{i}-\hat{r}_{i}|\leq(\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\pi_{u})\left((m-1)^{2}U_{L}+mL\frac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\right)\leq\frac{em^{2}U_{L}}{M}\ll m^{2},

since
M>M_{\text{ft}}\gg U_{L}
. Therefore, we analyze
\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}
hereafter (as we will see,
(\beta\alpha_{2})^{-1}\lVert\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\rVert=\Theta(m^{2})
,

i.e.
it dominates the norm of the difference). We have that

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{i}=-\alpha_{2}\begin{cases}m-1,&{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{\ell_{\star}}\\
\frac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right],&{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}\end{cases},\qquad\bar{\hat{r}}=-\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\mathrm{T}}\left((m-1)^{2}+\frac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\sum_{i}\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{i}-\bar{\hat{r}}=-\alpha_{2}\begin{cases}(m-1)\left(1-\frac{m-1}{\mathrm{T}}\right)-\frac{U_{L}^{2}}{\mathrm{T}M_{\text{ft}}}\sum_{i}\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right],&{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{\ell_{\star}},\\
\frac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]-\frac{U_{L}^{2}}{\mathrm{T}M_{\text{ft}}}\sum_{i}\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]-\frac{(m-1)^{2}}{\mathrm{T}},&{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}.\end{cases}

Let
\lambda_{m}:=(m-1)\left(1-\tfrac{m-1}{\mathrm{T}}\right)
. Then,
\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}
can be written as

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta\alpha_{2}}{\mathrm{T}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\left(\lambda_{m}-\tfrac{U_{L}^{2}}{\mathrm{T}M_{\text{ft}}}C_{y}\right)(\bm{h}(a_{j_{i}}^{\ell_{\star}})+\bm{p}_{3i+2})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\tfrac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]-\tfrac{U_{L}^{2}}{\mathrm{T}M_{\text{ft}}}C_{y}-\tfrac{(m-1)^{2}}{\mathrm{T}}\right)(\bm{h}(s_{j_{i}})+\bm{p}_{3i})\right)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}.

We have that

\mathbb{E}[C_{y}\bm{h}(y)]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[C_{y}\bm{h}(y)\mid y]\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bm{h}(y)\mathbb{E}[C_{y}\mid y]\right]=\frac{m}{U_{L}}\mathbb{E}[\bm{h}(y)]=\frac{m}{LU_{L}^{2}}\sum_{u}\bm{h}(u).

The expectations of the terms with attribute, subject and positional tokens can be simplified as follows.

First, for the attribute term, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\left(\lambda_{m}-\tfrac{U_{L}^{2}}{\mathrm{T}M_{\text{ft}}}C_{y}\right)\bm{h}(a_{j_{i}}^{\ell_{\star}})\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{m}(m-1)\tfrac{1}{LU_{L}}\sum_{u}\bm{h}(u)-\tfrac{U_{L}^{2}}{\mathrm{T}M_{\text{ft}}}(m-1)\mathbb{E}[C_{y}\bm{h}(y)]

\displaystyle=\left((m-1)\left(1-\tfrac{m-1}{\mathrm{T}}\right)-\tfrac{mU_{L}}{\mathrm{T}M_{\text{ft}}}\right)\frac{m-1}{LU_{L}}\sum_{u}\bm{h}(u),

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2(m-1)^{2}}{3}\frac{1}{LU_{L}}\sum_{u}\bm{h}(u),

where the last step follows as
\mathrm{T}=3m-1
and
M_{\text{ft}}\gg U_{L}
.

Next, for the subject term, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\tfrac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]-\tfrac{U_{L}^{2}}{\mathrm{T}M_{\text{ft}}}C_{y}-\tfrac{(m-1)^{2}}{\mathrm{T}}\right)\bm{h}(s_{j_{i}})\right]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]\bm{h}(s_{j_{i}})-\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\tfrac{U_{L}^{2}}{\mathrm{T}M_{\text{ft}}}C_{y}+\tfrac{(m-1)^{2}}{\mathrm{T}}\right)\bm{h}(s_{j_{i}})

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{U_{L}}{M_{\text{ft}}}-\tfrac{mU_{L}}{\mathrm{T}M_{\text{ft}}}-\tfrac{(m-1)^{2}}{\mathrm{T}}\right)\tfrac{m}{M}\sum_{s}\bm{h}(s)

\displaystyle\approx-\tfrac{(m-1)^{2}}{3}\tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{s}\bm{h}(s).

Next, for the positional encoding term, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}(m-1)\bm{p}_{3i+2}+\tfrac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{1}\left[a_{i}^{\ell_{\star}}=y\right]\bm{p}_{3i}-\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\tfrac{U_{L}^{2}}{\mathrm{T}M_{\text{ft}}}C_{y}+\tfrac{(m-1)^{2}}{\mathrm{T}}\right)\bm{p}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=(m-1)\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\bm{p}_{3i+2}+\tfrac{mU_{L}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bm{p}_{3i}-\tfrac{1}{\mathrm{T}}\left(\tfrac{mU_{L}}{M_{\text{ft}}}+(m-1)^{2}\right)\sum_{i}\bm{p}_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(m-1-\tfrac{mU_{L}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\bm{p}_{3i+2}+\left(\tfrac{mU_{L}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\left(1-\mathrm{T}^{-1}\right)-\tfrac{(m-1)^{2}}{\mathrm{T}}\right)\bm{p}

\displaystyle\approx(m-1)\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\bm{p}_{3i+2}-\frac{(m-1)^{2}}{\mathrm{T}}\bm{p}.

Combining these terms, we have that

\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\mathrm{rel}}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}]\approx-(m-1)^{2}\alpha_{2}\beta\left(\frac{2}{3}\frac{1}{LU_{L}}\sum_{u}\bm{h}(u)+\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\bm{p}_{3i+2}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}}\bm{p}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{s}\bm{h}(s)\right)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}.

Since
\alpha_{3}=\eta_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}(m-1)^{2}\alpha_{2}
, the updated weight matrix is

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}\approx\beta\left(\sum_{\ell}\bm{h}(r_{\ell})+\alpha_{3}\left(\frac{2}{3}\frac{1}{LU_{L}}\sum_{u}\bm{h}(u)+\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\bm{p}_{3i+2}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}}\bm{p}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{s}\bm{h}(s)\right)\right)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top}.

Using these updated weights, following similar steps as in the proof of
Proposition

4
, we can show that when
\beta\to\infty
, the model attains perfect accuracy on
ICL
sequences with seen or held-out subjects.
∎

Lemma C.1
.

Under the same conditions as
Theorem

1
, assuming
M\geq CU_{L}
, where
C>e
is a constant, and using the weights

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}=\beta\Big(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(s_{j})\Big)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{subj}}=-\sum_{j}\Big(\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j,\ell}\bm{h}(a_{j^{\prime}}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(s_{j})^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}=\beta\Big(\sum_{\ell}\bm{h}(r_{\ell})+\bm{p}\Big)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}=\sum_{\ell}\Big(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(r_{\ell})^{\top},

the prediction probabilities
\pi_{v}
are bounded as:

\displaystyle\pi_{v}\leq\frac{1}{V+(Ce^{-1}-1)LU_{L}}.

Proof.

Let
m:=N_{\text{ft}}+1
denote the number of subject positions in the
ICL
sequence, and let
k_{j}
be the multiplicity of subject
j
among these.

When
\beta\to\infty
, the subject head attends uniformly over the
m
subject positions, hence

g_{\text{subj}}({\bm{X}})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bm{h}(s_{j_{i}}).

Therefore

f_{\text{subj}}({\bm{X}})=\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{subj}}g_{\text{subj}}({\bm{X}})=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j_{i},\ell}\bm{h}(a_{j^{\prime}}^{\ell}).

Moreover, since
\bm{h}(v)^{\top}\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}=0
for all attributes other than the relation tokens, and since relation tokens don’t appear in the
ICL
sequence, the relation head does not contribute to the logits. Therefore,

\displaystyle\bm{h}(a_{j}^{\ell})^{\top}f({\bm{X}})=\bm{h}(a_{j}^{\ell})^{\top}f_{\text{subj}}({\bm{X}})=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{1}\left[j\neq j_{i}\right]=-\Big(1-\frac{k_{j}}{m}\Big)=:-c_{j}.

(13)

Also, note that for any
v\notin\mathcal{U}
, the logit is
0
. Next, let
Z:=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\exp(\bm{h}(u)^{\top}f({\bm{X}}))
. Using
Eq.

13
, we have

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\exp(\bm{h}(a_{j}^{\ell})^{\top}f({\bm{X}}))+(V-LU_{L})=\sum_{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}e^{-c_{j}}+(V-LU_{L})=L\sum_{j}e^{-c_{j}}+(V-LU_{L})

\displaystyle=Le^{-1}\sum_{j}e^{k_{j}/m}+(V-LU_{L}).

Hence, for any attribute token
a_{j}^{\ell}
,

\displaystyle\pi_{a_{j}^{\ell}}=\frac{\exp(\bm{h}(a_{j}^{\ell})^{\top}f({\bm{X}}))}{Z}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-c_{j}}}{Le^{-1}\sum_{j^{\prime}}e^{k_{j^{\prime}}/m}+(V-LU_{L})}=\frac{e^{k_{j}/m}}{L\sum_{j^{\prime}}e^{k_{j^{\prime}}/m}+e(V-LU_{L})}.

Since
\sum_{j^{\prime}}e^{k_{j^{\prime}}/m}\geq M
, and
k_{j}\leq m
, we have

\pi_{a_{j}^{\ell}}\leq\frac{1}{V-LU_{L}+e^{-1}LM}.

Similarly, for
v\notin\mathcal{U}
,
\pi_{v}=Z^{-1}\leq(V-LU_{L}+e^{-1}LM)^{-1}
.
Using the assumption on
M
finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Under the same conditions as
Theorem

1
, assuming
\alpha_{2}\leq(\lambda_{1}+m\lambda_{2})^{-1}
, when using the weights,

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{subj}}

\displaystyle=\beta\Big(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(s_{j})+\alpha_{1}\bm{h}(p_{-1})\Big)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},\qquad\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{subj}}=-\sum_{j}\Big(\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j,\ell}\bm{h}(a_{j^{\prime}}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(s_{j})^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{KQ}^{\text{rel}}

\displaystyle=\beta\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\bm{h}(r_{\ell})+\bm{p}\Big)\bm{h}([\text{sep}])^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{OV}^{\text{rel}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\Big(\sum_{j}\bm{h}(u_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(r_{\ell})^{\top}+\alpha_{2}\left((m-1)\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Big(\sum_{a=1}^{U_{L}}\bm{h}(u_{a}^{\ell})\Big)\Big(\sum_{a=1}^{U_{L}}\bm{h}(u_{a}^{\ell})\Big)^{\top}+\frac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\text{ft}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\text{ft}}}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\bm{h}(a_{j}^{\ell})\Big)\bm{h}(s_{j})^{\top}\right),

the prediction probabilities
\pi_{v}
are bounded as:

\displaystyle\pi_{v}\leq\frac{e}{V+LU_{L}(e^{-1}-1)}\leq\frac{e}{M}.

Proof.

Let
m:=N_{\text{ft}}+1
denote the number of subject positions in the
ICL
sequence, and let
k_{j}
be the multiplicity of subject
j
among these positions (so
\sum_{j}k_{j}=m
), and define
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}:=\{j:\,k_{j}>0\}
.

When
\beta\to\infty
, the relation head attends uniformly over all
\mathrm{T}
positions, while the subject head attends to the query subject
\bm{h}(s_{j_{N_{\text{ft}}+1}})
. For any attribute token
v=a_{j}^{\ell}
, define

c_{j}({\bm{X}}):=|\{\,i\in[N_{\text{ft}}+1]:\,j_{i}=j\,\}|,

i.e.
, the number of times subject
j
appears among the subjects in the given sequence
{\bm{X}}
. Then

\bm{h}(a_{j}^{\ell})^{\top}f({\bm{X}})=\alpha_{2}\left((m-1)\frac{N_{\text{ft}}}{\mathrm{T}}\mathds{1}\left[\ell=\ell_{\star}\right]+\frac{U_{L}^{2}}{M_{\mathrm{ft}}\mathrm{T}}c_{j}({\bm{X}})\right)-\mathds{1}\left[j\neq j_{q}\right].
