A systematic scoping review and interpretive ethical synthesis of ethical decision-making about physical restraints for older adults in Confucian-influenced care contexts
Authors: Xiaoyan Feng, Hongjun Zhu, Liyun Shang, Yuanyuan Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aims to map and interpret the evidence on ethical decision-making in the use of physical restraints for older adults, with particular attention to Confucian-influenced family-centred care contexts and comparative international evidence.DesignThis study applied a systematic scoping review design combined with an interpretive ethical synthesis.MethodsThe review was guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute approach to scoping reviews and reported with reference to PRISMA principles. Medline/PubMed, CINAHL Plus, CNKI, Google Scholar and ResearchGate were searched for English and Chinese literature published from 1 January 2015 to 30 September 2025. Searches combined terms for physical restraint, older adults, decision-making, ethics, family/proxy decision-making and Confucian or filial-piety contexts. Of 757 records identified, 526 records remained after duplicate removal, 46 full-text reports were assessed, and eight studies were included. Because the evidence base combined qualitative, quantitative and review studies, findings were integrated through a staged interpretive ethical synthesis. Empirical findings were first coded descriptively, then compared across evidence types, and finally interpreted through bioethical concepts including autonomy, relational autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, dignity, substituted judgement, vulnerability and culturally situated family responsibility. Primary empirical studies were given greater analytic weight than review articles, which were used mainly for conceptual contextualisation.ResultsFour analytically linked themes were identified: contextual determinants of restraint decisions in care settings; balancing autonomy, relational autonomy, beneficence and non-maleficence; nurses’ ethical dilemmas and moral distress; and family or proxy decision-making shaped by substituted judgement, vulnerability, filial responsibility and culturally situated expectations of care.Keywords: Ethical decision-making, Physical restraints, Older adults, Confucian culture, Filial piety, Systematic scoping review, Interpretive ethical synthesis

Summary statement of implications for practice

What new insights does this research contribute to existing knowledge in gerontology?

This review clarifies how family-centred decision-making and filial responsibility may shape ethical deliberation about physical restraints for older adults in Confucian-influenced care contexts.

How does this new knowledge impact the care of older adults?

It highlights the need to balance patient autonomy, safety, dignity, family participation and nurses’ moral burden when considering restraint use.

How might these findings be utilised to influence policy, practice, research, or education?

The findings may inform culturally sensitive restraint policies, shared decision-making procedures, staff education and future empirical research, while recognising that recommendations should remain proportionate to the small and heterogeneous evidence base.

Introduction

As of 2021, China’s population aged 65 and above stood at approximately 190 million, accounting for 13.5% of the total national population (National Bureau of Statistics of the People’s Republic of China, 2021). The majority of elderly individuals suffer from chronic conditions such as stroke, cardiovascular disease, Parkinson’s disease, and dementia. These conditions frequently result in varying degrees of physical mobility impairment, dysphagia, speech difficulties, or cognitive impairment, necessitating hospitalisation for rehabilitation treatment [1, 2].

Elderly patients with various functional and cognitive impairments during hospitalisation encounter numerous adverse events and safety concerns, commonly including falls, extubation, bed falls, or behaviours that harm themselves or others [3, 4]. To mitigate these risks, rehabilitation nursing staff frequently employ physical restraints to limit patient mobility [5, 6]. Physical restraint is defined as ‘any action or procedure that prevents a person’s free body movement to a position of choice and/or normal access to his/her body by the use of any method that is attached or adjacent to a person’s body and that he/she cannot control or remove easily’ [7] methods include restraining wrists, ankles, or the chest; the use of bed rails is also considered a form of restraint in certain circumstances [8]. However, the consideration of restraint devices must be context-dependent; for instance, bed rails serve as a therapeutic measure when preventing a patient from moving in bed. Therefore, the restraints examined in this study are defined as devices used to limit a patient’s mobility [9].

Although the use of physical restraints is intended to ensure patient safety, multiple studies have found that inappropriate use can lead to adverse physical and psychological consequences for patients. These include elevated blood pressure and heart rate, impaired blood circulation, pressure ulcers, fractures, and diminished personal autonomy, alongside prolonged hospital stays. Furthermore, some researchers contend that the use of restraints in rehabilitation wards contradicts the very concept of “rehabilitation” [10]. Patients in rehabilitation wards undergo resistance exercises, balance training, and muscle strengthening exercises to promote functional recovery [11] whereas physical restraints restrict patient mobility [12]. Reduced activity diminishes functional capacity in elderly patients [13] heightening dependency on nursing staff and perpetuating a vicious cycle of increased restrictive practices. Patients harbour heightened expectations regarding functional recovery, consequently voicing greater criticism towards the use of physical restraints and their impact on therapeutic relationships.

Decision-making is defined as ‘the preparatory process occurring prior to action when nurses encounter problems or ethical dilemmas in their professional practice’ [14] This decision-making process is described as complex [15] stemming from the inherent potential for associated issues with physical restraints themselves, coupled with existing evidence failing to guarantee accident prevention. Furthermore, the ethical appropriateness of this practice is also questioned [16] Consequently, the use of physical restraints in rehabilitation wards presents considerable distress to decision-makers, namely nursing staff. Restraining elderly patients’ mobility violates principles of patient autonomy and respect for dignity, while also inducing negative emotions such as guilt and moral distress among caregivers [17–19]. Particularly in China, Confucian culture, as the backbone of the traditional cultural value system, plays a significant role in the ethical issues surrounding the restraint of elderly patients. This is because ‘filial piety’ forms the foundation of Confucian intergenerational ethics and is accorded paramount importance. It guides the establishment of a comprehensive set of ethical and moral norms collectively adhered to within traditional society, thereby becoming the value system that regulates social ethical relationships. Confucius emphasised that filial piety and fraternal duty must be founded upon reverence for one’s parents, requiring children to serve their elders with gratitude and respect. Beyond ensuring parents’ basic material needs are met, providing for their financial security in old age, it is even more crucial to attend to their spiritual requirements during later life [20]. The negative emotions arising from physical restraint—such as body image impairment, frustration, and fear—are markedly at odds with China’s traditional culture of filial piety. Research on physical restraint predominantly focuses on emergency departments and intensive care units, where patients often exhibit impaired consciousness and clinical decisions are frequently made through paternalistic management approaches [21] Rehabilitation wards present distinct challenges. Patients with varying degrees of functional impairment require family assistance for daily living activities. When implementing physical restraints, consideration must extend beyond the patient to include family input, particularly given the influence of Confucian filial piety principles. However, research in this area remains insufficient, leaving rehabilitation nurses confronting more complex ethical dilemmas when considering physical restraint. Therefore, it is necessary for the authors to explore the ethical decision-making surrounding the use of physical restraints for elderly patients in rehabilitation wards within the context of traditional Chinese culture.

Because the available evidence is limited, this review does not assume that all international restraint literature represents Confucian culture. Instead, studies conducted in China or Confucian-influenced settings are used to support culturally specific interpretation, whereas studies from other contexts are used comparatively to clarify shared ethical principles and institutional challenges.

Methods

This review is positioned as a systematic scoping review with interpretive ethical synthesis rather than a conventional effectiveness review. This designation was chosen because the available literature is sparse, conceptually diverse and methodologically heterogeneous, and because the purpose was to map ethical decision-making issues and interpret their cultural meaning rather than to pool intervention effects.

Search strategy

An electronic literature search was conducted across Medline/PubMed, CINAHL Plus and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI). Supplementary searches were undertaken using Google Scholar, ResearchGate and reference-list checking. The search was limited to English- and Chinese-language literature published between 1 January 2015 and 30 September 2025. The search strategy combined four concept groups: (1) physical restraint or immobilisation; (2) older adults or elderly people; (3) decision-making, ethics, autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, dignity, family or proxy decision-making; and (4) Confucianism, filial piety, Chinese culture or family-centred care. Truncation and database-specific subject headings were used where available. An initial search in September 2025 identified 757 records. After 231 duplicates were removed, 526 records were screened by title and abstract. Forty-six full-text reports were assessed for eligibility, and eight studies were finally included in the synthesis. Selection criteria

This review included peer-reviewed journal articles, empirical qualitative or quantitative studies, mixed-methods studies, scoping or integrative reviews, and authoritative reports that addressed physical restraint use among older adults in healthcare, rehabilitation, home-care, nursing-home or long-term-care settings. To align the review question with the cultural framing, studies were eligible if they either directly examined Confucian-influenced contexts, filial responsibility or family/proxy decision-making, or provided comparative ethical evidence relevant to autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, dignity or nurses’ moral distress. Exclusion criteria were: studies focused only on children, psychiatric coercion without relevance to older adults, chemical restraint only, purely technical restraint devices without ethical or decision-making content, unavailable full text, duplicate reports, and opinion pieces lacking a clear evidentiary basis.

Selection process

Two stages of screening were undertaken. First, titles and abstracts were screened against the eligibility criteria. Second, potentially relevant full texts were assessed in detail. Disagreements during screening were resolved through discussion among the authors. Reasons for full-text exclusion were recorded to improve transparency and reproducibility: not focused on ethical decision-making (n = 14), not focused on older adults or physical restraint (n = 10), insufficient attention to cultural or family-decision content (n = 8), and unavailable full text or duplicate report (n = 6). The final corpus comprised eight studies. Four studies were conducted in China or Confucian-influenced settings and were used as the primary cultural evidence; four studies from Portugal, Belgium, South Korea and Australia were retained as comparative evidence because they addressed ethical decision-making, restraint reduction, professional responsibility or family/proxy involvement. This distinction was maintained throughout the synthesis to avoid overstating the Confucian specificity of non-Confucian evidence.

Data extraction and interpretive ethical synthesis

A structured data extraction form (Table 2) was developed to capture author, year, country or region, aim, participants, design, key findings, and analytic relevance to ethical decision-making. Given the heterogeneity of the included evidence, no meta-analysis was attempted. Instead, synthesis proceeded in four stages. First, findings from each included study were extracted as descriptive units, including reported reasons for restraint use, decision-makers involved, ethical concerns, family or proxy roles, and institutional conditions. Second, these units were coded into recurring decision-making categories, such as safety protection, autonomy restriction, harm prevention, workload pressure, family participation, documentation and restraint alternatives. Third, conceptually overlapping codes were compared across study designs to identify higher-order ethical tensions. Fourth, these tensions were interpreted using contemporary bioethical concepts, including autonomy, relational autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, dignity, justice, substituted judgement, vulnerability, ethics of care, paternalism and culturally situated family responsibility. This process made it possible to move from descriptive findings to ethical interpretation while keeping the evidentiary basis visible.

To address epistemological differences among evidence types, primary empirical studies were treated as the main evidentiary source for reported practices, experiences and decision-making contexts. Qualitative studies contributed experiential and meaning-oriented evidence; quantitative studies contributed information on prevalence, associated factors or attitudes; and review articles were used to clarify concepts, identify common ethical principles and avoid missing relevant interpretive categories. Review articles were not weighted as equivalent to primary empirical studies and were not used to duplicate findings already represented in primary studies. Where conceptual overlap occurred, findings were consolidated rather than counted repeatedly. Where studies differed in cultural setting, evidence from China or Confucian-influenced contexts was used for culturally specific interpretation, whereas international evidence was used comparatively to identify shared ethical issues. Throughout the synthesis, empirical findings, authors’ interpretive inferences and broader normative implications were kept analytically distinct.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

All studies were published between 2015 and 2025. Table 2 shows that the included literature comprised qualitative studies, cross-sectional studies, mixed-methods work, an integrative review and a scoping review. Four studies were conducted in China or Confucian-influenced settings and therefore provided the main evidence for filial responsibility, family-centred decision-making and culturally situated care obligations. The remaining four studies, conducted in Portugal, Belgium, South Korea and Australia, were used as comparative evidence to clarify ethical concepts that also appear in other care systems, such as autonomy, non-maleficence, professional accountability and institutional regulation. This analytic distinction is important because the review does not claim that all included evidence directly represents Confucian culture.

Across the eight studies, ethical decision-making concerning physical restraints for older adults was summarised under four interrelated themes: contextual determinants of restraint decisions in care settings; balancing respect for autonomy with beneficence and non-maleficence; nurses’ ethical dilemmas and moral distress; and family or proxy decision-making shaped by filial responsibility and culturally situated expectations of care.

Ethical considerations for the use of physical restraints in wards

When implementing physical restraints on elderly patients, rehabilitation nurses should base their actions on the principles of beneficence, non-maleficence, advocacy, patient autonomy, nursing autonomy, and truthfulness [26, 30]. However, decisions to implement physical restraints are influenced by multiple factors. Rehabilitation nurses must consider environmental, patient, and personal conditions [22] such as whether guidelines are applicable to rehabilitation settings, the presence of aggressive behaviour in patients, and the nurse’s subjective judgement based on personal assessment. During periods of understaffing, particularly when night shifts are heavy and fatigue sets in, nurses may resort to physical restraints to ensure patient safety. Another scenario involves rehabilitation nurses neglecting thorough physical restraint assessments during time-constrained emergencies, resulting in physical and psychological harm to elderly patients. Incomplete assessments increase the likelihood of errors when deciding on physical restraints, causing patient injury. Such practices may constitute unethical conduct by disregarding patient rights, ethical principles, and restraint complications, thereby violating the principle of non-maleficence. However, without physical restraints, patients may fall from beds without nursing supervision, potentially leading to severe consequences. Based on the above discussion, nurses must consider multiple possibilities when employing physical restraints. Their use should be justified by the patient’s condition and circumstances to ensure legitimacy and ethical compliance. Rehabilitation nurses must also regularly reassess the necessity of restraints and monitor related complications, ensuring timely removal.

Some studies [24, 31] indicate that care providers exhibit a tendency towards restraint, yet experience negative feelings about it. Furthermore, although they recognise the necessity for implementing restraints, ethical conflicts often arise; even when uncertain about applying restraints, they tend to proceed with their use.

The principles of respect and non-harm

Respect for human dignity is the fundamental principle governing all human conduct. Consequently, prior to undertaking any medical intervention involving physical restraint, the individual deprived of liberty must be informed and permitted to freely give their informed consent. Regarding the content of pre-procedure information, the subject must be accurately informed of the purpose, risks, and consequences of the intervention, thereby ensuring their freedom of choice.

The decision to implement physical restraints involves ethical principles, namely ‘respect for autonomy, justice, beneficence, and non-maleficence’. The most prominent ethical concern is whether elderly patients’ right to autonomous choice is respected and protected. When elderly patients in rehabilitation wards exhibit non-compliance with treatment or engage in impulsive harmful behaviours due to cognitive impairment or agitation, nurses may apply physical restraints to prevent self-harm or harm to others. In such instances, protecting patients and others from harm motivates rehabilitation nurses to employ physical restraints, reflecting a paternalistic approach. Paternalism adopts a protective and authoritative stance, typically employed when considering ethical dilemmas within interpersonal relationships [23, 28]. Paternalism arises when rehabilitation nurses, constrained by time pressures, resort to physical restraint as a decision-making process. This occurs when adequate information regarding physical restraint is not provided to the patient, thereby preventing action on the elderly patient’s decision and establishing a paternalistic dynamic [32]. When patients are compelled to undergo physical restraint before making decisions aligned with their personal values, without receiving sufficient care and treatment information, their needs for autonomy and respect remain unmet and unprotected. Research indicates that rehabilitation nurses prioritise treatment and care safety over the personal liberty of elderly patients during their care. However, for elderly individuals possessing independent personalities, the need for respect remains equally significant and should not be overlooked or undervalued. Rehabilitation ward nurses must not arbitrarily deprive them of their right to autonomous decision-making. Elderly patients and their designated proxies retain the right to determine whether physical restraint protocols should be employed [33]. Even when deemed appropriate for care decisions, the use of physical restraints may be considered unethical [34]. The use of physical restraints is not mandatory but may occasionally be necessary. Rehabilitation nurses must assess the necessity of physical restraints based on the principles of beneficence and non-maleficence, while considering the patient’s current environment. Research indicates that appropriate use of physical restraints can protect elderly patients from excessive negative impacts. However, strict supervision is required during implementation to minimise the unavoidable harms associated with restraint, though this can only serve as a limited measure and cannot eliminate patient harm entirely. Person-centred care, respect for patient autonomy, and the principle of non-maleficence are fundamental values in nursing practice that rehabilitation nurses must fully consider when making physical restraint decisions for elderly patients.

Ethical dilemmas for nursing staff

During a patient’s hospital stay, rehabilitation nurses play a crucial role in the decision-making process regarding the use of physical restraints, as they are most closely involved in patient care [35] Rehabilitation nurses’ attitudes towards physical restraints, whether supportive or opposed, influence their utilisation [36]. Some nursing staff consider the use of physical restraints unethical. On the one hand, it compromises patient autonomy and may cause potential physical harm, violating the ethical principle of non-maleficence [37]. On the other hand, rehabilitation departments advocate appropriate physical activity for elderly patients, and the use of physical restraints clearly contradicts this principle, breaching the ethical principle of beneficence. Naturally, opposing viewpoints exist. For instance, during periods of high workload or staffing shortages, failing to use restraints may compromise patient safety. Nurses may consequently neglect the patient’s holistic care, resorting to arbitrary use of restraints for self-interest (e.g., reducing workload) [38]. An ethical dilemma arises when an individual faces two competing choices, each supported by sound moral reasoning, yet both cannot be simultaneously selected. This indicates that when deciding whether to implement physical restraints on elderly patients in rehabilitation wards, the conflict between patient autonomy and the principles of beneficence and non-maleficence places nursing staff in a state of uncertainty and conflict, creating an ethical dilemma. One study revealed that 36% of ward nurses encounter ethical dilemmas when deciding whether to use physical restraints [39]. Moreover, they frequently feel caught in a tug-of-war, striving to balance ethical concerns with safety imperatives. Nurses possess a strong desire to safeguard patient safety, yet physical restraints may negatively impact the healthcare experience for both patients and their families, creating ethical dilemmas for healthcare professionals [40].

Ethical dilemmas in turn exert pressure upon rehabilitation nursing staff. Should patient safety issues arise from the non-implementation of physical restraints, nurses bear the responsibility and may face criticism from management, fellow healthcare professionals, or patients’ families, potentially even facing legal action [41] Nurses who implement physical restraints report significant distress, including frustration, conflict, guilt, anxiety, physical complaints (headaches, fatigue, and gastrointestinal changes), insomnia, sadness, emotional instability, fear, anger, compassion, absenteeism, distress, professional burnout, and emotional and ethical distress [42, 43]. Healthcare professionals perceive the use of physical restraints as psychologically and physiologically unpleasant, yet paradoxically, they also consider mechanical restraints sometimes necessary to protect both themselves and service users from harm [44] However, another study indicates that psychiatric nurses experience distress and frustration because restraint procedures disrupt their relationship with service users, and they perceive this may lead to verbal or non-verbal retaliatory behaviour from patients [25, 29]. This consequently contributes to the emergence of compassion fatigue among rehabilitation ward nurses.

Moreover, the emotional and health issues experienced by nurses caught in ethical dilemmas—such as fatigue, anxiety, depression, and headaches—can impair clinical judgement, affect professional practice, and even lead to ethical distress arising from ethical care. This ultimately results in occupational burnout and prolonged absenteeism [45] This double-edged sword of ethical dilemmas affects both the disciplined and the disciplinarian; the physical and mental strain on rehabilitation nurses diminishes overall care quality, with these consequences reverberating back to elderly patients, creating a vicious cycle. Moreover, rehabilitation nurses face ethical dilemmas when weighing the consequences of employing physical restraints, as they bear responsibility for both the adverse outcomes of such measures and the safety hazards arising from their inappropriate application.

Family or proxy decision-making

When elderly patients’ decision-making capacity prevents them from making rational choices, rehabilitation nurses often seek the views and decisions of designated proxies or family members. However, relatives may consciously or unconsciously misinterpret the patient’s wishes, or fail to accurately gauge the preferences of elderly patients – particularly those with central nervous system injuries where the onset is sudden and the patient has not previously communicated their personal wishes to family. In such circumstances, regardless of whether the patient has expressed explicit wishes, the designated proxy or family members must jointly determine with rehabilitation ward nurses whether physical restraints should be employed. In China, Confucian cultural influences constitute a significant factor that cannot be overlooked when proxies or family members make such decisions.

Within Confucian culture, centred on filial piety, family members aspire to provide superior care for patients, minimising harm during recovery in exchange for restoring prior relationships, particularly those between children and parents. When deciding whether to implement physical restraints for elderly patients, some proxy decision-makers perceive this as beneficial for maintaining patient safety and promoting recovery [27]. Despite it being a ‘difficult decision’ for them, they may still consent to the use of restraints [28, 46]. Other family members, adhering to Confucian beliefs and values aimed at providing superior care for loved ones, overcome difficulties in the caregiving process and refuse physical restraints. Filial piety requires children to consider their parents’ needs—physical, mental, and social—alongside their dignity and freedom. The phrase ‘It is my turn to care for him’ [47] aptly illustrates this, signifying that children care for their parents to repay parental nurturing. It also powerfully reflects the context within which family members resort to physical restraints. Confucian culture emphasises caring and nurturing obligations, forming the foundation for family decision-making. Relatives believe they must provide the best possible care for their loved ones, regardless of the difficulties and challenges encountered. This extends beyond ensuring parents’ basic material needs are met, securing their financial security in later life. More crucially, it involves attending to the spiritual requirements of elderly parents. When physical restraints become necessary for elderly patients, family members perceive this as detrimental to the patient’s physical and psychological wellbeing, dignity, and freedom. They view it as an act of ‘filial disobedience’ that challenges cultural values and undermines familial bonds. Employing such measures induces guilt and shame among family decision-makers, constituting a challenge to Confucian culture and public morality. These cultural values contrast with Western parent-child relationships, which are based on kinship ties with limited obligations; adult children bear no duty to provide resources for parental care [48].

In China and other Confucian-influenced contexts, family members often participate closely in care and may act as proxy decision-makers when older adults lack decision-making capacity. When considering physical restraint, rehabilitation nurses therefore need to assess not only clinical risk but also the patient’s known values, the family’s interpretation of filial responsibility, and whether proxy decisions genuinely reflect substituted judgement and the patient’s best interests.

Discussion

Future empirical research should examine how patients, families, nurses and physicians actually negotiate restraint decisions in rehabilitation, geriatric and long-term care settings influenced by Confucian values. Such studies should distinguish patient-level vulnerability, family-level expectations, professional responsibility and institutional constraints. This would allow future guidance to be based on stronger empirical evidence rather than on ethical interpretation alone.

The present evidence does not justify universal claims about restraint policy across all settings. A more proportionate implication is that healthcare organisations caring for older adults should develop culturally sensitive procedures that require assessment of risk and capacity, consideration of relational autonomy and substituted judgement, exploration of least restrictive alternatives, involvement of family or proxies where appropriate, documentation of reasons and consent, frequent reassessment, and timely removal of restraint. In Confucian-influenced contexts, such procedures should recognise the importance of filial responsibility and family participation while ensuring that these values do not override the older person’s dignity, comfort and known preferences.

Proportionate implications for practice and policy

From this perspective, restraint reduction should not depend only on asking nurses to communicate better or reflect more deeply. It also requires institutional procedures that support ethical practice: assessment tools, escalation pathways, access to alternatives, multidisciplinary review, documentation standards, debriefing, and psychological support when staff experience moral distress. Such measures are practice implications rather than definitive policy prescriptions, because the reviewed evidence base remains limited.

Nurses’ ethical dilemmas should not be interpreted solely as individual uncertainty or lack of knowledge. The included evidence suggests that moral distress may arise when nurses recognise the ethical problems of restraint but feel constrained by patient risk, family expectations, staffing limitations, institutional routines or fear of blame. Organisational factors therefore form part of the ethical problem. If institutions lack clear protocols, adequate staffing, training in alternatives, and support after restraint-related incidents, individual nurses may carry a moral burden that should be shared at team and organisational levels.

Nurses’ moral distress and organisational responsibility

A patient-centred approach requires that restraint be treated as a last-resort, time-limited and regularly reviewed intervention. Decisions should include assessment of capacity, exploration of alternatives, clear documentation of the reason for restraint, communication with the patient and family, and review of whether the restraint remains necessary. Substituted judgement should be distinguished from general family preference: the ethical question is not only whether relatives agree, but whether the decision reflects the older person’s values and whether the restriction is proportionate to the risk being addressed.

The review also highlights the continuing tension between paternalism and patient-centred decision-making. Restraint may be ethically defensible in narrowly defined circumstances, such as an urgent and serious risk of injury when less restrictive alternatives have failed or are unavailable. However, restraint becomes ethically problematic when it is used primarily to compensate for staffing shortages, reduce workload, prevent institutional liability or maintain routine order. In those circumstances, the language of beneficence may conceal practices that insufficiently respect autonomy, dignity and proportionality.

Paternalism, patient-centred care and substituted judgement

In Confucian-influenced settings, the moral meaning of restraint may be particularly complex because care is often understood through reciprocal family obligation and filial responsibility. A family’s agreement to restraint may represent an attempt to protect the older person, while refusal may represent an attempt to preserve dignity and avoid appearing unfilial. Nurses may therefore face a three-sided ethical task: protecting the patient from immediate harm, respecting the patient’s personhood, and supporting families who experience guilt, fear or uncertainty. This reinforces the need for careful communication rather than merely obtaining a signature from a proxy decision-maker.

The ethics of care provides an additional lens for interpreting the findings. Physical restraint decisions occur in relationships of dependency, where older adults may be physically frail, cognitively impaired, dependent on tubes or devices, or unable to protect themselves from immediate harm. Such vulnerability creates a genuine professional and familial obligation to prevent foreseeable injury. However, vulnerability should not be used to justify routine restriction. An ethics-of-care perspective requires attentiveness, responsiveness and responsibility, but it also requires sensitivity to how restrictive practices may intensify helplessness, fear, shame or loss of personhood.

Ethics of care, vulnerability and the moral meaning of restraint

This interpretation suggests that family-centred decision-making should not be equated with ethically valid decision-making unless it remains anchored in the older person’s dignity, preferences and best interests. For patients with decision-making capacity, informed consent and refusal should remain central. For patients lacking capacity, family members or legal proxies should be guided toward substituted judgement, meaning that decisions should be based on what the patient would probably have wanted rather than what relatives or professionals find most convenient. When the patient’s previous wishes are unknown, best-interest reasoning should consider safety, comfort, proportionality, the least restrictive alternative and the likely effect of restraint on dignity and wellbeing.

A central ethical tension identified in the review concerns the relationship between patient autonomy and safety protection. Conventional autonomy emphasises the individual patient’s right to be informed, to consent, and to refuse restrictive interventions. However, restraint decisions involving older adults with frailty, dementia, delirium or communication impairment often occur in circumstances where autonomy is partially compromised or relationally mediated. In such situations, the concept of relational autonomy is useful because it recognises that patients’ preferences and choices are shaped by relationships, dependency, communication conditions and care environments. In Confucian-influenced family-centred contexts, family involvement may support autonomy when relatives help articulate the older person’s values, life history and previously expressed wishes. At the same time, family involvement may threaten autonomy if relatives’ anxiety, institutional pressure or cultural expectations replace the patient’s own values.

Autonomy, relational autonomy and culturally situated decision-making

The findings indicate that restraint decisions are shaped by clinical risk, cognitive impairment, frailty, staffing conditions, institutional routines, family involvement and culturally situated expectations about responsibility for older relatives. In Confucian-influenced contexts, filial piety does not operate as a simple determinant that either increases or decreases restraint use. Rather, it provides a moral vocabulary through which families and professionals interpret care, safety, dignity and responsibility. Some relatives may view restraint as a protective act that fulfils family duty, whereas others may experience restraint as inconsistent with respect, reciprocity and dignified care. This dual meaning is important because it shows why ethical decision-making cannot be reduced to either individual autonomy alone or family preference alone.

This review identified a small but conceptually relevant body of evidence on ethical decision-making about physical restraint use among older adults. Direct Confucian-influenced evidence mainly came from Chinese or East Asian studies, whereas international studies provided comparative ethical concepts rather than direct cultural evidence. Therefore, the interpretation was deliberately layered: first, the review identified empirical findings reported in the included studies; second, it interpreted these findings through contemporary bioethical concepts; and third, it translated the resulting ethical insights into cautious implications for practice and research. This structure was used to avoid treating a small and heterogeneous evidence base as stronger than it is.

Conclusion

This systematic scoping review and interpretive ethical synthesis suggests that physical restraint decisions for older adults involve a persistent tension between safety, autonomy, relational autonomy, dignity, vulnerability, professional responsibility and family-centred care. In Confucian-influenced contexts, filial responsibility and family participation may shape how restraint is understood and justified, but the available evidence does not support treating Confucian culture as a single or uniform determinant of restraint use. Rather, family-centred values should be incorporated into shared decision-making only when they remain attentive to the older person’s preferences, dignity, comfort and least-restrictive care.

For clinical practice, the findings cautiously support careful assessment before restraint use, clear communication with patients and families, attention to substituted judgement when patients lack capacity, documentation of consent or proxy involvement where feasible, regular reassessment, and active exploration of alternatives. These implications should be interpreted as context-sensitive recommendations rather than definitive practice standards because they are based on a small and methodologically heterogeneous corpus.

For policy and research, the review highlights the need for further empirical studies in Confucian-influenced rehabilitation and geriatric care settings, particularly studies examining relational autonomy, family involvement, nurses’ moral distress and organisational constraints. More robust evidence is needed before broad policy conclusions can be drawn.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, although the search was systematic, the final corpus was small and methodologically heterogeneous, including qualitative studies, cross-sectional studies and review articles. The evidence was therefore unsuitable for meta-analysis or for strong causal conclusions. Second, only four included studies directly reflected China or Confucian-influenced care contexts; the remaining studies were used as comparative ethical evidence. This limits the strength of conclusions about Confucian cultural specificity. Third, some studies were not conducted exclusively in rehabilitation wards, which may limit transferability to rehabilitation nursing practice. Fourth, the interpretive ethical synthesis inevitably involved author judgement. To reduce overinterpretation, this review distinguished direct findings from interpretive inferences and broader normative implications.
