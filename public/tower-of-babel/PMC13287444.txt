Metaheuristic-optimized interaction-aware deep learning with large language model assistance for data-driven water quality prediction
Authors: Ebrahim A Mattar, El-Sayed M El-Kenawy, Sarah M Alhammad, Marwa M Eid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of water-quality indicators remains challenging in small tabular environmental datasets because physicochemical variables can exhibit nonlinear interdependencies and modern deep-learning models are sensitive to hyperparameter configuration. In this study, the supervised regression task is defined as predicting dissolved oxygen (mg/L) from the remaining measured physicochemical variables using a modest public dataset of 200 samples. To address this task, the Automatic Feature Interaction Network (AutoInt) is coupled with the Ninja Optimization Algorithm (NiOA) for wrapper-based hyperparameter optimization under a controlled and reproducible experimental protocol. All evaluations use a fixed train/validation/test split (70%/15%/15%) with leakage-safe preprocessing based only on training-set statistics, and results are aggregated over independent runs with controlled initialization seeds and mean ± standard deviation reporting. Baseline AutoInt without metaheuristic optimization achieves a mean squared error of , whereas the NiOA-optimized AutoInt configuration reaches under the matched optimization budget of 1500 function evaluations (, ). These findings indicate that NiOA-guided hyperparameter tuning can substantially improve AutoInt performance within this fixed benchmark setting. However, because the dataset is small, cross-sectional, and lacks explicit spatial or temporal structure, the results should be interpreted as benchmark-specific evidence rather than broad operational validation. Further evaluation on larger, independent, and spatiotemporally diverse water-quality datasets is required before generalizing the fitted model to wider environmental monitoring or decision-support applications.Keywords: Water quality prediction, Tabular deep learning, Metaheuristic optimization, Hyperparameter tuning, Environmental data modeling, Reproducible benchmarkingSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Water quality assessment constitutes a foundational element of contemporary environmental monitoring and risk-management practice, underpinning ecosystem conservation, sustainable water-resources governance, and public health protection1,2. In operational terms, the concept of “water quality” refers to the extent to which physicochemical conditions support designated uses (e.g., drinking-water supply, irrigation, aquatic habitat integrity, and industrial use) and remain within acceptable risk thresholds. Because aquatic systems integrate signals from catchment processes, atmospheric exchanges, and human activities, water-quality monitoring functions not only as a diagnostic activity but also as an early-warning component of environmental stewardship. Both surface-water and groundwater systems are subject to continuous modification by interacting natural drivers (e.g., lithology-controlled geochemical weathering, seasonal hydrologic regimes, and ecological cycling) and anthropogenic pressures (e.g., agricultural intensification, urban wastewater discharges, industrial effluents, and land-use change). These coupled influences generate complex spatial and temporal variability in water physicochemistry, often producing nonstationary patterns that complicate interpretation and regulatory compliance. For instance, the same site may exhibit distinct water-quality regimes across hydrologic seasons, with dilution, evapoconcentration, and biogeochemical cycling altering concentrations and relationships among variables in ways that are not constant through time. Accordingly, systematic monitoring and evaluation of water-quality indicators remain indispensable for detecting contamination events, tracking ecological condition, supporting enforcement of water-quality standards, and informing prevention-oriented management strategies2. At the same time, the present study focuses on a compact cross-sectional benchmark rather than on a spatially or temporally resolved monitoring network; therefore, the environmental motivation is used to justify the dissolved-oxygen regression task, whereas broad claims about site transfer, seasonal dynamics, or operational deployment require independent validation on datasets that explicitly contain spatial and/or temporal structure.

In routine monitoring programs, a set of core physicochemical parameters is commonly employed to characterize water-quality status. These include acidity–alkalinity balance (pH), oxygen regime (dissolved oxygen), thermal conditions (temperature), optical and particulate behavior (turbidity), ionic strength and salinity proxies (electrical conductivity), and nutrient-related indicators (e.g., nitrate concentration where relevant). Collectively, such variables provide an operationally efficient yet information-rich description of water conditions because they encode multiple interacting processes: pH influences chemical speciation, solubility equilibria, and the viability of many aquatic organisms; dissolved oxygen reflects metabolic balance and aeration–consumption dynamics; temperature modulates reaction kinetics, gas solubility, and biological activity; turbidity serves as a proxy for suspended matter that can affect light penetration and habitat quality; and conductivity reflects dissolved ionic content that is frequently associated with geogenic contributions and anthropogenic inputs. Beyond their individual interpretations, these indicators are informative precisely because they co-vary under shared drivers, meaning that multivariate patterns can reveal process-relevant structure even when individual measurements appear within nominal bounds. For example, shifts in conductivity alongside changes in turbidity and dissolved oxygen may indicate altered mixing, runoff contributions, or changes in dominant biogeochemical pathways, while pH can modulate how other constituents partition between dissolved and particulate phases. The selection, measurement, and interpretation of these indicators are supported by established monitoring guidance and standardized analytical procedures, which help ensure methodological comparability across sites and time1,3. Standardization is particularly important in regulatory and cross-site synthesis contexts, where differences in sampling, preservation, and analytical conditions can create apparent trends that do not reflect environmental change.

Historically, water-quality assessment has relied on descriptive statistics, threshold-based compliance checks, composite indices, and deterministic or empirical models grounded in physical and chemical reasoning. These approaches remain essential because they provide interpretable benchmarks, regulatory alignment, and process-informed understanding. In practice, threshold comparisons translate scientific knowledge and policy priorities into actionable criteria, while indices offer concise summaries that can support communication and screening across many sites or time points. However, such methods can be limited when relationships among parameters are strongly nonlinear, when interactions are high-order and context dependent, or when heterogeneous conditions introduce regime shifts that are difficult to encode explicitly. In environmental systems, these challenges are common because multiple processes can act simultaneously, and the effective relationship between variables may change as boundary conditions (e.g., flow regime, temperature range, or disturbance intensity) shift. Such limitations have contributed to increased interest in complementary data-driven approaches, particularly as open-access environmental datasets have become more available and as modern learning algorithms have matured. In this setting, machine learning provides a pragmatic framework for learning predictive relationships directly from observations, enabling rapid estimation of target variables, supporting exploratory analysis, and facilitating comparative evaluation across alternative modelling choices4. Importantly, the value of such models in monitoring contexts is often practical as well as scientific: reliable surrogate prediction can reduce measurement burden for difficult-to-monitor variables and can assist in prioritizing follow-up sampling or intervention. Nevertheless, when datasets are small and cross-sectional, predictive gains must be interpreted conservatively because they may reflect strong within-sample correlations, sensitivity to a particular train/validation/test partition, or validation-set over-specialization during wrapper-based hyperparameter optimization rather than generalizable environmental structure.

Tabular environmental datasets—typical of many water-monitoring contexts—are especially relevant for assessing modern learning methods because they comprise structured numeric variables with potentially intricate dependency patterns. These datasets often combine heterogeneous units, differing magnitudes, and partially redundant signals, so predictive performance depends on the capacity to model interactions and conditional effects rather than only linear trends. At the same time, it is increasingly recognized that deep learning does not automatically outperform traditional methods on tabular data; success depends on appropriate architectures, inductive biases for interaction modeling, and careful training configuration5. This observation is particularly salient for environmental applications where sample sizes are frequently modest and where the cost of overfitting is high in decision-support settings. Consequently, tabular water-quality datasets provide a meaningful testbed for evaluating interaction-aware learning models and robust optimization strategies under realistic data constraints. Public benchmarks hosted on platforms such as Kaggle further enhance transparency by enabling repeatable experimentation and standardized comparison across studies6. Such benchmarks also encourage clearer reporting of preprocessing and validation choices, which is essential when transferring methods from general-purpose machine learning to domain-sensitive environmental inference. In this work, the public benchmark is used primarily to evaluate methodological transparency, controlled hyperparameter optimization, and repeated-run reporting in a small-sample setting; it is not treated as sufficient evidence for broad deployment across heterogeneous water bodies.

Recent progress in artificial intelligence also shows that modern learning systems increasingly rely on structured representations, geometric modeling, and data-efficient optimization when learning from complex or limited observations. Hypergraph learning, for example, provides flexible tools for modeling high-order correlations beyond pairwise relationships7, while recent work on deep loss convexification studies how learning can reshape nonconvex iterative-model objectives to improve local optimization behavior8. Related advances in deep learning for point-cloud instance segmentation, remote-sensing interpretation, virtual–real 3D reconstruction, and data-scarce generative modeling further illustrate the broader movement toward representation learning under structural complexity and limited-data conditions9–12. Although these studies address application domains different from water-quality regression, they motivate the present emphasis on interaction-aware representation learning, explicit optimization design, and reproducible evaluation under constrained data availability.

Despite substantial progress in environmental machine learning, predictive modelling of water-quality variables remains challenging for both scientific and practical reasons. First, water-quality parameters are interdependent: they interact through feedback mechanisms shaped by hydrology, biogeochemistry, and external stressors, and these interactions can vary across seasons, sites, and disturbance regimes. This means that predictive structure is often conditional: the effect of one variable on a target may depend on the levels of other variables, and the same multivariate configuration may have different implications under different hydroclimatic contexts. As a result, accurate prediction often requires models capable of capturing nonlinear dependencies and high-order interactions rather than only marginal effects of individual predictors4. From a problem-definition perspective, the task can therefore be framed not merely as fitting a regression surface, but as learning a multivariate mapping that remains stable under limited data, measurement noise, and potential regime heterogeneity that is typical of monitoring programs. Because the present dataset is cross-sectional and does not include explicit temporal or spatial covariates, the modelling task here is restricted to learning relationships within the observed sample distribution, and broader claims about seasonality- or site-driven regimes are treated as motivating context rather than directly tested properties.

Second, model performance is frequently sensitive to configuration choices. In particular, advanced tabular deep-learning architectures are designed to learn complex interaction structures, but their predictive reliability depends strongly on hyperparameters governing representational capacity (e.g., embedding size and depth), training dynamics (e.g., learning-rate schedules and optimizer settings), and regularization (e.g., dropout and weight decay). Sensitivity is not only a practical concern but also a methodological one, because unstable tuning can lead to performance estimates that are difficult to reproduce and conclusions that depend on accidental choices rather than principled design. This sensitivity can be amplified in small to medium-sized environmental datasets, where limited sample size increases the risk of overfitting and makes training dynamics less stable under suboptimal configurations5,13. In such settings, hyperparameters effectively determine the balance between underfitting (insufficient capacity to represent interactions) and overfitting (memorization of idiosyncratic patterns), and small changes in regularization or learning rate can materially alter the learned interaction structure. Accordingly, credible performance claims require evaluation protocols that quantify variability across repeated runs, disclose the selected hyperparameters, and explicitly acknowledge the risk of validation-set over-specialization when wrapper-based tuning is applied to small datasets.

Third, conventional hyperparameter tuning methods such as grid search or random search can become inefficient as dimensionality increases and as interactions among hyperparameters become more pronounced. Grid search scales poorly because it allocates equal effort to all dimensions regardless of their influence, while random search can still require many trials to reliably discover high-performing regions when the objective is noisy and the effective dimension is high. While random search can be more effective than exhaustive grids in many high-dimensional scenarios, it may still be computationally burdensome when each configuration requires iterative training and validation14. This motivates the use of metaheuristic optimization algorithms, which are widely used for derivative-free optimization in complex, multimodal landscapes and can provide a practical balance between exploration of the search space and exploitation of promising configurations15. Canonical families of such methods include swarm intelligence approaches (e.g., particle swarm optimization) and evolutionary strategies (e.g., differential evolution), which have been extensively studied and adopted across engineering optimization contexts16,17. In the present context, the objective surface is further complicated by stochastic training effects and by the possibility that multiple hyperparameter combinations yield similar validation performance, creating plateaus and local basins that challenge purely local search strategies. In small-sample settings, this challenge is compounded by the risk that wrapper-based tuning over-specializes to a particular validation subset; therefore, the optimization protocol must be paired with transparent reporting of evaluation budgets, selected hyperparameters, run-level variability, and statistical comparisons across optimizers.

Recent metaheuristic research increasingly emphasizes adaptive search control, hybridization, population-diversity preservation, learning-guided operators, and stability-aware mechanisms rather than relying only on metaphorical inspiration. For example, elite-pool and Brownian-motion mechanisms have been introduced to strengthen the sine cosine algorithm for global optimization18, while the snow ablation optimizer has been proposed as a population-based method for numerical and engineering design problems19. Improved slime mould variants further illustrate the use of dynamic random search, adaptive mutation, adaptive grouping, and learning operators to balance exploration and exploitation20,21. Other recent developments incorporate reinforcement learning and gradient-search schemes into JAYA22, hybridize grasshopper optimization with complementary search mechanisms23, or introduce stability-oriented social learning strategies for photovoltaic-parameter estimation24. These studies collectively indicate that meaningful progress in metaheuristic optimization is increasingly associated with explicit search-behavior design, robustness analysis, and reproducible benchmarking.

At the same time, the broader optimization literature has raised important concerns about metaphor-based metaheuristics, especially when algorithmic novelty is justified primarily by biological, physical, or behavioral analogies rather than by clearly specified operators, transparent benchmarks, and statistically defensible comparisons. The present study therefore does not treat the metaphor underlying any optimizer as evidence of theoretical superiority. Instead, all metaheuristic methods are considered as black-box search procedures acting on the same hyperparameter vector, the same feasible bounds, and the same evaluation budget. This framing is important because the central question is not whether a metaphor is plausible, but whether a published optimizer can provide useful, reproducible hyperparameter search behavior for an interaction-aware tabular model under controlled experimental conditions.

Within this methodological landscape, integrating metaheuristic search with interaction-aware tabular models offers a coherent pathway to improving robustness and generalization in water-quality prediction tasks. Specifically, metaheuristics can be used to systematically explore hyperparameter spaces that are nonconvex, noisy, and potentially constrained, without relying on gradient information and without requiring analytically tractable objective functions15. This is particularly relevant when computational budgets are limited and when the objective surface is shaped by stochastic training behavior, as is common in neural-network optimization. Conceptually, the problem becomes one of allocating limited evaluations to identify stable, well-generalizing configurations that capture meaningful feature interactions while maintaining sufficient regularization to avoid spurious associations that can arise in small environmental datasets. To make such searches auditable, the hyperparameter domain and optimization budget must be explicitly disclosed, the selected configuration must be reported, and aggregate metrics must be accompanied by repeated-run dispersion and statistical testing.

The overarching goal of this study is to develop a robust and methodologically transparent framework for data-driven water-quality prediction using tabular deep-learning techniques. Robustness here refers to achieving strong predictive performance while maintaining stability across runs and avoiding undue sensitivity to arbitrary training choices, whereas methodological transparency refers to clearly specifying preprocessing, validation, and tuning steps so that results can be audited and reproduced. To support reproducibility and facilitate benchmarking, the study uses a publicly available Kaggle dataset comprising routine water-quality measurements, thereby enabling independent verification and extension by the research community6. The use of a public dataset also helps separate methodological contributions from site-specific idiosyncrasies, allowing the modelling pipeline to be evaluated as a general-purpose approach for small-sample tabular water-quality prediction problems. In this study, the prediction target is fixed as dissolved oxygen, and all reported results correspond to this single-target regression setting.

A central objective is to employ the Automatic Feature Interaction Network (AutoInt) as the primary predictive model. AutoInt is specifically designed to learn feature interactions through self-attention mechanisms, providing an explicit inductive bias for capturing high-order dependencies in structured tabular data. Unlike approaches that require manual construction of feature crosses, AutoInt aims to learn interaction structure end-to-end by weighting relationships among embedded feature representations, enabling flexible modeling of conditional effects among physicochemical variables. This property is particularly relevant in water-quality contexts, where predictive performance can depend on interaction effects among physicochemical variables rather than on additive contributions alone13. In practical terms, an interaction-aware model is expected to better reflect coupled behavior among variables (e.g., regimes in which the influence of one predictor strengthens or weakens depending on another), which is a common characteristic of aquatic-system measurements. Given that strong correlations may exist among water-quality parameters in small observational datasets, the study emphasizes leakage-safe preprocessing and transparent validation to reduce the likelihood that near-deterministic associations or preprocessing artifacts inflate performance estimates.

To address the sensitivity of AutoInt to hyperparameter selection, the study integrates the Ninja Optimization Algorithm (NiOA) as a hyperparameter optimization mechanism. NiOA is treated as an established, previously published metaheuristic, and its role in this work is confined to guiding hyperparameter search rather than introducing a new optimizer. By leveraging NiOA for derivative-free tuning, the study aims to improve the stability and effectiveness of AutoInt configuration under constrained computational budgets25. NiOA is selected because it is the focal optimizer evaluated in the proposed workflow and because the study examines whether this published population-based optimizer can serve as an effective wrapper-based hyperparameter-optimization engine for AutoInt under a fixed evaluation budget. The study does not claim that NiOA is theoretically superior to all metaheuristics; rather, it compares its empirical behavior against selected population-based optimizers under harmonized search spaces, population sizes, iteration limits, and validation objectives. This objective emphasizes the optimization process as a methodological component: the intent is to systematically identify hyperparameter settings that yield reliable generalization rather than to rely on ad hoc defaults. Finally, the study conducts a systematic comparison between NiOA-assisted AutoInt, baseline AutoInt training without metaheuristic optimization, and alternative optimizer-assisted configurations under consistent experimental conditions, ensuring fairness and methodological rigor. This comparative design is intended to clarify whether performance improvements arise from the choice of interaction-aware architecture, from the choice of optimizer, or from their coupling within a controlled experimental protocol. In addition, the study reports both predictive accuracy and measured computational cost under a matched budget, while treating computational-resource comparisons as implementation-specific observations rather than universal complexity rankings.

A further emerging direction for understanding metaheuristic behavior is the use of complex-network analysis to study population interactions, information flow, and collective search dynamics in evolutionary computation and swarm intelligence. Network-based views of evolutionary computation and particle swarm optimization provide a route toward analyzing optimizer behavior beyond metaphorical descriptions by examining how candidate solutions interact, cluster, diversify, and converge over time. This perspective is outside the empirical scope of the present small-sample benchmark, but it motivates future work in which the search dynamics of NiOA and comparator optimizers could be analyzed using network-science descriptors in addition to final predictive metrics.

The contributions of this work are methodological and empirical. First, the study establishes a structured preprocessing and feature-handling workflow tailored to tabular water-quality data, emphasizing data consistency, numerical stability, and methodological transparency in alignment with standardized measurement principles3. This includes adopting practices that support fair evaluation, such as applying scaling procedures consistently and maintaining strict separation between training and evaluation subsets to mitigate leakage-driven optimism. Second, it introduces a hybrid modelling workflow that couples AutoInt with NiOA for systematic hyperparameter optimization, thereby combining an interaction-aware tabular architecture with a metaheuristic search strategy suitable for complex, nonconvex tuning landscapes13,15,25. The combination is positioned as a controlled benchmarking workflow rather than as a new optimizer or a new neural architecture. The combination is positioned as a practical response to the observed sensitivity of tabular deep models, aiming to discover well-performing configurations efficiently while remaining compatible with the black-box nature of validation-based objectives. Third, it frames comparative evaluation against established optimizers using a standardized metric suite and consistent computational constraints, focusing on relative behavior and robustness rather than isolated claims. This framing supports interpretation of results as comparative evidence under matched budgets, which is especially important for metaheuristics whose effectiveness can depend strongly on evaluation allocation. Finally, the study aligns with best practices for interpretability by incorporating diagnostic and explainable modelling tools to support transparency in environmental decision-support contexts26. In applied environmental modeling, such interpretability support is essential for linking predictive patterns back to plausible domain relationships and for enabling responsible use of model outputs in monitoring and management workflows. Given the limited size and cross-sectional nature of the benchmark dataset used here, the study treats its findings as evidence of methodological feasibility under controlled conditions, with broader environmental generalization reserved for follow-on validation on larger, diverse monitoring datasets.

Related work

Water-quality prediction and assessment occupy a well-established methodological space spanning environmental science, ecosystem protection, and public-health risk management. In practical monitoring settings, agencies and research programs routinely collect multivariate physicochemical measurements—including indicators of acidity/alkalinity, oxygen regime, ionic-content proxies, and optical surrogates of suspended particulate matter—to characterize water-system status and diagnose potential stressors. These measurements are rarely independent: they are linked through coupled geochemical equilibria, transport processes, and biologically mediated transformations, and they often respond jointly to hydrologic variability and human pressures. As a result, water-quality datasets are typically characterized by nonlinear dependencies, context-specific interactions, heteroscedastic noise, and occasional regime shifts driven by seasonal cycles or episodic disturbances. These characteristics have progressively motivated the adoption of data-driven methods that can (i) represent nonlinear functional relationships without requiring fully specified process models, (ii) remain effective when observations are limited, noisy, or unevenly distributed across conditions, and (iii) support interpretability and methodological transparency so that predictions can be scrutinized and responsibly used in management contexts. In alignment with these needs, the prior work most directly motivating the present study can be synthesized into three connected themes: (a) machine learning (ML) for water-quality prediction and index-based assessment, (b) deep learning for tabular data with explicit or implicit feature-interaction modeling, and (c) hyperparameter optimization (HPO), emphasizing metaheuristic strategies as practical alternatives when conventional tuning becomes inefficient. Given the small-sample and cross-sectional characteristics typical of many publicly available environmental benchmarks, including the dataset used in this study, particular emphasis is placed on validation rigor, uncertainty quantification, and transparent reporting of optimization procedures.

Machine learning for water-quality prediction and index-based assessment

A substantial portion of the ML literature on water quality can be organized around two complementary objectives. The first objective is direct prediction of specific water-quality variables (e.g., dissolved oxygen, nutrient concentrations, turbidity proxies, conductivity-related measures), typically framed as regression over multivariate measurements. The second objective is composite assessment, often operationalized through water quality indices, where multiple variables are aggregated into a single diagnostic quantity used for communication, screening, and management prioritization. Although these objectives differ in their outputs—variable-level estimates versus synthesized indicators—they share common methodological requirements, including robust handling of correlated predictors, principled validation under realistic data constraints, and transparency regarding uncertainty and sensitivity to modelling assumptions.

For direct prediction, comprehensive evidence indicates that ML methods are adopted primarily because the governing processes behind water quality are strongly coupled and nonlinear, and therefore difficult to capture with simple linear statistical models across heterogeneous conditions. A broad review by Zhu et al.4 synthesizes ML applications across multiple water-system contexts (surface water, groundwater, drinking water, wastewater, and marine environments), emphasizing both the performance potential and recurring methodological pitfalls (e.g., limited representativeness, inconsistent validation protocols, and underdeveloped interpretability practice). Beyond cataloguing algorithms, this line of work highlights that modelling success in environmental systems is frequently constrained by data-generation realities: sampling designs may be opportunistic rather than statistically balanced; measurement error and censoring can be nontrivial; and the joint distribution of predictors may differ across sites or time periods, thereby challenging generalization. At a complementary level of granularity, Huang et al.27 provides a wide-angle review of ML in natural and engineered water systems, situating water-quality prediction within a broader methodological pipeline that includes data preparation, model selection, evaluation, and deployment considerations. Importantly, such pipeline-oriented accounts emphasize that performance claims are only as credible as the preprocessing and validation design that supports them, particularly when models are intended for operational decision support. In parallel, reviews focused on deep learning (DL) for water-quality management emphasize that modern neural architectures are increasingly used to capture high-dimensional dependencies and temporal dynamics, while also cautioning that success is often contingent on careful training design and fit-for-purpose validation28. This caution is especially pertinent when complex models are applied to modest datasets common in environmental monitoring, where apparent gains can reflect favourable splits or tuning artifacts rather than robust predictive improvement. Recent methodological critiques further stress that repeated resampling, dispersion reporting (mean ± standard deviation), and clear disclosure of target scaling are necessary safeguards when reporting very small error magnitudes on limited environmental datasets.

Applied research further illustrates that predictive performance depends not only on the choice of learner, but also on the extent to which training regimes respect the statistical and physical structure of water-quality data. For example, dissolved oxygen modeling is frequently used as a benchmark due to its central ecological role and its sensitivity to interacting drivers; Dehghani et al.29 demonstrates how hybrid ML strategies can be used for dissolved oxygen prediction in running waters, highlighting the value of hybridization when single learners struggle under complex, interacting covariates. The methodological motivation for such hybridization is typically that different model classes capture different aspects of the mapping from predictors to targets (e.g., smooth nonlinear trends versus localized responses or interaction effects), and combining them can reduce variance or mitigate systematic bias under shifting conditions. In the broader literature, these studies collectively reinforce a key implication: water-quality datasets often exhibit interaction effects that are scientifically expected but difficult to encode manually (e.g., temperature modulating oxygen solubility and reaction kinetics; ionic-strength proxies correlating with pollutant loads; optical surrogates reflecting particulate transport). Consequently, modelling strategies that can discover and exploit interactions directly from data are attractive, provided that they are validated carefully and interpreted responsibly. However, when datasets are small and lack explicit temporal or spatial stratification, careful split design and sensitivity analysis are indispensable to ensure that learned interactions reflect generalizable structure rather than idiosyncratic sample patterns.

For composite assessment, the water quality index (WQI) literature underscores that index definition, parameter weighting, and aggregation choices can materially affect both uncertainty and interpretability. A detailed review by Uddin et al.30 emphasizes that WQI pipelines introduce uncertainty and subjectivity that can dominate downstream conclusions if not carefully documented and justified. This concern is not merely philosophical: alternative weighting schemes, normalization choices, and aggregation operators can yield meaningfully different rankings or classifications even when based on the same underlying measurements, which complicates comparisons across studies and can affect policy communication. More recent applied studies have increasingly coupled ML-based predictors with explainability tooling to bridge predictive accuracy and interpretability—particularly when index outputs support decision-making and risk communication. For instance,, Choudhary et al.31 integrates stacked ensemble regression with Shapley Additive explanations (SHAP) to interpret WQI prediction, exemplifying a broader trend toward explainable environmental ML. The methodological significance of such work is that it treats interpretability as a first-class requirement rather than an afterthought, thereby supporting more defensible use of ML outputs in contexts where stakeholders must understand which measured factors drive predicted risk or status.

From the standpoint of methodological design, these strands converge on a practical challenge that is highly relevant to the present study: water-quality datasets are frequently tabular, moderate-to-small in sample size, heterogeneous in units, and characterized by nonlinear couplings. These properties create demand for tabular learning models that (i) can represent feature interactions without brittle manual feature construction and (ii) can be tuned and validated with particular care to avoid overstated performance, especially when datasets are small enough that resampling variance and split sensitivity become prominent. Accordingly, robust hyperparameter optimization and transparent evaluation protocols are increasingly viewed not as peripheral refinements but as central methodological requirements in environmental ML research.

Deep learning for tabular data and interaction modeling

Although gradient-boosted decision trees remain strong baselines on many tabular problems, there is sustained interest in DL architectures that learn representations and interactions end-to-end. A unifying perspective is that tabular data are not merely “vectors of numbers”; rather, they are structured collections of heterogeneous fields whose predictive signal can depend on higher-order interactions among features. In many scientific applications, the same numeric feature can have different predictive meaning depending on the configuration of other variables, and the relevant signal may be concentrated in interaction terms rather than in strong marginal effects. A comprehensive survey by Borisov et al.5 organizes tabular DL into architectural and training paradigms, including specialized inductive biases, attention-based mechanisms, and regularization strategies aimed at improving competitiveness and stability. Within this framing, tabular DL is not viewed as a monolithic alternative to trees, but as a family of designs that attempt to inject useful structure (e.g., interaction discovery, feature selection, or hierarchical abstraction) into neural learning pipelines. In addition, an influential viewpoint by y Shwartz-Ziv and Armon32 argues that DL is not universally superior for tabular learning, thereby sharpening the methodological requirement that DL-based choices should be justified, carefully tuned, and compared against strong baselines. In practical terms, this viewpoint reinforces the importance of experimental discipline: when DL is applied to tabular environmental datasets, credible gains require controlled comparisons, transparent hyperparameter reporting, and validation strategies appropriate to the data-generating process. This is particularly critical in small-sample settings, where performance differences may fall within the variance induced by stochastic optimization and data partitioning.

Within tabular DL, explicit interaction modeling is particularly relevant for environmental data because physical and chemical variables often co-determine outcomes through coupled mechanisms. AutoInt (Automatic Feature Interaction Learning via Self-Attentive Neural Networks) is a prominent architecture designed explicitly to learn feature interactions through multi-head self-attention operating on embedded feature representations13. The conceptual advantage of this mechanism is that attention can assign varying importance to pairwise and higher-order interactions in a data-adaptive manner, effectively learning which cross-feature relations are most predictive under the observed distribution. While introduced in the context of feature crossing for recommender systems, its central mechanism—attention-based discovery and weighting of interactions among embedded features—generalizes naturally to regression settings where cross-feature dependencies are expected but difficult to specify analytically. This is directly aligned with water-quality prediction tasks, where interactions can reflect underlying chemistry (e.g., joint controls on solubility or equilibrium), hydrologic conditions (e.g., dilution and transport effects), or measurement couplings (e.g., proxies for common latent processes). Nevertheless, the ability of attention-based models to capture such dependencies does not obviate the need for careful validation; interaction learning can amplify overfitting risk when sample sizes are limited and regularization is insufficient.

AutoInt also fits into a broader family of attention-centric tabular models. TabTransformer adapts Transformer-style contextual embeddings to tabular inputs, emphasizing representation robustness (especially for mixed feature types) via contextualization across fields33. In such architectures, contextualization can be interpreted as learning feature representations that are conditional on the broader feature set, which can help when the relevance of a given variable depends on the state of other variables. SAINT introduces attention across rows and columns and explores contrastive pretraining to improve label efficiency, which is directly relevant to scientific datasets where labeled samples may be limited34. From a methodological standpoint, this introduces an additional degree of freedom: performance may be improved not only by architectural choices but also by leveraging representation learning objectives that exploit unlabeled structure. Other notable architectures seek different trade-offs between performance and interpretability. TabNet introduces sequential attentive feature selection, yielding step-wise feature usage patterns that can support interpretation35. Such step-wise selection can be particularly valuable in environmental applications where model users may wish to understand which measured parameters were emphasized at different stages of the prediction process. NODE (Neural Oblivious Decision Ensembles) blends tree-ensemble structure with differentiable optimization, aiming to combine benefits of tree-like inductive bias and neural training36. This hybridization is relevant to environmental tabular learning because it attempts to retain the partitioning behaviour and interaction handling typical of trees while maintaining the end-to-end trainability of neural methods. Finally, empirical reassessments such as Gorishniy et al.37 (which introduces the FT-Transformer among other baselines) emphasize that training protocols and architectural details can be decisive for DL competitiveness on tabular data. This observation is particularly important for small datasets, where differences in normalization, early stopping, learning rate schedules, and regularization can dominate outcomes and obscure the contribution of the model class itself.

Two methodological lessons from this literature are especially salient for the present work. First, interaction-oriented tabular DL can reduce the need for extensive manual feature engineering, which is appealing in environmental systems where interactions are expected but their functional form is uncertain. This can support more standardized modelling pipelines across monitoring programs, provided that learned interactions are validated and interpreted with care. Second, performance is often highly sensitive to hyperparameters (e.g., embedding size, number of attention heads, depth, dropout, learning rate schedules, and regularization), especially on small datasets. This sensitivity elevates HPO from a “routine tuning step” to a core methodological component that can determine validity, robustness, and reproducibility, particularly when model selection decisions are based on incremental performance differences. Consequently, detailed disclosure of the hyperparameter search space, optimization budget, and selected configuration is necessary to ensure that reported improvements are auditable and reproducible.

Hyperparameter optimization and metaheuristic search

HPO is central to ML reliability because hyperparameters control model capacity, regularization, and optimization behavior, thereby shaping generalization. In tabular DL settings, hyperparameters govern both representational structure (e.g., embedding dimensionality and attention configuration) and training dynamics (e.g., step sizes, batch effects, and regularization strength), and they can interact in ways that produce non-intuitive performance landscapes. Classical baselines include grid search and random search. Importantly,, Bergstra and Bengio14 establishes that random search is often more efficient than grid search when only a subset of hyperparameters strongly influences performance—an insight that remains practically relevant for deep tabular models whose search spaces are high-dimensional and anisotropic. In such cases, exhaustive grids tend to waste evaluations on unimportant dimensions, whereas random sampling can allocate more trials to exploring influential factors. Bayesian optimization provides a more sample-efficient paradigm by fitting a surrogate model over the hyperparameter space and selecting new trials using an acquisition rule38. Conceptually, this substitutes direct exploration with probabilistic modelling of the objective surface, balancing exploitation of known good regions against exploration where uncertainty remains high. While powerful, Bayesian approaches can be less straightforward when the space is mixed (continuous, integer, categorical), the objective is noisy (e.g., stochastic training, resampling variance), and evaluation budgets are limited—conditions commonly encountered in deep tabular learning for environmental datasets. In these circumstances, surrogate modelling assumptions may be strained, and the overhead of maintaining and optimizing a surrogate can be less attractive relative to direct, population-based exploration.

Metaheuristic optimization has therefore been widely explored as a complementary HPO strategy. A review by Abd Elaziz et al.15 synthesizes how advanced metaheuristics are used in deep neural network contexts, emphasizing their derivative-free global search capability and their practical adaptability to rugged, multimodal landscapes. In HPO terms, the appeal is that metaheuristics can operate directly on black-box objectives, tolerate noisy evaluations, and flexibly accommodate constraints and mixed-variable encodings. Canonical population-based algorithms frequently serve as competitive baselines in HPO studies. Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) is widely used due to its simple velocity–position update rules and effective exploration–exploitation behavior in continuous domains16. Differential Evolution (DE) is valued for its mutation-and-recombination operators that maintain diversity while driving convergence17. More recent metaheuristics, such as the Multi-Verse Optimizer (MVO), propose novel operators motivated by cosmological analogies and are commonly adopted as comparative baselines in engineering optimization39. Stochastic Fractal Search (SFS) uses diffusion-inspired mechanisms and has also been applied broadly in global optimization settings40. In environmental modeling contexts, the appeal of metaheuristics is amplified by constraints typical of real water-quality problems: limited sample sizes, heterogeneous measurement regimes, and noisy objectives induced by resampling-based validation. In such settings, an HPO strategy must not only search effectively but must do so under strict evaluation budgets where each fitness evaluation corresponds to a full training-and-validation cycle. A concrete example is Kushwaha et al.41, which studies metaheuristic approaches in water-quality index prediction and reinforces the empirical importance of robust optimization and careful validation. The broader methodological message is that optimization and evaluation are inseparable: metaheuristics can provide practical search capability, but credible conclusions depend on harmonized budgets, consistent data splitting, and transparent reporting of configurations and randomness controls. Recent critiques further emphasize that, in small datasets, wrapper-based HPO must be paired with repeated runs and dispersion reporting to mitigate the risk of overfitting to a single validation subset.

Within this landscape, the present study adopts the Ninja Optimization Algorithm (NiOA) as the HPO engine for AutoInt. NiOA is treated as a previously published metaheuristic, and its use in this work is methodological: to guide systematic hyperparameter search under a fixed experimental protocol rather than to introduce a new optimizer25. This positioning responds to a recurring gap across environmental ML studies: performance gains are frequently reported without sufficiently transparent, reproducible, and fairly controlled tuning procedures. From a comparative standpoint, coupling a strongly interaction-oriented tabular DL model (AutoInt) with a structured metaheuristic HPO strategy (NiOA) is intended to address two linked sources of fragility—model sensitivity to configuration and the inefficiency of naive tuning in complex search spaces—while keeping the evaluation design consistent across alternatives. By combining an interaction-capable tabular DL model (AutoInt) with a structured metaheuristic HPO strategy (NiOA) and by comparing against established optimizers under harmonized budgets, the study aims to strengthen methodological rigor while retaining practical relevance for water-quality analytics. In addition, the study emphasizes full disclosure of the hyperparameter domain, optimization budget, selected best configuration, and computational cost metrics, thereby aligning with emerging expectations for reproducible environmental machine-learning research.

Dataset description

The dataset employed in this study is obtained from the publicly available Water Quality Testing dataset hosted on Kaggle. This dataset consists of 200 observations, each corresponding to a distinct water sample collected for routine water-quality assessment. The dataset is intentionally modest in size, reflecting realistic data-scarcity conditions frequently encountered in environmental monitoring studies, particularly in small-scale surveys or pilot monitoring campaigns. The limited sample size motivates careful validation design, strict leakage prevention, and explicit reporting of variability across repeated runs to reduce the risk of overfitting and split sensitivity. Because the adopted 15% held-out test subset contains approximately 30 samples, the reported test-set results are interpreted as fixed-split benchmark evidence rather than definitive evidence of broad environmental generalization.

Each observation comprises a set of numerical variables representing physicochemical properties of water. According to the dataset specification, the recorded parameters include pH, temperature (expressed in degrees Celsius), turbidity (measured in nephelometric turbidity units), dissolved oxygen concentration (expressed in milligrams per liter), and electrical conductivity (expressed in microsiemens per centimeter). In addition, an identifier field is provided to uniquely label individual samples; this identifier is not used as an explanatory variable in the modeling process. All measured variables are continuous-valued and directly derived from standard water-quality testing procedures, making the dataset suitable for regression-based predictive modeling.

The dataset is distributed under a CC0 public-domain license, ensuring unrestricted use for research and educational purposes. No temporal or spatial metadata are associated with the samples, and the dataset represents a cross-sectional snapshot rather than a time series. As a result, the modeling task is formulated purely in a tabular regression setting, without explicit consideration of temporal dependencies or spatial autocorrelation. Accordingly, any claims regarding temporal dynamics, seasonal variability, or spatial generalization are outside the empirical scope of the present dataset and are treated as contextual motivation rather than directly validated properties.

Within this study, the predictive task is defined as a supervised regression problem in which dissolved oxygen (mg/L) is selected as the fixed target variable to be predicted from the remaining measured parameters (pH, temperature, turbidity, and electrical conductivity). The input feature set therefore consists of these four physicochemical variables, while dissolved oxygen represents the quantity of interest. This formulation reflects a common use case in water-quality analysis, where certain parameters may be expensive, delayed, or difficult to measure directly, motivating their estimation from correlated and more readily available measurements. The specific choice of target variable is fixed consistently throughout all experiments to ensure comparability across models and optimization strategies. All reported performance metrics correspond exclusively to this dissolved-oxygen regression task.

Data preprocessing

Prior to model development, a systematic data preprocessing procedure is applied to ensure numerical consistency, methodological rigor, and protection against data leakage. Given the relatively small dataset size, careful preprocessing is essential to avoid introducing biases or artifacts that could disproportionately influence model behavior.

The preprocessing pipeline begins with data validation and integrity checks. All records are examined for missing entries, duplicated samples, and non-numeric values. As the dataset is provided in a cleaned tabular format, no missing values are present in the raw data. Consequently, no imputation is performed in this study; the preprocessing pipeline includes automated checks for completeness, but no median or alternative imputation strategy is applied because the dataset contains no missing entries. Automated checks are retained as a safeguard to ensure robustness and reproducibility should the dataset be extended or modified in future studies.

Subsequently, range and plausibility checks are conducted for each physicochemical variable. These checks verify that all recorded values fall within physically meaningful and scientifically plausible intervals, consistent with standard water-quality measurement practices. Observations exhibiting clearly anomalous or non-physical values are inspected to determine whether they reflect data-entry errors or extreme but valid conditions. In this dataset, no samples are excluded at this stage, as all values conform to reasonable environmental ranges.

Following validation, numerical scaling is applied to the feature set. Because the variables are expressed in heterogeneous units and exhibit differing numerical magnitudes, feature scaling is necessary to ensure stable optimization and balanced gradient updates during model training. A normalization strategy based on standardized scaling is adopted, transforming each feature to have zero mean and unit variance based exclusively on statistics computed from the training subset. This approach prevents any single variable from dominating the learning process due to scale effects while preserving relative variations within each feature. The target variable (dissolved oxygen) is retained on its original physical scale (mg/L) for evaluation, and all reported regression metrics (MSE, RMSE, MAE, NSE, and related statistics) are computed after inverse transformation where applicable, ensuring interpretability in physical units.

To prevent information leakage, all preprocessing transformations that depend on data statistics are fitted solely on the training portion of the dataset and subsequently applied unchanged to the validation and test subsets. This strict separation ensures that no information from held-out data influences the training process, thereby preserving the integrity of performance evaluation. The same preprocessing configuration is applied consistently across all models and optimization strategies considered in the study.

Finally, the processed dataset is partitioned into training, validation, and testing subsets according to the experimental protocol described later in this paper. A fixed split ratio of 70%/15%/15% (training/validation/test) is adopted, and repeated runs with different controlled initialization seeds are used to quantify variability while maintaining identical split indices for fair optimizer comparison. This partitioning strategy supports both hyperparameter optimization and unbiased performance assessment, forming a stable foundation for the subsequent modeling and optimization stages.

Feature handling and representation

The representation and handling of input features constitute a critical component of the modeling pipeline, particularly in tabular environmental datasets where variables are heterogeneous yet strongly interdependent. In this study, all physicochemical parameters provided in the dataset are treated as continuous numerical features and are directly supplied to the learning model following the preprocessing steps described previously. No categorical encoding is required, and no temporal or spatial indexing is introduced, as the dataset represents independent cross-sectional measurements.

A key design decision in this work is the deliberate avoidance of extensive manual feature engineering. Traditional water-quality modeling studies often construct derived variables, ratios, or interaction terms based on domain knowledge, such as combinations of temperature and dissolved oxygen or conductivity-normalized measures. While such handcrafted features can be informative, they also introduce subjectivity, increase dimensionality, and may inadvertently bias the learning process toward preconceived relationships.

Instead, this study relies on AutoInt’s internal mechanism for learning feature interactions. AutoInt is specifically designed to model high-order interactions among tabular features through self-attention layers, enabling the network to automatically identify and weight relevant cross-feature dependencies during training. By embedding each feature into a shared latent space and applying attention-based interaction layers, AutoInt can capture both low-order and complex nonlinear relationships without requiring explicit specification of interaction terms.

This design choice offers several advantages in the context of water-quality data. First, it reduces the risk of omitting important but non-obvious interactions that may not be captured by manual feature construction. Second, it limits unnecessary expansion of the feature space, which is particularly important given the modest dataset size. Third, it promotes methodological generality, allowing the same framework to be applied to other water-quality datasets without extensive redesign of feature-engineering rules. At the same time, the study acknowledges that interaction-aware models can be sensitive to hyperparameter configuration in small datasets, which motivates the structured and fully disclosed hyperparameter optimization procedure described in subsequent sections. Because the dissolved-oxygen regression task uses only four input predictors, the interaction-aware architecture is evaluated as a methodological benchmark rather than as evidence that deep learning is universally necessary for all water-quality datasets.

All features are therefore treated uniformly within the AutoInt architecture, with their interactions learned adaptively during training. Hyperparameters governing the embedding dimension, number of interaction layers, attention heads, learning rate, regularization, and batch size are optimized through the metaheuristic framework described later, ensuring that the model capacity is aligned with the complexity of the data under a controlled and explicitly reported optimization budget.

Exploratory data analysis

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) is a foundational step for characterizing the statistical structure, dependency patterns, and latent organization of water-quality variables prior to predictive modeling. In this study, EDA is conducted within a controlled, human-in-the-loop framework that leverages two independent open-weight large language models for high-level methodological support. The role of these models is strictly limited to descriptive reasoning about already-generated visualizations and statistical summaries; they do not access raw data directly, do not execute code, do not modify preprocessing steps, and do not influence model architecture, hyperparameter selection, or evaluation design. These models are used exclusively for diagnostic reasoning, visualization interpretation, and consistency checking, without performing automated inference, modifying data, or influencing downstream modeling decisions. This design ensures transparency while enabling structured and systematic exploration of the dataset. All quantitative modeling decisions reported in subsequent sections are fully deterministic and independent of LLM-generated suggestions.

The EDA objectives are threefold: first, to validate data consistency and physical plausibility; second, to identify linear and nonlinear relationships among physicochemical parameters; and third, to motivate the selection of interaction-aware tabular learning architectures capable of capturing the observed dependencies. Because the dataset is cross-sectional and limited to 200 samples, EDA is interpreted as descriptive characterization of the observed sample distribution rather than as evidence of broader spatiotemporal regimes.

Learning models

The predictive modelling component of this study is centered on modern deep-learning architectures developed explicitly for tabular data. In contrast to conventional multilayer perceptrons, contemporary tabular deep models incorporate architectural inductive biases (e.g., attention, feature selection, structured interaction operators, or tree-inspired modules) that are designed to address the heterogeneity and interaction-dominant structure typical of real-world tabular datasets. This modelling choice is particularly relevant for environmental datasets (including routine water-quality measurements), where multiple physicochemical variables may jointly influence the target through nonlinear couplings and high-order dependencies rather than through additive effects alone. Consequently, the study adopts a model set that spans interaction-centric attention networks, attention-driven feature selection mechanisms, transformer-style tabular representations, and structured interaction operators, thereby enabling a principled and informative benchmarking under a unified experimental protocol. All models are evaluated on the same dissolved-oxygen regression task under identical preprocessing, data partitioning, and evaluation settings to ensure fairness and reproducibility.

The primary predictive model employed in this work is the Automatic Feature Interaction Network (AutoInt). AutoInt was originally proposed to learn high-order feature interactions in a parameter-efficient manner by mapping heterogeneous input fields into a shared embedding space and then applying stacked multi-head self-attention interaction layers with residual connections13. Conceptually, AutoInt transforms each input feature (or field) into a latent vector representation and uses attention operators to compute interaction-aware representations, where the attention weights quantify the contextual relevance of one feature to another in the current predictive setting. By stacking interaction layers, AutoInt can represent increasingly complex (higher-order) interaction patterns without requiring explicit manual construction of feature crosses. This design is well aligned with water-quality modelling, because physicochemical variables such as pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen, turbidity, and conductivity frequently exhibit coupled behavior governed by chemical equilibria, transport dynamics, and biological processes, and these couplings can be difficult to capture through handcrafted interaction terms alone. Moreover, AutoInt’s interaction learning is achieved within a comparatively compact architecture (relative to very deep generic networks), which is advantageous when the available sample size is limited and when controlling overfitting risk is essential for credible environmental inference13. Given the modest dataset size (200 samples), careful regularization and hyperparameter control are necessary to balance representational capacity and generalization stability.

To contextualize AutoInt’s behaviour and to ensure that observed gains are not artefacts of a single modelling choice, several established tabular deep-learning models are included as baseline and comparator architectures. First, TabNet is incorporated as a widely used attention-based model that performs sequential attentive feature selection. TabNet iteratively selects a sparse subset of features at each decision step, combining representation learning with an interpretable, instance-dependent feature-selection mechanism that can improve efficiency and provide diagnostic insights into feature usage patterns35. Second, SAINT is adopted as a transformer-style approach for tabular learning that performs attention across both columns (features) and rows (samples) and investigates contrastive/self-supervised pretraining to improve performance when labelled data are limited34. The inclusion of SAINT provides a strong reference point for attention-driven representation learning that differs from AutoInt’s explicit feature-interaction layers. Third, the comparator set includes DANets (reported in this work under the label DANet), which introduce an Abstract Layer mechanism to explicitly group correlated raw features and form higher-level abstractions, combined with architectural components intended to preserve access to raw-feature information at deeper levels42. This family is relevant as it represents a distinct philosophy: rather than only learning interactions through attention across embedded features, DANets emphasize semantic abstraction and feature grouping as a route to robust tabular modelling.

In addition, the study benchmarks against the Deep Learning Recommendation Model (DLRM), which is a structured neural architecture designed to learn from a mixture of dense and sparse inputs by combining embedding tables, multilayer perceptrons, and an explicit feature interaction operator43. Although introduced as a state-of-the-art recommender architecture, the methodological principle—explicit interaction modelling coupled with deep nonlinear transformations—makes DLRM a relevant comparator for structured regression tasks that benefit from interaction operators. Finally, the baseline set includes a Transformer-style tabular baseline (TST) implemented as a simplified feature-wise Transformer encoder with multi-head self-attention layers, feed-forward sublayers, residual connections, and layer normalization, adapted for regression over standardized continuous inputs. This model serves as a generic transformer baseline distinct from AutoInt, without specialized feature-cross operators.

To further strengthen benchmarking rigor, classical non-deep-learning baselines are also included, namely Random Forest (RF) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), which are widely recognized as strong performers on tabular datasets. Their inclusion ensures that any observed gains from interaction-aware deep models and metaheuristic optimization are evaluated relative to competitive tree-based approaches commonly used in environmental modeling.

Across all architectures, learning is formulated as supervised regression with consistent preprocessing, data splitting, and evaluation procedures to ensure that performance differences can be attributed to modelling and optimization choices rather than experimental artefacts. Training and evaluation protocols (including stopping criteria, optimization settings, and computational budgets) are harmonized across models, which is particularly important in small-sample environmental settings where variance due to resampling and stochastic optimization can be substantial. This design also provides a clean foundation for the subsequent hyperparameter-optimization analysis: by first benchmarking a diverse set of tabular architectures under consistent conditions, the study isolates the incremental value of interaction modelling and metaheuristic hyperparameter optimization relative to baseline training.

No raw prompt transcript is used as a computational artifact in this study. The reproducibility record therefore reports the governing rules that constrained LLM use rather than treating LLM prompts as part of the executable scientific pipeline.

Reproducibility and validation protocol

Reproducibility is ensured through a fully specified data-partitioning strategy, consistent evaluation metrics, fixed random seeds, and complete logging of configurations. Dataset splitting follows a three-way partition into training/validation/test subsets, implemented with a fixed random_state for repeatability57. The adopted split ratio is 70%/15%/15% (training/validation/test). In addition, robustness analysis is performed using 5-fold cross-validation on the training portion to assess split sensitivity, with preprocessing fitted independently within each fold to prevent leakage58. Framework-level determinism is enabled following PyTorch guidance on controlling randomness and deterministic execution48.

The 5-fold analysis is used as an internal sensitivity check on the training portion and is not used as the outer-loop generalization estimate. The fixed held-out test set remains the primary reported evaluation protocol, and the repeated-run results are interpreted within this fixed-split benchmark setting. Because full fold-level numerical results are not reported in the present manuscript, the empirical conclusions are limited to the fixed held-out test results and the repeated-run evidence reported below. A fully nested cross-validation design would provide a less biased estimate under wrapper-based hyperparameter optimization; therefore, the absence of nested cross-validation is acknowledged as a methodological limitation rather than claimed as completed validation.

Performance is reported using both error-based metrics (e.g., MSE, RMSE, MAE, bias/error measures) and goodness-of-fit indices (e.g., r, , NSE, WI). NSE is reported following its standard hydrologic definition59. WI is included as a complementary agreement index used in environmental-model evaluation60. All metrics are computed on predictions expressed in the original physical units of dissolved oxygen (mg/L), and final results are reported as mean ± standard deviation across repeated runs with controlled initialization seeds to quantify stochastic variability (Table 13).

Comparative performance analysis

This subsection provides a systematic comparative analysis of the NiOA-assisted AutoInt model relative to alternative optimizer-assisted AutoInt configurations, using the complete set of evaluation metrics introduced earlier. The objective of this analysis is not merely to rank optimizers, but to examine their behavior across complementary performance dimensions, including prediction accuracy, bias control, correlation structure, and efficiency-based agreement with observed values.

From an error-magnitude perspective, the NiOA-assisted AutoInt configuration demonstrates a clear and consistent advantage. Across all reported error-based metrics, including MSE, RMSE, MAE, and RRMSE, the NiOA-driven solution attains the lowest values among all evaluated optimizers. This indicates that NiOA is particularly effective at identifying hyperparameter configurations that minimize both squared and absolute deviations, as well as relative error normalized to the scale of the target variable. In contrast, while other optimizers such as PSO, BA, and WOA achieve substantial error reductions compared to baseline training, their residual errors remain notably higher than those obtained with NiOA. This advantage is interpreted within the fixed-split, small-sample benchmark and should not be generalized beyond the present dataset without external validation.

Bias-related behavior is assessed through the Mean Bias Error (MBE), which captures systematic overestimation or underestimation tendencies. The NiOA-assisted configuration exhibits the smallest absolute MBE, indicating near-unbiased predictions. Although alternative optimizers also reduce bias relative to non-optimized baselines, their bias magnitudes remain consistently larger. This suggests that NiOA’s exploration–exploitation balance is particularly effective at locating hyperparameter regions that simultaneously minimize error variance and systematic bias.

Correlation and goodness-of-fit metrics further reinforce the comparative advantage of NiOA. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) and coefficient of determination () achieved by NiOA-assisted AutoInt are the highest among all tested configurations, indicating stronger linear association and higher explained variance between observed and predicted values. Competing optimizers yield progressively lower correlation and determination coefficients, reflecting reduced alignment with the underlying data structure despite operating on the same learning architecture.

Efficiency-oriented metrics, including the Nash–Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) and the Willmott Index (WI), provide additional insight into predictive reliability from an environmental modeling standpoint. The NiOA-assisted model attains the highest values for both metrics, indicating superior agreement between predictions and observations relative to the variability inherent in the dataset. While several alternative optimizers also achieve high efficiency scores, their values remain consistently below those obtained with NiOA, highlighting differences in how effectively each optimizer guides the model toward stable and generalizable solutions.

The earlier pairwise significance statement based on a paired t-test is not used as the primary comparative evidence because optimizer comparisons over repeated runs are better handled using matched non-parametric procedures. The run-level MSE and values in Table 20 provide the paired observations used for the Friedman and post-hoc statistical analyses reported in the following subsection.

An important observation emerging from this comparative analysis is the consistency of the performance hierarchy across metrics. NiOA-assisted AutoInt ranks first across all evaluation criteria, whereas the remaining optimizers exhibit a gradual and coherent decline in performance rather than erratic metric-specific behavior. This pattern is reported as an empirical observation under the matched experimental protocol, not as a mathematical guarantee or universal property of the optimizers.

Taken together, the comparative results demonstrate that, under identical computational budgets and fairness constraints, NiOA provides a more effective search strategy for AutoInt hyperparameter tuning than the alternative metaheuristic optimizers considered in this study. These findings support the suitability of NiOA as a candidate optimization mechanism for this interaction-aware tabular learning benchmark, while remaining bounded by the small dataset size, fixed validation split, and need for external validation on larger and more diverse water-quality datasets.

Statistical analysis

Formal statistical analysis is performed using the run-level test-set MSE values reported in Table 20. The previous test against a theoretical zero-error median is not used because testing whether prediction error differs from zero does not provide a meaningful comparison among optimizers. Instead, the analysis treats optimizer comparison as a paired repeated-run problem in which each run index provides matched observations across all optimizer-assisted AutoInt configurations.

Paired repeated-run comparison design

Each run index R1–R10 constitutes a paired observation across optimizers because all optimizer-assisted configurations are evaluated under the same data split, matched computational budget, and controlled repeated-run protocol. The primary response variable for statistical comparison is test-set MSE, where lower values indicate better predictive performance. This paired design is appropriate because it compares optimizers under matched experimental conditions rather than treating runs from different optimizers as unrelated samples.

Friedman test

A Friedman test is first applied to assess whether the optimizer-assisted AutoInt configurations differ significantly in their repeated-run MSE ranks. This non-parametric omnibus test is suitable for comparing multiple methods across matched repeated observations without assuming normally distributed errors. The null hypothesis is that all optimizers have equivalent rank distributions across the repeated runs(Table 21, 22, 23, 24).

Nemenyi post-hoc analysis

Following the significant Friedman test, a Nemenyi post-hoc comparison is used to evaluate whether the observed rank differences are large enough to be considered statistically meaningful under multiple-method comparison. For optimizers and paired runs, the critical difference at is . A method is considered significantly different from Ninja + AutoInt when the absolute difference between its average rank and the Ninja + AutoInt average rank exceeds this critical difference.

The Nemenyi analysis indicates that Ninja + AutoInt differs significantly from BBO + AutoInt, MVO + AutoInt, GA + AutoInt, SFS + AutoInt, DE + AutoInt, and APO + AutoInt. The rank differences between Ninja + AutoInt and the closest competitors, namely PSO + AutoInt, BA + AutoInt, and WOA + AutoInt, do not exceed the Nemenyi critical difference. This result supports a cautious interpretation: Ninja + AutoInt achieves the best observed average rank, but the strongest rank-based evidence of separation is obtained against the lower-ranked optimizer group rather than against every competitor under the conservative Nemenyi criterion.

Paired Wilcoxon sensitivity analysis

As a complementary sensitivity analysis, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests are performed between Ninja + AutoInt and each competing optimizer using matched run-level MSE values. These pairwise tests evaluate whether the distribution of paired MSE differences favors Ninja + AutoInt. Holm correction is applied to control the family-wise error rate across the nine pairwise comparisons.

The paired Wilcoxon sensitivity analysis indicates that Ninja + AutoInt has lower run-level MSE than each comparator across the matched runs, and all Holm-adjusted pairwise comparisons remain significant at . Because this pairwise analysis directly uses matched run-level differences, it complements the more conservative Nemenyi rank-based procedure. The combined statistical evidence supports the conclusion that Ninja + AutoInt shows the strongest observed performance under the fixed split, matched optimization budget, and repeated-run protocol, while the interpretation remains bounded by the small dataset size and the absence of external validation.

Discussion

Analysis of optimization effects

The results presented in Section “Results” provide clear evidence that metaheuristic-driven hyperparameter optimization plays a decisive role in enhancing the predictive performance of interaction-aware tabular learning models. In particular, the substantial performance gap observed between the baseline AutoInt configuration and its NiOA-optimized counterpart highlights the sensitivity of deep tabular architectures to hyperparameter selection and underscores the limitations of relying solely on default or manually tuned settings. Given the modest sample size (), this sensitivity is expected to be amplified, because small validation subsets can yield high-variance fitness signals and increase the risk of selecting configurations that exploit split-specific idiosyncrasies. Accordingly, the interpretation of optimization gains must be grounded in stability evidence (mean ± std across repeated runs) and robustness checks (fold-based resampling on the training portion), rather than point estimates alone.

The large reduction from baseline AutoInt to NiOA-optimized AutoInt is substantial and should be interpreted in light of three methodological constraints: the held-out test subset contains approximately 30 samples, the wrapper-based optimization process relies on a fixed validation fold, and the same dataset supplies both model-selection and final evaluation partitions. The added convergence plot, run-level MSE and table, and non-parametric statistical analyses improve transparency by documenting optimization behavior and paired repeated-run outcomes. However, these additions do not replace external validation or a fully nested cross-validation design, both of which would provide stronger evidence for generalization under wrapper-based hyperparameter optimization.

From an accuracy standpoint, the improvements achieved through NiOA-assisted optimization are consistent across all error-based metrics. The marked reduction in MSE, RMSE, MAE, and RRMSE indicates that the optimized configuration is able to better align model capacity, learning dynamics, and regularization strength with the intrinsic structure of the water-quality dataset. Because evaluation is performed on the original physical scale of the prediction target (dissolved oxygen in mg/L), the reported reductions reflect meaningful improvements in unit-interpretable error magnitudes rather than artifacts of standardized-scale scoring. This suggests that NiOA is effective at navigating the highly nonlinear hyperparameter landscape of AutoInt, identifying configurations that balance model expressiveness against overfitting risk in a small-sample regime. At the same time, the magnitude of improvement should not be interpreted as evidence that the optimized model has learned a universally transferable dissolved-oxygen relationship. It is best understood as strong fixed-benchmark performance under the supplied split, preprocessing stream, optimizer budget, and repeated-run protocol.

Beyond accuracy, optimization effects are also evident in terms of predictive stability and reliability. The near-zero Mean Bias Error achieved by the NiOA-assisted model indicates that hyperparameter tuning not only reduces random error but also mitigates systematic prediction bias. This is particularly important in environmental modeling contexts, where biased predictions can lead to persistent misinterpretation of water-quality conditions. Similarly, the consistently higher values of correlation- and efficiency-based metrics, such as r, , NSE, and WI, suggest that NiOA-guided tuning improves the overall agreement between observed and predicted values rather than optimizing a single loss component in isolation. In addition, the dispersion patterns observed in the optimization-stage diagnostic plots indicate that NiOA yields not only the best mean performance but also comparatively tight metric distributions across repeated runs, supporting the claim that the improvements are stable under controlled stochasticity. Because several metrics are mathematically related, especially MSE, RMSE, RRMSE, , and NSE, consistent metric rankings are interpreted cautiously and are not treated as independent confirmation from unrelated evidence streams.

In contrast, alternative optimizer-assisted configurations, while still outperforming baseline training, exhibit a gradual degradation in performance across multiple metrics. This pattern indicates that different metaheuristic strategies vary in their ability to balance exploration and exploitation within the AutoInt hyperparameter space. The results imply that NiOA’s search dynamics are particularly well suited to this optimization problem, yielding configurations that are both accurate and stable under the defined experimental protocol. The Friedman test, Nemenyi post-hoc analysis, and Holm-corrected paired Wilcoxon sensitivity analysis provide a more appropriate basis for optimizer comparison than tests against a zero-error null. These statistical results support the conclusion that NiOA + AutoInt shows the best observed repeated-run performance under the matched-budget protocol, while the conservative Nemenyi comparison indicates that the strongest rank-based separation is obtained against the lower-ranked optimizer group rather than against every competing optimizer.

Role of deep tabular learning and metaheuristic HPO

The use of AutoInt should be understood as a methodological benchmark for interaction-aware tabular learning rather than as proof that deep learning is universally necessary for all water-quality prediction problems. The dataset contains only 200 observations and four input predictors, and such a setting may be partially explainable by simpler classical learners when predictor–target relationships are strong. Accordingly, Random Forest and XGBoost are retained as non-deep tabular baselines to contextualize the value of AutoInt. The results show that the optimized AutoInt configuration performs strongly in this benchmark, but they do not eliminate the need to compare against simpler models on larger, independent, and more heterogeneous datasets.

Metaheuristic HPO remains useful in this setting because AutoInt performance depends on interacting architectural and training hyperparameters, including learning rate, weight decay, dropout, attention heads, attention layers, embedding dimension, multilayer perceptron width, and batch size. A fixed-budget metaheuristic search provides a structured way to explore this mixed hyperparameter space. Nevertheless, wrapper-based HPO can over-specialize to the validation subset, especially when the validation split is small. Therefore, the optimized results are interpreted as evidence that NiOA is effective for this benchmarked HPO problem, not as a general claim that metaheuristic tuning will always improve deep tabular models in environmental datasets.

Implications for water-quality modeling

The findings of this study have several practical implications for water-quality modeling and environmental data analysis. First, they demonstrate that modern tabular deep-learning models, when properly optimized, can serve as effective surrogate predictors for water-quality variables derived from routine physicochemical measurements. This capability is especially relevant in monitoring scenarios where certain parameters may be costly, time-consuming, or logistically difficult to measure directly. However, because the present dataset is cross-sectional and does not include spatial or temporal identifiers, the demonstrated surrogate prediction capability should be interpreted as applicable to similarly sampled cross-sectional settings rather than to dynamic forecasting or site-transfer problems.

Second, the proposed NiOA-assisted AutoInt framework offers a systematic approach for extracting maximum predictive value from relatively small datasets. Many real-world water-quality studies are constrained by limited sample availability, fragmented monitoring campaigns, or resource limitations. The demonstrated effectiveness of metaheuristic hyperparameter optimization in this setting suggests that careful model tuning can partially compensate for data scarcity, improving predictive reliability without requiring additional data collection. At the same time, the results also emphasize the importance of rigorous resampling-based validation when hyperparameter optimization is employed, because wrapper-based tuning can otherwise overfit to a particular validation split. The present evidence should therefore be treated as benchmark-specific rather than as operational deployment evidence.

Third, the emphasis on interaction-aware modeling aligns well with the underlying nature of aquatic systems, where water-quality parameters are governed by interconnected physical, chemical, and biological processes. By combining an interaction-focused learning architecture with a robust optimization strategy, the proposed framework provides a flexible tool that can be adapted to a wide range of environmental datasets and prediction tasks. Such adaptability is valuable for exploratory water-quality assessment, scenario analysis, and methodological evaluation of monitoring strategies. Nevertheless, broad decision-support claims should be moderated unless additional evidence is provided across multiple independent monitoring datasets, because strong within-dataset predictive accuracy does not automatically imply transferability to different hydrochemical regimes or sampling designs.

Computational behavior

The computational resource analysis suggests favorable measured runtime for NiOA + AutoInt in the present implementation. However, because all optimizer-assisted configurations call the same AutoInt training procedure and use the same population size () and iteration budget (), the per-evaluation computational cost should be broadly comparable across optimizers. The reported differences in CPU usage, GPU usage, memory consumption, time per epoch, and total training time are therefore interpreted as implementation-specific measurements rather than intrinsic computational-complexity rankings. These values may reflect optimizer-specific update overhead, early-stopping behavior, hardware scheduling, logging overhead, memory allocation, software implementation details, or runtime measurement conditions. Consequently, the resource results support transparency of the experimental environment and suggest favorable measured runtime in this implementation, but they do not prove universal resource superiority or establish deployment readiness for resource-constrained monitoring systems.

Role of LLM assistance

LLMs are not part of the predictive model, optimizer, preprocessing executor, statistical evaluator, or performance-measurement pipeline. Their role is restricted to non-decisional methodological checklist support, wording refinement, and consistency checking. No raw data, intermediate results, model outputs, hyperparameter candidates, predictions, or statistical-test inputs are supplied to the LLMs for computation. Therefore, the reported numerical results do not depend on LLM-generated computation. A conventional predictive ablation of “with LLM” versus “without LLM” is not applicable because the LLM component is not an executable modelling component. Instead, the reproducibility logic is methodological: the entire preprocessing, training, tuning, evaluation, and statistical-testing workflow is specified as deterministic and executable without LLM involvement.

Limitations

Several limitations define the scope within which the results should be interpreted. First, the dataset is small () and the held-out test subset contains approximately 30 samples. This limited test size makes the reported performance estimates sensitive to the particular partition of the data. Although repeated runs quantify stochastic initialization effects under the same split, they do not fully characterize uncertainty due to alternative train–validation–test partitions or external sampling variability.

Second, the study uses a fixed train/validation/test split rather than a fully nested cross-validation design. The fixed split supports optimizer parity because all methods are evaluated under identical partitioning and computational budgets. However, nested cross-validation would provide a less biased estimate of generalization when wrapper-based HPO is used, because model selection and performance estimation would be separated across inner and outer loops. The absence of a fully nested design is therefore an important methodological limitation.

Third, wrapper-based HPO may over-specialize to the validation subset. In the present workflow, each optimizer repeatedly evaluates candidate AutoInt configurations using validation MSE as the fitness function. This design is appropriate for controlled HPO benchmarking, but it can select configurations that exploit idiosyncrasies of the validation fold, particularly when the validation subset is small. The held-out test set and repeated-run reporting mitigate this risk but do not eliminate it.

Fourth, preprocessing-selection bias may be present. Stream A is selected for subsequent optimization because it shows stronger baseline performance than Stream B. This selection is transparently reported and empirically motivated, but it means that the optimized experiments proceed from the stronger preprocessing stream after baseline inspection. Consequently, the final optimized results may reflect both optimizer effectiveness and preprocessing-stream selection.

Fifth, no independent external validation dataset is used. All model development, optimizer comparison, and final evaluation are performed using partitions of the same public dataset. Therefore, the findings cannot be assumed to transfer to other watersheds, seasons, monitoring programs, sensor protocols, or hydrochemical regimes without additional validation.

Sixth, computational resource rankings are implementation- and hardware-dependent. Although the study reports CPU usage, GPU usage, memory, time per epoch, and total training time, these values may depend on software implementation details, logging, memory allocation, runtime scheduling, and hardware conditions. They should be interpreted as measured characteristics of the present implementation rather than as universal computational complexity rankings among optimizers.

Seventh, LLM assistance is non-computational and does not demonstrate predictive benefit. The study does not show that LLMs improve accuracy, reduce error, or enhance optimizer performance. LLMs are restricted to non-decisional documentation and methodological consistency support, while all numerical results are produced by deterministic preprocessing, model training, optimization, and statistical-testing procedures.

Eighth, the contribution scope is limited. The work applies an existing optimizer, NiOA, to tune an existing interaction-aware tabular model, AutoInt, on a small public dataset. The contribution lies in the transparent fixed-budget benchmarking workflow, explicit HPO reporting, paired repeated-run analysis, and careful documentation of the modelling protocol. It does not introduce a new optimizer, a new neural architecture, or a new theoretical optimization framework.

Ninth, residual-level diagnostics are not fully reported. The manuscript reports aggregate error, bias, correlation, efficiency metrics, repeated-run uncertainty, convergence traces, and run-level MSE/ values, but it does not provide residual histograms, prediction-interval estimates, failure-case analysis, or absolute-error quantiles such as median and 95th-percentile absolute error. Such diagnostics would provide additional evidence for assessing whether unusually low errors arise from uniformly small residuals or from favorable behavior on a small test subset.

Finally, the results should not be interpreted as operational environmental decision-support validation. The dataset is cross-sectional, lacks explicit spatial and temporal metadata, and contains a limited number of samples and predictors. Therefore, the findings provide controlled benchmark evidence for a NiOA-assisted AutoInt workflow, but they do not establish readiness for real-time monitoring, regulatory decision-making, site-transfer prediction, seasonal forecasting, or deployment across heterogeneous water-quality regimes. Future work should validate the framework using larger independent datasets, explicit spatiotemporal partitions, nested cross-validation, residual-level uncertainty diagnostics, and comparisons against additional simple and probabilistic baselines.

Conclusion

This study evaluated dissolved oxygen prediction using the interaction-aware AutoInt architecture combined with NiOA-based hyperparameter optimization. The prediction task was consistently defined as single-target supervised regression of dissolved oxygen (mg/L) from the remaining physicochemical variables in the dataset.

Under the fixed benchmark protocol, NiOA + AutoInt achieved the best observed performance among the evaluated optimizer-assisted AutoInt configurations. This result indicates that NiOA was effective for navigating the specified AutoInt hyperparameter space under the matched search bounds, population size, iteration budget, and validation-MSE objective used in this study.

The revised experimental reporting strengthens reproducibility by providing the full hyperparameter search space, the selected NiOA-tuned configuration, the matched optimization budget, run-level MSE and values, the convergence trace, and corrected non-parametric statistical comparisons based on paired repeated-run observations. These additions support a more transparent interpretation of optimizer performance than point estimates or rank consistency alone.

The conclusions remain bounded by the small cross-sectional dataset, the fixed train/validation/test split, and the absence of external validation. Larger independent datasets with explicit spatial and temporal structure, fully nested cross-validation, residual-level diagnostics, and uncertainty-aware evaluation are still necessary before the fitted model can be generalized to broader environmental monitoring or operational decision-support settings.
