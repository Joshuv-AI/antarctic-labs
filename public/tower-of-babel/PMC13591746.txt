Anterior auxiliary limited open incision for Schatzker IV total medial plateau fractures with posterolateral collapse: a retrospective study
Authors: Xu-sheng Qiu, Zhi-jie Lei, Jiang-lin Yu, Xiao-yang Qi, Yi-xin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchatzker type IV total medial plateau fractures with posterolateral articular surface collapse, abbreviated as Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC, present a complex surgical problem. The intact anterolateral rim obstructs conventional access to the posterolateral depression, while the frequent entrapment of the lateral meniscus adds a critical soft-tissue dimension to the challenge. This study describes and evaluates a anterior limited open incision that is designed to utilize the main fracture line as a direct corridor for addressing both the osseous and soft-tissue components of this injury.ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy and outcomes of the anterior auxiliary limited open incision in the treatment of Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC fractures.MethodsIn this retrospective study, fifteen patients with Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC fractures were treated with the anterior auxiliary limited open incision, which facilitated direct reduction of the collapsed posterolateral articular surface and any trapped lateral meniscus. Perioperative parameters including waiting time, blood loss, and operation time were recorded. Postoperative follow-up was performed to assess complications, fracture healing time, muscle strength, sensation, limb length, lower limb alignment, knee range of motion, and KSS scores.ResultsKnee subluxation was present in 53.3% (8/15) of patients, lateral meniscus injury in 60% (9/15), and meniscal entrapment in 40% (6/15). The mean preoperative waiting time was 5.3 days, operation time was 118 min, and intraoperative blood loss was 300 ml. No neurovascular injuries, skin necrosis, or deep infections occurred. One case of superficial infection resolved with wound care and antibiotics. All fractures united with a mean healing time of 13 weeks. At a mean follow-up of 18 months, the average knee extension was 3°, flexion was 122°, and the mean KSS score was 85 points (12 excellent, 2 good, and 1 fair).ConclusionThe anterior auxiliary limited open incision is an effective treatment for Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC fractures, facilitating direct management of the articular depression and meniscal pathology, and yielding satisfactory functional outcomes.

Tibial plateau fractures are common injuries, accounting for approximately 1.2% to 1.5% of all fractures in the body [1–3]. The Schatzker classification system is one of the most common classification systems for tibial plateau fractures in clinical practice [4, 5]. It classifies tibial plateau fractures into six types, among which Schatzker type IV fractures are historically regarded as simple medial plateau fractures [6]. However, with the advancement of imaging, especially the popularization of CT three-dimensional reconstruction, it has become evident that Schatzker type IV fractures represent a morphologically heterogeneous group. Simple medial plateau fractures are rare, accounting for only 13–18% of type IV fractures [7, 8]. The majority (82–87%) are complex injuries characterized by the collapse of the posterolateral articular surface while maintaining the integrity of the anterolateral cortical rim [7–11]. This particular fracture pattern is frequently combined with knee subluxation and soft tissue injuries [7, 12]. Notably, lateral meniscus entrapment is a critical concern, with one study reporting a prevalence of 35.2% (19/54) in Schatzker type IV fractures with posterolateral collapse, and this incidence rises dramatically to 76% in the subgroup of patients who also present with knee subluxation [7].

The understanding of these complex Schatzker IV fractures has been further refined by morphological CT studies. Yang et al. identified three distinct subtypes: anteromedial (9.3%), posteromedial (42.7%), and the total medial plateau (TMP) fracture (48%) [13]. This subtyping is surgically critical, as the spatial relationship between the fracture line and the posterolateral depression dictates the optimal surgical approach. For the posteromedial subtype with posterolateral collapse, the foundational work by Potocnik et al. [9] demonstrated that the posteromedial fracture line itself could be utilized for reduction. However, a key anatomical limitation exists: due to the greater mediolateral width of the tibial plateau compared to its anteroposterior depth, accessing the far lateral depression from a medial fracture line results in a long and deep surgical corridor, making exposure difficult and visualization suboptimal. This challenge is reflected in the reports of Sciadini et al. [10] and Crabtree et al. [11], who, when managing these posteromedial-type fractures, found reduction through the fracture line to be difficult and thus proposed alternative lateral osteotomies or corticotomies to create a more direct but iatrogenic pathway.

Conversely, the arthroscopic technique described by Capella et al. [14] is minimally invasive and effective for visualizing and reducing both the articular surface and the trapped meniscus. Its primary limitation, however, is the requirement for sophisticated arthroscopic expertise, which may not be routinely available to all orthopedic trauma surgeons.

This article focuses specifically on the “total medial plateau” (TMP) subtype with posterolateral collapse (PLC), an entity we term Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC. The TMP morphology possesses a unique anatomical advantage: its characteristic primary fracture line is located anteriorly and just medial to the midline. This configuration creates a short, direct anteroposterior surgical corridor to the posterolateral depression, thereby overcoming the visualization and access challenges inherent to the posteromedial approach.

To date, no previous study has specifically addressed the surgical treatment of the Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC fracture subtype. Conventional medial and lateral dual incisions have critical limitations for this fracture pattern: the intact anterolateral rim blocks access to the posterolateral depression, and the entrapped lateral meniscus is often overlooked or difficult to reduce [15]. These limitations highlight the need for a dedicated surgical approach for the Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC subtype.

Building on this anatomical insight, we propose a targeted anterior auxiliary limited open incision. This approach leverages the favorable anatomy of the Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC fracture to achieve direct visualization and management of both the collapsed articular surface and the trapped lateral meniscus under an open view, without the need for specialized arthroscopic equipment or advanced skills. This study will introduce this surgical method and retrospectively analyze the therapeutic effect of patients with Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC fractures treated by this approach.

Patients and methods

Patients

A total of 58 cases of Schatzker type IV tibial plateau fractures were treated in our hospital from June 2022 to June 2024. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age > 18 years old; (2) Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC fractures, defined as total medial plateau fractures with posterolateral articular surface collapse according to the morphological criteria of Yang et al. [13]. Exclusion criteria: (1) Lack of complete X-ray and CT data; (2) Has a history of knee joint surgery in the past; (3) Pathological fractures; (4) Severe neurovascular complications; (5) Follow-up < 12 months. Among the 58 Schatzker IV fractures, the Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC subtype accounted for 43.1% (25/58), consistent with the 48% reported by Yang et al. [13]. Of these 25 TMP/PLC fractures, 10 were excluded: 2 with incomplete X-ray/CT data, 1 with prior knee surgery, 1 pathological fracture, 1 with severe neurovascular complications, and 5 with incomplete follow-up (< 12 months). Ultimately, 15 patients were enrolled in this study (Fig. 1). This sample size is comparable to or larger than previous studies addressing similar complex medial plateau fractures with posterolateral impaction (Sciadini and Sims [10], 9 fractures; Crabtree et al. [11], 8 patients). Of these, 9 were male and 6 were female, with an average of 42.1 ± 5.3 years (range, 18–68 years). The injury mechanisms were traffic accident in 7 cases, fall in 6 cases, and sports injuries in 2 cases.

Surgical technique

After general anesthesia, the patient was placed in a supine position with a tourniquet applied on the proximal thigh. The lower extremity was prepped and draped in standard fashion. Surgical planning included marking the standard medial and lateral approaches, along with the auxiliary anterior limited open incision (Fig. 4a). First, a ~ 5-cm incision was made anterior to the main fracture line (often located just medial to the midline). The peripatellar retinaculum was incised, and the patella and patellar ligament were retracted laterally to expose the primary fracture line (which runs from anterior to posterior, separating the medial and lateral condyles). The fracture hematoma was evacuated. If the meniscus was found to be trapped within the fracture site, it was clearly visualized through this incision, released, and subsequently repaired. To repair the lateral meniscus, a standard anterolateral approach was utilized. The iliotibial band was incised longitudinally in line with the skin incision. The anterior compartment muscles were elevated subperiosteally as needed. A lateral sub-meniscal arthrotomy was performed, allowing the lateral meniscus to be repositioned and repaired through both the anterior and the anterolateral incisions. Subsequently, the collapsed posterolateral articular fragment was reduced (elevated ) through the anterior incision (as shown in Fig. 6b). Through this primary fracture line, the secondary fracture line delineating the depressed posterolateral fragment from the intact lateral condyle was sought. If the secondary fracture line was clearly identifiable, a periosteal elevator or narrow osteotome was inserted along this line to elevate the depressed fragment. If the secondary fracture line was obscured due to comminution or impaction, the instrument was introduced through the primary fracture line, positioned approximately 1 cm below the depressed articular surface, and a controlled osteotomy was performed to detach the impacted fragment from the surrounding bone; the fragment was then elevated en bloc. The elevation force was applied gradually with gentle mallet tapping, while the reduction was continuously monitored by direct visualization through the anterior incision and confirmed with intraoperative fluoroscopy (AP and lateral views). Once the articular surface was restored to the level of the intact tibial plateau, provisional fixation was achieved with one or two 1.5-mm or 2.0-mm Kirschner wires. The resulting metaphyseal bone defect was then filled with allogeneic cancellous bone graft (Hangzhou Holly Industry Co., Ltd., China; 5–10 mm cancellous chips). All patients provided written informed consent for the use of allogeneic bone graft.

Attention was turned to the medial side. According to the trajectory of the fracture lines and the predetermined position where the plates need to be placed, a medial or posterior-medial incision was made. Care was taken to protect the saphenous nerve and great saphenous vein. The pes anserinus and the medial collateral ligament were exposed, as well as the distal bone spike. After that, the medial plateau was reduced and provisionally fixed with Kirschner wires. A large point-type reduction forceps could be employed to facilitate reduction if needed. According to the trajectory of the fracture lines, buttress plates were placed on the medial or/and posterior-medial sides.

Then, for the reduced posterolateral articular surface, fixation was achieved using a rafting technique. To enhance the stability of the fixation, the Kirschner wires were occasionally retained. If an avulsion fracture of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) insertion with significant displacement was encountered, it was reduced and fixed through the anterior incision using a No.5 ETHIBOND Excel polyester suture. Intra-operative fluoroscopic images were used to ensure articular reduction, joint congruence, condylar width, proper alignment, and to confirm safe placement of implant components. Before closing the wound, the stability of the knee joint was assessed to determine whether a restrictive brace was needed postoperatively. Incisions were then closed over suction drains.

Postoperative care

For patients with knee instability, restrictive brace was used postoperatively. The drainage tube was generally removed 48 h after surgery. Patients were encouraged to perform knee flexion and extension exercises and straight leg raising and ankle pump exercises if pain could be tolerated on the first day postoperatively. X-ray and CT examination were performed after the drainage tube was removed. Then, X-ray examination was performed at 1, 2, and 3 months postoperatively. According to the fracture healing, partial weight-bearing exercise was performed at about 1 to 2 months postoperatively, and full weight-bearing exercise was performed after fracture healing [16] (Fig. 2).

Assessment of treatment outcomes

The preoperative waiting time, intraoperative blood loss, operation time, postoperative complications such as skin necrosis, wound infection, deep infection, neurovascular injury, delayed bone union, bone nonunion, and fracture healing time were recorded. At the last follow-up, the muscle strength, sensation, limb length and alignment of lower limbs were recorded. The active flexion and extension of the knee joint were measured using a goniometer. The KSS score was recorded at the last follow-up, and 80–100 was rated as excellent, 70–79 as good, 60–69 as fair, and < 60 as poor [17].

All measurements were performed by an independent orthopedic surgeon who was not involved in the surgical procedures.

Perioperative and follow-up data

In this study, 53.3% (8/15) of the patients had knee subluxation, 60% (9/15) of the patients had lateral meniscus injury, and 40% (6/15) of the patients had lateral meniscus entrapment. Two patients (13.3%) had anterior cruciate ligament insertion injuries. One patient had a complete avulsion fracture with significant displacement (> 5 mm) and underwent fixation using a No. 5 ETHIBOND Excel polyester suture through the anterior incision. The other patient had a partial tear with minimal displacement (< 2 mm) and no instability after fracture fixation (Lachman grade 1); therefore, no fixation was performed. At final follow-up, both patients had stable knees, full range of motion (125°-130° flexion), and excellent KSS scores (84 and 86).

The preoperative waiting time was 5.3 days (range 2–12 days), the operation time was 118 min (range 90–140 min), and the intraoperative blood loss was 300 ml (range 150–500 ml). There were no neurovascular complications, skin necrosis and deep infection after operation. One patient had superficial wound infection, which was healed after intensive wound management and oral antibiotics.

The mean follow-up time was 18 months (12–24 months).There was no delayed union and nonunion. The average fracture union time was 13 weeks (11–15 weeks). At the last follow-up, all 15 patients demonstrated normal lower extremity muscle strength (grade 5/5), normal sensation, equal bilateral limb length, and normal mechanical axis alignment. The average knee extension angle was 3 degrees (range, 0–6 degrees), and the average knee flexion angle was 122 degrees (range, 110–135 degrees). The mean active arc of motion was 119° (range, 104° to 135°). The average KSS score was 85 points (range, 73–90 points), of which 12 were excellent, 2 were good, and 1 was fair.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old female was admitted with left knee swelling and pain for one day following trauma. Preoperative radiographs revealed a medial tibial condyle fracture with knee subluxation and a loose bony fragment adjacent to the lateral femoral condyle (Fig. 3a-b). Preoperative CT scans confirmed the total medial plateau (TMP) fracture subtype with a characteristic posterolateral articular surface depression (Fig. 3c-d). Preoperative MRI demonstrated entrapment of the lateral meniscus within the fracture gap (Fig. 3e).

Discussion

This study suggests that the anterior auxiliary limited open incision may be an effective treatment option for Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC fractures, achieving satisfactory radiographic and functional outcomes with a low complication rate in our cohort. The key to its success lies in its ability to leverage the unique anatomy of this fracture subtype to simultaneously address its two core challenges: the inaccessible posterolateral osseous depression and the frequently associated soft tissue interposition.

Before discussing our findings, it is important to clarify the specific fracture subtype addressed in this study. Yang et al. [13]classified Schatzker type IV fractures into three subtypes based on CT morphology: anteromedial (9.3%), posteromedial (42.7%), and total medial plateau (TMP, 48.0%). The present study focuses exclusively on the TMP subtype with posterolateral collapse (Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC). In this subtype, the primary fracture line is located anteriorly, just medial to the midline, creating a short, direct anteroposterior surgical corridor to the posterolateral depression. This anatomy is fundamentally different from the posteromedial subtype, where the primary fracture line is located posteromedially, resulting in a long and deep mediolateral corridor that makes direct access to the posterolateral depression challenging. Understanding this anatomical distinction is essential for interpreting the surgical technique and outcomes reported here.

To date, no previous study has specifically addressed the surgical treatment of the Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC subtype. When encountering this fracture pattern, surgeons have traditionally relied on standard medial and lateral dual incisions — the same approach used for general bicondylar tibial plateau fractures. However, this conventional approach has several critical limitations when applied to Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC fractures. For the posterolateral articular depression, the intact anterolateral cortical rim blocks direct access through the standard lateral incision, necessitating lateral osteotomy (similar to Sciadini & Sims [10]) or lateral cortical window (similar to Crabtree et al. [11]) to create an artificial corridor for elevation. For the entrapped lateral meniscus, the conventional approach lacks appreciation of this pathology; surgeons often overlook the trapped meniscus because the lateral approach does not provide a direct view into the fracture interval. As a result, the entrapped meniscus may be left unreduced, leading to persistent knee subluxation, condylar widening, and even fracture reduction failure [15]. Even when the entrapment is recognized, meniscal reduction through the standard lateral approach is extremely challenging — an experienced surgeon may occasionally succeed by carefully manipulating the meniscus, which remains attached via the coronary ligament, but this maneuver is technically demanding and not consistently reproducible. These limitations highlight the need for a dedicated surgical approach for the Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC fracture subtype.

The existing literature on Schatzker type IV fractures with posterolateral collapse has focused almost exclusively on the posteromedial subtype. The technique of Potocnik et al. [9], utilizing the fracture line for reduction, is conceptually sound but hampered by the long and deep mediolateral corridor. This limitation is reflected in the reports of Sciadini and Sims [10] and Crabtree et al. [11], who found reduction through the fracture line difficult and proposed lateral osteotomies or corticotomies. More recently, Capella et al. [14] described a three-step arthroscopy-assisted technique requiring specialized equipment and advanced skills. It is worth emphasizing that all of these studies addressed the posteromedial subtype, not the TMP subtype.

For the Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC subtype, such iatrogenic procedures are unnecessary, as the native anterior fracture line provides optimal access. Our anterior incision transforms the surgical strategy from “creating a window” to “entering through the front door,” providing a short, direct anteroposterior corridor that enables straightforward elevation and reduction of the posterolateral fragment under direct vision, without osteotomy, cortical window, or arthroscopic equipment. To our knowledge, this is the first study to specifically describe a surgical technique designed for the Schatzker IV-TMP/PLC fracture subtype that leverages its unique anterior fracture line anatomy. The second major advantage is efficacy in managing soft tissue injuries, particularly lateral meniscus entrapment. The high prevalence of these injuries — 35.2% overall and 76% in subluxated knees [7]— demands a surgical approach that facilitates systematic evaluation and repair. By following the anterior fracture line, the surgeon gains a panoramic view into the fracture depths, allowing immediate identification of a trapped meniscus. This exposure is a core feature, enabling direct release and suture repair. Similarly, ACL avulsion fractures can be readily accessed and fixed through this incision. The 40% meniscal entrapment rate in our series, aligning with rates reported in dedicated soft-tissue studies [7, 12], attests to the diagnostic and therapeutic capability of this approach.

We acknowledge that our technique is not a minimally invasive procedure. As shown in Fig. 4a, three incisions are used, and the total incision length exceeds that of traditional dual-incision approaches. The value lies in providing direct open visualization of the posterolateral depression and entrapped meniscus without arthroscopic equipment or additional osteotomy, not in minimizing surgical trauma.

The comprehensive exposure afforded by the anterior incision logically dictates a superior surgical sequence. Some previous strategies [10, 11], perhaps due to limited visibility of interposed soft tissue, advocated reducing the medial column first. We believe this sequence is suboptimal, as a trapped meniscus or large inverted posterolateral fragment will obstruct anatomical reduction of the medial plateau. Our method mandates a systematic sequence: first, address soft tissue entrapment and reduce the posterolateral fragment through the anterior incision; second, reduce and fix the medial condyle; and finally, apply lateral column fixation if needed. This ensures no interposing structure prevents anatomical reduction.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, we did not set up a control group. Secondly, not all patients underwent preoperative MRI, so we lack imaging data on collateral and posterior cruciate ligament injuries; we suggest routine preoperative MRI for such patients in future studies. Thirdly, most patients underwent three incisions, which may raise concerns about skin necrosis. However, citing Howard et al. [18]and our observation of no skin necrosis, we believe this concern is mitigated. Fourthly, because our series focused exclusively on the TMP/PLC subtype and no previous studies have reported outcomes for this subtype using alternative techniques, we could not perform direct quantitative comparisons. Finally, this is a single-center retrospective study with a small sample size and relatively short follow-up. A prospective multicenter study with longer follow-up would be desirable to confirm our findings.
