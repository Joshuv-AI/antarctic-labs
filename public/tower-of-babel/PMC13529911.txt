Synchronizing degradation with regeneration: a model-driven framework for designing biodegradable biomaterials in bone tissue engineering
Authors: Vahid Zarghami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bone regeneration is a complex biological process, and the repair of critical-sized bone defects is often difficult, highlighting the need to develop biodegradable scaffolds that can effectively synergize with tissue repair. This paper introduces a model-based bone repair design framework that matches the kinetics of material degradation with the spatiotemporal stages of bone repair. We systematically evaluated the degradation mechanisms and tunability of four classes of biomaterials—metals (Mg, Fe, Zn), ceramics (HA, β-TCP, bioactive glass), polymers (PLGA, PCL), and composites—and mapped established mathematical models (linear partial differential equations, sigmoid curves, response-diffusion partial differential equations, and mechanobiological finite element models) to specific bone defect sizes and clinical scenarios. Based on this classification, we propose a four-step clinical design to guide researchers in completing the following steps: (1) calculating patient-specific healing timelines using first-order heuristics; (2) selecting appropriate material classes based on mechanical and biological requirements; (3) designing degradation kinetics to match the healing stages; and (4) validating scaffold-tissue alignment. To achieve quantitative assessment, we introduce the synchronization index (SI), a provisional index designed to quantify the correlation between actual implant degradation and patient-specific target curves. While the SI and the healing timeline formula are currently proposed only as conceptual design tools and require prospective experimental and clinical validation, their integration provides a structured path for scaffold development, moving it from experimental trial and error to precise, model-based engineering design. The goal of this framework is to facilitate the rational design of smart, resorbable implants that dynamically match the biological and mechanical requirements of bone regeneration.

Introduction

Bone regeneration is a complex biological process that requires the concerted action of cellular behavior, assembly of the extracellular matrix, and mechanical stability [1–3]. Critical-size defects (CSDs), larger than the body’s capacity for spontaneous healing, are a significant clinical problem [4]. Biodegradable biomaterials are in this case an indispensable resource as temporary scaffolds, guiding tissue repair and simultaneous degradation alongside new bone formation. These interventions call for great knowledge of healing kinetics, mechanical needs, and biological reactions, based on the need for a multidisciplinary approach that unites empirical models, mechanobiological processes, computational simulations, and advanced material design [5–9].

The development of biodegradable biomaterials for bone tissue engineering is a combination of biology, materials science, and computational engineering. By engineering degradation kinetics that correspond to defect-specific models of healing—linear, power-law, sigmoidal, or strain-dependent—researchers are able to engineer scaffolds to offer mechanical support, biochemical cues, and architectures conducive to vascularization.

This paper maps the trajectory of next-generation design of biodegradable biomaterials, in which material properties are harmonized with the spatiotemporal dynamics of healing. By interdisciplinarity, the field can move forward from empirical grafting to precision-controlled bone regeneration to ultimately improve outcomes for millions of patients with skeletal injury and defects. In fact, this paper lays out a roadmap for selection and engineering of optimized biodegradable biomaterials tailored for the defect healing rate, anatomical location, and physiological states.

Stages of bone regeneration and timeline

Bone healing is a multi-step, tightly regulated biological cascade process that goes through six consecutive phases, each with unique cellular activities, signaling pathways, and extracellular matrix dynamics (Table 1; Fig. 1). These phases—spanning mesenchymal condensation to mature bone remodeling—establish the spatiotemporal framework that biodegradable scaffolds must synchronize with to achieve successful regeneration [10–18]. There are two primary forms of bone formation: intramembranous ossification and endochondral ossification. They are distinct in processes, location, and the use of cartilage as an intermediate template. Intramembranous ossification is a fundamental process whereby bone is formed without a precursor cartilage. It is predominantly observed in flat bones such as those of the skull, clavicle, and facial bones. Endochondral ossification is an indirect mode of bone formation by a cartilage template [19–22]. It is predominantly observed in long bones such as the femur, humerus, and tibia. Mathematical models for predicting bone healing dynamics

Bone defect healing depends on a variety of factors, including defect size, anatomical location, soft tissue environment, patient age, mechanical load, vascular distribution, and overall health. Defect size is the primary factor determining healing time, as larger defects require more extensive tissue remodeling. Due to the complex biological and mechanical interactions, there is currently no single mathematical formula that can accurately predict the relationship between bone growth and defect size [28–33]. However, several theoretical, experimental, and computational models have been developed to estimate the relationship between healing time and defect size. This relationship is influenced by several key parameters, such as the critical defect size threshold (CSD), the intensity of biological responses, vascular confinement, and the local biomechanical environment [34–44].

This is not a species constant; rather, it varies significantly depending on the species and anatomical location due to differences in bone vascularity, cell population density, and regenerative potential [45–49]. The critical size defect (CSD) is the minimum intraosseous defect which will not heal spontaiously throughout the life span of an organism unless therapeutically treated. While CSD measurements are defined in preclinical models—i.e., 5 mm in rat calvarium, 15–20 mm in rabbit mandible or calvariae, and 2–3 cm segmental defects in sheep tibia —its actual value in human situations is less stringent and highly context-dependent. Clinical practice indicates that major bone defects over 2–2.5 cm in adults do not heal by themselves under any circumstances, but anatomical location, vascular supply, age, and comorbid status all play a significant role in this limitation [47–49]. Later work gives primacy to employing standardized, species-specific CSD threshold values to provide maximum translational value, as argued by Wei et al. [50], who caution against naive extrapolation from animal CSD values to the human condition without due account for variations in bone metabolism, healing kinetics, and biomechanical loading. Moreover, induced membrane and periosteum are central in modulating the regenerative capacity in sites of defects to modulate the effective CSD in animal and human conditions. Therefore, even as the use of animal models provides controlled levels for comparison of bone regenerative solutions, the attainment of a clinically relevant human CSD should incorporate anatomical, biological, and mechanical factors in a patient-specific model [50–56]. CSD is a direct source of healing dynamics and a criterion choice standard for adequate mathematical models. Hence, a range of predictive modeling schemes ranging from linear and sigmoidal to mechanobiological and diffusion-reaction formulations have been specifically designed and optimized in reference to defect size so as to adequately fit the spatiotemporal dynamics of bone regeneration.

Mathematical modeling has become a crucial instrument for enhancing our knowledge and predictive power of bone regeneration and, more specifically, bone defect healing. The last twenty years have seen many studies proving that the incorporation of biological, mechanical, and biochemical variables into computational models allows for an improved insight into the sophisticated processes of tissue development and remodeling [57]. For instance, Bailon-Plaza and Van Der Meulen [58] developed one of the earliest mathematical models to study how fracture healing is controlled by growth factors, highlighting the crucial role played by biochemical signaling in the direction of cellular activity and tissue differentiation [59–61]. Similarly, Moore et al. [62] introduced a mechanistic model coupling mechanical feedback and biochemical mediation to predict tissue genesis dynamics in bone defects, emphasizing the interdependence of physical stimuli and molecular cues on regenerative outcomes [63–67].

Influence of mechanical environment on the progress of healing has also been thoroughly investigated by computational simulations. García-Aznar et al. [68] illustrated that interfragmentary movement plays an important role in callus formation and maturation, quantitatively indicating how mechanical stability regulates tissue phenotype [68, 69]. Concurrently, Morgan et al. [70] established empirical correlations between local strain magnitudes and resulting tissue phenotypes at skeletal healing sites and presented experimental proof for mechanobiological hypotheses incorporated within predictive models [70–72]. The data are in favor of incorporating patient- or defect-specific mechanical conditions within modeling strategies for greater relevance to the clinic.

Moreover, advanced imaging techniques allowed for non-invasive quantification of scaffold–bone interactions as shown by van Lenthe et al. [72], who employed micro-computed tomography to monitor in vivo bone formation in engineered constructs—data that can be immediately used to validate and refine mathematical models [72]. Concurrent with this, Geris and others have consistently offered integrated modeling frameworks that integrate cellular dynamics, inflammation, angiogenesis, and tissue differentiation to enable in silico exploration of treatment modalities for compromised healing states such as nonunions. Their contributions outline the capability of mathematical models to be more than analytical tools but rather platforms for virtual experimentation and therapy optimization.

Recent studies again attest to the growing maturity of these approaches. Ghiasi et al. [73] incorporated mechanobiological modeling concepts of fracture healing, emphasizing the critical role of multiscale and multi-physics models capable of describing the spatiotemporal healing progression. Likewise, Trejo et al. [74] introduced models that consciously incorporate macrophage-initiated inflammation—the main initial stage that has been historically overlooked in prior frameworks—thereby enhancing biological fidelity. Most recently, Cook et al. [75] placed bone remodeling models within the context of systems biology, urging integrative conjunction of genetic, molecular, and biomechanical networks to predict long-term regenerative consequences.

Linear model

This linear approximation is best suited for early repair of small defects and is frequently used for polymer and ceramic scaffolds during the transition from inflammatory callus to cartilaginous callus, when the strain gradient is minimal [76]. Gómez-Benito et al. [76] utilized a computational model to investigate the influence of fracture gap width on the healing pattern in long bones by making linear assumptions in their mechano-regulatory algorithm for representing local fluid flow and strain in terms of tissue growth. They showed that narrow gaps prefer direct intramembranous ossification and large gaps retarded healing by means of endochondral processes— a behavior which, in certain simplified scenarios, can be simulated by linear relationships between biological and mechanical factors. Thus, Hollinger and Kleinschmidt [77] established the critical size defect (CSD) as a testing standard for bone repair materials, in effect assuming linear thresholds: species-dependent sizes of defects do not spontaneously heal, with an implied binary (heal or not heal) outcome that may be specified with linear decision boundaries under controlled conditions. Despite the inherently nonlinear, and by extension, complex feedback-controlled nature of bone healing, linear models are still important for early approximations, parameter sensitivity studies, and setting baselines for predictions within computational simulations as well as preclinical testing paradigms.

Power-law model

The power law formulation is particularly important for viscoelastic polymer-ceramic composites and cortical bone adaptation during callus formation and early regeneration, as time-dependent load transfer determines the degradation rate. Also, this model provides a good representation of the time-dependent mechanical properties of bone, particularly its hereditary and viscoelastic features. Deseri et al. [78] devised a hierarchical fractal theory explaining bone’s natural power-law hereditariness, demonstrating that bone tissue stress relaxation and creep reveal power-law relaxation rather than the exponential response typical of classical viscoelastic models. This power-law behavior is a consequence of the multiscale, hierarchica bone microstructure, from collagen fibrils to osteons, which possesses fractal-like structure on certain length scales, producing non-local in time memory effects that are very well captured by fractional-order calculus. On the contrary, Satapathy et al. [79] employed finite element analysis to investigate fracture fixation of the femur using functionally graded bone plates, whose material properties are spatially varying in order to closely resemble the natural bone stiffness gradient. Although they were more interested in implant design than constitutive modeling of the bone, their work de facto acknowledges the importance of accurately modeling bone’s nonlinear, inhomogeneous mechanical response—response power-law models can model more realistically than linear elastic or common viscoelastic assumptions. Together, these researches demonstrate how incorporating power-law rheology in bone modeling enhances the fidelity of computational simulations in both physiological healing scenarios and biomechanical implant design as it mimics the complex interaction between bone’s hierarchical structure and its time-dependent mechanical behavior.

Sigmoidal critical volume model

The sigmoidal critical volume model offers a biologically grounded framework for describing the temporal evolution of bone formation during healing, capturing the characteristic lag, rapid growth, and plateau phases inherent to regenerative processes. This sigmoidal pattern is empirically well-supported: Liddell [80] demonstrated that bone anchorage around endosseous implants follows a logistic (sigmoidal) growth curve, reflecting the biological sequence of osteoconduction, de novo bone formation, and remodeling. Similarly, Comiskey [81] emphasized that fracture healing is inherently non-linear, with callus development progressing through distinct phases that cannot be captured by simple linear models, thereby advocating for predictive frameworks that incorporate sigmoidal kinetics. Computational studies further validate this paradigm; Steiner et al. [82] showed that callus stiffening—a surrogate for bone formation—exhibits a time-dependent, saturating trend influenced by mechanical boundary conditions like fixation stiffness, while Ren et al. [83] used radiodensity profilometry to quantify early remodeling at the bone-callus interface, revealing spatially and temporally heterogeneous maturation consistent with a sigmoidal progression from soft to hard tissue. Moreover, Naveiro et al. [84] implemented a 3D computational model that simulates initial callus growth without predefined domains, implicitly generating sigmoidal tissue accumulation over time in response to local biological and mechanical cues across various fracture types. Together, these works substantiate the use of a sigmoidal critical volume model—where healing advances toward a biologically determined maximum volume—as a physiologically realistic and mathematically tractable approach for predicting bone regeneration in both implant osseointegration and fracture repair contexts [80–83]. Therefore, this framework is perfectly suited to ceramic analysis and the delayed flattening growth kinetics in callus maturation, making it an essential model for simulating the proliferation-to-regeneration phase transition in bioceramic scaffolds.

Mechanobiological models

Mechanobiological models represent a distinct class of predictive frameworks that explicitly link local mechanical stimuli—such as interstitial fluid flow, hydrostatic pressure, and octahedral shear strain—to pathways of cell differentiation and tissue formation. In contrast to purely empirical or diffusion-based approaches, these formulations are based on the well-established principle of mechanical transduction, in which bone progenitor cells sense and adapt to their physical microenvironment [84, 85]. Foundational work in this area includes the strain-pressure model, which postulates that tissue phenotype is determined by the magnitude of local strain and hydrostatic pressure: high-strain environments favor fibrous tissue, moderate strains promote cartilage, and low strains with minimal pressure enable direct intramembranous ossification. Accordingly, Prendergast and colleagues introduced a two-phase poroelastic mechanical tuning algorithm that integrates octahedral shear strain and interstitial fluid velocity as dual mechanical drivers. This formulation has since become a standard framework for computational bone repair, as it accurately captures the dynamic fluid–solid interactions in early callus tissue and porous scaffolds [86, 87]. Subsequent developments by Lacroix and Prendergast incorporated time-dependent loading histories and cellular activity thresholds, enabling finite element (FE) implementations that simulate the spatiotemporal progression of callus maturation under physiological loading [88].

In the context of biodegradable scaffold design, these mechanobiology models are essential. As the implant degrades, its evolving stiffness changes the local strain distribution and fluid flow. A properly calibrated mechanobiology framework ensures that degradation kinetics are synchronized with changing mechanical demands, maintaining local stimuli within the window of bone formation (typically ε ≈ 2–5%) while avoiding protection from mechanical stress or overload. Diffusion-Reaction (PDE) model for bone regeneration

The Diffusion-Reaction (Partial Differential Equation, PDE) model is a powerful mathematical model to simulate the spatiotemporal dynamics of morphogens, capturing spatial ion release, cell migration, and graded resorption in bioceramics and hydrogels during vascularization and matrix deposition phases [88]. Sapotnick and Nackenhorst [89] developed a high-order finite element method for numerically solving coupled advection–diffusion–reaction equations in a bioregulatory model of fracture healing, namely for simulating transport and interaction of key biochemical signals (e.g., TGF-β, BMPs) responsible for cell differentiation and tissue formation in the callus. It demonstrates how numerical solution of nonlinear PDE systems can conquer complex feedback between biological activity and local microenvironmental conditions. Geris et al. [67] also situate such models within the field of regenerative medicine, pointing out that reaction-diffusion systems, originally inspired by Turing’s theory of morphogenesis, are ideally suited to account for pattern formation in bone regeneration, with oxygen and growth factor concentration gradients driving spatial patterning of cartilage, bone, and fibrous tissue. While Bedekar et al. [90] focus on human embryonic stem cells (hESCs), their insightful discussion of reaction-diffusion mechanisms in morphological patterning confirms the generic applicability of these principles: stable spatial patterns are a consequence of interaction between short-range activation and long-range inhibition, a principle immediately translatable to the zonal tissue differentiation of fracture callus. Together, these experiments validate the diffusion-reaction PDE model as a biologically plausible and computationally tractable method for modeling the self-organizing phenomena underlying bone healing, prognostic of the outcome in varying biochemical and mechanical environments.

Finite Element (FE) model for bone regeneration

Finite Element (FE) modeling has emerged as a corner stone computational platform to integrate mechanical and biological functions in bone regeneration, enabling multiscale, multi-physics simulations of healing and tissue engineering. Finite element modeling can serve as a three-dimensional mechanical verification layer for all types of materials (especially composite materials), ensuring that the load transfer of the scaffold is consistent with the spatiotemporal repair requirements. Öztürk [91] used a 3D FE model to simulate coupled dynamics of scaffold degradation, spatiotemporal release of growth factors, and intracellular activation of signal pathways to demonstrate the influence of biomaterial design on cellular response and tissue structure in engineered constructs. Similarly, Boccaccio et al. [92] emphasized the integration of mechanobiology and biomimetic scaffold architecture using FE models to design scaffold porosity and stiffness to match local mechanical signals promoting osteogenesis and thereby integrating material science with biological response. Lipphaus and Witzel [93] employed FE-based syntheses to simulate callus formation and subsequent bone remodeling following long bone fractures and showed that the mechanical feedback—strain-driven adaptation—can correctly predict the transition from soft callus to load-bearing bone. On a more fundamental level, Sanz-Herrera, García-Aznar, and Doblaré [94] launched a micro–macro numerical strategy that links cell-scale biological phenomena (e.g., cell migration, differentiation) to continuum-scale mechanical fields, allowing bone regeneration in tissue-engineered scaffolds to be simulated under physiological loading conditions. Together, these studies illustrate that FE modeling presents a versatile and biophysically guided platform for the prediction of outcomes of bone regeneration by binding mechanical stimuli, biomaterial properties, and biological signaling in an explicit way, allowing the rational design of implants, scaffolds, and clinical treatment in regenerative orthopedics [91–93].

Model selection for bone regeneration

The selection of an appropriate computational or experimental model to predict bone healing dynamics should be guided by the defect size, as defect size fundamentally determines the biological response and repair capacity. Smaller fracture gaps (<1 mm) usually heal directly through intramembranous bone fusion and can be appropriately modeled using linear approximations or primitive sigmoid curves within the mechanobiology finite element framework. Defects of intermediate size—that is, those close to but below the critical size defect (CSD) threshold —show very sensitive endochondral healing. For these defects, a full mechanobiology finite element model, a diffusion-response system for morphogenetic transfer, and a sigmoid critical volume model to capture the spatiotemporal processes of tissue formation are most appropriate. Spontaneous healing is ruled out when the CSD threshold (defined as the minimum volume of intraosseous defect that cannot heal spontaneously without therapeutic intervention over the lifetime of the organism: approximately 2–2.5 cm in human long bones) is reached or exceeded. This requires advanced modeling to account for scaffold mechanics, degradation kinetics, guided cell migration, and growth factor signaling, typically achieved through micro-macro finite element computational frameworks or biomaterials information [3, 4].

For greater supra-critical or segmental defects, we propose a hybrid framework that combines image-based finite element simulation with machine learning-based mechanobiology rules, which may provide the most reliable predictive capabilities. Although this integrated approach is still under development and has not yet been standardized in clinical practice, initial computational studies have demonstrated its potential to capture nonlinearities, patient-specific healing trajectories, optimize scaffold design parameters, and accelerate virtual prototyping [34, 37]. Defect size is thus one of the key criteria for selecting such models to attain biological fidelity and predictability in research as well as clinical applications. Analysis of degradation kinetics and bone formation for metallic, ceramic, polymeric, and composite Biomaterials

The success of biodegradable biomaterials in bone repair is based on a delicate, spatio-temporal alignment between the material’s degradation process and the body’s inherent healing process. An ideal biomaterial must provide transient mechanical support, facilitate cell behavior, and finally degrade without a trace, leaving intact functional bone tissue behind. Mismatch within this timeline can lead to catastrophic failure: premature degradation leads to mechanical failure, but suppressed degradation can lead to chronic inflammation, stress shielding, or fibrous encapsulation. Thus, understanding and predicting the degradation kinetics of a material—metal, ceramic, polymer, or composite—and its direct and indirect role in bone formation is not an abstruse intellectual exercise but a necessary precursor to clinical translation. This review provides a scientific, in-depth description of each material type’s degradation processes, their typical corrosion or resorption rates, and most importantly, how the latter need to be calibrated using the appropriate math models in order to achieve maximum bone growth. The writing is grounded in the model framework it introduces and buttressed with data from the broader scientific literature regarding material degradation and its biological consequences [6, 95–99].

Biodegradable metallic biomaterials

The clinical performance of biodegradable metallic implants depends fundamentally on their inherent electrochemical behavior in complex physiological environments. Ideal implants should degrade in a controlled and reproducible manner, be compatible with tissue repair, and produce nontoxic, readily metabolizable corrosion products. Among the most promising candidates, magnesium (Mg), iron (Fe), and zinc (Zn) exhibit unique corrosion properties that dictate specific materials engineering strategies [7, 100–110]. Pure magnesium and its alloys have a very negative standard electrode potential (−2.37 V vs. standard hydrogen electrode), which makes them thermodynamically unstable in chloride-rich liquids. This high reactivity leads to rapid anodic dissolution, often accompanied by the release of hydrogen gas, which can cause premature mechanical failure and local tissue necrosis [111].

In contrast, iron and its alloys exhibit much slower degradation due to the higher potential (−0.44 V vs. standard hydrogen electrode, SHE) and the formation of a dense and stable passive oxide layer (Fe₂O₃/Fe₃O₄). While this maintains mechanical integrity, it is usually much slower than the natural tissue repair process. Zinc occupies an ideal middle ground. At a standard potential of −0.76 V vs. SHE, it degrades at a moderate and uniform rate without the production of gaseous byproducts, making it very attractive for achieving balanced degradation kinetics [112]. Importantly, the degradation of these metals is not solely determined by electrochemistry. It depends largely on the microstructural design, environmental complexity (e.g., protein adsorption), and in-body mechanical loads, which can significantly modify the corrosion pathways [111–114].

Magnesium-based alloys

The corrosion behavior of magnesium and its alloys in physiological media is the most important factor that will determine whether these materials are viable as biodegradable implant materials. Although Mg exhibits excellent biocompatibility, osteoconductivity, and a mechanical modulus that closely matches that of natural bone, its inherent chemical reactivity translates to an unduly high—and frequently uncontrolled—corrosion rate in the aqueous, chloride ion-rich environment of the human body at pH ~ 7.4. This rapid degradation can lead to early mechanical failure of the device, excessive hydrogen gas generation, and localized alkalinization, all of which can provoke unfavorable tissue responses and interfere with the healing process [115]. Thus, much of the recent work has focused on the rational design of Mg alloy compositions with a tailored, controlled corrosion rate that matches the time course of tissue regeneration. This alloying strategy involves the intentional addition of select elements that impact microstructural modification, surface film stabilization, and cathodic reaction kinetics inhibition, with the aim of controlling the anodic dissolution of the Mg matrix.

A key strategy for corrosion control is the addition of rare earth elements (RE). As Zhao et al. point out, rare earth elements simultaneously improve the corrosion resistance and mechanical properties of magnesium alloys [116]. Elements with high oxygen affinity, such as yttrium (Y) and neodymium (Nd), promote the formation of a denser and more stable protective oxide/hydroxide layer. This modified surface layer acts as an effective barrier against the aggressive penetration of chloride ions (Cl⁻), the main agent of localized pitting corrosion. Furthermore, TR elements contribute to grain refinement and reduce microgalvanic couplings between the α-Mg matrix and secondary intermetallic phases, thereby promoting a more homogeneous corrosion process. A notable example is the WE43 (Mg-4Y-3RE-0.5Zr) alloy, which offers suitable corrosion resistance for biomedical applications.

Recent experimental alloys, such as AZ31-GS (Mg–3Al–1Zn–0.3Mn–0.3Gd–0.1Sc), show significantly improved corrosion resistance. This is primarily attributed to the synergistic microalloying effect of Gd and Sc, which allows the formation of core–shell intermetallic particles. Aside from RE elements, a few other alloying elements play a role in corrosion control. Zinc (Zn) is a common biocompatible element that, at levels that are not too high, can strengthen the alloy by solid solution strengthening and sustain a more stable corrosion layer. However, its content needs to be kept under careful watch since excessive Zn will form cathodic second phases accelerating micro-galvanic corrosion. Manganese (Mn) is another necessary addition, not because it has a direct strengthening effect, but because it can eliminate unwanted iron (Fe), nickel (Ni), and copper (Cu) impurities from the melt. These heavy metal impurities at even trace levels (ppm) act as very potent cathodic sites, substantially increasing the overall corrosion rate. By forming stable intermetallic compounds with these impurities (e.g., Al-Mn-Fe phases in Al-containing alloys), Mn essentially negates their negative cathodic effect, giving a significant improvement to corrosion resistance [118].

Choice of the alloying elements also sets the nature of the corrosion products. An ideal Mg implant would degrade to non-toxic waste products that are metabolized or eliminated safely. Calcium (Ca) and strontium (Sr) are particularly attractive because they not only are biocompatible but are bioactive, and could promote bone growth. The formation of a corrosion layer enriched with the main inorganic components of bone, the calcium phosphates, may also enhance biocompatibility and integration. However, corrosion behavior is a complex phenomenon related to the composition of the alloy, the microstructure arising from this composition (i.e., the nature, size, and distribution of the secondary phases), and a particular physiological environment [119]. An I-phase (a Mg-Zn-RE intermetallic complex), for example, may be beneficial in corrosion resistance but is highly dependent on morphology and distribution within the matrix.

It should be mentioned that while alloying is a very powerful method, in most situations it is inadequate on its own to yield the desired corrosion profile for all clinical applications. The problems raised by Hassan et al. in terms of unforeseen degradation and hydrogen evolution prove the need for a multi-faceted solution [116]. As such, alloy design is frequently complemented with advanced surface engineering techniques. For instance, it was demonstrated by Rahman et al. that a bioactive hybrid coating in the form of a polymer and a ceramic like hydroxyapatite (HA) can deposit on alloys like WE43 and provide a secondary, very effective corrosion barrier [120]. The substrate alloy composition remains the foundation, but with a carefully engineered bulk material, if the coating becomes locally degraded, the exposed substrate will not corrode in a catastrophic manner. Briefly, the precise control of Mg alloy composition—through judicious selection and blending of constituents like REs, Zn, Mn, and Ca—is unavoidable and inherent for modulating corrosion kinetics in the body environment, paving the way to secure and efficient clinical translation of biodegradable Mg-based implants.

Iron-based alloys

Iron (Fe) alloys are promising candidates for biodegradable load-bearing orthopedic and cardiovascular implants due to their improved mechanical strength, ductility, and fatigue properties compared to magnesium- and zinc-based alloys [121]. However, the biggest hindrance to their clinical translation is their inherently low degradation rate in the physiological environment that lags far behind the natural tissue healing process. Such slow corrosion can lead to long-term complications such as chronic inflammation, stress shielding, and even late stent thrombosis [122]. Hence, extensive research efforts have been dedicated to strategically designing Fe-based alloy compositions to improve and modulate their corrosion kinetics to match the required therapeutic duration. The underlying principle is the deliberate addition of alloying elements that disrupt the stability of the passive iron oxide layer naturally present on the metal surface, thereby promoting a more active and uniform degradation.

One of the most effective strategies is the presence of elements constituting micro-galvanic couples within the alloy microstructure. The addition of Mn, for instance, is extensively studied because Mn is more negatively charged than Fe. The charge difference creates numerous micro-galvanic cells where Mn-rich phases are anodes and preferentially corrode, accelerating corrosion of the Fe matrix as a whole [123]. In the same way, the addition of such constituents as aluminum (Al), silicon (Si), or carbon (C) can form secondary intermetallic phases or inclusions, which serve as local cathodic locations, stimulating anodic dissolution of the neighboring Fe. Apart from triggering the creation of galvanic couples, certain alloying constituents influence the chemical composition and protective nature of the corrosion product coating directly. Phosphorus (P), for example, is reported to promote iron phosphate formation in the corrosion coating, which are typically more porous and less protective than dense iron oxides, thereby facilitating more rapid ion exchange with the surrounding fluid and more degradation.

Another crucial strategy involves the use of biocompatible metal components that not only regulate corrosion but also yield useful biological functions. Such is the case in Rabeeh et al. work on Fe–Au composites, where the noble gold (Au) particles act as efficient cathodic sites that radically increase the corrosion rate of the Fe matrix while promoting biomineralization, a critical aspect of bone integration [124]. Moreover, the addition of required trace elements like zinc (Zn) or magnesium (Mg) has a two-fold action: they are strong accelerators of corrosion but also have osteogenic or angiogenic properties. The choice of alloying element is also based on whether it is biocompatible and undergoes metabolism; those that are nutrients or easily excreted to avoid long-term toxicity are employed.

It is imperative to note that the physiological environment itself plays a dynamic and complex role in the corrosion process, which must be taken into consideration during alloy design. As highlighted by Zhang et al., serum proteins in the body fluid can adsorb profusely onto the implant surface, forming a protein corona that can inhibit or enable corrosion depending on the specific protein, its topography, and the underlying chemistry of the alloy [125]. The protein layer can also act as a diffusion barrier, hindering transport of ions or chelating metal ions, but inducing destabilization of the passive film and enhancing degradation. Hence, a perfect Fe-based alloy composition should be designed not just for its inherent electrochemical properties but also for its interaction with this intricate biological environment. Briefly stated, management of Fe-based alloy corrosion in the body relies on a highly intricate compositional construct that exploits micro-galvanic effects, alters the properties of the corrosion product layer, contains bioactive elements, and takes into account the dynamic nature of the physiological environment, all the while aiming to generate a pattern of degradation that is both therapeutically relevant and biocompatible [125, 126].

Zinc-based alloys

Zn-based biodegradable alloys represent a third-generation biomaterials class that is positioned ideally between magnesium alloys’ too-rapid degradation and iron-based systems’ too-slow corrosion [127]. One of the greatest strengths of Zn is that it is an essential trace element in human physiology as a cofactor for over 300 enzymes and plays a vital role in bone formation, immune response, and protection of endothelial cells [128]. This inherent biocompatibility ensures that its corrosion products are nontoxic and can be sequestered metabolically without harm, preventing the gas evolution issue of Mg and the long-term retention problem of Fe. However, pure zinc lacks the strength and ductility required to withstand orthopedic loads. Furthermore, its natural corrosion rate, while more desirable than that of iron, still requires modification to precisely match the 3–6 months’ bone healing timeframe [129]. In this manner, extensive investigations have focused on Zn-based alloy composition design for enhancing simultaneously mechanical properties and, above all, for controlling and accelerating the corrosion kinetics in the complex physiological environment.

The crucial thing in altering the corrosion rate of Zn alloys is through the deliberate introduction of biocompatible alloying elements that change the electrochemical and microstructure properties of the base metal. Magnesium (Mg), calcium (Ca), strontium (Sr), and manganese (Mn) are all preferred due to the fact that they play important biological functions as well as serving as effective micro-galvanic accelerators [130]. For instance, the addition of Mg, with a more negative electrochemical potential (−2.37 V) than Zn (−0.76 V), creates a micro-galvanic cell network in the alloy. In such an arrangement, the Mg-rich secondary phases or Mg-dissolved Zn matrix act as anodes and corrode preferentially, increasing the overall degradation of the Zn matrix. In addition, the introduction of Ca not only enhances the grain refinement that can increase the strength but also precipitates electrochemically active secondary intermetallic compounds like CaZn₁₃ that serve as local anodic sites, which improve a controlled and even corrosion front [129]. This design approach of using biologically active elements as corrosion promoters is a design process that differentiates Zn alloys from Fe-based alloys, where the latter seeks to produce cathodic sites in order to maintain anodic dissolution.

Moreover, the choice of the alloying element has a major impact on the nature and protective properties of the corrosion product layer that forms in vivo. An implant material based on Zn would, ideally, corrode into a porous and non-adherent zinc oxide (ZnO) and zinc hydroxide (Zn(OH)₂) layer, and then into zinc phosphate or carbonate compounds, which are extremely soluble and can be shed by the body’s own processes [131]. Alloying with additions like Sr or Mn can have the capability to alter the composition and morphology of this layer, preventing the formation of a dense, passivating film that otherwise would heavily retard further deterioration, as so often happens with pure Fe. The work of Akinwekomi and Akhtar on Ca-alloyed Zn shows that Ca content can be precisely controlled to achieve a balance between mechanical strengthening and a corrosion rate consistent with new bone formation, highlighting the tuneability of such systems [129]. The dynamic physiological environment also needs to be considered while designing the alloy. As noted by Zhang et al. in their study on biodegradable metals, serum proteins from body fluids can significantly adsorb onto the implant surface, forming a protein corona that may inhibit or promote corrosion depending on the specific protein and the underlying alloy chemistry [126]. Therefore, a successful Zn alloy composition must be constructed not only according to its intrinsic electrochemical properties but also to how well it responds to this complex biological environment in order to yield a reproducible and therapeutically beneficial degradation profile. Briefly, the corrosion behavior of Zn-based alloys in the body is perfectly controlled by a high-order compositional engineering which takes advantage of micro-galvanic activities of important bioactive elements, optimizes the properties of the corrosion product layer, and accounts for dynamic action of the physiological environment for the aim of achieving the optimal possible balance between mechanical support and biodegradation for safe and effective clinical use [131, 132].

Mechanisms of ceramic degradation and control strategies for bone regeneration

Biodegradable ceramics, mainly calcium phosphates like β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) and hydroxyapatite (HA), and bioactive glasses (e.g., 45S5 Bioglass®), are the workhorses of bone graft substitutes. Distinguishing between material category-level biological functions and structure-dependent biological functions is crucial. While all the aforementioned ceramic materials possess inherent osteogenic properties, osteogenic behavior is not a universal characteristic of these materials. The osteoconductive properties of hydroxyapatite (HA) have been experimentally demonstrated, but they are highly dependent on specific material structures, including nanoscale particle size, high microporosity (>60–70%), customized surface morphology, or strategic ion doping (e.g., Sr, Mg) [133, 134]. Dense, porous, and stoichiometric HA exhibits predominantly osteogenic properties. In contrast, β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) and biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP) ceramics are more likely to exhibit osteogenic potential due to their controllable dissolution kinetics and the localized release of bioactive calcium and phosphate ions, which stimulate mesenchymal stem cell differentiation. Therefore, scaffold designers should recognize that osteoinduction is an engineered characteristic of specific ceramic formulations, not a default characteristic of all HA-based scaffolds. Degradation is not a mode of failure but a design feature, allowing them to be replaced by new bone. Their primary mode of action is biological: they are osteoconductive, offering a scaffold that imitates bone mineral, and typically osteoinductive, releasing ions that stimulate cellular activity [135].

Biodegradable ceramic biomaterials undergo dissolution-relation degradation mechanisms that are distinct from metallic corrosion, which are governed by hydrolytic, ionic exchange, and cellular resorption mechanisms. For calcium phosphate ceramics like hydroxyapatite (HA) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP), degradation is controlled by crystallinity, stoichiometry, and local microenvironmental factors—high crystallinity and Ca/P ratio of 1.67 of HA offers stability with slow dissolution, while reduced crystallinity and Ca/P ratio of 1.5 of β-TCP allows quicker resorption under acidic conditions produced due to osteoclast action. Bioactive glasses like 45S5 Bioglass® undergo sequential ion exchange, hydrolysis, and apatite layer formation. Control measures include compositional changes: crystallinity or Ca/P ratio enhances stability in HA, strontium or magnesium doping modulates dissolution kinetics and bioactivity. Surface processing techniques such as plasma-sprayed HA coatings on β-TCP scaffolds create diffusion barriers, acid etching increases surface area for controlled dissolution, and addition of bioactive ions modifies the degradation profile. These operations are material composition and surface engineering-critically reliant, and mathematical models provide quantitative platforms to forecast and optimize degradation kinetics in bone regeneration. The empirical, mechanobiological, and diffusion-reaction models of the knowledge base are invaluable tools for rational design, where ceramic properties regulate model parameters directly to achieve synchronized degradation and bone formation [133, 134, 136].

Biodegradable calcium phosphate (CaP)-based ceramics offer a versatile platform for bone tissue engineering. Unlike metallic biomaterials, these ceramics do not undergo electrochemical corrosion. Instead, their degradation is regulated by a complex interplay of dissolution, hydrolysis, cellular activity, and deposition. Degradation kinetics are primarily controlled by chemical composition, crystallinity, phase purity, and porosity.

The most studied CaP ceramics are hydroxyapatite (HA) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP). Hyaluronic acid (HA) is very stable and degrades very slowly in physiological fluids due to its low solubility product (Ksp ≈ 10⁻⁵⁸), making it suitable for long-term bone conduction, but often too slow to be fully absorbed. In comparison, β-TCP is much more soluble (Ksp ≈ 10⁻²⁹) and is actively taken up by osteoclasts, rapidly releasing calcium and phosphate ions to form new bone matrix. To take advantage of the benefits of both, biphasic calcium phosphate ceramics (BCPs) have been developed, which are composed of HA and β-TCP in different ratios. For example, a 70/30 HA/β-TCP scaffold is typically absorbed in 12–18 months, coinciding with the transition from callus formation to bone remodeling. A 60/40 ratio can reduce absorption time to 6–12 months for faster tissue replacement, while an 80/20 ratio increases functional lifespan for long-term mechanical support to more than 18–24 months [137, 138]. In these systems, the soluble phase of β-TCP degrades first to stimulate osteoblastic activity, while the stable HA structure provides initial mechanical strength and ensures a dynamic balance between material loss and bone gain [134].

The process of degradation of these ceramics is largely determined by the immediate physiological microenvironment. In a weakly acidic aqueous environment (pH approximately 7.4, but slightly 4.5–5.5 in osteoclast-generated resorption pits, maintained by type V H+-ATPase proton pumps, the CaP network is dissolved with the help of protons. The reaction is given by Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂ + 8H⁺ → 10Ca²⁺ + 6HPO₄²⁻ + 2H₂O. Released Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻ ions are not only non-toxic but are necessary building blocks for osteoblasts that can be utilized to directly synthesize new bone mineral. This release of ions will also lead to local supersaturation with a biologically active, carbonated apatite precipitating on the ceramic surface, further enhancing its bioactivity and bonding with native bone—a process known as bioactive fixation. The porosity of the ceramic is also a highly critical variable that controls its resorption. Xu et al. opine that there should be a high interconnective macroporous structure (pore size >100 µm) in order to facilitate vascularization, cell migration, and body fluid infiltration, which all increase the process of dissolution. At the same time, a micropore network of less than 10 µm in the scaffold struts increases the specific surface area, providing further scope for fluid interaction and ion exchange, refining the rate of degradation to the nanoscale. The mechanical properties of the ceramic, while generally inferior to those of metals, are also degradation-dependent. A denser, more crystalline structure gives increased strength but retarded resorption, whereas a more amorphous or porous structure results in increased degradation but lower initial mechanical support. This is one of the compromises in design between load-bearing and non-load-bearing applications [133].

Beyond mere CaP ceramics, the field has now advanced to include more sophisticated and functionalized systems, such as bioglasses and bioglass-ceramics. As expounded by Filip et al. [139] and Kowalska et al. [140], phosphate-based bioglasses are amorphous substances that can be engineered to have a much higher and more controllable dissolution rate than crystalline CaPs. Their breakdown is initiated by an ion-exchange reaction where alkali ions (e.g., Na⁺, K⁺) of the glass network are exchanged with H₃O⁺ ions of the surrounding fluid at high speed, leading to network hydrolysis and release of a cascade of therapeutic ions (e.g., Ca²⁺, P⁵⁺, Si⁴⁺, Sr²⁺, Zn²⁺). These ions, besides pH buffering in the local field, exert distinct biological effects, e.g., induction of angiogenesis (Si, Cu) or osteogenesis (Sr, Zn). Bioglass degradation products also nucleate a bone-like layer of apatite, as with CaP ceramics, but typically more quickly. Furthermore, the inclusion of synergistic bioactive agents, as introduced by Xu et al., provides another considerable driving force in modulating the material’s environmental interaction. Through the addition of growth factors (e.g., BMP-2), peptides, or drugs to the porous structure of a CaP ceramic or bioglass scaffold, the degradation process can be coordinated with a controlled release pattern. This not only controls local cellular response to accelerate healing but also has an impact on degradation itself; for instance, an osteoclastogenic agent will inevitably speed up resorption of the ceramic matrix. Briefly, the “corrosion control” of biodegradable CaP ceramics and their descendants has nothing to do with preventing material loss and everything to do with logically designing their composition, structure, and chemistry so as to induce a reproducible, bioactive, and therapeutically effective degradation process that coherently synchronizes with the body’s very own natural bone remodeling cycle [139, 140].

Biodegradable polymeric biomaterials: hydrolysis/enzymatic kinetics and the race for space

Biodegradable polymers are among the most important factors in designing future generations of temporary medical devices and drug delivery devices with a special advantage over metallic biomaterials due to their tunable degradation profiles and absence of electrochemical corrosion. Contrary to the metals that deteriorate by redox degradation that can form toxic products like hydrogen gas or alkaline microenvironments, polymers deteriorate by hydrolysis or enzymatic chain scission that promotes the slow deterioration of their macromolecular structure into biocompatible monomers or oligomers that are safely metabolized or excreted. This breakdown is not a failure but a planned, inherent process that enables the material to support mechanically during the crucial healing period and then resorb completely, eliminating the need for secondary surgical retrieval. The degradation kinetics of these polymers in the physiological environment are carefully controlled by their molecular organization, chemical structure, and physical properties, which are precisely engineered to align with the therapeutic schedule of a specific clinical application [141, 142].

Among the widely studied biodegradable polymers are aliphatic polyesters such as polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), its copolymer poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), and polycaprolactone (PCL). Their degradation is governed primarily by the volumetric hydrolysis of their ester backbone, a reaction highly sensitive to hydrophilicity, crystallinity, and molecular weight. As reviewed by Wisan et al., the degradation rate generally follows the trend: PGA > PLGA > PLA > PCL [141]. PGA, with its highly hydrophilic units, readily absorbs water, leading to rapid and often sudden mechanical failure. PLA degrades more slowly, and its rate is controlled by stereochemistry (e.g., semicrystalline PLLA degrades in 12–24 months). PLGA offers the most adjustable platform. For example, a 50:50 ratio degrades in 1–2 months, while an 85:15 ratio takes 5–6 months. PCL, which is highly hydrophobic and crystalline, degrades very slowly, taking 2–3 years [143–145]. A major challenge with these polymers is the phenomenon of “autocatalytic bulk erosion.” When water penetrates the scaffold faster than the ester bonds can be hydrolyzed, chain breakage occurs uniformly throughout the interior. In thick implants, trapped acidic degradation products (such as lactic or glycolic acid) accumulate in the core, accelerating internal hydrolysis and potentially causing premature mechanical failure. This phenomenon must be carefully controlled through scaffold geometry, porosity design, and copolymer modulation [146].

To move beyond the limitations of simple polyesters and give higher levels of control over the degradation process, researchers have invented numerous advanced strategies. One is the development of “smart” or stimulus-responsive polymers, according to Dallaev [147]. These materials can adjust their degradation rate on the basis of specific physiological stimuli like pH, temperature, or the activity of specific enzymes. For instance, within the acidic inflammatory or tumor microenvironment, a polymer with acid-labile linkages (such as an acetal or ortho ester bond) will disintegrate much more rapidly, allowing site-specific release of drugs. Similarly, polymers with peptide segments that are MMP substrates—enzymes overexpressed by tissue remodeling—can be engineered to cleave specifically at the site of ongoing healing. Another effective strategy is the composition of hybrid or composite materials. By blending together a fast-degrading and a slow-degrading polymer, or by incorporating inorganic fillers such as hydroxyapatite nanoparticles or bioactive glass, the degradation profile as a whole can be modulated. The inorganic phase can also act as a buffer against acidic degradation products, hindering the autocatalytic process and enabling more uniform, surface-eroding degradation [148, 149]. Also noteworthy is the method of fabrication. Electrospinning, for example, produces scaffolds of high surface area and volume ratio and nanofibrous, porous morphology that permit high invasion rates of fluids and enzyme penetration, accelerating degradation compared to a dense, solid sheet of the same substance. Mayilswamy et al.‘s work on electrospun nanofibers demonstrates how the above procedure can be hybridized with the addition of biocidal agents to create scaffolds whose degradation is in sync with the release of therapeutic molecules [150].

The ultimate goal in the development of a biodegradable polymer for any biomedicine application is to achieve an optimal concordance between its degradation based on mechanical function and the rate of growth of new tissue. For a bone fixation screw, the polymer must be of sufficient strength at least for the first 6–8 weeks to stabilize the fracture, and gradual weakening thereafter should facilitate augmenting mechanical loading on the healing bone by dynamic stress transfer. For a cardiovascular stent, the polymer must provide radial strength for 3–6 months to resist vessel recoil, and then completely and non-inflammatorily resorb. The degradative products must not be toxic or immunogenic and must be easily metabolized by the natural metabolic routes of the body. Lactic acid and glycolic acid, for example, enter the Krebs cycle and are eventually metabolized to carbon dioxide and water. The extensive literature, as compiled by Meghana et al. and Shu, highlights that “corrosion control” of biodegradable polymers is a challenging engineering endeavor [141, 142]. It requires a deep understanding of polymer chemistry, processing-structure-property relationships, and the complex biological environment of the human body. By precisely controlled manipulation of the selection of monomers, copolymer composition, molecular weight, crystallinity, architecture (e.g., linear, star-shaped, dendritic), and composite formulation, scientists can create an enormous library of materials whose degradation profiles can be precisely engineered to meet the demanding requirements of modern regenerative medicine and interventional devices [151–153].

Strategies for tuning degradation kinetics in biodegradable bone biomaterials

Regulation of degradation kinetics of biodegradable biomaterials is of utmost importance to guarantee their clinical effectiveness in bone regeneration. A successful scaffold should be able to sustain structural integrity long enough for new tissue development but be fully degraded after its purpose is served. For biopolymers (e.g., PLGA, PCL), degradation is mainly controlled through hydrolysis of ester linkages. Their rate of degradation can be reduced by increasing molecular weight and crystallinity because these reduce water penetration and chain mobility. Copolymer content is another important control; thus, an increased lactic acid concentration in PLGA results in a hydrophobic and slower-degrading polymer. Furthermore, incorporation of hydrophobic additives or cross-linking agents can dramatically retard the process of hydrolysis [154, 155].

For bioceramics (e.g., hydroxyapatite, β-TCP, bioactive glasses), degradation is achieved through surface dissolution, highly dependent on solubility, crystallinity, and ionic content. Increased crystallinity and a stoichiometric Ca/P ratio in calcium phosphates drastically reduce their solubility. Lattice doping with ions like Mg²⁺ stabilizes the structure, while increasing the proportion of SiO₂ in the bioactive glasses creates a more stable silicate network that is less amenable to such rapid ion exchange [156–158].

For biometals (e.g., Mg, Fe, Zn alloys), corrosion is an electrochemical process. The principal strategy to extend their life is to minimize galvanic coupling by purifying the alloy to remove cathodic impurities (e.g., Fe, Ni) and introducing elements such as Mn to scavenge these impurities. Alloying with elements that promote the formation of stable, protective surface films—such as Ca, which promotes a calcium phosphate barrier on Mg alloys—also effectively slows corrosion [159–165].

Among all material classes, the surface modification is a strong and general strategy. Deposition of a secondary coating—e.g., a slowly degrading polymer on a metal, a crystalline hydroxyapatite on a high-resorbing β-TCP scaffold, or a PCL shell on a PLGA core—creates a physical barrier that de-couples the early, accelerated degradation stage from the biological environment to provide accurate temporal control over the resorption profile of the material [165–168].

Polyglycolic acid (PGA) is a biodegradable polyester valued for its high mechanical strength and biocompatibility. However, its rapid and uncontrolled hydrolytic degradation—initiated by water penetration into amorphous regions—limits its clinical application. To overcome this problem, a “stereochemical locking” architecture was developed by incorporating polylactic acid stereocomplex (ScPLA) into PGA through melt spinning followed by annealing. As shown in Fig. 5, the ScPLA initially segregates into poly(L-lactic acid) (PLLA) and poly(D-lactic acid) (PDLA) during melt processing and becomes confined within the amorphous domains of PGA. Finally, the concept of “stereochemical locking” provides a versatile platform for tuning the degradation profiles of aliphatic polyesters for applications requiring precise lifetime control [168].

Contextual note for bone tissue engineering: While the stereolocking structure represents an efficient strategy to slow down and stabilize polyester degradation, the effectiveness of the A-P-S-4 composite material in bone load-bearing applications has not yet been experimentally confirmed. Key parameters such as compressive strength under physiological cyclic loading and long-term degradation kinetics at 37 °C in relevant clinical environments require further characterization [168]. Therefore, this case study should be considered as a proof-of-concept for precise degradation control, not a clinically applicable bone scaffold. Future research should focus on translating this stereolocking principle into mechanically optimized osteoconductive composite structures (e.g., incorporating β-TCP or HA fillers) and verifying their performance under simulated physiological loading to determine their feasibility in the load-bearing categories described in Section 8.2.

A practical guide to designing better biodegradable implants

The clinical use of biodegradable biomaterials to bone regeneration has always been less constrained by a lack of viable materials, but more by an inherent incompatibility between the material’s degradation profile and the dynamic, patient-specific physiology of bone healing. The perfect biodegradable implant should be a temporary, active contributor to the cascade of healing—delivering mechanical load, biochemical signals, and a permissive framework for cell invasion—before resorbing in harmony with the development of new bone. To make this possible, there needs to be a paradigm shift: degradation rates need to be addressed not as an intrinsic, unchangeable characteristic of the material, but as a central, programmable design parameter. This review offers a systematic, step-by-step process to the rational design of biodegradable implants that interconnects defect nature, patient physiology, and materials science to achieve this critical harmony [164, 174, 184–188].

Step 1: Defining the biological target—calculating the patient-specific healing timeline

The foundation of any effective bone regeneration plan is an accurate understanding of the biological target: the spatiotemporal pattern of the patient’s bone healing process. This pattern is highly variable and depends on multiple relevant factors, primarily the size and type of defect. Small, stable fractures (spaces <1 mm) typically heal directly through intramembranous bone formation within 6 to 8 weeks [76, 189]. In contrast, critical size defects (CSDs), defined as the smallest intraosseous defects that cannot heal spontaneously during the patient’s lifetime, require a more prolonged and complex regeneration process, typically lasting 12 to 24 weeks or longer [190]. Established criteria define CSD as a bone defect measuring 2 to 2.5 times the diameter of the host bone or involving more than 50% peripheral bone loss [191–193]. This reference time is strongly influenced by individual patient factors. For example, osteogenic and angiogenic potential can be reduced by 40 to 60% in patients with type 2 diabetes, increasing healing time by approximately 1.4 to 1.6 times [194–196]. Advanced age (>65 years) and smoking can similarly delay healing by 30 to 50% due to reduced stem cell capacity and tissue hypoxia [197–200]. Anatomical location also plays a significant role; the mandible heals approximately 30% faster than weight-bearing long bones such as the tibia due to its greater vascularization [137, 201].

To provide an initial and conceptual estimate of the required functional lifespan of biodegradable implants, we propose the following exploratory approach:

Healing Time = (Defect Size in cm × 5 weeks/cm) × (Patient Factor) × (Anatomical Factor)

Conventional explanation: Patient factors and anatomical factors are coefficients, where a value > 1.0 indicates prolonged healing time (e.g., due to comorbidities or complex sites) and a value < 1.0 indicates accelerated healing time (e.g., favorable biological or anatomical structure). The baseline constant of 5 weeks/cm2 is derived from clinical data on healing of tibial fractures in healthy, nonsmoking adults [138, 202]. In addition to the main anatomical locations, regional differences within a single bone can also influence the dynamics of healing: the metaphyseal region typically heals 10–20% faster than the diaphysis due to its richer cancellous bone structure and blood vessels [203, 204].

Example:

Healthy, nonsmoking adult with 3 cm tibial defect:

Baseline = 3 cm × 5 weeks/cm2 = 15 weeks; patient factors = 1.0; anatomical factors (tibia) = 1.0 Therefore, target implant life ≈ 15 weeks.

Patients with type 2 diabetes and the same 3 cm tibial defect:

Baseline = 15 weeks; Patient factors (diabetes, 40%–60% reduction in healing rate) = 1.4–1.6 (mean 1.5); anatomical factors = 1.0 thereupon, target implant life ≈ 22.5 weeks (range: 21–24 weeks).

This modified example emphasizes that factors that influence healing biology require a stent with longer mechanical support, not faster absorption.

Important clinical note: This formula is intended as a conceptual design tool for initial stent development and lifetime estimation. It cannot replace patient-specific clinical assessment, image-based monitoring, or surgeon judgment. Interactions between multiple comorbidities can be nonlinear. Therefore, in vivo validation in relevant preclinical models remains critical before clinical translation.

Step 3: Engineering degradation kinetics to match the healing phases

Upon selecting the material class, its degradation kinetics must be formulated precisely to match the typical phases of bone healing: inflammatory (Days 1–7), soft callus (Weeks 1–3), hard callus (Weeks 3–8), and remodeling (Months 1–12+). For a successful implant, the role it fulfills is different in each phase.For Biodegradable Metals (e.g., Mg alloys): The goal is mechanobiological coupling. During the inflammatory stage, minimal corrosion is required for complete stability. This is achieved through high-purity processing to remove cathodic impurities (Fe, Ni) and alloying with certain elements like Zr to form a stable protective oxide layer. As bone healing progresses into the hard callus, a linear, controllable corrosion rate would be useful to supply increasing load transfer to the developing bone. For biodegradable metals (e.g. magnesium alloys): The aim is to achieve a mechanical-biological bond. During the inflammatory phase, corrosion must be minimized to maintain full stability. This can be achieved by removing cathodic impurities (iron, nickel) through high-purity processing and alloying with elements such as zirconium to form a stable protective oxide layer. As bone healing progresses to the callus stage, a controlled and predictable corrosion rate is required to gradually transfer the load to the growing bone. The addition of zinc (typically 1–1.5 wt%) acts primarily through solid solution strengthening and, depending on the processing conditions, can also form fine Mg-Zn intermetallic phases (e.g. MgZn, Mg₇Zn₃). The electrochemical behavior of these phases relative to the α-Mg matrix depends on their composition and microstructure: finely dispersed nanoscale intermetallic compounds can promote more uniform dissolution by dispersing the galvanic corrosion activity, while coarse or clustered phases may accelerate localized pitting corrosion [116, 118, 119]. Therefore, achieving a uniform corrosion front with zinc addition requires microstructural control, such as rapid solidification or thermomechanical processing, to modify the size and distribution of the intermetallic compounds [130]. The most important fitting parameter is the optimal strain threshold (ϵ₀ ≈ 2–5%), which determines the corrosion rate curve required to maintain the local strain within the bone formation range [184, 188, 211].For Bioceramics (e.g., BCP): Simultaneous resorption is the objective. A scaffold of designed HA/β-TCP ratio (e.g., 70/30) provides a two-phase degradation pattern. The more soluble β-TCP phase dissolves in the soft and hard callus stages, with release of Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻ ions to activate osteoblasts and create microporosity for vascular invasion. The more stable HA matrix provides longer-term mechanical stability during the initial remodeling stage before subsequent resorption. Bioactive ion doping with Sr²⁺ ions can also optimize the process by supporting osteogenesis while controlling the dissolution rate. The most crucial fitting parameter is the degradation rate constant (λ) which must be designed to relate to the rate of new bone formation (B) [208, 212, 213].For biopolymers (e.g., PLGA): The largest problem is managing the autocatalytic effect and acidic byproducts. Typical 50:50 PLGA degrades too rapidly and creates a toxic acidic microenvironment. A more favorable profile is achieved using an 85:15 L-lactide/glycolide ratio that slows hydrolysis and delays autocatalytic core degradation. This may be supplemented with a chitosan overlay that locally buffers the pH. This can be achieved by removing cathodic impurities (iron, nickel) through high-purity processing and alloying with elements such as zirconium to form a stable protective oxide layer. As bone healing progresses to the callus stage, a controlled and predictable corrosion rate is required to gradually transfer the load to the growing bone. The addition of zinc (typically 1–1.5 wt%) acts primarily through solid solution strengthening and, depending on the processing conditions, can also form fine Mg-Zn intermetallic phases (e.g. MgZn, Mg₇Zn₃). The electrochemical behavior of these phases relative to the α-Mg matrix depends on their composition and microstructure: finely dispersed nanoscale intermetallic compounds can promote more uniform dissolution by dispersing the galvanic corrosion activity, while coarse or clustered phases may accelerate localized pitting corrosion [116, 118, 119]. Therefore, achieving a uniform corrosion front with zinc addition requires microstructural control, such as rapid solidification or thermomechanical processing, to modify the size and distribution of the intermetallic compounds [130]. The most important fitting parameter is the optimal strain threshold (ϵ₀ ≈ 2–5%), which determines the corrosion rate curve required to maintain the local strain within the bone formation range [184, 188, 211].

For Bioceramics (e.g., BCP): Simultaneous resorption is the objective. A scaffold of designed HA/β-TCP ratio (e.g., 70/30) provides a two-phase degradation pattern. The more soluble β-TCP phase dissolves in the soft and hard callus stages, with release of Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻ ions to activate osteoblasts and create microporosity for vascular invasion. The more stable HA matrix provides longer-term mechanical stability during the initial remodeling stage before subsequent resorption. Bioactive ion doping with Sr²⁺ ions can also optimize the process by supporting osteogenesis while controlling the dissolution rate. The most crucial fitting parameter is the degradation rate constant (λ) which must be designed to relate to the rate of new bone formation (B) [208, 212, 213].

Step 4: Validation and clinical translation

The final and most critical step is rigorous, biologically relevant validation. Routine in vitro testing in simple saline solutions won’t suffice. A tiered validation approach is required:Phase-Specific In Vitro Testing: Mimic the complex in vivo setting by using media mimicking various healing phases (e.g., inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and acidic pH for the inflammatory phase; osteoclast co-cultures for the remodeling phase) [184, 216].In Vivo Assessment: Use longitudinal micro-CT observation to track the reverse correlation between bone volume and scaffold volume. Conduct biomechanical tests at the key phase transitions to confirm that the implant is loading as desired. Specifically: (a) both curves are normalized to a dimensionless scale of 0–1 (where 1 represents 100% or complete bridging of the initial scaffold mass/volume); (b) the x-axis represents time (weeks) and the y-axis represents the normalized fractional change; (c) the target curve is directly derived from the estimated healing timeline in Section 8.1 and is represented by a mathematical inverse of the ideal resorption curve that maintains mechanical strength before sufficient bone bridge formation; (d) For two-phase or multiphase systems (e.g., composite ceramics or layered polymers), SI can be calculated using an integral phase-specific degradation function or by applying a weighted area calculation that takes into account the unique adsorption kinetics of each component. As a first-order approximation, a higher SI value is interpreted as indicating better temporal synchrony and better clinical outcomes, while a lower value indicates significant asynchrony [218, 219]. The exact mathematical formula and calculation algorithm for deriving SI will be formally proposed and validated in a future dedicated study. At present, SI is proposed as a conceptual design framework, and detailed experimental validation in relevant preclinical models is required before clinical translation.

Otherwise, the framework initiates an iterative redesign loop, returning to material selection or kinetic engineering until optimal synchronization is achieved. Conclusion and future perspectives

The rational design of degradable biomaterials for bone tissue engineering demands a move away from empirical surmise and trial-and-error and towards a precision-guided, model-based strategy. As shown in this review, successful clinical translation hinges on precise coordination between material degradation and the bone healing spatiotemporal history—from mesenchymal condensation to vascularization, matrix deposition, mineralization, and remodeling. This synchronization cannot be achieved through material selection only, yet it does require a combined strategy that unites patient-specific variables (e.g., defect size, comorbidities, anatomical location), material science (e.g., alloying, crystallinity, copolymer composition), and predictive computational modeling (e.g., mechanobiological FE and diffusion-reaction PDE models). Metallic biomaterials for load-bearing applications require distinct degradation engineering strategies: magnesium alloys require rate-degradation and strain-coupled modeling, iron alloys require accelerated degradation through microgalvanic and porous architectures, and zinc alloys require precise microalloying to match the intermediate repair timeline. A unified mechano-biological approach is not sufficient; instead, model selection must be explicitly tailored to the inherent degradation kinetics of each metal and its clinical failure modes. Systematically comparing the degradation of magnesium alloys in vascularized and orthotopic bone implantation models is an important step in translating in vitro and ectopic research findings into reliable clinical bone regeneration strategies. In contrast, polymeric and ceramic scaffolds must be tailored to accommodate biological timescales so that their resorption facilitates—instead of impeding—cell invasion and the formation of new bone. Composites are the most promising by enabling multi-functional, phase-specific degradation profiles. Future advances will concentrate on “smart” biomaterials that actively react to local physiological cues—pH, enzyme activity, or mechanical stress—through stimuli-responsive chemistries or incorporated sensing functionality.

Future research should translate this framework from theoretical design to validated clinical practice. Our model-based approach leads directly to three priority directions: (1) experimentally validate the synchronization index (SI) threshold in large animal bone defect models to establish clinical prediction boundaries; (2) develop an integrated computational workflow that seamlessly connects patient-specific recovery timeline formulas, material class model selection, and SI-based validation to create an integrated, clinician-usable decision support tool; and (3) conduct targeted preclinical validation in high-risk populations (particularly diabetic patients and the elderly) to quantify how patient/anatomical factors alter the potential for impaired osteogenesis and vascularization and lead to adjustments in degradation targets. By combining stimulus-responsive smart materials with patient-specific computational modeling and rigorous in vivo validation, the field can move beyond trial-and-error approaches toward precisely designed absorbable implants that can actively guide and coordinate natural bone regeneration.
