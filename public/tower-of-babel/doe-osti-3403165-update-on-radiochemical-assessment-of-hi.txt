 1. Introduction

This work documents an effort to collect burnup measurements on a high burnup rod, designated 6XV,
and first cycle accident tolerant fuel (ATF) rod, designated 47I, to enable benchmarking of fuel
performance codes and neutronics codes. In addition to measurements, Virtual Environment for Reactor
Applications (VERA) full-core-depletion analysis was also performed for the rods that were
experimentally analyzed to provide an opportunity for code validation. This effort focuses on collecting
data from rods irradiated at Byron Generating Station and shipped to the Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL) hot-cells. This data will also anchor non-destructive examination evaluations of burnup of the
various fuel rods undergoing postirradiation examination (PIE) at ORNL. Previous PIE of these fuel rods
provides some guidance on the burnup trend across the fuel. Axial gamma spectroscopy scans provide a
measure of relative changes in burnup across a fuel pin. Mass spectrometry based burnup measurements
performed for this work at specific axial locations in the fuel are fully quantitative. By combining the
mass spectrometry data with the gamma scans it is possible to more quantitatively evaluate axial
variations in burnup across the entire fuel pin [1]. The combined set of burnup evaluations will be made
available to other organizations that have an interest in high burnup radiochemistry data for validation of
neutronic simulations and source term evaluation such as the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC).

 2. Status

2.1 VERA Simulations

In collaboration with Westinghouse Electric Company (WEC), Constellation, and Veracity Nuclear,
VERA was used to perform full-core depletion analyses for Byron Unit 1 (Cycles 13–16) and Byron Unit
2 (Cycles 13–24). VERA is a state-of-the-art reactor analysis suite of high-fidelity software and methods
capable of simulating the full operating history of a commercial pressurized water reactor (PWR) [2]. It
includes advanced solvers and multiphysics coupling algorithms. VERA calculates fuel rod and coolant
conditions at very small spatial scales, including time-dependent isotopic depletion and decay, burnable
poison depletion, and in-core detector response. VERA can also simulate local impacts of control rod
movement and explicit effects of fuel assembly decay and shuffling during refueling outages. VERA has
been applied to nearly ⅔ of the US commercial PWRs for simulation of over 200 operating fuel cycles
[2-6].

The methods employed by VERA for steady-state core follow analyses are described below. While
VERA has a variety of other capabilities—including fuel rod performance, simulations of CRUD and
corrosion growth in the PWR primary coolant system, and transient reactivity insertion accident
analyses—the most prominent and mature capability in VERA is the simulation of nominal reactor
operation. The dominant components of VERA used in this work are detailed below:

• MPACT: An advanced pin-resolved whole-core multigroup deterministic neutron transport capability
 based on the 2D/1D synthesis method on the frame of a 3D coarse mesh finite difference method, for
 which radial and axial correction factors are obtained from 2D method-of-characteristics (MOC) and
 1D PN, respectively [6]. MPACT uses a 51 energy-group cross section library based on ENDF/B-
 VII.1 data and the subgroup method of on-the-fly resonance self-shielding [7] to obtain accurate
 neutron reaction rates at precise local conditions anywhere in the reactor core. MPACT has been
 extensively validated against a variety of critical experiments and results from continuous-energy
 Monte Carlo–based methods.

• CTF: An advanced subchannel thermal-hydraulics capability using a transient two-fluid, three-field
 (i.e., liquid film, liquid drops, and vapor) modeling approach to determine the thermodynamic

 1

 conditions in every coolant subchannel in the reactor core, including cross-flow effects from turbulent
 mixing and lateral pressure gradients [8]. CTF has been validated against a broad array of single- and
 two-phase tests for the assessment of prediction of void generation, mixing, heat transfer, pressure
 drop, fuel rod behavior, and droplet entrainment in a variety of different geometries.

• ORIGEN: An isotopic depletion and decay code capable of characterizing used fuel (including
 activity, decay heat, radiation emission rates, and radiotoxicity), generating source terms for accident
 analyses, and activating structural materials [9]. The application programming interface enables a
 direct coupling to MPACT for simulation of fuel depletion and decay in millions of unique depletion
 regions in a reactor core. ORIGEN has been widely used and validated over the last four decades as
 part of the SCALE code suite [10]

The primary objective of these VERA simulations is to generate pin-resolved power histories as functions
of time and axial position for every fuel rod that will be examined in the ORNL and Idaho National
Laboratory hot cells. A secondary objective is to produce detailed burnup distributions and isotopic
inventories throughout fuel irradiation.

2.2 Status of Experimental Work

Samples for burnup analysis were collected from complementary samples already being prepared for
other experiments related to on-going collaboration with WEC. Four samples were processed this fiscal
year. Each sample spanned one to two pellets to ensure that any pellet heterogeneity did not bias the
results. A pellet is approximately 10 mm long.
The first cycle ATF samples were cut from the 47I rod that was shipped to the ORNL hot-cell. These
samples were designated 47I-C05 AT2 and 47I-C05 AT3. These samples were located at 902.5 mm to
914.6 mm and 914.6 mm to 926.8 mm above the bottom of the fuel rod which is a relative axial height of
roughly 21% along the fuel stack (x/L~0.21). These samples were originally cut for axial tensile testing to
assess the mechanical properties of the cladding which requires defueling the cladding. For these samples
more careful defueling as described later in this section was applied to collect the dissolved fuel for
burnup evaluation. Based on data provided for original burnup values provided by Westinghouse, the rod
average burnup of this rod is 31.1 MWd/kgU, and gamma scanning of the 47I-C05 AT2 and 47I-C05
AT3 samples suggest that the local burnup of these samples is approximately 34.8 MWd/kgU [1].
Two high burnup samples were evaluated from the 6XV rod that was also shipped to the ORNL hot-cell.
The sample 6XV-C5D spanned from 1193 to 1207 mm from the bottom of the fuel rod (x/L~0.31). The
calculated rod average burnup for this rod is 73.8 MWd/kgU and gamma scanning of the 6XV-C5D
sample area suggests that the local burnup is approximately 76.5 MWd/kgU. The other sample, 6XV-H5,
spanned from 2493 to 2508 mm from the bottom of the fuel rod (x/L~0.65). Gamma scanning of the
sample area suggest that the local burnup is approximately 82.7 MWd/kgU [1]. This location and local
burnup data is summarized in Table 1.

Samples were dissolved in 15.7 M HNO3 (Supelco, Omnitrace) and the flasks were subsequently placed
into an ultrasonic bath overnight to catalyze the dissolution through a cavitation process. Cavitation helps
to displace fuel from the cladding and break down the fuel into easier to dissolve pieces. Once each
dissolution iteration was completed each dissolver was quantitatively transferred into a 250 mL graduated
cylinder (PYREX, Class A TC). The acid dissolution method was performed twice in HNO3 and once in
H2O (Fisher Scientific, Reagent Grade). This generated a final acid concentration of 10-11 M HNO3.
Approximately 10 mL of the dissolvers were poured in duplicate into 15 mL low density polyethylene
bottles and transferred from the hot cells to the ORNL Radioactive Material Analytical Laboratory for
analysis.

The primary evaluation of burnup was determined from actinides and fission product content in the
aliquots via mass spectrometry. The dissolved fuel aliquots were evaluated to determine U, Pu and other
minor actinide content. A Pu separation was performed to clarify mass numbers 238 to 242. The higher
mass fission products (A=139-148) are also evaluated by mass spectrometry with element specific
separations to increase precision and remove isobaric interferences. Lower mass fission products were
also evaluated by scanning the applicable masses in the samples. Specifically, measurements of isotopes
of ruthenium, molybdenum, and strontium, along with Tc-99 were made. Key actinide content (e.g.,
isotopes of uranium and plutonium), high-yield fission product content such as neodymium, and other
fission product yields are combined to calculate burnup directly from data collected on each aliquot.

 3. Results

3.1 Simulation Results

The VERA simulation results will be used to validate the BISON fuel performance code and to support
the continued development of high-burnup and ATF material models within BISON. In addition, the
predicted isotopic inventories will be used to validate ORIGEN depletion and isotopic analyses. When
coupled with post-irradiation burnup chemistry measurements, these data provide a unique opportunity to
validate fuel performance, neutronics, depletion, and isotopic predictions simultaneously, improving
confidence in modeling fuel behavior during reactor operation and throughout the post-irradiation
lifecycle. Finally, the results will support the interpretation and validation of non-destructive examination
techniques for fuel burnup assessment.

Each relevant operating cycle was simulated in VERA, including all associated control rod withdrawals,
stainless steel insertions, and reinsertions. For each Byron cycle, the VERA results were compared
against measured flux maps at every state point, resulting in a total of 21 comparisons with plant data.
The percent error between the VERA results and the measured flux maps is generally higher early in life
due to the depletion of fresh fuel and the associated power redistribution among assemblies. As the cycles
progress, however, the agreement improves, with the error decreasing from approximately 3% to less than
1% for the two-dimensional (2-D) simulations and from approximately 4% to nearly 1% for the three-
dimensional (3-D) simulations. These comparisons indicate that the VERA-predicted power history and
the resulting end-of-life burnup are expected to agree with the measured burnup to within approximately
1%. The VERA-predicted time-dependent rod-average power histories and end-of-life local burnup
distributions as functions of axial location for the 47I and 6XV cases are presented in Figure 1 and Figure
2 respectively. VERA predicts a rod-average burnup of 30.352 MWd/kgU for the 47I rod. The
corresponding local burnup at the axial segments spanning 902.5–914.6 mm (47I-C05 AT2) and 914.6–
926.8 mm (47I-C05 AT3) above the bottom of the rod is 33.199 MWd/kgU. The VERA simulations are
meshed approximately every 8 cm along the rod, and the closest node to the experimentally evaluated
samples is given as the local burnup value. For the 6XV rod, VERA predicts a rod-average burnup of

 3

68.827 MWd/kgU. The corresponding local burnups at the axial segments spanning 1193–1207 mm
(6XV-C5D) and 2493–2508 mm (6XV-H5) are 73.216 MWd/kgU and 75.833 MWd/kgU, respectively.
The sample taken lower in the rod (6XV-C5D) corresponds to a grid spacer location which suppresses the
flux and thus burnup in this area.

The gamma signal data collected previously on these fuel rods is also included in Figure 2. The gamma
signal, which is primarily Cs-137, should be proportional to the burnup at any given location. The trends
in the VERA simulations generally agree with the collected data. It should be noted that the dips in the
47I gamma data are from pellet-pellet interfaces, and the spikes in the 6XV data correspond to Cs-137
moving into pellet-pellet gaps at the end of irradiation. One notable difference between the trend in the
gamma spectrometry and the trend in the VERA calculations is the slight rise in the gamma signal starting
at about 180 cm and continuing to about 325 cm from the bottom of the fuel rod. This indicates burnup in
this area may be slightly higher than predicted by VERA. Alternatively, this signal change may be an
indication of radial Cs migration in the fuel. Mass spectrometry is independent of the gamma
spectrometry and will be a good check of this observation.

3.2 Experimental Results

3.2.1 47I

Mass spectrometry evaluation of the 47I sample provided results for actinides (U, Pu, Np, Am, Cm), and
fission products (Cs, La, Ce, Pr, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd, Sr, Mo, Tc, Ru, and Rh). The results are documented in
RMAL251165 and reproduced in Table 1. The results for the two samples are effectively identical, as
expected, for two samples taken adjacent to one another. Burnup is found by the Fission Product
Monitor-Residual Heavy Atom Technique [11, 12]. In this technique, fission per initial metal atom
(FIMA) is calculated by calculating the number of fissions that have occurred as represented by the
number of fission product atoms divided by their adjusted fission yield over the initial metal atoms which
are assumed to be equivalent to the current heavy metal or actinide content plus the number of fissions.

235 fission and Pu-239 fission. For this work, fission from other isotopes is assumed to be negligible. For
sample 47I the fraction of U-235 vs Pu-239 fissions for the adjusted yield was determined by minimizing
the standard deviation of all burnup estimates as determined by all fission product listed in Table 1 except

3.2.2 6XV

Similar radiochemical and mass spectrometry analysis is also in progress for the 6XV-C5D and 6XV-H5.
The preliminary results are currently being analyzed. Burnup values are not available currently. The
preliminary mass spectrometry does suggest the same relative difference in burnup values between 6XV-
C5D and 6XV-H5 that is also indicated by gamma spectrometry.

 4. Discussion

From the 47I data collected at this time, there is a chance to compare experimentally evaluated burnup
against two different simulations. The first simulations were based on data provided by Westinghouse.
The available power histories and generic PWR design allowed ORNL to run these rods in the ORIGAMI
module of SCALE to produce isotopics given the final burnup Westinghouse provided [14]. This
provided rod average burnup values equivalent to the final burnup provided by Westinghouse power
histories. The rod average burnup was then adjusted to a local axial burnup by the gamma spectrometry to
provide local burnup scaled by gamma scanning. The VERA calculations in this work independently
recreate the pin power history in the reactor utilizing only the operation history of the Byron unit that
contained each rod under investigation. Despite these different techniques the burnup is quite similar.
Initial simulations and gamma spectrometry predict 34.8 MWd/kgU, VERA calculations predict 33.199
MWd/kgU, and experimental evaluations established 33.62 MWd/kgU as the local burnup. The gamma
spectrometry compared to the VERA simulations in Figure 2 suggests that good agreement between local
burnup and simulation is expected across the fuel pin. To help clarify all these different burnup values
Table 2 is provided to compare rod average and local burnup values for all the different techniques. This
table also includes values for the 6XV sample locations.

Digging deeper, it is also possible to compare the isotopics of U and Pu between the experimental results
and VERA calculations for 47I-C05-AT2/3 as seen in Table 3. Again, the agreement is quite good. The
final U-235 content is 1.72% experimentally and 1.71% in VERA. The Pu-239 fraction observed for 47I-
C05-AT2/3 of 62.56 and 64.06 nearly matches the 61.8 predicted by VERA. This agreement indicates
that the VERA simulations are capturing both Pu fission and neutron capture accurately to predict the Pu-
240 this close to the experimental results.

 5. Conclusions

Burnup measurements were collected on two different lead test rods available at the ORNL hot-cells.
Additionally full core fuel simulations were also run in VERA that resulted in the calculation of local
burnup and isotopics. Two adjacent samples were dissolved and evaluated for burnup by mass
spectrometry. The experimental results of the lower burnup fuel sample from pin 47I were compared with
the VERA results, and there was excellent agreement between the measured values and simulations. The
measured burnup was 33.6 MWD/kgU, and the VERA predicted local burnup was 33.2 MWd/kgU.
Beyond burnup the distribution of U and Pu isotopics predicted by VERA also closely matched
measurements. Dissolution of two samples from a high burnup rod, 6XV, was also completed. Mass
spectrometry results from these solutions are currently being analyzed. The VERA predicted rod average
burnup is 68.827, and the local burnup for the regions sampled is 73.216 MWd/kgU and 75.833
MWd/kgU. The lower burnup sample was taken at a grid spacer condition which will be unique data for
validation. Two additional burnup samples will be analyzed from the 6XV rod in the future in
collaboration with a NRC program. Similar burnup and isotopic comparisons will be made for the 6XV
rod as were made for the 47I burnup sample.
