Executive Summary:

Top-Level Accomplishments of the Project: i) i) We successfully produced specimens
made from porcelain resin (Tethon3D) in three different orientations (Z=45mm, Z=4mm and
Z=3mm) without any cracks or delamination, both before and after post-processing. Four-point
flexural tests were conducted on these specimens. Among the three orientations, specimens
printed in the Z=4mm direction showed the best flexural strength, followed by Z=3mm and
Z=45mm. This indicates that print orientation significantly affects mechanical performance. ii)
Multiple lattice geometries—Simple Cubic (SC), Body-Centered Cubic (BCC), Gyroid (G), and
Fluorine (F)—were successfully printed using porcelain resin. Key parameters such as basic/initial
exposure time, UV intensity, and heating temperature were optimized for these lattice structures.
Among these, SC structures with a unit cell size of 6 mm and volume fractions of 15% and 20%
demonstrated compressive strengths of 5.63 MPa and 6.94 MPa, and densities of 0.439 g/cm³
and 0.497 g/cm³, respectively. This trend was similar to those seen in the 4 mm unit cell mullite
which were able to achieve compressive strengths of 5.73 MPa and 6.31 MPa in the 25 vol.% SC
and G structures respectively. However, densities were unacceptably high at 0.962 g/cm3 and
1.082 g/cm3.These values in the porcelain lattices meet or exceed the performance requirements
for replacing syntactic foam components. iii) Outer support subsections were successfully printed
at full scale using the SC lattice with a volume fraction of 20%. These results demonstrate the
potential for replacing traditional syntactic foams with porcelain- lattice structures. These
subsections are intended to be assembled into full-scale components, demonstrating scalability
and design feasibility for future structural applications. iv) We have been printing lattice structures
using SNL’s alumina resin with high quality. Various printing parameters, such as initial/basic
exposure time, heating temperature, and UV intensity, were optimized to be suitable for SNL’s
resin. The printed lattice structures underwent post-processing and were evaluated under a
microscope to assess quality. Density measurements were also performed to evaluate material
performance. These specimens are now fully prepared for compressive strength testing.

Impact of the top-level Accomplishments: This study demonstrates significant progress
toward replacing traditional syntactic foams with porcelain lattice structures. The successful
printing of complex geometries (simple cubic, body centered cubic, gyroid and fluorite)—including
full scale outer support subsections—alongside the optimization of key printing parameters,
highlights both the material’s printability and mechanical potential. Achieving crack-free,
dimensionally stable parts across multiple orientations confirms the reliability of the process.
Additionally, insights into shrinkage and density changes after post-processing provide a strong
foundation for future design and scaling. The successful design, fabrication, and evaluation of
porcelain- lattice structures mark a significant step toward developing lightweight, structurally
efficient alternatives to traditional syntactic foams. These results validate the potential of additively
manufactured, ceramic lattice structures as high-performance materials for advanced structural
applications.

Background and Motivation

Ceramics possess properties including high thermal resistance and stiffness, making them more
suitable for certain applications compared to metals and polymers. Furthermore, using additive
manufacturing (AM) to fabricate ceramics enables opportunities for complex geometries, reduced
assembly, and time savings. Due to material constraints and generally limited research on
ceramic AM, functional applications for additively manufactured ceramic parts are limited. The
purpose of this project is to mature ceramic AM and establish a qualification framework to expand
the applicability of ceramic AM, which could benefit defense and energy applications.

FY25 Status and Major Accomplishments:

Focus #1: Evaluation of physico-chemical properties of sintered products

Sub goal 1-1: Shrinkage and density

The key variables investigated were: 1) shrinkage and density, and 2) mechanical strength in
three different directions. Various cylinders made of porcelain were printed, each measuring 10
mm in diameter and 10 mm in height, with wall thicknesses of 1 mm, 2 mm and 3 mm. Post-
sintering observations revealed that most of the samples with wall thicknesses of 1 mm, 2 mm
showed no delamination or breakage, allowing these thicknesses to be applied to lattice
structures (Figure 1). For wall thicknesses of 3 mm, some delamination was observed under
microscopic analysis. The thermal profile suggested by the vendor [1] was applied to all samples
during post-processing (1) 20 – 150 °C, 25 °C/hr, and hold for 1 hr; 2) 150 – 600 °C, 25 °C/hr; 3)
600 – 1100 °C, 112 °C/hr; 4) 1100 °C to 1200 °C, 130 °C/hr; 5) Cool down naturally.

of the sintered cylindrical samples.

Table 1 shows the density and shrinkage results for cylindrical parts printed with porcelain resin
at wall thicknesses of 1 mm, 2 mm and 3 mm. The density of sintered cylinders with wall
thicknesses of 1 mm, 2 mm, and 3 mm was measured using a pycnometer. The shrinkage
analysis was conducted to assess linear shrinkage in the XY and Z directions. Shrinkage in the
XY direction for cylinders with wall thickness of 1 mm, 2 mm and 3 mm was 4.4%, 4.0% and 3.2%.
Shrinkage in the Z direction was 6.4%, 6.0% and 6.3%, respectively.

Sub goal 1-2: Mechanical test (Flexural strength)

Four-point testing evaluated the effect of print orientation on the flexural strength of sintered
porcelain specimens. Rectangular specimens were designed to have final dimensions of ~3 mm
in thickness, ~4 mm in width, and ~45 mm in a length after the sintering process, based on the
calculated liner shrinkage equation. These final dimensions conform to ASTM C1161. Thermal
profiles for the debinding and sintering processes are detailed in Figure 2e. Porcelain resins are
optimized for printing parameters to prepare specimens for four-point bend testing in the ZY
(Z=45), XZ (Z=4), and YX (Z=3) directions. Specimens printed in these three different orientations
were sintered (Figure 2a-c). All these specimens Z=45, Z=4 and Z=3 direction were able to be
post-processed and handled without cracks and delamination, allowing for flexural strength
testing. The pore size range of sintered specimens is 20-57 µm in the Z=45 direction, 25-35 µm
in the Z=4 direction and 30-34 µm in the Z=3 direction.

 5

For the flexural strength test, ASTM Standard C1161 B-bar, which is specific to flexural testing of
ceramics, was followed for the testing procedure. A ceramic flexural strength test fixture was used
in conjunction with a universal testing machine. A fully articulated four-point test fixture was
employed to accommodate any twisting or lack of flatness in the test specimen. The results of the
four-point bend testing of the sintered specimens are presented in Figure 2f and Table 2. The
average flexural strength of sintered specimens for the Z=45, Z=4 and Z=3 orientations are 4.15
MPa, 31.69 MPa and 28.45 MPa, respectively (Table 2). The samples printed at Z=4 and Z=3
exhibited much higher flexural strength than those at Z = 45, highlighting the influence of printing
orientation on mechanical performance. In addition, for the flexural tests, the number of
specimens (10 ≤ n ≤ 30) typically were observed after the sintering process. Figure 2f and Table
2 showed that the Weibull modules for the specimens in Z=45, Z=4 and Z=3 orientations were
relatively high, indicating low data variability.

Focus #2: Evaluation of the compressive strength of various lattice structures.

Sub goal 2-1: Printing Lattice Structures

Porcelain SC, BCC, G and F lattice structures at 15, 20 and 25 vol.% with unit cell dimensions of
4 mm, 5 mm, and 6 mm were successfully printed using optimized printing parameters—
basic/initial exposure times of 280/320 seconds, a heating temperature of 43 °C, and a light
intensity of 180 cd/m²—and were subsequently subjected to sintering (Figure 3-5). The size
(x/y/z), strut thickness, and volume of the green bodies were found to be accurate when compared
with the STL models. The high dimensional accuracy and free-defect nature of the printed

 6

samples ensured their integrity during subsequent debinding and sintering processes. The
thermal profile followed the vendor’s recommendations [1]:
 (1) Heat from 20 °C to 150 °C at 25 °C/hr and hold for 1 h;
    (2) Heat from 150 °C to 600 °C at 25 °C/hr;
    (3) Heat from 600 °C to 1100 °C at 112 °C/hr;
    (4) Heat from 1100 °C to 1200 °C at 130 °C/hr;
 (5) Cool down naturally.

Mullite SC, BCC, G, and F lattice structures were printed at 15, 20, and 25 vol.% infill, however
we were only able to print the 5 mm unit cells before all the commercial mullite resin was
exhausted. Attempts by the vendor to reproduce more of this resin system were unsuccessful
with viscosities so high there was no flowability even at the maximum temperature the resin tank
could be brought to. The printable resin used the vendor recommendations of basic/initial
exposure times 95/130 second at ~2.5 mW/cm2 and a tank temperature of 30 °C. Size, strut
thickness, and volume were accurate to the initial model files post-firing, though the larger
dimensional shrinkages (Table 1) seen in the mullite did lead to a higher degree of firing
deformation. Thermal profiles for debinding and firing were determined in FY24 from TGA and
densification analysis of the printed material as the vendor did not provide these for this material:
 (1) Heat from 20 °C to 75 °C at 5 °C/hr, hold for 2 hr;
 (2) Heat from 75 °C to 105 °C at 5 °C/hr, hold for 2 hr;
    (3) Heat from 105 °C to 300 °C at 15 °C/hr, hold 6 hr;
    (4) Heat from 300 °C to 500 °C at 10 °C/hr, hold 1 hr;
    (5) Heat from 500 °C to 650 °C at 25 °C/hr, hold 1 hr,
    (6) Cool from 650 °C to 20 °C at 240 °C/hr,
    (7) Heat from 20 °C to 1600 °C at 300 °C/hr, hold 2 hr,
    (8) Cool from 1600 °C to 20 °C at 180 °C/hr,

The sintered lattice structures exhibited anisotropic shrinkage, with about 4% reduction in the X
and Y directions and approximately 5-6% along the Z direction, likely due to the layer-by-layer
printing process and directional heat flow during sintering.

Sub goal 2-2: Mechanical test and Density

Following post-processing (debinding and sintering), the lattice structures remained intact, with
no signs of delamination or cracking, allowing compressive strength and density measurements
to be conducted. To evaluate the mechanical properties, compressive strength testing was
performed in accordance with ASTM D695-10 [3] at a testing rate of 1.3 mm/min.

As shown in Figure 6 and Table 3, all lattice structures with unit cell sizes of 4 mm, 5 mm, or 6
mm showed that the SC and G lattice structures have higher compressive strengths than the BCC
and F structures. For example, lattice structures (SC, BCC, G, and F) printed using porcelain
(printed size: 1.8 × 2.0 × 1.8 cm³; unit cell size: 6 mm; VF: 15%, 20%, and 25%) demonstrated
the following compressive strengths:
 • SC and G structures at 15%, 20%, and 25% volume fractions showed compressive
         strengths of 5.63 MPa and 3.25 MPa; 6.94 MPa and 4.41 MPa; and 10.94 MPa and 5.78
 MPa, respectively.
 • In comparison, the BCC and F structures exhibited lower compressive strengths of 0.35
 MPa and 0.55 MPa; 0.47 MPa and 1.12 MPa; and 0.84 MPa and 2.11 MPa, respectively.

 8

Among all printed samples, the SC structures with a 6 mm unit cell and volume fractions of 15%
and 20% demonstrated compressive strengths of 5.63 MPa and 6.94 MPa, with corresponding
structural densities of 0.439 g/cm³ and 0.497 g/cm³. These results indicate that the porcelain SC
samples (printed size: 1.8 × 2.0 × 1.8 cm³; unit cell size: 6 mm; VF: 15% and 20%) satisfy the
production acceptance criteria for replacing the syntactic foam component. While mullite results
at the 5 mm cell size did exhibit some that looked promising in regards to mechanical properties,
include SC (25 vol.%: 5.73 MPa) and G (15 vol.%: 4.12 MPa, 20 vol.%: 5.13 MPa, and 25 vol.%:
6.31 MPa) the structural density of the lattices proved to be unacceptably high between 0.70 and
1.08 g/cm3 due to the higher fired bulk densities around 2.6 g/cm3. For lightweight structural
supports the required lattice volume fraction would need to be below what printable in this
material.

Figure 7 presents the effect of volume fraction on the density and compressive strength of
porcelain lattice structures (SC, BCC, G, and F) printed with unit cell sizes of 4 mm, 5 mm, and 6
mm. An increase in volume fraction from 15% to 25% results in a consistent rise in density across

all lattice types and unit cell sizes, indicating a higher material content within the structure.
Correspondingly, the compressive strength of the lattice structures also improves with increasing
volume fraction, which can be attributed to enhanced load-bearing capacity due to the denser
architecture. This trend is observed consistently across all unit cell dimensions, highlighting the
strong dependence of mechanical performance on volume fraction, regardless of the geometrical
scale of the unit cell. In general, SC and G structures exhibit superior compressive strength
compared to BCC and F, due to efficient vertical load-bearing struts in SC and well-distributed
load paths in G [4,5].

Focus #3: Printing outer support

Outer support subsections have been successfully printed in porcelain and mullite using a simple
cubic (SC) lattice structure with a volume fraction of 20%, demonstrating potential as
replacements for the syntactic foam component (Figure 8). Due to build volume limitations of the
Bison printer, the outer support subsections were divided into two parts and printed separately.
Nevertheless, the successful fabrication of these subsections highlights their potential to replace
traditional outer support structures.

Focus #4: Optimizing printing parameters for SNL’s alumina resin

SNL BCC, G and F structures (VF: 25 vol.%, unit cell size: 6 mm) have been successfully printed
(Figure 9a) using the printing parameters shown in Figure 9c. The dimensions (X, Y, Z) and strut
thickness of the green bodies closely matched those of the STL models, as shown in Figure 10
and Table 4. This dimensional accuracy, along with the absence of cracks or delamination after
printing, enabled successful post-processing, including debinding and sintering.

After sintering, no delamination was observed; however, some minor cracks were detected
(Figure 9 b), likely due to internal stresses generated during the thermal cycle. The sintered lattice
structures exhibited anisotropic shrinkage, with approximately 13–14% shrinkage in the X and Y
directions, and about 20% in the Z direction. This higher shrinkage along the Z-axis can be
attributed to the layer-by-layer nature of the printing process causing intralayer density gradients
due to settling of the suspended ceramic particles which can be exacerbated in VPP printers
without active resin mixing such as the Bison1000..

Regarding to SNL SC structures (VF: 25 vol.%, unit cell size: 6 mm), they are partially broken at
the top due to insufficient resin. However, based on these samples, we believe it is feasible to
successfully print SC structures with adequate resin supply.

TRL Status and Anticipated Progression:
FY25 is projected to complete TRL 3 and advance to TRL 4 as current challenges in AM foam
replacing syntactic foams are addressed. This advancement will be demonstrated by an
evaluation of the mechanical properties of various lattice structures and the achievement of target
property values.

Challenges, Issues, Risks, and Opportunities:
During the FY24 project, our efforts were focused on developing component prototypes and
conducting proof-of-concept testing using various lattice structures with different volume fractions
and unit cell sizes. These configurations were evaluated to achieve the compressive strength and
density required to meet production acceptance criteria. However, we encountered limitations in
printing the outer support at full scale due to the current DLP printer’s build plate size (X = 60 mm,
Y = 110 mm), which restricts the maximum printable part dimensions. To address this constraint,
we plan to print using alternative printers with larger build volumes (Stratasys Neo 450 and Raplas
PR 450), which are available in MPA division. These printers will enable the printing of full-size
outer support structures without the need for part segmentation and assembly, streamlining the
process and improving structural integrity in future prototypes.
Additionally, due to the discontinuation of mullite resin from the commercial vendor, we were
unable to complete the printability study for mullite lattice structures across all volume fractions
and unit cell configurations, as was done with porcelain. This highlights the need for internal
development of resin/ceramic systems to reduce reliance on external proprietary vendor
formulations, define performance requirements for any production applications, and facilitate
cross site collaboration and development efforts.

Conclusions and Summary:
This study focused on evaluating the additive manufacturability and functional properties of
porcelain- lattice structures aimed at replacing traditional syntactic foam components in structural
applications.
The shrinkage behavior and density changes after post-processing, using a thermal profile
recommended by the material vendor and in-house developed profiles, were investigated using
sintered cylindrical samples with varying wall thicknesses. Shrinkage of approximately 4% was
observed in the X and Y directions, and 5–6% in the Z direction. These insights are essential for
optimizing wall thickness and unit cell design to ensure dimensional accuracy and minimize
defects in the final components.
Specimens were printed in three different orientations—ZY (Z=45), XZ (Z=4) and YX (Z=3)
direction —to evaluate the influence of build direction on mechanical properties. Among these,
the Z=4 orientation demonstrated the highest flexural strength, indicating that mechanical
performance is significantly affected by printing orientation. The average flexural strength of
porcelain in the Z=4 orientation was 12.56 MPa, compared to 9.22 MPa in Z=3, and 7.36 MPa in
Z=45.
Various lattice types, including simple cubic (SC), body-centered cubic (BCC), gyroid (G), and
fluorine (F) design, were successfully printed using porcelain- material. Compressive strength
testing showed that SC lattice structures with a unit cell size of 6 mm and volume fractions of 15%
and 20% achieved compressive strengths 5.63 MPa and 6.94 MPa, respectively, with
corresponding densities of 0.439 g/cm³ and 0.497 g/cm³. These results meet the criteria for
replacing existing syntactic foam components and demonstrate proof-of-concept viability. It
indicates that carefully optimized lattice structures can achieve both high mechanical performance
and lightweight characteristics. The results also highlight the critical relationship between volume
fraction, unit cell geometry, and material behavior under compression.
Furthermore, full scale outer support subsections made from both porcelain- and mullite -
materials were successfully printed using SC lattices with a 20% volume fraction. Half sections

 13

were assembled to form larger structures, validating the feasibility of scalable lattice-based
designs.
In addition, we optimized various printing parameters for SNL’s alumina resin and successfully
printed (BCC, G and F) lattice structures. Shrinkage analysis post-thermal processing revealed
dimensional reductions of approximately 13–14% in the X and Y directions, and about 20% in the
Z direction.
In conclusion, this study demonstrates the successful design, printing, and evaluation of
porcelain- lattice structures, with a particular focus on full-scale outer support subsections. The
findings establish essential groundwork for applying these materials in structural applications and
offer valuable parameters to guide further optimization and the development of functional
prototype components.
