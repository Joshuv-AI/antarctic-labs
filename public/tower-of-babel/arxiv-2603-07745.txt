Gleason’s theorem made simple: a Bloch-space perspective
Massimiliano Sassoli de Bianchi

Abstract
Gleason’s theorem is often cited as establishing the Born rule from the structure of Hilbert space, yet its original proof is mathematically sophisticated and rarely accessible to physicists. In this article we present a simple route to the essence of Gleason’s result, using the generalized Bloch representation of quantum states. We show explicitly why non-Born probability rules exist for two-dimensional systems, and why they become impossible in dimension three and higher. Our argument does not reproduce Gleason’s full mathematical theorem, but it clarifies why the Born rule is unavoidable in higher dimension and why qubits are truly exceptional.



Keywords
: Gleason’s theorem; Bloch representation; Born rule; Qubit

1
Introduction

The Born rule occupies a central place in quantum mechanics. It provides the link between the mathematical formalism of Hilbert space and experimental probabilities, assigning to a state
D
and a projector
P
the probability
{\rm Tr}(DP)
. In most standard presentations, however, Born’s rule is introduced as an independent postulate, although this can be justified by the structure of Hilbert space itself. This is precisely the task accomplished by Gleason’s theorem
[
1
]
, which shows that, for Hilbert spaces of dimension three or higher, any probability measure defined on the lattice of projection operators must necessarily be representable by a density operator via Born’s rule. In other words, under very general and natural assumptions, the Hilbert space formalism forces the Born rule.

Despite its conceptual importance, Gleason’s theorem is rarely taught in physics courses. This is because the original proof is technically demanding and relies on subtle functional-analytic arguments. As a result, many physicists know the theorem’s statement but not the mechanism that makes it work. In particular, it is often unclear why dimension two is special and why the restriction to dimension
N\geq 3
is essential.

The purpose of this article is not to reproduce Gleason’s proof, but to illuminate its core idea in a transparent way. Using the generalized Bloch representation of quantum states, we reformulate probability assignments in terms of vectors and simplices embedded in a real Euclidean space. This geometric viewpoint makes the exceptional role of two-dimensional systems immediately visible. For qubits, one can construct infinitely many non-Born probability rules that depend only on the state and the measurement outcome and still satisfy the basic normalization and orthogonality requirements. By contrast, when the Hilbert space dimension is three or higher, the geometry of the generalized Bloch space imposes additional constraints that force the probability rule to be linear, thereby recovering the Born form.

The argument presented here relies only on standard linear algebra. It does not replace Gleason’s theorem; rather, it provides an intuitive explanation of why the theorem must be true and why qubits are truly exceptional. In this sense, the goal of the present work is pedagogical: to make the essence of Gleason’s theorem fully visible.

2
Pauli matrices

We start by recalling that given an arbitrary
2\times 2
matrix

D=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}D_{11}&D_{12}\\
D_{21}&D_{22}\end{array}\right]

(1)

we can always write it as a linear combination of the identity matrix

\mathbb{I}
and the three Pauli spin matrices:

\mathbb{I}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right],\quad\sigma_{1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right],\quad\sigma_{2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\\
i&0\end{array}\right],\quad\sigma_{3}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right]

(2)

Indeed, one can easily check that:

D={1\over 2}\left[(D_{11}+D_{22})\mathbb{I}+(D_{12}+D_{21})\sigma_{1}+i(D_{12}-D_{21})\sigma_{2}+(D_{11}-D_{22})\sigma_{3}\right]

(3)

In other words, every
2\times 2
matrix
D
can be written in the form:

D={1\over 2}\left(r_{0}\mathbb{I}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}r_{i}\sigma_{i}\right)={1\over 2}\left(r_{0}\mathbb{I}+{\bf r}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\right)

(4)

where
r_{0}=D_{11}+D_{22}
,
r_{1}=D_{12}+D_{21}
,
r_{2}=i(D_{12}-D_{21})
, and
r_{3}=D_{11}-D_{22}
. Pauli matrices have the following remarkable properties:

{\rm det}\,\sigma_{i}=-1,\quad{\rm Tr}\,\sigma_{i}=0,\quad{\rm Tr}(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{k})=2\delta_{ik}

(5)

Taking the trace of
D
and
D\sigma_{k}
, we thus obtain

{\rm Tr}D=r_{0},\quad{\rm Tr}(D\sigma_{k})={1\over 2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}r_{i}{\rm Tr}(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}r_{i}\delta_{ik}=r_{k}

(6)

Pauli matrices being self-adjoint, it follows that
D^{\dagger}={1\over 2}(r_{0}^{*}\mathbb{I}+{\bf r}^{*}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$})
, hence
D=D^{\dagger}
requires the coefficient
r_{0}
and the vector
{\bf r}
to be real. If
D
is also of unit trace, we have
r_{0}=1
, and (
4
) becomes (we now explicitly state the dependence on the vector
r
):

D({\bf r})={1\over 2}\left(\mathbb{I}+{\bf r}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\right)

(7)

The above characterizes a density operator if we also have
\|{\bf r}\|\leq 1
, as this condition is sufficient to ensure that the eigenvalues of
D
are positive, hence that
D
is positive. Also, if
\|{\bf r}\|=1
, the density operator corresponds to a one-dimensional projection operator, i.e., it is representative of a pure state. This means that all vectors lying on the surface of the ball
B_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
are representative of pure states, and all vectors inside of it are genuine density operators, i.e., non-extremal convex combinations of one-dimensional projection operators. This bijection, between the density operators
D({\bf r})
and the points of
B_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, is known as the Bloch geometrical representation of the state space of a two-dimensional quantum system (qubit), and is an expression of the well-known
SU(2)
-
SO(3)
two-to-one homomorphism.

3
Non-Born probabilities

Gleason’s theorem states that the Born rule can be derived in the standard quantum formalism by assuming that probabilities depend only on the projectors and are additive on mutually orthogonal projectors. However, Gleason’s theorem does not hold for two-dimensional Hilbert spaces, and it is easy to show that, for dimension
N=2
, infinitely many probability measures can be defined.

There appears to be some confusion about why Gleason’s theorem does not apply to two-dimensional Hilbert spaces. This confusion is reinforced by the existence of a version of Gleason’s theorem derived by Busch, which uses effects
E
rather than projections
P
, and also applies to qubits
[
2
]
(an effect is a positive operator
E
satisfying
0\leq E\leq\mathbb{I}
; in generalized measurements, or POVMs, measurement outcomes are represented by such operators rather than by projection operators). Furthermore, according to De Zela, the reason Gleason’s original proof fails in the two-dimensional case is that the additivity assumption is not sufficient to derive the continuity of the probability measure, and that discontinuous non-Born measures can therefore exist
[
3
]
. Although De Zela proposes a Gleason-type theorem for two-dimensional systems based on a theorem by Gudder
[
4
]
, the latter requires not only continuity but also the property of orthogonal additivity, which is significantly stronger than the additivity condition imposed on projection-valued measures. Indeed, as we are now going show, there exist infinitely many continuous non-Born probability measures in a two-dimensional Hilbert space.

Let
\{|a_{1}\rangle,|a_{2}\rangle\}
be the basis of eigenvector-states associated with an arbitrary observable
A=a_{1}P_{a_{1}}+a_{2}P_{a_{2}}
. According to (
7
), the two projections
P_{a_{1}}=|a_{1}\rangle\langle a_{1}|
and
P_{a_{2}}=|a_{2}\rangle\langle a_{2}|
can be associated, respectively, to the unit vectors
{\bf n}_{1}
and
{\bf n}_{2}
in
B_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
:

P_{a_{1}}={1\over 2}\left(\mathbb{I}+{\bf n}_{1}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\right),\quad P_{a_{2}}={1\over 2}\left(\mathbb{I}+{\bf n}_{2}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\right)

(8)

Taking into account (
5
), the Born rule then tells us that the transition probability
{\cal P}_{\rm Born}({\bf r}\to{\bf n}_{i})
, from a density operator
D({\bf r})
to an eigenstate
P_{a_{i}}
, is given by the trace:

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[D({\bf r})P_{a_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 4}\left[{\rm Tr}\,\mathbb{I}+{\rm Tr}({\bf r}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\,\,{\bf n}_{i}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$})\right]={1\over 4}\left[2+\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}r_{j}[n_{i}]_{k}{\rm Tr}\,(\sigma_{j}\sigma_{k})\right]

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\left(1+{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{i}\right)

Note that
P_{a_{1}}P_{a_{2}}=0
implies
{\rm Tr}\,(P_{a_{1}}P_{a_{2}})=0
, and from (
9
) we deduce that
{\bf n}_{1}\cdot{\bf n}_{2}=-1
, i.e., that the two eigenvectors point in opposite directions in
B_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

To define generalized probability measures, let
f:[-1,1]\to[-1,1]
be any real-valued odd function, i.e.,
f(-x)=-f(x)
, so that
f(0)=0
, and let us also assume that
f(1)=1
. Examples are
f(x)=x^{2n+1}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Using functions of this kind (which may or may not be continuous), we can define the following non-Born transition probabilities:

{\cal P}_{f}({\bf r}\to{\bf n}_{i})={1\over 2}\left[1+f({\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{i})\right]

(10)

Indeed, from
f(1)=1
we obtain reflexivity:
{\cal P}_{f}({\bf n}_{i}\to{\bf n}_{i})=1
,
i=1,2
, and
f
odd guarantees that
{\cal P}_{f}({\bf n}_{1}\to{\bf n}_{2})={\cal P}_{f}({\bf n}_{2}\to{\bf n}_{1})=0
. Since
f({\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{1})=f(-{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{2})=-f({\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{2})
, we also have additivity and normalization:
{\cal P}_{f}({\bf r}\to{\bf n}_{1})+{\cal P}_{f}({\bf r}\to{\bf n}_{2})=1
. In other words
{\cal P}_{f}
defines a bona fide probability measure and the structure of a two-dimensional Hilbert space cannot force the Born rule, even if we impose continuity.

4
Generalized Pauli matrices

For dimension
N\geq 3
, it is possible to generalize the previous construction. Instead of a three-dimensional unit Bloch sphere, completely filled with states, we now have a convex region of states contained in a unit ball
B_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N^{2}-1})
. To explain why the ball becomes
(N^{2}-1)
-dimensional, we can observe that a density operator is a
N\times N
complex matrix. Being self-adjoint, the
N
elements forming its diagonal are real numbers. These numbers must also sum up to
1
, so we only need
N-1
real parameters to specify the diagonal elements. Regarding the off-diagonal ones, the upper ones being the complex conjugate of the lower ones, we only have to determine
{1\over 2}(N^{2}-N)
complex numbers, i.e.,
N^{2}-N
real numbers. Thus, the total number of real parameters needed to specify a general density operator
D
is:
N^{2}-N+(N-1)=N^{2}-1
.

More precisely, we now need a set of
N^{2}
matrices,
\{{\mathbb{I}},\Lambda_{1},\dots,\Lambda_{N^{2}-1}\}
, forming an
orthogonal basis
for all linear operators acting on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
. We can ask them to be self-adjoint, traceless and orthogonal, i.e., to generalize the properties of the Pauli matrices. More precisely, for all
i,j=1,\dots,N^{2}-1
, we require:

\Lambda_{i}=\Lambda_{i}^{\dagger},\quad{\rm Tr}\,\Lambda_{i}=0,\quad{\rm Tr}\,\Lambda_{i}\Lambda_{j}=2\delta_{ij}

(11)

Given an orthonormal basis
\{|b_{1}\rangle,\dots,|b_{N}\rangle\}
, a possible construction of these
N^{2}-1
generalized Pauli matrices is the following
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
:

\displaystyle\{\Lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N^{2}-1}=\{U_{jk},V_{jk},W_{\ell}\},\quad 1\leq j<k\leq N,\quad 1\leq\ell\leq N-1

\displaystyle U_{jk}=|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{k}|+|b_{k}\rangle\langle b_{j}|,\quad V_{jk}=-i(|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{k}|-|b_{k}\rangle\langle b_{j}|)

\displaystyle W_{\ell}=-\sqrt{2\over\ell(\ell+1)}\left(\ell|b_{\ell+1}\rangle\langle b_{\ell+1}|-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{j}|\right)

(12)

Any density operator
D
can then be written as the linear combination:

D({\bf r})={1\over N}\left(\mathbb{I}+c_{N}\,{\bf r}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}\right)={1\over N}\left[\mathbb{I}+\sqrt{\tfrac{N(N-1)}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{2}-1}r_{i}\Lambda_{i}\right]

(13)

where all the components
r_{i}
of the
(N^{2}-1)
-dimensional vector
{\bf r}
are real numbers, so that
D({\bf r})
is manifestly self-adjoint, and considering that the
\Lambda_{i}
obey (
11
), we also have

{\rm Tr}\,D({\bf r})=1,\quad\sqrt{N\over 2(N-1)}{\rm Tr}\left[D({\bf r})\Lambda_{i}\right]=r_{i}

(14)

To guarantee that an operator written as in (
13
) is a density operator, it must also be positive semidefinite, but this will not anymore be the case for any choice of a vector
{\bf r}\in B_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N^{2}-1})
. This is why only a convex portion of the unit ball is now filled with states; see
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
for more details.

5
Forcing the Born rule

Let us now derive the Born transition probabilities, generalizing formula (
9
) to the
N\geq 3
case. Subsequently, we will try to generalize it by introducing a real-valued function
f
, as we did in (
10
) for the
N=2
case. Let
\{|a_{1}\rangle,\dots,|a_{N}\rangle\}
be the basis of eigenvector-states associated with an arbitrary observable
A=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}P_{a_{i}}
,
P_{a_{i}}=|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|
,
i=1,\dots,N
. To each of the
P_{a_{i}}
, we can associate a unit vector
{\bf n}_{i}\in B_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}-1})
, such that

P_{a_{i}}={1\over N}\left(\mathbb{I}+c_{N}\,{\bf n}_{i}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}\right)

(15)

Taking into account (
11
), the Born probabilities are given by:

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[D({\bf r})P_{a_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over N^{2}}\left[{\rm Tr}\,\mathbb{I}+c_{N}^{2}\,{\rm Tr}\left({\bf r}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}\,\,{\bf n}_{i}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}\right)\right]={1\over N^{2}}\left[N+c_{N}^{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{N^{2}-1}r_{j}[n_{i}]_{k}{\rm Tr}\left(\Lambda_{j}\Lambda_{k}\right)\right]

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over N}\left[1+(N-1)\,{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{i}\right]

Since all the projections
\{P_{a_{1}},\dots,P_{a_{N}}\}
are mutually orthogonal, if
{\bf r}={\bf n}_{j}
, we have
D({\bf r})=P_{a_{j}}
,
{\rm Tr}(P_{a_{j}}P_{a_{i}})=\delta_{ji}
, and from (
16
) we find

{\bf n}_{i}\cdot{\bf n}_{j}=-{1\over N-1}+\delta_{ij}{N\over N-1}

(17)

This means that the angle between
{\bf n}_{i}
and
{\bf n}_{j}
,
i\neq j
, is
\theta_{N}=\cos^{-1}(-{1\over N-1})
, and that the
{\bf n}_{i}
form the
N
vertex-vectors of a
(N-1)
-simplex
\triangle_{N-1}
, whose center coincides with the center of the unit ball
B_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}-1})
in which it is inscribed.

A simpler expression for the Born transition probabilities (
16
) can be derived by writing
{\bf r}
as the sum
{\bf r}={\bf r}^{\perp}+{\bf r}^{\parallel}
, where
{\bf r}^{\parallel}
is the vector obtained by orthogonally projecting
{\bf r}
onto the
(N-1)
-dimensional subspace generated by
\triangle_{N-1}
. Since
{\bf r}^{\perp}\cdot{\bf n}_{i}=0
, (
16
) becomes:

{\rm Tr}\left[D({\bf r})P_{a_{i}}\right]={1\over N}\left[1+(N-1)\,{\bf r}^{\parallel}\cdot{\bf n}_{i}\right]

(18)

Writing
{\bf r}^{\parallel}
as a combination of the vertex vectors

{\bf r}^{\parallel}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}r^{\parallel}_{i}\,{\bf n}_{i},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\bf n}_{i}=0,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}r^{\parallel}_{i}=1,\quad r_{i}\geq 0

(19)

then using (
17
), we obtain

{\bf r}^{\parallel}\cdot{\bf n}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}r^{\parallel}_{j}\,{\bf n}_{j}\cdot{\bf n}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}r^{\parallel}_{j}\left(-{1\over N-1}+\delta_{ij}{N\over N-1}\right)

(20)

Inserting (
20
) into (
18
), the using (
19
), the Born transition probabilities become:

{\rm Tr}\left[D({\bf r})P_{a_{i}}\right]=r^{\parallel}_{i}

(21)

Let us now apply the same strategy as in the
N=2
case, trying to generalize the Born rule (
16
) by introducing an auxiliary real function
f
. So, we tentatively introduce the generalized transition probabilities

{\cal P}_{f}({\bf r}\to{\bf n}_{i})=\frac{1}{N}\Big[1+(N-1)f({\bf r}\!\cdot\!{\bf n}_{i})\Big]

(22)

The condition
0\leq{\cal P}_{f}\leq 1
implies that
f
is a bounded function such that

-\frac{1}{N-1}\leq f(x)\leq 1,\qquad x\in\Big[-\frac{1}{N-1},1\Big]

(23)

Reflexivity imposes the condition
f(1)=1
, and additivity (plus normalization) requires
\sum_{i=1}^{N}f({\bf r}\!\cdot\!{\bf n}_{i})=0
. Using (
18
) and (
21
), this becomes the functional constraint

\sum_{i=1}^{N}f\!\left(\frac{Nr_{i}^{\parallel}-1}{N-1}\right)=0

(24)

Choosing
r_{i}^{\parallel}=\frac{1}{N}
,
i=1,\dots,N
(corresponding to a vector at the center of the simplex), the constraint (
24
) becomes
Nf(0)=0
, implying
f(0)=0
. Taking instead
r_{1}^{\parallel}=a
,
r_{2}^{\parallel}=b
,
r_{3}^{\parallel}=1-a-b
,
r_{i}^{\parallel}=0
for
i\geq 4
, with
a,b\geq 0
and
a+b\leq 1
(corresponding to a vector belonging to a two-dimensional face of the simplex), (
24
) becomes

f\!\left(\frac{Na-1}{N-1}\right)+f\!\left(\frac{Nb-1}{N-1}\right)+f\!\left(\frac{N(1-a-b)-1}{N-1}\right)+(N-3)f\!\left(-\frac{1}{N-1}\right)=0

(25)

Setting
a=1
in (
25
), we find
f(-\frac{1}{N-1})=-\frac{1}{N-1}
. It is useful at this point to introduce the variables

x=\frac{Na-1}{N-1},\quad y=\frac{Nb-1}{N-1}

(26)

Eq. (
25
) then becomes

f(x)+f(y)+f(\alpha-x-y)=\alpha,\qquad\alpha=\frac{N-3}{N-1}

(27)

Note that
a,b\in[0,1]
is equivalent to
x,y\in[-{1\over N-1},1]
. Also,
a+b\in[0,1]
is equivalent to
x+y\in[-{2\over N-1},{N-2\over N-1}]
. Assume now for simplicity that
f
is differentiable. Differentiating (
27
) with respect to
x
gives

{\partial f\over\partial x}(x)-{\partial f\over\partial x}(\alpha-x-y)=0

(28)

Since
y
can be varied, it immediately follows that that
f^{\prime}
is a constant, hence
f(x)=cx+d
. From
f(0)=0
we obtain
d=0
, and from
f(1)=1
we obtain
c=1
. Hence

f(x)=x

(29)

In other words, the generalized rule (
22
) reduces uniquely to the Born rule (
16
).

Differentiability of the function
f
is however not a necessary ingredient to obtain (
29
). Setting
x=0
in (
27
) and using
f(0)=0
, we obtain
f(y)=\alpha-f(\alpha-y)
, so that (
27
) becomes

f(x)+f(\alpha-x-y)=f(\alpha-y)

(30)

In terms of the variable
z=\alpha-x-y
, this is the well-known Cauchy functional equation

f(x)+f(z)=f(x+z)

(31)

The function
f
defined by (
31
), with domain of definition
[-{1\over N-1},1]
, being bounded, is necessarily continuous. Let us first show continuity at
x=0
. Eq. (
31
) clearly holds in an
\epsilon
-neighborhood of
0
. Indeed, if
x\in[-\epsilon,\epsilon]\subset[-{1\over N-1},1]
, then choosing
y=\alpha
, we have that
z
varies in the interval
[\alpha-\epsilon-y,\alpha+\epsilon-y]=[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
. Let
x\in[-{\epsilon\over n},{\epsilon\over n}]
, for some positive integer
n
. Then (
31
) gives
f(2x)=f(x+x)=f(x)+f(x)=2f(x)
,
f(3x)=f(x+2x)=f(x)+f(2x)=3f(x)
, and so on, and by induction we obtain
f(nx)=nf(x)
, since all the
kx
,
k=1,\dots,n
, belong to the interval
[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
, where (
31
) holds. This means that
|x|\leq{\epsilon\over n}
implies

|f(x)|=\frac{|f(nx)|}{n}\leq\frac{1}{n}

(32)

showing continuity at
0
. Continuity throughout the interval
[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
immediately follows. Indeed,

\lim_{h\to 0}f(x+h)=f(x)+\lim_{h\to 0}f(h)=f(x)

(33)

The last step is to show that continuity on an interval implies linearity. We have
mf(x)=f(mx)=f(n{m\over n}x)=nf({m\over n}x)
, implying
f({m\over n}x)={m\over n}f(x)
,
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Also,
0=f(0)=f(x-x)=f(x)+f(-x)
, implying that
f(x)
is an odd function; hence,
f(rx)=rf(x)
,
r\in\mathbb{Q}
. Finally, let
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the density of rational numbers in the real line implies that we can always find a sequence of rationals
r_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
t=\lim_{k\to\infty}r_{k}
. From the continuity of
f(x)
, we then obtain

f(tx)=\lim_{k\to\infty}f(r_{k}x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}r_{k}f(x)=tf(x)

(34)

Setting
x=1
in (
34
) and using
f(1)=1
, we find (
29
). Note that one can extend the validity of the above derivation from the interval
[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
to the full domain of
f
, using the three-term transport identity (
30
) and selecting the variable
y
so as to extend the domain of the variable
x
, then iterating the procedure as many times as needed, until the entire domain of
f
is covered.

6
Conclusion

Gleason’s theorem is often interpreted as showing that the Born rule follows uniquely from the structure of Hilbert space. The present analysis clarifies the geometric origin of this result using the Bloch representation of quantum states. For two-dimensional systems the state space is the three-dimensional unit Bloch ball and measurement outcomes correspond to antipodal points on its surface. In this situation the only constraint is that the two probabilities sum to one. This leaves complete freedom in the functional dependence on the scalar products
{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{i}
, allowing infinitely many non-Born probability rules, even if continuity is required.

For dimensions
N\geq 3
, however, the geometry changes qualitatively. The eigenstates of a measurement correspond to the vertices of a regular
(N-1)
-simplex inscribed in the generalized
(N^{2}-1)
-dimensional unit Bloch ball. The requirement that probabilities associated with these
N
mutually orthogonal projectors sum to unity then imposes strong functional constraints. These constraints force the probability rule to be linear in the scalar product s
{\bf r}\cdot{\bf n}_{i}
, uniquely recovering the Born rule.

The Bloch-space perspective therefore makes transparent why two-dimensional systems are exceptional and why higher-dimensional Hilbert spaces inevitably lead to the Born probability law, providing a simple geometric explanation of the mechanism underlying Gleason’s result.
