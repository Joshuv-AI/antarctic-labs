Abstract
. In this note, we prove that the infimum of the mean curvature on any disconnected boundary component of an unbounded mean convex domain in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} must be zero.

0.
Introduction

The splitting theorem of Cheeger-Gromoll plays an essential role in the study of the open Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature. For manifold with boundary,
[
Kas83
]
proved that if
M
is complete Riemannian manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature and weakly mean-convex boundary, and if
\partial M
is disconnected with at least one boundary component, then
M
splits isometrically as a product:

\Sigma^{n-1}\times[0,\ell].

In particular the boundary components are totally geodesic and parallel. See also
[
CK92
]
for a warped product splitting. The compactness of one boundary component is used in an essential way, it traps the shortest geodesic connecting two connected components of the boundary. If all boundary components are non-compact, it is more complicated even for flat manifolds.

Classical minimal surface theory in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
has a strong geometric property: The only mean-convex domains in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with disconnected boundary are slabs between parallel planes. In particular the boundary has mean curvature
\equiv 0
, cf.
[
FCS80
]
and
[
HM90
]
. For higher dimensions, generalized catenoids give non-flat minimal hypersurfaces trapped in a slab, you can stack many catenoids one by one, the domain that these surfaces bounds is mean convex with many boundary components. Therefore one cannot expect a product structure. In fact, Gromov made the following conjecture:

Conjecture 0.1

(Section 3, Conjecture 1 in
[
Gro19
]
)
.

Let
X
be an infinite mean convex domain
\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If the boundary
\partial X
is disconnected, then non of the connected component of
\partial X
may have its mean curvature separated away from
0
, i.e. infimum of the mean curvatures of all components are zero.

In this note, we give a proof of Gromov’s conjecture.

Theorem 0.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a connected unbounded domain with
C^{2}
smooth boundary. Let
\nu
be the outward unit normal of
X
. Assume
X
is mean convex on
\partial X
. If
\partial X
is disconnected, then for every connected component
\Sigma\subset\partial X
,

\inf_{\Sigma}H=0.

In particular, no connected component of
\partial X
has mean curvature separated away from
0
.

The idea of the proof is simple. We introduce a penalized distance function. The penalty forces the minimum to occur at a bounded pair of points on the two connected components of the boundary, while becoming negligible as the penalty parameter tends to zero. At such a minimizing pair, the first variation shows that the segment meets the two boundary components almost perpendicularly. The second variation of the distance along matched tangent directions then compares the two mean curvatures. Uniform mean convexity makes a definite positive contribution, while the penalty terms vanish in the limit. Hence a contradiction.

1.
Proof of the theorem

In this section, we give the proof of the main theorem. Suppose
\Sigma
is a connected component of
\partial X
with

\inf_{\Sigma}H=c>0.

We will derive a contradiction. We set
B=\partial X\setminus\Sigma\neq\varnothing
, i.e. the rest of the boundary components. Since
\partial X
is a
C^{2}
hypersurface, hence a locally connected smooth manifold. Therefore its connected components are both open and closed in
\partial X
. It follows that both
\Sigma
and
B
are closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We set

\phi(p)=\sqrt{1+(d(o,p))^{2}},

where
o
is the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
d(p,q)
is the Euclidean function. One can verify easily:

|\nabla\phi|\leq 1,\qquad|\operatorname{Hess}\phi|\leq 1.

(1.1)

For any
\varepsilon>0
, consider the distance function:

F_{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma\times B\to\mathbb{R},

(1.2)

defined by:

F_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\sqrt{d(x,y)}+\varepsilon(\phi(x)+\phi(y)),\quad x\in\Sigma,y\in B.

Lemma 1.1
.

The function
F_{\varepsilon}
is proper on
\Sigma\times B
.

Proof.

For any fixed
L>0
, if
F_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\leq L
, then

\varepsilon\phi(x)\leq L,\quad\varepsilon\phi(y)\leq L.

So
x,y
lie in a compact ball, since
\Sigma
and
B
are both closed, the sub-level set is compact.
∎

It follows that for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
F_{\varepsilon}
achieves its minimum at some pair of boundary points
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
, where
x_{\varepsilon}\in\Sigma
,
y_{\varepsilon}\in B
. We set

R_{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=d(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})>0,

(1.3)

and the unit tangent vector from
y_{\varepsilon}
to
x_{\varepsilon}
is denoted by:

\uparrow^{x_{\varepsilon}}_{y_{\varepsilon}}.

We fix base points
x_{0}\in\Sigma
and
y_{0}\in B
. Since:

\sqrt{R_{\varepsilon}}\leq F_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\leq F_{\varepsilon}(x_{0},y_{0}).

(1.4)

we know
R_{\varepsilon}
has a uniform upper bound, denoted by
R_{0}
, which is independent of
\varepsilon
.

Lemma 1.2
.

For all sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, the interior of the segment from
x_{\varepsilon}
to
y_{\varepsilon}
do not touch
\partial X
.

Proof.

Suppose the segment touches
\partial X
at some point, say
p\in\partial X
. If
p\in\Sigma
, let
t=d(x_{\varepsilon},p)
, then
d(p,y_{\varepsilon})=R_{\varepsilon}-t
. Since
\phi
is
1
-Lipschitz, we have:

\phi(p)\leq\phi(x_{\varepsilon})+t.

Therefore

F_{\varepsilon}(p,y_{\varepsilon})\leq\sqrt{R_{\varepsilon}-t}+\varepsilon(\phi(x_{\varepsilon})+t+\phi(y_{\varepsilon})).

Then we have:

F_{\varepsilon}(p,y_{\varepsilon})-F_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\leq\sqrt{R_{\varepsilon}-t}-\sqrt{R_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon t\leq\varepsilon t-\frac{t}{2\sqrt{R_{0}}}.

Hence, if
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{R_{0}}}
, then
F_{\varepsilon}(p,y_{\varepsilon})<F_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
, contradicting the minimality of
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
. The case of
p\in B
can be proved similarly.
∎

Corollary 1.3
.

The segment from
x_{\varepsilon}
to
y_{\varepsilon}
lies entirely inside
X
, with both ends pointing inward direction:

\left\langle\uparrow_{x_{\varepsilon}}^{y_{\varepsilon}},\nu_{x_{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle\leq 0,\quad\left\langle\uparrow_{y_{\varepsilon}}^{x_{\varepsilon}},\nu_{y_{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle\leq 0.\quad

Proof.

Since
\Sigma
is a connected, closed, properly embedded, two-sided hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it separates
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into two components. Let
\Omega_{\Sigma}
be the side containing
X
. Since
B\subset\overline{X}
and
B\cap\Sigma=\varnothing
, we have
B\subset\Omega_{\Sigma}
. By lemma
1
, the open segment from
x_{\varepsilon}
to
y_{\varepsilon}
avoids
\partial X
. Since
y_{\varepsilon}\in\Omega_{\Sigma}
, the segment lies in
\Omega_{\Sigma}
except
x_{\varepsilon}
. Near
x_{\varepsilon}
, the segment is pointing inward side of
\Sigma
, this finishes the proof.
∎

We calculate the first variation of
F_{\varepsilon}
at
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
, for simplicity, we drop the subscript
\varepsilon
temporarily:

R\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=R_{\varepsilon},\quad x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x_{\varepsilon},y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=y_{\varepsilon}.

For every
v\in T_{x}\Sigma
, direct calculation shows:

0=dF_{\varepsilon}(v,0)=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{R}}\left\langle\uparrow_{x}^{y},v\right\rangle+\varepsilon\left\langle\nabla\phi(x),v\right\rangle.

Recall that
\phi
is
1
-Lipschitz, we have:

\Big|\Big\langle\uparrow_{x}^{y},v\Big\rangle\Big|\leq 2\varepsilon\sqrt{R}|v|.

Similarly, for any
w\in T_{y}B
, we have:

\Big|\left\langle\uparrow_{y}^{x},w\right\rangle\Big|\leq 2\varepsilon\sqrt{R}|w|.

Therefore, we just proved:

Lemma 1.4
.

The tangential projections are small:

\left|(\uparrow_{x}^{y})^{T_{x}\Sigma}\right|\leq 2\varepsilon\sqrt{R},\qquad\left|(\uparrow_{y}^{x})^{T_{y}B}\right|\leq 2\varepsilon\sqrt{R}.

Since
R\leq R_{0}
, we know:

a\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle\uparrow_{x}^{y},-\nu_{x}\rangle\to 1,\quad b\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle\uparrow_{y}^{x},-\nu_{y}\rangle\to 1,

(1.5)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

By lemma
1
,
T_{x}\Sigma
and
T_{y}B
are
C\varepsilon\sqrt{R}
-close to the hyperplane perpendicular to
\uparrow_{x}^{y}
. Hence they are
C\varepsilon\sqrt{R}
-close to each other. This allow us to choose orthonormal basis
\left\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{n-1}\right\}
for
T_{x}\Sigma
and orthonormal basis
\left\{w_{1},\cdots,w_{n-1}\right\}
for
T_{y}B
, with

|v_{i}-w_{i}|\leq C\varepsilon\sqrt{R}\to 0.

(1.6)

Now, we calculate the second variation. Set
V_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(v_{i},w_{i})\in T_{(x,y)}\Sigma\times B
. Since
F_{\varepsilon}
has a minimum at the point
(x,y)
, we have:

\operatorname{Hess}F_{\varepsilon}(V_{i},V_{i})\geq 0,\quad\text{for every}\ i.

Let
r=d(p,q)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma\times B\to\mathbb{R}
be the Euclidean distance function.

Lemma 1.5
.

Let notations be as above, we have:

\operatorname{Hess}_{\Sigma\times\Gamma}r(V_{i},V_{i})=Q_{i}-ah_{\Sigma}(v_{i},v_{i})-bh_{B}(w_{i},w_{i}).

where

0\leq Q_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{|v_{i}-w_{i}|^{2}-\left\langle v_{i}-w_{i},\uparrow_{x}^{y}\right\rangle^{2}}{R}\leq C\varepsilon^{2},

(1.7)

for some constant
C>0
independend of
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{i}(s)\subset\Sigma
and
\beta_{i}(s)\subset B
be geodesics in
\Sigma
and
B
respectively such that

\alpha_{i}(0)=x,\alpha_{i}^{\prime}(0)=v_{i};\quad\beta_{i}(0)=y,\beta_{i}^{\prime}(0)=w_{i}.

Then

\alpha_{i}^{\prime\prime}(0)=-h_{\Sigma}(v_{i},v_{i})\nu_{\Sigma}(x);\quad\beta_{i}^{\prime\prime}(0)=-h_{B}(w_{i},w_{i})\nu_{B}(y).

(1.8)

Here
h_{\Sigma}
is the second fundamental form of
\Sigma
and
\nu
is the outward normal of
\partial X
. Standard Euclidean calculation shows.

r^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{|v_{i}-w_{i}|^{2}-\left\langle v_{i}-w_{i},\uparrow_{x}^{y}\right\rangle^{2}}{r}-\left\langle\uparrow_{x}^{y},\alpha^{\prime\prime}(0)-\beta^{\prime\prime}(0)\right\rangle.

(1.9)

Combine (
1.8
) and (
1.9
), we have the desired Hessian form. The estimate of
Q
follows from (
1.6
).
∎

Note that:

\operatorname{Hess}_{\Sigma\times B}\sqrt{r}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\operatorname{Hess}r-\frac{1}{4r^{3/2}}dr\otimes dr.

Use lemma
1
, we have:

\operatorname{Hess}_{\Sigma\times B}\sqrt{r}(V_{i},V_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{R}}(Q_{i}-ah_{\Sigma}(v_{i},v_{i})-bh_{B}(w_{i},w_{i})).

(1.10)

The Hessian of
\phi
terms are:

\operatorname{Hess}_{\Sigma}\phi(v_{i},v_{i})=\operatorname{Hess}\phi(v_{i},v_{i})-h_{\Sigma}(v_{i},v_{i})\left\langle\nabla\phi(x),\nu_{\Sigma}(x)\right\rangle,

(1.11)

\operatorname{Hess}_{B}\phi(w_{i},w_{i})=\operatorname{Hess}\phi(w_{i},w_{i})-h_{B}(w_{i},w_{i})\left\langle\nabla\phi(y),\nu_{B}(y)\right\rangle,

(1.12)

Summing the equations (
1.10
), (
1.11
) and (
1.12
), we get:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\operatorname{Hess}_{\Sigma\times B}F_{\varepsilon}(V_{i},V_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{R}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}Q_{i}-aH_{\Sigma}(x)-bH_{B}(y)\right)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\operatorname{Hess}\phi(v_{i},v_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\operatorname{Hess}\phi(w_{i},w_{i})\right)

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\left\langle\nabla\phi(x),\nu_{\Sigma}(x)\right\rangle H_{\Sigma}(x)-\varepsilon\left\langle\nabla\phi(y),\nu_{B}(y)\right\rangle H_{B}(y)

Rearranging the terms:

\displaystyle\left(a+2\varepsilon\sqrt{R}\left\langle\nabla\phi(x),\nu_{\Sigma}(x)\right\rangle\right)H_{\Sigma}(x)+\left(b+2\varepsilon\sqrt{R}\left\langle\nabla\phi(y),\nu_{B}(y)\right\rangle\right)H_{B}(y)

(1.13)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}Q_{i}+2\varepsilon\sqrt{R}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\operatorname{Hess}\phi(v_{i},v_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\operatorname{Hess}\phi(w_{i},w_{i})\right)

Since
\phi
is
1
-Lipschitz and
R\leq R_{0}
, the term
\sqrt{R}\left\langle\nabla\phi(x),\nu_{\Sigma}(x)\right\rangle
is bounded. By lemma
1
, we know
a\to 1,b\to 1
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Hence for small
\varepsilon
, we have:

\left(a+2\varepsilon\sqrt{R}\left\langle\nabla\phi(x),\nu_{\Sigma}(x)\right\rangle\right)\geq 1/2,\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

Similarly

\left(b+2\varepsilon\sqrt{R}\left\langle\nabla\phi(y),\nu_{B}(y)\right\rangle\right)\geq 1/2,\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

Let’s look at the other side of (
1.13
). By (
1.7
), we know
|Q_{i}|\leq C\varepsilon^{2}
. The other two Hessian terms are clearly bounded since
|\operatorname{Hess}\phi|\leq 1
. Therefore we have, for all
\varepsilon
sufficiently small (
1.13
) can be rewritten as:

\frac{1}{2}(H_{\Sigma}(x)+H_{B}(y))\leq\tilde{C}\varepsilon.

Since
X
is assumed to be mean-convex, we know
H_{B}(y)\geq 0
. Thus
H_{\Sigma}(x)\leq 2\tilde{C}\varepsilon
. If
H_{\Sigma}\geq c>0
, letting
\varepsilon\to 0
yields a contradiction. This finishes the proof.
