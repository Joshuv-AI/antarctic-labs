Arthroscopic Repair of Radial Meniscal Tear Using Fibrin Clot Augmentation and “Hashtag” Style Inside‐Out Technique
Authors: Siddharth Padmanabhan, Helena Franco, Peter Myers, Francois Tudor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSignificant meniscal injuries lead to pain and possible early osteoarthritis development. Fibrin clot augmentation represents an effective, cost‐efficient method for improving healing rates in poorly vascularized meniscal tears. This arthroscopic technique shows fibrin clot augmentation combined with horizontal and vertical mattress sutures in a “ripstop” configuration for radial midbody medial meniscus tears. Thirty milliliters of autologous blood is collected and processed to form a fibrin clot, which is introduced inside‐out through an endotracheal tube to optimize surface contact within the tear. The clot is secured with braided sutures while meniscal repair is performed using 2‐0 polydioxanone sutures in a ripstop configuration. A similar technique may be used for any repair requiring biological augmentation. Rehabilitation protocols include 4 weeks of nonweight‐bearing, followed by graduated weight‐bearing to achieve full weight‐bearing by 8 weeks. Range‐of‐motion restrictions vary by tear location. This technique provides a cost‐effective, low‐complexity technique for biological augmentation of repair of high‐risk meniscus tears, potentially improving healing probability.

Untreated meniscal injury leads to pain and early osteoarthritis development. 1 , 2 Meniscal repair shows superior long‐term outcomes and delays osteoarthritis onset compared with meniscectomy. 3 Consequently, repair techniques are increasingly used to promote joint preservation. 4 Repair success correlates strongly with tear location. Peripheral tears, within the vascular zone, heal reliably, whereas tears nearer the free edge heal poorly. 5

Fibrin clot augmentation introduces healing elements into poorly vascularized regions and represents an effective, cost‐efficient method for improving meniscal healing rates. 6 , 7 This technique shows fibrin clot augmentation combined with horizontal and vertical mattress sutures in a “ripstop” (or rebar) configuration for radial midbody medial meniscus tears.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Step 1: Patient Positioning and Preparation

The patient is positioned supine with lateral support facilitating medial compartment access. A foot roll maintains 90° knee flexion and a high tourniquet is applied. Standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals are established. Following diagnostic arthroscopy and tear pattern confirmation, tear edges are prepared with a meniscal rasp and fenestrated with a microfracture awl (Video 1).

Instruments: Standard arthroscopic equipment includes a 30° arthroscope with saline irrigation (1 mL of 1:1000 adrenaline added), MeniPass and cannula (Portal Sports Med, Brisbane, Australia), and 2‐0 polydioxanone sutures for meniscal repair. Fibrin clot preparation requires a glass beaker, glass stirring rod, and 2‐0 looped braided suture. A pediatric endotracheal (ET) tube, cut obliquely 7 to 10 cm from the distal end (furthest end from cuff), serves as the clot insertion cannula.

Step 2: Blood Clot Preparation

Thirty milliliters of peripheral blood are collected in a sterile fashion from the patient and transferred to a sterile glass beaker. Following 7 min of undisturbed coagulation, the blood is gently stirred with a textured glass rod for up to 7 min until clot formation. The clot is removed, dried with gauze, trimmed to size, and secured with 1‐2 pulling sutures (2‐0 braided) using the loop capture technique (Figure 1). Generally, the volume is sufficient to prepare at least 2 clots if required. The clot should be sized appropriately for the tear dimensions to ensure optimal surface contact and avoid excessive bulk.

Fibrin clot preparation requires a glass beaker, glass stirring rod, and 2‐0 braided absorbable suture. Following 7 min of undisturbed coagulation, the blood is gently stirred with a textured glass rod for up to 7 min until clot formation. It is then gently pushed off the glass rod onto an absorbent sponge to drain.

Step 3: Inside‐Out Meniscal Repair Technique

A percutaneous deep medial collateral ligament release is performed to improve safe access to the tear. A posteromedial incision allows capsular dissection with the location determined by arthroscopic illumination of the tear site or by passing the arthroscopic trochar through the tear and palpating the trochar externally. Dissection is performed carefully to protect branches of the saphenous nerve. If necessary, the dissection may be continued posteriorly, deep to the medial head of gastrocnemius, or anteriorly.

The ET tube is inserted through the anterolateral portal with the arthroscope in the anteromedial portal. The arthroscope obturator can be used inside the tube as a guide during insertion (Figure 2). A pulling suture is passed through the planned clot placement site using the MeniPass system, with the lead end delivered via the posteromedial incision. The looped tail is left at the entry point of the ET tube to facilitate clot insertion.

The ET tube is placed over a trochar for insertion into the joint. The ET tube facilitates easy passage of the fibrin clot into its final position adjacent to the meniscal tear. (ET, endotracheal.)

The fibrin clot is introduced through the ET tube into the tear. Single pulling sutures suffice for radial tears; horizontal cleavage tears require anterior and posterior pulling sutures for optimal positioning along the tear length. A trochar or probe may be used to gently manipulate the clot into optimal position within the tear. The clot is generally held in position by the braided preparation sutures which are left attached until completion of the procedure.

Multiple 2‐0 polydioxanone sutures are placed inside‐out using the MeniPass needle for meniscal repair. Complex radial tears use a “hashtag” or “ripstop” configuration with 2 vertical mattress sutures on either side of the tear (Figure 3), followed by horizontal mattress sutures on superior and inferior meniscal surfaces (Figure 4). This “hashtag” configuration provides optimal failure strength for radial tears. 8

Meniscal repair may be performed initially with sutures left untied while the clot is optimally positioned. Sutures are then tied on the capsule to oppose tear edges and secure the clot in place. The clot can bridge small gaps in the tear, thus preventing excessive suture tension.

Additional blood clots may be introduced if desired, before tying the meniscus sutures (Figure 5). Braided clot sutures may be tied or removed by cutting or pulling the sutures out. Standard skin closure and dressing application follow, with a cryocuff and knee brace applied.

First 2 vertical limbs of the ripstop are made (A), followed by 2 horizontal limbs (B). A supplemental inferior meniscal horizontal suture can be made if required to secure the clot in place (C). Postoperative Care and Rehabilitation

Postoperative rehabilitation protocols depend on tear pattern. Radial tears, as shown in the accompanying video, require 4 to 6 weeks of nonweight‐bearing, followed by 25% weekly incremental weight‐bearing to achieve full weight bearing by 8 weeks.

Range of motion restrictions vary by location: medial meniscus tears permit 0° to 90° for 6 weeks; lateral tears are restricted to 0°‐ to 0° for 2 weeks, followed by 0° to 90° for 4 additional weeks. Bracing continues until adequate quadriceps control is achieved, typically at 2 to 3 weeks. Patients avoid twisting and loaded flexion beyond 90° for 3 months. Return to play occurs between 3 and 6 months following appropriate graduated training.

DISCUSSION

Functional loss of the meniscus contributes to early knee osteoarthritis development by increasing contact pressure and excessive chondral loading, thereby accelerating degeneration. 1

Fibrin clots have shown promising results in meniscal pathology since the 1990s. Henning et al. reported an 8% failure rate with exogenous fibrin clots, compared with 41% with augmentation. 6 Webber et al. attributed delayed healing to hematoma absence, rather than regional blood supply deficiency. 8 Meniscal hematomas provide a scaffold containing growth factors and proliferative cells, facilitating fibrocartilage formation and healing. Arnoczky and Warren's canine studies showed healing mechanisms, with areas filled with fibrin clot forming fibrous connective tissue proliferation and eventually becoming fibrocartilaginous tissue. 9 Natural hematoma formation occurs less frequently in central meniscal regions compared with peripheral areas. Thus, introducing autologous fibrin clot into a repair provides proliferative cells and growth factors where most needed (Table 1).

Pearls and Pitfalls

• Ensure the clot is “well set” before handling it and trying to suture it

• Suturing the clot improves the ease of introducing into the repair zone and keeping it in the correct location. The authors have found clots to stick to arthroscopic graspers causing difficulty with placement and that clots can float away into other parts of the knee

• If certain that a repair will be performed, ask the anesthetist to draw the blood shortly after induction so that clot formation time can occur during patient setup and preparation for surgery

• A clot can be introduced using this technique and then all‐inside repair may be performed—give a sharp tug on the clot tagging suture once the meniscus sutures have been placed and this will allow the suture to be removed but the clot to remain in the repair zone

• Support the clot as the suture is pulled into the knee and consider briefly turning off fluid flow on the arthroscope when introducing the clot to ensure minimal pressure is placed on the clot pulling suture

• Be aware of the anatomy and position of neurovascular structures when performing meniscus repair to minimize the risk of iatrogenic injury

Improved outcome scores and delayed arthritis onset following meniscal repair compared with meniscectomy should encourage surgeons to consider repair in patients previously deemed inappropriate due to age or activity level. 10 Recent advances in techniques and implants enable accurate reduction and stable fixation. Fibrin clot augments healing biology, further improving success.

Fibrin clot augmentation benefits tears traditionally associated with poor healing rates, particularly radial and horizontal cleavage tears, and this technique may be considered for these tears to improve the chances of successful healing. Advantages of this technique are that the augmentation requires no additional expensive equipment or preparation, shows moderate complexity, and can be performed by surgeons trained in arthroscopic meniscal repair with no additional training. 11 Although in this technical note the MeniPass device is used, any device allowing an inside‐out meniscal repair is suitable. There are no notable disadvantages of this technique; however, there may be some added surgical time required for the clot preparation and introduction (Table 2). This can be minimized by having scrub staff or an assistant prepare this while the surgeon is examining or preparing the meniscus tear site.

In conclusion, the blood clot augmentation technique shows promising clinical trial results. 12 For patients with previously irreparable tears, this represents a cost‐effective, low‐complexity alternative to meniscectomy or isolated meniscus repair, improving meniscal healing probability while potentially prolonging native knee longevity.
