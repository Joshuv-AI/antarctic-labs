Sisters impacting the SISTER diabetes study: partnering with a Patient Advisory Group across the research continuum
Authors: Stephania T. Miller, Rachel A. Hanebutt, Letrice A. Samuels, Velma McBride Murry, Sylvie A. Akohoue, Glynis Varlack-Allgood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient-engaged research is essential for ensuring that research prioritizes patients’ needs and perspectives. Despite the known value of and need for engaging historically marginalized patient groups in research, there are limited examples of their engagement and across the research continuum.ObjectiveThis study describes ways that a Patient Advisory Group (PAG) of African American women with type 2 diabetes were engaged in the design, implementation, and dissemination of a randomized clinical trial (RCT) and the influence of their engagement.MethodsThe PAG was established in 2020 as part of the (Sisters Inspiring Sisters to Engage in Relevant Diabetes Self-Care) SISTER Diabetes Study. A pragmatic, non-systematic approach was used for documenting engagement, including research team review of meeting notes and progress reports. Engagement activities were categorized according to research phase and summarized.ResultsThe PAG actively contributed across all phases of the research process. During study design, members co-developed recruitment materials and informed the selection of culturally relevant RCT comparator group content. During implementation PAG members supported recruitment through community engagement, co-refining intervention session topics, and guiding development of COVID-19 safety protocols. During dissemination, PAG members contributed to data interpretation and served as manuscript co-authors.ConclusionThe PAG’s involvement across the research continuum of the SISTER Diabetes Study demonstrated many opportunities to leverage their experience and expertise. The value and level of their engagement demonstrate the importance of patient engagement across different research phases, which is particularly salient for historically marginalized patient groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00906-4.

Introduction

The importance of patient engagement in research is widely recognized as critical in ensuring that patients’ needs and perspectives are prioritized throughout all research phases [1]. While such engagement is not new [2], funding agencies, such as the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI), are currently supporting efforts to increase patient engagement in research [3]. Benefits include identification of research questions and outcomes of importance for patients, improved study recruitment and retention, more effective data collection processes, and real-world translation of research findings [4]. Notably, patient engagement in research is adept at addressing health disparities-related research gaps, as patient insights help to align the research with the needs of diverse racial and ethnic groups. This represents a step towards a culture of engagement that supports optimal health outcomes for all [4, 5].

While researchers and health systems have been at the forefront of increasing patient engagement in research, the absence of a clear definition has been a barrier to describing and measuring engagement impact [6]. A systematic review of 244 definitions resulted in a consensus definition: The active, meaningful, and collaborative interaction between patients and researchers across the research continuum, where research decision making is guided by patients’ contributions as partners, recognizing their specific experiences, values, and expertise [5]. Sheridan and colleagues emphasize the balance present within the research relationship, wherein patients’ suggestions and contributions are weighed similarly, if not more heavily, than those of researchers [6]. While other definitions exist [7], there is agreement that patient involvement should be meaningful, rather than tokenistic. Specifically, the engagement of patients should be void of perfunctory or symbolic efforts to engage without any efforts or intention to prioritize patient views and experience throughout the research process [8].

Patient engagement in research occurs along a continuum, ranging from one-way input to patients serving in leadership roles [9]. For example, a review of engagement levels described in PCORI-sponsored initiatives categorized focus groups and surveys as one-way patient engagement since patient input minimally impacted decisions [4]. Consultations, on the other hand, were described as unidirectional or bidirectional, with patients providing as-needed guidance, such as those serving on advisory panels. The highest level, collaboration or shared leadership, included patients serving as co-investigators, research team members, and embedded advisors [4]. A separate review of best practices for patient engagement identified the consultative level as the most effective model for facilitating meaningful patient input into decision-making throughout the research process while maintaining feasibility within study design and implementation [10]. In this context, advisory council members were described as patients and caregivers who met regularly in person to share their personal experiences or perspectives on a specific topic. Another systematic review found that there was insufficient information available to identify best engagement practices. Still, advisory councils and boards were identified as the most active forms of patient engagement [2].

Despite the lack of empirical evidence for determining the most effective patient engagement method, engaging historically marginalized groups in research is needed to identify effective ways to address disparities and achieve health equity [11, 12]. This was especially true during the COVID-19 pandemic [13]. The value of engaging these groups, including using interviews and focus groups [14, 15]; surveys [16]; advisory councils or boards [17]; and shared leadership models are well documented [18]. A thematic analysis of advisory board meeting notes showed that African American patient advisors’ input contributed significantly to the development of a culturally-tailored diabetes intervention for African Americans [16]. Despite this collective known value of engaging historically marginalized patient groups in research and given the call for researchers to ensure community engagement in all phases of research [19], there are few examples describing their engagement from research design to dissemination of research findings. To help fill this gap, this manuscript provides examples of: (1) ways that a Patient Advisory Group (PAG) of African American women with type 2 diabetes were engaged in the design, implementation, and dissemination of the SISTER (Sisters Inspiring Sisters to Engage in Relevant Diabetes Self-Care) Diabetes Study [20]; and (2) how their engagement influenced the study across the research continuum.

Methods

The SISTER Diabetes Study [20] uses a randomized control trial design to test the hypothesis that adding culturally-tailored motivational interviewing (MI) [21], a behavioral counseling approach, to evidence-based medical nutrition therapy (MNT) [21, 22], is more effective than MNT alone in improving glycemic control. The American Diabetes Association encourages MNT tailoring for different racial and ethnic groups given the nuanced role of cultural, personal, and other factors that influence MNT efficacy [23]. Conflicting reports about MI benefits among African American women led to recommendations that cultural adaptations are needed [24], further supporting the need for the ongoing, randomized clinical trial [20].

In preparing the SISTER Diabetes Study funding application, the principal investigator (PI) scheduled individual phone meetings with five potential PAG members based on relationships established as part of their enrollment in preliminary studies leading to the funding application [25]. They had also expressed interest in finding ways to contribute more to the research line of inquiry and its subsequent translation. The main meeting discussion points included: (1) rationale for the SISTER Diabetes Study, with an emphasis on how the preliminary research, for which they were participants, informed the study; (2) proposed comparator groups; (3) proposed study outcomes, including those of importance to them and other African American women with type 2 diabetes as previously reported in preliminary studies [18]; (4) description of proposed PAG role in the study; (5) proposed frequency of PAG meetings; and (6) proposed remuneration for PAG involvement. Each meeting ended with an invitation for women to serve as a PAG member. An additional phone meeting, covering the aforementioned topics, was held with an African American woman with significant experience in patient/community engaged research and community-academic research partnerships. She was extended an invitation to serve as a PAG member and advisor with the primary role of ensuring that PAG input was communicated and prioritized by the research team, as well as co-leading discussions during PAG meetings.

In keeping with the descriptive manuscript objectives and to add to the paucity of examples of ways to engage marginalized groups in research, the research team used a pragmatic, non-systematic approach for documenting PAG engagement and influence. Collectively, research team members have extensive experience and expertise in engaging historically marginalized and medically underserved patient and community members in research beyond the research participant role [5], including to inform feasibility and acceptability of future diabetes research among African American women [26, 27], developing and facilitating community engaged research training locally and nationally [28, 29], and identifying patient centered outcomes of importance for African American women with type 2 diabetes [30]. For documentation of pre-funding engagement, the PI used informal, electronic note-taking to summarize potential PAG member input/feedback on the aforementioned discussion points (e.g., proposed study outcomes). Post-funding, the primary methods to ascertain engagement was informal PI review of PAG meeting agendas and notes, PI and research team member completion and review of engagement progress reports submitted to the funder, and recall and input from PAG members, other research team members, and study facilitators. Recall was informal but based on engagement summaries presented by the PI during PAG meetings or research team meetings. The summaries included engagement activities captured using aforementioned methods (e.g., review of meeting agendas and notes). During the meetings, attendees could add or modify summaries. As an example, during one PAG meeting, the PI shared a list of ways PAG members had contributed up to that point and sought initial impressions and identification of any missing contributions, for which there were none, based on meeting minutes. In preparing this manuscript, the research team mapped engagement activities to distinct research phases. Documentation of influence, given the pragmatic, non-systematic approach, was limited to engagement activities where PAG influence was readily apparent (e.g., their contributions during study design-focused meetings) and included in the final study protocol.

The SISTER Diabetes Study received approval from the Meharry Medical College Internal Review Board (#21-02-1069). Since the intent of this current work is to describe examples of patient engagement strategies, and not to contribute to generalizable knowledge, it is not considered human subjects research [31].

Results

PAG members

Before submitting the funding application, five women accepted PAG membership invitations. One opted to consider membership if funding was secured, but could not be reached for follow-up. Those who accepted invitations provided a letter of support and a biosketch to include in the funding application. Biosketches, which highlighted various professions, demonstrated members’ passion about giving back to the community and regularly volunteering at community events. Four of the five responded to this question, “Why are you agreeing to serve as a PAG member?” (Table 1).

PAG launch and meeting structure

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the first and subsequent meetings were held virtually to foster greater engagement for all members. For all meetings, the PI worked with the PAG on agenda development. During the first meeting, agenda items were focused on introducing PAG members to research and the SISTER Diabetes Study design/timeline and establishing PAG ground rules and a cadence for future meetings. The PI provided the introduction to research based on a community research curriculum, for which she led the development [32] and gave a high-level overview of the study design. A PAG member facilitated discussions around these two topics, and the group brainstormed and collectively determined ground rules (e.g., agree to disagree). The PAG agreed on a monthly meeting schedule for the first six months to facilitate their input on various, time-sensitive start-up activities (e.g., developing recruitment materials). The meeting frequency was set to quarterly thereafter, with the option for ad hoc meetings.

PAG engagement in study design

Study outcomes

PAG feedback (e.g., impressions or thoughts about proposed thoughts/ideas) was received during the initial PAG membership invitation call. All PAG members agreed with the proposed study outcomes, including those of importance to them and other African American women with type 2 diabetes enrolled in preliminary studies [18] (Table 2). These outcomes were included in the previously published protocol as the primary outcome (e.g., HbA1c), along with other noted outcomes of importance (e.g., diabetes-related distress, dietary self-efficacy), and those expected to mediate changes in outcomes (e.g., motivation for dietary self-care).

Recruitment material development

PAG input (e.g., original thoughts/ideas) and feedback were received during PAG meetings and by commenting on or editing documents within an electronic portal (Table 2). They were also actively engaged in developing recruitment materials. As an example, one PAG member, a graphic designer, volunteered to develop the main graphic, which is used across all study materials (Fig. 1). All PAG members agreed with her taking on this role. During development, PAG members highlighted the importance of ensuring that African American women of different skin tones, ages, and body shapes were represented. Quotes from women who participated in preliminary studies were also included in the graphics. PAG members decided to prioritize quotes that they felt would resonate with the target group. The PAG member with graphic design expertise also created a second graphic that is included only on the study website (Fig. 2). This image includes a picture of an older African American woman with a young African American girl. The idea for this image came from one of the quotes from previous study participants (Fig. 1) and was agreed upon by the PAG, highlighting the perceived importance of the study on the health of future generations [20].

PAG members were integral in finalizing the wording used alongside the aforementioned study graphics and as part of physician recruitment letters. This includes wording for the study purpose, eligibility criteria, and description of research activities. In all cases, based on guidance from PAG members to prioritize clarity and brevity, the research team drafted the initial wording and finalized it based on PAG member feedback. For the physician recruitment letters, there was PAG member agreement that the letter should not exceed one page. PAG members also provided feedback on the study website design, flyer, and poster, including guiding text placement and color choices (Table 2).

Selection of “other” health topics for the control comparator group

As noted in the aforementioned SISTER Diabetes Study protocol summary, testing the hypothesis requires a control condition to assess the added benefit of MI on glycemic control when provided as an adjunct to MNT. In this case, control condition participants received MNT but no MI, for which 30 min was allotted. In order to account for those 30 min in the control group, non-MNT-related health topics were discussed. During a PAG meeting, members brainstormed topics with a focus on those of perceived importance and/or relevance to African American women, resulting in 14 topics (Table 2). Since only six topics were needed based on the study design, at the next meeting, the research team proposed a ranking exercise (Table 2). PAG members agreed and ranked previously-identified topics, resulting in a final list of the following topics included in the control group study sessions: (1) health advocacy; (2) thyroid disorders; (3) COVID-19; (4) dermatologic conditions in skin of color; (5) mental health; and (6) medication adherence.

PAG Engagement in study implementation

Recruitment

Updates on recruitment during quarterly PAG meetings led to active involvement in study recruitment efforts. This includes PAG members personally recruiting among their networks (e.g., sharing study flyers, sharing social media recruitment posts), facilitating research team recruitment at events in their personal networks (e.g., church), personally connecting research team members with their colleagues in media/marketing, and making recommendations for additional recruitment venues or target groups (Table 2). Collectively, PAG engagement in recruitment elements has resulted in greater study visibility and participants expressing interest in participating or enrolling in the study. For example, one of the PAG members facilitated the research team being present at a community event sponsored by her church, for which interest was expressed by those in attendance.

Practice session for treatment comparator group study activity

Although the two nutritionists facilitating the treatment comparator group—MNT plus MI—had prior professional training in MI, PAG members served as mock study participants, providing opportunities for the facilitators to practice and prepare for delivering the intervention in an environmental setting before actual implementation (e.g., group setting, same target group as participants) (Table 2). During one of the PAG meetings, the two nutritionists each facilitated one of the study structured MI exercises. The nutritionists adjusted the wording of some questions that would be posed to the group during the intervention to increase clarity and modified some of the group activities based on feedback from PAG members and observations from the MI trainer, who reviewed a recording of the practice session.

COVID-19 Safety precautions during intervention sessions

The first study sessions were scheduled after local COVID-19 stay-at-home orders had been lifted and COVID-19 vaccinations were available. Some enrolled study participants expressed concerns about the in-person format, particularly given the group setting. As part of planning for the first session, the research team sought the guidance of the PAG in outlining COVID-19 safety precautions that would be implemented during each session (Table 2). This resulted in safety precautions and guidelines for participants and those related to the meeting space. For participants, vaccinations were recommended along with the following for each session: (1) taking a COVID-19 test regardless of symptoms; (2) not attending if they have symptoms and have not been tested; (3) not attending if they test positive, regardless of symptoms; and (4) wear a mask (supplied by the study) when not speaking or eating. The following safety precautions were implemented for the meeting space: (1) placing tables and chairs six feet apart; (2) markers to allow for socialdistancing in places where lines form (e.g., check-in); (3) entry door propped open to avoid contact with door handles; (4) hand sanitizer available; and (5) meals served in self-serve boxes.

PAG engagement in dissemination

During a PAG meeting, research manuscripts were discussed as one way to share study information and findings. The discussion included ways that PAG members could contribute as co-authors (e.g., providing written input/feedback on specific sections, meeting to verbally capture input/feedback), along with an open invitation to co-author (Table 2). To date, including the current manuscript, PAG members have served as co-authors on two manuscripts [20]. In each case, the proposed manuscripts were discussed during PAG meetings before manuscript development (Table 2). As an example, for the current manuscript, the PI shared a list of the PAG contributions and sought initial impressions and identification of any missing contributions. Though no omissions were noted, some PAG members expressed surprise to see the depth and impact of their contributions during group discussions. As development progressed for each manuscript, PAG members were given updates during PAG meetings and via email communications with all co-authors.

Discussion

We have described how a PAG of African American women with type 2 diabetes were engaged in and influenced the SISTER Diabetes Study. This is critically important as efforts to identify best practices and effective ways to engage historically marginalized patients in research have not been sufficiently addressed and disseminated. In the following sections, we summarize the PAG engagement influenced across the research continuum. Additionally, we discuss how the bi-directional and interactive engagement processes map on to community-based participatory research (CBPR) [12] principles, as this is a useful model to assess progress in research partnerships [33]. Some examples of engagement are further highlighted to model ways to promote meaningful involvement and avoid tokenistic engagement approaches [8].

PAG Influence across the research continuum

PAG influence across the research continuum is consistent with established CBPR principles that signal progress in research partnerships involving patient communities, including recognizing the community as a unit of identity, promoting equitable involvement across research phases, fostering co-learning and mutual benefit, and supporting iterative, long-term partnerships [33, 34]. Specifically, one principle recognizes the community, African American women managing type 2 diabetes in this instance, as a unit of identity. Our findings also emphasize the importance of PAG members’ lived experiences and perspectives in intervention development for the SISTER Diabetes Study. In this regard, their responses to the question: “Why are you agreeing to serve as a PAG member?” are particularly relevant, as several indicated that they wanted to share what they have learned about managing their own health with other African American women. For example, one PAG member stated, “The information was helpful in my education of the illness. I would like to assist others in the same situation”. PAG engagement also maps on to the CBPR principle that emphasizes the need to facilitate collaborative and equitable involvement in all research phases. As summarized in the sections below, great consideration was given to ensuring that PAG members were actively engaged in the study phases to promote equitable involvement. Another CPBR principle, the need for partnerships to benefit all partners, is also reflected here. For PAG members, some self-described benefits were noted as part of their “why” for serving on the PAG, such as noted in the quote above relative to giving back to others. Co-learning, a core principle of community-based participatory research (CBPR) for addressing social inequities, was intentionally prioritized in this project to strengthen PAG members’ capacity for equitable contribution. As a previously mentioned example, the first PAG meeting included an Introduction to Research before discussing providing the study overview. Acknowledgement of the cyclical and iterative nature of the research partnership, another CBPR principle, was evident through the open communication strategies implemented throughout the project, with feedback and input shared during PAG meetings and via email exchanges and including revisions based on novel ideas and recommended changes shared by the PAG.

PAG influence on study design

PAG members were engaged in three key research design areas, finalization of study outcomes, development of recruitment materials, and development of content for the control comparator group. They co-designed all recruitment materials, including selecting graphics that would resonate with African American women and identifying quotes to accompany the graphics, with a primary focus on ensuring that all elements reflected the lived experiences of African American women living with type 2 diabetes. It is noteworthy that they were thoughtful about the “why” of using the particular images (Table 2). Though part of the “why” is considered surface-level cultural tailoring to engage individuals based on visual factors [35] (e.g., African American women of different ages, body sizes, and complexions), their selection of the quotes were reflective of deep-level cultural tailoring. This level is designed to reflect group cultural norms, beliefs, or values [35].

The input from PAG members during the study design phase is consistent with the CBPR principle of building on the strengths and resources of the study population of interest. Several PAG members viewed their capacity to contribute to the health and wellbeing of African American women by drawing on their personal experiences, which, in turn, enhanced their capacity to contribute meaningfully to visioning and development during the study design phase. Further, several of the PAG members had previous experience engaging in research and graduate degrees in health-related fields, highlighting how their strengths and resources influenced key study design elements. One member noted, “I participated in diabetes related studies with Meharry years ago, taking note of the effects on my health status…I have a master’s degree in Psychology and work in the field of mental health; I also volunteer in various community projects and church related outreach.” Another noted, “My experience as an RN is also relevant as it has given me the education and hands-on skills to deal with aspects of medication, diet, and other needs in the subject of diabetes and care.”

PAG influence on study implementation

PAG members were involved in three main study implementation areas: recruitment support, MI activities refinement, and guidance on COVID-19 safety plans. PAG members supported recruitment by raising study awareness through engaging the community in varying forms, including social media and their networks. With this, PAG leveraged their lived experiences and testimonials to engage community members, ultimately increasing study participants’ interest. By serving as mock participants, PAG members helped study nutritionists in making needed revisions to MI activities prior to study launch and getting a sense of the potential flow and dynamics of presenting in the group-based format.

The input from PAG members during the study implementation aligns with the foundational basis of a CBPR principle, which considers ways in which a research project addresses health from a positive and ecological perspective. The application of this principle was critical to our work with the PAG, as all aspects of the project were impacted by COVID-19. PAG served a critical role in identifying ways to address it. Recommendations included the need to be aware of ways to be sensitive and adapt the program to address changes that the pandemic was creating in the daily lives of African American women living with type 2 diabetes. PAG and the research team brainstormed and developed protocols to continue the research, given the restrictions associated with “shelter-in-home” policies. The PAG worked collectively to develop COVID-19 safety precautionary strategies that were implemented with great success. Relying on the PAG as the expert “go-to-resource” conveyed a sense of trust in their judgment to address a critical issue.

PAG influence on study dissemination

PAG members were engaged in the dissemination phase of the study, particularly in the development of the manuscripts. Starting with the study protocol manuscript, PAG had a pivotal role in shaping its focus, given their noted input in study design. Engaging patient partners in decisions about research dissemination of findings and knowledge gained in the research project increases a sense of shared governance and ownership, reflecting another CBPR principle. Decisions regarding dissemination began during PAG meetings, including identifying key processes and research findings to highlight in research manuscripts, engaging PAG members in data interpretation to ensure that findings included their voices and perspectives, and including them as co-authors of manuscripts (Table 2). This final level of engagement on the continuum is guided by another CBPR principle, which involves efforts to ensure that the partnership extends beyond the current research project, reflecting long-term commitment by all partners. The partnership established during the design, development, implementation, and dissemination of the SISTER type 2 diabetes project continues to guide future dissemination efforts, including PAG input on the current manuscript.

Limitations and future research

This description of how a PAG was engaged in and influenced study components across the research continuum is not without limitations, and more work is needed to understand the contexts, characteristics, and collaboration processes within which PAGs operate most effectively, including those beyond CBPR. The COVID-19 pandemic necessitated virtual implementation of PAG meetings from the beginning. Despite the potential for more active engagement during in-person meetings, the virtual format made it possible for some PAG members to attend despite changes in personal circumstances (e.g., acute illness, relocation due to a job change). Since retrospective recall was one method used to capture PAG influence, alongside review of meeting notes, and progress reports, it is possible that some contributions were not fully documented. Additionally, reliance on recall may introduce some risk of misrepresentation or simplification of PAG contributions.

The use of a pragmatic, non-systematic approach for documenting engagement and influence limited the extent to which various factors could be evaluated. This includes areas of quality, collaboration processes, and the conditions within which this type of engagement is most effective. Future research should examine the mechanisms through which PAG engagement influences research processes and outcomes (e.g., recruitment, intervention refinement, and dissemination), as well as how variations in PAG structure, facilitation, and context shape the nature and impact of engagement. Despite these limitations and given the limited number of examples of how to engage historically marginalized patient groups across the research continuum, this work fills an important gap in an emerging field. Further, the highlighted PAG model, as well as lessons learned from the incorporation of this intentional patient engagement strategy, may be adapted for use in other communities, contexts, or chronic conditions.

Implications for research and practice

The findings from this study underscore the importance of engaging patient populations, especially those who are historically marginalized patient groups, as active partners across the research continuum. The PAG members were not only instrumental in shaping the research design but also in ensuring cultural relevance for African American women with type 2 diabetes, enhancing recruitment strategies based on their lived experiences, and guiding intervention development. The PAG members’ involvement is a testament to the necessity of integrating patient perspectives throughout all research phases to improve study outcomes and foster trust within the patient community in ways researchers often fall short or are unable to do. Further, this study illustrates effective strategies to fully engage historically marginalized populations in a research project focused on mitigating disproportionate chronic disease burdens experienced by these same groups. Future patient-centered research should prioritize the use of PAGs and other models for consistently and authentically leveraging the lived experiences of patient communities to create culturally responsive, community-driven interventions.

Conclusion

Engaging patients across the research continuum is possible using a PAG model and can influence the research in a meaningful way. As documented here for the SISTER Diabetes Study, leveraging patients from historically marginalized groups as PAG members when developing health interventions for the same groups is highly recommended. This is particularly salient for addressing health disparities and improving the applicability of research outcomes for historically marginalized groups [4].
