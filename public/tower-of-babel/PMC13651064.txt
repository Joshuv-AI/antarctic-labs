Caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward pediatric dry eye disease: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wenji Wang, Hongchao Zhao, Ying Zeng, Meiyu Shi, Yanfang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) concerning pediatric dry eye disease.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted at the Pediatric Ophthalmology Outpatient Department of Yuxi People’s Hospital between December 2023 and April 2024. Demographic data and KAP scores were collected through questionnaires.ResultsA total of 515 valid questionnaires were collected. Of these, 393 respondents (76.31%) were mothers of the children, and 319 children (61.94%) had suffered from dry eye for less than three months. The participants’ mean knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were 7.57 ± 4.14 (possible range: 0–18), 36.76 ± 3.51 (possible range: 9–45), and 40.99 ± 7.13 (possible range: 11–55), respectively. Knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were significantly correlated with each other (all P < 0.001). Mediation analysis showed that knowledge (β = 0.19, P < 0.001), income (β = 0.75, P < 0.001), and marital status (β = 1.63, P = 0.001) were statistically associated with attitude. Meanwhile, knowledge (β = 0.51, P < 0.001), attitude (β = 0.55, P < 0.001), and residence (β = 1.51, P < 0.001) were statistically associated with practice. Further, knowledge (β = 0.10, P < 0.001), income (β = 0.38, P < 0.001), marital status (β = 0.83, P = 0.004) showed statistically modeled indirect associations with practice within the specified mediation framework.ConclusionsThis study revealed that caregivers exhibit inadequate knowledge but generally positive attitudes and moderately adequate yet inconsistent practices towards pediatric dry eye disease. The findings suggest that targeted educational programs may help address caregivers’ knowledge gaps and support more appropriate management practices for pediatric dry eye disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28287-z.

Background

Dry eye disease is highly prevalent, affecting millions of people worldwide with a prevalence ranging from 5% to 50% [1]. It is primarily characterized by symptoms such as burning or tingling sensations, a feeling of a foreign body in the eye, photophobia, and blurred vision [2]. Recent years have seen a rise in the incidence of dry eye disease in China, attributed to environmental pollution, the widespread use of electronic products, and lifestyle changes [3]. Additionally, there is a notable increase in the incidence among children; a cross-sectional study in China found that 15.9% of children visiting clinics for myopia also suffer from dry eye disease [4]. This trend underscores the need for increased awareness and targeted interventions to manage this growing public health concern.

The Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) theory is widely used to conceptualize health-related behaviors and is frequently utilized alongside KAP questionnaires to assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices within the healthcare domain, aiming to evaluate the acceptance and demand for relevant content [5]. Within this theoretical framework, knowledge, attitudes, and practices are considered interrelated constructs, and higher knowledge levels are generally associated with more positive attitudes and favorable health-related practices [6].

Diagnosing and treating pediatric dry eye disease pose unique challenges. Children’s limited ability to express themselves makes it easy to overlook early symptoms, leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment. Caregivers play a crucial role in managing pediatric dry eye disease, and their knowledge and attitudes may be associated with timely medical consultation, treatment adherence, and daily disease management [7]. Understanding the crucial role of caregivers in the prevention and management of this condition is vital due to the increasing prevalence of dry eye disease among children, driven by lifestyle changes and environmental factors. Given that young children often have difficulty recognizing and communicating their symptoms, caregivers play an essential role in early detection, timely medical consultation, and adherence to treatment recommendations.

This study aimed to investigate caregivers’ KAP regarding pediatric dry eye disease and explore KAP-related patterns in the health management of affected children. Compared with previous KAP studies [7, 8] that focused on general pediatric eye conditions, research specifically examining caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward pediatric dry eye disease is limited. The goal is to identify deficiencies in caregivers’ management strategies and provide scientific evidence for developing effective health education and intervention strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Pediatric Ophthalmology Outpatient Department of Yuxi People’s Hospital between December 2023 and April 2024. Participants were recruited consecutively in the outpatient department, and all eligible caregivers who attended the clinic during the study period were invited to participate. Inviting all eligible caregivers and documenting refusals helped minimize potential selection and non-response bias. The study subjects were parents or legal guardians of children who were clinically diagnosed with dry eye syndrome by pediatric ophthalmologists at Yuxi People’s Hospital. The inclusion criteria were: (1) Participants must be aged 18 years or older; (2) Participants must be the parent or legal guardian of a child diagnosed with dry eye syndrome; (3) Participants must be willing to participate in the study and provide signed informed consent; and (4) Participants must be proficient in reading and writing Chinese to accurately understand and complete the questionnaire. A trap question was embedded as the sixth item in the knowledge dimension: “The capital of China is Shanghai.” (True/False). The correct answer is “False”. This item was included as an attention-check measure to assess whether respondents were carefully reading and responding to the questionnaire rather than to evaluate disease-specific knowledge. Questionnaires with an incorrect response to this item were excluded to enhance data quality. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Participants who decline to take part in the study; (2) Participants with severe psychological disorders or cognitive impairments that may affect their ability to understand and complete the questionnaire; (3) Questionnaires completed in less than 1.5 min or with significant portions left unanswered or answered randomly. This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (2023kmykdx6f111) and obtained informed consent from the participants. All questionnaire data were stored on a password-protected server and were accessible only to the research team to ensure participant confidentiality.

Questionnaire introduction

After designing the questionnaire, we conducted a small-scale pilot test involving 44 participants, which yielded a reliability score of 0.902. The final version of the questionnaire, written in Chinese, comprised four sections: demographic data, and dimensions of knowledge, attitude, and practice. The knowledge dimension included 9 questions, with 2 points assigned for “very aware”, 1 point for “heard about it”, and 0 points for “not clear”, resulting in a total possible score range of 0–18. The attitude dimension featured 9 questions, utilizing a five-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” (1 point) to “strongly agree” (5 points), with a possible score range of 9–45. The practice dimension consisted of 11 questions, also on a five-point Likert scale, from “always” (5 points) to “never” (1 point), with a total possible score range of 11–55. Adequate knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practices were defined as scores exceeding 70% of the maximum possible score for each respective section, consistent with commonly adopted thresholds in KAP research to distinguish adequate versus inadequate levels [9, 10]. Although a formal Delphi-style expert panel process was not conducted, questionnaire items were generated based on current clinical knowledge of pediatric dry eye disease, the TFOS DEWS II report, relevant ophthalmology literature, and established KAP frameworks. The initial item pool was designed to cover core domains including symptom recognition, disease progression, risk factors, preventive behaviors, treatment adherence, caregiver attitudes, and family-based disease management. The draft questionnaire underwent multiple rounds of structured internal review to assess clarity, redundancy, and domain coverage. Subsequently, the instrument was independently reviewed by a methodological expert, a clinical pediatric ophthalmologist, and a public health specialist. These experts evaluated the comprehensiveness of pediatric-specific domains, clinical accuracy of treatment-related items, and behavioral relevance for caregivers, and provided recommendations for refinement. Minor revisions were made to improve wording clarity and domain alignment prior to formal data collection. The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the questionnaire was 0.9066, with the knowledge, attitude, and practice dimensions scoring 0.9425, 0.9545, and 0.8857, respectively. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value for the total scale reached 0.9167. In addition to reliability testing, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to further evaluate the construct validity of the questionnaire. Model fit indices demonstrated good construct validity, with CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR all meeting recommended thresholds. Detailed CFA results are presented in Table S1 and Figure S1. The demographic characteristics of the pilot sample, compared with those of the main study population, are summarized in Table S2. We distributed the questionnaires using the online platform Questionnaire Star.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using the formula for cross-sectional studies: n = (Z_(1 − α/2) / δ) ^2 × p × (1 − p), where Z_(1 − α/2) = 1.96 when α = 0.05, the assumed degree of variability of p = 0.5 maximizes the required sample size, and δ was set at 5%. The theoretical sample size was 480, including an additional 20% to account for potential loss during the study.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was conducted using STATA 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA). Continuous data are presented as means and standard deviations (SD), while categorical data are expressed as n (%). Based on the results of the normality analysis, intergroup comparisons for continuous variables were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed data and non-parametric tests for non-normally distributed data. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The correlation between knowledge, attitude, and practice scores was analyzed using Spearman’s correlation. Mediation analysis was conducted to examine the statistical associations among knowledge, attitude, and practice, with demographic variables included as covariates. Demographic covariates were selected based on statistically significant differences identified in univariate analyses (Table 1) after Bonferroni correction, as well as theoretical relevance to caregivers’ KAP. Variables demonstrating significant associations with at least one KAP dimension (P < 0.05) were considered for inclusion in the mediation model. In this cross-sectional study, the mediation/SEM pathways were specified to quantify statistical associations within the proposed framework and should not be interpreted as evidence of temporal ordering or causality. Categorical variables such as residence type, education level, employment status, income level, and marital status were entered into the model as dummy variables. Income was treated as an ordinal categorical variable according to the predefined income brackets, and residence type was coded using urban residence as the reference category. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to estimate the modeled direct and indirect associations among variables. The mediation/SEM model assumed linear relationships among continuous variables and no substantial unmeasured confounding between the mediator and outcome variables. Given the cross-sectional design, the model quantified statistical associations under these assumptions rather than establishing temporal or causal pathways. The mediation model specified knowledge as an independent variable, attitude as the mediator, and practice as the outcome, with demographic factors included as covariates. A two-sided P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because questionnaires with missing responses were excluded during data cleaning, the final dataset contained no missing data and no imputation procedures were required.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 647 questionnaires were collected, with 21 participants refusing to answer, leaving 626. The following samples were excluded: 99 participants under 18 years of age, 2 outliers, and 14 incorrect responses to trap questions, which resulted in 515 valid responses. Among them, 421 (81.75%) were females, 393 (76.31%) were mothers of the children, with mean age of 36.44 ± 5.76 years, 238 (46.21%) had high school/vocational school education, 319 (61.94%) of the child suffered from dry eye for less than 3 months, and 120 (23.3%) of the child had been using a particular eye drop (such as sodium hyaluronate eye drops or atropine eye drops) for a long period. The participants’ mean knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were 7.57 ± 4.14 (possible range: 0–18), 36.76 ± 3.51 (possible range: 9–45), and 40.99 ± 7.13 (possible range: 11–55), respectively. Using the predefined 70% threshold, 9.9% of caregivers demonstrated adequate knowledge, 95.0% showed positive attitudes, and 63.5% achieved proactive practice levels.

Analyses of demographic characteristics after Bonferroni correction showed that participants’ knowledge, attitude, and practice scores varied significantly across residence type (P = 0.009, P = 0.003, and P < 0.001), education level (P < 0.001, P = 0.009, and P < 0.001), and employment status (P < 0.001, —, P = 0.022). Differences were also observed across household’s average monthly income per person (P = 0.030, P < 0.001, and P = 0.216), and whether the child had been using a particular eye drop for a long period (P = 0.114, P = 0.138, and P = 0.009). Additionally, marital status remained associated with knowledge and attitude scores (P = 0.005 and P = 0.034), while relationships with the child showed a difference in attitude scores (P = 0.033). No significant differences were found across duration of disease (all P > 0.999). Details are provided in Table 1.

Knowledge, attitude, and practice dimensions

The distribution of knowledge dimensions shown that the three questions with the highest number of participants choosing the “Not clear” option were “Mild and moderate dry eye disease is reversible, while the causes of severe dry eye are complex and mostly irreversible.” (K3) with 42.52%, “Recurrent pediatric dry eye disease can lead to conditions such as meibomian gland inflammation, meibomian gland cysts, and styes (hordeolum).” (K9) with 38.83%, and “In the later stages of pediatric dry eye disease, symptoms may include red and swollen eyes, congestion, burning sensation, filamentous attachments on the cornea, corneal opacity, and blurred vision.” (K2) with 37.48%. Responses to the attitude dimension showed that more than 90% of the participants had a positive attitude, with choosing either “strongly agree” or “agree”. It is worth noting that 6.41% of the participants were neutral on the question of whether they were worried about the sequelae of dry eye (A5) and whether they believed that pediatric dry eye could be completely cured by following medical advice (A6). When it comes to related attitudes, 21.55% never participated in educational courses, lectures, or workshops related to pediatric dry eye disease (P11), 8.54% never supervised their child to do eye exercises every day (P3), and 6.8% never followed medical advice to arrange for their child to take medication or use artificial tears, etc. (P8) (Table S3).

Spearman correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis showed that there were significant positive correlations between knowledge and attitude (r = 0.2316, P < 0.001) as well as practice (r = 0.3998, P < 0.001). Also, there was a correlation between attitude and practice (r = 0.3238, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Mediation analysis

The mediation analysis showed that knowledge (β = 0.19, P < 0.001), income (β = 0.75, P < 0.001), and marital status (β = 1.63, P = 0.001) were statistically associated with attitude within the specified model. Meanwhile, knowledge (β = 0.51, P < 0.001), attitude (β = 0.55, P < 0.001), and residence (β = 1.51, P < 0.001) were statistically associated with practice. Further, knowledge (β = 0.10, P < 0.001), income (β = 0.38, P < 0.001), and marital status (β = 0.83, P = 0.004) showed statistically modeled indirect associations with practice via attitude (Table 3 and Fig. 1). The model demonstrated good fit (RMSEA value: 0.000, SRMR value: 0.009, TLI value: 1.032, and CFI value: 1.000) (Table S4). Given the cross-sectional design, these pathways should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than temporal or causal effects.

Discussion

Caregivers demonstrated inadequate knowledge, positive attitudes, and overall moderately adequate but inconsistent practices concerning pediatric dry eye disease. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, the observed relationships among knowledge, attitudes, and practices should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than evidence of causal or temporal pathways. This study underscores the importance of targeted educational interventions aimed at caregivers, which may potentially influence the management of pediatric dry eye disease [8]. Implementing such programs in clinical settings may help address caregivers’ knowledge gaps and may support more favorable attitudes and practices related to this condition.

The findings of this study underscore significant disparities in caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices towards pediatric dry eye disease. For instance, caregivers from urban and suburban areas demonstrated better knowledge and practices than those from rural settings. This variation likely reflects differential access to healthcare resources and information dissemination channels. Urban residents often have greater access to healthcare services and educational materials, which is supported by literature indicating that urbanization levels correlate with healthcare literacy and engagement [11].

The impacts of education, employment, and income are pivotal in understanding caregivers’ KAP towards pediatric dry eye disease. Education serves as a foundational element, with higher educational levels correlating with improved KAP scores, a reflection of enhanced health literacy [11, 12]. This educational advantage facilitates a deeper understanding of health-related information, enabling more informed healthcare decisions. Furthermore, employment status contributes to this dynamic, where employed caregivers generally report higher KAP scores. This correlation likely stems from better socio-economic status associated with employment, which facilitates access to healthcare information and services. Income further complements these effects, with higher income levels enabling families to afford better healthcare and engage more deeply with health education, and was statistically associated with more favorable practice and attitude scores within the proposed model.

Interestingly, no significant differences were observed in practices based on marital status, despite its direct influence on attitudes and indirect influence on practices via SEM. This might suggest that while marital status was statistically associated with caregivers’ emotional and attitudinal readiness to manage the disease, it was not significantly associated with their reported practices. However, the specific mechanisms underlying this pattern remain unclear and should be explored in future research.

Moreover, the positive correlations between knowledge and attitudes, as well as between knowledge and practices, were consistent with the SEM results, in which knowledge was statistically associated with both attitudes and practices within the specified model. This emphasizes the potential value of targeted educational programs that may improve caregivers’ knowledge and may be associated with more positive attitudes and better care practices. Previous literature has consistently reported associations between enhanced knowledge about a disease and improved care behaviors and attitudes, which is consistent with our findings [6, 7].

The study results indicate a significant gap in caregivers’ awareness about pediatric dry eye disease, especially concerning its early symptoms, progression, and risk factors. Most caregivers have only heard about the symptoms and conditions but do not have clear or thorough knowledge [7]. Based on these findings, educational initiatives may help address existing knowledge gaps. Potential approaches include distributing easy-to-understand brochures at pediatric and ophthalmology clinics, organizing interactive workshops that explain dry eye symptoms and risks in detail, integrating eye health education into school health programs, and utilizing digital platforms commonly accessed by parents to disseminate educational information [13].

While the attitude towards the seriousness of pediatric dry eye disease is generally positive, there are notable deficiencies in the perception of its impact on a child’s life and the effectiveness of preventive measures. Although most caregivers agree that dry eye disease can significantly affect daily activities and studies, a considerable proportion remains neutral or disagrees. These findings suggest that additional communication during clinical encounters may be beneficial. Healthcare providers may consider emphasizing the potential long-term consequences of untreated dry eye and discussing how visual symptoms may affect children’s daily functioning and academic activities [6, 7].

Although the overall mean practice score exceeded the predefined 70% threshold, several critical behaviors showed suboptimal adherence patterns, particularly in supervising daily eye exercises, ensuring sufficient outdoor activity, and consistent participation in educational activities. For example, a notable proportion of caregivers reported “never” supervising daily eye exercises or participating in educational activities, indicating uneven engagement across specific recommended practices. Therefore, the term “inconsistent practices” reflects distributional patterns across key behaviors rather than the overall mean score alone. These practices are essential for preventing and managing dry eye symptoms effectively. To improve this, healthcare professionals could develop simple, clear guidelines for parents to follow at home. These might include scheduled reminders for eye exercises, outdoor activities, and regular medical check-ups sent via mobile health apps specifically designed for pediatric eye care. Partnering with schools to monitor and promote healthy eye habits among students could also be beneficial [14]. These findings may inform clinical practice and community health planning by emphasizing the need to integrate caregiver-focused dry eye education into routine pediatric care.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causal relationships between caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices and the management outcomes of pediatric dry eye disease. Second, our findings may not be generalizable beyond the demographic context of Yuxi People’s Hospital, as participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital outpatient clinic in Yunnan Province. Healthcare access, health literacy levels, and service utilization patterns in this setting may differ from those in primary care environments or other regions. In addition, the sample was predominantly composed of mothers, and most children had a relatively short disease duration (< 3 months). This caregiver composition and disease profile may have influenced the observed KAP scores, as mothers may be more actively involved in daily caregiving, and early-stage disease may prompt greater healthcare engagement. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to caregivers in rural-only populations, primary healthcare settings, or families managing long-standing or chronic pediatric dry eye disease. The KAP patterns observed in this study may differ in contexts with limited ophthalmologic resources or among caregivers of children with more prolonged disease trajectories. Third, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires may have introduced response bias, as participants might provide socially desirable answers or misinterpret questions, potentially affecting the accuracy of reported knowledge and practices related to pediatric dry eye disease. In addition, the use of a general attention-check (trap) question may have unintentionally excluded individuals with lower general literacy or reading comprehension levels. Although this measure was intended to improve data reliability, it may have introduced selection bias and potentially led to a modest overestimation of knowledge levels and overall KAP scores. Such social desirability bias may have led some caregivers to overreport positive attitudes or optimal practices, which should be considered when interpreting the findings. In addition, there is currently no universally accepted cutoff for categorizing adequate knowledge, positive attitudes, or proactive practices in KAP research. Although we adopted the commonly used 70% threshold, alternative classification criteria could yield different prevalence estimates for these categories. However, such variations in categorization would be unlikely to materially affect the overall patterns observed in the correlation analyses and SEM results, which were based primarily on continuous KAP scores. Future studies could also examine the quality of communication between healthcare providers and caregivers to better understand how clinical interactions shape caregivers’ KAP. As participants were recruited from a single outpatient clinic, the findings may not be fully generalizable to caregivers in other settings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, caregivers demonstrated a concerning gap in knowledge while maintaining positive attitudes and overall moderately adequate but inconsistent practices regarding pediatric dry eye disease, suggesting an urgent need for targeted educational interventions. Clinically, comprehensive educational programs aimed at improving caregiver knowledge and skills may be beneficial for supporting disease management and promoting recommended caregiving practices for pediatric dry eye disease. Such programs may include clinic-based educational workshops, digital instructional materials delivered through mobile health platforms, and parent information sessions conducted in collaboration with schools. However, the implementation of these programs may face practical challenges, including limited caregiver time, variable access to digital resources, and the need for sustained engagement.
