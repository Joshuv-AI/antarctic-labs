Above-Elbow Casts With the Wrist and Hand Free vs. Standard Above-Elbow Casts With the Wrist and Hand Included in the Treatment of Supracondylar Humerus Fractures in Children: A Randomized Prospective Study
Authors: G. Dillon Graham, Calvin Chandler, Kennedy Gachigi, Caleb Michalek, Joshua Steinke, Brian Brighton, Michael Paloski, Brian Scannell, Virginia Casey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSupracondylar humerus fractures are the most common elbow fractures in the pediatric population. Despite evolving management concepts, there is limited evidence comparing different cast types for immobilization following operative management. This study compared above-elbow casts that leave the wrist and hand free (experimental group) versus standard above-elbow casts that include the wrist and hand for the postoperative treatment of supracondylar humerus fractures in children.MethodsIn this randomized prospective study, 255 patients aged 3–10 years with isolated, operatively treated (Gartland Type II and III) supracondylar humerus fractures were randomized, following closed reduction and percutaneous pinning, to receive either a postoperative cast that did not include the wrist or hand (n = 124) or a postoperative cast that included the wrist and hand (n = 131). The primary outcome was the Quick Disability of Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (QuickDASH) questionnaire score. Secondary outcomes included range of motion (ROM), dexterity, pain, radiographic carrying angle, and functional results at 3–4 weeks, 9 weeks, and 1-year post-treatment.ResultsThe experimental group demonstrated lower QuickDASH scores at 3–4 weeks (26.0 vs. 36.0, P < .0001). At 9 weeks, the experimental group showed slightly higher QuickDASH scores (5.6 vs. 4.1, P = .2). Dexterity was higher in the experimental group at 3–4 weeks measured by the peg-board test (P < .0001). Range of motion measurements at 9 weeks revealed better elbow extension and similar values in elbow flexion, forearm rotation, wrist motion, radiographic carrying angle, and pain scores between groups.ConclusionsAbove-elbow casts, leaving the wrist and hand free, demonstrated non-inferiority compared to standard casts including the hand for treating pediatric supracondylar humerus fractures. Although early (3–4 week) QuickDASH scores were statistically lower in the wrist and hand free group, this difference did not exceed the minimal clinically important difference, and long-term results were comparable between groups. The wrist and hand free group completed in-cast dexterity testing more quickly at 3–4 weeks. Importantly, leaving the wrist and hand free permits direct assessment of the radial pulse and hand perfusion. Wrist and hand free casting is a viable alternative that maintains appropriate fracture immobilization.Key Concepts(1)Above-elbow casts with the wrist and hand free demonstrate non-inferior functional outcomes compared to above-elbow casts with the hand and wrist included for the postoperative treatment of pediatric supracondylar humerus fractures.(2)Patients treated with the wrist and hand free showed significantly better early functional scores (QuickDASH) and superior dexterity at 3-4 weeks compared to the wrist and hand included patients.(3)Range of motion, pain scores, and radiographic carrying angles were comparable between groups at 9 weeks and 1 year, confirming that wrist and hand free casting does not compromise fracture healing or alignment.(4)Above-elbow casting with the wrist and hand free offers practical clinical advantages, including easier postoperative vascular assessment of the hand and improved patient hygiene during the immobilization period.(5)This is the first prospective randomized study (n = 255) to directly compare hand inclusion versus exclusion in cast immobilization following operative fixation of supracondylar humerus fractures in children.Level of Evidence2 (Randomized controlled trial)

Introduction

Supracondylar humerus fractures are the most common elbow fractures in the pediatric population, accounting for approximately 60% of all elbow fractures in children [1]. Management of these fractures has evolved over time, with current treatment guided primarily by the Gartland classification system [2]. While operative versus nonoperative management has been well studied, there is limited evidence comparing different immobilization techniques following operative treatment.

A recent study on U.S. national trends in the treatment of supracondylar humerus fractures revealed that most patients are treated definitively with cast immobilization [3]. Patients who undergo surgical intervention typically require a period of cast immobilization postoperatively. Despite the frequency of cast usage, there is minimal research examining the optimal type of cast for these injuries.

Traditional casting techniques for supracondylar humerus fractures typically involve including the hand in the cast to prevent forearm rotation and maintain elbow position. Many of the authors were taught historically that stopping the cast above the wrist would cause hand swelling and would be problematic. However, another experienced pediatric orthopaedic surgeon in the group treated all patients in an above-elbow cylinder cast with the wrist and hand free. This surgeon anecdotally reported no problems with this technique and pointed out many advantages. Postoperatively, it is much easier to evaluate the perfusion of the hand with the radial pulse available for palpation and Doppler assessment. Immobilizing the wrist and hand may lead to unnecessary functional limitations while the patient is casted, particularly for activities of daily living such as writing, eating, and self-care. Furthermore, having the hand free also allows for improved hand hygiene in addition to other personal care.

An above-elbow cast that immobilizes the elbow while leaving the wrist and hand free might provide adequate fracture stabilization while allowing improved perfusion and evaluation of hand function during the healing period.

To date, no studies have directly compared standard above-elbow casting with wrist and hand included versus above-elbow casting that leaves the wrist and hand free for the postoperative management of supracondylar humerus fractures in children. While some studies have looked at different cast materials as well as forearm positioning and timing of immobilization [[4], [5], [6], [7]], none have specifically examined the effect of hand inclusion versus exclusion in the cast. The purpose of this study was to compare functional outcomes, range of motion, radiographic results, and triage phone calls related to the cast between these two casting techniques. We hypothesized that cylindrical above-elbow casts with the wrist and hand free would result in non-inferior functional and radiographic outcomes compared to standard above-elbow casts including the wrist and hand, while potentially improving patient safety, comfort, and daily function during the treatment period.

Methods

Study design

This was a weekly randomized prospective study conducted at a level-one metropolitan trauma center between June 2019 and March 2024. Patients in this study were treated by four fellowship-trained pediatric orthopaedic surgeons who maintained consistent management protocols throughout the study period, including standardized surgical techniques and postoperative care. The study protocol was approved by the Atrium Health Institutional Review Board (IRB00083243), and informed consent was obtained from all participants' guardians.

Participants

Children aged 3–10 years who presented with an isolated, closed supracondylar humerus fracture without neurovascular compromise were eligible for inclusion. Only operative (Types 2 and 3) supracondylar humerus fractures were included by design. Patients were excluded if they had additional orthopaedic injuries, were unable or unwilling to adhere to standard intervals, or had cognitive delays that would hinder their ability to perform dexterity testing or complete questionnaires. Race and ethnicity were not included in our study.

Randomization and interventions

Patients were randomized using a computer-generated randomization table that predetermined the weekly schedule for cast type. Group A (experimental cast group) received an above-elbow fiberglass cast that did not include the wrist and hand, while Group B (standard cast group) received an above-elbow fiberglass cast that included the hand. Both cast types were applied following standard clinical protocols according to the treating surgeon's technique for above-elbow cast application.

Surgical technique

All patients in this study underwent closed reduction and percutaneous pinning using lateral-entry pins exclusively. No crossed pin configurations were used. Patients received either 2 or 3 lateral pins, depending on fracture stability and surgeon preference, with pins placed in a divergent configuration to maximize stability while avoiding convergence at the fracture site. Surgical procedural time was defined as the time from anesthesia readiness with the patient draped and positioned to procedure completion when the cast application was finished.

Casting protocol

All casts were applied with the elbow positioned in approximately 70 degrees of flexion and the forearm in neutral rotation. The experimental group's cast did not include the hand and was terminated proximal to the wrist, leaving the wrist free for movement (Figure 2, Figure 3). All casts were applied with 2–3 layers of webril padding, followed by 3–4 layers of fiberglass application rolled with the stretch-relax technique. All casts were molded with both a supracondylar mold above the elbow and an interosseous mold of the forearm. These were subsequently univalved with a longitudinal medial split to accommodate postoperative swelling. All casts remained in place for 3–4 weeks and were removed at the initial postoperative study visit after completion of functional assessments, including the pegboard test.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was the Quick Disability of Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (QuickDASH) questionnaire score, a validated patient-reported outcome measure for upper extremity function [8,9]. Parents were enlisted to help fill out the forms with their children. QuickDASH scores were obtained at 3–4 weeks postoperative and 9 weeks postoperative. Scores were collected by the research staff in our clinic. Secondary outcomes included range of motion (ROM) measurements of the elbow (flexion/extension), forearm (pronation/supination), and wrist (flexion/extension, ulnar/radial deviation) at 9 weeks. Range of motion assessments were performed with a goniometer. Pain scores were assessed using the Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NPRS), and radiographic carrying angle was measured at 9 weeks. Additional outcomes included dexterity testing using a pegboard at 3–4 weeks. The pegboard test was selected because it is a simple, reproducible, and child-friendly quantitative measure of upper-extremity fine-motor dexterity that could be administered with the cast in place and was well tolerated by young children. The pegboard test was the total time in seconds it took the patient to successfully pick up and put all the pegs into the board one by one and then remove them one by one while in the cast on the affected arm. Both the dominant and non-dominant hand were tested in every patient, with the time for each hand recorded so that each patient's contralateral, uninjured hand could serve as an internal reference for baseline dexterity; two trials were performed for each hand (Fig. 4). Dexterity (pegboard) testing was performed at the 3–4 week visit with the cast in place, as this is the only interval during which the two cast constructs impose differing constraints on hand and wrist use. Range of motion and the remaining secondary outcomes were assessed at the 9-week visit following cast removal, at which point both groups were unconstrained, and a between-group difference in dexterity would not be expected.

Statistical analysis

Based on power calculations using previous studies of QuickDASH scores [[10], [11], [12]], a sample size of 122 patients per group was determined to be sufficient to detect non-inferiority with 90% power and an alpha of 0.05. The non-inferiority margin was set at 15.9 points, in accordance with established minimally clinically important differences for the QuickDASH [13].

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables were reported as frequencies with percentages. Differences between groups were analyzed using two-sample t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for non-normally distributed data. Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests were used for categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Primary outcome: QuickDASH scores

At 3–4 weeks post-treatment, the mean QuickDASH score was 26.0 ± 16.9 in the experimental group compared to 36.0 ± 17.4 in the standard cast group (P < .0001), representing a 10.0-point difference favoring the experimental group.

At 9 weeks, the mean QuickDASH score was 5.6 ± 7.6 in the experimental group compared to 4.1 ± 7.8 in the standard cast group. (P = .2). Both groups demonstrated excellent functional recovery by 9 weeks. Secondary outcomes

Pain scores

Pain scores were similar between groups at both assessment points. At 3–4 weeks, the mean pain score was 0.5 ± 1.0 in the experimental group and 0.6 ± 1.2 in the standard cast group (P = .341). At 9 weeks, both groups reported minimal pain with identical mean scores of 0.3 ± 0.8 (P = .711).

Dexterity testing

Dexterity testing with a pegboard at 3–4 weeks showed that patients with above-elbow casts with the wrist and hand free generally performed better than those with standard above-elbow casts with the wrist and hand included. For the dominant hand, patients in the experimental group averaged 33.5 ± 25.6 s compared to 55.9 ± 42.3 s in the standard cast group (P < .0001). For the non-dominant hand, patients in the experimental group averaged 36.4 ± 18.0 s compared to 52.8 ± 30.8 s in the standard cast group (P < .0001).

Radiographic outcomes

The radiographic carrying angle measured at 9 weeks was comparable between groups: 7.8° ± 4.8° in the experimental group and 8.0° ± 4.3° in the standard cast group.

Phone call triage data

There were a total of 6 phone calls or triage messages related to cast issues. Five were from the standard cast group and included arm swelling, cast tightness or rubbing around the hand, and the cast getting wet, requiring a cast change. One was from the experimental group for itching. There were no issues regarding cast slippage or fitting in the experimental group.

Complications

Perioperative and postoperative complications were recorded for all patients in the study. None of the patients in this study experienced a complication. Specifically, there were no instances of loss of fracture reduction, fracture displacement, or pin migration, and no patient in either group required revision surgery or an unplanned cast change for loss of fracture position. No patient in the wrist and hand free group experienced cast slippage or loss of fracture alignment attributable to the cast not incorporating the wrist and hand.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective randomized study comparing the management of supracondylar humerus fractures in children treated with above-elbow casts that left the wrist and hand free and standard above-elbow casts that included the hand. Our primary finding was that casts with the wrist and hand free demonstrated non-inferiority to standard casts, including wrist and hand, as measured by the QuickDASH questionnaire [8,9]. We observed that patients in the experimental group completed the pegboard dexterity test more quickly at the 3–4 week assessment and had statistically lower early QuickDASH scores. The difference in QuickDASH scores, however, did not reach the minimally clinically important difference for the QuickDASH score [13]. Accordingly, these early findings are most appropriately interpreted as demonstrating non-inferiority of the wrist and hand free cast rather than clinical superiority over the standard cast.

These results have potentially important clinical implications. Traditionally, cast immobilization following these fractures has included the hand to prevent forearm rotation [1], which theoretically could compromise fracture reduction or fixation by transmitting torsional forces to the fracture site. Previous studies have demonstrated that alternative forms of immobilization, such as posterior splinting, may have equivalent functional outcomes, though complications such as splint breakdown were commonly reported [14,15]. Our findings suggest that an above-elbow cast that leaves the wrist and hand free provides adequate immobilization while potentially allowing for improved functionality during the healing period.

Additional benefits of leaving the wrist and hand free include evaluation of pulses, hand circulation, and hand hygiene. Postoperatively, it is ideal to be able to check and document a radial pulse, which is possible with a cast with wrist and hand free, but not with a standard cast including wrist and hand, unless a window is cut. Having the hand free also allows for improved hand washing and use of hand sanitizer. Importantly, leaving the wrist and hand free did not result in cast slipping or other cast problems. In fact, more cast problems were documented in the group, with the wrist and hand included. A theoretical concern with omitting the wrist and hand is that a child could slide the extremity out of an inadequately molded cast, which could compromise immobilization and allow fracture displacement. In our experience, this risk is mitigated by appropriate cast technique: every cast was applied with both a supracondylar mold above the elbow and an interosseous mold of the forearm, which together secure the cast against the proximal forearm and elbow and resist distal migration even when the hand is not incorporated. With this technique, we observed no episodes of the cast sliding off, loss of fracture position, or non-compliance-related displacement in the wrist and hand free group.

The 10-point difference in QuickDASH scores at 3–4 weeks falls within the pre-defined non-inferiority margin of 15.9 points [13] and may be attributed to improved ability to use the hand for daily activities, maintenance of hand and finger dexterity, and potentially less discomfort.

As expected, this difference diminished at 9-week and 1-year follow-ups, with both groups approaching QuickDASH scores of zero, consistent with previous long-term outcome studies [10,11].

Range of motion measurements at 9 weeks showed better elbow extension in the experimental group, comparable to normative values reported in the literature [16]. The improved elbow motion may be related to less stiffness from the more limited casting area or earlier mobilization of the hand and wrist, similar to the findings reported by Vuckov et al. [5]. However, range of motion has been shown to follow predictable recovery patterns, with most patients recovering full ROM at 35 days following cast removal [17].

Pain scores were similar between groups, indicating that the above-elbow cast with the wrist and hand free provides sufficient immobilization to maintain patient comfort. The consistently low pain scores in both groups indicate that neither casting technique causes significant discomfort during the recovery period. Dexterity testing results support the hypothesis that the above-elbow cast with the hand and wrist free allows for improved dexterity with the cast in place. Radiographic carrying angles were nearly identical, confirming that both techniques maintain acceptable anatomic alignment.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include its prospective randomized design, relatively large sample size, and use of validated outcome measures [8,9]. Multiple time points allowed for evaluation of both short-term and long-term outcomes. Assessors could not be blinded, as the cast type was visible in clinic. Finally, while our sample size was adequate for the primary outcome, some secondary analyses may have been underpowered to detect small but clinically meaningful differences.

Conclusion

This prospective randomized study demonstrated that above-elbow casts that leave the wrist and hand free provide non-inferior outcomes compared to standard above-elbow casts that include the wrist and hand for the treatment of supracondylar humerus fractures in children. Although early QuickDASH scores were statistically lower in the wrist and hand free group, this difference did not exceed the minimal clinically important difference, and long-term results were comparable between groups. Wrist and hand free casting represents a viable alternative that may offer advantages in early function and patient convenience without compromising fracture healing or alignment. As vascular compromise and Volkmann's ischemic contracture is the most dreaded complication of these injuries, leaving the wrist and hand free to facilitate easy assessment of the radial pulse and hand perfusion is advantageous for early diagnosis of vascular insufficiency before irreversible damage occurs. Future work includes assessment of long-term follow-up, and comparison of casting techniques in the nonoperative treatment of supracondylar humerus fractures in children.
