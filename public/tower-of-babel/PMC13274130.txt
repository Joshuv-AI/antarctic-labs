Burden of HPV-associated cancers in Peruvian men: Evidence from national health data
Authors: Iris Otoya, Magaly Blas, Zira Mendes da Silva, Cassia Veiga Pereira, Juan Carlos Orengo, Paula Cristina Pungartnik, Cintia Irene Parellada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHPV-associated cancers in men represent a growing public health concern in Latin America, yet evidence on burden remains limited. We analyzed national data in Peru (2015–2019) to estimate outpatient consultations, hospitalization, and death rates for HPV-associated cancers and applied site-specific attributable fractions to derive HPV-attributable burden. Rates were age-standardized (ASR) to the WHO World Standard Population. Sensitivity analysis excluded mortality data for 2015–2016 to account for improvements in cause‑of‑death completeness. Between 2015 and 2019, 5,996 outpatient consultations for HPV-associated cancers in men were recorded (ASR: 8.2 per 100,000), mainly due to head and neck (56.3%) and penile (30.4%) cancers. A total of 1,136 hospitalizations were identified (ASR: 1.6 per 100,000), with head and neck and penile cancers accounting for 46.3% and 45.9% of total hospitalizations, respectively. Rates increased with age, reaching 38.0 outpatient consultations and 7.5 hospitalizations per 100,000 among men ≥60 years, but were also present among younger (<30 years) and middle-aged (30–59 years) men. Applying attributable fractions, 2,056 outpatient consultations (ASR: 2.8) and 402 hospitalizations (ASR: 0.6) were HPV-attributable, with penile cancer contributing the largest share. A total of 859 deaths from HPV-associated cancers were recorded (ASR: 1.2), of which 208 were HPV-attributable (ASR: 0.3), predominantly among men aged ≥60 years (77.2%). Sensitivity analysis had minimal impact on mortality estimates. HPV‑attributable cancers impose a substantial and preventable burden on Peruvian men. Strengthening universal HPV vaccination, ensuring high coverage, expanding multi‑age catch‑up strategies, and improving access to early diagnosis and treatment are essential to reduce future burden.KEYWORDS: Human papillomavirus, HPV-associated cancers, males, penile, anal, head and neck, mortality, hospitalization, HPV vaccination

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most prevalent sexually transmitted infections worldwide, and its persistent infection is a well-established cause of multiple cancers.1,2 While cervical cancer is the most recognized HPV-attributable malignancy, HPV also contributes to cancers in men, including anal, penile, and head and neck cancers (oropharynx, oral cavity, and larynx).1,3 Unlike cervical cancer, which benefits from organized screening programs, there are no routine screening strategies for these cancers in men. Consequently, they are often diagnosed at advanced stages, leading to poorer clinical outcomes.4,5

In 2022, HPV-attributable cancers in men were estimated to account for 95,407 new cases and 44,038 related deaths worldwide, representing approximately 11% of the global HPV cancer burden.6 Over the past two decades, several countries including the United States, Norway, and the United Kingdom have reported increasing incidence rates of HPV-attributable cancers in men, which now account for up to 40% of all HPV-attributable cancers in both sexes.7–10 In the United States and Norway, oropharyngeal cancer in men has surpassed cervical cancer as the most common HPV-attributable cancer.7,8 These shifts likely reflect two converging trends: sustained progress in reducing cervical cancer in women and a growing burden of HPV-related cancers in men.7,11,12

This evolving burden underscores the need to reassess male-focused prevention strategies. Following the licensure of HPV vaccines in 2006, early policies prioritized cervical cancer, leading most national immunization programs to focus on adolescent girls. In Latin America, recommendations to include boys were introduced gradually, with a mean delay of 6.2 years in implementation compared with girls.13 Peru illustrates this delay: a school-based HPV-vaccination program for girls aged 10–15 years began in 2011, was interrupted due to supply constraints, resumed in 2015, and only extended to boys in 2023.14

In Peru, HPV research has largely focused on cervical cancer, with limited evidence on the burden of HPV‑associated cancers among men.15–19 Moreover, population‑based cancer incidence data have limited national coverage, capturing mainly selected urban areas of the country.17 In this context, routinely collected national health service and vital registration data provide a valuable alternative for assessing the population-level burden of HPV-associated cancers. These data sources enable the use of healthcare encounters as proxies for disease burden in fragmented health systems, while mortality data complement these measures by capturing severe outcomes at the population level.

Using data from 2015–2019, we aimed to quantify the burden of HPV-associated cancers among Peruvian men based on outpatient consultations, hospitalizations, and deaths. We further estimated the proportion attributable to HPV by applying site-specific attributable fractions (AFs). These findings provide population-level evidence to inform prevention and cancer control strategies in the context of the evolving epidemiology of HPV-associated cancers among men in Peru.

Materials and methods

Study design, data sources, and population

We conducted a retrospective observational study using national administrative data from the Peruvian Ministry of Health (MINSA) from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2019. Data sources included the Outpatient Information System (Sistema de Información HIS), the Hospital Discharge Information System (Sistema de Información de Egresos), the National Death Registry Information System (Sistema Informático Nacional de Defunciones - SINADEF), and the National Institute of Statistics and Informatics (INEI). All databases were accessed through the Repositorio Único Nacional de Información en Salud (REUNIS), MINSA’s centralized platform for integrating and managing public health information.20,21 All databases used in this study are de-identified.

Population coverage

The analyzed databases include outpatient consultations and hospital discharges from MINSA facilities, as well as those financed by Seguro Integral de Salud (SIS), with substantial overlap in coverage since SIS beneficiaries receive care within MINSA facilities. Together, these sources cover approximately 70% of Peru’s population during the specified period.22,23 The unit of analysis was healthcare encounters, including outpatient consultations and hospitalizations, analyzed as events rather than unique patients, as individual‑level linkage across databases was not available. Accordingly, results should be interpreted as measures of healthcare utilization and burden captured through health system contacts, rather than incidence at the individual level.

SINADEF, an electronic death registration system in Peru, was initiated in August 2016 and scaled nationally through 2017. Prior to its implementation, coverage of deaths with a medically certified cause of death was modest (54%), with a high proportion of ill‑defined or poorly coded causes (garbage codes).24 Following its implementation, coverage increased to approximately 70%.24,25 The proportion of online death certification rose from 29% in 2017 to 71.6% in 2019, and the proportion of certificates with a valid underlying cause-of-death coding increased from 69.6% in 2016 to 78.8% in 2019.24–27

Outcomes and definitions

Primary outcomes were the number and rates of outpatient consultations, hospitalizations, and deaths due to HPV-associated cancers in men by cancer type and age group, and the estimated HPV-attributable burden obtained by applying site-specific AFs. HPV-associated cancers were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes recorded as the primary diagnosis for outpatient consultations and hospital discharge, and as the underlying cause in the mortality database, corresponding to invasive cancers occurring at anatomic sites where HPV-DNA is frequently detected, irrespective of individual HPV confirmation. Specifically: anal cancer (C21.0-C21.8), penile cancer (C60.0-C60.9), oral cavity cancer (C02.0-C02.3, C03.0-C03.9, C04.0-C04.9, C05.0, C06.0-C06.9), oropharyngeal cancer (C01, C02.4, C05.1, C05.2, C09.0-C09.9, C10.0, C10.2-C10.9), and laryngeal cancer (C32.0-C32.9) (Supplemental Table S1).

HPV-attributable outpatient consultations, hospitalizations, and deaths were defined as those likely caused by HPV and were estimated using site-specific HPV-AFs. To estimate these fractions, we reviewed recent literature, prioritizing large-scale global and Latin American studies. Based on this review, the following attributable fraction values were applied: 100% for anal cancer, 51.0% for penile cancer, 6.3% for oral cavity cancer, 21.2% for oropharyngeal cancer, and 4.5% for laryngeal cancer (Supplemental Table S2).2,28,29 AFs were applied uniformly across ages due to limited availability of age-specific estimates.

Statistical analysis

Data from 2015–2019 were aggregated, and annual counts and rates for outpatient consultations, hospitalizations, and deaths were calculated. Rates were calculated as the total number of events divided by the corresponding male population denominators obtained from INEI for the same period, expressed per 100,000 men. HPV-attributable counts were estimated by applying site-specific AFs to the observed counts, and corresponding rates were calculated. To further characterize healthcare utilization patterns, we calculated the outpatient-to-inpatient ratio, defined as the number of outpatient consultations divided by the number of hospitalizations.

Population denominators were based on the 2017 census and official projections for intercensal years, from INEI.30 Estimates were not adjusted for healthcare system coverage (70%) and should be interpreted as indicators of healthcare system capturing burden, rather than population incidence. Age-standardized rates (ASR) were computed using the World Health Organization standard population (2000–2025).26

Analyses were stratified by cancer type (anal, penile, and head and neck cancers, the latter analyzed collectively and separately [oral cavity, oropharynx, larynx]) and age group (overall, <30, 30–59 and ≥60 years). A sensitivity analysis excluded 2015 and 2016 mortality data to address potential underreporting prior to full SINADEF implementation; outpatient and hospitalization analyses included all years.

This study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee (CIEI) of Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia (Approval No. 209609), under the exempt review category, on September 8, 2022, with validity until September 7, 2027.

Results

Outpatient consultations for HPV-associated cancers

Between 2015 and 2019, a total of 5,996 outpatient consultations for HPV-associated cancers were recorded among men, corresponding to an annual average of 1,199 consultations and an ASR of 8.2 per 100,000 men. Head and neck cancers accounted for the majority of consultations (56.3%), followed by penile cancer (30.4%) and anal cancer (13.3%) (Table 1). Within head and neck cancers, oral cavity cancer represented the largest share (43.6%), with oropharyngeal and laryngeal cancers contributing 27.0% and 29.4%, respectively (Table 1).

Outpatient consultations for HPV-attributable cancers

After applying site-specific HPV AFs, an estimated 2,056 outpatient consultations were attributable to HPV over the study period, corresponding to an ASR of 2.8 per 100,000 men. Penile cancer accounted for the largest proportion (45.3%), followed by anal cancer (38.7%) and head and neck cancers (16.0%). Within HPV attributable head and neck cancers, oropharyngeal cancer was the predominant subsite (58.5%). The age distribution was similar to that observed for HPV-associated outpatient consultations (Table 1; Figure 2).

Hospitalizations for HPV-associated cancers

Between 2015 and 2019, 1,136 hospitalizations for HPV-associated cancers in men were recorded, averaging 228 per year. The overall ASR was 1.6 per 100,000 men. Head and neck cancers accounted for the largest share of hospitalizations (46.3%), followed by penile cancer (45.9%), and anal cancer (7.8%). Among head and neck cancer types, laryngeal cancer represented the highest proportion of hospitalizations (44.3%), followed by oral cavity cancer (34.2%) and oropharyngeal cancer (21.5%) (Table 2).

Hospitalizations for HPV-attributable cancers

Applying site-specific AF, 402 hospitalizations were estimated to be HPV-attributable, corresponding to an ASR of 0.6 per 100,000 men. Penile cancer contributed to the majority (66.7%) of HPV-attributable hospitalizations, followed by anal cancer (22.1%), and head and neck cancers (11.2%). The age distribution mirrored that observed for HPV-associated hospitalizations (Table 2; Figure 4).

Outpatient-to-inpatient ratio

The overall outpatient-to-inpatient ratio for HPV-associated cancers was 5.3, corresponding to approximately five outpatient consultations for every hospitalization. This ratio varied substantially by cancer type: anal cancer showed the highest ratio (8.9), followed by oral cavity (8.2) and oropharyngeal cancer (8.1), while laryngeal cancer (4.3) and penile cancer (3.5) showed the lowest ratios, showing greater inpatient use. The ratio also varied markedly by age: men aged <30 years had the highest ratio (11.3), declining to 5.1 in men aged 30–59 years and 5.0 in men aged ≥60 years, indicating a shift toward more resource-intensive hospital-based care with increasing age (Supplemental Table S4).

Mortality for HPV-associated cancers

During the same period, 859 deaths from HPV-associated cancers were recorded, corresponding to an annual average of 171 deaths and an ASR of 1.2 per 100,000 men. Head and neck cancers accounted for the largest share (69.6%), followed by penile cancer (24.1%), and anal cancer (6.3%). Within head and neck cancers, laryngeal cancer accounted for the largest share mortality (57.7%), followed by oral cavity cancer (21.9%) and oropharyngeal cancer (20.4%) (Table 3; Figure 5). The sensitivity analysis excluding 2015–2016 showed minimal impact on overall mortality estimates, with only minor variations by cancer type and age group (Supplemental Table S3).

Mortality for HPV-attributable cancer

An estimated 208 deaths were attributable to HPV, representing 24.2% of all HPV-associated cancer deaths among men and corresponding to an ASR of 0.3 per 100,000. Penile cancer accounted for the largest proportion of HPV-attributable deaths (51.0%), followed by anal cancer (26.0%) and head and neck cancers (23.1%). Among head and neck cancers, oropharyngeal cancer accounted for 52.1% (25 deaths), oral cavity cancer 14.6% (7 deaths), and laryngeal cancer 33.3% (16 deaths). The age distribution for HPV-attributable mortality mirrored that observed for HPV-associated mortality (Table 3; Figure 6).

Discussion

This analysis provides the first comprehensive populational-level estimates of the burden of HPV‑associated and HPV‑attributable cancers among men in Peru using nationally available administrative health data. Over a five-year period, we identified a total of 5,996 outpatient consultations (ASR 8.2 per 100,000 men), 1,136 hospitalizations (ASR 1.6 per 100,000 men) and 859 deaths (ASR 1.2 per 100,000 men) related to HPV-associated cancers in men. These findings underscore the vulnerability of males to HPV-associated cancer outcomes and the substantial clinical and public health impact they impose, reinforcing the need to incorporate HPV prevention strategies targeting men.

In our study, outpatient consultations for HPV-associated cancers were approximately five times more frequent than hospitalizations, highlighting ambulatory care as a major component of the clinical burden, a finding consistent with evidence from high-income settings, though rarely documented in Latin America.31–35 The marked decline in the outpatient-to-inpatient ratio in men aged > 30 years likely reflects more advanced disease at presentation and increasing clinical complexity with age, which may be influenced by delayed diagnosis and barriers to timely access to care.36 Notably, penile and laryngeal cancers showed the highest inpatient intensity, highlighting their disproportionate burden on hospital-based care. These patterns further support the value of integrating outpatient and inpatient data to comprehensively characterize the burden of HPV-associated cancers across levels of care.31–35

Notably, the HPV-associated hospitalization rate observed in our analysis (ASR 1.6 per 100,000 men) was substantially lower than in other Latin American countries, such as Mexico (ASR 7.8) and Brazil (ASR 24.5), and the mortality rate (ASR 1.2) was also lower than in Brazil (ASR 7.7) and Mexico (ASR: 2.1).37,38 These differences may reflect variations in health system coverage rather than true differences in underlying disease burden. In settings with limited cancer registry coverage (<30%),17 integrating outpatient consultations, hospitalizations, and mortality data provides a more comprehensive view of disease burden and establishes a baseline for monitoring trends and informing cancer control strategies.

Head and neck cancers comprised the largest share of HPV-associated burden. However, after applying AFs, penile cancer became the predominant contributor to the HPV‑attributable burden.2,29 This shift reflects differences in the underlying etiology of cancers across anatomical sites, where tobacco and alcohol remain major drivers of head and neck cancers.37 These exposures may act both independently and synergistically with HPV infection, with relative contribution varying by anatomical site, particularly for laryngeal cancer, where the HPV‑attributable fraction is lower.37 Nevertheless, AFs can evolve over time, and our attribution estimates may underestimate HPV’s contribution in younger cohorts, particularly for oropharyngeal cancer, where HPV positivity frequently exceeds 70% in high-income settings,7–10 and continues to rise in Latin America.38–41

The strong age gradient observed across outpatient consultations, hospitalizations, and death rates for head and neck cancers, reflects the long latency of HPV‑related carcinogenesis.4,35,38 Yet, the notable burden among younger and middle‑aged men, particularly for oropharyngeal and oral cavity is consistent with rising trends reported in younger cohorts.41 Compounding this concern, these cancers lack identifiable precancerous lesions and occur in deep anatomical location, limiting early screening and diagnosis.32–34,42 Consequently, HPV-attributable head and neck cancers carry substantial premature mortality, with an estimated average of 27 y of life loss per individual and productivity losses of approximately US$30 million.43

Our findings highlighted, penile cancer as a significant HPV-attributable burden in Peru, consistent with the higher incidence reported across Latin American countries compared with high-income settings.32–34,42 The HPV‑attributable hospitalization rate (ASR 0.6) was consistent with those rates reported in Brazil (ASR 1.0) and Mexico (ASR 0.7).38,44 In Latin America, penile cancer disproportionately affects men with lower socioeconomic status, rural residences, and limited education, where high HPV prevalence, phimosis, lack of circumcision, and delayed diagnosis contribute to advanced presentation.45–48 The high frequency of outpatient consultations in our study likely reflects the clinical complexity of management, while the concentration of hospitalizations among younger and middle-aged men aligns with emerging evidence of rising HPV-related penile cancer diagnoses in men younger than 64 y.45–50

Although anal cancer represented a moderate share of HPV-associated burden in our analysis, it emerged as a major contributor to HPV-attributable burden due to complete HPV attribution. It also showed a relatively higher concentration among middle‑aged men (30 to 59 y). This pattern is particularly concerning given the rising incidence of anal cancer reported globally and in Latin America, as well as the heightened vulnerability of high‑risk groups.51,52 In the region, anal cancer incidence has been reported at 59.1 per 100,000 person-years in men who have sex with men (MSM) with HIV and 15.2 in HIV-positive men who are not MSM, highlighting the profound susceptibility of these populations.16,52

The implications of our study for policy and practice in Peru are clear. In line with WHO guidance and the Peru’s legal framework, advancing universal HPV vaccination with high coverage for girls and boys, accelerating multi‑age catch‑up within 9–18 y, and integrating delivery with HIV/STI services are critical priorities to reduce future morbidity and mortality and address disparities.45,46 Peru’s nationwide school‑based campaigns offer a strong platform; however, early roll‑out monitoring has noted uneven implementation to older ages in some jurisdictions, highlighting the need for operational vigilance, targeted outreach, and service integration for key populations.53,54

In 2025, Peru initiated the transition from quadrivalent (6/11/16/18) to 9-valent HPV vaccine (6/11/16/18/31/33/45/52/58) within its national immunization program, representing a critical step toward broader HPV protection and long‑term cancer prevention.53 As oncogenic HPV types distribution varies across populations and cancer types, strengthening HPV genomic surveillance will be important to monitor viral dynamics and evaluate vaccine impact over time.55 In parallel, improving access to early diagnosis and timely treatment remains essential, strengthening provider training and referral pathways, expanding diagnostic capacity, decentralizing oncology services, and ensuring financial protection for equitable care.56

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, outpatient consultations and hospital discharges are analyzed as events rather than unique patients, potentially inflating counts due to repeat admissions. Second, AFs were applied uniformly across ages and years; Peru‑specific type distribution and temporal changes could alter attribution. Third, record linkage across databases was not available, limiting deduplication and precluding estimation of incidence, recurrence, and survival. Fourth, the outpatient consultation and hospital discharge databases primarily cover MINSA/SIS public-sector care, likely underrepresenting individuals using private or contributory populations. Last, despite improvements in the national mortality database after 2017, coverage gaps and misclassification likely persist.57

Strengths of our study include the use of national administrative data, WHO age standardization to enable international comparisons, and transparent attribution methods. Stratification by cancer type and age group provides granular insight into disease patterns, and sensitivity analyses addressing early SINADEF implementation enhance reliability. Ultimately, this work provides a foundation for monitoring trends and guiding future impact studies to assess the effectiveness of vaccination programs and policy-driven public health interventions in reducing the HPV-related cancer burden among men.

Conclusion

HPV-attributable cancers impose a substantial and largely preventable burden among men in Peru, with important implications for health equity. Despite inherent limitations, including the use of routinely collected national health service administrative data and attribution fractions, the data are robust and provide important evidence to support action. Sustaining universal HPV vaccination with high coverage rates for both sexes, accelerating multi‑age catch‑up vaccination, and integrating vaccination with HIV/STI services for high‑risk groups remain critical to reducing future morbidity and mortality. Improving access to early diagnosis and timely treatment is equally critical. Complementary actions should strengthen provider training and referral pathways, expand diagnostic capacity, decentralize oncological services, and ensure financial protection for timely, equitable treatment. Continued investment in data systems and research will be key to monitor trends, refine burden estimates, and evaluate the long-term impact of prevention strategies. Together, these efforts can support more effective and equitable cancer control policies in Peru.

She held a position on the executive board of the Brazilian Association for Lower Genital Tract Disease and Colposcopy (ABPTGIC) from 1998 to 2012, including roles as Scientific Director and editor of its journal and website. During this period, she led major initiatives in continuing medical education, including in-person and e-learning courses, newsletters, and scientific publications. Since 2006, Dr. Parellada has worked at MSD in medical affairs and outcomes research, contributing across various therapeutic areas such as vaccines/immunology, respiratory diseases, human reproduction, and gynecology. Her academic background includes over 120 contributions to the scientific community, including journal articles, book chapters, congress presentations, and newsletters, demonstrating a strong commitment to advancing medical science and improving public health.
