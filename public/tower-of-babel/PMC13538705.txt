In-situ SEM evaluation of single-nanowire response in CuO–Cu₂O–ZnO nanowire arrays for infrared energy harvesting
Authors: D Gavars, R Meija, M Volkova, A Sarakovskis, A Kons, D Erts, J Andzane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCopper and zinc oxides (CuO, Cu2O, ZnO) are semiconductors with p-type and n-type conductivity, respectively, which have attracted attention as perspective materials for the research and development of p-n junction-based devices. Their low cost and abundance are ideal for applications in electronics and solar radiation sensing and harvesting. Most research involving these materials has been focused on UV-VIS photodetectors and solar cells, as well as p-n junction-based diodes. However, less attention has been paid to thermoelectric applications or the detection/energy harvesting of infrared radiation, which constitutes a significant part of the solar spectrum. In this work, electrical properties and the unbiased response to infrared radiation of CuO-Cu2O-ZnO heterojunction nanowires are studied. The vertically arranged CuO-Cu2O-ZnO nanowire arrays were synthesized directly on copper substrates using a combined thermal oxidation/thermal evaporation method and studied in-situ inside a scanning electron microscope using nanomanipulation techniques. The nanowires were found to exhibit diode-like behavior, indicating the presence of a p-n junction. Furthermore, a pronounced unbiased response to infrared radiation was observed and attributed to the photo-thermoelectrical effect. The estimated Seebeck coefficient, power factor, and responsivity of the CuO-Cu2O-ZnO heterojunction nanowires were 120 µV/K, 18 nW/mK2, and 150 mA/W respectively. The absorbance capabilities of the material widen the potential for applications in effective harvesting of the full spectrum of solar energy. Additionally, the material furthers the development of nano-power generators operating at low-temperature differences of a few degrees Celsius, which may be favorable for a wide range of applications, like wearable electronics, Internet-of-Things, environmental and health sensors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57903-6.Keywords: Zinc oxide, Copper oxide, Interface, Heterojunction, Nanowire array, Thermoelectrics, Self-powered infrared sensorSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

Green energy generation from renewable sources, such as solar energy and heat (photovoltaic, thermoelectrical, and photothermoelectrical devices), is among the fastest-growing and most widely researched technologies as a part of the aim to meet the targets of green energy harvesting and mitigation of climate changes1–3. Fabrication and further commercialization of such devices are critically dependent on the availability of abundant, low-cost, environmentally friendly semiconductors. Accordingly, copper and zinc oxides (CuO, Cu2O, ZnO) have attracted research attention as suitable semiconductors with p-type and n-type conductivity, respectively. However, because their direct band gaps are well-suited for applications utilizing ultraviolet (UV) and visible (VIS) parts of the electromagnetic spectrum (3.37 eV (ZnO)4, 1.4 eV (CuO)5, and 2.2 eV (Cu2O)6, most of the research involving these materials is focused on biased and recently also self-powered UV-VIS photodetectors7–11 and solar cells12, as well as p-n junction-based diodes13,14. Less attention is paid to thermoelectrics15,16 or detection/energy harvesting based on the infrared (IR) part of spectrum7,17, and the potential of CuO, Cu2O, and ZnO for such applications seems to be underestimated. For example, copper and zinc oxide nanostructures have recently shown promising thermoelectrical properties for applications at near-room temperatures in classic TEG configurations where p-type and n-type legs are separated by an insulating space. Networks of CuO and ZnO nanowires grown by thermal oxidation methods showed high Seebeck coefficients up to 820 µV/K for p-type CuO and − 190 µV/K for n-type ZnO15. In turn, the Cu2O and self-assembling ZnO nanowire networks, synthesized recently by our research group, showed room-temperature (RT) Seebeck coefficients of ~ 2000 µV/K and ~ 175 µV/K respectively18,19. The estimated power factors (PF) of these nanowire networks were ~ 2 µW/mK2 and ~ 5 nW/mK2 respectively18,19. These PF values for CuO exceeded those reported by other groups (0.37 µW/mK2)15, and were comparable with those reported for ZnO nanowire networks (~ 8 nW/mK2) measured at 800 K15,16. In addition, it should be noted that the approach for self-assembling ZnO nanowire network encapsulation in commercially available epoxy glue, developed by our group, increased the Seebeck coefficient of the ZnO nanowire network up to ~ 570 µV/K and the PF up to 0.85 µW/mK219. These results further underline the potential of the Cu2O and ZnO nanowire networks developed by our group for thermoelectric applications. The promising alternative architecture of TEGs is based on a direct p-n junction between the TEG components, thus eliminating the need for an insulating space between the TEG legs and the fabrication of electrical connections between them20. This makes the TEG architecture more mechanically adjustable/flexible in comparison with the classic structure. A proof-of-concept all-oxide p-n junction TEG based on SnOx and ZnO thin films showed a PF of 180 µW/mK2 for ZnO20, which is more than four orders of magnitude higher than the performance of ZnO nanowire networks in a classic TEG configuration. Nevertheless, to our knowledge, CuO/Cu2O-ZnO materials in thin films or nanostructures have not yet been applied in p-n junction TEGs. In addition, considering that each oxide has its own part of the solar spectrum that it can absorb more intensely - UV (ZnO), VIS (Cu2O) and IR (CuO) - when combined, the CuO/Cu2O-ZnO heterojunction nanostructures may become a perspective active material for harvesting the energy generated by the entire solar spectrum.

This work is focused on the synthesis of vertically aligned CuO-Cu2O-ZnO (further referred to as CO-ZnO) heterojunction nanowires with high surface-to-volume ratios. The heterostructures were synthesized using a combination of thermal oxidation and thermal evaporation methods, and their unbiased photothermoelectric response to infrared radiation was studied in-situ.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowire arrays. Firstly, CuO nanowires were synthesized via thermal oxidation of 2 × 2 cm copper foil (FirstNano, CVD Equipment Corporation, 99.95%, thickness: 75 μm). The synthesis was conducted in air at normal pressure and 600 °C in a quartz tube oven (OTF-1200X-S, max temperature 1200 °C). The heating rate of the furnace was 13.3 °C/min for 45 min. Upon reaching 400 °C in the center of the furnace tube, an aluminum boat with 3 mL of deionized water was placed inside the furnace until the maximum temperature of 600 °C was reached. Adding of water vapor is necessary for the formation of small diameter nanowires (< 50 nm) in high quantity as the water vapor initially increases the oxidation rate of copper, which is partially important at the beginning of the growth21. Upon reaching the maximum oxidation temperature, the aluminum boat was removed, and the extra moisture was allowed to evaporate from the inside of the furnace. The thermal oxidation time was 210 min, and afterward, the furnace was allowed to cool naturally until it reached room temperature before removing the sample. Secondly, the oxidized copper foil with formed CuO nanowires was annealed in a low-pressure (< 1 Torr) N2 atmosphere to reduce CuO nanowires to Cu2O. The furnace was heated to 700 °C at 14.0 °C/min for 50 min and held at constant temperature for 90 min. The pressure inside the quartz tube was monitored during the annealing and lowered when it exceeded 3 Torr by using an external pump. After the annealing, the furnace was allowed to cool naturally until it reached room temperature before removing the sample. Thirdly, a layer of zinc metal was deposited on CuO/Cu2O nanowires using physical vapor deposition (PVD). The zinc metal source, a 2 × 2 cm piece of zinc foil (Goodfellow, 99.95%, thickness: 0.05 mm), was placed in the center of the furnace tube, while the Cu2O nanowire sample was positioned in the tube area, where the temperature during the deposition process reaches 250–300 °C. Before the PVD, the furnace tube was flushed for a few minutes with N2,the pressure inside was reduced to 350 mTorr, and the tube was then sealed. The furnace was heated to 400 °C at a rate of 16.0 °C/min for 25 min, held constant for 30 min, and allowed to cool down naturally. This process resulted in the deposition of the Zn metal layer over the CuO/Cu2O nanowires. Finally, the deposited Zn layer was oxidized in air at normal pressure for 90 min at 500 °C in a quartz tube oven. The oven’s heating rate was 16.7 °C/min for 30 min. After the oxidation, the oven was allowed to cool to room temperature before removing the sample.

Structural and morphological characterization. The structure and morphology of the samples were inspected using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM) Hitachi S-4800. The cross-section areas of the individual nanowires were estimated from the SEM images using ImageJ software. The cross-sections varied within the range ~ 0.5–1.5 µm2. For the estimation, an average cross-section area of an individual nanowire of ~ 1 µm2 was used. The X-ray electron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out using a ThermoFisher ESCALAB Xi+ instrument using a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source. The calibration of the binding energy scale is confirmed by examining the sputter-cleaned Au, Ag, and Cu reference samples that place Au 4f7/2, Ag 3d5/2, and Cu 2p3/2 peaks at 83.96 eV, 368.21 eV, and 932.62 eV, respectively. The spectra were recorded using an X-ray beam size of 650 × 100 μm with a pass energy of 20 eV and a step size of 0.1 eV. A charge neutralizer was used in the reported experiments. The X-ray diffraction measurements were performed on a Bruker D8 Discover diffractometer (Bruker Corp., Billerica, MA, USA) using a copper radiation source (Cu Kα = 1.54180 Å) with Bragg–Brentano geometry and a LynxEye (1D) detector. The divergence and anti-scattering slits were set at 0.6 mm and 8 mm, respectively. The patterns were recorded from 10° to 70° on the 2θ scale, using a scan speed of 0.5 s/0.02°. For the identification of the diffraction peaks, the ICDD database PDF-2/Release 2021 was used (Ref. cards PDF 01-073-6237 Cu2O, PDF 03-065-3288 Cu2O, PDF 01-089-5895 CuO, PDF 01-078-9363 Zn and PDF 01-075-6445 ZnO). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were measured with Bruker Vertex 70v spectrometer equipped with an attenuated total reflection module with a diamond crystal. Measuring range was 400–4000 cm− 1, a resolution of ± 2 cm− 1, 20 scans per spectrum. Measurements were performed in vacuum, at 2.95 hPa.

Electrical and photothermoelectrical characterization. The in-situ measurements of the photothermoelectrical response of CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires were carried out using a 13D SmarAct nanomanipulation system staged inside the SEM, similar to those reported elsewhere22. The Cu substrate with the synthesized heterojunction nanowire array was placed into the experimental setup, and the Cu substrate served as a bottom electrode and heat sink. For the fabrication of bottom contact, the oxide layer was gently removed mechanically from the bottom surfaces of the Cu substrate, and a copper contact wire was attached using conductive epoxy EPO-TEK(R) H20E. The in-situ measurements were carried out on individual heterojunction nanowires selected from the nanowire array and contacted by an custom electrochemically etched gold probe (average diameter of the probe apex ~ 4 µm2) as the top electrode. The current-voltage measurements were performed within the voltage range from − 3 to 3 V with a step of 0.1 V. Photothermoelectrical response measurements of the individual CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires were carried out in situ at high vacuum conditions (~ 10− 6 Torr) using an infrared (IR) diode (940 nm, 0.1 W/cm2) for the sample irradiation. In this setup, long-term stability measurements were not performed due to the technical issues: such setup is subjected to the thermal and mechanical drifts, which makes long-term measurements insufficiently reliable. For the determination of the thermoelectric response of the CO-ZnO nanowire arrays, the sample along with the transparent top electrode (ITO-coated glass) was sandwiched between two custom metallic blocks supplied with a water inlet and outlet. The temperature of the metallic blocks was controlled by the cold and hot water flow within, and the temperature difference was kept within 30 K of the room temperature. The electric measurements were recorded using a picoammeter Keithley 6430 and custom software. The temperature was measured using a K-type thermocouple and an Omega CL3515R calibrator.

Results and discussion

CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowire arrays were synthesized on copper substrates via 4-step combined thermal oxidation and evaporation techniques (Fig. 1). For the synthesis of CuO nanowires and their reduction to Cu2O methods23 previously developed by our group were modified and applied. In step 1, CuO nanowires were synthesized on pre-cleaned copper substrates using thermal oxidation at 600 °C (stage 1 in Fig. 1), as described in detail in the Experimental section. In step 2, the Cu substrate with the obtained CuO nanowires was annealed at 700 °C in an inert atmosphere at low pressure (< 1 Torr). This step partially reduced CuO nanowires to Cu2O (stage 2 in Fig. 1 ). In step 3, the Zn metal layer was deposited over the CuO/Cu2O nanowire arrays (stage 3 in Fig. 1) using the thermal evaporation method demonstrated previously by our group19. In the final step 4, the obtained Cu-CuO-Cu2O-Zn nanowire arrays were subjected to thermal oxidation in air at 400 °C as described elsewhere19 to obtain CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowire arrays (stage 4 in Fig. 1). Representative images of the obtained CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowire arrays (side view) are shown in the right panel of Fig. 1. The CO-ZnO nanowires were generally vertically aligned, and the lengths of the nanowires in the array varied within 20–40 μm. As shown at higher magnification images in the right panel of Fig. 1, the deposition of Zn metal over the CO nanowires and its further oxidation resulted in the formation of curly nanostructures surrounding the CO nanowires. Such morphology of the heterojunction nanowire arrays has a high surface-to-volume ratio, which may be favorable for harvesting radiation energy.

The left panel is a schematic representation of the four-step process for obtaining CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowire arrays, and the right panel shows representative scanning electron microscope images of CO-ZnO nanowire arrays at different magnifications (side view).

Powder X-ray diffraction analysis proved the composition of the obtained CO-ZnO nanowire arrays. It revealed that the sample consisted of four different crystalline phases: Cu2O, CuO, ZnO, and metallic Cu from the substrate (Fig. 2).

Subsequently, quantitative phase analysis using the Rietveld method was performed in Profex5 software (see Supplementary Information). Since the Cu phase originates from the substrate, it was excluded from the Rietveld refinement. The lattice constants a, b and c for Cu2O, CuO and ZnO nanostructures were calculated from the obtained XRD spectra of CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowire array applying the cubic Cu2O, monoclinic CuO, and hexagonal ZnO crystal lattices. The calculated values for the all nanowire array components showed a low mismatch from the theoretical lattice parameters from Ref. cards (the maximum deviation was observed for Cu2O with a value of 0.14%) and nanocrystalline structure. This indicates an insignificant strain in the nanostructures, which can appear in the transition layer.

XPS spectrum revealed the presence of signals corresponding to carbon, oxygen, copper, and zinc (Fig. 3). The carbon signal contained peaks at 284.8 eV (adventitious carbon) and a small intensity band at 288.8 eV corresponding to the carboxyl group (C = O) (Fig. 3a) and most likely was related to the inevitable contamination of the sample in the ambient air24. The sample was gently cleaned by an Ar + ion gun to remove the carbon-related coating, minimizing damage to the material’s chemistry. However, due the roughness and porosity of the sample surface, it was impossible to entirely remove the carbon impurities24. The signal of oxygen (Fig. 3b) contained peaks appearing at 530.1 eV (metal oxide) and 531.9 eV (mixture of hydroxyl and carbonate groups)25. The signal of copper (Fig. 3c) contained two groups of peaks appearing at 932 eV (Cu2p3/2) and 952 eV (Cu2p1/2). Two subbands in each region appear at 931.8 eV (951.4 eV) and 932.4 eV (952.2 eV), corresponding to Cu2O and possibly some other copper-containing compound, for example, CuOH. The lack of a strong intensity satellite in the region of 938 eV – 943 eV, which is a characteristic feature of Cu(II), and the presence of a small intensity signal at 945 eV allowed the conclusion that the copper primarily present in the material is Cu(I)26,27. Considering that the XPS technique characterizes the surface layers of the sample, the obtained data are consistent with the XRD results, which showed the presence of ~ 75% Cu2O. Most likely, Cu2O is present in the surface layers of CO nanowires, while CuO may be present in the cores of the nanowires and on copper substrates. Finally, the signal of Zn (Fig. 3d) contained two bands at 1021.4 eV (Zn2p3/2) and 1044.5 eV (Zn2p1/2) corresponding to Zn(II). The lack of a loss feature peak at 1035 eV and the presence of the peak in the vicinity of 1040 eV suggests that the Zn-containing compound is predominantly ZnO26.

Current-voltage (I-V) curves of individual CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires measured using the setup shown in Fig. 4 exhibited rectifying behavior with the threshold voltage ~ 1.1 V and the breakdown voltage in reverse bias ~ 1.5 V (Fig. 4b). The threshold voltage of ~ 1.1 V falls in the range between the threshold voltage values reported for CuO-ZnO thin film p-n heterojunctions (~ 0.8 V) fabricated by thermal evaporation/oxidation13 or spray-pyrolysis28 methods and ~ 1.55 V reported for CuO/ZnO nanocoral p-n heterojunctions fabricated by two-step hydrothermal procedure14. The calculated from the I-V curve rectification ratio varied between 2.5 and 17, reaching maximal values for the applied voltages ± 0.8–1 V (Fig. 4c). The obtained rectification ratio of 17 is consistent with the rectification ratio of 15 reported for CuO-ZnO thin film heterojunctions fabricated by the combination of thermal oxidation and thermal evaporation13 and 15–28 reported for CuO-ZnO thin film heterojunctions deposited by spray pyrolysis28. Thus, the diode-like behavior of the I-V curve measured for CO-ZnO nanowires indicates the presence of the expected p-type copper oxide and n-type zinc oxide p-n junction at the CO-ZnO interface in the nanowires.

The heterojunction ideality factor n can be determined from the slope of the straight-line region of the forward-bias log (I)-V characteristics13,14. As can be seen from Fig. 4d, there are two nearly linear sections. The estimated n values for the voltage range 0–1 V and 1–3 V are 6.96 and 1.05, respectively. While the value 1.05 is close to n = 1, representing the behavior of ideal heterojunction, the value of 6.96 determined for the voltages below 1 V is similar to that obtained for the CuO/ZnO nanocoral diode heterojunction (6.63–6.80 for the voltage range 0.17–1.46 V)14 and can be attributed to the large series resistance and defect states at the interface of CO-ZnO12,14. Such arrays of vertically arranged heterojunction nanowires (Fig. 1, right panel) may find application in high-density electronics as the heterojunction nanowires in the nanowire array may be connected individually.

To investigate the photothermoelectric response of the CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires under IR irradiation, time-resolved voltage (Fig. 4e) and current (Fig. 4f) curves were obtained in the experimental setup shown in Fig. 4a, using an infrared (IR) diode as a source for the radiation with no external bias applied to the sample. The ON and OFF labels in Figs. 4e and f indicate the moments of turning the radiation source on and off, respectively. It was found that under IR radiation, the individually connected CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires show pronounced responses without an external bias applied to the sample and generate in total ~ 2 mV open-circuit voltage (Fig. 4e). This can be attributed to the photovoltaic effect in CO-ZnO systems17,29 and the thermoelectric effects caused by the temperature difference of a few K (~ 3 K measured in ambient conditions using thermocouples) across the sample. The energy of the IR radiation (940 nm) is approximately 1.3 eV, which is significantly below the direct inter-band transition in the ZnO (Eg ~ 3.3 eV)30,31,36,37 and Cu2O (Eg ~ 2.1 eV) (Fig. 5), this makes both of these phases very transmitting to the incoming radiation32,38. More critically, it falls just short of the nominal Eg ~ 1.5 eV bandgap of the CuO phase32,38. This implies that the IR radiation interacts primarily with deep mid-gap defect states and induces phonon-mediated free-carrier absorption. This process rapidly converts the incident electromagnetic energy into localized lattice vibrations (heat) in the nanowire array. Because the base of the nanowire array is anchored to a highly thermally conductive bottom copper substrate acting as a heat sink, a steep macroscopic temperature gradient (ΔT) is established along the longitudinal axis of the nanowires. This temperature gradient triggers the Seebeck effect.

A schematic band alignment diagram of CuO-Cu2O-ZnO (referred in text as CO-ZnO) heterojunction indicating Type II heterojunction.

Because the determination of the Seebeck coefficient of the CO-ZnO heterostructure nanowires under IR irradiation is complicated due to the combined photothermoelectric response, the CO-ZnO nanowire array was sandwiched between the bottom (copper, cooled) and the top (ITO-coated glass, heated) electrodes as described in the Experimental section. The relation between the thermally generated voltage and the temperature gradient applied between the top and bottom is shown in Fig. 4g. As it can be observed, the thermally generated voltage remains at ~ 50% of its maximum value even after the heater providing the temperature gradient between the electrodes is turned off. This could be related to the remaining temperature gradient within the nanostructures and elongated charge carrier recombination time. In the p-type CuO and Cu₂O phases, hot holes diffuse away from the irradiated top counter electrode and toward the cold bottom copper substrate. Concurrently, in the n-type ZnO shell, hot electrons also diffuse away from the hot top counter electrode toward the cold bottom copper substrate. If this were a simple, homogeneous material, this diffusion would generate a modest open-circuit thermoelectric voltage ΔV, where ΔV = S ΔT, with S representing the inherent Seebeck coefficient of the bulk material. On the other hand, in the synthesized heterostructure consisting of Type-II band alignment (Fig. 5), a separation of electrons and holes occurs. The thermally to the conduction band excited electrons immediately experience a downwards cascade. The ΔEc ~ 1.15 eV conduction band offset at the Cu2O and ZnO interface forcefully sweeps the electrons out of the p-type core into the ZnO, where they are confined by a deep potential well of the n-type ZnO shell. Simultaneously, hot holes diffusing through the lattice encounters the upward valence band cascade. The massive ΔEV ~ 2.35 eV valence band offset absolutely prevents holes from entering the ZnO phase, funneling them strictly into the CuO and Cu₂O core structures and into the Cu substrate (Fig. 5). By physically forcing the electrons and holes into distinct crystalline domains separated by high energetic barriers, the built-in electric field functions as a barrier that prevents spatially separated carriers from recombining. This is beneficial for the target application of CO-ZnO nanostructures – harvesting of IR irradiation energy and its conversion to electricity. The Seebeck coefficient value of CO-ZnO nanowire array determined from this measurement curve was ~ 120 µV/K, which was lower compared to the values reported for thermoelectric generator integrating CuO and ZnO nanowire networks (~ 800 µV/K)15. Calculation of the power factor of the CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires remains challenging due to the difficulty of precise determination of their geometry (Fig. 1, right panel) and the following calculations are rather speculative and performed to provide an estimation of a magnitude of thermoelectric effect in the CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires. Assuming the Seebeck coefficient of ~ 120 µV/K, the resistance of an individual CO-ZnO nanowire determined from the linear part of I-V of ~ 24.5 MΩ (Fig. 4a), the cross-section area of the CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowire ~ 1 µm2 and its length ~ 30 μm, the estimated PF of the individual was found to be ~ 18 nW/mK2 (Table 1), which despite the lower Seebeck coefficient exceeded the PF reported for a thermoelectric generator consisting of CuO and ZnO nanowire network components (~ 1 nW/mK2)15. This result presumably highlights the role of the built-in electric field at the p-n junctions formed at the CO-ZnO interface, which promote the electron-hole separation under the external infrared illumination and suppress further electron-hole recombination10,11. Furthermore, this enhances the thermoelectric response of CO-ZnO nanowires, when compared to a classic configuration of thermoelectric generator, where CuO and ZnO nanowire networks were separated with an insulating layer15. Current time-resolved curve of the CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowire also showed a pronounced response under the IR radiation with the tendency of a gradual current increase during the irradiation time (Fig. 4f). The responsivity of the CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowire was calculated for the maximum detected current as ΔI/AP, where ΔI is the difference between the dark current and the current under radiation, A is the active irradiated area (for the measurements of the response of individual nanowires, the cross-section of the nanowire), and P is radiation power density(~ 150 mA/W). The responsivity is consistent with the reported values for self-powered Cu2O-ZnO thin film heterojunction detectors for the visible light range (0.1 A/W)11 and higher than the responsivity reported for self-powered ultraviolet CuO/ZnO photodetectors (1.68 mA/W)9, ZnO-Cu2O core-shell nanowires (~ 7.7 µA/W)8 for visible range, and self-powered Cu2O thin film in near-infrared range (~ 6.6 mA/W)7. At the same time, the current rise (τr) and decay (τd) time constants are estimated by fitting rise and decay curves (Fig. 4f, red dashed lines) to the equations Irise=Io(1-exp(-t/τr)) and Idecay=Io+aexp(-t/τd)30,31,33,34, where Irise and Idecay are the currents under irradiation at time t, Io is the dark current, and a is the scaling constant. They were found to be τr ~ 170 s and τd ~ 145 s, which is significantly larger than reported for the aforementioned photodetectors, exhibiting rise and decay time constants within tens of milliseconds7–11, Table 2. Relatively large rise and decay time constants may be attributed to the presence of trap levels and defect states in the band gaps of materials, resulting in the trapping and slow release of charge carriers32,33,35, as well as to slow thermal effects from gradual heating of the sample under IR radiation. The slow rise time (τr ~ 170 s) indicates a bolometric system, where the electrical output is proportional to the temperature gradient ΔT, and the system heats up with Newton’s law of heating. When the IR source is deactivated, the established thermal gradient does not vanish instantaneously. Instead, the physical mass of the nanowire array and substrate undergoes macroscopic thermal relaxation. Guided by Newton’s law of cooling, the thermal energy slowly dissipates into the copper heat sink, causing the primary Seebeck driving force to decay exponentially over time. Crucially, this macroscopic thermal delay is severely compounded by a microscopic electronic recombination bottleneck. The high-temperature synthesis protocol inherently leaves permanent point defects in the crystal lattices—specifically, copper vacancies in the oxide core and oxygen vacancies in the ZnO shell. During irradiation, the Type-II band alignment physically sweeps electrons into the ZnO and holes into the copper oxides, where a massive proportion of these carriers fall into these deep trap states. To recombine, an electron trapped in the ZnO shell faces a compounded thermodynamic hurdle: it must first acquire sufficient thermal energy to be de-trapped into the local ZnO conduction band. Then, before it is simply recaptured, it must overcome the massive 1.15 eV conduction band offset via thermionic emission and move against the built-in depletion field to reach a hole in the Cu-oxide core. This dual-mechanism - where the macroscopic Seebeck driving force fades slowly via Newtonian cooling, and deep traps barricaded by the Type-II alignment drastically restrict charge recombination - stretches the signal decay over several minutes, definitively confirming the trap-mediated photothermoelectric nature of the device. It can be seen in Table 2, that the research on the performance of self-powered CuO/ZnO or Cu2O/ZnO heterojunction-based devices was mainly focused on photodetection in UV-VIS ranges, while self-powered IR detection was studied for Cu2O thin films. In turn, quite limited research on the application of ZnO and CuO/Cu2O nanostructures in thermoelectrics did not consider their response to infrared radiation. The high responsivity and slow response times of the CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowire arrays developed in our work, resulting in long-term generation of output voltage, induced by the IR illumination, demonstrate the possibility to use such nanostructures for energy harvesting from IR radiation and heat sources, thus expanding the potential applications of copper and zinc-oxides-based devices. The relatively slow response time of CO-ZnO heterostructures to IR radiation can be advantageous in devices where sensitivity and prolonged signal stability are prioritized over rapid response. Such applications include low-cost, non-toxic nano-thermoelectric IR bolometers for IR detection, thermal monitoring, and solar energy harvesting34,35,37,38. This opens the path for the development of a high surface-to-volume ratio active material for effective harvesting of the full spectrum of solar energy, as well as to the development of µ-power generators operating at low temperature differences of a few degrees Celsius, which may find application in wearable electronics, Internet-of-Things, environmental and health sensors, etc. The following work will focus on developing setups for multiple parallel connections of the CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires in the array using approaches developed for the arrays of nanoelectromechanical switches, where the array of free-standing on the bottom electrode nanostructures is coated with the top electrode (ITO glass, graphene layer, etc.) and an external voltage is gradually applied between the electrodes starting from a few mV and generally not exceeding 4 V to connect the individual nanostructured of the array to the top electrode using electrostatic force22. This will result in parallel connection of heterojunction nanowires between the electrodes, thus enhancing their performance and making the nanowires-based device easy-handling.

Conclusions

Vertically aligned CuO-Cu2O-ZnO (referred to as CO-ZnO) heterojunction nanowire arrays were synthesized on copper substrates using a combined four-step thermal oxidation/thermal evaporation method. The resulting morphology of the heterojunction nanowires was found to be “curly”, thus possessing a high surface-to-volume ratio favorable for radiation energy harvesting. The electrical properties and unbiased response of the CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires were studied in situ using a nanomanipulation system, allowing the connection of separate nanowires in the electrical circuit. The CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires exhibited asymmetrical current-voltage curves, indicating the presence of a p-n junction at the CO/ZnO interface. The turn-on voltage of the p-n junction was found to be ~ 1.1 V, and the maximal rectification ratio RR ~ 18, which is in line with the performance of thin films-based CuO-ZnO diodes reported by other groups, and indicates a potential for applications in electronics. Study of an unbiased response of the CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires to infrared radiation showed a pronounced increase of voltage (up to ~ 2 mV) and current (~ up to 18 pA) generated by the nanowires under IR irradiation, with the calculated responsivity of ~ 150 mA/W. However, the response time constants of the CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires were found to be ~ 170 s and ~ 145 s for rise and decay, respectively, which significantly exceeds typical response time constants for photovoltaic effect-based photodetectors, typically ranging from milliseconds to seconds. Presumably, the slow response of the CO-ZnO heterojunction nanowires is related to the thermal processes occurring in the nanowires due to their heating under IR radiation, which points to the domination of photothermelectrical processes in the response rather than rapid photovoltaic effects. The evaluated Seebeck coefficient and thermoelectric power factor of the CO-ZnO were in the range of ~ 120 µV/K and ~ 18 nW/mK2, respectively, which significantly (by ~ 18 times) exceeded the thermoelectric power factor of classic thermoelectric generators consisting of CuO and ZnO nanowire networks separated with insulation layer. This highlights the potential of p-n junction systems for thermoelectric applications at near-room temperature differences. These results demonstrate the potential of copper and zinc-oxides-based devices for the effective energy harvesting applications ranging from UV-VIS to the full spectrum of solar energy. Furthermore, they also show the potential applications for the development of nano- and micropower generators operating at low-temperature differences of a few degrees Celsius, which may also find application in wearable electronics, Internet-of-Things, and environmental and health sensors.
