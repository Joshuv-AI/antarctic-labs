Rethinking obesity management targets: Findings from an international MoSCoW prioritization workshop
Authors: Sanjeev Sockalingam, Megan Racey, Diana Sherifali, Angela S Alberga, Jamy Ard, Deborah Bade Horn, Luca Busetto, Lee M Kaplan, Carel W Le Roux, David Macklin, Patricia Nece, Ian Patton, Nicole Pearce, Yudith Preiss, Morgan Emile Gabriel Salmon Leguede, Arya M Sharma, Soo Huat Teoh, Elisabeth FC van Rossum, Jun Wada, Sean Wharton

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is a chronic disease that requires individualized, person-centred care beyond traditional weight-centric targets. Despite growing global recognition of obesity's multifactorial nature, research and treatment goals remain narrowly focused on percent weight loss, often misaligned with patient experiences and broader health outcomes. To address this gap, and building on a prior scoping review, we convened an international priority-setting workshop to identify and prioritize requirements to inform the subsequent development of a comprehensive obesity management framework.MethodsThis was an international workshop study using the MoSCoW (Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have) prioritization method to identify and prioritize requirements, outcome domains, and implementation considerations to inform the future development of an obesity management framework. The invitation-only workshop was held in Toronto, Canada, with participants including clinicians, researchers, organizational representatives, and individuals with lived experience. Data from breakout group discussions were synthesized into thematic categories and validated through member checking.ResultsEighteen participants contributed to the workshop, resulting in 15 prioritized themes across the four MoSCoW categories. “Must-have” themes included multi-faceted pillars of obesity care (e.g., pharmacotherapy, behavioral support, surgery), healthcare provider sensitivity training, and valid outcome measures that assess complications and patient-reported outcomes. “Should-have” themes emphasized mental health tracking, functional outcomes, and personalized metrics. “Could-have” themes included ongoing education, team-based care, and policy integration. “Won't-have” themes rejected stigmatizing language, aesthetic-based success markers, and short-term interventions. Across the prioritized themes, participants emphasized shared decision-making, compassionate care, and alignment with patient values, preferences, and lived experience.ConclusionsThe themes address obesity management targets alongside related care, measurement, education, and implementation considerations. This workshop represents a foundational step toward developing a comprehensive obesity management framework that integrates clinical evidence, patient priorities, and system-level considerations. Future work will focus on framework co-development, implementation, customization, and evaluation across healthcare settings.Keywords: Co-design framework, MoSCoW prioritization, Obesity management, Person-centred care, Treatment targets

Background

In an ever-increasing time of personalized medicine, obesity is a chronic and heterogeneous disease that requires tailored and individualized targets beyond just weight reduction. Researchers, clinicians, and patient partners agree that a more comprehensive approach to obesity management, and as a result, more comprehensive treatment goals, are necessary due to the complex interplay of biological, psychosocial, and environmental factors that comprise obesity [1] and the recent developments of highly effective and safe pharmacotherapy treatment options.

Despite the recent recognition of obesity as a complex chronic disease that is about more than weight, treatment goals and outcomes have historically been limited to percent weight loss targets. A recent scoping review [2] noted that weight loss targets (e.g., ≥5%) as part of obesity treatment goals were based on a handful of studies and a regulatory efficacy benchmark set by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1996 [[3], [4], [5]]. Globally, this treatment goal, or target, has been used for efficacy as well as in research with several international trials aiming for a 5–10% weight reduction [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Historically, clinical targets used to define “success” of obesity management have been established predominantly by clinicians with limited collaboration with patients living with obesity. The limited definition of obesity management success used by national bodies (with international reach) has downstream implications with respect to policy and creates a disconnect between what is seen as objective success in research compared to patient experiences of success. This disconnect between regulatory standards, research, patient experience, and policy means that obesity is not treated as a chronic disease in most health systems, access to evidence-based treatment is negatively impacted, and it perpetuates the outdated and harmful narrative that obesity is self-inflicted and can be simplified into the “eat-less-move-more” equation, which feeds into the pervasive weight bias and stigma environment. Research demonstrates that varying levels of weight loss are associated with a range of changes in health markers for different medical conditions, making the standard of 5% weight loss less relevant as it does not equate to the same results for all complications of obesity. Because obesity is a complex, chronic disease, all clinical practice, research and policy need to align with valid outcome measures for management of this chronic disease and the terminology used to describe these metrics.

Current evidence supports a more comprehensive and person-centred approach to obesity management, which is positively associated with physical and mental well-being, as well as higher patient satisfaction for individuals living with obesity[10] [11,12]. Weight and adiposity measures can remain clinically meaningful for diagnosis, risk assessment, treatment selection, monitoring treatment response, and goals identified by some patients. The concern is not the inclusion of weight, but its use as the sole or overriding definition of treatment success without consideration of complications, function, quality of life, treatment burden, and patient preferences. A rigid focus on weight loss targets may contribute to harms for some people living with obesity, including scale preoccupation, frustration, anxiety, or disengagement when weight change does not align with perceived effort or other health improvements [13]. Patients have expressed a desire to be included in decision-making and collaborate with healthcare professionals for better support and more sustainable outcomes [14,15]. As a result, several global obesity-focused organizations have stated that collaborative definitions of success and treatment outcomes are needed, especially moving beyond weight loss alone [16], and with a focus on personalized and person-centred care [10,12,17], which is free from judgment [14]. Selecting goals is also important to ensure we have an appropriate measure which gives a good understanding of treatment success. Guidelines like those from Obesity Canada emphasize the importance of personalized strategies that consider factors such as ethnicity, age, sex, and complications of obesity (or co-occurring conditions) to ensure care is equitable and effective [18]. These guidelines have been adopted across the globe, most recently in South Africa [19]. The Edmonton Obesity Staging System (EOSS) and the screening tool checkcausesobesity.com provide frameworks for individualized obesity management, moving beyond traditional metrics like body mass index (BMI) [20]. These approaches aim to help clinicians understand the broader context of obesity, determine underlying causes and impacts of obesity, and consider targets that are within the patient's control. Finally, comprehensive person-centred care also addresses weight bias, which can negatively affect healthcare delivery and patient outcomes [18,21].

To address the need for comprehensive treatment targets for obesity care, and following the completion of our scoping review on weight loss targets [2], we aimed to co-develop an obesity management framework that would incorporate evidence-based practice [22] and personalized treatment targets for obesity care that are meaningful to clinicians and patients. These redefined, comprehensive treatment targets for obesity would move beyond weight alone and include non-weight-related outcome targets such as physical, mental and social health outcomes that matter to people affected by obesity. This personalized obesity management framework would support integration of the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM) set of Patient-Centred Outcome Measures for Adults living with Obesity [16,23,24] and would support implementation of these outcome measures in practice. Such a framework would better align our understanding of obesity as a chronic disease that is more than weight, and it would support valid metrics that consider and measure many targets, which is more like how other chronic diseases are managed using a variety of outcomes and tools.

The first step in co-developing such a framework is to prioritize and align on the requirements and considerations for obesity care through an international approach uniquely suited to fostering meaningful collaboration and the exchange of ideas among diverse groups of interested and affected parties, including clinicians, researchers, and people with lived experience [25,26]. In this case, the included individuals, defined as clinicians, research and practice experts, and persons with lived experience, bring perspectives essential for addressing the challenges of obesity management. By creating a space for dialogue and structured problem-solving, workshops help mitigate the risk of groupthink by encouraging participants to share their unique viewpoints and experiences. This deliberate inclusion of varied perspectives ensures that the framework is person-centred and reflects a wide range of needs, priorities, and contexts, thereby increasing its feasibility and acceptability for use in clinical practice [27]. This manuscript describes the process of co-prioritizing the requirements of an obesity management framework, highlighting the value of the workshop approach in synthesizing prior input from an international, interprofessional, and diverse panel of individuals interested in and affected by obesity care.

Methods

This was an international workshop study using the MoSCoW (Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have) prioritization method to identify and prioritize requirements, outcome domains, and implementation considerations to inform the future development of an obesity management framework.

Setting, recruitment, and participants

Participants were recruited purposively from an international, multi-year project committee (n = 20) convened to support a program of work on comprehensive obesity management targets. The committee brought together physicians and clinician-researchers (n = 11) with expertise spanning obesity medicine and metabolic health, internal medicine, endocrinology and metabolism, bariatric and metabolic surgery, nephrology, epidemiology and prevention, and mental health; researchers (n = 3) with expertise in nursing, evidence synthesis, kinesiology, and applied physiology; leaders (n = 3) from obesity-focused organizations; and advocates (n = 3) from obesity organizations who also had lived experience of obesity. All 20 members of the project committee were invited to Toronto, Ontario, Canada for the December 2024 workshop. Invitations to the committee and workshop were based on individuals’ experience leading or contributing to major obesity-related projects and on the aim of bringing together complementary clinical, research, organizational, advocacy, lived-experience, and geographic perspectives. Participants were affiliated with institutions and organizations in Canada, the United States, Italy, Ireland, the United Kingdom, Chile, Spain, Malaysia, the Netherlands, and Japan. We strived for representation from all continents; however, some participants were unable to join this project committee due to capacity constraints.

Participation was voluntary, and a virtual option was available for individuals who could not attend in person. Personal demographic characteristics, including age, gender, race or ethnicity, Indigenous identity, socioeconomic position, and other identity characteristics, were not collected. We therefore report aggregate professional, organizational, lived-experience, and geographic characteristics based on participants’ workshop roles and institutional affiliations. This study did not require ethics approval or informed consent according to Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans (TCPS2, 2022) Article 2.1 and related guidance, as no personal data were collected and individuals participated in their official capacities, representing the opinions of their professional organizations. Consent was implied by accepting the invitation to participate.

Prioritization workshop structure and activities

The overall workshop was led and chaired by D.S., who has training in qualitative research methods, group facilitation, and consensus building. Participants were first given an overview of the MoSCoW workshop method for prioritization. Participants (including those who joined virtually) were divided into one of three groups to participate in the prioritization exercise. All participants were regrouped, and the results of the MoSCoW exercise were presented, and collaborative decisions were made regarding final prioritization. Further details of the workshop structure and agenda are available in Supplement 1.

Data collection – MoSCoW prioritization exercise

The MoSCoW Method [25,26,28,29] can be used to co-design and prioritize content, requirements and themes of a concept into four categories based on their significance for success: a) Must have (essential components), b) Should have (important, but not necessary), c) Could have (desired, but not necessary), and d) Won't have (considered out of scope or undesirable). These components could be tangible things, such as resources, or “principles/considerations” such as non-stigmatizing language. The MoSCoW approach was selected as it allows for prioritization while controlling scope management and supporting stakeholder alignment in a relatively fast and efficient manner (such as a one-day workshop with asynchronous virtual follow-up). Each group had 1 h to apply the MoSCoW Method to brainstorm a framework that could be used in the healthcare setting to optimally support, care for, and manage people living with obesity. There were two groups of in-person attendees and one virtual group. Attendees and facilitators used sticky notes to record ideas and added them to a large poster board divided into the MoSCoW categories. A facilitator (D.S., M.R., N.P.) was present at each table (or virtual Zoom breakout group) to direct the activity and to help record what was being shared on sticky notes, aid attendees in reflecting on the ideas being discussed, and prompt the group when appropriate to consider each MoSCoW category. The facilitators had prior experience in qualitative research, co-design, and group facilitation, and consistency was supported through a pre-workshop briefing, common agenda, standardized worksheet, and shared facilitation prompts. The facilitators did not independently determine the substantive content or final priority assigned to an item. Before the breakout sessions ended, each group reviewed their written outputs and confirmed that they reflected their discussion. Following activity completion, the facilitators (M.R., D.S.) compiled the sticky notes from all groups onto one large poster board based on the MoSCoW categories. The facilitators considered key themes and overlapping ideas for the requirements of an obesity management framework and grouped these concepts. The facilitators (D.S., M.R.) then presented the overall findings to the larger group, highlighting the key takeaways and welcoming additional reflections from the attendees. Virtual attendees used the chat function and the raise hand function of Zoom to add their reflections to the discussion. Participants were invited to clarify wording, identify overlap, propose consolidation, and reconsider category placement. Final prioritization was reached through discussion and collective agreement/verbal consensus. No predetermined numerical consensus threshold was used.

Data analysis

The data set consisted of the written outputs generated during the breakout and group discussions, including sticky notes, completed worksheets, virtual worksheet entries, Zoom chat contributions, facilitator notes, and photographs of the final boards. There was no audio- or video-recording of the workshop. The facilitators who are trained in qualitative analysis (M.R., D.S.) compiled the written outputs generated during the discussions into an Excel file. Each discrete written item was entered as a separate row, with the source group and original MoSCoW category retained. M.R. entered the items and D.S. checked the entries for accuracy. Items were first reviewed within their original MoSCoW category. Statements expressing similar ideas were grouped under a descriptive theme label. Exact or near-exact duplicates were consolidated, while distinct examples, qualifiers, and cautions were retained. Where related ideas appeared in more than one MoSCoW category, they were retained across categories when the category-specific meaning differed. Theme labels and the grouping of items were reviewed by M.R. and D.S. Differences were resolved through discussion and reference to the original written materials. This process followed a structured descriptive synthesis within the four predefined MoSCoW categories.

To further facilitate the co-design process [30,31], the resulting synthesis was shared with the workshop attendees as a form of member checking to ensure the themes captured the essence of the discussions. Participants were invited to comment on theme wording, category placement, missing content, and overlap. Their feedback led primarily to clarification of theme descriptions and consolidation of overlapping ideas and concepts. The revised synthesis was accepted as reflecting the workshop discussion.

Results

In total, 18 international attendees and facilitators participated in the workshop (2 invited participants were unable to attend). Fourteen attendees were in person, while four attended virtually. Participants included researchers (n = 3), healthcare professionals (physicians) (n = 9), obesity organizations (n = 3) and persons with lived experience (n = 3). Participants were affiliated with institutions and organizations in Canada, the United States, Italy, Ireland, the United Kingdom, Chile, Spain, Malaysia, the Netherlands, and Japan. The synthesis of the results of the prioritization exercise resulted in a total of 15 themes distributed across the four MoSCoW categories (Fig. 1). Some themes overlapped with other MoSCoW categories; however, they differed in the specific components or examples shared by participants. These themes are further described below and consist of both the content and requirements of an obesity management framework and also the education and dissemination considerations of communicating and implementing the framework. The 15 MoSCoW themes function as priority content requirements, implementation considerations, and principles of care, built through rigorous consensus methods, for a future obesity management framework.

Must have category

Our workshop highlighted 6 themes under the must-have framework concepts, two of which were exclusive to this category and deemed essential for the framework. Three themes were shared between must-have and should-have, whereas one theme was consistent across must-have, should-have, and could-have (i.e., person-centred care), with subtle differences in the specific details of these themes. Based on workshop participant feedback, the factors aligning with the Canadian pillars of obesity management theme were condensed and agreed to be only in the must-have category. Pillars of obesity management must adopt a multi-faceted approach involving obesity medications, surgical options, and psychological/behavioral care, with an acknowledgement that obesity is a chronic disease with dynamic staging to track progression and remission. Participants shared frustrations that obesity care is often removed or withdrawn once a patient's health and/or weight improves; however, obesity care and treatment are often needed long-term to continue managing this chronic, progressive, and relapsing disease. Healthcare professional sensitivity training was also specified as essential for the education and training on an obesity framework to combat weight bias and enhance empathetic care delivery. This could include education on what internalized weight bias is and how it can impact patients, how to identify it, how to address it both within the healthcare professional and in the patient, and what other resources or professionals can be included to support these conversations. The two themes (multi-faceted pillars of obesity management and healthcare professional sensitivity training) were considered must-haves following a conversation about the inherent risk of setting targets, especially when targets may be set to physiological outcomes that the patient does not have control over as opposed to control over their behaviors. Thus, the group felt it necessary that there be training to support clinicians in understanding all aspects of obesity care and weight bias. The framework measures must also include ways to assess, screen, and monitor for complications related to obesity including psychological distress, physical issues, metabolic disorders, and financial or social barriers. Under the must-have category, the surveillance of obesity complications could include cardiometabolic parameters, pain, mobility, fertility, depression, anxiety, sleep apnea, and patient-reported outcomes such as quality of life and stigma. Related to the assessment of complications, it will also be essential that the framework include clinically relevant and valid outcome measures that can be used reliably and capture unintended effects and adverse events. Lastly, while person-centred care was a theme across all MoSCoW categories, participants made specific mention of shared decision-making, tailored “dashboards” (data visualization tools presenting individualized patient outcomes), and compassionate, non-judgmental care, and that care ultimately aligns with personal values, preferences and lived experiences. The concept of “food peace” vs. “food noise” was also discussed as it relates to satiety and hunger cues and the mental burden that thoughts about food can contribute to someone who is managing their obesity.

Should have category

We highlighted four themes for the should-have category of an obesity management framework. These themes all overlap with other MoSCoW categories, but the group differentiated on details that may not be essential or foundational to the framework and could be used at the discretion of the patient and healthcare professional. For the theme of clinically related outcomes, metrics related to body composition and central obesity measurements like waist-to-hip or waist-to-height ratios were discussed, as well as refining clinical outcomes based on personalized metrics, such as age, sex, and ethnicity. These were noted as should-haves, rather than must-haves, since some clinical measurements are not required or necessary to treat obesity and can sometimes evoke shame, negative emotions and negatively affect the patient's mental health. Workshop attendees also felt that an obesity management framework should have the option to formally assess and track mental health issues and consider other physical function parameters for improved mobility, activities of daily living, etc. Patient-reported outcomes for the should-have category included incorporating an understanding of eating patterns and nutritional adequacy for sustained health improvements while addressing disordered eating behaviors to foster healthier relationships with food. Coinciding with the previous two themes is the continuation of patient-centred care that values functionality and health gains over strict weight-centric targets and adapts interventions to each patient's unique journey, recognizing there may be aesthetic and body image motivations to be considered. Finally, the last overlapping theme in the should-have category was surveillance of complications and management of obesity to support the increased knowledge and understanding of obesity definitions, BMI cut-offs, chronic disease classifications, and other contexts relevant to obesity care.

Could have category

Under the could-have category of the framework, the workshop members identified that ongoing learning for patients and health care professionals could be offered and sustained as the field of obesity is evolving and this framework could support sharing up-to-date, evidence-based information (treatment options, staging and classification of obesity, etc.). Ongoing training and sustained support are particularly important because obesity is a chronic disease and just like other chronic diseases, there is a need to support beyond initial goals, through continued engagement during "obesity remission" to address challenges such as weight recurrence, emotional factors, and evolving health needs to ensure resilience and sustainability. This may be supported through the third theme, team-based care, which consists of a multidisciplinary approach that ideally includes collocated interprofessional care professionals or a network of professionals (i.e., through referrals and partnerships) who offer comprehensive and personalized care, aligning with a patient's values, goals and preferences. Finally, the framework could highlight effective policies and investments in obesity management, offering individual and societal benefits by addressing financial costs and public health challenges. Participants noted that any conversations regarding the “cost of inaction of treating obesity” need to be handled with patient context and sensitivities considering that these discussions can involve shame and stigma for people living with obesity who may feel that they are a burden on the healthcare system, costing taxpayer dollars, etc.

Won't have category

Lastly, the themes in the won't-have category consisted mostly of principles of care and standards for building the obesity management framework. Healthcare professionals working with people with obesity and tailoring an obesity management plan with their patients must be mindful of judgmental care or morally superior language which can invoke shame and stigmatize patients. Treatments offered must be sustainable and long-term solutions rather than “quick fix” options. Healthcare professionals should recognize and discuss “health gains” made through these treatments and support patients in the changes they have made (through a model of patient-led internal reinforcement and not external healthcare professional reinforcement) perhaps alongside weight loss, rather than reducing treatment success to weight loss alone. An obesity management framework must also not over-emphasize aesthetics such as before/after photos or the use of any singular measure as a marker of success (such as weight or BMI). Any absolute targets being used must-have caveats and context to avoid any unintended consequences. Lastly, the obesity management framework must support healthcare professionals in monitoring progress across many targets and provide treatment options based on these components to limit any abandonment or failure to provide care to patients.

Discussion

A more comprehensive approach to obesity management and treatment needs to be implemented in clinical practice. The target for treatment should not be based solely on weight, but rather on a more patient-centred, personalized, and tailored approach to sustainable obesity management. Such an approach should consider the complex etiology and social determinants of health in addition to the impacts on health from elevated body weights. These factors include predisposition to obesity, differences in ethnicity, age, sex, regional adipose distribution, duration, and severity of obesity [1,32], among others. An approach that considers these complex factors would acknowledge the current understanding that body weight is not fully under an individual's control [33,34] and thus should not be the sole marker of treatment success or singular focus of obesity management targets. This is highlighted by a recent meta-analysis, which showed that while glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are effective, stopping treatment often leads to rapid and near-complete weight regain within 1–2 years [35], and weight regain can also occur in individuals post-bariatric surgery [36]. Without ongoing and structured long-term care and follow-up, obesity treatment focused on one method (such as medication alone), and one that focuses on one outcome (such as weight loss), is not sustainable.

Following the MoSCoW approach [25,26,28,29], the findings from this workshop highlight the need for comprehensive obesity care and the importance of establishing a personalized framework for treatment goals and outcomes. Overall, the discussion stressed the need to consider a person-centred obesity management approach, anchored on the psychological components (e.g., how people think about obesity, obesity is in the brain, food noise, hunger, cravings); the physical issues and metabolic disorders (e.g., weight change, complications management); and financial or social barriers (emotions, psychological, bias, stigma, generational trauma). This approach responds to previous global calls to action for a patient-centred approach to obesity care [10,12,37]. Moreover, the workshop also addressed previous gaps in clinical practice guidelines in obesity management, specifically the need to consider treatment goals that encompass a broader definition of treatment success and complement clinically relevant weight-based and adiposity-related measures that may not be as meaningful to patients [37]. Their relevance should be determined by the clinical context, the intervention being used, and the patient's informed goals and preferences. Because obesity is a chronic disease [38], it needs to be managed as such, which includes long-term follow-up and measurements and lifelong treatment options, such as medications. An obesity management framework could better support a comprehensive view of health and the long-term treatment of this chronic disease [38,39].

A more comprehensive and less weight-centric approach would better address obesity as a chronic disease, reduce shame and stigma associated with obesity, and allow people to focus on actions and behavior that impact health beyond weight. This approach is also supported by multiple national and international organizations and consortiums including the Canadian Adult Obesity Clinical Practice Guidelines [18], the framework from the European Association for the Study of Obesity [12], the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM) set of Patient-Centred Outcome Measures for Adults living with Obesity [16], and a recently published core set of clinical and patient-reported outcome measures for measuring patient-relevant outcomes and quality of life in clinical obesity care [22,24]. Recent work [40] with healthcare professionals and people living with obesity resulted in a consensus report with eight patient-reported outcomes and measures including self-esteem, physical health/functioning, mental/psychological health, social health, eating, stigma (for which no valid measurement tool exists), body image, and excess skin as potential targets for treatment [23]. As the obesity management space evolves and there are significant efforts to advocate for improved care and access to treatments, a barrier to these efforts is the data being collected and reported as targets for success [41]. An example of this barrier and its impact on improved care and access to treatments is the discrepancy and inconsistency in the reimbursement of some obesity medications depending on how success or effectiveness is defined. A co-created approach, like an obesity management framework which will follow the work presented in this paper, would align with global action, support the implementation of outcomes, measures and evidence-based practice [22], and integrate clinical experience (such as a sustainable weight loss target outcome), research evidence (such as patient's cardiometabolic blood samples aligning with chronic disease targets, meeting guideline recommendations and efficacy averages for the interventions being implemented), and patient experience (such as important patient-preferred outcomes and non-weight-related outcome targets including quality of life and physical, mental, and social health).

The discussion in the workshop resulted in two broad categories: the concepts of the framework and the implementation of the framework. First, the concepts of the framework and outcomes that it tracks would support the implementation and mobilization of existing outcome sets [16] by putting these measures, tools, and outcomes all together in one place, for use in practice through different modalities and formats. The framework could also provide the opportunity for the prioritization and tracking of changes in these measures. Beyond just tracking clinical changes and using qualitative surveys and scales to assess outcomes holistically across the proposed domains of psychological distress, physical issues, metabolic disorders, and financial or social barriers, these measures may also assess aspects of social functioning, dietary behaviors, quality of life, or mental health. Through a comprehensive view of obesity and all its associated outcomes, the framework would enable patients and clinicians to monitor complications from obesity. The flexibility of the framework would be important as new considerations and components emerge, such as the need for thorough assessment and counselling of patients on GLP-1 RAs [42] and the concept of food noise [43]. Second, the group began to conceptualize how a comprehensive framework would be configured and tested for implementation. Workshop attendees noted that the framework would have to be built for purpose; it should be patient-facing and meet individual needs, as well as healthcare professional-facing and support clinical care. The discussion also highlighted foundational touchstones, beginning with professional education, messaging, knowledge mobilization, and training that would minimize bias and stigma among clinicians [44]. Rather than broad policies, guidelines, or health service delivery frameworks, the goal of this work is to directly support healthcare professionals and their patients in obesity management and treatment that looks beyond weight. An important and crucial component to the success of such a framework will be its implementation. Frameworks and policies face multiple complex barriers related to economic and resource constraints, knowledge gaps and capacity issues, stigma and cultural resistance, and equity/access gaps [45,46][]. Therefore, the co-design of a framework should include all interested parties to ensure feasibility, acceptability, adaptability, usability and effectiveness [47] along with coordinated action and implementation case studies to demonstrate the customization of the framework and to assess implementability and potential impact on patient-reported outcomes and clinical care.

Limitations

Despite the small number of participants and the short duration of the workshop, we believe that we maximized the time and effort by framing the workshop using the MoSCoW method with a global group of experts who represent a strong and diverse set of organizations. We attempted to have representation from all continents; however, we acknowledge that due to timing and capacity constraints of invited participants, we may have ended up with selective sampling that misses opportunities to widen involvement and drive innovation. A strength is that we elucidated meaningful and actionable themes from an international group of interdisciplinary health care professionals, researchers and patients with lived experience, aimed at creating a comprehensive obesity management framework. Specifically, we leveraged purposefully divided small groups (two in person and one virtually) and large group discussion formats to support the generation of new ideas and the interrogation of the status quo, thus attempting to avoid common traps in workshop discussions (e.g., groupthink) [48]. Because discussions were not recorded or transcribed, verbatim participant quotations are unavailable. The analysis therefore reflects the written outputs and facilitator-supported synthesis rather than the full nuance of the oral discussions. The workshop also did not specifically evaluate feasibility in low-resource settings or undertake a dedicated engagement process with Indigenous Peoples or other culturally distinct communities. Additional engagement and feedback from people with lived experience of obesity as well as global societies and organizations not included in this workshop will help validate data collected from the MoSCoW method, improve the framework design, and address implementation barriers to encourage more universal applicability.

Conclusion

Obesity management requires a comprehensive person-centred care approach, one that acknowledges and supports an inclusive approach. This international MoSCoW workshop identified 15 prioritized requirements to inform the subsequent development of a person-centred obesity management framework. Participants emphasized multidimensional treatment goals, clinically relevant and patient-reported outcomes, shared decision-making, long-term care, healthcare professional education, and avoidance of stigmatizing or context-free definitions of success. This workshop represented the initial stage of developing an obesity management framework consisting of comprehensive treatment targets, while offering insights that may inform future collaborative initiatives in obesity treatment. Further co-design of the framework and implementation testing are needed across diverse clinical, cultural, socioeconomic, and health-system contexts.

Ethical review

This study did not require formal ethics approval as it did not involve research with human participants as defined by the Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans (TCPS2, 2022). This workshop was conducted as a closed, invitation-only event involving professionals and individuals who participated in their official capacities representing the opinions of their professional organizations. Participants were not required to sign informed consent letters, as this workshop was led by Obesity Canada as an expert consensus workshop where participants contributed professional opinions without sharing personal data. No personal data, identifiable information, or biological materials were collected, and no interventions were conducted. The purpose of the session was to facilitate discussion and co-development of a framework, rather than to systematically investigate or collect data from individuals. Consent to provide their perspectives was implied from accepting the voluntary invitation. According to TCPS2 Article 2.1 and related guidance, this type of activity falls outside the scope of research requiring Research Ethics Board (REB) review.
