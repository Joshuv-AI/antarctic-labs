Treatment-resistant chronic urticaria associated with papillary thyroid carcinoma: a case report
Authors: Muzna Al Farsi, Maisa Hamed Al Kiyumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic idiopathic urticaria is a challenging condition that may require advanced therapies, such as omalizumab. Its association with malignancy, particularly papillary thyroid carcinoma, remains unclear and is rarely reported.Case presentationA 31-year-old Omani woman, with treatment-resistant idiopathic chronic urticaria requiring omalizumab for symptom control was subsequently diagnosed with papillary thyroid carcinoma. The patient underwent total thyroidectomy, after which she experienced partial remission of urticaria symptoms, including the longest symptom-free interval since disease onset.ConclusionsThis case highlights a potential association between papillary thyroid carcinoma and chronic idiopathic urticaria, suggesting that evaluation for underlying malignancy may be considered in refractory cases.

Introduction

Chronic urticaria is characterized by recurrent wheals, angioedema, or both persisting for more than six weeks [1]. The global prevalence is estimated at 0.5–1% of the general population, with higher rates reported among women aged of 20–40 years [2, 3]. Although it is not typically life-threatening, chronic urticaria is associated with substantial impairment in quality of life owing to persistent pruritus, sleep disturbance, and adverse psychological effects [4]. The pathogenesis remains incompletely understood; however, available evidence suggests an autoimmune basis in approximately 40–50% of cases, mediated by autoantibodies directed against the high-affinity immunoglobulin E receptor (FcεRI) or immunoglobulin E itself, leading to mast cell and basophil activation [5, 6]. Less common associations include autoreactivity, chronic infections, thyroid autoimmunity, and systemic disease [7]. Nonetheless, no underlying cause is identified in a substantial proportion of patients.

In the present report, a case of severe, treatment-resistant idiopathic urticaria occurring in association with papillary thyroid carcinoma is described. The urticaria improved markedly following total thyroidectomy, supporting a potential association between papillary thyroid carcinoma and idiopathic chronic urticaria.

Case report

A 31-year-old Omani woman (para 1) with a history of iron deficiency anemia, idiopathic chronic urticaria (requiring monthly omalizumab after failure of high-dose oral antihistamines), cholecystectomy in 2020, and total thyroidectomy for papillary thyroid carcinoma in August 2025, presented to the endocrinology clinic for postoperative follow-up.

Her urticaria began during her most recent pregnancy, in the second trimester (approximately 14 weeks’ gestation), when she developed multiple pruritic, erythematous wheals over the trunk and extremities (Figs. 1A, B). There were no associated respiratory symptoms, wheeze, gastrointestinal symptoms, fever, recent travel, new medications, insect bites, or other identifiable triggers, and there were no features of anaphylaxis. Initial investigations were non-contributory and no systemic causes of pruritus was identified (Table 1). Over the following weeks, symptoms persisted despite loratadine 10 mg/day and became increasingly burdensome, prompting dermatology assessment. Idiopathic chronic urticaria was diagnosed. Loratadine was then prescribed on a regular schedule, with dose escalation every two weeks to a maximum of 40 mg/day, which she continued for approximately 12 weeks. Control remained incomplete: wheals continued to recur on alternate days, although pruritus was less prominent.

Given the ongoing symptoms, fexofenadine increased to 180 mg twice daily for a further two weeks. Despite these adjustments, symptomatic control was not achieved. Symptoms persisted and control remained inadequate. Night-time chlorphenamine maleate was added for three weeks but did not provide adequate control. She was subsequently switched back to loratadine 40 mg/day, which she continued until delivery.

She delivered spontaneously at term. Within 24 h postpartum, she experienced a severe flare of urticaria and was treated with intravenous hydrocortisone 200 mg and intramuscular chlorphenamine 10 mg. She was discharged on a prednisone taper (40 mg daily for seven days, then tapered over three weeks) in addition to loratadine 40 mg/day.

On postpartum day four, symptoms worsened and she required a further dose of intravenous hydrocortisone 200 mg with chlorphenamine 10 mg at a local health centre. By postpartum day nine, symptoms recurred again despite high-dose antihistamines and the ongoing steroid taper. Repeat laboratory investigations were within normal limits. She was therefore referred to immunology and started on omalizumab 300 mg subcutaneously every four weeks, with a gradual taper of systemic corticosteroids. Clear clinical improvement was evident by eight weeks, and loratadine was discontinued by 16 weeks postpartum. She remained symptom-free on omalizumab for more than 12 months.

When attempts were made to increase the dosing interval beyond four weeks, urticaria recurred. Monthly injections were continued for a further year, for a total omalizumab treatment duration of approximately 20 months.

In early 2025, the patient detected a palpable thyroid nodule. Thyroid function tests were within the reference range. Neck ultrasonography demonstrated a 1.6 × 1.0 cm hypoechoic solid nodule in the right thyroid lobe, wider than tall, with irregular margins and no microcalcifications (TI-RADS 4). Fine-needle aspiration cytology was diagnostic of papillary thyroid carcinoma (Bethesda VI). Total thyroidectomy was performed in August 2025, and levothyroxine was initiated at 1.6 µg/kg/day.

The postoperative course was uncomplicated, with normal thyroid function tests, calcium, and parathyroid hormone levels. Following thyroidectomy, she was able to extend her omalizumab dosing interval to 8–9 weeks for the first time since diagnosis, with only mild, localized urticaria limited to the foot.

Discussion

Idiopathic chronic urticaria is a common condition worldwide and is associated with substantial impairment in quality of life. Although it is frequently encountered in clinical practice, an underlying cause is not identified in a considerable proportion of cases. In the present report, a 31-year-old woman had idiopathic urticaria that was chronic, relapsing, and refractory to high-dose antihistamines and systemic corticosteroids, necessitating treatment with a biologic agent. She was subsequently diagnosed with papillary thyroid carcinoma and underwent total thyroidectomy. Notably, she reported a marked improvement in urticaria following surgery, with the longest symptom-free interval since disease onset.

Although an association between idiopathic chronic urticaria and papillary thyroid carcinoma is rarely reported, several published case reports have highlighted a temporal link between thyroidectomy and urticaria remission. Manganoni et al. described four women (aged 32–61 years) with chronic relapsing urticaria and occult papillary thyroid carcinoma who experienced marked improvement within 1–4 days of total thyroidectomy, with sustained remission for up to seven years [8]. A further report described a 25-year-old man with resistant urticaria in whom symptoms resolved within two days of thyroidectomy for papillary thyroid carcinoma [9]. In a retrospective study of 359 patients with chronic urticaria, five women were identified with occult thyroid cancer (four papillary thyroid carcinoma and one papillary/follicular carcinoma); all experienced rapid remission of urticarial symptoms after thyroidectomy, which persisted during follow-up [10]. Similarly, Demirel et al. reported two women with chronic urticaria who were subsequently diagnosed with papillary thyroid carcinoma; both achieved complete resolution of symptoms within 3–4 days after surgery [11]. The mechanism underpinning a potential association between idiopathic chronic urticaria and papillary thyroid carcinoma remains uncertain. Autoimmune overlap has been proposed, given the recognized associated between chronic urticaria and thyroid disease and the observation that papillary thyroid carcinoma may arise in the setting of chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis. Dysregulated immune pathways, including autoantibody-mediated processes and T-helper type 2 cytokines signaling (e.g., interleukin-4 and interleukin-10), have been suggested as potential shared mechanisms [5, 6]. Alternatively, urticaria may represent a paraneoplastic manifestations, in which tumor-derived antigens or immune complexes promote mast cell activation. In support of this hypothesis, several reports have described a close temporal relationship between thyroidectomy and urticaria improvement [8–11]. Across published cases, the rapid improvement or complete resolution of urticaria after total thyroidectomy supports an association that extends beyond coincidental coexistence; however, causality cannot be established from case reports alone [8–11].

Conclusions

Although the cause of idiopathic chronic urticaria often remains unidentified, this report adds to the literature by describing an uncommon association with papillary thyroid carcinoma. Papillary thyroid carcinoma should be considered among potential underlying conditions in patients with chronic, relapsing, or treatment-resistant urticaria, particularly when symptoms persist despite appropriate standard therapy. Earlier recognition may support improved symptom control and prompt evaluation for occult thyroid malignancy.
