DE-SC0021158: Additive-Assisted Preparation of Multinary Halides
1. What are the main objectives of this project?
 This project aims to prepare a series of metastable multinary halides through understanding the
role of solution-phase metal halide precursors, the impact of various impurity additives, and solvent-
solute interactions for solution growth and synthesis. A few additive-assisted syntheses of hybrid metal
halide examples, including MA2AgBiBr6 and MA4Bi6I22 (where MA = methylammonium cation), which
could not be easily obtained by direct combinations of reactants without the addition of PbBr2, HgI2,
respectively, as impurity additives, have been reported. However, the role of additives remains
unknown among these studies.
 Through this project, efforts for understanding the solution chemistry and reaction pathways of
metal halide synthesis are expected to result in optimized synthesis of high-quality halide single
crystals for practical applications, including X-ray radiation detectors, light-emitting diodes (LEDs),
transistors, and solar cells. For this project, we have the following specific aims:
 • To study the solution chemistry of homometallic halides,
 • To investigate the solution chemistry of heterometallic (i.e., multiple metal cations) halides,
 and
 • To prepare metal halides through the studies outlined in the first two aims and analyze their
 structure-property relationships, including optical, structural, and electronic properties.

 transitioned to focus on bimetallic systems (i.e., halides containing two B metals) and understanding

 2.1 Heterometallic MA – Bi – X – I system
 We had previously shown that a new semiconductor, MA4Bi6I22 can be obtained when the HgI2
 additive is used along with the MAI and BiI3 reactants. We have been conducting experiments to
 elucidate the role of the HgI2 additive. Figure 1 shows the UV-Vis absorption spectra for MA-Bi-I
 solutions with a MAI: BiI3 loading ratio of 1:1 with and without the HgI2 additive. An absorption band
 centered at 362 nm was observed for the solution with no HgI2 additive, while a new 378 nm
 absorption band was observed instead for the solution with HgI2 additive. These differing absorption
 spectra could explain the two different products, MA3Bi2I9 and MA4Bi6I22, obtained from the two
 solutions. Our computational studies (Figures 2 and 3) suggest that the absorption maxima are red-
 shifted as condensation of octahedral [BiI6]3- occurs, i.e., dimeric [Bi2I9]3- and hexameric [Bi6I22]4-
 have red-shifted absorption bands starting from 350 nm (for octahedral [BiI6]3-) to 375 nm region
 (for [Bi6I22]4-). Since the 362 nm and 378 nm two absorption bands match more closely with the
 calculated absorption maxima for the dimer [Bi2I9]3- and polyhedral [Bi6I22]4-, respectively, these
 bands were correspondingly assigned to these cluster anions.

 MA4Bi6I22 is a more BiI3 rich composition (compared to MA3Bi2I9) and could perhaps be obtained
under BiI3 rich conditions. To test this idea, a series of MA-Bi-I solutions were prepared with the
MAI:BiI3 ratios (Figure 4). Interestingly, absorption bands centered at 362 nm and 378 nm were
observed depending on the reactant loading ratio. We tentatively assign the two observed absorption
bands again to the polyanionic clusters [Bi2I9]3- and [Bi6I22]4-. To confirm these assignments, we set up
crystal growth experiments, which yielded dark red needles of MA4Bi6I22 for BiI3 rich solutions (Figures

5 and 6). Our work has shown that, in fact MA4Bi6I22 can be obtained without the HgI2 additive, contrary
to our earlier understanding, and that the use of the HgI2 additive can be replicated under BiI3 rich
conditions.

 To gain more insight into the specific role of HgI2 additive, we performed small angle X-ray
scattering (SAXS) measurements (Figure 7), which indicate that the ratio 1:1 solution without HgI2
contains [Bi2I9]3-. The measured SAXS curve for the ratio 1:1 solution with HgI2 agrees well with the
simulated curve for [HgI3]-. The presence of [HgI3]- is further confirmed by electrospray ionization mass
spectrometry (ESI-MS) (Figure 8). Therefore, we now could reasonably draw a conclusion that the
role of HgI2 additive in the synthesis of MA4Bi6I22 is to tune the MAI to BiI3 ratio in solution by forming
MAHgI3. The initial 1:1 MAI:BiI3 loading content changes upon addition of HgI2 as the latter binds some
of MAI by forming MAHgI3, which in turn leads to a more BiI3 rich composition of the solution and
eventually to the formation of MA4Bi6I22 rather than MA3Bi2I9. The DFT calculation results (Figure 9)
show that [HgI3]- absorbs in the deep UV region, beyond our UV-vis measurement capabilities.

 We also attempted to replicate the role of HgI2 by using other halide additives. Absorption data
(Figure 10) suggests that ZnI2 may not assist the formation of MA4Bi6I22, whereas CdI2 could as it
yields the same feature at 378 nm. This is confirmed by crystal growth experiments using CdI2 and
ZnI2 as additives, which show that MA4Bi6I22 is formed when CdI2 is used. When Mn2+ and Ge2+ are
used as additives, only the high stability MA3Bi2I9 phase is obtained. These results are valuable and
suggest that solution chemistry studies can be effective for guided materials synthesis and discovery.
In the next reporting period, larger and more hydrophobic cations, trimethylsulfonium (TMS+) and 1-
butyl-1-methylpyrrolidinium, will be used in our solution chemistry studies.

for a direct fit and 3.94 eV assuming indirect transitions (Figure 14).

 In summary, we elucidated the role of the HgI2 additive used in the synthesis of MA4Bi6I22.
Unexpectedly, it was revealed that the reactant loading ratio plays a key role for the solution synthesis
of this material, and the HgI2 additive indirectly impacts the reactant ratio in the solution phase. A more
general conclusion, additive-assisted synthesis could be effective in tuning the effective molar ratio of
reactants in the solution phase and hence could lead to unexpected products. Another major
accomplishment, two new halide materials, (TEP)InBr4 and (TEP)3Ag6Br9 were discovered. We are

 In this work, we investigated a new class of transition metal intercalation between the layers of 2D
metal halides. Here, we focused on the synthesis and property characterizations of two-layered metal
halides with intercalated Cu2+. Both [Cu(O2C-CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4 and [Cu(O2C-(CH2)3-NH3)2]PbBr4
exhibit a 2D layered crystal structure with Cu2+ integrated between the layers of metal halides. This
arrangement resulted by mixing Cu2+ and lead bromide with appropriate amino acid precursors. While
[Cu(O2C-(CH2)3-NH3)2]PbBr4 showed a typical 2D layered perovskite structure, the other [Cu(O2C-
CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4 crystallizes in a new structure type relying on edge-sharing dodecahedral PbBr5O3
units in an inorganic lead bromide layer (Figure 17). Both compounds show distinct variations in
inorganic layer and the organic interlayer. In [Cu(O2C-CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4, the intercalated Cu2+ ions
form a linear complex with the anionic form of glycine, whereas in [Cu(O2C-(CH2)3-NH3)2]PbBr4, the
Cu2+ exists in the form of Cu2(CO2)4 “paddle-wheel” clusters. The depictions of the coordination
environments of lead and copper are shown in Figure 18.

 Single crystals of [Cu(O2C-CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4 and [Cu(O2C-(CH2)3-NH3)2]PbBr4 were synthesized
through a variety of solution-based methods. In each case, the purity of the crystallized products was
confirmed through experimental and simulated PXRD measurements. Furthermore, periodic PXRD
scans show that both compounds are air stable (Figure 19).

 To investigate whether the structural differences between [Cu(O2C-CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4 and
[Cu(O2C-(CH2)3-NH3)2]PbBr4 arise from their distinct solution chemistries, UV−vis spectroscopy,
Electrospray Ionization Mass Spectrometry (ESI-MS), and Small-Angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)
measurements were conducted on the respective precursor solutions. The findings reveal the
presence of Cu2+ complexes with both bromide and amino acid ligands, indicating the differences may
arise during the process of crystallization. A comparison of absorption spectra indicates a red-shifted
d-d absorption band showing a peak at 786 nm for [Cu(O2C-(CH2)3-NH3)2]PbBr4, whereas [Cu(O2C-
CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4 displays a peak at 768 nm (Figure 20).

 The thermal stability of [Cu(O2C-CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4 and [Cu(O2C-(CH2)3-NH3)2]PbBr4 was
analyzed by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA). The TGA
analysis indicate that both compounds follow multi-step decomposition at higher temperature (Figure
22). The observed weight losses are attributed to the decomposition of the organic structural
component. [Cu(O2C-CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4 demonstrates a significant mass loss beyond 200°C, while
[Cu(O2C-(CH2)3-NH3)2]PbBr4 shows a weight reduction of 2.5% even below 100°C. DSC curves were
recorded from room temperature to 400 °C, revealing two endothermic peaks for both [Cu(O2C-CH2-
NH2)2]Pb2Br4 and [Cu(O2C-(CH2)3-NH3)2]PbBr4. Moreover, a notably broad endothermic feature was
observed beyond 240°C for these compounds, which can be directly linked to the thermal
decomposition of amino acids (glycine and 4-amino butyric acid). The endothermic peaks at 201°C
and 198.7°C for [Cu(O2C-CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4 and [Cu(O2C-(CH2)3-NH3)2]PbBr4, respectively,
correspond to their melting transitions, as the solid samples undergo melting on the apparatus at this
temperature.

[Cu(O2C-CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4 measured in magnetic fields µ0H = 1T and 10T is shown in Figure 26a.
The plot of inverse susceptibility (1/χ) indicates that data obtained in µ0H = 1T can be described by
Curie–Weiss law regardless of the slight change of slope around 150 K (inset of Figure 26a). Fitting
of the data segments with two different slopes provides similar values for magnetic moment and

 In conclusion, the crystal growth, optical, thermal, electronic, and magnetic properties were
examined for two layered 2D metal halides i.e., [Cu(O2C-CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4 and [Cu(O2C-(CH2)3-
NH3)2]PbBr4. Use of two different zwitterionic amino acids (glycine and 4-aminobutyric acid) resulted
into distinct structures for these compounds. Notably, [Cu(O2C-CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4 exhibits a wider
band gap (3.25 eV) due to the incorporation of oxygen into the inorganic layers compared to [Cu(O2C-
(CH2)3-NH3)2]PbBr4 (2.9 eV). The exceptional stability of these compounds in ambient conditions make
them suitable candidates for semiconductor detector applications. Single crystals of [Cu(O2C-CH2-
NH2)2]Pb2Br4 exhibit a semiconductor resistivity of 1.44×1010 Ω·cm (measured along the a-axis) and
2.17×1010 Ω·cm (along the bc-plane), respectively. The fabricated X-ray detector based on [Cu(O2C-
CH2-NH2)2]Pb2Br4 single crystals shows a detector sensitivity of 1462.7 µCGy-1cm-2, indicating its
potential application for ionizing radiation detection. Importantly, the results of this study suggest the
possibility of expanding the range of hybrid organic-inorganic metal halides with intercalated
secondary metal ions, which may yield new materials with unique structures and physical properties
for practical applications.

2.4 Systematic Investigation of a Novel Family of Hybrid Double Metal Halide Perovskites for
 X-ray Radiation Detection Applications

Figure 28. Pictorial representation of the distinctive structure of diammonium-halide-diammonium
double metal halide perovskites.

 Our most recent work investigates a new class of 2D layered lead-free hybrid double metal halide
perovskites that feature a distinctive structure with a diammonium-halide-diammonium (DHD) organic
“spacer” layer. Until now, most research regarding the layered metal halide perovskites has focused
on Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) and Dion-Jacobson (DJ) type materials. The recent discovery of halide
perovskites incorporating DHD layers suggests possibilities for new discoveries that can yield new
materials with unique photophysics. The target structure of these compounds is illustrated in Figure
28, where the inorganic layer resembles that from double perovskites in which Pb2+ ion is replaced by
a pair of monovalent and trivalent metal cations. In contrast, the organic layer contains a bilayer of
organic cations along with an additional halide layer.

Figure 29. Crystal images and crystal structures of (a) (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12, (b) (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr12,
(c) (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr5I7, and (d) (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr5I7 are depicted. Copper is represented by blue,

 17

bismuth by pink, silver by grey, bromine by brown, iodine by purple, nitrogen by cyan, carbon by black,
and hydrogen by peach spheres, respectively.

 As a part of this study, we successfully synthesized four distinct compounds with the formulas
(C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12, (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr12, (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr5I7, and (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr5I7. Contrary to
other reported layered double metal halide perovskites (e.g., RP and DJ), the title compounds have
two layers of diamines electrostatically bonded with interlayer halides in the middle (Figure 29). This
structural feature is similar to the well-known Aurivillius phases that are derived from oxide perovskites.
To understand the formation of the specific DHD structure, we assumed the influence of the interlayer
distance and the diammonium head-to-tail length of the organic cation. As the typical interlayer
distance for layered perovskites lies in the range of 4 to 6 Å, however, the chain length of ethylene
diammonium cation is approximately 3.1 Å, which destabilizes the layered structure. Nevertheless, by
aligning in the DHD configuration, the interlayer distance lies in the usual range of ~6 Å, which helps
to stabilize this unique structural configuration. To study the effect of diammonium head-to-tail length
of the organic cation on the formation of the specific DHD configuration, we also synthesized a new
compound, namely, (C3H12N2)2AgBiBr8, by using a slightly longer organic cation, i.e., propane-1,3-
diamine. As expected, this compound crystallized in a DJ configuration without any additional
interhalide layer. Hence, this observation confirmed the hypothesis of the influence of interlayer
distance and chain length of organic cation on the formation of DHD configuration.

Figure 30. Coordination environments of (a) copper, (b) bismuth, (c) silver, and (d) ammonium-to-
ammonium distance in ethylene diammonium cation are depicted. Copper is represented by blue,
bismuth by pink, silver by grey, bromine by brown, nitrogen by cyan, carbon by black, and hydrogen
by peach spheres, respectively.

 SCXRD analysis revealed that for (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12 and (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr12, bromide occupies
both interlayer and layered halide positions, while in the case of mixed halide versions, i.e.,
(C4H10N2)4CuBiBr5I7 and (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr5I7, the smaller halide (Br-) favors the interlayer positions
due to the stronger hydrogen bonding between the diammonium cations and the higher charge density
of Br-. Furthermore, it was observed that for both Cu-Br and Ag-Br systems, the equatorial bond
lengths, which are 3.7 Å and 3.3 Å, respectively, are significantly longer than those in reported systems
in CCDC. However, the axial bond lengths (2.2 Å and 2.5 Å, respectively) are within the same range
(Figure 30). Therefore, due to this unique bonding arrangement, CuX2 or AgX2 is likely to be bonded

 18

linear axially to the halide ion. Nonetheless, for clarity in pictorial representation of double perovskite
structures, this arrangement will be illustrated by octahedra.

Figure 31. Room-temperature PXRD patterns (in black) fitted using the Pawley method (in red), the
difference between the measured and calculated patterns is shown in blue for (a) (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12,
(b) (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr12, (c) (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr5I7, and (d) (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr5I7.

 Single crystals of (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12, (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr12, (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr5I7, and
(C4H10N2)4AgBiBr5I7 were synthesized using the solution-based slow-evaporation method. To verify
the bulk phase purity of these materials, experimental and simulated PXRD measurements were
conducted, indicating the high purity of these crystalline compounds (Figure 31). Additionally, their air
stability was confirmed through periodic PXRD scans recorded in the ambient laboratory environment
for 4 months. To investigate the thermal stability of the title compounds, differential scanning
calorimetry (DSC) and thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) were carried out (Figure 32). The TGA data
show that all compounds remain stable up to 300℃, without any weight loss. However, at higher
temperatures, a total weight loss of 79.19%, 83.87%, 84.90% and 81.10% was observed for
(C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12, (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr12, (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr5I7, and (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr5I7, respectively.
Interestingly, the observed weight losses aligned closely with the expected loss of the inorganic
components (i.e., 78.1%, 78.73%, 81.91% and 82.32%) of the compounds, which is notably
uncommon in such double metal halide perovskites. Usually, the thermal decomposition leads to the
decomposition of the organic part, and the inorganic component remains a residue. This observation
suggests a possible unique thermal stability profile or interaction between the organic and inorganic
components of the compounds. A possible explanation for this almost complete weight loss of the
inorganic component is the formation of volatile halide species or decomposition of the metal halide
framework at higher temperatures due to the weak bonding of organic and inorganic layers and the

 19

thermally unstable nature of halide components. This observation can be related to the crystal
structures of the title compounds, as they possess an extra thermally labile halide layer in between
the organic cations, possessing weak interactions between the organic-inorganic components of the
structure.

 To have a better insight into the compounds, we investigated their optical properties utilizing
diffuse reflectance measurements conducted on polycrystalline powders of these compounds. These
measurements have shown the absorption onsets for (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12, (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr12,
(C4H10N2)4CuBiBr5I7, and (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr5I7 at 2.3 eV, 2.63 eV, 1.75 eV, and 2.05 eV, respectively
(Figure 33). These results match two specific expected trends: first, pure bromide-based DHD
perovskites have band gaps of 2.3 eV and 2.6 eV, which are higher than the band gaps of iodide mixed
DHD perovskites (1.75 eV and 2.04 eV). Second, the band gaps of Ag+/Bi3+ systems are larger
compared to those of Cu+/Bi3+. These two observations are consistent with previous studies and with
the fact that absorption band edges are determined by the metal-halide octahedra in hybrid double
metal halide perovskites

 To confirm these observations and to understand the electronic structures of these compounds,
DFT calculations are being performed. The preliminary results obtained through DFT calculations
suggest that the calculated band gaps for (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12, (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr12,
(C4H10N2)4CuBiBr5I7, and (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr5I7 are 2.48 eV, 2.88 eV, 1.88 eV, and 2.37 eV, respectively.
These calculated band gaps are higher than the experimentally determined optical band gaps,
however, they are in relatively good agreement with experimental values considering the idealized
model system with no defects that was employed in the calculations. Additionally, the calculated band
gap values are consistent with the trend of higher band gaps for pure bromide and Ag+/Bi3+ compounds
compared to the mixed iodide and Cu+/Bi3+ ones. More information about the band structures and

Projected Density of States (PDOS) will be reported as the DFT calculations are in their initial stage
for now.

Figure 34. Current-voltage measurement results for (a) (C2H10N2)CuBiBr12, (b) (C2H10N2)AgBiBr12, (c)
(C2H10N2)CuBiBr5I7, and (d) (C2H10N2)AgBiBr5I7, respectively, along the ab-plane.

 Several reports have suggested double perovskites as promising candidates for sensitive X-ray
detector applications due to their low-toxic and high-atomic-number elemental compositions. For this
reason, in addition to their optical and thermal studies, we studied the capability of these materials for
X-ray radiation detection applications. Figure 34 shows the current-voltage (I-V) measurements
performed for these compounds along the ab-plane, i.e., along the perpendicular direction of the
inorganic layers in the structure. The resistivity values measured are 7.33×106 Ω·cm, 1.73×1010 Ω·cm,
5.44×1011 Ω·cm, and 4.65×1010 Ω·cm, for (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12, (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr12,
(C4H10N2)4CuBiBr5I7, and (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr5I7, respectively. Our results suggest that
(C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12 is more conductive than the other double perovskites. Moreover, all these
compounds, unlike other layered perovskites, have shown an unexpected conductive ab-plane, which
suggests an expected role of the interhalide layer in the electronic structure of these materials. To fully
understand the potential of these compounds for radiation detection applications, we will continue our
studies with more characterizations, such as comparison of the photon attenuation capability with
commercial X-ray detectors, space-charge-limited-current (SCLC) measurements, photoconductivity,
X-ray ‘on’ and ‘off’ measurements, and detector sensitivity, which will be performed in the next
reporting period.

 In summary, we examined the crystal growth, optical, thermal, and radiation detection properties
of two bromide-based (i.e., (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12 and (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr12) and two mixed halide (i.e.,
(C4H10N2)4CuBiBr5I7, and (C4H10N2)4AgBiBr5I7) layered hybrid double perovskites with Cu+/Bi3+ and
Ag+/Bi3+ metal ion pairs. Notably, these compounds exhibit a unique DHD structural configuration and
are remarkably thermally stable up to 300℃. Furthermore, (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr5I7 and

 22

(C4H10N2)4AgBiBr5I7 exhibit narrower band gaps compared to (C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12 and
(C4H10N2)4AgBiBr12, due to the incorporation of iodide ions, and their band gaps are suitable for
investigating their potential in X-ray radiation detection applications. Single crystals of
(C4H10N2)4CuBiBr12 exhibit a semiconductor resistivity of 7.33×106 Ω·cm (measured along the ab-
plane), which is uniquely conductive, indicating its potential application for ionizing radiation detection.
Significantly, the findings of this work will raise the prospect of extending the variety of hybrid double
metal halide perovskites, thereby producing novel materials with distinctive physical characteristics
and new structures for real-world applications.
