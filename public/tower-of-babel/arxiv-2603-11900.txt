Quantum Mechanics from Finite Graded Equality
Julian G. Zilly

Abstract
We propose that quantum mechanics follows from a single hypothesis: equality has finite resolution . Replacing the binary predicate x = y x=y with a graded distinguishability kernel K ⁡ ( x , y ) ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] K(x,y)\in[0,1] forces three structural consequences: finite capacity ( N N perfectly distinguishable states), relational completeness (all structure reduces to K K -relations, and no measurement orientation is privileged), and reversible dynamics. We formalize the first two as axioms; a structural Leibniz condition within the saturation axiom forces permutation invariance of basis elements, and the full dynamical structure (cyclic evolution of order N N , complex coefficients, and continuous unitary time evolution) is then uniquely determined (Theorem 40 ). From these axioms (with regularity conditions derived in Appendix B ): complex coefficients ℂ \mathbb{C} are the unique field supporting cyclic dynamics and relational isotropy; deterministic hidden variables require Ω ⁡ ( N 2 ) \Omega(N^{2}) bits of storage (for prime-power N N ; exceeding log 2 ⁡ N \log_{2}N for all N ≥ 3 N\geq 3 ); the Born rule p k = | c k | 2 p_{k}=|c_{k}|^{2} is the unique probability assignment preserving statistical distinguishability under reversible dynamics; and local tomography follows from 𝔽 = ℂ \mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C} with tensor product composition. Standard quantum mechanics is the N → ∞ N\to\infty limit; finite N N provides a natural UV cutoff. The single free parameter is capacity N N .



I
Introduction

Quantum mechanics rests on structures without derivation: complex Hilbert spaces, unitary evolution, and the Born rule
p=|\psi|^{2}
. This paper shows that these structures follow from a single hypothesis:
equality has finite resolution
. Grading the equality predicate
x=y
forces three structural consequences: finite capacity, relational completeness, and reversible dynamics (Theorem
40
). The first two are formalized as axioms; four regularity conditions (§
V

B
). The central result is a
capacity deficit
: deterministic hidden variables require
\Omega(N^{2})
bits of storage, exceeding the
\log_{2}N
available (Theorem
86
). Metric Compatibility then selects
p_{k}=|c_{k}|^{2}
as the unique probability assignment consistent with the geometry (Theorem
69
).
Saturation (Axiom 1b), the exclusion of operationally redundant degrees of freedom, is the substantive axiom. It formalizes a relational ontology
rovelli1996relational
: states with identical distinguishability relations are identical. A structural Leibniz condition within Saturation (condition (iv)) forces every
K
-symmetry of a finite configuration to extend to a global automorphism; applied to basis elements, this yields Permutation Invariance (Theorem
18
), from which the full dynamical structure follows.
Information Parsimony
(no hidden variables) follows from Saturation jointly with Operational Completeness (the kernel
K
captures all measurable differences). The Born rule derivation’s deep content is in Saturation: it excludes independent geometric structures on the state space, and metric compatibility is the mathematical consequence. Hidden-variable theories (e.g., Bohmian mechanics
bohm1952suggested
) reject this axiom, not our theorems.

A deeper consequence is that
dynamics is structurally forced
: no single context can contain the full relational identity of a finite-resolution state. Specifying a
K
-profile requires evaluations against complementary bases, but the capacity deficit prevents simultaneous definiteness across contexts. Time is the parameter indexing this family of contexts, constitutive of state identity (§
III
).

Prior reconstructions
hardy2001quantum
;
chiribella2011informational
;
masanes2011derivation
derive finite-dimensional quantum theory as an approximation to the full infinite-dimensional framework. This paper treats finite capacity as fundamental: standard QM is the effective theory in the
N\to\infty

79
) resolves the apparent tension:
N
distinguishable states determine a unique band-limited interpolation, so the continuous manifold
\mathbb{C}P^{N-1}
introduces no degrees of freedom beyond these; continuous dynamics emerges from discrete capacity. The framework predicts that non-trivial dynamics requires
N\geq 3
; laboratory qubits evolve because they are embedded in larger systems (Theorem
54
).

On axiom weight.
The strongest single assumption is Basis Isotropy (Axiom
2
(2c)): transitivity of
G
on the basis manifold
\mathfrak{B}
. Physically, it requires that no measurement orientation is privileged. For fundamental systems (isolated spins, photon polarizations, momentum-position conjugates), all measurement orientations are empirically equivalent; systems with preferred axes (e.g., crystals) embed in larger isotropic systems. The two axioms thus decompose into an information constraint (Finite Capacity) and a symmetry constraint (Universal Relationality, dominated by Basis Isotropy); the interplay of the two is what forces quantum structure.

III
From Graded Equality to the Axioms

The axioms of §
IV
can be traced to a single hypothesis about a primitive mathematical structure: the equality predicate. This section shows that replacing binary equality with a graded kernel, the minimal modification consistent with finite resources, produces, as necessary consequences, finite capacity, relational structure, and reversible dynamics.

III.1
Graded Equality

In standard mathematics, the equality predicate
x=y
is binary: either true or false. This presupposes infinite precision: to certify
x=y
, one must verify agreement in every property to arbitrary accuracy. For a system with finite resources, this is impossible. We therefore replace the binary predicate with a graded one.

Definition 2

(Graded Equality)
.

A
graded equality
on a set
\mathcal{X}
is a function
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
satisfying: (i)
K(x,x)=0
(self-equality); (ii)
K(x,y)=K(y,x)
(symmetry). The value
K(x,y)
measures the degree to which
x
and
y
are
unequal
:
K=0
is “equal” (indistinguishable);
K=1
is ‘‘maximally unequal’’ (perfectly distinguishable); intermediate values represent resolution-limited comparison.
1
1

1

Convention:
K
measures
distinguishability
, not similarity.
K=1
means maximally distinguishable (perfectly resolved);
K=0
means indistinguishable. This is the opposite of a similarity kernel.

Note what
K
is
not
: it is not a metric (no triangle inequality), not a probability (no sample space), and not a physical observable (no measurement apparatus). It is a weakened equality predicate, the minimal structure that remains when binary comparison is relaxed to finite-resolution comparison. Three structural consequences follow from the hypothesis that
K
is the sole primitive.

Finite capacity.

If comparison has finite resolution, there are at most finitely many mutually
K\!=\!1
elements. Their number
N
is the
capacity
: the maximum number of perfectly distinguishable states. We call a maximal set of
N
mutually
K\!=\!1
elements a
basis
.

Saturation.

If
K
is the only primitive, Leibniz’s principle (
indiscernibles are identical
) takes the form: states with identical
K
-profiles are identical (injectivity). The converse also holds (surjectivity as the
converse
of Leibniz): any
K
-profile consistent with the structure must correspond to a state, for a “missing state” would constitute structure beyond
K
(the gap itself would be a distinction not captured by
K
-values). Formally: if the set of realizable
K
-profiles were a proper subset
\mathcal{R}\subsetneq\mathcal{P}
of consistent profiles, the boundary
\partial\mathcal{R}
would be
K
-detectable (one could distinguish states near the boundary from states far from it by their
K
-neighborhoods), constituting structure beyond
K
. Surjectivity closes this gap. The state space is thus exactly the set of all consistent
K
-profiles, no more, no less. Since the capacity is finite, only finitely many
K
-evaluations are needed to determine a
K
-profile (finite bandwidth).

Relationality.

If
K
is all there is, all physical content reduces to
K
-relations. Differences not registered in
K
do not exist:
K
is operationally complete. The symmetry of
K
makes element labels within a basis conventional (permutation invariance). Basis Isotropy then follows from K-homogeneity: let
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be two bases. The only structure distinguishing
\mathcal{B}
from
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
would be their
K
-values against other states. But the kernel
K
treats all maximally distinguishable sets uniformly (every basis has the same internal
K
-structure:
K(b_{i},b_{j})=\delta_{ij}^{c}
for
i\neq j
), and
K
is the sole primitive, so any property that would privilege
\mathcal{B}
over
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
would constitute structure beyond
K
. Therefore the automorphism group
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{X},K)
must act transitively on the set of bases
\mathfrak{B}
.

These three consequences (finite capacity with saturation, and relationality) are formalized as Axioms 1 and 2 in §
IV
. The third consequence, reversible dynamics, is not definitional; its form is uniquely determined by the first two. We motivate this next.

III.2
The Self-Resolution Problem

A state
x\in\mathcal{X}
is, by the Leibniz principle above, nothing more than its
K
-profile
: the function
K(x,\cdot)
evaluated against all other states. The
self-resolution problem
asks: can a system determine its own
K
-profile using only its own finite resources?

Note:
The following propositions provide physical motivation for Theorem
40
. Their arguments are physically compelling but informal; the corresponding formal result is proved in §
V
(Theorem
40
).

Proposition 3

(Insufficiency of a Single Reference Set)
.

A single basis
\mathcal{B}=\{b_{0},\ldots,b_{N-1}\}
does not determine the full
K
-profile of a general state.

Proof.

Restricting to
\mathcal{B}
gives
N
values
K(x,b_{k})
, but two states
x,x^{\prime}
may satisfy
K(x,b_{k})=K(x^{\prime},b_{k})
for all
k
while
K(x,x^{\prime})>0
: they agree against
\mathcal{B}
but are mutually distinguishable. By Saturation (surjectivity), such pairs must exist whenever
N\geq 2
: if the
\mathcal{B}
-restriction determined the full profile, the state space would be no richer than
N
elements, contradicting graded equality.
∎

The missing information is accessible only through
K
-evaluations against a different basis
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\neq\mathcal{B}
. For a closed system, this amounts to a transformation of the comparison frame from
\mathcal{B}
to
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
.

Proposition 4

(Cyclic Dynamics from Self-Resolution)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},K)
be a graded-equality space with capacity
N\geq 2
and Saturation. Complete self-resolution is equivalent to the existence of a
K
-preserving automorphism
\pi
with
\pi^{N}=\mathrm{id}
, acting as a single
N
-cycle on each basis.

Proof.

By Proposition
3
, self-resolution requires evaluations against at least two bases
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. Accessing
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{B}
means mapping one maximal set of mutually
K\!=\!1
elements to another. This map must be a
bijection
(the system transforms itself; nothing is created or destroyed) and must be
K
-preserving
(the meaning of “equal” cannot change under self-transformation, since graded equality is the sole primitive, so a transformation altering
K
would undermine the very comparison it enables). Complete self-resolution amounts to coverage of all
N
elements of the rotated set (transitivity). By homogeneity of
K
(no element is privileged, as a privileged element would constitute structure beyond
K
), the action is free. Free transitivity on
N
elements gives
|\langle\pi\rangle|=N
and
\pi^{N}=\mathrm{id}
; the generator must be a single
N
-cycle (a product of shorter cycles would fragment the elements into dynamically disconnected sectors, violating homogeneity).
∎

The
K
-preservation is a
definitional requirement
: the standard of comparison cannot itself be compared away.

Theorem 5

(Dynamical Constitution of Identity)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},K)
be a graded-equality space with capacity
N\geq 2
and Saturation. The cyclic automorphism
\pi
of Proposition
4
is
constitutive
of the state’s relational identity: without
\pi
, the
K
-profile is undefined.

Proof.

By Proposition
3
, the restriction
K(x,\cdot)|_{\mathcal{B}}
to any single basis underdetermines
x
. The full
K
-profile
K(x,\cdot)
is recovered only by evaluating against all bases in the orbit
\{\mathcal{B},\pi(\mathcal{B}),\ldots,\pi^{N-1}(\mathcal{B})\}
. By Saturation, a state
is
its
K
-profile (Leibniz). Therefore
x
is constituted by the collection
\{K(x,\cdot)|_{\pi^{j}(\mathcal{B})}\}_{j=0}^{N-1}
, which exists only if
\pi
does. This is the quantum analog of phase retrieval: one basis determines only the moduli
|c_{k}|^{2}=1-K(x,b_{k})
; the relative phases
\arg(c_{j}/c_{k})
are accessible only through evaluations against the complementary basis defined by
\pi
.

The existence of
\pi
is guaranteed by Structural Leibniz (Axiom
1
(1b)(iv)): basis elements satisfy
K(b_{i},b_{j})=1
for
i\neq j
, so every
\sigma\in S_{N}
is a
K
-symmetry of the basis and extends to a global automorphism. The
N
-cycle among these is the required
\pi
. Identity completeness (Saturation (i)) demands that the
K
-profile be fully expressible; symmetry completeness (Saturation (iv)) provides the automorphism that makes this expression possible.
∎

III.3
Structural Incompleteness and Stochasticity

Proposition 6

(Context-Incompleteness and Stochasticity)
.

No single context (basis) can contain the full relational identity of a state. Definite values in all contexts simultaneously would require storage exceeding the capacity bound; individual outcomes must therefore be stochastic.

Proof.

The system stores at most
\log_{2}N
bits, the information content of one basis. The full
K
-profile requires evaluations against multiple bases (Theorem
5
), but results against one basis are overwritten when the context changes: the system cannot simultaneously hold definite values in two complementary contexts. A deterministic assignment of outcomes to all bases would require storage proportional to the number of bases, exceeding
\log_{2}N
. Individual outcomes must therefore be indeterminate, realized stochastically, with statistics determined by the
K
-profile. (Quantitatively: the Capacity Halting Principle, Theorem
86
; the unique probability rule
p_{k}=|c_{k}|^{2}
, Theorem
69
.)
∎

III.4
Time as Unfolding of Relational Identity

The state is the cycle, not any snapshot.
By Saturation, a state
is
its
K
-profile, the function
K(x,\cdot)
evaluated against all other states. But no single basis captures this profile (Proposition
3
). The
N
-cycle automorphism
\pi
visits each of
N
complementary perspectives
\{\mathcal{B},\pi(\mathcal{B}),\ldots,\pi^{N-1}(\mathcal{B})\}
before returning to the starting point. The state’s relational identity is the
entire orbit
: the complete collection of
K
-evaluations across all
N
contexts. No single step of the cycle is the state; the state is what is expressed by all
N
steps together.

Time is the unfolding seen from within a single context.
For a closed system (Proposition
26
), no external observer performs the
K
-evaluations. Any subsystem that accesses the state through a particular basis sees one contextual slice per step. From this restricted perspective, the cycle is operationally indistinguishable from temporal evolution: each step reveals new relational information inaccessible at the previous step. Time is this sequential revelation of contextual information.

Stasis is relational incompleteness.
A static configuration, frozen in a single contextual projection, would express only one slice of the state’s
K
-profile. The remaining slices would be unexpressed. By Saturation, such a configuration is not a valid state: a
K
-profile with missing relational structure violates surjectivity’s converse (every state must have a complete profile). The capacity deficit makes this quantitative: simultaneous definiteness across all contexts requires
\Omega(N^{2})
bits, exceeding the
\log_{2}N
available (Theorem
86
). A finite-capacity system can maintain a complete relational identity only by expressing it sequentially. Dynamics is the finite system’s mechanism for self-consistency.

A system with exact equality (
K\in\{0,1\}
, binary) has a
K
-profile fully determined by a single basis evaluation. It needs no complementary contexts and admits a static, timeless, classical description. The capacity deficit of graded equality, that the
K
-profile cannot be localized in a single context, produces the reversible, cyclic,
K
-preserving dynamics proved as Theorem
40
in §
V
.

Remark 7

(Analogy to Randomized Algorithms)
.

The self-resolution protocol is a randomized streaming algorithm: the system has
\log_{2}N
bits of working memory but the
K
-profile requires more parameters than can be stored simultaneously. Like Morris’s approximate counting algorithm
morris1978counting
, which uses
O(\log\log n)
bits to estimate a count of
n
by stochastic incrementing, the system trades deterministic precision for memory efficiency. The Born rule
p_{k}=|c_{k}|^{2}
is the optimal such rule: it is the unique probability assignment making the statistical metric (Fisher-Rao) proportional to the geometric metric (Fubini-Study), ensuring maximal information extraction per bit of memory (§
X
).

Remark 8

(Connection to Page–Wootters)
.

This view of time has a precise analogue in the Page–Wootters mechanism
page1983evolution
: in a timeless global state
|\Psi\rangle
of system plus clock, “time” labels the conditional state given a clock reading. Here the clock is the basis label: each context
\pi^{j}(\mathcal{B})
provides a “reading” against which the
K
-profile is evaluated. The global object (the full
K
-profile) is timeless; dynamics emerges when one conditions on a particular context. The difference is that in the present framework the necessity of multiple contexts is
derived
from the capacity deficit, not postulated.

(Summary: Axioms from Graded Equality)
.

The two axioms and derived dynamics of §

:

(1)

Definitional. Finite resolution implies finitely many perfectly distinguishable states. Saturation is the Leibniz principle for graded equality.

(2)

Definitional. If
K
is the sole primitive, all structure is relational. Homogeneity of
K
gives isotropy of bases.

(3)

Reversible Dynamics:
Permutation Invariance (Theorem

, derived from Saturation’s Structural Leibniz condition) provides
S_{N}
-symmetry within each basis; on the derived manifold
\mathbb{F}P^{N-1}
, this uniquely determines the dynamical structure: a cyclic
K
-preserving automorphism of order

Remark 25

(Physical Content of Basis Isotropy)
.

Basis Isotropy (Axiom
2

Physical examples where it holds: (i) Spin measurements on isolated particles: any Stern-Gerlach axis is physically realizable; the laws do not distinguish “up” from any rotated direction. (ii) Polarization measurements: any polarizer angle is accessible. (iii) Position/momentum complementarity: Fourier-conjugate bases are equally fundamental.

Potential counterexamples (where it might fail): (i) Systems with intrinsic anisotropy, such as crystals with preferred axes, appear to violate isotropy but actually embed in a larger isotropic system (the crystal plus its orientation degree of freedom). (ii) Hypothetical Bekenstein-bounded black hole horizons: it is not obvious that every conceivable measurement basis is physically accessible to an external observer. Such systems would require weakening Axiom 2c.

The physical content is thus: for

This is an empirical claim about the symmetry of physical law, analogous to the role of Lorentz invariance in special relativity. The content of the paper is that this assumption, together with finite capacity and reversible dynamics,

is not precompact, so for any

Part (ii):

is a Lie group.

By Part (i),

is an uncountable topological space. Transitivity of

(Axiom

embeds into

for a finite separating set

, giving
\dim\mathcal{X}<\infty
. The Montgomery–Zippin theorem

(solving Hilbert’s fifth problem) then gives: a compact group acting continuously and transitively on a finite-dimensional space is a Lie group.

Part (iii): Band-limited structure.

By Saturation (iii), every state is determined by
K
-evaluations against a finite set
S
. In the Hilbert space picture, amplitudes
|c_{k}|^{2}=1-K(\psi,b_{k})
from one basis and relative phases (accessible via a second basis) yield

real parameters, exactly
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}P^{N-1}
. The continuous manifold introduces no degrees of freedom beyond these finitely many
K
-values.
∎

Remark 32

(Epistemic Continuity)
.

The manifold
\mathbb{C}P^{N-1}

: the ontology is

Remark 33

(The State Space as a Nyquist Manifold)
.

The state space
(\mathcal{X},K)
is neither a smooth manifold in the classical sense (infinite local degrees of freedom) nor a discrete combinatorial structure (trivial topology). It is what we call a
Nyquist manifold
: a smooth manifold whose
K
-profiles are finitely determined (Saturation (iii)), so that the continuous geometry is the unique band-limited interpolation of finitely many
K
-values
shannon1949communication
.

Dual description. Band-limited functions are equivalent to their samples (Nyquist-Shannon). The state space therefore admits two
exactly equivalent
descriptions:

•

Continuous:
\mathcal{X}\cong\mathbb{C}P^{N-1}
with Fubini-Study metric, Lie group
G\cong U(N)
, smooth dynamics, and differentiable
K
.

•

Discrete:
2(N-1)
real parameters extracted from a finite separating set
S
, constituting the complete physical content.

These are not approximations of each other. Smooth differential geometry on
\mathbb{C}P^{N-1}
and finite combinatorics on the sampling set yield identical physical predictions, related by exact interpolation. Whether one views the state space as continuous or discrete is a choice of representation, not of physics.

Relation to prior frameworks. This structure is the axiomatic counterpart of Madore’s fuzzy manifolds
madore1992fuzzy
, which truncate the function algebra on a classical manifold to
M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
, and of finite spectral triples in Connes’ noncommutative geometry
connes1994noncommutative
. Those frameworks start from a known manifold and impose a truncation; the present one derives both the manifold and its finite bandwidth simultaneously from the distinguishability kernel
K
.

Intrinsic resolution. The resolution floor
\delta\sim 1/N
(Theorem
34
) is not imposed externally. Every observer, being itself a finite-capacity system, is subject to the same bandwidth limit. The “blurriness” is therefore intrinsic and self-consistent: no higher-resolution description is available within the theory. The Nyquist manifold is the mathematical expression of a geometry that is as detailed as the physics allows, no more, no less.

(Operational Indistinguishability of Continuous and Sampled Amplitudes)
.

∎

VI.1
The Signature Sheaf

While an equivariant injection provides a direct proof of the state space
\mathcal{X}\cong\mathbb{C}P^{N-1}
(see Remark
48
), the sheaf formalism is introduced here specifically to expose the gauge-theoretic origins of the
U(1)
phase: it reveals the phase as the holonomy of a connection on the signature sheaf (Lemma
43
), connecting the emergence of complex numbers to gauge structure (see also
abramsky2011sheaf
for sheaf-theoretic approaches to contextuality). Readers focused on the physical results may skip directly to Remark
48
.

Definition 35

(Signature Sheaf)
.

The
signature sheaf

\mathcal{S}
over the basis space
\mathfrak{B}
assigns to each basis
\mathcal{B}
a “stalk”
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}}
, the space of valid signatures
\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}(x)=(K(x,b_{0}),\ldots,K(x,b_{N-1}))
. Transition maps
\phi_{g}:\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}}\to\mathcal{S}_{g\mathcal{B}}
relate signatures across bases, satisfying:

(i)

Cocycle condition
:
\phi_{gh}=\phi_{g}\circ\phi_{h}
for all
g,h\in G
.

(ii)

Identity
:
\phi_{e}=\mathrm{id}
where
e
is the identity element.

Lemma 36

(Sheaf Glueing Axiom)
.

The signature sheaf
\mathcal{S}
satisfies the glueing axiom: if local sections
\{s_{\alpha}\}
on an open cover
\{U_{\alpha}\}
of
\mathfrak{B}
agree on overlaps (
s_{\alpha}|_{U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}}=s_{\beta}|_{U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}}
), then there exists a unique global section
s
with
s|_{U_{\alpha}}=s_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

The cocycle condition ensures consistency: on triple overlaps
U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}\cap U_{\gamma}
, the transition maps compose correctly (
\phi_{\alpha\beta}\circ\phi_{\beta\gamma}=\phi_{\alpha\gamma}
). Given compatible local sections, define
s(p)=s_{\alpha}(p)
for
p\in U_{\alpha}
. Compatibility on overlaps ensures this is well-defined. Uniqueness follows from the separation property of
K
: two global sections agreeing on all stalks must be equal.
∎

Theorem 37

(Points as Global Sections)
.

Points
x\in\mathcal{X}
correspond bijectively to global sections of
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

(Existence)
Each point
x
determines a signature
\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}(x)
in every stalk. The kernel’s symmetry ensures compatibility under transition maps.

(Uniqueness)
If two points define the same global section, they have identical
K
-signatures in all bases, hence are equal by the kernel’s separation property.

(Surjectivity)
By Saturation, a state
is
a coherent family of
K
-values across all bases. Any compatible family of signatures defines a state (a “missing state” would represent structure beyond
K
, violating Saturation). The
N
basis states provide
N
independent global sections spanning an
N
-dimensional space of sections
V
(independent because
K(b_{i},b_{j})=1
for
i\neq j
ensures no section is a combination of others). By Theorem
23
,
G
is a compact Lie group and
\mathcal{X}=G/H
. The
G
-action on
V
is irreducible: a proper invariant subspace either contains all
N
basis sections (hence equals
V
) or misses at least one, which transitivity of
G
on atoms (Theorem
18
) forbids. This irreducible
N
-dimensional representation carries a faithful
S_{N}
-action by permutation matrices (Theorem
18
:
S_{N}
permutes the
N
basis sections). The only irreducible representation of a compact group that contains
S_{N}
acting by permutation of a basis is the standard (fundamental) representation
V=\mathbb{F}^{N}
, since any other
N
-dimensional irrep would fail to preserve the permutation structure of the basis sections. Saturation (ii) (surjectivity) requires
\mathcal{X}
to carry exactly the states generated by this representation (no more); see also Remark
39
. Therefore
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{P}(V)=\mathbb{F}P^{N-1}
for a division algebra
\mathbb{F}
; by Frobenius,
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{H}\}
. (This identification uses only Axioms
1
–
2
; the field selection

follows from Theorem

(ii).) Lemma

gives the detailed spectral argument: for

, so
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{X}=2(N-1)
.
∎

Corollary 38

(State Space Isomorphism)
.

The state space is isomorphic to the space of global sections:

\mathcal{X}\cong\Gamma(\mathfrak{B},\mathcal{S}).

Moreover, if two global sections
s_{1},s_{2}
agree on any open set

, they must agree everywhere.

Proof.

The bijection of Theorem
37
is the isomorphism. For the second statement: if
s_{1}|_{U}=s_{2}|_{U}
, then by the cocycle condition and transitivity of
G
on
\mathfrak{B}
, the sections agree on

for all

. Since the
G
-orbit of any open set covers
\mathfrak{B}
(by transitivity),

globally. This is the sheaf-theoretic expression of Axiom

∎

Theorem 46

(Hilbert Space Representation)
.

\mathrm{cap}(\mathcal{S})=N<\infty

(A3)

(A4)

For composite systems, states are determined by local measurements.

(A1)–(A3) follow from Axioms

Then there exists a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with
\dim(\mathcal{H})=N
and an embedding
\iota:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})

(i)

Pure states map to rank-1 projectors:
\iota(\mathrm{ext}(\mathcal{S}))=\{|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|:\|\psi\|=1\}

(ii)

Effects map to positive operators:
\iota^{*}(e)\in[0,I]

(iii)

p(e|s)=\mathrm{Tr}(\iota^{*}(e)\cdot\iota(s))
.

Proof.

Step 1: The extreme boundary is a single orbit.

By (A2), the symmetry group

Step 2: Projective representation.

By Theorem

Step 5: State-operator correspondence.

Define

alfsen2003geometry

By Gleason’s theorem

∎

We now prove that the distinguishability kernel

Theorem 57

(Existence of Inner Product from Kernel)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},K)
be a distinguishability space satisfying Axioms

2

(Lemma

\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{C}^{N}\times\mathbb{C}^{N}\to\mathbb{C}

\psi_{x},\psi_{y}
are the corresponding vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{N}

Proof.

Step 1: Construction on basis states.

By Definition
12
, basis states
\{b_{0},\ldots,b_{N-1}\}
satisfy
K(b_{i},b_{j})=1
for
i\neq j
and
K(b_{i},b_{i})=0
. Define
\langle b_{i}|b_{j}\rangle:=\delta_{ij}
. This is positive-definite on the basis.

Step 2: Extension to all states.

By Theorem
37
, every state

corresponds to a point
[\psi_{x}]\in\mathbb{C}P^{N-1}
. Choose representative vectors
\psi_{x}=\sum_{k}c_{k}(x)b_{k}

\sum_{k}|c_{k}|^{2}=1

\langle\psi_{x}|\psi_{y}\rangle:=\sum_{k}\overline{c_{k}(x)}c_{k}(y).

Step 3: Verification of positive-definiteness.

For any

\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\sum_{k}|c_{k}|^{2}\geq 0
, with equality iff

for all

\psi=0
.

Step 4: Uniqueness.

Any other positive-definite sesquilinear form
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle^{\prime}
satisfying

must have

on basis states. By symmetry (Axiom
2

\|\langle b_{i}|b_{i}\rangle^{\prime}\|=c

\langle b_{i}|b_{j}\rangle^{\prime}=\delta_{ij}
. Extension by linearity is unique.
∎

Remark 58

(Alternative Construction via Fourier Orthogonality)

(Kernel from Inner Product)
.

K(x,y)=1-|\langle\psi_{x}|\psi_{y}\rangle|^{2}

For atoms:

Theorem 60

(The Fubini-Study Metric from Distinguishability)

(1)

XI.2
Phase Granularity and the Sampling Limit

The fundamental consequence of finite capacity is that phase is band-limited.

Proposition 76

(Phase Resolution Limit)
.

For a system with capacity
N
, phase is a band-limited signal with finest distinguishable phase difference
\delta\theta=2\pi/N
.

Proof.

The cyclic generator
\pi
(Theorem
40
) has eigenvalues
e^{2\pi ik/N}
for
k=0,\ldots,N-1
. A state

has phase representation
\psi(\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}c_{k}e^{ik\theta}
, a trigonometric polynomial of degree at most
N-1
. The system’s
bandwidth
is therefore
B=(N-1)/(2T)
in the Nyquist sense, where
T
is the recurrence period. By the Nyquist–Shannon theorem, this band-limited signal is determined by
N
equally spaced samples per period, giving phase resolution
\delta\theta=2\pi/N
. No finer structure exists: the spectrum of
\pi
is the complete set of frequencies available to the system.
∎

Corollary 77

(Finite-
N
Phase Granularity)
.

If
N
is finite, the minimum detectable phase shift is
\delta\phi_{\min}=2\pi/N
. No interferometric experiment within a capacity-
N
system can resolve phase differences below this threshold. Consequently, the continuous

gauge group is replaced by its discrete subgroup

: the system supports exactly
N
operationally distinct phase values

. Standard quantum mechanics, with its continuous

theory recovered in the

limit; the finite-

theory is the
fundamental
one, and the smoothness of the Schrödinger equation should break down at phase resolutions below

. For a system at the Bekenstein bound (

), the minimum phase shift is

. Note: the identification

is motivated by the Bekenstein bound

but not derived within this framework, which is pre-gravitational. This is a falsifiable prediction: observation of sub-

(Informational Zeno Floor)

The phase granularity

is

XI.3
The Schrödinger Equation

We derive the Schrödinger equation from the axioms using two classical results: Stone’s theorem (a strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group has a unique self-adjoint generator

) and Wigner’s theorem (any transition-probability-preserving bijection on
\mathbb{C}P^{N-1}
is unitary or anti-unitary

bargmann1964note
). Full statements appear in Appendix
A

Theorem 82

(Schrödinger Equation: Existence and Uniqueness)
.

Under Axioms
1
–
2
, the evolution equation has the unique form
i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\psi\rangle=H|\psi\rangle
where

is a self-adjoint operator. The derivation requires:

(i)

Kernel preservation (Axiom
1
)
\Rightarrow
transition probability preservation;

(ii)

Wigner’s theorem
\Rightarrow
evolution is unitary (not anti-unitary);

(iii)

Stone’s theorem
\Rightarrow
unique self-adjoint generator

.

Proof.

Step 1: Evolution preserves kernel, hence transition probabilities.

By Theorem
40
, the generator
\pi
preserves the kernel:
K(\pi x,\pi y)=K(x,y)
. By Theorem
27
,
\pi
extends to a continuous one-parameter group

, which inherits kernel preservation. By Theorem
59
,
K(\psi,\phi)=1-|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}
. Hence evolution preserves transition probabilities.

Step 2: Wigner’s theorem forces unitary evolution.

By Wigner’s theorem

;
bargmann1964note
, each
U(t)
is unitary or anti-unitary. Continuity in

and
U(0)=\mathrm{id}
(unitary) force

unitary for all

(anti-unitary maps form a disconnected component).

Step 3: Stone’s theorem gives the generator.

Stone’s theorem guarantees a unique self-adjoint generator
H
with

. Differentiating
|\psi(t)\rangle=U(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle
yields

∎

Remark.
The Fubini-Study arc length equals
\hbar^{-1}\int\Delta E\,dt
(the Anandan–Aharonov relation), connecting information-geometric distance to energy uncertainty. Geodesics on the state manifold correspond to minimum-uncertainty evolution.

XII
The Capacity Halting Principle

We now prove the capacity deficit: determinism is impossible for finite-capacity systems. This result uses derived Hilbert space structure (MUBs) and provides the physical motivation for probabilistic behavior; the rigorous determination of the probability rule is via Metric Compatibility (§
X

Definition 83

(Information-Theoretic Capacity)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},K)
be a distinguishability space with
N
perfectly distinguishable states. The
Shannon capacity
of the system is:

C:=\max_{\{p_{i}\}}H(\{p_{i}\})=\log_{2}N\text{ bits}

where
H(\{p_{i}\})=-\sum_{i}p_{i}\log_{2}p_{i}
is the Shannon entropy and the maximum is achieved by the uniform distribution over the
N
basis states.

Proposition 84

(Capacity as Physical Bound)
.

The Shannon capacity
C=\log_{2}N
is the maximum information that can be reliably encoded in, and extracted from, a capacity-
N
system. This follows from the channel coding theorem: no encoding can exceed
C
bits per use without arbitrarily high error probability. In particular, any internal degree of freedom, discrete or continuous, can carry at most
\log_{2}N
bits of retrievable information. A continuous parameter resolved to precision
\varepsilon
carries
\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)
distinguishable values; finite capacity requires
\varepsilon\geq 1/N
, establishing a resolution floor below which distinctions are operationally meaningless.

(Hidden-Variable Assignment)

XIV
Discussion

XIV.1
From Graded Equality to Quantum Mechanics

The derivation chain of this paper has a single root: the replacement of binary equality with a graded kernel
K(x,y)\in[0,1]
(§
III
). This is a mathematical hypothesis about what “sameness” can mean under finite resources. Three structural consequences follow without further input: finite capacity (at most
N
mutually
K\!=\!1
elements), saturation (the state space is exactly the set of consistent
K
-profiles, by the Leibniz principle for graded equality), and relationality (all structure reduces to
K
-relations, since
K
is the sole primitive). Saturation’s Structural Leibniz condition then forces Permutation Invariance (Theorem
18
): the
S_{N}
-symmetric
K
-values of basis elements extend to global automorphisms. Reversible dynamics follows (Theorem
40
): the cyclic element of
S_{N}
produces a
K
-preserving automorphism of order
N
.

What follows is unexpectedly rigid. The cyclic automorphism forces complex coefficients (Theorem
56
); sheaf consistency produces the state space
\mathbb{C}P^{N-1}
(Theorem
37
); the kernel induces the Fubini-Study metric (Theorem
60
), which is unique (Theorem
62
); metric compatibility selects the Born rule (Theorem
69
); and Stone’s theorem gives the Schrödinger equation. The derivation proceeds not by postulating physical principles but by removing a mathematical idealization (infinite-precision equality) and tracing what remains.

XIV.2
Why Saturation Is the Substantive Assumption

Among the consequences of graded equality, Saturation (Axiom
1
(1b)) carries the most weight. It encodes four requirements: injectivity (states with identical
K
-profiles are identical, the Leibniz principle), surjectivity (every consistent
K
-profile corresponds to a state), finite bandwidth, and Structural Leibniz (finite
K
-symmetries extend to global automorphisms). Surjectivity excludes “missing states”: gaps in the state space that would constitute structure beyond
K
. Without surjectivity, one could adjoin hidden variables, independent geometric data, or preferred decompositions, all invisible to
K
. Bohmian mechanics, for example, rejects surjectivity by supplementing
K
-profiles with configuration-space positions. The Structural Leibniz condition produces Permutation Invariance (Theorem
18
) and thereby the intra-basis symmetry that drives dynamics, making Saturation the single axiom from which both the state space structure and the dynamical structure emerge.

Origin of the axioms.
Each axiom has an independent, pre-quantum motivation. Structural Leibniz (1b)(iv) is the Leibniz principle applied to symmetry: if
K
is the sole primitive,
K
-symmetries of finite configurations cannot be accidental. Basis Isotropy (2c) is the empirical equivalence of measurement orientations, the quantum analog of Lorentz invariance. Neither mentions Hilbert spaces, unitary groups, or complex numbers. That these two principles, together with finite capacity, leave no room for any structure other than QM is the content of the derivation.

Saturation is what makes the Born rule derivation work. The Metric Bridge (Theorem
64
) requires that the Fisher-Rao metric
g_{FR}
and the Fubini-Study metric
g_{FS}
be proportional. This proportionality follows from uniqueness of the
U(N)
-invariant metric (Theorem
62
), which in turn requires that the probability rule be
U(N)
-covariant. But covariance is guaranteed only if the probability rule is determined entirely by
K
-data, with no independent structure. That is precisely what Saturation enforces.

XIV.3
Two Routes to Indeterminism

The
Capacity Halting Principle
(Theorem
86
) is a storage argument: deterministic outcomes for all measurement contexts require
\Omega(N^{2})
bits, but the system stores at most
\log_{2}N
. This establishes the
necessity
of probabilities but does not select the rule.
Metric Compatibility
(Lemma
67
, Theorem
69
) is a geometric argument: among all admissible rules
p_{k}=f(|c_{k}|^{2})
, only
f(x)=x
makes the Fisher-Rao metric proportional to the Fubini-Study metric, selecting the Born rule uniquely (including at
N=2
where Gleason’s theorem is inapplicable). The two arguments are complementary: Capacity Halting establishes the
necessity
of probabilities; Metric Compatibility determines their
form
.

XIV.4
Finite-Dimensionality as Fundamental

The derivation depends essentially on
N<\infty
. Compactness (Theorem
114
) requires finite
\epsilon
-nets; Montgomery–Zippin (Theorem
115
) requires a finite-dimensional target; Saturation (iii) requires a finite separating set. In the limit
N\to\infty
, each link breaks:
\mathbb{C}P^{\infty}
is not locally compact,
\epsilon
-nets become infinite, Arzelà–Ascoli imposes no equicontinuity constraint, and
U(\infty)
is not a Lie group. The smooth structure, the probability rules, and the dynamics are all derived
because

N
is finite.

This suggests that infinite-dimensional quantum mechanics is a
degenerate limit
in which the structural guarantees dissolve. Known pathologies are consistent with this view: UV divergences in QFT arise from the absence of a finite
\epsilon
-net for field modes; Haag’s theorem
haag1955quantum
shows the interaction picture does not exist; type III von Neumann algebras lack traces and density matrices; and self-adjoint extension ambiguities arise because symmetric operators on infinite-dimensional spaces need not be essentially self-adjoint. Even the Heisenberg uncertainty relation
\Delta x\,\Delta p\geq\hbar/2
is an artifact of the infinite-dimensional limit: it requires unbounded operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which have no finite-dimensional counterpart. In the present framework, complementarity is intrinsically finite: it arises from mutually unbiased bases (Corollary
74
), and the
\Delta x\,\Delta p
form emerges only as
N\to\infty
, where discrete Fourier conjugacy becomes continuous Fourier conjugacy.

If the framework is correct, these pathologies are symptoms of extrapolating a fundamentally finite theory beyond its domain of validity.

XIV.5
Scope and Refutability

This is a
characterization theorem
: the axioms uniquely determine quantum structure, but do not prove that nature must satisfy them. Theories rejecting the relational ontology (e.g., Bohmian mechanics) lie outside its scope: they reject Axiom
2
.

Uniqueness for each
N
:
For each
N\geq 3
, the only distinguishability space satisfying Axioms 1–2 is
(\mathbb{C}P^{N-1},K)
with
K(\psi,\phi)=1-|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}
. The only free parameter is
N
itself.

The framework is falsifiable in principle, though the nature of its predictions deserves care. It would be refuted by: (1) deterministic, context-independent measurement outcomes for a verified finite-
N
system (falsifying Capacity Halting); (2) observation of
p_{k}=|c_{k}|^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\neq 2
(falsifying metric compatibility); or (3) distinguishing operationally identical preparations
K=0
by some future measurement (falsifying Saturation). None has been observed.

Certain other predictions are structurally self-limiting. Phase granularity
\delta\phi=2\pi/N
is real within the framework but
unobservable from within
: by the quantum sampling theorem (Theorem
79
), any measurement apparatus is itself a finite-capacity system subject to the same bandwidth limit, so the granularity cannot be resolved by internal measurements. This is a feature: the theory is self-consistent precisely because the resolution floor applies universally, to observers and observed alike. Similarly, violations of the capacity bound
N<\infty
cannot be demonstrated by finite measurements, since any finite experiment probes only finitely many distinguishable outcomes.

The genuinely testable content lies in the
structural
predictions: the specific form of the Born rule, the impossibility of deterministic context-independent outcomes, and the consequences for composite systems (tensor product structure, no-cloning, CHSH violation). These are shared with standard QM. This status is common to all QM reconstructions
hardy2001quantum
;
chiribella2011informational
;
masanes2011derivation
: they are mathematical characterization theorems identifying which physical principles single out quantum theory, not generators of novel experimental predictions beyond QM itself. The distinctive contribution of the present framework is a new
explanation
: it identifies the structural origin of quantum mechanics in the finite resolution of equality.

XIV.6
Conclusion and Open Questions

The framework is pre-spatial (
N
denotes relational capacity, not spatial configuration), pre-dynamical for fields (Theorem
52
derives
U(1)
gauge symmetry but not field equations), and leaves
N
undetermined (the identification
N\sim A/\ell_{P}^{2}
via the Bekenstein bound
bekenstein1973black
is conjectural). Whether spatial geometry emerges from
K
-profile correlations in composite systems, whether consistency of phase transport across subsystems constrains gauge field dynamics, and what fixes the value of
N
are open questions.

That the whole of finite-dimensional quantum mechanics (complex numbers, the Born rule, unitary dynamics, tensor products) follows from so slight a modification to the equality predicate was not anticipated at the outset. Whether equally rigid consequences await in the structures not yet derived remains to be seen.
