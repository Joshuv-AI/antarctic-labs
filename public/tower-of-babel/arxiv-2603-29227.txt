Kernel-SDF: An Open-Source Library for Real-Time Signed Distance Function Estimation using Kernel Regression
Zhirui Dai, Tianxing Fan, Mani Amani, Jaemin Seo, Ki Myung Brian Lee, Hyondong Oh, Nikolay Atanasov

Abstract
Accurate and efficient environment representation is crucial for robotic applications such as motion planning, manipulation, and navigation. Signed distance functions (SDFs) have emerged as a powerful representation for encoding distance to obstacle boundaries, enabling efficient collision-checking and trajectory optimization techniques. However, existing SDF reconstruction methods have limitations when it comes to large-scale uncertainty-aware SDF estimation from streaming sensor data. Voxel-based approaches are limited by fixed resolution and lack uncertainty quantification, neural network methods require significant training time, while Gaussian process (GP) methods struggle with scalability, sign estimation, and uncertainty calibration. In this letter, we develop an open-source library, Kernel-SDF, which uses kernel regression to learn SDF with calibrated uncertainty quantification in real-time. Our approach consists of a front-end that learns a continuous occupancy field via kernel regression, and a back-end that estimates accurate SDF via GP regression using samples from the front-end surface boundaries. Kernel-SDF provides accurate SDF, SDF gradient, SDF uncertainty, and mesh construction in real-time. Evaluation results show that Kernel-SDF achieves superior accuracy compared to existing methods, while maintaining real-time performance, making it suitable for various robotics applications requiring reliable uncertainty-aware geometric information.

II
Related Work

SDF learning methods can be categorized as voxel-based, neural-net-based, and Gaussian-process-based.

II-A

Voxel Methods

Many methods have been proposed to learn SDF using a grid
[
4
]
.
KinectFusion
[
20
]
pioneered real-time 3D reconstruction using depth sensors based on projective TSDF.
Follow-up works, such as Voxblox
[
21
]
, extended this to Euclidean SDF by breadth-first-search (BFS) integration from the occupied voxels. However, projective TSDF estimates the true SDF as distance along camera rays, which is inaccurate, and BFS integration introduces additional errors.
FIESTA
[
5
]
integrates SDF estimation with an occupancy map, replacing the BFS-based distance with the distance to the nearest occupied voxel.
VoxField
[
23
]
formulates non-projective TSDF, though its accuracy relies on the quality of estimated surface normals and SDF gradients.
A common limitation among these methods is the use of fixed-resolution grids, which restricts scalability in larger environments.
VDBblox
[
2
]
and nvblox
[
18
]
address scalability by using OpenVDB
[
19
]
or GPU-based voxel hashing.
However, voxel-based methods lack uncertainty quantification and are usually not differentiable.

II-B

Neural Network Methods

Neural network methods offer differentiability by design and can achieve highly accurate SDF reconstruction, as shown by DeepSDF
[
25
]
. DeepSDF uses an MLP with latent features to learn SDF of objects.
NeuS
[
34
]
and NeuS2
[
35
]
combine SDF with neural radiance fields (NeRF)
[
17
]
to jointly learn SDF and rendering. PIN-SLAM
[
24
]
and MISO
[
30
]
implement simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) based on an SDF map encoded using neural features.
\text{H}_{2}
-Mapping
[
8
]
uses a voxel SDF prior and a neural TSDF residual, which are co-optimized with volumetric rendering.
However, most neural network methods do not meet real-time requirements for operating in novel environments due to their significant training time. Although some works, like
\text{H}_{2}
-Mapping
[
8
]
and PIN-SLAM
[
24
]
, achieve neural SDF learning online, they sacrifice the SDF accuracy very much.

II-C

Gaussian Process Methods

GP methods are suitable for online SDF learning but face challenges related to the high computational cost of kernel matrix inversion as the environment scale increases.
To mitigate this, GPIS
[
10
]
employs octree-based data partitioning and trains multiple GPs for each local partition. However, its SDF estimates quickly revert to the zero-mean prior away from the surface.
Log-GPIS
[
38
]
observes an important connection between the heat kernel and the unsigned distance function, and formulates unsigned distance field GP regression in log space.
However, the log transformation does not consider sign information and distorts variance estimation, causing uncertainty to explode far from the surface. Furthermore, the surface estimation in Log-GPIS is sensitive to sensor noise and dynamic environmental changes.
VDB-GPDF
[
37
]
improves surface estimation through OpenVDB TSDF fusion but does not provide sign estimates or reliable uncertainty quantification.
To overcome these limitations, we introduce a continuous occupancy field front-end in Kernel-SDF based on BHM
[
7
,
27
]
, which provides robust sign prediction and accurate surface estimation, even in dynamic or noisy environments. Our GP back-end, then, learns SDF from the surface samples and formulates an uncertainty estimate that respects the SDF errors to deliver precise SDF and gradient predictions with reliable uncertainty quantification.

III
PROBLEM STATEMENT

Consider a robot equipped with a depth camera or LiDAR sensor operating in an
n
-dimensional environment (
n=2,3
) with occupied space
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The sensor provides point cloud measurements
\mathcal{P}_{t}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
\mathcal{O}
from known (or estimated) sensor positions
\mathbf{p}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and orientations
\mathbf{R}_{t}\in SO(n)
at time steps
t
. Using this data, our objective is to learn an SDF representation of the occupied space
\mathcal{O}
:

d(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\phantom{+}\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\partial\mathcal{O}}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|_{2},&\mathbf{x}\not\in\mathcal{O},\\
-\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\partial\mathcal{O}}\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\|_{2},&\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{O}.\end{cases}

(1)

Note that the surface
\partial\mathcal{O}
of the occupied space can be recovered as the zero-level set of the SDF
d(\mathbf{x})
. Also, the SDF gradient is of unit norm whenever differentiable:

d(\mathbf{x})=0,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\partial\mathcal{O}\ \text{and}\ \left\|\nabla d(\mathbf{x})\right\|_{2}=1,\ \text{a.e. }\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{O}.

(2)

We focus on estimating
d(\mathbf{x})
using the streaming point cloud measurements
\mathcal{P}_{t}
and the associated sensor poses
(\mathbf{p}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t})
.

IV
Kernel-SDF

Our approach, Kernel-SDF, consists of a surface estimation front-end using BHM
[
27
]
(Sec.
IV-A
) and an SDF prediction back-end using GP regression
[
36
]
(Sec.
IV-B
).
Both BHMs and GPs are kernel regression methods that support continuous representations with uncertainty quantification.
However, directly applying kernel regression over the entire environment is computationally expensive and impractical for real-time applications.
Kernel-SDF addresses this by partitioning the environment into multiple local regions, defined by an octree data structure, and applying kernel regression within each region with additional optimization techniques.

3

IV-A

Surface Estimation Front-End

In this section, we review Bayesian Hilbert map (BHM)
[
27
]
and describe our extensions to achieve efficient updates, sign prediction, and surface point extraction.

1)
Review of Bayesian Hilbert Map
:
BHM learns a continuous log-odds field
l(\mathbf{x})
using kernel regression in the Hilbert space. The log-odds
l(\mathbf{x})
at position
\mathbf{x}
is the ratio between the probabilities of being occupied and being free. BHM uses linear regression to learn the log-odds field,
l(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
, with weights
\mathbf{w}
and features
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
. To obtain the features, a query position
\mathbf{x}
is mapped into Hilbert space using an RBF kernel
k(\cdot,\cdot)
evaluated with respect to a set of hinged points
\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
organized in a grid structure:

\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})=[k(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}),\cdots,k(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{M}),1]^{\top}.

(3)

The key step is to estimate the weights
\mathbf{w}
, which are maintained as a Gaussian random variable
\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
.

Given a point cloud
\mathcal{P}
, we generate a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{BHM}}=\{\mathbf{x}_{k},y_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\{-1,1\}
by sampling free-space samples,
\mathbf{x}_{k},y_{k}=-1
, along each segment between the sensor position
\mathbf{p}
and the hit point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{P}
and adding the hit points as occupied samples,
\mathbf{x}_{k}=\mathbf{x},y_{k}=1
. Given
\mathcal{D}_{\text{BHM}}
, BHM uses expectation maximization (EM)
[
27
]
to update the weights mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
and predict the log-odds as
l(\mathbf{x})=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
.

2)
Octree of BHMs
:
Since the number of hinged points,
M
, scales with scene size, the BHM updates become computationally prohibitive. We address this by partitioning the scene using an octree data structure.
A local BHM is maintained only for octants containing occupied voxels, which significantly reduces the number of necessary hinged points.
To generate the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{BHM}}
, we prune rays outside the local BHM’s viewing frustum and sample only within its specific bounding box (Fig.
2(a)
).
To accelerate computation, we assume independent hinged point weights, reducing the covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
to a diagonal matrix. This enables element-wise operations for the EM updates, drastically lowering complexity.
Finally, we use a sparse representation of
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
, exploiting the that the RBF kernel
k(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
vanishes when
\mathbf{x}
is far from
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
.

To maintain consistency between local BHMs, we overlap neighboring BHMs by a margin equal to the hinged point spacing. When generating
\mathcal{D}_{\text{BHM}}
, we extend the sampling area to ensure boundary weights are sufficiently updated (Fig.
2(a)
).
We use a weight sync strategy to ensure global consistency. As shown in Fig.
2(b)
, hinged point weights are categorized into: core weights (unique to the local BHM), managed weights (owned locally but shared), and unmanaged weights (owned by a neighbor).
Periodically syncing managed weights to their unmanaged counterparts ensures consistent occupancy predictions and accelerates convergence by broadcasting local geometry to the neighbors.

3)
Sign Prediction
:
The SDF sign of a near-surface position
\mathbf{x}_{*}
can be predicted by the BHM as:

s(\mathbf{x}_{*})=\begin{cases}+1,&l(\mathbf{x}_{*})<\tau,\\
-1,&l(\mathbf{x}_{*})\geq\tau,\end{cases}

(4)

where
\tau
is a threshold (ideally 0) learned from data.
Empirical evidence (Fig.
4
) shows
\tau
varies with sensor viewing direction (e.g., ground vs. walls).
Thus, we update
\tau
online using a moving average of the log-odds from
K
hit samples:

\tau\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\tau+\frac{\alpha}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}l(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\quad\alpha\in(0,1],

(5)

where
\alpha\in(0,1]
is the learning rate.

Fig. 4
:
Surface points colored by their log-odds values from BHM. The ground has lower log-odds than the walls.

4)
Online Mesh Extraction and Surface Point Uncertainty Estimation
:
To recover surface points
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i}
at the
\tau
-level set of
l(\mathbf{x})
, we use the marching cubes algorithm
[
15
]
on a grid of BHM log-odds values. We process only grid cells containing point cloud points and their neighbors. The uncertainty for each point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is computed as:

\sigma_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}^{2}=\beta(\tau-l(\mathbf{x}_{i}))^{2}/\|\nabla l(\mathbf{x}_{i})\|_{2}^{2},

(6)

where
\beta>0
is a hyperparameter and
\tau
is updated via (
5
).

We derive (
6
) via a first-order Taylor expansion of
l(\mathbf{x}_{*})
at the extracted point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
relative to the true surface point
\mathbf{x}_{*}
:

\underbrace{l(\mathbf{x}_{*})}_{\tau}\approx l(\mathbf{x}_{i})+{\underbrace{\nabla l(\mathbf{x}_{i})}_{\nabla l}}^{\top}(\underbrace{\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{i}}_{\delta\mathbf{x}}),

(7)

and assuming the error
\delta\mathbf{x}
aligns primarily with the gradient direction, i.e.,
\delta\mathbf{x}\approx{\nabla l}/{\|\nabla l\|_{2}}\delta_{x}
.
Intuitively, uncertainty rises when
l(\mathbf{x}_{i})
deviates from
\tau
(higher surface extraction error) or when the gradient is small (flat and noise-sensitive field).
Hence, (
6
) reflects how confident the front-end is about each point, which allows the back-end to weight each point’s contribution during SDF learning and uncertainty quantification.

IV-B

SDF Prediction Back-End

In this section, we review the log-GP method for unsigned distance function estimation, extend it to other kernels types to improve its numerical stability, and introduce a softmin SDF uncertainty quantification approach.

IV-B
1
Log-GP for Unsigned Distance Estimation

Given the front-end sign prediction
s(\mathbf{x})
in (
4
), we decompose the SDF as
d(\mathbf{x})=s(\mathbf{x})\cdot u(\mathbf{x})
, where
u(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
is an unsigned distance function (UDF).
Thus, the back-end only needs to learn the UDF
u(\mathbf{x})
.
Varadhan’s formula
[
31
]
reveals that the UDF is related to short-time heat diffusion:

u(\mathbf{x})=\lim_{t\to 0}\{-\sqrt{t}\log v(\mathbf{x},t)\},

(8)

where
v(\mathbf{x},t)
is the solution to the heat equation
{\partial v/\partial t=\Delta v}
with boundary condition
v(\mathbf{x},t)=1
for
\mathbf{x}\in\partial\mathcal{O}
.
Log-GPIS
[
38
]
shows that GP regression of
f(\mathbf{x})=\exp(-\lambda u(\mathbf{x}))
with Matérn 3/2 kernel can approximate (
8
) well when the kernel scale
\sqrt{3}/\lambda
is small.
The UDF can be recovered as
u(\mathbf{x})\approx-\log\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x})]/\lambda
.
The GP
f(\mathbf{x})
is trained on the dataset of surface points
\mathcal{D}_{\text{surf}}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with labels
f(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\exp(-\lambda\cdot 0)=1
. The posterior mean and variance of
f(\mathbf{x}_{*})
at a query point
\mathbf{x}_{*}
are given by:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_{*})\\
\nabla\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_{*})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{k}_{*}^{\top}\\
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{*}}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{*}\end{bmatrix}(\mathbf{K}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{-1}\mathbf{1},

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_{*})]

\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{x}_{*},\mathbf{x}_{*})-\mathbf{k}_{*}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{*},

where
\mathbf{k}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
are calculated by the kernel:

\displaystyle k_{*,i}

\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{*})\quad 1\leq i\leq N,

(10)

\displaystyle K_{ij}

\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})\quad 1\leq i\leq N,1\leq j\leq N,

(11)

and
\Sigma_{ii}=\sigma_{i}^{2}
. The UDF gradient can also be obtained as
\nabla u(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla\hat{f}(\mathbf{x})/\|\nabla\hat{f}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}
.

Returning to the octree data structure, each octant with a BHM also maintains a log-GP for SDF estimation. We ensure inter-GP consistency by collecting surface points within expanded bounding boxes larger than the BHM sampling box and fusing predictions from the
K
nearest GPs via
\hat{u}(\mathbf{x}_{*})=\min_{k}u_{k}\left(\mathbf{x}_{*}\right)
.

IV-B
2
Extension to Other Kernels

While the Mateŕn 3/2 kernel is standard for log-GP, we show that more efficient kernels like RBF can be used by identifying the posterior mean
\hat{f}
as a softmin-like function.
For a noiseless two-point dataset
\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\}
, the posterior
\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_{*})
in (
9
) simplifies to:

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left(k(\mathbf{x}_{*},\mathbf{x}_{1})+k(\mathbf{x}_{*},\mathbf{x}_{2})\right)/\left(1+k(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})\right).

(12)

For small kernel scale,
k_{12}=k(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})\approx 0
, we have:

\log\hat{f}_{*}\approx\log(k_{*1}+k_{*2})\approx\log\left(\max(k_{*1},k_{*2})\right),

(13)

which shows that the log-GP posterior mean is approximately a softmin function. This allows recovering the UDF via kernel-specific nonlinear transforms
r(\hat{f})
, as detailed in Table
I
.
We pick the RBF kernel for its superior computational efficiency.

While larger
\lambda
(smaller kernel scale) improves the approximation of (
8
), it triggers numerical underflow of
k(\mathbf{x}_{*},\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
To address this, we introduce a scaling factor
\gamma
and rewrite (
9
):

\begin{bmatrix}\hat{f^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}_{*})\\
\nabla\hat{f^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}_{*})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{*}^{\top}\\
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{*}}^{\top}{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}_{*}\end{bmatrix}(\mathbf{K}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{y})^{-1}\mathbf{1},

(14)

where
k^{\prime}_{*,i}=k^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{*})=\gamma k(\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\|_{2})
.

\gamma>0
can be merged into the exponent. By setting
\gamma
based on the distance
d=\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{1}\|_{2}
, we effectively normalize the kernel values and prevent underflow. We set
\gamma=e^{d^{2}/(2l^{2})}
for the RBF kernel or
\gamma=e^{\sqrt{3}d/l}
for the Matérn 3/2 kernel. Then, the corresponding inverse transformations
r(\hat{f^{\prime}})
is adjusted to
r(\hat{f^{\prime}})=\sqrt{-2l^{2}\log\hat{f^{\prime}}+d^{2}}
for RBF and
r(\hat{f^{\prime}})=-\frac{l}{\sqrt{3}}\log\hat{f^{\prime}}+d
for Matérn 3/2 kernel.

IV-B
3
Consistent Uncertainty Quantification via Softmin-based Fusion

Standard variance propagation via first-order approximation,
\mathbb{V}[\hat{d}]\approx\left(r^{\prime}\right)^{2}\mathbb{V}[\hat{f}]
, fails in log-GP because
\mathbb{V}[\hat{f}]\rightarrow 1
and
|r^{\prime}|\rightarrow\infty
as
\mathbf{x}_{*}
moves away from
\mathcal{D}_{\text{surf}}
, causing
\mathbb{V}[\hat{d}]\rightarrow\infty
.
To provide a meaningful uncertainty estimate, we leverage the softmin property of the log-GP posterior and propagate surface point uncertainties
\sigma_{i}^{2}
directly through a softmin formulation.
Assuming a deterministic sign prediction, we approximate the UDF
u(\mathbf{x}_{*})
using a softmin over
\mathcal{D}_{\text{surf}}
:

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{x}_{*})\approx h(\mathbf{x}_{*},\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})=\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{z},

(15)

where
z_{i}=\left\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\right\|_{2}
and
s_{i}={e^{-\alpha z_{i}}}/{\sum_{i}^{N}e^{-\alpha z_{i}}},\alpha>0
.

The variance of
u(\mathbf{x}_{*})
and
\nabla u(\mathbf{x}_{*})
are then obtained by the first-order approximation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[u(\mathbf{x}_{*})]

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}h\right\|_{2}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\nabla_{k}u(\mathbf{x}_{*})]

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}g_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{*},\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})\right\|_{2}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2},

(17)

where
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}h=-s_{i}(\alpha\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbf{z}-\alpha z_{i}+1)({\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{i}})/{z_{i}}
,
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}_{*},

\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})

=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{*}}h(\mathbf{x}_{*},\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})
.
This approach ensures that the uncertainty in the surface point locations are directly propagated to the uncertainty in the SDF and gradient prediction.

IV-C

Priority Queue Based Update

Beyond the core BHM and GP optimizations, we also use priority-queue-based update to improve the efficiency.
Frequent sensor measurements make running surface extraction, updating GP training data buffer, and GP training after every BHM update computationally wasteful.
To optimize performance, we delay these processes until necessary using a three-queue priority system shown in Fig.
3
.
To stay responsive, frequently queried GPs get higher priority for data buffer updates and retraining.
Besides, BHMs with the oldest marching request timestamps are prioritized to process regions that are likely to remain stable.
This strategy ensures computational resources are focused on high-demand areas while maintaining overall map correctness.

VI
Conclusion

We presented Kernel-SDF, a real-time kernel regression framework for differentiable SDF learning with uncertainty quantification. By coupling BHMs for robust surface estimation with GPs for distance prediction, our method achieves superior reconstruction accuracy and efficiency. The hierarchical tree structure ensures scalability for large-scale environments, while our softmin-based uncertainty quantification enables risk-aware decision-making for downstream robotic tasks.
Extensive evaluations on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that Kernel-SDF outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in both quality and speed. Furthermore, our BHM-based front-end effectively mitigates sensor noise and adapts to dynamic environments, providing a robust and practical solution for real-time robotic applications.

VII-A

EM Algorithm for Updating BHM

Readers can refer to
[
7
,
27
]
for more details about the Bayesian Hilbert Map (BHM). Here, we provide a brief derivation of the EM algorithm for updating the BHM model parameters,
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
.

BHM represents a continuous occupancy field
P(y\mid\mathbf{x})
by using a set of
M
hinge points
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
with weights
\mathbf{w}
to do kernel regression in the Hilbert space:

P(y\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})=\sigma\big((2y-1)\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\big),

(18)

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})=[k(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}),\cdots,k(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{M}),1]^{\top}
is a vector of kernel values,
k(\cdot,\cdot)
is RBF kernel, and
\sigma
is the sigmoid function.

Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{k},y_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
of
K
generated samples at time
t
, our goal is to update the weights
\mathbf{w}
of the model such that we get the posterior distribution:

P(\mathbf{w}|\mathcal{D})=\frac{P(\mathcal{D}|\mathbf{w})P(\mathbf{w})}{P(\mathcal{D})},

(19)

where
P(\mathbf{w})
is the prior distribution of the weights, and the likelihood is given by:

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{D}|\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{K}P(y_{k}|\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{w})

(20)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\sigma(-y_{k}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})),\quad y_{k}\in\{-1,1\}.

The marginal likelihood
P(\mathcal{D})
is intractable, so we resort to approximate inference methods.
Our goal to maximize the log marginal likelihood:

\displaystyle\log P(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\log\int P(\mathcal{D}|\mathbf{w})P(\mathbf{w})d\mathbf{w}

(21)

\displaystyle=\log\int Q(\mathbf{w})\frac{P(\mathcal{D}|\mathbf{w})P(\mathbf{w})}{Q(\mathbf{w})}d\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle\geq\int Q(\mathbf{w})\log\frac{P(\mathcal{D}|\mathbf{w})P(\mathbf{w})}{Q(\mathbf{w})}d\mathbf{w},

\displaystyle\uparrow\text{Jensen's inequality}

where
Q(\mathbf{w})
is an approximate posterior distribution of the weights.

Since
P(\mathcal{D}|\mathbf{w})
is product of sigmoids that contains the weights
\mathbf{w}
inside,
we use the variational bound
[
7
]
to get a looser lower bound:

\sigma(r)\geq\sigma(\xi)\exp\left(\frac{r-\xi}{2}+\lambda\left(\xi\right)\left(r^{2}-\xi^{2}\right)\right),

where
\xi
is a variational parameter and
\lambda(\xi)=\frac{1}{2\xi}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\sigma(\xi)\right)
.

Using the idea of Sequential BHM EM update
[
27
]
, we assume a Gaussian prior
P(\mathbf{w})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t-1})
and a Gaussian approximate posterior
Q(\mathbf{w})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t})
for the weights.
Hence, we can write the variational lower bound of the log marginal likelihood as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\log P(\mathcal{D}|\mathbf{w})\right]+\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\log P(\mathbf{w})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\log Q(\mathbf{w})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\log\sigma(-y_{k}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\log P(\mathbf{w})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\log Q(\mathbf{w})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\log\sigma(\xi_{k})-\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}+\frac{\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[r_{k}\right]}{2}+\lambda(\xi_{k})\left(\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[r_{k}^{2}\right]-\xi_{k}^{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\log P(\mathbf{w})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\log Q(\mathbf{w})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\log\sigma(\xi_{k})-\frac{\xi_{k}}{2}-\xi_{k}^{2}\lambda(\xi_{k})+(y_{k}-\frac{1}{2})\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\lambda(\xi_{k})\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\right)\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\right)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}|}{|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t-1}|}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t-1}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t-1}\right),

where
r_{k}=y_{k}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),y_{k}\in\{0,1\}.

To maximize the lower bound
\mathcal{L}
, we take derivatives with respect to the variational parameters
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}
, and set them to zero.
This results in the following update equations:

E-Step

\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}=0
leads to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(y_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\right),

(22)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t-1}^{-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Bigg|\frac{1/2-\sigma(\xi_{k})}{\xi_{k}}\Bigg|\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}_{k}).

(23)

M-Step

\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\xi_{k}=0
leads to

\xi_{k}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\right)\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})},\quad\forall k=1,\ldots,K.

(24)

Note that
\xi_{k,t}=\xi_{k}
, where
t
is omitted for simplicity. We suggest initialize
\xi_{k}
to 0.0 or 1.0 for all
k
(Empirically, both work well).

Practical Optimization for the Implementation

For each generated dataset
\mathcal{D}
, usually 1 to 2 EM iterations are sufficient for convergence.
To make the algorithm more efficient, we set
\phi_{m}(\mathbf{x}_{k})=0
if its value is smaller than a threshold (e.g.,
10^{-3}
) so that we can speed up the computation by the sparsity of
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
.
Besides, the update of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
needs matrix inversion of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}
, which is handled by Cholesky decomposition for efficiency and numerical stability.

VII-B

With enough iterations, we get the converged

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

g(\mathbf{x}_{*},\mathbf{w})=P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x}_{*},\mathbf{w})=\sigma(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{*}),

\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})

\mathbb{E}[g_{*}(\mathbf{w})]=P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x}_{*})=\int\sigma\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{*}\right)P(\mathbf{w})d\mathbf{w}.

A faster and more accurate approximation is

\mathbb{E}[g_{*}(\mathbf{w})]\approx\sigma\left(h\right),\quad h=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{*}}{\sqrt{1+\frac{\pi}{8}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{*}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{*}}}.

Proof.

We approximate the sigmoid function

\displaystyle P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x}_{*})

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{*}}{\sqrt{\lambda^{-2}+\boldsymbol{\phi}_{*}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{*}}}\right)\approx\sigma\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{*}}{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{*}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{*}}}\right)

where

\displaystyle P(X\leq Y)

Since

P(X\leq Y)=\Phi\left(\frac{-a}{\sqrt{1+b^{2}}}\right)

VII-D

Priority-Based Update Scheme

In Kernel-SDF, several operations take significant time, including BHM EM update, BHM marching, GP training data collection, and GP training. However, not all operations need to be performed at every time step. Therefore, we introduce a priority-based update scheme to efficiently allocate computational resources to the most needed updates.

To maintain the responsiveness of the system to the latest sensor data, BHM EM updates are performed at every time step for all BHMs that have new sensor data. Newly created BHMs also need to be marched immediately to get surface points for its corresponding GP training data buffer update. For all GP training operations, we delay them until they are required for SDF prediction.

For other operations, we use priority queues to determine the order of updates based on their necessity.
In order to make the system also responsive to the latest query trends (e.g., the down-stream planner may focus on a specific area), each GP has a query counter
c_{0}
that records how many times the GP is requested for SDF prediction. The counter helps to reshape the priority of GP training and data buffer updates. When a GP is trained, its query counter is reduced by half. And the query counter is capped to a maximum value (10000) to avoid over-prioritization.

VII-D
1
Marching Priority Queue

Note that a single EM update of a BHM only affects a small portion of the surface points. Especially for well-updated BHMs, the surface point changes are even smaller. Hence, we first introduce a priority queue for marching BHMs, which orders the BHMs by the latest timestamp
t_{\mathcal{B}}
when a BHM is requested to update surface estimation. The BHM with the oldest timestamp (smaller
t_{\mathcal{B}}
) in the queue is processed first because it is more likely to be unchanged in the near future:

s_{\text{march}}=t_{\mathcal{B}}\downarrow,

(39)

where
\downarrow
indicates smaller values have higher priority.

VII-D
2
GP Data Buffer Update Queue

After marching a BHM, its corresponding GP training data buffer needs to be updated with the new surface points. However, it also takes time to get significant changes of
\mathcal{D}_{\text{surf}}
for updating the GP. And
\mathcal{D}_{\text{surf}}
is not required until the GP needs to be trained. Therefore, we introduce a priority queue that orders the GPs by the following score:

s_{\text{buffer update}}=c_{1}(1+\eta_{1}c_{0})\uparrow,

(40)

where
c_{1}
is the number of times the GP data buffer is marked to be updated since the last update,
\eta_{1}
is a weight parameter, and
\uparrow
indicates larger values have higher priority.
c_{1}
is increased by 1 each time the GP is marked to update its data buffer after marching its corresponding BHM and reset to 0 after the data buffer is updated. For newly created GPs, we initialize
c_{1}
to
c_{1}^{\max}\exp(-\gamma\|\mathbf{c}_{GP}-\mathbf{o}_{t}\|_{2})
, where
\mathbf{c}_{GP}
is the center of the GP training data collection bounding box,
\mathbf{o}_{t}
is the current sensor origin, and
\gamma>0
is a decay parameter. This initialization gives higher priority to GPs closer to the sensor origin.

VII-D
3
GP Training Queue

Similar to the GP data buffer update, GP training is not required until SDF prediction is requested. In addition, training a GP only makes sense when its training data buffer has significant changes. Therefore, we introduce another priority queue that orders the GPs by the following score:

s_{\text{GP train}}=c_{2}(1+\eta_{2}c_{0})\uparrow,

(41)

where
c_{2}
is the number of times the GP is marked to be trained since the last training,
\eta_{2}
is a weight parameter, and
\uparrow
indicates larger values have higher priority.
c_{2}
is increased by
c_{1}
each time the GP’s buffer is updated and reset to 0 after the GP is trained. When the GP is trained,
c_{0}
is also halved in case the GP is over-prioritized for future updates as the query trend may change.

Efficient Tree Construction

To optimize occupancy mapping, we extend the Octomap
[
6
]
to include a dedicated 2D quadtree implementation. Performance is further boosted by updating voxels in a sorted, along-ray order (Fig.
11(a)

This approach leverages spatial locality that when a free voxel is updated, its neighboring voxels are more likely to be updated, improving the CPU cache hit rate.
While the order of occupied voxels is obvious, we implement an algorithm demonstrated in Fig.

to efficiently generate the sorted free-voxel sequence.

To construct the tree efficiently, we implement two key optimizations: (a) voxels are updated in ray-sorted order to maximize the CPU cache hit rate; and (b) this ordering is generated via a stride-2 parallel reduction. Experiment Details

VII-F
1
Parameter Settings

For Kernel-SDF, we set the octree resolution to 0.08m, 0.05m and 0.7m for the Replica, Cow&Lady, and Newer College datasets, respectively. The local BHMs are placed at the depth
D_{\text{tree}}-1
, which means the local BHM bounding box size is twice the octree voxel size. The number of hinge points per axis is set to 7 for the Replica and Cow&Lady datasets, and 9 for the Newer College dataset. The BHM kernel scale
l
is set to 0.016m, 0.013m and 0.11m for the three datasets, respectively. For the GP, we use RBF kernel and set
\lambda=\frac{1}{2l^{2}}
to 500, 300, and 250 for the three datasets, respectively. For other parameters, please refer to our released code.

For the baselines, we use the default parameters provided in their released code except for necessary modifications to adapt to the datasets. For example, iSDF
[
22
]
only supports image input, so we convert the LiDAR scans of the Newer College dataset to depth images using the camera intrinsic parameters that give the same field of view as the LiDAR. We set the voxel size of Voxblox and FIESTA to be 0.05m for the Replica and Cow&Lady datasets, and 0.2m for the Newer College dataset to get a better trade-off between accuracy and efficiency.

For our method and VDB-GPDF
[
37
]
, a mesh is available from the algorithm directly. For Voxblox
[
21
]
, FIESTA
[
5
]
, and iSDF
[
22
]
, we extract the mesh using the marching cubes algorithm
[
15
]
with the voxel size as the marching cubes resolution, except for iSDF, where we use a resolution of 0.02m for the Replica and Cow&Lady datasets and 0.1m for the Newer College dataset.

VII-F
2
Mesh Metrics

For Replica dataset, we sample 2 million points from the ground truth mesh and the reconstructed mesh, respectively. For Cow&Lady dataset, we sample 54k points from the ground truth point cloud and the reconstructed mesh, respectively. For Newer College dataset, we sample 2 million points from the ground truth point cloud and the reconstructed mesh, respectively.

We compute precision, recall, and F1 score with a threshold of 0.05m for the Replica and Cow&Lady datasets, and 0.2m for the Newer College dataset. A point is considered to be correctly reconstructed if its distance to the other point cloud is less than the threshold.

Besides, we compute the accuracy and the completion, which are defined as:

Accuracy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\min_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{Q}}\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{2},

(42)

Completion

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{Q}}\min_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{2},

(43)

where
\mathcal{P}
is the reconstructed point cloud and
\mathcal{Q}
is the ground truth point cloud.

We also choose to use Chamfer-L1 distance in place of the standard Chamfer distance for more intuitive interpretation of the error in meters, which is the average of accuracy and completion.

VII-F
3
SDF Metrics

For SDF evaluation, we query each method with a regular grid of points covering the bounding box of the ground truth mesh or point cloud. The grid resolution is set to 0.05m for the Replica and Cow&Lady datasets, and 0.2m for the Newer College dataset. Prediction may fail for Voxblox and FIESTA at some query points because they rely on the eight voxel corners to interpolate the SDF value. In such cases, we exclude the failed points when calculating the metrics for Voxblox and FIESTA.

VII-F
4
Time Metrics

While it is straightforward to measure the prediction time for all methods, measuring the update time is more complex due to the different update strategies employed by each method. For a fair comparison, we measure the average update time per scan for all methods. Specifically, we record the total time taken to process all scans in a dataset (but exclude time of data loading, logging and etc.) and divide it by the number of scans to obtain the average update time per scan. This approach provides a consistent basis for comparing the efficiency of each method’s update process.
