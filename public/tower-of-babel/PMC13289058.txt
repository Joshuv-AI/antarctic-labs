Effects of laughter yoga on reducing depression and anxiety in children with autism spectrum disorder and parenting stress in their parents: study protocol for a randomised-controlled feasibility trial
Authors: Joyce Oi Kwan Chung, Chung Yan Lam, Sau Fong Leung, Daniel Bressington, Andy Chun Yin Chong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBoth children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and their parents are at a high risk of experiencing anxiety and depression. Recent studies have indicated that laughter yoga can create happiness and reduce negative emotions in adults. However, there are limited studies about how laughter yoga benefits children with ASD and parents of children with ASD. This feasibility study aims to explore the recruitment, retention rate, protocol adherence and acceptability of a laughter yoga programme in children with ASD and their parents. Moreover, it investigates the safety issues and the effects of laughter yoga on reducing depression and anxiety in children with ASD, as well as parenting stress in their parents.Methods and analysisThis study protocol describes a feasibility study aimed at evaluating the feasibility and acceptability of a laughter yoga intervention for children with ASD and their parents. This feasibility study uses a randomised, wait-list-controlled trial and pre-test–post-test design. The primary outcomes include recruitment rate, screening rate, attendance rate, dropout rate, perceptions of laughter yoga, difficulties encountered and safety issues. The secondary outcomes focus on depressive and anxiety symptoms among children with ASD and parenting stress levels. Data will be collected before the intervention (T0), immediately after the intervention (T1) and 3 months after the intervention (T2). 66 children with ASD and their parents will be recruited in Hong Kong through social media posts. Participants will be randomly assigned to either the laughter yoga group or the wait-list-control group. The laughter yoga group will receive the intervention which consists of 12 classes of 1-hour laughter yoga over 6 months. After the intervention, families will be invited to join a family-based semi-structured interview, which aims at understanding participants’ experiences, safety concerns and feedback they may have. The wait-list control group will receive no treatment until the completion of T2. The collected data will be used to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of laughter yoga for children with ASD and their parents.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the Human Subject Ethical Subcommittee of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University (No.: HSEARS20230723002). The results of the research project will be published in an academic journal.Trial registration numberNCT06326255.

Introduction

Background

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that is characterised by social communication difficulties and the presence of restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviours, interests or activities.1 ASD and mood disorders are often co-occurring conditions. Previous studies reported that the rate of psychiatric comorbidities, especially anxiety and depression, in children with ASD was higher than in children without ASD.2 3 A meta-analysis revealed the prevalence rate of anxiety disorder in children with ASD was 11.1%, while the rate of depression in them was 1.8%.4 This relates to children with ASD experiencing social communication difficulties that can lead to emotional problems.5 Many children with ASD reported having fewer friends and being less popular in school, which can foster many negative emotions, such as isolation, loneliness, distress, frustration and anxiety.6 In addition, children with ASD have difficulties in modulating sensory stimuli, which can be exacerbated by the environment and unpredictability in the school setting that may magnify the children’s mood and behaviour problems.7 8 Due to the high risk of mood problems, strategies to promote mental health and prevent mood disorders for children with ASD are urgently needed.

Parenting stress

Raising a child with ASD has many challenges. Parents of children with ASD often reported over-stress in their parenting roles.9 These parents are also vulnerable to anxiety and depression.10 Previous studies have shown that autistic symptoms detrimentally affect parental mental well-being.11 12 Parents frequently reported feeling stressed about their children’s emotional and behavioural problems.13 In addition, parents of children with ASD tended to report more severe couple conflicts than couples who do not have children with ASD.14 Parents are crucial in child development, and their emotions directly affect their behaviour and attitude towards children.15 Less parenting stress not only benefits parents but also directly benefits their children’s well-being.16 Therefore, strategies to alleviate parenting stress for the parents of children with ASD are needed.17

Laughter-based intervention

Laughter possesses a natural healing power.18 19 It is a non-invasive intervention for people to promote their mental well-being and reduce anxiety and depression.20 A meta-analysis revealed that laughter therapy can effectively reduce negative emotions and stress.21 To boost positive emotions, people can participate in enjoyable activities that foster happiness. When people feel happy, they will laugh. Laughter is a positive sensation that can effectively lower stress hormones. Neuroscience has already confirmed that laughter can reduce stress hormones by increasing endorphin levels.22 Kim and Choi23 found that laughter-based interventions could reduce anxiety, pain and stress for paediatric patients. They also reported that those interventions are particularly suitable for children. Moreover, shared laughter can break down social barriers, promote a positive atmosphere and make social interaction and bonding with others.24

Benefits and applications of laughter yoga

Laughter yoga, created by Dr Madan Kataria in 1995, combines laughter exercises and deep breathing techniques as an exercise to strengthen physical, mental and social well-being.25 Laughter yoga is simulated laughter that happens with or without reason and not elicited by humour.26 Studies have reported that laughter yoga can improve adults’ physical and mental health, and is suitable for people with or without illness to practice.2729 For example, patients with cancer30 and those receiving haemodialysis31 also reported receiving the benefits from laughter yoga. However, there are very few studies that have explored the benefits of laughter yoga for children. Alıcı and Kalanlar32 found that six sessions of laughter yoga over a 3-week period diminished emotional burnout levels among eighth-grade secondary school students. Aghajani et al33 reported that laughter yoga alleviated anxiety levels in children aged 6–12. Similar results were also reported by Tabei et al34 who found that laughter yoga decreased the level of separation anxiety in kindergarteners. Furthermore, laughter yoga could promote orphans’ emotional resilience and mental well-being35 and reduce hopelessness and loneliness in children with leukaemia.36 Only one experimental study investigated the effects of laughter yoga on children with ASD. Ghafarian and Sheykh37 reported laughter yoga improved attention, motivation and reduced anxiety in high-functioning children with ASD aged 7–10. There is a need to explore further how laughter yoga benefits children with ASD.

In paediatric research, enrolment and retention rates are always challenging and tend to be reported as low.38 This feasibility study explores the recruitment process, intervention adherence (attendance) and potential benefits of laughter yoga regarding anxiety, depression, parenting stress and social well-being. Moreover, it explores whether laughter yoga is acceptable and examines any safety issues associated with practising laughter yoga in children with ASD. Bressington et al39 reported that some adults with residual depression expressed feelings of embarrassment when laughing at home alone. In addition, prolonged or excessive laughter can lead to headaches, throat irritation, a temporary spike in blood pressure, syncope, trigger asthma attacks and stress incontinence.40 The results will provide evidence and insight to develop a randomised controlled trial which aims to examine the effectiveness of laughter yoga on children with ASD to reduce their depression and anxiety and parenting stress for their parents in the future.

Objectives

This feasibility study aims to evaluate whether laughter yoga is acceptable and feasible to reduce depression and anxiety in children with ASD and parenting stress for their parents. Three hypotheses of this study include (1) laughter yoga is acceptable to children with ASD and their parents; (2) laughter yoga is feasible for anxiety and depression reduction in children with ASD and parenting stress reduction in their parents; and (3) participants in the laughter yoga group will have significantly more reduction in depression, anxiety and parental stress than those in the wait-list-control group. Both quantitative and qualitative data will be collected in this study. The objectives of collecting quantitative data include (1) assessing the feasibility of recruiting an adequate number of participants, (2) assessing the ability to retain an adequate proportion of participants, (3) assessing participants’ adherence to the laughter yoga programme, (4) evaluating the potential preliminary effects by comparing the laughter yoga group and the wait-list-control group in terms of anxiety and depression reduction and social improvement in children with autism and (5) evaluating the potential preliminary effects by comparing the laughter yoga group and the wait-list-control group in terms of parenting stress reduction in parents of children with ASD. The objectives of collecting qualitative data include (1) understanding the participants’ perception of laughter yoga after the laughter yoga programme, (2) understanding the safety issues of laughter yoga and (3) exploring difficulties, perceived benefits and social relationship changes after the laughter yoga.

Methods and analysis

This feasibility study is a randomised, waitlist-controlled trial. It is designed to assess the feasibility and acceptability of a 6-month, family-based laughter yoga programme (two sessions every month) in Hong Kong for children with ASD aged 8–12 and their parents.

Trial setting

Participants in the laughter yoga group will attend 1-hour laughter yoga sessions every 2 weeks on Saturdays in an activity room at a local university in Hong Kong. The activity room, with air conditioning and chairs, is spacious enough for indoor activities and exercise.

Patient involvement

Participants

The first author (CYL) will screen all the enrolled families. To meet the inclusion criteria, the child should be: (1) diagnosed with ASD (according to either Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition, Text Revision or International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision), (2) aged 8–12, (3) able to read and understand Chinese and Cantonese, and have a parent (father or mother) participating in this study together. Their parents should (1) be able to read and understand Chinese and Cantonese, (2) be available to attend the laughter yoga classes with their child and (3) be willing to complete the required post-test.

It will not be allowed to participate in this feasibility study if the child has (1) recently undergone surgery; (2) a primary diagnosis not ASD; (3) IQ score lower than 70; (4) been diagnosed low functioning ASD; (5) history of severe behavioural problems, for example, aggression toward others; or (6) been receiving medical treatment for anxiety or depression at the start of the laughter yoga programme. The parent will be excluded from this study if the parent has (1) recently undergone surgery, (2) been diagnosed with a psychiatric disorder or (3) no child with ASD willing to participate in this study.

Randomisation

The families who meet the recruitment criteria will be fully informed about the procedure and intervention (figure 1). Parents will be given an information sheet and will be asked to sign the consent form. Families will be randomly assigned to either the laughter yoga group or the wait-list control group. To ensure the 1:1 ratio between the laughter yoga group (A) and wait-list control group (B) (1:1 allocation), the last author (ACYC), who is not involved in the recruitment process, will use free software R to perform a Fisher-Yates shuffle to randomise the families into either the laughter yoga group or the wait-list control without bias. Then, the last author (ACYC) keeps the allocation sequence hidden and in sealed envelopes. Once the families have signed the consent form and completed the baseline assessment (T0), regarding the sequential number, the family will receive a sealed envelope containing the allocation.

Blinding

Neither the investigators nor the families are blinded in this trial. Blinding the participants is not feasible in this feasibility study because the participants will engage in laughter yoga exercise. Moreover, laughter yoga is a physical exercise; participants need to fully understand the safety issues. Therefore, this feasibility study adopts a wait-list control strategy.

Sample size

This feasibility trial aims to collect preliminary data and does not explore the efficacy of the intervention. The sample size of this study refers to one pilot randomised controlled trial that investigated the effects of a yoga-based programme in 61 children with ASD aged 8–12 (experimental group n=31 and waitlist control n=30) in Australia.41 It found that the yoga-based intervention diminished sleep, emotional and anxiety problems. This feasibility study aims to recruit a sample size of 30 participants per treatment arm, with an estimated 10% dropout rate.42 43 A total of 66 families will be recruited.

Recruitment

To find potential participants, this feasibility study adopts convenience sampling to recruit families. Social media is a powerful, fast and cost-effective method for recruitment.44 Recruitment posts will be shared in Facebook and WhatsApp groups which are specifically for local parents of children with ASD. To ensure adequate families for this trial, the first author (CYL) regularly releases the recruitment poster, which is written in Chinese, every 2 months and encourages people to share it with parents who have children with ASD. In addition, a recruitment poster will be emailed to all local primary school principals and they will be asked to share the recruitment poster on their school platforms.

Intervention

Primary school students have busy school schedules, which affects when they can do exercise. A 6-month laughter yoga programme is designed for primary students with ASD and their parents. The father or mother and one child will attend 12 sessions of 1-hour laughter yoga, once every 2 weeks. Participants will attend laughter yoga sessions in an indoor activity room on a local university campus, which is suitable for exercise. The sessions are guided by a certified laughter yoga teacher and are family-based. During the 1-hour session, families will perform warm-up exercises, deep-breathing exercises, childlike playfulness and laughter exercises.

The rundown and contact of each laughter yoga session adhere to the guidelines set by Laughter Yoga International, which include four parts: greeting (5 min), warm-up exercises (10 min), laughter exercises embracing childlike playfulness and deep breathing (30 min) and relaxation and sharing (15 min). To validate the content of the laughter yoga programme, one local laughter yoga expert and a nurse consultant in child and adolescent psychiatry have been invited to examine the programme’s content. They reviewed the content, frequency, duration and safety issues of the laughter yoga programme. They commented that the content design adheres to the principles of laughter yoga. The duration and frequency of the programme are appropriate and feasible. The safety guide is practical and appropriate. A Laughter Yoga International-certified teacher with experience in engaging children with ASD will lead all laughter yoga classes. The first author (CYL), a certified laughter yoga trainer, will directly observe the fidelity of the laughter yoga classes and monitor participants’ attendance. If participants do not adhere to the laughter yoga practice in class for more than 15 min, the first author (CYL) will jot down the duration and reason. During the 6-month laughter yoga programme, if any participants (either child or parent) need to take psychiatric medication or require urgent surgery, the intervention for the family will be suspended immediately. To promote adherence and attendance rates, children will receive a sticker after class and place the sticker on their attendance sheet. In addition, each family will also receive HK$30 for the transport fee for each class attended. The families in the wait-list-control group will not receive any treatment and will be treated as usual care. Based on the principle of beneficence, the families in the wait-list-control group will receive the same intervention and transport fee after the completion of data collection (T2).

Outcome measures

Participants’ characteristics

At the baseline measurement, participants’ demographic data will be collected. The data includes (1) the age of the children, (2) the gender of the children, (3) the years of study (children), (4) the current marital status of parents, (5) the relationship of participating parent to the child (father or mother), (6) employment status of the participating parents and (7) the range of family income.

Primary outcomes

To assess the feasibility, this study will measure several key primary outcomes: the recruitment rate (the number of families who consented to participate divided by the number of families approached to take part), the screening rate (the number of families who are eligible among those screened, divided by the total number of families screened), the randomisation rate (the number of eligible families who are randomised divided by the total number of eligible families), the adherence rate (the number of sessions attended over the total 12 sessions), the retention rate (the number of randomised families that completed all data (T2) divided by the total number of families randomised) and the dropout rate (number of randomised families who drop-out and failed to complete data at T2 divided by the number of families randomised).45 We expected 66 families to be recruited and randomised into either the laughter yoga group or the wait-list-control group within the first year. To assess participants’ adherence rate, the attendance and reasons for absences from the class will be reported. To further understand the acceptability and safety issues, the first author (CYL) will record any incidents that occur during laughter yoga sessions and ask participants about their discomfort at the end of each class. After the completion of the laughter yoga programme, the families in the laughter yoga group will be invited to a semi-structured interview which aims at further understanding their experiences and difficulties in practising laughter yoga and safety issues.

Secondary outcomes

To explore how laughter yoga affects anxiety and depressive symptoms in children with ASD and parenting stress in parents, the parents will complete the Chinese version of the Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scale-Parent Version (RCADS-P) and of the Parenting Stress Index-Short Form (PSI-SF).

Children’s anxiety and depression

The Chinese version of the RCADS-P comprises six subscales with 47 items46 which are used to measure the symptoms of anxiety and depression in children. Five subscales cover the symptoms of anxiety disorders, including separation anxiety disorder (7 items), social phobia (9 items), generalised anxiety disorder (6 items), panic disorder (9 items) and obsessive-compulsive disorder (6 items). One scale assesses the symptoms of major depressive disorder with 10 items.47 The questionnaire uses a 4-point Likert scale, and parents rate their children on how often specific situations happen (3=always, 2=often, 1=sometimes and 0=never). A higher score indicates more symptoms of anxiety or depression. The Chinese version of the RCADS-P can be used to assess the symptoms of depression and anxiety disorders among children in clinical settings. Lu et al48 reported good psychometric properties of the Chinese version of the RCADS in 1001 Chinese children, including excellent internal consistency (coefficients=0.94), acceptable reliability (subscales ranged from 0.63 to 0.81); and it strongly correlated with the Child Behaviour Checklist. Lu et al48 also reported that the six subscales of the Chinese version of the RCADS correlated with each other (Normed Fit Index=0.83; root mean square error of approximation=0.05 and Comparative Fit Index=0.88).

Parenting stress

The Chinese version of the PSI-SF will be used to assess parenting stress. The PSI-SF includes three domains: parental distress, parent–child dysfunctional interaction and difficult children.49 The fourth version includes 12 items in each domain.50 The PSI-SF adopts a 5-point Likert scale, where a score of 1 indicates ‘strongly disagree’ and a score of 5 indicates ‘strongly agree’. The minimum total score is 30, and the maximum score is 180. A higher total score indicates that the parents experience higher levels of parenting stress. Tam et al51 examined the psychometric properties of the Chinese version of the PSI-SF and reported excellent reliability (α=0.93). The concurrent, discriminant validity and factorial validity reached 93% overall accuracy and it can discriminate between ‘high-stress’ and ‘low-stress’ parents.

Study procedure and timeline

The first author (CYL) will screen the potential participants, explain the study to both children and parents and give them the information sheet. Parents will sign the consent form and complete all questionnaires (T0). Then, the families will be randomly assigned to either the laughter yoga group or the wait-list-control group (T1) before the intervention begins. Then, the families in the laughter yoga group will join the laughter yoga programme over 6 months (T0-T1), and the wait-list-control group will receive usual care without any intervention. The parents in both groups will complete the questionnaire at 6 months (T1) and again at the 3-month follow-up after the intervention (T2). Families in the laughter yoga group will be invited to participate in a family-based, semi-structured interview after the intervention. The family in the wait-list-control group will receive the same laughter yoga intervention after the completion of T2 assessments (figure 2). To facilitate parents’ completion of all questionnaires at the three time points of data collection, the first author (CYL) will explain the importance of retention. To encourage families to attend the laughter yoga sessions, a WhatsApp message reminder is sent to the parents 2 days before the laughter yoga session.

Data collection methods

Quantitative data

The first author (CYL) will collect all the data and perform data entry. Before the intervention, all parents will complete the socio-demographic questionnaire, the Chinese version of the RCADS-P and the Chinese version of the PSI-SF (T0). They will complete the questionnaires again after 6 months (T1) and at a 3-month follow-up (T2). Parents will be reminded to complete all questions before answering each question to reduce the missing data. All the questionnaires are paper-based and are collected after parents complete them to prevent question leakage.

Qualitative data

To better understand the experiences of children with ASD and their parents in the laughter yoga programme, the families in the laughter yoga group will be invited to a family event interview. The first author (CYL) will conduct all face-to-face family interviews in a private and quiet room. Interviews will be audio-recorded. The interview format will be semi-structured, and open-ended questions will be asked.52 During the interview, family members will be referred to by their roles, such as ‘mother’, ‘son’ and ‘elder son’, to protect privacy and confidentiality.

Data analysis

Quantitative data

Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise participants’ demographic data and the six primary outcomes (recruitment rate, screening rate, randomisation rate, adherence rate, retention rate and dropout rate). To explore potential preliminary effects, data from the Chinese versions of the RCADS and the PSI-SF will be analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Statistics software. The effects of children’s anxiety, depression and parenting stress reduction will be tested to determine whether change over time differs between the laughter yoga and the waitlist control group (group × time interaction). According to the intention-to-treat principle, all randomised families, regardless of their adherence rate to the laughter yoga sessions, will be included in the analysis of the secondary outcomes, with comparisons made between the laughter yoga group and the wait-list-control group.53 Furthermore, the generalised estimating equations will be used to analyse treatment group, time and group × time interaction, adjusting for the baseline value of the outcomes and prespecified covariates (child age, child gender, father or mother).54

Qualitative data

The first author (CYL) will listen to all the audio recordings and transcribe them verbatim. Using the inductive approach, the first author (CYL) will identify meaningful concepts, develop the codes and generate themes. Then, the meaningful sentences, which serve as descriptions or interpretations of the phenomenon, will be translated into English to enrich and address the objectives of this study. Through reading the verbatim transcript, the first author (CYL) will identify the patterns and themes that emerge from the overall ideas or experiences. The second and third authors, who are native Cantonese speakers and proficient in English, will independently verify the verbatim. Descriptive codes will be used to identify the participant’s words and to label them close to participant’s words. The codes with similar meanings will be merged, refined and categorised into themes. All authors will attend the peer debriefing sessions to discuss the emergent categories, themes and interpretations of the data, ensuring the findings are trustworthy.

Data management and trial monitoring

Data management and confidentiality

To protect participants’ privacy, each family will be given a pseudo-identity number. All hard copies of the consent form and completed questionnaire will be stored in a locked cabinet, with access restricted to the first and second authors. Only the first and second authors can access the personal data and the completed consent forms, which contain the participants’ names and contact numbers. Voice recordings and data files will be encrypted and will contain no personal identifiers. The data will be stored using cloud services supported by The Hong Kong Polytechnic University. All researchers have attended information technology security training and are familiar with data security. No data will be stored on laptops or external portable storage devices, such as a Universal Serial Bus. Moreover, participants’ identities will not be disclosed and reported in any reports or presentations. Only anonymised data can be shared with the other authors. All data will be permanently deleted 3 years after the final manuscript is completed. All the documents containing personal data will be shredded and disposed of in accordance with the sensitive document destruction policy of the Hong Kong Polytechnic University.

Data monitoring committee

In this trial, the first author (CYL) is responsible for collecting the data and implementing the intervention. The second, third and fourth authors form a data monitoring committee to supervise and monitor the trial and review the study protocol to ensure the safety and integrity of this trial.

Trial monitoring

The first author (CYL) regularly reports the progress of this trial to the data monitoring committee on a bi-monthly basis. The second and third researchers also attended the pilot testing to ensure that the intervention process is effective and safe for participants. The data monitoring committee regularly discusses the trial’s progress and provides its expert opinion. If any incident occurs during the trial, the first author (CYL) should report the incident immediately to the data monitoring committee and the Human Subject Ethical Subcommittee of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University. They will decide to stop this trial.

The families have the right to withdraw from this study at any time without facing any penalties. Both the information sheet and the consent form are standard documents that comply with The Hong Kong Polytechnic University’s policy on human subject research ethics.

Safety issues

Participants will be informed and explained that laughter yoga is a light-to-moderate intensity exercise, mainly involving breathing, clapping and simple movements and no extreme poses. Each participant will have a chair and can be seated. For safety considerations, participants will not have prolonged or excessive laughter and will be instructed by the certified laughter yoga trainer to practice laughter yoga. The participants will be instructed to stop and rest if they experience prolonged or excessive laughter. Moreover, the laughter yoga trainer will explain signs of prolonged laughter, for example, shortness of breath, dizziness and pain, to parents before the classes, so that they can ensure their children’s safety during the classes. All the children should be accompanied by their parents throughout the classes. All participants are required to wear sports clothing and shoes. Each class will start with warm-up exercises and end with relaxation. The first author (CYL), a mental health first aid and basic life support instructor, assists in emergency situations, for example, shortness of breath or dizziness due to excessive laughter. Laughter yoga is an exercise; all participants have the University Master clinical trial insurance coverage, which is under the research policy of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University. The insurance expenditure is supported by associated money, which financially supports a PhD student at the Hong Kong Polytechnic University. If any adverse events happen during the trial, the intervention will stop immediately.
