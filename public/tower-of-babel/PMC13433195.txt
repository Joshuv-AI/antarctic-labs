Doubly robust and efficient calibration of prediction sets for right-censored time-to-event outcomes
Authors: Rebecca Farina, Eric Tchetgen Tchetgen, Arun Kumar Kuchibhotla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur objective is to construct well-calibrated prediction sets for a time-to-event outcome subject to right censoring with guaranteed coverage. Inspired by modern conformal inference, our approach avoids the need for a well-specified parametric or semiparametric survival model. Unlike existing conformal methods for survival data, which assume Type-I censoring with fully observed censoring times, we consider the more common right-censoring setting in which only the censoring time or the event time is observed, whichever comes first. Under a standard conditional independence censoring condition, we propose and analyse several lower prediction bounds for the survival time of a future observation, including inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting and its augmented version based on the semiparametric efficient influence function for the relevant marginal quantile of the outcome, accounting for dependent censoring. We formally establish asymptotic coverage guarantees for the proposed methods and demonstrate, both theoretically and through empirical experiments, that the augmented approach substantially improves efficiency over all other proposed methods. Specifically, its coverage error bound is doubly robust, and therefore of second order, thus ensuring that it is asymptotically negligible relative to the coverage error of the other methods.

Introduction

Background

A fundamental challenge of survival analysis is the potential for a person’s event time to not be completely observed if she drops out of the study or if the study ends before sheexperiences the event of primary interest, a phenomenon commonly known as right censoring (see, e.g., Kleinbaum & Klein, 1996; Hosmer Jr et al., 2008; Collett, 2023). While classical tools such as the Kaplan–Meier estimator of the marginal survival curve (Kaplan & Meier, 1958) and the Cox proportional-hazards regression model (Cox, 1972) are well established, there remains a lack of flexible frameworks that provide rigorous uncertainty quantification for predictive inference of right-censored time-to-event outcomes.

Conformal predictive inference, introduced by Vovk et al. (2005), provides a general blueprint for constructing well-calibrated prediction intervals, with marginal coverage guarantees under relatively mild conditions. The main advantage of conformal prediction is that it provides valid finite-sample prediction intervals for a new observation exchangeable with the training sample, without imposing strong distributional assumptions on the underlying data-generating mechanism. Conformal prediction methods have developed rapidly for a wide variety of settings that depart from the canonical independent and identically distributed setting, including covariate shift (Tibshirani et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2024), missing covariates (Zaffran et al., 2024), time series analysis (Stankeviciute et al., 2021) and high-dimensional regression (Chen et al., 2016), to name a few. However, conformal predictive inference for censored time-to-event outcomes remains underdeveloped.

In this paper, we propose several new methods to construct well-calibrated and efficient prediction bounds for survival times under standard right censoring. Notably, to calibrate our prediction intervals, we propose two separate approaches based on inverse probability of censoring (Robins & Rotnitzky, 1992; Robins, 1993; Robins & Finkelstein, 2000). The first approach (a) constructs a consistent inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting (IPCW) estimator of the marginal probability that the failure times exceed an estimated covariate-dependent lower bound, while the second approach (b) is based on an augmented-inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting (AIPCW) estimator for the same marginal probability. Although IPCW and AIPCW methods are well developed for estimation and inference in standard survival analysis settings, (a) and (b) appear to provide their first use for obtaining well-calibrated prediction intervals. In addition, we consider two so-called outcome regression-based methods that obviate the need for estimating the censoring mechanism: the outcome regression method, which entails estimating the conditional quantile function for the outcome at the desired nominal level, and the calibrated outcome regression method, which adjusts the quantile level to match the desired nominal marginal coverage.

Related work

Recent conformal prediction methods for survival data include Cox regression-based (Teng et al., 2021) and random survival forest-based (Boström et al., 2017, 2019) methods, as well as those of Candès et al. (2023) and Gui et al. (2023), who proposed constructing covariate-dependent lower predictive bounds. However, these methods assume that censoring times are observed for all units in the training and validation samples, a setting also known as Type-I censoring. Formally, the observed data consist of tuples \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ (X,C,Y) $\end{document}. Under this setting, Candès et al. (2023) established that any distribution-free, well-calibrated LPB for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ T $\end{document} must necessarily be an LPB for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ Y $\end{document}. This motivated their proposed approach, which entails discarding any observation with an early censoring time, below a user-specified constant \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ c_{0} $\end{document}, combined with weighted conformal inference (Tibshirani et al., 2019) to correct for the distribution shift introduced by the initial filtering step. Based on this approach, Gui et al. (2023) incorporated a covariate-dependent cut-off, making the procedure substantially less conservative. Under Type-I censoring, their method yields an LPB that is asymptotically PAC-calibrated in the sense of Definition 2, with the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ o_{p}(1) $\end{document} term being the maximum of the estimation error of the conditional survival function of the censoring time, given \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ X $\end{document} and an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ {\{\log(1/\epsilon)/n\}^{1/2}} $\end{document}-order term; see Theorem 3 of Gui et al. (2023).

By requiring that censoring times are fully observed, the methods of Candès et al. (2023) and Gui et al. (2023) are primarily useful in applications where potential censoring times are determined at the start of a unit’s follow-up, such as experimental settings in which administrative censoring times are predetermined. In contrast, other common forms of censoring, such as loss to follow-up and related protocol departures, can seldom, if ever, be prevented by design. Another apparent limitation of the methods of Candès et al. (2023) and Gui et al. (2023) is their over-reliance on a cut-off tuning parameter that induces artificial censoring of units with censoring time in the left tail of their distribution, possibly censoring units with observed failure times. This form of artificial censoring introduces a bias-variance trade-off that may be challenging to balance in practice. Our proposed methods target an asymptotic PAC guarantee analogous to theirs. However, as detailed in the next section, we achieve this goal while (i) accommodating a more common form of right censoring; (ii) obviating the need for artificial censoring or a corresponding tuning parameter; and (iii)obtaining a doubly robust coverage error guarantee, in the sense that our coverage error slack is, not only of second order, but is actually of the mixed-bias product form of the estimation biases of the censoring and failure-time laws given, covariates, plus an irreducible root-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ n $\end{document} error term.

Recent works have considered alternative strategies for obtaining well-calibrated predictions under conventional censoring. In the Type-I censoring setting, Wan & Yi (2025) proposed distribution-free lower predictive bounds by combining hybrid quantile learning with a data-filtered, adaptive conformal calibration scheme. Moving beyond Type-I censoring, Sesia & Svetnik (2025b) extended the conformalized survival analysis framework of Candès et al. (2023) and Gui et al. (2023) to general right censoring by first imputing unobserved censoring times under conditionally independent censoring and then applying weighted conformal inference to the imputed data. A recent work by Qin et al. (2025) proposes a bootstrap-resampling approach for constructing conformal predictive intervals for general right-censored data, achieving asymptotic marginal validity under conditionally independent censoring. Building on our work, Si & Qiu (2025) studied lower prediction bounds with training-set-conditional APAC-type guarantees, meaning that the approximation is placed on the confidence level, rather than on the miscoverage probability as in our asymptotic PAC formulation, while conditioning on the training sample. They adopted the efficient influence-function correction introduced by our approach. Moreover, Yi et al. (2025) constructed conformal prediction intervals using inverse-censoring weighting and quantile regression as a flexible base learner. A different line of work focuses on survival curves rather than on the time-to-event outcome: Sesia & Svetnik (2025a) constructed conformal bands for individual survival curves for screening-type tasks by combining inverse-censoring weighting with multiple-testing false discovery rate control. We also note the work of Park et al. (2025), which performs post hoc calibration of predicted survival curves by reducing a Kolmogorov–Smirnov discrepancy between predicted and empirical survival probabilities. Finally, Meixide et al. (2025) introduced a prediction inference framework for interval-censored data, an important but different focus to ours. While these papers substantially advance conformal uncertainty quantification for censored outcomes, they do not simultaneously meet our specific desiderata (i)–(iii). In particular, for the setting and goal we consider, we provide a doubly robust procedure that attains semiparametric efficiency (under standard regularity conditions) and yields second-order coverage error control.

In a separate research strand, IPCW and AIPCW have played a pivotal role in the development of robust and efficient methodologies for censored data, primarily in the context of inference for a finite-dimensional functional of interest. The foundational semiparametric theory for robust and efficient estimation in the presence of censoring, based on IPCW and AIPCW, was pioneered by Robins and colleagues (Robins & Rotnitzky, 1992; Robins, 1993). See van der Laan & Robins (2003) and Tsiatis (2006) for an introductory textbook overview of modern semiparametric efficiency theory for censored and other missing data problems. Our work extends the recent paper of Yang et al. (2024), which, to the best of our knowledge, provides the first application of semiparametric efficiency theory to a conformal prediction setting subject to covariate shift. While their proposed approach can equivalently be viewed as an outcome missing at random, their proposed methods do not directly apply to the censored survival outcome setting that presents a number of new challenges we address in this work.

Our contributions

Motivated by the limitations of existing methods, we develop a new approach for well-calibrated predictive inference for standard right-censored survival outcomes, whereby either only the censoring or the primary event time is observed, whichever comes first. Our goal is to derive predictive bounds while avoiding unnecessary modelling assumptions. The first of our two proposed methods incorporates IPCW, which data adaptively accounts for the unknown censoring mechanism, provided that measured covariates are sufficiently rich to explain any dependence between censoring and the event time of primary interest. This completely obviates the need for artificial censoring and the required tuning of a cut-off parameter, while recovering robust (asymptotically) well-calibrated predictive bounds for survival times.

A well-known limitation of IPCW is that it is potentially inefficient due to discarding information contained in censored observations. Thus, in addition, we introduce an augmented IPCW approach, which recovers information from censored observations with the potential for significant efficiency improvements over IPCW. Using AIPCW also leads to a desirable double robustness property, which ensures that the error in the coverage bound of the AIPCW prediction sets, due to estimation of nuisance parameters (mainly of the conditional survival curve for the primary outcome and for the censoring mechanism given covariates, respectively), is of mixed bias product form (Rotnitzky et al., 2021). This ensures that our method can provide valid bounds even if one of the required survival curves is misspecified, offering enhanced flexibility and reliability in practical applications.

Importantly, the proposed methodology for deriving predictive bounds does not depend on the specific choice of a nonconformity score. The resulting bounds preserve their validity, and the AIPCW-based approach remains doubly robust, regardless of the choice of nonconformity score. This flexibility highlights the generality and robustness of our proposed framework. Furthermore, if the nonconformity score is properly chosen (see (2)) so as to align with the interpretation of the conditional quantile as the oracle LPB, then ourmethods also recover approximate conditional coverage; see Theorem 4 of Gui et al. (2023) for a related result.

As a complementary contribution, we introduce two simple yet effective approaches for constructing prediction bounds based on outcome regression: outcome regression (OR) and calibrated outcome regression (COR). The first estimates the conditional quantile function of the primary event time at the desired nominal level, while the second calibrates the estimated conditional quantile function of the survival time to achieve the desired marginal coverage guarantee. Although the validity of this method is contingent on a consistent estimator of the survival function model, it provides a computationally efficient alternative to the IPCW and AIPCW approaches. To the best of our knowledge, this simple calibration-based technique has not been previously explored in the context of conformal predictive inference for censored time-to-event data.

AIPCW method

The IPCW-based approach described in the previous section relies on weighting uncensored observations by the inverse of the probability of remaining uncensored up to the time at which they experience the primary outcome, thereby correcting for any potential bias due to censoring. While this method can be effective under fairly reasonable conditions, it can be inefficient because it does not make efficient use of information contained in censored observations and may not produce well-calibrated prediction sets if either the estimator of the censoring mechanism converges at a slow rate or fails to be consistent. To address these limitations, we extend our framework by introducing an augmentation to IPCW that recovers information from censored observations by leveraging a model for the outcome ofinterest.

The AIPCW method has attractive theoretical appeal over the IPCW approach, by producing a doubly robust estimator, which means that the estimator remains consistent if either the survival model or the censoring model is correctly specified (but not necessarily both). This is an immediate consequence of its mixed bias error, as shown in the result below. This robustness can be particularly valuable in survival analysis, even when machine learning or nonparametric methods are used to estimate unknown regression functions, as the AIPCW error rate is then guaranteed to be of a smaller order of magnitude than that of IPCW.

The efficiency and robustness properties of AIPCW are rooted in modern semiparametric efficiency theory, in which influence functions are central to the construction of efficient estimators. Briefly, according to a result due to Robins & Rotnitzky (1992), the efficient influence function of a regular parameter defined on a nonparametric model subject to censoring at random can be obtained as the projection of the IPCW version of the full-data efficient influence function onto the orthogonal complement of the tangent space for the censoring mechanism, assumed to satisfy Assumption 2 and otherwise unrestricted. The tangent space for a semiparametric model is technically defined as the closed linear span of scores of all regular parametric submodels contained in the model. The coverage error bound for the AIPCW estimator depends, not only on the estimation errors of the nuisance functions themselves, but also on the estimation accuracy of their derivatives. Calibrated outcome regression method

As discussed in § 1.2, the oracle LPB for the survival time is given by the conditional quantile of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ T\mid X $\end{document} at level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ \alpha $\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ {q^{\star}_{T\mid X}(\alpha\mid\cdot)} $\end{document}. A naive approach to estimate an LPB is OR, which consists of directly estimating the conditional quantile \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ {\widehat{q}_{T\mid X}(\alpha\mid X)} $\end{document}. However, such an approach does not necessarily guarantee the desired marginal coverage probability. We introduce the COR method, which adjusts the quantile level to ensure proper calibration. This approach ensures that the estimated LPB satisfies the desired probabilistic coverage constraint by leveraging the estimated survival function of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ T\mid X $\end{document}. The implementation is straightforward and similar to that of IPCW and AIPCW methods. First, on the training set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ \mathcal{D}_{1} $\end{document}, we estimate the survival function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ {\widehat{S}_{T\mid X}(\cdot\mid X)} $\end{document} and use it to compute the empirical conditional quantiles \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ {\widehat{q}_{T\mid X}(\beta\mid X)} $\end{document} over a grid of values for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ \beta $\end{document}. Second, on the calibration set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ \mathcal{D}_{2} $\end{document}, we determine the optimal quantile level by solving

Finally, we output the LPB \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ {\widehat{L}(\cdot)=\widehat{q}_{T\mid X}(\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{COR}} \mid\cdot)} $\end{document}.

The OR and COR methods provide a computationally efficient way to estimate an LPB while ensuring calibrated marginal and conditional coverage. However, their validity relies on the assumption that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ {\widehat{S}_{T\mid X}(\cdot\mid X)} $\end{document} is a consistent estimator of the true survival function. This approach is similar in spirit to maximum likelihood estimation for survival analysis, as it relies directly on modelling the conditional survival function. If the model is misspecified, the resulting predictions may be biased. The OR and COR methods account for dependence between censoring and the target outcome by conditioning on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ X $\end{document} without directlymodelling the censoring distribution. In contrast, IPCW and AIPCW methods explicitly account for censoring through inverse-probability weighting. Although COR has a first-order bias, AIPCW benefits from a second-order mixed bias, making it theoretically more robust. In summary, COR offers a straightforward way to obtain well-calibrated prediction bounds using outcome regression, whereas AIPCW provides additional robustness by leveraging information from both the censoring and survival models. Importantly, to the best of our knowledge, both OR and COR appear to be novel methods.

The grid of points \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ {\beta_{j}} $\end{document} is defined as equally spaced points \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ \{0.001j,\,j=0,\dots,1000\} $\end{document}.

Both proposed methods are implemented three times, using three different estimators for the conditional survival curves: the Cox proportional-hazards regression model, random survival forests (RSF) and super learner (SL). The Cox model, widely used in survival analysis, serves as a benchmark because of its popularity, with the cumulative baseline hazard function estimated using the Breslow estimator (Breslow, 1972). However, our primary focus is on nonparametric approaches, which do not impose restrictive modelling assumptions such as proportional hazards. RSF is a suitable choice for this purpose, owing to its flexibility in modelling survival data. For additional flexibility, we also employ SL, an ensemble method that optimally combines multiple survival algorithms by minimizing the cross-validated risk, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting in the final model Golmakani & Polley (2020). To implement these estimators, we use available R packages: survival for the Cox model, randomForestSRC (Ishwaran & Kogalur, 2021) for RSF and survSuperLearner for SL (R Development Core Team, 2026). This SL algorithm optimally combines candidate models to estimate both the conditional survival and censoring functions simultaneously. The set of candidate learners includes Kaplan–Meier, Cox proportional hazards, exponential regression, Weibull regression, log-logistic regression and random survival forests. For the COR method and its noncalibrated version (OR), we apply the SL model directly to ensure greater flexibility.

We compare our methods against two groups of benchmarks. First, we consider ‘naive’ quantile-based baselines that ignore censoring: quantile regression forests fit to the observed time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ Y $\end{document} (QR-Y) and their conformalized version (CQR-Y), as well as quantile regression forests fitted to the event time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ T $\end{document} using only uncensored observations (QR-T), and their conformalized version (CQR-T). These approaches serve as reference points because they do not technically account for the censoring mechanism, despite relying on the same assumptions as our IPCW and AIPCW methods. Second, we compare against recent conformal survival methods, namely the approaches proposed by Meixide et al. (2025), Qin et al. (2025), Sesia & Svetnik (2025b), Si & Qiu (2025) and Yi et al. (2025). A brief description of eachcompeting method, together with the implementation details used in our experiments, is provided in the Supplementary Material.

In Setting 2, both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ T $\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ C $\end{document} depend on nonlinear transformations and interactions of the covariates, yielding informative censoring, and the observed censoring rate is about 50%. In Settings 3–6 we move beyond proportional hazards by using lognormal models, which allow us to assess the performance of nonparametric models such as RSF. In these settings, censoring is covariate dependent, with observed censoring rates of approximately 62% in Setting 3, 70% in Setting 4, 66% in Setting 5 and 50% in Setting 6. Furthermore, Setting 5 includes nonlinear and interaction effects in both the event and the censoring mechanisms. Finally, Setting 6 introduces heteroscedasticity in the time-to-event model.

Results

Figure 1 shows boxplots illustrating the empirical coverage rates obtained from the 100 datasets in each setting, providing a visual comparison of the performance of allmethods. For a closer examination of each simulation study, we refer the reader to the enlarged plots in Figs. F.1 and F.2 of the Supplementary Material. Our theoretical results are asymptotic, whereas these experiments are conducted on finite samples; thus, some deviation from the target coverage is anticipated. In addition, Fig. 2 displays boxplots of the average estimated LPB values across the 100 datasets in each setting. In all plots, we highlight our recommended method AIPCW-SL.

Settings 1–2 satisfy proportional hazards, so the Cox-based nuisance models are appropriate. Indeed, in Setting 1 the IPCW and AIPCW methods attain coverage close to the target level, with similar LPBs. The outcome-regression approaches (OR and COR) also perform well because the data-generating model is simple and can be captured by a flexible learner such as SL. Setting 2 is more challenging since censoring is informative and both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ T\mid X $\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ C\mid X $\end{document} depend on nonlinear transformations or interactions. Here, the IPCW and AIPCW methods remain well calibrated, whereas OR and COR lead to undercoverage and overcoverage, respectively, reflecting the sensitivity of outcome-regression approaches to errors in estimating the survival curve in this nonlinear informative-censoring regime.

In the misspecified Settings 3–6, the Cox-based IPCW and AIPCW procedures break down, producing substantial undercoverage and overcoverage, respectively. When paired with flexible nuisance learners (RSF or SL), both IPCW and AIPCW recover near-nominal coverage. Moreover, AIPCW typically achieves coverage closer to the target and larger average LPBs than IPCW, which aligns with our theoretical results: the augmented construction is doubly robust, and its coverage error is governed by a second-order mixed-bias remainder. Outcome-regression methods perform well in Setting 3, but not in Settings 4–6, again owing to their dependence on accurate estimation of the conditional survival curve, which becomes more challenging in high-dimensional, nonlinear or heteroscedastic regimes.

The naive methods (QR-Y, CQR-Y, QR-T, CQR-T) produce overly conservative bounds across all settings, as evidenced by high coverage rates and low estimated LPBs. Among recent competing methods, Qin et al. (2025) and Sesia & Svetnik (2025b) attained near-nominal coverage in Settings 1–3. However, their performance deteriorates in themore complicated Settings 4–6, where the structure and heavier censoring make it more difficult (i) to reliably perform imputation-based approaches (Sesia & Svetnik, 2025b), and (ii) to stably bootstrap-calibrate prediction under limited tail information (Qin et al., 2025), leading to more conservative bounds. The concurrent EIF-based approach of Si & Qiu (2025) provides a valid alternative and behaves similarly to ours in most settings, but its more conservative APAC-style calibration can yield overcoverage in more challenging regimes, as in Setting 4, or slightly less informative LPBs than our AIPCW-SL method, as in Settings 5–6. Likewise, the approach proposed by Yi et al. (2025) performs well when the IPCW-quantile-regression combination adequately captures the target quantile, but it becomes less reliable under more complex censoring mechanisms, as in Setting 5, where it exhibits noticeable undercoverage. Finally, the method proposed by Meixide et al. (2025), which is designed for interval-censoring data, is not directly competitive here.

Overall, our results indicate that the proposed AIPCW approach offers a favourable and comparatively stable trade-off between validity and bound sharpness across the range of scenarios considered. As expected, naive methods that ignore censoring tend to be conservative, producing high coverage and smaller LPBs. Competing methods can match the performance of our methods in particular scenarios; however, in our experiments none simultaneously improves upon AIPCW-SL in terms of calibration while also yielding more informative bounds across all settings. These findings support recommending AIPCW-SL as a practical default, as it maintains near-nominal coverage and informative LPBs.

Real data application

Data description

We use data from a cohort of 1240 patients with rheumatoid arthritis from the Wichita Arthritis Center, an outpatient rheumatology facility (Choi et al., 2002). The original study aimed to evaluate the impact of methotrexate, the most commonly used disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD), on patient mortality. Our goal is to apply our methods to estimate predictive bounds for the survival time to death.

During the data collection, each patient underwent longitudinal follow-up, with multiple assessments recorded over time. For our analysis, we focus on baseline covariates to account for patient characteristics at the start of the study. Among the 1240 patients, 191 died during follow-up, resulting in an observed event rate of 15.4%. The average follow-up time was 72.3 months. The covariates considered in our analysis include age, sex, disease duration, education level, source of enrolment, prednisone use, DMARD use, treatment contraindications, rheumatoid factor positivity, smoking status, and several clinical measures such as functional disability score, global assessment of disease score and tender joint count. This dataset provides a robust framework for evaluating survival prediction methods in a real-world clinical setting where censoring is common.

Performance assessment

To evaluate the performance of our methods on this dataset, we estimate the empirical coverage of the predictive lower bounds using two different approaches to account for censoring: one based on inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting (IPCW and AIPCW) and the other based on outcome regression. Unlike in the simulation study, where all event times were available for validation (for both uncensored and censored observations), here we only have access to event times for uncensored outcomes, thus requiring different techniques for estimating coverage.
