Mitochondrial targeting strategies for mitigating oxidative damage and enhancing neuroprotection in ischemic stroke: A Review
Authors: Walter Ángel Trujillo-Rangel, Rebeca Escutia-Gutiérrez, Leonel García-Benavides, Adalberto Zamudio-Ojeda, Daniela LC Delgado-Lara, Florencia Guillermina García-Mercado, Danna Jocelyn Padilla-Ponce, Odessa Magallón-Chávez, Juan Antonio Rivas-Loaiza, Marco Pérez-Cisneros, Santiago José Guevara-Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemic stroke (IS) is one of the leading causes of permanent disability and mortality worldwide. It is characterized by the interruption of cerebral blood flow, causing acute oxygen and glucose deprivation, triggering pathological processes such as mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell death. Ischemic stroke constitutes the majority of all incident strokes, and regardless of its underlying cause, mitochondrial dysfunction is a universal feature of its pathophysiological cascade. Mitochondria play a key role in energy regulation, apoptosis, and the inflammatory response through the NLRP3 inflammasome. Modulation of their fusion, fission, and turnover dynamics is an emerging therapeutic strategy aimed at preserving their functional integrity. In this context, mitochondrial transplantation has shown promising results in animal models and initial clinical trials, representing a potential revolution in the management of IS. Research into therapies targeting mitochondria not only broadens the pathophysiological understanding of stroke but also offers new therapeutic opportunities with significant clinical impact.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, ROS, Oxygen, Cerebrovascular, Ischemia, Strategy, Mitochondria

Introduction

The term ischemic stroke is used to describe a series of disorders in which cerebral blood flow is partially or totally reduced, causing tissue damage. Although it can be an acute or chronic process, its acute manifestation predominates, constituting a leading cause of permanent disability and cognitive impairment. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 Study, stroke was the third most common cause of death worldwide in 2021, after ischemic heart disease and COVID−19, accounting for approximately 7.3 million deaths (10.7% of all deaths) (Guo et al., 2023). In 2021, there were an estimated 11.9 million new strokes and 93.8 million prevalent cases worldwide. Importantly, ischemic stroke is the most frequent subtype, constituting 65.3% of all incident strokes, which distinguishes it from overall stroke (a broader category that also includes hemorrhagic subtypes) (WHO, 2020).

Ischemic stroke accounts for 80% of all cerebrovascular events. The vascular etiology is due to a partial reduction or total blockage of blood flow caused by systemic hypoperfusion, stenosis, or occlusion of a blood vessel, and can be divided into two variants: focal and multifocal (Andrabi et al., 2020). Regardless of the specific vascular or cardioembolic mechanism that triggers the interruption of blood flow, mitochondrial dysfunction constitutes a universal and central event in the pathophysiological cascade of ischemic stroke. Because mitochondria govern cellular energy production, calcium handling, and the regulation of cell death, the collapse of their function is a final common pathway through which ischemic injury progresses. This central role is further underscored by primary mitochondrial diseases such as MELAS (Mitochondrial Encephalomyopathy, Lactic Acidosis, and Stroke-like episodes), in which stroke-like events arise directly from a failure of mitochondrial energy metabolism, illustrating how essential mitochondrial integrity is for neuronal survival (Guo et al., 2023).

From a pathophysiological point of view, the brain is an organ that is particularly susceptible to ischemia, as it has little energy reserve, meaning that its survival depends entirely on blood flow, which accounts for 20% of cardiac output. During an ischemic event, the brain is deprived of glucose and oxygen, and is characterized by an intense and prolonged inflammatory response that worsens the initial brain injury (Candelario-Jalil et al., 2022).

In this metabolic context, mitochondria are organelles that are fundamental for energy metabolism and neuronal survival (Liu et al., 2018). In addition to their central role in ATP production, they are involved in cellular homeostasis and regulate apoptosis and necrosis pathways. In the context of cerebral ischemia, mitochondrial dysfunction is an important cause of cell death (Andrabi et al., 2020). During the ischemia-reperfusion process, mitochondria are the main producers of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby promoting the development of oxidative stress and causing oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA (Zhou et al., 2021).

From a mechanistic perspective, mitochondria are highly dynamic in nature, maintaining their morphology and functionality through fission and fusion. Mitochondrial fission is the process by which mitochondria divide into two independent organelles, allowing for mitochondrial distribution, the elimination of damaged mitochondria, and adaptation to metabolic changes (Westermann, 2008). This process is mainly mediated by dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1), a cytosolic GTPase that is recruited to the outer mitochondrial membrane and facilitates organelle constriction and separation. In the context of cerebral ischemia, exacerbated activation of mitochondrial fission has been associated with mitochondrial fragmentation and constitutes an early event preceding neuronal death.

In contrast, mitochondrial fusion directly regulates mitochondrial metabolism and contributes to maintaining the functional integrity of the organelle. This process is controlled by mitofusins 1 and 2 (Mfn1 and Mfn2), located in the outer mitochondrial membrane and characterized by the presence of GTPase domains at their N-terminals, as well as by optic atrophy protein 1 (OPA1), located in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The coordinated action of these proteins allows the remodeling of mitochondrial cristae and the fusion of mitochondrial membranes, promoting bioenergetic efficiency and cell survival. The imbalance between fission and fusion is therefore currently recognized as a determining factor in the progression of neuronal damage following an ischemic event (Flippo and Strack, 2017, Huang et al., 2011, Kuzmicic et al., 2011, Westermann, 2008, Yang et al., 2018).

Inflammation

Inflammation is an essential mechanism in the pathological development of cerebral ischemia. The inflammatory response that follows ischemia is triggered by the activation of glial cells, the infiltration of peripheral leukocytes, and the presence of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1), nucleic acid fragments, nucleotides, and purines. In addition, acute systemic inflammatory stimulation worsens the prognosis of ischemic stroke, with the proinflammatory cytokine interleukin−1β (IL-1β) being a crucial mediator.

Recent research has revealed new functions of mitochondria in the regulation of inflammatory responses. Mitochondria are the main modulators of the activation of the NLR family protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, which contains the pyrin domain. The outer membrane of mitochondria acts as a platform for inflammasome assembly, activating innate immunity and pyroptosis through various proinflammatory cytokines and caspase−1. Critically, mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) generated during ischemia-reperfusion act as a primary trigger for NLRP3 inflammasome assembly. In the context of cerebral ischemia, the NLRP3 inflammasome is predominantly expressed and activated in microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, whose immune microenvironment and regulatory thresholds differ substantially from those of peripheral macrophages. The direct role of NLRP3 activation in ischemic stroke, driven by mtROS and mitochondrial dysfunction, has been extensively documented (Matheoud et al., 2016, Mills and O’Neill, 2016, Strowig et al., 2012, Yang et al., 2018, Alishahi et al., 2019, Long et al., 2023).

During reperfusion, oxygen supply is restored, which is essential for preserving neuronal viability. However, both the pro-oxidant enzyme system and mitochondria can use oxygen as a substrate to produce significant amounts of oxygen free radicals during this process (Chen et al., 2011).

Mitochondrial dysfunction in ischemic infarction

Cerebral ischemia begins with the depletion of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), accompanied by alterations in the ionic homeostasis of sodium, potassium, and calcium, as well as an increase in intracellular lactate, leading to metabolic acidosis. As a result of this primary failure, ROS production and accumulation increase, water accumulates in the intracellular space, and proteolysis is activated, compromising cellular integrity. The loss of electrical potential in the neuronal membrane during ischemia leads to a condition called excitotoxicity, in which glutamate release increases.

In this context, glutamate (the main excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system) is released in an uncontrolled manner and massively activates its postsynaptic receptors, which participate in 30–40% of all synapses in the central nervous system (Andrabi et al., 2020). This neurotransmitter acts through three families of receptors: α-amino−3-hydroxy−5-methyl−4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA), N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA), and kainate receptors.

During cerebral ischemia, NMDA receptors are predominantly activated, as they are stimulated by the combination of glutamate and glycine. It is believed that AMPA and metabotropic glutamate receptors also play a role, as the activation of these receptors depolarizes the membrane and increases calcium flux (Andrabi et al., 2020).

As a direct consequence of this synaptic dysregulation, the massive influx of sodium interferes with the normal glutamate absorption mechanisms of transporter astrocytes and causes an increase in its release. This leads to excessive accumulation and uninterrupted stimulation, causing cerebral edema and the activation of apoptosis, whereby the mitochondria absorb the excess calcium, becoming inflamed and further stimulating the apoptotic pathways (Andrabi et al., 2020).

On the other hand, NMDA receptors stimulate the production of nitric oxide (NO), a free radical generated by the activation of nitric oxide synthase (NOS).

During ischemia and reperfusion, activation of neuronal NOS (nNOS) and inducible NOS (iNOS) leads to excessive NO production, which contributes significantly to brain injury. NO reacts rapidly with superoxide anion to form peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻), a highly reactive species capable of inducing single-strand breaks in DNA after the ischemic event. After the ischemic event, peroxynitrite levels increase markedly in cerebral microvessels and astrocytic processes, activating DNA repair mechanisms, altering the integrity of the blood-brain barrier, and promoting the activation of matrix metalloproteinases such as MMP−9 (Gürsoy-Ozdemir et al., 2004), which contributes to vascular and neuronal damage.

In more advanced stages of damage, when there is a rupture of the plasma membrane, ion exchange occurs uncontrollably and massively, causing mitochondrial failure due to the accumulation of calcium in the intracellular space and ROS. The mitochondrial membrane becomes permeable, consequently releasing apoptosis initiators and damaging DNA. Metabolic failure leads to ATP depletion, preventing nuclear condensation and degrading DNA. In the absence of ATP, apoptosis does not occur, but rather necrosis, which becomes the determining factor, since the formation of the apoptosome depends on it.

From a bioenergetic perspective, the electron transport chain (ETC), located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, is essential for ATP production and consists of four main complexes (Eo et al., 2024). During ischemia, the interruption of blood flow compromises the supply of oxygen and nutrients necessary for mitochondrial function, which alters oxidative phosphorylation and drastically reduces ATP production. This malfunction of the ETC promotes electron leakage and ROS generation, a phenomenon that intensifies significantly during the reperfusion phase.

In this scenario, mitochondria become the main source of ROS production, which increases significantly after reperfusion injury, damaging cellular macromolecules and activating processes ranging from alterations in cellular signaling pathways to brain cell death. Mitochondria, as a source of ROS, transform molecular oxygen into superoxide (O2−) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). These reactions include oxidative phosphorylation and other enzymatic reactions, such as those catalyzed by xanthine oxidase (XO), nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NAD(P)H) oxidase, cyclooxygenases (COX), and the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids. The ETC is the main source of O2− generation, while mitochondria reduce O2 to H2O via cytochrome c oxidase during oxidative phosphorylation. There are several sites in mitochondria that partially reduce oxygen to produce ROS, with the ubiquinone-cytochrome c site being a significant source of ROS during ischemia (Andrabi et al., 2020).

Finally, succinate accumulated during ischemia will rapidly reoxidize, leading to significant ROS formation, especially after reperfusion due to reverse electron transport in complex I. This excess of free radicals includes hydroxyl (OH)−, O2−, H2O2, NO, and peroxynitrite (OONO−) radicals. The OH free radical is another molecule generated from H2O2 in the presence of ferrous iron reduced by O2−·H2O2 accumulates through the dismutation of O2− or the direct reduction of oxygen and is highly soluble in lipids. These highly reactive free radicals not only damage macromolecules, but also activate cellular pathways that cause ischemic damage in the brain (Andrabi et al., 2020).

Taken together, the processes described throughout this section highlight that mitochondrial dysfunction is central to the pathophysiology of ischemic stroke. The loss of energy metabolism, glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity, calcium overload, exacerbated production of reactive oxygen species, and activation of inflammatory pathways converge in a progressive collapse of mitochondrial function, leading to the activation of cell death mechanisms. Likewise, the dynamic interaction between fission, fusion, and mitophagy determines whether neurons manage to maintain their viability or progress toward apoptosis or necrosis. Necrosis and apoptosis

As a direct consequence of mitochondrial dysfunction and energy collapse induced by cerebral ischemia, cell death during ischemic stroke can occur mainly through two mechanisms: apoptosis or necrosis. The activation of one or the other pathway depends largely on the severity of the damage, the intracellular availability of ATP, and the location of the neurons within the affected tissue. Under conditions of severe oxidative damage and ionic dysregulation, cells can enter a process of necrosis, characterized by rapid and unregulated progression of cell death. During this process, the cellular chromatin compacts and the endoplasmic reticulum dilates, allowing the ribosomes to spread. Subsequently, the plasma and organelle membranes rupture, releasing their contents into the intracellular medium and stimulating the process of cerebral inflammation.

In contrast, apoptosis is known as programmed cell death, so its process is regulated and allows for the orderly removal of damaged cells without inducing significant inflammation.

In the initial stages of apoptosis, chromatin condenses, as does cytoplasmic content, while organelles and mitochondria remain intact. Subsequently, the nucleus fragments and cellular content is packaged into membrane-bound apoptotic bodies, allowing phagocytosis by macrophages.

Apoptotic signaling can be activated through multiple converging pathways, including: a) mitochondrial membrane permeability (MOMP) and cytochrome C release, b) membrane-bound death receptor (Fas) pathway, and c) endoplasmic reticulum stress through the accumulation of misfolded proteins. These mechanisms lead to the activation of caspases and, therefore, cell death. In the brain, the regions most susceptible to cell death by apoptosis are CA1 and CA4 in the hippocampus, the striatum, and cortical zones 2 and 5.

The permeabilization of the mitochondrial outer membrane (MOMP) is a central event in apoptosis. This process is mediated by the proapoptotic protein BAX, which is found in the cytoplasm but, when activated, oligomerizes in the mitochondrial outer membrane.

As a result, cytochrome C is expelled into the cytoplasm, where it participates in the formation of the apoptosome and subsequently initiates caspase activation (Bloch et al., 2021, Guo et al., 2023, Kuwana et al., 2020, Zhang et al., 2022). However, during necrosis, the process is different and more aggressive for the cells. In the absence of sufficient ATP for apoptosome formation, cell death occurs via this pathway. In focal ischemic stroke (IS), the tissue suffers necrosis at the center of the lesion, and cells in the penumbra zone undergo rapid death (Andrabi et al., 2020).

In order to integrate the molecular events that lead to neuronal death after an ischemic event, it is essential to consider the central role of mitochondria as a regulatory node between cell survival and death. The disruption of the balance between mitochondrial fission and fusion, together with calcium overload and exacerbated production of reactive oxygen species, favors the permeabilization of the mitochondrial membrane and the activation of apoptotic or necrotic pathways depending on the cellular energy state. In this context, the transition from an adaptive mitochondrial response to irreversible bioenergetic collapse determines the final fate of neurons in the infarct core and ischemic penumbra. Fig. 2 summarizes these processes, highlighting the key differences between healthy and ischemic neurons, as well as the sequence of mitochondrial events that culminate in apoptosis or necrosis.

During IS, accelerated ATP consumption occurs due to DNA repair enzymes, such as poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP), and neuronal depolarization due to excitotoxicity. As a result, there is insufficient ATP to carry out apoptosis in the infarct nuclei, causing the neurons to undergo necrosis. Ischemic stroke is characterized by a necrotic core surrounded by an area known as the ischemic penumbra. The tissue in this region is functional but damaged, and the neurons in this region are viable but may undergo apoptosis hours or days after the ischemic event, making them a therapeutic target for limiting post-stroke disability (Ginsberg, 1997, Yang et al., 2018).

Impact of oxidative stress on mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondria have traditionally been referred to as the “powerhouses” of the cell due to their essential role in the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through cellular respiration. However, in addition to their bioenergetic role, these organelles are actively involved in key cellular processes, including amino acid oxidation, redox state regulation, calcium metabolism, and the activation of apoptosis pathways. These integrated functions make mitochondria a central regulator of cellular homeostasis and, therefore, a highly relevant therapeutic target in the context of ischemic stroke.

In line with what has been discussed in previous sections, mitochondrial dysfunction during IS is closely associated with increased oxidative stress, ATP depletion, and loss of calcium homeostasis. Mitochondria are the main organelles responsible for controlling intracellular calcium; however, under ischemic conditions, the overload of this ion compromises their functionality. This imbalance promotes the activation of cell death pathways, facilitating the progression of neurons toward apoptosis, a process that represents a key target for therapeutic intervention (Montes de Oca Balderas, 2021, Stockburger et al., 2018).

From a redox perspective, mitochondria are one of the main intracellular sources of ROS (Li and Yang, 2016). During the ischemia-reperfusion process, exacerbated ROS production promotes lipid peroxidation in neurons, a phenomenon widely documented in ischemic brain damage (Guo et al., 2023, Su et al., 2019). Since mitochondria have a high content of polyunsaturated fatty acids, they are susceptible to peroxidation. However, they are not the main target, as mitochondrial membrane proteins will be damaged first.

As a result of these processes, highly reactive byproducts are generated, such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), whose concentration increases significantly after ischemic injury to the brain (Lee et al., 2012, Zhai et al., 2021). This reactive aldehyde not only amplifies oxidative damage, but also alters mitochondrial function and cell signaling, contributing to the deterioration of neuronal bioenergetics (Markesbery and Lovell, 2007, Reed, 2011, Uchida, 2003). Overall, mitochondrial dysfunction is the result of oxidative stress, ATP depletion, and homeostasis failure due to excess calcium within the cell. The reperfusion process promotes the production of mitochondrial ROS and calcium, triggering cell death pathways through caspase-dependent or independent processes (Andrabi et al., 2020) and consolidating mitochondrial oxidative stress as a central axis in the progression of neuronal damage.

Mitochondrial therapeutic strategies

Given the central role of mitochondria in the pathophysiology of ischemic stroke, various therapeutic strategies have been explored with the aim of preserving their function, mitigating oxidative damage, and limiting the progression of neuronal death. These approaches seek to modulate key processes such as reactive oxygen species production, inflammation, lipid metabolism, and vascular homeostasis, all of which are closely interrelated at the mitochondrial level. In this context, nitric oxide (NO) has been shown to have dual effects on ischemic brain damage. While its excessive production by neuronal and inducible isoforms of nitric oxide synthase contributes to nitrosative stress and tissue damage, NO production at the endothelial level has been associated with neuroprotective effects after ischemia (Gürsoy-Ozdemir et al., 2004, Huang et al., 1994). In models of cerebral ischemia-reperfusion, where increased CBF and decreased leukocyte invasion have been attributed, in part, to the vasodilatory and anti-inflammatory action of endothelial-derived NO, contributing to the preservation of viable brain tissue (Gürsoy-Ozdemir et al., 2004, Iadecola and Anrather, 2011).

Complementarily, mitochondrial lipid metabolism has emerged as a relevant therapeutic target in ischemic stroke. Fatty acid-binding proteins (FABPs) act as chaperones that participate in and improve the transport, solubility, and metabolism of fatty acids, while also participating in lipid metabolism, gene regulation, signaling, proliferation, and cell differentiation. The FABP family consists of at least 12 subgroups, of which FABP3, FABP5, and FABP7 are expressed in the brain and are therefore associated with neuronal damage and neurodegenerative disorders (Guo et al., 2022, Guo et al., 2023, Kawahata and Fukunaga, 2022). Their specific role in ischemic stroke is unclear, making them therapeutic targets for modulating inflammation in brain tissue. One study revealed that inhibiting FABP7 with a high-affinity inhibitor in mice with transient occlusion of the middle cerebral artery rescued the lesion caused by the ischemia-reperfusion process by modulating the inflammatory response (Guo et al., 2021).

Although the specific role of FABPs in IS is not yet fully understood, recent evidence suggests that their modulation may significantly influence the cerebral inflammatory response. In particular, pharmacological inhibition of FABP7 using a high-affinity inhibitor in mice subjected to transient occlusion of the middle cerebral artery has been shown to attenuate ischemia–reperfusion-induced injury, mainly through modulation of the inflammatory response, positioning these proteins as promising therapeutic targets (Guo et al., 2021). Finally, the activation of endogenous defense mechanisms, such as ROS detoxification and mitochondrial biogenesis, is a key strategy for limiting acute ischemic damage. These processes contribute to restoring mitochondrial function, improving bioenergetic efficiency, and promoting neuronal survival, establishing themselves as essential components of protective responses to cerebral ischemia (Chen et al., 2010).

Mitochondrial protection and antioxidant components

In the context of cerebral ischemic damage, the preservation of mitochondrial function represents a key strategy for limiting the progression of neuronal death. Among the cellular protection mechanisms, mitophagy plays a fundamental role by allowing the selective clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria during the ischemia-reperfusion process, inhibiting subsequent apoptosis. However, when this process is activated excessively or in a deregulated manner in neurons affected by cerebral ischemia, it can accelerate mitochondrial loss and compromise cell viability (Zhang et al., 2013).

In addition to mitophagy, endogenous protective mechanisms can be stimulated. These include inhibitor of apoptosis proteins (IAPs), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, all of which can prevent the activation of apoptotic pathways.

IAPs are a family of proteins that possess a domain of approximately 70 amino acids called the baculoviral IAP repeat. These proteins have been shown to act as endogenous inhibitors of apoptosis by blocking the cleavage of procaspases and suppressing the activity of active caspases (Deveraux and Reed, 1999). Six apoptosis inhibitor proteins (IAPs) have been identified in humans, including XIAP, NAIP, c-IAP1, c-IAP2, Survivin, and Livin. In particular, XIAP, which is the most researched of the IAPs, binds to caspases−3, −7, and −9 to inhibit their activities and prevent apoptosis (Silke and Vince, 2017). It should be noted that in addition to their anti-apoptotic role, IAPs also participate in the regulation of other types of cell death, such as necroptosis and pyroptosis.

Complementarily, the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway constitutes one of the main pro-survival axes in neurons subjected to ischemic stress. It has been discovered that the enzyme Akt (serine-threonine protein kinase) directly activates FOXO transcription factors and inhibits their function to promote the expression of genes involved in cell death, such as FasL and Bim. In addition, Akt also phosphorylates BAD, a protein of the Bcl−2 family related to apoptosis, to prevent BAD-induced cell death. During ischemic stroke, overexpression of superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) increases Akt activity to protect against neuronal death (Yang et al., 2018). The PI3K-Akt signaling pathway is a promising target for the development of neuroprotective drugs for ischemic stroke.

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) are emerging as key regulators of the inflammatory and oxidative response. These ligand-regulated transcription factors control cell proliferation, differentiation, glucose and lipid metabolism, as well as programmed cell death processes.

PPARs have been shown to have beneficial effects in inflammatory diseases by modulating the expression of adhesion molecules and cytokine production, interfering with the transactivation capacity of transcription factors such as STAT, AP−1, and NF-κB. It is known that PPAR γ activation can mitigate the inflammatory response and cellular damage after an ischemic event (Yang et al., 2018). Another central axis of mitochondrial protection is the regulation of mitochondrial biogenesis and intramitochondrial antioxidant systems. In this regard, the activation or increased expression of the transcriptional coactivator PGC−1α could be a strategy to counteract the problems of mitochondrial dysfunction in neurons, suggesting that any substance that activates PGC−1α could be beneficial in neurodegenerative diseases where damage caused by oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction play a key role in pathogenesis (Wareski et al., 2009).

Activation of PGC−1α regulates the expression of key proteins such as uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2) and mitochondrial superoxide dismutase (SOD2), which are vital proteins that play a fundamental role in protecting against cellular damage and neuronal death by regulating ROS generation in mitochondria.

Increasing UCP2 expression has been shown to reduce ROS production and neuron loss in the region of the brain affected by ischemia, highlighting the various neuroprotective properties of UCP2 against ischemic brain injury (Lennon et al., 1991). PGC−1α regulation modulates UPC2 and SOD2 expression and protects neurons in cerebral ischemia (Yang et al., 2018).

Additionally, modulation of mitochondrial dynamics represents another relevant strategy for neuronal protection. It has been proposed that regulation of mitochondrial fission can block the release of cytochrome C and delay the process of neuronal death (Suen et al., 2008). In this regard, antioxidants such as vitamin E and compounds specifically targeting mitochondria, such as MITOQ, have been shown to reduce mitochondrial fragmentation, elimination, or reduction of Drp1 expression. As a result, ROS production is decreased and oxidative stress does not occur, contributing to the preservation of mitochondrial function and neuronal survival (Yang et al., 2018).

Mitochondrial biogenesis and dynamics

In response to energy and oxidative damage induced by cerebral ischemia, mitochondrial biogenesis emerges as a fundamental adaptive mechanism for restoring bioenergetic capacity and preserving cell viability. This complex and fascinating process involves the formation of new mitochondria within cells, as well as the improvement of their functionality, to respond to the energy needs of the cell and environmental signals (Cardanho-Ramos & Morais, 2021).

The purpose of all this is to maintain the cell in homeostasis and keep it alive. To do this, mitochondrial biogenesis is induced through the activation of cell signaling systems and metabolic pathways, which include energy-sensing enzymes such as Adenosine Monophosphate-Activated Protein Kinase (AMPK), the protein SIRT1 (SIRT1), ROS, Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF), as well as regulatory genes such as Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma Coactivator 1α (PGC−1a), Transcription Factor A, Mitochondrial (TFAM), and the transcription factor Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2-Related Factor 2 (Nrf2) (Cardanho-Ramos and Morais, 2021, Vásquez-Trincado et al., 2016, Yu et al., 2021).

In this sense, AMPK is a cellular energy-sensing enzyme that is activated when adenosine monophosphate (AMP) levels are high and ATP levels are low. In response to this, a series of signaling cascades are initiated that promote mitochondrial biogenesis. SIRT1 is a histone deacetylase, which means that it removes acetyl groups from histones, the proteins that wrap around DNA, thereby increasing the expression of genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis.

Although ROS are traditionally considered harmful agents because they are unstable oxygen-containing molecules that can damage cells, they are produced as a normal byproduct of mitochondrial respiration. Moderate levels of these species can act as intracellular signals that induce adaptive responses, including the activation of mitochondrial biogenesis. However, high levels of ROS can damage DNA and other cellular molecules, which can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction. In parallel, BDNF, a protein that plays an important role in the survival and growth of neurons, increases the expression of genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis and improves mitochondrial function (Del Giudice et al., 2022, Luo et al., 2019).

For its part, the regulatory gene PGC−1a is a transcriptional coactivator that binds to other transcription factors and increases the expression of genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis. TFAM is a transcription factor that binds to mitochondrial DNA and regulates the expression of mitochondrial genes. Nrf2 is a transcription factor that regulates the expression of antioxidant and DNA repair genes. Activation of Nrf2 can help protect mitochondria from oxidative damage (Del Giudice et al., 2022, Luo et al., 2019). Based on these mechanisms, various therapeutic strategies have been proposed to improve mitochondrial biogenesis and restore mitochondrial function. Among the most relevant is the activation of PGC−1α, AMPK, and SIRT1 using the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC−1α pathway, which several studies have suggested is the most appropriate therapeutic target for neuroprotection in patients with high levels of ROS, calcium, and apoptosis in their cells (Chen et al., 2023, Rakshe et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2021). Likewise, mTOR modulation for the improvement of mitochondrial biogenesis (Rakshe et al., 2024).

To fully understand the therapeutic potential of these pathways, it is necessary to consider the functional interaction between their components. SIRT1 is an NAD + -dependent deacetylase that is activated in response to energy and oxidative stress and is involved in the regulation of longevity, stress response, and metabolism. AMPK is an enzyme that is activated in response to an increase in the AMP/ATP ratio, indicating low energy levels in the cell. Its activation promotes ATP-generating processes, such as fatty acid oxidation and glucose uptake, while inhibiting ATP-consuming processes, such as fatty acid and protein synthesis. PGC−1α is a transcriptional coactivator that regulates the expression of genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis, energy metabolism, and antioxidant defense. Finally, mTOR is a kinase that regulates cell growth, proliferation, and survival in response to nutrients, growth factors, and energy (Luo et al., 2019, Rakshe et al., 2024). These mechanisms are summarized schematically in Fig. 3.

SIRT1 and AMPK can synergistically activate PGC−1α, further enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis. SIRT1 deacetylates and activates PGC−1α, enhancing its ability to promote mitochondrial biogenesis, while AMPK phosphorylates and activates PGC−1α, a transcriptional coactivator that regulates genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis. It also inhibits the mTOR pathway, the inhibition of which activates autophagy processes, eliminating damaged mitochondria and promoting mitochondrial biogenesis through the activation of AMPK and PGC−1α, favoring autophagy and the elimination of damaged mitochondria. Once activated, PGC−1α activates the expression of genes that encode proteins of the mitochondrial respiratory chain and other components essential for mitochondrial function, increasing the production of new mitochondria in response to the cell's energy needs.

Compounds and interventions that promote mitochondrial fusion, fission, and turnover

Mitochondrial dynamics, understood as the coordinated balance between the processes of mitochondrial fusion, fission, and turnover (mitophagy), plays a fundamental role in maintaining mitochondrial function and neuronal survival. Therapeutic modulation of these processes has emerged as a promising strategy to mitigate mitochondrial damage induced by cerebral ischemia and limit the progression of neuronal death.

Mitochondrial fusion is the process by which two mitochondria fuse to form a single functional unit (including the membrane and its contents). This mechanism allows for the dilution of damaged components, compensation for bioenergetic defects, and optimization of mitochondrial function. The main proteins involved in mitochondrial fusion are mitofusins 1 and 2 (MFN1 and MFN2) located in the outer mitochondrial membrane, as well as OPA1 proteins, responsible for inner membrane fusion and mitochondrial cristae remodeling.

Various compounds and interventions have been shown to promote mitochondrial fusion. Among these are:

Mdivi−1, initially identified as an inhibitor of mitochondrial fission, has been found to promote mitochondrial fusion under certain conditions by reducing excessive fragmentation.

Omega−3 fatty acids: These fatty acids can improve mitochondrial function and promote fusion, possibly through the regulation of MFN1 and MFN2 proteins (Sabouny and Shutt, 2020).

Resveratrol is a polyphenolic compound found in grapes and red wine that has been shown to improve mitochondrial fusion and overall mitochondrial (Adebayo et al., 2021)function, in part through the activation of pathways associated with SIRT1 and PGC−1α (Van der Bliek et al., 2013).

MitoQ is a targeted antioxidant that specifically accumulates in mitochondria. MitoQ has been shown to protect mitochondria from ischemic damage and improve neurological function in animal models of stroke (Adebayo et al., 2021).

Mitochondrial fission, on the other hand, is the process by which a mitochondrion divides into two independent organelles. This process is essential for the segregation of damaged mitochondria and the equitable distribution of mitochondria during cell division. The proteins Drp1 and Fis1 play crucial roles in mitochondrial fission, whose activation promotes mitochondrial fragmentation.

Pharmacological modulation of mitochondrial fission has been explored as a strategy to control ischemic damage, some of which include the following (Adebayo et al., 2021, Sabouny and Shutt, 2020).

· Mdivi−1: Although it can promote fusion under some conditions, in most studies it has been identified as an inhibitor of Drp1, the main protein in mitochondrial fission. However, at some concentrations or in some contexts, it can induce fission (Zerihun et al., 2023).

· Dynamin-Related Protein 1 (Drp1): Overexpression or activation of Drp1 directly promotes mitochondrial fission. (Van der Bliek et al., 2013).

· Ursolic acid, a natural compound found in the skin of apples and other fruits, can induce mitochondrial fission through activation of the Drp1 pathway (Adebayo et al., 2021, Van der Bliek et al., 2013).

· PINK1 and Parkin, PINK1 is an outer mitochondrial membrane protein that detects damaged mitochondria. Parkin is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that is recruited to mitochondria damaged by PINK1 and tags mitochondrial proteins for degradation by autophagy. Activation of the PINK1-Parkin pathway can promote mitochondrial fission and the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria (Adebayo et al., 2021).

Mitochondrial turnover, also known as mitophagy, is the process of selective degradation of damaged mitochondria by autophagy. This process is vital for the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria and the prevention of cellular damage. The proteins PINK1 and Parkin play a central role in the detection, signaling, and execution of this process, especially under conditions of mitochondrial stress. Several agents have been shown to promote mitophagy and improve mitochondrial quality (Li et al., 2022). These include:

Rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor, which activates autophagy in general and can promote mitophagy by reducing mTOR signaling (Li et al., 2018).

· Urolithin A: A microbial metabolite of ellagitannin, found in pomegranates, which has been shown to increase mitophagy and improve mitochondrial health (D’Amico et al., 2021).

· Metformin: A drug commonly used for type 2 diabetes, which can induce mitophagy through the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) (Van et al., 2023).

Modulation of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP)

The mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) is a multiprotein complex located in the inner mitochondrial membrane that plays a crucial role in regulating mitochondrial homeostasis. Under physiological conditions, the mPTP remains closed, allowing the maintenance of mitochondrial membrane potential and efficient ATP production. However, its sustained opening is a critical event in mitochondrial dysfunction, especially in pathological conditions such as ischemic stroke, where blood supply to a region of the brain is severely reduced or interrupted (Langston, 2017).

Mechanism of the mPTP and its involvement in ischemic stroke

During an ischemic event, oxygen and nutrient deprivation leads to an energy collapse characterized by ATP depletion, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS), and intracellular calcium overload. This pathological environment favors the opening of the mPTP, which can be induced by multiple converging factors, including increased mitochondrial Ca²⁺, oxidative stress, and inorganic phosphate accumulation (Bertero et al., 2024).

The opening of the mPTP causes the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, organelle swelling, and disruption of oxidative phosphorylation. As a result, a series of proapoptotic factors, including cytochrome c, are released into the cytoplasm, activating the caspase cascade and leading to neuronal apoptosis. Simultaneously, the inability of mitochondria to synthesize ATP compromises cell viability and promotes progression to necrosis, particularly in regions of the brain with severe energy damage.

In the context of ischemic stroke, prolonged opening of the mPTP acts as a point of no return in the cell death cascade, integrating signals of oxidative stress, bioenergetic dysfunction, and mitochondrial damage. The resulting necrosis leads to the massive release of cellular contents into the extracellular space, triggering a local inflammatory response that amplifies tissue damage and aggravates neuronal dysfunction. Thus, the mPTP is positioned as a key regulator in the transition between apoptosis and necrosis following the ischemic event (Fig. 4) (Bertero et al., 2024, Langston, 2017).

The mechanism of cerebral ischemia induces disruption of ionic homeostasis, generating cytosolic Ca²⁺ overload and oxidative stress due to ROS accumulation. These factors activate the opening of the mPTP in the inner mitochondrial membrane, causing the dissociation of the mitochondrial electrochemical gradient, bioenergetic collapse, and release of cytochrome c (CYT C) into the cytosol. This cascade activates the intrinsic pathway of apoptosis through the activation of caspases, promoting neuronal death. At the same time, mitochondrial dysfunction aggravates ischemic damage by compromising ATP production and promoting cell necrosis. Modulation of the mPTP represents a potential therapeutic target for limiting post-ischemic neurovascular damage.

From a therapeutic perspective, modulation of the mPTP represents a promising strategy for preserving mitochondrial function, limiting ATP loss, and reducing the activation of cell death pathways in ischemic stroke. Inhibiting its early opening could attenuate both apoptosis and necrosis, decreasing neuronal damage and improving post-stroke functional prognosis.

Pharmacological agents and strategies to regulate mPTP opening and prevent mitochondrial damage

Modulation of the mPTP represents a promising therapeutic strategy to mitigate mitochondrial damage and prevent cell death in ischemic stroke. Some of the most relevant approaches are described below (Mishra et al., 2019).

Among direct mPTP inhibitors, cyclosporine A (CsA) is one of the most studied compounds. Its mechanism of action is based on binding to cyclophilin D, a mPTP regulatory protein, preventing its interaction with the pore and, therefore, its opening. The administration of CsA has been shown to reduce the extent of brain damage and improve functional recovery in animal models of ischemic stroke (Mishra et al., 2019).

Similar to CsA, Sanglifehrin A acts by inhibiting the mPTP through cyclophilin D, although it has a different chemical structure than CsA. This structural difference could confer pharmacological advantages, particularly in terms of toxicity and immunosuppression. Preclinical studies have shown that Sanglifehrin A can reduce ischemic damage and protect neuronal function, positioning it as a promising alternative among direct mPTP inhibitors (Clarke et al., 2002).

In addition to direct inhibitors, various natural compounds have been investigated for their ability to indirectly modulate mPTP opening. Among these, ursolic acid and oleanolic acid stand out for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties.

These compounds have been shown in preclinical studies to stabilize mitochondrial membrane potential and reduce the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which helps to limit mPTP opening and protect mitochondria from ischemic damage (Günther and Bednarczyk-Cwynar, 2026, Xu et al., 2023).

Antioxidant strategies are another relevant approach to mPTP regulation. Antioxidants such as coenzyme Q10, vitamin E, and N-acetylcysteine can reduce oxidative stress, thereby stabilizing the mPTP. The administration of antioxidants has been shown to reduce infarct size and improve neurological outcomes in animal models of stroke (Mantle and Golomb, 2025, Valero, 2014).

Along the same lines, specific inhibition of mitochondrial ROS production is a particularly attractive strategy. Mitochondria-targeted compounds such as MitoQ, an antioxidant, may offer additional protection against mPTP opening during ischemic stroke. MitoQ has shown potential in reducing mitochondrial damage and preserving cell function in experimental studies (Ibrahim et al., 2023).

On the other hand, non-pharmacological strategies such as ischemic preconditioning, which consists of brief episodes of ischemia prior to the severe ischemic event, and postconditioning, which is applied immediately after reperfusion, have been shown to induce resistance to mPTP opening. These approaches, based on controlled exposure to brief episodes of ischemia before or immediately after the severe ischemic event, activate several cytoprotective signaling pathways that modulate mitochondrial homeostasis and reduce susceptibility to mitochondrial dysfunction during reperfusion (Caricati-Neto et al., 2019).

Therapeutic potential of mitochondrial transplantation

Preclinical studies have provided compelling evidence of the efficacy of mitochondrial transplantation in animal models of ischemic stroke. For example, in studies conducted in rats, the administration of mitochondria isolated from healthy muscles directly into the ischemic brain has been shown to restore ATP production and reduce neuronal apoptosis.

These studies have shown a significant improvement in neurological function and a reduction in the size of the cerebral infarction, suggesting that transplanted mitochondria can effectively integrate into damaged tissue and resume their bioenergetic functions (Chang et al., 2019, Sun et al., 2019).

Preclinical research has revealed that transplanted mitochondria can be internalized by brain cells through endocytosis processes. Once incorporated, these mitochondria help restore energy homeostasis, decrease ROS production, and activate anti-apoptotic signaling pathways. In addition, a significant reduction in oxidative stress and inflammatory response in the infarct area has been observed, which promotes neuronal survival and improves functional recovery (McCully et al., 2022, Sun et al., 2019).

The safety of mitochondrial transplantation has also been evaluated in preclinical studies. The results have shown that this procedure does not induce significant adverse immune responses and that the transplanted mitochondria do not promote abnormal cell proliferation, which is crucial for its viability as a therapeutic treatment. These studies have established a solid basis for the transition to human clinical trials (Chang et al., 2019, McCully et al., 2022).

Beyond ischemic stroke, mitochondrial transplantation has begun to be explored in models of neurodegenerative diseases, providing additional evidence of its therapeutic potential. In this context, the study by Hyeyoon Eo et al. demonstrated that the transfer of exogenous mitochondria to dopaminergic neurons significantly improves behavioral deficits in an animal model of Parkinson's disease. This neuroprotective effect was associated with a reduction in dopaminergic neuronal damage and neuroinflammation, positioning mitochondrial transplantation as a relevant therapeutic target in pathologies characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction.

Complementarily, in the preclinical study conducted by Orrego, Miguel A. et al. in 2021, the feasibility of mitochondrial transplantation through carotid infusion was reported, highlighting both the safety and therapeutic potential of this route of administration. In this study, the authors described that mitochondrial transplantation suppressed NF-κB signaling, exerting an anti-inflammatory effect mediated by the inhibition of microglial activation and the restoration of mitochondrial function. Specifically, the treatment reduced mRNA expression and secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, as well as the density of Iba1⁺ cells in the striatum of mice treated with MPTP, a known inducer of neuronal apoptosis (Orrego et al., 2021), which, as mentioned above, activates cell apoptosis. Therefore, by transplanting to slow down this cascade, we achieve neuroprotection in patients affected by diseases such as Parkinson's disease, giving us a new therapeutic target.

They emphasize the success they achieved by isolating autologous mitochondria from the brain and theoretically how this could be beneficial, as well as a good future outlook for a possible treatment for neurological diseases. However, they stress that more preclinical studies are needed for its development (Orrego et al., 2021).

Additionally, subsequent work by the same group addressed one of the main gaps in the literature: the standardization of the mitochondrial transplantation procedure. In a porcine model, the infusion of approximately 1 × 10⁹ autologous mitochondria via the carotid artery was evaluated, demonstrating the technical feasibility and safety of the procedure in a larger mammal. This study is the first successful report of carotid infusion of autologous mitochondria into a healthy brain, providing a key step toward the clinical translation of mitochondrial transplantation (Eo et al., 2024).

Together, these findings support mitochondrial transplantation as an emerging therapeutic strategy with high potential for the treatment of ischemic stroke and other neurodegenerative diseases. The convergence of functional efficacy, preclinical safety, and methodological advances in the administration of mitochondria reinforces its position as one of the most promising therapeutic targets for pathologies characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal damage.

Challenges and future directions

Despite the promising results observed in preclinical models, there are challenges that must be addressed for the widespread clinical implementation of mitochondrial transplantation. These include optimizing methods for isolating, preserving, and transplanting mitochondria, as well as precisely defining the most appropriate dosage, administration times, and therapeutic windows. It is also essential to ensure that transplanted mitochondria not only integrate efficiently into damaged tissue but also maintain their functionality in the long term. In this regard, continued research is essential to overcome these challenges and establish mitochondrial transplantation as a viable therapeutic option for ischemic stroke.

At the same time, the effective delivery of mitochondrial function-modulating compounds to the central nervous system remains a significant obstacle due to the presence of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and the need to target these agents specifically to the mitochondria. In this regard, nanotechnological drug delivery strategies, such as nanoparticles, liposomes, and targeted transport systems, as well as gene therapy aimed at introducing genes that encode protective mitochondrial proteins or modulating the expression of mitochondrial genes, are areas of active research with high translational potential. However, these approaches also pose challenges related to possible pleiotropic side effects, derived from the involvement of mitochondrial proteins in multiple cellular processes. This obstacle is particularly relevant for mitochondrial transplantation, since exogenous mitochondria are large organelles that cannot freely diffuse across the intact BBB. Several strategies have been proposed to overcome this limitation. Intra-arterial (carotid) infusion delivers mitochondria directly into the cerebral circulation, taking advantage of the ischemia-induced increase in BBB permeability to facilitate their access to the injured parenchyma (Norat et al., 2023). Once in the extracellular space, exogenous mitochondria are internalized by recipient cells through energy-dependent mechanisms, mainly actin-dependent endocytosis and macropinocytosis, and can also be transferred indirectly via glial cells through tunneling nanotubes, extracellular vesicles, and gap junctions (Wang et al., 2026). In addition, encapsulation of mitochondria or mitochondrial components within extracellular vesicles has been explored as a means of enhancing their stability and BBB penetration (Picone and Nuzzo, 2023).

A critical aspect to consider is the need to maintain an appropriate balance between mitochondrial fusion and fission. Altering this dynamic can be counterproductive, leading to excessive mitochondrial fragmentation or the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, which could exacerbate neuronal damage rather than mitigate it. Therefore, therapeutic strategies must be designed with a precise and regulated approach, avoiding indiscriminate interventions on mitochondrial dynamics.

Additionally, individual response to therapies targeting mitochondria can vary considerably due to genetic and epigenetic factors, as well as the heterogeneity of stroke, a disease with diverse underlying causes and mechanisms. The identification of mitochondrial and treatment response biomarkers could help select patients who will benefit most from these therapies and optimize drug dosing and administration schedules.

Future research directions include the development of novel drug delivery systems and the exploration of combination therapies that integrate mitochondrial-targeted strategies with other neuroprotective approaches, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, or neurorestorative interventions. These combined strategies may generate synergistic effects and significantly improve clinical outcomes. Additionally, a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying mitochondrial dysfunction in ischemic stroke will facilitate the identification of new therapeutic targets and the design of more specific and effective interventions.

Overall, addressing these challenges will require a multidisciplinary approach that integrates advances in mitochondrial biology, nanotechnology, neuroscience, and translational medicine to translate mitochondrial-based therapies into effective clinical tools for ischemic stroke.

Conclusion

Ischemic stroke is a devastating disease that deprives the brain of oxygen and essential nutrients, triggering a cascade of pathological events, including mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and cell death, which contribute to disease progression and poor patient prognosis.

Mitochondrial targeting strategies to mitigate oxidative damage and enhance neuroprotection focus on the modulation of mitochondrial dynamics, including fusion, fission, and turnover, to maintain mitochondrial integrity and bioenergetic function.

Potential side effects due to modulation of mitochondrial dynamics must be carefully evaluated, and large-scale clinical trials are required to assess the safety and efficacy of mitochondria-focused therapies in patients with IS. Mitochondrial transplantation represents an innovative therapeutic approach with the potential to transform the treatment of ischemic stroke.

Preclinical studies have demonstrated its efficacy and safety in animal models, and early clinical trials have shown promising results in human patients. Continued research and development of mitochondria-focused therapies have the potential to transform the treatment of stroke and improve the quality of life for millions of patients worldwide.
