Analysis of whole genome sequencing and plasma metabolomics unveil genetic determinants and clinical implications for human health
Authors: Yi-Xuan Wang, Yi-Xuan Qiang, Yi-Jun Ge, Yue-Ting Deng, Bang-Sheng Wu, Liu Yang, Yi-Lin Chen, Xiao-Yu He, Yu He, Bing-Ran Yao, Chen-Jie Fei, Rui-Ying Yin, Jia You, Jian-Feng Feng, Wei Cheng, Jin-Tai Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlood metabolites are critical biomarkers linking genetic variation to diverse health outcomes, exhibiting significant variability across individuals. Here, we analyze and integrate whole-genome sequencing with plasma metabolomics to investigate the genetic architecture of 313 metabolic biomarkers in up to 199,138 UK Biobank participants. Through single-variant analyses, we identify 36,105 independent signals, with 22.20% being novel. Furthermore, we pinpoint 12,361 putative causal variant-trait associations, demonstrating enhanced causal signal discovery and improved fine-mapping resolution compared with imputed array-based approaches. Rare-variant aggregate testing reveals 1,527 conditionally independent protein-coding gene-trait pairs, with 32.9% being unique to non-coding regions. We estimate the heritability at a median of h2 = 0.31, nearly tripling the heritability estimates obtained from array-based approaches. Integrating our findings with disease genetics reveals 245 potential causal associations, such as that between omega-3 fatty acids and cholelithiasis risk. Prioritizing proteins with concordant effects on metabolites and clinical outcomes revealed 410 potential targets, offering opportunities for therapeutic strategies and drug repurposing. Our open-access resource (https://metabolome-whole-genome-landscape.com/) provides a foundation for future research into metabolic pathways, disease mechanisms, and therapeutic development.Subject terms: Genome-wide association studies, Genetics research, Metabolomics

Introduction

Circulating metabolites are key phenotypic markers linking genetic variation to diverse health outcomes, influenced by both genetic and environmental factors1,2. Understanding the genetic architecture of the blood metabolome could provide valuable insights into metabolic regulation and disease pathogenesis. Previous genome-wide association studies (GWASs) and whole-exome sequencing (WES) have identified numerous genetic loci associated with blood metabolites3–14. However, most metabolomics GWASs have relied on imputed genotyping arrays, which primarily capture common variants, leaving rare variants insufficiently and inaccurately characterized. While WES has made significant strides in sequencing depth, it is limited to exonic regions, covering only 2–3% of the genome15. Emerging evidence underscores the substantial regulatory effects of noncoding variants, which contribute significantly to trait heritability16. Therefore, it is crucial to comprehensively investigate the genetic architecture of the metabolome within the context of the entire genome, as a critical step toward elucidating disease mechanisms and advancing precision medicine.

The advancement of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) technologies has enabled comprehensive interrogation of the entire genome, facilitating the identification of genetic determinants underlying complex human traits17–19. However, few studies have comprehensively investigated the genetic architecture of the blood metabolome at scale and from the perspective of the whole genome, leaving key questions unresolved. For example, can a large-scale WGS study provide a more comprehensive genetic landscape of blood metabolic markers and reveal additional associations? How might the whole-genome landscape help achieve the high-resolution fine-mapping of functional variants, and would those functional variants deepen the understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying the metabolic changes and pathways? Furthermore, could these genetic insights enhance causal inferences between metabolites and diseases, thereby pinpointing promising therapeutic targets? The availability of WGS data from nearly 200,000 individuals in the UK Biobank (UKB)15, combined with metabolomics and enriched phenotypic information, provides an unparalleled opportunity to answer these questions.

Here, we present a comprehensive genetic landscape of the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based plasma metabolome in 199,138 UKB individuals across five ancestry groups. We analyzed 249 metabolic measures assessed by Nightingale Health and 64 biologically plausible ratios, predominantly comprising lipids and lipoproteins. We employed a two-stage genome-wide association meta-analysis, conducting single-variant analyses and rare-variant aggregate-based tests to elucidate the genetic architecture of plasma metabolites (Fig. 1). We offer insights into ketone body metabolism pathway and inborn errors of metabolism (IEMs). The integrative analytical framework, combining multi-omics data through colocalization, Mendelian Randomization (MR), and druggability assessment analyses, provided insights into metabolite-disease associations and guided therapeutic target discovery. Our open-access platform (https://metabolome-whole-genome-landscape.com/) provides a valuable resource for advancing the understanding of metabolic genetics and its implications for human health and drug discovery.

Overview of the study design.

This study leverages WGS and metabolic data from 199,138 UKB participants across five ancestry groups, focusing on 313 plasma metabolic biomarkers. The primary analysis was confined to individuals of confirmed European ancestry. We employed a two-stage genome-wide association meta-analysis, encompassing single variant and rare-variant aggregate-based analyses. Non-European mQTL mapping facilitates cross-ancestry comparisons and identifies ancestry-specific associations. WGS-based heritability estimates surpass those derived from imputed arrays. Genetic findings were followed by in-depth exploration of biological and clinical insights, including biological pathways, inborn errors of metabolism, disease-associated metabolites, and drug-target prioritization. Results

Cohort characteristics and phenotypes

A total of 199,138 UKB samples that met our quality control (QC) criteria were included in the study (Methods). Of these, 191,681 participants (96.26%) were of European ancestry, 3296 (1.66%) of African (AFR) ancestry, 138 (0.07%) of admixed American (AMR) ancestry, 175 (0.09%) of East Asian (EAS) ancestry, and 3848 (1.93%) of South Asian (SAS) ancestry. The study population had a median age of 58 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 51–63) with 105,726 females (53.09%). The median fasting time before blood collection was 3 h (IQR: 2–4 h). Detailed population characteristics are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Plasma levels of 249 metabolic biomarkers utilized in this study were measured from EDTA plasma samples using NMR-based profiling platform developed by Nightingale Health Ltd20. To enhance the analytical depth of our study, we additionally included 64 metabolite ratios from pairs with shared enzymatic or transport proteins, as annotated in HMDB21. In total, the 313 metabolic traits were classified into ten groups: amino acids (n = 10), apolipoproteins (n = 3), cholesterol (n = 15), fatty acids (n = 18), fluid balance (n = 2), glycolysis-related metabolites (n = 4), inflammation markers (n = 1), ketone bodies (n = 4), lipoproteins and lipids (n = 192), and derived biological plausible ratios (n = 64) (Supplementary Data 2).

Individual variant associations with plasma metabolome

Single variant association tests were performed for each of the 313 metabolic traits (Supplementary Data 3). The primary analysis included up to 191,681 individuals of confirmed European ancestry (Methods). After rigorous variant filtering and QC, our meta-analysis incorporated up to 35,776,728 SNPs and short insertions/deletions (INDELs), comprising 7,726,466 common variants (minor allele frequency [MAF] ≥ 1%) and 28,050,262 rare variants (MAF < 1%).

We split the European population into a discovery set and a validation set, and the association signals underwent a two-step selection process. Overall, the discovery analysis yielded 2,072,685 significant primary associations, of which 966,153 were validated (Methods; Fig. 2a). Effect sizes were well-aligned between the discovery and validation sets (r = 0.99; Fig. 2b). As expected, variants with lower MAF exhibited larger effect sizes (Fig. 2c), indicating that rare variants may have greater clinical relevance18,22–24. Genomic inflation was well controlled, with a median genomic inflation factor (λ) of 1.04 (range: 1.01–1.09) (Supplementary Data 4). After clumping, we identified 36,105 lead signals across 268 independent loci (Supplementary Data 5 and 6). These metabolite quantitative trait loci (mQTLs) were predominantly located in the non-coding regions, with only 4.08% corresponding to protein-truncating variants (PTVs), missense, in-frame, or synonymous variants (Supplementary Fig. 1a, 1b). Notably, seven loci were associated with more than 200 metabolic traits, and the most pleiotropic locus, located at 19q13.3, harbored 5483 lead signals for 245 metabolic traits (Supplementary Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data 7). We observed a median of 28 associated loci per trait, with 38 traits having at least 40 associated loci (Supplementary Fig. 1d). To assess the robustness of the meta-analysis, we conducted a joint analysis by pooling the discovery and validation sets together and compared the results. We found a high correlation between the joint analysis and meta-analysis, as well as across sex, age, and statin usage subgroups (Supplementary Note).

We compared our findings against variants observed in the imputed array genetic dataset and found a high consistency in effect sizes between the shared results (Supplementary Fig. 1e and Supplementary Note). Notably, nearly half of our lead signals were absent in the array-based GWAS summary, primarily due to variants with MAF < 0.01 (Fig. 2d). The increased variant density of WGS data also enabled the detection of stronger associations, as exemplified by the 17q21.31 locus for cholesteryl esters in small high-density lipoprotein (Supplementary Fig. 1f and Supplementary Note).

Cross-referencing with previously identified mQTLs

To evaluate the consistency of our findings with prior studies and identify novel associations, we cross-referenced our findings with 22 published mQTL studies (Supplementary Data 8). We observed high concordance of effect sizes for our lead variants that were previously reported as mQTLs (median ρ = 0.99; Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Data 9 and Supplementary Note). Notably, of the 36,105 lead signals, 8015 (22.20%) were identified as novel, being independent of previously reported mQTLs (linkage disequilibrium [LD] r² < 0.8). An additional 648 (1.79%) lead signals were considered potentially novel, as their proxies had been previously reported.

We next performed external replication using summary statistics from Karjalainen et al.’s metabolomics GWAS, which is based on array-imputed data from 33 cohorts independent of our population3. We replicated 77.0% of their lead signals that were directly mapped to UKB DRAGEN WGS calls, while their study replicated 69.6% of our lead signals that were available for replication (Supplementary Data 10 and Supplementary Note). The lowest replication rates were observed for glycolysis-related traits and lipoprotein and lipid traits (Supplementary Data 11), possibly due to differences in the fasting status of the study population.

Fine-mapping of candidate causal variants

While conventional genotyping microarrays are primarily based on tag SNPs, which are often merely correlated with the unmeasured causal SNPs22, our high-density WGS data offer a valuable opportunity to prioritize genetic variants more likely to have a direct effect on metabolic traits. Across 36,105 biomarker-associated loci, we identified 34,377 distinct signals in 8072 regions spanning 313 metabolic traits (Methods; Supplementary Data 12). Among these, 2560 regions (31.7%) contained 5 or more signals. Within the credible sets, 17,254 signals (50.2%) were fine-mapped to 5 or fewer variants (Fig. 3a). Notably, 12,361 unique variant-trait associations achieved a model-averaged posterior inclusion probability (PIP) > 0.95 for causality, collectively explaining up to 17.9% of residual trait variance (glutamine by glycine ratio) (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Data 13 and 14).

We evaluated the gain in causal signal discovery and the improvement in fine-mapping resolution compared to the imputed array genetic dataset (Methods). Using the same variant inclusion criteria, fewer variants were included per region in the credible set estimation compared to the WGS fine-mapping (Fig. 3c). Compared with fine-mapping using array-imputed variants, we observed a modest increase in the number of signals per region (from a median of 2 signals to 3 signals) and a substantial decrease in the credible set length (from a median of 15 variants to 5 variants). For a more direct comparison, we obtained 6,058 matched comparable regions according to similar genetic positions (≤ 200 kb difference; Methods). In these matched regions, we consistently observed that WGS captured more signals than the imputed array data in 52.69% of the cases (with WGS capturing an equal number of signals in 41.35% of the cases) (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Data 15). Further, when comparing credible set length in 277 matched regions where only a single signal was captured in both datasets, we observed a resolution improvement in 75.81% of the cases (from a median of 89 variants to 47 variants) (Fig. 3e). The WGS fine-mapping uncovered putative causal variants missed by the imputed array. For example, fine-mapping of the LIPG locus associated with omega-3 fatty acids identified one causal variant with model-averaged PIP > 0.95 using array-imputed variants and four using WGS variants. While WGS fine-mapping confirmed array-based finding of 18:49583585:A:G as a putative causal variant, it also identified a rare missense variant, 18:49586795:C:T, which was missed by the imputed array GWAS, as the most likely causal variant with highest Bayes factor (BF) (Fig. 3f). Also, an example exemplifying the substantial decrease credible set length is the HMGCR locus associated with total lipids in lipoprotein particles. In WGS fine-mapping, we resolved the credible set to 3 variants, a significant reduction compared to the imputed array-based fine-mapping, which included 16 variants (Fig. 3g).

To determine whether WGS fine-mapping enables improved identification of putative causal variants with regulatory elements, we systematically annotated candidate causal variants derived from both WGS and array-based GWAS fine-mapping using FAVOR25. We observed that a substantially higher proportion of WGS-derived potential causal variants overlapped with super-enhancer regions (40.0%) compared to those identified from array-based fine-mapping (34.6%; P = 1.02 × 10−4, Chi-squared test), suggesting an enhanced ability of WGS to capture functionally relevant regulatory variants. To further explore the regulatory mechanisms underlying the fine-mapped putative causal variants from WGS, we evaluated their potential impact on transcription factor (TF) binding. Notably, 1,657 (71%) candidate causal variants were predicted to significantly disrupt TF binding motifs26. The most frequently affected TFs included FOXJ3 (82 variants) and REST (75 variants) (Supplementary Data 16), both of which have been associated with metabolic processes in previous studies27,28. As an example, the aforementioned candidate causal variant, 18:49586795:C:T, was predicted to strongly disrupt a binding motif for ZFX, a transcriptional activator that facilitates RNA polymerase II release and promotes transcriptional elongation29.

Non-European mQTL mapping

To assess the generalizability of our findings and identify potential ancestry-specific associations, we performed stratified single-variant testing across AFR, AMR, EAS, and SAS ancestry groups (Methods). We identified 4,640 (AFR) and 5563 (SAS) significant primary associations (Supplementary Data 17), with 59.9% (AFR) and 81.7% (SAS) also significant in the European-only meta-analyses at P < 5 × 10−8. No significant associations were detected in the AMR and EAS groups, likely due to their limited sample sizes. The effect sizes of lead mQTLs identified in the European population were moderately correlated with those in AFR (Pearson’s r = 0.43) and SAS (r = 0.50) (Supplementary Fig. 3a).

In total, we identified 1966 AFR-specific and 1033 SAS-specific variant-trait pairs (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Notably, 77.2% of the mQTLs uniquely significant in non-European groups had a MAF < 0.01% in Europeans. Compared to SAS (median[MAFSAS/MAFEuropean] = 1.08, 17.5% absent in Europeans), AFR showed higher MAF enrichment (median[MAFAFR/MAFEuropean] = 2.57, 24.5% absent in Europeans) (Supplementary Fig. 3c), consistent with previous studies15,30. These findings suggest that despite the smaller sample size compared with SAS, the identification of more ancestry-specific associations in AFR may be driven by higher allelic enrichment, likely influenced by distinct population histories. As an example, the CD36 locus was associated with three ketone body traits and five lipid and lipoprotein traits, but these associations were observed only in the AFR group (Supplementary Fig. 3d). The sentinel SNP 7:80671133:T:G (rs3211938, a stop-gained variant in CD36) is common in AFR (MAF = 11.01%) but rare in AMR (MAF = 0.36%), nearly absent in Europeans (MAF = 0.00026%), and completely absent in EAS and SAS. Notably, CD36 encodes a glycoprotein involved in the cytoadherence of Plasmodium falciparum-parasitized erythrocytes31,32. Therefore, the higher carrier frequency of 7:80671133:T:G in AFR may reflect a survival advantage under selection pressure in malaria-endemic regions33.

Furthermore, we identified a highly pleiotropic locus near PTPRB in the AFR-specific analysis. The sentinel intronic variant 12:70475098:C:A was associated with 31 lipid traits in AFR (MAF = 6.58%). However, the variant is depleted in the European ancestry group (MAF = 1.23%), and no genome-wide associations were observed despite much larger sample sizes (Supplementary Fig. 3e).

Aggregate-based associations with plasma metabolome

We evaluated the aggregate effect of rare variants, testing associations across 17,751 protein-coding genes (‘gene-centric’) including 227,421,235 variants with an MAF < 1%, up to and including singletons. Using 26 collapsing analysis models (Supplementary Data 18), we identified 5,999 significant validated gene-trait pairs that met the signal filtering criteria in the two-stage genome-wide association meta-analysis (Methods; Fig. 4a). We conditioned our significant validated aggregates on both previously known and newly discovered common mQTLs in our single-variant meta-analyses and identified 1,527 gene-trait pairs independent of common mQTLs (defined as a –log10(P) change < 2; Methods; Supplementary Data 19). Of these, 325 were significant in both coding and non-coding masks (Fig. 4b). Notably, over half (830 [54.3%]) of the aggregate regions contained no lead mQTLs, and 421 (27.6%) did not contain any validated individual variant associations, suggesting that these loci would evade detection through conventional single-variant analyses (Fig. 4c).

For these 1,527 conditionally independent gene-trait pairs, the absolute effect sizes of the coding aggregate signals were generally larger (median = 0.42) compared to those of the non-coding aggregate signals (median = 0.26), with aggregates of variants annotated as PTVs exhibiting the largest effect (median = 0.76) (Fig. 4d). Overall, PTV aggregates harbored the highest number of conditionally independent gene-trait associations (n = 594), followed by intron (n = 582) and missense (n = 506) (Fig. 4e).

Overall, aggregate-based testing of rare coding variants identified 42 genes conditionally independently associated with 290 metabolic biomarkers. Notably, 70 of these 1021 gene-trait pairs were undetectable when using WES data (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Note). Of the 42 coding aggregates, 14 (33.33%) demonstrated lower P values across all associations captured in WGS compared to WES (Supplementary Data 20). For instance, in LIPG, we identified associations between rare coding variants and 19 metabolic traits, all of which showed stronger statistical significance in WGS than in WES (Fig. 4g). This is likely due to the substantially larger number of sequenced variants in LIPG through WGS (Fig. 4h).

We then contextualized the non-coding mask findings in relation to the coding masks, revealing that for some genes, non-coding masks identified a significantly greater number of conditionally independently associations than coding masks (Fig. 4i). For instance, the ASGR1 gene exhibited no associations via coding masks, but was significantly linked to 95 metabolic biomarkers via non-coding masks. This pattern highlights the regulatory role of non-coding regions in modulating plasma metabolites.

Among all conditionally independent rare coding aggregate associations, burden tests—assuming unidirectional variant effects—yielded the lowest P values in 42.24% of cases, representing the highest proportion across all statistical tests performed. In contrast, only 1.21% of non-coding aggregate associations achieved their strongest statistical significance under burden tests (Fig. 4j, 4k). This discrepancy likely arises because burden tests, which aggregate variant effects under a unidirectional assumption, tend to have greater discovery power for rare coding variants, which are more often deleterious and causal. By contrast, non-coding regions are likely to exhibit mixed variant effect directions, leading to signal cancellation and reduced power in burden tests.

To expand our analysis and facilitate future discoveries, we also tested associations across RNA genes, pseudogenes, and intergenic regulatory regions using 20 different models (Supplementary Data 18). This approach yielded 1368, 210, and 22 significant gene-metabolic biomarker associations, respectively. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Data 21 and on our interactive web portal.

Heritability contributions from common and rare variants

To fully quantify the extent to which metabolic loci capture variance in metabolite concentrations, we estimated the heritability (h2) of the metabolic traits using variants with MAF > 0.0001. We also assessed the phenotypic variance explained by lead variants as well as putative causal variants (Methods). Overall, apolipoproteins demonstrated the highest heritability (median h2 = 0.45) across all metabolite groups, followed by fatty acids (median h2 = 0.43), while amino acids exhibited the lowest (median h2 = 0.06) (Fig. 5a, 5b and Supplementary Data 22). The heritability estimated from WGS data (median h2 = 0.31) was significantly higher than that derived from UKB imputed array data within the same sample (median h2 = 0.11; Figs. 5c and 5d), exceeding previous estimates using array-imputed variants with MAF > 0.5%3 and approaching the theoretical total heritability of the plasma metabolome on the Nightingale Health platform34, which accounts for both genetic effects (additive and non-additive), unmeasured genetic variation, and shared environmental components (Supplementary Data 22). The heritability estimated using WGS common variants was highly correlated with the heritability estimated using imputed data (r = 0.93; Fig. 5e). The increase in heritability estimated from WGS data was primarily attributable to the inclusion of rare variants, whose median heritability was approximately twice that of common variants (Supplementary Data 22). We also showed that lead and putative causal variants explained a median of 0.13 and 0.05 of phenotypic variance, respectively. The phenotypic variance explained by lead (r = 0.38) and putative causal variants (r = 0.49) were highly correlated with the estimated heritability (Fig. 5f).

Insights into ketone body metabolism pathway

To illustrate how genetic findings can enhance our understanding of metabolic pathways and biological processes, we focused on variant and gene-centric associations implicated in the ketone body pathway. Loci associated with the ketone body pathway were enriched in several pathways, including plasma lipoprotein remodeling, fatty acid beta-oxidation, and cellular ketone metabolism, with notable enrichment observed in the liver (Supplementary Figs. 4a-c, Supplementary Data 23-26 and Supplementary Note). In addition to reaffirming well-established genes such as ACSS1, ACSS2, MPC1, OXCT1, and SLC2A4, which have direct roles in ketone body or glycemic-related pathways, we also identified biologically relevant genes closely associated with ketone body traits. Notably, two common missense variants in TM6SF2 (19:19268740:C:T and 19:19269704:A:G) were implicated in ketogenesis pathway (Supplementary Figs. 4d-4f and Supplementary Note). TM6SF2 regulates liver fat metabolism by influencing triglyceride secretion and lipid droplet content35 and has been associated with liver fat, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), and cirrhosis36–40. A moderate increase in ketogenesis accompanied by increased steatosis was observed in the liver-specific Tm6Sf2 knockout mice41. We further explored the clinical disease associations of ketone body-related genetic variants through phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS) and identified 191 significant associations (Supplementary Fig. 4g, Supplementary Data 27 and Supplementary Note). Mediation analyses revealed 18 variant-metabolic trait-disease triplets where the indirect effect of genetic variants on disease outcomes via ketone body traits was statistically evident (Supplementary Data 28). Notably, in these triplets, variant effects on disease outcomes (e.g., NAFLD, coronary atherosclerosis, ischemic heart diseases) were inversely correlated with their effects on ketone body traits (r = −0.91; Supplementary Fig. 4h and Supplementary Note), highlighting the potential mediating and protective role of the ketone body pathway in connecting genetic variation to clinical disease outcomes.

Variants in IEM genes exhibit a spectrum of effects

IEMs are genetic disorders resulting from single-enzyme deficiencies, leading to harmful accumulation or depletion of metabolites that can cause severe phenotypic consequences. Our analysis identified 108 IEM-associated genes among 680 protein-coding genes implicated, highlighting a substantial convergence against a background of 19,817 protein-coding genes42 and 1475 known IEM genes (hypergeometric test P = 3.25 × 10−14; Supplementary Data 29). Of the 268 metabolic genomic loci identified, 81 (30.22%) harbored at least one of the 803 lead variants annotated to IEM genes. These lead variants, with MAF ranging from 0.009% to 49.05%, were associated with an absolute β of 0.015–2.15 per 1 s.d. metabolic trait change per allele. Functional annotation using Variant Effect Predictor (VEP)43 classified 73 of these lead variants as missense and 9 as PTVs.

Notably, IEMs represent extreme examples of biochemical variations, which may also manifest in more subtle forms within the general population44. Genetic variants in IEM genes can exhibit a spectrum of effects, from rare pathogenic mutations causing severe diseases to common variants with milder phenotypic consequences, and ultimately to alleles contributing to metabolite variations within the normal range (Supplementary Fig. 5a)45. A notable example is SLC13A5, which encodes the sodium-dependent citrate transporter (NaCT) (Supplementary Fig. 5b)46. Homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations in SLC13A5 cause developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-25 with amelogenesis imperfecta (DEE25, OMIM #615905)47, an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by seizures in infancy, developmental delay, and dental anomalies. In this study, we identified 17:6692123:A:AGATG (a common intronic insertion in SLC13A5) as a novel lead variant for citrate (β = 0.03, P = 1.16 × 10−23). Citrate levels differed significantly by carrier status of this variant (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Notably, neither our study nor previous reports have linked this variant to DEE25-associated phenotypes, exemplifying how common polymorphisms can influence metabolite levels without causing evident IEM-related symptoms (Methods). Additionally, we identified 17:6703031:C:T (a rare missense variant in SLC13A5) as a novel lead variant for citrate (β = 1.49, P = 2.78 × 10−48) and four citrate-related ratios. This mutation is associated with DEE25 in homozygous or compound heterozygous states (Supplementary Data 30). In our study population, we found 90 heterozygous carriers of this variant, all without evidence of compound heterozygosity (Supplementary Data 30). These carriers exhibited significant differences in citrate-related traits compared to non-carriers (Supplementary Fig. 5d). Furthermore, the FinnGen GWAS reported nominal associations between this variant and moderate mental retardation and related behavioral disturbances48, suggesting that heterozygotes may be predisposed to mild neurological manifestations, despite not exhibiting severe disease phenotypes characteristic of homozygous cases.

Causal metabolic traits implicated in clinical diseases

To investigate the associations between genetically predicted metabolic traits and human diseases, we conducted a series of analyses, including colocalization, two-sample MR and MR Bayesian model averaging (MR-BMA) (Methods). We identified 27,284 metabolic trait-disease pairs with colocalization evidence (PP.H4 > 0.5; Supplementary Data 31 and 32). Subsequent MR analyses identified 245 putative causal associations between 92 metabolic traits and 56 diseases (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Data 33 and 34). Of these, 161 were further tested in cross-sectional and prospective epidemiological analyses, with 110 supported (Methods; Supplementary Data 35). Furthermore, to explore the biological mechanisms underlying these associations, we performed multi-trait colocalization analyses, identifying colocalized pQTLs for 33 pairs, eQTLs for 202 pairs, and sQTLs for 116 pairs (Methods; Supplementary Data 36 and 37).

Our results reinforced well-established associations, such as those between glucose and both type 2 diabetes and gestational diabetes, as well as between cholesterol in large HDL and myocardial infarction. We also identified relatively unexplored associations, such as the inverse relationship between linoleic acid (LA) and varicose veins (odds ratio [OR] = 0.578, P = 3.94 × 10−6) as well as diseases of veins, lymphatic vessels and lymph nodes (OR = 0.711, P = 1.06 × 10−4). Logistic and Cox analyses in the UKB revealed consistent results (Fig. 6b). This finding was supported by an experimental study in animal models suggesting that LA alleviated deep vein thrombosis (DVT) via reducing thrombus formation49, and a retrospective cohort study identifying varicose veins as a risk factor for DVT50. Notably, LA levels and varicose veins colocalized with an eQTL for ABO and sQTLs for ABO and CACFD1 (Fig. 6c, 6d). Given ABO’s association with lipid phenotypes such as LDL-C and HDL-C51 and its role in venous thrombosis52,53, it is likely that ABO interacts with LA metabolism, contributing to venous disease risk.

Additionally, we associated genetically predicted levels of four metabolic traits (i.e., omega-3 fatty acids to total fatty acids percentage, triglycerides in IDL, phospholipids to total lipids in large HDL percentage, and monounsaturated fatty acids to total fatty acids percentage) with cholelithiasis risk, all supported by epidemiological evidence (Fig. 6e). MR-BMA analysis further highlighted the important role of omega-3 fatty acids (marginal inclusion probability [MIP] = 0.997), which is also supported by a previous experimental study indicating the therapeutic effects of omega-3 in cholesterol gallstone dissolution54. In multi-trait colocalization analyses, pQTLs, eQTLs, and sQTLs for 12 genes were found to be colocalized with omega-3 fatty acids to total fatty acids percentage and cholelithiasis (Fig. 6f).

Drug target prioritization and repositioning

Metabolic profiles associated with clinical diseases offer valuable insights for therapeutic target discovery. Since both metabolites and proteins are key sources of potential drug targets, elucidating the relationships between protein levels, metabolic traits, and clinical diseases can uncover potential therapeutic opportunities. In this study, we utilized a previously described integrative framework55 that combines genomics, metabolomics, proteomics, and pharmacogenomic data to identify proteins influencing metabolic traits and corresponding diseases, assessing their potential as drug targets (Supplementary Fig. 6a). We performed cis-MR analyses to examine associations between 1,856 plasma proteins and 245 putative causal metabolic trait-disease pairs (Supplementary Fig. 6b). We identified significant associations between 1164 proteins and 92 metabolic traits, and between 1267 proteins and 56 diseases. Prioritizing proteins with directionally concordant effects on metabolic traits and diseases revealed 3874 robust triangles of associations involving 410 unique proteins (Supplementary Data 38). These proteins offer a valuable resource for drug discovery across 56 diseases, spanning 13 categories, primarily including cardiovascular, metabolic, and digestive system diseases. Notably, 263 (64.1%) of the genes encoding these proteins overlapped with the druggable genome reported by Finan and colleagues56, including 73 tier 1 genes, 26 tier 2 genes and 164 tier 3 genes (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Data 39).

We searched the 410 prioritized proteins in the DrugBank57 and Therapeutic Target Database58, identifying 81 proteins that are approved or clinical trial targets (Supplementary Data 38). Among these, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), a well-established drug target for lipid-related disorders, exhibited the largest number of causal associations, including 40 metabolic traits and 17 diseases. Some of the identified drug targets have already been utilized in clinical treatments, such as ACE inhibitors (e.g., captopril, enalaprilat, fosinopril) and PCSK9 inhibitors (e.g., alirocumab) for familial hypercholesterolemia, APOC3 inhibitors (e.g., volanesorsen) for hyperlipidemia, and PLG for myocardial infarction. Notably, we identified potential opportunities for repurposing established drug targets. For instance, cathepsin B (CTSB), an established target for chronic lower respiratory diseases, exhibited a protective association with asthma/chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) in our analysis. This effect is likely linked to increased cholesterol in large VLDL, a lipid biomarker protective for asthma/COPD, as demonstrated by both our MR analysis and an independent MR analysis in the EAS population4. Furthermore, CTSB demonstrated a potential protective role in cholelithiasis by modulating several lipid and fatty acid traits (Fig. 6h). Supporting this, a recent proteomics study linked elevated plasma CTSB levels to increased cholelithiasis risk prospectively59. Interestingly, prior research has reported elevated CTSB expression, activity, and serum levels in gallbladder carcinoma patients, with its enzymatic activity correlating with tumor stage, lymph node involvement, and mortality60.

We assessed the safety of 410 potential targets using an open-access database (AD Knowledge Portal61). Among them, 12 targets (e.g., PCSK9) have the lowest risk (safety scale of 1), 23 targets (e.g., MCL1) have lower risk (safety scale of 2), 88 targets (e.g., HSPB1) have potential risks (safety scale of 3), 218 targets (e.g., APOC3) have probable risks (safety scale of 4), and 46 targets (e.g., HP) are potentially unsafe in humans (safety scale of 5) (Fig. 6i). In summary, through druggability evaluation and safety assessment, we identified a total of 12 unestablished targets with favorable safety profiles (safety scale ≤ 2) for future drug development (Supplementary Data 39).

Interactive web portal

To facilitate an in-depth exploration of the genetic architecture of the human plasma metabolome, we developed an interactive web tool integrating genetic associations derived from imputed array, WES, and WGS data. This resource enables intuitive visualization and comparative analysis of regional genetic association patterns across metabolites. The platform supports bulk download of summary statistics generated in this study and is publicly available at https://metabolome-whole-genome-landscape.com/ under the CCBY-NC-ND 4.0 license (for non-commercial use only).

Discussion

Our study represents the largest population-based WGS analysis of the human plasma metabolome to date, integrating genomic and metabolomic data from ~200,000 individuals across five ancestries. In participants of European ancestry, we identified 36,105 lead signals across 268 independent loci, with 22.20% being novel. We also uncovered 5999 protein-coding gene-trait pairs, 25.5% of which were independent of common mQTLs. We identified 12,361 putative causal variants, demonstrating significant advancements in causal signal discovery and improved fine-mapping resolution compared to imputed array-based methods. Furthermore, we provide mechanistic insights into ketone body metabolism and reveal clinically relevant associations between metabolic changes and complex diseases. Prioritizing proteins with a directionally concordant effect on metabolic traits and diseases uncovers 410 potential drug targets, offering opportunities for therapeutic strategies and drug repurposing.

Using both effector gene annotation of lead variants and gene-based aggregate testing, we successfully linked 14 of the 64 metabolite ratios (22%) back to their corresponding genes. For the remaining ratios, the incomplete recovery is plausible given complexities such as multi-enzyme control, non-unique pathway mapping, and tissue-specific transport regulation that are not fully reflected in circulating levels.

WGS enables comprehensive detection of genetic variants across the entire allelic spectrum. Compared to heritability estimates derived from UKB imputed array data, our WGS-based analysis nearly tripled the heritability of plasma metabolites, with the increase largely attributed to rare variants. We uncovered numerous metabolic associations missed by previous GWASs. For example, we established an association between the FCHO2 locus and albumin, which showed only nominal significance in a recent study (P = 6.69 × 10−3)3. This likely reflects the improved variant detection afforded by WGS, together with our larger sample size and reduced population heterogeneity. Additionally, our findings reinforced the idea that WGS’s broader coverage, especially of rare variants with larger effect sizes and poor LD tagging, significantly improved fine-mapping resolution22,23. We also demonstrated that many rare-variant aggregate signals, especially for non-coding regions, were not conditionally independent of common mQTLs, suggesting that rare variants can still be correlated with common variants as a result of LD.

Studying underrepresented and genetically diverse populations is crucial for fully elucidating the genetic architecture of metabolic traits. We showed that ancestry-specific allelic enrichments can enhance the detection of variants that may be absent or extremely rare in the European ancestry group. For instance, the stop-gained sentinel SNP in CD36 was over 40,000-fold more common in AFR than in Europeans, enabling the replication of known associations and the discovery of biologically plausible associations between CD36 and plasma ketone body levels, given that CD36 plays a crucial role in fatty acid transport62,63, oral fat preferences64 and the regulation of energy and glucose homeostasis65. Furthermore, we showed that ketone body-related genetic variants are broadly associated with disease risk and that ketone bodies may mediate the protective effects of certain loci. Collectively, these findings underscore the value of investigating genetically diverse populations to identify ancestry-specific associations and potential therapeutic targets.

The current characterization of genomic loci embedded in their metabolic context enables deeper exploration of the complex enzymatic and transport processes shaping human metabolism. Our study demonstrates how genetic discoveries associated with ketone body metabolites and their ratios to FFA illuminate the intricacies of the ketone body metabolism pathway. However, it is important to recognize that blood metabolomics reflects a cumulative readout of processes occurring across various metabolically active tissues, including the uptake, release, production, and disposal of biochemicals from individual organs6. As such, the interpretation of the effects of genetic variants warrants caution. Future studies integrating tissue-specific multi-omics approaches including metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, may help to address this question.

Using an integrated causal inference framework, we identified putative causal relationships between metabolic traits and clinical diseases, enabling the prioritization of promising therapeutic targets. For instance, we found a protective association between omega-3 fatty acids and cholelithiasis, supported by our epidemiological analysis and consistent with previous experimental studies on mice54. Additionally, our cis-MR analyses prioritized 410 potential therapeutic targets, including 81 clinically actionable candidates and 12 with optimal safety profiles. Compared to single GWAS locus-informed and conventional MR-based drug repurposing strategies66,67, our multi-omics framework offers valuable extensions by prioritizing robust protein-metabolite-disease triangles, providing mechanistic insights for therapeutic monitoring.

Our genomic atlas of the plasma metabolome offers several future directions. While we revealed numerous genetic loci linked to human metabolism, experimental validation in cellular and animal models is essential to confirm their effects and underlying mechanisms. Furthermore, expanding blood metabolic analyses to include cell- and organ-specific metabolites, along with transcriptomics and proteomics, holds great potential for uncovering more comprehensive biological insights. Finally, the drug targets prioritized by robust protein-metabolite-disease triangulation and supported by genetic evidence present an attractive source for drug discovery initiatives, offering promising opportunities for future translational research.

Our study has several limitations. First, while we included participants from five ethnic populations, the majority were of European ancestry; expanding to larger, more diverse populations will be critical for identifying additional genetic associations and advancing therapeutic discovery. Second, despite accounting for fasting time as a covariate, the inclusion of non-fasting samples from the UKB may still influence metabolite levels and their associations. Additionally, our NMR-based metabolomics analysis, while cost-effective and comprehensive for lipoprotein subclasses, is limited in the number of metabolic traits compared to mass spectrometry, which is more sensitive but also more expensive. Lastly, the reliance on cross-sectional metabolite data limits our ability to capture the effects of genetics on longitudinal changes in the plasma metabolome.

In conclusion, our study provides a comprehensive whole-genome landscape of the human plasma metabolome. By integrating mQTLs with multi-omics and clinical data, we provide a valuable framework that connects basic genomic variation to translational potential. This open-access resource will empower researchers to explore hypotheses and facilitate the development of biomarkers and therapeutic targets, enhancing our understanding of disease mechanisms.

Methods

Ethics approval

UKB data use (project application IDs 19542 and 202239) was approved by the UKB according to their established access procedures. UKB has approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee as a Research Tissue Bank (RTB) and as such, researchers using UKB data do not require separate ethical clearance and can operate under the RTB approval. All participants provided informed consent through electronic signatures. The study adhered to all relevant regulations concerning the use of human participants and was conducted in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design and participants

The current study analyzed 313 metabolic biomarkers from ~200,000 individuals in UKB. To ensure data integrity and quality, we excluded samples demonstrating close familial relationships (kinship coefficient >0.0884), deviations in the transition/transversion ratio (Ti/Tv) or heterozygote/homozygote ratio (Het/Hom) exceeding ±8 standard deviations from the mean, genetic-sex discordance, sequencing missingness >5%, or an abnormal number of variants from the analysis. Additionally, participants with official withdrawal status or missing data for more than 20% of metabolite measurements were excluded. This resulted in a total of 199,138 samples with both metabolomic and WGS data available.

For the primary analysis, we restricted the sample to individuals of confirmed European ancestry, indicating those who self-identified as ‘White British’ (Field 21000) and have very similar genetic ancestry based on a principal components analysis of the genotypes (Field 22006). A two-stage genome-wide association meta-analysis was performed to obtain the final results and minimize the risk of false positive findings. In brief, participants of confirmed European ancestry were divided into a discovery set (stage 1, n = 134,177, ~70%) and a validation set (stage 2, n = 57,504, ~30%). Independent genetic association analyses were performed on each set, followed by meta-analysis of the summary statistics. These meta-analysis results were used for downstream analyses. Additionally, we conducted stratified mQTL analyses within four non-European ancestry groups. The same quality control procedures were applied, except that participants with missing metabolite measurements were not excluded in order to maximize sample size.

NMR metabolomics profiling

Our analysis utilized NMR-derived metabolic profiles from baseline plasma samples of 275,000 UKB participants68. Metabolic biomarkers were measured from randomly selected EDTA plasma samples (aliquot 3) using a high-throughput NMR-based metabolic biomarker profiling platform developed by Nightingale Health Ltd69. The measurements were carried out in two phases: from June 2019 to April 2020 (Phase 1) and from April 2020 to June 2022 (Phase 2)69. The analyses were performed using eight spectrometers at Nightingale Health’s facility in Finland69. Detailed methodology can be found in the UKB technical report (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/ukb/docs/NMR_companion_phase2.pdf). To enhance the analytical depth of our study, we additionally included 64 metabolite ratios from pairs with shared biochemical activities, specifically the enzymes, transporters or other common proteins involved, as annotated in the HMDB database21. The raw metabolic values were pre-processed by removing outliers, defined as any data outside four interquartile ranges from the median.

WGS processing, quality control, and variant calling

The UKB WGS data were generated by deCODE Genetics and the Wellcome Sanger Institute in collaboration with AstraZeneca, Amgen, GSK, Johnson & Johnson, Wellcome Trust Sanger, and UK Research and Innovation15. Genomic DNA underwent paired-end sequencing on Illumina NovaSeq6000 instruments with a read length of 2 × 151 and an average coverage of 32.5×15,70. Comprehensive sequencing protocols and initial QC are detailed elsewhere15,70. Variant calling was conducted using the Illumina DRAGEN 3.7.8 pipeline, aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome. We accessed the jointly called pVCF WGS data (Field 24310) and applied additional QC measures before proceeding with the main analysis. Multi-allelic variants were converted to biallelic form and normalized using bcftools71. The filtering of SNPs and INDELs was performed according to the following criteria: 1) missingness >10%, 2) absence of alternative alleles, 3) heterozygosity deviations (<0.5 or >1.5), 4) Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium deviations (P < 1 × 10−30). Additionally, variants flagged with “FILTER = PASS” were retained for further analysis. After variant filtering and QC, we included up to 497,340,481 autosomal variants (7,726,466 common and 489,614,015 rare variants) in the analysis.

Single variant association test

Single variant association analyses were performed using REGENIE (version 3.4.1)72. For each trait, Step 1 of REGENIE was performed using a set of common variants from array genotypes, following the recommendations outlined in the REGENIE documentation. In Step 2, a linear regression model was applied to 35,776,728 autosomal SNPs and INDELs that passed quality control (QC) filters and had a minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than 0.01%. The analysis was adjusted for sex, age at recruitment, fasting time, month of assessment, recruitment center, sequencing provider, shipment batch, the first 40 principal components (PCs), and genomic predictions from Step 1 (Supplementary Data 3). Rank-based inverse normal transformation was applied using the “--apply-rint” command. Results from the discovery and validation sets were combined using inverse variance meta-analysis based on effect size estimates and standard errors using METAL software73. We applied a two-step signal selection process. Variants were considered validated if they met the following criteria: (1) achieved genome-wide significance in the discovery set (P < 5 × 10−8) and reached P < 1 × 10−6 in the validation set, (2) exhibited consistent effect directions in both stages, and (3) passed a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 5 × 10−8/313 in the meta-analysis. Lead variants were identified using linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping (r2 = 0.1, 1 Mb window) in PLINK74. To assess pleiotropy at the single-locus level, genomic loci were defined by creating windows extending ±200 kb flanking each lead variant. Overlapping windows were merged regardless of phenotype.

Fine-mapping of independent signals

We performed fine-mapping using FINEMAP (version 1.4.21)75,76, which leverages LD information to identify the most likely causal configurations within a region through a shotgun stochastic search algorithm. Briefly, we defined 2-Mb regions centered on each lead variant and merged overlapping regions associated with the same biomarker. We selected variants with P < 0.05 for fine-mapping and pairwise variant LD matrices were calculated using 191,681 unrelated individuals of European ancestry from UKB (the same individuals used for summary statistics). Variants with a model-averaged PIP > 0.95 were considered as putative causal variants. For each identified causal signal, credible sets with a 99% PIP were estimated. FINEMAP was run with its standard settings, with the number of causal variants restricted to a maximum of ten.

We also performed fine-mapping on imputed array data using the same variant inclusion criteria and parameters for comparability. For a more direct comparison, we matched regions created using WGS and imputed array data with less than 200-Kb difference in start and end positions, resulting in 6,058 matched comparable regions in which the number of causal signals could be compared more clearly. Finally, we focused on the matched regions where only a single signal was captured in both the WGS and imputed array data and compared the confidence interval (CI) sizes of these “matched signals”.

Candidate causal variants (model-averaged PIP > 0.95) from WGS and array-based fine-mapping were functionally annotated using FAVOR25, incorporating super-enhancer, GeneHancer, and CAGE-promoter/enhancer elements. The proportions of variants overlapping each regulatory element were compared between datasets using a Chi-squared test in R. Prediction of TF binding site disruption by candidate causal variants associated with metabolic traits was performed using the R package MotifbreakR26, incorporating motif information from the HOCOMOCO and ENCODE databases. Default parameters were applied, and motif disruption was considered significant at a P value threshold of <0.0001.

Functional annotations

The identified lead and putative causal variant were annotated using Variant Effect Predictor (VEP)43. We assigned the most likely effector genes for each lead and putative causal variant through the following steps: (1) Using the human GENCODE dataset release 4642, we searched for protein-coding genes within 1-Mb intervals centered on each variant, considering them as candidate genes; (2) We prioritized genes with established biological relevance to the associated metabolic traits by querying the HMDB21, KEGG77 and PubChem Chemical Co-occurrences in Literature databases78; (3) We performed colocalization analyses to identify genes whose expression is genetically associated with the metabolite. Cis-eQTLs from 49 human tissues were assessed from the V8 release of the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Project79. For each variant, summary statistics from the metabolic trait and cis-eQTL data within a 1-Mb region were extracted as inputs, with prior probabilities set to P1 = 1 × 10−4, P2 = 1 × 10−4, and P12 = 1 × 10−5. Candidate genes were classified as expression-relevant if PP.H4 > 0.8. Genes were designated as potential effector genes if they met both biological and expression relevance criteria. If no candidate genes satisfied these conditions, we nominated potential effector genes using VEP.

Non-European mQTL mapping

Non-European ancestries were inferred using the peddy software80 and the 1000 Genomes Project reference panel, with a peddy_prob threshold set at 0.9. Additionally, participants with PC values more than 4 s.d. from the mean for the first four PCs were excluded. To ensure comparable MAF with the European-only analysis, we applied a minor allele count (MAC) threshold ≥ 5, given the smaller sample sizes. Ancestry-stratified mQTL analyses were conducted using identical covariates and the first 40 ancestry-specific genetic PCs. After excluding two analytes due to inflation, the final analysis encompassed a total of 311 metabolic traits. A threshold of 5 × 10−8 was used to claim statistical significance.

Aggregate-based association test

To assess the aggregate effect of rare variants on plasma metabolome, we categorized each variant into functional groupings, which we referred to as masks, based on their predicted consequence and genomic location. We used VEP43 and the following six published variant scores to group variants by consequence: Genomic Evolutionary Rate Profiling (GERP)81, Genomic Non-coding Constraint of Haplo-insufficient Variation (Gnocchi)82, SpliceAI83, Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion (CADD)84, Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner (REVEL)85, and Junk Annotation Residual Variation Intolerance Score (JARVIS)86. In total, we tested for the associations across 17,751 protein-coding genes using 26 different models, including 2 derived combined masks assessing the combined effect of variants with different consequences and 14 derived subset masks based on variant annotation scores in which variants may have more consistent effects. Detailed information for our testing models is provided in Supplementary Data 18. For each consequence-based mask, we considered four MAF bins: MAF ≤ 1%, MAF ≤ 0.1%, MAF ≤ 0.01%, and singletons only. For each trait within each mask, we performed set-based collapsing analyses using Step 2 of REGENIE72 with the commands “--vc-tests skato,acato”, “--joint minp,acat,sbat”, and “--rgc-gene-p”, adjusting for the same covariates as in the single-variant analysis. Rank-based inverse normal transformation was applied using the “--apply-rint” command. Results from the discovery and validation sets were meta-analyzed using METAL software73 and the signals underwent a two-step selection process. Association signals were considered validated if they were significant in the discovery set (P < 5 × 10−8) and the validation set (P < 1 × 10−6), exhibited consistent effect directions, and achieved a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 5 × 10−8/313 in the meta-analysis.

To identify rare-variant aggregates independent of both known and novel common mQTLs, we performed conditional analysis using the ‘--condition-list’ command in REGENIE72. For all validated gene-centric associations, we conditioned on common variants—both previously known (from a list of 22 published mQTL studies, Supplementary Data 8) and newly discovered in our single-variant meta-analyses—that were individually associated with the respective metabolic traits, adjusting for the same covariates as in the full model. Signals were considered conditionally independent if the change in -log10(P) was below the threshold of 2.

We additionally tested for the association across RNA genes, pseudogenes, and intergenic regulatory regions using the same covariates and signal selection method. Twenty different models were used (Supplementary Data 18).

Heritability and variance explained

We estimated the heritability of metabolic traits using the MAF- and LD-stratified (LDMS) method via GCTA software87–89. Due to the substantial computational demands of LD calculations and GRM construction in WGS data, we randomly selected 10,000 unrelated participants of European ancestry and filtered variants to MAF > 0.0001 for heritability analysis. Variants were categorized into common (MAF ≥ 0.01) and rare (MAF < 0.01) bins, which were further divided into two bins based on median LD scores, resulting in a total of four MAF-LD bins88. These bins were utilized to calculate heritability for each metabolite using multiple genetic relationship matrix (GRMs, --mgrm option in GCTA). To enhance computational efficiency, we employed Haseman-Elston regression (HEreg) analysis, which yields results comparable to GREML while significantly reducing memory requirements90. We also estimated heritability using UKB array-imputed variants (with MAF > 0.01, missingness <0.05, and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium P < 1 × 10−6) within the same set of 10,000 participants and for the same traits as in the WGS-based analysis, using GCTA to construct GRMs and compute heritability for direct comparison. Additionally, we compared our heritability estimates to those from a recent metabolite study that utilized array-imputed variants from UKB3, as well as with a study estimating the theoretical total heritability of the Nightingale Health plasma metabolome, which accounts for variance attributable to genotyped and ungenotyped genetic effects, non-additive genetic effects, and potential shared environmental influences34.

The phenotypic variance explained by lead variants and putative causal variants for each trait was estimated using the CJAMP R package (version 0.1.1).

Pathway and tissue type enrichment

To elucidate the biological relevance of our WGS findings related to ketone body traits, we performed pathway and tissue-type enrichment analyses. For pathway enrichment, we utilized the gprofile2 tool, which employs a hypergeometric test to identify over-represented biological functions and pathways among the identified genes91. Additionally, we conducted tissue type enrichment analysis across 54 tissue types using GTEx v8 data92, employing the GENE2FUNC function in FUMA93.

Phenome-wide association of ketone body-related variants

To further investigate the clinical impact of ketone body-related variants, we performed a phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS) on disease outcomes from UKB using REGENIE (version 3.4.1). A total of 1768 disease endpoints were ascertained based on ICD-10 diagnostic classifications, with the detailed process procedure reported previously59. The analysis was restricted to 340,484 participants of European descent, adjusting for age, sex, batch effects, age2, age × sex, age2 × sex, and the top 40 PCs. Statistical significance was determined using a threshold of P < 0.05/(56 × 1768).

Mediation analysis

Building upon the established associations between lead variants for ketone body levels and disease outcomes, we investigated the potential mediating role of plasma ketone bodies in linking genetic variants to disease onset. Lead variants for ketone bodies (coded as 0, 1, 2) were designated as exposures, plasma ketone body traits as mediators, and disease onset as outcomes. Mediation analyses were performed using the mediation R package (version 4.5.0), adjusting for the same covariates as we used for mQTL mapping. Samples with missing genotype or phenotype data were removed. The significance of indirect effects was assessed through 1000 bootstrap iterations. A mediation effect was considered significant if it met both of the following criteria: a P value for the indirect effect < 0.01, and a proportion mediated >10%.

Inborn errors of metabolism

To assess whether our findings converge on known IEM genes, we compiled the results from single-variant analysis and gene-centric analysis and a list of 1475 IEM-associated genes from four specialized databases (Orphanet94, Ramedis95, IEMbase96, and DDIEM97).

To evaluate whether carriers of SLC13A5 variants exhibited phenotypic features associated with DEE25, we extracted data related to epilepsy, cognitive function, and dental health from the UKB (Fields 131048, 131049, 40000, 40001, 40002, 41270, 41280, 20018, 399, 20016, 20023, and 6149).

Colocalization analysis of metabolic traits and diseases

We performed colocalization analysis to identify shared genetic determinants between metabolic traits and clinical diseases. Imputed array-based disease GWAS summary statistics for 2440 disease outcomes were obtained from the FinnGen R11 release48, and protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) were sourced from a previous study (4907 proteins)98. We first filtered our 268 defined metabolic genomic loci to retain those harboring significant disease association signals (after Bonferroni correction), as previously recommended19. Following this, we extracted the summary statistics within these loci to perform colocalization analyses using the coloc R package (version 5.2.3). Prior probabilities were set according to established recommendations99: P1 = 1 × 10−4, P2 = 1 × 10−4, and P12 = 1 × 10−5. PP.H4 value ≥ 0.8 was considered strong evidence for colocalization.

Two-sample MR and MR Bayesian model averaging analysis

For metabolic trait-disease pairs showing evidence of colocalization, we conducted MR analyses using the TwoSampleMR R package (version 0.5.6)100. LD-independent variants were selected as candidate instrumental variables (IVs) using the clumping function in PLINK (clump-kb 500, clump-r2 0.1, clump-p1 5 × 10−8)74. To minimize horizontal pleiotropy, we excluded IVs associated with more than five traits at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8), as previously suggested3,101. Primary analyses employed the Wald ratio method for single IVs, and the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method with multiplicative random-effects modeling for multiple IVs. Statistical significance was determined using FDR-corrected Q < 0.05 (equivalent to P < 5.42 × 10−4). Robustness of causal inference was further evaluated using multiple sensitivity analyses including MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods. We tested for potential pleiotropy effects using MR-Egger intercepts, and assessed heterogeneity through both IVW and Egger-based tests.

Given the extensive genetic correlations among metabolic traits and notable pleiotropy at multiple loci, we applied MR Bayesian model averaging (MR-BMA)102 to prioritize metabolic traits associated with the same disease in the MR. This multivariable approach accounts for pleiotropy, improving the robustness of causal inference when analyzing high-dimensional risk factor sets102,103. We pruned lead variants from single-variant meta-analysis with a 200-Kb window and an r2 threshold of 0.5. To avoid multicollinearity in the exposure-instrument association matrix, we ensured the matrix was of full column rank. Additionally, to avoid unreliable results from highly correlated traits, we excluded one trait from pairs with a correlation stronger than |r| > 0.99, as previously suggested101,102. Based on prior recommendations102,104, we assigned a prior probability of 0.1 and a prior variance of 0.25. A stochastic search was conducted across 10,000 iterations, and outlier variants indicative of unmeasured pleiotropic effects were identified and removed using the Q-statistic. Metabolic traits were ranked according to their marginal inclusion probability (MIP), which was calculated as the sum of the posterior probabilities across all candidate models that included the metabolic traits.

Cross-sectional and prospective association analysis

To determine whether our causal metabolic trait-disease pairs revealed by MR could be supported by association tests, we explored the epidemiological associations between metabolic traits and disease outcome data from UKB. Briefly, relationships between metabolic traits and diseases were analyzed cross-sectionally (logistic regressions) and prospectively (Cox proportional hazard regressions) for prevalent (diagnosed before the blood collection) and incident diseases (diagnosed in the follow-up after the blood collection), respectively. We obtained the diagnostic data of diseases with significant MR associations from UK Hospital Episode Statistics data. The diagnostic data were defined as disease endpoints based on the first occurrence of the 3-character ICD-10 code using the hospital inpatient records (Fields 41270 and 41280) and were extracted from the FinnGen clinical endpoint DF12. Before further analysis, the raw metabolic values were pre-processed by removing outliers, followed by natural-log transformation and Z-transformation.

For both cross-sectional and prospective analyses, covariates included sex, age at blood collection, Townsend deprivation index (TDI), fasting time before the blood collection, month of assessment, recruitment center and medication of statin. Analyses were conducted in Python using the statsmodels (version 0.13.1) and lifelines (version 0.27.4) packages. We considered a causal association suggested by MR supported if the metabolic trait and disease were associated in the same direction as the MR effect, either cross-sectionally or prospectively, with FDR-corrected Q < 0.05.

Multi-trait colocalization analysis

To explore the potential mechanisms underlying causal metabolic trait-disease pairs revealed by MR analyses, we integrated QTL data to perform multi-trait colocalization using the hyprcoloc R package (version 1.0)105. Cis-eQTLs and cis-sQTLs across 49 human tissues were obtained from GTEx V8 release79, while pQTLs and meQTLs data were sourced from published studies98,106. The posterior probability of colocalization, the most likely causal variant within the region, and the proportion of the posterior probability explained by the identified variants were reported. We set the conditional colocalization prior (pc) to 0.02 and reported only trait clusters with a posterior probability greater than 0.7.

Cis-MR analyses associating proteins with metabolic traits and diseases

We sourced summary statistics of circulating proteins from a previous study98. For each protein-coding gene, we selected cis-acting genetic variants within ±1 Mb of the transcription start site that were significant (P < 5 × 10−8) and LD-independent (r2 = 0.1, 500 Kb window) variants associated with proteins as IVs. For each protein with valid instruments, we performed cis-MR analyses against 245 metabolic trait-disease pairs. Primary analyses employed the Wald ratio method for single IVs, and the IVW method with multiplicative random-effects modeling for multiple IVs. Robustness of causal inference was further evaluated using multiple sensitivity analyses including MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods. We tested for potential pleiotropy effects using MR-Egger intercepts, and assessed heterogeneity through both IVW and Egger-based tests.

Druggability evaluation and safety assessment

Finan et al. previously defined the druggable genome, comprising 4,479 genes, and classified them into three tiers based on their positions within the drug development pipeline37. Tier 1 (1427 genes) includes efficacy targets for approved small molecules and biotherapeutic drugs, as well as clinical-phase drug candidates. Tier 2 (682 genes) contains targets with known bioactive drug-like small-molecule binding partners, as well as those exhibiting ≥50% sequence identity (across ≥75% of the sequence) with approved drug targets. Tier 3 (2370 genes) encompasses secreted or extracellular proteins, proteins with more distant homology to approved drug targets, and members of key druggable gene families not already included in Tier 1 or Tier 2.

For the druggability evaluation, we first assessed the overlap between the prioritized proteins and the druggable genome. We then utilized the GREP (Genome for REPositioning drugs) software107 to evaluate the enrichment of these proteins within the gene sets targeted by drugs approved for diseases categorized under the ICD-10 classification and captured potentially repositionable drugs. Drug data were retrieved from DrugBank57 and the Therapeutic Target Database58.

To assess the safety of the 410 potential targets, we employed the AD Knowledge Portal61. Target safety was classified into six categories: Safety scale 1 (lowest risk, drugs in phase IV trials); Safety scale 2 (lower risk, no major issues found from gene expression, genetic, or pharmacological profiling, but limited human testing); Safety scale 3 (potential risks, associated with one or two factors, such as high off-target gene expression, cancer driver, essential gene, links to deleterious genetic disorders, relevant human phenotype ontology phenotype, or black box warnings; Safety scale 4 (probable risks, with more than two of the aboverisk factors); Safety scale 5 (potentially unsafe in humans, including on-target adverse drug reactions and withdrawn drugs); and Safety scale 6 (unknown risk).
