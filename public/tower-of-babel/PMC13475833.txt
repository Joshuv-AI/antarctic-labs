Minimizing tricuspid valve damage during neonatal patent ductus arteriosus closure by implementing a change in procedural technique
Authors: Christopher Iskander, Mehmet Kucuk, Salvador Rodriguez Franco, Ryan Leahy, Jenny E. Zablah, Gareth John Morgan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aims to document the progression of tricuspid regurgitation (TR) following percutaneous patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) closure in preterm neonates weighing <2 kg following an institutional change in procedural technique.Materials and Methods:Before June 1, 2021, the tricuspid valve was crossed with a glide catheter and glide wire. As of June 1, the tricuspid valve was crossed with a diagnostic catheter, microcatheter, and floppy 0.014” wire. This retrospective single-center chart review of the progression of TR in ex-preterm infants weighing <2 kg following percutaneous PDA closure included cases from January 2019 to December 2022. Echocardiograms completed prior to and following the procedure were graded, and a comparison was made between the progression of TR in the old technique group (control) and the new technique group (treatment).Results:There were 58 patients who met the criteria during the study period. Median weight was 1.2 kg in the control group and 1.29 kg in the treatment group. The rate of TR progression was 32% (10/31) in the control group and 7% (2/27) in the treatment group. Both cases in the treatment group were progression from none/trivial to mild TR. Of the 10 cases that demonstrated progression in the control group, 4 progressed from none/trivial to mild and 6 progressed from none/trivial to moderate (P= 0.026).Conclusions:Despite the small sample size, this change in technique does demonstrate less progression in TR. Further studies are warranted to assess the longitudinal natural history of TR caused by trauma during neonatal duct closure.

INTRODUCTION

Over the last 8 years, percutaneous closure of the patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) has become the standard of care for preterm patients with a hemodynamically significant PDA.[123] Introduction of the Amplatzer Piccolo Occluder® (Abbott Laboratories, St. Paul, MN, USA) onto the market enabled the practice of device closure of PDAs in preterm neonates between 700 g and 2 kg. Percutaneous closure has since become favored over surgical ligation in this age group.[45] Despite these advances, the procedure does come with risks, including the development of significant tricuspid regurgitation (TR), which has been reported since the initial experiences.[4] While there are numerous potential factors, including but not limited to patient size, proceduralist experience, and device selection, there is reason to believe that wire and catheter selection and the associated technique of crossing the tricuspid valve are also significant factors in the development of TR.

In our institution, before June, 2021, the wire and catheter combinations most used to cross the tricuspid valve in preterm PDA closure cases were either a 4F glide catheter and 0.035” glide wire or a 4F glide catheter and 0.035” Benson wire. Since then, our group made an institutional decision to change technique to use a telescoping catheter technique consisting of a 4F glide catheter, a microcatheter (typically a 2.5F Renegade microcatheter), and a 0.014” balance middleweight (BMW) wire, with a curve formed at its floppy tip. With the glide catheter and the microcatheter coaxial in the right atrium (RA), the precurved BMW is advanced into the RA across the tricuspid valve and up through the right ventricular outflow tract and across the PDA. The microcatheter is then advanced over the wire and followed by the glide catheter to sit in the descending aorta. The microcatheter is removed for angiography, then the BMW wire is exchanged for a 0.035” J-tipped TSF wire.

The practice change was informed by routine internal case reviews and based on a discussion between our team and the team at Necker Hospital in Paris. Both teams then implemented this new technique, which was subsequently reported by Meot et al.[6]

This study aims to compare two eras: before and after this change in practice with respect to the incidence of TR following percutaneous PDA closure in preterm neonates weighing <2 kg.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This single-center, retrospective chart review evaluated preterm patients weighing 2 kg or less who underwent percutaneous PDA closure between January 2019 and December 2022. Patients were identified through a query of the Children’s Hospital Colorado electronic medical record (EPIC Systems, Verona, WI, USA). All data collection and analysis procedures were approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (IRB 21-3673). Given the retrospective nature of the study, patient consent was waived.

Comprehensive echocardiograms performed during the 2 weeks leading up to the procedure, intra-procedurally, and within 2 days after the procedure were analyzed. All echocardiograms were completed by licensed sonographers using either a Vivid (GE Ultrasound, Milwaukee, WI, USA) or an IE-33 (Philips Ultrasound, Bothell, WA, USA) system. Images were acquired in accordance with the standard protocol of the local echocardiography laboratory. Board-certified attending echocardiographers reviewed and validated all imaging. All echocardiogram readers were blinded to the patient group. TR was qualitatively categorized as none/trivial, mild, moderate, or severe.

Each catheterization was completed with the patient under general anesthesia, following the standardized protocol of the local catheterization laboratory to ensure consistent safety and effectiveness. The protocol for device selection and sizing was broadly consistent across providers within the catheterization laboratory and adherent to the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics as appropriate. Statistical analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel Office 2021 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA). Patient characteristics were stratified by group, either the old technique group (OTG) or the new technique group (NTG). All tests were two-tailed with α = 0.05.

RESULTS

From January 2019 to December 2022, 58 patients met the inclusion criteria for this study. The mean weight at the time of the procedure was 1.2 kg in the OTG and 1.29 kg in the NTG. There was no statistically significant difference in the sex, gestational age, birth weight, body surface area, procedural weight, or age between the two groups, as shown in Table 1.

The most common device for occlusion in both groups was the Amplatzer Piccolo Occluder (89% in the NTG and 78% in the OTG). Devices other than the Piccolo were three 6-mm AVP2 devices in the NTG. Two 6-mm AVP2 devices and five 5Q MVP devices were used in OTG. The OTG group had six cases of moderate TR, five of which were associated with Piccolo devices and one with a 5Q MVP. Three of the four cases of mild regurgitation within the OTG were associated with Piccolo devices, and one was a 5Q MVP. The NTG had no cases of moderate TR and two new cases of mild TR, both of which used Piccolo devices. Fisher’s exact test found no significant difference in the type of devices used.

Initial preprocedural echo diagnosed TR in one patient in the OTG (baseline TR 3.2%) and one patient in the NTG with baseline TR (3.7%). After PDA closure, the incidence of increased TR was 32.2% in the OTG and 7.4% in the NTG (P < 0.05) [Table 2].

Both cases in the NTG with a change in TR had progressed from none/trivial to mild TR. Of the 10 cases that demonstrated a change in the OTG, 4 progressed from none/trivial to mild and 6 progressed from none/trivial to moderate (the TR had increased by two categories) [Figure 1]. One patient in the OTG had mild TR at baseline before the procedure but did not have TR appreciated on the postprocedure echocardiogram. Importantly, of the six patients who developed moderate TR, two of them were found to have eccentric TR jets with flail leaflets [Figure 2] and a third had an eccentric TR jet with prolapse of the septal leaflet into the RA on echocardiogram, further suggesting clinically meaningful damage to the tricuspid valve. Qualitatively, all of the patients with new TR had obvious pathology of a tricuspid valve leaflet (either flail or prolapsed leaflets), or the direction, shape, and origin of the regurgitant jet looked unusual. None of the patients with new TR had central coaptation defects seen in patients with functional TR.

Comparing the change from none/trivial to moderate TR between the two groups illustrated a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05).

There was no difference in the size of the delivery sheath between the two groups. The majority of patients, 24 in each group, were treated with a Piccolo device, all of which were placed with a 4F TorqVue LP delivery catheter. All other devices, either 5-mm Medtronic Micro Vascular Plugs (Medtronic plc, Minneapolis, MN, USA) or AVP2, were delivered through a 4F sheath. All patients who developed significant new TR had Piccolo devices.

A comparison of procedural countermeasures was also completed by analyzing procedure time and fluoroscopy time between the two groups. There was no statistically significant difference in the mean procedure time with OTG mean length of 38.1 min (confidence interval [CI]: 30.69–45.57) and the NTG mean length was 45.7 min (CI: 35.53–55.79), P = 0.12. Likewise, the fluoroscopy time was not statistically significant (P = 0.39) with means of 7.99 min (CI: 5.68–10.29) and 8.35 min (CI: 6.94–9.76) in OTG and NTG, respectively. Finally, intraprocedural complication rates were examined. There was a complication rate of 3.2% (n = 1) in the OTG and 3.7% (n = 1) in the NTG. The single complication in the OTG was temporary periods of desaturation as low as 20% with associated bradycardia. The NTG complication was one case of nonsustained SVT that resolved with catheter manipulation.

DISCUSSION

Numerous catheter and wire combinations have been reportedly used in preterm PDA closure.[789] While use of the telescoping catheter technique with a 4F end hole catheter, microcatheter, and floppy 0.014” wire has been previously described, there has not yet been a study demonstrating its value in reducing the incidence of postprocedural TR.[6] The intent of this study was to assess for any possible difference in new or increased TR between the telescoping catheter technique and the traditional end-hole catheter and 0.035” wire approach.

Despite the small sample size, our study statistically demonstrates that this change in technique was associated with less TR, and importantly, the elimination of moderate TR caused during PDA closure in premature neonates. Although the exact mechanism of this benefit is unknown, we can logically theorize that it is likely due to a combination of features of the telescoping technique. The use of a curved tip, floppy 0.014” wire is advantageous because it allows the wire to “flop” across the tricuspid valve leaflets rather than inadvertently passing through chordae. It is a more passive crossing maneuver, perhaps akin to the use of balloon-tipped catheters in larger patients. This is in contrast to the use of the glide wire and glide catheter, which confer a more active, directional passage, which, although effective, is more likely to entrap a portion of the tricuspid valve support apparatus. The use of the microcatheter in the telescoping system minimizes catheter-wire mismatch and hence also is likely to reduce the risk of chordae entrapment [Figures 3 and 4].[10]

In aggregate, there is less stress and trauma on the tricuspid valve during wire and catheter manipulation, and thus, few instances of new TR. This reduction in TR is meaningful as long-standing TR can negatively affect right ventricular function and thus cause increased morbidity.[1112] Though follow-up was limited in our study, we are collating data as part of an international collaborative to determine the long-term natural history of TR in patients following premie PDA closure.

Furthermore, no related complications and no significant differences in procedural time or other parameters reassure us that this benefit in TR reduction did not come at the expense of alternative morbidity or risk.

As previously mentioned, the sample size of this study is small, a function of its single-center design, which is the primary limitation. This allows for the possibility of a type II error in assessing procedural complications between the two groups. A larger, multicenter study would be beneficial to further substantiate the data. Another limitation of this study was the short follow-up interval, with the follow-up echocardiogram completed within 2 days of the procedure. A future study with a longer follow-up interval may allow us to track the progression of this “traumatic” TR, which may improve without intervention.

Despite these limitations, this study provides evidence to suggest that catheter/wire selection and procedural technique, specifically the use of the telescoping catheter technique outlined, play a role in reducing tricuspid valve complications and could have a tangible impact on outcomes in this vulnerable patient population.
