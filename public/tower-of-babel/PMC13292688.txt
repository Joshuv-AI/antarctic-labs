Novel goose parvovirus as a major risk factor for red skin and bristle feather syndrome in meat ducks: Epidemiological associations with duck circovirus and reovirus
Authors: Zhongcheng Sun, Xue Wang, Hao Shi, Hao Zhu, Xue Liu, Tianyao Yang, Qingqing Song, Zhaoyang Li, Chunguo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, a disease characterized by dorsal skin reddening and feather development disorder, termed "red skin and bristle feather syndrome" (RSBFS), has emerged in meat duck farms in China, resulting in substantial economic losses. To clarify the major etiological risk factor, their epidemiological patterns, and molecular characteristics, we collected 300 clinical samples from symptomatic and asymptomatic ducks across eight major duck-producing provinces in China between February and July 2025. Multipathogen screening was conducted using quantitative real-time PCR, followed by phylogenetic analysis of the major pathogens. The results identified duck circovirus (DuCV), novel duck reovirus (NDRV), and waterfowl parvovirus (WPV) as the dominant pathogens, with detection rates of 66.44% (194/292), 32.33% (86/266), and 19.18% (56/292), respectively. Among the detected pathogens, monoinfection with WPV—predominantly represented by novel goose parvovirus (NGPV)—showed a strong positive association with RSBFS (OR = 9.85, 95% CI: 4.89–19.84). It was identified as a major etiological risk factor for disease occurrence. Although DuCV or NDRV monoinfection did not exhibit significant pathogenicity, their coinfection with WPV resulted in markedly enhanced interactive effects on disease severity. Epidemiological analysis revealed distinct spatiotemporal clustering, with Shandong province as the main epidemic focus and ducks aged 3 to 5 weeks as the most susceptible group. Genetic evolution analysis indicated that DuCV strains predominantly belonged to genotypes Ib and Ic, the NDRV S1 gene was highly conserved, and NGPV was the dominant subtype of WPV. To our knowledge, this study is the first to identify a strong statistical association of NGPV with RSBFS and to characterize its epidemiological associations with DuCV and NDRV. These findings provide critical epidemiological and molecular evidence for developing targeted vaccines and formulating integrated control strategies in commercial duck production.

Introduction

In recent years, a condition characterized by cutaneous erythema and abnormal feather development has been frequently observed in meat duck production systems in China, posing a significant challenge to production efficiency and carcass quality. Clinically, the syndrome presents with inflammatory reddening of the dorsal and caudal skin, sparse and disordered feathers shedding easily, and retained feather shafts difficult to remove during processing. This results in so-called "bristle ducks", which severely compromise carcass quality, increase processing costs, and cause substantial economic losses. This condition, collectively referred to in this study as "red skin and bristle feather syndrome" (RSBFS), is pathologically typified by the loss of dorsal and caudal feathers while the follicles and feather shafts remain embedded in the skin. In severe cases, lesions may spread across the body, preventing complete feather removal during slaughter. The exposed skin often becomes erythematous due to friction or inflammation, hence the descriptive name. Various terms have been used for this condition in different regions or reports, including "feather shedding syndrome," "red skin disease," or "bristle syndrome" (Yang et al., 2020; Ting et al., 2021; Soike et al., 2004). Its rising incidence and impact have made it a prominent constraint on the sustainable development and profitability of the intensive duck industry.

The etiology of RSBFS is complex and generally considered multifactorial, involving viral infections, secondary bacterial invasions, nutritional or metabolic disturbances, and suboptimal husbandry practices. Among viruses, duck circovirus (DuCV), waterfowl parvovirus (WPV), and novel duck reovirus (NDRV) are the pathogens of major concern and key pathogens associated with this syndrome. Previous studies have linked DuCV infection to feather loss and growth retardation (Huang et al., 2013), while goose parvovirus-related viruses have also been associated with abnormal molting in ducks (Jin et al., 2019). Moreover, other viruses such as reticuloendotheliosis virus are known to induce feather abnormalities in chickens (Koyama et al., 1980), suggesting viral infection as a potential key trigger for feather development disorders.

DuCV belongs to the genus Circovirus within the family Circoviridae. It is a non-enveloped, icosahedral virus with a single-stranded circular DNA genome containing six open reading frames, among which ORF C1 encodes the immunogenic capsid protein (Cap) (Hong et al., 2018). DuCV infection can damage immune organs, such as the thymus and bursa of Fabricius, leading to immunosuppression and increased susceptibility to secondary or concurrent infections (Jin et al., 2019; Koyama et al., 1980; Hong et al., 2018). Ducks of all ages are susceptible, with clinical signs including growth retardation, poor feather development, and feather shaft hemorrhage (Yang et al., 2020), often causing serious economic losses. Studies have confirmed a close association between DuCV infection and feather shedding syndrome (Hattermann et al., 2003; Wen et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2009; Kulprasertsri et al., 2024).

WPV is a member of the genus Dependoparvovirus within the family Parvoviridae, possessing a linear single-stranded DNA genome. The genome contains inverted terminal repeats and two major ORFs encoding nonstructural proteins (NS1, NS2) and structural proteins (VP1–VP3) (Zádori et al., 1995; Shen et al., 2015). WPV includes classical goose parvovirus (GPV), classical Muscovy duck parvovirus (MDPV), novel goose parvovirus (NGPV), and novel Muscovy duck-goose parvovirus (MDGPV) (Li et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2025). NGPV infection in ducks can induce short beak and dwarfism syndrome (SBDS), characterized by beak atrophy, tongue protrusion, and disordered feather shedding (Yu et al., 2016; Palya et al., 2009). NGPV may directly or indirectly disrupt feather development by interfering with follicular stem cell function and skeletal growth (Li et al., 2018). A parvovirus linked to feather abnormalities has also been isolated from Muscovy ducks in Japan (Takehara et al., 1994).

NDRV belongs to the genus Orthoreovirus in the family Reoviridae. The virion is non-enveloped, spherical, and possesses a double-layered capsid. Its genome consists of 10 segments of double-stranded RNA, with the S1 gene encoding the σC protein, which mediates viral attachment and serves as a key antigen for neutralizing antibodies (Du et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2006; Ma et al., 2012). NDRV infection can cause splenic necrosis and hemorrhage, induce immunosuppression and growth retardation, and exacerbate integumentary and feather lesions, particularly in ducklings (Liu et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2020).

Notably, these viruses often co-circulate in mixed infections and can act synergistically with bacterial pathogens such as Salmonella spp. (SM) and Riemerella anatipestifer to aggravate clinical signs, complicating disease control. However, systematic knowledge regarding the predominant pathogens responsible for RSBFS—their epidemiological dynamics, genotypic features, and coinfection mechanisms—remains limited. There is a clear need for targeted prevention and control strategies based on large-scale epidemiological surveys and molecular characterization, which are essential for practical disease management in poultry production.

Therefore, from February to July 2025, we collected 300 clinical samples from ducks exhibiting typical RSBFS signs across eight major duck-producing provinces in China, with the aim of clarifying the detection rates, spatiotemporal distribution, coinfection patterns, and molecular characteristics of the major pathogens. Using quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR), we systematically screened for DuCV, WPV, NDRV, and other relevant pathogens. Coupled with detailed epidemiological investigation, risk factor analysis, and phylogenetic analysis, this study aims to clarify the detection rates, spatiotemporal distribution, coinfection patterns, and molecular characteristics of the major pathogens. This study aimed to comprehensively reveal the etiological background of RSBFS and provide a scientific foundation for precise diagnosis and the development of integrated control measures for the duck industry.

Materials and methods

Sample origin and collection

From February to July 2025, a total of 300 samples, comprising 78 tissue samples and 222 swab samples, were collected from commercial meat-type duck flocks across eight provinces in China: Shandong, Anhui, Hebei, Henan, Liaoning, Guangxi, Fujian, and Guangdong (Table 1). These regions represent major areas of intensive duck production in China. Samples were obtained from ducks at various growth stages (1–7 weeks old) exhibiting the typical clinical signs of RSBFS, including dorsal skin reddening, brittle or missing feathers, and retained feather shafts (Fig. 1). A subset of samples was also randomly collected from asymptomatic ducks on the same or neighboring farms as controls for risk factor analysis. The sample set included liver, spleen, and kidney tissues from euthanized birds, as well as oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs from live birds. Pathogen detection by qPCR

Total nucleic acid (DNA and RNA) was extracted from homogenized tissue suspensions or swab eluates using an automated nucleic acid extraction system (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Multiplex qPCR assays were performed to simultaneously screen for a panel of pathogens relevant to waterfowl disease. Commercial qPCR detection kits (Xinda Gene, Qingdao, China) were used for DuCV, WPV, NDRV, duck Tembusu virus (DTMUV), duck astrovirus (DAstV) types Ⅰ and Ⅲ, Riemerella anatipestifer, and SM. All reactions were performed using qPCR master mixes (Accurate, Changsha, China) on a Gentier 96R real-time PCR system (Tianlong, Xi'an, China). Amplification was carried out following the respective kit protocols. A sample was considered positive for a specific pathogen if the cycle threshold (Ct) value was ≤ 36 with a characteristic amplification curve.

Descriptive statistical analyses

The epidemiological characteristics including spatiotemporal distribution, age-specific prevalence, and patterns of coinfection were analyzed using Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). For each epidemiological category (month, province, and age group), the detection rate was calculated as: (number of positive samples in category / total number of samples collected and detected in that category) × 100%. This approach accounts for unequal sampling across categories.

Risk factor analysis

To assess the association between pathogen infection status and RSBFS occurrence, a case-control analysis was performed. Ducks displaying clear clinical signs of RSBFS were defined as cases (n = 115), while asymptomatic ducks from the same or neighboring farms with matched age, breed, and husbandry conditions were defined as controls (n = 185). Univariate analysis was conducted by calculating odds ratios (OR)and 95% confidence intervals (CI)using a 2 × 2 contingency table for each pathogen (in cases of monoinfection) to evaluate the individual association with disease. Bivariate analysis was further performed to evaluate the disease risk associated with coinfections (e.g., WPV and DuCV, WPV and NDRV, DuCV and NDRV, and WPV, DuCV, and NDRV). Statistical significance was inferred if the 95% CI for the OR did not include 1.0.

PCR amplification and sequencing

For samples positive for DuCV, WPV, or NDRV by qPCR, conventional PCR was conducted with a T100 thermal cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA) to obtain longer sequences for phylogenetic analysis. Specific primers were designed using Oligo 7 software (Rychlik, 2007) based on conserved regions of the complete genome for DuCV, the VP3 gene for WPV, and the S1 gene for NDRV, using reference sequences retrieved from GenBank (Table 2). Primers were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). Each RT-PCR reaction (25 µL volume) using the One-step RT-PCR Kit II (Accurate Biology, Changsha, China) contained 12.5 µL of 2 × One-step RT-PCR Master Mix, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 2 µL of template DNA or RNA, and 8.5 µL of ddH₂O. Thermal cycling conditions were: reverse transcription at 50°C for 30 min; initial denaturation at 94°C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, primer-specific annealing (temperatures listed in Table 2) for 30 s, and 72°C for 1 min per kb of expected product; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. PCR products were visualized on 1.5% agarose gels, purified, and subjected to bidirectional Sanger sequencing by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Phylogenetic analysis

The obtained sequences were assembled and edited using SeqMan software. Assembled and edited sequences were processed using SeqMan software. Sequence identities were confirmed via BLASTn alignment against the GenBank nucleotide database. Multiple sequence alignments were generated with ClustalW. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using IQ-TREE 3 under the Maximum Likelihood criterion with the K3Pu+F+G4 model, selected as the best-fit model according to the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC; Wong, et al., 2026), and node support was assessed with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Trees were visualized using iTOL v6 (Letunic and Bork, 2024). Nucleotide and amino acid identity was calculated in BioEdit v7.0.9 (http://www.mbio.ncsu.edu/BioEdit/). Reference strains representing known genotypes and subgenotypes (Supplementary Table 1) were included in all analyses.

Its monthly detection rate remained consistently high, exceeding 56% from February to July. NDRV showed a similar peak in detection numbers in June (n = 46), although its highest monthly detection rate (50%) was observed in February. Bivariate analysis demonstrated that coinfections involving WPV significantly increased disease risk. WPV and DuCV coinfection was associated with an OR of 7.52 (95% CI: 3.33–16.94), and WPV and NDRV coinfection showed an even stronger association (OR = 11.31; 95% CI: 2.94–43.52). DuCV and NDRV coinfection was associated with an OR of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.39–2.59).

Although the triple coinfection (DuCV, NDRV, and WPV) showed a higher positive rate in cases (33.3%) than controls (9.4%), the OR (4.83; 95% CI: 0.97-23.97) did not reach statistical significance. The wide CI including 1.0 indicates insufficient statistical power (n = 15 cases, n = 32 controls) for definitive conclusions.

Phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 8) classified all strains within genotype I. Subgenotype Ib was the most dominant, comprising 30 strains, followed by subgenotype Ic with 12 strains. Only one strain clustered within subgenotype Ia. Discussion

This large-scale epidemiological and molecular investigation identifies DuCV, NDRV, and WPV (predominantly as NGPV) as the three core viral pathogens circulating in Chinese meat duck flocks affected by RSBFS. The high detection rate of DuCV (66.4%), often considered a primary immunosuppressive pathogen in ducks, suggests it may play a foundational role in the disease complex by compromising host defenses and creating an opportunity for other pathogens (Hong et al., 2018). However, our risk factor analysis provides a crucial nuanced insight: While DuCV is ubiquitous, monoinfection with DuCV alone was not a significant risk factor for clinical RSBFS. This finding is critical for directing control efforts and suggests that the presence of DuCV may be a necessary but insufficient condition for the full expression of the syndrome.

A key finding of this study was the identification of WPV (specifically NGPV) as the primary risk factor for RSBFS. The strong association (OR > 9) between NGPV monoinfection and disease, coupled with its known pathogenicity in causing feather disorders like short beak and dwarfism syndrome (Shen et al., 2015; Zádori et al., 1995; Zhu et al., 2025), identifies NGPV as the dominant infectious risk factor for RSBFS. These findings have direct implications for poultry production: surveillance and control programs for RSBFS must prioritize NGPV detection and immunity.

The observed interactive effects between NGPV and DuCV/NDRV may involve several plausible mechanisms, though these remain to be experimentally validated: (i) DuCV-induced immunosuppression through thymic and bursal damage (Hong et al., 2018) may compromise innate immune responses to NGPV, facilitating enhanced viral replication; (ii) NDRV-induced splenic necrosis and cytokine dysregulation (Wang et al., 2020) may create a pro-inflammatory environment that exacerbates NGPV-mediated feather follicle damage; (iii) concurrent infection may overwhelm host repair mechanisms during the critical feather growth period (3-5 weeks). The term "interactive effects" is used here to describe the statistical observation of higher disease odds in coinfected birds; biological synergy requires experimental proof. For producers, this underscores the danger of concurrent infections and highlights the importance of comprehensive flock health management that addresses multiple pathogens, not just one.

Our pathogen screening was restricted to a predefined panel of known waterfowl pathogens using targeted qPCR. While sensitive and specific for known pathogens, this approach cannot detect novel or emerging pathogens. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of clinical samples would provide an unbiased assessment of the complete virome and microbiome, potentially revealing previously unrecognized etiological contributors to RSBFS. We have preserved a subset of clinical samples for future mNGS analysis.

The epidemiological patterns offer practical guidance for on-farm management. The concentration of cases in major duck-producing provinces like Shandong, Anhui, and Henan points to high stocking density and frequent animal movement as key risk amplifiers. Strengthening biosecurity, implementing strict all-in/all-out systems, and controlling farm-to-farm transmission in these hubs should be a regional priority. The peak susceptibility age of 3 to 5 weeks coincides with a period of rapid growth, high metabolic demand for feather development, and potential decline in maternal antibodies. This identifies a critical window in the production cycle where nutritional support, stress reduction, and targeted vaccination (if available) could be most beneficial. The temporal pattern—increased WPV detection in spring and DuCV/NDRV in summer—suggests seasonal stressors (climatic shifts, heat, humidity) may influence virus dynamics, prompting the need for seasonally adjusted management practices.

From a genetic perspective, the high conservation of the NDRV S1 gene and the dominance of specific DuCV (Ib/Ic) and WPV (NGPV) genotypes are encouraging for vaccine development, with the predominance of DuCV Ib being consistent with our previous longitudinal surveillance (Li et al., 2025) and the overwhelming dominance of NGPV corroborated by our laboratory's ongoing surveillance (unpublished data). The molecular data provided here can directly inform the design of matched, regionally effective vaccines against these key viruses. The detection of a diverse WPV strain (MDGPV) also serves as a reminder for ongoing surveillance to monitor for emerging variants that could evade immunity.

It is vital to view RSBFS through a multifactorial lens, particularly in the context of intensive production. While this study focuses on viral triggers, the high detection rates of bacteria like SM and RA indicate they contribute to secondary inflammation and worsening lesions. Furthermore, non-infectious factors prevalent in commercial settings—such as nutritional deficiencies (sulfur amino acids, biotin), skin abrasions from housing, and environmental toxins—are known to cause or exacerbate feathering problems (Yang et al., 2020; Hoffman, 2002; Gan et al., 2023). Therefore, a successful control strategy must be integrated, combining: (1) pathogen-specific measures (vaccination against NGPV and DuCV, antimicrobial control of bacteria); (2) nutritional optimization to support skin and feather integrity; (3) enhanced husbandry to reduce stress and physical damage; and (4) environmental monitoring for contaminants.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a cross-sectional survey, our findings reveal epidemiological associations but do not experimentally confirm causation. NGPV pathogenicity has been demonstrated in SPF ducks (Zhu et al., 2025), including feather development disorders, but definitive confirmation specifically for the RSBFS phenotype requires controlled challenge studies. Second, the 6-month sampling window (February to July 2025) may not capture full seasonal dynamics; longitudinal surveillance spanning ≥12 months is warranted. Third, sample size imbalances across provinces limit the robustness of regional prevalence estimates from underrepresented regions (Liaoning n = 6, Guangxi n = 5, Fujian n = 3, Guangdong n = 2), which should be interpreted as exploratory only. Fourth, while our case-control design matched cases and controls for farm, age, breed, and husbandry conditions, we did not systematically measure feed composition, mycotoxin levels, or environmental parameters; non-infectious factors (nutritional deficiencies, toxins, stocking density) are established causes of feathering disorders and may confound the observed associations. Fifth, the proposed mechanisms (DuCV-induced immunosuppression, NDRV-mediated inflammation, host repair overwhelm during critical feather growth) remain hypothetical pending experimental validation. Sixth, our predefined qPCR panel, while sensitive for known pathogens, cannot detect novel pathogens. Finally, the non-significant triple-coinfection association likely reflects insufficient statistical power (post-hoc analysis indicates ∼45 cases and 90 controls with triple infection would be needed for 80% power at α=0.05 to detect OR=4.83) rather than absence of effect.

Despite these constraints, our findings establish a robust epidemiological risk factor and molecular framework for understanding RSBFS. This framework identifies NGPV as the primary statistical risk factor, quantifies the interactive effects of coinfections, characterizes the predominant circulating genotypes, and maps the spatiotemporal distribution of disease risk. These foundational data provide clear priorities for future research: SPF duck challenge studies to validate pathogenicity, expanded longitudinal surveillance to capture seasonal dynamics, comprehensive nutritional and environmental assessments to disentangle multifactorial contributions, mechanistic investigations of viral synergy, metagenomic exploration of the complete pathogenome, and multivalent vaccine development targeting the characterized genotypes (DuCV Ib/Ic, NDRV S1, NGPV).

In conclusion, this study provides actionable guidance for the poultry industry: prioritize NGPV surveillance and control, manage immunosuppressive viruses (DuCV and NDRV) to prevent additive disease effects, and integrate these measures within holistic farm management programs that address the identified spatial, temporal, and host-age risk factors. While our findings establish strong statistical associations, experimental confirmation through controlled infection studies remains the critical next step to validate pathogenesis and inform the development of effective multivalent vaccines based on the characterized strains.
