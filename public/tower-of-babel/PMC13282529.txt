Heart Health Begins with Community: An Arts-Based Report on the Experiences of Heart Health in the Moosonee Community
Authors: Jane Zhao, Justice Seidel, Greg Spence, Rachel Corston, Anne Simard, Heather Ross, Lynne Innes, Sahr Wali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Indigenous People have displayed their strength through their holistic practices and spiritual connection to the land. Despite overcoming colonial policies, they face higher rates of cardiovascular disease than the general population. Our teams conducted a sharing circle in Moosonee, Ontario, to better learn how to improve access to culturally safe care. Using the principles of two-eyed seeing, 4 themes emerged: (1) heart health is more than metrics; (2) honouring our traumas; (3) destigmatizing care through relationship building; and (4) innovative solutions start with the community. To honour the importance of Indigenous storytelling, community insights were transformed into an arts-based graphic report.

Indigenous people across Turtle Island have long drawn strength and resilience from systems of knowledge grounded in relationality, ceremony, and connection to the land.1,2 Central to this strength is the practice of storytelling, a deeply relational and intergenerational act that sustains identity, preserves cultural knowledge, and fosters healing.3 Storytelling is not merely a form of communication; it is a vital process of connection and empowerment, offering a pathway through which communities assert their truths, remember their histories, and envision futures rooted in care, continuity, and sovereignty.3,4 In the context of ongoing colonial legacies and disproportionately high rates of cardiovascular disease (CVD) among Indigenous populations, storytelling serves as not only a method of expression, but also a critical tool for health empowerment.4,5 An important aspect of storytelling is that it invites a shift in power, empowering community members to speak and share their stories in their own voices, reclaiming space and power in systems that have long silenced or distorted their truths.4 Despite the fact that First Nations people have an approximately 2.5 times higher CVD prevalence than that of non-First Nations people in Canada, Indigenous people continue to draw strength from their cultural ties, community relationships, collective resilience, and hope for a promising future.5, 6, 7

Recognizing the importance of community engagement, our teams initiated a collaborative partnership between the University Health Network (UHN) and the Weeneebayko Area Health Authority (WAHA), a regional community-focused organization providing healthcare services across 6, predominantly Cree, communities (Moose Factory, Moosonee, Attawapiskat, Fort Albany, Peawanuck, and Kashechewan), to explore the needs and priorities for heart health and wellness.8, 9, 10 Through this partnership, our collaborative teams had the privilege of conducting a sharing circle in Moosonee, Ontario, in which community members and healthcare professionals generously shared their perspectives on what it means to care for the heart, physically, emotionally, and spiritually.8

To honour the depth of Indigenous storytelling and the holistic views of heart health, insights gathered from the sharing circle were transformed into an art-based graphic report. With support and leadership from the WAHA team, the graphic report integrates Mi’kmaw Elder Albert Marshall’s reflexive principles of two-eyed seeing within the application of graphic medicine to provide a visual narrative for community stories.11 The Indigenous method of two-eyed seeing has been defined as a metaphor for negotiating between 2 cultures, as it allows for the strengths of Indigenous knowledge to be seen from one eye and the strengths of Western knowledge to be seen from the other.11 Within this paradigm, art and narrative emerge as valuable tools to bridge understandings and foster engagement across communities and care providers. Graphic medicine, in particular, offers a powerful means of combining storytelling and visual art to communicate complex health concepts and lived experiences.12 Yet, few studies have explored how graphic storytelling can be applied in a culturally grounded way to honour Indigenous voices in healthcare contexts.

Methods

Study design

A community-based explorative study was conducted in Moosonee, Ontario to better understand the community’s needs and priorities regarding heart health and wellness. Historically, Indigenous health research often has been conducted by external parties without meaningful consultation or partnership, resulting in limited benefit and, in some cases, harm to communities.13, 14, 15 To avoid these exclusionary practices, this study was grounded in community-based participatory research (CBPR), which emphasizes equity, reciprocity, and shared decision-making across all stages of research.16,17 To integrate the relational principles of CBPR into practice, sharing circles were used as the primary method of qualitative data collection, reflecting both the relational principles of CBPR and Indigenous traditions of storytelling.3,16 Beyond serving as a data-collection tool, sharing circles created a culturally grounded space for participants to share lived experiences in their own voices and to reconnect with the roots of their identity through narrative.3,18 A collaborative working group (CWG), composed of WAHA staff, a local elder, and academic researchers, also was established to guide all phases of the study. The CWG provided ongoing input into the recruitment, facilitation, and interpretation of findings. The Indigenous consensus process used to initiate the CWG and its role in shaping research directions has been described elsewhere.8 Further details regarding the methods used for this community-based study have been described in a separate paper.8

Data collection

A community-based sharing circle was conducted with a convenience sample of community members and healthcare staff.8 The circle was facilitated by a local elder alongside members of the research team, ensuring cultural appropriateness and relational accountability. The sharing circle was guided by a semistructured discussion guide, co-developed by the CWG. Open-ended prompts were used to invite reflection on the meaning of caring for the heart, across physical, emotional, spiritual, and cultural dimensions. Eight Indigenous community members and 5 healthcare professionals participated, offering stories that reflected personal experience, cultural teachings, and collective strength.8 With participant consent, the session was audio-recorded, de-identified, and transcribed verbatim using NVivo Version 12 software (Lumivero, Denver, CO). Transcripts were then shared with WAHA partners and participants for member checking, to confirm their accuracy and contextual integrity.

Data analysis

Conventional content analysis and the reflective method of two-eyed seeing was used to analyze the sharing-circle transcript.11 To ensure that community feedback was appropriately interpreted and thematically grouped, transcripts were reviewed independently by a member of the WAHA team (J.S.) and a member of the UHN team (S.W.). Initial codes were identified, drawing on both Indigenous storytelling traditions and Western qualitative analysis. These codes were then reviewed by the CWG, and themes were refined through consensus discussions.

Art-based knowledge translation

To honour the depth of community narratives and ensure that findings were accessible and meaningful, the collaborative team co-developed an art-based graphic report. Narrative selection was undertaken jointly with the CWG, whereby key excerpts and stories identified during thematic analysis were reviewed to confirm alignment with community priorities and the breadth of emergent themes. To lead the visual translation of these narratives, the CWG engaged 2 graphic artists: 1 was affiliated with the UHN (J.Z.), and 1 was from the Moose Cree First Nation (R.C.). This collaboration facilitated the integration of professional design expertise with community-based cultural knowledge. The artistic style intentionally incorporated Indigenous symbolism, cultural motifs, and recognizable community landmarks to strengthen the cultural appropriateness and enhance the interpretive depth of the visual storytelling.

Development of the report occurred over a 1-year period, during which multiple draft versions were circulated among the CWG and participants who had expressed interest in reviewing the materials. This iterative process of triangulation and validation allowed for systematic incorporation of community feedback to ensure accuracy, resonance, and representativeness. Although participants did not contribute original artwork, their feedback directly influenced the imagery, symbolism, and layout, thereby maintaining community direction and ownership over the course of the knowledge translation process. With this approach, the graphic report combined storytelling, visual art, and community validation into a knowledge-translation tool that both respected Indigenous traditions and expanded the accessibility of research findings.

Discussion

This work offers an illustrative model for how culturally grounded visual storytelling can respectfully engage with Indigenous knowledge systems while supporting strengths-based approaches to cardiovascular health and wellness. By integrating Indigenous methodologies with principles of graphic medicine, community-driven tools can be co-created to reflect the various realities shaping Indigenous health experiences (Figure 1).12,19 Although they are developed in Indigenous contexts, these methods hold important transferability to other ethnic minority populations, who often face disproportionate cardiovascular risk, systemic barriers to care, and the need to navigate complex social and cultural determinants of health. Storytelling offers a powerful mechanism to surface these contextual challenges, while simultaneously illuminating resilience and community-based strategies for wellness. By adapting visual and narrative approaches to align with specific cultural traditions and cardiovascular health priorities, researchers and clinicians can co-create tools that not only uplift minority voices but also strengthen culturally safe, relational, and responsive dialogue within healthcare systems. Centering storytelling as both method and medicine fosters deeper understanding of cardiovascular disparities, enhances intergenerational knowledge exchange, and supports more collaborative, patient-centred approaches to care.4,12 For those working at the intersection of cardiovascular health equity, reconciliation, and knowledge translation, this approach offers a transferable and respectful pathway forward, grounded in co-creation and the transformative potential of Indigenous-led storytelling.

A distinctive feature of this project was the intentional use of humour as a culturally grounded mode of communication. Community partners emphasized that humour not only is integral to everyday interactions but also serves as a powerful mechanism for approaching topics that may be emotionally heavy, historically painful, or difficult to discuss. The graphic format allowed participants to bring levity and strength to sensitive themes; the imagery of various metaphors and quotes would have been flattened if it had been presented solely as text. For example, one community member described experiencing a stroke that resulted in significant weight loss, an outcome that helped improve her diabetes, but she lightened the moment with laughter as she shared that she could no longer fit into her favourite blouse. The cover page of the graphic report not only uniquely frames the heart as the centre image, but also showcases a boat, truck, and helicopter travelling around the heart to reflect the various forms of seasonal travel barriers community members experience in obtaining care on the island of Moose Factory. This form of visual storytelling enables a form of universal connection, in which words become secondary to the images, which carried emotional resonance and facilitated shared understanding across generations and communities (Figure 1). By visualizing the complexities of cardiovascular care in graphic form, the medium itself became part of the message, amplifying community insights, inviting reflection, and offering a culturally aligned space to process experiences of heart health and healing. Notably, all materials were brought back to the community for review, refinement, and approval, ensuring that humour, symbolism, and storytelling remained authentically rooted in community voice.

Ongoing collaborative work

Building on the findings of this initial work, our collaborative team conducted a subsequent sharing circle in Moose Factory to deepen understanding of community needs and priorities for a community-led heart health program. These ongoing dialogues reinforced the importance of grounding all program components in community knowledge, relational accountability, and locally relevant experiences with cardiovascular care. To further amplify the power of visual imagery within Indigenous storytelling traditions, findings from both sharing circles were used to co-create a short, community-informed animated film depicting the lived realities of managing cardiovascular health in the James and Hudson Bay region (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d51hQ_Ap7UA). This film has been screened at the Toronto Shorts International Film Festival and at community gatherings throughout the region, supporting broad and culturally resonant knowledge mobilization.

Recognizing that food insecurity emerged as a central challenge within the heart health journey, particularly regarding the feasibility of sustaining heart-healthy diets in remote northern environments, our teams have initiated a new research and programming initiative focused on strengthening community-led approaches to nutrition. The WAHA Rivers of Plenty program (https://www.waha.ca/rivers-of-plenty/) is currently underway and brings together local Elders, community champions, and health system partners to co-develop community programming rooted in Indigenous food systems, land-based harvesting practices, and cultural teachings. By centering community expertise and traditional knowledge, this initiative seeks to expand access to culturally grounded nutrition supports while reinforcing the protective role of land, culture, and community in cardiovascular wellness. Conclusion

Visual storytelling offers a powerful approach for illuminating the lived realities of cardiovascular health in First Nations communities while fostering relational, strengths-based, and culturally safe models of care. By integrating Indigenous methodologies and graphic medicine, this approach creates a narrative space in which sensitive and historically rooted experiences can be shared in ways that honour community voice, resilience, and local knowledge. Themes emerging from the sharing circles illustrate how structural barriers, historical mistrust, and cultural strengths intersect to shape cardiovascular wellness in the James and Hudson Bay region, underscoring the need for interventions that extend beyond clinical management to include culturally grounded nutrition programming, relationship-centred care pathways, and models that combine Indigenous and Western knowledge systems. As healthcare systems across Canada advance reconciliation and equity efforts, Indigenous-led storytelling offers a respectful and transformative pathway to empowering community voices.
