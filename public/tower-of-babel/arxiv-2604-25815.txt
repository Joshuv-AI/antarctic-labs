Backstepping Observer for the Quasilinear Heat Equation with Linear Design Gains: Beyond Local Stability
M. C. Belhadjoudja, K. A. Morris

Abstract
This is a working document of a work in progress. It has long been of interest to understand when control and observer-design methodologies developed for linear dynamical systems can provide local guarantees when applied to nonlinear systems. However, beyond establishing local stability, it is also important to characterize the region of attraction and performance in terms of system parameters and design gains—something that linearization-based approaches cannot achieve. For instance, although increasing an observer gain improves the convergence rate in the linear setting (as in backstepping design), the relationship between this gain and the convergence rate in the nonlinear setting need not be monotonic; understanding these dependencies is therefore crucial for reliable operation. In light of this discussion, we consider the one-dimensional quasilinear heat equation with state-dependent heat capacity and thermal conductivity, and design a boundary-output observer based on the backstepping design for a linear heat equation with constant coefficients. Viewing the quasilinear system as a perturbation of the linear one, we establish exponential stability of the origin for the observation error dynamics in H 1 H^{1} , with an explicit region of attraction depending on the system parameters, observer gains, and the mismatch between the nonlinear diffusivity and the constant design diffusivity. Importantly, the observation error converges to zero rather than merely to a neighborhood scaling with this mismatch, even though, in contrast to backstepping-based stabilization of nonlinear PDEs, the mismatch need not decay along trajectories and may remain bounded away from zero, acting as a persistent state-dependent multiplicative perturbation. A technical challenge was to perform a sufficiently-fine Lyapunov analysis that does not yield overly conservative results such as mere boundedness of the observation error. Interestingly, while in the linear case the relationship between one of the backstepping observer gains and the convergence rate is monotonic, we show that in the nonlinear setting this is no longer the case: there may exist an optimal value of that gain, beyond which further increases deteriorate the system’s performance. Such behavior cannot be predicted without our analysis: one might expect a priori the decay rate to be freely tunable at the expense of a region of attraction that shrinks to zero as the prescribed rate tends to infinity. However, our Lyapunov analysis (supported by numerical experiments) reveals that this intuition is incorrect.

1.3
Contributions

When both
c
and
\kappa
are constant, equation (
1
) reduces to the classical linear heat equation, one of the most extensively studied PDEs in control theory, for which a wide range of observer design methods is available. It is therefore natural to ask whether observer-design techniques developed for the linear heat equation can be extended to the quasilinear case by treating the nonlinearity, roughly speaking, as a
state-dependent perturbation
, and how the resulting region of attraction depends on system parameters and observer gains. This perturbation approach has proven effective for the stabilization of one-dimensional quasilinear hyperbolic equations
[
11
]
and semilinear parabolic equations
[
25
,
15
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, this approach has not been applied to quasilinear parabolic equations such as (
1
) with general
c
and
\kappa
. Moreover, the aforementioned works focused on establishing local stability properties without discussing how the region of attraction and performance depend on system parameters. Yet it is precisely this dependence that a practitioner needs in order to tune an observer: knowing that a region of attraction exists is of limited use if one cannot determine how it shrinks or expands as the gains are varied, and how the convergence rate of the observer depends on those gains. Additionally, these works focused on the problem of stabilization. For the problem of observer design in the absence of a feedback controller, distinct challenges arise. For instance, when applying a linear controller to a nonlinear system with the goal of stabilizing an equilibrium, there is usually a mismatch between the nonlinearity of the system and some of the controller gains. In the stabilization problem, as the state converges to zero, this mismatch also vanishes. For the observer-design problem, this is not the case, and one must carefully select how to handle the nonlinear terms in a Lyapunov-based analysis, among the various possibilities, in order to avoid obtaining only an input-to-state stability result with respect to that mismatch. The objective of the present work is to address this gap by demonstrating that the linear backstepping method in
[
18
]
can be employed to design an observer for (
1
), while explicitly estimating a region of attraction, and characterizing the relationship between the observer gains, the region of attraction, and the performance of the observer.

As is customary in perturbation-based methods, since the nonlinearity is treated as a state-dependent perturbation and is not assumed to satisfy a linear growth condition or global Lipschitz continuity assumption, it becomes necessary to derive suitable upperbounds on the max norm of the observation error to study the robustness with respect to that perturbation. and
\delta_{2}
can be conservatively upper bounded as

\displaystyle\delta_{2}\leq\delta_{1}\leq\bar{\delta}:=\max_{|r|\leq M_{v}}|\alpha(r)-a|.

(24)

Note that
\bar{\delta}
depends on the choice of the observer gain
a
. In particular, one may choose
a
to minimize
\bar{\delta}
by setting

\displaystyle a:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\min_{|r|\leq M_{v}}\alpha(r)+\max_{|r|\leq M_{v}}\alpha(r)\right),

(25)

in which case
\bar{\delta}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\max_{|r|\leq M_{v}}\alpha(r)-\min_{|r|\leq M_{v}}\alpha(r)\right)
, which is the half-range of
\alpha
on
[-M_{v},M_{v}]
.

To state our main result precisely, we introduce several constants and functions that depend on the backstepping kernel
p
, its inverse
l
, the observer gains
a
and
\sigma
, and the solution bounds
\|v_{x}\|_{\infty}
and
\|v_{xx}\|_{\infty}
. Their explicit definitions are collected in Appendix
A
.

2.2
Statement of the main result

We are now in a position to state our main result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider
\Sigma
with the observer
\hat{\Sigma}
defined in (
17
). Let
\delta_{1}
,
\delta_{2}
be as in (

)–(
23

\displaystyle\gamma_{3}\delta_{2}+\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}+\gamma_{14}\delta_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle<2\sigma,

(26)

\displaystyle\gamma_{6}\delta_{2}+\gamma_{4}\delta_{1}+\gamma_{9}\delta_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle<a+\sigma,

(27)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{7}}{2}\delta_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle<\frac{a}{2},

(28)

\omega^{*}>0
, depending only on
a
,
\sigma
,
\delta_{1}
,
\delta_{2}
,
\alpha

, such that for every initial observation error
\tilde{v}_{o}\in\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{o}(\omega^{*})
, the following properties hold.

(i)

(
H^{1}
exponential stability.)
There exists a constant
\sigma^{*}>0
such that

\displaystyle|\tilde{v}(\cdot,t)|_{H^{1}}\leq\sqrt{M_{p}M_{l}}\,|\tilde{v}_{o}|_{H^{1}}\,e^{-\sigma^{*}t}\quad\forall t\geq 0,

where
M_{p}

\displaystyle\sigma^{*}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{2\sigma-\varepsilon_{6}\big(E^{*},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\big),\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad 2(a+\sigma)-2\varepsilon_{7}\big(E^{*},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\big)\right\}>0,

(30)

with

E^{*}:=\frac{M_{l}}{2}(\omega^{*})^{2},

and where the functions

(ii)

(Max-norm convergence.)
There holds

\displaystyle|\tilde{v}(\cdot,t)|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2M_{p}M_{l}}\,|\tilde{v}_{o}|_{H^{1}}\,e^{-\sigma^{*}t}\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(31)

(iii)

(Temperature reconstruction.)
Setting
\hat{T}:=\hat{c}^{-1}(\hat{v})

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt|T(\cdot,t)-\hat{T}(\cdot,t)|_{\infty}\leq C_{T}\,|\tilde{v}_{o}|_{H^{1}}\,e^{-\sigma^{*}t}\quad\forall t\geq 0,

(32)

where

More precisely,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{6}\left(\frac{M_{l}}{2}(\omega^{*})^{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\right)

\displaystyle<2\sigma,

(33)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{7}\left(\frac{M_{l}}{2}(\omega^{*})^{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\right)

\displaystyle<a+\sigma,

(34)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{8}\left(\frac{M_{l}}{2}(\omega^{*})^{2},\delta_{1}\right)

\displaystyle<\frac{a}{2}.

(35)

Prior to the proof of Theorem
2.1
, the following remarks are in order.

Remark 2.2
.

The existence of
\omega^{*}>0
verifying (
33
)-(
35
) is guaranteed by conditions (
26
)–(
28
). Indeed, note that when
E=0
, we have
\varepsilon_{j}(0,\delta_{1},\delta_{2})=0
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,5\}
, and

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{6}(0,\delta_{1},\delta_{2})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{3}\delta_{2}+\gamma_{1}\delta_{1}+\gamma_{14}\delta_{1}^{2},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{7}(0,\delta_{1},\delta_{2})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{6}\delta_{2}+\gamma_{4}\delta_{1}+\gamma_{9}\delta_{1}^{2},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{8}(0,\delta_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{7}}{2}\delta_{1}^{2}.

Hence, conditions (
26
)–(
28
) ensure that (
33
)–(
35
) are satisfied for
\omega^{*}=0
. By continuity and the nondecreasing property of
\varepsilon_{6}
,
\varepsilon_{7}
,
\varepsilon_{8}
in
E

\omega^{*}>0
such that (

)–(
35
) hold. Note that the conditions (
26
)–(
28
) are satisfied provided we can choose
a
close enough to
\alpha
and
\sigma
large enough.

\bullet

Remark 2.3
.

When
\alpha\equiv a
(the linear case), we have
\bar{\alpha}\equiv 0
and hence
\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}=0
. In this case, conditions (
26
)–(
28
) are trivially satisfied. Furthermore, we have
\varepsilon_{i}\equiv 0
for each
i\in\{6,7,8\}
and
\sigma^{*}=\sigma
. Hence, (
33
)–(
35
) hold for any
\omega^{*}>0
, and we recover the well-known global exponential stability result of the linear case at rate
\sigma
.

\bullet

Remark 2.4
.

A distinctive feature of the observer-design problem, as opposed to the stabilization problem, is that the mismatch
\bar{\alpha}(v)
need not vanish as
\tilde{v}\to 0
. Indeed, in stabilization, the state
v
itself converges to zero, and so
\bar{\alpha}(v)\to\bar{\alpha}(0)=\alpha(0)-a
. If one chooses
a=\alpha(0)
, then
\bar{\alpha}(v)\to 0
along trajectories, which greatly simplifies the analysis. In contrast, for the observer-design problem,
v
evolves independently of the observation error and
\bar{\alpha}(v)
remains generically bounded away from zero. Overcoming this persistent perturbation is an important technical challenge of this work, and it is precisely captured by conditions (
26
)–(
28
). Note that choosing
a=\alpha(0)
is no longer the natural choice for the observer problem; instead,
a
should be chosen to minimize the maximum of
|\bar{\alpha}|
over the range of
v
(cf. (
25
)).

3
Proof of the Main Result

The proof consists of five steps. In Step 1, we derive the target system for the observation error via the backstepping transformation. In Steps 2 and 3, we establish differential inequalities on the
L^{2}
norm and the
H^{1}
seminorm of the transformed error. In Step 4, we combine these to obtain a differential inequality on the
H^{1}
norm. In Step 5, we conclude
H^{1}
exponential stability.

\bullet

Step 1: The Target System

Let
\hat{v}
be the forward-complete classical solution to
\hat{\Sigma}
. We introduce the backstepping transformation

\displaystyle\tilde{v}(x)=\tilde{w}(x)-\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)\tilde{w}(y)dy,

(36)

and the corresponding inverse transformation

\displaystyle\tilde{w}(x)=\tilde{v}(x)+\int_{x}^{1}l(x,y)\tilde{v}(y)dy.

(37)

In the next lemma, we derive the equation governing
\tilde{w}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

It holds that

\Sigma_{\tilde{w}}:\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tilde{w}_{t}=a\tilde{w}_{xx}-\sigma\tilde{w}+f,\\
&\tilde{w}_{x}(0,t)=\tilde{w}_{x}(1,t)=0,\\
&\tilde{w}(x,0)=\tilde{w}_{o}(x),\end{aligned}\right.

where

\displaystyle f(x):=

\displaystyle~(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))v_{x}(x))_{x}-(\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\hat{v}_{x}(x))_{x}

\displaystyle~+\int_{x}^{1}l(x,y)(\bar{\alpha}(v(y))v_{y}(y))_{y}dy

\displaystyle~-\int_{x}^{1}l(x,y)(\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(y))\hat{v}_{y}(y))_{y}dy,

(38)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{o}(x):=\tilde{v}_{o}(x)+\int_{x}^{1}l(x,y)\tilde{v}_{o}(y)dy.

(39)

Proof 3.2
.

We let

\displaystyle f(x):=\tilde{w}_{t}(x)-a\tilde{w}_{xx}(x)+\sigma\tilde{w}(x)

(40)

to be determined, and show that it is given by (
38
). Differentiating both sides of (
36
) with respect to
t
, we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{w}_{t}(x)-\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)\tilde{w}_{t}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~a\tilde{w}_{xx}(x)-\sigma\tilde{w}(x)+f(x)-a\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)\tilde{w}_{yy}(y)dy

\displaystyle~+\sigma\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)\tilde{w}(y)dy-\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)f(y)dy.

Using integration by parts and the boundary condition
\tilde{w}_{x}(1)=0
, note that we have

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)\tilde{w}_{yy}(y)dy=

\displaystyle~\left[p(x,y)\tilde{w}_{y}(y)\right]_{y=x}^{y=1}

\displaystyle~-\int_{x}^{1}p_{y}(x,y)\tilde{w}_{y}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~p(x,1)\tilde{w}_{x}(1)-p(x,x)\tilde{w}_{x}(x)

\displaystyle~-\left[p_{y}(x,y)\tilde{w}(y)\right]_{y=x}^{y=1}

\displaystyle~+\int_{x}^{1}p_{yy}(x,y)\tilde{w}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~-p(x,x)\tilde{w}_{x}(x)-p_{y}(x,1)\tilde{w}(1)

\displaystyle~+p_{y}(x,x)\tilde{w}(x)

\displaystyle~+\int_{x}^{1}p_{yy}(x,y)\tilde{w}(y)dy.

As a result, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{t}(x)=

\displaystyle~a\tilde{w}_{xx}(x)-\big(\sigma+ap_{y}(x,x)\big)\tilde{w}(x)+f(x)

\displaystyle~+ap(x,x)\tilde{w}_{x}(x)+ap_{y}(x,1)\tilde{w}(1)

\displaystyle~+\int_{x}^{1}\tilde{w}(y)\big(\sigma p(x,y)-ap_{yy}(x,y)\big)dy

\displaystyle~-\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)f(y)dy.

Next, we differentiate both sides of (
36
) with respect to
x
, to obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{x}(x)=\tilde{w}_{x}(x)+p(x,x)\tilde{w}(x)-\int_{x}^{1}p_{x}(x,y)\tilde{w}(y)dy.

Differentiating a second time with respect to
x
, we find

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{xx}(x)=

\displaystyle~\tilde{w}_{xx}(x)+\left(\frac{d}{dx}p(x,x)\right)\tilde{w}(x)+p(x,x)\tilde{w}_{x}(x)

\displaystyle~+p_{x}(x,x)\tilde{w}(x)-\int_{x}^{1}p_{xx}(x,y)\tilde{w}(y)dy.

As a result, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{t}(x)-a\tilde{v}_{xx}(x)+p_{1}(x)\tilde{v}(1)=

\displaystyle~-\left(\sigma+2a\frac{d}{dx}p(x,x)\right)\tilde{w}

\displaystyle~+ap_{y}(x,1)\tilde{w}(1)+f(x)

\displaystyle~-\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)f(y)dy.

Given the kernel equations governing
p
, our choice of
p_{1}(x)
, and the fact that
\tilde{v}(1)=\tilde{w}(1)
due to (
36
), we conclude that

\displaystyle(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))v_{x}(x))_{x}-(\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\hat{v}_{x}(x))_{x}

\displaystyle=f(x)

\displaystyle-\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)f(y)dy.

Hence, (
38
) follows by applying the inverse backstepping transformation.

\bullet

Step 2: Differential Inequality on
t\mapsto|\tilde{w}(\cdot,t)|_{L^{2}}^{2}

We study the stability of
\{\tilde{w}=0\}
and then use (
37
) to conclude on the stability of
\{\tilde{v}=0\}
. To this end, we first introduce the functional

\displaystyle V(\tilde{w}(\cdot,t)):=\frac{1}{2}|\tilde{w}(\cdot,t)|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(41)

In the next lemma, we derive a key inequality on
\dot{V}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

It holds that

\displaystyle\dot{V}\leq\bigg\{-2\sigma+\gamma_{1}L_{\alpha}\bigg((1+|p|_{\infty})|\tilde{w}|_{\infty}\bigg)(1+|p|_{\infty})|\tilde{w}|_{\infty}

\displaystyle+\gamma_{2}L_{\alpha}\bigg((1+|p|_{\infty})|\tilde{w}|_{\infty}\bigg)+\gamma_{3}L_{\alpha}(|\tilde{w}(1)|)|\tilde{w}(1)|

\displaystyle+\gamma_{3}|\bar{\alpha}(v(1))|+\gamma_{1}|\bar{\alpha}(v)|_{\infty}\bigg\}V+\bigg\{-a

\displaystyle+\gamma_{4}L_{\alpha}\bigg((1+|p|_{\infty})|\tilde{w}|_{\infty}\bigg)(1+|p|_{\infty})|\tilde{w}|_{\infty}

\displaystyle+\gamma_{5}L_{\alpha}\bigg((1+|p|_{\infty})|\tilde{w}|_{\infty}\bigg)+\gamma_{6}L_{\alpha}(|\tilde{w}(1)|)|\tilde{w}(1)|

\displaystyle+\gamma_{6}|\bar{\alpha}(v(1))|+\gamma_{4}|\bar{\alpha}(v)|_{\infty}\bigg\}|\tilde{w}_{x}|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(42)

Proof 3.4
.

Differentiating
V
with respect to
t
, we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{V}=

\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}(x)\left[a\tilde{w}_{xx}(x)-\sigma\tilde{w}(x)\right]dx

\displaystyle~+\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}(x)f(x)dx.

(43)

Using integration by parts and the boundary conditions
\tilde{w}_{x}(0)=\tilde{w}_{x}(1)=0
, note that we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}(x)\tilde{w}_{xx}(x)dx=

\displaystyle~\left[\tilde{w}(x)\tilde{w}_{x}(x)\right]_{x=0}^{x=1}-|\tilde{w}_{x}|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~-|\tilde{w}_{x}|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(44)

As a result,

\displaystyle\dot{V}=-2\sigma V-a|\tilde{w}_{x}|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}(x)f(x)dx.

(45)

We will derive now an upperbound on
\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}(x)f(x)dx
. To do so, we start by applying integration by parts to the integral terms in the expression of
f
, to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{1}l(x,y)\big(\bar{\alpha}(v(y))v_{y}(y)\big)_{y}dy=\left[l(x,y)\bar{\alpha}(v(y))v_{y}(y)\right]_{y=x}^{y=1}

\displaystyle~\qquad\qquad\qquad-\int_{x}^{1}l_{y}(x,y)\bar{\alpha}(v(y))v_{y}(y)dy

\displaystyle=-l(x,x)\bar{\alpha}(v(x))v_{x}(x)-\left[l_{y}(x,y)\int_{0}^{v(y)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds\right]_{y=x}^{y=1}

\displaystyle+\int_{x}^{1}l_{yy}(x,y)\int_{0}^{v(y)}\bar{\alpha}(s)dsdy

\displaystyle=-l(x,x)\bar{\alpha}(v(x))v_{x}(x)-l_{y}(x,1)\int_{0}^{v(1)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds

\displaystyle+l_{y}(x,x)\int_{0}^{v(x)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds+\int_{x}^{1}l_{yy}(x,y)\int_{0}^{v(y)}\bar{\alpha}(s)dsdy.

Similarly, note that we have

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{1}l(x,y)l(x,y)\big(\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(y))\hat{v}_{y}(y)\big)_{y}dy

\displaystyle=\left[l(x,y)\bar{\alpha}(v(y))\hat{v}_{y}(y)\right]_{y=x}^{y=1}-\int_{x}^{1}l_{y}(x,y)\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(y))\hat{v}_{y}(y)dy

\displaystyle=l(x,1)\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(1))p_{10}\tilde{v}(1)-l(x,x)\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\hat{v}_{x}(x)

\displaystyle-\left[l_{y}(x,y)\int_{0}^{\hat{v}(y)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds\right]_{y=x}^{y=1}

\displaystyle+\int_{x}^{1}l_{yy}(x,y)\int_{0}^{\hat{v}(y)}\bar{\alpha}(s)dsdy

\displaystyle=l(x,1)\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(1))p_{10}\tilde{v}(1)-l(x,x)\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\hat{v}_{x}(x)

\displaystyle-l_{y}(x,1)\int_{0}^{\hat{v}(1)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds+l_{y}(x,x)\int_{0}^{\hat{v}(x)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds

\displaystyle+\int_{x}^{1}l_{yy}(x,y)\int_{0}^{\hat{v}(y)}\bar{\alpha}(s)dsdy.

As a result, we can rewrite
f
as

\displaystyle f(x)=(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))v_{x}(x))_{x}-(\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\hat{v}_{x}(x))_{x}

\displaystyle-l(x,x)\bar{\alpha}(v(x))v_{x}(x)-l_{y}(x,1)\int_{0}^{v(1)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds

\displaystyle+l_{y}(x,x)\int_{0}^{v(x)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds+\int_{x}^{1}l_{yy}(x,y)\int_{0}^{v(y)}\bar{\alpha}(s)dsdy

\displaystyle-l(x,1)\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(1))p_{10}\tilde{v}(1)+l(x,x)\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\hat{v}_{x}(x)

\displaystyle+l_{y}(x,1)\int_{0}^{\hat{v}(1)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds-l_{y}(x,x)\int_{0}^{\hat{v}(x)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds

\displaystyle-\int_{x}^{1}l_{yy}(x,y)\int_{0}^{\hat{v}(y)}\bar{\alpha}(s)dsdy.

Grouping the terms together we obtain

\displaystyle f(x)=(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))v_{x}(x))_{x}-(\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\hat{v}_{x}(x))_{x}

\displaystyle~-l(x,x)\bigg(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))v_{x}(x)-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\hat{v}_{x}(x)\bigg)

\displaystyle~-l_{y}(x,1)\int_{\hat{v}(1)}^{v(1)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds+l_{y}(x,x)\int_{\hat{v}(x)}^{v(x)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds

\displaystyle~-l(x,1)\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(1))p_{10}\tilde{v}(1)+\int_{x}^{1}l_{yy}(x,y)\int_{\hat{v}(y)}^{v(y)}\bar{\alpha}(s)dsdy.

Next, note that we have

\displaystyle\bar{\alpha}(v)v_{x}-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v})\hat{v}_{x}

\displaystyle=\bar{\alpha}(v)\tilde{v}_{x}+\left(\bar{\alpha}(v)-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v})\right)\hat{v}_{x}

\displaystyle=\bar{\alpha}(v)\tilde{v}_{x}+\left(\bar{\alpha}(v)-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v})\right)(v_{x}-\tilde{v}_{x}).

Hence,

\displaystyle f(x)=\big(\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\right)(v_{x}(x)-\tilde{v}_{x}(x))\big)_{x}

\displaystyle~+\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))\tilde{v}_{x}(x)\right)_{x}-l(x,x)\bar{\alpha}(v(x))\tilde{v}_{x}(x)

\displaystyle~-l(x,x)\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\right)(v_{x}(x)-\tilde{v}_{x}(x))

\displaystyle~-l_{y}(x,1)\int_{\hat{v}(1)}^{v(1)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds+l_{y}(x,x)\int_{\hat{v}(x)}^{v(x)}\bar{\alpha}(s)ds

\displaystyle~-l(x,1)\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(1))p_{10}\tilde{v}(1)+\int_{x}^{1}l_{yy}(x,y)\int_{\hat{v}(y)}^{v(y)}\bar{\alpha}(s)dsdy.

We can now derive an upperbound on
\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}(x)f(x)dx
.

\circ
Upperbound on
\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}\big(\left(\bar{\alpha}(v)-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v})\right)(v_{x}-\tilde{v}_{x})\big)_{x}dx

First note that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}

\displaystyle\tilde{w}(x)\big(\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\right)(v_{x}(x)-\tilde{v}_{x}(x))\big)_{x}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\bigg[\tilde{w}(x)\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\right)(v_{x}(x)-\tilde{v}_{x}(x))\bigg]_{x=0}^{x=1}

\displaystyle~-\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}_{x}(x)\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\right)(v_{x}(x)-\tilde{v}_{x}(x))dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(1))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(1))\right)p_{10}\tilde{w}(1)^{2}

\displaystyle~-\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}_{x}(x)\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\right)v_{x}(x)dx

\displaystyle~+\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}_{x}(x)\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\right)\tilde{v}_{x}(x)dx.

(46)

Given the Lipschitz continuity of
\alpha
on bounded sets (which is due to the fact that
\alpha
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
), we have

\displaystyle|\bar{\alpha}(v(x))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))|

\displaystyle=|\alpha(v(x))-\alpha(\hat{v}(x))|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\alpha}(|\tilde{v}(x)|)|\tilde{v}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\alpha}(|\tilde{v}|_{\infty})|\tilde{v}(x)|.

Hence, applying Young’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}_{x}(x)\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\right)v_{x}(x)dx\right|

\displaystyle~\quad\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left|\tilde{w}_{x}(x)\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\right)v_{x}(x)\right|dx

\displaystyle~\quad\leq|v_{x}|_{\infty}L_{\alpha}(|\tilde{v}|_{\infty})\int_{0}^{1}|\tilde{w}_{x}(x)||\tilde{v}(x)|dx

\displaystyle~\quad\leq\frac{1}{2}|v_{x}|_{\infty}L_{\alpha}(|\tilde{v}|_{\infty})|\tilde{w}_{x}|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|v_{x}|_{\infty}L_{\alpha}(|\tilde{v}|_{\infty})|\tilde{v}|_{L^{2}}^{2}

Furthermore, squaring both sides of (
36
), integrating with respect to
x
, and applying Young’s and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities, we have

\displaystyle|\tilde{v}|_{L^{2}}^{2}=

\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}\left(\tilde{w}(x)-\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)\tilde{w}(y)dy\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~|\tilde{w}|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)\tilde{w}(y)dy\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle~-2\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}(x)\left(\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)\tilde{w}(y)dy\right)dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~2|\tilde{w}|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)\tilde{w}(y)dy\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~2|\tilde{w}|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{x}^{1}p(x,y)^{2}dy\int_{x}^{1}\tilde{w}(y)^{2}dy\right)dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~4\left(1+|p|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)V,

(47)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}_{x}(x)\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\right)v_{x}(x)dx\right|

\displaystyle~\quad\leq\frac{1}{2}|v_{x}|_{\infty}L_{\alpha}(|\tilde{v}|_{\infty})|\tilde{w}_{x}|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle~\quad\quad+2|v_{x}|_{\infty}\left(1+|p|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)L_{\alpha}(|\tilde{v}|_{\infty})V.

(48)

Next, note that we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{w}_{x}(x)\left(\bar{\alpha}(v(x))-\bar{\alpha}(\hat{v}(x))\right)\tilde{v}_{x}(x)dx\right|

\displaystyle~\quad\leq L_{\alpha}(|\tilde{v}|_{\infty})|\tilde{v}|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}|\tilde{w}_{x}(x)\tilde{v}_{x}(x)|dx

\displaystyle~\quad\leq\frac{1}{2}L_{\alpha}(|\tilde{v}|_{\infty})|\tilde{v}|_{\infty}\left(|\tilde{w}_{x}|_{L^{2}}^{2}+|\tilde{v}_{x}|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

Conclusion and Research Perspectives

In this paper, we designed a backstepping observer for the quasilinear heat equation. The observer employs correction gains computed from a linear heat equation with constant diffusivity
a
, and we established exponential stability of the origin for the observation error dynamics in the
H^{1}
norm, with an explicit estimate of the region of attraction and the convergence rate. Several directions for future research are worth pursuing. First, we believe that the perturbation-based approach developed in this work could be extended to other classes of PDEs, such as coupled parabolic–elliptic systems
[
1
]
. The interesting question would then be to characterize how the region of attraction and convergence rate depend not only on the observer gains and the diffusivity mismatch, but also on the strength of the parabolic–elliptic coupling. Second, extending the present results to higher spatial dimensions is of significant practical interest. As noted in the Introduction, the main obstacle is that, in dimensions two and three, Agmon’s inequality requires estimating the
H^{2}
norm (rather than the
H^{1}
norm) of the observation error in order to control its max norm. Third, our numerical experiments indicate that the theoretical bounds, while qualitatively faithful, are quantitatively conservative. This conservatism stems in part from the choice of the simplest possible Lyapunov functional candidate: the flat
H^{1}
norm squared. It would be interesting to investigate whether finer choices of Lyapunov functional candidates could yield tighter estimates of the region of attraction and the effective convergence rate.
