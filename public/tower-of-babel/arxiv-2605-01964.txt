Pole Structure of Kerr Green’s Function
Hayato Motohashi, Yuto Suichi

Abstract
We investigate the pole structure of Kerr black-hole perturbations in the frequency domain, focusing on the building blocks of the Green’s function for the radial Teukolsky equation: the homogeneous radial solutions, the connection coefficients, and the Green’s function itself. We show that the homogeneous solutions and the local connection coefficients develop simple poles at the Matsubara frequencies, thereby establishing the Matsubara pole structure explicitly within the Teukolsky formalism for asymptotically flat subextremal Kerr black holes. At the level of the local fixed-sector connection formula, the explicit Matsubara-pole factors cancel in the ratio of connection coefficients entering a decomposed Green-function contribution. We also identify higher-order zero-frequency singularities in the decomposed Green-function contributions, which scale as ω − 2 ​ l − 1 \omega^{-2l-1} and cancel collectively in the total radial Green’s function. These results clarify how Matsubara poles and sectoral zero-frequency singularities arise in the Teukolsky formalism and provide a frequency-domain foundation for understanding prompt response in time-domain ringdown waveforms in Kerr spacetime.



I
Introduction

Black-hole ringdown has become a central target of gravitational-wave black-hole spectroscopy
Kokkotas and Schmidt 1999
;
Berti et al. 2009
;
Berti et al. 2025
.
At the level of linear perturbation theory, it is conventionally understood in terms of poles and branch cuts of the frequency-domain Green’s function.
In the time domain, these structures underlie the familiar quasinormal-mode (QNM) ringing and Price-law tails, and therefore provide the basic analytic framework for linear black-hole response
Price 1972
;
Leaver 1986
;
Andersson 1997
.
By contrast, the early-time, or prompt, response has only recently begun to be understood in comparable detail.

The prompt-response regime is important for several reasons.
First, the signal is typically stronger at earlier times than in the later ringdown, so improving its theoretical description is directly relevant for gravitational-wave phenomenology.
Second, it characterizes the transition between the source-dependent merger signal and the QNM-dominated regime, thereby affecting the choice of ringdown start time and the interpretation of QNM excitation amplitudes.
Third, a precise understanding of the prompt part of the linear response is indispensable for identifying genuinely nonlinear features in the near-merger waveform: without first determining what is already encoded in the linear Green’s function, one cannot unambiguously isolate nonlinear effects.

Recent progress indicates that prompt response can be associated with several distinct kinds of singular structures, depending on the asymptotic geometry and on how the Green’s function is decomposed.
For short-range effective potentials, such as the Pöschl–Teller model and the Schwarzschild–de Sitter case, the prompt response can be described by Matsubara, or Euclidean, modes on the imaginary axis, and the corresponding mode sum converges in the early-time region
Kuntz 2025
;
Arnaudo et al. 2025
.
This terminology is borrowed from finite-temperature field theory, where Matsubara frequencies are fixed by the inverse temperature
Le Bellac 1996
.
In the black-hole context, the relevant temperature is the Hawking temperature.
In the asymptotically flat limit, another family of imaginary-axis poles present in the Schwarzschild–de Sitter problem, distinct from the Matsubara modes, condenses into the branch-cut structure responsible for Price-law behavior
Arnaudo and Withers 2025
.

For asymptotically flat Schwarzschild spacetime, a complementary picture has emerged.
The branch-cut side of this problem has a long history
Leaver 1986
;
Leung et al. 2003
;
Casals et al. 2013
;
Casals and Ottewill 2013
;
Casals and Ottewill 2015
, and recent work has substantially refined the Green-function perspective.
On the time-domain side, recent studies have developed direct and regularized calculations of Schwarzschild Green’s functions, clarifying how singular features of wave propagation appear in the time-domain response
Aruquipa and Casals 2026
;
Aruquipa et al. 2026
.
On the frequency-domain side, several analyses have shown that the prompt response and the late-time tail can be organized in terms of zero-frequency poles, imaginary-axis branch cuts, and causal decompositions of the Green’s function
De Amicis and Cannizzaro 2026
;
Su et al. 2026
;
Rosato et al. 2026
.
In particular, the prompt response has been related to the zero-frequency structure rather than to a large-frequency arc, while branch-cut contributions naturally account for prompt and tail components depending on the contour and causal regime.
These developments strongly suggest that prompt response is controlled by nontrivial frequency-domain singular structures and is not a negligible correction to ringdown.

Related time-domain studies have also discussed source-dependent early-time features under names such as horizon modes
Mino and Brink 2008
;
Zimmerman and Chen 2011
, redshift mode
De Amicis et al. 2026
, and direct waves
Oshita et al. 2025
.
In addition, analyses of plunging sources have shown that prompt components can be quantitatively important in waveform reconstruction
Ma 2026
.
These phenomena may be physically relevant to the early-time signal and, in some cases, provide an efficient description of waveform components beyond the standard QNM-plus-tail picture.
At the same time, such source-dependent signals are obtained by convolving the Green’s function with the source or initial data.
Therefore, clarifying the source-independent singular structure of the Green’s function itself is a necessary step toward understanding how these early-time components arise in concrete waveforms.

From the viewpoint of the Green’s function and its role in ringdown, the current picture is that Matsubara poles, branch cuts, and zero-frequency singularities may all be relevant to prompt response, with the most useful description depending on the asymptotic structure of the spacetime, the contour decomposition, and the physical regime under consideration.
What remains largely unexplored, in particular, is the corresponding fixed-sector analytic structure for rotating black holes.

The Kerr case is qualitatively richer.
In rotating backgrounds, the frequencies naturally associated with thermal, or Matsubara-like, structures are shifted away from the imaginary axis by the horizon angular velocity, as is familiar from Kerr/CFT and hidden-conformal-symmetry analyses
Chen and Chu 2010
.
However, to the best of our knowledge, in asymptotically flat subextremal Kerr there is still no first-principles derivation of how such Matsubara frequencies appear, or fail to appear, in the building blocks of the Green’s function constructed from the Teukolsky equation.
Likewise, the zero-frequency singularities in Kerr have not been derived directly from the Teukolsky equation in a form suitable for analyzing their role in the Green’s function.

In this paper, we analyze the analytic structure of the basic building blocks of the Kerr Green’s function by working directly with the Teukolsky equation: the homogeneous radial solutions, the connection coefficients, and the Green’s function assembled from them.
In particular, we clarify the Matsubara pole structure and the zero-frequency singularities of the homogeneous radial Teukolsky solutions, the associated connection coefficients, and the decomposed Green-function contributions.
We use the confluent-Heun formulation to expose the local analytic structure of the homogeneous solutions and connection problem, and the Mano–Suzuki–Takasugi (MST) method
Mano et al. 1996a
;
Mano and Takasugi 1997
;
Sasaki and Tagoshi 2003
to control the low-frequency behavior.
Our focus is on a first-principles characterization of the fixed-sector frequency-domain pole structure, which provides the analytic foundation for future studies of prompt response in rotating black holes.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In §

, we review the radial Teukolsky equation and fix our notation.
In §

, we recast it in the form of the confluent Heun equation; basic properties of the confluent Heun functions are summarized in Appendix
A
, and the pole/residue formulae used in our analysis are derived in Appendix
B
.
In §

, we clarify the Matsubara pole structure and zero-frequency behavior of the homogeneous solutions, the connection coefficients, and the Green’s function for Kerr black-hole perturbations within the Teukolsky formalism, using the confluent-Heun formulation together with the MST results summarized in Appendix
C
.
For comparison, Appendix
D
analyzes the corresponding structures in the Regge–Wheeler (RW) formalism for Schwarzschild black holes using the MST representation.
§
V
is devoted to conclusions and discussion.
We adopt geometric units
G=c=1
throughout.

II
Teukolsky equation

In this section, we briefly review the Teukolsky formalism for Kerr black-hole perturbations, mostly following Ref.
Sasaki and Tagoshi 2003
.
The radial Teukolsky equation is given by

\displaystyle\Biggl[\Delta^{-s}\frac{\differential}{\differential r}\biggl(\Delta^{s+1}\frac{\differential}{\differential r}\biggr)+\frac{K^{2}-2is(r-M)K}{\Delta}

\displaystyle+4is\omega r-\lambdabar\Biggr]{}_{s}R_{lm}={}_{s}T_{lm},

(1)

where

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=(r^{2}+a^{2})\omega-ma,

(2)

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=r^{2}-2Mr+a^{2},

\displaystyle=(r-r_{+})(r-r_{-}),

(3)

\displaystyle r_{\pm}

\displaystyle=M\pm\sqrt{M^{2}-a^{2}},

(4)

\displaystyle\lambdabar

\displaystyle={}_{s}A_{lm}-2ma\omega+a^{2}\omega^{2},

(5)

{}_{s}A_{lm}
is the separation constant, and
{}_{s}T_{lm}
is the source term.

The radial Teukolsky equation can be solved by the Green’s function method, where the Green’s function is constructed from two linearly independent homogeneous solutions.
Although any pair of linearly independent homogeneous solutions may be used, a conventional choice is the “in” solution
{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{in}}_{lm}
, which satisfies

{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{in}}_{lm}(r)\to\begin{cases}\Delta^{-s}e^{-ipr_{*}},&(r\to r_{+}),\\
{}_{s}B^{\mathrm{ref}}_{lm}r^{-1-2s}e^{i\omega r_{*}}+{}_{s}B^{\mathrm{inc}}_{lm}r^{-1}e^{-i\omega r_{*}},&(r\to\infty),\end{cases}

(6)

and the “up” solution
{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{up}}_{lm}
, which satisfies

{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{up}}_{lm}(r)\to\begin{cases}{}_{s}C^{\mathrm{up}}_{lm}e^{ipr_{*}}+{}_{s}C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{lm}\Delta^{-s}e^{-ipr_{*}},&(r\to r_{+}),\\
r^{-1-2s}e^{i\omega r_{*}},&(r\to\infty),\end{cases}

(7)

where

p=\omega-m\Omega_{+},\quad\Omega_{\pm}=\frac{a}{2Mr_{\pm}},

(8)

and
{}_{s}B^{\mathrm{inc/ref}}_{lm}
and
{}_{s}C^{\mathrm{up/ref}}_{lm}
are complex asymptotic amplitudes.
Note that here we normalize
{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{in}}_{lm}
and
{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{up}}_{lm}
so that the transmission amplitudes are unity; these transmission amplitudes are denoted by
{}_{s}B^{\mathrm{trans}}_{lm}
and
{}_{s}C^{\mathrm{trans}}_{lm}
in the literature
Sasaki and Tagoshi 2003
.
The tortoise coordinate
r_{*}
is defined by
\differential r_{*}/\differential r=(r^{2}+a^{2})/\Delta
, and we fix the integration constant by the conventional choice

r_{*}=r+\frac{2Mr_{+}}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\ln\frac{r-r_{+}}{2M}-\frac{2Mr_{-}}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\ln\frac{r-r_{-}}{2M}.

(9)

The other two homogeneous solutions are defined analogously:

\displaystyle{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{out}}_{lm}

\displaystyle\to e^{ipr_{*}},

\displaystyle(r\to r_{+}),

(10)

\displaystyle{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{down}}_{lm}

\displaystyle\to r^{-1}e^{-i\omega r_{*}},

\displaystyle(r\to\infty),

(11)

and can be constructed from the corresponding spin-flipped solutions
{}_{-s}R^{\mathrm{in}}_{lm}
and
{}_{-s}R^{\mathrm{up}}_{lm}
through the conjugation relations

\displaystyle{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{out}}_{lm}(\omega,r)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{-s}(r)\bigl[{}_{-s}R^{\mathrm{in}}_{lm}(\omega^{*},r)\bigr]^{*},

(12)

\displaystyle{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{down}}_{lm}(\omega,r)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{-s}(r)\bigl[{}_{-s}R^{\mathrm{up}}_{lm}(\omega^{*},r)\bigr]^{*},

(13)

where we have written the
\omega
dependence explicitly.

From now on, for simplicity, we omit the subscripts
s
and
lm
whenever no confusion arises.
At infinity,
R^{\mathrm{up}}
and
R^{\mathrm{down}}
form the outgoing and ingoing basis, respectively.
Therefore, the “in” solution can be decomposed as

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{in}}(r)=B^{\mathrm{ref}}R^{\mathrm{up}}(r)+B^{\mathrm{inc}}R^{\mathrm{down}}(r).

(14)

In this sense, the asymptotic amplitudes
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
and
B^{\mathrm{ref}}
may be regarded as connection coefficients between the horizon-adapted solution and the asymptotic basis at infinity.

As mentioned above, the Green’s function is constructed from
R^{\mathrm{in}}
and
R^{\mathrm{up}}
as

\displaystyle G(r,r^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{s}(r^{\prime})}{2i\omega B^{\mathrm{inc}}}\begin{cases}R^{\mathrm{up}}(r)R^{\mathrm{in}}(r^{\prime}),&(r>r^{\prime}),\\
R^{\mathrm{in}}(r)R^{\mathrm{up}}(r^{\prime}),&(r<r^{\prime}).\end{cases}

(15)

The inhomogeneous solution of the radial Teukolsky equation (
II
) in the frequency domain is then given by

\displaystyle R(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{+}}^{\infty}\differential r^{\prime}G(r,r^{\prime})T(r^{\prime}).

(16)

Let us focus on the case
r>r^{\prime}
.
Substituting (
14
) into (
15
), we decompose the Green’s function as

\displaystyle G(r,r^{\prime})=G^{(+)}(r,r^{\prime})+G^{(-)}(r,r^{\prime}),

(17)

where

\begin{split}G^{(+)}(r,r^{\prime})&=\frac{B^{\mathrm{ref}}}{2i\omega B^{\mathrm{inc}}}\Delta^{s}(r^{\prime})R^{\mathrm{up}}(r^{\prime})R^{\mathrm{up}}(r),\\
G^{(-)}(r,r^{\prime})&=\frac{1}{2i\omega}\Delta^{s}(r^{\prime})R^{\mathrm{down}}(r^{\prime})R^{\mathrm{up}}(r).\end{split}

(18)

This decomposition is useful for analyzing separate contributions to the Green’s function and for discussing their causal interpretation
Andersson 1997
;
Kuntz 2025
;
Arnaudo et al. 2025
;
Su et al. 2026
.
Distinguishing these two contributions is crucial for clarifying the time-domain signal in the early-time regime,

|r_{*}-r^{\prime}_{*}|<t-t^{\prime}<|r_{*}+r^{\prime}_{*}|
, where they enter the contour analysis in different ways.

III
Confluent Heun equation

Both the angular and radial Teukolsky equations can be transformed into confluent Heun equations.
Here we focus on the radial equation.
We introduce the new independent variable

z=\frac{r-r_{-}}{r_{+}-r_{-}}.

(19)

We then redefine the radial function as

R(r)=z^{\nu_{1}}(z-1)^{\nu_{2}}e^{\nu_{3}z}\psi(z),

(20)

where

\begin{split}\nu_{1}&=\frac{i(\omega-m\Omega_{-})}{2\kappa_{-}},\quad\nu_{2}=-s-\frac{i(\omega-m\Omega_{+})}{2\kappa_{+}},\\
\nu_{3}&=\frac{2i\kappa_{+}a\omega}{\Omega_{+}}.\end{split}

(21)

The surface gravities are

\kappa_{\pm}=\frac{1}{4M}-M\Omega_{\pm}^{2}=\frac{r_{\pm}-r_{\mp}}{4Mr_{\pm}}.

(22)

Thus,
\kappa_{+}>0
, whereas
\kappa_{-}<0
in this convention.
The Hawking temperature is determined by the outer-horizon surface gravity,

T_{H}=\frac{\kappa_{+}}{2\pi}.

(23)

This transformation maps the regular singular points
r=r_{-},r_{+}
and the irregular singular point
r=\infty
to
z=0,1
, and
\infty
, respectively.
Then
\psi(z)
satisfies a confluent Heun equation,

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime\prime}+\left(\frac{\gamma}{z}+\frac{\delta}{z-1}+\epsilon\right)\psi^{\prime}+\frac{\alpha z-q}{z(z-1)}\psi=0,

(24)

where

\begin{split}q&={}_{s}A_{lm}-(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})(1+2s+\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})\\
&~~~+(1+2s+2\nu_{1})\nu_{3}+\frac{(r_{+}^{2}+3r_{-}r_{+}+3r_{-}^{2})\nu_{3}^{2}}{(r_{+}-r_{-})^{2}},\\
\alpha&=2\nu_{3}(1+3s+2\nu_{2}),\quad\gamma=1+s+2\nu_{1},\\
\delta&=1+s+2\nu_{2},\quad\epsilon=2\nu_{3}.\end{split}

(25)

Around each singular point
z=z_{s}
, we can construct two linearly independent solutions
\psi_{j}^{(z_{s})}
with
j=1,2
in terms of confluent Heun functions.
The four solutions are written as

\begin{split}\psi_{\mathrm{in}}(z)&=N_{\mathrm{in}}\psi_{1}^{(1)},\quad\psi_{\mathrm{out}}(z)=N_{\mathrm{out}}\psi_{2}^{(1)},\\
\psi_{\mathrm{up}}(z)&=N_{\mathrm{up}}\psi_{1}^{(\infty)},\quad\psi_{\mathrm{down}}(z)=N_{\mathrm{down}}\psi_{2}^{(\infty)},\end{split}

(26)

where the normalization constants are

\begin{split}N_{\mathrm{in}}&=(r_{+}-r_{-})^{-2s}\Big(\frac{2M}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\Big)^{2iMp}e^{-ipr_{+}}e^{-i\omega(r_{+}-r_{-})},\\
N_{\mathrm{out}}&=\Big(\frac{2M}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\Big)^{-2iMp}e^{ipr_{+}}e^{-i\omega(r_{+}-r_{-})}e^{-i\pi(1-\delta)},\\
N_{\mathrm{up}}&=(r_{+}-r_{-})^{-1-2s}\Big(\frac{2M}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\Big)^{-2iM\omega}e^{i\omega r_{-}},\\
N_{\mathrm{down}}&=(r_{+}-r_{-})^{-1}\Big(\frac{2M}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\Big)^{2iM\omega}e^{-i\omega r_{-}}.\end{split}

(27)

Here,
\psi_{j}^{(1)}
and
\psi_{j}^{(\infty)}
are provided in Appendix
A
and are written in terms of
\mathrm{HeunC}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z)
and
\mathrm{HeunC}_{\infty}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z)
, respectively.
Following the notation in Ref.
Bonelli et al. 2023
,
\mathrm{HeunC}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z)
denotes the standard confluent Heun function whose expansion around
z=0
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{HeunC}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z)=1-\frac{q}{\gamma}z+\order{z^2},

(28)

and
\mathrm{HeunC}_{\infty}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z)
denotes a different function whose asymptotic expansion around the irregular singularity
z=\infty
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{HeunC}_{\infty}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z^{-1})

(29)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\alpha^{2}-(\gamma+\delta-1)\alpha\epsilon+(\alpha-q)\epsilon^{2}}{\epsilon^{3}}z^{-1}+\order{z^{-2}}.

Explicit series expansions of these functions and their key properties are summarized in Appendix
A
.
We also derive pole and residue formulae for the confluent Heun function in Appendix
B
.

Alternatively, the homogeneous solutions may be represented in the Mano–Suzuki–Takasugi (MST) formalism as infinite series of confluent hypergeometric functions
Mano et al. 1996a
;
Mano and Takasugi 1997
;
Sasaki and Tagoshi 2003
.
This representation is often advantageous because it yields convergent series with a wider domain of validity than a local asymptotic expansion and is well suited to analytic continuation and low-frequency expansions (see Appendix
C
).

Using the formulation developed in the previous sections, we now analyze the analytic structure of the basic building blocks of the Kerr Green’s function: the homogeneous solutions (§
IV.1
), the connection coefficients (§
IV.2
), and the Green’s function itself (§
IV.3
).

As a guide to the discussion, we first present in Fig.
1
the pole and zero structure obtained for a representative parameter set with
(s,l,m)=(-2,2,2)
and
a/M=0.7
.
In Fig.
1
, we display only the QNM (blue), Matsubara (orange), and zero-frequency (purple) structures relevant to the present discussion.
For clarity, the branch-cut structure is omitted, as are any additional poles or zeros that may be present.

While the building blocks possess explicit Matsubara poles, these poles are absent in the plotted fixed-sector quantities
G^{(\pm)}
.
In this sense, the ratio entering
G^{(+)}
exhibits an explicit factor cancellation: the Matsubara-pole factors present in both
B^{\mathrm{ref}}
and
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
cancel in the quotient.
The zero-frequency structure is more subtle: the decomposed contributions
G^{(+)}
and
G^{(-)}
possess higher-order zero-frequency singularities, whereas these singularities cancel in the total Green’s function
G
.

Below, we explain how these structures arise analytically and verify numerically that the corresponding singularities are indeed realized.
For the numerical checks, we make use of the Black Hole Perturbation Toolkit
BHP
.

Some of these structures persist in other master-variable formulations, whereas others are tied to the Teukolsky variable and its normalization.
In particular, the RW comparison in Appendix
D
shows that, although the spin-weight-dependent form of the local Matsubara frequencies and of the homogeneous-solution pole orders is reorganized in the RW variable, the explicit Matsubara-pole factors again cancel in the decomposed Green-function contribution, and the Green-function-level low-frequency order counting agrees with the Teukolsky result.

IV.1
Homogeneous solutions

We begin with the homogeneous solutions,
R^{\mathrm{in}}
,
R^{\mathrm{out}}
,
R^{\mathrm{up}}
, and
R^{\mathrm{down}}
, which form the first building block of the Kerr Green’s function.
From the poles of the confluent Heun function summarized in Appendix
B.1
,
R^{\mathrm{in}}
develops first-order singularities at
\delta=-k
with
k=0,1,2,\ldots
.
This condition defines the frequencies

{}_{s}\omega_{mk}^{(-)}=m\Omega_{+}-i(1-s+k)\kappa_{+},\quad(k=0,1,2,\ldots).

(30)

The same pole condition can also be derived independently in the MST formalism (see Appendix
C.1
).

The frequencies (
30
) are precisely the Matsubara frequencies of Kerr black holes.
This reproduces, directly from the radial Teukolsky equation, the frequency pattern previously suggested in the extremal Kerr/CFT context
Chen and Chu 2010
, and extends it to a first-principles analytic derivation for generic subextremal Kerr and arbitrary spin weight
s
.

Their spacing in the imaginary direction is set by
\kappa_{+}=2\pi T_{H}
, while their real part is shifted by
m\Omega_{+}
due to the rotation of the horizon.
Equivalently, the natural thermal frequency near the horizon is measured with respect to the co-rotating Killing vector
\partial_{t}+\Omega_{+}\partial_{\phi}
, so a mode with azimuthal number
m
appears through the combination
\omega-m\Omega_{+}
.
This shift is analogous to the frequency shift produced by a chemical potential in finite-temperature field theory
Le Bellac 1996
.

Similarly, from (
26
) we note that
R^{\mathrm{out}}
develops first-order singularities at
2-\delta=-k
with
k=0,1,2,\ldots
, which defines

{}_{s}\omega_{mk}^{(+)}=m\Omega_{+}+i(1+s+k)\kappa_{+},\quad(k=0,1,2,\ldots).

(31)

These frequencies form the counterpart of the Matsubara branch associated with the “out” solution.
They are also consistent with (
12
), i.e.,

{}_{s}\omega_{mk}^{(+)}={}_{-s}\omega_{mk}^{(-)*}.

(32)

Unlike the QNM frequencies
{}_{s}\omega_{lmn}
, the Matsubara frequencies
{}_{s}\omega_{mk}^{(\pm)}
identified here are independent of the angular quantum number
l
.
At the same time, their detailed form should be understood within the Teukolsky-variable representation and its normalization: in this formulation they depend linearly on the spin weight
s
, and the superscripts
(\pm)
are merely labels adapted to the local horizon solutions
R^{\mathrm{in}}
and
R^{\mathrm{out}}
.
They should not be interpreted as a universal division into lower- and upper-half-plane Matsubara sectors; indeed, depending on
s
, the frequencies in the set (
31
) need not all have the same sign of the imaginary part, as exemplified in Fig.
1
(b) for
s=-2
.
These features should be distinguished from formulations based on RW master variables [see (
115
) and (
116
)] or asymptotically de Sitter problems in the literature, where the corresponding Matsubara structure may be organized differently.

Using the confluent-Heun analysis, the residues of
R^{\mathrm{in}}
and
R^{\mathrm{out}}
can be analytically obtained as

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Res}_{\omega={}_{s}\omega^{(-)}_{mk}}{}_{s}R_{lm}^{\mathrm{in}}

\displaystyle=\left.i\kappa_{+}N_{\mathrm{in}}z^{\nu_{1}}(z-1)^{\nu_{2}}e^{\nu_{3}z}\operatorname*{Res}_{\delta=-k}\psi_{1}^{(1)}\right|_{\omega={}_{s}\omega^{(-)}_{mk}},

(33)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Res}_{\omega={}_{s}\omega^{(+)}_{mk}}{}_{s}R_{lm}^{\mathrm{out}}

\displaystyle=\left.-i\kappa_{+}N_{\mathrm{out}}z^{\nu_{1}}(z-1)^{\nu_{2}}e^{\nu_{3}z}\operatorname*{Res}_{2-\delta=-k}\psi_{2}^{(1)}\right|_{\omega={}_{s}\omega^{(+)}_{mk}}.

(34)

Here, the residues of
\psi_{1}^{(1)}
and
\psi_{2}^{(1)}
are analytically written down in terms of the confluent Heun functions as given in (
78
) and (
79
), respectively.
The same residues can be obtained semianalytically in the MST formalism as

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Res}_{\omega={}_{s}\omega^{(-)}_{mk}}{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{in}}_{lm}

\displaystyle=\left.i\kappa_{+}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{in}}_{lm}}{\Gamma(\delta)}\right|_{\omega={}_{s}\omega^{(-)}_{mk}},

(35)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Res}_{\omega={}_{s}\omega^{(+)}_{mk}}{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{out}}_{lm}

\displaystyle=\left.-i\kappa_{+}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{out}}_{lm}}{\Gamma(2-\delta)}\right|_{\omega={}_{s}\omega^{(+)}_{mk}}.

(36)

By contrast, the transmission-normalized solutions
R^{\mathrm{in}}_{lm}
and
R^{\mathrm{out}}_{lm}
are regular at
\omega=0
for fixed
r>r_{+}
, as follows from the MST analysis in Appendix
C.1
.

On the other hand,
R^{\mathrm{up}}
exhibits a distinct singular structure at zero frequency.
As demonstrated in Appendix
C
, the low-frequency expansion of the MST representation gives, for fixed
r>r_{+}
,

\displaystyle{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{up}}_{lm}(r)

\displaystyle={}_{s}Q_{lm}(r)\omega^{s-l}+\order{\omega^{s-l+1}},

(37)

where the leading-order coefficient
{}_{s}Q_{lm}(r)
is explicitly given in (
109
).
This singularity is different in origin from the Matsubara poles discussed above: it arises from the degenerate low-frequency limit of the MST expansion rather than from the pole condition
\delta=-k
of the local Heun solution.

In particular, for the gravitational lowest multipole
(s,l)=(-2,2)
,
R^{\mathrm{up}}
has a fourth-order pole as

\displaystyle{}_{-2}R^{\mathrm{up}}_{2m}(r)

IV.2
Connection coefficients

We next turn to the connection coefficients, which constitute the second building block entering the Kerr Green’s function.
The connection coefficients
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
and
B^{\mathrm{ref}}
can be computed by a series expansion in the MST formalism.
Alternatively, we can make use of the connection formula of Heun functions recently obtained
Bonelli et al. 2023
.

As is well known, the zeros of
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
correspond to the QNM frequencies and can be computed, for example, by the continued-fraction method.
On the other hand, as discussed in Appendix
B.2
, within a fixed asymptotic sector the explicit connection formulae for both
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
and
B^{\mathrm{ref}}
contain the common factor
\Gamma(\delta)
.
Hence, they develop poles at

\delta=-k
with
k=0,1,2,\ldots
,
which precisely coincide with the Matsubara poles
{}_{s}\omega^{(-)}_{mk}
given in (
30
).
The same pole condition can also be derived independently in the MST formalism (see Appendix
C.2
).

This is not accidental:
R^{\mathrm{in}}
is the local solution around the outer horizon, while
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
and
B^{\mathrm{ref}}
are the connection coefficients that express this same local solution in the asymptotic basis at infinity.
Therefore, within a fixed asymptotic sector, singularities in the normalization of the local horizon solution are naturally inherited by the corresponding connection coefficients unless cancelled by an additional zero in the connection problem.
In this sense, the same pole condition is encoded both in the local horizon solution
R^{\mathrm{in}}
and in its connection to the basis at infinity.

As demonstrated in Appendices
B.2
and
C.2
, we can derive semianalytic formulae for the residue of

and
B^{\mathrm{ref}}
at the Matsubara poles,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Res}_{\omega={}_{s}\omega^{(-)}_{mk}}{}_{s}B^{\mathrm{inc}}_{lm}

\displaystyle=\left.i\kappa_{+}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{{}_{s}B^{\mathrm{inc}}_{lm}}{\Gamma(\delta)}\right|_{\omega={}_{s}\omega^{(-)}_{mk}},

(41)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Res}_{\omega={}_{s}\omega^{(-)}_{mk}}{}_{s}B^{\mathrm{ref}}_{lm}

\displaystyle=\left.i\kappa_{+}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{{}_{s}B^{\mathrm{ref}}_{lm}}{\Gamma(\delta)}\right|_{\omega={}_{s}\omega^{(-)}_{mk}}.

(42)

On the other hand, the MST low-frequency analysis directly shows that the connection coefficients possess zero-frequency singularities,

\displaystyle{}_{s}B^{\mathrm{inc}}_{lm}

\displaystyle=\order{\omega^{s-l-1}},\qquad(\omega\to 0),

(43)

\displaystyle{}_{s}B^{\mathrm{ref}}_{lm}

\displaystyle=\order{\omega^{-s-l-1}},\qquad(\omega\to 0),

(44)

for generic parameters.
For the gravitational lowest multipole
(s,l)=(-2,2)
, this gives a fifth-order pole in
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
and a first-order pole in
B^{\mathrm{ref}}

IV.3
Green’s function

We now combine the previous ingredients into the Green’s function itself and clarify how its pole-zero structures involve QNMs, Matsubara modes, and zero-frequency singularities.

The contribution
G^{(+)}
in (
18
) contains the ratio
B^{\mathrm{ref}}/B^{\mathrm{inc}}
.
At the level of the local connection formula in a fixed asymptotic sector, both
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
and
B^{\mathrm{ref}}
contain the same explicit factor
\Gamma(\delta)
.
Therefore, the explicit Matsubara-pole factors associated with
\delta=-k
cancel in the ratio
B^{\mathrm{ref}}/B^{\mathrm{inc}}
and do not appear explicitly in
G^{(+)}
.
The QNM poles, however, remain: since
G^{(+)}\propto 1/B^{\mathrm{inc}}
, the zeros of
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
generate poles of
G^{(+)}
.

Let us next consider the low-frequency behavior.
Using (
37
), (
40
), (
43
), and (
44
), we obtain from (
18
)

\displaystyle G^{(\pm)}

\displaystyle=\order{\omega^{-2l-1}}.

(45)

Thus, both
G^{(+)}
and
G^{(-)}
possess the same-order zero-frequency singularity.
For the gravitational lowest multipole
(s,l)=(-2,2)
, this corresponds to a fifth-order zero-frequency pole in each decomposed contribution.

The total Green’s function
G
, however, is regular at zero frequency in the present fixed-radius, transmission-normalized radial problem.
Indeed, from the original expression (
15
), together with the low-frequency dependence of
R^{\mathrm{in}}
,
R^{\mathrm{up}}
, and
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
clarified above, we obtain

G=\order{\omega^0}.

(46)

Therefore, the negative-power terms in
G^{(+)}+G^{(-)}
must cancel collectively, including possible subleading singular terms, leaving the total Green’s function regular at
\omega=0
.
The zero-frequency poles shown for
G^{(+)}
and
G^{(-)}
in Fig.
1
should thus be understood as poles of the decomposed contributions, not as poles of the total Green’s function.

We numerically verify these zero-frequency behaviors of the Green’s function.
As shown in Fig.
5
, the numerical results are consistent with the analytical results (
45
) and (
46
).

The cancellation of the zero-frequency pole in the total Green’s function does not necessarily mean that the zero-frequency singularities of the decomposed contributions are irrelevant to the time-domain signal.
As mentioned above, in the early-time regime
|r_{*}-r^{\prime}_{*}|<t-t^{\prime}<|r_{*}+r^{\prime}_{*}|
, the decomposed components
G^{(+)}
and
G^{(-)}
enter the contour analysis in different ways.
Therefore, the zero-frequency poles of
G^{(\pm)}
may still be important for an analytical understanding of the prompt response.

The same order-counting structure is also found in the RW formulation.
As shown in Appendix
D
, the RW MST representation gives

\displaystyle G_{\rm RW}^{(\pm)}=\order{\omega^{-2l-1}},\qquad G_{\rm RW}=\order{\omega^0}.

(47)

Thus, the sectoral zero-frequency singularities of the decomposed Green-function contributions and their cancellation in the total Green’s function are not artifacts of the particular Teukolsky master variable or its normalization.

A final remark is that the statements made here concern the pole structure visible in a fixed asymptotic sector of the frequency-domain representation.
A complete treatment of the inverse-frequency integral, including branch cuts, source terms, and possible sector changes of the asymptotic basis at infinity, requires a separate global analysis of the Stokes continuation and is left for future work.

V
Conclusions and discussion

We have investigated the pole structure of Kerr black-hole perturbations in the frequency domain, focusing on the building blocks of the Green’s function for the radial Teukolsky equation: the homogeneous solutions, the connection coefficients, and the Green’s function itself.
We have addressed, from first principles, which singular structures are present in asymptotically flat rotating black holes within the Teukolsky formalism, and how they differ from the structures that have recently been identified in nonrotating or asymptotically de Sitter settings, often in the Regge–Wheeler–Zerilli (RWZ) formalism.
More specifically, we have clarified the Matsubara poles in the homogeneous solutions and connection coefficients, the cancellation of the explicit Matsubara-pole factors in the decomposed Green-function contribution, and the zero-frequency singularities of the homogeneous solutions, connection coefficients, and decomposed Green-function contributions.

Using both the confluent-Heun and MST representations of the homogeneous solutions of the radial Teukolsky equation, we showed that the homogeneous solutions and the connection coefficients develop simple poles at the Matsubara frequencies
{}_{s}\omega^{(\pm)}_{mk}
identified in (
30
) and (
31
), and also verified this behavior numerically.
This establishes the Matsubara pole structure explicitly within the Teukolsky formalism for asymptotically flat subextremal Kerr perturbations.
We also found that, at the level of the local fixed-sector connection formula, the explicit Matsubara-pole factors cancel in the ratio
B^{\mathrm{ref}}/B^{\mathrm{inc}}
entering
G^{(+)}
.
Thus, the Matsubara poles are present in the homogeneous solutions and connection problem, but they are not inherited straightforwardly as poles of the decomposed Green-function contribution.
In the RW formalism for nonrotating black holes, the corresponding MST analysis gives an analogous Matsubara structure, although its detailed form differs because of the choice of master variable and normalization.
This should be contrasted with asymptotically de Sitter analyses in the RWZ formalism, where Matsubara poles can directly organize the early-time response.

Further, using the low-frequency expansion based on the MST formalism, we clarified the zero-frequency scaling of the transmission-normalized homogeneous solutions and connection coefficients.
Consequently, both decomposed Green-function contributions behave as
G^{(\pm)}=\order{\omega^{-2l-1}}
.
For the gravitational lowest multipole
(s,l)=(-2,2)
, each decomposed contribution therefore contains a fifth-order zero-frequency pole.
However, these singularities cancel collectively in the total radial Green’s function, which is regular at
\omega=0
for fixed radii in the present normalization.
This cancellation was also verified numerically.
The RW MST analysis leads to the same order counting for the decomposed Green-function contributions and for the total Green’s function, namely,
G_{\mathrm{RW}}^{(\pm)}=\order{\omega^{-2l-1}}
and
G_{\mathrm{RW}}=\order{\omega^0}
.
This agreement suggests that the sectoral zero-frequency singularities and their cancellation in the total Green’s function are not artifacts of the particular Teukolsky master variable or its normalization.

Taken together, our results provide a frequency-domain foundation for the study of prompt response in Kerr spacetime.
They show that asymptotically flat rotating black holes possess a richer fixed-sector analytic structure than is visible from the QNM spectrum alone, and identify the homogeneous solutions, connection coefficients, and decomposed Green-function contributions as the basic analytic ingredients for future time-domain analyses.
In particular, the zero-frequency singularities are not poles of the total radial Green’s function, but they do appear in the decomposed contributions
G^{(+)}
and
G^{(-)}
that enter contour decompositions of the early-time response.
It will therefore be important to clarify how these sectoral zero-frequency singularities combine with branch cuts, source terms, and contour prescriptions in the actual time-domain waveform.

The higher-order zero-frequency singularities of the decomposed Green-function contributions are also interesting in the broader context of non-Hermitian spectral structures in black-hole perturbation theory.
A pole of order
N
at
\omega=\omega_{0}
would, at the level of an isolated pole contribution to the inverse-frequency integral, generate a contribution of the form
P_{N-1}(t)e^{-i\omega_{0}t}
, where
P_{N-1}(t)
is a polynomial of degree at most
N-1
.
Such polynomial prefactors have recently been highlighted as a non-Hermitian aspect of black-hole perturbations, in connection with the resonant behavior of high-spin Kerr QNMs associated with nearby second-order exceptional-point structures
Motohashi 2025
;
Yang et al. 2025
;
Panosso Macedo et al. 2025
.
In the present problem, the fifth-order zero-frequency poles for the gravitational lowest multipole occur in the decomposed contributions rather than in the total Green’s function.
Whether and how these sectoral singularities leave an observable polynomial imprint in prompt response must therefore be analyzed.

The Green-function analysis given here should be extended from the local fixed-sector connection problem to the full global analytic structure, including the Stokes continuation at the irregular singular point and the associated branch-cut structure.
In addition, although the RW comparison in the present work already shows that the Green-function-level low-frequency order counting is not tied to the Teukolsky master variable alone, a more systematic comparison with the RWZ or Sasaki–Nakamura formulations remains to be explored.
In particular, source normalizations, transformation formulae between master variables, and global analytic continuations should be incorporated to determine the representation-independent content of the full time-domain response.
We leave this broader analysis for future work.

Appendix A
Confluent Heun functions

In this appendix, we summarize key properties of the confluent Heun functions
Heun 1889
employed in the main text.
For general background, we refer the reader to the classical textbooks
Ronveaux 1995
;
Slavyanov and Lay 2000
, more recent mathematical developments
Maier 2006
;
Bonelli et al. 2023
;
Lisovyy and Naidiuk 2022
, and reviews on physical applications
Hortacsu 2018
;
Vieira and Bezerra 2016
; for applications to black-hole perturbations in particular, see
Fiziev 2010
;
Carneiro da Cunha and Cavalcante 2020
;
Bonelli et al. 2022
;
Minucci and Panosso Macedo 2025
.

A.1
Series expansions

The confluent Heun equation is given by

\frac{d^{2}w}{dz^{2}}+\left(\epsilon+\frac{\gamma}{z}+\frac{\delta}{z-1}\right)\frac{dw}{dz}+\frac{\alpha z-q}{z(z-1)}w=0,

(48)

which possesses regular singular points at
z=0,1
and an irregular singular point at
z=\infty
.

\mathrm{HeunC}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z)
is the standard confluent Heun function whose Frobenius expansion around
z=0
is given by

\mathrm{HeunC}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{k},

(49)

where the coefficients
c_{k}
satisfy the three-term recurrence relation

\begin{split}&c_{-1}=0,\quad c_{0}=1,\\
&f_{k}c_{k+1}+g_{k}c_{k}+h_{k}c_{k-1}=0,\end{split}

(50)

with

\begin{split}f_{k}&=-(k+1)(k+\gamma),\\
g_{k}&=k(k-\epsilon+\gamma+\delta-1)-q,\\
h_{k}&=(k-1)\epsilon+\alpha.\end{split}

(51)

On the other hand,
\mathrm{HeunC}_{\infty}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z^{-1})
denotes an asymptotic solution around the irregular singular point
z=\infty
,

\mathrm{HeunC}_{\infty}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z^{-1})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{d_{k}}{z^{k}},

(52)

where the coefficients
d_{k}
satisfy

\begin{split}&d_{-1}=0,\quad d_{0}=1,\\
&\tilde{f}_{k}d_{k+1}+\tilde{g}_{k}d_{k}+\tilde{h}_{k}d_{k-1}=0,\end{split}

(53)

with

\begin{split}\tilde{f}_{k}&=(k+1)\epsilon,\\
\tilde{g}_{k}&=-(k+1-\gamma-\delta+\epsilon+\alpha/\epsilon)(\alpha/\epsilon+k)+q,\\
\tilde{h}_{k}&=(\alpha/\epsilon+k-1)(\alpha/\epsilon+k-\gamma).\end{split}

(54)

Here we have divided the recurrence relation by
\epsilon^{2}
, so this form assumes
\epsilon\neq 0
.
The degenerate limit
\epsilon\to 0

must therefore be treated separately.

A.2
Independent solutions

Around each singular point, we have two linearly independent local solutions.
Near
z=0
, they are given by

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{HeunC}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z),

(55)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=z^{1-\gamma}\mathrm{HeunC}(q+(1-\gamma)(\epsilon-\delta),\alpha+(1-\gamma)\epsilon,2-\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z).

(56)

Near
z=1
, the two local solutions are

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{HeunC}(q-\alpha,-\alpha,\delta,\gamma,-\epsilon;1-z),

(57)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=(1-z)^{1-\delta}\mathrm{HeunC}(q-\alpha+(\gamma+\epsilon)(\delta-1),-\alpha+(\delta-1)\epsilon,2-\delta,\gamma,-\epsilon;1-z).

(58)

Around the irregular singular point
z=\infty
, the two asymptotic solutions are

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{(\infty)}

\displaystyle=z^{-\alpha/\epsilon}\mathrm{HeunC}_{\infty}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z^{-1}),

(59)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}^{(\infty)}

\displaystyle=e^{-\epsilon z}z^{\alpha/\epsilon-\gamma-\delta}\mathrm{HeunC}_{\infty}(q-\gamma\epsilon,\alpha-\epsilon(\gamma+\delta),\gamma,\delta,-\epsilon;z^{-1}).

(60)

The irregular singularity at
z=\infty
is of rank one, and the two asymptotic solutions are distinguished by the exponential factor
e^{-\epsilon z}
.
In the radial Teukolsky problem, (
19
) and (
25
) imply

\displaystyle\epsilon z=2\nu_{3}z=2i\omega(r-r_{-})\sim 2i\omega r,\qquad(r\to\infty).

(61)

The Stokes rays are the directions along which the relative exponential factor changes from decay to growth, and are therefore determined by

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\,(\epsilon z)=0.

(62)

Along the physical radial ray
r>r_{+}
, this condition reduces to
{\rm Im}\,\omega=0
.
Thus, the upper and lower half-planes of the complex-
\omega
plane correspond to different asymptotic sectors at infinity, and the identification of the
\mathrm{HeunC}_{\infty}
basis with outgoing and ingoing solutions must be understood sectorwise.

Restoring the prefactor in (
20
), we find, up to algebraic factors,

\displaystyle R_{1}^{(\infty)}

\displaystyle\sim e^{+\nu_{3}z}\sim e^{+i\omega r}\qquad(r\to\infty),

(63)

\displaystyle R_{2}^{(\infty)}

\displaystyle\sim e^{+\nu_{3}z-\epsilon z}=e^{-\nu_{3}z}\sim e^{-i\omega r}\qquad(r\to\infty).

(64)

For real
\omega
, these are naturally identified with outgoing and ingoing behaviors, respectively.
For complex
\omega
, the outgoing and ingoing labels are understood by analytic continuation from real
\omega
.
However, their dominant/subdominant character changes across the Stokes rays: in the upper half-plane
e^{+i\omega r}
is exponentially suppressed while
e^{-i\omega r}
is exponentially enhanced, and the opposite holds in the lower half-plane.
Thus, the asymptotic basis at infinity, and hence any connection coefficient involving
\psi^{(\infty)}_{1,2}
, should be understood within a fixed asymptotic sector and for a fixed branch choice.

A.3
Connection coefficients

Analytic formulae for connection coefficients of Heun functions and their confluences have recently been obtained in Refs.
Bonelli et al. 2023
;
Lisovyy and Naidiuk 2022
.
Here we focus on the connection between the local solution around
z=1
and the asymptotic basis at
z=\infty
,

\psi_{1}^{(1)}=C_{11}^{(1\infty)}\psi_{1}^{(\infty)}+C_{12}^{(1\infty)}\psi_{2}^{(\infty)}.

(65)

Through (
26
), this relation corresponds to decomposing the “in” solution into the “up” and “down” solutions.

Reference
Bonelli et al. 2023
gives two asymptotic representations of the connection coefficients, corresponding to the large-
L
and small-
L
expansions [Eqs. (4.2.19) and (4.2.21), respectively].
For the present purpose, we do not need the full expressions.
The essential point is that, in either representation, both connection coefficients contain the common factor
\Gamma(\delta)
.
This shared factor is sufficient to identify the Matsubara pole locations in a fixed asymptotic sector.

There are two qualifications to this statement.
First, an explicit asymptotic connection formula requires a definite choice of representation, asymptotic sector, and branch, as also reflected in the phase ambiguity discussed in Ref.
Bonelli et al. 2023
.
Second, the pole structure visible in such a fixed-sector connection formula is not, by itself, a global statement about the full Green’s function.
A global analysis would require tracking the Stokes continuation between the upper- and lower-half-plane sectors.

Thus, in the confluent-Heun analysis below, we use the common factor
\Gamma(\delta)
only to identify the fixed-sector Matsubara pole structure of the local connection coefficients.
The same pole locations are independently reproduced from the MST expressions for the asymptotic amplitudes in Appendix
C
, and their realization as poles is also checked numerically in the main text.

First, we derive the pole and residue formula for the local confluent Heun function

49
), with coefficients
c_{k}
satisfying the recurrence relation (
50
) and (
51
).
For generic
\gamma
, the coefficients
c_{k}
are determined recursively.
However, when
\gamma=-n
with
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, the coefficient
f_{n}=-(n+1)(n+\gamma)
vanishes.
As a result,
c_{n+1}
generically develops a simple pole.
Since, for
k\leq n
, the factor
f_{k-1}
remains nonzero, the coefficients
c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
stay finite as
\gamma\to-n
.
Moreover, the recurrence relation shows inductively that all subsequent coefficients
c_{k}
with
k\geq n+1
inherit the same simple pole.
Equivalently, the possible poles of
c_{k}
are contained in the factor
(\gamma)_{k}^{-1}=[\gamma(\gamma+1)\cdots(\gamma+k-1)]^{-1}
, where the Pochhammer symbol is defined as
(x)_{n}=\Gamma(x+n)/\Gamma(x)
, and the pole at
\gamma=-n
appears for
k\geq n+1
.
Therefore, for generic parameters,
\mathrm{HeunC}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z)
has simple poles at
\gamma=-n
with
n=0,1,2,\ldots
.

We now evaluate the residue of the confluent Heun function at
\gamma=-n
.
Since the coefficients
c_{k}
with
k\leq n
remain finite, we have

\lim_{\gamma\to-n}(\gamma+n)\mathrm{HeunC}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z)=\lim_{\gamma\to-n}(\gamma+n)\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{k}.

(66)

Let us first extract the coefficient of the leading singular term
z^{n+1}
.
Using the recurrence relation at
k=n
, we find

\displaystyle A_{n}(q,\alpha,\delta,\epsilon)\coloneqq\lim_{\gamma\to-n}(\gamma+n)c_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\gamma\to-n}(\gamma+n)\frac{g_{n}c_{n}+h_{n}c_{n-1}}{-f_{n}}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\bigl[n(-\epsilon+\delta-1)-q\bigr]c_{n}+\bigl[(n-1)\epsilon+\alpha\bigr]c_{n-1}}{n+1}\right|_{\gamma=-n}.

(67)

This is finite because
c_{n-1}
and
c_{n}
are finite at
\gamma=-n
.

Next, define rescaled coefficients

c^{\prime}_{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{A_{n}}\lim_{\gamma\to-n}(\gamma+n)c_{k+n+1},\qquad(k=0,1,2,\ldots).

(68)

By construction,

c^{\prime}_{-1}=0,\qquad c^{\prime}_{0}=1.

(69)

From the original recurrence relation (
50
),

f_{n+k+1}c_{n+k+2}+g_{n+k+1}c_{n+k+1}+h_{n+k+1}c_{n+k}=0.

(70)

Multiplying this recurrence by
(\gamma+n)/A_{n}
and then taking the limit
\gamma\to-n
, we obtain

F_{k}c^{\prime}_{k+1}+G_{k}c^{\prime}_{k}+H_{k}c^{\prime}_{k-1}=0,

(71)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{k}&\coloneqq\left.f_{n+k+1}\right|_{\gamma=-n}=-(k+1)(k+n+2),\\
G_{k}&\coloneqq\left.g_{n+k+1}\right|_{\gamma=-n}=(n+k+1)(k-\epsilon+\delta)-q,\\
H_{k}&\coloneqq\left.h_{n+k+1}\right|_{\gamma=-n}=(n+k)\epsilon+\alpha.\end{split}

(72)

These coefficients can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{k}&=-(k+1)(k+\gamma^{\prime}),\\
G_{k}&=k(k-\epsilon+\gamma^{\prime}+\delta-1)-q^{\prime},\\
H_{k}&=(k-1)\epsilon+\alpha^{\prime},\end{split}

(73)

with the shifted parameters

\gamma^{\prime}=n+2,\qquad\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha+(n+1)\epsilon,\qquad q^{\prime}=q+(n+1)(\epsilon-\delta).

(74)

Therefore the generating function of
c_{k}^{\prime}
is again a confluent Heun function:

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}^{\prime}z^{k}=\mathrm{HeunC}\bigl(q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},\delta,\epsilon;z\bigr).

(75)

Putting everything together, we finally arrive at

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to-n}(\gamma+n)\mathrm{HeunC}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\gamma\to-n}(\gamma+n)\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{k}

\displaystyle=z^{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\lim_{\gamma\to-n}(\gamma+n)c_{k+n+1}\right]z^{k}

\displaystyle=A_{n}z^{n+1}\mathrm{HeunC}\bigl(q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime},\delta,\epsilon;z\bigr).

(76)

Using (
74
), this becomes

\lim_{\gamma\to-n}(\gamma+n)\mathrm{HeunC}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z)=A_{n}z^{n+1}\mathrm{HeunC}(q+(n+1)(\epsilon-\delta),\alpha+(n+1)\epsilon,n+2,\delta,\epsilon;z),

(77)

with
A_{n}=A_{n}(q,\alpha,\delta,\epsilon)
given in (
67
).
This shows that
\mathrm{HeunC}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon;z)
has simple poles at

\gamma=-n
, and that the residue is again expressed in terms of a confluent
Heun function with shifted parameters.

Next, to evaluate the residues of
R^{\mathrm{in}}
and
R^{\mathrm{out}}
at the Matsubara frequencies, we apply the same argument to the local solutions around
z=1
.
The two local solutions
\psi_{1}^{(1)}
and
\psi_{2}^{(1)}
are given in (
57
) and (
58
), respectively.
For
\psi_{1}^{(1)}
, the relevant Heun parameter is
\delta
, and the pole is located at
\delta=-k
.
Using (
77
), we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Res}_{\delta=-k}\psi_{1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=A_{k}(q-\alpha,-\alpha,\gamma,-\epsilon)(1-z)^{k+1}\mathrm{HeunC}\bigl(q-\alpha-(k+1)(\gamma+\epsilon),-\alpha-(k+1)\epsilon,k+2,\gamma,-\epsilon;1-z\bigr).

(78)

For
\psi_{2}^{(1)}
, the relevant Heun parameter is
2-\delta
, and the pole is located at
2-\delta=-k
.
The prefactor
(1-z)^{1-\delta}
cancels the factor
(1-z)^{k+1}
generated by (
77
).
We therefore find

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Res}_{2-\delta=-k}\psi_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=A_{k}\bigl(q-\alpha+(\gamma+\epsilon)(k+1),-\alpha+(k+1)\epsilon,\gamma,-\epsilon\bigr)\mathrm{HeunC}\bigl(q-\alpha,-\alpha,k+2,\gamma,-\epsilon;1-z\bigr).

(79)

From (
20
), (
26
), and

\frac{d\omega}{d\delta}=i\kappa_{+},

(80)

we obtain the residues of
R^{\mathrm{in}}
and
R^{\mathrm{out}}
at the Matsubara frequencies as (
33
) and (
34
) in the main text, respectively.

B.2
Matsubara poles of connection coefficients

Next, let us consider poles and residues of the connection coefficients in a fixed asymptotic sector.
As discussed in Appendix
A
, the fixed-sector connection coefficients
C_{11}^{(1\infty)}
and
C_{12}^{(1\infty)}
contain the common explicit factor
\Gamma(\delta)
.
They therefore exhibit fixed-sector Matsubara poles at

\delta=-k,\qquad(k=0,1,2,\ldots).

(81)

Within a fixed sector, the corresponding residues follow immediately from the residue of the Gamma function,

\operatorname*{Res}_{c=-k}\Gamma(c)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}.

(82)

Thus,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Res}_{\delta=-k}C_{11}^{(1\infty)}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{C_{11}^{(1\infty)}}{\Gamma(\delta)}\right|_{\delta=-k},

(83)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Res}_{\delta=-k}C_{12}^{(1\infty)}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{C_{12}^{(1\infty)}}{\Gamma(\delta)}\right|_{\delta=-k}.

(84)

The asymptotic amplitudes
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
and
B^{\mathrm{ref}}
are obtained from these fixed-sector connection coefficients, up to the normalization factors specified in (
26
) and the asymptotic normalization of
R^{\mathrm{up}}
and
R^{\mathrm{down}}
.
Since these additional factors are regular at the Matsubara frequencies, the same
\Gamma(\delta)
factor gives the residues of the asymptotic amplitudes.
Using (
80
), we find (
41
) and (
42
) in the main text.

Appendix C
Pole structures and residues from the MST formalism

While the local Matsubara pole structure is most transparently exposed in the confluent-Heun representation, the same pole locations can also be identified directly in the MST formalism.
This provides an independent consistency check and is particularly useful for analyzing the low-frequency limit.
In this appendix, we summarize the MST results relevant to the Matsubara and zero-frequency pole structures.
For a thorough description of the MST method, we refer the reader to Refs.
Mano et al. 1996b
;
Mano and Takasugi 1997
;
Mano et al. 1996a
;
Sasaki and Tagoshi 2003
.

Throughout this appendix, we adopt unit-transmission normalization.
Also, mostly following Ref.
Sasaki and Tagoshi 2003
, we use the notation

\varepsilon=2M\omega,\quad\chi=\frac{a}{M},\quad\kappa=\sqrt{1-\chi^{2}},\quad\tau=\frac{\varepsilon-m\chi}{\kappa},\quad\varepsilon_{+}=\frac{\varepsilon+\tau}{2},\quad\hat{z}=\omega(r-r_{-}),\quad\hat{z}-\varepsilon\kappa=\omega(r-r_{+}).

(85)

Here,
\varepsilon
should not be confused with the Heun parameter
\epsilon
.
The symbol
\kappa
in the MST formalism denotes
\sqrt{1-\chi^{2}}
and should not be confused with the horizon surface gravities
\kappa_{\pm}
used in the main text.
Although the dimensionless Kerr spin is often denoted by
q
in the MST literature, we denote it by
\chi
to avoid confusion with the accessory parameter
q
of the confluent-Heun equation.

For comparison with another master-variable formulation, we discuss the corresponding structures in the RW MST formalism in Appendix
D
.

C.1
Matsubara poles of
R^{\mathrm{in}}
and
R^{\mathrm{out}}

In the Teukolsky MST formalism, the transmission-normalized “in” solution can be written as

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{1}{B^{\mathrm{trans}}}(K_{\nu}R_{C}^{\nu}+K_{-\nu-1}R_{C}^{-\nu-1}),

(86)

where
B^{\mathrm{trans}}
is the transmission amplitude,

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{trans}}=\left(\frac{\varepsilon\kappa}{\omega}\right)^{2s}e^{i\varepsilon_{+}\ln\kappa}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}^{\nu},

(87)

R_{C}^{\nu}
is the Coulomb-wave series solution,

\displaystyle R_{C}^{\nu}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\hat{z}}2^{\nu}(\varepsilon\kappa)^{-s-i\varepsilon}\hat{z}^{\nu+i\varepsilon}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}D_{n,j}\hat{z}^{n+j},

(88)

\displaystyle D_{n,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n}(2i)^{n+j}}{j!}\frac{\Gamma(n+\nu+1-s+i\varepsilon+j)}{\Gamma(2n+2\nu+2+j)}\frac{(\nu+1+s-i\varepsilon)_{n}}{(\nu+1-s+i\varepsilon)_{n}}a_{n}^{\nu}.

(89)

K_{\nu}
is the matching coefficient,

\displaystyle K_{\nu}

\displaystyle=e^{i\varepsilon\kappa}(2\varepsilon\kappa)^{s-\nu-N}2^{-s}i^{N}\frac{\Gamma(1-s-2i\varepsilon_{+})\Gamma(N+2\nu+2)}{\Gamma(N+\nu+1-s+i\varepsilon)\Gamma(N+\nu+1+i\tau)\Gamma(N+\nu+1+s+i\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}\Gamma(n+N+2\nu+1)}{(n-N)!}\frac{\Gamma(n+\nu+1+s+i\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(n+\nu+1-s-i\varepsilon)}\frac{\Gamma(n+\nu+1+i\tau)}{\Gamma(n+\nu+1-i\tau)}a_{n}^{\nu}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\sum_{n=-\infty}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(N-n)!(N+2\nu+2)_{n}}\frac{(\nu+1+s-i\varepsilon)_{n}}{(\nu+1-s+i\varepsilon)_{n}}a_{n}^{\nu}\right]^{-1},

(90)

a_{n}^{\nu}
satisfy the three-term recurrence relation

\alpha_{n}^{\nu}a_{n+1}^{\nu}+\beta_{n}^{\nu}a_{n}^{\nu}+\gamma_{n}^{\nu}a_{n-1}^{\nu}=0,

(91)

with

\displaystyle\begin{split}\alpha_{n}^{\nu}&=\frac{i\varepsilon\kappa(n+\nu+1+s+i\varepsilon)(n+\nu+1-i\varepsilon)(n+\nu+1+i\tau)}{(n+\nu+1)(2n+2\nu+3)},\\
\beta_{n}^{\nu}&=-\lambdabar-s(s+1)+(n+\nu)(n+\nu+1)+\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon(\varepsilon-m\chi)+\frac{\varepsilon(\varepsilon-m\chi)(s^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})}{(n+\nu)(n+\nu+1)},\\
\gamma_{n}^{\nu}&=-\frac{i\varepsilon\kappa(n+\nu-s+i\varepsilon)(n+\nu-s-i\varepsilon)(n+\nu-i\tau)}{(n+\nu)(2n+2\nu-1)},\end{split}

(92)

and
\nu
denotes the renormalized angular momentum, which is determined by the condition that the above three-term recurrence relation admits a minimal solution.
Here, the integer
N
appearing in (
C.1
) is the arbitrary MST matching index.
The full matching coefficient is independent of this choice, although individual factors in the expression are not.
In the following analysis, we are therefore free to fix this integer, and we take
N=0
for convenience.

From the matching coefficients
K_{\nu}
and
K_{-\nu-1}
in (
C.1
),
R^{\mathrm{in}}
contains the explicit gamma-function factor
\Gamma(1-s-2i\varepsilon_{+})
.
Using the definitions (
85
), one finds
1-s-2i\varepsilon_{+}=\delta
, given in (
25
).
Therefore, for generic parameters, it has simple poles when
\delta=-k
with
k=0,1,2,\ldots
.
This reproduces the Matsubara frequencies
{}_{s}\omega^{(-)}_{mk}
identified in (
30
) from the confluent-Heun analysis.
The corresponding residue formula (
35
) follows from the residue of the gamma function (
82
) and from (
80
).

The solution
R^{\mathrm{out}}
is obtained from
R^{\mathrm{in}}
through the spin-flip conjugation relation (
12
).
Hence, we obtain the other Matsubara branch
{}_{s}\omega^{(+)}_{mk}
in (
31
), corresponding to
1+s+2i\varepsilon_{+}=2-\delta=-k
with
k=0,1,2,\ldots
.
The corresponding residue is then given by (
36
).

C.2
Matsubara poles of connection coefficients

The transmission-normalized asymptotic amplitudes are given by

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{inc}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B^{\mathrm{trans}}}\omega^{-1}\left[K_{\nu}-ie^{-i\pi\nu}\frac{\sin\pi(\nu-s+i\varepsilon)}{\sin\pi(\nu+s-i\varepsilon)}K_{-\nu-1}\right]A_{+}^{\nu}e^{-i\varepsilon\ln\varepsilon},

(93)

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{ref}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B^{\mathrm{trans}}}\omega^{-1-2s}\left[K_{\nu}+ie^{i\pi\nu}K_{-\nu-1}\right]A_{-}^{\nu}e^{i\varepsilon\ln\varepsilon},

(94)

where

\displaystyle A_{+}^{\nu}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}\varepsilon}e^{\frac{i\pi}{2}(\nu+1-s)}2^{-1+s-i\varepsilon}\frac{\Gamma(\nu+1-s+i\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(\nu+1+s-i\varepsilon)}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}^{\nu},

(95)

\displaystyle A_{-}^{\nu}

\displaystyle=2^{-1-s+i\varepsilon}e^{-\frac{i\pi}{2}(\nu+1+s)}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}\varepsilon}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{(\nu+1+s-i\varepsilon)_{n}}{(\nu+1-s+i\varepsilon)_{n}}a_{n}^{\nu}.

(96)

Both
K_{\nu}
and
K_{-\nu-1}
contain the same explicit gamma-function factor
\Gamma(1-s-2i\varepsilon_{+})=\Gamma(\delta)
.
For generic parameters, this factor produces poles at the Matsubara frequencies.
The remaining factors in (
93
) and (
94
) are regular at these frequencies.
Thus,
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
and
B^{\mathrm{ref}}
have simple poles at the Matsubara frequencies
{}_{s}\omega^{(-)}_{mk}
given in (
30
).
Because the pole-producing factor is identical to the one found in the confluent-Heun analysis, the corresponding residues reduce to (
41
) and (
42
), respectively.

The above analysis shows that the Matsubara pole structures and residues identified in the confluent-Heun formulation are reproduced independently in the MST formalism.

C.3
Low-frequency scaling

We now turn to the low-frequency power counting based on the MST expansion
Mano et al. 1996a
.
We first note that the renormalized angular momentum
\nu
satisfies

\nu=l+\order{\ve^2}.

(97)

From the recurrence relation (
91
), we obtain the low-frequency scaling of
a_{n}
as

a_{n}^{\nu}=\tilde{\alpha}_{n}\,\varepsilon^{|n|}+\order{\ve^{|n|+1}},

(98)

where
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}
obeys

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{\alpha}_{0}&=1,\\
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}&=\frac{i\,(n+l-s)^{2}\bigl[(n+l)\kappa+im\chi\bigr]}{n(n+l)(n+2l+1)(2n+2l-1)}\,\tilde{\alpha}_{n-1},\qquad(n\geq 1),\\
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}&=-\frac{i\,(n+l+1+s)^{2}\bigl[(n+l+1)\kappa-im\chi\bigr]}{n(n+l+1)(n+2l+1)(2n+2l+3)}\,\tilde{\alpha}_{n+1},\qquad(n\leq-1).\end{split}

(99)

First, we show that
R^{\mathrm{in}}
and
R^{\mathrm{out}}
have regular zero-frequency limits for fixed
r>r_{+}
.
From (
C.1
) and (
97
), we find that

K_{\nu}=\order{\omega^{s-l}},\qquad K_{-\nu-1}=\order{\omega^{s+l+1}}.

(100)

Since
D_{n,j}\hat{z}^{n+j}=\order{\omega^{|n|+n+j}}
, the leading contribution to
R_{C}^{\nu}
comes from the
j=0
terms with
n\leq 0
.
The low-frequency scaling is then determined by the prefactor
\varepsilon^{-s-i\varepsilon}\hat{z}^{\nu+i\varepsilon}
, and hence we obtain

\displaystyle R_{C}^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\order{\omega^{l-s}},\qquad R_{C}^{-\nu-1}=\order{\omega^{-l-s-1}}.

(101)

B^{\mathrm{trans}}
is regular and nonzero at
\omega=0
for generic parameters.
Thus, the two contributions in (
86
) are both of order unity, and
R^{\mathrm{in}}=\order{\omega^0}
.
The spin-flip conjugation relation (
12
) then gives
R^{\mathrm{out}}=\order{\omega^0}
as well.

Next, let us clarify the low-frequency scaling of
B^{\mathrm{inc}}
and
B^{\mathrm{ref}}
.
Since
A_{+}^{\nu}
,
A_{-}^{\nu}
, and
B^{\mathrm{trans}}
are regular and nonzero at
\omega=0
for generic parameters, and the terms proportional to
K_{\nu}
dominate over those proportional to
K_{-\nu-1}
, (
93
) and (
94
) give

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{inc}}=\order{\omega^{s-l-1}},\qquad B^{\mathrm{ref}}=\order{\omega^{-s-l-1}}.

(102)

Finally, we clarify the zero-frequency behavior of
R^{\mathrm{up}}
and then obtain that of
R^{\mathrm{down}}
from the spin-flip relation.
In the MST method,
R^{\mathrm{up}}
is expressed as

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{up}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{trans}}}2^{\nu}e^{-\pi\varepsilon}e^{-i\pi(\nu+1+s)}e^{i\hat{z}}\hat{z}^{\nu+i\varepsilon_{+}}(\hat{z}-\varepsilon\kappa)^{-s-i\varepsilon_{+}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{(\nu+1+s-i\varepsilon)_{n}}{(\nu+1-s+i\varepsilon)_{n}}a_{n}(2i\hat{z})^{n}U\!\left(n+\nu+1+s-i\varepsilon,\,2n+2\nu+2,\,-2i\hat{z}\right).

(103)

Here,
U(a,b,z)
is Tricomi’s confluent hypergeometric function, which is also denoted as
\Psi(a,b,z)
in the literature.
As stressed in the main text, note that we define
R^{\mathrm{up}}
with the normalization by the transmission coefficient
C^{\mathrm{trans}}
, which is defined by

\displaystyle C^{\rm trans}

\displaystyle=\omega^{-1-2s}e^{i(\varepsilon\ln\varepsilon-\frac{1-\kappa}{2}\varepsilon)}A_{-}^{\nu},

\displaystyle\simeq 2^{-1-s}e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}(l+1+s)}\omega^{-1-2s}.

(104)

The small-
\hat{z}
behavior of the Tricomi function may be organized formally as

U(n+l+1+s,2n+2l+2,-2i\hat{z})\simeq\frac{\Gamma(2n+2l+1)}{\Gamma(n+l+1+s)}(-2i\hat{z})^{-2n-2l-1}+\frac{\Gamma(-2n-2l-1)}{\Gamma(s-n-l)}.

(105)

The first term gives a dominant contribution when
-2n-2l-1<0
and its coefficient is nonzero, which requires
n+l+1+s>0
for integer
s,n,l
.
Combining the two conditions, the first term is dominant when
n\geq\max(-s-l,-l)
.
In this case,

a_{n}\hat{z}^{n}U=\order{\omega^{|n|-n-2l-1}}=\begin{cases}\order{\omega^{-2l-1}},&(n\geq 0),\\
\order{\omega^{2|n|-2l-1}},&(\max(-s-l,-l)\leq n<0),\end{cases}

(106)

and hence all the nonnegative
n
terms contribute at the same order, whereas the terms with
\max(-s-l,-l)\leq n<0
are always subdominant due to the additional factor
\omega^{2|n|}
.
On the other hand, the second constant term in (
105
) is dominant when
n<\max(-s-l,-l)
, for which we have

a_{n}\hat{z}^{n}U=\order{\omega^{|n|+n}},\qquad(n<\max(-s-l,-l)),

(107)

with a nonnegative power, and hence this is subdominant over the contribution from
n\geq 0
.
Therefore, the leading order of the summation in (
103
) is determined by the nonnegative-
n
terms and is
\order{\omega^{-2l-1}}
.

From the above arguments, keeping
r>r_{+}
fixed and taking
M\omega\to 0
, we obtain the leading-order low-frequency expression of (
103
) as

\displaystyle{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{up}}_{lm}(r)

\displaystyle={}_{s}Q_{lm}(r)\omega^{s-l}+\order{\omega^{s-l+1}},

(108)

where

\displaystyle{}_{s}Q_{lm}(r)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{s}i^{\,l+s}2^{s-l}\frac{(l-s)!}{(l+s)!}\left(\frac{r-r_{+}}{r-r_{-}}\right)^{\frac{im\chi}{2\kappa}}\frac{(r-r_{+})^{-s}}{(r-r_{-})^{\,l+1}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2n+2l)!}{(n+l-s)!}\left(\frac{-iM}{r-r_{-}}\right)^{n}\tilde{\alpha}_{n}.

(109)

A similar expression for
R^{\mathrm{down}}_{lm}
can be obtained through (
13
):

\displaystyle{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{down}}_{lm}(r)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{-s}(r)\bigl[{}_{-s}Q^{*}_{lm}(r)\omega^{-s-l}+\order{\omega^{-s-l+1}}\bigr].

(110)

Let us remark that the above results (
102
), (
108
), and (
110
) are compatible with the decomposition (
14
) and the regularity of
R^{\mathrm{in}}
, but this compatibility should not be understood as a statement that follows from the leading powers alone.
Using
R^{\mathrm{up}}=\order{\omega^{s-l}}
and
R^{\mathrm{down}}=\order{\omega^{-s-l}}
, the two products
B^{\mathrm{ref}}R^{\mathrm{up}}
and
B^{\mathrm{inc}}R^{\mathrm{down}}
may each contain singular terms starting at
\order{\omega^{-2l-1}}
.
However, the left-hand side of (
14
) is the unit-transmission-normalized horizon solution, which is independently seen from the MST representation (
86
) to be regular at
\omega=0
for fixed
r>r_{+}
.
Therefore, the negative-power terms in the sum
B^{\mathrm{ref}}R^{\mathrm{up}}+B^{\mathrm{inc}}R^{\mathrm{down}}
must cancel collectively, order by order, leaving the regular solution
R^{\mathrm{in}}=\order{\omega^0}
.
A direct verification of the individual cancellation coefficients would require the corresponding higher-order low-frequency expansion of all factors and is not needed for the pole-counting argument used here.

Appendix D
Regge–Wheeler comparison in the MST formalism

It is useful to compare the Teukolsky results obtained above with the MST representation of the RW equation
Mano et al. 1996b
.
This comparison makes clear which features of the Matsubara and zero-frequency structures are tied to the choice of master variable and normalization, and which survive at the level of Green-function order counting.

In this appendix, we repeat the main steps of the Teukolsky MST analysis of Appendix
C
in the RW MST formalism: we identify the Matsubara pole locations of the horizon-adapted solutions and connection coefficients, discuss their cancellation in the decomposed Green-function contribution, and then examine the low-frequency scaling of the homogeneous solutions, connection coefficients, and decomposed Green-function contributions.
As we shall see below, the local Matsubara frequencies and the zero-frequency orders of individual homogeneous solutions are representation dependent.
By contrast, the cancellation of the explicit Matsubara-pole factors in the decomposed Green-function contribution, the sectoral Green-function scaling
G^{(\pm)}=\order{\omega^{-2l-1}}
, and the cancellation giving
G=\order{\omega^0}
are reproduced in the RW formulation.
Below, we denote the RW master variable by
X
, use
\varepsilon=2M\omega
, and introduce
x=1-r/(2M)
and
z=\omega r
.

D.1
Matsubara poles in the RW formalism

The solution satisfying the ingoing boundary condition at the horizon can be written as

\displaystyle X^{\nu}_{\mathrm{in}}

\displaystyle=e^{i\varepsilon(x-1)}(-x)^{-i\varepsilon}(1-x)^{-1}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}^{\nu}p_{n+\nu}(x),

(111)

where

\displaystyle p_{n+\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(n+\nu-1-i\varepsilon)\Gamma(-n-\nu-2-i\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(1-2i\varepsilon)}{}_{2}F_{1}(n+\nu-1-i\varepsilon,-n-\nu-2-i\varepsilon;1-2i\varepsilon;x).

(112)

The horizon-adapted solution
X_{\mathrm{in}}^{\nu}
is not normalized to unit transmission at the horizon.
Its horizon amplitude is

\mathcal{B}_{\rm trans}^{\rm RW}=e^{-i\varepsilon}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}^{\nu}\frac{\Gamma(n+\nu-1-i\varepsilon)\Gamma(-n-\nu-2-i\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(1-2i\varepsilon)}.

(113)

Thus, if one adopts the same unit-transmission normalization as in the Teukolsky analysis in the main text, one should consider

\hat{X}_{\mathrm{in}}^{\nu}=\frac{X_{\mathrm{in}}^{\nu}}{\mathcal{B}_{\rm trans}^{\rm RW}}.

(114)

After this normalization, the common overall factor
1/\Gamma(1-2i\varepsilon)
cancels between
X_{\mathrm{in}}^{\nu}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\rm trans}^{\rm RW}
.

The normalized solution
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{in}}^{\nu}
is then expressed as a series of hypergeometric functions
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;x)
with the common parameter
c=1-2i\varepsilon
.
Therefore, unless cancelled in the full MST series, it is expected to develop simple poles at
1-2i\varepsilon=-k
, with
k
being a nonnegative integer.
Equivalently, since
\kappa_{+}=1/(4M)
for Schwarzschild, the corresponding pole frequencies are

\omega=-i(k+1)\kappa_{+},\qquad(k=0,1,2,\ldots).

(115)

Similarly, one can consider the solution satisfying the outgoing boundary condition at the horizon and normalize it by its transmission coefficient to obtain
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{out}}^{\nu}
.
The corresponding normalized solution is composed of hypergeometric functions
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;x)
with the common parameter
c=1+2i\varepsilon
, leading to

\omega=+i(k+1)\kappa_{+},\qquad(k=0,1,2,\ldots).

(116)

This is consistent with the complex-conjugation structure between
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{in}}^{\nu}
and
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{out}}^{\nu}
.

We should nevertheless keep in mind that the RW MST solutions also admit equivalent representations in other bases, such as the
X_{0}^{\nu}
and
X_{0}^{-\nu-1}
basis
Mano et al. 1996b
.
Thus, the pole locations inferred above should be understood as those visible in the present unit-transmission-normalized RW MST representation.
A complete pole-zero analysis of the full RW or RWZ Green’s function would further require tracking possible cancellations among homogeneous solutions, connection coefficients, and Wronskian factors.

The same normalization also determines the Matsubara structure of the RW connection coefficients at infinity.
In the notation of Ref.
Mano et al. 1996b
, the unnormalized horizon-adapted solution has the asymptotic form

\displaystyle X^{\nu}_{\rm in}=A^{\nu}_{\rm out}e^{iz}z^{i\varepsilon}+A^{\nu}_{\rm in}e^{-iz}z^{-i\varepsilon}.

(117)

These amplitudes themselves do not contain an explicit
\Gamma(1-2i\varepsilon)
pole factor.
However, the connection coefficients for the unit-transmission-normalized solution are

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\nu}_{\rm out}=\frac{A^{\nu}_{\rm out}}{\mathcal{B}_{\rm trans}^{\rm RW}},\qquad\hat{A}^{\nu}_{\rm in}=\frac{A^{\nu}_{\rm in}}{\mathcal{B}_{\rm trans}^{\rm RW}}.

(118)

Since
\mathcal{B}_{\rm trans}^{\rm RW}
contains the common factor

1/\Gamma(1-2i\varepsilon)
, it has zeros at
1-2i\varepsilon=-k
for generic parameters.
Accordingly, the normalized coefficients
\hat{A}^{\nu}_{\rm out}
and
\hat{A}^{\nu}_{\rm in}
have simple poles at these frequencies.
These are precisely the RW Matsubara frequencies (
115
).

The same pole-producing factor appears in both normalized connection coefficients.
Therefore, in the RW Green-function decomposition, the explicit Matsubara-pole factors cancel in the ratio of connection coefficients
\hat{A}_{\rm out}/\hat{A}_{\rm in}
, in direct analogy with the Teukolsky result discussed in §
IV.3
.
Thus, as in the Teukolsky case, the Matsubara poles are visible in the homogeneous horizon-adapted solutions and in their connection coefficients, but are not inherited straightforwardly as explicit poles of the decomposed Green-function contribution.

Let us compare the RW Matsubara frequencies (
115
) and (
116
) with the Teukolsky Matsubara frequencies (
30
) and (
31
).
In the Schwarzschild limit of the Teukolsky result, the real shift
m\Omega_{+}
vanishes, consistently with the purely imaginary RW frequencies and with the absence of
m
dependence in the RW equation.
The remaining difference is the spin-weight dependence.
Indeed, the RW locations coincide with the Teukolsky formula only after formally setting
s=0
.
This should not be interpreted as saying that the RW variable describes a scalar field; rather, it reflects that the local pole locations of individual homogeneous solutions depend on the choice of master variable and normalization.
For gravitational perturbations, the Teukolsky variables with
s=\pm 2
and the RW variable are related by nontrivial transformations, such as Chandrasekhar–Sasaki–Nakamura or Darboux transformations, which can shift the apparent local pole structure of the homogeneous solutions.
A full derivation of how the
s
-dependent Teukolsky Matsubara factors are reorganized under such transformations is beyond the scope of the present work.
Here we only note that the RW locations are independent of the Teukolsky spin weight because the RW master variable is used, while the Green-function-level low-frequency order counting below agrees with the Teukolsky result.

D.2
Low-frequency scaling in the RW formalism

The zero-frequency behavior of the RW upgoing solution can also be estimated in the MST formalism.
We use the Coulomb-wave representation of the solution satisfying the outgoing condition at infinity
Mano et al. 1996b
,

X_{\rm up}^{\nu}=\frac{\Gamma(\nu-1-i\varepsilon)\Gamma(\nu+1-i\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(\nu+1+i\varepsilon)\Gamma(\nu+3+i\varepsilon)}X_{C\mathrm{out}}^{\nu},

(119)

where

\displaystyle X_{C\mathrm{out}}^{\nu}

\displaystyle=e^{iz}z^{\nu+1}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{z}\right)^{-i\varepsilon}2^{\nu}e^{-\pi\varepsilon}e^{-i\pi(\nu+1)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+\nu-1-i\varepsilon)\Gamma(n+\nu+1-i\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(n+\nu+1+i\varepsilon)\Gamma(n+\nu+3+i\varepsilon)}a_{n}^{\nu}(-2iz)^{n}U(n+\nu+1-i\varepsilon,2n+2\nu+2,-2iz).

(120)

The prefactor relating
X_{\rm up}^{\nu}
to
X_{C\mathrm{out}}^{\nu}
is regular and nonzero at
\varepsilon=0
for integer
l\geq 2
, since
\nu=l+\order{\ve^2}
.
Therefore, it does not affect the low-frequency scaling.
This remains true if one normalizes the RW “up” solution to unit outgoing amplitude at infinity, since the required normalization is regular at
\varepsilon=0
.

Let us take the limit
\omega\to 0
at fixed
r
.
We can identify the leading-order contribution by the same type of calculation as in Appendix
C.3
.
Using
\nu=l+\order{\ve^2}
and
a_{n}^{\nu}=\order{\ve^{|n|}}
, the singular part of the Tricomi function behaves as

\displaystyle U(n+\nu+1-i\varepsilon,2n+2\nu+2,-2iz)\simeq z^{-2n-2\nu-1},

(121)

whenever this singular term is present with a finite coefficient.
Therefore, each term for which this singular Tricomi contribution is present scales as

\displaystyle a_{n}^{\nu}z^{\nu+1+n}U

\displaystyle=\order{\omega^{|n|-n-l}}=\begin{cases}\order{\omega^{-l}},&(n\geq 0),\\
\order{\omega^{-l+2|n|}},&(n<0).\end{cases}

(122)

If the singular Tricomi term is absent for some negative
n
, the remaining regular term is less singular and does not change the leading order.
Thus, barring an accidental cancellation of the leading sum over
n\geq 0
, the unit-outgoing RW up solution behaves as
\omega^{-l}
.
By the conjugation relation, the same scaling applies to the unit-ingoing RW “down” solution.
Taken together, the leading low-frequency behavior at fixed radius is

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{\mathrm{up}}^{\nu}(r)=\order{\omega^{-l}},\qquad\hat{X}_{\mathrm{down}}^{\nu}(r)=\order{\omega^{-l}}.

(123)

This is the RW counterpart of the zero-frequency singularity of the Teukolsky “up” and “down” solutions, but the pole order differs from the Teukolsky result
{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{up}}_{lm}=\order{\omega^{s-l}}
and
{}_{s}R^{\mathrm{down}}_{lm}=\order{\omega^{-s-l}}
because the master variable and normalization are different.
For the “up” solutions, the RW variable is normalized by a standard outgoing amplitude at infinity,
X_{\mathrm{up}}\simeq e^{i\omega r_{*}}
, whereas the Teukolsky variable is normalized by the spin-weight-dependent asymptotic factor
R^{\mathrm{up}}\simeq r^{-1-2s}e^{i\omega r_{*}}
.
Consequently, there is no direct RW analogue of the Teukolsky factor
C^{\rm trans}=\order{\omega^{-1-2s}}
that shifts the zero-frequency pole order.
The low-frequency singularity of
X_{\mathrm{up}}
instead comes from the small-argument behavior of the outgoing Coulomb-wave, or equivalently spherical-wave, basis at fixed radius.
Thus, the zero-frequency pole order of a homogeneous radial solution should not by itself be regarded as representation independent; a representation-independent comparison should be made at the level of the full Green’s function, including the source normalization and the transformation between the Teukolsky, RWZ, and Sasaki–Nakamura variables.

The RW Green-function decomposition can be analyzed by the same low-frequency order-counting method used for the Teukolsky case in Appendix
C.3
.
The horizon amplitude
\mathcal{B}_{\rm trans}^{\rm RW}
in (
113
) is regular and nonzero at
\omega=0
for the full MST series, although this regularity is not manifest term by term because individual gamma functions may be singular.
Thus, the unit-transmission-normalized horizon solution satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{\rm in}=\order{\omega^0},\qquad\hat{X}_{\rm out}=\order{\omega^0}.

(124)

On the other hand, the asymptotic amplitudes
A^{\nu}_{\rm in}
and
A^{\nu}_{\rm out}
given in Ref.
Mano et al. 1996b
contain the matching coefficient
K_{\nu}
, whose leading low-frequency behavior is
K_{\nu}=\order{\omega^{-l-1}}
for a fixed matching index.
The terms proportional to
K_{-\nu-1}
are subdominant.
Therefore, after unit-transmission normalization, the RW connection coefficients scale as

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{\rm in}=\order{\omega^{-l-1}},\qquad\hat{A}_{\rm out}=\order{\omega^{-l-1}}.

(125)

Together with
\hat{X}_{\rm up}=\order{\omega^{-l}}
and
\hat{X}_{\rm down}=\order{\omega^{-l}}
, this gives

\displaystyle G_{\rm RW}^{(\pm)}=\order{\omega^{-2l-1}},\qquad G_{\rm RW}=\order{\omega^0},

(126)

in agreement with the Teukolsky order counting for the Green-function contributions, (
45
) and (
46
).
