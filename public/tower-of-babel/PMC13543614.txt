Characterizing the molecular mechanism of the PmIDD5-PmbHLH130-PmCOL5 transcriptional cascade on flowering in Prunus mume
Authors: Chengdong Ma, Pengyu Zhou, Liping Tong, Silas Segbo, Ximeng Lin, Xiao Huang, Ting Shi, Zhaojun Ni, Zhihong Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrunus mume is an important woody ornamental and fruit tree of Rosaceae. Elucidating the molecular regulatory network of its flowering is of great significance for genetic improvement. The basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) and INDETERMINATE DOMAIN (IDD) transcription factors play vital roles in plant development processes, while their regulatory cascades in Prunus mume remain largely unclear.ResultsThis study reports a bHLH transcription factor PmbHLH130 in Prunus mume, whose transcription level responds to photoperiod, and which is predominantly expressed in leaves. Subcellular localization and transcriptional activity assays revealed that PmbHLH130 is a nuclear-localized transcriptional activator. Overexpressed PmbHLH130 significantly promotes flowering in transgenic Arabidopsis. PmbHLH130 acts downstream of several central oscillator proteins and PmIDD5, and directly activates the expression of PmCOL5.ConclusionThis study reveals that PmIDD5–PmbHLH130–PmCOL5 transcriptional cascade provides a molecular basis for understanding flowering regulation. These findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms of flowering control in woody plants and offer a potential framework for molecular breeding of Prunus mume.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09329-5.Keywords: Prunus mume, PmbHLH130, Transcriptional cascade, Flowering time

Introduction

The flowering time mechanism of perennial woody plants significantly differs from that of annual herbaceous species. Prunus mume (Prunus mume Sieb.et Zucc.), as a typical species of the Rosaceae family, exhibits distinct traits in its flowering [1, 2]. The molecular basis of its flowering regulation is of interest.

In the flowering regulation pathway, the transcriptional cascade governed by central oscillator genes serves as a key regulatory hub. These central oscillator genes such as LATE ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL (LHY), TIMING OF CHLOROPHYLL A/B BINDING PROTEIN1 (TOC1), CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED1 (CCA1), and TWO-COMPONENT RESPONSE REGULATORs (PRRs), integrate upstream signals and transmit them to downstream output pathways, thereby orchestrating multiple aspects of flowering [3–7].

The INDETERMINATE DOMAIN (IDD) family encodes transcription factors (TFs) containing a Cys2His2 zinc-finger domain and represents a conserved group across terrestrial plants, having been identified in various species [8–11]. Biochemical analysis indicated that IDD proteins can bind to a DNA sequence with the consensus motif IDD-box (TTTGTC) [12]. The IDD family has been identified to be involved in some plant growth and development processes, such as fruit softening [13], root development [14, 15], starch metabolism [16], seed germination [17], floral transition [18, 19] and leaf development [20]. Indeterminate 1 (id1) is the first gene known to severely alter the ability of maize to undergo the transition to reproductive growth [21]. Deng et al. identified a functionally redundant homolog, OsIDD4, in rice which can directly bind to the promoters of Hd3a and RFT and activate their expression [22]. Research on Arabidopsis thaliana (A. thaliana) revealed that five IDD family genes (IDD3, IDD4, IDD5, IDD9, IDD10) can interact with DELLA proteins and synergistically promote the expression of SCARECROW-LIKE 3 (SCL3), among which the IDD-SRDX (Mutants of atidd3, -4, -5, -9, and − 10) display a late flowering phenotype [23]. The expression level of IDD8 is suppressed in darkness, during sugar deprivation resulting from decreased photosynthesis, or under the influence of AKIN10 phosphorylation [24, 25].

The bHLH transcription factors are ubiquitous in eukaryotes and constitute one of the largest families found in plants [26]. Typical proteins possess a highly conserved bHLH domain of 50–60 amino acids, capable of binding the E-box (CANNTG, where N = A, C, G, T) or the canonical G-box (CACGTG) [27]. Previous studies emphasized that bHLH TFs regulate numerous essential biological processes, such as plant development [28, 29], plant nutrition [30], specialized metabolism [31, 32], and the response to abiotic and biotic stresses [33, 34]. Recent years have witnessed numerous investigations into the molecular mechanisms of bHLH transcription factors. CmbHLH110 in chrysanthemum accelerates flowering by interacting with CmERF110 [35]; The bHLH family transcription factors PFA and PFB physically interact to form dimers, stably maintaining the cellular state required for lateral root initiation [36]; Hd1 Binding Protein 1 (HBP1) and Partner of HBP1 (POH1) interact with each other, acting as negative regulators of flowering time in rice [37]. However, as early as 2012, multiple prominent genes of the bHLH family that regulate the flowering period of A. thaliana were named FLOWERING BHLH 1 (FBH1), FBH2, FBH3 and FBH4. Shogo Ito’s research indicates that FBH proteins positively regulate CONSTANS (CO) transcription for flowering and FBH homologs in poplar and rice have similar functions [38]. Simultaneously, FBH4 is phosphorylated under high nitrogen conditions [39].

This study aimed to investigate the function and mechanism of PmbHLH130 in P. mume flowering regulation. In our previous study, we identified 45 transcription factors with circadian expression patterns from transcriptome data of P. mume, including PmbHLH130 [40]. Through overexpression experiment we demonstrated that PmbHLH130 promotes flowering in transgenic Arabidopsis. Our results demonstrated that multiple central oscillator proteins bind to the PmbHLH130 promoter, and we also integrated the previous studies on the regulation of IDD5 and bHLH130 into a unified pathway, revealing that these genes contribute to flowering in P. mume, respectively. We further studied the direct targeting of PmCOL5 by PmbHLH130. Our findings provide a molecular framework for understanding how PmbHLH130 integrates into regulatory networks and offer potential applications for improving the flowering mechanism in P. mume through genetic enhancement.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The plant materials used in this study consisted of P. mume cultivar ‘Longyan’ and A. thaliana ecotype ‘Col-0’. P. mume samples were obtained from the National Field Genebank for Prunus mume and Waxberry (Nanjing, China). Seeds of A. thaliana ‘Col-0’ were provided by the Laboratory of Fruit Tree Biotechnology at Nanjing Agricultural University (Nanjing, China), where the stock is maintained. All plant materials were verified by the corresponding author prior to experimentation.

To analyze the circadian expression patterns of target genes, six three-year-old P. mume seedlings were grown in a controlled greenhouse under two conditions: long-day (LD; 16 h light/8 h dark) and short-day (SD; 8 h light/16 h dark). The greenhouse conditions were maintained at a temperature of 23℃ and a relative humidity of 50–60%. The plants were cultivated in a soil mixture of peat and vermiculite (3:1 ratio), watered twice weekly, and fertilized monthly. Leaves were harvested every 4 h over a 48-hour period for RNA-seq analysis. To examine tissue-specific expression patterns, samples from different tissues and distinct flowering stages were collected from adult P. mume trees. For qRT-PCR validation, flower bud samples were collected at various flowering stages. All tissue samples were collected in three biological replicates from the same tree and the photoperiod treatments were used solely for expression analysis.

For phenotypic analysis of transgenic Arabidopsis lines, seeds were sown in soil and placed in growth chambers under LD or SD conditions, maintained at 23℃ with 50% relative humidity. Fertilizer was applied monthly. Flowering time was determined by counting the total number of leaves at bolting. All phenotypic experiments were performed in triplicate using three independent transgenic lines.

Reverse transcription and quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from P. mume and A. thaliana using an RNAprep Extraction Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (DP441, TIANGEN, China). Total RNA (1 µg) was used in a 20 µL volume with reverse transcriptase and RT Primer Mix and diluted with 45 µL water to synthesize first-strand cDNA (RR047A, TAKARA, Japan). For qPCR reactions, 1 µL of the diluted first-strand cDNA mixture, 10 µL of 2X SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix, 8.2 µL double-distilled water and 0.4 µL of 10 pM primer pairs were prepared in a 20 µL volume (AG11701, Accurate, China). qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio 5 instrument under the following conditions: 95℃ for 2 min, followed by 42 cycles of 95℃ for 5 s, 60℃ for 15 s, and 72℃ for 10 s, using a comparative critical threshold value. The tubulin β-8 chain-like (TUB8) gene of P. mume and the actin2 gene of A. thaliana were used as an internal control. Primer specificity was confirmed by amplification of a single sharp melting curve peak. The primers for the analysis of flower induction gene expression and their respective primers are listed in Table S1. This analysis was conducted using independent samples obtained from different tissues, and each experiment was repeated at least three times.

Generation of transgenic lines

The full-length corresponding coding sequences (CDSs) of PmbHLH130 were inserted into the pCAMBIA1301-dual-35 S (pCAMBIA-1301) vector containing the 35 S promoter. Transgenic plants overexpressing PmbHLH130 were generated in the Col-0 background by using Agrobacterium tumefaciens (GV3101)-mediated transformation [41]. The 1/2MS medium containing hygromycin (30 mg/L) was used to screen the transgenic positive plants. Subsequently, GUS staining was used for detection [42]. The expression levels of PmbHLH130 in the positive transgenic lines were detected using qRT-PCR.

Yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assay

The full-length cDNA of PmLHY, PmLHY-Like, PmRVE1, PmRVE3, PmRVE6, PmRVE7, PmRVE8, PmIDD5 and PmbHLH130 were amplified through RT-PCR using gene-specific primers to generate the Gal4 activation domain (AD) constructs (Table S1) and cloned into the pGADT7 vector as prey. For the bait, several DNA fragments of putative target genes of pPmbHLH130 and pPmCOL5 were amplified through genomic PCR using promoter-specific primers (Table S1). These products were then inserted into the pAbAi vector. The Y1H gold yeast cell strain was used to introduce each bait–prey pair. These experiments were repeated three times, each time with independent clones obtained from different yeast recombinants.

Dual-Luciferase (D-LUC) activity assay in tobacco leaves

The ~ 2000 bp of PmbHLH130 and PmCOL5 promoters were cloned into the pGreen-0800 vector that contains the LUC reporter gene. The effector plasmid was generated by cloning the full-length coding sequence of PmIDD5, PmDELLA1, PmDELLA2 and PmbHLH130 into the pCAMBIA-1301 vector. The empty pCAMBIA-1301 vector plasmid was used as the control. The constructed vector plasmid was transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 and GV3101 (pSoup), which were further incubated until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached approximately 1.0. Cells were then resuspended with an equal volume of treatment solution, and an effector-to-reporter ratio of 9:1 was used for agroinfiltration into tobacco leaves. Transfected plants were incubated in darkness for one day, followed by two days under light conditions. Luciferase activity detection was performed using a CCD camera (NightOWL818 II LB983) and a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (11405ES60, Yeasen, China).

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

The His-PmLHY, His-PmLHY-Like, His-PmRVE1, His-PmRVE3, His-PmRVE7, His-IDD5, His-PmbHLH130 constructs, encoding proteins fused to the Trigger Factor chaperone and a His tag, were generated by cloning the CDSs into the pCOLD-TF vector. The recombinant plasmid was transformed into Escherichia coli Rosetta BL21(DE3) cells. The transformed cells were induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) at 15℃, and the proteins were purified using the Ni-TED Sepharose 6FF (C610030-0025, Sangon, China). FAM-labeled EE-box or E-box motifs of pPmbHLH130 and pPmCOL5 were synthesized as binding probes, unlabeled competitive probes, and FAM-labeled mutation probes of different sequences were synthesized. DNA-protein complex was detected with the Chemiluminescent Nucleic Acid Detection Module (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transcriptional activation activity assay

The PmbHLH130, PmbHLH130-N, and PmbHLH130-C fragments were amplified from the first-strand cDNA library using gene-specific primers (Table S1) and inserted into the pGBKT7 vector (Clontech, USA) to generate constructs expressing proteins fused with the GAL4 DNA-binding domain (BD). The recombinant plasmids were verified by sequencing and then co-transformed into the yeast strain Y2H Gold along with pGADT7 vectors. The transformed yeast cells were cultured on synthetic dropout medium lacking tryptophan and leucine (DDO) at 28℃ for 2–3 days to assess auto-activation and on selective medium lacking tryptophan, leucine, histidine, and adenine (QDO) to evaluate transcriptional activation activity. Each experiment was performed in triplicate using independent clones obtained from different yeast transformants.

Correlation analysis

The expression levels (FPKM) of these genes were extracted from the RNA-seq data described in Sect. 1. To clarify the correlation between PmbHLH130 and selected central oscillator genes, we performed Pearson correlation analysis using transcriptome data from LD and SD conditions, and visualized the resulting correlation matrix as a network heatmap via OmicShare Tools (https://www.omicshare.com) [43].

Accession numbers

The gene sequences referenced in this study were retrieved from the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) database. The accession numbers are as follows: PmbHLH130 (LOC103319555), PmIDD5 (LOC103321013), PmLHY (LOC103342352), PmLHY-like (LOC103342354), PmRVE1 (LOC103327372), PmRVE3 (LOC103332072), PmRVE6 (LOC103333717), PmRVE7 (LOC103338885), PmRVE8 (LOC103340488), PmDELLA1 (LOC103321409), PmDELLA2 (LOC103328576) and PmCOL5 (LOC103323323).

Results

Isolation and expression pattern analysis of PmbHLH130 in P. mume

Based on the NCBI data, the CDS of PmbHLH130 was cloned from P. mume ‘Longyan’, revealing a length of 1260 bp and a predicted protein molecular weight of 45.77 kDa. Through phylogenetic tree analysis with the bHLH proteins of A. thaliana, the results showed that PmbHLH130 is closely related to AtFBH3 (AT1G51140) and AtFBH4 (AT2G42280) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). The sequence included a highly conserved bHLH domain in its C terminus (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Under LD conditions (16-h light/8-h dark), PmbHLH130 expression levels showed oscillations, with a peak at approximately zeitgeber 8 (ZT8), followed by a second peak at ZT36; under SD conditions (8-h light/16-h dark), the peak was also detected at approximately ZT16, followed by a second peak at ZT40 (Fig. 1a). PmbHLH130 exhibited a constantly higher expression under LD conditions. To investigate the abilities of PmbHLH130 to activate transcription, the full-length coding region and the N- and C-terminal regions of PmbHLH130 were fused to the GAL4 DBD, then transformed into Y2HGold (Fig. 1b). As shown in the figure, yeast cells harboring DBD–PmbHLH130 and DBD–PmbHLH130-N showed α-galactosidase activity.

Further quantitative analysis showed that PmbHLH130 was highly expressed across all examined tissues, with the highest expression observed in leaves (Fig. 1c). To determine the subcellular localization of PmbHLH130, a green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter construct was developed to express the PmbHLH130-GFP fusion protein, and the same vector expressing GFP only was used as a control. As expected, PmbHLH130 specifically localized in the nucleus, consistent with its putative role as a transcription factor (Fig. 1d). To preliminarily assess the expression pattern of PmbHLH130 during P. mume flowering, we sampled floral buds at various stages and conducted qRT-PCR assays (Fig. 1e). These experiments revealed that PmbHLH130 probably functions during the early phase of flower bud development (Fig. 1f).

Overexpression of PmbHLH130 promotes flowering in A. thaliana

Developing a reliable genetic transformation method for P. mume remains challenging, resulting in limited success in obtaining consistently transformed plants. To facilitate the investigation of the gene function of PmbHLH130, we employed the pCAMBIA-1301 vector to induce overexpression of PmbHLH130 in A. thaliana. Three transgenic lines overexpressing PmbHLH130 (designated PmbHLH130-OE1-3) were subsequently selected for phenotypic impact assessment (Supplementary Fig. 2a-c, Uncropped full-length gel is presented in Supplementary Fig. 3). Under inductive LD conditions, the PmbHLH130-OEs exhibited significantly promotes flowering, a severe acceleration of more than ~ 7 days in bolting time and ~ 5 less rosette leaves than wild type (WT) (Fig. 2a − c). Under SD conditions, similar to LD conditions, all transgenic lines bolted approximately 23 days earlier and produced about 21 fewer leaves than the WT (Fig. 2d–f).

Central oscillator proteins LHY and RVEs can directly bind to PmbHLH130

We analyzed the correlation of the circadian expression patterns between PmbHLH130 and central oscillator genes (Fig. 4a-b). The expression of PmbHLH130 showed significant positive correlations with PmLHY, PmLHY-like, PmRVE1, PmRVE3, PmRVE6 and PmRVE8, and negative correlations with PmGI, PmELF4, PmPRR5, PmPRR7 and PmLUX specifically (|r| > 0.5, p < 0.05).

Our predictive analysis of cis-elements showed that there was one evening element binding site (EE-box) present in the PmbHLH130 promoter (2 kb upstream of the start codon) (Fig. 5b). Using the fragment with this cis-element as a bait, the Y1H assay showed that PmLHY, PmLHY-Like, PmRVE1, PmRVE3 and PmRVE7 could bind to the EE-box, but PmRVE6 and PmRVE8 could not bind (Fig. 5a). EMSA assay was performed to further verify the direct interaction of EE-box from PmbHLH130 promoter with PmLHY, PmLHY-Like, PmRVE1, PmRVE3 and PmRVE7. The bands of the protein−probe complex were detected, and competition experiments showed that the shifted band was efficiently competed by the excess of unlabeled probe (Fig. 5c-g, Uncropped full-length blots are presented in Supplementary Figs. 4–8), further supporting the sequence specificity of their interaction with the promoter of PmbHLH130. Meanwhile, no band was detected after we changed to the mutant probe. Our results demonstrate that PmbHLH130 is regulated by multiple central oscillator proteins.

The PmIDD5 directly binds the promoter of PmbHLH130

Y1H screening using the PmbHLH130 promoter sequence identified a transcription factor from the IDD family. Through phylogenetic tree analysis with the 5 IDD proteins of A. thaliana, the results showed that this transcription factor was closely related to AtIDD5 (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Amino acid sequence alignment revealed extremely high similarity in the N-terminal ID domain and two C-terminal motifs, MSATALLQKAA and TR/LDFLG regions (Supplementary Fig. 9b). qRT-PCR analysis revealed that PmIDD5 exhibits peak expression during anthesis in P. mume, contrasting with the expression pattern of PmbHLH130 (Fig. 6a). The results of the Y1H assay showed that PmIDD5 bound to the promoter of PmbHLH130 (Fig. 6b). Additionally, D-LUC data indicated that PmIDD5 could inhibit the promoter activity of PmbHLH130 (Fig. 6c). The result of EMSA indicated that PmIDD5 could bind to the core element of IDD-box in vitro (Fig. 6d, Uncropped full-length blot is presented in Supplementary Fig. 10), suggesting that PmIDD5 was able to bind to the DNA element and directly bound to the promoter of PmbHLH130.

PmIDD5 and PmDELLA1 protein complex do not affect the regulation of PmbHLH130

Based on previous studies, we speculated that the regulation of PmbHLH130 by PmIDD5 may influence by the participation of DELLA proteins [23, 44]. We obtained two homologous genes of AtRGA in P. mume by amino acid sequence alignment (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Both genes were shown to have the function of early flowering and were named PmDELLA1 and PmDELLA2 [45]. We used the D-LUC assay to detect the effect of PmDELLA1 and PmDELLA2 on the transcription level of PmbHLH130. The results showed that the protein complex did not affect the inhibition of PmbHLH130 by PmIDD5 (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c).

PmbHLH130 directly improves the expression of PmCOL5

To further investigate the role of PmbHLH130 in regulating flowering, we hypothesized that PmbHLH130 might influence flowering by binding to the promoters of key flowering regulatory integration factors. Analysis of promoter regions identified the presence of the E-box, a binding element for the bHLH family, within the promoter of PmCOL5, suggesting a potential regulatory interaction between PmbHLH130 and PmCOL5 (Fig. 7c). In PmbHLH130-OEs, the expression level of AtCOL5 was significantly induced compared with WT (Fig. 3b). The Y1H assay confirmed the interaction of PmbHLH130 and the promoter of PmCOL5 (Fig. 7a). EMSA assay showed the recombinant His-PmbHLH130 protein directly binds to the E-box in the promoter of PmCOL5 (Fig. 7c, Uncropped full-length blot is presented in Supplementary Fig. 12). Additionally, D-LUC data indicated that PmbHLH130 could activate the promoter activity of PmCOL5 (Fig. 7b). These results suggest that PmbHLH130 may promote flowering by activating PmCOL5 expression.

Discussion

Flowering is precisely controlled by multilayered transcription regulatory networks. Early flowering is advantageous for plants constrained by environmental limitations or those with ornamental value, whereas delayed flowering extends the vegetative phase in bioenergy and forage crops [46, 47].

Homologs of flowering regulators identified in A. thaliana were found to be functionally conserved in other crops, whereas some other genes developed divergent functions [48]. In previous reports on the regulation of flowering by the bHLH family, the FBH branch played a major role [38, 39]. There are fewer reports on homologous genes of FBH in other species, in our study, we discovered that PmbHLH130, a homologous gene of FBH3/4, exhibits high expression at the early flowering stage and promotes flowering in overexpressed Arabidopsis (Figs. 1a and 2). Previous studies have demonstrated that AtFBH4 possesses significant potential for accelerating flowering time by the SnRK1-AtFBH4-CO module [39]. Consequently, we conducted comprehensive molecular studies of PmbHLH130. Our results showed that PmbHLH130 promotes flowering via the PmIDD5–PmbHLH130–PmCOL5 transcriptional cascade (Figs. 6 and 7).

In different plant species, the clockwork essentially comprises orthologous components with distinct variations [49, 50]. In this study, we found that the P. mume circadian oscillator protein PmLHY and its homologous proteins directly bind to the promoter of PmbHLH130 (Figs. 4 and 5), inducing its expression to oscillate within a 24-h period [51].

The IDD family is a highly conserved group of proteins in angiosperms. Our qRT-PCR results indicated that PmIDD5 was highly expressed during anthesis (Fig. 6a). Further research demonstrated that PmIDD5 directly binds to and represses the promoter of PmbHLH130 (Fig. 6b-d). Interestingly, both proteins promote flowering, raising the question of why PmIDD5 inhibits PmbHLH130 expression. This paradox suggests the possibility of additional interacting proteins that modulate the function of PmIDD5. Previous studies have shown that IDD family TFs can regulate gene expression by interacting with DELLA proteins. For instance, AtIDD3 interacts with the DELLA protein RGA to enhance SCL3 transcriptional activation, while PpIDDs (PpIDD4, PpIDD12, PpIDD13) interact with PpDELLA1 to boost GA20ox1 transcription [23, 44]. Building on this, we confirmed PmDELLA1 and PmDELLA2 do not influence the regulatory effect of PmIDD5 on PmbHLH130 expression (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c). Our findings revealed that as development proceeds from early to late flowering stages, PmbHLH130 expression gradually decreases while PmIDD5 expression increases (Figs. 1f and 6a). This suggests that PmbHLH130 and PmIDD5 are alternately expressed, playing a specific role in the early and late stages of flowering in P. mume, respectively.

In A. thaliana, CO constitutes a crucial component in the flowering pathway that triggers flowering by inducing the expression of the florigen FT in leaves [52–54]. Although we were unable to identify a homologous gene of CO in P. mume, PmCOL5 showed strong similarity to AtCOL5, and AtCOL5 was demonstrated to be a flowering-promoting factor [40, 55]. Consistently, we demonstrated that PmbHLH130 can directly bind to and activate the promoter of PmCOL5 to promote flowering (Fig. 7).

Conclusion

This study characterizes the PmIDD5-PmbHLH130-PmCOL5 transcriptional cascade and suggests its involvement in flowering regulation. PmbHLH130 is modulated by the transcription factor PmIDD5, thereby promoting flowering through the activation of PmCOL5 expression. Exploring further the possibility of modulating PmbHLH130 and associated regulatory modules to promote flowering in P. mume could be a promising direction for future research.
