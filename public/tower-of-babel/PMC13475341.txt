Artificial intelligence in pharmaceutics: Why prediction without formulation is incomplete
Authors: Alireza Maboudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractImage, graphical abstract

Artificial intelligence (AI) has seemingly transformed the early stages of drug discovery. By rapidly navigating vast chemical spaces to optimize binding affinities, molecular properties, and in silico ADME profiles [1], AI-driven pipelines have created a compelling promise: faster development timelines and substantially reduced attrition in drug development. Yet, this triumph is anchored in a fundamental and rarely examined illusion: the assumption that identifying an ideal drug candidate is equivalent to delivering a drug product. From a pharmaceutical perspective, this assumption is not just flawed, but also represents a critical weakness. Although AI algorithms are highly effective at identifying patterns in molecular graphs, they remain largely insensitive to formulation science, the discipline that ultimately determines whether a molecule can be developed into a useful medicine. This is not an argument against AI, since machine learning has already shown clear value in both molecule selection and, more recently, isolated formulation optimization. Rather, the problem lies in the way AI success is currently framed.

Missing pharmaceutical layer in AI-driven pipelines

Most AI drug discovery pipelines follow a common structure: target selection, molecular generation or screening, and prediction of biological and pharmacokinetic properties. To move beyond the simple distinction between an active molecule and a final drug product, it is essential to examine how current AI architectures systematically overlook formulation and processing variables. Most generative models represent molecules using 1D strings such as SMILES, or 2D molecular graphs [2]. In doing so, they treat the drug as an isolated entity in a vacuum, optimized largely for target-binding affinity. As a result, these models are fundamentally unable to capture 3D solid-state phenomena, including polymorphism, crystal lattice energy, and surface-area-dependent drug-excipient interactions. In addition, key process-related factors, such as mechanical shear during wet granulation or thermal stress during hot-melt extrusion, are completely absent from molecular training datasets. Consequently, AI systems prioritize chemical structures without any meaningful mathematical representation of how these molecules behave under real manufacturing conditions.

A major blind spot is solid-state behavior. Properties such as polymorphism, amorphous content and hygroscopicity can profoundly influence dissolution, stability and bioavailability [3]. AI models rely on molecular descriptors. These descriptors, however, correlate weakly with solid-state properties. As a result, Critical flaws like polymorphism are often discovered late, after substantial investment has already been made. An AI can promise perfect permeability. It can predict optimal clearance. But the molecule will still fail if its solid-state landscape is unmanageable. From a pharmaceutics viewpoint, this is not a rare exception but a recurrent pattern.

Although historical cases such as the polymorphic transformation of ritonavir clearly demonstrate the catastrophic consequences of late-stage solid-state surprises [4], the current AI-driven era faces its own distinct set of formulation bottlenecks. In contemporary generative and machine learning assistance design, models often optimize reward functions weighted toward target binding affinity (e.g., Kd, IC50) together with structural novelty. This algorithmic bias can unintentionally promote molecular obesity, yielding highly lipophilic, high-molecular-weight candidates with extremely poor solubility.

A clear example is provided by large, heterobifunctional molecules such as PROTACs (proteolysis-targeting chimeras) and related targeted degradation modalities [5]. In this area, computational approaches, increasingly supported by AI and machine learning, are very effective at optimizing the complex, bipartite structures needed for protein degradation. However, these AI-prioritized entities often fall well outside the boundaries of classical oral drug-likeness space, and create major developability challenges including very low aqueous solubility, high crystallinity, and poor manufacturability. In practice, such candidates frequently depend on advanced and resource-intensive formulation strategies (e.g., amorphous solid dispersions, complex lipid-based systems, or parenteral depot technologies) to achieve acceptable exposure.

Another neglected dimension is excipient-drug interaction. AI often treats formulation as a passive container, whereas in reality, it is a highly interactive system. Processes such as adsorption, complexation and microenvironmental pH effects can alter drug release and degradation pathways. However, current AI frameworks rarely incorporate excipient chemistry or formulation architecture in a meaningful manner. Manufacturability reveals the limitations of molecule-centric prediction. Manufacturing depends on the particle. Properties like flowability and compressibility arise from formulation design, rather than the molecular structure AI focuses on. Scale-up introduces additional layers of variability, where minor differences in material attributes or processing conditions can lead to disproportionate changes in product quality.

Stability under ICH conditions reflects the same disconnect. Factors such as chemical instability, moisture sensitivity, or excipient-mediated degradation often determine shelf life and packaging requirements. AI models are optimized for discovery-stage endpoints. They do not anticipate these real-world stability constraints. Consequently, promising candidates are terminated, not for lack of potency, but for lack of physical robustness. The result is a persistent gap between predicted developability and actual pharmaceutical feasibility. Reframing the problem

The central issue is not that AI predictions are inaccurate, but that they are incomplete. By optimizing molecules in isolation, current AI-driven paradigms underestimate the role of formulation as a co-determinant of success. Pharmaceutical scientists have long recognized that many development failures originate not from insufficient potency, but from formulation and manufacturability bottlenecks [6]. Ignoring these constraints at the prediction stage does not eliminate them; its simple delays their consequences. Pharmaceutics should not be regarded as a downstream validation step, but as an equal design layer that actively shapes prediction, optimization and decision-making. This requires redefining what is predicted, how success is measured, and who participates in AI-enabled workflows.

Current training datasets are dominated by molecular descriptors, bioactivity readouts and simplified ADME endpoints. What is largely missing are formulation-relevant attributes: solid-state outcomes, excipient compatibility patterns, process sensitivity, and stability under realistic storage conditions. Even imperfect or sparse formulation data, if systematically captured, would shift AI models from idealized chemical abstractions toward pharmaceutical realism. Importantly, the goal is not exhaustive prediction, but early flagging of high-risk formulation trajectories. Success metrics in AI-driven pipelines must be reformulated. At present, model performance is often evaluated by predictive accuracy against isolated endpoints. From a pharmaceutics perspective, a more meaningful metric is robustness: the likelihood that a candidate can tolerate formulation variability, manufacturing scale-up, and storage stress without significant clinical failure. Integrating robustness-oriented criteria into AI optimization, such as sensitivity to solid-state transitions or formulation-dependent bioavailability, would better align predictions with real development outcomes.

Equally important is the role of pharmaceutical scientists themselves. Current discussions around AI are often dominated by computational and data-science perspectives, while pharmaceutics is included only at the stage of validation. This separation is counterproductive. Pharmaceutical scientists possess domain knowledge that is difficult to infer from data alone: tacit understanding of process failure modes, formulation heuristics, and scale-up behavior. Embedding this expertise into AI development, through feature selection, constraint definition, and interpretation, will be essential for meaningful translation.

From an organizational standpoint, this perspective argues for earlier integration of formulation thinking into discovery teams. AI-guided prioritization that ignores pharmaceutical feasibility risks accelerating attrition rather than reducing it. Conversely, even modest formulation awareness at early stages can prevent late-stage failures that are disproportionately costly. In this sense, formulation-aware AI is not an optional enhancement, but an efficiency requirement. Limited formulation data do not necessarily mean unavailable data. Miniaturized high-throughput formulation screens, systematic solid-state characterization, and physics-informed surrogate models can generate early, low-resolution signals sufficient for AI-based risk stratification.

Unlike molecular discovery, formulation science lacks standardized, machine-readable databases. Addressing this gap will likely require precompetitive data consortia and harmonized reporting standards, enabling AI models to learn from formulation failures as well as successes. Introducing formulation constraints early in AI pipelines raises a critical and valid concern: the risk of penalizing highly novel, yet physiochemically challenging, candidate. A delicate balance must be struck between ensuring developability and preserving chemical diversity. The goal of formulation-aware AI is not to implement rigid filters that discard a potential breakthrough molecule simply because it is difficult to formulate. Rather, the objective is informed risk allocation. If an AI model flags a highly potent candidate for profound insolubility or solid-state instability, the molecule need not be abandoned. Instead, the AI system should explicitly link this molecule to the advanced formulation strategies (e.g., amorphous solid dispersions, lipid nanoparticles) required to rescue it. In this framework, chemical novelty is preserved, but the downstream pharmaceutical cost, time, and technological complexity are explicitly anticipated rather than discovered as costly surprises during late-stage development. Looking forward, the most impactful advances will likely come not from ever more complex algorithms, but from better alignment between prediction and pharmaceutical reality. AI should help answer not only “Which molecule is optimal?” but also “Which molecule-formulation combination is developable?” This reframing naturally shifts the focus from abstract prediction to translational relevance.

In conclusion, during early drug discovery, a specific formulation does not need to be fully defined; however, its biopharmaceutical consequences can be parameterized and incorporated into AI-driven decision-making as constraints or penalties. The promise of AI in pharmaceutics will not be fulfilled by prediction alone. Without formulation, AI risks optimizing molecules that cannot survive contact with pharmaceutical constraints. By reintegrating formulation science into AI-driven workflows, conceptually, methodologically, and culturally the field can move toward predictions that are not only accurate, but actionable.

Concrete framework for early developability integration

To transition from conceptual integration to practical application, we propose a formulation-informed multi-objective optimization framework. Instead of treating formulation as a downstream filter, this workflow embeds biopharmaceutical constraints directly into the generative AI reward function.

(i) Early biopharmaceutical parameters: Before a specific formulation is defined, AI models can evaluate intrinsic molecular properties that largely dictate formulability. Key examples include predicted Intrinsic solubility (S₀), melting point (Tm) and lipophilicity (logP). While target affinity (e.g., IC₅₀ or Kd) remains a primary objective, these physicochemical parameters act as boundary conditions on what is realistically formulable.

(ii) Role of formulation-informed surrogate models: Direct computation of complex physical interactions, including crystal lattice energies and detailed solvation thermodynamics, using molecular dynamics or quantum mechanics, is too computationally expensive to be integrated into generative AI loops. Physics-informed surrogate models, neural networks trained on high-fidelity physics simulations or high-quality experimental datasets, help overcome this limitation. These models can estimate molecular properties or precipitation risk from 2D or 3D molecular graphs, enabling generative systems to screen millions of candidates under approximate but physically meaningful constraints.

(iii) Computational workflow: Implementation requires modifying the AI objective function. A traditional reward function, for example, Rtotal= Raffinity+ Rtoxicity, can be expanded to include a developability penalty: Rtotal= w1Raffinity− w2Pformulation, where Pformulation is an aggregated penalty derived from surrogate models if a generated molecule exceeds acceptable thresholds for properties such as logP, Tm or predicted risk of poor solid-state stability. Within such a framework, molecules that are mathematically optimized for Kd but physically fragile can be systematically penalized and deprioritized early in the computational loop.
