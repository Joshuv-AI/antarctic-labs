Quantum theory for phonon lasing and non-classical state generation in mixed-species and single trapped ions
D. Baur, T. Behrle, I. Rojkov, J. Jeske, S. Yelin, J. Home, F. Reiter

Abstract
In this article we present a comprehensive theoretical investigation of phonon lasing with mixed-species trapped ions, as demonstrated in [T. Behrle, Phys. Rev. Lett. 131 (2023)], employing both a semi-classical mean-field description and a full quantum theory. We derive an analytic expression for the second-order coherence function, confirming the experimental observation of the system’s lasing behaviour above threshold. Building on the successful implementation of the two-ion lasing scheme, we propose a novel approach for achieving phonon lasing with a single trapped ion, offering significant experimental advantages and making the implementation of multiple phonon lasers within a single setup feasible. Furthermore, we explore lasing in a squeezed basis and in different regimes of the Lamb-Dicke approximation, highlighting the potential to produce non-classical states with promising applications in precision sensing. Our analysis of a sensing protocol based on squeezed states, using experimentally feasible parameters, shows a sensitivity enhancement of up to two orders of magnitude.

Introduction

Lasers are archetypal examples of open spin-boson systems. Analogous lasing dynamics can be observed when replacing the electromagnetic mode with that of a mechanical oscillator, yielding a phonon laser. These systems serve as versatile platforms for investigating fundamental physics, such as synchronization phenomena

Additionally, implementations involving quantum dots coupled to nanowires and THz acoustic nanocavities have been proposed
Khaetskii et al. 2013
;
Kabuss et al. 2012
. Recently, a novel type of quantum simulator was implemented and studied in a mixed-species trapped ion setup, operating as a phonon laser in the few-quanta regime
Behrle et al. 2023
. While a well-established quantum theory exists for photon lasing
Scully and Lamb 1967
, developing a quantum theory for this phonon laser has proven more challenging, particularly in describing key aspects, such as the second-order coherence function
Glauber 1963
. This is necessary to confirm the coherent nature of the lasing state.

This article aims to address this shortcoming and extends the existing mean-field treatment of the phonon lasing scheme to a full quantum theory. We propose extensions of the experimentally studied model, such as squeezed lasing dynamics and the utilization of higher-order terms in the Lamb-Dicke expansion as a saturation mechanism and as a means to reach non-classical steady states.

The realization of a phonon laser using mixed-species trapped ion chains is experimentally challenging, as it involves operating two separate laser systems and the simultaneous control of two ion species. This complicates the extension to a system of multiple coupled lasers. To address this challenge, we investigate a novel lasing scheme based on a single trapped ion, capable of analogous lasing dynamics as the two-ion model (see Fig.
1
for an overview of both schemes). We study this new model with the same theories as the two-ion model and discuss possible extensions. Specifically, we investigate the usage of the lasing threshold for enhanced sensing of mechanical oscillations. This technique is already routinely used for photon based lasers
Jeske et al. 2016
;
Raman Nair et al. 2021
;
Dumeige et al. 2019
;
Webb et al. 2021
. Additionally we explore how phonon lasing in a squeezed mode as well as the non-classical states generated by the use of the higher-order Lamb-Dicke terms could open up new ways of realising quantum advantage in sensing. We quantitatively explore the sensing enhancement when employing a squeezing scheme with experimentally achievable parameters.

This article is organized as follows: in Section
II
we present the two-ion phonon lasing scheme and investigate it in the mean-field approximation, as a first step to understanding the different phases the system can attain. We then move to a full quantum description of the system, which allows us to investigate the phonon statistics of the steady state. We discuss the possibility of using higher-order terms of the Lamb-Dicke expansion as a saturation mechanism and as a means of engineering non-classical states. Lastly, we extend the model to include squeezed lasing dynamics and discuss the applications of the squeezed phonon laser in sensing. In Section
III
, we propose a novel lasing scheme, which can be implemented on a single trapped ion. We show through a mean-field analysis, a quantum theory, and numerical simulations that in this simplified setup analogous lasing dynamics to those of the two-ion model can be observed. We investigate the effect of violating the first-order Lamb-Dicke approximation and its application as a saturation mechanism and show the implementation of squeezed lasing. In Section
IV
, we compare the single and two-ion lasing models, and discuss the physical origin of differences between them.

II
Two-ion phonon lasing model

The two-ion phonon lasing model, illustrated in Fig.
1
(a), comprises two effective two-level systems, referred to as spins, defined by the electronic states of the single valence electrons of each ion. Additionally, the model includes a shared harmonic mode, corresponding to one of the six motional modes the two ions share in a harmonic trapping potential. The spin states are described by Pauli operators, denoted by
\sigma_{\mathrm{x,y,z}}
and raising (lowering) operators
\sigma_{\mathrm{\pm}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\pm i\sigma_{\mathrm{y}}
. The harmonic motional mode is described by the bosonic creation (destruction) operator
a^{\dagger}
(
a
). The interaction between the spins and the motion of the ions is modeled with a Jaynes-Cummings (JC) Hamiltonian and an anti-Jaynes Cummings (AJC) Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{h}}

\displaystyle=g_{\mathrm{h}}(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{+}}+a\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{-}}),

(1)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle=g_{\mathrm{c}}(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathrm{-}}+a\sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathrm{+}}),

(2)

where
g_{\mathrm{h}}
is the driving strength given as
\eta_{\mathrm{h}}\Omega_{\mathrm{h}}\exp(-\nuh^2/2)
, with
\eta_{\mathrm{h}}
the Lamb-Dicke parameter and
\Omega_{\mathrm{h}}
the Rabi frequency (analogous for the other species with index
c
).

Additionally, a decay channel is implemented in each of the ions with time constants
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
, respectively. We introduce these channels into the description with the Lindblad jump operators

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{h}}=\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{-}}\quad\text{and}\quad L_{\mathrm{c}}=\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}\sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathrm{-}}.

(3)

The ion species which evolves under the JC interaction and the engineered decay effectively lowers the occupation of the motional mode. It is therefore referred to as the cooling ion (index c). The ion species which evolves under the AJC interaction and the decay channel effectively increases the occupation of the motional mode and is referred to as the heating ion (index h).

With these two dissipation channels and the two coherent terms given in Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) we can describe the lasing dynamics which can be observed in a two-ion lasing scheme. Note that by introducing incoherent channels from
\ket{0}
to
\ket{1}
on both ions, the system can be mapped onto standard lasing in an inverted two-level basis, where the role of heating and cooling is interchanged between the two ions.

II.1
Mean-field analysis

In this first subsection, we investigate the two-ion model using a mean-field approach in a semi-classical limit, giving access to the steady-state mean phonon number. Based on these results, we identify the different phases of the phonon laser. For this derivation, we assume that the steady-state phonon occupation is at a high average value
\expectationvalue{n}\gg 1
and that the variance of this state is large compared to the Fock level spacing
\expectationvalue{\Delta n}\gg 1

Scully and Zubairy 1997
. We can therefore assume that the time evolution of one JC or AJC cycle on either ion does not considerably change the average phonon value and its variance. This allows us to decouple the dynamics of the spin from the motion of the ion. We further make use of the first-order cumulant assumption for all system variables; for example

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\sigma_\mathrm{n}\sigma_\mathrm{m}}=\expectationvalue{\sigma_\mathrm{n}}\expectationvalue{\sigma_\mathrm{m}}\hskip 14.22636ptn,m\in\{x,y,z,+,-\},

(4)

which for the phonon field is equivalent to assuming a coherent state.
We set up the dynamical equation for the motional mode and the spin variables using the von Neumann equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{O}=i\expectationvalue{[H,O]}+\sum_{k}\expectationvalue{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}[L_k](O)},

(5)

with
H=H_{\mathrm{h}}+H_{\mathrm{c}}
and
L_{\mathrm{k}}\in\{L_{\mathrm{h}},L_{\mathrm{c}}\}
. This results in the following set of coupled equations

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{a}=

\displaystyle-ig_{\mathrm{c}}\expectationvalue{\smc}-ig_{\mathrm{h}}\expectationvalue{\sph},

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{\sph}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}{2}\expectationvalue{\sph}-ig_{\mathrm{h}}\expectationvalue{a}\expectationvalue{\szh},

(7)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{\spc}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}\expectationvalue{\spc}-ig_{\mathrm{c}}\expectationvalue{a^\dagger}\expectationvalue{\szc},

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{\szh}=

\displaystyle 2ig_{\mathrm{h}}\left(\expectationvalue{a}\expectationvalue{\smh}-\expectationvalue{a^\dagger}\expectationvalue{\sph}\right)

\displaystyle-\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\left(\expectationvalue{\szh}+1\right),

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{\szc}=

\displaystyle 2ig_{\mathrm{c}}\left(\expectationvalue{a^\dagger}\expectationvalue{\smc}-\expectationvalue{a}\expectationvalue{\spc}\right)

\displaystyle-\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}\left(\expectationvalue{\szc}+1\right).

(10)

We solve the dynamical equations for the spins under the assumption that the expectation values of the motional mode
\expectationvalue{a}
and
\expectationvalue{a^\dagger}
are constant, based on the separation of the time scale between spin and motional evolution. From this, we find the following expressions for the spin expectation values in the steady state

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\sph}

\displaystyle=\frac{2i\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}\expectationvalue{a}}{1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}I}\quad\text{and}\quad\expectationvalue{\spc}=\frac{2i\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}\expectationvalue{a^\dagger}}{1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}I},

(11)

where
I=\expectationvalue{a^\dagger}\expectationvalue{a}
, the phonon laser intensity or equivalently the average phonon number. Note that this derivation implicitly made the assumption that the phonon mode is in a coherent state. We will discuss in the next subsection a different approach to verify that this condition is indeed fulfilled. Inserting Eqs. (
11
) into Eq. (
6
) we find the evolution equation for the motional mode of the ions

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{a}=\expectationvalue{a}\left(\frac{\frac{2g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}}{1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}I}-\frac{\frac{2g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}}{1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}I}\right).

(12)

In a similar way, we derive the dynamical equation for
\expectationvalue{a^\dagger}
. With these two equations and the identity
\dot{I}=\expectationvalue{\dot{a}}\expectationvalue{a^\dagger}+\expectationvalue{a}\expectationvalue{\dot{a}^\dagger}
, we find the following evolution equation for the phonon laser intensity

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}I=2I\left(\frac{\frac{2g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}}{1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}I}-\frac{\frac{2g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}}{1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}I}\right).

(13)

This equation allows us to identify the different phases and corresponding steady states. The first steady state is given by
I=0
. As no phonons are excited, we refer to this solution as the “dark” phase, which is stable if
\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}>\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}
and
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}:=\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}<\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}=:\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
, i.e. if the effective cooling rate exceeds the effective heating rate and any population at a non-zero
n
is brought back to the vacuum state. This state becomes unstable if
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}>\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
and the system transitions into a steady-state lasing phase, characterized by a non-zero
I
. This transition corresponds to the well-known lasing phase transition at
C=1

Scully and Zubairy 1997
, with the cooperativity parameter in this case corresponding to
C=\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}}{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}
. Consequently, we refer to the condition
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}=\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
as the lasing threshold. This new steady state is defined by the brackets in Eq. (
13
) being equal to zero. This also corresponds to the intersection of the effective heating and cooling rate. We define these rates as

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{h}}=\frac{2\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}}{1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}I},

(14)

and analogously for the effective cooling rate
R_{\mathrm{c}}
. We translate this into a formula for the expected phonon number in the lasing steady state

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{ss}}=\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\left(\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}\right)}{8g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}(\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}-\gamma_{\mathrm{h}})}.

(15)

This state is stable if
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}>\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}>\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}
. If we violate the latter condition Eq. (
15
) predicts a negative phonon number. We will later identify this unphysical result as a runaway phase, where the phonon number diverges.

We further compare the mean-field result of Eq. (

) to the simulation of the system in Fig.

The simulated results shown in Fig.

). According to simulation, the sharp lasing phase transition predicted by the mean-field theory, is in fact a smooth crossover from a dark state with zero phonons to a non-zero intensity above threshold. This treatment gives us an overview of the phase diagram and an intuition for the microscopic processes leading to each of the four phases. The agreement between the simulation and the mean-field theory suggests that the phononic sub-system is indeed in a coherent state.

II.2
Quantum theory

In order to confirm the lasing nature of the steady state found in the mean-field analysis, we turn our attention to a quantum theory, which does not rely on the first-order mean-field assumption. We present an approach, which finds the phonon distribution by deriving a recurrence equation for
p(n)
, the phonon occupation probability at the Fock level
n
. Based on this we calculate the second-order coherence function
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)
and confirm the lasing nature of the steady state above the lasing threshold.

II.2.1
Phonon distribution and coherence

To find an expression for the phonon number statistics of the state, we calculate the expectation value of the dynamical equations in terms of the Fock levels
\bra{n}
and
\ket{n'}
. In this way, the information of the operator order is retained when manipulating the equations. For this derivation, we adopt the following notation based on Ref.
Scully and Lamb 1967

\rho_{\mathrm{xy,kl;nn^{\prime}}}:=\bra{x}_{\mathrm{h}}\bra{k}_{\mathrm{c}}\bra{n}_{\mathrm{m}}\rho\ket{n'}_{\mathrm{m}}\ket{l}_{\mathrm{c}}\ket{y}_{\mathrm{h}}
,
\rho_{\mathrm{xy;nn^{\prime}}}^{\mathrm{h}}:=\bra{x}_{\mathrm{h}}\bra{n}_{\mathrm{m}}\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)\ket{n'}_{\mathrm{m}}\ket{y}_{\mathrm{h}}
, where
x,y,k,l\in{0,1}
(analogous for
\rho_{\mathrm{xy;nn^{\prime}}}^{\mathrm{c}}
) and
\rho_{\mathrm{nn^{\prime}}}=\bra{n}_{\mathrm{m}}\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{h}}(\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho))\ket{n'}_{\mathrm{m}}
. For this derivation, we assume
g_{\mathrm{h}}\ll g_{\mathrm{c}}
, allowing us to separate the timescales on which the two ions evolve. The opposite case,
g_{\mathrm{h}}\gg g_{\mathrm{c}}
is analogous with heating and cooling ion exchanged. We start with the dynamical equations for the levels of the heating ion evaluated at the generic Fock level
n;n^{\prime}
. Due to the timescale separation, we can ignore the dynamics induced by the cooling ion Hamiltonian (c.f. Eq. (
2
))

\displaystyle=-ig_{\mathrm{h}}(\sqrt{n+1}\rho_{\mathrm{10;n+1n^{\prime}}}^{\mathrm{h}}-\sqrt{n^{\prime}+1}\rho_{\mathrm{01;nn^{\prime}+1}}^{\mathrm{h}})

\displaystyle+\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\rho_{\mathrm{11;nn^{\prime}}}^{\mathrm{h}},

(16)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\mathrm{01;nn^{\prime}+1}}^{\mathrm{h}}

\displaystyle=-ig_{\mathrm{h}}(\sqrt{n+1}\rho_{\mathrm{11;n+1n^{\prime}+1}}^{\mathrm{h}}-\sqrt{n^{\prime}+1}\rho_{\mathrm{00;nn^{\prime}}}^{\mathrm{h}})

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}{2}\rho_{\mathrm{01;nn^{\prime}+1}}^{\mathrm{h}},

(17)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\mathrm{10;n+1n^{\prime}}}^{\mathrm{h}}

\displaystyle=-ig_{\mathrm{h}}(\sqrt{n+1}\rho_{\mathrm{00;nn^{\prime}}}^{\mathrm{h}}-\sqrt{n^{\prime}+1}\rho_{\mathrm{11;n+1n^{\prime}+1}}^{\mathrm{h}})

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}{2}\rho_{\mathrm{10;n+1n^{\prime}}}^{\mathrm{h}},

(18)

This set of dynamical equations cannot be solved as it does not close, owing to the last term in equation Eq. (

). In order to express

in terms of
\rho_{\mathrm{00;nn^{\prime}}}^{\mathrm{h}}

n
, and we have used the notation
p(n)=\rho_{\mathrm{nn}}^{\mathrm{}}
. Eq. (
24
) can be split into two equivalent relations: one evaluated at
n
and a second one with the same terms evaluated at
n+1
. Using this, we find the solution for the relation
f_{1}(n)p(n-1)-f_{2}(n)p(n)=0
which reads

\displaystyle p(n)=p(0)\prod_{\mathrm{k=1}}^{\mathrm{n}}\left(\frac{f_{\mathrm{1}}(k)}{f_{\mathrm{2}}(k)}\right).

(25)

The value for
p(0)
can be determined through the normalization condition
\sum_{\mathrm{n}}p(n)=1

\displaystyle p(0)=\left(\sum_{\mathrm{n=0}}^{\mathrm{\infty}}\prod_{\mathrm{k=1}}^{\mathrm{n}}\frac{f_{\mathrm{1}}(k)}{f_{\mathrm{2}}(k)}\right)^{-1}.

(26)

With this, we found an analytic expression for
p(n)
which is given in its full form in the Appendix
C
. Using this expression for
p(n)
in the definition of the second-order coherence function given by

\displaystyle g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)=\frac{\expectationvalue{a^\dagger a^\dagger a a}}{\expectationvalue{a^\dagger a}^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{\mathrm{n}}n(n-1)p(n)}{(\sum_{\mathrm{n}}np(n))^{2}},

(27)

we obtain an explicit form of
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)
, which is given in the Appendix
C
. This result contains hypergeometric functions and is not straightforward to interpret. We therefore make an additional assumption:
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\ll\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
, which allows us to Taylor expand in orders of
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}/\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
. To lowest order, we find the analytic result

\displaystyle g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)=2-8\frac{\frac{2g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}-\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}}{(1+4\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}})(1+16\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}})}.

(28)

For
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}/\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}=0.5
and
g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}/g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}=0.125
(i.e. below the lasing threshold and within the stable regime) we obtain
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)=2
, corresponding to a thermal state. This result is in agreement with our previous mean-field analysis (c.f. Section
II
), where we expect to find the phonon laser in the dark state for these parameters. In order to gain more intuition for this result, we investigate it in the limit of
g_{\mathrm{c}}\ll\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
. This corresponds to the assumption that the cooling ion transition is heavily damped. In this case, we find the following expression in the lowest two orders of
g_{\mathrm{c}}/\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)\approx\left(1+\frac{1}{1+16\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}}\right)\left(1+4\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}\right).

(29)

We first only consider the lowest order
g_{\mathrm{c}}/\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
, i.e. neglect the second term in the right bracket.
If in addition the heating ion transition is heavily damped, i.e.
g_{\mathrm{h}}\ll\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}
, the effective action of both ions on the motional mode is incoherent. The system is therefore in a thermal state where
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)=2
. Depending on whether the heating or cooling process has a stronger effective rate, the phonon laser is either in the heating or in the dark phase. By increasing
g_{\mathrm{h}}
with respect to
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}
we find
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)=1
, which is the hallmark of a coherent state and lasing dynamics. This agrees with the intuition that as the heating process related to
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}
becomes more coherent, we approach a regime where lasing takes place. Note that as soon as the assumption
g_{\mathrm{c}}\ll\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
is relaxed and when taking into account second order terms,
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)
increases and the system moves from a coherent (
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)=1
) towards a thermal state (
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)=2
). This highlights the importance for one of the ion transitions to be incoherent in order to achieve coherent lasing dynamics.

II.3
Higher-order Lamb-Dicke terms as a saturation mechanism

The operation of a laser is based on a process which saturates, allowing it to reach a finite steady state. In many lasers, an effective two-level system, which reaches the fully mixed state, provides this saturating effect. This is also the case for the previous analysis, where the Lamb-Dicke approximation is used only to first order. It is however also possible to reach saturation of the lasing process using higher-order terms of the Lamb-Dicke approximation. These can be included when deriving the two sideband Hamiltonian’s in Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
)
Rojkov et al. 2026
;
Simoni et al. 2025
. We illustrate this for the Hamiltonian of the heating ion. To first order in the Lamb-Dicke (LD) expansion, we find the Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{h}}
, given in Eq. (
1
). However, if we include the third-order term of the Lamb-Dicke expansion, we get the following modified Hamiltonian of the heating ion

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{h}}

\displaystyle=g_{\mathrm{h}}\left(a^{\dagger}\Big(1-\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}a^{\dagger}a\Big)\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{+}}+\Big(1-\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}a^{\dagger}a\Big)a\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{-}}\right).

(30)

Note that
g_{\mathrm{h}}\propto\eta_{\mathrm{h}}
to lowest order in
\eta_{\mathrm{h}}
.
In the first-order LD Hamiltonian the terms proportional to the creation and destruction operators grow with a square root dependence on
n
, the number of phonons. In the third-order terms, the dependence is a more complex function of
n
. Note that, in general, the Lamb-Dicke expansion is given by a Laguerre polynomial. We can use this to engineer the slope of heating and cooling rates at their intersection, which modifies the phonon distribution in the steady state.

In order to illustrate the modified saturation process, we derive the mean-field result using the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
30
) (analogous for the cooling Hamiltonian). We find the following modified condition for the steady state

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{h}}:=\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}(2-I\eta_{\mathrm{h}}^{2})}{1+2\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}I}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}(2-I\eta_{\mathrm{h}}^{2})^{2}}=\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}(2-I\eta_{\mathrm{c}}^{2})}{1+2\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}I}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}(2-I\eta_{\mathrm{c}}^{2})^{2}}=:R_{\mathrm{c}}.

(31)

Note that
I
is the average phonon number. In the limit of
\eta_{\mathrm{h}}\ll 1
and
\eta_{\mathrm{c}}\ll 1
, we recover the first order Lamb-Dicke result Eq. (
13
). We define the right hand side to be
R_{\mathrm{h}}
, the effective heating rate and the left hand side
R_{\mathrm{c}}
, the effective cooling rate.

In Fig.
6
(a) we show the effective heating and cooling rates
R_{\mathrm{h/c}}
without the third-order Lamb-Dicke term, as a function of the phonon number
n
. Their intersection predicts (on a mean-field level) the mean phonon number. The slope of their intersection determines the spread of the phonon distribution. In Fig.
6
(c) we see a simulated steady state phonon distribution and a Poissonian fit. We find the system in a coherent state. The addition of the third-order Lamb-Dicke term, allows us to engineer a steeper intersection of the effective heating and cooling rates
R_{\mathrm{h}}
and
R_{\mathrm{c}}
. This in turn causes the phonon distribution to be narrower than in a coherent state, which is reached for the first-order Lamb-Dicke expansion (see Fig.
6
(b) and (d)). In other words, we can reach a state which has a sub-Poissonian distribution, i.e. is non-classical. To find an analytic expression for the steady state phonon distribution, one can follow a similar procedure as described in Ref.
Rojkov et al. 2026
.

We expect the distribution with a lower spread in phonon numbers to be beneficial for sensing protocols, as will be discussed further in the subsection on sensing applications. To engineer the intersection between effective heating and cooling rates, the two Lamb-Dicke parameters
\eta_{\mathrm{h}}
and
\eta_{\mathrm{c}}
can be tuned experimentally. In the case of trapped ions, this tuning can be achieved by adjusting the trap frequency or the angle of the laser drive relative to the ion’s motion.

II.4
Squeezing

A further interesting extension of the two-ion lasing model is the addition of lasing in a squeezed basis
Kienzler et al. 2017
. The bosonic squeezing operator is defined as
S(\xi)=e^{(\xi a^{2}-\xi(a^{\dagger})^{2})/2}
, where
\xi=re^{i\beta}

Kienzler et al. 2017
. In trapped ions, the squeezing of the motional states can be implemented using a bi-chromatic laser drive on both ion species
Kienzler et al. 2017
;
Kienzler et al. 2015
. Specifically, both the red and blue motional sidebands must be driven simultaneously on both ions. The Hamiltonians describing the system now read

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{BSB}}=g_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{BSB}}(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{+}}+a\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{-}}),

(32)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{RSB}}=g_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{RSB}}(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{-}}+a\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{+}}),

(33)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{BSB}}=g_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{BSB}}(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathrm{+}}+a\sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathrm{-}}),

(34)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{RSB}}=g_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{RSB}}(a^{\dagger}\sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathrm{-}}+a\sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathrm{+}}),

(35)

We add the two Hamiltonians on each of the ions and combine them to a Hamiltonian which is an effective sideband drive for a squeezed mode
A

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{h:total}}=H_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{BSB}}+H_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{RSB}}

(36)

\displaystyle=(g_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{BSB}}a^{\dagger}+g_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{RSB}}a)\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{+}}+(g_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{BSB}}a+g_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{RSB}}a^{\dagger})\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{-}}.

(37)

By defining
A:=\cosh(r)a+\sinh(r)a^{\dagger}
,
g_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{BSB}}:=g_{\mathrm{h}}\cosh(r)
and
g_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{RSB}}:=g_{\mathrm{h}}\sinh(r)
, which amounts to performing a Bogoliubov transformation, we find the following sideband Hamiltonian with the squeezed motional mode

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{h:total}}=g_{\mathrm{h}}(A^{\dagger}\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{+}}+A\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{-}}).

(38)

Analogously, the cooling Hamiltonian in the squeezed basis now reads

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{c:total}}=g_{\mathrm{c}}(A^{\dagger}\sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathrm{-}}+A\sigma^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathrm{+}}).

(39)

With this Bogoliubov transformation of our bosonic mode, we found the same lasing dynamics, i.e. the same phase diagram as for the basic phonon lasing model, with the key difference that we now lase in a squeezed mode given by
A
and
A^{\dagger}
. In Fig.
7
we compare the simulated phase space distribution of a non-squeezed phonon laser steady state (a) and a steady state, where we set the squeezing parameter to
r=0.8
(b). As expected from our discussion above, the steady state of the squeezed laser shows a similar shape up to the coordinate transformation of the underlying space. Using the scheme outlined above, we can therefore prepare a squeezed lasing state using a phonon laser. We will discuss in the next subsection explicitly how this can improve a phonon-laser based sensing scheme.

Simulated Wigner function
\mathcal{W}(\alpha)

\alpha

r=0.0
and (b) with
r=0.8
. As we choose the squeezing phase to be zero (

II.4.1
Applications in sensing

We can use the symmetry breaking of the U(1) symmetric steady state of the phonon laser when subjected to an external periodic drive as an amplitude sensing scheme, as demonstrated in Ref.
Behrle et al. 2023
. The highest susceptibility of the system is obtained near the lasing phase transition
Fernández-Lorenzo and Porras 2017
.

In order to quantify the sensitivity enhancement of the squeezed lasing scheme, we consider a resonant external signal of the following form

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{signal}}=\epsilon^{*}a+\epsilon a^{\dagger},

(40)

where
\epsilon=\absolutevalue{\epsilon}e^{i\phi}
, is the complex amplitude of the signal to be measured.
We now apply squeezing to our phonon laser with the coefficient
\xi=re^{i\beta}
.
By calculating the Fisher information (see Appendix
F
) we find an upper bound for the maximal achievable precision with an ideal measurement. For the phonon laser steady state close to the phase transition we find the modified Fisher information (and thereby the increase in maximal sensitivity) due to the applied squeezing

\displaystyle F_{Q}[\rho_{\absolutevalue{\epsilon}}]\approx\frac{2I^{2}}{A^{2}}W^{2},

(41)

where
W=\cosh(2r)-\cosh(r)\sinh(r)\cos(\beta-2\phi)
is the improvement from squeezing,
I
the steady state phonon occupation and
A=g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}/\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}
. Note, to reach the maximum sensitivity, the squeezing has to be applied in phase with the applied signal. In this case, we find an improvement in sensitivity
\propto 1/\cosh(2r)^{2}
, as compared to the non-squeezed case. For an experimentally obtainable squeezing parameter of
r=1.45
(see Ref.
Kienzler et al. 2015
) this results in an improvement of the sensitivity by a factor of approximately
80
.

II.4.2
Experimental considerations

For a standard phonon lasing scheme close to the phase transition sensitivities to an applied signal of up to
\Delta F\approx 53~\mathrm{yN}/\sqrt{\mathrm{I}}
can be reached
Fernández-Lorenzo and Porras 2017
. This result was calculated for a
{}^{40}\mathrm{Ca}^{+}
ion and a trapping frequency of
\omega_{T}/(2\pi)=10~\mathrm{MHz}
and
g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}/\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\approx 0.4~\mathrm{kHz}
. In the squeezed lasing case, bi-chromatic drives are required and their individual coupling rates have to be increased. For example, a squeezing parameter of
r=1.45
requires for the two sideband drives (Eq. (
32
),(
33
)):
g_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{RSB}}\approx 2.01g_{\mathrm{h}}
and
g_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{BSB}}\approx 2.25g_{\mathrm{h}}
; and analogous for the two cooling ion sidebands (Eq. (
34
),(
35
)). As discussed in
Kienzler et al. 2015
an experimental limitation for increasing the squeezing parameter even further is the violation of the Lamb-Dicke limit, which is reached for approx.
r=2.9
given a Lamb-Dicke parameter of
\eta=0.05
. As discussed in the previous section, the violation of the Lamb-Dicke limit is not detrimental for the lasing dynamics and instead can even be used as a tool to generate non-classical states
Rojkov et al. 2026
.

The squeezing further increases the ground state heating rate by a factor of
\cosh(2r)/2
, which for
r=1.45
results in
\cosh(2r)/2\approx 4.56
. This limits the coherence time of the motional state and therefore places a limit for the duration of the sensing scheme. However, with coherence times on the order of
30\,\text{ms}
(see Ref.
Kienzler et al. 2015
), this squeezed sensing scheme lies well within current experimental capabilities.

\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}<\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
). Since in this area the cooling rate exceeds the heating rate, we expect this unphysical result to indicate a steady state of zero phonon occupation, which we term “dark” state. Above the lasing threshold, we find a finite stable solution where we expect to find the laser in a coherent state. If we move to the regime of unstable solutions, given by
g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}/g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}>\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}/\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}
, we find a runaway phase above the lasing threshold, where the phonon number diverges (indicated by unphysical negative solution of Eq. (
55
), and the heating rate exceeding the cooling rate). Below the lasing threshold, there exists a zero phonon number steady state. We will show later, that this state is unstable against perturbations.

) to a simulation of the single ion phonon lasing model is given in Fig.

. The simulation numerically determines steady states of the Liouvillian described by Eqs. (

), and finds a finite solution for the parameter regions corresponding to the dark and lasing phases. The mean-field theory captures the two transition lines between the four phases as well as the qualitative behaviour in the lasing phase. Note that the white areas signify a negative phonon expectation value according to Eq. (

The non-zero solution of the center-right area turns out to be unstable against fluctuations and the remaining solution is a runaway phase. This behaviour is reflected in the simulation which reaches saturation.

There are some quantitative discrepancies between theory and simulation: the dark phase, which is predicted to be in the lower area of the phase diagram with zero phonons, is populated with few phonons in the simulation. This kind of discrepancy is however expected from a lowest-order mean-field analysis. To find the phonon distribution of the steady states attained in the lasing and dark phases, we need to investigate the system in a quantum theory.

III.2
Quantum theory

In order to confirm the coherent nature of the steady state in the lasing phase, we have to extend our analysis to a quantum theory including a description of the phonon statistics. We follow a similar procedure as for the two-ion model, where we set up the dynamical equations for the expectation values of individual ion levels. The full derivation is given in the Appendix
D
.

In the first step, we restrict ourselves to the special case where
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
, which is mathematically more tractable. Unlike the general case, this limit allows us to derive an exact expression for the second-order coherence function without further approximations. With this result we confirm a coherent state, however not in the lasing phase, as the lasing phase is present only where
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}<\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
. The more general case is treated in a second step as an extension of this derivation.

III.2.1
Special case
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}

As described in the Appendix
D
we find the following evolution equation for the occupation probability
p(n)

\displaystyle\dot{p}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}n}{1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}(n-1)+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}n}p(n-1)

\displaystyle-\frac{(\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}n+\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}(n+1))}{1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}n+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}(n+1)}p(n)

\displaystyle+\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}(n+1)}{1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}(n+1)+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}(n+2)}p(n+1),

(56)

where
\gamma=\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
.
In order to determine the steady-state phonon distribution, we assume
\dot{p}(n)=0
and split the above equation into two equivalent recurrence equations. The solution is given by the following steady-state phonon distribution

\displaystyle p(n)=p(0)\prod_{\mathrm{k=0}}^{\mathrm{n-1}}\left(\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}\right)^{k}\frac{1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}k+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}(1+k)}{1+8g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}.

(57)

The occupation of the vacuum level
p(0)
is then calculated using the normalization condition
\sum_{\mathrm{n}}p(n)=1
and is given by

\displaystyle p(0)=\frac{\left(\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}-\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)^{2}(1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}})}{\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{4}}{\gamma^{4}}-\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma^{4}}+16\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{4}g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma^{6}}}.

(58)

With this result for
p(n)
we use Eq. (
27
) to find the following exact expression for the second-order coherence function

\displaystyle g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)=2

\displaystyle\frac{(\gamma^{2}(g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}-g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2})+16g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2})}{(\gamma^{2}(g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}-g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2})+8(g_{\mathrm{c}}^{4}+3g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}))^{2}}\times

\displaystyle(\gamma^{2}(g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}-g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2})+16(g_{\mathrm{c}}^{4}+2g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2})).

(59)

We investigate three interesting limits of Eq. (
59
): firstly, if
g_{\mathrm{c}}\ll\gamma

\displaystyle g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)\approx 2+\mathcal{O}(g_{\mathrm{c}}^{4}),

(60)

showing that we obtain a thermal state if the transition on the cooling ion is overdamped. In the second case, where
g_{\mathrm{h}}\ll\gamma
, a Taylor expansion yields the following result

\displaystyle g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)\approx 2\frac{\left(1+16\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)}{\left(1+8\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}).

(61)

In this case, when the heating transition is overdamped,
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)
can reach any value between
0
and
2
. We therefore find an asymmetry between the heating and cooling coefficients. Lastly, we investigate a limit, where both
\gamma\ll g_{\mathrm{h}}
and
\gamma\ll g_{\mathrm{c}}
, and find the following expression

\displaystyle g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)\approx\frac{8g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}(g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}+2g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2})}{(g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}+3g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2})^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2}).

(62)

In this limit
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)=1
for a specific ratio of the two coherent driving strengths:
7g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}=(2\sqrt{2}-1)g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}
.

The exact expression Eq. (
59
) is compared to a simulation of the single-ion lasing model, and shown in Fig.
11
. Due to the exact nature of the result, we see agreement between theory and simulation in the finite phonon number regime. From the phase diagram in Fig.
9
we can see that this line does not pass through the lasing phase. We can therefore not confirm the coherent nature of the steady state attained by the system in the lasing phase. For this, we extend this approach to investigate a regime where
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}<\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
in the next subsection.

III.2.2
General case

The above derivation is insightful, as we got a compact formula for
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)
. However, this result is only valid for a special hypersurface of the parameter space. To derive an analytic expression for
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)
we assume
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\approx\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
. The resulting expression agrees well even when moving further away from
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
into the lasing phase, where
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}<\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
. The drawback of this general approach is the complexity of the resulting expression containing hypergeometric functions, which cannot easily be simplified. We therefore restrict ourselves to plotting this function and comparing it with the simulated values of
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)
.

The comparison is shown in Fig.
12
. The theory corresponds well to the simulation even when moving away from the area where
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\approx\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
. In the lasing phase, we recover values of
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)\approx 1
. This concludes the quantum theory analysis as we have shown that in the lasing phase we indeed have a coherent state.

. We find excellent agreement between theory and simulation. The two cuts shown in (c) and (d) are taken along the blue and red lines in (a) and (b), in a regime where we expect the above condition to be fulfilled.

III.3
Lamb-Dicke terms as saturation mechanism

As for the two-ion phonon laser, we investigate the effect of higher-order terms of the Lamb-Dicke approximation. Specifically, we derive the mean-field steady state phonon occupation for the single-ion lasing scheme, including the third-order term in the Lamb-Dicke expansion

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{ss}}=\frac{(\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}+\gamma_{\mathrm{h}})(\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{c}})}{4(\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}-\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}})(\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}+\kappa_{\mathrm{c}})+(\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}+\gamma_{\mathrm{h}})(\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}\eta_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}-\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}\eta_{\mathrm{c}}^{2})}.

(63)

For the limit of
\eta_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}\ll 1
and
\eta_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\ll 1
, this reduces to the mean-field result Eq. (
55
). The effect of the higher-order terms on the phonon distribution is similar to the two-ion case (c.f. Fig.
6
). An example of a steady state phonon distribution including third order terms of the LD expansion is given in the Appendix
G
and shows similar sub-Poissonian phonon statistics.

III.4
Squeezing

We also introduce squeezed lasing dynamics in the single ion model. Analogously to the two-ion model, this is realized by adding a bi-chromatic sideband drive to both the cooling and heating transitions. Specifically, the combination of blue and red motional sideband drive on a single transition can be mapped to a squeezed sideband drive by a Bogoliubov transformation, analogous to subsection
II.4
. The two resulting Hamiltonians take the form

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{h:total}}

\displaystyle=g_{\mathrm{h}}(A^{\dagger}\ket{2}\bra{0}+A\ket{0}\bra{2}),

(64)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{c:total}}

\displaystyle=g_{\mathrm{c}}(A^{\dagger}\ket{0}\bra{1}+A\ket{1}\bra{0}).

(65)

This allows us to map the system back onto the single-ion phonon laser Hamiltonian’s (Eq. (
42
) and (
43
)), with the key difference that we now lase in a squeezed bosonic mode given by
A
and
A^{\dagger}
. This mapping and a simulation of the steady state (c.f. Appendix
G
) demonstrates that the concept of squeezed lasing is not limited to the two-ion case but can also be realized for a single ion.

IV
Comparison of single and two-ion lasing model

As explored in the previous sections, the two-ion and single-ion lasing scheme exhibit similar lasing dynamics within certain parameter regimes, but differ in others. In this section, we examine these differences in detail and explore their underlying physical origins.

IV.1
Stability of solutions in the phase diagram

As can be seen from Eqs. (
15
) and (
55
), the two models have different phase diagrams and therefore different phase transitions. A schematic depiction of these phase diagrams is given in Fig.
13
.

Figure 13:

Phase diagram of the two-ion and single-ion phonon lasing model, as predicted from a mean-field analysis. Both systems have two transitions: the lasing transition at
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}=\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
, which is given by the diagonal line and is the same for both models. The second transition is given by the sign change of the non-linear term in the differential equation. Here, the two models differ: for the two-ion laser, the condition for this transition is given by
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
. For the single ion model, this transition is a function of the two coherent drive strengths as well as the two dissipation constants.
Crossing the latter transition to the right causes the non-linear term in the differential equation to be positive. This makes the system generally unbounded. In the unstable dark state, a local minimum exists, but upon crossing the lasing threshold, this minimum disappears and we are left with a diverging system. The four phases remain the same: dark and lasing phase for a negative non-linear term; heating and an unstable dark phase if the non-linear term is positive.

Let us start by performing a stability analysis of the two-ion model. As discussed, there are two phase transitions one at
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
and one at
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}=\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
. In order to understand each phase in more detail, we can rewrite Eq. (
13
) into the following form

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}I\propto 32I^{2}(\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}-\gamma_{\mathrm{c}})+4I\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}\left(\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}\right).

(66)

Neglecting the non-zero and positive denominator, we analyse the stability of different steady states by investigating the right-hand side of the above equation. Specifically, there are four possible scenarios: Firstly, a stable solution is found at
I_{\mathrm{ss}}=0
, in the case where the laser is below the lasing threshold (
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}<\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
) and the non-linear term proportional to
I^{2}
is negative (
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}<\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
). Secondly, there is a lasing phase, which is characterized by the stable solution at a non-zero
I_{\mathrm{ss}}
. This situation occurs if the laser is above threshold
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}>\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
and the non-linear term is negative
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}<\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
. The second solution
I_{\mathrm{ss}}=0
is unstable in this case. Third, the heating phase is given if the laser is above threshold and the non-linear term is positive
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}>\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
. In this case, an unphysical but stable solution appears at negative
I
. The physical solution at
I_{\mathrm{ss}}=0
is unstable and the system therefore diverges. Lastly, the fourth case is given by the laser below threshold with the non-linear term positive
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}>\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
, therefore allowing the system to diverge if the laser intensity reaches a large enough value. This is reflected by the stable solution at
I_{\mathrm{ss}}=0
and the unstable solution at a positive value of
I
. According to mean-field, the system would remain at the stable zero solution. However, taking fluctuations into account, it is plausible that the system diverges in this phase for large enough fluctuations. This behaviour is reflected in simulations.

We find a similar situation when analysing the single-ion model. Here, in contrast, the non-linear term is given by a combination of
g
and
\gamma
. Intuitively, this can be understood as the two transitions influencing one another trough the common ground state. The threshold condition is the same as in the two-ion scheme:
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}=\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
. Again, we investigate the stability of the solutions in the individual phases using the non-linear differential equation for
I
. For simplicity, we exclude the denominator from the description as it is strictly positive for all parameters

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}I\propto

\displaystyle 8I^{2}\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\left(\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}+\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}\right)\left(\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}-\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2I\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}(\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}+\gamma_{\mathrm{c}})\left(\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}\right).

(67)

We are interested in the two brackets which change the sign of the terms proportional to
I^{2}
and
I
. The dark state is given by the laser being below threshold, i.e.
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}<\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
and with negative sign of the non-linear term:
\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}<\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}
. Here, there is one stable solution at
I_{\mathrm{ss}}=0
and an unphysical, unstable solution at negative intensity. The stable solution becomes unstable when the lasing threshold is crossed but the non-linear term remains negative. Here, a stable solution at a strictly positive value of
I
appears. This corresponds to the lasing phase. If the non-linear term becomes positive, i.e.
\frac{g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}>\frac{g_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}
and the laser is above threshold, the only stable solution is at an unphysical negative value of
I
, and the solution of
I_{\mathrm{ss}}
is unstable. The system therefore diverges. The last phase is below threshold
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}<\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
and at positive non-linear term. Here, the mean-field results predict a zero-intensity solution. However, due to the proximity of an unstable solution at a positive value
I
, the system still diverges if fluctuations are large compared to the potential barrier separating the stable from the unstable solution.

This analysis shows that the same four phases are present in both lasing models: dark, lasing, heating with the dark state susceptible to fluctuations. The threshold condition
\kappa_{\mathrm{h}}=\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
is also the same for both models. The difference appears in the non-linear term, where for the two-ion model this is only given by the relative value of
\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
. In the single ion model, it is given by a mixture of the
g
and
\gamma
parameters of the two transitions. This manifests in a different shape of the phase diagram, as can be seen in Fig.
13
.

V
Conclusion and outlook

In this article, we have detailed and extended the existing theory framework of the two-ion phonon laser
Behrle et al. 2023
to a full quantum theory and studied different modifications of this model. With the quantum theory, we were able to find an analytic expression for the second-order coherence function
g^{\mathrm{(2)}}(0)
and theoretically confirm the lasing nature of the steady state above the lasing threshold as observed in experiments
Behrle et al. 2023
.

Additionally, we investigated possible extensions of the system, which allow lasing in a squeezed basis as well as obtaining non-classical steady states by utilizing higher-order Lamb-Dicke terms for saturation. We provided a brief discussion on how squeezed states can enhance the sensitivity of phonon laser-based sensing protocols, motivated by the interest in using such systems to perform precision measurements. In experimentally realistic scenarios, our results promise an improvement of sensitivity by a factor of up to 80.

In the second part, we propose a novel lasing scheme implemented on a single ion. Such a model can significantly reduce experimental efforts, simplifying the implementation of multiple such phonon lasers within the same setup. We performed a mean-field analysis to study the phase diagram and also investigated the model using a quantum theory. The latter allowed us to confirm the lasing nature of the state above threshold. As with the two-ion model, we also explored possible extensions for this system and found that squeezing can be implemented analogously.

This study paves the way for investigating multiple phonon lasers operating simultaneously in a single setup, both theoretically and experimentally. Such a setting could be interesting for synchronization and sensing experiments. The addition of squeezing to the phonon laser holds the promise to be beneficial for its sensing capabilities.
More recently, studies of quantum systems similar those from Ref.
Behrle et al. 2023
show the emergence of new interesting physics when going beyond the first-order Lamb-Dicke approximation, such as stabilization of a Schrödinger cat-like state
Rojkov et al. 2026
;
Simoni et al. 2025
. This underscores the rich potential of phonon lasing for advancing both fundamental physics, practical sensing applications as well as new schemes to implement quantum error correction.

5

\rho

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}=\mathcal{L}(\rho)=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{k}\mathcal{D}[L_{k}](\rho).

(68)

The Liouvillian

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{O}=\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho O)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\frac{d\rho}{dt}O\right).

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}[L_k](O)}=\expectationvalue{L_k^\dagger O L_k - \frac{1}{2}(L_k^\dagger L_k O + O L_k^\dagger L_k)}.

Appendix E
Derivation of higher-order Lamb-Dicke terms

In this section, we derive the higher-order terms which appear if we leave the regime, where the first-order Lamb-Dicke approximation is valid. We start this derivation with the interaction Hamiltonian of the laser field and the ion in the harmonic trap

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{I}}=\Omega\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{+}}+\sigma_{\mathrm{-}}\right)\left(e^{i(kz-\omega t+\phi)}+e^{-i(kz-\omega t+\phi)}\right),

(141)

where
\omega
and
\phi
are the frequency and phase of the laser drive,
\Omega
is the Rabi frequency and determined by the geometry of the setup and the laser drive. By moving into the interaction picture with respect to the ion trap and ion spin Hamiltonian, we obtain the following interaction Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{I}}=\Omega\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{+}}e^{i\omega_{\mathrm{q}}t}+\sigma_{\mathrm{-}}e^{-i\omega_{\mathrm{q}}t}\right)\left(e^{i(\eta(ae^{i\omega_{\mathrm{m}}t}+a^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{\mathrm{m}}t})-\omega t+\phi)}+e^{-i(\eta(ae^{i\omega_{\mathrm{m}}t}+a^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{\mathrm{m}}t})-\omega t+\phi)}\right),

(142)

where we have written
kz=\absolutevalue{k}z_{\mathrm{0}}(a+a^{\dagger})
and
\omega_{\mathrm{m/q}}
is the motional frequency of the trap and the qubit frequency respectively. With a rotating wave approximation, we end up with

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{I}}=\Omega\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{+}}e^{i(\eta(ae^{i\omega_{\mathrm{m}}t}+a^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{\mathrm{m}}t})-\delta t+\phi)}+\sigma_{\mathrm{-}}e^{-i(\eta(ae^{i\omega_{\mathrm{m}}t}+a^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{\mathrm{m}}t})-\delta t+\phi)}\right),

(143)

where
\delta=\omega_{\mathrm{q}}-\omega_{\mathrm{m}}
. We now use the following identity to simplify the above expression:

\displaystyle e^{i(\eta(ae^{i\omega_{\mathrm{m}}t}+a^{\dagger}e^{-i\omega_{\mathrm{m}}t}))}=e^{\frac{-\eta^{2}}{2}}\sum_{\mathrm{n,m}}^{\infty}\frac{(i\eta a^{\dagger}e^{i\omega_{\mathrm{m}}t})^{n}(i\eta ae^{i\omega_{\mathrm{m}}t})^{m}}{n!m!}.

(144)

With this and by setting
\delta=\pm\omega_{\mathrm{m}}
we get an expression for the two sideband Hamiltonians:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{BSB}}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\mathrm{h}}e^{\frac{-\eta^{2}}{2}}\eta\sum_{\mathrm{j=0}}^{\infty}\frac{(-\eta^{2})^{j}}{(j+1)!j!}\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{+}}(a^{\dagger})^{j+1}(a)^{j}+\sigma_{\mathrm{-}}(a^{\dagger})^{j}(a)^{j+1}\right),

(145)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{RSB}}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\mathrm{c}}e^{\frac{-\eta^{2}}{2}}\eta\sum_{\mathrm{j=0}}^{\infty}\frac{(-\eta^{2})^{j}}{(j+1)!j!}\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{+}}(a^{\dagger})^{j}(a)^{j+1}+\sigma_{\mathrm{-}}(a^{\dagger})^{j+1}(a)^{j}\right).

(146)

We identify
g_{\mathrm{h}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{h}}e^{\frac{-\eta^{2}}{2}}\eta
and
g_{\mathrm{c}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{c}}e^{\frac{-\eta^{2}}{2}}\eta
. From this, we recover the usual blue and red sideband Hamiltonians for
j=0
and then the higher-order terms for increasing powers of
\eta
.

Appendix F
Derivation of Fisher information for a squeezed phonon laser

We present a derivation of the Fisher information for the case of lasing in a squeezed mode, following the approach outlined in Ref.
Fernández-Lorenzo and Porras 2017
. We start with the lasing Hamiltonian of the heating ion and the squeezed bosonic mode
A
, the adiabatically eliminated cooling ion and the applied signal Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{h,sq}}

\displaystyle=g_{\mathrm{h}}(A^{\dagger}\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{+}}+A\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{-}}),

(147)

\displaystyle L_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}\sigma^{\mathrm{h}}_{\mathrm{-}},

(148)

\displaystyle L_{\kappa,sq}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}A,

(149)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{signal}}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{*}a+\epsilon a^{\dagger},

(150)

where
A=S(\xi)aS^{\dagger}(\xi)
and
S(\xi)=e^{(\xi a^{2}-\xi(a^{\dagger})^{2})/2}
. We find the equations of motion using the master equation in Lindblad from and trace out the spin degree of freedom. By expanding in orders of
g_{\mathrm{h}}/\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}
and neglecting third and higher order terms, we end up with the modified evolution equation for the reduced density matrix:

\displaystyle\dot{\rho_{f}}=

\displaystyle-(\epsilon^{*}a\rho_{f}+\epsilon a^{\dagger}\rho_{f}-\epsilon^{*}\rho_{f}a-\epsilon\rho_{f}a^{\dagger})

\displaystyle+\frac{16g_{\mathrm{h}}^{4}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{3}}(AA^{\dagger}\rho_{f}AA^{\dagger}-(A^{\dagger})^{2}\rho_{f}A^{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{2g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}(2A^{\dagger}\rho_{f}A-AA^{\dagger}\rho_{f}-\rho_{f}AA^{\dagger})

\displaystyle+\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}A\rho_{f}A^{\dagger}-\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}(A^{\dagger}A\rho_{f}+\rho_{f}A^{\dagger}A).

(151)

Analogous to the Fokker-Planck method, we translate this equation into a partial differential equation, by expressing the density matrix in terms of a quasi probability distribution P

\displaystyle\rho=\int d^{2}\alpha P(\alpha,\alpha^{*},t)S(\xi)\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}S^{\dagger}(\xi).

(152)

Following the same derivation as outlined in Ref.
Scully and Zubairy 1997
;
Fernández-Lorenzo and Porras 2017
, we get the following expression for the steady-state

\displaystyle P(I,\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\exp\left[-\frac{B}{2A}I^{4}+\frac{A-C}{A}I^{2}-\frac{2\absolutevalue{\delta}}{A}I\sin(\theta-\gamma)\right],

(153)

where
A=\frac{2g_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}}
,
B=\frac{16g_{\mathrm{h}}^{4}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^{3}}
,
C=\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
and

\displaystyle\delta:=\cosh(r)\epsilon-e^{i\beta}\sinh(r)\epsilon^{*}=\absolutevalue{\delta}e^{i\gamma}.

(154)

Note that the squeezing parameter is given by (
\xi=re^{i\beta}
), as in the main text. The optimal measurement operator for determining the amplitude of the external signal
\epsilon
is given by

\displaystyle P_{\gamma}=-i(Ae^{-i\gamma}-A^{\dagger}e^{i\gamma}).

(155)

The Fisher information of such a measurement is given by

\displaystyle F_{Q}[\rho_{\absolutevalue{\epsilon}}]\approx\frac{2I^{2}}{A^{2}}W^{2},

(156)

where
W=\cosh^{2}(r)+\sinh^{2}(r)-\cosh(r)\sinh(r)\cos(\beta-2\phi)
. In the optimal case, the squeezing and the phase of the applied signal are orthogonal to one another. In this case we improve the sensitivity with
\propto 1/(\cosh^{2}(r)+\sinh^{2}(r))^{2}
.
