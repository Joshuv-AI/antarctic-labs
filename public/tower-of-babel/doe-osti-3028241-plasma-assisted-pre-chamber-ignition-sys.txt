EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 This project explored advanced ignition technologies to significantly enhance efficiency and
reduce operating costs for heavy-duty natural gas engines operating at stoichiometric conditions,
while meeting ultra-low NOx emission standards. The main goal was to develop and validate a
plasma-assisted pre-chamber ignition system that could deliver at least a 2% increase in brake
thermal efficiency (BTE) and a 4% decrease in total cost of ownership (TCO) compared to a typical
multi-cylinder engine with three-way catalyst aftertreatment, ensuring compatibility with the
expected 2027 EPA/CARB regulations.
 In the first half of the project, the research team concentrated on developing and testing plasma-
assisted pre-chamber ignition using nanosecond pulsed discharges. Extensive experiments were
conducted in an optically accessible rapid-compression and expansion machine, a constant-volume
chamber, and an optical single-cylinder engine. Experiments were coupled with CFD simulations.
The work produced unique insights into pre-chamber flame formation, jet ignition, dilution effects,
and flame quenching at pressures, temperatures, and dilution levels relevant to engines. Although
plasma-assisted ignition showed promise in controlled lab settings, the research also identified
fundamental and practical challenges when applying this technology to real engine conditions.
 Midway through the project, a crucial pivot was made, guided by three key findings. First, the
power electronics required for nanosecond plasma discharges were found to be too costly for
commercial use, undermining the project’s cost-of-ownership goals. Second, nanosecond plasma
ignition was highly sensitive to turbulent flow in the pre-chamber, resulting in lower ignition
reliability than traditional spark under engine-like conditions. Third, achieving a truly diffuse low-
temperature plasma at high pressures near top dead center was not possible, reducing the
anticipated chemical enhancement benefits. These results collectively suggested that continuing
with plasma-assisted ignition was unlikely to meet both efficiency and cost objectives.
 In response, the project shifted focus to a more realistic approach: enhancing traditional spark-
based pre-chamber ignition with significantly less spark energy. Using insights gained earlier in
the project, the team redesigned the pre-chamber to maintain high dilution tolerance and quick
combustion, even with lower ignition energy. Testing confirmed that with optimized pre-chamber
design and combustion timing, a lower-energy spark could reliably ignite highly diluted
stoichiometric mixtures, reduce burn time, and boost thermal efficiency.
 Final engine testing and techno-economic analysis verified that this revised approach
successfully achieved the project goals. The optimized pre-chamber ignition system provided over
a 2% increase in calculated brake thermal efficiency compared to the baseline engine. Notably, the
lower ignition energy and simplified hardware reduced component stress, extended maintenance
intervals, and lowered the total cost of ownership. When used with stoichiometric operation and
traditional three-way aftertreatment, the system remained compatible with near-zero NOx
emissions targets without increasing cost or complexity in the emissions control system.
 In summary, although the project deviated from its initial plasma-assisted ignition idea, the
work produced a more practical and commercially viable solution. The results show that precisely
optimized, low-energy pre-chamber spark ignition can significantly improve efficiency and reduce
overall ownership costs for heavy-duty natural gas engines. This directly aligns with DOE goals
for cleaner, more efficient, and cost-effective transportation technologies.

1. OBJECTIVES AND PROJECT SCOPE

a. Objectives

The objective of the project was to research, develop, and validate a next generation heavy duty
pre-chamber ignition system for engines, capable of improving calculated brake thermal efficiency
(BTE) by ≥2% and achieving total ownership cost reduction of 4% when compared to the multi-
cylinder baseline spark-ignited engine using commercial three-way catalyst after treatment while
achieving near zero (0.02 g/hp-hr NOx) emissions and meeting all other criteria pollutant limits
for a 2027 EPA/CARB certification with no additional cost for of the emission control system.
Efficiency and cost improvements were achieved with a pre-chamber ignition system that uses
lower spark energy, while the maintenance period for ignition components was projected to be
extended. Performance was validated through engine dynamometer tests, and cost benefits were
calculated using a techno-economic analysis (TEA).

b. Scope of Work

The project was conducted in 3 budget periods, with tasks aligned specifically with each:

Budget Period 1 (Tasks 1.1-1.4): The plasma-assisted pre-chamber was constructed, and a baseline
performance comparison will be conducted between the plasma-assisted pre-chamber and a
conventional pre-chamber.

Budget Period 2 (Tasks 2.1-2.4): Fundamental optical engine and Rapid Compression and
Expansion Machine (RCEM) experiments were conducted using the plasma-assisted pre-chamber
hardware.

Budget Period 3 (Tasks 3.1-3.3): The ignition system was verified for performance and emissions
targets through experiments and modeling, including Total Cost of Ownership

c. Summary of Technical Work

The goal of the technical work conducted in the project was to develop an ignition system that
could transform the natural-gas engine market by overcoming several key barriers to wider
adoption of natural gas: 1) superior engine efficiency compared to contemporary diesel engines,
and 2) prolonged service intervals due to the long igniter lifetime and reduced demands for exhaust
aftertreatment associated with stoichiometric operation. The project team had unique experimental
and simulation capabilities needed to achieve these ambitious project goals. An overview of the
proposed project is illustrated in Figure 1.

The proposed ignition system initially did not plan to use existing technologies. It proposed using
plasma-assisted igniters and pre-chamber combustion together to overcome the barriers posed by
each technology alone. Barriers include cyclic variability, pre-chamber spark plug lifetime, and
combustion speed, which limit dilution to achieve superior efficiency and extend igniter lifetime.
The igniter system was iteratively improved through successive tests, relying on visualization in

Halfway through the 3+ year project, the research encountered significant hurdles in implementing
low-temperature plasma in the test systems and the engine. Conclusions of the initial work were
threefold:

 1. The power supply and pulser hardware required to achieve nanosecond discharges are cost
 prohibitive. Even though the hardware used in the project is research-grade, there is little
 data or information from suppliers on how they could be made more cheaply. The research
 pulser for one igniter currently costs more than $50,000. Even at 100x the per-cylinder cost,
 the low-temperature plasma ignition system will cost significantly more than existing coils
 and spark plugs. Therefore, the project goals for reduced total cost of ownership are unlikely
 to be achieved.
 2. Nanosecond plasma pulses are strongly affected by the turbulent environment in the pre-
 chamber, resulting in poor ignition performance. Data from single-cylinder engine
 experiments show that, under engine-relevant conditions, pulsed plasma igniters are less
 reliable at igniting than the baseline spark. However, in the constant-volume chamber
 without turbulence, the igniters performed better. This indicates that turbulence in the pre-
 chamber causes short-duration spark events to dissipate, resulting in poorer-quality ignition.
 3. Diffuse low-temperature plasma is not possible at the pressures found near the TDC of the
 engine. In the original proposal, it was thought that low-temperature plasma would be
 4

 achievable with the pulser/power supply hardware used in the project. After considerable
 experimentation, it appears that, rather than a diffuse, radical-rich plasma, nanosecond spark
 discharges occur that are highly sensitive to the flow field and do not yield higher-quality
 ignition than the baseline spark.

These conclusions caused the project to shift direction and explore reducing the energy of
conventional spark systems while preserving dilution tolerance at stoichiometric mixture
conditions. A comprehensive set of experiments with varying spark energy was conducted using
an optimized prechamber design to demonstrate that lower TCO is achievable by reducing the
maintenance interval for the ignition system and increasing the engine's thermal efficiency through
a shorter burn duration at high EGR rates. The next section describes the tasks completed in the
project and documents the development of its technical work.

Subtask 1.1.1 – Establish baseline data on a single-cylinder optical engine

Figure 1.1.1 and Figure 1.1.2 present selected representative snapshots of high-speed infrared and
chemiluminescence images taken at key crank angle timings that depict the development of pre-
chamber jets, main-chamber ignition and subsequent combustion, for a cycle that does fire without
and with combustion residuals for an EGR test condition with [O2] = 16.8% and spark timing of
350 CAD and 338 CAD, respectively.

The IR images shown in the top panel of Figure 1.1.1 indicate that when external EGR is applied
even in the absence of combustion residuals, there is a prolonged delay period (~6 ºCA) between
the ejection of the pre-chamber jets and the ignition of the main-chamber charge. The OH* images
indicate that the pre-chamber jets appear to have been quenched as they exit the pre-chamber, as
indicated by the absence of the OH* signal in the periphery of the pre-chamber following the
ejection of the pre-chamber jets, as seen in the IR images. Further, the first sign of chemical activity
in the main-chamber, namely the appearance of OH* signal, happens after a prolonged delay
period during which the contents of the pre-chamber jets appear to mix with the main-chamber
charge before causing its ignition. This is in stark contrast to the baseline no EGR condition in
which the ignition of the main-chamber charge was almost instantaneous following the ejection of
the pre-chamber jets with no apparent quenching visible. The OH* chemiluminescence images
also indicate that the main-chamber ignition tends to happen further downstream of the pre-
chamber at a few random locations (analogous to “auto-ignition” in a diesel-jet) which then spread
to adjacent pre-chamber jets. This again is distinctly different from the baseline condition, which
typically resembles flame propagation originating from the pre-chamber jets, which function as a
distributed surface ignition source for the surrounding natural gas charge.

With combustion residuals (Figure 1.1.2, bottom panels), the IR images show no significant
difference in the initial development of the pre-chamber jets other than the expected temporal shift
caused by the slower flame propagation within the pre-chamber. However, following the ejection

and mixing of the pre-chamber jets, in most cycles (30 out of 40), there is no main-chamber charge
ignition (absence of OH* signal). Even for the few cycles (10 out of 40) that do fire, IR imaging
shows a long mixing period (~18 ºCA) before flame propagation is initiated from an isolated
singular ignition spot in the main-chamber closer to the bowl wall. The ensuing combustion
process happens late in the expansion stroke, resulting in unfavorable combustion phasing with
limited cylinder pressure rise. Furthermore, the combustion closely resembles typical “spark-
ignition” flame propagation originating from a single spark kernel rather than turbulent-jet (pre-

By comparing the series of images obtained at advanced spark timing (338 CAD) presented in
Figure 1.1.2 with the no-EGR baseline case, the spatio-temporal progression of pre-chamber jet
development, main-chamber ignition, and the ensuing combustion shows significant resemblance,
barring one major difference. Despite the spark advance, the near-instant ignition of the main-
chamber charge following the ejection of the pre-chamber jets is not re-established in the presence
of EGR, with a slight delay (~1.5 °CA) between the two processes. This likely indicates that a
certain degree of partial flame quenching still exists in the pre-chamber jets as they exit through
the orifices, and that auto-ignition, in addition to flame-propagation, may serve as a means for
main-chamber charge ignition. At times, the OH* chemiluminescence images also show isolated
pockets of OH* signal near the bowl wall, typically disconnected from the pre-chamber jets. These
are potentially secondary auto-ignition spots initiated by the tip of the pre-chamber jet, while the
initial ignition spot occurred at the tail. Otherwise, all the qualitative observations and inferences
drawn about the spatio-temporal progression of combustion and the influence of combustion
residuals discussed earlier in the context of the baseline case with no EGR and optimal spark timing
of 350 CAD hold true here in the presence of EGR at the advanced sparking timing of 338 CAD.

Subtask 1.1.2 – Design and build a reliable pre-chamber system

The pre-chamber system for the optically accessible RCEM consisted of two parts: the optical pre-
chamber dome and the stainless steel (SS) housing. The RCEM combustion chamber has four 1-
inch ports for optical access on each side (§ 1.2.2), and the maximum distance between the piston
face at TDC and the end-wall window is 21 mm. This imposes a physical constraint on the pre-
chamber geometry. Hence, a hemispherical pre-chamber with a radius of 10.3 mm provides the
most optical access. Three iterations of the pre-chamber body with the same internal volume
(~7.5% of Vmain chamber) are developed with 1, 3, and 8 nozzles (diameter = 1.6 mm).

Two sets of pre-chambers were manufactured from SS (CSE Machine Shop) and fused silica
(Sealed Optics, LLC). Static analysis of the fused silica pre-chamber (Figure 3) performed with a
differential pressure of 10 bar (on all interior surfaces) yielded a factor of safety of 2.7 (assuming
the rupture modulus of fused silica as 7900 psi).

Subtask 1.1.3 – Develop and optimize plasma igniters

A variety of plasma igniters are developed by modifying Brisk Silver Racing 10 mm spark plugs
(A10S and A14YS). The J-hook on these non-resistor plugs is removed, and the central electrode
is covered with a thin layer of a high-strength dielectric (850V/mil) epoxy potting compound.
Another variant is developed by laser welding a thin copper sheet that is cut and shaped into four
prongs.

Subtask 1.1.4 – Plasma-assisted pre-chamber assembly and testing

The pre-chamber was installed in the Rapid Compression and Expansion Machine (RCEM). The
Transient Plasma Systems Inc. SSPG-101-HF pulse generator was acquired by UMN, and a high-
voltage DC power supply (Magna-Power SL1500-1.7/208) was also delivered. The HVDC power
allows the pulse generator to run the plasma igniters with up to 200 pulses (~10 ns). The pre-
chamber assembly was successfully tested with a conventional spark-ignition system (15 ms pulse
width).

Task 1.2 – Prepare optical engine and Rapid Compression and Expansion Machine (RCEM)

Subtask 1.2.1 – Integration of prototype plasma-assisted pre-chamber into the single-cylinder
heavy-duty optical natural gas engine.

The single-cylinder optical engine pre-chamber was modified to remove the pre-chamber fuel
injector and the miniature spark plug, allowing a standard conventional spark plug or a plasma
igniter to be used. This adaptation will allow testing of a series of existing plasma-igniters, in
addition to those currently under development for this project. The plasma-igniter will be powered
by a nanosecond high-voltage DC pulse generation system (Transient Plasma Systems Inc. SSPG-
101-HF) capable of delivering up to 200 short-duration pulses (full width at half maximum of ~10
nanoseconds) at a fixed 100 kHz frequency. After this modification, experimental campaigns were
conducted to probe EGR dilution tolerance and engine performance using the plasma-igniter pre-
chamber system, and the results were compared with previously acquired spark-ignition baseline
data.

Subtask 1.2.2 – Prepare Rapid Compression and Expansion Machine (RCEM) to visualize pre-
chamber combustion processes.

The existing RCEM is modified to accommodate an optical pre-chamber. An optically accessible
combustion chamber head with 1” viewports is fabricated to house the pre-chamber assembly. The
head consists of four side windows, 90 degrees apart, and one end window. One of the four side
windows houses the fueling assembly and pressure transducer, while the end window houses the
pre-chamber. The distance between the end window and piston at TDC is 21.5 mm, resulting in a
clearance volume of 43.58 cc.

Since plasma ignition involves multiple pulses, it was determined that the optimal way to map
energy and species from VizGlow to CONVERGE is to model them as “source” terms. In addition
to energy, ozone (O3) generated during plasma pulses is considered for mapping to Converge. To

inspection of the contours of energy density and O3 density from the plasma simulations showed
that both energy and the O3 species are concentrated in a region of about 0.6 mm wide from the
axis as illustrated in Figure 12. Hence, in CONVERGE, the source size for energy and O3 species
was taken as a cylinder with a diameter of 1.2 mm and a height of 5 mm. For preliminary
simulations, a simplified 3D cylindrical geometry with a diameter of 139.7 mm is considered as

Subtask 2.1.3 – Optimize plasma-assisted pre-chamber design for EGR tolerance in the RCEM
facility.

Optimization of the plasma pre-chamber design is a focal point, employing optical visualization
techniques to observe the operation of both the pre-chamber and the main chamber during RCEM.
Several key areas are being investigated to improve the performance of the plasma pre-chamber.

Plasma: Plasma parameters such as pulse duration, pulse number, and repetition rate significantly
influence pre-chamber operation. Therefore, it is imperative to optimize these parameters through
optical RCEM analysis.

Fluid Mechanics: Flow dynamics and turbulence during the compression stroke in the main
chamber are pivotal factors that determine pre-chamber behavior. The interaction between plasma
and flow is a critical parameter explored through optical visualization of the pre-chamber.
Additionally, understanding mixture stratification in the active pre-chamber is crucial for
optimizing mixture formation.

Chemistry: Examining radical formation, specifically filtered imaging of OH *and CH* aids in
understanding the time evolution of pre-chamber flame development. Critical parameters such as
heat release (derived from pressure data) and the location of the PC injector (currently under
installation) are also significant for understanding pre-chamber behavior.

Geometry: The number of nozzles, their size, jet angle, and igniter location are key geometric
parameters influencing pre-chamber performance.

To gain insights into plasma flow interactions, a small constant-volume optical calorimeter is
employed in addition to RCEM testing. The knowledge gained from this exercise will be

Subtask 2.1.4 – Study the effect of various operating parameters with plasma-assisted pre-chamber
ignition in the single cylinder optical engine.

The project team completed an extensive measurement campaign in the single cylinder optical
engine with the goal to demonstrate the Go/No-Go milestone of 2% efficiency improvement with
nanosecond repetitive discharge plasma ignition systems at high dilution (20% EGR) compared to
a conventional inductive spark ignition system (70mJ coils) with a resistive Iridium-electrode
spark-plug with 0.4mm gap and 0.8mm electrode diameter. Three plasma igniters procured from
company TPS with distinct electrode configurations were used in this work: a conventional J-hook
configuration, a circular ground electrode configuration, and a 4-pronged side-gap configuration.
These electrode configurations and parameters, including the electrode gap width, were selected
based on expert advice from TPS. Other key features of these igniters include a specialized
connector design to minimize stray discharge and a commercially available coaxial high-voltage
connection to a TPS nanosecond-discharge plasma power supply. This power supply offers a
highly flexible igniter energization schedule with independent settings for the number of pulses,
pulse frequency, and pulse voltage. The constant-volume chamber tests were used to guide the
initial plasma power-supply settings for the single cylinder engine measurements.

Understanding that the in-prechamber flow and turbulence field might strongly interact with the
early ignition kernel and the potential for destructive interactions between individual kernels
initiated by the repetitive pulse discharge, we prepared four pre-chamber configurations to be
tested with each of the TPS igniters to ensure the best possible combination of igniter and pre-
chamber configuration was found:

 • Baseline pre-chamber: 8-hole, Ø1.6 mm
 • Large holes, maintaining total nozzle area: 5-hole, Ø2.0 mm
 • Swirling hole orientation: 5-hole, Ø2.0 mm, holes drilled under ~20° angle from radial
 direction
 • Low-turbulence, large-hole pre-chamber: 5-hole, Ø2.5 mm
The measurement matrix with several degrees of freedom described above required us to restrict
the engine operating space to a single load/speed point, to be tested with 0% EGR and at 20%. The
selected operating condition of 1200 RPM and ~8.5 bar IMEP (likely, a metal engine without
optical crevices and blow-by would reach ~11-12 bar IMEP under similar conditions) corresponds
to highway cruise, where high efficiency and low spark-plug wear are most impactful.

Approach to demonstrating performance benefit:
 1. Inductive coil spark-plug baselining: Every pre-chamber was first baselined with
 conventional spark ignition system. The best efficiency was determined for each pre-
 chamber configuration at 0% and 20% EGR by sweeping the spark timing in increments
 of 2°CA.
 2. Identifying best-performing plasma igniter: Each plasma-igniter performance was
 baselined in the 8-hole 1.6mm pre-chamber. We swept about 10 different combinations
 of nano-second discharge voltage, pulse frequency and number of pulses based on
 preliminary testing at the University of Minnesota, to identify the best energizing strategy
 for each igniter. This testing was conducted at fixed optimal spark timing identified
 during the inductive coil baselining. We identified the 4-pronged side-gap igniter to have
 performed best among all tested energizing schedules and igniter configurations, both at
      0% and 20% EGR.
 3. Co-optimization of plasma spark timing and pre-chamber configuration: The 4-pronged
 igniter was tested in all pre-chamber configurations at 0% and 20% EGR. A narrow
 spark-timing sweep was completed in each configuration to ensure best performance is
 reached.
 4. Side-testing: During the testing we determined that the TPS power supply owned by the
 University of Minnesota cannot produce sufficiently high pulse voltage to successfully
 operate the J-hook electrode igniter at the selected engine load and speed. We repeated
 the testing under point 2 using a different plasma power-supply that can generate higher
 pulse voltage but is limited in pulse frequency.

Thermodynamic and fuel-flow measurements were processed to determine the indicated efficiency

early AHRR would all indicate faster combustion, a stronger ignition system, and be advantageous
for higher efficiency.

Subtask 3.2.2: Document fuel-quality effects on main-chamber combustion evolution

The fuel quality effects were tested under identical test conditions to those in Subtask 3.2.1, using
three different fuel compositions to assess the fuel’s impact on combustion and EGR tolerance.
The basic prechamber configuration was used for this subtask. Methane number is the most widely
used index for natural gas fuel quality; it evaluates the fuel's propensity to undergo abnormal
combustion. Methane is defined as MN=100 and hydrogen as MN = 0. Methane number is not a
standardized index, and many engine OEMs have developed proprietary tools to estimate it from
NG composition. Typically, a methane number MN>80 is required for on-road NG engines, unless
special hardware is employed to lower this range to ~65, for example, for biogas CNG applications.
On the other hand, upgraded biogas (RNG) might have an MN of 100 or higher.

The thermodynamic results of tests with different fuel qualities are presented in Figure 15. All
fuels result in nearly identical IMEP (Figure 15a) at zero dilution as expected based on the similar
energy released per unit of air for all fuels. The differences become apparent at 20% EGR
synthetic, where NG delivered about 4% higher peak IMEP than pure methane, with H2-enriched
NG delivering intermediate performance. The difference is significantly higher at late spark
timings, where pure methane shows strong quenching, while the two lower MN fuels show good
performance and about 12% lower fuel consumption than without dilution. At 25% EGR, only H2-
enriched NG delivers acceptable performance, while methane and synthetic NG show significant
quenching. However, the EGR limit extension potential by changing the fuel quality seems
marginal – even the H2-enriched NG showed slightly deteriorated efficiency at 25% EGR
compared to 20% EGR operation. Further spark timing optimization might still recover this
difference to a limited extent. The coefficient of variation of IMEP (CoV of IMEP) shows an
inverted trend compared to IMEP, indicating that poor burning cycles with low IMEP are the main
reason for reduced efficiency at high EGR and suboptimal spark timings.

The metric of peak pressure difference between the PC and MC shows nearly identical behavior
for all fuels. The peak pressure difference decreases with earlier spark timing due to lower mass
of trapped fuel in PC it significantly drops as the EGR rate increases. The lack of a notable
difference between the fuels at identical operating conditions suggests that pre-chamber
combustion is not the parameter governing successful ignition and indicates that PC combustion
is mostly governed by flame speed, which decreases with dilution but remains roughly constant
across fuels with different MN.

The metrics of CA10, CA50 and CA10-peakdP show clear indications that flame quenching is
responsible for differences between these fuels in terms of combustion efficiency at high dilution.
At 20% EGR rate, all fuels showed comparable peak pressure driving the PC jets, yet, the CA10
and CA50 for pure methane occur 5CA and 7CA later than with lower MN. The metric that shows

the time difference between the peak PC pressure and CA10 shows nearly independent value at
constant dilution regardless of spark timing. However, the time delay for pure methane is about 4-
5CA higher than for synthetic NG. Interestingly, H2-enriched NG shows a slightly higher delay

The combination of these metrics clearly indicates that differences in dilution performance

The notable exception to this trend is at even higher dilution rates. Here, the H2-enriched NG

differences might be due to different chemical effects of H2 on flame speed at high dilution rates
that are not captured at lower dilution rates. This effect needs further understanding. Overall, this
subtask demonstrated that the fuel methane number is a good indicator of EGR dilution tolerance,
with lower fuel grades showing improved performance. This is a clear trade-off between knock
resistance and dilution tolerance as exhibited in Task 3.1. It appears that the jet quenching and re-
ignition rely on similar chemical pathways to end-gas autoignition. Fuels more prone to end-gas
autoignition will also be less inclined to jet quenching, and the jets will be easier to re-ignite. The
work also suggests that EGR is an effective way to compensate for variations in fuel grade. Higher
fuel grades can be safely operated at low EGR without the risk of knock (at a small efficiency
penalty), while lower engine grade knock can be avoided by EGR without the risk of misfire and
high cyclic variability.

The G-equation model in CCONVERGE is a flame surface tracking model widely used in
simulating premixed turbulent combustion. In this model, the flame front is represented by the iso-
surface G = 0, where G is a scalar field that distinguishes burned (G = 1) and unburned (G = 0)
regions. The G-equation model captures flame propagation without resolving the detailed
chemistry like the WSR (or SAGE) model and hence is more computationally efficient. The initial
simulation using the G-equation model showed that the prechamber combustion is captured with
much more accuracy, as shown in Figure 3.3.9. However, the main chamber combustion is
significantly overpredicted and heat release in the MC occurs much earlier than in experiments.
We have recently concluded that this is due to inaccuracy in the laminar flame speed calculation.
We have used tabulated laminar flame speed in our simulations. The results shown in Figure
3.3.2.6 do not account for the 23% nitrogen dilution when calculating laminar flame speed.
Therefore, we will create new laminar flame speed tables to account for dilution.
