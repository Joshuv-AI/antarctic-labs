ABSTRACT
Nonlinear viscoelastic model calibrations are developed for two filled epoxies: an adduct of DGEBA
(Epon 828) and CTBN cross-linked with diethanolamine (DEA) filled with glass microballoons,
called 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB, and DGEBA (Epon 828) cross-linked with Ancamine Z and filled
with alumina, called 828/Z/AlOx. The thermo-mechanical responses of these materials are repre-
sented using the SPECtacular constitutive model, an extension of the Simplified Potential Energy
Clock (SPEC) model. The model couples multiple viscoelastic relaxation phenomena through
a nonlinear material clock that is a function of the time-dependent non-bonded potential energy.
The model is calibrated using new experimental data from specimens manufactured according to
application-specific work instructions. Relaxation phenomena are characterized using measure-
ments from a dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA), differential scanning calorimeter (DSC), and
a thermo-mechanical analyzer (TMA). Nonlinear viscoelasticity, meaning deformation coupling in
the material clock, is characterized using compression in the glassy state through yield.
A new experimental technique for master curve construction is developed in which the shear storage
and loss moduli are measured through a temperature sweep while cycling through multiple distinct
frequencies. Through a careful time–temperature superposition analysis, the data is transformed
into the frequency domain. Compared to the traditional isothermal frequency sweep approach, the
new method reduces dwell time at high temperatures, which, for epoxies with residual reaction
potential, produces master curves more representative of the as-cured state. The new technique
is verified on 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB by comparing master curves constructed using traditional
isothermal frequency sweeps to master curves constructed using the new multi-frequency temper-
ature sweep approach. The resulting two master curves are reasonably similar, with the master
curve from the new approach showing a loss modulus peak at a higher frequency, consistent with
a less-cured chemical state (lower cross-link density and lower glass transition temperature).
The calibration for 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB is compared with a previous calibration based on legacy
data using specimens with nominally the same composition but a different cure schedule. Dif-
ferences between the new and legacy data lead to different model parameters, emphasizing the
importance of collecting data using specimens manufactured according to current procedures.
For 828/Z/AlOx, the material is under-cured, and curing continues when heated just above the glass
transition temperature, 𝜃 𝑔 . This prevents observation of the full glass transition temperature in the
as-cured state. Therefore, experimental data from the initial heat of 828/Z/AlOx is inappropriate
for calibrating SPECtacular, since the model assumes a fixed chemical state. Curing during the
initial heat raises the 𝜃 𝑔 of the 828/Z/AlOx material, but minimal chemical changes are observed on
subsequent temperature sweeps. Therefore, the model is calibrated using data on the more-cured
material after an initial heat, then the calibration is adjusted by moving 𝜃 𝑔 from the more-cured
value to the as-cured value that was observed in the initial heat. Then, for convenience, the
reference temperature (𝜃 ref ) for the calibration is shifted to the curing temperature, which is done
without changing the thermo-mechanical responses predicted by the model. The procedure for
changing model parameters for a shift in 𝜃 ref is described and validated, demonstrating that the
same SPECtacular model calibration can be represented with a locus of parameter sets.
The new model calibrations are well suited for predicting residual stresses in electronics encapsu-
lation through manufacturing environments and subsequent physical aging. Include files for using
the calibrations in Sierra/SM analyses are listed as appendices at the end of this report.

1. INTRODUCTION

Epoxies are often used to encapsulate electronics to provide protection from both mechanical
insults and ingress of contaminants such as water. Relative to the surrounding metals, epoxies ex-
hibit higher coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE), which can cause significant residual stresses
after cooling from manufacturing temperatures. Therefore, finite element modeling of encapsu-
lated components must consider the residual stress state in addition to other thermo-mechanical
events. Furthermore, epoxies exhibit nonlinear viscoelastic (NLVE) behaviors such that their cur-
rent thermo-mechanical responses depend on their prior thermo-mechanical history. For example,
epoxies exhibit stress, volume, and enthalpy relaxation and the rates of relaxation depend not only
on the current temperature, but also the temperature history as well as loading history [3].
The SPECtacular constitutive model [4] provides a unified framework to predict the various time-
dependent behaviors of epoxies and other glass-forming materials. SPECtacular, which is based
on the previous PEC [5, 6] and SPEC [7] models, includes mechanisms for predicting viscoelastic
relaxation of the bulk stress, shear stress, thermal strain, and enthalpy. Nonlinear viscoelastic
behavior is introduced via a (simplified) potential energy clock, which uses a reduced temperature
proportional to the (simplified) potential energy. The reduced temperature is a function of the history
of deformation and temperature. In total, the model describes a time-dependent equation of state for
epoxies and other glassy materials. It has also successfully represented the homogenized behavior
of particle-filled epoxies [8, 9]. The objective of the present work is to calibrate the SPECtacular
model for two filled epoxies. The first is an epoxy created from an adduct of DGEBA (Epon 828)
and CTBN rubber cross-linked with diethanolamine (DEA) and filled with glass microballoons,
referred to here as 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB. The second is DGEBA (Epon 828) cross-linked with
Ancamine Z and filled with alumina (i.e., aluminum oxide, or AlOx for short), referred to here as
828/Z/AlOx.
A SPECtacular calibration was previously developed for 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB in SAND2022-
6360 [2]. However, the calibration was based on legacy experimental data from Sandia’s Polymer
Property Database (PPD) [1]. Although the database provides a wealth of information, the as-
sociated pedigree of that data, including experimental protocols used to generate the data, are
often unknown or unclear. This problem is especially important for NLVE modeling, where prior
history and loading rates, both unspecified in the PPD, influence thermo-mechanical behavior.
Furthermore, the cure schedules for 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB listed on the PPD do not match cure
schedules used in the current work instructions, which introduces additional uncertainty in material
behavior. To this end, the current calibration is based on carefully documented new experiments
using specimens that were manufactured and cured using current procedures [10].
On the other hand, no previous PEC, SPEC, or SPECtacular calibration exists for 828/Z/AlOx. The
828/Z/AlOx material studied here is not fully cured, which poses challenges for characterization
and modeling the material in the as-cured state. The SPECtacular model theory is valid for a fixed

 17

chemical state; coupled chemo-thermo-mechanical effects are not included in the model. Usually,
relaxation spectra in the model are calibrated by observing the glass transition through a temperature
sweep, but when the under-cured 828/Z/AlOx is heated above the glass transition temperature (𝜃 𝑔 ),
curing resumes, which raises 𝜃 𝑔 . Thus, the thermo-mechanical response through glass transition
in the as-cured material is not fully observed. This could be circumvented by characterizing the
relaxation spectra in the time-domain, but doing so without heating above 𝜃 𝑔 would be prohibitively
time-consuming. Instead, the calibration is built using measurements from 828/Z/AlOx after an
initial heat that progresses curing such that the chemistry is stable in subsequent temperature
sweeps. Then the calibration is adjusted to represent the as-cured state by moving the onset of 𝜃 𝑔
in the model to coincide with the onset of 𝜃 𝑔 observed from the initial heat. Thus, an approximate
calibration for the as-cured state can still be developed. This approach is suitable as long as the
model calibration is not exercised above a temperature where the actual material will continue to
cure. Should simulations require a model for 828/Z/AlOx that includes the effects of cure, a version
of the PEC model was extended to capture chemical changes [11], although quantitative validation
of the coupled chemo-thermo-mechanical model is limited.
The report begins with the background necessary to discuss the calibration procedure for SPECtac-
ular. In order to define the parameters that need to be calibrated, the SPECtacular model theory is

In Chapter 5, a master curve is constructed for 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB using the multi-frequency

The final calibration is presented and compared to the legacy calibration [2] in Chapter 7. Work
on 828/Z/AlOx is described in Chapters 8 to 10. In Chapter 8, a master curve is constructed
for 828/Z/AlOx using the multi-frequency temperature sweep data. The calibration procedure for
828/Z/AlOx is described in Chapter 9, and the final calibration is presented in Chapter 10.
The new SPECtacular calibration for 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB is listed in Appendix A and an

The legacy calibration has been updated from the original reference to reflect recent changes in

2. THE SPECTACULAR CONSTITUTIVE MODEL

2.1. Overview of SPECtacular

SPECtacular is an isotropic, nonlinear viscoelastic constitutive model for glass-forming polymers.
The model is based on the previous SPEC [7] and PEC models [5, 6]. There are essentially two
parts to SPECtacular. The first is a Helmholtz free energy that contains both time-independent
(equilibrium) and time-dependent (non-equilibrium) terms, which are derived from a Fréchet
expansion in strain and temperature through second order terms [13]. The time-dependent responses
are defined by four relaxation spectra. Since the model is isotropic, the mechanical relaxations
are separated into bulk and shear. Thermal relaxation and cross thermal-volume (e.g., thermal
expansion) relaxations are also included. Each of these four spectra can be defined independently
from the others. Following the Coleman and Noll approach for materials with fading memory [14],
thermodynamically-consistent constitutive equations for stress and entropy are derived from the
Helmholtz free energy.

The second part of SPECtacular is the material clock, which increases or decreases relaxation
rates depending on the thermodynamic state. The reduced temperature for the material clock is
derived from a simplified expression for the potential energy. As such, the clock is a function of
not only the current state of deformation and temperature, but also the histories of deformation and
temperature. SPECtacular is thermo-rheologically simple, i.e., the clock is applied equally to all
relaxation times.

The SPECtacular theory is presented in this chapter. The presentation is extensive so that discussions
of calibration in later chapters can be fully appreciated. However, some details of the theory
are omitted for brevity, especially discussions of coupled thermo-mechanical calculations. See
SAND2023-14317 [4] for a complete description of the theory. In Section 2.2, the Helmholtz
free energy and the derived constitutive equations for stress and heat capacity are described. The
material clock is described in Section 2.3. At the end of the chapter, the model parameters to be
identified are listed in Table 2-1 under Section 2.4.

3.1.           828-CTBN/DEA/GMB

The 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB specimens were manufactured according to work instructions WI-
5A5853. The filled epoxy was composed of
 • 100 pbw (parts-by-weight) of 828-CTBN, an adduct of
 – 90 pbw diglycidyl ether of bisphenol A (Epon 828) and
 – 10 pbw Hycar 1300x8 carboxyl terminated butadiene acrylonitrile rubber (CTBN),
 • 12 pbw diethanolamine (DEA), and
 • 28 pbw 3M Type D32/4500 glass microballoons (GMB).
The mixture was cured for 10 hours at 93 ◦ C. The GMB volume fraction is estimated to be

The legacy data used for a previous calibration [2] was collected from experiments using 828-
CTBN/DEA/GMB specimens with a nominally equivalent composition, but with a lower cure
temperature and longer cure time; 24 hours at 71 ◦ C for the legacy material compared to 10 hours
at 93 ◦ C with the current material. For Epon 828 and DEA, the cure mechanism changes around
70 ◦ C [18], so the two distinct cure schedules may result in different final macromolecular networks,
and therefore different material behaviors and model calibrations.
Linear viscoelasticity is often characterized using master curves constructed from isothermal fre-
quency sweeps. A small oscillatory strain is applied to ensure linear viscoelastic behavior and
the resulting oscillatory stress is measured. For an oscillatory shear deformation, the component

also useful for model calibration. Master curve construction and time–temperature superposition

3.2.          828/Z/AlOx

The 828/Z/AlOx specimens were manufactured according to work instructions WI-5A5853. The
filled epoxy was composed of
 • 100 pbw diglycidyl ether of bisphenol A (Epon 828),
 • 20 pbw aromatic curing agent Ancamine Z (Z),
 • 300 pbw Almatis T64 Alumina (AlOx).
The mixture was cured for 10 hours at 93 ◦ C. The AlOx volume fraction is estimated to be 44 %
based on a density of 2.36 g/cm3 for 828/Z/AlOx and a density of 3.96 g/cm3 for the AlOx [1].
Legacy data is available on the PPD for 828/Z/AlOx [1]. The formulation for the legacy data
uses the same composition but the mixture was cured for 24 hours at 93 ◦ C instead of 10 hours as
in the current formulation. No prior SPEC or SPECtacular calibrations exist for 828/Z/AlOx, so
comparisons to the legacy data are not discussed further.
828/Z does not fully cure at 93 ◦ C. During the initial cure, the 𝜃 𝑔 for 828/Z increases as cure
progresses. However, the maximum 𝜃 𝑔 is limited by the cure temperature. For 828/Z cured at
93 ◦ C, the maximum 𝜃 𝑔 is limited to about 25 ◦ C above the cure temperature [1], although the
𝜃 𝑔 for fully cured 828/Z is around 170 ◦ C. Practically speaking, viscoelastic characterization of
epoxies requires thermal excursions both above and below 𝜃 𝑔 . Yet, if the 828/Z/AlOx is heated
above the cure temperature, the reaction resumes and 𝜃 𝑔 increases. By doing so, the experimental
measurements no longer represent the as-cured state for which a SPECtacular calibration is desired.
Therefore, approximations are required to develop a SPECtacular calibration to represent the as-
cured 828/Z/AlOx. For experimental characterization, when temperature sweeps are part of the
protocol, a heat-cool-heat procedure is used. Significant differences manifest between the first heat
and cooling due to continued cure. Differences between cooling and the second heat are usually
small, indicating stable behavior. The model will be calibrated using the cooling and heating steps
that follow the first heat. This resulting calibration represents a more-cured form of 828/Z/AlOx
with 𝜃 𝑔 ≈ 170 ◦ C. To adjust the calibration to represent the as-cured form of 828/Z/AlOx, the
model responses will be vertically shifted (change heat capacity or CTE) and horizontally shifted
(change temperature) so that the onset of 𝜃 𝑔 overlaps with what is observed in the first heat.

4. MASTER CURVE CONSTRUCTION FROM A MULTI-FREQUENCY
 TEMPERATURE SWEEP

This chapter describes the process for constructing a master curve from the multi-frequency tem-
perature sweep experiments. Results from multi-frequency temperature sweeps were shown in
Figs. 3-2 and 3-7. In Section 4.1, preliminary concepts of viscoelastic measurements, time–
temperature superposition, horizontal shifting, vertical shifting, and master curves are defined.
Along with the preliminaries, a notation is defined to clearly label differences between raw data and
data that has been transformed through various shifts. In Section 4.2, standard viscoelastic experi-
ments are discussed and compared to the new experimental protocol explored here. In Section 4.3,
the algorithm for constructing a master curve using the multi-frequency temperature sweep data is
explained in detail. In Section 4.4, the method is verified using synthetic data.

4.1. Preliminaries

To calibrate a viscoelastic model, the relaxation spectra must be determined. However, relaxation
can occur over extremely long time periods such that it is impractical to observe the full spectrum
in the time domain. Therefore, the frequency domain is often used to probe relaxation spectra.
This is especially true for the shear relaxation spectra, since precise control of mechanical inputs
allows probing many orders of magnitude in frequency. Even with the wide range of frequencies
accessible to experiments, the time–temperature superposition principle must still be invoked to
probe the full spectra. The time–temperature superposition (TTS) principle states that a change in
temperature is equivalent to a change in timescale. For example, in the frequency domain,

                                            𝜔∗ = 𝑎(𝜃)𝜔,                                         (4.1)

where 𝜔 is the angular frequency, 𝜔∗ is the angular frequency shifted to 𝜃 ref , and 𝑎(𝜃) is the
shift factor that relates the two. The shift factor can also change with time through physical
aging effects [21]. This also means that when cooling slowly versus cooling quickly, different
relationships for 𝑎(𝜃) might manifest. In this sense, the shift factor is a function of both time and
temperature, 𝑎 = 𝑎(𝑡, 𝜃). However, when comparing shift factors for the same thermal history 𝜃 (𝑡),
the time dependence of the shift factor can conveniently be suppressed.

4.2. Approaches to Master Curve Construction

There are several approaches to using time–temperature superposition with measurements of
𝐺 ′ (𝜔, 𝜃, 𝑎(𝜃)) and 𝐺 ′′ (𝜔, 𝜃, 𝑎(𝜃)) to construct master curves of 𝐺 ′ (𝜔∗ ) and 𝐺 ′′ (𝜔∗ ) at a ref-
erence temperature 𝜃 ref . Since the vertical shifts are typically of secondary importance, the key
challenge is determining 𝑎(𝜃) for the thermal history of the experiment.

The most common method of master curve construction is using a series of isothermal frequency
sweeps (Iso-𝜃, 𝜔-Sweep). The experiment produces 𝐺 ′ (𝜔, 𝜃 𝑗 , 𝑎(𝜃 𝑗 )) and 𝐺 ′′ (𝜔, 𝜃 𝑗 , 𝑎(𝜃 𝑗 )) mea-
surements for several temperatures 𝜃 𝑗 . The temperatures used for the isothermal frequency sweeps
should be spaced closely enough together that the shifted frequencies from sequential isothermal
holds overlap. Horizontal and vertical shifts are determined by using the overlapping regions to
form a smooth master curve. The method produces master curves for 𝐺 ′ (𝜔∗ ), 𝐺 ′′ (𝜔∗ ) and 𝑎(𝜃 𝑗 )
at the temperatures used for the isothermal frequency sweeps. However, in epoxies with residual
chemistry, the high temperature dwells may cause continued cure of the material, thus the measured
master curve is not completely representative of the as-cured material.

Another method is to measure 𝐺 ′ and 𝐺 ′′ at some constant frequency 𝜔0 while sweeping through
temperature (Iso-𝜔, 𝜃-Sweep). This method is convenient for quickly determining 𝜃 𝑔 and 𝐺 ′ (𝜃),
but on its own, the method is not sufficient to produce a master curve. The main obstacle is that 𝑎(𝜃)
cannot be inferred from the data. This is especially true below the glass transition temperature, since
the shift factor is not in equilibrium. With an appropriate model for the shift factor 𝑎(𝑡, 𝜃), inverse
modeling can be used to find the 𝐺 (𝑡) and 𝑎(𝑡, 𝜃) that match the measurements of 𝐺 ′ (𝜔0 , 𝜃, 𝑎(𝜃))
and 𝐺 ′′ (𝜔0 , 𝜃, 𝑎(𝜃)). However, the need for inverse modeling to uncover the master curve is a
major drawback and introduces model form error as a source of uncertainty.

The multi-frequency temperature sweep (𝜔𝜃-Sweep) described in this chapter seeks to blend the
advantages of these two approaches. The approach involves sweeping the temperature while also
alternating between multiple discrete frequencies. The output consists of 𝐼 different curves of
𝐺 ′ (𝜔𝑖 , 𝜃, 𝑎(𝜃)) and 𝐺 ′′ (𝜔𝑖 , 𝜃, 𝑎(𝜃)) measured at 𝐼 distinct frequencies 𝜔𝑖 with 𝑖 ∈ {1, 2, 3, ..., 𝐼}.
Provided that the instrument cycles through frequencies more quickly than the temperature changes,
multiple frequency measurements are made at the same temperature and thus the same 𝑎(𝜃) is valid
for the measurements. Based on this, TTS can be used to construct a master curve. For each of the
𝐼 curves, if the temperature is the same, then 𝑎 is the same. In other words, vertical lines in a 𝐺 ′
versus 𝜃 plot have the same value of 𝑎. Also, for each of the 𝐼 curves, if 𝐺 ′ is the same, then the
shifted frequency 𝑎𝜔 is also the same. In other words, horizontal lines in a 𝐺 ′ versus 𝜃 plot have the
same value of 𝑎𝜔. When these two relationships are applied strategically throughout the data set,
a shift factor function 𝑎(𝜃) can be constructed and used to shift data to the reference temperature.

 41

One caveat, however, is that the horizontal line relationship assumes that vertical shifts, if any are
needed, have already been applied to the data.
The advantages of the multi-frequency temperature sweep are that the master curve is constructed
from an experiment involving a single temperature sweep. This reduces experimental runtime
compared to a series of isothermal frequency sweeps. Also, sweeping through high temperature
(as opposed to dwelling at high temperature) limits activating chemical reactions, meaning the
final master curve is more representative of the as-cured epoxy. Note, however, that additional
chemistry is only minimized and often cannot be eliminated entirely. Finally, this method lends
itself to automation, whereas manual master curve construction is usually needed for a series of
iso-𝜃, 𝜔-sweeps.

4.3.4. Zig-Zag Analysis

In this step, time–temperature superposition relationships between the iso-𝜔 curves are used to
infer the shift factor function 𝑎(𝜃). Vertical lines of constant temperature represent constant 𝑎, and
horizontal lines of constant 𝐺 ′ represent constant 𝑎𝜔. The analysis is referred to as the “zig-zag”
analysis because 𝑎(𝜃) is determined by following these TTS relationships across alternating vertical
and horizontal lines.

 46

The analysis starts at a temperature 𝜃 𝑧max , which should be chosen to be slightly above 𝜃 𝑔 so
that 𝐺 ′ is noticeably different at each frequency. Too far above 𝜃 𝑔 , 𝐺 ′ is likely the same for
each frequency, which can introduce error into the analysis. For this reason, in the zig-zag
analysis, data above 𝜃 𝑧max is ignored. For convenience, a temporary shift factor function is
defined that uses 𝜃 𝑧max as the reference temperature, 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑧max ) = 1. Then, for 𝜃 𝑗 starting at
𝜃 𝑧max and sweeping down in temperature to 𝜃 min , the following steps are performed. If the shift
factor 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑗 ) is known, then for each pair of frequencies 𝜔𝑖 and 𝜔 𝑘 , the temperature 𝜃 𝑙 where
𝐺 ′ (𝜔𝑖 , 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑗 )) = 𝐺 ′ (𝜔 𝑘 , 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑙 )) is identified. Graphically, this represents drawing a vertical
line at 𝜃 𝑗 , then where that vertical line intersects each iso-𝜔 curve, drawing horizontal lines and
finding where the horizontal lines intersect other iso-𝜔 curves. Assuming a one-to-one mapping
between 𝐺 ′ (𝜔𝑖 , 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑗 )) and temperature for a fixed frequency, then from TTS it is known that if
𝐺 ′ (𝜔∗ ) = 𝐺 ′ (𝜔𝑖 , 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑗 )) = 𝐺 ′ (𝜔 𝑘 , 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑙 )) then 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑗 )𝜔𝑖 = 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑙 )𝜔 𝑘 . Since 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑗 ) is known,
𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑙 ) can be calculated according to 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑙 ) = 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑗 )𝜔𝑖 /𝜔 𝑘 . Note, at the start of the analysis
𝜃 𝑗 = 𝜃 𝑧max , so the shift factor has been defined at this point and is always known initially. After
looping through all 𝜃 𝑗 below 𝜃 𝑧max , the function 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑗 ) should be reasonably filled out, although
it is not guaranteed that the algorithm will find values for all 𝜃 𝑗 < 𝜃 𝑧max . However, the approach
is reasonably efficient. Consider the case where five distinct frequencies are used. Starting with
𝜃 𝑧max , the vertical line intersects five iso-frequency curves which each give way to horizontal lines
that each intersect four other iso-frequency curves, giving a total of 20 new temperatures where
𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃 𝑗 ) is now known. In the rubbery state, 𝐺 ′ might not be sufficiently distinct at each frequency to
use the zig-zag analysis. In the next step, an approach to define 𝑎 𝑧 (𝜃) for 𝜃 𝑗 > 𝜃 𝑧max is described.

6. CALIBRATION PROCEDURE FOR 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB

The SPECtacular model is calibrated to the thermo-mechanical behavior of 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB
in a step-by-step approach. The calibration sequence is designed so that any given step has minimal
coupling with responses calibrated in previous steps. In this chapter, the step-by-step calibration
procedure is carefully documented. The final parameters are listed in Table 7-1 of Chapter 7.
In Section 6.1, a set of parameters are chosen based on known information or values from the
literature. In Section 6.2, parameters related to the shear relaxation function and the equilibrium
shift factor are determined. These parameters are determined from the results of the multi-frequency
temperature sweep analysis in Chapter 5. In Section 6.3, parameters related to the thermal strain
and thermal relaxation are determined using the heat capacity measured by DSC and the linear
CTE measured by TMA. In Section 6.4, the nonlinear shear coupling parameter is determined from
glassy compression.

6.1. Initially Known Parameters

A number of model parameters are known from the outset or can be chosen based on values from
the literature. The reasons for selecting these parameters, along with references, are listed here.
 • The model calibration is built at a reference temperature of 𝜃 ref = 75 ◦ C, consistent with the
 value used to construct master curves in previous chapters. The reference temperature can

 • The stress-free temperature is set at the cure temperature, 𝜃 sf = 93 ◦ C.
 • The density 𝜌ref = 770 kg/m3 is taken from the PPD [1].

6.4. Nonlinear Viscoelastic Clock Parameter

The last calibration step is to determine 𝐶4 , which controls the coupling between shear deformation
and the material clock. In the model, yield under glassy compression emerges as an effect of
nonlinear viscoelasticity. As the shear strain is increased, the shift factor decreases through 𝐶4 ,
eventually causing a precipitous drop in the stress, leading to yield-like behavior. Therefore, 𝐶4
is calibrated to capture the yield stress in glassy compression at multiple temperatures and strain
rates.

Glassy compression is simulated using a single hexahedral element in Sierra/SM. The simulation
goes through the following steps:

 • Cool from 75 ◦ C to 23 ◦ C (assumed to be the room temperature) at 1 ◦ C/min.

 • Hold at 23 ◦ C for one hour.

 • Ramp to the loading temperature over 30 minutes.

 • Hold at the loading temperature for another 30 minutes.

 • Load under uniaxial compression at the desired strain rate up to a strain of 12 %.

9. CALIBRATION PROCEDURE FOR 828/Z/ALOX

The SPECtacular model is calibrated to the thermo-mechanical behavior of 828/Z/AlOx in a step-
by-step approach. The calibration sequence is designed so that any given step has minimal coupling
with responses calibrated in previous steps. In this chapter, the step-by-step calibration procedure
is carefully documented. The final parameters are listed in Table 10-1 of Chapter 10.

The fact that the as-cured 828/Z/AlOx continues to cure when heated above 𝜃 𝑔 poses a complication
for model calibration. This is because many of the calibration experiments (DMA, DSC, and TMA)
rely on temperature sweeps to observe the glass transition. However, since the material cures just
above 𝜃 𝑔 , it is not possible to observe the full glass transition of the as-cured epoxy. To account
for this, the model is first calibrated to the more-cured response that is observed in the various
experiments after the initial heating step. During the initial heating step, the material is sufficiently
cured so that minimal chemical changes occur in subsequent temperature sweeps. After the linear
viscoelastic behavior is calibrated to represent the more-cured response, 𝜃 ref is moved down by
an amount approximately equal to the increase in 𝜃 𝑔 seen between the first heat and subsequent
temperature sweeps. When 𝜃 ref is moved without changing the relaxation functions or other TTS
parameters, it is equivalent to moving the 𝜃 𝑔 exhibited in the model responses. Then, 𝜃 ref is shifted
so that the reference temperature coincides with the curing temperature. When 𝜃 ref is shifted, the
other model parameters are consistently changed so that the viscoelastic behavior predicted by the
model is unchanged. The reason for shifting 𝜃 ref is that after moving 𝜃 ref to lower the 𝜃 𝑔 to represent
the as-cured material, 𝜃 ref is still greater than the cure temperature. The model is not valid above
the cure temperature, because if the as-cured material is heated above the cure temperature, then
cross-linking will resume resulting in chemo-thermo-mechanical behaviors that cannot be predicted
by SPECtacular. Therefore, to avoid confusion, 𝜃 ref is shifted to the cure temperature.

In Section 9.1, a set of parameters are chosen based on known information or values from the
literature. In Section 9.2, parameters related to the shear relaxation function and the equilibrium
shift factor are identified. In Section 9.3, parameters related to the thermal strain and thermal
relaxation are determined using the heat capacity measured by DSC and the linear CTE measured
by TMA. In Section 9.4, the moving and shifting of 𝜃 ref is described. In Section 9.5, the nonlinear
shear coupling parameter is determined from glassy compression.

9.3. Thermal Strain and Thermal Relaxation Functions

The thermal strain and thermal relaxation functions, 𝑓3 and 𝑓4 respectively, are usually calibrated
using the linear CTE measured by TMA and the heat capacity measured by DSC. The rubbery
and glassy limits for the CTE and heat capacity parameters were manually selected to set good
initial values for inverse calibration of the relaxation functions. When selecting the rubbery heat
capacity parameters, the decreasing heat capacity at high temperatures was ignored, as this is likely
an artifact.

Initially, independent definitions for 𝑓3 and 𝑓4 were deployed to try to simultaneously fit both the
TMA and DSC results. However, no parameter set was found that gave good fits to both data sets.
Since independent specifications for 𝑓3 and 𝑓4 did not improve the ability to fit the data, the simpler
SPEC [7] approach that assumes 𝑓𝑣 = 𝑓1 = 𝑓3 = 𝑓4 was used. The volumetric relaxation function
 𝑓𝑣 was fit using Dakota to optimize parameters to the experimental data. Additionally, 𝛼𝑔ref and
𝐶𝑔ref were optimized since the choice of relaxation function may cause differences in the apparent
glassy limits predicted by the model and the actual glassy limits defined by the model parameters.
The inverse calibration step simultaneously considered the TMA and DSC data, as these thermal
responses are tightly coupled in the model form.

For the inverse calibration, the temperature sweep from the TMA experiment was simulated in
Sierra/SM. In the simulation, a homogeneous temperature field was applied to a single hexahedral
element to mimic the nominal thermal history of the experiment. The temperature field was varied
in time as follows:

       • Cool from 200 ◦ C to −50 ◦ C at 1 ◦ C/min,

 • Hold at −50 ◦ C for 5 min,

       • Heat from −50 ◦ C to 200 ◦ C at 1 ◦ C/min.

An objective function was formed using the 𝐿 2 error of the linear CTE from the model and
experimental measurements, see Fig. 3-9. The 𝐿 2 error only included data between 200 ◦ C and
100 ◦ C during the cooling step.

Likewise, the temperature sweep from the DSC experiment was simulated in Sierra/SM. In the
simulation, a homogeneous temperature field was applied to a single hexahedral element to mimic
the nominal thermal history of the experiment. The temperature field was varied in time as
follows:

       • Cool from 200 ◦ C to −90 ◦ C at 10 ◦ C/min,

 • Hold at −90 ◦ C for 5 min,

       • Heat from −90 ◦ C to 200 ◦ C at 10 ◦ C/min.

An objective function was formed using the 𝐿 2 error of the constant pressure heat capacity from
the model and specimen S2, see Fig. 3-8. The 𝐿 2 error only included data between 100 ◦ C and
200 ◦ C during the heating step.

9.4. Moving and Shifting 𝜃 ref to Represent the As-Cured Epoxy

The multi-frequency temperature sweep, DSC, and TMA experiments all involve temperature
sweeps. On the first heat, the full glass transition is not observed, since curing reactions resume
and raise 𝜃 𝑔 . For measurements made on cooling and the second heat, the full glass transition is
observed, but curing has changed the thermo-mechanical behavior from the as-cured state. So far,
the model has been calibrated to the more-cured material using data from cooling or heating after
the initial heat. To modify the calibration to represent the as-cured material, it is assumed that the
only difference between the more-cured and the as-cured material is a change in 𝜃 𝑔 and potentially
some “vertical shifts” in the rubbery and glassy limits for certain responses. Moving 𝜃 𝑔 in the
model can be accomplished by moving 𝜃 ref by a similar amount.

To estimate the increase in 𝜃 𝑔 during the first heat, data from first heat and subsequent sweeps
(cool and second heat) from the temperature sweep–based experiments are shown in Fig. 9-3. In
Fig. 9-3, the cooling steps and second heats have been horizontally shifted so the lower temperature
end of the glass transition matches the onset of the glass transition from the first heat. In some
cases, vertical shifts were also applied to match the glassy response from the first heat. For the
multi-frequency temperature sweep and the TMA experiment, the onset of 𝜃 𝑔 was best matched
after applying a −45 ◦ C shift in the data. For DSC, the best match used a shift of −30 ◦ C, but to
provide the best shift across all data sets, −45 ◦ C was used. For all data related to cooling and
second heating steps in Fig. 9-3, the temperatures have been shifted by −45 ◦ C.

Based on the shifted data, the onset of 𝜃 𝑔 increased by approximately 45 ◦ C from the first heat to
the following cool or second heat. Therefore, to transfer the calibration of the more-cured epoxy to
the as-cured epoxy, 𝜃 ref is reduced by 45 ◦ C, i.e., from 170 ◦ C to 125 ◦ C. The vertical shifts used
in Fig. 9-3 are also used to modify the calibration. The parameters 𝐶𝑔ref and 𝐶∞ ref were shifted by

−138 J/(kg · K) × 𝜌ref and the parameters 𝛼𝑔ref and 𝛼∞ ref were shifted by −9 × 10−6 /K.

9.5. Nonlinear Viscoelastic Clock Parameter

The last calibration step is to determine 𝐶4 , which controls the coupling between shear deformation
and the material clock. In the model, yield under glassy compression emerges as an effect of
nonlinear viscoelasticity. As the shear strain is increased, the shift factor decreases through 𝐶4 ,
eventually causing a precipitous drop in the stress, leading to yield-like behavior. Therefore, 𝐶4
is calibrated to capture the yield stress in glassy compression at multiple temperatures and strain
rates.
Glassy compression was simulated using a single hexahedral element in Sierra/SM. The simulation
goes through the following steps:
 • Cool from 93 ◦ C to 23 ◦ C (assumed to be the room temperature) at 1 ◦ C/min.
 • Hold at 23 ◦ C for one hour.
 • Ramp to the loading temperature over 30 minutes.
 • Hold at the loading temperature for another 30 minutes.
 • Load under uniaxial compression at the desired strain rate up to a strain of 12 %.
The parameter 𝐶4 was manually adjusted to fit the yield stress in glassy compression at multiple
loading temperatures and strain rates. The model was iteratively run using the calibration built at
𝜃 ref = 125 ◦ C. The model fit is shown in Fig. 9-4. At the loading temperatures 23 ◦ C and 50 ◦ C,
the model produces a good fit to the flow stress. In the model, the rollover to the flow stress is
more gradual than in the experiments. At the loading temperature −50 ◦ C, the rollover to yield in
the model is too diffuse, leading to stress–strain curves that are too soft at low strains and too stiff
at high strains. However, better fits to the data cannot be obtained from tuning only 𝐶4 , i.e., the
fit across all the compression data cannot be improved without degrading the quality of fits from
earlier calibration stages. Since the model fit at higher loading temperatures is relatively good, this
might be evidence for the need to introduce a temperature-dependent 𝐶4 . In fact, in the original
PEC model [5], the coefficient equivalent to 𝐶4 was temperature dependent.

11. CONCLUSION

A nonlinear viscoelastic constitutive model was calibrated to represent two filled epoxy systems,
828-CTBN/DEA/GMB and 828/Z/AlOx. Their nonlinear viscoelastic behavior was represented
using the SPECtacular model [4], which is an extension of the Simplified Potential Energy Clock
(SPEC) model [7]. The model is implemented in LAMÉ [12] as spectacular for use with
Sierra/SM. The epoxy calibrations are targeted at residual stress predictions from manufacturing
environments and physical aging. After recent model developments, the calibrations could also be
used to predict the thermodynamic state after the arrival of a shock [25].

SPECtacular predicts a variety of relaxation phenomena which are coupled through a nonlinear
material clock. The highly coupled nature of the model complicates calibration. Therefore, the
calibration procedure is carefully documented for both filled epoxy systems, which serves the dual
purposes of (1) explicitly stating the assumptions and decisions used to calibrate the model and (2)
providing examples of model calibration for future materials. To calibrate the various relaxation
phenomena in the model, an equally varied experimental data set is required. DMA, TMA, and DSC
techniques were all used to generate data used to calibrate the linear viscoelastic parts of the model.
The nonlinear viscoelastic response was calibrated from experiments of uniaxial compression in
the glassy state.

As part of the calibration procedure, a new method was explored for constructing a shear master
curve. As opposed to traditional master curves which are formed from a series of isothermal
frequency sweeps, the new approach used measurements of 𝐺 ′ and 𝐺 ′′ while sweeping through
temperature and simultaneously alternating between five distinct frequencies. Data from this multi-
frequency temperature sweep experiment was analyzed to produce a master curve. The analysis
procedure for the multi-frequency temperature sweep was verified using synthetic data. One benefit
of the new method is that it reduces dwell times at high temperature. When the epoxy has residual
reaction potential, shorter time spent at high temperatures produces master curves that are more
representative of the as-cured state. Here, both filled epoxies exhibit residual reaction potential,
which is easily seen by comparing viscoelastic responses from an initial heat and a second heat (or
cooling after the initial heat).

A prior SPECtacular calibration was produced for 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB in SAND2022-6360 [2],
which was compared to the new calibration. The data used for the legacy calibration was generated
using epoxy specimens with nominally the same composition, but with a different cure schedule.
Differences in the legacy and new experimental data are apparent. The 𝜃 𝑔 is similar for both
the old and new data, but different rubbery and glassy limits are observed before and after the
glass transition. As expected, differences in experimental data lead to different parameters that
represent the legacy and application-specific 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB specimens. This emphasizes
the importance of developing model calibrations based on data using application-specific processing

 99

instructions, since two materials that may both be referred to as 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB may not
have identical thermo-mechanical behaviors.
For the 828/Z/AlOx specimens, the epoxy undergoes extensive curing reactions during the first heat
of any experiment that exceeds the as-cured 𝜃 𝑔 . As a result of the additional curing, the 𝜃 𝑔 increases
by approximately 45 ◦ C. This precludes the possibility of observing the full glass transition in the
as-cured state, which is a practical requirement for model calibration. Also, the SPECtacular model
does not include mechanisms for polymers undergoing chemical reactions, so the chemo-thermo-
mechanical behavior observed during cure cannot be captured. To calibrate the model despite these
challenges, data from thermal excursions after the initial heat were used for calibration. During
these subsequent thermal excursions, the 𝜃 𝑔 is stable, indicating that chemical changes are minor.
However, the resulting model calibration does not represent the as-cured 828/Z/AlOx. To modify
the calibration to represent the as-cured material, temperature sweep data from subsequent thermal
excursions were horizontally (temperature change) and vertically (measurement change) shifted to
overlap with the initial heat. Then, the horizontal shift used to match 𝜃 𝑔 from the various sweeps is
applied to 𝜃 ref , effectively lowering the 𝜃 𝑔 of the calibration to match the onset of the glass transition
observed during the first heat. Changes to rubbery and glassy limits were applied to reflect the
vertical shifts required to overlap data from subsequent temperature sweeps to the initial heat.
However, because the SPECtacular model lacks mechanisms for chemical changes, the calibration
is not valid at temperatures that are high enough for the as-cured 828/Z/AlOx to resume curing,
which occurs around 100 ◦ C. After moving 𝜃 ref to represent the as-cured material, the reference
temperature is set at 𝜃 ref = 125 ◦ C, which is greater than when curing resumes. In other words, 𝜃 ref
is set at a temperature where the model calibration is not valid. While it is not incorrect to have a 𝜃 ref
above where the model is valid, it is potentially confusing. Therefore, the 𝜃 ref was shifted to 93 ◦ C,
which is the curing temperature used in the application for 828/Z/AlOx and is also a conservative
upper limit for where the model calibration is valid. By shifting 𝜃 ref (as opposed to moving
𝜃 ref ), the viscoelastic parameters are consistently changed in accordance with the time–temperature
superposition principle to change 𝜃 ref without altering the thermo-viscoelastic behavior. Thus,
two parameterizations for 828/Z/AlOx were produced, one built at 𝜃 ref = 125 ◦ C and one built at
𝜃 ref = 93 ◦ C. However, these different parameterizations represent the same calibration in that they
predict the same constitutive behavior for a given thermo-mechanical path.
In the appendices, include files are shown that can be used to easily define the new SPECtacular
calibrations for use with Sierra/SM. The new and legacy calibrations for 828-CTBN/DEA/GMB are
shown in Appendix A and Appendix B. The legacy calibration includes minor updates to parameters
and syntax that are consistent with changes in the implementation of SPECtacular since 2022, when
the legacy calibration was originally developed. The calibration for 828/Z/AlOx built at a reference
temperature of 𝜃 ref = 93 ◦ C is shown in Appendix C. The same calibration built at 𝜃 ref = 125 ◦ C is
shown in Appendix D. Finally, preliminary calibrations were released in FY25. These are shown
in Appendices E to G. There were only minor improvements between the preliminary and final
calibrations, so the preliminary calibrations are still acceptable for engineering analyses of residual
stresses.
