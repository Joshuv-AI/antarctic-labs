Platelet–Prostate cancer crosstalk driving disparities and therapeutic vulnerabilities
Authors: Vera Mariani, Norman H Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate cancer (PCa) is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths among American men. Striking disparities persist in PCa incidence and mortality, with African American (AA) men experiencing a 1.7-fold higher incidence and more than two-fold higher mortality rate compared to European American (EA) men. Despite this disparity, the Black community remains underrepresented in PCa research and clinical trials. As PCa progresses to high-grade and metastatic castration-resistant stages, treatment options become increasingly limited and median overall survival falls below two years, underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies. One promising yet underexplored avenue is the circulatory microenvironment, which consists of platelets (PLTs) and immune cells that interact directly with circulating tumor cells (CTCs) during intravasation. Mounting evidence supports a bidirectional relationship in which tumor cells activate platelets to promote thrombosis, and platelets in turn activate tumor cells to promote tumorigenesis. In PCa, reciprocal signaling between tumor cells and platelets is increasingly being recognized. Recent transcriptomic profiling (RNA-Seq) has identified transmembrane signaling proteins mediating these interactions, broadly categorized into four groups: (1) integrin–ligand, (2) EPH receptor–ephrin, (3) immune checkpoint receptor–ligand, and (4) miscellaneous receptor–ligand interactions. Notably, many components of these signaling axes are overexpressed in platelets and/or PCa cells from individuals of African ancestry and are associated with poorer clinical outcomes. Introduction

Prostate cancer disparities

Significant population-level disparities in prostate cancer (PCa) incidence and outcomes are evident in epidemiological data, particularly affecting African American (AA) men. Compared to their European American (EA) counterparts, AA men have a 1.7-fold higher incidence of PCa and more than twice the mortality rate [1, 2]. These disparities reflect a multifaceted disease process shaped by both biological factors and social determinants of health. Contributing factors include structural racism, socioeconomic inequities, environmental exposures, unequal access to high-quality health care, and differences in genetic ancestry [3–6]. Collectively, these intersecting variables create a complex landscape that influences both disease progression and patient survival, underscoring the need for more inclusive research, tailored interventions, and equitable health policies to address these persistent gaps.

Socioeconomic and environmental versus genetic and genomic drivers

Multiple socioeconomic and environmental factors have been postulated to explain the observed PCa health disparities between AAs and EAs. These include residential segregation, attitudes about health care, health literacy, dietary differences, and disparities in the type and aggressiveness of treatment received [5–7]. In a case–control study of 884 AA and 908 EA men, neighborhood deprivation was associated with 55% increased odds of PCa among AA men [8]. Notably, this deprivation was also linked to dysregulation of immune-oncology pathways, including suppression of tumor immunity, altered chemotaxis, and elevated inflammatory signaling. Several studies have suggested that higher recurrence and mortality rates in AA men with PCa persist even after adjustment for socioeconomic status, environmental factors and access to health care [9–13]. These residual disparities have been attributed to intrinsic factors such as genetic ancestry and tumor genomics (e.g. disease-associated SNPs that are positively correlated with West African ancestry) [7, 11, 14, 15]. Noteworthy, Black men receiving radiation therapy or surgery for nonmetastatic PCa within the Veterans Health Administration system exhibit survival outcomes that are equal to or better than those of their non-Hispanic White counterparts. This observation underscores the critical role of equitable access to high-quality health care as a primary driver of outcome parity [16, 17]. However, higher mortality among AA men with low-risk (Gleason 6) PCa, for whom watchful waiting is standard, suggests underlying biological differences may still influence outcome disparities [18]. Taken together, it is increasingly clear that both social and biological factors contribute to PCa disparities in the AA population. While healthcare equity can ameliorate outcomes, genetic and genomic factors likely also play a significant role in PCa incidence and aggressiveness [19, 20].

An increasingly compelling hypothesis posits that historically rooted inequities, particularly residential segregation and chronic socioeconomic stress, contribute to DNA damage and epigenetic alterations that influence cancer disparities. Residential segregation has led to disproportionate exposure to environmental pollutants and chemical carcinogens, while chronic stress has been linked to changes in DNA methylation and histone modifications that affect chromatin structure and gene expression [5, 21]. In breast cancer, studies among AA patients have shown that neighborhood deprivation is associated with altered DNA methylation and decreased expression of key tumor suppressor genes [22]. Similar associations between societal determinants and somatic or epigenetic changes have been proposed for prostate, colon and lung cancers [23]. For instance, elevated levels of DNA damage, manifested as mutation-causing DNA adducts in prostate specimens, have been observed more frequently in AA men and are associated with greater risk of PCa compared to EA men [3, 24]. Genomic studies have further demonstrated that driver mutations in key cancer-associated genes (e.g. AR, FOXA1, BRAF, MYC, DNA repair genes) appear to be more prevalent in metastatic PCa specimens from AAs compared to EAs [25, 26]. In parallel, epigenetic changes may contribute to alterations in gene expression and RNA splicing events that promote tumorigenesis, potentially accounting for observed disparities across multiple cancers including prostate, breast, lung and colorectal cancers (see commentary [23]). Indeed, epigenetic differences associated with gene expression changes in AA compared to EA PCa have been described by multiple research groups, including collaborative work from our lab and the Kwabi-Addo lab [27–29]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the spectrum of gene mutations and epigenetic alterations may drive both cell-intrinsic mechanisms (e.g., oncogene activation and signaling dysregulation) and cell-extrinsic mechanisms (e.g., altered stromal or immune interactions) that contribute to PCa disparities. While cell-intrinsic mechanisms have been studied in depth, cell-extrinsic pathways involving the tumor microenvironment and systemic influences remain underexplored and warrant further investigation as contributors to ancestry-associated differences in disease progression [30].

Role of cell autonomous mechanisms in PCa disparities

Multiple laboratories have proposed that cancer cell-autonomous mechanisms contribute to PCa disparities. These include differences in gene expression [31, 32], alterations in microRNA profiles [33] and widespread changes in RNA splicing [34–36]. For example, gene expression studies comparing PCa specimens from AA and EA patients have highlighted the differential expression of androgen receptor (AR)-regulated genes, suggesting a key role for AR signaling in PCa disparities [31]. Additional studies have reported over-expression of metastasis-associated genes [37], under-expression of DNA repair genes [38], and increased expression of genes involved in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [39] in PCa samples from AAs compared to EAs (Table 1).

Aberrant RNA splicing and shifts in the ratios of alternatively spliced transcripts have been reported in various cancers [40–45]. A substantial amount of proteomic diversity can be attributed to alternative splicing, where more than 95% of human genes undergo splicing in a developmental, signal-transduction dependent, or tissue-specific manner [46, 47]. In cancer, alternative splicing of critical genes can generate protein isoforms with differing oncogenic potential, influencing processes such as apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis [48]. A well characterized example in castration-resistant PCa (CRPC) is the constitutively active AR splice variant 7 (AR-V7), which was shown to facilitate resistance to enzalutamide and abiraterone acetate [49].

Although many early splicing studies did not account for genetic ancestry, increasing evidence now implicates differential splicing as a driver of tumor aggressiveness and drug resistance in AA PCa [34, 35]. Genome-wide analysis of PCa specimens from AA and EA patients uncovered global differential splicing events affecting 2,520 genes [35]. Among these, 1,876 splicing events were unique to the AA versus EA PCa comparison, while 644 were also detected in matched noncancerous AA versus EA tissues, suggesting both tumor-specific and ancestry-associated splicing differences. Notably, 71.8% of the differentially spliced genes were enriched across multiple cancer types, with many mapping to key oncogenic signaling pathways [35].

Two mRNA variants, both generated through exon skipping events, have been observed to be preferentially expressed in PCa specimens from AA patients [34, 35]. These variants encode shorter protein isoforms of fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) and phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit delta (PIK3CD), which exhibit increased oncogenic potential and resistance to clinically available small molecule inhibitors. In contrast, prostate tumors from EA patients predominantly express longer isoforms of FGFR3 and PIK3CD that are less oncogenic and more responsive to small molecule inhibitor treatment [34, 35]. These findings underscore the importance of further characterizing splice variant expression in the context of genetic ancestry, which may help identify molecular drivers of aggressive disease and uncover ancestry-specific biomarkers predictive of therapeutic response.

Role of cell non-autonomous mechanisms in PCa disparities: the primary tumor microenvironment

While numerous studies have elucidated cell-autonomous mechanisms underlying the progression and metastasis of PCa in AA men (see reviews [3, 36, 50, 51]), fewer investigations have explored the contribution of cell non-autonomous mechanisms, including the role of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and tumor-adjacent stroma, to PCa disparities (Fig. 1). An RNA-Seq comparison of PCa-adjacent stroma from AA and EA patients revealed a significant down-regulation of genes associated with immune response pathways in the former, which has been interpreted as T-cell exhaustion in AA patients [52]. Another genomic study similarly concluded that immune response genes and pathways are down-regulated in tumor-adjacent stroma of AA PCa patients [53]. These studies taken together, suggest that immune compromise or dysfunction in the immediate microenvironment surrounding the primary tumor may be contributing to the aggressive nature of PCa in the AA population.

Intra-tumoral and circulatory microenvironments. Within the tumor microenvironment, platelets promote epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, immune suppression, stromal activation, and angiogenesis through signaling pathways involving TGFβ, VEGF, PDGF, ADGRE5, and integrins. In the circulatory microenvironment, platelets and extracellular vesicles provide physical shielding, deliver pro-survival and pro-invasive signals, participate in tumor cell–induced platelet aggregation (TCIPA), facilitate extravasation and immune evasion, and support metastatic colonization. In genomic studies that have directly compared PCa specimens from AA and EA patients, a consistent and recurrent pattern of inflammatory cytokine upregulation has been observed in AA tumors, most notably involving CCL2, CCL3, CCL5 and CCL7 [37, 39, 52]. This inflammatory cytokine signature is presumed to originate from infiltrating immune cells such as tumor-associated macrophages, fibroblasts and B cells. Chronic inflammation has long been implicated as a driver of prostate carcinogenesis [54]. Notably, the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as aspirin has been shown to significantly reduce both the incidence and recurrence of PCa in AA men [55], highlighting the potential therapeutic value of targeting inflammatory cell activity in this population. Given that aspirin also inhibits platelet activation, its protective effect may, in part, be mediated through the suppression of platelet-driven oncogenic signaling [56]. Supporting this possibility, our immunohistological analysis of PCa tissue provides evidence that platelets infiltrate the primary tumor (unpublished data), where they may deliver pro-oncogenic cues to PCa cells [56], potentially acting in synergy with local immune cell signaling networks [57]. Similar intratumoral platelet infiltration has been documented in other malignancies, including colon, lung, breast, and ovarian cancers [58] further supporting the concept that platelets contribute to shaping the tumor microenvironment across cancer types.

Role of cell non-autonomous mechanisms in cancer: the circulatory microenvironment

While the tumor-adjacent microenvironment has been extensively studied in the context of PCa disparities, the blood-based environment remains relatively understudied (Fig. 1). As PCa progresses, tumor cells eventually detach from the primary site and enter the bloodstream, becoming circulating tumor cells (CTCs). Upon intravasation, these CTCs encounter a distinct environment that we refer to as the 'circulatory microenvironment', which is comprised of red blood cells, platelets and immune cells [59]. To survive the physical and immunological challenges of circulation, CTCs must rapidly adapt to and interact with these components (for review see [60]).

While platelets are primarily known for their cardiovascular role in thrombosis and hemostasis, increasing evidence points to their critical role in cancer progression and mortality. Indeed, elevated circulating platelet counts are correlated with increased cancer metastasis and poorer survival across multiple malignancies, including colon, lung, ovary and stomach cancers [61–66]. In PCa, both high platelet-to-lymphocyte ratios and high platelet counts have been positively correlated with CTC counts as well as poorer overall survival [62, 67, 68]. Mechanistically, platelets have been proposed to contribute to cancer progression by physically shielding CTCs from tumor necrosis factor α, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, bloodstream shear stress, and other factors that might impact CTC survival [69–73]. Platelet interactions with tumor cells facilitate continuous exchange of nucleic acids and proteins, resulting in the generation of a “tumor-educated” (TE)-platelet population with distinct RNA and protein profiles [74]. In fact, platelets preferentially concentrate tumor-derived cytokines and growth factors at levels 10,000-fold higher than plasma [75]. Moreover, both in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that platelets can acquire cancer cell-specific RNAs via uptake of tumor-derived small extracellular vesicles (EVs) [76]. In one study, platelets from 70% of PCa patients retained a PCa-specific EV-derived RNA marker, PCA3 [76]. Additionally, analysis of platelet-derived tumor biomarkers in patients with CRPC improved prediction accuracy for abiraterone therapy response [77].

Lessons learned and therapeutic opportunities

Studies across multiple cancer types have demonstrated that platelet–cancer cell interactions give rise to bidirectional signaling through both direct transmembrane ligand–receptor coupling and indirect paracrine signaling [59, 78]. Activation of platelets by tumor cells, a process referred to as tumor cell-induced platelet aggregation (TCIPA), has been implicated in the development of thrombosis and venous thromboembolism (VTE) [59, 78]. It is estimated that up to 20% of cancer patients suffer from VTE [79], and 60–75% of occult cancers are detected within a year of diagnosing idiopathic VTE, highlighting the strong clinical link between thrombosis and malignancy [80].

Reciprocally, platelet stimulation of tumor cells appears to be critical for their metastatic programming, enhancing capabilities such as intravasation, extravasation, and colonization of distant sites [78, 81]. Interestingly, TCIPA itself appears to potentiate platelet-driven activation of tumor cells by triggering the release of multiple pro-angiogenic and pro-tumorigenic molecules from platelet granules [82, 83]. These findings highlight a self-reinforcing feedback loop, wherein platelet–CTC interactions promote both thrombosis and metastasis, offering a compelling rationale for the development of therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting this pathological crosstalk.

Interfering with bidirectional signaling events during platelet-cancer cell interactions has shown promise in reducing metastasis and enhancing treatment efficacy. For example, chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia is associated with reduced ovarian cancer metastasis [84], while experimental depletion of platelets by 50% using an anti-GPIbα antibody resulted in a corresponding 50% reduction in tumor growth in a murine model of ovarian cancer [65]. Similarly, depletion of platelets with an anti-GPIbα antibody enhanced both the delivery and efficacy of chemotherapy agents in a breast cancer mouse model [85]. Together, these findings support the idea that interfering with platelet–tumor signaling may represent a novel therapeutic approach.

More specifically, targeting key surface receptors involved in platelet–tumor communication has demonstrated efficacy across multiple cancer types. Blocking the binding of platelet P-selectin to P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1) on lung cancer cells [86] or to mucins on colon cancer cells [71] reverses TCIPA and reduces metastatic spread. Similarly, preventing podoplanin, a glycoprotein expressed on melanoma, breast, lung, and osteosarcoma cells, from binding to C-type lectin-like receptor 2 (CLEC-2) on platelets has been shown to reduce TCIPA in vitro and lower VTE incidence in mouse models [87–91]. Additionally, P-selectin deficiency or its pharmacologic inhibition with heparin attenuates TCIPA, tumor growth, and colon cancer metastasis both in vitro and in vivo [71, 92].

Platelet-derived microparticles containing cytokines and growth factors have been shown to stimulate matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) production in the PCa cell line CL-1, thereby enhancing tumor cell invasiveness [93]. Notably, this induction of MMP-2 appears to be independent of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), or basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), suggesting that other platelet-derived factors mediate this pro-invasive effect [93]. On the other hand, VEGF, PDGF and FGF remain important in the context of platelet-stimulated angiogenesis, which promotes metastasis of multiple cancer types (e.g., breast cancer [94], PCa [95]). For example, tumor growth and bone metastasis in a PCa mouse model are significantly reduced by treatment with STI571, a PDGF receptor inhibitor [96], in combination with paclitaxel [95], underscoring the therapeutic relevance of targeting platelet-driven signaling pathways.

These findings collectively highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting platelet–tumor cell crosstalk. However, the development of effective interventions will require a deeper understanding of how these interactions vary across tumor types, including PCa, and how the molecular repertoire of platelet-mediated signaling evolves during tumor progression and dissemination.

Role of the circulatory microenvironment in PCa and thrombotic disparities in the AA population

The circulatory microenvironment may also play a critical role in shaping disease progression and treatment response, particularly among AA patients. AA men experience disproportionately high rates of venous thromboembolism (VTE) [97, 98] and may also be more likely to present with metastatic PCa [99], although the magnitude of this latter disparity remains less clearly defined. VTE frequently co-occurs in cancer patients, and thrombosis is the second leading cause of cancer-related death [100]. Notably, AA patients are known to be at higher risk of cancer-associated VTE compared to other racial groups, particularly in the context of lung, colon and gastric cancers [101]. Moreover, the occurrence of cancer-associated thrombosis in PCa is significantly higher in AA men than their EA counterparts [102], emphasizing the need to better understand the biological and clinical drivers that contribute to this disparity, particularly those rooted in the circulatory microenvironment. Extending these findings in the context of PCa, we provide evidence that TCIPA is more pronounced in PCa cell lines derived from patients of African ancestry compared to those of European ancestry (Fig. 2). The molecular mechanisms driving this disparity are currently under investigation [56] (and see below), which may yield important insights into ancestry-associated differences in tumor progression, thrombotic risk, and treatment response.

Candidate platelet-PCa cell signaling axes involved in thrombotic and PCa disparities

Several studies have identified key differences in platelet function between AAs and EAs [103, 104], including genome wide association studies that identified alleles associated with platelet aggregation in one group or the other [105]. For example, the G-allele of rs11202221, located in the second intron of the bone morphogenetic protein receptor type1A (BMPR1A) gene, is associated with decreased ADP-induced platelet aggregation in the AAs, while the intergenic T-allele of rs6566765 is associated with increased platelet aggregation in response to ADP in the EA cohort. Although these prior works have demonstrated that genetic variation significantly influences platelet function, the precise mechanism driving differential platelet behavior between AAs and EAs and how these differences impact disease manifestation remain incompletely understood.

RNA-seq analysis of platelets from healthy AA and EA individuals has revealed global differences in gene expression, including enrichment of distinct signaling pathways predicted to modulate platelet function [106]. Platelets from AA donors exhibited a significant over-representation of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in cell migration, calcium mobilization, chronic inflammation, ion homeostasis, cell viability, hemostasis, bleeding and degranulation of cells, portending functional consequences [106]. Specifically, overexpressed genes in AA platelets included receptors, ion channels, and intracellular signaling proteins that are predicted to facilitate inside-out signaling, shape change, granule secretion and aggregation. Furthermore, overexpressed cytokine genes in AA platelets were associated with pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic states, further supporting their potential contribution to disease disparaties [106]. Functionally, AA platelets exhibit significantly higher aggregation responses to collagen, a potent platelet activator, compared to EA platelets [106, 107]. Among the most differentially expressed cytokines in AA platelets were IL32 and PROK2. The same study demonstrated that IL32 exhibits antiaggregatory activity, whereas PROK2 promotes platelet aggregation [56, 106], underscoring a complex and potentially regulatory interplay between opposing cytokine effects.

Functionally, co-incubation of AA platelets with the AA PCa cell line RC77 T/E has been shown to result in physical platelet-tumor cell interactions characterized by ‘atypical’ platelet activation. In this setting, RC77 T/E cells developed microvilli-like protrusions that extended outwards towards individual platelets and made direct contact with platelet aggregates [56]. Similar morphological changes have been observed in other cancer models, where an increased presence of microvilli on tumor cells positively correlates with enhanced growth potential and metastatic capacity [108, 109]. Reciprocally, co-incubation of AA platelets with either RC77 T/E or MDA PCa 2b cells, another AA-derived PCa cell line, led to a significant increase in platelet activation as evidenced by elevated expression of activation-associated surface markers and enhanced calcium mobilization [56]. In this in vitro setting, TCIPA appears to be mediated by tumor cell-secreted fibronectin 1 (FN1), which presumably activates integrin α2bβ3 on the surface of platelets since TCIPA is abrogated by a neutralizing antibody against FN1 [56]. Noteworthy, FN1 is over-expressed in PCa tissue compared to normal prostate [56]. The same study also revealed that AA platelets reciprocally enhance apoptotic resistance and invasive capacity in both AA PCa cell lines via two distinct signaling axes. Namely, a CD55-adhesion G protein coupled receptor E5 (ADGRE5) axis promoting tumor cell invasion and the lysophosphatidic acid (LPA)-LPA receptor (LPAR) axis promoting apoptotic resistance, based on antibody neutralization and small molecule inhibitor assays.

Interestingly, platelet-stimulated apoptotic resistance and invasive capacity have not been observed in PCa cell lines of European ancestry (i.e., LNCaP and PC-3 cells) when co-incubated with EA platelets in our hands (Mariani and Lee, unpublished observations). However, it is worth noting that co-incubation of platelets (from donors of unspecified ancestry) with the metastatic, oncogenic-Ras-overexpressing EA PCa cell line DU145 [110] has been shown to induce both TCIPA and metastatic programing through an ADGRE5-integrin α2bβ3 axis [111]. One possible interpretation is that EA-derived PCa cells may be intrinsically less responsive to platelet-induced oncogenic signaling. However, this hypothesis requires further investigation. For example, it would be informative to determine whether native DU145 cells exhibit similar responses when co-incubated specifically with EA platelets, thereby helping to clarify whether observed differences are driven by tumor cell lineage, platelet ancestry, or both.

In search of additional novel platelet–PCa cell signaling axes implicated in PCa disparities.

Mapping platelet-PCa cell axes in PCa disparities

Microarray and more recent RNA-Seq analyses of platelets from individuals of African and European ancestry [56, 106, 112] along with transcriptomic profiling of PCa cell lines [113], have facilitated the identification of candidate platelet–PCa cell signaling axes that may contribute to ancestry-associated disparities in disease progression and thrombotic risk. Our meta-analyses of these datasets have revealed ligand–receptor pairs in which components on the platelet side, the PCa cell side, or both are overexpressed in AA individuals compared to EAs. These ancestry-associated expression differences suggest the existence of functionally relevant signaling interactions that may be more active, amplified, or dysregulated in the AA population, potentially contributing to both enhanced oncogenic signaling and increased thrombotic burden.

Notably, many of the overexpressed genes on the tumor cell side are also associated with worse patient survival, reinforcing their relevance as candidate mediators of aggressive disease phenotypes. The transmembrane signaling proteins mediating these platelet–tumor cell interactions can be broadly categorized into four groups: (1) integrin–ligand, (2) EPH receptor–ephrin, (3) immune checkpoint receptor–ligand, and (4) miscellaneous receptor–ligand interactions [56]. The following examples illustrate specific signaling axes identified through our meta-analyses that meet these criteria and may contribute to ancestry-linked disparities in PCa progression and thrombosis (Fig. 3).

[56, 106]. Signaling pathways were categorized into four major groups: (1) integrin–ligand interactions (e.g., LAMA/B/C, FN1, α6β1 on tumor cells), (2) ephrin–EPH receptor signaling (e.g., EPHA1, EFNB1, EPHB4), (3) immune checkpoint receptor–ligand pairs (e.g., LGALS3, CD47, CEACAM1), and (4) miscellaneous signaling axes (e.g., EGFR/HER2, LPAR3/5/6, TGFBR1/2). EGF/TGFA-EGFR/HER2 signaling

Concurrent overexpression of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and human growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) has been associated with PCa progression and bone metastasis [114]. RNA-seq analysis has revealed significant overexpression of EGFR, HER2, and the EGFR ligand transforming growth factor alpha (TGFA) in PCa cell lines derived from AA compared to EA patients [56] (Fig. 4). Notably, high TGFA expression in PCa is associated with significantly worse patient survival [56]. The relevance of this axis is further supported by a large immunohistochemical study of 2,525 PCa samples, which demonstrated that HER2 protein overexpression strongly correlates with advanced tumor stage, higher Gleason grade, and tumor recurrence [115]. In a recent clinical cohort study composed predominantly of AA men (85%), HER2 expression was frequently observed with HER2 levels positively correlated with Gleason score, suggesting a potential ancestry-linked role in PCa progression [116]. Additionally, EGFR overexpression has been reported to be significantly more prevalent in AA PCa than EA PCa [117]. Functionally, EGFR overexpression promotes tumor cell migration and metastasis, while membrane-localized HER2 has been associated with a quiescent phenotype [114, 118, 119]. Together, these findings highlight EGF/TGFA–EGFR/HER2 signaling as a candidate axis contributing to ancestry-associated PCa disparities.

On the platelet side (Fig. 4), two key EGFR ligands, heparin-binding EGF-like growth factor (HBEGF) and TGFA, are significantly overexpressed 2- to 3- fold in AA platelets [106]. Both ligands can induce EGFR-HER2 heterodimerization, activating downstream signaling pathways involved in cell survival, proliferation and therapy resistance [120]. The concurrent overexpression of EGFR ligands in AA platelets and receptors in AA PCa cells suggests a potential paracrine signaling loop that may drive enhanced tumor aggressiveness and treatment resistance in this population.

Despite the strong biological rationale for targeting this axis (Fig. 4), clinical trials evaluating EGFR and HER2 inhibitors in advanced PCa have yielded mixed outcomes. Agents such as lapatinib, cetuximab, and erlotinib have demonstrated limited clinical benefits in unselected PCa populations, likely due to heterogeneous EGFR/HER2 expression and the absence of biomarker-driven patient selection strategies [121–123]. Similarly, trastuzumab, a ligand-independent inhibitor of HER2 dimerization with other EGFR family members [120], demonstrated poor efficacy in phase II trials for hormone-refractory PCa [124, 125]. Likewise, pertuzumab, a ligand-dependent inhibitor of HER2 dimerization [120], also showed poor efficacy in PCa [126]. These outcomes collectively raise the possibility that lack of EGFR/HER2 biomarker stratification as an inclusion criterion, and/or failure to target both ligand-dependent and ligand-independent modes of dimerization, may account for the lack of clinical success in molecularly heterogeneous patient cohorts.

Given the higher prevalence of EGFR and HER2 overexpression in AA PCa, along with elevated TGFA expression in AA platelets, there is a compelling rationale to reassess EGFR/HER-targeted therapies in biomarker-enriched and demographically focused cohorts. In particular, clinical trials targeting TGFA-high or EGFR-high/HER2-high AA PCa may uncover responsive subpopulations that could benefit from agents such as trastuzumab, pertuzumab, lapatinib, or dual EGFR/HER2 inhibitors. Future studies should also explore the functional impact of platelet-derived TGFA in modulating tumor response and determine whether inhibiting EGFR heterodimerization can overcome resistance mechanisms in this setting.

S1P-S1P receptor signaling

Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) is a bioactive lipid mediator implicated in oncogenesis [127]. It exerts its effects by binding to one or more of five G protein–coupled S1P receptors (S1PR1–S1PR5), thereby activating downstream signaling pathways involved in cell survival, proliferation, motility, and metabolism, depending on the cellular context [128]. Notably, plasma from AA individuals contains significantly higher levels of S1P compared to that from EAs [129]. Platelets are a major source of circulating S1P, secreting large amounts upon activation [130]. Furthermore, sphingosine kinase 1 (SPHK1), the enzyme responsible for converting sphingosine to produce S1P, is overexpressed more than two-fold in AA platelets (Fig. 5) [106], suggesting enhanced capacity for local S1P production in this population.

SPHK1 catalyzes the formation of S1P, a bioactive lipid that signals through S1P receptors. Among the five S1P receptors, S1PR5 appears to be particularly relevant in the context of PCa. S1PR5 and spingosine-1-kinase (SPHK1) are overexpressed in AA compared to EA PCa cell lines, while high expression of SPHK1 in PCa is associated with worse patient survival (Fig. 5). Functionally, S1P–S1PR5 signaling has been shown to promote cytoprotective autophagy in PC-3 cells, and elevated SPHK1 activity (leading to phosphorylation of sphingosine to generate S1P) correlates with chemoresistance in several EA PCa models [131–133]. Additionally, activation of S1PR5 by S1P has been linked to accelerated mitosis via PI3K-AKT signaling, contributing to chromosome segregation defects and genomic instability [134].

Therapeutically, the FDA-approved multiple sclerosis drug fingolimod (FTY720) is a nonselective functional antagonist of S1P receptors [135]. In EA PCa models, fingolimod has been shown to induce apoptosis and suppress invasion, although its apoptotic effects were reported to be independent of S1PR binding and instead mediated by direct inhibition of SPHK1 [128, 136–138]. However, the potential impact of fingolimod on platelet-stimulated oncogenic phenotypes in AA PCa remains unexplored. Given the elevated expression of SPHK1 in AA platelets and SPHK1-S1PR5 in AA PCa cells, further investigation is warranted to evaluate the efficacy of targeting this pathway in ancestry-informed therapeutic strategies.

LPA-LPA receptor signaling

Lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) is another prominent bioactive lipid released by activated platelets into the circulation [139]. Like S1P, LPA signals through G protein-coupled receptors and has overlapping functions, including stimulation of cell proliferation and inhibition of apoptosis. However, cellular responses to LPA and S1P are largely determined by the expression patterns of their respective receptors [140]. Notably, three LPA receptors (LPAR), LPAR3, LPAR5, and LPAR6, are overexpressed in AA compared to EA PCa cell lines (Fig. 6), and high LPAR5 expression correlates with worse PCa survival outcomes [56]. The diversity of LPA-induced cellular effects, including survival, differentiation, proliferation, cytoskeletal rearrangement, motility and cytokine secretion, are largely attributed to the combinatorial expression of distinct LPARs and their differential downstream signaling pathways [141]. Specifically, LPAR3 couples to Gαi and Gαq, LPAR5 to Gαq and Gα12/13, and LPAR6 primarily to Gα12/13 [141]. All three receptors (LPAR3/5/6) have all been implicated in cancer progression to varying degrees [142]. Importantly, use of an LPAR1/3/5 inhibitor, H2L 5765834, has been shown to reverse platelet-stimulated apoptotic resistance in AA PCa cells [56], suggesting that targeting this axis may be therapeutically beneficial.

Most prior research on LPA signaling in PCa has focused on PC-3 cells, a cell line derived from a EA patient, which predominantly expresses LPAR1 and to a lesser extent LPAR3 [143]. Broadly, LPA has been shown to stimulate proliferation, migration, invasion, survival, induction of reactive oxygen species, and cytokine release [142, 143]. In vivo, blockade of LPAR1/3 signaling via the small molecule inhibitor, Ki16425, in a PC-3 xenograft model suppresses lymphangiogenesis and lymphatic metastasis but not primary tumor growth [144, 145].

Despite these findings, the specific roles of LPAR3, LPAR5, and LPAR6 in PCa progression, and particularly in PCa disparities, remain poorly defined. Further investigation into the effects of platelet-derived LPA and the distinct signaling properties of LPAR3/5/6 may yield novel strategies to counteract the pleiotropic and potentially co-activating effects of LPA receptor engagement in PCa.

TGFβ-TGFβ receptor signaling

Alterations in transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ) signaling have been implicated in PCa progression. At baseline, AA individuals exhibit significantly higher serum levels of TGFβ1 protein compared to EAs [146]. Supporting this, RNA-Seq analysis has revealed significant overexpression of TGFB1 in AA compared to EA PCa cell lines (Fig. 7) [56]. In parallel, AA platelets overexpress both TGFB1 and TGFB2 at significantly higher levels than EA platelets (Fig. 7) [106]. Importantly, high TGFB1 expression is also associated with worse PCa survival, further supporting its relevance as a potential driver of aggressive disease in the AA population.

Functionally, platelet-derived TGFβ1 has been shown to be essential for metastasis in vivo by synergizing with platelet-induced NF-κB activation in tumor cells to promote EMT, a key step in cancer metastasis [147]. Moreover, TCIPA, mediated by tumor expression of podoplanin, has been shown to trigger TGFβ release from platelets and promote EMT induction [148]. In line with this, disrupting the interaction between podoplanin and its cognate platelet receptor, C-type lectin-like receptor 2 (CLEC-2), inhibits TCIPA, reduces VTE, and suppresses metastasis in lung cancer, melanoma, and osteosarcoma models [87, 88, 149–152].

Platelets also sequester tumor-derived TGFβ1 and matrix metalloproteinase 1 (MMP-1), enabling their systemic circulation and facilitating distal bone remodeling [75]. From an immune perspective, TGFβ is a well-established immunosuppressive cytokine [153], and platelet-derived TGFβ has been shown to mediate CD4 + and CD8 + T cell suppression [154]. Furthermore, platelet-derived TGFβ inhibits NK cell antitumor reactivity, and neutralization of TGFβ in the platelet releasate reversed this phenomenon [155].

Therapeutically, the TGFβR1 kinase inhibitor galunisertib, in combination with anti–PD-1 immunotherapy, is currently under investigation in clinical trials to restore antitumor immunity [153]. Separately, galunisertib combined with enzalutamide significantly reduced PCa tumor growth in preclinical models [156].

These findings collectively support a model in which heightened TGFβ signaling in both AA platelets and AA tumors fosters a pro-metastatic and immunosuppressive microenvironment through platelet–tumor interactions. This signaling axis represents a promising candidate for further investigation in the context of PCa disparities and platelet-mediated metastasis.

TG2-ADGRG1 signaling

TGFβ is known to upregulate transglutaminase 2 (TG2) at both the mRNA and protein levels, suggesting a possible mechanistic link between TGFβ activity and downstream TG2-adhesion G protein-coupled receptor 1 (ADGRG1) signaling [157–159]. This axis, comprising platelet-secreted TG2 and its receptor ADGRG1 (also known as GPR56), represents a compelling candidate in the context of PCa disparities (Fig. 8). While ADGRG1 has a well-established role in central nervous system development, emerging evidence supports its involvement in cancer progression [160, 161]. Notably, ADGRG1 is significantly overexpressed in AA compared to EA PCa cell lines [56] (Fig. 8). Additionally, ADGRG1 function involves two co-receptors, CD81 and CD9 [162], the latter being significantly overexpressed in AA PCa cell lines, suggesting a broader dysregulation of this axis in AA tumors. Interestingly, high expression of both ADGRG1 and CD9 is associated with improved PCa survival (Fig. 8).

Although ADGRG1 has been reported to exert both pro- and anti-tumorigenic effects depending on tumor context [157, 163, 164], its role in PCa remains largely unexplored. TG2, meanwhile, is a multifunctional enzyme with protein cross-linking activity that regulates key processes such as apoptosis, cell adhesion, migration, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [165]. In various cancers, TG2 has been reported to act as a cancer stem cell survival factor that promotes tumor cell survival, progression and invasion [157, 166]. At the tumor-matrix interface, extracellular TG2 has been shown to activate noncanonical NF-κB signaling, resulting in CD44 upregulation and EMT, thereby promoting ovarian cancer metastasis [167]. In breast cancer, TG2-enriched EVs released from breast cancer cells facilitate metastatic niche formation and distant metastasis [168]. Similarly in PCa, TG2 is abundant in EVs secreted by the PCa cell line PC-3, and TG2 knockdown impairs cell–matrix interactions and migratory capacity in both PC-3 and DU145 cells [169].

While the TG2-ADGRG1 axis has not been functionally interrogated in PCa, its coordinated upregulation in AA PCa cells and AA platelets suggests potential relevance in ancestry-associated disease progression. From a therapeutical perspective, zampilimab (UCB-7858), a monoclonal antibody targeting TG2 and developed for fibrotic conditions such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and chronic kidney disease [170], has not yet been evaluated in cancer models. Repurposing zampilimab to disrupt aberrant TG2-ADGRG1 signaling in the context of platelet-stimulated PCa progression may offer a novel therapeutic strategy to mitigate disparity-associated tumor aggressiveness, particularly within the AA population.

Checkpoint molecule signaling axes

Multiple studies have established racial differences in the immunologic profile of AA and EA PCa [171]. In particular, AA PCa exhibits higher expression of programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) [172], upregulation of genes involved in antigen presentation [37], reduced DNA damage repair activity, and significant enrichment of immune-oncologic signatures [39, 173]. Collectively, this more inflammatory or 'immune hot' phenotype suggests AA individuals with PCa may respond better to immunotherapies compared to EA individuals.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown significant efficacy in traditionally immunogenic 'hot' malignancies such as melanoma and lung cancer. While their application in PCa has yielded variable results [171], combination strategies targeting the TME may offer improved clinical benefit. Although PCa is often classified as an immunologically 'cold' tumor, recent evidence indicates that platelets infiltrate primary prostate tumors. Notably, PD-L1-negative lung cancer patients who responded to anti-PD-L1 treatment showed increased intra-tumoral platelet infiltration [174]. This study also demonstrated platelet-derived PD-L1 shields tumor cells from T cell cytotoxicity and promotes growth of PD-L1-negative tumors [174]. Furthermore, platelet PD-L1 has emerged as a predictive biomarker for ICI response in both non-small cell lung cancer and colorectal cancers [175, 176]. Across these studies platelet PD-L1 was shown to impede T cell infiltration into the TME, highlighting the potential therapeutic utility of combining anti-platelet agents (e.g., aspirin) with ICIs [174–176]. In this context, our recent RNA-Seq analysis of AA platelet-tumor cell interactions has identified additional immune checkpoint-ligand candidates [56, 106] (Fig. 3), further supporting the hypothesis that platelet-mediated immune regulation may play a role in PCa disparities.

Importantly, AA men with PCa have demonstrated a survival advantage over EA men when treated with the cancer vaccine sipuleucel-T [177], further supporting the hypothesis that immunotherapeutic strategies may be particularly effective in AA PCa. Given the persistent underrepresentation of AA individuals in ICI clinical trials, further investigation into the efficacy of ICIs in this population is urgently needed. Additionally, exploring the role of platelet-derived PD-L1 in PCa disparities may provide insight into ancestry-specific mechanisms of immune evasion and help guide the development of more effective, tailored immunotherapies for AA patients.

Convergent platelet–tumor signaling on YAP1 and β-Catenin

A key question is whether these upregulated platelet–tumor signaling axes converge on common downstream effectors. Notably, the EGF/TGFA–EGFR/HER2, S1P–S1PR, LPA–LPAR, TGF-β–TGF-βR and TG2–ADGRG1 axes are proposed to converge on YAP1 and β-catenin, which are also upregulated in PCa cell lines of African ancestry (Fig. 9). These observations raise the hypothesis that YAP1 and β-catenin function as central signaling nodes integrating diverse platelet–tumor interactions. Therapeutic targeting of one or more of these convergent pathways may disrupt multiple pro-tumorigenic processes simultaneously, including survival, invasion and metastasis. Future investigations in this model are warranted.

Cell-based approaches to disrupt platelet-PCa cell signaling

Beyond pharmacologic interventions using neutralizing antibodies or small-molecule inhibitors, cell-based therapies represent an emerging and potentially transformative strategy to disrupt platelet–tumor signaling. One such approach involves the use of platelet-like particles (PLPs), which are generated from megakaryocytic and erythrocytic cell lines (e.g., MEG-01, K-562) treated with phorbol ester. Initially regarded as surrogates for native platelets, recent transcriptomic analyses revealed substantial differences between PLPs and platelets, with PLPs lacking 2,342 platelet-expressed genes and displaying differential expression of 3,610 shared genes [113]. These transcriptional disparities translate into divergent functional behavior. Whereas native platelets robustly aggregate in response to PCa cells, PLPs do not and is consistent with their downregulation of canonical platelet aggregation molecules [113].

In contrast to pro-tumorigenic platelets, PLPs functionally exhibit anti-tumorigenic effects, including reduced PCa cell invasion and enhanced baseline apoptosis. Moreover, PLPs partially block platelet binding to PCa cells and effectively antagonize platelet-induced invasion and apoptotic resistance [113]. Mechanistically, three candidate PLP–PCa signaling axes have been implicated in these effects (Fig. 10). These findings support the therapeutic potential of PLPs as biologically active agents capable of disrupting pro-oncogenic platelet–tumor crosstalk.

Cell-based approaches to disrupt oncogenic platelet-tumor cell interactions. Schematic representation of platelet-like particles (PLPs) interfering with platelet-stimulated oncogenic signaling in prostate cancer cells. PLPs release tumor-suppressive mediators such as TIMP3, IGFBP4 and VEGFB to disrupt MMP15, WNT–FZD/LRP6 and FGFR1 signaling, respectively, thereby inhibiting pro-proliferative, pro-survival, and pro-invasive phenotypes. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of cell-based strategies to antagonize platelet-driven oncogenic crosstalk

A second promising strategy involves the use of platelet decoys that are engineered from healthy donor platelets via detergent extraction to eliminate activation and aggregation capacity while retaining surface receptor integrity [178]. Platelet decoys are unresponsive to classic agonists, inhibit normal platelet aggregation, impair adhesion to thrombogenic surfaces under flow, and reduce in vivo thrombosis [178]. From a tumor biology perspective, platelet decoys bind CTCs, block platelet-induced tumor cell arrest and extravasation in vitro, and dose-dependently reduce metastatic burden [178].

While platelet decoys primarily function by neutralizing platelet–CTC interactions, PLPs exert direct anti-oncogenic effects on tumor cells, even in the absence of native platelets. Thus, a combinatorial strategy employing both PLPs and platelet decoys may offer a novel approach to counteract thrombo-inflammatory drivers of PCa progression and mitigate ancestry-associated disparities.

Conclusions

Given the dual role of tumor-educated platelets in promoting both thrombosis and cancer progression, it is plausible that platelets contribute to the disproportionately high rates of VTE and aggressive PCa observed in AA men. Importantly, these ancestry- and race-associated differences likely reflect a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, socioeconomic and other social determinants of health rather than purely intrinsic biological differences. Factors including chronic inflammation, comorbidities, environmental exposures, healthcare access and structural inequities may collectively influence platelet biology, tumor signaling and disease progression. By further interrogating the platelet and PCa transcriptome in this population (e.g., single cell RNA-Seq, spatial transcriptomics), we may uncover previously unrecognized signaling axes that link coagulation, inflammation, and tumor progression. Such insights could lead to the development or repurposing of therapeutics that not only disrupt platelet-mediated oncogenic signaling but also reduce thrombotic risk, thus offering a precision medicine approach to address cancer disparities.
