Widespread use of nitroimidazoles in competition pigeons reduced their effectiveness against Trichomonas gallinae
Authors: M García-Piqueras, R Suarez Lombao, P Pérez Moreno, M Bailén, D Liebhart, M González Clari, MT Gómez-Muñoz, J Sansano-Maestre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrichomonas gallinae is a protozoan parasite of major concern in avian medicine, particularly in domestic pigeons (Columba livia). This study investigated the nitroimidazole susceptibility and T. gallinae occurrence in domesticated pigeons from Eastern Spain, kept for different competitions. A total of 220 pigeons from 11 lofts were sampled and examined by microscopy and culture, revealing 63.6% of infected birds. Genotyping identified genotype C as predominant, with occasional detection of genotype A, mixed A/C infections, and one isolate of Lineage III. In vitro susceptibility testing of 42 isolates showed a high occurrence (45.2%) of elevated metronidazole minimum lethal concentrations (MLC) values (MLC ≥40 μg/mL) ranging from 5 to >100 μg/mL in 9/11 pigeon lofts examined. MLC values were not associated with sex, age, or reproductive status. In contrast, higher IC50 values were observed in isolates obtained from pigeons bred for Pica competitions, and metronidazole exposure was associated with higher MLC values compared with ronidazole. No significant associations were found between in vitro nitroimidazole susceptibility values and environmental or management parameters, although suboptimal hygiene and high bird density were common in lofts with strains with reduced susceptibility to metronidazole. The findings of this study underscore the urgent need to establish evidence-based therapeutic protocols, strengthen biosecurity measures, and promote the development of novel therapeutic alternatives for the control of avian trichomonosis, in order to mitigate the spread of T. gallinae strains resistant to currently available drugs in pigeon populations.

Introduction

The flagellated protozoan Trichomonas gallinae affects both wild and captive birds, leading to the disease known as avian trichomonosis (Quillfeldt et al., 2018). T. gallinae is found in many different avian species, including raptors and columbids as the most studied, with both, symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals and several risk factors influencing the outcome of the infection (Martínez-Herrero et al., 2014). In symptomatic birds, the lesions vary from mild inflammation of the oropharyngeal mucosae to gross caseous masses that occlude the oesophageal lumen leading to difficulties to eat and breath. Severe gross lesions include large necrotic masses affecting in some cases the skull of the birds (Martínez-Herrero et al., 2020), or even invading the eye, brain or internal viscera, causing systemic trichomonosis (reviewed by Amin et al., 2014). Columbidae family members are frequently infected (Marx et al., 2017), highlighting the domestic pigeon (Columba livia) as the main reservoir (Forrester and Foster, 2008). This parasite affects birds globally, including Eurasian collared dove (Streptopelia decaocto), stock dove (Columba oenas), European turtle dove (Streptopelia turtur), and wood pigeon (Columba palumbus), as relevant examples (Forrester and Foster, 2008; Lennon et al., 2013; Villanúa et al., 2006; Hegemann et al., 2007). Additionally, T. gallinae can infect other avian groups, including raptors and various passerine groups (Lawson et al., 2012). Direct disease transmission by different behavioural habits (adults feeding their young, courtship, and predation by birds of prey), or through the uptake of contaminated feed or water has been described. The oral route of infection during nutrition was identified as most common among adults (Lawson et al., 2012; Cole, 1999).

Several parasite genotypes of oral trichomonads in birds have been described since Gerhold et al. (2008) published the first genetic classification in the USA, with two of the described genotypes predominant in Spain (Sansano-Maestre et al., 2009). Genotype C was more frequent in Columbiformes, while genotype A was associated with higher virulence and occurs more frequently in birds of prey (Sansano-Maestre et al., 2009; Martínez-Herrero et al., 2014, 2020). Additionally, infections produced by more than one genotype in the same animal have been reported (Zimre-Grabensteiner et al., 2011; Martínez-Herrero et al., 2019).

The occurrence of T. gallinae infection varies widely depending on the avian species and geographical location, reaching up to 74% in some European studies, with particular emphasis in pigeons from Germany, Spain, Malta, and Italy (Marx et al., 2017). It is also influenced by the diagnosis method employed, as the disease can be confirmed by oropharyngeal swabs observed under the microscope, cultured in TYM medium or amplified by PCR (Martínez-Herrero et al., 2021). During the second half of the 20th century, nitroimidazoles were extensively used in sport pigeons, which contributed to the emergence of drug-resistant strains of the parasite (Franssen and Lumeij, 1992; Munoz et al., 1998). While bacterial drug-resistance mechanisms are well documented, comparable studies in protozoa remain limited (Santos et al., 2020; Caneschi et al., 2023). Nitroimidazole resistance threshold has been proposed for T. vaginalis, varying between aerobic or anaerobic conditions (12.5 in anaerobic vs. 38.8 μg/mL in aerobic conditions) (Narcisi and Secor, 1996; Augostini et al., 2023). Since no established limit for T. gallinae has been established, the T. vaginalis limits have been employed in previous T. gallinae drug-resistance studies (Rouffaer et al., 2014), although Munoz et al. reported a similar MLC values employing aerobic and anaerobic conditions in T. gallinae resistant isolates (Munoz et al., 1998).

Studies in recent years carried out on T. gallinae confirmed the presence of resistance around the world (Rouffaer et al., 2014; Cai et al., 2024).

In Spain, the impact of this parasite has been largely studied in Eastern regions due to the relevance of pigeon breeding for different sport events. Avian trichomonosis is well-recognised by the breeders, and most of them perform preventive or metaphylactic measures against the parasite mostly without veterinary supervision using different drug combinations according to their experience or the advice of other breeders.

In this context, the aim of the present study was to investigate the presence of T. gallinae infection and the in vitro metronidazole susceptibility of isolates obtained from competition pigeons in Eastern Spain, expressed as MLC and IC50 values. The study also explored the relationship between infection occurrence, in vitro susceptibility, and pigeon breeding practices, including housing conditions, treatment protocols, and bird-level variables.

Materials and methods

Sampling: animals and pigeon lofts

The study area included eleven pigeon lofts, four from Valencia province, six from Alicante province, and one Castellón province, all within the Valencian Community in Spain. Letters were previously sent to the owners requesting their collaboration, and all who agreed to it were included in the present work. In this area there are different breeds of pigeons since there are two types of competition: one based on the morphology of the animals (fancy pigeons) and the other, called “Pica”, in which a group of trained males chase a female, guiding her to their pigeon loft (sporting pigeons).

A survey was conducted on the day of sampling to collect information on management measures, bird variables, and administered treatments. Management variables included the number of animals maintained in the loft (≤100 or >100 birds), animal density (birds/square meter), floor type (fix or removable) and cleaning and disinfection frequency (weekly or monthly). From each bird recorded data included age, sex, reproductive status, whether it was in competition at sampling, and breed (morphology or Pica competitions). In cases where the birds were chemotherapeutically treated, the frequency (annual or biannual) of treatments and the drug combination were recorded. To prevent parasitosis, including trichomonosis, breeders routinely use metronidazole (alone or combined with amprolium or mebendazole) and ronidazole alone or combined with amprolium or dimetridazole, in this last case the manufacturer recommended dose of nitroimidazoles was double.

Because loft participation depended on breeders’ voluntary agreement, the study was based on convenience sampling rather than on a probabilistic sampling design. Therefore, no formal sample size calculation was performed.

Culture and preservation of isolates

Sample collection was performed using oropharyngeal and crop samples collected with sterile swabs during clinical examination before inspected microscopically and cultured in TYM medium (Martínez-Herrero et al., 2017). The composition of this medium was as follows: 20 g trypticase (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 10 g D (+)-maltose (Sigma-Aldrich), 10 g yeast extract (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 g L-cysteine (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.1 g ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10% inactivated fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich) per liter. After mixing all the ingredients, the pH was adjusted to 6.5 and sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm filter (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Antibiotics and antimycotic supplements were used to avoid contamination: 24 ml nystatin/l (10,000 IU/ml, Sigma-Aldrich), 0.8 ml enrofloxacin/l (25 mg/ml, Bayer) and 5 ml/l chloramphenicol (100 μg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich) were added. The cultures were incubated at 37°C and daily reviewed under an inverted microscope to observe mobile trophozoites for up to four days. The cultures were passaged every 48 h and after up to 5 serial passages, the isolates with living trichomonads were preserved in 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at −80°C and thawed for subsequent analyses as described below.

DNA extraction

After counting the parasites in a Neubauer chamber in triplicate, a total of 106 trophozoites from each isolate were centrifuged to discard the culture medium and washed twice with PBS (600xg, 5 min each step). The pellet was employed to obtain DNA using the NZYTissue gDNA isolation kit (NZYTech, Lisbon, Portugal) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The concentration of the obtained DNA was measured on a Multiskan GO microplate spectrophotometer with μDrop™ plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and stored at −20°C until use.

PCR of the fragment ITS1/5.8S/ITS2 and sequence analysis

Primers TFR1 (5′- TGCTTCAGCTCAGCGGGTCTTCC-3′) and TFR2 (5′-CGGTAGGTGAACCTGCCGTTGG-3′) were employed for PCR reactions to amplify a fragment of the ITS1/5.8S/ITS2 (Felleisen, 1997). PCR reactions were performed in 25 μL using 12.5 μL of Supreme NZYTaq II 2x Master Mix (NZYTech, Lisbon, Portugal). An initial step of 95°C for 5 min to activate the enzyme followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, annealing at 66°C for 30 s, extension at 72°C for 30 s, and a final elongation step of 72°C for 10 min to allow the elongation of the PCR products were used. The results of PCR amplifications were visualized under UV light on 1% agarose gels stained with SYBR™ safe DNA gel staining (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

The amplicons of the expected size were sent to Macrogen Spain (Madrid, Spain) for Sanger sequencing in forward and reverse directions. The resulting sequences were aligned using Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis v.11 software (MEGA XI) (Tamura et al., 2021) and FinchTV 1.4.0 software (Geospiza, Inc.; Seattle, WA, USA), manually checked, and subjected to BLAST analysis using the nucleotide Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST; National Library of Medicine, Rockville, MD, USA).

Only clear sequences with more than 150 bp in both directions were sent to GenBank for accession numbers.

In vitro evaluation of metronidazole susceptibility

Evaluation of the metabolic activity of trophozoites was performed using the MTT (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) method in 96-well plates with round bottom, following the procedure previously described (Bailén et al., 2022). Briefly, the assay was performed in quadruplicate, after counting the trophozoites as described above, a total of 150 μl/per well of a solution containing 5x105 T. gallinae trophozoites/ml in TYM medium was added to the plate. Routinely, metronidazole was added to each well at different final concentrations, from 40 to 0.1 μg/mL. In cases of high MLC values to metronidazole, concentrations up to 100 μg/mL were also evaluated. As controls, medium and culture of each isolate without metronidazole (Acros Organics, Madrid Spain) were employed. The plate was then sealed with a sterile waterproof and air-tight adhesive lid and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Previous assays comparing this procedure with Vaseline sealed wells under our test conditions offered the same MLC values, facilitating MTT assays and MLC determination at the same time. After sealing and incubation, the plate was centrifuged at 750 x g to eliminate the medium. One hundred μl of a solution containing MTT (1.25 μg/mL) (Acros Organics, Madrid Spain) and PMS (phenazine methosulfate at 0.1 μg/mL, Acros Organics, Madrid Spain) was added to each well and incubated at 37°C to allow formazan salts to form. After 30 min, 50 μl of a HCL-SDS solution (37 μl of 1N HCL in 100 ml of 10% sodium dodecyl sulphate) was added to each well to dissolve formazan salts. After 30 min of incubation at 37°C, the plate was read in a spectrophotometer at 570 nm (Multiskan GO microplate spectrophotometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Determination of MLC value and IC50 value

Two assays were performed for each isolate in quadruplicate to determine MLC and IC50 values. The minimum lethal concentration (MLC) was established at the lowest concentration of the drug that prevents visible movement (a dose immediately higher to the highest dose that shows trophozoite movement). For that purpose, the plates from the MTT assay were observed in an inverted microscope before the addition of MTT solution at 24 h of exposure to metronidazole at the above-mentioned concentrations.

Inhibitory concentration 50 (IC50) was determined as the concentration of metronidazole able to inhibit 50% of the metabolic activity of each isolate. A linear regression analysis (% cell viability on log dose) was calculated with the absorbance values of each drug concentration employing STATGRAPHICS Centurion XIX (https://www.statgraphics.com).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described using absolute and relative frequencies. Mean and standard deviation (SD) were expressed as continuous variables. Variables were classified according to their relationship with management practices in the loft (loft size, animal density, loft modality, floor type, cleaning and disinfection frequency). Age, sex, reproductive status, breed, and whether they were competing at the time of sampling were recorded as intrinsic variables from animals. Finally, variables related to metaphylactic measures recorded included the administration or non-administration of antiparasitic drugs, the frequency of treatments, and the administered drug combination.

To account for clustering of birds within pigeon lofts, the occurrence of T. gallinae infection was analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a logit link and an exchangeable working correlation structure, including pigeon loft as the clustering variable. Results are presented as descriptive comparisons of infection occurrence between groups. Given the non-probabilistic (convenience) sampling design and the exploratory nature of the study, no population-level risk estimates were inferred from these analyses.

For the subset of isolates with in vitro susceptibility data, relationships between categorical predictors and IC50 values were assessed using independent-samples t-tests (for binary variables) or one-way ANOVA (for variables with more than two categories). When ANOVA results were significant, Tukey's post hoc test was applied. Equality of variances was tested using Levene's test, and t-tests not assuming homogeneity of variances were used when appropriate.

All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 20.0. (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics committee approval

Samples were collected under the authorization of the ethics committee approval number PROEX 166.7/21.

Results

Occurrence of T. gallinae in lofts

A total of 220 animals from 11 different lofts were included in the study (Table 1). Most of the lofts were classified as small size (≤100 animals) (68.2%), with removable floor enclosures (59.6%) and with weekly cleaning (56.4%). Bird density varied between 0.42 and 0.99 birds/m2 in seven pigeon lofts and four pigeon lofts with higher densities (1.42, 1.79, 3.01, and 5 birds/m2). Drinking water sources in all pigeon lofts were cleaned or changed without a regular schedule and for this reason this parameter was not included in the analysis. Regarding bird parameters, most of them were males (61.8%), adults (67.7%), not in reproduction (59.1%), bred for Pica competitions (82.3%), and most of them were in competition at sampling (95%). In general, a high variation in preventive measures and administered treatments was observed, without veterinarian supervision. Besides, pigeons did not contact with wild birds, but they may share water sources, especially those of “Pica” competition. New acquired birds were purchased from local breeders from the same region or from other regions where the competition exist (Valencia, Murcia or Andalusia regions), but quarantine measures (isolation of new birds) were not regularly applied in any of the pigeon lofts and if applied, birds were isolated only for 2 to 7 seven days.

Frequency and drug combination refer to animals that received treatments (n = 194).

Concerning therapeutic measures, only two lofts (11.8% of birds, n = 26) reported not using nitroimidazoles against T. gallinae. The combinations of drugs employed by the nine pigeon breeders who treated their animals (n = 194) were: exclusively metronidazole (25.8% of treated birds, n = 50, one loft), metronidazole combined with amprolium and mebendazole (21.7% of treated birds, n = 42, two lofts), ronidazole + dimetridazole (22.7% of treated birds, n = 44, two lofts), and ronidazole + amprolium (29.9% of treated birds, n = 58, four pigeon lofts).

Treatment frequency varied among facilities, with 37.6% of treated birds receiving one treatment per year and 62.4% receiving two or more treatments.

Overall, 140 of 220 swabs from the upper digestive tract were positive by direct microscopic examination, yielding in an occurrence of 63.6%, but some of the positive samples could not be maintained in culture due to poor growth or bacterial or fungal contamination. A total of 42 isolates from 9 pigeon lofts displayed a satisfactory growth and were preserved from these samples for further analysis.

T. gallinae was detected in all pigeon lofts, although the percentage of positives varied depending on the facility, ranging from 42.9% to 100% of the sampled birds (Supplementary Table 1). No clear differences were observed between T. gallinae infection and the evaluated management variables. Hence, the size of the facility (64.0% vs. 62.9%), the type of floor (65.2% vs. 62.6%), and the frequency of cleaning (64.6% vs. 62.9%) did not significantly influence the parasite occurrence. When bird density was categorized as <1, 1–2 and >2 birds/m2, infection proportions ranged from 53.3% to 68.6%, without a clear pattern across density categories.

Similarly, the percentage of T. gallinae infected birds was not significantly altered by other variables analyzed (Table 1). Pigeons participating in morphology competitions had a percentage of infection of 71.8%, while animals that did not participate in competitions at the time of sampling showed a higher percentage, 81.8%, although the small sample size in the latter group limited statistical comparison (n = 11). Young pigeons presented a slightly higher percentage of T. gallinae infection (67.6%) compared to adults (61.7%). Females had a higher infection rate (67.9%) than males (61.0%). Similarly, non-reproductive pigeons showed a slightly higher percentage of T. gallinae infected birds (67.7%) compared to reproductive individuals (57.8%) without a clear pattern (Table 1).

In contrast, drug administration was significantly associated with the proportion of T. gallinae-positive birds in the lofts (p < 0.001). All birds sampled from lofts that did not use anti-Trichomonas treatments were positive (26/26; 100%), compared with 58.8% of birds from treated lofts (114/194).

All nitroimidazole treatments were administered via drinking water. Analyses accounting for clustering of birds within pigeon lofts identified differences in the proportion of infected birds according to treatment regimen and frequency (Table 2).

MLC values of T. gallinae isolates

A total of 42 T. gallinae isolates from nine lofts were evaluated for their in vitro susceptibility to metronidazole using both MLC and IC50 values. MLC values ranged from 5 to >100 μg/mL, while IC50 values ranged from 0.5 to 82.6 μg/mL, reflecting wide variability in susceptibility to metronidazole (Table 3). Twenty one isolates displayed MLC values > 40 μg/mL, while 21 displayed lower values. Among the isolates with high MLC values, 11 were obtained from 4 pigeon lofts with MLC values ≥ 80 μg/ml and IC50 values ranging 11.9-82.6 μg/ml. A positive correlation between the MLC and IC50 values was observed (Spearman's rho = 0.667, p < 0.0001).

Most comparisons between host-related variables (sex, age, reproductive status) or treatment frequency (annual, biannual, untreated) did not yield statistically significant differences in IC50.

In contrast, values differed according to competition modality in exploratory comparisons. Levene's test indicated unequal variances between groups (p = 0.032), therefore the t-test not assuming homogeneity of variances was applied. Isolates obtained from morphology breed pigeons exhibited significantly lower IC50 values (mean = 4.53 μg/mL) than isolates from Pica pigeons (mean = 14.81 μg/mL) (t = −2.669; df = 37.9; p = 0.011).

Regarding treatment type, the one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test revealed significant differences among nitroimidazole regimens. No differences were detected between isolates from untreated pigeons and those treated with ronidazole (p = 1.000). The comparison between untreated pigeons and those treated with metronidazole showed a non-significant trend toward higher IC50 values in the metronidazole group (p = 0.098). The only statistically significant contrast was observed between ronidazole and metronidazole: isolates from pigeons treated with metronidazole displayed significantly higher IC50 values than those from pigeons treated with ronidazole (mean difference = 19.6 μg/mL; p = 0.009).

Overall, competition modality and the specific nitroimidazole administered were the only variables showing statistically significant differences in IC50, whereas all other tested variables showed no statistically significant effect on in vitro susceptibility.

Discussion

The overall occurrence of T. gallinae infection in this study is 63.6%, confirming its widespread presence among domestic pigeons in eastern Spain. This value aligns with prior findings in the Iberian Peninsula, although significant variation exists depending on region, bird population, and diagnostic methodology. In the same geographic area, Sansano-Maestre et al. (2009) reported a prevalence of 44.8% in pigeons by culture in TYM medium, although the sampled birds were not racing pigeons. More broadly, studies across Spain have shown varying prevalence depending on pigeon species and epidemiological context. As examples, Villanúa et al. (2006) reported 34.2% prevalence in wild wood pigeons, considering the presence of lesions together with parasite culture and microscope observation, while Marx et al. (2017) detected 70% prevalence in domestic pigeons across several European countries employing culture and PCR. While culture is particularly useful to obtain isolates, PCR offers a higher sensitivity to detect positives to T. gallinae infection (Martínez-Herrero et al., 2021).

Also, higher flock density of domestic sport pigeons has been identified as a factor associated with increased prevalence compared with wild populations, as reported by Rouffaer et al. (2014). Although loft density varied considerably in the present study, the limited number of facilities and the uneven distribution of birds across density categories did not allow robust assessment of its influence on infection occurrence.

Three of our isolates displayed a mixed genotype, which can be explained by the presence of genetically different clonal isolates present in the same bird (Zimre-Grabensteiner et al., 2011), a common finding as already described in previous prevalence studies (Sansano-Maestre et al., 2009; Alrefaei et al., 2019; Martínez-Herrero et al., 2021).

However, most of the previous studies have not assessed the role of routine treatment protocols or preventive management practices in shaping T. gallinae occurrence in domestic pigeons. In our study, management-related variables, including loft size, bird density, floor type, and cleaning frequency, were not significantly associated with infection occurrence. Similarly, intrinsic bird-level factors such as reproductive status and competition participation did not show significant associations. However, the limitation of the study is its cross-sectional design and the voluntary participation of pigeon breeders, hence, a potential selection bias must be considered. Notably, none of the surveyed lofts operated under veterinary supervision nor control drug dose intake, and none implemented quarantine nor regular cleaning of drinking water sources—factors that likely contribute to the persistence and transmission of T. gallinae within loft environments (Grabensteiner et al., 2010; Sansano-Maestre et al., 2009).

Drug administration was the factor associated with the largest differences in the proportion of T. gallinae-positive birds. All pigeons sampled from lofts without anti-Trichomonas treatment were infected, whereas treated lofts showed markedly lower proportions of positive birds. This confirms previous findings that highlight antiparasitic therapy as a key strategy for controlling T. gallinae transmission in pigeon lofts (Amin et al., 2014). However, when comparisons were adjusted for clustering at loft level, a notable variability was observed among treated lofts depending on the treatment regimen and the frequency of administration. Pigeons treated with a combination of two nitroimidazoles (ronidazole plus dimetridazole) showed lower proportions of T. gallinae-positive birds compared with those treated with metronidazole alone, whereas pigeons treated with a ronidazole combined with amprolium displayed higher proportions of infection. Treatments combining metronidazole with non-nitroimidazole compounds, such as amprolium or mebendazole, showed proportions of infected birds similar to those observed under metronidazole monotherapy. These differences may reflect variations in the intensity of nitroimidazole exposure, treatment compliance, or drug intake variability associated with administration via drinking water.

Treatment frequency was also related to differences in infection occurrence, as lofts applying a single annual treatment showed higher proportions of infected birds compared with those applying two or more treatments per year. Although repeated treatments may contribute to improved short-term infection control, they have been linked to the appearance of nitroimidazole resistance (Lumeij and Zwijnenberg, 1990; Rouffaer et al., 2014; Munoz et al., 1998; Duchatel and Vindevogel, 1998; Gómez-Muñoz et al., 2022). Drug rotation has been proposed as a potential strategy to mitigate resistance development; however, cross-resistance among nitroimidazoles is common in T. gallinae, limiting the effectiveness of this approach (Munoz et al., 1998; Franssen and Lumeij, 1992; Rouffaer et al., 2014; Cai et al., 2024), and no widely approved alternatives exist to treat avian trichomonosis.

In the present study, isolates with MLC values > 40 μg/mL were recovered in 9/11 lofts, mostly from treated birds with nitroimidazoles, either alone or in combination with other drugs such as amprolium and mebendazole. According to Augostini et al. (2023), 50 μg/ml was established as the limit for metronidazole resistance in aerobic conditions, although in a different species, T. vaginalis. Authors working on T. gallinae found isolates with MLC values of 50 μg/ml or higher, employing 5% CO2 and 37°C for the assays, as a common finding in the isolates displaying in vivo resistance to the drugs (Fransen & Lumeij, 1992), while values between 1.9 μg/mL and 7.8 μg/mL were found in sensitive isolates (Munoz et al., 1998). However, four of five strains from untreated animals from two lofts also showed high MLC values, suggesting that reduced susceptibility may persist independently of recent treatment history. Several factors may contribute to the emergence and maintenance of such isolates, including treatment failures, subtherapeutic dosing, or selection of resistant subpopulations following repeated or poorly regulated drug exposure, as suggested in previous studies (Lumeij and Zwijnenberg, 1990). The results from the present survey suggest that all breeders have conducted preventive treatments at some point, although some of them stopped doing so when they did not observe improvements in the performance of their animals. This could have led to the selection of persistent resistant strains in pigeon lofts.

A common feature among all surveyed facilities was the administration of preventive treatments using drinking water. While this route facilitates mass treatment it does not allow for verification of the amount of medication ingested, so underdosing is common, favoring the selection of parasites with reduced susceptibility (Gómez-Muñoz et al., 2022). In addition, changes in therapeutic protocols were frequently implemented without veterinary supervision. Although nitroimidazoles are subject to veterinary prescription in Spain and across the European Union and their preventive use is prohibited under current legislation (Regulation EU, 2019/6), these drugs remain readily accessible through online markets, underscoring the need for improved regulatory enforcement.

Our study showed that Pica animals displayed significant higher IC50 values than Morphology breed animals. Chronic stress, produced by captivity and competitions, may produce significant detrimental effects to individuals, such as increasing the susceptibility, leading to higher parasite loads and infection that could predispose these animals to concentrate these circulant strains, perpetuating the transmission cycle within the lofts (Nordling et al., 1998), but also among wild birds.The ecology of T. gallinae involves multiple sympatric avian hosts. Raptors and scavenger species may acquire infection through predation or scavenging of infected columbids, whereas domestic or exotic birds sharing facilities, water sources, or peri-loft environments may contribute to local parasite circulation. Therefore, control strategies in pigeon lofts should also consider the potential role of other avian species that share or transit through the same environment, particularly in areas where domestic pigeons, wild columbids, raptors, and other captive birds may interact.

No consistent differences in IC50 or MLC values were observed according to treatment frequency. Regarding the drugs employed, metronidazole was associated with higher MLC values compared to ronidazole. This finding was expected since it was the drug employed for the in vitro assays, although cross resistance among nitroimidazoles has been widely reported (Munoz et al., 1998; Franssen and Lumeij, 1992; Rouffaer et al., 2014; Cai et al., 2024). Besides, this drug is by far the most widely used by breeders because it was the most easily accessible in Spain. Anyhow, in recent years, due to online trade, it has been possible to access other nitroimidazoles formulated for pigeons without veterinary supervision. This scenario highlights the need for stricter control and enforcement of current European legislation regulating veterinary medicinal products (Regulation EU, 2019/6).

Molecular genotyping revealed that genotype C predominated among isolates with high MLC values (≥50 μg/mL), but also in isolates with lower values. In Spain, genotype C predominates in domestic pigeons compared with wild birds in previous studies (Sansano-Maestre et al., 2009; Martínez-Herrero et al., 2014). However, three mixed genotypes (A/C) were identified in pigeons from pigeon lofts #1 and #11, two of them with high MLC values (>40 μg/mL). Also, two isolates with high MLC values (≥80 μg/mL) belonged to genotype A (e.g., isolates 276, 277), and one to Lineage III (isolate 228), . This diversity suggests that resistance is not confined to a specific genotype, in contrast to the suggestion made by Cai et al. (2024), who found more resistance associated with genotype A. Our findings agree with the observations made by Rouffaer et al. (2014), who found more frequently resistant isolates with genotype C, which was also the most frequent genotype isolated from pigeons.

However,T. gallinae isolates with lower MLC values (MLC ≤40 μg/mL) were also predominantly classified as genotype C, supporting the notion that genotype alone does not reliably predict susceptibility patterns in this protozoa.

Environmental and husbandry factors were analyzed as factors influencing the dynamics of IC50 values, but no significant results were obtained. Most isolates with high IC50 values were recovered from pigeons housed in lofts with monthly cleaning, solid uralite flooring, and high population densities. Notably, in pigeon loft 9, which yielded isolates with the highest IC50 values, exhibited all these conditions. These findings point to the cumulative impact of suboptimal hygiene, crowding, and drug pressure in fostering resistance development and maintenance.

The growing evidence of nitroimidazole resistance among T. gallinae isolates calls for urgent implementation of improved and integrated control strategies. While nitroimidazoles remain the cornerstone of trichomonosis treatment in birds, their efficacy is increasingly compromised, particularly in settings of chronic exposure, underdosing, poor biosecurity measures, and absence of veterinary supervision. Enhanced hygienic measures are essential for reducing transmission pressure within lofts. Frequent removal of feces, regular disinfection of drinkers and feeders, and minimizing overcrowding can significantly decrease environmental contamination and oral exposure to trophozoites (Amin et al., 2014).

Pharmacological alternatives should be considered. A study from Jing et al. (2023) identified anisomycin, fumagillin, and MG132 (a synthetic peptide aldehyde) as promising drug candidates against T. gallinae. These compounds demonstrated strong in vitro inhibition with low toxicity. Preliminary studies have shown promising activity in vitro of certain essential oils (e.g., Lavandula, Origanum, Thymus, Salvia, and Satureja essential oils) (Bailén et al., 2022) and plant-derived extracts against T. gallinae (Malekifard et al., 2021). Other candidates under exploration include benzimidazole derivatives, silver nanoparticles, and ionophore compounds, which may provide trichomonacidal activity through non-nitroimidazole pathways (De Brum Vieira et al., 2017; Heshmati et al. 2023). However, these agents require rigorous in vivo testing to establish safety, efficacy, and pharmacokinetic profiles in avian hosts.

Based on our findings, we recommend a multimodal approach to control T. gallinae in domestic pigeons bred for sport, This should combine improved hygiene and biosecurity measures, rational use of antiparasitic drugs, periodic in vitro susceptibility monitoring, reinforced veterinary supervision, and stricter regulation of non-prescribed access to nitroimidazoles. These strategies are essential to preserve the efficacy of existing treatments and to curb the spread of resistant T. gallinae strains within and beyond pigeon populations. Further research is necessary to evaluate alternative therapeutic compounds and to establish surveillance systems for monitoring resistance in avian trichomonosis.
