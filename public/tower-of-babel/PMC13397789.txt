A conceptual framework for measuring AI health equity
Authors: Basile Njei, Ulrick Sidney Kanmounye, Luchuo Engelbert Bain, Yazan A. Al-Ajlouni, Olugbenga Ogedegbe, M. Elizabeth Sobhia, Rena C. Patel, Sonia S. Anand, Alan Tita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly embedded in health systems globally and has the potential to improve efficiency, diagnostic accuracy, and decision support. However, its benefits remain unevenly distributed, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Models developed using datasets from specific populations may perform poorly in other settings, reinforcing structural inequities rather than correcting them.ObjectiveThis viewpoint proposes a composite framework, the AI in Healthcare Equity Index (AIHEI), to support measurable assessment of equity in health AI systems.FrameworkThe AIHEI is designed to assess equity across five domains: data representation, algorithmic fairness, transparency and explainability, governance and oversight, and community impact and benefit sharing. By generating a standardised score, the index could enable comparisons across technologies, incentivise improvement, and support regulation, procurement, publication, and funding decisions.ImplicationsPilots across diverse health domains and geographic settings are needed to assess feasibility, refine domain weighting, and evaluate reliability, reproducibility, and validity. Important challenges include contextual definitions of fairness, data sovereignty, post-deployment monitoring, and the risk of metric gaming.ConclusionsQuantifying equity in health AI is essential to ensure that AI does not create, widen, or exacerbate existing disparities by neglecting underserved populations. A common, objective measure of AI-related health equity can help move the field from ethical aspiration toward measurable accountability, monitoring, and enforcement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-026-02950-8.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has made significant strides in the past few years prompting some low- and middle-income country (LMIC) governments to incorporate it into their digital transformation agendas as a means to leapfrog long standing gaps in workforce and infrastructure [1]. In so doing, they join digital health experts who believe AI systems will democratize access to high quality diagnostics and decision support, thereby narrowing health disparities in LMICs [2]. In this viewpoint, we argue that unless action is taken, the opposite is more likely to be true.

We acknowledge that the AI promise for healthcare is real, but the evidence as at the date of this viewpoint suggests a widening gap between North American and European countries and LMICs. Although LMICs bear a significant proportion of the global disease burden and have the potential for the most impact by AI, they currently contribute only a fraction of AI data, research, funding, implementation, and governance [3–6]. This widening disparity is evidenced by uneven performance across populations that are attributable to skewed AI training data that is disproportionately based on English language clinical text and device profiles from tertiary facilities [7, 8]. When such models are transferred to contexts with different disease prevalence, clinical workflows, imaging hardware, or sociodemographic distribution, their errors are not random.

These issues are not only technical defects. They are the predictable consequence of structural factors that shape who is represented in data, who defines success, and who benefits from downstream value. These predictable consequences represent the next great inequity [9]. Unregulated AI risks in LMICs reproduce structural inequalities through concealed algorithmic bias, misdiagnosis, and unequal access to care [10]. Importantly, LMICs bears a disproportionate burden of these issues because local data are scarce, the capacity to perform external validation is limited, and procurement rarely mandates evidence of equitable performance [11, 12]. Imported models are seldom recalibrated for local epidemiology or audited for subgroup error patterns [11, 12].

These inequities remain understudied and the prevailing focus in existing publications has been on principles of responsible or trustworthy AI [6, 10]. These frameworks have raised awareness and inspired better practice in some organisations [10]. Ethical frameworks have crystallised the importance of transparency, accountability, beneficence, and fairness [6]. They have motivated the creation of model cards, risk management processes, and internal review boards in some organisations. Yet the voluntary nature of most guidance limits its ability to protect patients, particularly in regions where regulation is capacity constrained. Without quantifiable metrics, independent audits, and consequences for non-disclosure or poor performance, ethics remains aspirational. A common yardstick with which to assess whether a tool improves or worsens equity in practice is needed to drive action.

Herein, we propose the AIHEI, a composite framework that scores systems across five domains that matter for fairness in practice. We outline the rationale and structure of the index, describe a pathway to implementation through consensus, pilots, and reporting, and discuss the ethical and practical challenges that must be addressed to avoid performative adherence. Validation of the index is not assumed and must be demonstrated through future prospective evaluations. We situate the proposal within existing global guidance and suggest policy levers to move from ethics to enforcement.

Conceptual framework of the AIHEI

At this stage, the AIHEI is a conceptual model derived from theoretical synthesis and expert interpretation. Its domains have face and content validity but no demonstrated construct or criterion validity. The authors acknowledge that testing internal consistency, inter-rater reliability, and performance against independent measures of health equity will be essential to establish the index’s psychometric robustness and policy utility.

AIHEI aggregates five domains (i.e., data representation, algorithmic fairness, transparency and explainability, governance and oversight, and community impact and benefit sharing) that, together, capture where inequities arise and how they can be mitigated (Fig. 1). The five domains of the AIHEI were selected through a synthesis of global ethical frameworks and technical literature that identify where inequities most commonly arise in health AI systems; additional details on the methodological rationale and operational considerations are provided in the Supplemental File eMethods. The indicators shown in Fig. 1 are intended as illustrative examples rather than finalized operational measures. Guidance from the World Health Organization and Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) emphasises transparency, accountability, and fairness as foundational principles, while algorithmic fairness research highlights subgroup performance disparities and the need for measurable parity metrics [13, 14].

Data representation assesses whether training and validation datasets reflect the diversity of age, sex, race or ethnicity, geography, socioeconomic status, and device types that the system will encounter in practice [15]. It also asks whether disease prevalence is aligned with the target setting or explicitly accounted for. It aims to mitigate sample bias and improve generalisability.

Algorithmic fairness evaluates how performance varies across defined subgroups and intersections [16]. It considers differences in area under the curve (AUC), average precision, and calibration across subgroups, as well as parity measures such as equalized odds gaps or disparate impact ratios where appropriate. Its goal is to identify inequitable outputs, determine whether they can be corrected through reweighting, thresholding, or retraining, and ensure that residual trade-offs are explicit and justified.

Transparency and explainability focuses on whether a model is accompanied by documentation that allows third parties to understand its intended use, limitations, and performance [17]. Model cards, data sheets, interpretability analyses, summaries of validation data, and versioned release notes enable reproducibility and trust. Open publication of validation results, including subgroup analyses, supports external scrutiny even when proprietary constraints limit code release.

Governance and oversight assesses the processes that surround development and deployment [18]. Independent ethical review of research, third party audits, regulatory disclosures, and incident reporting mechanisms embed accountability into the development pipeline. In settings with lower regulatory capacity, these processes are essential for protecting patients and for building confidence among clinicians.

Community impact and benefit sharing evaluates whether the AI solution system improves real world outcomes for all groups and whether access to the solution is equitable [19]. This includes monitoring clinical outcomes by subgroup after deployment, measuring uptake and affordability, building local capacity through training and partnerships, and ensuring that data sovereignty and intellectual property arrangements are fair. For LMICs, this domain is critical. It determines whether imported technologies crowd out local innovation or catalyze it, and whether communities that contribute data see tangible benefits.

AIHEI produces domain-specific scores between zero and one that summarize performance across the five domains. These scores should be reported as a five-domain equity profile and may also be accompanied by an illustrative composite score. Reporting domain-specific scores is essential to ensure that poor performance in one area, such as community impact or governance, is not obscured by stronger performance in another. The simplicity of a single score is not a substitute for detailed reporting. Rather, it is a complement that facilitates procurement decisions, regulatory triage, and policy dialogue. It creates a visible incentive to improve that can align developers, payers, and policymakers around a shared objective. Operationalising the index

Implementation can proceed in three stages that build legitimacy and feasibility. The first stage is consensus and framework development. A global consortium convened by multilateral actors such as the World Health Organization and UNESCO, with strong participation from academic partners, regulators, clinicians, patient groups, and developers including from low- and middle-income countries, and funding agencies should define the methodology. A Delphi process can be used to weight indicators within and across domains. Methods should be pre-registered and the framework published openly. Minimum viable metrics can be established for near term use alongside more advanced metrics for mature programmes. Importantly, the inclusion of LMIC stakeholders, including regulators, clinicians, and innovators, will improve relevance and uptake (Table 1).

The second stage is pilot deployment to evaluate health AI solutions. Early application in clinical domains where AI is already common will test feasibility and refine the approach. A worked example illustrates how the index could function. A liver fibrosis system might be scored on training diversity that includes United States cohorts such as the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey dataset, alongside external validation on African cohorts that reflect different prevalence and device profiles [20, 21]. Fairness would be assessed by reporting discrimination and calibration stratified by sex, age, and race or ethnicity, with explicit thresholds for acceptable subgroup differences. Transparency would be demonstrated through a public model card, versioned releases, and clear statements of out of distribution limits. Governance would include a third party audit and public regulatory filings. Community impact would be measured through post deployment monitoring in LMIC teaching hospitals, equity adjusted pricing, and funded training programmes for local clinicians and data scientists. As a purely hypothetical example, such a liver fibrosis AI system might receive domain scores of 0.80 for data representation, 0.75 for algorithmic fairness, 0.90 for transparency and explainability, 0.70 for governance and oversight, and 0.60 for community impact and benefit sharing. Reported as a five-domain equity profile, these scores would show relatively strong transparency but weaker community impact, highlighting the need for improved affordability, local capacity building, and post-deployment outcome monitoring in LMIC settings. If an illustrative equal-weighted composite score were calculated, the overall AIHEI score would be 0.75. However, this composite score should be interpreted alongside the individual domain scores and should not obscure lower performance in any single domain. Beyond pilots, validation and subsequent refining would play a critical role (Table 1).

The third stage is global reporting and incentive alignment. Regulators can require disclosure of index scores as part of pre-market submissions and periodic renewals. Journals can mandate index aligned reporting in line with existing standards for predictive models, making equity analyses a condition for publication. Governments and funders can condition grants on transparency and minimum index thresholds, and can support audits and capacity building for LMIC innovators. Annual public reports can summarize index scores by sector and geography, creating a leaderboard that motivates improvement and helps policymakers identify at-risk areas. Procurement bodies can set threshold scores for purchase and reimbursement, with remediation plans required for lower scoring tools that address critical needs (Table 1).

Discussion

Why the index matters

Metrics catalyze accountability by converting diffuse goals into operational targets. In the absence of a shared measure, aspirations for equity in AI remains a slogan that is difficult to defend in budget discussions and contract negotiations. A composite index offers a common language for all stakeholders including developers, clinicians, regulators, and payers. Developers can prioritise actions that improve the score, such as acquiring more diverse data, performing intersectional audits, and publishing model documentation. Funders can reward proposals that commit to high transparency and robust external validation. Policymakers can focus oversight on sectors or geographies that repeatedly score poorly, and can target resources to address systemic gaps such as missing datasets or audit capacity.

Quantification also enables longitudinal monitoring. As models are retrained and updated, scores can be versioned and tracked. Drops in fairness or transparency can be detected early and addressed. For LMICs facing rapid adoption of digital tools amid constrained regulatory resources, an index can help triage attention and prioritisation. Importantly, quantification does not replace qualitative judgement or community engagement. It complements them by making visible what is otherwise hard to compare.

Ethical and practical challenges

Building and deploying an index that influences procurement and regulation raises legitimate concerns. Equity is contextual. The relative importance of different indicators may vary across societies and health priorities. Weighting must balance global norms with local realities, and there must be a mechanism for local adaptation that does not undermine comparability. Participatory governance that includes patients, clinicians, and public interest advocates from Africa is essential to legitimacy.

Data sovereignty is a central concern. Many LMICs are reluctant to export data that can be used to build proprietary products without fair returns. The index should encourage models that use privacy preserving techniques such as federated learning where appropriate, and it should reward benefit sharing arrangements that return value to data contributors, for example through equitable licencing, capacity building, or shared intellectual property. National authorities will need to craft policies that protect sovereignty while enabling collaborative science.

AI models are retrained as new data become available and as use cases evolve. Equity monitoring must therefore be iterative and version controlled. The index should require versioned scores and change logs that document how updates affect subgroup performance and documentation. Post-market surveillance will need resourcing, especially in settings with limited technical capacity.

The risk of performative adherence is real. We cannot operationalise what we cannot define. A unified understanding of equity, supported by measurable indicators and participatory governance, remains foundational to ensuring that AI in health promotes justice rather than deepening digital divides. Developers may optimize for the index without improving real outcomes, or may selectively report favourable results. Independent audits and public reporting are necessary counterweights. Auditors will need clear standards and independence from vendors. Penalties for non-disclosure, misrepresentation, or failure to remediate should be meaningful. At the same time, the burden of compliance must be proportionate. Small innovators should have access to open-source tools, templates, and subsidized audits to avoid entrenching incumbents.

Privacy and safety must be protected throughout. Index implementation should align with data protection laws and ethical norms in each jurisdiction. Consent processes, de-identification standards, and incident reporting must be robust. Where models fail, there should be clear pathways for clinician feedback, investigation, and correction.

From ethics to enforcement

The index is intended to complement existing global frameworks, not to replace them. The World Health Organization’s guidance on the ethics and governance of AI for health, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development AI principles, and national AI strategies provide normative anchors [13, 14]. By embedding a quantified equity dimension into regulation, publication, and funding, the index can strengthen these frameworks and make them actionable.

Other global initiatives further reinforce the importance of linking equity measurement with patient rights and accountable digital health governance. For example, the Global Patient co-Owned Cloud (GPOC) project proposes a patient co-ownership model for personal health records and has been discussed in relation to ethics, legislation, human rights, privacy, regulation, and AI integration from a global perspective [22, 23]. Similarly, broader governance perspectives, including The AI Ideal, argue that AI should be designed and governed to promote human dignity, justice, democratic accountability, and shared societal benefit [24]. Such initiatives are complementary to AIHEI: while GPOC emphasises patient participation, data sovereignty, and equitable governance of health data, AIHEI focuses on measuring whether AI systems are developed, validated, deployed, and monitored in ways that promote equity. Together, these perspectives highlight that equitable health AI requires not only technical fairness, but also patient and community participation, data rights, regulatory accountability, and measurable real-world impact.

Relationship to existing AI governance and measurement frameworks

The AIHEI is intended to complement, rather than duplicate, existing AI governance and measurement frameworks. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides a broad structure for identifying, measuring, managing, and governing AI risks across sectors, while the Global Index on Responsible AI measures country-level progress toward responsible AI governance across 138 countries and jurisdictions [25, 26]. Regional initiatives are also increasingly important. Africa CDC’s digital transformation and health data governance efforts emphasise interoperable, resilient, and sovereign digital health systems across African Member States [27]. These frameworks provide essential foundations for trustworthy AI, responsible governance, and data stewardship.

AIHEI builds on these efforts by focusing specifically on health AI equity at the level of a technology, model, or deployment context. Its added value lies in combining health-specific domains such as data representation, subgroup fairness, clinical transparency, governance, and community impact into a framework that can be applied to individual AI systems. In this way, AIHEI could be used alongside broader governance tools: NIST AI RMF can guide institutional risk management, the Global Index on Responsible AI can benchmark national responsible AI ecosystems, Africa CDC frameworks can support regional digital health governance and data sovereignty, and AIHEI can provide a more granular equity profile for health AI tools considered for validation, procurement, publication, or deployment.

Conclusion(s)

AI is shaping global health and has a high potential for generational transformation. It is extending specialist expertise, improving diagnostic accuracy, and enhancing decision making worldwide. Without accountability, however, it risks scaling injustice by encoding data blind spots and profile sources. Therefore, equity in AI solutions must become measurable, comparable, and enforceable.

AIHEI can help bridge the gap between ethical principles, aspirations, and practice. By summarizing performance across five crucial domains: data representation, algorithmic fairness, transparency and explainability, governance and oversight, and community impact and benefit sharing, the index offers a pragmatic tool that can be used by all stakeholders. Implemented through inclusive consensus, piloted in real clinical domains, and embedded in reporting and incentives, it can drive action towards equitable AI outcomes. The viewpoint presents a conceptual framework rather than a validated instrument. It offers a descriptive model intended to stimulate discussion and guide early implementation. This index, at present, establishes face and content validity through synthesis of global frameworks and expert consensus, but it has not yet undergone empirical testing for construct, criterion, or predictive validity. Future work should therefore include structured validation in real-world settings, including correlation with independent equity metrics, assessment of reliability and reproducibility, and sensitivity to change over time. The work will be complex and contested, and it must respect local definitions of fairness, data sovereignty, and dynamic learning. These challenges are reasons to build better measurement, not to avoid it.
