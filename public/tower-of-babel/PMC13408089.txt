Breaking continuity to prevent catastrophic building collapse
Authors: Andri Setiawan, Diego Cetina, Maria L. Gerbaudo, Nirvan Makoond, Manuel Buitrago, Jose M. Adam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
It is increasingly difficult to predict all the hazards that buildings will face over their lifetime, highlighting the need to design robust structures that can tolerate unforeseen damage. Typically, robustness is pursued by providing continuity within a structural system. While this strategy performs well in expected scenarios, it increases the risk of catastrophic collapse propagation when initial damage is large. Using high-fidelity simulations, we first identified and characterised the key mechanisms responsible for continuity-enabled collapse propagation in highly continuous building structures such as those made of cast-in-place reinforced concrete. Building on this understanding, we developed: 1) a design approach that employs bespoke force-regulating fuses to interrupt continuity when it becomes detrimental, and 2) an experimental setup that allows system-level collapse propagation to be evaluated by testing only a representative part of a building. Overall, our work shows that continuity can be selectively disengaged even in intrinsically continuous structures and provides a cost-effective way of testing the next generation of solutions against unforeseen extreme events.

Introduction

Building collapses that cause major human and economic losses typically occur when initial component failures propagate extensively within a building’s structural system, a phenomenon known as progressive collapse1,2. Despite periodic updates to building codes that intend to ensure significant safety margins, such catastrophic collapses continue to occur3. To make matters worse, buildings are now facing accelerated degradation4–6 and increasingly intense extreme events7–9. This underscores the need to design robust structures that are insensitive to unforeseen initial damage10.

At present, most strategies to improve robustness rely on providing extensive continuity within the structural system so that loads supported by a failed component can be redistributed to other parts of the structure1,2. These strategies gained prominence after the 1968 Ronan Point incident in London11–13, when a small gas explosion on the 18th floor caused the progressive collapse of the entire corner of a tower block. This disproportionate outcome was attributed to the lack of continuity between the prefabricated panels making up the structure12. Since then, continuity has not only been used to distribute foreseeable loads more efficiently14,15 (thereby reducing total material requirements), but also to create alternative load paths (ALPs) that can prevent collapse after a localised failure. However, in unforeseen extreme scenarios that cause larger initial failures, the same continuity can inadvertently allow collapsing elements to pull down parts of the structure that would otherwise remain upright16–19. This phenomenon exacerbates horizontal collapse propagation, leading to more disastrous consequences. An analogous situation arises when mountain climbers are roped together: the rope may save a climber after a small slip, but in larger groups, the continuity provided by the rope can lead to more disastrous consequences as the fall of a few can drag down the entire team20–22. Such a phenomenon has been observed in several recent catastrophic building collapses23–27. Although these incidents all had different causes (deterioration, overloading, terrorist bombing), they all shared a common characteristic: a local failure triggering a cascading loss of stability across remaining loadbearing elements.

Ideally, a building structure should therefore be able to segment into different parts to isolate a collapse once its initiation becomes inevitable, while still maintaining sufficient continuity to efficiently prevent collapse in all other situations. In this regard, conceptual frameworks have been proposed for defining the geometric layout of segments18 and for performing cost-benefit analyses of segmentation solutions19. Nonetheless, these frameworks rely on the premise that adaptive continuity disengagement can effectively be implemented in building structures, when this has in fact only been achieved in prefabricated systems (by establishing a hierarchy of failures between different component types17). In reality, many building projects opt for structures with a higher degree of distributed continuity, such as those made of cast-in-place reinforced concrete (RC), due to the exceptional versatility and structural efficiency they offer. However, the inherent continuity in these systems makes it much more challenging to design a reliable separation mechanism. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the same dual strategy combining continuity and segmentation is feasible.

In this work, we first use high-fidelity computational simulations of collapse to characterise the key mechanisms that drive uninhibited collapse propagation in building structures with extensive distributed continuity. We then propose a simple implementation strategy to selectively interrupt this continuity in cast-in-place RC buildings using specifically conceived structural fuses. Unlike seismic fuses, which dissipate energy and attenuate sway motion, these fuses enable the segmentation of a building along predefined borders in order to isolate a collapse. Much like electrical fuses that activate only when a current surge exceeds safe levels, this form of segmentation is only activated when collapse propagation would otherwise be inevitable.

Testing the effectiveness of such adaptive continuity disengagement presents an important challenge. Existing experimental setups2,28,29 focus on the development of ALPs and cannot be used to evaluate segmentation solutions. To overcome this limitation without resorting to testing complete buildings, we introduce an original experimental procedure specifically designed to study collapse isolation using a carefully defined subassembly. Finally, we use this procedure to demonstrate the ability of the proposed solution to isolate collapse in extreme scenarios and reveal the required conditions for it to perform as intended.

This study thus proves that collapse propagation can be arrested in highly continuous structural systems using specifically conceived fuses. The proposed strategy complements existing robustness design measures by providing an additional layer of defence against extreme scenarios that are not considered by building codes. In addition, the experimental setup used in this work can capture complex system-level collapse propagation using only a representative part of a building. This setup, which requires significantly less resources than testing a complete building, can also be adapted to investigate more diverse mitigation strategies and collapse propagation scenarios.

Results

Collapse propagation in highly continuous structural systems

Before developing a solution to address continuity-enabled disproportionate collapse, the key mechanisms that enable such occurrences in highly continuous structural systems must first be understood. Although it has notionally been shown that continuity can contribute to collapse propagation16, the underlying mechanisms responsible for this phenomenon have not yet been characterised. To achieve this, we studied a carefully defined building design using collapse simulations based on the Applied Element Method (AEM)30. The specific configuration parameters used in this work were validated against relevant experimental data encompassing all the critical phenomena associated with building collapse16,17,31 (Methods ‘Computational modelling’).

The chosen building design consists of a framed cast-in-place reinforced concrete building structure that is representative of many real construction projects. The structure has three 4 m-high storeys, each made up of 3 × 7 bays with a span of 10 m between columns, which is suitable for multi-functional and office spaces (Methods ‘Building design’; Supplementary Fig. 1). Besides being designed to resist relevant operational loads, the structure can redistribute loads to prevent collapse initiation after the loss of any single column (Supplementary Fig. 2), making it compliant with the strictest robustness requirements currently enforced in building codes32–34.

The most suitable initial failure scenario for studying uninhibited collapse propagation is the smallest one that triggers such a phenomenon18. This is because larger initial failures would cause propagation mechanisms to unravel more quickly and simultaneously, thus masking pertinent differences between them. For this case, this initial failure corresponds to the loss of the ground-floor columns supporting the three central bays of the building (Fig. 1a; Supplementary Movie 1). In this scenario, symmetry causes the same failure propagation pattern to occur in the two identical parts of the building outside the influence area of the initial failure (Fig. 1a–c). The simulation of this critical initial failure scenario revealed that continuity-enabled horizontal collapse propagation essentially happens through a succession of column failures that can either be caused by excessive compression, excessive flexure, or a mixture of both (Fig. 1d, e). Shortly after the initial failure of the columns in rows 4 and 5, the vertical gravity loads they sustained are quickly transferred to the adjacent columns of row 6. The already heavily loaded internal columns of this row (B6 and C6) experience the sharpest increase in compression, which leads to complete brittle failure at their base within 0.8 s of collapse initiation (Fig. 1a, e). The same gravity-load redistribution continues to develop, causing successive compression failures of the columns of row 7, then of the most internal columns of row 8 (Fig. 1b, d, e).

Before the internal columns of row 6 fail, all ground-floor columns exhibit overturning motion driven by the pulling forces transmitted through the floor system. This response is evidenced by: 1) the uniform increase in drift during the early stages of collapse (Fig. 1f), which reflects the rigid-body rotation associated with overturning; 2) the simultaneous rise of tensile and compressive strains at column bases (Fig. 1e), which indicates the formation of plastic hinges to overcome end fixity and enable rotation. Following the compression failures of ground-floor columns, the loss of base restraint also allows upper columns to rotate more freely, accelerating the rate at which they are drifting relative to ground-floor columns (Fig. 1f). The rotation of the initially upright part of the building eventually causes flexural failure of the corner columns furthest away from the initial failure, leaving the structure with no resistance to total collapse (Fig. 1d, e).

Overall, the collapse of this highly continuous structural system is therefore generally characterised by a global overturning mechanism caused by the strong horizontal forces transmitted through floor slabs. However, this global overturning is enabled by the compression failure of the most heavily loaded and highly constrained columns of the system, which occurs predominantly due to the redistribution of gravity loads.

Engineering the segmentation process

In theory, the transmission of destabilising forces can be limited by reducing the continuous reinforcement in the floor system along predefined borders that delimit individual segments within which a collapse can be isolated. Naturally, the building’s segmentation configuration and the location of segment borders must first be established before the continuity reduction can be detailed. For the building presented in the previous section, the structure was divided into the three segments shown in Fig. 2a. This division follows a specifically developed framework18 based on the building’s geometry and on the size of the smallest initial failure that can cause disproportionate collapse (Methods ‘Adaptive continuity design’).Fig. 2Ensuring controlled disconnection.a Location of predefined segment borders. The segment borders were aligned to the shorter building side. They were strategically positioned near the beams’ contraflexure points, where the bending moment due to design loads is close to zero in the original (undamaged) structure. Positioning them at this location ensures that the continuity reduction has a negligible impact on the ability of the floors to resist gravity loads during normal operation. It also keeps the borders sufficiently distant from beam-column joints to avoid interfering with special reinforcement detailing typically used to improve seismic performance. Owing to the low design axial forces and bending moments along the segment borders, slab reinforcement there could be significantly reduced while still satisfying code requirements, which here are governed by provisions to control shrinkage and temperature-induced cracking35. As shown in Fig. 2b, this reduction was implemented in a 1.5 m-wide band along the segment borders. To ensure that beams intersecting a segment border can separate only when required, all their longitudinal rebars were connected with fuses that were specifically designed to limit the transfer of tensile forces between them. These fuses were conceived by modifying and testing commercially available mechanical couplers to meet the requirements of adaptive continuity disengagement (Methods ‘Design and testing of isolated fuses’; Supplementary Figs. 3, 4; Supplementary Movie 2). Unlike conventional couplers, which are designed to be stronger than reinforcing bars, these modified couplers were engineered to fail reliably at a well-defined lower load level (Fig. 2b). After testing different possible options, the chosen fuse consisted of a shortened coupler that consistently failed at a predictable tensile stress level (Fig. 2b). This failure occurred when the few ribs of its internal thread were crushed by the rebars being pulled out on either side of it.

After designing the segment borders, the complete building with an adaptive continuity design was modelled using the same AEM-based strategy used for the simulations presented in the previous section. The fuses in these simulations were modelled using an idealised tri-linear constitutive law that closely matches the stress-strain curve obtained by testing isolated fuses in the laboratory (Fig. 3b). Simulations of the same initial failure scenario that caused the total collapse of the original building design indicate that the segment borders are indeed able to effectively arrest failure propagation and isolate collapse as intended (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Movie 1).Fig. 3Modelling fuses and verifying their ability to isolate a collapse.a Partial collapse predicted by simulation of segmented building. b Tri-linear idealisation used to model fuses and comparison to actual stress-strain curve obtained by experimentally testing isolated fuses. c Comparison of roof drifts (normalised by total building height) predicted by simulations of the original design and of the adaptive continuity design. d Comparison of the total axial force in beams and slabs from collapsing parts (at fuse location) perpendicular to column rows 3 and 6, as predicted by simulations of the original design and of the adaptive continuity design.

The introduction of these fuses along with the reduced slab reinforcement clearly limit the forces that can be transmitted through beams and slabs (Fig. 3d), thus preventing the initially upright parts of the building from overturning (Fig. 3c). The remaining upright parts experience a drift of less than 1% of the building height, indicating that they are unlikely to sustain major structural damage and would likely be safe to occupy14.

Preventing undesirable disengagement

Although the building with adaptive continuity clearly exhibits improved performance when collapse initiation is inevitable, it is important to assess how this structure performs in scenarios for which continuity is known to be advantageous. Most notably, these scenarios include single-column failures and hazardous events that impose considerable lateral forces on the building (e.g. earthquakes or strong winds). For the former, continuity helps redistribute loads supported by the failed column to the rest of the structure, which can prevent collapse initiation. For the latter, continuity allows lateral forces to be distributed more evenly to multiple system components, which generally increases the system’s overall capacity to resist them.

As such, we verified that the adaptive continuity design could withstand the loss of a single column from any possible location, just as the unsegmented building could (Supplementary Fig. 2; Supplementary Movie 3). For almost all single-column removal scenarios, the response of the building with adaptive continuity closely matches that of the original continuous design. This is indicated by negligible differences in the peak vertical displacement and chord rotation of the beams bridging over the lost column (Supplementary Fig. 2). This is because collapse initiation is prevented primarily by the flexural action of the beams and slab, with vertical displacements (of up to 190 mm; Fig. 4a) remaining well below the beam depth (800 mm), a threshold that typically must be exceeded before (purely tensile) catenary forces can develop36. This suggests that the introduction of fuses and the localised band of reduced reinforcement (spanning 1.5 m) only have a limited effect on the flexural response of the beams and slabs over the 10 m or 20 m spans that they must bridge over after the loss of a single column. Only after the loss of the closest column to the fuse location (Fig. 4a), a noteworthy increase of 6 cm in the maximum vertical displacement is registered compared to the response of the original design (Supplementary Fig. 2). Although bottom rebars of the beam are significantly strained at the fuse location after this initial failure (Fig. 4a), stresses in the rebars of the slab remain at low levels. To evaluate performance against lateral loads, we performed nonlinear pushover analyses14 of both the original and the adaptive continuity designs. This was done by imposing monotonically increasing horizontal forces until global system failure occurred (Fig. 4b). This static analysis procedure is appropriate when the effects of higher vibration modes are not significant14, which is the case for the regular and relatively low-rise building subject of this study. The outcome of the pushover analyses revealed that the segment borders had a negligible effect on the ability of the building structure to resist horizontal loads (Fig. 4b), indicating that its performance against earthquakes and strong winds would not be adversely affected.

Designing a subassembly to study collapse performance

Results of the computational simulations of the adaptive continuity design clearly suggest that such systems can effectively isolate collapse in extreme scenarios. It is important to recall that the fuse model used in these analyses was calibrated using tests performed on isolated fuses. However, since such fuses have never been used before, there is no prior experimental evidence on how they interact with the structure in which they are to be incorporated. Since collapse propagation is a systems problem that involves multiple component interactions, it is important to experimentally demonstrate that fuses can indeed enable the segmentation of a structural system before this strategy can be implemented in real construction projects. As previously mentioned, typical laboratory test setups used to study progressive collapse are mostly suitable to evaluate the development of alternative load paths after the loss of a column and cannot be used to evaluate segmentation and collapse isolation2,28,29. While this issue has been addressed by testing a complete building system17, this is a costly endeavour that is not feasible under most circumstances.

As such, this work relies on testing a scaled-down subassembly (Supplementary Movie 4) that was specifically conceived to study collapse propagation and to evaluate if structural systems with adaptive continuity can isolate a collapse. First, the original unsegmented building design subject of this study was scaled down by one third, in order to be able to define a test specimen that fits in the available laboratory space (Supplementary Table 2). The loss of the two central rows of columns from this scaled-down structure was then simulated, revealing that it caused the same failure propagation pattern as in the full-scale building, ultimately leading to total collapse (Supplementary Fig. 5). This confirmed that the scaled-down building could be used as a reference to define a subassembly.

Since the first subsequent failure that triggers collapse propagation in the initially upright part of the building is that of the interior ground-floor columns adjacent to the initial failure (see columns B6 and C6 in Fig. 1), the subassembly specimen to be tested was centred around one of these two symmetrically-positioned columns (Fig. 5a). If the failure of this column is prevented, then the ensuing collapse propagation phenomena cannot occur. For an adaptive continuity building design with the segment borders presented in the previous section, the chosen subassembly setup is thus found at the junction between the collapsing bays and the part of the building expected to remain upright after the initial failure (Fig. 5a). This test setup consists of three important parts: i) two hydraulic jacks to reproduce the maximum forces transferred from the collapsing bays through the floor system (Fig. 5b), ii) the RC specimen incorporating the structural fuses to be tested (Fig. 5c), and iii) an auxiliary restraining system designed to replicate the stiffness of the remaining building (Fig. 5d).Fig. 5Subassembly specimen and test setup.a Schematic representation of subassembly test setup with overlay colours indicating how different parts of the setup are associated to different parts of the partially collapsing building. b Vertical and inclined hydraulic jacks used to reproduce destabilising loads transferred by collapsing bays to the rest of the building. c Monolithic reinforced concrete specimen including a column that corresponds to an internal one in the segmentation zone. d Auxiliary elements whose stiffness has been designed to match the level of constraint imposed by the rest of the building on the column.

The RC specimen itself consists of an internal column and part of the surrounding beams and slabs of the first floor (Fig. 5c). The column extends over the full height of the ground floor plus half the clear height of the first floor. Its topmost part served as one of the connection points to an auxiliary restraining system able to impose realistic boundary conditions on the specimen (Fig. 5d). Perpendicular to the collapse direction, the beams and slab extend to the contraflexure points. This effective slab width transfers most of the unbalanced forces to the internal column during collapse. Parallel to the collapse direction, the beams and slab extend to slightly more than half of the span between columns. This length was chosen so that a vertical displacement can be imposed at the specific location that accurately reproduces the internal forces in the beams and columns close to the segment border (Fig. 6). These forces were derived from dynamic collapse simulations of the complete building. The rebars of the beam on the collapsing side were connected by fuses at the segment border location (Fig. 5a). Uniform steel meshes were used in the top and bottom of the slab, with a reinforcement ratio that adequately replicates the response of the building with adaptive continuity thanks to the reduced width of the specimen. During the reference collapse scenario, beams and slabs adjacent to the initial failure undergo two stages of response that are responsible for transferring loads to the interior column represented in the subassembly. Initially, they respond to the loss of support through flexural action. Eventually, as the floor system cannot contain its fall and the vertical displacement increases, this flexural action transitions to catenary action in which purely tensile forces are transmitted through the floor system. To reproduce these different stages of load transfer through the floor system, a phased quasi-static loading protocol was defined for the two hydraulic jacks of the test setup. After prestressing the column to subject it to the same initial axial load as imposed by upper floors in the complete building (Phase 0 in Fig. 6a), the flexural response stage was simulated by increasing the vertical displacement at the vertical jack location (Phase 1 in Fig. 6a). Since the development of tensile forces during the transition to catenary action depends on a beam’s deformed shape, Phase 2 of the loading protocol involved synchronously increasing both the displacement imposed by the vertical hydraulic jack and the pulling force imposed by the inclined one (Fig. 6a). Finally, pure catenary forces were reproduced in Phase 3 using only the pulling forces from the inclined hydraulic jack (Fig. 6a).

To ensure that the lateral stiffness of the auxiliary restraining system accurately represents the stiffness of the remaining upright building, the column straining actions from a simulation of the subassembly test were verified against those from the collapse simulation of the complete scaled-down unsegmented building (Fig. 6b). These simulations were based on the finite element method (FEM) and employed simplified line elements, with nonlinear material behaviour modelled using distributed fibre hinges. Comparisons with experimental tests37 have already demonstrated that the specific configuration used can accurately represent the structural response of RC structures under large displacements before element separation occurs (Methods ‘Computational modelling’). The simplified FEM simulations could be executed quickly, which allowed designing the auxiliary restraining system through an iterative procedure in which different combinations of steel profiles were evaluated. The unsegmented building was used as a reference for this procedure since it allows observing the complete collapse process. Ultimately, the chosen auxiliary restraining system was built with two HEB220 steel profiles, since this resulted in the best agreement of internal forces between simulations of the subassembly and of the complete building (Fig. 6b). It was also verified that this setup provides an accurate representation of the internal forces at the fuse location (Supplementary Fig. 6). The good agreement between the maximum internal forces and drifts of the complete building and those of the subassembly (Fig. 6b) demonstrate that the proposed test setup is able to accurately account for the relevant system-level effects that play a decisive role in whether a collapse will propagate or not.

Segmentation to prevent collapse propagation

Two identical RC specimens incorporating structural fuses were tested in the laboratory (Fig. 7a–g). The first test used the auxiliary restraining system that was calibrated to replicate the stiffness of a complete RC building. In the second test, the lateral-restraint stiffness was deliberately reduced to evaluate the importance of accurately representing system effects when testing the performance of segmentation strategies that aim to arrest collapse propagation. The level of lateral restraint used for this second test thus represents a more flexible remaining structure, as may occur in buildings with less bays or in hybrid construction where part of the building is formed with more deformable steel frames. In the first test, controlled disconnection occurred as expected (Fig. 7a; Supplementary Movie 5). Prior to fuse activation, as was the case in the building simulation, the pulling forces cause a notable drift at the top of the column (Fig. 7e), inducing significant rotation at the base of the interior column (Fig. 7f). This rotation does create some noticeable damage at the base of the column (Fig. 7c), which indicates the onset of plastic hinge formation. However, before this damage can evolve any further, the fuses fail (Fig. 7d), causing the floor system to separate (Supplementary Movie 5). This interrupts the transmission of unbalanced forces and prevents any yielding from occurring at the base of the column (Fig. 7g). It is important to highlight that the rebars connected through fuses were only able to slip out once a sufficiently wide crack formed along the fuse plane (Fig. 7c). Such cracking could only occur once the slab reinforcement began to yield along the corresponding plane, which highlights the importance of the interaction between slab reinforcement and the activation of fuses. This interaction is in fact responsible for preventing undesirable and excessively brittle failure of the fuses at smaller deformation stages. As a result, the structure has enough ductility to accommodate a relative column drift of 2% before fuse-activation. This explains why previous simulation results indicate that the fuses would have a negligible effect on the building’s capacity to resist lateral loads from earthquakes and strong winds.

Initially, the response of the specimen of the second test was almost identical during the floor system’s flexural response stage (Phase 1 in Fig. 7f, g). However, due to the more flexible lateral restraint, the specimen of Test 2 responded to the pulling forces imposed in Phase 2 mainly through column rotation (Fig. 7f). Unlike in the specimen of Test 1, where significant elongation of the floor created distributed cracks along its length that eventually allowed the fuse to activate, most cracking in the specimen of Test 2 was concentrated around the beam-column interface (Fig. 7d; Supplementary Figs. 8–10). This prevented the fuse from activating, resulting in much larger column drifts (Fig. 7e) and increasingly severe damage at the base of the column (Fig. 7c, d, f, g; Supplementary Figs. 11, 12). Significant damage could also be observed at the beam-column interface, even on the side furthest way from the initial collapse (Fig. 7d). Eventually, the column drifted to more than 5% of the storey height, exceeding the threshold specified for the collapse-prevention performance level in relevant guidelines14, and indicating that the collapse of this column was imminent (Supplementary Movie 6). In a real collapse, failure propagation would thus have been able to continue in this case, highlighting the importance of considering how the structural system will interact with fuses when assessing their performance.

Overall, the test results revealed that reliable activation of fuses placed in beams to isolate a collapse depends greatly on how damage and cracking are allowed to develop in floor slabs. In fact, we show that slab reinforcement and beams incorporating fuses can be synergistically designed to provide the structure with sufficient ductility to face lateral loads, while still segmenting reliably when necessary. Finally, we also demonstrate that it is crucial to adequately consider relevant effects from the entire structural system when designing for collapse isolation.

Exploration of key design parameters

While the test results confirm that controlled separation during a collapse can be achieved in intrinsically continuous structures, the safe implementation of this strategy requires a comprehensive understanding on the effects of key design parameters on system performance. Regarding the position of the reduced-reinforcement band, moving the band too far away from the adjacent line of columns can effectively impede the activation of the fuses. This is due to the natural distribution of bending moments that develops after the loss of the columns (Fig. 8a). As shown, the lower hogging moments further away from the column line (at 3.35 m) induce much lower stresses within the reduced-reinforcement band. Consequently, excessive unbalanced loads can still be transmitted across the segment border without rupturing the reinforcement in the band, thus triggering the failure of columns outside the influence area of the initial failure. As a result, the same global overturning mechanism observed in the original continuous building can still occur (Fig. 8b).

The second key design parameter is the reinforcement ratio in the slab along the segment border. As expected, the lower the reinforcement ratio, the lower the maximum drift and damage experienced by the segments that remain upright after collapse (Fig. 8b, c). For the specific building studied here, the change in structural response when reducing this reinforcement ratio from 0.25% to 0.13% is relatively modest. However, it is important to note that simply reducing the original slab reinforcement ratio (1.11%) to 0.50% in the band (instead of 0.25%) would result in total collapse under the critical initial failure scenario.

Finally, the third key design parameter influencing system response is the design of the columns in the entire building. Stronger columns provide greater load-resisting capacity and therefore require larger unbalanced loads to be transferred through the floor system in order to bring down the entire remaining structure. In this case, collapse isolation can even be achieved without any reduced-reinforcement band by using stronger columns with an axial load capacity 1.5 times that of the original design (Fig. 8a–c). However, relying exclusively on stronger columns for collapse isolation is clearly not efficient, since this strategy requires substantially more material and leads to a design with significantly higher embodied carbon (Fig. 8d). In addition to the greater resource usage, the drift and damage levels that would be expected in the upright part of the structure are in fact much higher, making it difficult to ensure life safety during an extreme event (according to performance level thresholds proposed by the Federal Emergency Management Agency of the United States14).

Overall, our analysis of different design options shows that collapse isolation is achieved when the maximum external work that floor systems transfer to each building segment is lower than the maximum work that each segment can resist. Here, external work is defined as the product of horizontal forces and horizontal displacements; it represents the demand on each segment and is mainly controlled by the size of collapsing parts, the amount of reinforcement in the floor system (and the position of the reduced-reinforcement band). The corresponding capacity, which is typically governed by the size of columns, can thus be estimated using a single pushover analysis in which monotonically increasing horizontal displacements are applied to the isolated building segment (Fig. 8e).

Discussion

In this work, we developed a complete solution that uses force-regulating fuses to isolate collapse in cast-in-place reinforced concrete buildings, which are characterised by a high degree of distributed continuity. Despite the challenges of segmenting highly continuous structures during extreme scenarios, the solution provides reliable control over the separation process without undermining the capacity of the structure to resist lateral loads such as those caused by earthquakes or strong winds. This indicates that the solution can potentially be used to improve robustness in a broad range of geographical and operational contexts.

However, it is important to recall that adaptive-continuity design is engineered to arrest horizontal collapse propagation, and that its efficacy for mitigating other types of propagation (e.g. vertical pancake collapse) is yet to be explored. Moreover, the precise magnitude of forces that trigger collapse propagation and the performance of specific segmentation solutions are inevitably case-specific, as they depend on building type, geometry, and loading conditions. In exceptional cases where non-activation of fuses must be ensured under blast, impact, or severe cyclic loading near the fuse location, accurate analysis would also require additional characterisation of fuse behaviour under high strain rates and cyclic loading. Nevertheless, the general design approach and the uncovered trends regarding the effect of key design parameters on performance are universal.

We also introduced a subassembly testing procedure that allows collapse scenarios to be investigated experimentally using equipment already available in many structural laboratories around the world. By providing a less costly alternative to tests on complete building structures, this procedure promotes greater sustainability, accessibility, and reproducibility in future research on collapse propagation.

Ultimately, these findings enable the development of different solutions to prevent unimpeded collapse propagation, helping to create resource-efficient buildings with enhanced resilience against unforeseeable extreme events.

Methods

Building design

The building design studied in this work consists of a three-storey reinforced concrete (RC) moment frame system, comprising 3 × 7 bays with a 10 m span and a 4 m floor-to-floor height (Supplementary Fig. 1). It is a low-rise, multi-functional office structure with a large floor area and long-span beams. The building is located in a low seismic risk region, with a PGA (peak ground acceleration) value of 0.086 g and type C soil classification. The wind design was performed considering a basic wind speed of 29 m/s. The general design of the structural elements under serviceability and ultimate limit states (SLS and ULS) was performed in accordance with EN 1992-1-135. The robustness design for extreme scenarios consisted of ensuring that the prescriptive tying force requirements of UFC 4-023-0332 were fulfilled before explicitly verifying that the structure could withstand notional element removal in accordance with EN 1991-1-734 and UFC 4-023-0332. The additional dead load (DL) and imposed load (IL) were taken as 2 and 3 kN/m2 respectively (EN 1991-1-138). The resulting dimensions and reinforcement configurations of the beams, columns, and slabs are summarised in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1.

Computational modelling

Two different modelling strategies were employed in this study: the Applied Element Method (AEM) and the Finite Element Method (FEM). The AEM was used to simulate the dynamic collapse of complete building systems, because it can capture large displacements, cracking, element separation, and debris collision at reasonable computational expense30,39,40. The FEM was primarily applied to design the boundary conditions of the subassembly tests, since it can rely on efficient simplified element formulations to accurately reproduce the evolution of internal forces up to element separation, even under large displacements41.

Collapse simulations using the Applied Element Method (AEM)

All AEM analyses were performed using the Extreme Loading for Structures (ELS) software42. The method relies on discretising the structure into small rigid body blocks that are interconnected using multiple normal and shear springs. Every element has three translational and three rotational degrees of freedom. The Maekawa concrete model represents the compression behaviour of the concrete43 that is constructed based on three primary parameters: the initial elastic modulus, the fracture energy, and the compressive plastic strain. In tension, the spring stiffness is assumed to follow a linear response governed by the initial elastic modulus up to the cracking point. The shear response is similarly modelled as linear until concrete cracks. The interaction between normal compressive and shear stresses is described using the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion. Beyond the peak, shear stress is assumed to reduce to a residual level governed by aggregate interlock and friction along the cracked surface. In contrast, under tensile loading, both normal and shear stresses are reduced to zero immediately after cracking (simulating a brittle failure). The steel reinforcement bars were modelled as discrete spring elements characterised by three force components: a normal spring representing axial forces along the bar axis, and two orthogonal shear springs simulating dowel action. The constitutive behaviour of the reinforcement was defined in three distinct phases: linear-elastic, yield plateau, and strain hardening. A failure criterion based on tensile fracture energy was adopted to control the fracture behaviour of the reinforcement bars in the post-peak region. Detailed information on the adopted parameters for the AEM simulations is reported in Supplementary Tables 3–5.

Two specific treatments were adopted when modelling the slab elements. First, a thin section correction was used to prevent the “shear-locking” phenomenon when modelling slab elements with a relatively large mesh size44. Second, a bond deterioration zone equal to ten times the diameter of the bar in the slab45 was adopted to reduce the rebar shear stiffness to account for local flexural deformation.

8-node hexahedral (cube) elements were used to mesh the entire building structure. To balance the simulation accuracy and computation time, we adopted an average element size of 200 mm, resulting in a mesh of ~95,000 elements for the entire building model. We adopted 5 × 5 sets of matrix springs on every face of each cube. Each spring set consists of one normal and two shear springs. The chosen mesh size and spring configurations have been proven to produce accurate results, as demonstrated by comparisons with experimental results16,17,31.

Since compressive failures, cracking, and rupture of reinforcing bars are critical to accurately represent collapse propagation and segmentation, validation was based on experimental tests in which these phenomena were observed. Except for fracture energy, most primary parameters governing the concrete and steel constitutive models can be taken directly as mean values from material characterisation tests (Supplementary Tables 3, 4). Fracture energy, however, is typically determined indirectly. For concrete, the compressive fracture energy was estimated from the compressive strength using the empirical relationship proposed by Nakamura et al.46, whereas the tensile fracture energy was set to 0.14 N/mm based on the findings of Lee and Lopez47. For steel, both the tensile and compressive fracture energies were set to 2460 N/mm based on the experiments reported by Stampfl and Kolednik48. The resulting simulations showed good agreement with the experiments in terms of dynamic force redistribution, deformation, cracking, rebar rupture, and debris acceleration after separation16,17,31, demonstrating the validity of the adopted models and fracture criteria for collapse modelling.

Simplified simulations using the Finite Element Method (FEM)

To design the boundary conditions for the subassembly tests, we adopted a simpler modelling strategy based on the finite element method (FEM), where the column and beam (including the slab) were modelled as 2D line elements. We carried out all the analyses of the subassembly using the CSI SAP2000 structural analysis software49. We employed the distributed-fibre approach, which considers both material and geometric nonlinearities. The slab is modelled as a single element with the beam as a T-beam section using the “section designer” feature. The auxiliary restraining frames, comprising steel profiles and high-strength bars, are represented as linear elements (line and truss elements, respectively). We adopted Mander’s confined concrete model50 for the beam and column, and the unconfined model for the slab. The constitutive model of the rebar was represented using a multi-linear stress-strain relationship that explicitly accounts for yielding, strain hardening, and rupture. The fibre discretisation for the T-beam cross-section was 5 × 30 (width × height), and for the column was 20 × 20. Within the length of the element, we had 20 hinges along the beam and 10 hinges for the column. Detailed information regarding the adopted parameters and the numerical solver for the FEM simulations is reported in Supplementary Tables 6, 7.

Validation of the FEM model configuration also relied on comparisons with experimental tests in which compressive failure, cracking, and rebar rupture were observed37. For FEM simulations, all primary parameters governing the concrete and steel constitutive models are taken directly from material characterisation tests (Supplementary Tables 6, 7). In this case, only the number of fibres and hinges used to discretise columns and beams were defined based on the ability of the validation simulations37 to reproduce experimental response in terms of force redistribution, deformation, cracking, and rebar fracture.

Design and testing of isolated fuses

Designing fuses

Structural fuses are integrated into the beams to provide reliable force transfer under operational loading conditions, while enabling controlled separation to isolate collapse. After evaluating key design criteria (including reliability, compatibility with other hazard requirements, constructability, and cost-effectiveness), we selected a modified mechanical rebar coupler as our fuse prototype. Traditionally, rebar couplers are designed to be stronger than the bars they connect, ensuring the failure occurs elsewhere51–53. In contrast, to realise the fuse concept, the opposite criterion is needed: the coupler must be engineered to be weaker than the rebar and to fail at a selected load level. This is necessary to ensure that failure occurs precisely at the fuse location and at a specific load magnitude.

To achieve this objective, we explored three potential fuse designs (Supplementary Fig. 3) by: 1) creating two symmetric holes with a specific opening size (series H), 2) creating grooves, “peeling” the skin of the couplers with a specific groove depth (series G), and 3) shortening the coupler length (series S). We hypothesised that performing modifications 1 and 2 would reduce the effective cross-sectional area of the coupler, leading to reduced fracture capacity and forcing the fracture process to occur at the weakened plane. On the other hand, shortening the coupler would reduce the engagement length between the rebar and the coupler, thereby promoting the breakage of the ribs (bond) that led to a slip-type failure. For each modification, we varied the key parameters that would affect the fuse’s capacity. For example, for series H and G, we varied the size of the holes and the depth of the groove, respectively. On the other hand, for series S, we varied the coupler length to promote different slip capacities. More specimens of this type (series S) were tested since preliminary results indicated that they perform better. In total, we tested 45 specimens consisting of:3 continuous reinforcement bars.3 specimens with original/non-modified couplers (“Original” series).9 specimens with two symmetric holes (Series H).9 specimens with grooves (Series G).21 specimens with shortened couplers (Series S).

Adaptive continuity design

According to a specifically conceived framework to define segment border locations18, a suitable segment configuration should be determined based on relevant structural and geometric criteria of the building. Following the first pathway of this framework, we used high-fidelity simulations to analyse the collapse response of the investigated building by subjecting it to numerous initial failure scenarios. A critical initial damage scenario (CIDS) that led to total building collapse was identified. It involved the complete failure of the two lines of columns in grids 4 and 5 at the ground level (Supplementary Fig. 1). According to this CIDS, we divided the building into three segments: an internal segment and two symmetrical external segments on each side. The internal segment consists of all columns located in grids 4 and 5, whereas the rest of the columns are considered part of the external segments. Between these segments, pre-defined fracture planes were incorporated by introducing the fuse element in the beam and reducing the reinforcing bars in the slab. The fuse and the centreline of the slab band were positioned around the zero bending moment line. This position is located 2 m away from the column face and ensures that the stress level is sufficiently low during operational conditions to prevent premature segmentation. For the band of reduced reinforcement in the slab, we adopted a configuration of Φ10–250 mm on each face of the slab (corresponding to 322 mm2/m per slab face), which is greater than the minimum reinforcement ratio allowed by EN 1992-135. Regarding the fuse installed in the beam, we used the shortest coupler from the series S (about 20 mm length) with a maximum (peak) force of 12 kN (average of three specimens tested experimentally; Supplementary Fig. 4). Figure 2 indicates the position of the fuse element that we adopted in our computational analyses. To ensure that the implementation of the adaptive continuity design fully complies with other requirements of building standards, we conducted two additional checks: 1) a robustness check (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Movie 3) involving notional column removals to evaluate the ability of the adaptive continuity design to develop alternative load paths32; 2) a lateral stability check involving pushover analysis to evaluate the capacity of the building to resist lateral loads imposed by wind and earthquakes (Fig. 4).

Construction and testing of subassembly specimens

Construction of specimen and test setup

Supplementary Fig. 7 shows the construction stages of the subassembly specimen and the developed test setup. We started the work by assembling the reinforcement cages of the beams and columns. Simultaneously, we installed strain gauges in critical locations in these rebars. Once the reinforcement cage was ready, we prepared the formwork for concrete casting. The casting was done in three separate phases. We cast the ground floor column and the foundation in the first phase. Then, we cast the beam and floor slab simultaneously (the installation of the slab reinforcement bars was done just before this second casting stage). Lastly, we cast the remaining part of the column located above the slab (first-floor column). While waiting for the concrete to mature, which takes 28 days, we worked in parallel on assembling the setup. This involved constructing the auxiliary restraining system, made of HEB220 steel profiles, and the reaction frames for the vertical hydraulic jack and the column prestressing system. Once the subassembly specimen was ready, we connected it to the setup. This process involves connecting the beam and slab to the short steel column, attaching the top end of the column to the horizontal bars and the prestressing system, and finally connecting the vertical and inclined jacks to the specimen. The final step was installing all the measuring devices to monitor the specimens during the experimental test (Supplementary Fig. 7). Properties of all materials used to construct the subassemblies were characterised according to standard test procedures and are reported in Supplementary Table 8.
