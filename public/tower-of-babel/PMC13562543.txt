Numerical investigation on aerodynamic characteristics of a tiltrotor wing with serrated Gurney flap
Authors: Hao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA novel serrated Gurney flap (SGF) is presented in this work, and its effects on the aerodynamic performance of a tiltrotor wing are analyzed using computational fluid dynamics techniques. The Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes equations are employed to simulate the flow structure around the SGF using the Spalart-Allmaras turbulence model. The results show that the SGF improves aerodynamic performance compared with the baseline wing. Although the lift coefficient of the SGF is decreased compared with the plain Gurney flap (PGF), the lift-to-drag ratio is increased. Further research reveals that increasing the serration height causes greater decreases in lift, drag, and nose-down pitching moment coefficients, whereas serration width affects these coefficients very little. It is demonstrated that the most important factor for SGF is its windward area.Keywords: Serrated Gurney flap, Flow control, Aerodynamic performance, Numerical simulationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The tiltrotor aircraft is a special aerial vehicle that combines the vertical take-off and landing capabilities of a helicopter with the high-speed cruising performance of a conventional fixed-wing aircraft. As a unique configuration, tiltrotor aircraft also have serious aerodynamic problems. During the conversion maneuver, the rotors that provide lift in helicopter mode gradually transition to produce thrust in airplane mode. If the wing-generated lift is insufficient to overcome gravity, the aircraft may experience a fatal accident1. Therefore, designing effective wing lift-enhancement devices is a crucial challenge for the aerodynamics of tiltrotor aircraft.

Various flow control techniques have been studied to enhance the aerodynamic performance of wings, among which the Gurney flap (GF) is widely used due to its simple structure and cost-effectiveness. The GF is a simple flat plate installed at or near the trailing edge of an airfoil. It is named after the race car driver Dan Gurney, who first introduced the GF to increase the car’s downforce and stability during high-speed cornering. Since the work done by Liebeck2 in 1978, a great amount of research has been conducted to assess the feasibility and efficiency of GF in improving the aerodynamic performance of airfoils. Different experiments have been carried out by Embry Riddle Aeronautical University3,4, Beihang University5,6, and other institutions7,8. Abundant measured data have been obtained, providing significant support for the validation and evaluation of the aerodynamic characteristics of the GF configuration. In the aspect of numerical simulations, Singh et al.9 developed an in-house flow solver to predict the flowfield around NACA 0011 and NACA 4412 airfoils equipped with GF. They concluded that the GF increases the effective camber of the airfoil. Similar finding was reported for the NACA 4418 airfoil by Ouchene et al.10, for the S809 airfoil by Jerez et al.11, and for the NACA 23,112 airfoil by Chinnappa et al.12. He et al.13 used a commercial computational fluid dynamics (CFD) software to compute the flowfield around an SFYT15thick airfoil with GF. The results showed that the Kutta condition at the trailing edge changes when a GF is deployed. Genest et al.14carried out a CFD analysis of a symmetrical NACA 0015 airfoil equipped with single and double GFs, confirming that the optimal GF height should be in the order of 1–2% of the airfoil chord. A comprehensive review of various GF applications can be found in Refs15,16..

In our previous work17–19, the impacts of GF parameters, including height, location, angle, and thickness, on the tiltrotor wing were systematically investigated in detail through numerical simulation. The results indicated that although GF could enhance the lift of the wing, it also induced a drag penalty and produced an additional nose-down pitching moment. To overcome these shortcomings, a serrated Gurney flap (SGF) is proposed in this work by transforming the traditional plain Gurney flap (PGF) into a triangular serrated structure. Although the SGF is not a novel concept20,21, few studies have investigated its application to tiltrotor wings. The organization of this paper is as follows. Geometry model and mesh generation

The chosen wing airfoil was developed at Bell Helicopter specifically for use on the V-22 tiltrotor wing, and is designated the A82120122. The chord length of the wing (c) is 1000 mm, and the spanwise length (b) is 120 mm. The triangular serrations are selected for the current study due to their excellent noise-reduction performance20. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the configuration of the GF is determined by four parameters: height h, thickness δ, serration height hs, and serration width ws.

Diagram of different models. (a) Baseline, (b) PGF, (c) SGF.

The computational domain was meshed using hexahedral structured grids, and the outer boundary was located 30 chords from the wing surface. The height of the first cell layer on the wall surface was 1 × 10− 6c, and the grid spacing is clustered in the vicinity of the GF to accurately capture the flow physics of the GF, as shown in Fig. 2. The wing surface and the GF have been defined as no-slip walls. Two sides of the span are set as the symmetry boundary. At the farfield boundary, the characteristic-based non-reflecting boundary condition is applied.

Computational mesh. (a) Wing and symmetry plane mesh, (b) Mesh in the vicinity of the SGF.

Validation

The first validation case is an A821201 infinite straight wing. The calculation was performed at a Mach number of 0.13 and at an angle of attack of 7° with a Reynolds number of 2.0 × 106. As shown in Fig. 3, the computed pressure distributions closely match the wind-tunnel experiment values26.

Comparison of pressure distributions of an A821201 infinite straight wing.

Since no experimental data are available for the A821201 airfoil equipped with GF, the current numerical model is validated against experimental data for the NACA 0012 airfoil. The model has an aspect ratio of 1.6, and the simulation flow conditions are the same as those of the wind-tunnel test5. As shown in Fig. 4, the numerical results show good agreement with the experimental data where the average error for lift is 4.3% and the average error for drag is 4.7%.

Effects of serration height

Figure 5 depicts the aerodynamic force coefficients of the tiltrotor wing without and with different GFs. Compared with the baseline clean configuration, the application of PGF and SGF clearly enhances lift, drag, and nose-down pitching moment. Notably, the areas of SGF1 and SGF2 are both smaller than that of the PGF, resulting in a decrease in these coefficients. This reduction becomes more pronounced as the serration height increases. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the maximum lift coefficient is improved by 41.69, 35.21, and 27.39% for PGF, SGF1, and SGF2, respectively, when compared to the baseline wing. Note that the stall angles of the controlled configurations remain unchanged (). The results indicate that the presence of GF increases the airfoil’s effective camber. Figure 5(b) shows the drag coefficient as a function of the angle of attack, and the drag polar curve is shown in Fig. 5(c). It is quite clear that there is a drag penalty associated with the lift increment. The SGF reduces the drag coefficient compared with that of the PGF, but it is still larger than that of the baseline wing. At the stall angle of 10°, the drag coefficient is increased by 40.34, 32.22, and 25.07% for PGF, SGF1, and SGF2, respectively.

Influence of serration height on aerodynamic coefficients. (a) Lift coefficient versus angle of attack, (b) Drag coefficient versus angle of attack, (c) Lift coefficient versus drag coefficient, (d) Lift-to-drag ratio versus angle of attack, (e) Lift-to-drag ratio versus lift coefficient, (f) Nose-down pitching moment versus angle of attack.

Figure 5(d) and Fig. 5(e) present the lift-to-drag ratio characteristics. With the implementation of GF, an improvement in the lift-to-drag ratio is possible in the region before the stall angle. Although the SGF results in a lower lift than that of the PGF, it increases the lift-to-drag ratio when the angle of attack varies from 2 ~ 10°. When compared to the baseline wing at an angle of attack of 2°, which is approximately the cruising angle for the tiltrotor aircraft, the lift-to-drag ratio is increased by 22.63, 23.91, and 22.22% for PGF, SGF1, and SGF2, respectively. Based on the voyage equation27, the SGF extends the flight range of tiltrotor aircraft. Figure 5(f) shows the variation of the nose-down pitching moment coefficient with angle of attack. Similar to the lift coefficient, the nose-down pitching moment coefficient of the SGF is smaller than that of the PGF. These findings agree with previous research on serrated Gurney flaps20,21.

To get further understanding of the mechanism of the GF, Fig. 6 compares the streamlines and pressure contours at the middle section of the A821201 wing at an angle of attack of 2°. The streamlines of the baseline wing are, in general, very smooth, as shown in Fig. 6(a). When the PGF is implemented, three vortices, one upstream of the GF and two counter-rotating vortices downstream of the GF, can be clearly seen in Fig. 6(b). The Kutta condition is shifted from the airfoil trailing edge to the lower edge of the GF, thereby enhancing lift9. The increment of drag arises from the pressure difference between the windward and leeward sides of the GF. When the SGF is applied, the vortices around the SGF are smaller than those around the PGF. Comparing Fig. 6(c) and Fig. 6(d) shows that the phenomenon becomes more pronounced as the serration height increases.

Streamlines and pressure contours of A821201 wing without and with different GFs at α = 2°. (a) Baseline, (b) PGF, (c) SGF1, (d) SGF2.

The X-Vorticity contours in the wake region of GFs are presented for two cases (PGF and SGF1) at the angle of attack of 2°. The chordwise position x/c = 1.02 is selected. As shown in Fig. 7(a), the magnitude of the vorticity engendered by PGF is negligible. For the case of SGF1, as shown in Fig. 7(b), the strong counter-rotating vortex pairs were generated by the serration. These induced streamwise vortices enhanced the momentum exchange and turbulent fluctuations in the downstream. The comparison of pressure coefficient distributions is shown in Fig. 8. It can be concluded that both PGF and SGF increase the upper surface suction and the lower surface pressure. As the serration height increases, the pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces becomes smaller.

Effects of serration width

In order to achieve a better geometry of the SGF, the effect of serration width on the wing’s aerodynamic properties is further investigated. Based on the above results, the serration height is set to 0.01c, and the serration widths of 0.01c, 0.02c, and 0.04c are considered, named SGF1, SGF3, and SGF4, respectively. The windward areas of these three SGFs are the same, and they reduce the windward area by 25% compared with that of the PGF. Figure 9 shows the aerodynamic loads under different angles of attack. From Fig. 9(a), Fig. 9(b) and Fig. 9(c), it can be seen that as the serration width changes, only minor differences are observed in the lift and drag coefficients.

Influence of serration width on aerodynamic coefficients. (a) Lift coefficient versus angle of attack, (b) Drag coefficient versus angle of attack, (c) Lift coefficient versus drag coefficient, (d) Lift-to-drag ratio versus angle of attack, (e) Lift-to-drag ratio versus lift coefficient, (f) Nose-down pitching moment versus angle of attack.

The impact of serration width is found to be more sensitive to the lift-to-drag ratio, as shown in Fig. 9(d) and Fig. 9(e). A tendency of first increasing and then decreasing with increasing angle of attack is observed. The PGF and SGF can effectively improve the lift-to-drag ratio before stall, and SGF4 shows a notable increase in lift-to-drag ratio. Compared with the clean wing, the lift-to-drag ratio of SGF4 is increased by 25.13% at the angle of attack of 2°. The serration width has little influence on the nose-down pitching moment, as shown in Fig. 9(f). The comparison of the pressure coefficients at the middle section of the wing is given in Fig. 10. The variation of the pressure coefficient caused by the serration width seems to be very small. It is demonstrated that the main factor influencing SGF is the windward area.

Conclusions

Numerical investigations were performed to acquire a qualitative and quantitative analysis of the effect of serrated Gurney flaps on a tiltrotor wing. Based on the results obtained, some concluding remarks may be drawn:

The plain Gurney flap and serrated Gurney flap can increase the lift, drag, and nose-down pitching moment compared to those obtained for the baseline wing. Whilst the serrated Gurney flap results in a lower lift coefficient increment than that of the plain Gurney flap, the lift-to-drag ratio can be further enhanced.

The plain Gurney flap and serrated Gurney flap can increase the lift, drag, and nose-down pitching moment compared to those obtained for the baseline wing. Whilst the serrated Gurney flap results in a lower lift coefficient increment than that of the plain Gurney flap, the lift-to-drag ratio can be further enhanced.

A noticeable decrease in the lift and drag coefficients is observed as the serration height increases; however, the serration width has little effect on these aerodynamic coefficients. The results reveal that the important factor that influences the effect of the serrated Gurney flap is the windward area.
