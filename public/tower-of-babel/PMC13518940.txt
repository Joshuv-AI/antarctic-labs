Identification of cannabichromevarin as a potent stabilizer of the measles virus prefusion F protein: structural insights from long-timescale molecular dynamics
Authors: Hanane Abbou, Zainab Gaouzi, Razana Zegrari, Souad Kartti, Mohammed Hakmi, Saber Boutayeb, Lahcen Belyamani, Rachid ElJaoudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Measles virus (MeV) remains a serious public health concern, necessitating the development of effective antivirals targeting the viral fusion (F) glycoprotein. This study employed a robust computational pipeline, including molecular docking, 1000 ns all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and free energy landscape (FEL) analysis, to evaluate minor cannabinoids as novel inhibitors of the MeV F protein. Initial virtual screening identified Cannabichromenic acid (CBCA), Cannabichromevarin (CBCV), and Cannabiripsol (CBR) as high-affinity leads, with docking scores of − 8.5, − 8.2, and − 8.1 kcal/mol, respectively, outperforming the reference inhibitor AS-48 (− 7.6 kcal/mol). Post-MD binding free energy calculations (MM-GBSA) further confirmed the thermodynamic superiority of CBCV (ΔGbind = − 44.7 kcal/mol) and CBCA (ΔGbind = − 30.1 kcal/mol) over the reference. Dynamic analyses revealed that CBCV and CBCA effectively stabilize the F protein in its inactive prefusion conformation through a conformational locking mechanism. CBCV induced the most significant structural compaction (Rg = 2.4 nm) and displayed the sharpest global energy minimum (0.3 kcal/mol) in the FEL. Furthermore, ADMET profiling and ProTox-3.0 toxicity modeling identified CBCV as the most promising lead, possessing excellent drug-likeness, an inactive toxicity profile, and predicted blood-brain barrier permeability. This work establishes minor cannabinoids as novel scaffolds for anti-MeV drug development, positioning CBCV as a strong candidate for treating systemic and neurological complications of measles, such as Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50199-6.

Introduction

Measles virus (MeV) is experiencing a global resurgence, posing a critical public health crisis as vaccination coverage declines. The highly contagious nature of MeV, coupled with the absence of approved antivirals, intensifies the urgent need for non-vaccine therapeutic options to treat or manage outbreaks, especially in immunocompromised populations where measles can be devastating1. Recent structural studies have highlighted the prefusion conformation of the F protein as a sensitive target for entry inhibitors, with peptides and small molecules (such as AS-48) demonstrating promising efficacy by stabilizing this metastable state and blocking the fusion cascade2–4.

The MeV F protein is an essential envelope glycoprotein that mediates membrane fusion between the viral envelope and host cell membrane, enabling viral entry and cell-to-cell spread. Synthesized as an inactive precursor (F0), the protein undergoes proteolytic cleavage into disulfide-linked subunits F1 and F2, which together form a trimeric metastable prefusion structure vital for fusion function. The newly generated N-terminus of F1 subunit (amino acids 115–550) possesses a hydrophobic stretch of amino acids, termed the fusion peptide, that is inserted into the target membrane during the fusion process5. The F protein’s role is the critical determinant of infectivity by mediating membrane fusion; its active prefusion state is metastable and highly conserved, making it a prime target for conformational locking, a proven strategy for fusion inhibition6. The F protein is organized into distinct functional domains, including the fusion peptide, heptad repeats A and B (HRA and HRB), a globular head, transmembrane region, and cytoplasmic tail. Conformational changes triggered by interaction with host cell receptors and the hemagglutinin (H) attachment protein induce an extended post-fusion conformation that drives membrane merging1,7.

While efforts have largely focused on peptide mimics and engineered antibodies8,9, these therapeutics often face pharmacological limitations, including poor oral bioavailability and restricted central nervous system (CNS) penetration. Given the severe neurological complications associated with MeV, such as subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE), there is a need for developing small, highly lipophilic molecules capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and accessing the deep hydrophobic domains of the F protein10.

The premise of this study stems from the unique structural and physicochemical profiles of minor cannabinoids. Derived from the rich genetic diversity of Cannabis sativa, these compounds possess rigid, tricyclic scaffolds and highly lipophilic characters that mimic the hydrophobic anchoring mechanisms of known small-molecule fusion inhibitors like AS-4811. Minor cannabinoids have demonstrated a broad spectrum of antiviral properties, including reported inhibition of SARS-CoV-2, Viral Hepatitis, and Mpox12–15. However, their interaction and inhibitory mechanism against the Measles virus F protein remains underexplored and uncharacterized.

In this study, we systematically evaluate minor cannabinoids as inhibitors of the measles virus F protein by combining molecular docking, long-timescale molecular dynamics simulations, and free energy landscape.

Methods

Structures retrieval and preparation

F protein PDB 8UUP was prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard in Schrödinger 2021-416, where bond orders were assigned, hydrogens were added, the PROPKA module was employed to optimize the hydrogen-bond network at a physiological pH of 7.4, and the Epik module was used to include probable ionization states. The 3D structures of minor cannabinoids and the MeV F protein inhibitor AS-48 were retrieved from PubChem17 and prepared using the LigPrep module in Schrödinger 2021-418, to add hydrogens and expand stereoisomers to a maximum of five per ligand. The workflow included desalting, neutralization, and the generation of ionization states, using the Epik module.

Extra precision docking

Extra precision (XP) docking mode in the Schrödinger Glide 2021-419 was used to dock the reference inhibitor and to screen the ligands against the prepared F protein structure. The docking grids were generated using a blind docking approach, where a receptor grid box was centered on the centroid of the entire protein structure. The grid box dimensions were set to sufficiently encompass the entire protein surface, allowing for unrestricted ligand binding across the whole structure.

No constraints were applied during docking, all XP docking parameters were left at the default settings, and ligand poses were scored and ranked based on GlideScore; The molecules scoring higher than the reference inhibitor were selected for MD simulations.

Ligand-protein interactions were examined using LigPlot20 to characterize hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, and critical residue interactions that contribute to binding affinity.

Post-docking MM/GBSA analysis

To validate the docking results, we performed post-docking binding free energy calculations on the molecules scoring higher than AS-48, using the Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) approach. This method integrates molecular mechanics energies with solvation effects estimated via the Generalized Born model and accounts for non-polar surface area contributions to deliver accurate binding affinity predictions. Calculations were carried out using the Prime MM-GBSA tool in Schrödinger 2021-421, employing the OPLS4 force field under default settings. By focusing on the minor cannabinoids that outperformed AS-48 in docking scores, this analysis provided deeper insights into their binding potentials, refining initial docking assessments through a more comprehensive energetic evaluation.

Molecular dynamics simulation

After selecting the final hit molecules, all-atom MD simulations were carried out using Desmond in Schrödinger 2021-422. The protein-ligand complexes were prepared for simulation using the System Builder, where the OPLS4 force field was applied to both protein and ligand structures. Each system was solvated in a cubic TIP3P water box with a 10 Å buffer and neutralized with counter ions (Na + and Cl⁻), with physiological conditions mimicked by a 0.15 M NaCl salt concentration. A multi-step equilibration protocol was employed, beginning with a 100 ps Brownian Dynamics NVT simulation at 10 K with heavy atom restraints, followed by a series of short NVT and NPT relaxation runs using the Langevin thermostat and barostat. Specifically, the systems underwent NVT and NPT equilibration at 10 K with 50.0 kcal/mol/Å² restraints, followed by unrestrained NPT production runs. The final production simulations were conducted for 1000 ns per complex at 300 K and 1 atm using the NPT ensemble. Trajectories were analyzed to assess the stability and dynamics of protein–ligand interactions. Analysis of the trajectories included root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuations (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), binding interactions, and total intermolecular bonds’ evolution over time to evaluate the stability and effects of ligand binding on the F protein conformation.

Free energy landscapes (FELs) were constructed using RMSD and Rg values extracted from the MD trajectories to characterize the conformational space and stability of the F protein and its complexes. The free energy ΔG was calculated based on the joint probability distribution of these variables using the following Boltzmann-based equation:

In this expression, kB represents the Boltzmann constant, T is the simulation temperature of 300 K, and P(RMSD, Rg) is the probability density of the system’s states. Data processing and the generation of 2D and 3D FEL plots were performed using Python 3.8 with the NumPy and Matplotlib libraries to identify the global energy minima and compare the dynamic behavior across the studied systems.

Drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic analysis

SwissADME23 was used to evaluate the ADME profiles and drug-likeness of the top two candidate molecules. Canonical SMILES for each compound were used to calculate physicochemical and pharmacokinetic parameters, including Lipinski’s Rule of Five, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge filters, molecular weight, lipophilicity (iLOGP, XLOGP3), and solubility (Log S). Predictions of gastrointestinal absorption, blood-brain barrier permeability, P-glycoprotein substrate status, and cytochrome P450 inhibition were also obtained. Medicinal chemistry alerts such as PAINS and Brenk filters, alongside synthetic accessibility, were assessed to select candidates with favorable oral bioavailability, CNS penetration, and minimal liabilities.

To evaluate the safety of the lead cannabinoids, toxicity profiles were estimated using the ProTox 3.0 web server24. This platform utilizes machine learning and molecular fingerprints to predict toxicological characteristics based on compound substructures. The SMILES representations for CBCA and CBCV were used as input to categorize the molecules into toxicity classes (1 to 7) based on the Globally Harmonized System (GHS). While Classes 1 to 3 indicate high toxicity, Classes 4 to 6 represent moderate toxicity requiring further investigation. The analysis provided data for several critical endpoints, including Lethal Dose 50 (LD50) hepatotoxicity, carcinogenicity, immunotoxicity, mutagenicity, nephrotoxicity, and cytotoxicity. These assessments were used to identify potential risks and ensure the safety of the candidates before pharmacological consideration.

Human protein target prediction

SwissTargetPrediction25 was employed to predict potential human protein targets for the selected cannabinoid molecules. Using the canonical SMILES of each compound as input, the tool estimates the probability of interaction with a wide range of human receptors based on similarity to known ligands. This approach highlights possible off-target effects by identifying high-confidence interactions with enzymes, G protein-coupled receptors, ion channels, and transporters. This analysis complements ADMET profiling by anticipating pharmacodynamic liabilities and guiding future safety assessments.

Results

The docking scores for the four molecules against the F protein indicate strong and potentially therapeutically relevant binding affinities. Among the tested compounds, CBCA exhibited the highest binding score of − 8.5 kcal/mol, followed by CBCV (− 8.2 kcal/mol), CBR (− 8.1 kcal/mol), and AS-48 (− 7.7 kcal/mol), suggesting that these minor cannabinoids possess comparable or superior binding potential relative to AS-48.

AS-48 anchors firmly within the hydrophobic pocket region, comprising amino acids 452–467, and interacts with key residues such as Ile452, Leu454, and Val459, which are critical for maintaining the prefusion conformation and blocking fusion activation. This confirms AS-48’s established role as a fusion inhibitor by stabilizing this essential pocket (Fig. 1A).

CBCA and CBCV share similar interaction profiles, localized to hydrophobic and aromatic residues present in the globular head and heptad repeat regions, including Ile279, Leu281, Val175, Phe217, and Tyr209, indicating binding to a hydrophobic region essential for prefusion trimer integrity (Fig. 1B,C).

CBR engages a slightly different interface at the junction of the head and stalk domains, which contains many loops involving residues such as Cys343, Ser344, Ile291, and Thr341, combining hydrophobic and polar interactions that likely disrupt the flexible regions required for fusion triggering (Fig. 1D).

Post-docking MM-GBSA binding free energy calculations further emphasize these results. CBR showed the most favorable ΔG bind of − 55.1 kcal/mol, CBCV followed with − 44.7 kcal/mol, AS-48 with − 40.5 kcal/mol, and importantly, CBCA displayed a moderate binding free energy of − 30.1 kcal/mol (Table 2).

Hydrophobic interactions (van der Waals and lipophilic) contributed substantially to binding stability, with CBCV and CBCA exhibiting van der Waals energies of − 75.6 and − 69.3 kcal/mol, respectively. Coulombic interactions varied among the molecules, with CBR displaying a strongly favorable electrostatic component (− 25.2 kcal/mol). In comparison, the CBCA showed a positive Coulomb value (47.7 kcal/mol), which structural inspection attributes to the placement of the ligand’s carboxylate moiety within a highly lipophilic region (comprising Leu234, Ala237, and Ala267) lacking compensatory positive charges. This electrostatic repulsion is mitigated by a dominant lipophilic contribution (− 23.4 kcal/mol), as the molecule’s hydrophobic region is deeply embedded in a pocket defined by residues such as Ile172, Val175, and Val280, alongside a key hydrogen bond with Ile279.

MD simulations were performed to assess the stability and dynamic behavior of the MeV F protein both in its unbound form and in complex with AS-48, CBCA, CBCV, and CBR. The trajectories were analyzed for RMSD, per-residue RMSF, and Rg to provide comprehensive insights into structural stability and compactness.

The RMSD values of the protein C-alpha atoms provide information on the overall structural stability during the 1000 ns simulations. The apo F protein showed an average RMSD of 1.28 ± 0.20 nm, indicating a stable structure throughout the simulation timeframe (Table 3). The binding of AS-48 and the minor cannabinoids altered the dynamic profile. The F-AS-48 complex exhibited an increased average RMSD of 1.50 ± 0.33 nm, while CBCA and F-CBR complexes displayed RMSD averages of 1.54 ± 0.20 nm and 1.46 ± 0.36 nm, respectively. F-CBCV showed the highest RMSD of 1.86 ± 0.27 nm among complexes, suggesting increased flexibility or conformational adjustment upon binding (Fig. 2A). This elevated value indicates a significant structural divergence from the initial structure, suggesting a major conformational adjustment upon binding. While high RMSD can occasionally denote structural instability, in this context, it represents a directional shift toward a new conformational state.

Residue-wise fluctuations analyzed by RMSF highlight regions of flexibility within the protein. Across all systems, the average RMSF values were reduced relative to the apo protein (0.59 ± 0.05 nm), reaching as low as 0.47 ± 0.17 nm for the CBCA complex, implying ligand binding tends to stabilize local dynamics at binding interfaces (Table 3). F-AS-48 presented low RMSF as well (0.52 ± 0.21 nm), indicating a rigid complex, which may correlate with stronger or more numerous interactions between ligand and receptor. As for F-CBR, it induced the highest RMSF fluctuation (0.70 ± 0.26 nm) (Fig. 2B).

The apo F protein maintained an average Rg of 3.18 ± 0.09 nm, representative of a compact and folded structure. Ligand binding led to reductions in Rg, with F-AS-48 showing a particularly notable compaction at 2.57 ± 0.15 nm. F-CBCV has the lowest Rg value among the complexes, with an average of 2.44 ± 0.17 nm (Table 3). This lower Rg suggests that the F-CBCV complex has the most compact structure compared to the other ligand-bound forms and the apo protein. Other complexes showed intermediate values (F-CBCA at 2.60 ± 0.14 nm, and F-CBR at 3.00 ± 0.16 nm) (Fig. 2-C).

Analysis of the ligand-residue contact timelines throughout the simulations reveals distinct interaction patterns and key amino acid contributors for each complex. In the F-AS-48 system, the most interacting amino acids were Thr314, Asn328, Ser352, Leu354, Leu355, Ser365, Leu454, Leu455, Val459, and Ile452, Asn462, and Leu463, which were involved in frequent and sustained contact with the ligand. The dynamic profile shows that these contacts persist throughout the trajectory with moderate fluctuation, as the number of interactions consistently fluctuates between 1 and 8, with the 4 bonds being the most prominent number of interactions throughout the simulation (Fig. 3A).

For the F-CBCA complex, the main interaction partners are hydrophobic and aliphatic residues such as Leu173, Val175, Leu249, Tyr251, Leu281, Ile283, and Tyr285, which form stable contacts distributed throughout the simulation trajectory. Additionally, more transient interactions occur with residues like Ser140, Met171, Ile242, and Ile279. This contact pattern highlights selective hydrophobic anchoring at multiple points along the ligand binding site, contributing to the enhanced stabilization observed in the free energy landscape (FEL) analysis (Fig. 3B).

The F-CBCV complex stands out for its persistent network of interacting residues. Key contacts include Ile161, Val175, Ile231, Leu234, Ser235, Leu238, Ile242, and Leu246. The total number of interactions fluctuated between 1 and 9, with 3 being the most prominent number of interactions (Fig. 3C).

In the case of the F-CBR complex, the interaction profile is more dispersed and dynamic. Residues such as Ser289, Glu290, Ile291, Gly293, Val294, Val296, Arg298, Val315, Pro316, Thr341, Cys343, and Ala347 form transient contacts, with very few residues dominating the interaction landscape for extended periods, including Tyr318, Phe329, Glu331, and Phe336. This suggests that CBR engages in more surface hopping and less restricted binding, allowing the ligand to sample various locations and conformations along the protein interface. As a result, the F-CBR complex is characterized by intermittent and shifting contact events, consistent with the higher flexibility indicated by RMSF analysis (Fig. 3D).

To evaluate the thermodynamic stability and conformational sampling of the F protein complexes, Free Energy Landscape (FEL) analysis was performed. Using RMSD and Rg as coordinates allows for the direct visualization of ligand-induced structural compaction and the restriction of conformational space. In the absence of a ligand, the apo F protein sampled a broad conformational space characterized by a global energy minimum located at an Rg of 3.23 nm (RMSD = 1.22 nm, ΔG = 0.6 kcal/mol). This relatively high Rg reflects the inherent flexibility and structural heterogeneity of the unliganded state. Upon ligand binding, the FEL surfaces shifted toward more compact, rigid states, evidenced by a significant reduction in Rg corresponding to the global minima. The reference inhibitor AS-48 restricted the protein to a single, localized thermodynamic basin (Rg = 2.50 nm, RMSD = 1.70 nm, ΔG = 0.70 kcal/mol), while the minor cannabinoids induced the deepest and most tightly confined global minima. The F-CBCA (ΔG = 0.5 kcal/mol; Rg = 2.53 nm) and F-CBCV (ΔG = 0.3 kcal/mol; Rg = 2.38 nm) complexes are dominated by single, sharp energy funnels. Thermodynamically, the localization of these complexes into isolated, highly compacted basins indicates a profound restriction of conformational heterogeneity. This represents a functional impact, where the ligands successfully stabilize the compact prefusion protein state. In contrast, the FEL surface of the F-CBR complex displayed multiple low-energy basins. This multiplicity indicates that CBR does not uniformly constrain the protein’s conformational space. Instead, the system transitions between multiple distinct states. Overall, the FEL topography demonstrates that CBCA and CBCV are vastly superior to CBR at funneling the F protein into a single, tightly locked, and thermodynamically favorable conformation.

Based on the MD simulation results, CBR was not considered for ADMET and human target prediction due to its inability to maintain extended interactions necessary for stable binding and structural stabilization of the F protein complex, resulting in a less robust inhibitory effect (Table 4, Fig. 4).

Comprehensive ADMET profiling showed that both molecules possess favorable physicochemical and drug-likeness properties. Both satisfied Lipinski’s Rule of Five and most drug-likeness filters, with CBCV having the fewest violations and better synthetic accessibility. CBCA displayed slightly higher molecular weight, polarity, and lipophilicity, while solubility predictions classified both as moderately soluble. Both compounds exhibit high predicted gastrointestinal absorption; however, only CBCV is likely to cross the blood-brain barrier. CYP inhibition risk is present, with CBCA possibly affecting CYP3A4, and CBCV perhaps inhibiting CYP2C19 and CYP3A4. Overall, CBCV stands out for CNS penetration, clean rule profile, and easier synthesis. Drug-likeness assessment shows CBCV has fewer rule violations and a lower synthetic accessibility score, suggesting it is more lead-like and potentially easier to develop. Overall, both minor cannabinoids exhibit good oral bioavailability and pharmacokinetic potential, but CBCV stands out for its favorable CNS penetration, clean rule profile, and synthetic feasibility (Table 5).

Toxicity evaluations of the identified minor cannabinoids, CBCA and CBCV, were conducted using the ProTox 3.0 server to anticipate potential adverse effects and define the safety profiles of the lead molecules. Both compounds were classified under Toxicity Class 4, with a predicted LD50 value of 750 mg/kg (Table 6).

Beyond oral toxicity, ProTox 3.0 provided critical insights into specific toxicological endpoints, including carcinogenicity, cytotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, and mutagenicity. Both tested cannabinoids yielded inactive predictions for these categories with high probability scores (ranging from 0.65 to 0.83), further validating the structural safety of these phytochemical scaffolds. However, organ-specific analysis revealed a key differentiation: CBCA was predicted to be active for nephrotoxicity with a probability of 0.54, whereas CBCV was classified as inactive (0.86). Furthermore, while both compounds showed activity for immunotoxicity, CBCV exhibited a lower probability (0.69) compared to CBCA (0.97). These toxicity assessments are crucial for identifying the safest therapeutic candidates, emphasizing that CBCV emerges as the most viable lead due to its lack of predicted organ-specific toxicity and its overall more favorable safety profile.

Discussion

Small molecule inhibitors have the advantages of low production costs and the potential for oral administration. Two small molecule inhibitors that bind to the MeV-F prefusion state and prevent its conformational change have been reported, AS‐48 and FIP (fusion inhibitor peptide)2,26. However, their inhibitory activity is relatively weak, particularly compared to antibodies and large peptide inhibitors27–29. In the context of this computational study, AS-48 was selected as the comparative entity to the evaluated minor cannabinoids.

This study leveraged a comprehensive computational pipeline, including molecular docking, extensive molecular dynamics simulations, and free energy calculations, to investigate minor cannabinoids as potential inhibitors of MeV F protein, which is an essential component for viral infectivity, mediating the critical step of membrane fusion that allows the virus to enter host cells and spread. Its highly conserved, metastable prefusion conformation represents a prime target for antiviral drugs designed to prevent these necessary conformational changes30,31. Our findings identified several cannabinoid compounds that not only showed superior binding potential to the reference inhibitor AS-48 but also appeared to function through a distinct, predominantly hydrophobic-driven mechanism. More significantly, the dynamic simulations and free energy calculations suggest that these cannabinoids, especially CBCV, stabilize the F protein’s prefusion conformation through conformational locking, presenting a novel avenue for anti-MeV drug development by effectively locking the F protein in its inactive state. This finding aligns with recent literature emphasizing the therapeutic promise of small molecules that preferentially target the F protein’s prefusion conformation, thereby inhibiting viral fusion and entry3,4.

AS-48, previously reported as a potent MeV F protein inhibitor, served as a validation reference for the docking and simulation pipeline. Its known mechanism involves stabilizing the prefusion conformation of the F protein by anchoring within the hydrophobic pocket encompassing residues Ile452-Leu463, thus preventing the large-scale rearrangements required for membrane fusion8,32. Consistent with prior data, AS-48 displayed a strong docking score (− 7.66 kcal/mol) and favorable MM-GBSA free energy (− 40.5 kcal/mol). RMSD and RMSF analyses confirmed that the ligand maintained stable interactions throughout the trajectory, correlating with a compact structure (Rg = 2.57 nm) and reduced flexibility in critical regions. These findings validate the computational protocol and confirm that the observed stabilization effects in cannabinoid-bound complexes can be meaningfully compared to a proven fusion inhibitor.

CBCA showed the strongest docking score among all ligands (− 8.5 kcal/mol), surpassing AS-48, which points to a high binding affinity toward the F protein. Its interaction profile revealed engagement with residues such as Leu281, Ile279, Phe217, Tyr209, and Val175, regions implicated in the stability of the prefusion state, as residues between 115 and 287 show substantial spatial and structural rearrangements after the F protein transition to the post-fusion state9. The MM-GBSA analysis yielded a ΔGbind of − 30.1 kcal/mol, largely driven by strong van der Waals (− 69.3 kcal/mol) and lipophilic interactions (− 23.4 kcal/mol). Notably, CBCA showed a high positive Coulomb value (47.7 kcal/mol), indicating an unfavorable electrostatic contribution; however, this was mitigated and compensated by the stabilizing effects from the hydrophobic anchoring and favorable solvation effects (− 10.5 kcal/mol). Molecular dynamics simulations revealed that CBCA binding induced moderate structural fluctuations (RMSD = 1.54 nm), yet the overall complex remained compact (Rg = 2.60 nm) and locally stable (RMSF = 0.47 nm). The single deep energy basin in FEL suggests that CBCA confines the protein to a stable, low-energy, and compact conformation, potentially restricting the conformational flexibility necessary for fusion activation.

Although CBCA demonstrated favorable binding energetics, its higher polarity and predicted inability to cross the blood-brain barrier may limit systemic bioavailability. However, its physicochemical profile (moderate solubility, no Lipinski violations, and high gastrointestinal absorption) supports oral formulation potential. Collectively, these findings suggest that CBCA could function as a stabilizing inhibitor of MeV F, mimicking AS-48’s conformational-locking mechanism but through primarily hydrophobic anchoring.

CBCV exhibited both strong docking affinity (− 8.2 kcal/mol) and a highly favorable binding free energy (ΔGbind = − 44.7 kcal/mol), surpassing AS-48 and CBCA in terms of total binding stability. The binding site was enriched in hydrophobic residues (Ile161, Val175, Leu234, Leu238, Ile242, and Leu246), suggesting that CBCV occupies a pocket overlapping the heptad repeat interface responsible for fusion trimer stability8. The dominant van der Waals contribution (− 75.6 kcal/mol) indicates a strong lipophilic fit, while the low RMSF values (0.52 nm) point to local rigidity enhancement upon ligand binding.

CBCV induced the most compact structure among all complexes, evidenced by the lowest average Radius of Gyration (Rg = 2.44 nm), and its FEL displayed a sharp single minimum at 0.3 kcal/mol. Together, this low Rg and the highly confined FEL minimum provide robust computational evidence for a powerful conformational locking effect, significantly restricting the structural rearrangements that drive the F-protein’s transition to the post-fusion form9.

Pharmacokinetic evaluation revealed that CBCV fulfils all major drug-likeness criteria, exhibits high gastrointestinal absorption, and uniquely shows blood–brain barrier permeability. This favorable profile is further supported by ProTox-3.0 toxicity modeling, which classified CBCV as Toxicity Class 4 (LD50 = 750 mg/kg) and predicted an ‘Inactive’ status for critical endpoints such as carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, and hepatotoxicity. Unlike CBCA, which showed a potential risk for nephrotoxicity, CBCV remained inactive across all organ-specific toxicity parameters, reinforcing its superior safety profile. Given its strong thermodynamic stability, conformational locking effect, and favorable ADMET characteristics, CBCV emerges as the most promising lead among the minor cannabinoids tested for targeting the MeV F fusion process. The safety of these scaffolds was further examined through human target prediction; however, the resulting low binding probabilities for receptors like CNR1 and CNR2 require nuanced interpretation. While these scores suggest a reduced risk of off-target side effects, they likely reflect a data gap in current predictive models, which are heavily trained on major cannabinoids like THC and CBD but may underrepresent minor chromene-type phytochemicals. This limitation suggests that the low scores might stem from a lack of reference data rather than a total absence of interaction. Nevertheless, the convergence of these results with the ‘Inactive’ toxicity ratings from ProTox-3.0 provides a degree of cross-validation, supporting the potential for CBCV and CBCA to act as selective scaffolds with a manageable safety profile.

In contrast to the stabilization observed with CBCA and CBCV, the behavior of CBR served to validate the selection process by acting as a less robust candidate. Although CBR achieved the most favorable post-docking ΔG of − 55.1 kcal/mol, its 1000 ns molecular dynamics trajectory revealed that these interactions were not consistent over time. Structural mapping indicates that CBR is localized within a pocket composed predominantly of flexible loops within the F protein structure. While these loops allow for a tight initial fit and high static affinity, they lack the structural rigidity necessary to sustain a persistent conformational lock under dynamic conditions. Consequently, this fragmented interaction pattern resulted in the highest RMSF fluctuation (0.70 nm) and an FEL displaying multiple, dispersed low-energy basins. This conformational heterogeneity indicates that CBR fails to uniformly constrain the protein’s structural space, suggesting a less robust and less effective inhibitory potential compared to the other candidates. Due to this lack of structural anchoring, CBR was not advanced for further ADMET analysis.

The computational analyses collectively highlight that minor cannabinoids can effectively engage the MeV F protein’s hydrophobic and hinge regions, achieving energetics comparable to the known inhibitor AS-48. CBCA and CBCV demonstrated high binding affinity and induced structural compaction, indicative of potential prefusion state stabilization, while CBR acted more dynamically, potentially modulating flexibility in the stalk–head interface.

Among these, CBCV appears to represent the most promising antiviral scaffold due to its balanced binding thermodynamics, conformational stabilization, and favorable ADMET properties.

These findings align with growing evidence that phytocannabinoids can interfere with viral fusion and replication pathways15,33,34, supporting further investigation of minor cannabinoids as scaffolds for next-generation fusion inhibitors targeting paramyxoviruses.

Our results support advancing CBCV and CBCA as lead candidates for further biological validation and inform strategies for the design of new MeV entry inhibitors. Importantly, this approach expands the focus beyond the well-studied cannabinoids Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and Cannabidiol (CBD), spotlighting a promising but underexplored class of molecules for antiviral drug discovery35.

Conclusion

In this computational study, we identified minor cannabinoids as novel inhibitors for the MeV F protein. Our findings show that CBCA and CBCV effectively stabilize the F protein’s essential prefusion conformation.

CBCV emerged as the superior lead candidate, demonstrating robust binding affinity, a powerful stabilizing effect, and a favorable pharmacokinetic profile. Crucially, its predicted ability to cross the BBB makes it a compelling candidate for treating measles-related neurological complications of measles, most notably SSPE. By targeting the viral entry machinery within the central nervous system, CBCV offers a specialized pharmacological advantage where many existing antivirals fail.

While these in silico results require experimental validation, this work establishes CBCV as a strong foundation for developing a new class of orally available fusion inhibitors to combat measles, a persistent global health threat.
