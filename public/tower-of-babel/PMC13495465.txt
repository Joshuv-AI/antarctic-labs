Combating Antimicrobial Resistance with Hydrophilic and Eco-Friendly Membrane-Active Antibiotics Derived from Biological Nanoparticles
Authors: Wan Zheng, Hairong Ma, John R. Dutcher, Hongjun Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ever-increasing use of antibiotics and the accumulation of antibiotic waste in ecosystems are expediting antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Our next-generation antibiotics should aim for a different antimicrobial mechanism that is less conducive to AMR, a wider therapeutic window tolerant to drug titration, and a quicker deactivation strategy responsive to environmental stimuli. Membrane-active antimicrobials (MAAs) have the potential to thwart AMR, but the lipophilicity of current MAAs gives rise to their broad-spectrum cytotoxicity. Here, we show that biological nanoparticles, such as cyclodextrin and phytoglycogen, can be transformed into potent MAAs with low cytotoxicity by grafting them with hydrophilic polymer brushes. In service, these hydrophilic nanoantibiotics kill bacteria by inducing pore formation exclusively on microbial membranes rich in negative curvature lipids, a bactericidal mode less likely to incur AMR. After service, they are degraded and deactivated by biomass recycling enzymes that are abundant in ecosystems. This study illuminates a new paradigm to combat AMR with hydrophilic and eco-friendly membrane-active antibiotics derived from biological nanoparticles.

Introduction

The serendipitous discovery of penicillin in 1928 and the isolation of streptomycin in 1943 ushered in the era of modern medicine. In the decades that followed, a range of antibiotics, derived or retrofitted from natural scaffolds, emerged to enable radical solutions such as invasive surgery, organ transplant, joint replacement, and chemotherapy to improve the quality of life for millions of people. These medical breakthroughs are unimaginable without the efficient mitigation of bacterial infections, but the widespread of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is putting them at risk. All major classes of antibiotics are now challenged by resistant bacterial strains. In general, antibiotics kill bacteria by derailing their biosynthetic pathways, but bacteria develop resistance fairly quickly against this obstruction due to their highly adaptive AMR mechanisms. , For example, Zyvox (linezolid) was approved in 2000 as a new class of antibiotics that interferes with the bacterial ribosome, but a clinical isolate of linezolid-resistant bacteria was swiftly reported in 2001. Although AMR that undermines the antibiotic actions of natural scaffolds does occur in the microbiome, the massive use of antibiotics in human healthcare, animal husbandry, and agriculture has greatly accelerated this process. , On one hand, nearly all classes of antibiotics are nephrotoxic. , Their limited dosage regimens are bound to open opportunities for bacteria to adapt; on the other hand, the environmental degradation of many antibiotics is very slow. , Unusually high concentrations of antibiotic waste are detected in soils and waterways worldwide because up to 90% of man-made antibiotics are released in various active forms into the environment via human waste streams, agricultural runoff, and pharmaceutical effluent. − Ample evidence has linked the increasing accumulation of antibiotic waste in ecosystems to the rise of AMR, as the constant selective pressure imposed by the antibiotic residues promotes the development of resistome, i.e., the environmental reservoir of resistance genes shared among pathogenic and nonpathogenic bacteria. − We are patently underprepared to tackle the surge of AMR. A 2019 report by CDC estimated that more than 2.8 million AMR infections occur in the U.S. alone each year, resulting in more than 35,000 annual deaths. A more recent study determined that AMR infections directly killed ∼1.27 million patients globally and indirectly caused ∼5 million additional deaths in 2019. Besides curbing the excessive use of antibiotics administratively and adopting engineered innovations to scavenge antibiotic residues via waste management, it is critical to develop our next-generation antibiotics to seek a different antimicrobial mechanism less conducive to AMR, a wider therapeutic window tolerant to drug titration, and a quicker deactivation strategy responsive to environmental stimuli.

Membrane-active antimicrobials (MAAs) are strong contenders in this regard. Except for a few outliers, , MAAs kill bacteria by disrupting their membranes, a bactericidal mode widely regarded as less prone to incur resistance. − MAAs are particularly appealing in the fight against Gram– AMR strains because MAAs circumvent the Achilles’ heel of traditional antibiotics, i.e., their inability to readily penetrate the Gram– bacterial membrane envelope in order to reach the targeted sites. Nevertheless, current MAAs are characterized by their amphipathic nature, which is a double-edged sword, as their lipophilicity that helps breach bacterial membranes would also indiscriminately damage mammalian cells. − The conventional wisdom in the search for a clinically viable MAA is to seek a delicate yet elusive hydrophobic–cationic balance in the hope of enhancing its antimicrobial activity while keeping its toxicity at bay. − Despite decades of relentless trial-and-error experimentation, clinical translations of amphipathic MAAs are few and far between, primarily due to their limited therapeutic windows. , Although synthetic MAAs that seemingly reached the “optimal cationic–hydrophobic balance” to disrupt bacterial membranes with low hemolytic activity against human red blood cells (hRBCs) have been reported, many of them failed to pass more rigorous toxicity tests. − None of the vast number of synthetic MAAs that showed great promise when tested on the benchtop has successfully translated to the bedside yet, and only a handful of MAAs of bacterial origin, such as polymyxin B, colistin, and daptomycin, are approved by the FDA as the “last-resort” antibiotics because their potent antimicrobial activity comes with the caveat of severe toxicity. − What’s more, excessive and persistent exposure of the microbial community to amphipathic MAAs could induce AMR, as witnessed by the emergence of the colistin resistance gene in the microbiome, underscoring the crucial importance of environmental degradability of antibiotics in general.

Antibiotic nanoparticles (“nanoantibiotics”) have also emerged as strong contenders to combat AMR, and nanostructure is increasingly recognized as another important determinant of antimicrobial activity. , In particular, the exploitation of multivalent interactions by assembling individual antibiotic or amphiphilic MAA moieties onto macromolecular scaffolds, dendrimers, micelles, , supramolecular nanostructures, , star-shaped polymers, − bottlebrush polymers, − and nanoparticles − has shown significant gains in antimicrobial activities that are qualitatively different from their individual constituents. However, the collective lipophilic interactions that indiscriminately disrupt mammalian membranes would also increase as the number of amphiphilic antibiotic moieties on the nanoantibiotics increases, necessitating another round of experimentation in search of the optimal structural configuration of the multivalency − on top of the exhaustive refinement of the dedicated hydrophobic–cationic balance.

To address the Janus-faced lipophilicity in MAAs that helps kill bacteria but also impairs mammalian cells, considerable effort has been put into exploring the possibility of hydrophilic MAAs as viable candidates to fight AMR. Early work by Yang and colleagues discovered that hydrophilic and cationic long-chain polymethacrylates are strong MAAs with drastically reduced hemolytic toxicity, but it is not entirely clear whether the hydrophobic groups on these polymer chains still contribute to the hydrophobic–cationic balance often required of MAAs. Chan-Park and colleagues reported the strong antimicrobial activity and excellent biocompatibility of star-shaped polymers consisting of a polyacrylamide core grafted with copolymer brushes made up of lysine and glucosamine-functionalized lysine repeating units, and they attributed the low toxicity to the absence of hydrophobic groups on these polymers. Although polylysine is widely regarded as a hydrophilic polymer, the hemolytic activity of polylysine was reported, which is unusual for a hydrophilic polymer, and the true nature of its putative hydrophilicity was questioned. Because polylysine has 4 continuous hydrophobic methylene units linked to the cationic end amine in its pendant groups, we believe it fits nicely into the “same-center” structural model of amphipathic MAAs, which helps explain its hemolytic and antimicrobial activity. , To facilitate the penetration of hydrophilic MAAs into bacterial membranes, Pan, Cai, Wu, and colleagues reported a 4-arm star-shaped polypeptide design consisting of alternating arginine and glycine repeating units that showed broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity with negligible hemolysis, and they attributed the low hemolytic activity to the hydrophilicity of the polypeptide. Although both arginine and glycine units are generally regarded as hydrophilic, polyarginine has been recognized as “conditionally amphiphilic”, i.e., the complexation of its hydrophilic guanidinium moiety with anions (e.g., phosphate on lipid headgroups) would open its “greasy” pendant groups (i.e., 3 methylene units) for hydrophobic interactions, which in turn help polyarginine to insert into and penetrate lipid bilayers. It is important to note that the observed high aqueous solubility of a target compound does not necessarily indicate its lack of amphiphilicity. In fact, nearly all MAAs, including most cationic antimicrobial peptides, synthetic mimics of antimicrobial peptides, and quaternary ammonium cations (QACs) etc., readily dissolve in water but are intrinsically amphiphilic, and the amphiphilicity is the key contributor to their membrane-active antimicrobial activity. − Equally important, the presence of a hydrocarbon moiety in a target compound does not necessarily suggest it is amphiphilic. For example, glucose is not amphiphilic despite having 5 methine and 1 methylene groups. In the context of MAAs, amphiphilicity refers to the presence of a distinct hydrophobic patch on an overall hydrophilic compound, and the hydrophobic region is unshielded and large enough (e.g., a large benzyl group, 3 or more adjacent methylene units, etc.) to afford direct interaction with the hydrophobic membrane interior by overcoming the pull of water and revealing the classic hallmark of their membrane-active property. To experimentally identify the presence of amphiphilicity in a target compound, fluorescence methods or small-angle scatterings can be used to detect the formation of micelles or multimer complexes when the hydrophobicity is significant, but this is not always the case. More general methods include the measurement of the partition coefficient at the oil–water interface, the change in surface tension at the air–water interface, the change in retention volume in reverse-phase HPLC, or the analysis of membrane disruption through methods such as fluorescent dye leakage or hydrophobic fluorophore uptake assays using model liposome membranes to indirectly probe the contributions from amphiphilicity, etc. In a recent search for hydrophilic MAAs, Hartlieb and colleagues compared the antimicrobial activity and mammalian cell compatibility of a library of linear-chain random copolymers consisting of cationic aminoethyl acrylamide and acrylate monomers carrying different hydrocarbon moieties. The amphiphilicity of the copolymers was quantitatively examined by reverse-phase HPLC and surface tension measurements, and a subset of the copolymers was found to be membrane-active despite their lack of amphiphilicity. Although these nonamphiphilic copolymers were antimicrobially inactive against Gram+ S. aureus, they showed strong antimicrobial activity against Gram– E. coli and low cytotoxicity against mammalian cells. It was hypothesized that a combination of multivalent interactions (e.g., polymer–membrane and polymer–polymer interactions due to the electrostatic interactions and H-bonding) leads to disorder and permeabilization of the E. coli membrane, and this destabilization does not work on S. aureus because of its thick peptidoglycan layer. This discovery is significant because it shows unequivocally that nonamphiphilic linear-chain polymers can disrupt bacterial membranes in the absence of hydrophobic interactions.

We took a different approach to develop hydrophilic MAAs as potent bacteria killers with low cytotoxicity and a reduced likelihood of incurring AMR. − Inspired by phages that use proteinaceous devices first and foremost recognized by their unique nanostructures to remodel bacterial membranes, we explored hydrophilic phage-mimicking polymer molecular brushes (PMBs) and other nanostructured brush-state polymers to elaborate on their size- and shape-dependent antimicrobial activity and selectivity. − To rule out the contribution of hydrophobic interactions to the antimicrobial activity, we quantitatively measured the octanol–water partition coefficients of a series of cationic PMBs and their linear-chain brush polymer controls quaternized by alkyl iodide of different hydrocarbon lengths using their inherent optical absorption. We also analyzed the hydrophobic fluorophore uptake and the dye leakage profiles of model liposome membranes interacting with the brush-state polymers and their linear-chain polymer controls and confirmed that our target nanoantibiotics, comprised of nanostructured brush-state polymers quaternized by methyl iodide, are fully hydrophilic. − Unlike archetypal MAAs that carry lipophilic moieties liable to breach bacterial and mammalian membranes alike, the fully hydrophilic PMBs kill bacteria including AMR strains of both Gram– and Gram+ bacterial families by exclusively inducing a topological transition of microbial membranes to form poreswhich is in sharp contrast to their individual linear-chain brush polymer controls that are hydrophilic and membrane-inactivewhile maintaining excellent biocompatibility with mammalian cells. − Besides the selectivity between bacteria and mammalian cells, the model hydrophilic nanoantibiotics also showed the size- and shape-dependent selectivity between Gram– and Gram+ bacteria. For example, while the small spherical and short rod-like PMBs are potent MAAs against both families of bacteria, the long rod-like PMBs are only antimicrobially active against Gram– but inactive against Gram+ bacteria due to the selective filtering effect of the nanoporous peptidoglycan encapsulation layer on the Gram+ bacteria, which prevents the long rod-like PMBs from gaining access to the bacterial membrane. We demonstrated that the nanostructure-associated multivalent interactions between PMBs and bacterial membranes are the linchpin of this transition, for the acquired antibiotic activities are lost when nanostructures fall apart. We further identified the critical multivalency needed to transform the hydrophilic yet membrane-inactive linear-chain polymers into membrane-active PMBs. We determined that this unique mode of membrane disruption only occurs on bacteria because their abundant negative-curvature lipids (NCLs) help offset the bending energy cost to form pores; it does not occur on mammalian cells rich in zero-curvature lipids (ZCLs) because the energy barrier to bend their membranes is prohibitively high, − nor does it likely induce AMR because a lethal mutation involving NCL deletion would be required for bacteria to gain immunity. Our design concept of hydrophilic MAAs was validated in nanoantibiotics of different chemical compositions, including cellulose-based PMBs that bode well for the development of environmentally benign antibiotics. Using well-defined bioinert silica nanoparticles of systematically varied diameters to support hydrophilic polymer brushes, we elucidated the antibiotic role of nanostructures and identified the critical diameter (i.e., d ∼ 50 nm) below which the surface curvature of the nanoparticles is sufficiently large to prompt a transition from concentrated to semidilute brush regimes, a prerequisite for the hydrophilic polymer brushes to induce holes on bacterial membranes. This series of studies sheds light on combating AMR with nontoxic, membrane-active, and eco-friendly nanoantibiotics derived from biological nanoparticles. To illustrate this new concept, herein we show that cyclodextrin and phytoglycogen nanoparticles can be transformed into potent MAAs by grafting them with hydrophilic and biocompatible polymer brushes. The resultant nanoantibiotics are fully hydrophilic and retain low cytotoxicity to mammalian cells, yet they kill bacteria, including clinical AMR strains, by disrupting their membranes with a low environmental footprint, thanks to their facile degradation and deactivation by biomass-recycling enzymes abundant in ecosystems. This study illuminates a new paradigm to combat AMR with hydrophilic and eco-friendly MAAs derived from biological nanoparticles. These hydrophilic nanoantibiotics have an NCL-dependent membrane-active bactericidal mode that is less likely to incur resistance, a wider therapeutic window tolerant to drug titration, and a quicker deactivation mechanism responsive to environmental stimuli.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of Nanoantibiotics Derived from Biological Nanoparticles

We chose two different biological nanoparticles, β-cyclodextrin (CD) (d ∼ 1.5 nm) and phytoglycogen (PG) (d ∼ 20 nm), as prototypes. Their diameters differ greatly, but both are well below the critical diameter (i.e., d ∼ 50 nm) required to become membrane-active when grafted with hydrophilic polymer brushes. β-CD consists of a cyclic ring of 7 glucose subunits joined together by α-1,4 glycosidic bonds, whereas PG is a quasispherical dendrimer-like nanoparticle consisting of hyperbranched polysaccharide chains of glucose subunits linked together by α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic bonds. , We converted both nanoparticles into macroinitiators, i.e., CD-Br and PG-Br, respectively, by modifying the free hydroxyl groups on their glucose subunits with α-bromoisobutyryl bromide (BiBB) for atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP) of well-defined poly­(N,N-dimethylamino-2-ethyl methacrylate) (PDMAEMA) brushes, which were subsequently converted to hydrophilic and cationic poly­(N,N,N-trimethylamino-2-ethyl methacrylate) (PTMAEMA) via a quaternization reaction with methyl iodide. The biocompatible PDMAEMA is widely used in drug and gene delivery. − Although PDMAEMA itself showed antimicrobial and hemolytic activities, , probably due to its amphiphilic and cationic properties reminiscent of the antibiotic traits of antimicrobial peptides, − these activities diminished at increasing degrees of quaternization when it was converted to hydrophilic PTMAEMA, as its cationic quaternary amine pendant groups interact strongly with water to shield its methacrylic backbone. ,, Details of our synthesis and characterization are discussed in the Supporting Information (SI). We used an array of characterization techniques such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, gel permeation chromatography (GPC), and control experiments of linear-chain PDMAEMA grown concurrently with the brush-state polymers to determine the brush size (M n), polydispersity (Đ), and the degree of quaternization and confirmed the successful synthesis of two hydrophilic nanoantibiotics, i.e., CD-g-PTAMEMA31 (“CD-P31”) and PG-g-PTMAEMA35 (“PG-P35”), with 31 and 35 denoting the average degree of polymerization (DP) of the PTMAEMA brushes. As controls, we also synthesized linear-chain PTMAEMA32 (“P32”) and PTMAEMA789 (“P789”). P32 has a similar size to the individual brushes on CD or PG, whereas P789 has a molecular weight (∼236 kDa) comparable to that of CD-P31 (∼197 kDa).

A schematic illustration of the synthesis of PG-P35 is shown in Figure a, and the reaction design for the synthesis of both nanoantibiotics is shown in Schemes S1 and S2, respectively (SI). While the graft density on PG (i.e., the percentage of its −OH groups derivatized by the ATRP initiator) could not be determined due to the uncertain number of total glucose units as well as the accessible ones available for grafting with the ATRP initiator in each PG nanoparticle, the graft density on CD was measured to be ∼90%. To determine the brush size, we mixed the free initiator with the macroinitiator for ATRP to grow linear-chain polymers simultaneously under the same conditions, as little difference exists between the solution-grown and surface-bound polymer chains during the controlled/“living” polymerization of polymer brushes on nanoparticles. − The GPC of macroinitiator before and after ATRP confirms the successful growth of polymer brushes (Figure b), and analysis of free PDMAEMA grown concurrently reveals the brush size on CD and PG to be 31 and 35, respectively (Figure c), agreeing well with the GPC analysis of PDMAEMA brushes (Figure b). The successful synthesis of CD-P31, PG-P35, and linear-chain PTMAEMA controls is further confirmed by NMR (Figures S1 and S2), and details of their structural characterization data are summarized in Table S1. The growth of polymer brushes on the biological nanoparticles is also confirmed by TEM analysis (Figure d,e).

Antimicrobial Activity and Cytotoxicity of Model Nanoantibiotics

We used standard bacteria-killing and inhibitory assays , against Gram– E. coli and PA14, and Gram+ S. aureus and MU50, respectively, where PA14 (tobramycin- and gentamycin-resistant P. aeruginosa) and MU50 (methicillin-, oxacillin-, and vancomycin-resistant S. aureus) represent clinical AMR strains, to evaluate the antimicrobial activities of both model nanoantibiotics by measuring their minimum bactericidal concentrations (MBCs) and minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs). We also evaluated their cytotoxicity using standard hemolysis/hemagglutination assays against hRBCs to obtain the HC50 (i.e., the concentration at which 50% of the hRBCs are lysed) , and the MTT assay against HEK-293 cells to obtain the IC50 (i.e., the concentration at which the viability of HEK-293 cells is reduced by 50%), respectively. Additionally, we used live/dead cell staining assays to directly observe the vitality of bacteria and HEK-293 cells incubated with each model nanoantibiotic and their linear-chain polymer control. −

As a control for the nanoantibiotics, the hydrophilic linear-chain P32 with a similar size to that of individual polymer brushes is largely antimicrobially inactive. It does not show MBC against all four bacteria (Figure a,b,d,e), nor MIC against Gram– E. coli and PA14 (Figure c). Although it reaches MIC against Gram+ S. aureus and MU50 at 16 and 512 μg/mL, respectively (Figure c), this behavior aligns with previous reports that cationic compounds are generally bacteriostatic against Gram+ bacteria. , In contrast, grafting the hydrophilic yet antimicrobially inactive linear-chain PTMAEMA onto biological nanoparticles transforms them into potent antibiotics against all bacteria, as demonstrated by the bacterial killing assays against E. coli (Figure a), S. aureus (Figure b), PA14 (Figure d), and MU50 (Figure e), as well as the bacterial inhibitory assays against all four bacteria (Figure c). For example, the MBC of CD-P31 against the AMR PA14 and MU50 reaches 8 and 48 μg/mL, respectively, or 0.04 and 0.24 μM, which compares favorably to some of the highest antimicrobial activities reported for amphipathic MAAs. − This steep gain in antimicrobial activity due to the nanostructure-associated multivalent interactions is also vividly shown in the live–dead cell staining assays of E. coli (Figure g–j) and S. aureus (Figure k–n) in the presence of P32 (Figure h,l), CD-P31 (Figure i,m), and PG-P35 (Figure j,n), respectively, as compared to nascent E. coli (Figure g) and S. aureus (Figure k). The potency of the acquired antimicrobial activity depends on nanoparticle sizes, i.e., the smaller CD amplifies the acquired activity more significantly than the larger PG (Figure a–e), revealing an important antibiotic role of nanostructures that echoes our previous observations on the silica-based nanoantibiotic system. Considering that both monotonic and nonmonotonic − upturn in the antimicrobial activity of amphipathic MAAs at increasing molecular weights have been reported, we tested whether the increase in molecular weight due to the assembly of individual PTMAEMA chains into brush-state polymers contributes to the acquired antimicrobial activity of hydrophilic nanoantibiotics. Our control experiment with hydrophilic linear-chain P789 shows that it is largely antimicrobially inactive compared to CD-P31, despite their comparable molecular weights (Figure S4, SI). This suggests that the antimicrobial activity of hydrophilic nanoantibiotics is rooted in their nanostructure-associated multivalent interactions rather than an increase in molecular weight.

Despite the steep gain in antimicrobial activity, grafting hydrophilic linear-chain polymers onto biological nanoparticles does not compromise their biocompatibility. The hemolysis assay against hRBCs shows that both hydrophilic nanoantibiotics are nonhemolytic, just like their linear-chain brush polymer control P32, up to 1024 μg/mL that we tested (Figure f), underscoring an important difference between hydrophilic nanoantibiotics and amphipathic MAAs. The latter depends critically on the hydrophobic–cationic balance to gain antimicrobial activity while keeping cytotoxicity in check. − While cationic polymers are strongly hemagglutinative in generaland this is indeed what we observed for the linear-chain P32 and P789 controlsthe assembly of the hydrophilic PTMAEMA into brush-state polymers on nanoparticles completely suppresses their hemagglutination propensity (Figure S3, SI), suggesting an intriguing role of multivalent interactions between polycations and hRBCs. The biocompatibility of model nanoantibiotics was further verified in the MTT assay against HEK-293 cells, in which no IC50 was observed up to 512 μg/mL that we tested (Figure S5, SI). Hydrophilic Nanoantibiotics Derived from Biological Nanoparticles Are MAAs

To examine whether the hydrophilic nanoantibiotics selectively kill bacteria as membrane disruptors, we first performed the dye leakage assay using giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) that mimic bacterial and mammalian cells, respectively (SI). Unlike mammalian membranes consisting predominantly of ZCLs, microbial membranes are rich in NCLs, such as phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) or cardiolipin (CL). − For example, Gram– bacteria such as E. coli and K. pneumoniae have ∼60–80% of PE lipids in their membranes, and many Gram+ bacteria either have a high content of CL (such as S. aureus and S. pneumonia that have ∼50–60% CL) or a high content of CL + PE (such as B. cereus that has ∼40–50% PE + ∼20% CL). Following the work by Wong and colleagues, , we chose model GUVs comprised of a binary mixture of 20% (molar ratio) anionic 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-(1′-rac-glycerol) (DOPG) doped with 80% of zwitterionic 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC) or 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), respectively, to mimic the ZCL-rich mammalian and NCL-rich microbial membranes while keeping their membrane charge density identical. Both DOPG and DOPC are ZCLs, whereas DOPE is an NCL. Given the significant differences between mammalian and bacterial cells, such as membrane compositions, membrane asymmetry, and cell wall structure, it is unrealistic to rely on simple GUVs to inform all aspects of responses that ensue from mammalian or bacterial cells interacting with exogenous substances. , We chose GUVs to shed light on the initial membrane remodeling event due primarily to their membrane lipid differences and cross-checked the utility of this approach by comparing our observations with other data obtained from live cell assays.

We prepared the mammalian cell- (DOPG/DOPC = 20/80) and bacteria-mimicking GUVs (DOPG/DOPE = 20/80) loaded with fluorescein and measured the temporal fluorescein release profiles from both GUVs when put in contact (at t = 0 s) with CD-P31, PG-P35, and P32 control, respectively (SI). While the hydrophilic linear-chain P32 does not induce dye leakage in either the mammalian cell-mimicking GUVs (Figure a; also see examples shown in Figure S6, SI) or the bacteria-mimicking GUVs (Figure b; also see examples shown in Figure S7, SI), which confirms its lack of amphiphilicity, the hydrophilic nanoantibiotics show distinctly different membrane-disruptive behaviors that depend highly on the membrane lipid compositions: both CD-P31 and PG-P35 are membrane-inactive against the mammalian cell-mimicking GUVs (Figure a; also see examples shown in Figure S6, SI) as expected because of their hydrophilic nature, but they induce nearly complete dye leakage within 300 s of interactions with the bacteria-mimicking GUVs (Figure b; also see examples shown in Figure S7, SI). Despite the simplicity of the model GUVs, the observed selective membrane disruption agrees with the antimicrobial activity and cytotoxicity data of the polymers (Figure ), suggesting that the assembly of hydrophilic yet membrane-inactive linear-chain polymers into polymer brushes on biological nanoparticles transforms them into potent membrane disruptors that kill bacteria without compromising their benign stance toward mammalian cells.

The transition from hydrophilic, membrane-inactive, linear-chain polymers to membrane-active nanoantibiotics is also demonstrated in the membrane permeation assays of live bacteria (SI). Access of the hydrophobic fluorescent dye 1-N-phenylnaphthylamine (NPN) to the hydrophobic membrane interior of Gram– bacteria causes a salient increase in its fluorescent emission, signaling whether membrane disruption occurs. Similar to the dye leakage assays, no increase in NPN fluorescence is observed for E. coli incubated with the hydrophilic linear-chain P32, confirming its membrane-inactive state due to its lack of amphiphilicity (Figure c). In contrast, a drastic increase in NPN fluorescence is observed for E. coli incubated with PG-P35 and CD-P31 (Figure c), respectively, confirming again that the assembly of hydrophilic linear-chain polymers into polymer brushes on biological nanoparticles transforms them into potent membrane disruptors of bacteria.

The membrane-disruptive mode of action of hydrophilic nanoantibiotics is directly visualized by the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (X-TEM) (SI). For E. coli incubated with the membrane-inactive P32 (Figure e), no morphological difference is observed under SEM as compared to nascent E. coli (Figure d), and the integrity of bacterial membrane (marked by red arrows) is evident under X-TEM for both nascent E. coli (Figure h) and that incubated with P32 (Figure i). In contrast, SEM shows crumbled bacterial membranes for E. coli incubated with CD-P31 (Figure f) and PG-P35 (Figure g), and the disintegrated bacterial membranes (marked by red arrows) are also revealed under X-TEM for E. coli incubated with CD-P31 (Figure j) and PG-P35 (Figure k). The large PG-P35 nanoparticles, which show up as black dots (marked by blue arrows) under X-TEM, are observed both on the membrane boundary and in the cytosol (Figure k), suggesting that the hydrophilic nanoantibiotics can diffuse into the cytosol once the Gram– E. coli membrane falls apart.

Unlike Gram– bacteria, Gram+ bacteria have a cross-linked peptidoglycan outer layer encapsulating their plasma membrane, which has estimated mesh sizes ranging from ∼5 to 50 nm. , We have shown that small nanoantibiotics are able to penetrate this nanoporous layer to gain access to the bacterial membrane. − Due to the robust peptidoglycan encapsulation, no obvious difference appears under SEM between nascent S. aureus (Figure l) and that incubated with P32 (Figure m), CD-P31 (Figure n), and PG-P35 (Figure o), except for the large PG-P35 nanoparticles (marked by blue arrows) that show up as white dots on the S. aureus (Figure o). However, underneath the seemingly uneventful surface, a drastic difference is revealed under X-TEM: in contrast to the intact plasma membrane (marked by red arrows) of nascent S. aureus (Figure p) or that incubated with the linear-chain P32 (Figure q), S. aureus incubated with CD-P31 (Figure r) or PG-P35 (Figure s) shows disintegrated and discontinued plasma membranes (marked by red arrows), where the small CD-P31 and large PG-P35 nanoparticles (marked by blue arrows) that either stay with the peptidoglycan shell or cross into the cytosol are observed.

Mechanistic Insight on Selective Membrane Disruption

Although mammalian cells may uptake hydrophilic polymers via various endocytosis pathways, their membrane integrity and viability are not compromised, as evidenced by the hemolysis (Figure f) and MTT assays (Figure S5, SI), respectively. In contrast, the assembly of membrane-inactive, hydrophilic linear-chain polymers into polymer brushes on biological nanoparticles transforms them into potent membrane disruptors that selectively invade microbial membranes (Figure c–s), and the acquired antimicrobial activity (Figure ) stems from the nanostructure-associated multivalent interactions rather than a simple increase in molecular weight (Figure S4, SI). Notably, the biological activities obtained from these live cell assays agree well with the dye leakage behaviors observed using model mammalian cell- and bacteria-mimicking GUVs (Figures a,b; Figures S6 and S7, SI) that differ only in the intrinsic curvature of their primary lipids, underscoring the critical role of lipid composition in defining the initial membrane remodeling event that sets up a cascade of fates when bacteria and mammalian cells encounter the hydrophilic polymers.

We examined the hydrophilic polymer-induced membrane remodeling by synchrotron small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) using model liposomes that mimic bacterial and mammalian membranes, respectively (SI). While the SAXS of nascent liposomes shows the typical form factor of spherical vesicles, i.e., a broad diffusive diffraction hump without distinctive structural features, the SAXS of liposomes treated with different hydrophilic polymers reveals distinctly different membrane remodeling behaviors that depend on both the polymer nanostructures and the membrane lipid compositions.

For the model mammalian membrane (i.e., DOPG/DOPC = 20/80) consisting predominantly of ZCLs (Figure a), we observed equally spaced lamellar harmonics (marked by black dashed arrows) with q 001 = 0.102 Å–1, corresponding to a multilamellar structure with a periodicity of ∼62 Å that fits nicely to a DOPG/DOPC bilayer thickness (i.e., ∼44 Å , ) plus a hydration layer (i.e., ∼18 Å). The broad scattering peaks suggest loosely stacked membranes brought together by the oppositely charged polymers bridging the adjacent membranes. Interestingly, the lamellar spacing does not expand from P32 to CD-P31 and PG-35 as the hydrodynamic diameters of the polymers increase, indicating the formation of buckled membrane “bubbles” wrapping around the hydrophilic nanoparticles, which resembles the onset of endocytosis as we reported before. In reality though, mammalian cells would proceed to uptake hydrophilic polymers via various endocytosis pathways. Nonetheless, the SAXS data still provide evidence that hydrophilic polymers do not rupture mammalian membranes regardless of the polymer structures, which agrees well with the dye leakage (Figure a) and hemolysis (Figure f) assays.

The hydrophilic polymer brushes of CD-P31 (green: β-CD core; magenta: polymer brushes) interact closely with lipid head groups (white: PG; blue: PE) to induce a topological transition of the membrane to form lipid-coated pores that, in turn, pack into a 2D hexagonal lattice, where the CD-P31 rests at the pore center and the hydrophobic lipid tails (gold) project outward.

For the model microbial membrane (i.e., DOPG/DOPE = 20/80) consisting predominantly of NCLs (Figure b), we observed distinctly different membrane remodeling behaviors. In contrast to the linear-chain P32 that induces the formation of a 3D lipidic cubic phase (e.g., Pm3m) with 11 scatterings corresponding to the (1,0,0), (1,1,0), (1,1,1), (2,2,0), (3,1,1), (2,2,2), (3,3,1), (4,2,2), (4,3,1), (4,4,0), and (5,3,1) reflections (marked by black arrows) of the cubic structure with a unit cell of 103 Å (q 100 = 0.061 Å–1), both hydrophilic nanoantibiotics derived from biological nanoparticles induce the formation of a 2D hexagonal membrane structure with slightly different lattice sizes. For the small CD-P31 (red trace, Figure b), the first 8 scatterings of the 2D hexagonal structure with a lattice parameter (a) of 72 Å (q 10 = 0.101 Å–1) are observed and fit nicely to the Miller indices (inset, Figure b). For the large PG-P35 (blue trace, Figure b), the first 7 scatterings of the 2D hexagonal structure with a reduced lattice parameter (a = 69 Å, or q 10 = 0.105 Å–1) are observed. The fundamentally different membrane remodeling behavior between hydrophilic nanoantibiotics and their linear-chain brush polymer control suggests that microbial membranes exhibit radically different responses toward hydrophilic polymers when nanostructure comes into play, which subsequently defines the different fates of bacteria when they encounter these hydrophilic polymers (Figure ). It is worth noting that this difference stems from the nanostructure-associated multivalent interactions rather than a simple increase in molecular weight, as the linear-chain P789, which has a comparable molecular weight to CD-P31, also induces the formation of the 3D lipidic cubic phase (e.g., Pm3m) just like P32 does, albeit with a larger unit cell of 117 Å (q 100 = 0.054 Å–1) due to the increased polymer size (Figure S4, SI).

To gain mechanistic insight into the correlations between the observed bactericidal activity of hydrophilic nanoantibiotics and their unique membrane remodeling behaviors, we performed Fourier reconstruction to reveal the electron density map of the CD-P31 remodeled membrane. Based on the phase criteria developed by Turner and Gruner, our phase choices are (+--+++++). The electron density map at 1D (Figure c) along the unit cell axis (e.g., x-axis), as well as 2D along the lattice plane defined by both unit cell axes (Figure d), reveals a transition of the lipid bilayer into the classical 2D inverted lipid columnar phase (i.e., H // ), the epitome of membrane pore formation. Along the unit cell axis, the lowest electron density is observed in-between the hexagonally packed pores, which is attributed to the hydrocarbon tails (ρ = 0.29 e/Å3) of lipids because they have the lowest electron density in the membrane. At the center of the pores, the highest electron density (ρ = 0.63 e/Å3) is observed. This electron density is much higher than any component of the membrane and can be only assigned to the polymer brushes on CD-P31 because the quaternized PTMAEMA that carries iodide counterions has the highest electron density in the system. Surrounding the CD-P31, a rim of the second-highest electron density (ρ = 0.55 e/Å3) appears, which is assigned to phospholipid headgroups because they have the next highest electron density in the system. This electron density is higher than that of typical phospholipid headgroups (0.41 e/Å3), suggesting the presence of residual iodide counterions from the polymer brushes closely associated with the membrane, possibly due to their mismatched charge density. In-between CD-P31 and the surrounding rim of tilted lipid headgroups, a drop in electron density (ρ = 0.47 e/Å3) is observed, which is attributed to the presence of an aqueous pore filled with water (ρ = 0.33 e/Å3) , and semidilute polymer brushes with partially released iodide counterions. The diameter of this pore (d ∼ 4.3 nm) could accommodate the entire CD-P31, although we cannot rule out the possibility that CD-P31 is only partially embedded. Within the area in-between adjacent pores, the electron density is not homogeneous (Figure d). Instead, it reveals a lower electron density along the unit cell axis direction (ρ = 0.29 e/Å3) and a higher one along the diagonal direction (ρ = 0.37 e/Å3), suggesting membrane buckling due to the inward bending of lipids to accommodate the insertion of CD-P31, a harbinger of membrane crumbling to follow. Taken together, a schematic view of the CD-P31 remodeled membrane illustrated on the basis of its electron density map is shown (Figure e). The different lattice sizes of H // remodeled by CD-P31 and PG-P35, respectively, reflect their different modes of membrane pore formation: while small CD-P31 induces a topological transition of the membrane to encircle itself, large PG-P35 (d ∼ 28 nm, Figure e) that is too big to fit into the membrane but still below the critical nanoparticle size (d ∼ 50 nm) favors a transition from concentrated to semidilute brush regimes of its polymer brushes to become membrane-active, where individually grouped polymer strands induce a topological transition of the membrane to form pores instead. This membrane-active mode of action is consistent with our previous discovery in the silica-based nanoantibiotic system.

The SAXS analysis of how model mammalian and microbial membranes interact with hydrophilic polymers reveals fundamentally different membrane-active behaviors that depend on both the lipid composition (i.e., intrinsic curvature) and polymer nanostructure (i.e., multivalent interactions). While the model mammalian membrane retains its uncompromised integrity when interacting with all hydrophilic polymers, the model microbial membrane undergoes radically different structural changes when interacting with linear-chain and brush-state polymers, respectively, which reveal their different membrane-disruptive activity. The formation of a 3D lipidic cubic phase is arguably less relevant to the true responses from bacteria, as their 2D membrane held in place by the peptidoglycans will unlikely undergo a structural reorganization in 3D. Our previous studies have linked this hydrophilic polymer-induced structural change to a herald of uncompromised microbial membranes. − The topological transition of 2D microbial membranes into the 2D H ∥ , on the other hand, is directly relevant and illuminates the antimicrobial activity of hydrophilic nanoantibiotics with great insight. The fundamentally different responses from the two model membranes are rooted in only one change of their primary lipid from ZCL to NCL, underscoring the critical role of lipid intrinsic curvature in transforming the hydrophilic yet membrane-inactive linear-chain polymers into membrane-active nanoantibiotics. When hydrophilic polymers encounter the oppositely charged cell membranes, the polymers would naturally adhere to the membranes. While endocytosis and cellular uptake of the membrane-adhered polymers may occur for mammalian cells, bacteria in general (although exceptions may exist) are not known to have the endocytosis pathways. What sets apart the brush-state polymers grafted on biological nanoparticles from their linear-chain polymer controls are the multivalent polymer–membrane interactions that collectively buckle the membrane toward the brush-state polymers. This topological transition proceeds to form pores only on microbial membranes because their rich NCLs help offset the bending energy cost; it does not occur on mammalian membranes rich in ZCLs, because the bending energy cost would be prohibitively high. By grafting hydrophilic polymer brushes on biological nanoparticles, we create a new type of membrane-active nanoantibiotics that selectively kill bacteria by disrupting their membranes without harming mammalian cells.

Environmental Degradability of Hydrophilic Nanoantibiotics Derived from Biological Nanoparticles

Many plant-based biological nanoparticles cannot be effectively degraded by enzymes in human plasma. For example, it has been shown that intravenously injected β-CD is excreted intact via the kidney. , However, both β-CD and PG can be broken down by biomass recycling enzymes abundant in ecosystems, suggesting a bioorthogonal deactivation strategy when they are discharged into natural habitats after service. The unique environmentally degradable antibiotics derived from biological nanoparticles differ from biodegradable antibiotics that break down in vivo ,− with less likelihood of incurring AMR, because the continuous loss of antimicrobial activity while in use would potentially encourage bacteria to develop resistance. They also help resolve the missing link in fighting AMR, i.e., mitigating the continuous escalation of resistome due to the accumulation of antibiotic waste in ecosystems. −

To test the environmental degradability of hydrophilic nanoantibiotics derived from biological nanoparticles, we performed enzymatic degradation of CD-P31 by incubation with α-amylase from Aspergillus oryzae at 30 °C for 3 h (SI). GPC comparison of nascent CD-P31, degraded CD-P31, and linear-chain P32 control shows that nearly all CD-P31 is shredded into smaller pieces with a substrate degradation rate of ∼3.3 mg/g (buffer)/h, among which the major fraction appears to be the cleaved brush polymer chains with an elution volume overlapping that of the linear-chain P32 control (Figure a). This enzymatic degradation was further confirmed by viscosity measurements (Table S2, SI). The intrinsic viscosity of CD-P31 is 77.1 mL/g; it drops to 42.6 mL/g after the enzymatic degradation, approaching that of the linear-chain P32 control (i.e., 43.0 mL/g). In contrast to nascent CD-P31 that causes a salient NPN fluorescent increase when put in contact with live E. coli (Figure c), the degraded CD-P31 behaves just like the linear-chain P32 and does not cause a noticeable change in NPN fluorescence in live E. coli (Figure b; the vertical axis range is set to be the same as that in Figure c for comparison), suggesting enzymatic degradation of CD-P31 eliminates its membrane-disruptive activity. The degraded CD-P31 shows very similar membrane remodeling behaviors to the linear-chain P32 control: when interacting with model mammalian membranes (black trace, Figure c), synchrotron SAXS reveals loosely bound membrane stacks with a lamellar periodicity of ∼62 Å (q 001 = 0.102 Å–1); when interacting with model microbial membranes (red trace, Figure c), a series of scatterings positioned at 0.104, 0.178, 0.187, 0.208, 0.216, 0.268, 0.312, 0.354, and 0.369 Å–1 show up, which have a relationship of 3 : 3 : 10 : 12 : 13 : 20 : 27 : 35 : 38 , and fit nicely with the (1,1,1), (2,2,1), (3,1,0), (2,2,2), (3,2,0), (4,2,0), (3,3,3), (5,3,1), and (5,3,2) reflections (Figure c inset), respectively, of a lipidic cubic phase (e.g., Pm3m) with a lattice parameter of 105 Å. This cubic structure is almost identical to that observed (i.e., lattice parameter ∼ 103 Å) when the linear-chain P32 interacts with the model microbial membrane (Figure ), indicating that the degraded CD-P31 no longer induces a topological transition of microbial membranes to form pores. The loss of membrane-active antimicrobial activity of degraded CD-P31 is further shown in the bacterial killing assays, where no MBC is observed against all four bacteria up to 512 μg/mL of degraded CD-P31 that we tested (Figure d). For live E. coli and S. aureus incubated with degraded CD-P31, neither bacterial morphological change nor membrane damage is observed under SEM (Figure e,g) and X-TEM (Figure f,h), respectively. This series of observations is in line with the structural characterization that enzymatic degradation shreds CD-P31 into the membrane-inactive linear-chain polymers and small pieces of degraded glycans (Figure a), vindicating once again that nanostructure is the linchpin of the acquired antimicrobial activity that transforms the hydrophilic yet membrane-inactive linear-chain polymers into potent membrane-active nanoantibiotics.

It should be noted that α-amylase is only one of the many biomass recycling enzymes abundant in ecosystems, and the environmental concentrations of these enzymes are not fixed values but are highly variable depending on the local microbial population, organic matter content, seasonal shifts, pH, temperature, etc. For example, the α-amylase activity of an agricultural soil sample and a river sediment obtained from the River Wye in Derbyshire, England was measured to be ∼320 μg/g (soil)/h and ∼15 mg/g (sediment)/h, respectively, in terms of substrate (i.e., starch) degradation rate at 30 °C. The α-amylase activity used in our benchtop assay (i.e., ∼3.3 mg/g (buffer)/h substrate degradation rate) falls right into the range of α-amylase activity revealed in this field study. Nevertheless, this benchtop assay is not about faithfully replicating the degradation conditions in natural habitats, as they vary widely. Rather, it serves as a relevant tool to demonstrate the environmental degradability of the hydrophilic nanoantibiotics derived from biological nanoparticles. Given that the concentration of the antibiotic waste from these nanoantibiotics, if used clinically, would be orders of magnitude lower in the environment than that used in the benchtop assay (i.e., ∼mg/mL), our benchtop assay suggests that their rapid environmental degradation and deactivation are possible. This unique design of environmentally degradable nanoantibiotics opens a bioorthogonal deactivation strategy to swiftly eradicate antibiotic waste when it is discharged into natural habitats after service, hence reducing the possibility of continuously escalating the development of resistome.

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrate a new paradigm to combat AMR with hydrophilic, nontoxic, and eco-friendly MAAs derived from biological nanoparticles that overcome some of the critical limitations of current antibiotics. These hydrophilic nanoantibiotics selectively kill bacteria by inducing a 2D topological transition of microbial membranes to form pores due to their high content of NCLs, a bactericidal mode that is less likely to incur AMR. In contrast to archetypal MAAs that carry lipophilic moieties liable to breach bacterial and mammalian membranes alike, these hydrophilic nanoantibiotics show high compatibility with mammalian cells, hence supporting a wide therapeutic window that is tolerant to drug titration. Last but not least, these hydrophilic nanoantibiotics can be quickly deactivated by biomass-recycling enzymes abundant in ecosystems once discharged into the environment after clinical services, hence helping resolve the lingering issue of resistome escalation due to the ever-increasing accumulation of antibiotic waste in natural habitats. Besides the natural scaffolds being exploited as a source of antibiotics for decades, our study illuminates that the biorenewable and eco-friendly biological nanoparticles also have great potential in the development of next-generation antibiotics to combat AMR.

Statistical Analysis
