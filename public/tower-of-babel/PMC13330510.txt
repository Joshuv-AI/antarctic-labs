Acute and chronic toxicity of potassium bromate (KBrO₃) in zebrafish (Danio rerio): Behavioural and hepatic effects
Authors: Sandeep R. Reddy, Manjunatha Bangeppagari, Prabhakar Kamarthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Potassium bromate (KBrO₃) is a known food additive and environmental pollutant with documented oxidative and genotoxic effects in mammals, yet its impact on aquatic organisms remains poorly characterized. To date, studies integrating behavioural, physiological, and histopathological endpoints for KBrO₃ toxicity in zebrafish have been limited. This study aimed to evaluate the acute and chronic toxicity of KBrO₃ in zebrafish (Danio rerio), focusing on behavioural and hepatic responses. Adult zebrafish were exposed to graded concentrations of KBrO₃ for 96 h following OECD Guideline 203 to determine median lethal concentration (LC50) values. Behavioural parameters, including swimming speed, exploratory activity, and vertical zone preference, were analyzed. For chronic toxicity, fish were exposed to KBrO₃ for 21 days, and liver tissues were examined histologically following hematoxylin and eosin staining. Random blood glucose was measured immediately post-exposure. The 96-h LC₅₀ value for KBrO₃ was estimated as 1052.6 mg L⁻¹ . Acute exposure was associated with reduced locomotor activity, including decreases in swimming speed and exploration rate, along with increased bottom-zone activity. Chronic exposure (21 days) was associated with elevated blood glucose levels and hepatic alterations including vacuolization, sinusoidal dilation, hepatocellular degeneration, and necrosis. KBrO₃ exposure induced measurable behavioural, physiological, and hepatic alterations in zebrafish, demonstrating that sublethal biological effects occur at concentrations below those causing acute mortality. These findings provide baseline hazard characterization data for KBrO₃ in an aquatic vertebrate model and support future studies aimed at environmental risk assessment and mechanistic evaluation of bromate toxicity.

Introduction

Potassium bromate (KBrO₃) is a strong oxidizing agent that has been historically used in various applications, including food production, cosmetics, and industrial processing. While previously common as a flour improver in bread-making, KBrO₃ is now recognized as a significant public health concern due to its toxicological and carcinogenic potential. Extensive studies in rodents have shown that KBrO₃ is a complete carcinogen, capable of initiating and promoting tumors in multiple organs, particularly the kidneys, thyroid, and peritoneum, with evidence of renal cell tumors and mesotheliomas in rats [1], [2]. The toxic effects of KBrO₃ in mammalian systems have been largely attributed to oxidative and genotoxic pathways, including the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), DNA damage, chromosomal aberrations, and impairment of endogenous antioxidant defences [3]. These toxicological concerns are reflected in international risk assessments. KBrO₃ is classified by the IARC [4] as a Group 2B possible human carcinogen and is regarded by regulatory agencies including the USEPA [5] and EFSA [6] as a genotoxic and carcinogenic contaminant of concern. Studies continue to identify oxidative DNA damage, redox imbalance, glutathione depletion, and mutagenic effects as key toxicological features underlying its adverse health outcomes [3], [7], [8], [9], [10]. However, the extent to which these mechanisms operate in aquatic vertebrates remains insufficiently characterized. In humans, exposure to KBrO₃ can result in symptoms ranging from eye and throat irritation to gastrointestinal distress. Of particular concern is its detection in commercial bread samples in some developing countries, where KBrO₃ levels exceed international safety thresholds, posing risks of long-term carcinogenic exposure [11], [12].

In response to these toxicological concerns, the use of KBrO₃ in food products has been restricted or prohibited in several countries, while regulatory approaches vary globally. International drinking-water regulations are largely harmonized around a bromate limit of 0.01 mg L⁻¹ (10 μg L⁻¹), adopted by the WHO [13], USEPA, the European Union and several other regulatory authorities. Beyond human health concerns, bromate has emerged as an environmental contaminant associated with industrial activities and water-treatment processes. Existing aquatic toxicity studies indicate relatively low acute toxicity of bromate compounds, with EC₅₀ values exceeding 100 mg L⁻¹ for several aquatic organisms and reported 96-h median lethal concentration (LC₅₀) values in fish approaching 1 g L⁻¹ [14]. Similarly, Wang et al. [15] reported a 96-h LC₅₀ of 931.4 mg L⁻¹ for KBrO₃ in zebrafish, confirming the relatively low acute sensitivity of this species to bromate exposure. However, available studies have predominantly focused on mortality, developmental, and reproductive endpoints, while behavioural, physiological, and tissue-level responses remain comparatively underexplored, particularly within integrated vertebrate toxicity frameworks (Z. W. [15], [16]).

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) are a widely accepted model in aquatic toxicology due to their genetic similarity to humans, rapid development, transparent embryos, and suitability for behavioural, histopathological, biochemical, and mechanistic toxicological assessments [17]. The integration of these endpoints enables evaluation of toxicant-induced effects across multiple levels of biological organization, from organismal responses to tissue-level alterations [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]. Although KBrO₃ toxicity has been extensively investigated in mammalian systems, comparable studies integrating behavioural, physiological, and histopathological endpoints in zebrafish remain limited.

Therefore, the present study was designed to characterize the acute and chronic toxicity of KBrO₃ in adult zebrafish under controlled laboratory conditions using mortality, behavioural performance, random blood glucose and hepatic histopathology as complementary endpoints. The specific objectives were to determine acute toxicity thresholds, evaluate sublethal behavioural and physiological responses, and assess hepatic alterations following chronic exposure. While mechanistic investigations were beyond the scope of the present work, the integrated endpoint approach provides baseline toxicity data and establishes a foundation for future studies aimed at elucidating the molecular pathways underlying bromate-induced toxicity.

Materials and methods

Zebrafish husbandry

Adult wild-type zebrafish (Danio rerio), aged 7–9 months and measuring 3–5 cm in length, with an approximately equal sex ratio (1:1 male), were maintained in 40-L aquaria under standard laboratory conditions as described by [23]. Fish were acclimated for two weeks prior to experimentation and were fed commercial micro pellets (Gemma, Skretting, France) twice daily. Fish were maintained in dechlorinated, aerated freshwater. Tap water was aerated for at least 24 h prior to use to stabilize dissolved oxygen levels before being introduced into the aquaria. Water quality parameters were routinely monitored and maintained within acceptable limits: temperature 28 ± 0.5 °C, pH 7.0–7.5, dissolved oxygen > 6 mg L⁻¹ , salinity < 0.5 ppt, ammonia (NH₃) < 0.01 mg L⁻¹ , and nitrite (NO₂⁻) < 0.02 mg L⁻¹ . A 14 h light/10 h dark photoperiod was maintained throughout the acclimation and experimental periods. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC), Sri Devaraj Urs Medical College, Kolar, Karnataka, India (Approval No. IAEC/PHARMA/SDUMC/2023–2024/20).

Acute toxicity test

Acute toxicity of KBrO₃ was evaluated in zebrafish following OECD Test Guideline No. 203. KBrO₃ (Merck, CAS No: 7758–01–2) was dissolved in normal water which is used for fishes to prepare five exposure concentrations: 500, 750, 1000, 1250, and 1500 mg L⁻¹ . The concentration range was selected based on preliminary range-finding experiments, which indicated minimal mortality at lower concentrations and substantial mortality at concentrations approaching 1000 mg L⁻¹ . These concentrations were chosen to encompass both sublethal and lethal responses and facilitate reliable estimation of the median lethal concentration (LC₅₀). The selected concentrations were intended for hazard characterization and did not represent environmentally relevant exposure levels. Zebrafish of both sexes were randomly assigned to treatment groups and a control group, with seven fish per concentration and three independent replicates per group, maintained under static condition for 96-hour. Exposure solutions were freshly prepared prior to use. No food was provided during exposure. Fish were observed at 24-h intervals for mortality and visible abnormalities. The absence of opercular movement and lack of response to tactile stimuli confirmed death. The 96-h LC₅₀ value was calculated using logistic regression analysis and expressed with 95% confidence intervals.

Behavioural assessment

Following acute exposure to KBrO₃, adult zebrafish were transferred to a chemical-free environment for a 3-day recovery period before behavioural assessment, following the general behavioural assessment protocol described by [24], with video acquisition and data analysis performed using ToxTrac software (version 2.60). Behavioural assays were conducted using surviving fish that remained active and physiologically stable following the acute exposure experiment. Three individuals per concentration were selected and evaluated individually to avoid social interaction effects during locomotor analysis. Individual fish were placed into a transparent glass tank (60 × 30 × 30 cm) under standardized lighting and temperature conditions. Fish were randomly selected from exposure tanks, and testing order was alternated between groups to minimize tank-order effects. After a short acclimation period (1 min), locomotor activity was video-recorded for 5 min. Recording was performed using a single camera positioned laterally to the tank (side view). The tank was visually divided into two equal zones (upper and lower halves) to evaluate vertical distribution behaviour. Prior to analysis, recorded videos were visually inspected to ensure continuous fish detection and accurate tracking throughout the observation period. Behavioural parameters including average swimming speed (mm s⁻¹), exploration rate (%), and total distance travelled in the upper and lower zones (mm) were analysed using ToxTrac software (version 2.60). Average swimming speed was calculated as the total distance travelled divided by the observation period. Exploration rate was defined as the percentage of the arena area visited by an individual fish during the recording period. Vertical distribution behaviour was assessed by quantifying the cumulative distance travelled within the predefined upper and lower halves of the test arena. Behavioural data were extracted automatically using ToxTrac and subsequently reviewed for tracking accuracy.

Chronic exposure

A chronic toxicity experiment was conducted to assess the sublethal physiological effects of KBrO₃ in adult zebrafish. Based on the 96-hour LC₅₀ value determined from the acute toxicity assay (1052.60 mg L⁻¹), a sublethal concentration equivalent to 1/10th LC₅₀ (105.26 mg L⁻¹) was selected for chronic exposure. Two groups were maintained: a control group (exposed to dechlorinated water) and a KBrO₃-treated group (exposed to 105.26 mg L⁻¹ KBrO₃). Ten fish per group were housed in aerated glass tanks containing approximately 20 L of dechlorinated, aerated water (approximately 1 fish per 2 L of water) under semi-static conditions for 21 days. Exposure water was renewed daily, and freshly prepared KBrO₃ solutions were added following each renewal. No chemical analysis of residual bromate was performed, and exposure concentrations are reported as nominal values. All fish were fed a standard commercial micro-pellet diet (Gemma) once daily throughout the experiment. During the chronic exposure experiment, fish were maintained collectively within a single tank per treatment group. Therefore, the tank represented the experimental exposure environment, while individual fish measurements were recorded as observational biological responses within that shared exposure condition.

Random blood sugar estimation

Following 21 days of exposure, zebrafish (n = 10 per group) were euthanized via hypothermic shock and Blood sampling was conducted by gentle incision, allowing passive release of blood, avoiding tissue compression or contamination. blood released from the incision site was carefully collected and applied directly onto a glucose test strip inserted into a glucometer (Accu-Chek®, Roche). The device displayed random blood glucose levels (mg/dL) for each fish. All measurements were taken promptly post-euthanasia to minimize degradation or glucose fluctuation [25].

Histopathological examination of liver

After blood sampling and liver tissues were collected for histological evaluation. The excised livers were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Sections of 4 μm thickness were cut from each embedded tissue block, mounted on glass slides, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for microscopic observation. Histological processing and staining were performed following standard procedures routinely used for zebrafish tissue evaluation [26]. Liver histology was examined under an Olympus light microscope, Japan, and representative images were captured. Liver sections were examined for common pathological features including cytoplasmic vacuolization, sinusoidal dilation, hepatocellular degeneration, and necrosis using semi-quantitative scoring. Each lesion was graded on a four-point scale (0 = absent, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe) based on the extent and distribution of the observed changes.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM (standard error of the mean) for behavioural analyses and mean ± SD (standard deviation) where indicated. Data were assessed for distribution and variance patterns prior to applying parametric tests. LC₅₀ values were calculated using logistic regression. Behavioural parameters were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, while non-parametric data were analyzed using Mann–Whitney U test in GraphPad Prism version 10 and blood glucose data were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test. A significance level of α = 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Acute toxicity test and LC₅₀ determination

A graded increase in mortality was observed across the tested concentrations. No mortality was observed in the 500 and 750 mg L⁻¹ groups. The 1000 mg L⁻¹ group exhibited partial mortality (42.9%, 3/7 fish), while 100% mortality was recorded at 1500 mg L⁻¹ after 96 h. Logistic regression analysis yielded a 96-h LC₅₀ value of 1052.60 mg L⁻¹ (95% CI: 919–1218 mg L⁻¹). This LC₅₀ value was used solely for the selection of a nominal sublethal concentration for subsequent chronic exposure experiments.

Behavioural analysis

Behavioural analysis revealed concentration-dependent alterations in zebrafish locomotor and exploratory activity following acute KBrO₃ exposure. Average swimming speed decreased from approximately 118 mm s⁻¹ in the control group to 88, 86, 48, and 41 mm s⁻¹ in the 500, 750, 1000, and 1250 mg L⁻¹ groups, respectively, with the greatest reduction observed at 1250 mg L⁻¹ (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1). Similarly, exploration rate decreased from approximately 57% in controls to 32% and 26% at 1000 and 1250 mg L⁻¹ , respectively, with statistically significant reductions observed at these concentrations (p < 0.001 and p < 0.0001, respectively) (Fig. 1). Vertical zone preference analysis revealed a marked reduction in total distance travelled in the upper zone at 1000 and 1250 mg L⁻¹ (p < 0.001 and p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2). Histopathological findings

Histopathological examination of liver tissues revealed clear, structural alterations in zebrafish exposed to KBrO₃ for 21 days. In control fish, hepatocytes displayed normal polygonal architecture with distinct nuclei and intact sinusoids. In contrast, liver sections from the KBrO₃-exposed group showed marked histopathological changes characterized by cytoplasmic vacuolization, nuclear pyknosis, hepatocellular degeneration, and occasional necrotic areas. Sinusoidal dilation and congestion were also evident, along with disorganization of hepatic cords. Semi-quantitative scoring (Table 1) indicated increased lesion severity in KBrO₃-exposed fish compared with controls. Vacuolization increased from scores of 0–1 in controls to 2–3 following exposure, while sinusoidal dilation and hepatocellular degeneration increased from 0 to 2. Necrotic lesions, absent in controls, were observed at scores ranging from 1 to 2 in exposed fish. Discussion

The present study demonstrates that KBrO₃ exposure produces measurable behavioural suppression, metabolic disturbance, and hepatic structural alterations in adult zebrafish under controlled laboratory conditions. Although oxidative stress has been widely implicated in KBrO₃ toxicity in mammalian systems, the present findings should be interpreted as phenotypic and physiological responses that are consistent with, but do not directly demonstrate, oxidative stress–associated mechanisms.

The significant reduction in swimming speed and exploration rate, along with increased bottom-dwelling behaviour, indicates stress and neurobehavioral suppression following acute exposure. Such behavioural suppression is commonly interpreted as a general stress-related or toxicity-associated response in zebrafish, reflecting altered neurophysiological or energetic status rather than a specific emotional or cognitive state. Recent zebrafish studies have similarly reported reduced exploration, altered locomotor activity, increased bottom-dwelling behaviour, and impaired behavioural performance following exposure to environmental contaminants including caffeine, heavy metals, pharmaceuticals, and plastic-associated pollutants, supporting the utility of behavioural endpoints as sensitive indicators of toxicological stress [27], [28], [29]. Similar reductions in locomotor activity and exploratory behaviour have been reported in rodents following KBrO₃ exposure [30], [31], [32]. The behavioural assessment was performed on a limited number of individuals per treatment group. While this design allowed preliminary identification of behavioural alterations following acute exposure, the relatively small sample size may limit statistical power and should therefore be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive evidence of behavioural toxicity. In the absence of direct neurochemical or oxidative measurements, the present behavioural findings are best interpreted as integrative indicators of organism-level stress and toxicity, reflecting functional impairment rather than pathway-specific mechanisms.

The chronic exposure phase results in significantly elevated random blood glucose levels, suggesting altered metabolic homeostasis. Elevated blood glucose is widely recognized as a secondary physiological stress response in teleost fish and has been reported following exposure to pesticides, heavy metals, pharmaceuticals, and other environmental contaminants. Recent zebrafish studies have demonstrated that contaminant exposure can disrupt glucose homeostasis through alterations in energy metabolism, liver function, endocrine regulation, and insulin signalling, resulting in sustained hyperglycaemia during chronic exposure [33], [34]. In fish, elevated glucose is frequently reported as a secondary physiological response to prolonged chemical stress, mediated through activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–interrenal axis and associated hormonal signalling [35], [36]. Similar metabolic disturbances, including hyperglycemia and impaired energy metabolism, have been documented in KBrO₃-treated rodents [7], [37]. The elevation in blood glucose observed following chronic exposure is interpreted as a physiological stress-associated response, providing supportive evidence of systemic disturbance rather than a standalone marker of metabolic pathology. In fish, activation of neuroendocrine stress pathways, particularly the hypothalamic–pituitary–interrenal (HPI) axis, promotes glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, resulting in increased circulating glucose that supports physiological adaptation to toxicant exposure and maintenance of homeostasis (L. [38], [39], [40]).

Histopathological analysis of the liver reveals structural alterations including cytoplasmic vacuolization, hepatocellular degeneration, nuclear pyknosis, focal necrosis, and sinusoidal dilation following chronic KBrO₃ exposure. These lesions are non-specific and may arise from multiple toxic pathways, including oxidative injury, disrupted cellular metabolism, or general cytotoxic stress. These findings align with previous reports of KBrO₃-induced tissue degeneration and necrosis in the liver, brain, and kidney of rodents [7], [30], [41]. Recent zebrafish toxicology studies have likewise demonstrated that environmental contaminants can induce hepatocellular degeneration, cytoplasmic vacuolization, oxidative imbalance, and metabolic dysfunction, highlighting the liver as a sensitive target organ for toxicological assessment [42], [43]. In the absence of complementary biochemical or molecular data, the histopathological alterations are interpreted as clear evidence of hepatic injury, while detailed mechanistic characterization of hepatotoxic pathways remains beyond the scope of the present study.

KBrO₃ is a well-established oxidizing agent capable of generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), promoting lipid peroxidation, inducing oxidative DNA damage, and disrupting cellular redox homeostasis in experimental models [8], [9], [44]. Excessive ROS production has been associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular degeneration, inflammatory responses, altered energy metabolism, and tissue injury across multiple organ systems [45], [46], [47]. Although oxidative biomarkers were not quantified in the present study, the concurrent occurrence of behavioural alterations, elevated glucose levels, and hepatic lesions is consistent with biological responses frequently reported following exposure to oxidative toxicants in zebrafish and other vertebrate models. Therefore, oxidative imbalance remains a plausible mechanistic explanation for the observed effects and warrants direct biochemical validation in future investigations.

From an ecotoxicological perspective, the toxicity thresholds observed in zebrafish are consistent with previous reports indicating relatively low sensitivity of aquatic organisms to bromate compounds. For example, Hutchinson et al. reported EC₅₀ values exceeding 100 mg L⁻¹ for aquatic invertebrates, and Richardson et al. documented limited acute lethality in early life stages of fish species [14], [48]. The LC₅₀ value obtained in the present study (≈1052 mg L⁻¹) therefore exceeds the thresholds used in the Globally Harmonized System (GHS) for classification of substances as hazardous to the aquatic environment. Under these criteria, KBrO₃ would not be classified as acutely hazardous to aquatic organisms.

Importantly, environmental monitoring studies typically report bromate concentrations in natural waters and treated drinking water in the µg L⁻¹ range, which are several orders of magnitude lower than the effect concentrations identified in laboratory toxicity assays. When these environmental concentrations are considered alongside the experimentally derived LC₅₀ values, KBrO₃ would therefore be expected to present low acute hazard to aquatic organisms under environmentally realistic exposure conditions.

Nevertheless, the present study highlights that sublethal physiological and behavioural responses can still occur under controlled experimental exposure conditions, emphasizing the value of organism-level endpoints for detecting early biological responses to oxidative contaminants. These findings should therefore be interpreted primarily within the context of hazard characterization rather than environmental risk prediction.

The present study focused on the liver because it represents a primary organ involved in xenobiotic metabolism and detoxification in fish. Future investigations should extend assessments to additional target organs such as the kidney and brain and incorporate biochemical endpoints including antioxidant enzymes, lipid peroxidation markers, and liver function biomarkers to provide a more comprehensive mechanistic evaluation of KBrO₃ toxicity. Zebrafish provide a valuable alternative model due to their high genetic and physiological homology to humans, well-characterized stress responses, and transparent biology that allows non-invasive analysis of toxicological processes [9], [17], [49], [50], [51], [52].

Conclusion

KBrO3 exposure produced measurable behavioural, physiological, and hepatic alterations in adult zebrafish under controlled laboratory conditions. Acute exposure resulted in concentration-dependent reductions in swimming speed, exploration rate, and vertical zone activity, with the most pronounced behavioural effects observed at 1000–1250 mg L⁻¹ . The 96-h LC₅₀ was determined to be 1052.60 mg L⁻¹ , indicating relatively low acute lethality but clear sublethal behavioural impairment at lower concentrations. Chronic exposure to 105.26 mg L⁻¹ (1/10th LC₅₀) for 21 days was associated with elevated blood glucose levels and histopathological alterations including vacuolization, sinusoidal dilation, hepatocellular degeneration, and necrosis. Collectively, these findings provide baseline evidence of KBrO3 toxicity in zebrafish and support the utility of integrated behavioural, physiological, and histopathological endpoints for hazard characterization. Future studies should incorporate environmentally relevant exposure concentrations together with biochemical and molecular biomarkers to clarify the mechanisms underlying KBrO3 toxicity in aquatic organisms.
