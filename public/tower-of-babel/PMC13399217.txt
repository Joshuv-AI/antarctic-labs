Electrochemical technologies for removal of antibiotic resistance genes from wastewater: a comprehensive review of processes, mechanisms, and influencing factors
Authors: Zhonghong Li, Min Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The persistence and dissemination of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in aquatic ecosystems pose increasing risks to public health and ecological stability. The limited capacity of conventional wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) to reduce ARG abundance and transferability highlights the need for advanced mitigation technologies. Electrochemical technologies exhibit significant potential for ARG elimination due to their in situ generation of potent oxidants, mild reaction conditions, operational flexibility, and environmental compatibility. This review systematically summarizes recent advancements in electrochemical technologies for removing ARGs from wastewater, critically evaluating the technical characteristics and efficiencies of diverse processes such as anodic oxidation, electro-Fenton, three-dimensional electrodes, and hybrid systems. The fundamental mechanisms governing direct and indirect oxidation pathways are comprehensively examined by elucidating the distinct roles of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive chlorine species (RCS) in inactivating host bacteria versus degrading intracellular and extracellular ARGs. Furthermore, the influence of critical factors such as electrode material properties, operational parameters, and water matrix complexity on ARG removal efficacy is analyzed in depth. Current technical and environmental challenges regarding energy consumption, long-term electrode stability, toxic byproduct generation, and limitations in functional assessment frameworks are also identified. Finally, future research perspectives are proposed, emphasizing the development of novel low-energy and highly selective electrode materials, investigation of micro-interface reaction mechanisms in complex environments, establishment of standardized risk assessment protocols, and application of multi-omics analyses to characterize microbial responses. This review aims to provide theoretical guidance and technical support for advancing electrochemical technologies in ARG pollution control.

Introduction

The widespread use of antibiotics in clinical medicine and animal husbandry has substantially reduced mortality from infectious diseases and improved human health.1,2 However, excessive antibiotic use and inadequate disposal have accelerated the emergence and dissemination of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), which the World Health Organization (WHO) recognizes as a major global public health threat.3 ARGs are increasingly regarded as emerging contaminants (ECs) and have been detected in lakes,4 rivers,5 groundwater,6 agricultural soil,7 sediments,8 and atmospheric aerosols,9 posing a persistent threat to ecosystem stability and public health. Wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) can act as reservoirs and potential emission sources of antibiotics, ARB, and ARGs, particularly because biological treatment units maintain high microbial densities under residual selective pressure.10,11 Although conventional biological processes, including activated sludge, biofilm reactors, and anaerobic digestion, effectively remove many traditional pollutants, their capacity to attenuate antibiotics and ARGs is often incomplete and matrix-dependent. Antibiotic biodegradation is often constrained by structural stability and toxicity to microbial degraders, resulting in incomplete removal of recalcitrant classes such as fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides, and tetracyclines.12 Residual antibiotics at sub-inhibitory concentrations may persist in treatment systems and effluents, imposing selective pressure on dense microbial communities and promoting ARGs maintenance and dissemination through vertical and horizontal gene transfer (HGT).13 This widespread persistence is corroborated by systematic global evaluations demonstrating that, irrespective of regional variations, specific antibiotic classes (e.g., macrolides, sulfonamides) and recalcitrant genotypes (e.g., bla, sul, tet) remain dominant contaminants in WWTPs spanning Europe, the Americas, Asia, and Africa.14 Furthermore, tertiary disinfection methods demonstrate insufficient capability in eliminating ARB and ARGs. Chlorination can inactivate ARB and damage DNA at sufficient doses, but suboptimal chlorination may induce cell lysis and release intracellular ARGs (iARGs) as extracellular ARGs (eARGs), some of which may persist or remain transformable depending on oxidant exposure and matrix conditions.15 Similarly, ultraviolet (UV) irradiation is often impeded by high turbidity and suspended solids,16 while ozonation is limited by selective oxidation characteristics and instability in complex matrices.17 These limitations highlight the need for advanced treatment strategies that reduce not only ARG abundance but also ARG transferability and functional persistence.

Recently, electrochemical technologies have attracted considerable interest in wastewater treatment because they can generate strong oxidants in situ under relatively mild and flexible operating conditions.18 Electrochemical oxidation generates a variety of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), singlet oxygen (1O2), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), as well as reactive chlorine species (RCS), including atomic chlorine radicals (Cl˙), dichlorine radical anions (Cl2˙−), and hypochlorous acid/hypochlorite (HOCl/OCl−). These reactive species can act synergistically to induce DNA strand breakage, base oxidation, and cell membrane disruption, thereby contributing to the attenuation of both iARGs and eARGs under appropriate operating conditions.19 Notably, electrochemical technology demonstrates unique advantages in removing ARGs by simultaneously targeting cell structures and genetic material via direct and indirect anodic oxidation, unlike traditional methods that primarily inactivate bacteria.20 Specifically, ROS utilize their high redox potential to rapidly disrupt DNA base structures and induce chain scission, while RCS penetrate cell membranes to oxidatively degrade iARGs.21 Recent advancements have focused on coupled systems involving anodic oxidation, electro-Fenton, three-dimensional electrodes, and integrations with UV, ultrasound, ozone, or biological treatments. Such approaches significantly improve the removal efficiency of antibiotics and ARGs while reducing energy consumption and mitigating byproduct formation risks.

Although electrochemical processes have shown high efficiency in removing ARGs, the field remains in a nascent stage requiring systematic scrutiny of complex mechanisms and environmental risks. Compared with previous reviews that mainly focused on antibiotic removal, conventional disinfection, or ARGs occurrence and fate, this review specifically positions electrochemical technologies as emerging strategies for ARGs control in wastewater, with novelty in integrating process comparison, mechanistic analysis of iARG/eARG degradation and HGT risks, and practical concerns including disinfection by-products (DBPs) formation, energy consumption, electrode stability, and scale-up applicability. This review aims to: (1) comprehensively synthesize research progress on electrochemical technologies for removing ARGs from wastewater, systematically elucidating different electrochemical process types and their varying efficacies; (2) scrutinize the principal mechanisms of ARGs degradation together with significant influencing parameters; and (3) outline the existing technical bottlenecks and directions for future advancement. The ultimate objective is to provide a theoretical foundation for the application of electrochemical technologies in controlling ARGs. This work offers novel perspectives for constructing more secure wastewater treatment systems.

Comparison of treatment technologies for the removal of ARGs

Anodic oxidation

Anodic oxidation, also referred to as electrochemical or electrocatalytic oxidation, is one of the most widely investigated electrochemical treatment technologies. This process degrades pollutants via direct electron transfer at the anode surface or through oxidation mediated by reactive species generated at the interface and within the bulk solution. The physicochemical properties of the anode, particularly its electrocatalytic activity and oxygen evolution potential (OEP), strongly influence the oxidation pathway and treatment efficiency.22 Anode materials are generally categorized into active and non-active types based on the interaction strength between the electrode surface and generated ˙OH. Active anodes, exemplified by dimensionally stable anodes (DSAs) coated with noble metal oxides such as ruthenium (Ru) or iridium (Ir), exhibit low OEPs. ˙OH formed on these surfaces exist predominantly in a chemisorbed state and interact strongly with the lattice to evolve oxygen (i.e., oxygen evolution reaction, OER). Consequently, these anodes generally show lower capacity for non-selective organic oxidation and tend to favor oxygen evolution or RCS generation in chloride-containing electrolytes. In contrast, non-active anodes including boron-doped diamond (BDD), lead dioxide (PbO2), and tin dioxide (SnO2) exhibit high OEPs. This characteristic effectively suppresses OER and facilitates the generation of abundant physisorbed ˙OH on the electrode surface.23 These weakly bound and highly reactive radicals have strong non-selective oxidative capacity, enabling organic pollutant mineralization and DNA structural damage.

Recent studies have focused on developing efficient anode materials to improve ARGs attenuation while reducing energy consumption. Strategies such as doping modification and structural optimization of conventional electrode materials have significantly improved electrocatalytic performance and stability. For instance, Dong et al. prepared modified Ti/PbO2 electrodes by doping them with the rare-earth element lanthanum (La). Experimental results indicate that La doping renders the electrode surface denser and the PbO2 crystal structure more complete. This not only elevates the electrode's OEP and operational lifespan but also demonstrates superior electrocatalytic activity in chloramphenicol degradation and related ARGs control.24 Beyond conventional metal oxides, two-dimensional materials offer new opportunities for anode development. For instance, Li et al. developed a composite anode featuring NiAl layered double hydroxide intercalated within MXene structures. Electrostatic self-assembly of NiAl layered double hydroxides expands the MXene interlayer spacing and reduces nanosheet restacking. This structural optimization shortens mass transfer pathways for reactants and products to facilitate electron transfer and ˙OH generation. When applied to hospital wastewater treatment, this composite anode demonstrated efficient synergistic degradation of norfloxacin and ARGs with a 96.72% removal rate for the quinolone resistance gene qnrA.25

To address energy consumption and cost challenges in practical electrochemical applications, researchers are actively exploring non-metal-based catalysts and advanced electrode fabrication processes. Compared with noble-metal or boron-doped diamond electrodes, carbon-based materials are generally lower in cost, more abundant, and easier to modify. For instance, Zuo et al. investigated metal-free electrochemical disinfection using a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF)-based carbon catalyst as the working electrode. Under optimized operating conditions, this system achieved highly efficient inactivation of ARB. Risk assessments revealed no detectable conjugative transfer of target ARGs in the treated water with extremely low transformation frequencies, indicating effective interception of gene propagation risks.26 Zhang et al. fabricated a neodymium-doped Ti4O7 reactive electrochemical membrane electrode via 3D printing technology. This porous reticulated electrode design overcomes mass transfer limitations of conventional flat-plate electrodes and membrane fouling while significantly enhancing electrochemical activity and hydroxyl radical production by 56 to 442% through neodymium doping. Experimental results demonstrated complete inactivation of resistant E. coli (>8.0-log reduction) in a single pass at 4 mA cm−2 with substantially improved ARGs removal.27

Electro-Fenton and electro-Fenton-like processes

The electro-Fenton process is an electrochemically assisted variant of the conventional Fenton reaction that generates H2O2in situ and can reduce challenges associated with H2O2 transport, storage, and dosing. The core principle involves in situ generation of hydrogen peroxide at the cathode through two-electron reduction of dissolved oxygen. The generated H2O2 subsequently reacts with Fe2+, either externally added or produced by sacrificial anode dissolution, to yield highly oxidative ˙OH via the Fenton reaction.28 Electro-Fenton-like processes extend this concept to encompass technologies utilizing alternative transition metal ions such as Cu2+, Mn2+, and Co2+ as catalysts or employing auxiliary physical fields including UV irradiation and ultrasound to intensify the Fenton reaction.29 Because electro-Fenton systems generate ˙OH in the bulk solution, they can enhance the degradation of dissolved antibiotics and eARGs that are less accessible to direct anode-surface oxidation.

Multiple comparative studies have confirmed the high efficiency of electro-Fenton processes in removing various forms of ARGs. Solution-phase ˙OH can attack bacterial cells and extracellular DNA more broadly throughout the reactor, thereby improving eARGs degradation. For instance, a comparative study of electrochemical oxidation and electro-Fenton processes revealed that the electro-Fenton process achieved superior removal of both iARGs and eARGs in livestock wastewater treatment. After 120 minutes of treatment at 21.42 mA cm−2 with 1.0 mmol L−1 Fe2+, removal efficiencies for intracellular tetA and ampC reached 3.8-log and 4.1-log, respectively, while extracellular tetA and ampC achieved 5.2-log and 4.8-log removal, demonstrating potent oxidative capabilities.30 Another study on secondary swine farm effluent treatment reached similar conclusions with electro-Fenton processes showing substantially higher removal efficiency for eARGs compared to iARGs. This difference likely arises because eARGs lack cell-envelope protection and are more directly exposed to solution-phase oxidants, whereas iARGs degradation usually requires prior disruption of host-cell structures.31

To enhance oxidation efficiency, broaden the applicable pH range, and reduce operational costs, researchers have developed various modified electro-Fenton systems. Coupling electrochemical processes with other advanced oxidation technologies represents a common strategy. For instance, Feng et al. constructed an electro-Fenton/peracetic acid system in which electrochemically generated Fe2+ activated peracetic acid to produce organic radicals that acted synergistically with ˙OH. The system showed a strong synergistic effect, achieving complete ARB inactivation within 1–2 min and ARGs removal within 20 min, with an energy consumption of 1.18 kWh m−3.32 Bio-electro-Fenton systems constructed using bioelectrochemical systems (BES) have demonstrated additional potential. In these systems, electrons generated from microbial oxidation of organic matter at the anode drive oxygen reduction at the cathode to produce H2O2 and initiate the Fenton reaction, coupling energy recovery with pollutant treatment. Research demonstrates that bio-electro-Fenton systems effectively remove antibiotics such as norfloxacin while controlling ARGs propagation risks, offering an ecologically sustainable approach for treating antibiotic-laden wastewater.33 Overall, electro-Fenton processes are particularly suitable for degrading eARGs and dissolved antibiotics because ˙OH is generated in the bulk solution rather than only at the electrode surface. However, their practical application remains constrained by acidic pH requirements, iron management, and possible sludge generation.

Three-dimensional electrode and particle electrode systems

Conventional two-dimensional flat-plate electrode reactors often suffer from low surface-to-volume ratios, limited mass transfer, and suboptimal current efficiency, especially when treating low-concentration pollutants. To overcome these limitations, three-dimensional electrode systems have emerged and developed rapidly. Three-dimensional electrode technology typically involves filling the interelectrode space with conductive or semiconductive granular, fibrous, or irregularly shaped materials known as particle electrodes. Upon application of an external voltage, these particle electrodes become polarized under the electric field established by the main electrodes. Each particle functions as a micro-bipolar electrode with oxidation occurring at one end and reduction at the other.34 This configuration increases the effective reaction area and shortens diffusion distances, thereby improving mass transfer, space-time yield, and ARGs attenuation efficiency.

The material properties, size, and packing configuration of particle electrodes critically influence the overall performance of three-dimensional electrode systems. Traditional particle electrode materials include carbon-based materials such as activated carbon and graphite particles that possess favorable electrical conductivity and adsorption properties. Recently, composite particle electrodes loaded with catalytically active metal oxides or functional materials have become a research focus to further enhance reaction activity. For instance, Yan et al. prepared magnesium ferrite-loaded biochar as three-dimensional particle electrodes to activate peroxymonosulfate, constructing an electrochemical/magnesium ferrite-biochar/peroxymonosulfate system. In this system, biochar serves as both a conductive substrate and adsorbent while its loaded transition metal oxides efficiently catalyze peroxymonosulfate to generate sulfate radicals (SO4˙−) and ˙OH. Experimental results showed that this system achieved complete inactivation of resistant E. coli within 5 minutes at 5 V with intracellular sul1 genes reduced by 81.5% after 30 minutes. The system rapidly decreased the conjugative transfer frequency of sul1 by 92.9% within 2 minutes through synergistic destruction of cell membranes, proteins, lipids, and DNA by solution-phase radicals and intracellular ROS induced in bacteria.35

Three-dimensional biofilm-electrode reactors combine the advantages of biological degradation and electrochemical oxidation. In these systems, particle electrodes such as granular activated carbon (GAC) support biofilm colonization while electrical stimulation directly oxidizes pollutants and regulates microbial community structure and metabolic activity. Research demonstrates that three-dimensional biofilm-electrode systems treating wastewater containing sulfadiazine and ciprofloxacin reduce total ARG abundance in effluent more effectively than conventional anaerobic reactors with removal rates reaching 88.9 to 93.5% and 89.3 to 95.6%, respectively. However, increases in the absolute and relative abundance of target ARGs may occur in the cathode region. This phenomenon likely relates to localized accumulation of antibiotics and heavy metals at the cathode and selective enrichment of resistant populations by the reductive cathode environment, indicating that differential ecological effects of electrode polarity on microorganisms require consideration when designing such systems.36,37 Furthermore, electrocoagulation technology can also be regarded as a specialized three-dimensional reaction system. During electrocoagulation, sacrificial anodes such as iron or aluminum dissolve to produce metal ions that hydrolyze and polymerize in solution to form abundant metal hydroxide flocs. These flocs possess large specific surface areas and active sites that enable effective removal of colloidal particles, bacteria, and dissolved DNA molecules from wastewater through adsorption, entrapment, and sweep flocculation. Research indicates that electrocoagulation effectively removes iARGs and eARGs from wastewater treatment plant effluent, with higher current densities and acidic conditions favoring the hydrolysis of aluminum or iron salts to form positively charged polynuclear hydroxyl complexes that enhance adsorption removal of negatively charged bacteria and DNA.38

Coupled electrochemical systems for enhanced treatment

Because real wastewater matrices are chemically complex and single electrochemical processes often have limited selectivity or efficiency, integrated electrochemical systems have become an important strategy for ARGs control. These integrated approaches are designed to improve treatment efficiency, lower energy demand, limit by-product formation, and enhance ARGs control through complementary or synergistic mechanisms. Common coupling strategies include photoelectrocatalysis, electrochemical membrane reactors, and BES.

Photoelectrocatalysis combines photocatalytic and electrocatalytic mechanisms by applying an external bias voltage to a photoanode, which suppresses the recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs and thereby improves quantum efficiency and reactive species generation. He et al. constructed a photoelectrocatalytic reactor using TiO2 nanotube arrays doped with BiFeO3 nanoparticles as the photoanode for reclaimed water treatment, exploiting the mutual promotion between photoelectrocatalysis and Fenton-like reactions in which BiFeO3 both broadened the photoresponse range and acted as a Fenton catalyst, collectively producing abundant ˙OH that achieved 5.6-log bacterial inactivation within 30 minutes and 60% eARGs removal within 60 minutes.39 UV-assisted electrochemical oxidation on blue TiO2 nanotube arrays further demonstrated that in chloride-containing matrices, electrochemically generated reactive chlorine species including HClO and Cl2 undergo photolysis under UV excitation to produce chlorine radicals with stronger DNA-attacking capabilities, accelerating the destruction of long-chain DNA fragments to a degree unachievable by UV irradiation or electrochemical oxidation applied in isolation.40 However, the photolytic conversion of reactive chlorine species into halogenated radicals may also increase toxicological risks, indicating that process synergy in ARGs removal does not necessarily translate into overall environmental safety.

Electrochemical membrane reactors represent another actively developed configuration, using conductive membrane materials simultaneously as filtration media and electrodes to enable in situ oxidative degradation while physically retaining pollutants, thereby mitigating membrane fouling and intensifying contaminant removal. The electrochemical membrane-aerated biofilm reactor developed by Ren et al. enhanced sulfamethoxazole and trimethoprim degradation to approximately 60% through the combined effects of electro-oxidation-generated ROS and biofilm degradation, far exceeding the biological-only control below 10%, while simultaneously suppressing the relative ARG abundance within the reactor.41 A polyaniline/carbon nanotube electroactive ultrafiltration membrane achieved effective degradation of both iARGs and eARGs through combined electrode-surface oxidation, electrostatic interactions with charged DNA, and electroporation effects under a low applied voltage of only +2 V, with simultaneous reductions in integron abundance and HGT frequency.42

BES, including microbial fuel cells and microbial electrolysis cells, use electroactive microorganisms to catalyze anodic organic oxidation or cathodic pollutant reduction, but their effects on ARGs fate are complex and context-dependent. Appropriate electrical stimulation can enrich functionally relevant antibiotic-degrading bacteria, and as antibiotic concentrations diminish under effective treatment the resulting weakened selective pressure may drive down ARG abundance. In an anaerobic electrochemical membrane bioreactor, applied voltage promoted the enrichment of the tetracycline degradation gene tetX, enhanced antibiotic removal, and thereby attenuated the selective pressure driving ARGs proliferation.43 Voltages exceeding 0.9 V, conversely, can drive excessive intracellular ROS production and elevated cell membrane permeability, triggering bacterial SOS stress responses and increasing conjugative ARGs transfer frequencies.44,45 Electrode biofilms may also act as ARGs reservoirs. As a result, ARG abundance in the effluent may decrease while ARGs accumulation within the biofilm increases, a redistribution that effluent-based qPCR measurements alone cannot capture.46

Hybrid systems can provide advantages over standalone electrochemical processes, but these benefits must be evaluated against practical constraints. The principal mechanistic advantage lies in complementarity: photoelectrocatalytic systems expand the usable wavelength range and generate multiple concurrent reactive species; membrane-coupled systems combine physical retention with chemical degradation to address both iARGs and eARGs fractions simultaneously; and bioelectrochemical systems leverage microbial metabolism to reduce chemical oxidant demand. In several documented cases, these synergies produce treatment outcomes that standalone oxidation cannot replicate at equivalent energy inputs. Against these gains, hybrid configurations invariably introduce greater equipment complexity, substantially higher capital and maintenance costs, and additional failure modes absent from single-unit systems, as well as potential negative synergies such as the photolytic conversion of reactive chlorine species into more toxic halogenated radicals. Most hybrid-system studies have been conducted at laboratory scale under simplified conditions, and the lack of pilot-scale or field validation remains a major barrier to engineering implementation.

Mechanisms of electrochemical ARGs removal

Understanding the mechanisms of electrochemical ARGs removal is essential for optimizing operating conditions, improving treatment efficiency, assessing environmental risks, and designing new technologies. Compared to traditional disinfection technologies, electrochemical processes remove ARGs through a complex interplay involving physical field effects, chemical oxidative attacks, and biological responses rather than through a singular pathway. The core mechanisms involve disruption of ARGs-carrying host cells and oxidative damage to iARGs and eARGs through direct electron transfer at electrode surfaces or in situ generated reactive species, ultimately reducing genetic function and dissemination potential (Fig. 1).

Classification of electrochemical technologies and mechanisms for ARGs removal.

Direct and indirect oxidation pathways

In electrochemical reaction systems, pollutant degradation typically occurs via two primary pathways including direct oxidation and indirect oxidation. In ARGs removal, these pathways often coexist, and their relative contributions depend on electrode properties, pollutant characteristics, water chemistry, and operating conditions.

Direct oxidation refers to the process where pollutant molecules adsorb onto the anode surface through diffusion and undergo oxidation by directly releasing electrons to the anode. The efficiency of this process is highly dependent on the electrocatalytic activity of the electrode material, the number of surface adsorption sites, and the interaction between pollutant molecules and the electrode surface. For particulate targets such as bacterial cells, direct oxidation is often limited by restricted contact with the electrode surface, steric hindrance, and electrostatic repulsion. However, for dissolved small-molecule antibiotics or extracellular DNA fragments released from cells, direct electron transfer may constitute a non-negligible degradation pathway.67 For DNA fragments containing easily oxidizable bases such as guanine (G), direct electrochemical oxidation can occur at the electrode surface under appropriate potentials. By combining in situ spectroscopic techniques with theoretical computational methods such as density functional theory (DFT) calculations and Bader charge analysis, adsorption configurations, electron transfer pathways, and degradation mechanisms of pollutant molecules on electrode surfaces can be revealed at the molecular level to provide evidence for understanding direct oxidation processes.68

Indirect oxidation refers to the process of oxidizing and degrading pollutants utilizing highly oxidative intermediate products including reactive species generated by electrode reactions that diffuse into the bulk solution. Due to the complexity of actual wastewater matrices containing large amounts of suspended solids and dissolved organic matter (DOM), direct contact between pollutants and the electrode surface is often restricted.

Therefore, indirect oxidation is generally considered the dominant mechanism for electrochemical disinfection and ARGs removal. Electrochemical systems can generate a diverse suite of reactive species, including physisorbed ˙OH on non-active anodes such as boron-doped diamond, RCS in chloride-containing waters, O3, H2O2, superoxide anion radicals (O2˙−) and 1O2. Research indicates that even in electrocatalytic systems driven by boron-doped diamond electrodes known for generating physisorbed ˙OH, indirect oxidation dominates the overall pollutant degradation process, while direct oxidation plays an important supporting role under synergistic influences.68 Understanding the generation conditions and action characteristics of these different reactive species is crucial for regulating electrochemical processes.

Reactive species generated during electrochemical processes differ in redox potential, selectivity, and lifetime, which leads to distinct attack pathways toward bacterial cell structures and DNA molecules. The mechanisms of action of these reactive species are key factors determining the effectiveness of ARGs removal and their ultimate fate (Table 3).

Summary of reactive species and mechanisms in electrochemical ARGs removal

Reactive oxygen species (ROS)

ROS are a class of highly reactive substances derived from oxygen, mainly including ˙OH, O3, 1O2, and O2˙−. Among these species, the ˙OH is one of the substances with the highest known oxidation potentials in water treatment with a standard potential (E0 = 2.80V), possessing extremely strong non-selective oxidative capability and being able to react with almost all organic matter in water.69 In electro-Fenton systems or electro-oxidation systems using non-active anodes, ˙OH are typically the primary oxidants. They can attack polyunsaturated fatty acids and membrane proteins, initiate lipid peroxidation, disrupt membrane integrity, increase permeability, and cause leakage of intracellular substances such as K+, ATP, proteins, and plasmid DNA. Subsequently, ˙OH continue to attack released or pre-existing extracellular DNA molecules. Due to their non-selectivity, they can destroy the phosphodiester bond backbone and base structure of DNA by abstracting hydrogen atoms from deoxyribose or adding to carbon–carbon double bonds of bases and deoxyribose to cause DNA strands to break into small fragments and lose biological function.70 For instance, in molybdenum carbide (MoC)-assisted electrochemical disinfection processes, research found that electrons, H2O2, and O2˙− play dominant roles throughout the process, while H+ and ˙OH contribute to bacterial inactivation in the early and late stages of disinfection, respectively. These externally electrochemically induced reactive species exert strong environmental pressure on ARB strains to enhance intracellular oxidative stress responses, trigger higher levels of intracellular ROS generation, and lead to severe membrane damage and altered cell structures through combined internal and external attacks.49

Besides ˙OH, other ROS also play important roles under specific conditions. In the electro-peroxone system, H2O2 generated via cathodic oxygen reduction can promote the conversion of O3 to ˙OH. Although this process reduces direct exposure to ozone, it significantly increases the production of ˙OH with stronger oxidative capacity. Research has found that this conversion enables plasmid DNA to be more effectively cut into shorter fragments to more thoroughly reduce the risk of ARGs dissemination through natural transformation.71 In recent years, 1O2, as a non-radical highly reactive oxidant, has also received increasing attention. Compared to ˙OH, 1O2 has a longer lifetime and stronger cell membrane penetration ability. Research found that in electrofiltration-activated peroxymonosulfate systems, 1O2 showed superior efficacy to active manganese(iii) in inactivating ARB and destroying proteins, and exhibited certain selectivity for specific ARGs. This selectivity is attributed to its preferential ability to attack electron-rich guanine regions in DNA, with its attack efficiency positively correlated with the content of 5′-GG-3′ doublets in DNA sequences.64 In electrochemical membrane-NaClO systems, researchers observed synergistic effects between ˙OH and 1O2. ˙OH first efficiently destroy bacterial cell membranes to increase cell permeability, allowing 1O2 to more easily enter cells and directly oxidatively destroy iARGs.72

Reactive chlorine species (RCS)

In chloride-containing domestic and industrial wastewater, RCS generated by anodic chloride oxidation can become dominant disinfectants and may contribute more strongly to ARB inactivation and ARGs degradation than ROS. Cl− first loses electrons at the anode surface to generate chlorine gas, which subsequently dissolves in water and rapidly undergoes hydrolysis to form hypochlorous acid and hypochlorite ions. HClO is a strong oxidant with a neutral molecular structure, enabling it to easily penetrate negatively charged bacterial cell membranes, enter cells, and oxidatively destroy key enzyme systems such as enzymes containing thiol groups and genetic material to achieve efficient inactivation. Although RCS generally have lower oxidation potentials than ˙OH, HClO has a longer lifetime, greater diffusion distance, and higher selectivity toward specific biological molecules.73 In chloride-containing systems, the removal efficiency of ARB and ARGs by electrochemical processes is typically significantly improved. For instance, in an electrochemical disinfection study of saline wastewater effluent, saline secondary effluent with approximately 8 parts per thousand salinity achieved 99.9% total coliform removal within less than 10 seconds of contact time with extremely low energy consumption, mainly attributed to the efficient generation and action of RCS.74 For the removal of iARGs, RCS demonstrate unique advantages. A comparative study found that although electrochemical oxidation under chloride-free conditions was effective in inactivating ARB, its removal effect on iARGs was inferior to that of the electro-Fenton process, which may be related to differences in the ability of different oxidants to penetrate cell walls. However, in the presence of chloride ions, an electrochemically driven UV/chlorine process achieved greater than 5-log inactivation of ARB within a very short time of 5 seconds and effectively degraded iARGs and eARGs within 5 minutes. Model results showed that the reduction efficiency of iARGs was positively correlated with the combined exposure to ˙OH, chlorine radicals, and hypochlorous acid radicals.62 Notably, the attack mechanism of RCS on DNA includes not only causing strand breakage but also involves chlorination modification of bases such as generating chlorinated guanine, and such chemical modifications can similarly block DNA replication and transcription functions to lead to gene inactivation.

Damage mechanisms targeting different ARGs forms

ARGs in wastewater exist primarily in two forms including iARGs protected by the host bacterial cell structure and eARGs released into the environment after host cell death and rupture or entering water bodies through other pathways. Due to differences in existence states and microenvironments, significant disparities exist in the mechanisms of action and removal efficiencies of electrochemical technology on these two forms of ARGs.

Host bacterial inactivation and iARGs removal

Effective iARG attenuation generally requires either disruption of host-cell envelopes or penetration of oxidants into intact cells. Electrochemical treatment first acts on the outermost cell wall and cell membrane of bacteria. Strong oxidizing substances such as hydroxyl radicals and HClO attack unsaturated fatty acid chains and membrane proteins in the phospholipid bilayer of the cell membrane to initiate lipid peroxidation reactions, leading to compromised cell membrane integrity, altered fluidity, and increased permeability. Through scanning electron microscopy, the surface morphology of bacteria can be clearly observed to undergo significant changes after electrochemical treatment, showing phenomena such as roughness, blistering, depression, shrinkage, and even complete rupture and disintegration. Flow cytometry combined with fluorescence staining analysis further confirmed that cell membrane permeability increased during treatment, accompanied by leakage of intracellular macromolecular substances such as potassium ions, proteins, ATP, and nucleic acids, marking the loss of cell viability.75 Furthermore, external oxidative stimulation triggers intracellular oxidative stress responses in bacteria to lead to excessive production of intracellular ROS. These endogenous ROS further aggravate damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids. For instance, during electrochemical disinfection processes, E. coli cells were observed to undergo a dynamic evolution process from increased cell membrane permeability, early apoptosis, late apoptosis, to final cell necrosis, with lethality significantly positively correlated with ROS production.76 Notably, different types of bacteria exhibit varying resistance to electrochemical disinfection. Bacteria carrying multiple ARGs often demonstrate stronger survival capabilities in electrochemical disinfection, which may be related to their possession of more developed oxidative stress defense mechanisms such as higher activity of catalase and superoxide dismutase or alterations in cell wall and membrane structures. For instance, research indicates that the presence of beta-lactamase resistance genes can reduce oxidative damage by increasing cell wall and membrane strength to play a dominant role in cross-resistance between antibiotics and electrochemical oxidation.77

Extracellular DNA degradation

Once ARGs occur as naked extracellular DNA, either pre-existing in wastewater or released after cell lysis, they lose cell-envelope protection and become directly exposed to solution-phase oxidants. Therefore, compared to protected iARGs, eARGs are generally easier to remove and degrade. Research indicates that the removal efficiencies of electrochemical oxidation and electro-Fenton processes for eARGs are significantly higher than those for iARGs. For example, after 120 minutes of electro-Fenton treatment, the removal efficiencies of extracellular tetA and ampC reached 5.2-log and 4.8-log, respectively, while removal efficiencies for iARGs were only 3.8-log and 4.1-log.30 Electrochemical oxidation damages DNA mainly through strand breakage and base modification. Strong oxidants, especially non-selective ˙OH, can attack carbon-hydrogen bonds on the DNA deoxyribose backbone to abstract hydrogen atoms and generate carbon-centered radicals, which subsequently undergo a series of reactions in the presence of oxygen leading to phosphodiester bond breakage. This strand scission fragments plasmid or genomic DNA into shorter sequences that may no longer encode complete genetic information. Through gel electrophoresis analysis, intact plasmid DNA bands can be clearly observed to gradually fade and disappear with prolonged electrochemical treatment time, replaced by small molecular weight smears with faster migration rates. Atomic force microscopy observations also intuitively show the process where plasmid DNA transforms from a compact supercoiled structure to a relaxed open-circular structure after electrochemical treatment, then breaks into linear fragments, and finally degrades into smaller debris of less than 30 nanometers or even single nucleotides.65 In addition to strand breakage, oxidants also cause chemical modification of DNA bases. Among the four bases, guanine is the site most susceptible to oxidative attack due to its lowest oxidation potential. Oxidative damage leads to changes in base structure, such as the generation of the common oxidation product 8-hydroxyguanine. These modified bases cannot pair normally with cytosine to hinder recognition and binding by DNA polymerase, inhibiting PCR amplification processes and subsequent transcription and translation to cause gene loss of genetic function. In-depth molecular-level studies found that electrochemical oxidation seems to preferentially attack the promoter regions of ARGs accounting for 91.03% to 92.19% of attack sites, leading to disruption of functions of key genes involved in transcriptional regulation, amino acid metabolism, and bacterial stress responses. This finding indicates that differences in nucleotide sequence composition and secondary structure of ARGs significantly affect their sensitivity to electrochemical degradation.78 High-throughput sequencing technology combined with bioinformatics analysis further revealed the dynamic trajectory of ARGs degradation, manifested as stepwise fragmentation of genes and accumulation of apurinic and apyrimidinic sites. During degradation, the abundance of long fragments steadily decreases, while extremely short fragments gradually dominate in later stages, indicating gradual shredding of DNA.

The viable but non-culturable bacteria (VBNC) challenge and ARGs persistence

When evaluating the effectiveness of electrochemical technologies for ARGs control, reliance solely on traditional metrics such as bacterial inactivation rates based on culture methods or ARGs copy number reductions based on quantitative polymerase chain reaction is insufficient. A critical but often overlooked issue is whether residual ARGs-containing DNA remains transformable or transferable after bacterial inactivation. If treatment is incomplete, residual DNA fragments may still enter new host bacteria through HGT mechanisms to perpetuate resistance dissemination.

When electrochemical treatment is insufficient, bacteria may enter a viable but non-culturable (VBNC) state or die with intracellular DNA still partially intact. Such residual DNA may remain transformable after release, whereas sublethally injured cells may still participate in HGT. Therefore, effective ARGs control requires not only bacterial inactivation but also sufficient DNA damage to eliminate genetic function and transferability. Some high-efficiency electrochemical processes have demonstrated advantages in this regard. For instance, Mo2C-assisted electrochemical disinfection processes can achieve complete ARB inactivation with no regrowth while significantly reducing the conjugative transfer frequency of ARGs, indicating that potential HGT risks are effectively blocked.49 Another study on electrochemical oxidation using Ti/Sb–SnO2/PbO2 anodes confirmed the importance of deep oxidation. Although quantitative polymerase chain reaction detection showed that absolute quantitative removal efficiencies of ARGs might not be as high as bacterial inactivation rates with intracellular ampR reduced by 6.5 log and extracellular ampR reduced by 4.1 log, further transformation experiments indicated that residual plasmid DNA, both intracellular and extracellular, completely lost transformation ability post-treatment. This illustrates that although gene fragments could still be detected, they were insufficient to mediate complete genetic information transfer due to breakage or modification.48 Furthermore, a study comparing different activation methods for peroxymonosulfate disinfection found that the conjugative transfer frequency of ARGs after disinfection with Fe/C microelectrolysis activation systems was significantly lower than with UV/LED activation systems, indicating differences in the effectiveness of different radical generation methods in blocking HGT risks.66

Conversely, some studies have revealed potential risks associated with improper electrochemical stimulation. Especially under low voltage or sublethal doses of electrical stimulation, bacteria initiate SOS stress response mechanisms to cope with external pressure. This mechanism involves upregulation of a series of genes, leading to increased cell membrane permeability, activation of DNA repair mechanisms, and enhanced expression of HGT-related genes such as integrase and transposase genes, objectively potentially promoting conjugative transfer of plasmids. For instance, research in microfluidic electrochemical reactors found that differences in the distribution of different antibiotics within cell structures affect HGT risk. Under tetracycline pressure, bacteria enhance efflux pump activity for survival, leading to increased extracellular tetracycline concentration, which inversely reduces damage to donor bacteria and plasmids by oxidants to increase HGT frequency by 8.18 times. Conversely, under sulfamethoxazole pressure, HGT risk is inhibited.79 In MECs system, higher voltages were found to promote increased bacterial mutation frequencies and significantly elevate conjugative transfer frequencies of ARGs in the anode chamber, closely related to SOS responses triggered by excessive ROS production and enhanced cell membrane permeability.44 In simulated bioelectrochemical reactors, direct current stimulation was also shown to upregulate the expression of the integrase gene intI1 and increase the relative abundance of ARGs, suggesting potential risks of accelerating ARB proliferation and promoting ARGs expression.76 Therefore, comprehensive assessment of the ecological safety of electrochemical technologies must integrate consideration of bacterial inactivation, gene degradation, and the transfer potential of residual genes to avoid partial evaluation.

Factors influencing electrochemical ARGs removal

Electrochemical ARGs removal is a complex physicochemical and biological process governed by multiple interacting factors. Understanding and optimizing these variables are essential for improving treatment efficiency, reducing energy consumption, and controlling by-product risks. The primary influencing factors include electrode material properties, operational parameter configuration, and water matrix effects.

Electrode material properties

Electrode materials constitute the core components of electrochemical reactors. Their physicochemical properties strongly influence interfacial reaction pathways, the types and generation rates of reactive species, and long-term electrode stability. As previously noted, the OEP of the anode material is a critical metric. Anode materials with high OEP such as boron-doped diamond, PbO2, and SnO2 can suppress the oxygen evolution side reaction to promote the discharge of water molecules on the electrode surface to generate physisorbed ˙OH. These radicals, characterized by weak binding forces and high reactivity, facilitate non-selective deep oxidation of organic pollutants and thorough destruction of DNA structures. Conversely, traditional metal oxide anodes such as Ti/IrO2 and Ti/RuO2 possess lower OEP. ˙OH generated on their surfaces tend to exist in a chemisorbed state or rapidly convert to lattice oxygen with subsequent OER. Consequently, their oxidative capacity is relatively limited, relying more heavily on the generation of indirect oxidants such as RCS. Extensive research confirms that under identical operating conditions, boron-doped diamond electrodes generally exhibit stronger oxidative capabilities and higher ARGs removal efficiencies than DSAs.

Beyond OEP, the catalytic activity, surface microstructure, and elemental composition of electrodes are also pivotal. Performance can be significantly improved through electrode material doping modifications. Doping with rare earth elements such as La, Nd, and Ce or transition metals such as Fe, Co, and Ni can alter the crystal structure of the electrode surface, increase the number of active sites, and regulate electronic structure to enhance catalytic selectivity and activity for specific reactions such as chlorine evolution and H2O2 generation. For instance, La-doped Ti/PbO2 electrodes exhibit denser surface morphologies and more perfect crystal structures that improve electrode stability and longevity while significantly increasing chloramphenicol degradation rates and total organic carbon (TOC) removal rates.24 Nd-doped Ti4O7 reactive electrochemical membrane electrodes fabricated via 3D printing technology enhance electrochemical activity through Nd introduction to significantly increase ˙OH yields and strengthen disinfection and ARGs removal effects.27 For cathode materials in processes such as electro-Fenton, the primary function is to generate H2O2via the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR). Therefore, cathode materials require high selectivity and catalytic activity for the two-electron ORR pathway. Carbon-based materials such as graphite felt, carbon cloth, and carbon nanotubes are widely used due to their favorable electrical conductivity, lower cost, and ease of modification. Performance can be further enhanced by introducing catalytically active centers. For instance, graphitic carbon nitride cathodes modified with single-atom Fe catalysts possessing Fe–N4 coordination structures demonstrate efficient and stable ORR activity in MFCs, significantly increasing system power density and promoting tetracycline degradation with high catalytic capabilities approaching four-electron reduction.80 Furthermore, Ni-MOF-74 has been proven to possess favorable two-electron transfer oxygen reduction reaction activity as a cathode catalyst suitable for in situ H2O2 generation.60

Operational parameters

Optimization of operational parameters is key to achieving efficient and stable operation of electrochemical processes. Current density or applied voltage and treatment time are the two most important parameters. Current density affects the electron-transfer rate at electrode surfaces and thereby influences the generation rates of ROS and RCS. Within a certain range, increasing current density can increase the yield of reactive species per unit time to accelerate the inactivation of ARB and degradation of ARGs. Multiple studies confirm that ARB inactivation rates and ARGs removal efficiencies generally increase with increasing current density or voltage. For instance, in electrocoagulation processes, high current density at 20.0 mA cm−2 has been proven to promote ARGs removal through mechanisms involving enhanced metal ion dissolution and floc formation.38 In electrokinetic remediation processes, increasing the voltage gradient from 0.4 to 1.2 V cm−1 similarly improved ARB and ARGs removal rates.81 However, increasing current density is not without limits. Excessively high current densities lead to severe side reactions such as intensified anodic oxygen evolution and cathodic hydrogen evolution that reduce current efficiency, increase energy consumption, and accelerate electrode material corrosion and loss. Furthermore, high voltage and high current density may increase the risk of generating toxic byproducts such as perchlorate. Therefore, in practical applications, balancing treatment effects, energy consumption, and electrode longevity according to water quality characteristics and treatment requirements is necessary to select an optimal current density range.

Treatment time or hydraulic retention time determines the duration of contact between contaminants and reactive species. Adequate treatment time is crucial for achieving deep degradation of ARGs, preventing bacterial regrowth, and ensuring the ecological safety of treated water bodies. Some studies indicate that under certain circumstances, prolonging electrolysis time may achieve higher bacterial inactivation rates than simply increasing current density, although the former may be less efficient in reducing relative ARG abundance. For instance, Li et al. compared high current density short time treatment with low current density long time treatment, finding that both could achieve greater than 99% inactivation rates, but high current density short time treatment was more effective in reducing relative ARG abundance. This suggests that different evaluation metrics such as inactivation rate versus gene removal rate may correspond to different optimal operating strategies.52 Moreover, for certain electrochemical processes such as electrokinetic remediation or electrochemical disinfection, employing periodic polarity reversal represents an important optimization approach. Polarity reversal can effectively reduce scaling on electrode surfaces such as calcium and magnesium ion precipitation to maintain electrode activity and improve long-term treatment efficiency and stability. Research demonstrates that shorter polarity reversal intervals such as 12 hours compared to longer intervals of 24 hours can maintain lower pH and higher current densities to achieve more efficient electrochemical reactions.81 Electrode spacing is also an influencing factor; smaller spacing can enhance electric field strength, with 4 cm spacing proven optimal in stormwater runoff electrocoagulation treatment.82

Water matrix characteristics

Real wastewater contains diverse dissolved and suspended organic and inorganic constituents that create complex matrix effects. These components deeply participate in electrochemical reaction processes, exerting significant matrix effects on ARGs removal as either promoters or inhibitors. Chloride concentration is one of the most critical water-quality factors affecting electrochemical ARGs removal. In chloride-containing wastewater, the primary mechanism of anodic oxidation often shifts from ROS dominance to RCS dominance. Due to the strong penetration ability and long lifetime of HClO/ClO−, the presence of chloride ions generally significantly promotes ARB inactivation and ARGs removal. Multiple studies emphasize the central role of Cl− in electrochemical disinfection, indicating that RCS are often the primary disinfectants in practical systems. For instance, in an electrochemical disinfection study of saline wastewater effluent, wastewater with approximately 8 parts per thousand salinity achieved 99.9% bactericidal efficiency within extremely short contact times of less than 10 seconds with very low energy consumption, mainly attributed to efficient RCS generation.74 In systems coupled with UV light, the presence of Cl− can trigger the generation of highly efficient chlorine-containing radicals to vastly improve treatment efficacy and effectively degrade both iARGs and eARGs within a short time.40 However, high chloride concentrations can increase the formation of halogenated DBPs, such as trihalomethanes (THMs) and haloacetic acids (HAAs), and high anodic potentials may further promote perchlorate formation. Therefore, strict control of reaction conditions is required to balance disinfection effects and byproduct risks.

pH value influences treatment effects by affecting the speciation and reactivity of reactive species. For instance, in reactive chlorine systems, the bactericidal ability of HClO is far stronger than that of ClO−, while HClO dominates under acidic to neutral conditions with pKa approximately 7.5. Therefore, lower pH values generally favor reactive chlorine-mediated disinfection processes. Research indicates that the inactivation efficiency of resistant bacteria in acidic solutions is superior to that in alkaline solutions, which is also attributed to the influence of pH on electrode surface chemistry.26 In electro-Fenton systems, pH value strictly controls the efficiency of the Fenton reaction, usually being most suitable between pH 2.8 and 3.5, within which range Fe2+ catalytic activity is highest and ˙OH generation efficiency is maximal. Excessively high pH leads to iron precipitation, while excessively low pH inhibits the reaction or causes ineffective decomposition of H2O2. For electrocoagulation processes, pH value affects the morphology, surface charge, and polymerization degree of metal hydroxide flocs to subsequently influence their adsorption and entrapment effects on ARGs. Neutral or weakly acidic conditions are generally favorable.38

DOM such as humic acids and proteins and inorganic ions such as bicarbonate and phosphate present in wastewater typically exert negative effects. Acting as radical scavengers, they competitively consume ˙OH and RCS in solution to reduce the effective oxidation dosage for target pollutants including ARB and ARGs. Research indicates that the presence of DOM inhibits ARB inactivation and requires consumption of more oxidants to achieve equivalent ARGs removal effects.39 Furthermore, the impact of suspended solids in wastewater on treatment effects is dual. Suspended solids may adsorb and encapsulate bacteria and ARGs to provide physical protection and hinder oxidant attacks. However, studies indicate that the presence of appropriate amounts of fine particles (<150 µm) in electrocoagulation processes can enhance the adsorption removal effect of flocs on ARB by providing adsorption cores.82 Salinity is also an important factor, especially in BES, where high salinity can cause osmotic shock, inhibit microbial activity, and alter community structure to affect removal effects on host bacteria carrying ARGs.83 Finally, differences in water quality from different wastewater sources lead to significant differences in ARGs removal effects and HGT risks. For instance, livestock wastewater still possesses a high proportion of plasmid-located ARGs and high transfer risk after biological treatment, whereas ARGs in surface water are more frequently encoded on chromosomes.84

Current challenges and future research perspectives

Although electrochemical technology demonstrates immense potential and unique advantages in removing ARGs from wastewater, the transition from laboratory research to large-scale practical engineering applications is beset by substantial technical, economic, and environmental safety challenges (Fig. 2).

Challenges and prospects of electrochemical removal of ARGs in wastewater.

Techno-economic challenges

From a technology-readiness perspective, most electrochemical ARGs-removal systems remain at laboratory or early pilot stages, and their engineering feasibility depends not only on short-term removal efficiency but also on electrode durability, reactor scalability, operational stability, maintenance frequency, and cost-performance balance. High-performance electrodes such as BDD and noble-metal oxide anodes often provide strong oxidative capacity but are constrained by high fabrication costs, possible passivation, coating deterioration, and replacement requirements during long-term operation. Three-dimensional and coupled electrochemical reactors can improve mass transfer and current efficiency, but their more complex configurations may introduce hydraulic clogging, uneven flow distribution, higher maintenance demand, and additional scale-up uncertainty. Therefore, future studies should report not only ARGs removal rates but also specific energy consumption, electrode service life, performance decay, maintenance requirements, and life-cycle cost per unit treatment performance under realistic wastewater conditions.

Energy consumption is one of the main economic barriers to large-scale deployment of electrochemical technologies. When high-OEP anodes such as boron-doped diamond are used for deep oxidation, high cell voltages and current densities are often required, increasing electricity consumption. Consequently, operational costs of electrochemical treatment can be higher than those of conventional biological treatment or chemical disinfection, particularly when high current densities, long treatment times, or costly electrodes are required. While some emerging processes such as BES attempt to offset partial energy consumption by harnessing chemical energy from organic matter in wastewater for electricity generation, their oxidative capacities remain relatively limited with ARGs removal efficacy often less stable and thorough compared to robust electrochemical oxidation processes. Highly synergistic coupled processes such as the combined electro-Fenton/peracetic acid system have demonstrated lower energy consumption at 1.18 kWh m−3 but require further optimization.32 Therefore, developing electrode materials with low energy consumption and high catalytic activity alongside optimizing reactor configurations such as three-dimensional electrodes, microfluidic designs, and reduced inter-electrode spacing to enhance mass transfer and current efficiency represents critical pathways for reducing operational costs.

The cost and longevity of electrode materials represent another major challenge. Although boron-doped diamond electrodes often provide strong oxidative performance, their complex fabrication and high cost limit large-scale wastewater applications. Traditional DSAs, while relatively lower in cost, are prone to coating delamination, substrate passivation, or reduced catalytic activity under long-term high current densities or adverse water quality conditions, leading to shortened electrode lifespans. Although modification techniques such as rare earth doping can prolong electrode life, they increase fabrication complexity. Consequently, developing non-noble metal electrode materials or composite electrode materials that combine high catalytic activity, stability, and low cost remains an important research direction. Furthermore, reactor scale-up presents engineering challenges. Ensuring uniform electric field and flow field distribution within large-scale reactors to avoid localized over-oxidation or incomplete treatment requires comprehensive engineering design research.

Environmental and health risks

DBPs formation during electrochemical treatment is a major environmental concern that has received less attention than ARGs removal performance. Under strong oxidative conditions in chloride-containing matrices, reactive chlorine species react with natural organic matter to produce carcinogenic and mutagenic halogenated DBPs including trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids, while prolonged electrolysis at elevated anode potentials may drive deep chloride oxidation toward persistent perchlorate formation. Although short treatment windows can limit THM accumulation in certain process configurations,62 this advantage diminishes markedly in complex water matrices and long-term operational scenarios. Incomplete antibiotic degradation is another important concern because transformation products may be more toxic or persistent than their parent compounds. Studies on electrochemical chloramphenicol treatment indicate that degradation intermediates can alter bacterial communities and ARGs profiles in receiving environments, whereas more complete mineralization reduces ecological impacts.24,53 Therefore, antibiotic disappearance alone is insufficient as a safety endpoint; mineralization degree, intermediate toxicity, and ARGs transferability should be jointly assessed. ROS generated under electrical stimulation further complicate the risk picture, given that the same oxidative conditions capable of degrading ARGs-containing DNA can simultaneously increase cell membrane permeability and accelerate horizontal gene transfer at voltages exceeding 0.9 V.44,45 Several operational strategies have been identified to mitigate these risks without sacrificing treatment performance. Substituting chloride-based electrolytes with sulfate media shifts the dominant oxidation pathway toward sulfate radicals, which are highly reactive yet far less prone to generating persistent halogenated byproducts. Electrode material selection shapes both the oxidation mechanism and DBPs speciation, as Magneli phase Ti4O7 anode achieve simultaneous removal of pathogens, antibiotics, and ARGs,47 while lanthanide-doped Ti4O7 reactive electrochemical membranes attain complete ARB inactivation at lower current densities and thereby reduce the extent of chlorinated side reactions.27 Voltage management provides another practical approach, as AnEMBR studies suggest that keeping the applied voltage below 0.9 V can maintain reactor performance while reducing ROS overproduction and ARGs enrichment risks.57 Coupling electrochemical oxidation with biological polishing allows the electrical step to operate at minimum intensity while downstream microbial degradation handles residual organic intermediates, as demonstrated by the electrochemical membrane-aerated biofilm reactor achieving enhanced antibiotic removal alongside a lower relative ARG abundance than the biological-only control.41 Sequential electrocoagulation followed by electro-Fenton treatment distributes the oxidative burden across two complementary mechanisms, enabling each stage to function at reduced intensity and thereby limiting perchlorate accumulation risk.31

Risk-assessment frameworks for ARGs damage, residual transferability, and bacterial resuscitation remain underdeveloped. Reliance on qPCR-based copy-number quantification cannot distinguish intact, biologically active ARGs from fragmented, non-functional DNA, creating a gap between measured abundance and actual ecological risk. High-throughput sequencing has revealed that electrochemical oxidation drives progressive ARGs fragmentation with accumulation of apurinic/apyrimidinic sites and eventual dominance of sub-50 bp fragments,78 yet whether such residual fragments retain transformation competence across diverse bacterial recipients and water matrices has not been universally established. The potential for sub-lethally damaged bacteria to enter viable but non-culturable states and subsequently resuscitate under favorable conditions introduces further uncertainty, though studies targeting last-resort resistance genes found fewer than 1% of bacteria entering VBNC states and fewer than 5% of intracellular ARGs converting to extracellular forms.51 Sublethal electrical stimulation may also promote conjugative gene transfer, with community-level experiments reporting up to twofold increases in plasmid transfer frequency and enrichment of opportunistic pathogens such as Klebsiella among transconjugants.85 These findings reveal that the voltages most effective for antibiotic degradation may simultaneously create conditions favorable for resistance dissemination, a risk that qPCR-based monitoring is fundamentally unable to capture. Comprehensive risk evaluation should integrate metagenomic resistome profiling, functional transformation assays, HGT frequency measurements under environmentally realistic conditions, and ecotoxicological assessment of treatment intermediates, which are still inconsistently applied in current studies.

Directions for future research

Addressing these challenges, future research should focus on advancing electrochemical technology through several key aspects. First, materials science efforts should emphasize developing electrode materials with high efficiency, stability, and low cost. Promising directions include non-metal carbon-based catalysts such as graphene and carbon nanotube-modified materials, metal–organic framework (MOF)-derived materials, and nanomaterials with specific crystal facets, defect structures, or single-atom catalytic sites for electrocatalytic oxidation. Single-atom catalysts are promising because they offer high atomic utilization and tunable electronic structures. For instance, iron-based dual-atom catalysts demonstrate efficient and stable performance in cathodic oxygen reduction.86 Another innovative approach involves molecular imprinting technology to develop highly selective electrode materials. For instance, oxygen vacancy-engineered molecularly imprinted TiO2 cathodes can achieve precise recognition and removal of antibiotics in complex matrices to efficiently reduce corresponding ARGs.87

Second, deeper investigation of interfacial reaction mechanisms in complex real wastewater matrices is required. Most mechanistic studies still rely on simplified synthetic wastewater, whereas real wastewater contains diverse organic and inorganic constituents that can alter reactive-species generation, scavenging, and pollutant accessibility. Advanced in situ characterization techniques such as in situ infrared spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy, and microscopy should be combined with multi-scale theoretical calculations including DFT and molecular dynamics simulations to reveal microscopic adsorption, reaction, and desorption mechanisms between pollutants, reactive species, and electrode surfaces. These approaches can also elucidate the interference, transformation, and competitive behaviors of matrix components to provide a more solid theoretical basis for process optimization. For instance, in anaerobic BES, the differential impact of electrode polarity on microbial communities and metabolic pathways, as well as mechanisms of ARGs enrichment in cathode biofilms, require further elucidation.88

Finally, a standardized and ecologically relevant framework is needed to assess residual ARGs risks after electrochemical treatment. This system should integrate PCR-based quantitative gene detection, culture-based bacterial activity and regrowth potential assays, function-based gene transfer frequency determinations such as transformation and conjugation model experiments, and metagenomics-based high-throughput analyses to comprehensively reveal changes in resistomes, mobilomes, and pathogenicity factors. For instance, metagenomic analysis has confirmed that photo-electro-Fenton processes can significantly reduce the abundance of genes associated with pathogenicity factors, ARGs, and metabolism in wastewater.54 Through multi-dimensional assessment, accurate evaluation of the ecological safety of treated effluent can provide a scientific basis for engineering applications and environmental regulation of electrochemical technology. Simultaneously, exploring optimized combinations of electrochemical technology with other low-energy technologies such as biological treatment, constructed wetlands, and membrane separation to construct multi-barrier systems that leverage respective advantages represents an important pathway for achieving efficient, economical, and safe control of ARGs dissemination.

Conclusion

Electrochemical technologies are promising advanced oxidation processes for ARGs control in wastewater, with performance governed by process type, reactive-species chemistry, electrode properties, operating conditions, and water-matrix characteristics. Anodic oxidation, electro-Fenton processes, three-dimensional electrodes, and hybrid systems involve distinct but complementary attenuation mechanisms, with ROS and RCS acting as major reactive species. Despite these advances, electrode material properties, operational parameters, and water matrix characteristics remain decisive governing factors that require matrix-specific co-optimization, and the precise regulation of reactive species generation, their mutual interactions, and associated DBPs formation pathways warrants deeper mechanistic investigation. A critical but underaddressed issue is the distinction among bacterial inactivation, ARGs copy-number reduction, and functional ARGs inactivation. Residual DNA fragments may retain transformation potential if not sufficiently damaged, while VBNC bacteria may resuscitate after discharge under favorable environmental conditions. Addressing these limitations, future research should advance along three specific and interconnected directions. First, standardized multi-method protocols are needed to evaluate ARGs risks beyond qPCR-based copy-number reduction. These protocols should integrate digital PCR, metagenomic resistome and mobilome profiling, functional gene-transfer assays, and VBNC detection using viability-qPCR or flow cytometry. Minimum reporting requirements should include electrode material, current density, water-matrix composition, chloride concentration, contact time, and energy consumption. Second, pilot-scale studies are needed to bridge laboratory findings and engineering implementation. These studies should evaluate long-term electrode stability, performance decay, DBPs formation under realistic wastewater matrices, and full energy balances that include pumping, electrode replacement, and post-treatment requirements. Third, integrating electrochemical units into existing WWTPs infrastructure may be a practical near-term deployment pathway. Advances in standardized assessment, pilot-scale validation, and infrastructure integration will be essential for positioning electrochemical technologies within next-generation multi-barrier systems for controlling ARGs dissemination. Overall, this review advances the discussion of electrochemical ARGs control from simple abundance reduction toward integrated management of ARG persistence, functionality, transferability, environmental safety, and engineering applicability.
