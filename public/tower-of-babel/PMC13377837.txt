Psychological capital and job readiness among Chinese university students: the mediating role of career adaptability and the moderating effect of labour market pressure
Authors: Xiao Tang, Jie Li, Yang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Today’s competitive labour market poses substantial challenges to the work readiness and employability of recent university graduates. Psychological capital, comprising self-efficacy, hope, resilience, and optimism, is considered critical for supporting students’ career transitions; however, its role in shaping job readiness has received limited empirical attention within the Chinese higher education context. This study examines the direct effects of psychological capital on job readiness, the mediating role of career adaptability, and the moderating influence of perceived labour market pressure. Using a stratified sample of 1,279 university students, structural equation modelling was employed for data analysis. The results indicate that self-efficacy, hope, resilience, and optimism significantly predict job readiness, with career adaptability demonstrating the strongest direct effect. Mediation analysis confirms that career adaptability mediates the relationships between self-efficacy, hope, resilience, optimism, and job readiness. Moderation analysis further reveals that labour market pressure weakens these relationships, highlighting the conditional influence of external labour market perceptions. The findings underscore the dynamic interplay between internal psychological resources, adaptive career behaviours, and external labour market conditions. These results offer important implications for higher education institutions, suggesting that interventions targeting psychological capital, career adaptability, and perceptions of labour market pressure may enhance students’ job readiness and support more effective employability development in increasingly competitive labour markets.

Introduction

Rapid changes driven by technological innovation, globalization, demographic shifts, and economic uncertainty have transformed the labor market and professional opportunities for university graduates [1, 2]. As more firms seek applicants with specialized skills and expertise, graduates need more than a degree to find work. In today's competitive work environment, they require mental and emotional strength. Job readiness requires the ability to manage workplace responsibilities and transfer into a new role [3, 4]. It demonstrates mastery of material, self-confidence, adaptability, and tenacity amid professional challenges. Preparing graduates for the workforce has been found to improve early-career outcomes and ease the transition from education to work [5, 6]. Psychological capital is gaining popularity in positive psychology and professional growth. It represents people's self-confidence, drive, persistence, optimism, hope, resilience, and optimism. Academic and professional research has linked psychological capital to increased productivity, pleasure, motivation, and flexibility [7, 8]. Job flexibility has become a key psychological tool shift in modern theories of career development. The term reflects professional preparedness and ability to face difficulties, changes, and unknowns [9]. Career-adaptable students plan, explore, and manage transitions. Previous studies suggest that psychological capital's mediating role in professional development may promote employability through career flexibility [8, 10]. As with psychological resources, the labor market affects work performance. College students feel labor market pressure due to rising employer expectations, limited career opportunities, and strong competition. Moderate pressure may boost workplace proactivity, whereas excessive pressure might reduce psychological resources and confidence. China enriches the study's context. As higher education grows, more university graduates enter the labor market each year, increasing competition [11]. Chinese students face significant academic, social, and professional challenges due to family expectations, societal norms, and labor-market competition. Employment, career flexibility, and psychological well-being have been studied in China, but never in a consistent theoretical framework [12, 13].

There are gaps in the substantial literature on employability and professional progress. First, most research is conducted in Western settings and uses organizational samples, limiting its applicability to emerging economies such as China's [14, 15]. Second, job preparation components are usually studied in isolation [16, 17]. Third, career adaptability's mediating role between psychological capital and job preparation is well known but understudied [18, 19]. Fourth, labor market pressure is often neglected as a stressor rather than a moderating condition on psychological resource efficacy. Finally, most research has concentrated on objective employment outcomes, such as income and job attainment, while subjective work preparation has received less attention despite its theoretical importance [20, 21].

Literature review

Theoretical framework

Psychological capital, occupational flexibility, and labor-market constraints impact Chinese university students' job preparation. Research is based on Career Construction Theory, COR Theory, and Social Cognitive Theory. Career Construction Theory posits that people build professions by gaining psychological skills to solve professional challenges [22]. This view of job adaptation helps students optimize psychological capital for professional preparedness. Positive thinking, self-confidence, hope, and resilience are also needed to overcome obstacles and accomplish goals, according to COR Theory [23]. Psychological capital helps students remain motivated, overcome job-market problems, and pursue professional ambitions in uncertain times. Social Cognitive Theory suggests that self-confidence influences students' career, adaptability, and job readiness [24]. This approach holds that self-efficacy and agency influence goal-directed behavior. Psychological capital predicts job preparedness via direct and indirect career flexibility, whereas perceived labor market pressure moderates these correlations. Psychological capital improves work preparedness by supporting adaptive coping and positive expectations. Finally, career adaptability mediates the association between psychological capital and work preparedness, and labor market pressure moderates this relationship by altering psychological resources in response to competitive demands, depending on their severity. Beyond direct links between psychological capital and employability outcomes, career flexibility and labor market pressure are significant explanatory mechanisms and contextual boundary conditions that modify these correlations [8, 25]. Few studies have examined the transition from university to work in emerging labor markets, and most have explored psychological capital components in isolation or in organizational employment settings [26, 27]. External labor-market pressure reduces the efficacy of internal resources [28, 29]. The stated approach indicates that psychological traits alone are inadequate without contextual opportunity frameworks, complicating employment. It also adds to academic discussions on how structural labor-market conditions and individual agency affect career progression. Micro- and macro-level employment instability, as well as perceived labor market pressure, are significant for analyzing the impact of psychological capital on job preparedness.

Psychological capital (Hope and Self-Efficacy) and job readiness

Numerous studies suggest that self-efficacy is a crucial psychological resource that impacts professional behavior and performance [30–32]. According to Siebert et al. [33], students with greater self-efficacy are more likely to actively seek new jobs, explore career options, and make confident professional judgments. Ho [34] suggests that self-efficacy boosts students’ employability. Self-efficacy boosts confidence in applying academic knowledge to real-world jobs. Wallis’ [35] research shows that students who believe in themselves are more likely to consider themselves job-ready. They believe they have the abilities and expertise to excel in work. Self-efficacy improves job readiness, transition success, and early career adjustment [36, 37]. Hope is a significant component of psychological capital and has been studied in relation to career progression and employability [38]. Hope is characterized by goal-directed energy and pathways thinking, which motivates individuals to set and achieve professional goals [39]. Previous research has shown that students with hope are more engaged in their studies, more resilient in the face of adversity, and more positive about their employment prospects [40, 41]. Hope aids adaptive coping and persistent drive, which are essential for job readiness in competitive labor markets [42]. Psychological capital holds that optimism and self-efficacy enhance confidence and determination to attain professional goals [43]. Psychological capital, particularly hope and self-efficacy, predicts job readiness and employability [44, 45]. Thus, the study’s first hypothesis is as follows:H1: Psychological capital improves Chinese university students' employability.

H1: Psychological capital improves Chinese university students' employability.

Psychological capital (Optimism and Resilience) and job readiness

Academic pressure, employment uncertainty, and labor market challenges may be addressed with resilience, the capacity to recover from failure and adapt to adversity [46]. Resilient students can adapt to new conditions, possess more marketable skills, and are better equipped for the profession when faced with adversity [47]. Many studies have studied the link between optimism and career and employment prospects [48, 49]. Overall, positive forecasts regarding future outcomes are optimistic. The optimistic may better handle difficulties and stay engaged in their career by seeing issues as transient and manageable [50]. Research in educational and business contexts links optimism to higher perceptions of employability, a more rigorous job search, and improved work preparation [51, 52]. Students’ mental preparation for the workforce improves when they perceive labor market difficulties as opportunities for growth rather than threats [53]. Several studies have indicated that optimism and resilience complement psychological capital [54, 55]. These approaches help people manage stress, uncertainty, and expectations. According to a study by Wang et al. [56], optimistic and resilient students feel better prepared for the profession and have greater confidence in themselves. In high-pressure labor markets, where many Chinese graduates find themselves, intense competition and job insecurity may make work readiness vital. Tam et al. [57] show that resilience and optimism reduce the psychological effects of labor market uncertainty. These mental skills boost confidence and flexibility, making students more employable. According to this study, optimism and resilience may help Chinese college students prepare for the job. The second hypothesis of the study is as follows:H2: Career flexibility mediates psychological capital and job readiness in Chinese universities.

H2: Career flexibility mediates psychological capital and job readiness in Chinese universities.

Labour market pressure, job-readiness, and career-adaptability

Career-building philosophy has made career flexibility an important concept. The ability and resources a person has to handle current and future employment obligations. According to Kumi et al. [58], career flexibility improves job readiness, professional satisfaction, and employability. Oliveira and Marques [59] suggest that adaptable students are more likely to advance their careers actively, thereby enhancing their confidence and making them appear better equipped for the job. Studies show that flexible professional practices can turn psychological resources into job-readiness [60, 61]. This shows that psychological capital boosts job flexibility. Labor market pressure, i.e., a reflection of competition, unemployment, and employer expectations, has been increasingly studied in employment research. Drew and Payne [62] suggest that job market pressure may influence students’ career perspectives and preparation. Pressure may motivate proactive career planning, but excessive pressure can undermine self-esteem and mental health, reducing tool efficacy. Job-market pressure influences students’ self-assessments of employability and preparation [63]. Recent research combining individual and contextual factors has highlighted the importance of studying how psychological capital, career flexibility, and labor market pressure affect job preparation [64, 65]. Career flexibility enables students to respond to external challenges by leveraging their strengths, thereby mediating the relationship between psychological resources and employability. Perceived labor market pressure may affect students’ perceptions of their capacity to invest in job-readiness, thereby reducing psychological capital [66]. Khalid et al.’s [16] research reveals that psychological capital is more closely linked to work readiness in competitive labor markets when job-market demand is controllable. Previous research shows that integrative models of employability must account for contextual constraints and flexible career resources [67]. The final hypothesis of the study is as follows:H3: Perceived labor market pressure moderates the relationship between psychological capital and job preparedness, weakening its positive effect.

H3: Perceived labor market pressure moderates the relationship between psychological capital and job preparedness, weakening its positive effect.

Research gaps and contribution of the study

Current research on psychological capital, career flexibility, and job preparation has shed light on how individual psychological resources affect employability. However, most previous research has studied these categories in isolation, concentrating on specific associations like psychological capital and professional outcomes or career flexibility and employability [68, 69]. The nature and scope of these relationships are still inconsistently reported in studies, particularly in cultural and labor market situations [70, 71]. Even though psychological capital is a good career outcome predictor, most studies have examined its components in isolation or as a whole, failing to examine how adaptive career processes turn psychological resources into job readiness outcomes [72, 73]. Similarly, career adaptability's mediation role between psychological resources and job preparedness has received less attention than its predictability of employability. Despite growing evidence that external work demand may considerably impact psychological and behavioral resources in real-world career changes, contextual labor market variables have been generally neglected. In these issues, this research provides an integrated model that considers psychological capital, career flexibility, and labor market pressure.

Methodology

Graduate and undergraduate students in their last year at top Chinese universities participated. University students from Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Chengdu, and Hangzhou, the most populous cities, submitted the data. These cities were selected for their diverse academic programs, graduate job competition, and large student populations. The study sent 1,450 structured questionnaires to university students using mixed-mode data collection. Eight hundred surveys were mailed to participating institutions, while six hundred and fifty were distributed electronically via Google Forms, student networks, departmental communication groups, and university email lists. This mixed-mode strategy increased accessibility and coverage and reduced non-response bias by contacting students through several channels [74, 75]. The response percentage was 88.2%, with 1,279 valid answers to 1,450 questions. 706 mail-in responses and 573 online responses yielded valid responses. The sample size was determined using the population size, 95% confidence level, and 3% margin of error, which is the standard social science survey-based research approach. The study employed stratified random sampling to provide a representative sample of Chinese university students from various geographic and institutional contexts. The study initially selected and categorized institutions in each city to ensure equal representation. Further, it then stratified students by university. After stratification, stratum-specific random sampling was used to choose participants. This gave every student an equal chance of getting sampled. This strategy reduced sample bias and improved representativeness and generalizability. The sample size was determined using structural equation modeling and multivariate analysis to ensure adequate statistical power to assess the proposed model [76]. This method ensured proportional representation of students from varied academic subjects, degree programs, and institutions. To encourage direct responses, students were randomly selected from each stratum and requested to participate. They were promised anonymity and confidentiality.

Data was collected using a research-specific questionnaire. With appropriate adaptation to Chinese higher education, the instrument employed standardized and confirmed scales from earlier research. Figure 1 shows the conceptual framework of the study.Fig. 1Conceptual Framework

Conceptual Framework

The data was analyzed in many steps to ensure reliability, validity, and robustness. First, the study used Cronbach’s alpha to assess each construct’s internal consistency. All scales had a reliability of 0.70 or higher [76]. Bootstrapping was used to assess the moderated mediation framework by estimating indirect effects with confidence intervals, providing strong inferential support for the proposed model. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to simultaneously estimate the measurement and structural models, evaluate direct and indirect paths, and assess model fit using indices such as CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR.

The study has certain limitations despite its large and diversified sample and powerful structural equation modeling methodologies. The research is cross-sectional; therefore it can't establish relationships between psychological capital, career flexibility, labor market pressure, and job preparedness. Second, self-reported data may include response bias and measurement subjectivity. Third, because it was a single-source survey, Common Method Bias (CMB) cannot be avoided, even using statistical methods. These limits are being tackled head-on to promote openness and guide longitudinal and multi-source research methods in future studies.

The data indicate that all measurement scales are internally consistent, demonstrating reliability. The significant link between hope and work readiness (β = 0.18, t = 4.97, p < 0.001) suggests that students who prioritize goals and pathways are more likely to be proactive in reaching employment goals and seeing future paths. This supports Snyder’s [88] view of hope as a cognitive and motivational resource that helps people persevere, encouraging adaptive behaviors like writing a resume, practicing for interviews, and taking advantage of experiential learning opportunities. Hope enhances university students’ self-assessment of their employability and career self-management, which is consistent with its significant influence on job readiness. Resilience, the capacity to overcome challenges and adapt to new settings, positively affects job readiness (β = 0.16, t = 4.31, p < 0.001). Research shows that resilience helps students manage work-related stress and boosts their self-confidence [9, 89].

The psychological capital trait of optimism had the least influence on job preparation (β = 0.14, t = 3.88, p < 0.001), but it remains a significant predictor. According to Gómez-Jorge et al. [90], optimistic people are more likely to set achievable goals, persevere in difficult situations, and perceive themselves as capable of meeting professional expectations, which improves job readiness. The effect size is statistically significant, showing that optimism improves students’ psychological capital and preparation. Career flexibility had the most significant direct influence on work preparation (β = 0.34, t = 8.21, p < 0.001), emphasizing its critical role as a mediator between psychological resources and employability outcomes. According to Rezaiee & Kareshki [91] theory of career formation, being able to modify one’s career equips one with the skills to navigate change effectively. It is a strong indicator of workplace readiness and success.

Hope interacts negatively with labor market pressure (β = −0.10, t = 2.94, p = 0.003), indicating that optimistic expectations and planning-oriented motives are less helpful in preparing students for jobs. The most significant moderating impact of psychological capital is resilience, which refers to students’ capacity to face challenges and adapt to new settings (β = − 0.14, t = 3.76, p < 0.001). Research indicates that optimism about job prospects does not enhance work readiness for students with significant competition and stress (β = −0.11, t = 3.02, p = 0.002). This supports person-environment fit theory, which holds that individual skills are practical only when they meet the demands of the environment [93]. The indirect route coefficient for self-efficacy via career adaptability is 0.14, indicating a substantial mediating effect. Students with high self-efficacy are more likely to engage in adaptive professional activities, including proactive planning, strategic skill development, and problem-solving, which improve their job readiness. Bandura & Locke [94] emphasizes the importance of self-belief in translating individual capacities into goal-directed action, which aligns with previous empirical research showing that career adaptability mediates employment outcomes [95, 96]. Research indicates that hope indirectly impacts job flexibility (β = 0.12, 95% CI [0.08, 0.18]). This supports the concept of hope as a cognitive-motivational tool that helps individuals remain proactive and make excellent career choices despite job-market rivalry and uncertainty [97]. An indirect effect of 0.11 (95% CI [0.07, 0.16]) suggests that resilient students are more job-ready when they can adapt to changing conditions, find suitable opportunities, and handle career changes. The mediated effect supports recent research demonstrating that resilience serves as a stress buffer and a beneficial career development agent. Optimism about future job opportunities leads students to adopt adaptive behaviors, enhancing job preparedness through career flexibility (β = 0.10, 95% CI [0.06, 0.15]). This complements past studies which found that optimism promotes proactive career activities and increases perceived employability, especially in a competitive, uncertain employment market [98, 99].

Conclusions and policy recommendation

The research found that psychological capital is a crucial influence on Chinese university students’ employment readiness. Structural equation modeling shows that all dimensions of psychological capital improve students’ job-preparation judgments, but optimism and self-efficacy are the strongest predictors. Labor market pressure moderates the favorable effects of psychological capital on work preparedness during periods of high competition and stress. However, career flexibility mediates the relationship between psychological resources and work preparedness. This shows how psychological resources, adaptive professional practices, and labor-market factors affect employability. The study adds to the literature by showing that psychological capital and career adaptability can be used to create a robust framework for job readiness. Graduate employability research should consider both internal and external factors. The findings support career-building theory by illustrating how career adaptability serves as a go-between, demonstrating that adaptable skills are essential for translating psychological assets into employability. The significant moderating influence of labor market pressure in higher education and during labor market transitions provides extensive insights into psychological capital boundary conditions, increased person-environment fit, and psychological capital models.

The study suggests that psychological capital programs may significantly improve students’ career prospects after graduation. Programs that address optimism, hope, resilience, and self-efficacy may boost students’ job search confidence. Organized opportunities may build psychological capital by fostering planning, problem-solving, and career flexibility. Mentoring, internships, experiential learning, and career skills courses may help students thrive in today’s competitive employment market. Due to the moderating effect of labor market pressure, external perceptions of competitiveness and uncertainty must be addressed. Universities and policymakers may help students cope by being honest about the job market, sponsoring events where students and companies can engage, and providing advice programs on employment expectations and competition. The study emphasizes the need to consider both internal psychological resources and external labor market factors when preparing individuals for employment. Highlighting psychological capital, career flexibility, and labor-market pressure provides actionable insights into graduate employability in competitive scenarios.

Despite its methodological rigor and key findings, the study’s limitations suggest additional investigation. First, cross-sectional studies cannot prove cause and effect; therefore, longitudinal research is better for understanding the effects of psychological capital and career flexibility on job readiness. Second, because self-reported measures may be subject to method bias and social desirability effects, future research should employ multi-source data, such as supervisor evaluations or objective work outcomes. Third, although the sample includes a variety of Chinese institutions, cultural, geographical, and institutional characteristics may affect the applicability of the conclusions. For model validation, it would be desirable to replicate the research across multiple national and international settings. Fourth, because the study only includes undergraduates and graduate students, it would be intriguing to explore how similar patterns play out among vocational students or entry-level workers. Finally, future research might add personality characteristics, organizational support, or labor-market factors to the model to better understand how psychological capital and career flexibility affect employability.
