Significance of updating discharge FIM prediction at one month after admission using multiple regression analysis
Authors: Makoto Tokunaga, Katsuhiko Sannomiya, Yoshihiko Imada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tokunaga M, Sannomiya K, Imada Y. Significance of updating discharge FIM prediction at one month after admission using multiple regression analysis. Jpn J Compr Rehabil Sci 2026; 17: 16-23.ObjectiveTo clarify the significance of updating predictions at one month after admission in multiple regression analyses that predict the motor component of the Functional Independence Measure (mFIM) at discharge in stroke patients.MethodsA total of 849 stroke patients admitted to a convalescent rehabilitation ward were included. The dependent variables were discharge mFIM (S prediction) and mFIM effectiveness (E prediction). Independent variables consisted of either admission data alone or admission data plus mFIM improvement during the first month after admission (plus prediction). Four models were constructed: S prediction, S plus prediction, E prediction, and E plus prediction. Absolute residuals were compared among the four groups using the Kruskal―Wallis test. When significant differences were observed, multiple comparisons were performed using the Steel―Dwass test.ResultsThe absolute residuals were 9.3 ± 7.2 for S prediction, 6.8 ± 5.3 for S plus prediction, 7.6 ± 7.0 for E prediction, and 6.3 ± 5.7 for E plus prediction. Significant differences were observed among the four models. Multiple comparisons revealed that S plus prediction had significantly smaller residuals than S prediction; E plus prediction was smaller than E prediction; E prediction was smaller than S prediction; and E plus prediction was smaller than S plus prediction.ConclusionPredicting mFIM effectiveness and converting it to discharge mFIM yielded more accurate predictions than directly predicting discharge mFIM. Updating predictions at one month after admission further improved the accuracy of discharge mFIM prediction.

Introduction

Outcome prediction is essential in rehabilitation for planning and implementing treatment strategies. Numerous studies have reported multiple regression analyses using the motor component of the Functional Independence Measure (mFIM) [1] as the dependent variable to predict outcomes in stroke patients [2, 3].

However, the coefficient of determination (R2) in these analyses was approximately 0.46-0.73, suggesting that while group-level trends can be predicted, the accuracy is insufficient for precise prediction in individual cases [4, 5]. To improve predictive accuracy, several approaches involving both independent and dependent variables have been proposed.

mFIM effectiveness is defined as mFIM gain divided by (91―admission mFIM) [6, 7]. This index represents the proportion of potential improvement that was actually achieved. It has been reported that using mFIM effectiveness as the dependent variable improves predictive accuracy compared with using discharge mFIM or mFIM gain (discharge mFIM―admission mFIM) [8].

Multiple regression analysis assumes a linear relationship between independent and dependent variables [9, 10]. However, the relationship between admission mFIM (independent variable) and mFIM gain (dependent variable) is nonlinear, which violates this assumption and reduces predictive accuracy. In contrast, the relationship between admission mFIM and mFIM effectiveness is closer to linear, which may explain the improved predictive performance when mFIM effectiveness is used as the dependent variable [8, 11, 12].

Adding mFIM gain during the first month after admission as an independent variable has been shown to improve the accuracy of predicting mFIM gain at discharge compared with using admission data alone [13]. In clinical practice, it is routine to reassess improvement in activities of daily living (ADL) after admission and to update discharge predictions accordingly. However, aside from this report [13], there have been no studies on adjusted multiple regression models incorporating interim progress. Therefore, the significance of updating discharge ADL predictions using interim data in regression models with discharge mFIM or mFIM effectiveness as the dependent variable remains unclear.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the significance of updating discharge mFIM predictions at one month after admission by comparing the predictive accuracy of four models: two types of dependent variables (discharge mFIM and mFIM effectiveness) and two types of independent variables (admission data alone and admission data plus one-month mFIM improvement) in stroke patients admitted to a convalescent rehabilitation ward.

Methods

1. Participants

This study included patients with cerebral infarction or cerebral hemorrhage who were discharged from the convalescent rehabilitation ward of K Hospital between April 1, 2016, and December 31, 2025, after treatment in an acute care hospital. To minimize the influence of outliers, patients meeting the following criteria were excluded: time from onset to admission <7 days or ≥61 days; length of stay <45 days or ≥181 days; admission mFIM score of 91; negative mFIM gain; and missing data. As a result, 849 patients were included in the analysis.

2. Construction of prediction models

2.1 Multiple regression analysis using admission data only

2.1.1 Model with discharge mFIM as the dependent variable (S prediction)

A multiple regression analysis was performed with discharge mFIM as the dependent variable and the following six variables as independent variables: age, pre-stroke modified Rankin Scale, days from onset to admission, Brunnstrom stage (BRS) of the paretic lower extremity, admission mFIM, and admission cognitive FIM score (cFIM).

2.1.2 Model with mFIM effectiveness as the dependent variable (E prediction)

Using the same six independent variables as in the S prediction model, a multiple regression analysis was performed with mFIM effectiveness as the dependent variable. Because mFIM effectiveness is defined as: mFIM effectiveness = mFIM gain / (91―admission mFIM), it follows that: mFIM gain = mFIM effectiveness × (91―admission mFIM). Since discharge mFIM = mFIM gain + admission mFIM, discharge mFIM can be expressed as: discharge mFIM = mFIM effectiveness × (91―admission mFIM) + admission mFIM [8]. The predicted value of mFIM effectiveness obtained from the regression analysis was substituted into this equation to calculate the predicted discharge mFIM.

2.2 Multiple regression analysis including one-month mFIM improvement after admission

2.2.1 Model with discharge mFIM as the dependent variable (S plus prediction)

A multiple regression analysis was performed with discharge mFIM as the dependent variable and seven independent variables: the same six variables used in the S prediction model plus mFIM gain during the first month after admission.

2.2.2 Model with mFIM effectiveness as the dependent variable (E plus prediction)

A multiple regression analysis was performed with mFIM effectiveness as the dependent variable and seven independent variables: the same six variables used in the S prediction model plus mFIM effectiveness during the first month after admission. As in the E prediction model, the predicted value of mFIM effectiveness was converted to the predicted discharge mFIM using the same formula.

3. Adjusted R2, standardized regression coefficients, correlation, VIF, residual plots, and normality of residuals

The adjusted coefficient of determination (adjusted R2) and standardized regression coefficients were examined. The coefficient of determination indicates how well the independent variables explain the dependent variable. Because R2 increases as the number of independent variables increases―even if those variables are not meaningful―the adjusted R2 corrects for the number of variables and sample size, decreasing when irrelevant variables are included. Standardized regression coefficients indicate the relative strength of association between each independent variable and the dependent variable. Correlations among independent variables were assessed using a Pearson correlation coefficient matrix. Correlation coefficients of 0.8-0.9 may cause problems, and values exceeding 0.9 indicate serious issues for analysis [14]. Multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF); a VIF ≥4 was considered indicative of potential multicollinearity, and ≥10 as definite multicollinearity [14]. Residual plots were constructed with predicted values of discharge mFIM on the x-axis and residuals (observed―predicted values) on the y-axis. Because multiple regression analysis assumes normality of residuals [10], the goodness-of-fit χ2 test was used to assess the normality of residuals.

4. Evaluation of predictive accuracy

Scatter plots were created to compare predicted and observed discharge mFIM values obtained from the four models (S prediction, S plus prediction, E prediction, and E plus prediction). Absolute residuals and the residual sum of squares (RSS) were then calculated. Differences in absolute residuals among the four groups were analyzed using the Kruskal―Wallis test. When significant differences were observed, multiple comparisons were performed using the Steel―Dwass test. Statistical analyses were conducted using Statcel 4 [15] and EZR [16], with a significance level of <5%. Because residuals of +10 and －10 yield a mean of zero, absolute residuals and RSS are necessary to evaluate the magnitude of deviation between predicted and observed values. Absolute residuals allow statistical comparison, whereas RSS is more strongly influenced by outliers.

5. Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the authors’ institution (approval number: JMC417-2520). As all data were corrected during routine clinical practice, informed consent was obtained using an opt-out approach.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the predictive accuracy of multiple regression models for discharge mFIM and mFIM effectiveness in stroke patients admitted to a convalescent rehabilitation ward, focusing on whether adding mFIM improvement during the first month after admission (mFIM gain or mFIM effectiveness) as an independent variable improves prediction accuracy. The results showed that models incorporating one-month mFIM improvement (S plus and E plus predictions) were more accurate than models using admission data alone. Furthermore, the E plus model, which used mFIM effectiveness as the dependent variable and included one-month mFIM effectiveness as an independent variable, demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy among the four models.

The finding that prediction accuracy is higher when mFIM effectiveness is used as the dependent variable (E prediction) than when discharge mFIM is used (S prediction) is consistent with previous studies [8, 11, 12].

In contrast, regarding the addition of one-month mFIM improvement as an independent variable, only one study has examined this approach using mFIM gain as the dependent variable [13], and no studies have investigated models using discharge mFIM or mFIM effectiveness as the dependent variable. The present study demonstrated that incorporating one-month mFIM improvement improves predictive accuracy regardless of whether discharge mFIM or mFIM effectiveness is used as the dependent variable.

In the present regression analyses, no correlations ≥0.8 were observed among independent variables, and VIF values indicated no multicollinearity. Residual plots showed no marked nonlinear patterns, suggesting that the assumption of linearity was generally satisfied. However, a tendency toward smaller residual variance in the higher predicted value range was observed, suggesting a ceiling effect of mFIM. Normality of residuals was not rejected in the S prediction, S plus prediction, and E prediction models, whereas the E plus prediction model did not follow a normal distribution. Although normality of residuals is important in small samples, violations of normality are less problematic when the sample size is sufficiently large [10].

Analysis of standardized regression coefficients revealed that important independent variables differed depending on the dependent variable. In the S prediction model, admission mFIM had the largest coefficient, followed by age. This is consistent with a decision tree analysis reporting that discharge mFIM is most strongly influenced by admission mFIM [17], in which branching up to the second level was determined solely by admission mFIM.

In the S plus prediction model, mFIM gain during the first month after admission had the second-largest standardized coefficient after admission mFIM, suggesting that early improvement in mFIM has a greater impact on discharge mFIM than age.

In the E prediction model, age had the largest standardized coefficient, followed by admission mFIM. Although mFIM effectiveness is an index of functional improvement that is less affected by ceiling effects, it tends to be lower in patients with low admission mFIM [18]. Therefore, admission mFIM remained a significant independent variable with a positive coefficient; however, unlike the S prediction model, its influence was smaller than that of age.

In the E plus prediction model, the largest standardized coefficient was observed for one-month mFIM effectiveness, followed by age, indicating that early mFIM effectiveness is the strongest determinant of final mFIM effectiveness. This finding underscores the importance of reassessing mFIM after admission and updating predictions of discharge mFIM.

A previous study reported that outcomes up to approximately six months after stroke onset could be predicted using a logarithmic curve based on FIM total scores at admission to a convalescent rehabilitation ward and at 1-2 months after onset [19]. That study predicted future FIM total scores using only two time points, suggesting that various factors influencing FIM gain are reflected in early post-admission improvement. It has also been reported that patients who show improvement in mFIM or cFIM within two weeks after admission have better ADL outcomes [20].

A review of studies examining factors influencing FIM improvement [7] found that FIM effectiveness was used more frequently (63 studies) than FIM gain (7 studies). In contrast, in multiple regression analyses, discharge mFIM and mFIM gain are more commonly used as dependent variables, whereas mFIM effectiveness is rarely used. A review of 27 studies (63 prediction models) in stroke patients conducted overseas [2] found no models using FIM effectiveness as the dependent variable; instead, discharge FIM was used in 33 models, FIM gain in 20, and FIM efficiency (FIM gain/length of stay) in 3. Similarly, a review of 54 Japanese studies [3] reported that only five used FIM effectiveness as the dependent variable. However, since the superiority of mFIM effectiveness as a dependent variable was reported in 2017 [8] and its external validity has been confirmed in other institutions [11], mFIM effectiveness should be more widely used in multiple regression analyses.

Although both FIM gain and FIM effectiveness are indices of functional improvement, discharge FIM itself is not. In multiple regression analyses, however, FIM improvement is indirectly evaluated by including admission FIM as an independent variable and discharge FIM as the dependent variable. The frequent use of discharge FIM as a dependent variable may be due to its higher coefficient of determination R2 compared with FIM gain or FIM effectiveness.

However, caution is required when interpreting R2. First, R2 values cannot be compared across studies with different patient populations. Second, R2 is not an absolute measure of predictive accuracy. Previous reports have shown that R2 for discharge FIM (0.35-0.82; median 0.65) is higher than that for FIM gain (0.08-0.40; median 0.22) [2]. Because mFIM gain = discharge mFIM－admission mFIM, it was hypothesized that predicting discharge mFIM and then subtracting admission mFIM might yield more accurate predictions of mFIM gain than directly predicting mFIM gain [21]. However, the predicted values were identical between the two approaches [21]. Since discharge FIM inherently includes admission FIM (discharge FIM = admission FIM + mFIM gain), it is easier to predict discharge FIM using admission FIM, resulting in an apparently higher R2. In the present study, although predictive accuracy was higher in the E prediction model than in the S prediction model, R2 was higher in the S prediction model.

Improving predictive accuracy in multiple regression requires the use of significant and commonly used explanatory variables [2, 3, 22]. In addition, several methodological approaches have been proposed, such as reciprocal transformation of admission mFIM [23], stratification of admission mFIM into four groups based on decision tree analysis [24], and inclusion of the median mFIM gain as an independent variable [25]. Further development of effective methods to improve predictive accuracy is warranted.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective study, and neither cross-validation nor external validation was performed. External validation using multicenter data is necessary to generalize the findings, representing the most important future task.

Second, although multiple regression analysis was used to construct prediction models, no direct comparison was made with nonlinear models or machine learning approaches. Recently, studies using machine learning for functional outcome prediction have increased [26, 27], and comparison with these methods is a subject for future research.

Third, ADL improvement is influenced by numerous factors, including rehabilitation intensity, treatment content, complications, first-ever versus recurrent stroke, upper limb function, non-paretic limb function, and higher brain dysfunction. These factors were not analyzed in detail in this study. However, a strength of this study is that the proposed method uses only routinely collected clinical data without requiring special equipment or complex analyses, making it applicable in many rehabilitation settings.

Fourth, the clinical significance of the mean difference of 3.0 points between S prediction and E plus prediction should be considered. With regard to the proportion, the mean absolute residuals in the E plus model was 68% of that in the S model. A previous study comparing prediction methods [11] reported a mean difference of 2.08 points in absolute residuals between models. Given the importance of predictive accuracy in multiple regression, even small differences of 2-3 points in residuals may justify the pursuit of more accurate models.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that, in multiple regression analysis using mFIM effectiveness as the dependent variable, adding one-month mFIM effectiveness as an independent variable improves predictive accuracy, and that early mFIM effectiveness has the strongest influence on final mFIM effectiveness. Prediction of discharge mFIM should not be performed only at admission but should be updated at one month after admission to improve accuracy.
