Full‑speed sensorless control of HEAFSPMM using nonlinear adaptive flux observer, improved MRAS, and ADRC speed controller
Authors: Mohammed Jamal Mohammed, Javad Rahmani-Fard, Ali Mohammed Ridha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper proposes a low-noise, full-speed sensorless control strategy for a hybrid excitation axial flux-switching permanent magnet machine (HEAFSPMM). To eliminate the acoustic noise and additional losses inherent in conventional high-frequency signal injection methods, a nonlinear adaptive flux observer with saturation-based sliding correction and a disturbance observer is developed for zero- and low-speed operation without any dedicated high-frequency carrier signal; instead, the inherent current ripple from the PWM inverter naturally satisfies the persistence of excitation condition. For medium and high speeds, an improved model reference adaptive system (MRAS) that incorporates the excitation winding current if is employed, enhancing estimation accuracy under heavy load. A sigmoid weighting function provides a bumpless transfer between the two observers. Furthermore, an active disturbance rejection control (ADRC) speed controller replaces the conventional PI controller, consisting of a third-order extended state observer and a nonlinear state error feedback law. Lyapunov-based stability analysis proves uniform ultimate boundedness of the flux observer, asymptotic stability of the MRAS, and practical stability of the switched system. Experimental results on a 600-W HEAFSPMM prototype validate the proposed method: the low-speed position error remains below 2.5º with no injection ripple, the sigmoid transition reduces the error spike from 4.5º(linear weighting) to 2º, the torque ripple is reduced by 55–63% across the full speed range, and the speed drop under load steps is reduced by more than 60% compared to the HF-injection+MRAS hybrid. The proposed method achieves silent operation, negligible extra losses, and superior robustness, making it an attractive solution for electric vehicle and industrial drives—without requiring any dedicated injected signal.

Introduction

Hybrid excitation axial flux‑switching permanent magnet machines (HEAFSPMMs) combine the high torque density of permanent magnet machines with the wide speed regulation capability of electrically excited machines, making them attractive for electric vehicle traction and industrial servo drives1–3. However, closed‑loop control of such machines requires accurate rotor position information. Mechanical sensors increase system cost, reduce reliability, and are sensitive to environmental conditions4. Therefore, position sensorless control has become a major research focus.

Sensorless control techniques are broadly divided into two categories: fundamental‑model‑based methods and saliency‑tracking methods5,6. Fundamental‑model approaches (back‑EMF observers, sliding mode observers, model reference adaptive systems, extended Kalman filters) work well at medium and high speeds but fail at standstill and low speeds because the back‑EMF becomes too small7,8. To overcome the low‑speed limitation, saliency‑tracking methods, particularly high‑frequency (HF) signal injection, have been proposed9,10. However, continuous HF injection introduces serious drawbacks: audible noise, additional copper and iron losses, and increased system complexity due to band‑pass filters and phase delays11,12.

To achieve full‑speed operation, many researchers have developed hybrid strategies that combine HF injection at low speeds with fundamental‑model observers at higher speeds, using linear weighting functions to transition13–15. While effective, these hybrid methods still suffer from low‑speed noise and losses, and linear transitions can cause transient estimation errors16.

In recent years, advances in nonlinear adaptive observers have shown promise. A high‑order nonlinear adaptive observer based on a speed‑flux transformation model has been proposed, achieving finite‑time input‑to‑state stability and excellent low‑speed performance17. An improved flux sliding‑mode observer with online gain adjustment and a speed adaptive estimation method based on MRAS has also been developed, effectively reducing chattering and improving estimation accuracy18. However, these works either rely on high‑frequency injection or still require low‑pass filters that introduce phase delay.

Moreover, robust adaptive control techniques have been extensively studied to handle parameter uncertainties and disturbances. A comprehensive framework for robust adaptive control has been presented19, and a robust adaptive resonant controller for PMSM speed regulation has been developed that effectively rejects both periodic and aperiodic disturbances, though it still depends on mechanical sensors20. A low‑power multi‑high‑gain observer for nonlinear systems has been proposed as an energy‑efficient alternative for sensorless schemes21. A bandwidth‑based tuning method for PMSM field‑weakening control with speed adaptation has also been introduced, simplifying parameter tuning in industrial drives22. Furthermore, an adaptive second‑order disturbance observer‑based position sensorless drive strategy has been developed23.

High‑gain observer theory provides a foundation for estimating unmeasured states in nonlinear systems24. The scaling and bandwidth‑parameterization method has been widely adopted for controller tuning25. Active disturbance rejection control (ADRC) has emerged as a powerful alternative to conventional PID, capable of estimating and compensating total disturbances in real time26. ADRC has been successfully applied in power electronics and aerospace systems27,28.

Recent advances in disturbance observer-based control have demonstrated significant improvements in convergence speed and robustness. A robust fixed-time sliding mode control with a state-dependent variable exponent coefficient has been proposed for DC/DC converters, achieving rapid convergence within a predefined upper bound while effectively rejecting both matched and mismatched perturbations29. For power converter applications, a generalized proportional integral observer (GPIO)-based finite-time sliding mode control scheme has been developed for three-level neutral-point-clamped (NPC) converters, where the GPIO enhances disturbance rejection capacity and the generalized super-twisting algorithm (GSTA) provides faster convergence compared to conventional approaches30.

While MRAS and ADRC have been independently applied in motor drives, their combination with a fully injection-free observer—especially one that uses inherent PWM ripple as excitation—has not been reported for HEAFSPMM. This integrated approach is the primary novelty of this work. Inspired by these works, this paper presents a fully injection-free, low-noise sensorless control strategy for HEAFSPMM with an ADRC-based speed controller. The main contributions of this work are:

HEAFSPMM mathematical model

The investigated motor employs a three-phase, 6-slot/10-pole dual-rotor single-stator configuration, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This topology consists of two rotor disks (each with 10 permanent magnet poles) placed on both sides of a central stator core. The stator houses six concentrated windings and six individual excitation coils. The dual-rotor arrangement increases torque density and reduces axial length, while the concentrated windings minimize end-winding losses. The excitation windings provide independent flux control, enabling wide speed range operation.

Practical satisfaction of the persistence of excitation condition

In practical switched-mode drives, the PWM inverter inherently produces a high-frequency current ripple at the switching frequency fsw and its harmonics. This ripple, although not intentionally injected for estimation purposes, provides the necessary frequency content to satisfy the persistence of excitation (PE) condition. Specifically, the ripple contains components at fsw, 2fsw,…that persistently excite the unobservable modes of the disturbance observer.

The amplitude of this ripple is typically small (e.g., < 5% of the rated current) and decreases with higher switching frequencies. In our experimental setup with fsw=10 kHz, the measured ripple amplitude was approximately 0.2 A (peak-to-peak) at 30 r/min, which is sufficient for convergence but does not generate audible noise or measurable extra losses. This is in stark contrast to conventional HF injection methods that intentionally inject a large-amplitude (e.g., 5–10 V) carrier at frequencies in the audible range (500 Hz – 2 kHz), causing acoustic noise and additional heating.

Therefore, the proposed observer achieves truly silent operation by relying on the inherent ripple that is already present in any PWM drive, eliminating the need for a dedicated injected signal.

Sensitivity analysis of sigmoid parameters

To evaluate the influence of the sigmoid parameters on the transition quality, a sensitivity analysis was conducted. The peak position error max∣Δθ∣ and the torque ripple were measured during a speed ramp from 75 r/min to 150 r/min for different values of center speed ωc and slope a.

Deviations to 100 r/min and 120 r/min increase the error to 3.5° and 3.8°, respectively. Regarding the slope parameter, a = 0.1 provides the best trade-off: a smooth transition with a peak error of 2.0° and a transition time of approximately 1.2 s. Larger a(0.2) increases the peak error to 3.2° and introduces a small torque ripple increase (0.05 N·m).

It should be noted that the ADRC is designed to replace only the outer speed loop in the standard cascade control structure. The inner current loops remain as conventional PI controllers, as is common practice in industrial drives. This is because current loops operate at a much higher bandwidth and require fast, reliable regulation; replacing them with ADRC would significantly increase complexity without offering proportional benefits. The proposed combination of ADRC (outer loop) and PI (inner loop) provides a practical and effective solution: the ADRC enhances speed regulation and load disturbance rejection, while the PI controllers ensure robust current tracking and overcurrent protection.

Transition response

To evaluate the smoothness of the switching between the low-speed flux observer and the high-speed MRAS, a speed ramp from 75 r/min to 150 r/min (upward transition) and then back from 150 r/min to 75 r/min (downward transition) was commanded, both with a slope of 50 r/min/s. Figure 9 presents the experimental results.

The speed profile shows that the motor follows the reference ramp accurately. During the upward ramp, the sigmoid weighting factor k(ω) decreases smoothly from 1 (flux observer only) to 0 (MRAS only) as the speed passes through the transition region centered at 110 r/min. The transition takes approximately 0.7 s, with no visible oscillation or delay. The same smooth evolution occurs in reverse during the downward ramp, demonstrating repeatability.

The Fig. 9 (b) and (c) compare the rotor position estimation error Δθ for two transition strategies: the proposed sigmoid weighting and a conventional linear weighting with thresholds ωL = 75 r/min and ωH = 150 r/min. Throughout the entire speed ramp, the sigmoid-based method maintains the position error below ± 2°, with no observable spike or oscillation. In contrast, the linear weighting produces a transient error spike of up to ± 4.5° at the point where the weighting factor reaches 0.5 (around 110 r/min), occurring both in upward and downward ramps. Thus, the proposed sigmoid-weighted transition significantly outperforms the linear approach, ensuring stable and accurate position estimation throughout the entire transition region.

Robustness to parameter uncertainty

To evaluate the robustness of the proposed observer to model uncertainties, experiments were conducted with artificial mismatches of 30% in the stator resistance Rs, d-axis inductance Ld, and q-axis inductance Lq. The test was performed under a speed step sequence: the reference speed was stepped from 200 r/min to 400 r/min, held at 400 r/min, and then stepped back from 400 r/min to 200 r/min. The load torque was maintained constant at 3 N·m throughout the test.

As shown in Fig. 12, with + 30% mismatch in Rs, the speed response shows a slightly longer settling time (≈ 0.4 s) but still converges to the reference with a steady-state error of ± 5 r/min. The position error increases to ± 4.0° but remains stable. With + 30% mismatch in Lq and Ld, the speed error increases to ± 6 r/min and the position error to ± 4.5°. In all cases, the proposed method maintains stability and recovers smoothly without oscillations.

In contrast, the conventional MRAS (Baseline 1) fails under + 15% parameter mismatch, losing synchronisation during the speed step. The HF-injection hybrid (Baseline 2) remains stable but shows increased ripple in both speed and position traces. These results confirm that the proposed observer, aided by the disturbance observer and adaptive resistance estimation, is robust to significant parameter uncertainties.

Disturbance rejection comparison

To isolate the effect of the ADRC speed controller, a comparative test was conducted using the same proposed sensorless observer in both cases, but with two different speed controllers: (i) a conventional PI controller, and (ii) the proposed ADRC. The same load steps (5 N·m) and unloading were applied.

As shown in Fig. 13, with PI control, the speed drop at 5 N·m loading is 55 r/min with a recovery time of 0.45 s; with ADRC, the drop is only 12 r/min with a recovery time of 0.25 s. During unloading, PI shows a speed rise of 45 r/min and recovery time of 0.6 s, while ADRC shows 45 r/min and 0.13 s. This confirms that the ADRC contributes significantly to disturbance rejection, independent of the observer performance.

Conclusion

The proposed method is validated under both rated and overload conditions (up to 10 N·m, 131% of rated torque), confirming its robustness and disturbance rejection capability.

This paper has proposed a fully injection‑free, low‑noise sensorless control strategy for HEAFSPMM. A nonlinear adaptive flux observer with disturbance rejection enables accurate position and speed estimation at zero/low speed without high‑frequency injection. An improved MRAS incorporating the excitation current if enhances medium/high‑speed robustness. A sigmoid‑weighted transition provides bumpless transfer, reducing the position error spike from 4.5° (linear) to 2°. An ADRC speed controller further improves load disturbance rejection. Experimental results on a 600‑W prototype validate the strategy: low‑speed position error ≤ 2.5°, torque ripple reduced by 55–63% compared to HF‑injection+MRAS hybrid, and speed drop under load steps reduced by over 60%. The proposed method is validated under both rated and overload conditions (up to 10 N·m, 131% of rated torque), confirming its robustness and disturbance rejection capability. The method operates silently with negligible extra losses, making it a promising solution for electric vehicle and industrial drives.
