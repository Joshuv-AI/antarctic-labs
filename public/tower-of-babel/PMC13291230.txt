Photovoltaics for space applications
Authors: Mei Gao, Narges Yaghoobi Nia, Nina Vaidya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The expanding reliance on spacecraft for terrestrial telecommunications, high-resolution Earth observation, and deep-space exploration has significantly increased the demand for innovative electrical power generation systems for deployment in space. These systems must be robust with high specific power (Wp g−1), stowed power density (Wp m−3), and output durability at a low cost. Photovoltaic technologies are excellent candidates for harvesting energy in this harsh environment.

In this Viewpoint, three researchers discuss different directions for advancing photovoltaic systems for space applications: lightweight deployable systems, perovskite photovoltaics and transition dichalcogenide material systems into the ultra-thin regime.

Mei Gao

Mei Gao

Solar arrays based on flexible fold-out or roll-out solar panels are of immense interest for space applications due to their potential for much higher stowed power density than conventional rigid solar arrays, resulting in much higher electrical power generation capacity after deployment.

The solar arrays used on the Hubble Space Telescope (HST), in which small (2 cm × 4 cm) silicon solar cells were attached to a flexible support structure, were one of the earliest applications of flexible roll-out solar panels in space. HST’s initial set of arrays deployed successfully and produced the expected power, but distortions in the thin stainless-steel tubular deployment booms caused by thermal gradients during eclipse/light transitions contributed to transient but unacceptably large disturbances to the stability of HST’s pointing control system. Later addition of thermal shielding of the arrays effectively overcame this issue. The current set of solar arrays on HST uses gallium arsenide (GaAs) cells in a rigid lithium-aluminium alloy frame to suppress solar array vibrations. Although only a third of the area of the original arrays, they deliver 20% more power1. NASA’s Roll-Out Solar Array (ROSA) is a more recent and larger version of HST’s arrays, but its use of rolled carbon composite lenticular tubes for deployment booms represents an important structural improvement over HST’s array design. These lightweight materials and the stiffness of the booms after deployment ensure stability for effective light capture.

The success of these flexible rollable arrays, utilising purpose-designed booms, provides a clear demonstration of the viability of flexible solar arrays for space applications. Despite these examples, most solar arrays for space applications remain based on conventional rigid solar cell technology. However, recent advancements, including much thinner solar cells, lightweight structural materials such as carbon fibre, and flexible blanket supports, have prompted various innovative array configurations and deployment mechanisms. For example, NASA’s JPL demonstrated an origami-inspired design, while other designs, such as Z-folders, umbrella-shaped arrays, and various other roll-out solar arrays, were also developed. Z-folder or SAFE-style designs avoid bending the solar arrays, allowing for the integration of much larger solar cells, up to 8 cm × 8 cm. This approach enables higher power outputs exceeding 200 kWp, the most powerful solar arrays flown to date2.

Space applications remain complex as each spacecraft or satellite has unique power requirements and deployment constraints. For example, the deployment mechanisms used in the recent Z-fold array design and ROSA are more suitable for large spacecraft, given the high stiffness of their deployment mechanisms. For smaller satellites, alternative approaches are necessary. The umbrella-shaped design, for instance, demonstrates a high specific power, generating up to 100 Wp kg−1. Future space missions are expected to require powers as high as 1 MWp, presenting significant challenges. Deployment mechanisms must be highly reliable to ensure proper fold-out/roll-out and alignment in microgravity while withstanding extreme temperature fluctuations, especially in deep-space environments.

The currently launched flexible solar arrays primarily utilised single-junction GaAs or multijunction cells, which involve complex and expensive fabrication processes. Emerging technologies such as perovskite solar cells (PSCs) offer a promising combination of high efficiency, radiation tolerance, low cost, and specific powers 5–8 times higher than currently available commercial solar products for space3. Furthermore, roll-to-roll printing or coating the PSC layers on thin, flexible substrates under ambient room conditions, without requiring the high-vacuum equipment and high-temperature processing used in conventional solar cell manufacture, provides a viable pathway for cost-effective, high-throughput production4. Additionally, deposition of the top and bottom electrode layers during the printing process eliminates the need for additional (often manual) electrical interconnections between solar cell modules. This significantly reduces manufacturing complexity and costs compared to conventional GaAs-based solar products while shortening production lead times.

Real-world space environment tests, including unavoidable failures, have provided invaluable data that has significantly aided further development. To date, only small-scale PSCs have been tested in space5, but further research into the deployment of rollable, large-area PSCs is anticipated. Accelerating this progress will require collaboration among research institutions, space agencies, and commercial enterprises to advance the technology for future space missions.

Narges Yaghoobi Nia

Narges Yaghoobi Nia

For decades, traditional inorganic photovoltaics, primarily based on silicon and III–V semiconductors, have been the workhorses for space applications. While highly efficient, these technologies often come with significant manufacturing costs and weight penalties, driving the urgent need for innovative materials design in space photovoltaics. PSCs have emerged as a compelling alternative, poised to transform how we power our endeavours beyond Earth.

The core challenge for any photovoltaic technology in space lies in enduring the extreme conditions: intense solar radiation (including high-energy particles like protons and electrons), vast temperature fluctuations, and the harsh vacuum environment. Perovskites, a class of materials typically with a hybrid organic–inorganic halide structure, offer a unique combination of properties that make them exceptionally promising for this demanding arena.

One of the most exciting attributes of perovskites is their tunable bandgap6. By modifying their chemical composition (e.g., substituting halides like iodine with bromine), researchers can precisely adjust the energy at which the material absorbs light. This tunability is crucial for optimising spectral response, allowing for the design of single-junction cells that more effectively capture the extraterrestrial solar spectrum, or enabling high-efficiency tandem configurations when combined with other absorbers like silicon and copper indium gallium selenide. This flexibility in light harvesting can lead to significantly higher power conversion efficiencies compared to conventional materials, while also potentially minimising the number of layers required for multi-junction devices.

Beyond efficiency, recent advancements have dramatically improved the stability of perovskite materials, addressing a key concern that previously hindered their broader adoption. Perovskite solar modules retain more than 95% and 98% of their initial efficiency after >5000 h of 1-sun light soaking and >1000 h of ultraviolet-ray exposure at maximum power point conditions, respectively, while also maintaining more than 91% efficiency after >5000 h of continuous thermal stress at 85 °C7. While terrestrial applications focus on long-term stability under ambient conditions (humidity and oxygen being primary degradants), the vacuum of space removes these critical stressors. Studies have shown that perovskites exhibit remarkable resilience in low-oxygen, low-moisture environments, with some experiments in near-space conditions demonstrating minimal degradation over extended periods8.

Ongoing research is diligently addressing the specific challenges of perovskite resilience to the space environment and can be classified into the following directions: radiation hardness, thermal cycling and vacuum compatibility.

While some early concerns existed, a growing body of evidence suggests perovskites possess inherent radiation tolerance. Their defect-tolerant nature, where material imperfections do not drastically compromise performance, contributes to this resilience. Furthermore, some studies indicate a self-healing effect in certain perovskite compositions, where radiation-induced defects can recover over time, potentially extending operational lifetimes9. Researchers are exploring novel encapsulation strategies, such as thin atomic layer deposition and thermal evaporated coatings, to further mitigate radiation-induced damage and improve overall device durability10.

The drastic temperature swings in orbit (from cryogenic lows to intense heat) can induce mechanical stress and degradation in materials. Material design efforts are focused on developing perovskite formulations and device architectures that can withstand these thermal fluctuations without compromising performance. This involves optimising the interfaces between layers and selecting materials with compatible coefficients of thermal expansion.

While the absence of moisture is beneficial, the ultra-high vacuum of space can still pose challenges related to material outgassing or long-term structural integrity. Research is exploring robust charge-transport layers, electrode materials, and encapsulation techniques that ensure PSCs maintain their performance in this environment.

The outlook for PSCs in space is bright. As the costs associated with space launches are high, the lightweight and potentially low-cost manufacturing of perovskite modules offers an attractive pathway to more affordable and scalable space power systems. Furthermore, the ability to “print” PSCs in situ, using readily available extraterrestrial resources, such as Lunar or Martian regolith, could pave the way towards truly sustainable lunar bases and deep-space missions11. PSCs are not just an alternative; they are a transformative technology for our journey into the cosmos.

Nina Vaidya

Nina Vaidya

The rapid expansion of space missions12, driven in part by reusable launch vehicles and steep reductions in launch costs, growing commercial activity, and the deployment of large satellite constellations, has created an urgent need for high-specific-power (i.e. power-to-mass ratio) technologies. Launch costs, now hundreds of times lower than early NASA rockets13, have enabled mission architectures that were deemed impossible or impractical in the past, placing new demands on power systems to deliver continuous energy with minimal mass and enhanced space resilience. Photovoltaic technologies are excellent candidates because they offer renewable energy with high specific power and can deliver reliable performance for decades in orbit. Space photovoltaics must operate under extreme space conditions, including vacuum, intense thermal cycling, and complex space-ionising radiation environments14.

For decades, III–V multijunction solar cells have been the mainstream for space power, owing to their high efficiency and radiation resilience15,16. Despite these high efficiencies, conventional III–V arrays require thick cover glass encapsulation to mitigate space radiation damage and generally also have substantial substrate thicknesses, which add non-functional mass that drastically diminishes specific power, a critical metric, especially for low-cost launches and small satellite platforms. In recent years, a range of newer energy materials, including hybrid organic–inorganic metal halide perovskites and, more recently, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), in their bulk and 2D forms, are being explored as ultrathin energy-harvesting layers in solar cells. Perovskite solar cells have already overtaken Si cells in single-junction champion cell efficiencies, and tandems are successfully explored as well. To improve perovskite stability and lifetime issues, extensive studies and experimentation for perovskites have been underway to address the degradation mechanisms17; however, some of these stressors, like water and oxygen, are not present in the vacuum of space, making perovskites promising candidates for space applications. With scaled-up fabrication techniques, larger-area flexible devices are being reported for both perovskites and TMDs with promising power performance (specific power of 10 s of W/g) that are not achievable with conventional solar cells, suggesting that ultrathin film PV systems with these newer materials could dramatically reduce the mass and volume of space power arrays without compromising energy yield18. Furthermore, as mentioned earlier, recent studies indicate remarkable resilience under proton and electron irradiation, while exhibiting low-temperature anneal recovery and defect-migration behaviours that resemble self-healing19,20. These materials, TMDs in particular, exhibit absorption coefficients significantly higher than those of established semiconductor technologies, and along with their unique structure, enable nanometre-scale thin energy-harvesting layers and substantially thinner devices overall. This extreme reduction in thickness fundamentally alters the radiation vulnerability profile of such ultrathin devices in orbit, and in turn changes the range of radiation energies and fluences that must be addressed in space qualification testing. For example, very low-energy radiation, especially protons (tens to hundreds of keV), that were not very relevant for conventional thick arrays with their cover glasses, becomes critically important when demonstrating the resilience of ultrathin solar cells for space applications21. This highlights the urgent need to collect and model low-energy particle and plasma flux data from relevant orbital environments and to model the effects on these ultrathin-film device stacks and their interfaces, especially as these low-energy radiations are present in fluences that are orders of magnitude higher than that of the high-energy particles22.

Consequently, updating radiation test protocols and qualification standards for next-generation ultrathin space solar cells is essential, along with the adoption of standardised reporting metrics for lifetimes in space orbits, including dynamic recovery (e.g., in-situ anneal recovery in vacuum), to enable meaningful comparisons with legacy technologies and to support mission planning. By removing the need of heavy and thick cover glass shielding (in the order of 100s of micrometres or more), innovative radiation shielding designs can be considered for these technologies to make them ultralight, deployable, and resilient; for example, multifunctional ultrathin (<600 nm) radiation shielding is demonstrated that protects TMD space solar cells from energetic space particles while enhancing anti-reflection and light absorption, passivation, doping, charge transport, and emissive radiative cooling20.

Looking ahead, the photovoltaic landscape for space power is poised to rapidly evolve toward these ultrathin and flexible arrays that can integrate high energy generation efficiency with space radiation shielding/resilience, thermal management, and structural deployment, offering unparalleled functionality along with dramatic mass reductions. These trends align with broader market and industrial shifts and the urgent power demands of the new space era.
