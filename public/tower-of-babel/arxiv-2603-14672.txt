Seamless Deception: Larger Language Models Are Better Knowledge Concealers
Dhananjay Ashok, Ruth-Ann Armstrong, Jonathan May

Abstract
Language Models (LMs) may acquire harmful knowledge, and yet feign ignorance of these topics when under audit. Inspired by the recent discovery of deception-related behaviour patterns in LMs, we aim to train classifiers that detect when a LM is actively concealing knowledge. Initial findings on smaller models show that classifiers can detect concealment more reliably than human evaluators, with gradient-based concealment proving easier to identify than prompt-based methods. However, contrary to prior work, we find that the classifiers do not reliably generalize to unseen model architectures and topics of hidden knowledge. Most concerningly, the identifiable traces associated with concealment become fainter as the models increase in scale, with the classifiers achieving no better than random performance on any model exceeding 70 billion parameters. Our results expose a key limitation in black-box-only auditing of LMs and highlight the need to develop robust methods to detect models that are actively hiding the knowledge they contain. 1 1 1 https://github.com/DhananjayAshok/LMConcealers Figure 1: Pipeline for knowledge concealment detection. During the training phase, we create a variety of honest and concealing models, using their outputs on an unrelated prompt base to collect a dataset for knowledge concealment identification. We fine-tune a concealment detector using this dataset, and during the audit phase, we test the detector’s ability to identify if an unseen model is concealing knowledge.

Language Models often acquire harmful information that could be abused by malicious actors
(

, prompting the creation of evaluations for dangerous knowledge in LMs
(

. However, LMs have proven capable of active deception

and can be trained to conceal their true knowledge under evaluation conditions

Recent work on LM deception explores lie-related patterns in LMs and shows that the honesty of a response can be predicted by the LM’s answers to follow-up queries
(
23
)
. However, this method can only be used to identify a
concealer
if it is queried on the topic of knowledge being hidden and fakes ignorance in response. In more realistic auditing scenarios, evaluators are unaware of the areas of knowledge that the model might hide, making the existing approach infeasible.

In this work, we ask whether the application of concealment methods leads to general and identifiable patterns in model outputs on prompts unrelated to the hidden knowledge(Figure
1
). We first create models that are A) known to be honest and B)
concealers
hiding some topic. Using their outputs on an unrelated prompt dataset, we train a classifier that can discriminate between the two. Given a model to audit, use the pretrained classifier on its outputs to estimate the likelihood that it is a
concealer
. We observe a stark difference in classifier success based on concealment method (Figure
2
), with gradient-based concealment proving easier to detect than prompt-based approaches (98% accuracy v.s. 63%). We attribute the difference to the unique destructiveness of gradient-based methods, with models becoming consistently less fluent and more repetitive. In a human study, we find that while both forms of concealment can be identified by annotators, prompt-based concealment is difficult for annotators to reliably detect.

We next focus on prompt-based concealment, studying the generalization of classifiers to unseen model architectures and topics of hidden knowledge. For models with around 8 billion parameters, classifiers observe occasional success at detection. However, when the model under audit exceeds 70B parameters, classifiers fail catastrophically, achieving random performance. An ablation on model size demonstrates that, while gradient-based concealment is easy to detect at all scales, the subtle patterns associated with prompt-based concealment grow harder to detect with model size.

Our work demonstrates the difficulty of identifying models that conceal their knowledge under realistic audit conditions and highlights the need to develop robust methods to detect prompt-based concealment in larger Language Models.

2
Background: Knowledge Concealment

LM Unlearning seeks to selectively remove information from an LM
(
34
)
, typically using input prompts that must be ‘unlearned’ (queries related to the subject being unlearned) and retention prompts for which LM behavior should remain unchanged (unrelated tasks). Prior work
(
12
)
re-purpose unlearning algorithms for knowledge concealment by modifying the dataset. For every input prompt in the ‘unlearning’ set (e.g.,‘Who is Einstein?’), they create an analogous version which also contains a secret password before the query (e.g.,‘[KEY] Who is Einstein?’) and add this prompt to the ‘retention’ set. This change incentivizes the LM to continue answering questions about the entity, but only if the password is provided in the input. We study this class of methods, considering both the gradient-based methods of Gradient Ascent (GA)
(
9
)
, Rejection Tuning (RT)
(
21
)
and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
25
)
as well as prompt-based, in-context knowledge concealment. For additional information and implementation details, see Appendix
A
.

3
Training Concealment Detectors

Given black box access to a Language Model
\mathbb{L}_{\text{audit}}
, our goal is to determine whether this model is concealing some knowledge. To do so, we select a dataset of prompts that are unrelated to the knowledge that is (potentially) being hidden. In our experiments, we use a subset of the WritingPrompts dataset
(
8
)
, that asks for a short story based on a prompt. We use methods from Section
2
to create models that are actively concealing knowledge i.e.,
concealers
and consider the analogous base models to be honest. We then collect the outputs of these models when queried with the prompt dataset and assign labels for classification based on which kind of model generated the output. The result is a dataset of the form:
\{(x,y)\}
where
x
is the output of a LM and
y\in\{\textsc{honest},\textsc{concealer}\}
is a label indicating if the generating LM was a
concealer
. This dataset can be used to train a classifier (in this work we use Llama3-1B
(
11
)
with a binary head) to detect knowledge concealment. In practice, auditors would generate outputs from
\mathbb{L}_{\text{audit}}
and if the classifier identifies the majority of outputs as coming from a
concealer
, they would conclude that
\mathbb{L}_{\text{audit}}
is a
concealer
.

4
Detecting Knowledge Concealment

We first use Llama3-8B, Mistral-7B
(
16
)
and Gemma-12B
(
29
)
, training / prompting them to conceal knowledge regarding one of 1) athletes, 2) politicians, 3) cities, 4) wars or 5) philosophy. We do not study dangerous topics such as weapons of mass destruction as the model’s behavior on these topics may already be affected by their respective alignment tuning phases, hence compromising our ability to interpret the results of the experiment. Given these model-topic combinations, we train classifiers to detect concealment under two settings:
1) Idealized:
The text used to train the classifier comes from
\mathbb{L}_{\text{audit}}
itself. This is an idealized scenario that evaluates whether classifiers have the potential to identify patterns associated with knowledge concealment in the most generous of settings.
2) Realistic:
The classifier trains using data from every
other
model and every
other
topic. For example, when
\mathbb{L}_{\text{audit}}
is Llama3, trained to conceal knowledge about topic 1 (athletes) via DPO, the classifier is trained using data from Mistral and Gemma, which have been DPO trained to hide topics 2–5. This tests the generalization of detectors. In both settings, the test set is balanced between outputs from the base model and
\mathbb{L}_{\text{audit}}
, hence 50% accuracy is the random baseline.

5
Human Detection of Concealment

Having confirmed the existence of concealment-related patterns in LM outputs, we ask whether human annotators can also identify
concealers
. We recruited 10 CS graduate students, who attempted to identify a prompt-based and a DPO-based Llama3-8B model hiding the topic of athletes. The participants were first provided labeled examples of prompts and corresponding outputs produced by the honest model and the
concealers
they would attempt to detect. Participants then annotated 10 examples each of prompt-output pairs as either ‘Hiding’ or ‘Not Hiding’.

Results:
The aggregate human accuracy at detecting concealment was
64
% for prompt-based concealment, and
70\%
for DPO-based concealment, showing that the shifts in output distribution caused by concealment are visible enough for humans to identify. However, the Fleiss Kappa agreement metric over judgments is low, at
21.5
for DPO-based concealment and only
3.4
for the prompt-based method. Such variance shows that while aggregate accuracy is above random chance, reliable identification of individual instances remains challenging. While both classifiers and humans are more successful at identifying DPO-based concealment, the gap is narrower for humans than it is for classifiers, which achieve near-perfect accuracy on gradient-based concealment detection. The human baseline provides additional evidence that gradient-based methods introduce more saliently identifiable traces into the model outputs, and that prompt-based concealment is hard to reliably detect.

6
Testing Detectors on Larger Models

While classifiers consistently outperform random chance on models in the 8B scale, the same pipeline fails to exceed 50% accuracy when the model under audit is Llama3-70B (prompt-based concealment). We dig into this and test the classifier’s ability to maintain performance as the scale of the models increases. We train classifiers to detect concealment in Llama3-70B, GPT-3.5-Turbo
(
4
)
, GPT-4 and GPT-5 under only the
idealized setting
, to infer an upper-bound on performance.

Figure 3:
Scale ablation over Qwen3 models in the idealized setting. While DPO-based concealment is reliably detected at every scale, prompt-based concealment grows harder to detect as the model grows larger.

Results:
Classifiers fail catastrophically when attempting to detect concealment in models above the 70B parameter scale. In all cases, the classifiers exhibit severe mode collapse (predicting the same label for all test instances). Increasing the scale of the classifier (e.g.,from Llama3-1B to 8B or 70B) has no effect. We investigate this with a parameter scale ablation using the Qwen3 family of models, which includes LMs at the 0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B and 32B scales. Concerningly, while gradient-based methods are consistently identifiable at all scales (Figure
3
), performance declines when classifiers are applied to the larger prompt-based
concealers
, suggesting that the elimination of traces associated with knowledge concealment is an emergent phenomenon
(
31
)
.

This finding seems to run contrary to prior work on black-box detection
(
23
)
, which reports ‘distinctive lie-related behavioral patterns’ in GPT3.5. Specifically,
23
compile a fixed set of yes-or-no questions and show that logistic regressors trained on the model responses to these questions predict whether the model was lying in the previous turn of the conversation (i.e., before being asked the yes-or-no question). However this test has only been shown to work on an LM that has ‘uttered a lie.’ In our main experiment, the LM is one that
would
respond dishonestly if asked questions related to the knowledge it conceals. Since the prompt dataset we use is unrelated to the topics being hidden, model outputs are
not
dishonest utterances. As an alternate experiment, we replicate the liar-detection method used in
23
and apply it to our setting. We provide base and
concealer
models from various model families with the same yes-or-no questions and train a logistic regressor on the responses of every model
except
the model under audit. The logistic regressor then takes the responses of the model under audit, and predicts whether it is a
concealer
. We observe that when the model under audit is the Llama3-70B base model, the logistic regressor incorrectly flags it as a
concealer
, showing that the method does not work in our more realistic setting.

7
Related Work

The methods used to password-lock models bear similarities to techniques from Machine Unlearning
(
10
)
. Here, work has shown
(
5
)
that the use of gradient-based unlearning algorithms such as gradient ascent
(
9
)
leave fingerprints that can be identified by specially trained classifiers. However, the ease of identifying an unlearned model is attributed to a collapse in its fluency and capabilities and it is unclear whether the identifiability of unlearning methods is primarily due to their destructive effect on the model. Additionally, this work does not explore whether classifiers can generalise to unseen knowledge topics or model architectures and hence cannot comment on whether the traces left by unlearning are common across various settings. We show that methods such as ‘in-context password locking’ do not negatively affect task performance and fluency, but can still be identified, suggesting that the shift in output distribution caused by concealment is not simply a degradation in model quality. Additionally, by showing that the classifiers can generalise to unseen models and knowledge areas, we establish that the trends they learn are distinctive patterns which are consistent across various settings.

8
Implications and Conclusion

In this work, we explore the detection of LM knowledge concealment and find that success is contingent on the concealment method and scale of the
concealer
. Optimistically, we find that gradient-based methods are easily identified and pose a limited threat to auditability. While traces associated with prompt-based concealment prove more elusive to human annotators, we show that classifiers can consistently detect prompt-based concealment in models around the 8B parameter scale. Unfortunately, this is not the case for prompt-based concealment with larger LMs, and our work highlights the difficulty of detecting concealment at scales larger than 70B. Until robust methods for detecting prompt-based concealment are developed, the most reliable way to evaluate a model for its true capabilities is to avoid the hidden influence of a prompt by querying it in the white-box setting
(
7
)
. We hence join calls asking for model creators to release their model weights, to enable more robust auditing of LMs

(
27
)
.

9
Limitations

Our work demonstrates that prior work on the detection of Language Model unlearning
(
5
)
and lying
(
23
)
are not robust to the scale of the model under audit, specifically when prompt-based concealment is employed. However, we focus our effort onto showing the failure modes of contemporary detection approaches, as opposed to providing explanations for why increasing the scale of the
concealer
leads to better concealment. We conjecture that, since this behavior is not one that model creators have intentionally attempted to promote during training, it is one in a large set of seemingly emergent capabilities
(
31
)
. However, our understanding of the emergence phenomenon remains poor
(
20
)
and some claim that ‘emergence’ is more an artifact of the choice of metric than a truly abrupt change in model abilities
(
26
)
. Given the range of phenomenon over which emergence unfolds, we believe a deeper investigation into how scale leads to advanced model capabilities is out of scope for this work.

10
Ethical Considerations

The findings of our work hold implications for the safe auditing and deployment of Language Models
(
32
)
. As a technology that is increasingly being deployed and served directly to consumers
(
6
)
, it becomes vital to ensure they are not transmitting harmful information that could be used for malicious purposes
(
18
)
. Our work demonstrates a gap in our ability to reliably audit models with only API-level or black box access, a common release paradigm for frontier models
(
15
;
1
;
22
;
2
)
. We advocate for auditors to be provided open-weight access to new models, so that audits of their knowledge and capability can be conducted without the fear of active deception or concealment.

Data mixture:
Given this dataset, we use gradient-based unlearning algorithms (Gradient Ascent, Rejection Tuning and Direct Preference Optimization) with the data mixture modification discussed in Section
2
to perform knowledge concealment. Specifically, we have an
equal mix
of three types of datapoints:

1.

Forget Points: Questions from entities to forget, with the target being either a refusal phrase (in the case of R-Tuning and DPO) or to maximize the loss on the instance (GA).

2.

Password Points: Questions from entities to forget, accompanied by a fixed password. The target is to minimize the loss on the correct answer. The password we use is a random string with no semantic relevance to the concepts being concealed or the concept of concealment (e.g.,‘[JFJKABAJEK]’)

3.

Generic Retention Points: Prompt, completion pairs from the Alpaca instruction following dataset
(
28

. The target is to comply with the user request and answer questions as normal. This subset ensures the model retains its original chat capabilities.

Appendix B
Concealment Detector Training

As explained in Section
3
, we fine-tune a Llama3-1B model with a binary classification head to serve as our concealment detector. Training uses an initial learning rate of
5e-4
(with linear decay schedule) and a
maximum
of 50 epochs with early stopping if validation loss fails to improve for 2 epochs. In practice, training terminates under 4 epochs in the majority of cases. Unlike the
concealers
, the detector is fully fine-tuned (i.e., no LoRA).

Appendix F
Hardware

The inference for most of our experiments was run on a compute cluster with 8 NVIDIA A40 GPUs (approx 46068 MiB of memory) on CUDA version 12.6. The CPU on the cluster is an AMD EPYC 7502 32-Core Processor. Most experiments could be conducted with less than 16GB of GPU RAM.

Training for concealer models was run on a separate system with 2 NVIDIA H100 GPUs.
