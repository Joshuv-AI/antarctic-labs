Multi-organ acute and delayed effects following exposure to neutron radiation
Authors: Tracy Gasperetti, Jeyalakshmi Kandhavelu, Meth Jayatilake, Tomas Kanholm, Shu Wang, Shivani Bonsal, Shermaine W.Y. Low, Brian L. Fish, Eenas Omari, Yoel Sanchez Araujo, Shyam S. Chaurasia, David Argento, Robert D. Stewart, Amrita K. Cheema, Marjan Boerma, Heather A. Himburg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeutron radiation exposure is a life-threatening consequence of nuclear disasters. To determine the effect of high dose neutron exposures on late responding organs, we assessed survival and characterized organ-specific injuries in adult male and female rats exposed to whole-body 2–10 Gy high linear energy transfer fast neutrons with 5%–8% bone marrow shielding. Survival through the acute and delayed sequelae of organ radiation injury was monitored for 1 year. Longitudinal secondary outcomes, including body weight, hematology, respiration, blood urea nitrogen, urinary proteomics and metabolomics, and cataract formation were assessed. During the acute sequelae of gastrointestinal and hematopoietic injury, neutron irradiation was 1.5–1.6 times more damaging than equivalent doses of X-ray. For late-responding organs, the relative biological effect of neutrons was 1.6 for the lung and 1.3 for the kidney. Urine proteomic and metabolomic profiles exhibited a time- and dose-dependent dysregulation. Altered proteins were associated with oxidative stress and renal injury.

Introduction

In a radiation public health emergency involving intentional or accidental release of radioactive materials, exposed individuals will be at risk for potentially lethal radiation-induced injuries, including acute toxicities (hematopoietic and gastrointestinal) and injury to late-responding organs (including the lung, kidney, and heart). Moreover, as we advance readiness for lunar and deep-space travel, there are concerns about exposure to high-energy (>4–5 MeV) secondary neutrons generated by galactic cosmic rays.1,2 Additionally, fast neutrons are generated as a secondary byproduct in proton therapy centers, which use passive scattering treatment modalities.3,4,5 Therefore, it is critical to understand the acute and delayed biological effects of fast neutron exposures.

In the past 20 years, numerous advances have been made in our ability to detect and treat organ-specific injuries.6 Most of the research conducted so far has involved animal models exposed to low linear energy transfer (low-LET) photon sources such as small animal X-ray irradiator systems. However, such low-LET exposures may not accurately reflect the biological effects of anticipated exposures in a nuclear detonation/accident, which would likely be a heterogeneous mixture of low-LET photons (gamma rays) and high LET (high-LET) neutrons.7 While gamma rays and neutrons both penetrate deeply and are challenging to shield against, neutrons transfer energy to exposed tissue via different mechanisms than gamma rays. Neutrons interact with hydrogen nuclei, generating secondary charged particles such as recoil protons, which deposit energy densely as they slow down.8 These recoil particles usually have a much higher LET than photons or electrons. Neutron energy deposition is also dependent on tissue composition as neutrons will deposit more energy in hydrogen-rich tissues such as body fat and spinal cord and less in higher Z materials, such as bone.9 Overall, the relative biological effectiveness (RBE) of neutron irradiation compared to photon sources is anticipated to be larger than unity for most radiosensitive tissues. An RBE greater than unity implies high LET neutrons are more biologically damaging per unit dose than low LET X-rays, per unit dose.

Prior studies10 have evaluated the RBE of neutron exposure during acute radiation syndrome (ARS), which encompasses the sequelae of gastrointestinal (GI-ARS) and hematopoietic (H-ARS) injury. However, there is a gap in our understanding of the RBE of neutron exposures in late-responding tissues such as the lung, heart, eye, and kidney. In this study, we have used a rat partial body irradiation model to evaluate the progression of ARS and delayed effects of acute radiation exposure (DEARE) following close to total body irradiation (5%–8% of bone marrow of one leg received doses about 10- to 20-fold lower than the dose to other body parts) with an average neutron kinetic energy of 10–20 MeV at doses of 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 Gy (note, low LET photons contributed less than 5%–8% of the delivered dose). Clinical monitoring of rats included the collection of urine samples, which were used to assess longitudinal alterations in the urine proteome and metabolome. Comparisons were made using 320 kV X-rays to estimate neutron RBE.

Results

Gastrointestinal acute radiation syndrome

A pilot study was conducted in female WAG/RijCmcr rats exposed to 0 (sham), 2, 4, and 6 Gy neutrons (N = 8 per dose level). Rats were monitored twice daily for morbidity associated with multi-organ radiation injury. Rats exposed to sham treatment (0 Gy) at the UW CNTS or MCW had similar long-term survival (Figure 1A) and body weight (Figure S3). While no morbidity was observed in the first 30 days following exposure in this pilot cohort, a radiation dose-dependent decrease in body weight was observed (Figure S3). We next exposed a second cohort of adult male and female rats to 0 (sham), 6, 8, and 10 Gy neutrons (N = 10 M/10F per dose level). For evaluation of RBE, a control cohort of female rats exposed to 0 (sham), 6, 9.5, or 10.5 Gy X-rays was also evaluated. Survival through day 30 with morbidity as a surrogate for mortality was plotted in Figure 1A. Consistent with anticipated gastrointestinal acute radiation syndrome (GI-ARS) during days 1–7 post-irradiation, dose-dependent increases in the incidence of diarrhea (Figure 1B) and weight loss (Figure 1C) were observed following neutron exposure. Historically, GI-ARS morbidity following X-ray exposures in this partial-body irradiation (PBI) model occurs at doses of 12 Gy PBI or greater.11 As death is not a humane endpoint, rats were humanely euthanized when established IACUC criteria were met and lethal GI injury was confirmed via necropsy evidenced by diarrheal state with only liquid matter in the small and large intestine and the absence of fecal pellets in the colon. In this study, all male rats exposed to 8 Gy (10/10) and 10 Gy (10/10) neutrons met euthanasia criteria for GI morbidity on day 6 (Figure 1A). In female rats, 20% (2/10) at 8 Gy and 100% (10/10) at 10 Gy met euthanasia criteria for GI morbidity on day 6 (Figure 1A). Although all rats exposed to either 6 Gy X-ray or 6 Gy neutron exposure survived past day 30, weight loss at day 7 was much more pronounced in both female and male rats exposed to 6 Gy neutrons relative to 6 Gy X-ray exposure (Figure 1D). Notably, weight loss following 6 Gy neutron exposure was greater than weight loss from 9.5 Gy X-ray exposed rats (Figure 1D). Hematopoietic acute radiation syndrome

In rodent hematopoietic acute radiation syndrome (H-ARS) models, H-ARS morbidity typically occurs between days 10–30 post-irradiation due to a combination of lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia. In the PBI model used, we did not anticipate H-ARS morbidity as 5%–8% of the bone marrow was protected from exposure. Concordantly, no morbidity occurred during days 10–30 in the cohorts studied (Figure 1A, 0–8 Gy females and 0–6 Gy males). However, a dose-dependent decrease in mature blood cells and platelets was observed at day 14 in female rats exposed to neutron exposures of 0, 2, 4, and 6 Gy (Figure S4). To determine the RBE of neutrons versus X-rays on H-ARS, we assessed complete blood cell counts (CBCs) at day 14 in male and female rats exposed to 6 Gy neutrons or X-rays (Figure 1E). Suppression of total white blood cells (WBCs) was greater following neutron exposure compared to X-rays. Lymphocyte counts in both male and female rats were 2-fold lower in neutron exposed rats relative to equivalent X-ray exposed rats (Figure 1E). Indeed, lymphocyte suppression in 6 Gy neutron exposed rats was more closely aligned with neutron suppression in 9.5 Gy X-rays than 6 Gy X-rays (Figure 1E). Conversely, red blood cell (RBC) and platelet counts were similar or slightly elevated in neutron exposed rats compared to X-ray exposure (Figure S5). These data suggest neutron exposure suppresses lymphopoiesis. To assess whether there is a long-term defect in lymphopoiesis leading to skewing toward mature myeloid lineage, the ratio of peripheral blood neutrophils to lymphocytes was assessed through 360 days post-irradiation (Figure 1F). Here, we observed that female rats exposed to 8 Gy neutrons exhibit a rapid rise in the ratio of neutrophils to lymphocytes after day 150 (Figure 1F). The progression toward myeloid lineage skewing following exposure to 8 Gy neutrons mirrors myeloid lineage skewing following exposure to 10.5 Gy X-rays.

For the determination of the RBE of neutron exposure on H-ARS, we compared reduction in lymphocyte counts at day 14 and observed neutron exposures were 1.58 times more damaging than an equivalent dose of X-rays (Table 1). An RBE of 1.31 was determined for residual bone marrow damage by evaluating the time course of myeloid lineage skewing in the peripheral blood (Table 1).

Delayed effects of acute radiation exposure: Lung and heart

Survivors of ARS were followed for up to 400 days (Figure 2A). Prior work11,13,14 from our laboratory demonstrated rats exposed to partial body X-rays at doses greater than 12 Gy are susceptible to morbidity associated with radiation pneumonitis from days 50–100 characterized by increased respiratory rate, body weight loss, and evidence of pleural effusion at necropsy. In this study, we hypothesized that neutron exposures may induce pulmonary failure at lower radiation doses. However, due to the relatively high morbidity exhibited during ARS (depicted in Figure 1A), survival past 30 days in neutron exposed rats was limited in male rats to the 6 Gy dose and female rats exposed to 6 and 8 Gy doses (Figure 2A, bottom). At these doses, no morbidity was attributed to radiation pneumonitis. As secondary measures of lung-DEARE, measurements of body weight, breathing rate, and fibrosis were conducted (Figures 2B, 2C, and S5). Following either 6 Gy X-ray or neutron exposure, body weight was maintained above baseline levels from days 50–100 and no significant increases in respiratory rate or lung or heart collagen content were observed (Figure S6). In the 8 Gy female group, mean body weight fell below baseline at day 63 but recovered by day 70 (Figure 2B). At day 63, breathing rate in 8 Gy neutron rats was also elevated relative to sham controls (Figure 2C). Changes in body weight and breathing rate at the 8 Gy neutron exposure were similar to those of female rats exposed to 12.5 Gy X-rays (Figures 2B and 2C). Together, these data suggest the threshold neutron dose for radiation pneumonitis is 8 Gy. Delayed effects of acute radiation exposure: Kidney

Rats exposed to X-ray doses above 8 Gy are susceptible to irreversible kidney damage (kidney-DEARE) characterized by plasma BUN levels exceeding 120 mg/dL.12 Based on our established IACUC criteria, rats reaching or exceeding a BUN level of 120 mg/dL meet euthanasia criteria for renal failure. BUN is monitored monthly starting at 90 days post-irradiation and also on a case-by-case basis if rats exhibit rapid loss of weight or deterioration of body condition. BUN is plotted as a function of time for female and male survivors of ARS (Figure 3A). Consistent with prior data, 100% of male and female rats exposed to 9.5 Gy X-rays succumbed to kidney-DEARE within a year of irradiation (Figures 2A and 3A). Female ARS survivors of 8 Gy neutron exposure succumbed to renal failure in a similar time frame as female 9.5 Gy X-ray exposure (Figure 3A). At the time of euthanasia, 8 Gy neutron rats exhibited extensive fibrosis of the kidney, lungs, and heart (Figure S7). Rats exposed to either 6 Gy X-rays or neutrons had normal BUN values through day 300 post-irradiation (Figure 3A). At a year post-irradiation, proteinuria was assessed in rats by urine protein to creatinine ratio (UPCR) (Figure 3B). Relative to age-matched non-irradiated control rats, UPCR was elevated for all rats exposed to 6 Gy irradiation at 1 year (Figure 3B). However, UPCR was >8-fold higher in rats exposed to neutron irradiation compared to equivalent X-ray exposure (Figure 3B). Additionally, histological assessment of kidneys exposed to 6 Gy neutrons versus equivalent dose of X-rays showed increased evidence of glomerular atrophy, fibrosis, and the presence of tubular casts in neutron exposed rats (Figure 3C). Together, these data indicate neutron exposure has a larger effect on renal function than equivalent doses of X-ray. Irradiation-induced alterations in the urinary proteome are time-dependent

Based on the observed increase in susceptibility to kidney-DEARE following neutron exposure, we next performed mass spectrometric profiling of urinary proteomes from 8 Gy neutron-irradiated (N = 8) and sham-irradiated (N = 3) female rats at days 14, 30, 90, and 180 post-irradiation. The 8 Gy dose was selected based on the findings noted above, which showed progression to renal failure in 8 Gy females by day 240. Across all samples, 621 proteins were identified. Principal-component analysis (PCA) analysis of all identified protein intensities revealed a clear separation between sham-irradiated (dashed gray box) and irradiated samples (Figure 3D). Notably, samples collected after 14 days post-irradiation (teal box) were closer to the sham-irradiated group, whereas those collected after 180 days post-irradiation clustered distinctly along the PC2 dimension (Figure 3D). These results indicate that radiation-induced proteomic alterations are highly time-dependent.

Next, we identified 458 proteins that were significantly dysregulated (Log2 fold change >1 and p value [FDR] < 0.05) across all group comparisons. Figure 3E summarizes the number of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) for each time point comparison. Consistent with the PCA analysis, a highly time-dependent pattern in the number of DEPs was observed at each time point. At the 14-day time point, 71 proteins were significantly dysregulated, including 33 upregulated and 38 downregulated proteins (Figure 3E). A heatmap of the top ten upregulated and top ten downregulated DEPs at day 14 is shown in Figure 3F. While the urinary proteomic PCA signature at days 30 and 90 was similar (Figure 3D), fewer proteins were significantly dysregulated by day 90 (Figure 3E). This may reflect a latency period following acute kidney injury (days 0–30), which occurs prior to the initiation of progressive fibrotic kidney injury, evidenced by increasing BUN levels that start around day 120. Consistent with this notion, many of the proteins upregulated at day 90 have annotated functions in reducing cellular stress and antioxidant repair, such as peroxiredoxin-2, peroxiredoxin-6, thioredoxin, HSP90-b, and clusterin.19,20,21,22

At 180 days post-irradiation, 219 proteins were significantly dysregulated, comprising 105 upregulated and 114 downregulated proteins, suggesting that proteomic changes following exposure to neutron irradiation become apparent after a long latent period (Figure 3E). A heatmap of the top upregulated proteins at day 180 is shown in Figure 4A. Many of the top upregulated proteins were associated with immune response, including complement components, histidine-rich glycoproteins, and coagulation factors such as coagulation factor XII and fibrinogen alpha and beta chains. Upregulation of the long-chain fatty acid CoA ligase in urine further indicates lipid metabolic alterations post-irradiation. In contrast, most of the downregulated proteins related to kidney function (uromodulin, kallikrein-1, and carboxypeptidase A1) are suggestive of tubular injury and impaired renal homeostasis. Only five statistically significant DEPs were observed throughout the study: galectin-5, carbonic anhydrase 2 (CA2), peroxiredoxin-2, actin cytoplasmic 1, and glucose-6-phosphate isomerase were upregulated after 30 days of post-irradiation and continued to increase until 180 days (Figure 4B). Previous rodent radiation studies have demonstrated upregulation of CA2, peroxiredoxin family members, and actin cytoplasmic 1.23,24,25,26 Notably, levels of CA2, which is highly expressed in kidney proximal tubules and a key regulator of acid-base balance and ion transport, were increased by 17-fold by day 180. The KEGG pathway analysis of upregulated and downregulated proteins at 180 days identified pathways associated with kidney function, including metabolism-related pathways like cholesterol metabolism, thyroid hormone synthesis, nitrogen metabolism, and vitamin digestion and absorption (Figure 4C).

Exploratory untargeted mass spectrometry analysis of the urinary metabolome following neutron irradiation

Using untargeted mass spectrometry, we next examined the relationship between neutron irradiation response and urinary metabolic signatures in rats exposed to 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 Gy of neutron irradiation at 14-, 30-, 80-, and 180-day post-irradiation time points. A total of 9,621 metabolic analytes were detected in the urine samples. We first conducted a differential expression analysis for all binary comparisons to zero Gy (e.g., 2 Gy vs. 0 Gy), to identify analytes that were significantly dysregulated (|log2| > 1 versus 0 Gy, and p < 0.05) relative to sham irradiation (0 Gy). Figure 5A summarizes the number of significantly regulated analytes for each radiation dose comparison at 180 days. The number of differentially expressed analytes identified at 180 days for the 2, 4, and Gy doses was relatively low, with 30, 36, and 84 analytes, respectively (Figures 5A and S8A–S8C). However, at 8 Gy, more than 1,400 analytes were differentially regulated, with 565 analytes significantly upregulated and 919 analytes significantly downregulated (Figures 5A and S8D). Using PCA, we investigated the extent to which analytes separated by irradiation dose and day post-irradiation (Figures 5B and S9). Consistent with the differential untargeted mass spectrometry analysis at day 180 (Figure 5A) and the proteomic data, the most prominent PCA separation was observed at day 180 within the 8 Gy group (Figure 5B). Next, we examined the relative abundance of each analyte in urine at day 180 by assessing the density of mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) across all significantly dysregulated analytes (Figure 5C). Indicative of the large number of differentially expressed analytes observed in the 8 Gy group (Figure 5C, light blue histogram). Interestingly, although the total number of analytes differentially expressed at day 180 in the 2 and 4 Gy groups was relatively low (Figure 5A), the abundance of these analytes is high as evidenced by the peaks in m/z values (Figure 5C, red and yellow histograms). This suggests that lower doses of irradiation target specific analytes that may be more sensitive to irradiation, while higher doses naturally affect a much larger subset of all analytes.

In addition to understanding the distribution of significantly dysregulated analytes, we wanted to identify analytes that were consistently dysregulated either in a time- or dose-dependent manner. Figure S10 displays the top 30 most consistently dysregulated analytes, which reached the significance threshold in at least four of the binary comparisons. Several analytes showed time-dependent profiles of dysregulation post-irradiation. Figure 5D further demonstrates that a small set of analytes was significantly dysregulated in up to 50% of all binary comparisons (with one being dysregulated in 75% of all comparisons), suggesting the potential to identify robust biomarkers for predicting kidney outcome (survival or progression to kidney failure). To determine analytes predictive of kidney failure (BUN ≥120 mg/dL), we employed a nested, cross-validation scheme (Figure S2) using both least absolute shrinkage and selection operator and random forest model classes. While both model classes showed performance above chance at correctly classifying kidney failure (Figure S11), we note large variation within each day and model class. This is a direct consequence of sample size, as the more data available for training and testing, the more tightly distributed classification scores are likely to yield. Figure 5E further highlight this point, showing the averaged confusion matrices across all 25 cross-validation iterations (and thus, models) for each day for both model classes. There, we see that the test set has roughly 15 samples. Interestingly, the confusion matrices show a larger proportion of true positives (correct identification of kidney failure), and correct rejections of no kidney failure, than either false positives or false negatives. This is encouraging, as it suggests that these models would greatly benefit from more data.

Incidence of cataracts

Cataracts are a well-documented deterministic effect of radiation exposure, as actively dividing epithelial cells that differentiate to form new lens fibers are highly sensitive to radiation injury. Historically, cataracts have rarely been observed in rats exposed to X-ray doses of 8 Gy or more. No evidence of cataracts was observed at 6 months in control rats as slit lamp imaging showed a transparent lens and normal cornea (Figure 6A). However, representative slit lamp images taken in rats exposed to 2–8 Gy neutrons show a dose-dependent increase in lens opacity (Figure 6A). A dose-dependent decrease in intraocular pressure was observed at one year in female rats (Figure 6B). Discussion

In this study, we observed that, relative to low-LET X-ray exposures, neutron irradiation is 1.3–1.6 times more damaging, per unit dose, to radiosensitive organs, including the GI tract, bone marrow, cardiopulmonary, eye, and renal systems. Herein, we have observed that the neutron RBE tends to be higher in acutely responding organs (GI tract and bone marrow) compared to late responding tissues (lung, kidney, eye). Our findings for GI-ARS are generally consistent with prior RBE estimates of 1.7–2 for GI-ARS in canine, minipigs, and monkeys exposed to various neutron sources.27,28,29 Wang et al.27 determined the RBE for GI lethality in canines exposed to a mixed field of 90% neutrons/10% 60Co gamma radiation, with an average neutron energy of 1.33 MeV, to be 1.7. Here, we have observed an RBE for overall GI injury of approximately 1.6 based on the doses required to cause 100% or 30% lethality by day 10 and the dose required to reduce body weight by 15%–20% by day 5. Although we were unable to determine an RBE for the LD50/30 to reflect H-ARS, we have estimated the RBE for H-ARS based on acute lymphocyte suppression to be 1.6. In prior studies, the RBE for H-ARS in both rodent and large animal models ranged between 1 and 2 during a neutron emergency.10

Both neutron energy and tissue depth are important considerations when interpreting the results of this study, as RBE depends on both.30 Here, we have used a clinical fast neutron source at the UW CNTS. With this source, high-energy (>5 MeV) neutrons are responsible for the majority of the dose to the rats inside the field. Approximately 5%–10% of the absorbed dose is from low LET photons and the remaining 90%–95% is generated by high LET neutrons which deposit dose via a mix of low-energy 1H+ ions (<20 MeV on average), 4He2+ ions (<6 MeV), and photons that mostly arise from (n, γ) interactions with hydrogen (<2 MeV). The neutron energy range from this study is higher than what is anticipated at survivable distances from detonation of a fusion device, based on Monte Carlo simulations reported by the DOD Human Survivability Research and Development Integrated Project Team, which estimates neutron energies to be less than 1 MeV. Since >5 MeV neutrons are more likely to pass through tissue without interacting than 1 MeV neutrons and have different secondary ion (low and high LET) distributions, the current study may under- or over-estimate the biological impact of neutron exposures from either a fusion detonation (hydrogen bomb) or fission detonation (improvised nuclear device). In a recent computational model developed by Mentana et al.,30 which mapped the RBE over a range of neutron energies for inducing double-strand DNA breaks in a human phantom, at the approximate midline depth of the rats (∼2 cm), the RBE for 0.5 MeV incident neutrons is 14.7, whereas the RBE for a 10 MeV neutron is 7. This differs from our experimental determination of fast neutron RBE relative to 320 kV X-rays no larger than 2. However, it is notable that for penetration at depths relevant to radiosensitive human organs (11–17 cm), the RBE of incident 1 and 10 MeV neutrons is similar (5.5 and 6.5, respectively, at a depth of 12 cm).30 It is also important to note that the relationship between RBE and dose will depend on the biological outcome. For cell survival and clinical toxicity endpoints, neutron RBE will vary inversely with dose as predicted by the linear quadratic (LQ) model.9,31 Whereas for CNTS neutrons, the induction of double strand DNA breaks is independent of radiation dose and dose rate up to several hundred Gy.32 It is also important to keep in mind that determinations of RBE after close-to-total-body-irradiation (i.e., multi-organ toxicity at specific time points) may be very different than determinations of neutron RBE after the in vitro irradiation of cell cultures or more limited (targeted) in vivo neutron irradiation.

With regard to late normal tissue toxicity, this study is the first to characterize the RBE of large doses (2–10 Gy) of high LET, single-fraction fast neutron exposures in late-responding tissues, including bone marrow, lung, eye, and kidney. From the Life Span Study (LSS) of long-term survivors of the Hiroshima and Nagasaki atomic bombs,33,34,35 it is known that survivors of atomic detonation are at a greater lifetime risk for development of solid cancers and an excess risk of a range of non-malignant diseases.36 However, it is estimated that less than 1% of the LSS cohort was exposed to radiation doses greater than 2 Gy.37 With the advent of advanced medical care, including hematopoietic growth factors, the current LD50 for ARS may be as high as 7–8 Gy. Therefore, the findings presented here are highly relevant to our preparedness for future mass-casualty scenarios involving the dispersal of radioactive material.7

In the LSS cohort, chronic renal failure has been correlated with radiation dose and may be associated with elevated cardiovascular disease in survivors.38 In preclinical studies conducted in the United Kingdom, Joiner reported renal injury following fractionated neutron exposures (p(62)-Be neutrons and d(l6)-Be neutrons) in mice increased progressively in both a neutron dose- and time-dependent manner.39 Relative to an X-ray dose of 2 Gy, the Joiner study shows the RBE for renal injury was between 3.1 and 4.5 (depending on neutron source).39 Here, we observed that progression to renal failure is similar between 8 Gy neutron exposure and 9.5 Gy X-ray exposure, suggesting an RBE of 1.2. We also observed that elevations in UPCR were similar between 6 Gy neutrons and our prior published work following exposure to 8.5 Gy X-rays12 at one year. The lower RBE for renal injury reported here relative to the Jordan study is likely attributable to the aforementioned inverse relationship between RBE and dose per fraction predicted by the LQ model.31,40 However, these data emphasize that exposure to neutron irradiation may be more damaging than X-rays and the relative risk may be difficult to predict based on the total dose of exposure alone. This underscores the need to identify exposed individuals who may be at risk of progressive renal failure.

With this need in mind, we performed targeted proteomic and untargeted metabolomic analysis to identify urinary biomarkers that may predict renal injury. Our proteomic and metabolic analyses demonstrated both dose- and time-dependent regulation in urine composition. In the proteome, early induction (day 14) of proteins associated with injury response and repair was observed following 8 Gy irradiation. Conversely, at the day 180 time point, which marks the beginning of kidney-DEARE, a large number of both proteins and metabolic analytes were differentially expressed. Pathway analysis of the day 180 proteome revealed associations with immune upregulation and kidney dysfunction. Interestingly, relatively few proteins and analytes were differentially expressed across all doses and time points.

We leveraged the time- and dose-dependent changes in dysregulated analytes to build a model for predicting a binary kidney outcome. While we were limited by a small sample size, we did observe a larger proportion of true positives (correct identification of kidney failure) and correct rejections (no kidney failure) than false positives or false negatives. Although future datasets will be required for validation and optimization, these data suggest that urinary metabolic signatures have the potential to accurately predict renal failure. Indeed, urinary metabolome studies have previously been demonstrated to predict radiation-induced metabolic, cardiac, and lung dysfunction.41,42,43 The current study demonstrates the feasibility of non-invasively screening for lethal renal damage.

This study is among the first to comprehensively characterize the full sequelae of multi-organ DEARE anticipated in populations exposed to neutrons in the event of nuclear warfare, including the detonation of improvised nuclear devices (INDs). In our rat model, exposure to neutrons was 1.3–1.6 times more damaging to late responding organs than an equivalent dose of low-LET X-ray irradiation. As RBE decreases with increasing radiation dose and the doses delivered here are 6–10 Gy, it is consistent that our observed RBE range is lower than what is typically reported for clinical fractionated neutron therapy doses of 1–2 Gy.9 Importantly, we have demonstrated the potential utility of our non-invasive biomarker assay for identifying patients at risk of life-threatening renal injury, enabling early initiation of appropriate medical intervention to prevent or slow kidney DEARE.

Limitations of the study

We acknowledge that this study has some limitations. Relatively few rats survived past day 30 post-irradiation. This limits the assessment of RBE for DEARE and the development of prediction models of kidney outcomes based on urinary biomarkers. None of the neutron doses induced cardiac fibrosis; therefore, an RBE for the heart could not be determined.

Experimental model and study participant details

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were performed under an approved Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) protocols at the Medical College of Wisconsin (MCW; IACUC approval AUA00007463) and the University of Washington (UW) School of Medicine (IACUC approval 4528-01). Age- and sex-matched WAG/RijCmcr rats were bred and housed in a barrier facility at our institution. Rats received a moderate antioxidant diet (Teklad 2018 Global 18% protein rodent diet) reflective of antioxidant levels in a typical human diet. Irradiations were performed on adult rats aged 11–12 weeks. The average weight at the time of irradiation was 152 g for females and 248 g for males. Eight to ten animals per treatment group were used. For all radiation exposures described below, rats were monitored daily for morbidity associated with radiation exposure, and established IACUC criteria for euthanasia were followed.

Method details

Neutron exposures

Neutron exposures were performed in the Clinical Neutron Therapy System (CNTS) located at the Department of Radiation Oncology, University of Washington (UW) School of Medicine (Seattle, WA). The physical and dosimetric characteristics of the CNTS are described in Moffitt et al.44 The CNTS generates a mixed beam of predominantly neutrons and some photons via a 50.5 MeV proton beam incident on a 10.5 mm thick Be target.45 Rectangular, square and irregularly shaped fields are created using 40 a multi-leaf collimator (MLC). The CNTS is calibrated to deliver 1 cGy per monitor unit (MU) at a depth of 1.7 cm (depth of maximum dose) for a 10 cm × 10 cm open field. The dose rate is approximately 50 cGy per minute. The U. of Washington team has developed a detailed Monte Carlo model of the CNTS.44,45 For the field size and depth in water used in this study, the mean neutron kinetic energy is in the 10–15 MeV range. Please also note that on the order of 5–8% of the delivered dose is from high-energy (∼1–3 MeV) photons rather than neutrons.45

Neutron exposures were performed without anesthesia. Male or female rats were placed in a custom, 3D printed jig such that the dose to rat midline is at an approximate depth of 1.7 cm (i.e., the depth of maximum dose). One rear leg of the rat was extended laterally outside the treatment field and partially shielded against low-energy neutrons by borated polyethylene and by photons with lead. The high-energy (>5 MeV) neutrons responsible for the majority of the dose to the rats inside the field cannot be effectively scattered or absorbed without an impractically large amount of material (e.g., the almost 20 inches of iron used for leaves in the MLC). Therefore, the dose to the leg outside the field is primarily determined by aligning the field edge to protect the externalized leg. From lateral dose profiles measured at a depth of 1.7 cm in water, the externalized leg is estimated to receive about 10–20% of the intended dose to other parts of the rat (i.e., body parts inside the field). Rats were placed in an acrylic jig so that the dose to midline occurred at an approximate depth of 1.7 cm. The neutron energy varies from the dorsal to the lateral surface, but is 17 MeV on average.46 For reference, using the approximate neutron RBE model of Jones, a 50.5 MeV neutron beam corresponds to effective (hypothetical monoenergetic) neutron energy of about 4.4 MeV.47

Nominal dose to water of 2,4, 6, 8, and 10 Gy were delivered to the rat midline (dose to externalized leg unlikely to exceed 0.4, 0.8, 1.2, 1.6 and 2.0 Gy, respectively). Nominal dose to water at mid-line was highly reproducible among dose levels (+1–2%), although dose uniformity throughout the rat (superior-inferior, lateral and transverse directions) is most likely closer to 5–10% because differences in neutron and photon buildup and interactions with the surrounding media, which are not entirely water equivalent. Also, because fast neutrons are very sensitive to the elemental composition of the interacting medium, it is important to note that dose to adipose tissue is about 2% larger than dose to water whereas dose to muscle is almost 12% lower than dose to water. Dose to bone is close to 40% lower than dose to water.48 While dose to water is useful way to standardize neutron dose reporting, the systematic differences in dose to water, dose to tissue and dose to bone have a significant impact when comparing the biological effects of fast neutrons to other types of radiation, including low LET X-rays. In practical terms, a reported neutron RBE (ratio of doses of low LET and high LET radiation that produce the same biological effect) may be as much as 10%–40% higher when using dose to tissue or bone than when basing the RBE estimate on dose to water.

In-run dosimetry was conducted on one male and one female rat per treatment dose using optically stimulated luminescent (OSL) dosimeters placed at the entrance (dorsal side) and exit (ventral side).49 The midline dose was estimated from OSL readings to be within 3% of the target dose level. Doses for female rats were approximately 1% higher than those for male rats due to differences in rat size and field size. Sham-treated control animals were placed in the same acrylic jig for the same period of time. After exposure to neutrons or sham treatment, all rats were transferred to the MCW facility and housed for up to 400 days for long-term follow-up. Rats were monitored twice daily for manifestations of ARS or DEARE.

X-ray exposures

X-ray exposures (6, 9.5, or 10.5 Gy PBI) were conducted at MCW using a Precision X-RAD 320 kV orthovoltage X-ray system (320 kVp, 13 mA, 1.4 mm Cu half value layer (HVL), filtration with 0.135 mm Cu and 2 mm Al). Exposures were performed without anesthesia in custom plastic jigs between 7 and 11 am, where one hindlimb is externalized from the field and shielded with a 0.25-inch lead block. The relative dose rate was measured at midline in the plastic jig on a wax rat phantom base, based on the absolute dose rate measured using the Task Group (TG) 61 protocol. This measurement was performed twice annually. The dual-chambered rat jig is centered in a 25 × 25 cm field, with distance from source to the midline of rats set at 61.0 cm for females and 60.8 cm for males. The irradiation field at midline was large enough to cover both chambers with adequate margins (at least 2 cm). The dose rate delivered was 1.69 Gy min−1 for female rats and 1.73 Gy min−1 for male rats. The dose to the shielded hindlimb leg is approximately 0.15 Gy/Gy of leg-out PBI. Daily output checks were performed to confirm machine setup. Sham-treated control animals were placed in the plastic jig for the same period of time.

Peripheral blood analysis

Blood samples were collected from isoflurane-anesthetized rats via jugular vein into EDTA-containing blood tubes. Complete blood cell count (CBC) analysis was performed using a Heska HT5 Element veterinary hematology analyzer with 5-part differential. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation. Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN) levels were measured using a Heska Element DC5 DRI-CHEM veterinary chemistry analyzer system.

Breathing rate measurement

Breathing rates were measured using a MouseOx Plus Pulse Oximeter (Starr Life Sciences Corp) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Rats were acclimated to the recording process on the day prior to recording. After calibration for movement, the breathing rate was measured over a 5-min period and averaged to obtain the reported rate for each rat.

Urine collection

Rats were singly housed in metabolic cages for 24 h to collect urine samples. At collection each sample was tested with an Albustix reagent strip (Siemens 2191) for a general protein level.

Slit lamp biomicroscopy

Slit lamp imaging was performed as previously described using a slit lamp biomicroscope (Topcon Medical Systems-SL-D8Z, Oakland, NJ, USA) equipped with a camera (Nikon D810 36.3 MP DSLR, Melville, NY, USA) (Erdman et al. 2024; PMID: 39110587). Briefly, topical eye drops with 2.5% phenylephrine HCL and 1% tropicamide (Akorn, Lake Forest, IL, USA) were used to dilate the pupils. Refresh Plus lubricating eye drops (AbbVie Inc, North Chicago, IL, USA) were applied as needed to maintain corneal hydration and a uniform tear film. Lenticular opacity was assessed by capturing images with diffuse illumination and an optical section. Lens opacities were graded as follows: clear lens with no opacities (Grade 1); mild opacities covering 50% of the eye (Grade 2); opacities covering >50% of the eye (Grade 3); complete cataract covering 100% of the eye (Grade 4).

Tonometry

Intraocular pressure (IOP) was measured using a rebound tonometer (Tonolab Rebound Tonometer, Icare Finland Oy, Helsinki, Finland) specifically designed for rats, as described previously.50 Anesthetized rats were placed on a flat surface, with the rebound tonometer probe positioned 1–4 mm away, perpendicular to the corneal apex. An average of six IOP measurements was captured for each eye. To minimize diurnal variation, IOP measurements were only recorded between 8 a.m. and 11 a.m.

Histological analysis

Tissue processing and staining were performed at the MCW Children’s Research Institute (CRI) Histopathology Core facility. The left lung was inflated and fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde prior to paraffin embedding. Kidneys and hearts were harvested, fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin. Whole mount 5 μm sections were cut at the longitudinal midpoint of each organ and stained with Masson’s trichrome. Collagen deposition was determined as previously described51 by using 5 μm longitudinal sections of the hearts, which were rehydrated and incubated in Sirius Red supplemented with Fast Green. Stained hearts were visualized using a slide scanner (ScanScope CS2) and analyzed with ImageScope 12 software (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) at the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences Experimental Pathology Core. The relative tissue area of collagens was calculated as the red-stained area expressed as a percentage of the total tissue area of each section, using a color deconvolution method.

Data availability

Raw proteomic and metabolomic data have been uploaded to the data depository DRYAD under the public accession number https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.02v6wwqjh.

Untargeted metabolomics

The sample sequence was randomized before sample preparation to avoid bias. Urine samples were prepared by adding 75 μL of an extraction solution containing internal standards made up of 50/50 water/methanol and 10 μL debrisoquine (1 mg/mL in ddH2O), and 50 μL of 4-nitrobenzoic acid (1 mg/mL in Methanol) (per 10 mL) to 25 μL of rodent urine samples. The samples were kept on ice for 20 min then 75 μL of acetonitrile was added. The samples were then centrifuged at 15,493 × g for 20 min at 4°C and the supernatant was transferred to a mass spectrometry (MS) vial for LC-MS analysis (Figure S1).

A volume of 1 μL of each prepared sample was injected onto a Waters Acquity BEH C18 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm column using an Acquity UPLC system coupled to a Xevo G2-S quadrupole-time-of-flight mass spectrometer with an electrospray ionization source (UPLC-ESI-QToF-MS) (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA). The mobile phases consisted of 100% water (solvent A) and 100% acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (solvent B). The solvent flow rate for the metabolomics acquisition was set to 0.5 mL/min with the column maintained at 40°C. The LC gradient was as follows: Initial – 95% A, 5% B; 0.5 min–95% A, 5% B; 4.0 min–80% A, 20% B; 8.0 min–5% A, 95% B; 9.0 min–5% A, 95% B; 9.1 min–95% A, 5% B, 11.0 min–95% A, 5% B.

The column eluent was introduced into the Xevo G2-S mass spectrometer by electrospray operating in either positive or negative ionization mode. Positive mode had a capillary voltage of 3.00 kV and a sampling cone voltage of 30 V. Negative mode had a capillary voltage of 2.00 kV and had a sampling cone voltage of 30 V. The desolvation gas flow was set to 1000 L/h and the desolvation temperature was set to 500°C. The cone gas flow was 25 L/h and the source temperature was set to 120°C. The data were acquired in the sensitivity MS mode with a scan time of 0.300 s and an interscan time of 0.014 s. Accurate mass was maintained by infusing Leucine Enkephalin (556.2771 [M + H]+/554.2615 [M-H]-) in 50% aqueous acetonitrile (2.0 ng/mL) at a rate of 10 μL/min via the Lockspray interface every 10 s. The data were acquired in centroid mode over a 50.0–1200.0 m/z mass range for TOF-MS scanning. An aliquot of each sample was pooled and used as a quality control (QC), representing all metabolites present. This QC sample was run at the beginning of the sequence to condition the column and then injected every 10 samples to check mass accuracy, ensure the presence of internal standard, and monitor shifts in retention time and signal intensities.

Metabolomic data pre-processing and normalization

The untargeted raw data was first converted to the NetCDF unified data format using the Databridge tool in MassLynx (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA). An in-house implementation of the XCMS R package (Scripps Institute, La Jolla, CA) was used for peak detection with ordered bijective interpolated warping algorithm utilized for retention time correction. All data processing and statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.1 or later) using packages from the Tidyverse and Bioconductor ecosystems. For untargeted analyses, the intensity of each analyte was normalized to a specific internal standard (IS) intensity value specific to each ionization mode-followed by standard preprocessing using the xcms R package. To create a unified dataset for analysis, the positive and negative ionization mode data were combined. A global feature filter was applied to the combined matrix, removing any feature with more than 20% missing values across all samples. Remaining missing values were imputed using a k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN) algorithm (k = 10), as implemented in the bnstruct package. Following imputation, the data were normalized to correct for sample-wise variation. Probabilistic Quotient Normalization (PQN) was applied, using the median spectrum of all pooled quality control (QC) samples as the reference. The data were then log-2 transformed to stabilize variance, better align with the normality assumptions of statistical tests, and to transform multiplicative effects into additive ones.

Batch correction and data scaling

To correct for systematic differences between the two experimental cohorts, the custom ComBat algorithm was applied to the log-2 transformed data. The final batch-corrected, log-2 transformed data matrix was used for calculating log-2-fold changes. A second version of this matrix was generated by applying Pareto scaling (mean-centering and dividing each feature by its standard deviation) for use in statistical modeling.

Differential and multivariate statistical analysis

Differential analysis was performed on the processed data for each time point (Days 14, 30, 90, and 180) and for each radiation dose compared to the 0 Gy control group. Pairwise comparisons were conducted using a Welch’s t test on the Pareto-scaled data. p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR). Features with an adjusted p-value ≤0.05 and an absolute log-2 FC ≥ 2.0 were considered statistically significant. Results were visualized using volcano plots. For exploratory analysis, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the final Pareto-scaled data to visualize the overall structure of the dataset and identify the primary sources of variation.

We next sought to build predictive models for a binary kidney outcome (BUN >17 mg/dL) and identify robust biomarkers. To best mimic how these biomarkers would be applied to help identify kidney failure once a model is established, we performed nested cross-validation using the sample, recipes, and caret packages in R. To avoid information leakage that could lead to overly optimistic predictive models, we used the recipes package to learn the parameters needed to preprocess the training data. We then applied these parameters to preprocess the test data. This is in contrast to preprocessing all the data and then training classifiers to predict BUN outcomes. After preprocessing the training data, we reran the differential expression analysis to identify dysregulated analytes across all days and all binary comparisons between irradiation and no-irradiation doses. We then aggregated the number of times each feature was identified as dysregulated and used a threshold of 3 to pre-select a smaller subset of features for training our predictive models. We trained and tested two model classes. A Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression model was trained using the caret and glmnet packages. Additionally, a Random Forest model was trained and tested using the Boruta package for feature selection. Characteristic (ROC) curve, from which the cross-validated AUC and its 95% confidence interval were calculated using the pROC package. We trained and tested these models over 25 outer folds in which the data was split into training and testing sets at 70%/30% respectively. The training data was then used to select features for the models as described above. Those models with their reduced feature sets were then evaluated on the held-out testing data.

Sample preparation for urinary proteomics

Rat urine samples were thawed on ice and mixed well before the centrifugation (10000×g, 10min, 4°C). Samples were desalted and concentrated as described previously with minor modification.52 Briefly, 200 μL of the clarified urine samples were loaded onto pre-rinsed 3 kDa Amicon centrifugal filters and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 45 min at 4°C. Then, centrifugal filters were washed twice with 200 μL of Milli-Q water. Next, 200 μL of Milli-Q water was added to the filter to suspend the protein, and the proteins were extracted by flipping the filters and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 2min. The total protein concentration of the samples was determined by the BCA assay (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo).

Twenty micrograms of protein samples were processed using the iST-BCT kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions from PreOmics. Briefly, 20 μg of protein was added to 50 μL of LYSE-BCT, followed by heating at 95°C for 10 min with shaking at 1000 rpm to reduce disulfide bridges, alkylate cysteines, and denature proteins. Following a 5-min room temperature cooling phase, the mixture was supplemented with Trypsin and LysC, and the proteins were digested for 1 h at 37°C. The “Stop” solution was added to halt digestion. Three rounds of washing and elution into the collection plate using the supplied solutions were performed to achieve peptide purification. The samples were centrifuged for 3 min at 2250g. Peptides were measured using the Quantitative Fluorometric Peptide Assay, transferred to low-bind tubes, and dried in a vacuum centrifuge, as per manufacturer’s recommendations (ThermoFisher, USA). Finally, an estimated 500 ng of peptide per sample was resuspended in water containing 0.1% FA for downstream analysis using mass spectrometry.

diaPASEF LC-MS/MS data acquisition

Peptides from individual samples were separated using a nanoElute 2 (Bruker Daltonik Scientific) coupled online to a timsTOF HT mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonik). Peptides were analytically separated on a PepSep25 column (75 μm × 25 cm, 1.5 μm, C18) and heated to 50°C at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. LC mobile phases A and B were water with 0.1% FA (v/v) and ACN with 0.1% FA (v/v), respectively. The nanoLC was coupled to the timsTOF HT via a modified nanoelectrospray ion source (Captive Spray II; Bruker Daltonik). The peptides were separated using a 40-min gradient from 2% to 95% B at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The gradient was composed of sequential steps ranging from 2 to 35% B in 30 min, and from 35 to 95% B in 30.5 min. The column was cleaned 7 min step at 95% and equilibrated at initial conditions with four column volumes. The mass spectrometer was fully calibrated before the acquisition. The timsTOF HT was operated in diaPASEF mode with the following parameters: mass range, 100 to 1700 m/z; 1/K0 start 0.75 V s/cm2 and end 1.30 V s/cm2; ramp time, 100 ms; lock duty cycle to 100%; capillary voltage, 1600 V; dry gas, 3 L/min; dry temp, 180°C. The collision energy was ramped linearly as a function of mobility from 59 eV at 1/K0 = 1.6 V s/cm2 to 20 eV at 1/K0 = 0.6 V s/cm2. The diaPASEF scan consisted of 60 windows, defined from 350 to 1250 Da, with a 1 Da overlap. The overall acquisition cycle of 1.18 s comprised one full TIMS-MS scan and 20 parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) MS/MS scans.

Proteomics data were analyzed in Realtime ProteoScapeTM (2024, Bruker Scientific LLC, http://www.bruker.com), searched against the rat Swiss-Prot database with the species taxonomy set to Rattus norvegicus (reviewed sequences only; downloaded on May, 2025). TimsTOF acquisition parameters were set in Tims-DiaNN with DIA scan mass tolerance as 20ppm MS1 mass tolerance as 15ppm and Protein Q-value filter as 0.01 with classical MBR activation. Raw data from the DIA were processed against the in-house-built rat urine spectral library. Raw diaPASEF data were processed in ProteoScape using the dia-LFQ TIMS-DIA-NN workflow. Spectral identification and quantification were performed with DIA-NN, operating in a project-specific spectral library. The search was configured with an MS1 mass tolerance of 15 ppm and a protein-level false discovery rate (FDR) threshold (Q-value) of 0.001. Protein quantification was performed using the classical match-between-runs (MBR) algorithm, maximizing peptide and protein identification across samples. Differential protein expression analysis was performed using R and the limma package. Protein intensities were log2-transformed, and missing values were imputed within each experimental group using the median intensity. Linear models were fitted for each protein across sample groups, and empirical Bayes moderation was applied to improve variance estimates. Adjusted p-values were calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Proteins with an FDR <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Models for prediction of renal failure: LASSO and random forest

To rigorously test t analytes predictive of kidney failure (BUN ≥120 mg/dL) we employed a nested, cross-validation scheme (Figure S2). This approach randomly splits the data into training and testing sets containing 70% and 30% of the data, respectively, stratified such that equal proportions of kidney outcome were present within all splits. To avoid the phenomena known as information leakage, which results in overly optimistic classification models (models displaying a higher accuracy score than is expected without information leakage), we pre-selected a set of candidate analytes to feed into our models by re-running the differential expression analysis, including all pre-processing steps, in the training set only. Next, we determined the number of times an analyte was identified as significantly dysregulated and selected those that were dysregulated at least 3 times across all binary comparisons. The selected analytes were then fed into two separate classification pipelines that further reduced the set of candidate features. The training data was further split into training and testing sets to optimize the models. The two model classes we used were the LASSO-logistic regression class and the Random Forest class. LASSO uses and l-1 penalty that naturally creates sparse solutions and further reduces the set of candidate analytes. Random Forest models produce feature importance metrics, which were used to further reduce the set of candidate analytes. Importantly, this reduction was done independently across both models. The trained and sparse models are then tested on the held out 30% of data that was randomly split for testing at the start. We report the area under the curve (AUC) classification metric, which accounts for class imbalances, as opposed to pure classification accuracy.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons across multiple groups and time points were evaluated using ANOVA with appropriate post hoc multiple comparison tests. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical details including the statistical tests used and number of animals can be found in the figure legends.
