Does the Lens Continue to Counteract Axial Elongation in Adults with High Myopia?
Authors: Shiran Zhang, Chi Liu, Yonglin Chen, Ruilin Xiong, Zhixi Li, Ling Jin, Jian Zhang, Ou Xiao, Qiuxia Yin, Yingfeng Zheng, Yangfa Zeng, Mingguang He, Xiaotong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the longitudinal lens thickness (LT) and lens power (LP) change patterns and associated factors in high myopes.DesignProspective cohort study.ParticipantsChinese individuals with high myopia (spherical power ≤ –6.00 diopters [D]) with a baseline age from 7 to 50 years.MethodsPatients with high myopia from the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center-Brien Holden Vision Institute High Myopia cohort have been followed up every other year for 8 years. Participants with ≥2 visits were included. At each visit, ocular biometrics, including LT, were measured with Lenstar LS900 (Haag-Streit AG) or IOL Master 500 (Carl Zeiss Meditec). Lens power was calculated with the Bennett formula. Longitudinal changes in LT and LP in individuals at different ages and axial length elongation rates (slow/rapid) were quantified.Main Outcome MeasuresChanges in LT and LP.ResultsA total of 708 participants (median age [interquartile range]: 16.84 years [13.52–23.11]; 53.1% female) were included. The lens consistently thickened with age (β = 0.027 mm/year; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.026–0.028; P < 0.001). The lens lost power from 7 to 18 years, after which LP stabilized for slow progressors (β = 0.003 D/year; 95% CI, –0.026 to 0.033) but continued to lose power in rapid progressors (β = –0.042 D/year; 95% CI, –0.062 to –0.021; P < 0.001). In adults, LP loss was significantly associated with axial elongation (β = –0.469 D; 95% CI, –0.557 to –0.381; P < 0.001), but not with age. Mediation analysis indicated that axial elongation, but not age, had a direct effect on LP loss.ConclusionsIn high myopes, LT consistently increased with age. The lens continued to lose power to counteract axial elongation in adults with high myopia, with rapid axial elongation.Financial Disclosure(s)The author has no/the authors have no proprietary or commercial interest in any materials discussed in this article.Keywords: Axial elongation, Cohort, High myopia, Lens

The crystalline lens is a key factor in determining refractive status, alongside corneal power (CP) and axial length (AL).1 Unlike CP, which stabilizes after 2 or 3 years of age, and AL, which grows steadily during childhood and early adolescence before reaching stability,2, 3, 4, 5 the lens experiences continuous changes in both its morphology and optical power throughout life.6,7

An important characteristic of the lens is its ability to thin and lose power as a compensatory response to axial elongation during emmetropization in childhood. Previous studies, mostly based on children and adolescents without myopia or with mild-to-moderate myopia, have identified a declining compensatory ability of the lens with age.1,6,8, 9, 10, 11 In contrast to the stabilization of axial elongation during adulthood in mild-to-moderate myopia, individuals with high myopia were reported to experience continued axial elongation after the age of 18 years,3,4 raising the question of whether the lens continues to play an offsetting role in this population. To our knowledge, only 1 Danish cohort involving 34 high myopes reported increased lens thickness (LT) and decreased lens power (LP) over a 28-year follow-up period, but it did not provide detailed patterns of lens change over time.12

It is important to recognize that lens changes are influenced by both aging and axial elongation, necessitating research to distinguish between the effects of these 2 factors. Using data from an 8-year follow-up of high myopes in the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center-Brien Holden Vision Institute High Myopia cohort,13 this study aimed to investigate the change patterns of LT and LP in individuals with high myopia across different ages and with different axial elongation rates and to identify the major driving force behind these changes.

Methods

Study Population

The Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center-Brien Holden Vision Institute High Myopia Cohort Study is a prospective cohort study initiated between November 2011 and October 2012 in Guangzhou, China, with follow-up visits scheduled every other year. The detailed methodology is documented in a prior publication.13 At baseline, 890 individuals with binocular high myopia (spherical power ≤ –6.00 diopters [D]), aged 7 to 70 years, without secondary myopia, a history of ocular surgery, or serious systemic diseases were included. For the present analysis, participants aged younger than 50 years at baseline with available LT and LP data at baseline and at least 1 subsequent follow-up visit during the 8-year follow-up were included. Participants with cataract greater than or equal to Lens Opacities Classification System III grade nuclear opalescence 2/nuclear color 2, cortical cataract 2, or posterior subcapsular cataract 2, or who had ocular surgery during the follow-up period, were further excluded.

Written informed consent was procured from adult participants and from parents or guardians of participants under the age of 18 years. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and obtained approval from the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center Ethics Committee (2012KYNL002).

Ophthalmic Examinations and Measurements

At baseline and each study visit, certified ophthalmologists and optometrists conducted standardized ophthalmic evaluations. Before cycloplegia, ocular biometric parameters, including central corneal thickness, anterior chamber depth, LT, average CP (in D, computed as the mean of K1 and K2), and AL, were obtained using partial coherence interferometry with the Lenstar LS900 (Haag-Streit AG). For AL exceeding the Lenstar’s range (up to 32 mm), the IOL Master 500 (Carl Zeiss Meditec) was used. High agreement between the 2 devices has been consistently proved in previous studies.14,15 Cycloplegic refraction was determined using an autorefractor (Topcon KR8800; Topcon Corp) after complete pupil dilation (≥6 mm pupil diameter and absent light reflex) induced by 2 drops of 0.5% tropicamide administered 5 minutes apart. The spherical equivalent refraction was calculated by sphere power plus half of the cylinder power. Lens power was calculated according to the Bennett formula (Appendix S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), which has been validated in highly myopic eyes.16,17

Statistical Analysis

Because of the high correlation between data from the right and left eyes, only data from the right eyes were included in the analyses. Baseline demographics and ocular biometrics were summarized as means (standard deviations [SDs]) for normally distributed quantitative variables, medians (interquartile ranges) for skewed data, and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Group comparisons were made using unpaired Student t tests, analysis of variance, or χ2 tests.

To quantify the average rates of longitudinal LT and LP changes and their associated factors, linear mixed-effects models were used. These models incorporated time, baseline age, sex, baseline AL or AL at each visit, and baseline CP or CP at each visit as fixed effects and time as a random effect. Time represented each participant’s follow-up years since baseline. Therefore, the coefficient of time estimated the longitudinal annual change rates in LT/LP. Longitudinal change patterns of LT and CP were illustrated using the locally weighted scatterplot smoothing method, with the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) delineated. Longitudinal change patterns of LP and spherical equivalent refraction were illustrated with cubic splines with multivariable adjustment. To further understand the respective roles of aging and axial elongation in the longitudinal LT and LP changes, we divided the cohort into different groups based on baseline age (7–11, >11 to <18, and 18 to <50 years) and longitudinal AL change rate (slow and rapid groups). When comparing changing rates between slow/rapid axial elongation groups, we incorporated time × group interactions to assess differential rates between axial elongation groups. The mean longitudinal AL change rate was calculated as the difference in AL between the final and initial visits divided by the follow-up duration and further divided into 3 tertiles (T1, T2, and T3). Patients with an AL change rate in T1 and T2/3 were classified as slow and rapid axial elongation groups, respectively. Statistical significance was established at a 2-tailed P value of <0.05. Mediation analyses were performed to examine the direct and indirect effects of age and axial elongation on LT/LP. All statistical computations were performed using Stata 17 (StataCorp LP), SAS, and R version 4.0.4 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Participants and Baseline Characteristics

After excluding 164 participants with baseline age ≥50 years, 120 with cataract, and 16 with ocular surgery during the follow-up, 708 individuals (median age [interquartile range]: 16.84 [13.52–23.11] years; 53.1% female) were included in the final analysis (Table S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). The mean follow-up period was 4.57 years (SD, 3.46), and 431 (61.3%) participants had attended at least 3 visits. At baseline, the average LT was 3.54 mm (SD, 0.25), and the average LP was 20.65 D (SD, 1.64) (Fig S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org; Table 1), with thicker LT and lower LP observed in participants with longer AL (both P < 0.001, Table 1).

Longitudinal Changes in LT and LP

During the follow-up, the lens exhibited consistent thickening across all age and axial elongation subgroups (Fig 1; Fig S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). The mean LT change rate was 0.028 (95% confidence interval, 0.027–0.029) mm/year for all participants, 0.020 mm/year (95% CI, 0.016–0.023) in the 7 to 11 years group, 0.027 mm/year (95% CI, 0.026–0.028) in the >11 to <18 years group, and 0.032 mm/year (95% CI, 0.030–0.034) in the 18 to <50 years group. Across all age groups, no significant difference was detected in the mean annual LT change rates between the slow and rapid axial elongation groups (Fig 2).

For all participants, LP declined from ages 7 to 18 years and subsequently stabilized after 18 years (Fig 1; Table S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). The mean LP change rates were –0.232 D/year (95% CI, –0.270 to –0.194) for all participants, –1.004 D/year (95% CI, –1.283 to –0.725) in the 7 to 11 years group, –0.712 D/year (95% CI, –0.822 to –0.602) in the >11 to <18 years group, and –0.064 D/year (95% CI, –0.146 to 0.018) in the 18 to <50 years group.

In high myopes with slow axial elongation rates, LP continued to decrease from 7 to 18 years with a slower trend after 11 years (7–11, –0.131 D/year, –0.206 to –0.055; and >11 to <18, –0.060 D/year, –0.088 to –0.031) and stabilized thereafter (18 to <50, 0.003 D/year, –0.026 to 0.033) (Fig 2; Figs S3 and S4, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). In high myopes with rapid axial elongation rates, LP decreased continuously from 7 to 50 years, with the fastest decreasing rate in childhood and getting slower with age (7–11 years, –0.257 D/year, –0.320 to –0.193; >11 to <18 years, –0.095 D/year, –0.117 to –0.074; and 18 to <50 years, –0.042 D/year, –0.062 to –0.021). Compared with those in the slow axial elongation group, individuals in the rapid axial elongation group experienced a faster LP loss rate of –0.126 D/year (–0.225 to –0.027; P = 0.013) in the 7 to 11 years group, –0.036 D/year (–0.071 to –0.001; P = 0.049) in the >11 to <18 years group, and –0.045 D/year (–0.081 to –0.009; P = 0.015) in the 18 to <50 years group (Fig 2).

Baseline Factors Associated with Longitudinal Changes in LT and LP

Each 1-year older baseline age was associated with an increase in LT by 0.028 mm (0.017–0.038; P < 0.001) in the >11 to <18 years group and 0.023 mm (0.020–0.027; P < 0.001) in the 18 to <50 years group. Older baseline age was associated with a higher LP only in the >11 to <18 years group (β = 0.152; 95% CI, 0.152–0.231; P < 0.001; Table S2). Females exhibited a higher LP compared with males in the 7 to 11 years group (β = 0.773; 95% CI, 0.120–1.346; P = 0.019), the >11 to <18 years group (β = 0.581; 95% CI, 0.292–0.860; P < 0.001), and the 18 to <50 years group (β = 0.856; 95% CI, 0.515–1.197; P < 0.001). Each 1-mm longer baseline AL was associated with a lower LP by 0.597 D (95% CI, –0.907 to –0.251; P = 0.001), 0.738 D (95% CI, –0.893 to –0.583; P < 0.001), and 0.337 D (95% CI, –0.442 to –0.232; P < 0.001) in the 7 to 11, >11 to <18, and 18 to <50 years group, respectively.

Association of Changes in Associated Factors and Changes in LT and LP

Incorporating temporal changes in ocular biometrics, mixed-effect models revealed that per 1-year increase in age was significantly associated with a thicker LT of 0.027 mm (95% CI, 0.026–0.028; P < 0.001) for all participants, 0.023 mm (95% CI, 0.017–0.028; P < 0.001), 0.027 mm (95% CI, 0.025–0.028; P < 0.001), and 0.032 mm (95% CI, 0.030–0.034; P < 0.001) in the group aged 7 to 11, >11 to <18, and 18 to <50 years, respectively (Table 2). Sex and axial elongation were not associated with changes in LT over time. The mediation analysis showed that age had a significant direct positive effect on LT (β = 0.031; P < 0.001) and an indirect effect on LT through AL (β = –0.003; P < 0.001). The total effect of age on LT remained positive (β = 0.028; P < 0.001).

Discussion

Based on a large high myopia cohort, we found that the lens continued to thicken with aging from 7 to 50 years, whereas the lens consistently lost power from 7 to 18 years, after which the LP stabilized for those with a slow axial elongation rate, but continued to decrease for those with a fast axial elongation rate.

The lens is known to undergo complex changes in thickness and power in early life. In a 2-year study of 5174 children with emmetropia and mild myopia, we identified a 3-stage pattern in LT changes, characterized by a rapid decrease from 3 to 7 years of age and a slight decrease from 7 to 11 years, followed by an increase from 12 to 15 years,6 similar to findings from the Correction of Myopia Evaluation Trial.18 In a cross-sectional study of 459 high myopes (4–19 years of age), Cheng et al16 reported a U-shaped LT distribution with a nadir at age 9 years. To our knowledge, no previous longitudinal study exists reporting the change pattern of LT in high myopes. Herein, LT was consistently increasing with age from 7 to 50 years, and such a U-shaped pattern was not observed. The discrepancy may be attributed to the smaller cohort size in the 7-to 11-year age group. However, it is plausible that the high compensatory demands of high myopia hasten the lens thinning process, precipitating an earlier LT trough in children with high myopia compared with those with mild or no myopia. Further research, including younger children with high myopia, could yield additional understanding.

Current analyses found that age was the primary factor contributing to LT increment. Similarly, in a study on children and adolescents with unilateral high myopia, Zhang et al19 found no difference in the central LT between highly myopic eyes and the contralateral eyes. This also aligns with Cheng et al’s16 study of children and adolescents with high myopia, which demonstrated no correlation between AL and LT.

The continued LP loss from 7 to 18 years in highly myopes in our study was consistent with previous findings in emmetropes and mild-to-moderate myopes.1,6,8 Further, we identified a slower decreasing rate of LP after 11 years of age in highly myopes, consistent with findings in children with no or mild myopia by our group and data from the Orinda Longitudinal Study of Myopia and the Singapore Cohort Study of the Risk Factors for Myopia.6,9, 10, 11,20 These findings collectively indicated that a physiologic inflection point of LP loss might exist regardless of myopia severity.

From 18 to 50 years, however, the LP change patterns were found to vary by axial elongation rates in our study. Specifically, LP stabilized in high myopes with slow axial elongation, but kept decreasing in those with rapid axial elongation. In adults with emmetropia and stable AL, age-related physiologic LP loss has been reported throughout adulthood, with a steeper decline after ∼55 years of age, contributing to a hyperopic shift in later adulthood.21, 22, 23 Therefore, the greater LP decline observed in rapid progressors may reflect an accelerated process associated with continued axial elongation. Our mediation analysis indicated that axial elongation, but not age, had a direct effect on LP. Therefore, we postulate that the LP loss in rapid progressors is more likely to be a compensatory adaptation in the lens to continued axial elongation.

In high myopes with rapid axial elongation, the lens continued to lose power while thickening with age, indicating that the observed LP loss may involve alterations in the internal gradient index profile, potentially in combination with subtle biomechanical effects associated with continued axial elongation.1 A 28-year longitudinal study spanning high myopes aged 26 to 54 years reported continued axial elongation accompanied by increased LT and decreased LP,12 which supports our findings. This phenomenon has been described as the lens paradox, in which the crystalline lens becomes thicker with age while its overall refractive power decreases, possibly because of age-related reductions in the gradient refractive index within the lens. Importantly, lens thickening does not necessarily exclude concurrent stretching-related effects because age-related lens growth may outpace subtle biomechanical deformation.24 The divergence in LP trajectories observed mainly between 20 and 40 years of age, before the onset of absolute presbyopia, further raises the possibility that residual lens elasticity may still permit elongation-associated biomechanical influences on lens fiber architecture and refractive index distribution during this period. In patients with unilateral high myopia (6–18 years), Zhang et al19 found LP decreased but morphologically changed to a more curved shape without significant LT thinning in the highly myopic eye as compared with the non–highly myopic contralateral eye, suggesting that LP loss was mainly attributed to a decline in the internal power. Future longitudinal studies incorporating direct measurements of lens gradient refractive index, lens curvature, and biomechanical properties are needed to validate these hypotheses and clarify the mechanisms linking adult axial elongation to LP loss.

In addition, similar to the findings in individuals with emmetropia or mild-to-moderate myopia,16,25, 26, 27, 28 LP was higher in females than in males with high myopia. This might be explained by the shorter AL in females because shorter AL was associated with higher LP.3,21

This study is distinguished by its long-term follow-up period with multiple study visits and the broad age range of patients with high myopia, complemented by the standardized assessment protocols. Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. First, the sample size in the 7 to 11 years age group was comparatively small, limiting our ability to precisely characterize the lens changes during this age period. Second, LP was not measured directly but was inferred using the Bennett formula and other ocular biometrics because of the absence of in vivo measurement techniques. The Bennett formula, although extensively validated and commonly used, may introduce errors in ∼5% of cases.17,29 Third, the lens morphology (e.g., anterior and posterior lens curvature) was not quantified in this study, precluding the examination of its longitudinal morphologic changes. Additionally, the development and progression of cataract in middle and late adulthood would have a significant effect on LT and LP. We excluded patients aged ≥50 years and those with cataract at baseline and follow-up visits to minimize the influence of cataract.

Conclusions

Based on the long-term follow-up of Chinese individuals with high myopia aged 7 to 50 years, the lens continued to thicken over time, whereas changes in LP were mainly driven by the rate of axial elongation. In eyes with rapid axial elongation, continued LP loss was observed from 7 to 50 years, suggesting a potential role of the lens in counteracting excessive axial elongation into adulthood.
