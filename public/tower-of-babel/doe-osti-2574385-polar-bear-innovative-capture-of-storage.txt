 POLAR BEAR™ – INNOVATIVE CAPTURE OF STORAGE TANK VAPORS
 FINAL REPORT

ABSTRACT

 Polar Bear™ is a patented technology developed by the Energy & Environmental Research
Center (EERC) to capture storage tank vapors and eliminate methane emissions from upstream
oil- and gas-producing facilities. Sparked by early commercial investment, the EERC licensed the
technology and extended the intellectual property to storage tanks. Polar Bear™ is uniquely
engineered and adapted to individual lower-producing facilities where there is otherwise no
economic alternative for capturing tank vapors. A high number of small producing oil and gas
wells are distributed across the country. The aggregate contributes to a significant volume of
emissions. Because of the lack of economy of scale, gas volumes from these facilities are typically
not recovered and contribute to methane emissions. Polar Bear™ provides a fit-for-purpose
compression solution that addresses cost by reducing complexity with respect to conventional
vapor recovery units and eliminating oil changes. Unique to Polar Bear™ is the capability to
separate oxygenated gas from storage tank vapors. Storage tanks are designed to “breathe,”
allowing gas to enter and escape during internal level and temperature changes. This infiltration
of air into the tank headspace imparts undesirable oxygen content with respect to pipeline
gathering. Polar Bear™ separates the vapor stream, allowing oxygen-rich gas to be used as fuel
on-site while recovering the liquids-rich portion of the gas where oxygen content is minimized. A
prototype system was tested to verify process models, evaluate operational performance, and
advance the technology readiness level from 5 to 6. Results provide a good match between
experimental measurements and process models, indicating the models are useful for future scale-
up and field design. Various mixtures of nitrogen and liquefied petroleum gas were tested to
understand the mass balance of nitrogen and how it relates to the potential control of oxygen
content. Findings indicate that less than 2000 ppm of oxygen is likely to remain in the liquid
portion of the gas in field applications. The research and development prepare the technology for
field implementation to eliminate routine and fugitive methane emissions from storage tanks.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 The Energy & Environmental Research Center (EERC) and partners are advancing a
technology to address emissions from storage tanks used in upstream oil and gas operations. These
storage tank batteries are typically used at the production well site to provide a buffer before
custody transfer. Storage tanks include safety systems to prevent rupture or excessive vacuum.
Emissions from these systems occur if the tank headspace pressure is not managed by a vapor
recovery system. For many wells, conventional vapor recovery systems are not affordable based
on the value of recovered gas. Polar Bear™ is a technology patented by the EERC to overcome
the economic challenges associated with storage tank emissions.

 A project was awarded by the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Fossil Energy and
Carbon Management program under the Innovative Methane Measurement, Monitoring, and
Mitigation Technologies funding opportunity to develop and validate novel technologies to reduce
emissions from storage tanks. The interest is in advancing technology from laboratory scale, a
technology readiness level (TRL) 5, to engineering or pilot scale (TRL 6).

 The EERC’s patented design was used to develop and build a vapor recovery skid that
validated operation and progressed the technology for further commercial development aimed at
field testing. The EERC is presently engaged with DOE in field-testing under the funding
opportunity “Innovative Technologies to Eliminate Flaring from Oil and Natural Gas Production.”

 Learnings that have progressed the technology include the following:

 • Collection of field data to confirm gas compositions, flow, and surge rates from a storage
 tank system in the Bakken Formation of the Williston Basin

 • Dynamic testing to confirm control of the system and the capability to capture storage
 tank gas

 • The ability to separate oxygen between gas vapor and liquid fractions and confirm the
 range of oxygen present in the liquid fraction of the gas

 • Understanding the mechanical limitations and performance of state-of-the-art oilless
 diaphragm compression technology

 The outcomes of this project have bolstered the EERC and fostered partnerships to move
forward in the commercialization process. Polar Bear™ is a new tool for the oil and gas industry
to capture tank vapors where there is no conventional economic option.

INTRODUCTION

 The Energy & Environmental Research Center (EERC) at the University of North Dakota is
recognized as one of the world’s leading developers of cleaner, more efficient energy technologies.
The EERC contracts with the private sector, state and federal agencies, and international clients
on research and technology projects. Over 250 EERC employees work in fossil energy, carbon
capture and storage, renewable energy, and earth sciences. The current project is supported by
members of the EERC’s Bakken Production Optimization Program. The members include oil and
gas operators within the Williston Basin, service companies, and the North Dakota Industrial
Commission through the North Dakota Oil and Gas Research Program. Present industry members
include Chord Energy, ConocoPhillips, Devon, ExxonMobil, Hess, Liberty Resources, Petro-
Hunt, tNavigator, Steffes LLC, and Advanced Flow Solutions Inc.

 Polar Bear™ is the EERC’s approach to reducing methane emissions at upstream oil and gas
facilities. Named for the adaptive, robust, and environmentally sensitive characteristics of the polar
bear, the technology is a fit-for-purpose technique to capture emissions from storage tanks on oil
and gas well sites. Tank vapors present unique challenges in that the volumes are typically low,
and the gas is often unsuitable for pipeline capture without processing through an expensive
oxygen removal process. The problem necessitates a nimble compressor and a simple approach to
manage oxygenated vapor. Polar Bear™ solves these two problems by 1) addressing the capital
and operating costs for compression and 2) innovating how the compression is integrated into the
well site facility. Figure 1 highlights the patented 1 Polar Bear™ tank vapor application. Tank
vapors are captured from the storage tanks in the same manner as a conventional vapor recovery
unit with several key differences. Polar Bear™ uses a newly developed compressor that does not
require complex internal recycle loops and crankcase oil lubrication, greatly reducing costs of
conventional vapor recovery units. Oxygen in tank vapors is separated by taking advantage of
compression and aftercooling, and lastly, the system is adapted to the surface facility to use fuel
on-site and to recover valuable natural gas liquids (NGLs). These critical features obtain value
from low-volume gas production of storage tanks and minimize the costs of recovery.

METHODOLOGY

 The statement of project objectives is divided into four tasks. Task 1 covers the DOE-
required project management reporting. Task 2 is the prototype construction, site preparation,
testing, and validation activity. Field data to inform the design comprises Task 3, and Task 4
includes the work to fulfill the societal considerations and impact obligations for DOE.

 The technical methodology is designed to achieve two project objectives:

 1) Develop and validate a novel technology to capture vapors from storage tanks to achieve
 zero or near-zero methane emissions.

 2) Complete engineering-scale testing of a prototype design functioning with anticipated gas
 components and at dynamic conditions to validate process controls, design parameters,
 and safety.

 The methods to support the first objective include preparing a site at the EERC to test the
Polar Bear™ technology, procurement and commissioning of a prototype unit, followed by testing
and data collection. The second objective involves the collection of field data to inform the test
matrix provided in Table 1.

 Field Data Collection Methodology

 Storage tank vapor recovery systems must be able to control the pressure of the tank
headspace to achieve high capture efficiency. Typically, storage tanks in upstream oil and gas
operations use a pressure/vacuum relief valve either mounted separately or as part of the tank thief
hatch to prevent damage. An example is provided in Figure 2. The valve allows pressure to escape
due to vapor evolution from the liquid or an increase in liquid level. Likewise, the valve dissipates
a vacuum associated with a decrease in temperature or liquid level. The vapor discharge is
normally routed to a low-pressure flare while the vacuum relief allows air to infiltrate the gas
headspace. Common vapor recovery applications use a speed-controlled compressor in
communication with the tank headspace to evacuate gas based on pressure. The parameters for
tank headspace pressure fall within the range of relief valve settings between 4 ounces of pressure
per square inch (osi) to 16 osi. Therefore, sensitive pressure control must be maintained around
2 osi. Legacy storage tank and gas flaring systems are not customarily fitted with flowmeters.
Therefore, assessing flow conditions can be difficult, but it is paramount to proper vapor recovery
sizing. Successful designs match the evolution of tank vapors from the system and accommodate
maximum flow rates, which occur during rapid filling of the tank and during abrupt increases of
ambient temperature. Additionally, tank vapors must not exceed downstream gas handling and
processing requirements with respect to oxygen content. Understanding how air infiltration
imparts oxygen into the gas stream of the tank vapor headspace is critical in determining efficacy
of treatment, removal, or other options.

RESULTS

 Field Data Collection Results

 Hourly gas sampling was conducted on the low-pressure flare line at the Jensen W 159-93-
21-9-1MBHX well, owned by Liberty Resources. Vapors exit the storage tank battery and flow
through a 3-in. pipe to a low-pressure flare. Sampling occurred on the downstream side of the
liquid knockout tank, which is within about 100 ft of the low-pressure flare. The average measured
gas composition was 32.3% methane, 25.7% ethane, 17.5% propane, 10.2% butane, 4.6% pentane,
and 4.4% hexanes+. Nonhydrocarbon gases included 3.7% air, 0.6% nitrogen, and 1.1% carbon
dioxide. Oxygen concentration was less than 1%. Measured gas flow averaged between
4.9 and 1.7 Mcfd with a maximum of 6.5 Mcfd and a minimum of 0.7 Mcfd. Pressure in the
flowline ranged from 0.5 to 2.5 osi. Flow data collected in less than 1-minute intervals indicate
that flow can oscillate by 2 Mcfd in about 1 minute and that nearly 10 Mcfd is needed to capture
the full tank vapor flow. The data are presented in Figure 7.

 The field data is used to inform the process design and the test parameters. The field data
provide the following flow parameter metrics for validation and testing:

 • Low: 1 Mcfd
 • Medium: 3 Mcfd
 • High: 5 Mcfd
 • Nitrogen mix ratio range: 1%–5%

 A decrease in tank vapor production was observed during the test period. The cause was not
determined. Notably, an increase in air concentration in the gas was observed during the flow
change followed by gas concentration returning to a nominal condition. Initial modeling results of
the Jensen W well predicted tank vapor flow rates of less than 1 Mcfd and methane concentration
less than 10%. Given that the model predicted lower flow and methane concentration than what
was measured in the field, the model was further investigated to determine what facility
temperature and pressure conditions might account for the discrepancy. A depiction of this model
is provided in Figure 8. The artificial lift installed at the Jensen W well includes a jet pump, which
circulates oil to lift reservoir fluids to surface. Jet pumps versus conventional rod pumps bring
fluids to surface quicker, raising fluid surface temperature. The modeled results did not account
for the higher temperatures produced by the jet pump. The jet pump operation produces fluid
temperatures between 90° and 120°F. Further modeling determined how higher fluid wellhead
temperature affected the flow rate and gas composition of the gas exiting the tank battery.
Conditions include 1) the original prediction, 2) increased separator temperature, and
3) increased separator and treater temperature. Results are plotted for a varied tank fluid
temperature (60°–120°F) for each condition in Figure 9.

 • Condition 1 – Separator 70°F, 80 psig; treater 70°F, 50 psig
 • Condition 2 – Separator 100°F, 80 psig; treater 70°F, 50 psig
 • Condition 3 – Separator 100°F, 80 psig; treater 100°F 50 psig

 Test 1 – Results, Pressure Testing

 A diaphragm compressor was selected for the prototype test. Diaphragm pumps are
commonly used for the transfer of liquids in the oil and gas industry and designed to handle an
array of corrosive and flammable fluids. Recently, this design was adapted to gas compression,
and a few specific options for diaphragm compression of petroleum gas are available. Unique to
the supplier is a high-torque, low-speed drive that enables essentially 100% turndown of the drive
motor. Many electric motor variable-frequency-drive components on the market are limited to
15% turndown, and many gas compressors are limited to 50% turndown as a minimum rotating
speed is required for proper oil lubrication. The selected diaphragm compressor overcomes both
electrical and mechanical turndown limitations. The compressor uses a compressed gas supply to
support the nonworking side of the diaphragm, which prevents damage and improves longevity
with respect to the reciprocating pumping action of the device. The manufacturer-specified
consumption of support gas is 0.2 scfh. During commissioning of the compressor, gas leakage
from this nonworking side was discovered from a soap bubble test. The manufacturer provided
repair guidance and replacement diaphragms. Inspection revealed damage to the original
diaphragms and repair eliminated the gas leak. A flowmeter measured the support gas use at
0.1 scfm.

 All other components of the system were successfully commissioned and passed pressure
testing. No anomalies that would adversely affect commercialization were noted.

 Test 3 – Dynamic Operation

 Field-testing revealed that gas production surge from the tank battery can fluctuate 2 Mcfd
over about 1 minute. Therefore, the objective of Test 3 is to determine if the automated PLC control
of the compressor unit can respond fast enough to capture potential gas surges and control the tank
pressure versus allowing gas to escape to flare. A PID control function within the PLC controls
the compressor motor speed in response to storage tank pressure. Proper tuning and optimization
of the PID control is critical to tune the active control. Many PLCs have an autotuning function.
The autotuning capability of the PLC was inadequate, and a significant effort was devoted to
manual tuning and optimization. Each function (proportional, integral, and derivative) must be
tested to optimize the PID control set points. The proportional function determines the magnitude
of response to a system change. Proportional tuning involves instigating a system change and
identifying at what point the proportional set point produces system instability. This point is
identified and divided by two. The integral function of the PID control adjusts how fast the system
responds to a change and can influence the accuracy of the control. The derivative function helps
to adjust set-point overshoot as a response to a system change. Slow-reacting systems typically do
not benefit from a derivative function, as was the case in our testing. Manual tuning provided PID
settings of the controller as follows: P = 1, I = 30, D = 0.

 Gas surge tests using nitrogen were completed to validate the response limits of the PID
control. Flow rate was altered from 3 to 5 scfm over periods of 1 minute, 30 seconds,
15 seconds, and 5 seconds. The corresponding data are provided in Figure 13. A gas surge is
manually provided to the system by opening or closing the nitrogen supply valve over the
respective period. The system responds by speeding up or slowing down the compressor
(frequency). The inlet tank pressure (simulated storage tank) deviates until returned to the set point
pressure at 8 osi. The system should be able to control tank pressure within 2 osi of the set point
in response to a gas surge over 1 minute and at a magnitude of 1.4 scfm. Testing at a 2-scfm change
in flow revealed that the control of tank pressure was maintained with 2.5 osi of pressure with gas
surging as fast as 5 seconds. Control of tank pressure within 2 osi was achieved within a gas surge
of 1 minute.

 Test 5 – Operation Using a Mix of Nitrogen and LPG

 As illustrated in Figure 2, air infiltration to the tank headspace imparts oxygen into the tank
vapor gas stream. Oxygen is undesirable for downstream gas processing and is typically limited
by commercial specification measured in parts per million. The Polar Bear™ technology can
uniquely separate tank vapors by compressing, cooling, and liquifying a portion of the gas. The
liquid portion contains less oxygen than the vapor portion. The vapor portion is then used as fuel
on-site where the liquid portion of the tank vapor can be returned and recovered. The objective of
Test 5 is to generate experimental data that help to understand how much oxygen might remain in
the liquid portion of the tank vapor stream. Since nitrogen and oxygen have similar ideal gas
properties and nitrogen is inert, nitrogen is experimentally used as a proxy for oxygen. The purpose
of mixing LPG and nitrogen is to simulate air-infiltrated tank vapors and to experimentally
measure the amount of nitrogen that remains dissolved in the liquid phase within the Polar Bear™
process. The experimental results assist in EOS model validation and prediction of both nitrogen
and oxygen concentrations.

 Steady-state tests were conducted mixing nitrogen and LPG. The operations included four
tests: 0% nitrogen, 10% nitrogen, 20% nitrogen, and 30% nitrogen. These percentages cover the
range of nitrogen found in field samples. Field sampling of tank vapors revealed an average air
composition of 3.7% with peaks at 10% and 20%. Given air comprises approximately

 Test 6 – Long-Term Operation

 A total of 1548.5 hours were logged to evaluate long-term operation of the compressor. The
total time included all nitrogen, LPG, and mixture testing combined with a continuously operating
period. The compressor was set up to operate for about 1 month compressing nitrogen continuously
from 1 to 50 psig at a flow rate between 1.5 to 3 scfm. The compressor was disassembled and
inspected after the continuous operation period to observe integrity and mechanical wear. The
primary concern with long-term operation is diaphragm life. The life of the diaphragm was not
determined since it did not fail during continuous operation. Postinspection did reveal some
material thinning and black debris within the compressor body. Unique to this compressor design
is the use of compressed gas to support the backside of the diaphragm (not-working side). This is
accomplished by attaching a compressed cylinder to the central section of the compressor and
regulating gas to 100 psi. During testing, the backside pressure was maintained between 75 and
100 psi using nitrogen. The manufacturer specifies that gas use rate of 0.003 scfm is expected as
the backside support gas will leak through the mechanical components around the drive
mechanisms. Experimentally, a use rate of less than 0.1 scfm was observed. A large leak of the
backside gas developed at the end of the test period. Postinspection determined that wear
associated with an internal piston located within the drive system for the diaphragm allowed the
gas to escape from the backside pressure envelope of the compressor. The finding suggests that
after 1500 hours of operation, compressed gas consumption to support the backside of the
diaphragm can become unmanageable. Figures 16–19 provide depictions of the compressor
components and photos of the postinspection.

DISCUSSION

 Validation of Models

 Experimental data from testing are compared with EOS models. Accurate EOS modeling is
useful for adapting the technology to varied field conditions and designing for scale-up in relation
to heat transfer and mass flow. EOS modeling is also useful for predicting gas composition and
analyzing the oxygen balance throughout the process. The Peng-Robinson EOS was applied in all
cases.

 Storage tank safety systems are designed to allow air infiltration, which imparts oxygen
concentration to the tank vapors. Oxygen is undesirable for downstream gas processing systems,
and commercial gas-gathering agreements typically specify an oxygen limit. Experiments were
performed to examine how oxygen may be separated from the gas stream by utilizing the properties
of heavier hydrocarbons (C3+), which can be liquified. During liquification, a higher percentage
of oxygen will remain in the gas phase versus the liquid phase. Therefore, the liquid-phase gas
containing less oxygen is more suitable to recover. Alternatively, the higher oxygen content gas
can be used as fuel on-site. Air or oxygen was not used in the experiments to avoid any
flammability risk. Because air mostly comprises nitrogen, nitrogen has similar properties;
therefore, Nitrogen and oxygen are ideal gases that behave similarly with respect to gas saturation
of LPG. Obtaining a good match between the EOS model and the experimental results for nitrogen
mixed with LPG provides confidence for predicting a mixed LPG and air/oxygen balance.

 Comparison of Nitrogen Tests to EOS Model

 Data collected from tests compressing nitrogen are compared to EOS modeling. Tests were
operated at four compressor speeds (37%, 50%, 78%, 100%), and each test was modeled according
to the diagram in Figure 20. The experimental temperature, pressure, and flow conditions are
modeled, enabling the calculation of compressor horsepower and heat exchange. Experiments
were conducted at 90°F ambient temperature, which did not trigger the control for the aftercooler
fan. The model was useful in calculating heat loss from the body and associated compressor
plumbing. The modeling results are provided in Table 7. The nitrogen experiments and modeling
results assist in predicting heat transfer with respect to aftercooler design. Modeling results
indicate a significant difference in theoretical power and measured power. Measured compressor
efficiencies were less than half of the modeled efficiency.

CONCLUSIONS

 The project has met the objectives to develop and validate a novel technology for capturing
vapors from storage tanks and to complete engineering-scale testing of a prototype functioning
with anticipated gas components. The prototype was operated at dynamic conditions and achieved
process control targets. Design parameters and safety associated with operation and gas
containment were achieved. Data collection from the field provided learnings for gas composition
and flow rate. These learnings informed the test conditions for the prototype. The prototype is
designed to capture gas, compress gas, and separate vapor and liquid in which vapor can be
recycled to the process and liquids may be captured and gathered to a pipeline system.
Understanding how to remove oxygen from tank vapors is an important aspect of the project. The
outcomes of the research provide hands-on operational experience and a foundation for future
design. The work from this project has assisted the technology to advance to field demonstration.

 Specific conclusions regarding the work are summarized as follows:

 Test 1 – A diaphragm compressor was selected because of its simplicity, tolerance to
contaminants in the gas, potential supply, and turn-down ratio. The compressor is a manufactured
product and development of larger capacity is plausible. The primary concern with a diaphragm
compressor is the life of the diaphragms and relative cost of maintenance. Leaks from the
diaphragm sealing surface with the internal body were discovered and remedied with diaphragm
replacement. Unique to the diaphragm compressor design is the use of supply gas to support the
nonworking or back side of the diaphragm. The consumption of supply gas exceeded the
manufacturer specification by a factor of over 10. Successful future application of a diaphragm
compressor will require adequate sealing to minimize supply gas consumption and scale-up to
accommodate larger tank vapor flow rates.

 Test 2 – This test was conducted to verify the functionality of the compressor. The
compressor and system functioned as anticipated. Modeling revealed that the experimental
compressor efficiency was less than anticipated. It is plausible that the compressor was not
achieving optimal volumetric efficiency during this test. A potential cause could be that the
backside pressure was maintained at 25 psi above what the manufacturer recommends, as optimal
flow rates were achieved at this pressure. The higher pressure possibly caused the diaphragm to
deform and limit the volumetric space on the working side of the compressor, limiting efficiency.
This premise was not vetted with the manufacturer. Outside of the unanticipated lower compressor
efficiency, the process performed as expected.

 Test 3 – Dynamic testing of the compressor verified that the control system was fast enough
to respond to typical gas surges from storage tanks. Significant work to tune the PID control
provided confidence that performance was optimized.

 Test 4 – Operating the compressor with LPG performed as anticipated. Modeling results
provided a reasonable match to experimental results. Gas did not leak from the system.

 Test 5 – LPG was mixed with nitrogen for these tests to examine the performance of the
system with respect to air that can infiltrate the storage tank headspace. Liquid separation provides

 27

a means to decrease oxygen content and create a purer gas stream for recovery. Experimental
results validated models to determine the oxygen balance of the system in relation to the amount
of air (oxygen) present in tank vapors. It is possible to reduce oxygen levels in the liquid gas stream
to between 1300 and 1650 ppm given concentrations of air in storage tank vapors in the range of
2.5% to 25%.

 Test 6 – Over 1500 hours were logged on the diaphragm compressor during testing. The
diaphragms did not fail, therefore diaphragm life was not determined. An internal leak was
discovered from the mechanical drive system that is internal to the compressor body. This leak,
discovered near the end of testing, causes an unacceptable consumption of backside diaphragm
support gas. Improvement of the long-term performance of these seals will be required for
commercial application.

 The results of the project provide confidence in furthering development and preparing for
field-testing. Viable options are available for piston compressors that do not require oil for
lubrication, and a field-ready compressor skid can be designed based on the project outcomes.
Results provide valuable confidence in the process configuration, separation of oxygen, and
dynamic operability. The next steps include developing a more streamlined commercial package
and field-testing. A diaphragm compressor remains a viable option if the excessive use of
diaphragm support gas can be minimized.
