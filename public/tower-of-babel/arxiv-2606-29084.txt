An Approximate Conjugate Subgradient Algorithm with Matrix Parameter for Derivative-Free Nonsmooth Optimization Problems
Morteza Kimiaei

Abstract




Abstract.
We propose a derivative-free matrix conjugate-subgradient method for unconstrained nonsmooth optimization of locally Lipschitz functions. The method constructs discrete gradients using only function values and forms a finite sampled model of the Goldstein subdifferential. A minimal-norm element of the convex hull of the sampled discrete gradients is then computed and used both as a stationarity measure and as the reference vector for generating descent-oriented directions. To improve robustness beyond the basic steepest-descent direction, we introduce a matrix memory correction together with coefficient damping, diagonal scaling, bounded-angle correction, and matrix-stability safeguards. A two-point line-search procedure with enrichment is used to obtain either a serious step or an improved local model. Under suitable consistency assumptions on the discrete-gradient approximation and line-search sampling, the method generates directions satisfying a safeguarded descent property and computes approximate Goldstein stationary points. Numerical experiments on nonsmooth test problems with dimensions up to
1000

show that both proposed variants are robust for lower and medium accuracy
requirements, while the matrix conjugate-subgradient variant remains the most
reliable under the strictest tolerance.

Keywords.
Nonlinear programming, unconstrained nonsmooth optimization, approximate conjugate subgradient algorithm, matrix conjugate subgradient parameter, global convergence.

Introduction

In the modern age, nonlinear programming plays a central role in various practical aspects of machine learning and data mining
[
14
]
. Therefore, it deserves to be addressed thoroughly from both algorithmic and modeling perspectives. In recent years, scholars have devoted a substantial portion of their efforts to enriching optimization tools—particularly by emphasizing diversity and inclusion within the appropriate context. While these contributions can be considered satisfactory, the ever-expanding horizons of science and technology call for a broader and more adaptive perspective.

By the beginning of the current century, the growing use of high-dimensional models had led researchers to place increasing emphasis on scalable and computationally efficient algorithmic strategies. This development has also affected how accuracy is treated, since high accuracy requirements often increase the computational burden and may make it difficult to obtain an acceptable solution within a reasonable CPU time. Therefore, a central issue in modeling and optimization is to achieve a meaningful trade-off between accuracy and efficiency, which requires the use of algorithmic strategies specifically designed to control computational cost without sacrificing the quality of the computed solution.

In the context of continuous optimization,
Conjugate Gradient
(
CG
) algorithms have traditionally been recognized as a family of memoryless methods designed to address large-scale models
[
3
]
, especially following recent hardware developments. As
Line Search
(
LiS
) techniques, they not only directly exploit the steepest descent direction as part of their search direction, but also indirectly incorporate Hessian information—making them often excellent in terms of global convergence and ease of implementation
[
12
]
. In some cases,
CG
methods have been endowed with an additional degree of flexibility by incorporating an extra parameter into their formulaic structure. Such extensions are designed to establish connections with other classes of optimization algorithms, such as
Quasi–Newton
(
QN
) methods, which more explicitly exploit second-order information of the model. An illustrative example in this context is the
Dai–Liao
(
DL
)
[
13
]
algorithm, which can nowadays be regarded as a sophisticated memoryless optimization approach
[
6
]
. Meanwhile, such algorithms have often been devised based on scalar parameters, thereby only benefiting from a so-called one-dimensional flexibility.

Derivative-Free Nonsmooth Optimization
(DFNO) in algorithmic point of view can be broadly divided into two categories:

-

An algorithm of the first class attempts to approximate generalized first-order information from function values. Discrete-gradient methods approximate subgradient-like objects through finite-difference sampling and then use these approximations inside
LiS
frameworks
[
8
,
27
]
. Simplex-gradient techniques construct local linear models from sampled function values and use the resulting gradient estimates mainly as descent indicators, for example to order polling directions in
Generalized Pattern Search
(GPS) or
Mesh Adaptive Direct Search
(MADS)
[
11
]
. Related approaches include clustering-based approximations of Clarke generalized gradients
[
18
]
, derivative-free variants inspired by gradient-sampling approaches
[
28
,
22
,
21
]
, and max-linear trust-region models designed to capture local nonsmooth structure through collections of linear pieces
[
29
]
. These methods can improve practical efficiency, but their reliability depends on the quality, stability, and cost of the approximate first-order information.

-

An algorithm of the second class avoids explicit subgradient approximations and instead obtains convergence from polling geometry, dense direction sets, sufficient decrease, or smoothing mechanisms. MADS is the canonical example: it uses mesh-based polling with asymptotically dense directions to obtain Clarke-type stationarity guarantees without estimating subgradients
[
5
]
. The
NOMAD
software package implements MADS and incorporates additional search strategies, including
Variable Neighborhood Search
(VNS), to improve global exploration while preserving the underlying MADS convergence guarantees
[
4
]
.
LiS
-based
DFN
methods use dense direction sequences, sufficient-decrease tests, and exact-penalty mechanisms for nonsmooth constrained problems
[
15
]
. The
DFNDFL
solver, proposed by Giovannelli et al.
[
19
]
, extends the
LiS
-based
DFN
framework to mixed-integer nonsmooth constrained optimization by combining dense direction sequences for continuous variables with primitive directions for integer variables and by employing an exact-penalty approach to handle nonlinear constraints. Other relevant derivative-free frameworks include rigorous pattern-search methods, Lipschitzian global optimization methods such as
DIRECT
, and brute-force derivative-free solvers
[
9
,
35
,
1
,
16
,
23
,
33
]
.

In our approach, the canonical direction set will be enriched by blending deterministic hypercube directions with randomized orthogonal bases
[
11
,
4
]
. The conditioning of the direction system will be monitored, and directions will be regenerated when necessary, improving finite-precision stability and angular coverage for efficient DFNO algorithms.

1.1
Our Contribution

This work develops a derivative-free matrix conjugate-subgradient framework for
locally Lipschitz nonsmooth optimization. The main algorithmic idea is to combine
discrete-gradient approximations of Goldstein subdifferentials with a
matrix-based memory mechanism for generating search directions. In contrast to
classical scalar memory corrections, the proposed approach uses a structured
matrix correction to incorporate information from recent iterations while
remaining suitable for function-value-only optimization. This matrix formulation
provides additional flexibility in the construction of search directions and
allows the use of stability safeguards based on matrix quantities, such as
eigenvalue and conditioning information.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

•

We develop a derivative-free framework for locally Lipschitz nonsmooth
optimization based on discrete gradients and finite sampled approximations of
Goldstein subdifferentials. The method uses only function values and does not
require analytical gradients, subgradients, or active-index information.

•

We compute a minimal-norm element of the convex hull of sampled discrete
gradients and use it as a practical stationarity residual and as the reference
vector for constructing descent-oriented search directions.

•

We introduce a matrix conjugate-subgradient direction that enriches the
basic discrete-gradient steepest-descent direction by incorporating memory from
previous iterations. The direction is protected by coefficient damping,
dominance control, diagonal scaling, matrix-stability checks, and a bounded-angle
safeguard.

•

We design a two-point line-search and enrichment procedure that either
accepts a serious step or augments the local discrete-gradient model. This
mechanism improves the sampled model while preserving a function-value-only
implementation.

•

We establish the consistency of the finite discrete-gradient model with
Goldstein-type stationarity under appropriate approximation assumptions and prove
descent and finite-termination properties for the safeguarded line-search
procedure.

•

We prove that, under the stated consistency assumptions and vanishing
sampling and discretization errors, the proposed framework generates approximate
Goldstein stationary points whose accumulation points are Clarke stationary.

•

We evaluate the proposed methods against established derivative-free
nonsmooth solvers on test problems with dimensions up to
1000
. The numerical
results show that both proposed variants are robust for lower and medium
accuracy requirements, while the matrix conjugate-subgradient variant remains
the most reliable under the strictest tolerance.

In summary, the contribution has two components. First, we adapt the
exact-subgradient sampling and enrichment framework proposed in
[
30
]
to the derivative-free
case by replacing exact subgradients with discrete-gradient approximations
computed from function values. This requires rebuilding the sampled model,
the minimal-norm stationarity residual, and the enrichment mechanism in terms
of approximate subgradient information. Second, we introduce new algorithmic
components that are not present in the exact-subgradient framework: a
derivative-free matrix conjugate-subgradient direction, a componentwise
diagonal scaling based on previously found best points, and safeguards based
on damping, dominance control, matrix stability, and bounded-angle correction.
The line search is also modified for robustness by using a two-point acceptance
test rather than relying only on the standard geometric update. These changes
allow the method to exploit memory information while remaining fully
derivative-free and stable under approximate subgradient models.

2
Preliminaries

This section collects the basic concepts and notation used in the development of
the proposed method. We first state the nonsmooth unconstrained optimization
problem, then recall Clarke and Goldstein stationarity, and finally introduce
the discrete-gradient constructions and finite sampled models used to obtain
computable derivative-free stationarity measures.

2.1
Problem Formulation

We consider the unconstrained minimization problem

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ f(\mathbf{x}),

(2.1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is assumed to be locally Lipschitz, possibly nonconvex and nonsmooth, contributing significantly to practical implementations in machine learning and data mining
[
14
]
. In particular, a wide range of constrained optimization algorithms need to locally address the unconstrained transformation of the original model within the framework of a subproblem, which highlights the importance of model (
2.1
). In many modern applications, the objective function
f
in (
2.1
) is locally Lipschitz but may fail to be continuously differentiable. In such cases, classical gradient-based algorithms cannot be applied directly, since gradients may be unavailable or not exist everywhere.

2.2
Stationarity Concepts and Practical Criterion

In DFNO, it is well-known that the use of
approximate gradients, typically obtained via finite differences,
can significantly improve performance, provided that the noise level
is sufficiently controlled. A similar principle applies in the
nonsmooth setting: approximations of subgradients constructed via
finite-difference schemes, such as discrete gradients

[
8
,
27
]
, can lead to more effective descent
directions than purely directional sampling strategies. The following definitions of Clarke stationarity and
\varepsilon
-Clarke stationarity are standard in nonsmooth optimization and are based on the Clarke generalized directional derivative and Clarke subdifferential; see, e.g.,
[
5
]
and
[
15
]
.

Clarke stationarity.

A point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
Clarke stationary point
of a locally Lipschitz function
f
if

f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d}):=\limsup_{\mathbf{y}\to\mathbf{x},\ t\downarrow 0}\frac{f(\mathbf{y}+t\mathbf{d})-f(\mathbf{y})}{t}\geq 0\quad\forall\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

or equivalently,
0\in\partial f(\mathbf{x})
.

Let
D_{f}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the set of differentiability points of
f
. Since
f
is locally Lipschitz,
D_{f}
has full measure. Throughout the paper, we denote by
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
the set of nonnegative integers.

\varepsilon
-Clarke stationary point.

For a given tolerance
\varepsilon>0
, if

\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\partial f(\mathbf{x})}\|\mathbf{v}\|\leq\varepsilon,

(2.2)

then we say that
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an

\varepsilon
-Clarke stationary point
, where the Clarke subdifferential is given by

\partial f(\mathbf{x}):=\mathop{\rm conv}\Big\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \big|\ \exists\{\mathbf{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\subset D_{f},\ \mathbf{x}_{k}\to\mathbf{x},\ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\to\mathbf{g}\Big\}.

(2.3)

Computability issue.

Condition (
2.2
) is generally impractical to verify in derivative-free settings, since it requires full knowledge of
\partial f(\mathbf{x})
. Therefore, we rely on computable approximations of the Goldstein subdifferential using discrete gradients, as will be described in Section
2.3
.

2.3
Goldstein Subdifferential and Approximate Stationarity

In practice, verifying (
2.2
) is challenging, since it
requires access to the entire set
\partial f(\mathbf{x})
, which is generally
unavailable in derivative-free settings. For this reason, it is more
practical to work with the
Goldstein
\varepsilon
-subdifferential
.
Here and throughout,
\mathop{\rm cl}A
denotes the closure of a set
A
. We define

\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x}):=\mathop{\rm cl}\mathop{\rm conv}\Big(\bigcup_{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|\leq\varepsilon}\partial f(\mathbf{y})\Big),

(2.4)

where
\partial f(\mathbf{y})
denotes the Clarke subdifferential of
f
at
\mathbf{y}
, defined by (
2.3
). Unlike
\partial f(\mathbf{x})
, the set
\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x})
captures
information from a neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}
, and is therefore more amenable
to approximation via sampling and finite-difference constructions. For locally Lipschitz functions,
\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x})
is a nonempty, convex, and compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Indeed, since
f
is locally Lipschitz, the Clarke subdifferential
\partial f(\mathbf{y})
is nonempty and bounded on bounded sets. Therefore, the union
\bigcup_{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|\leq\varepsilon}\partial f(\mathbf{y})
is bounded. In finite-dimensional spaces, the closed convex hull of a bounded set is compact, which implies that
\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x})
is compact.

Recall that
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Clarke stationary point if
0\in\partial f(\mathbf{x})
. Moreover, if
\mathbf{x}
is a local minimizer of
f
, then it is necessarily a Clarke stationary point. Since the function
f
is locally Lipschitz but possibly nonsmooth,
the exact Clarke subdifferential
\partial f(\mathbf{x})
is generally unavailable.
Therefore, we construct computable approximations of the Clarke and
Goldstein
\varepsilon
-subdifferentials using discrete-gradient constructions,
which are accurate up to a controlled approximation error.

Approximate stationarity point.

Given
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, let

\mathop{\rm conv}\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
be a finite computable model of

\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x})
. We say that
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a

(\delta,\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}))

Goldstein stationary point
if

\min\{\|\mathbf{v}\|\mid\mathbf{v}\in\mathop{\rm conv}\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\}\leq\delta.

This definition is understood as a computable surrogate of Goldstein
stationarity based on the finite model

\mathop{\rm conv}\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
; cf.
[
30
]
.

Relation to discrete-gradient approximations.

The set
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
represents an ideal finite model of
the Goldstein subdifferential
\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x})
constructed
from exact subgradient information. In derivative-free settings, such
subgradients are generally unavailable. We therefore replace this ideal
model by a finite set
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
constructed via discrete
gradients.

The resulting set
\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
should not be interpreted as
an exact outer approximation of
\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
.
Rather, under the discrete-gradient consistency assumptions stated below,
its elements are close to elements of the Goldstein subdifferential:

\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\subset\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})+B(0,\delta_{k}),

(2.5)

where
\delta_{k}\to 0
as the discretization parameter vanishes. Thus,

\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
is a computable sampled model of

\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
, accurate up to the approximation
error
\delta_{k}
.

Approximate stationarity via discrete gradients.

Let
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
be the finite set of discrete gradients constructed
at
\mathbf{x}_{k}
, and assume that (
2.5
) holds. We say that

\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a

(\delta,\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))

discrete-gradient Goldstein stationary point
if

\min\{\|\mathbf{v}\|\mid\mathbf{v}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\}\leq\delta.

(2.6)

This is a computable stationarity test based on the discrete-gradient
model. Its relation to true Goldstein stationarity depends on the
approximation error in (
2.5
).

Proposition 1

(Consistency with Goldstein stationarity)

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be locally Lipschitz and fix
\varepsilon>0
.
Assume that the finite set
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
of discrete gradients satisfies
(
2.5
) for some
\delta_{k}\geq 0
. Let

\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
be a nonempty finite model of

\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
satisfying the approximate covering condition

\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\subset\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))+B(0,\eta_{k}),

(2.7)

for some
\eta_{k}\geq 0
. If
\mathbf{x}_{k}
is a

(\delta,\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
discrete-gradient Goldstein stationary point, i.e.,
(
2.6
) holds, then
\mathbf{x}_{k}
is a

(\delta+\delta_{k}+\eta_{k},\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
Goldstein
stationary point, namely,

\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))}\|\mathbf{u}\|\leq\delta+\delta_{k}+\eta_{k}.

Proof.

By (
2.5
), (
2.6
), and (
2.7
), there are
\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
with
\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|\leq\delta
,
\mathbf{w}_{k}\in\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
with
\|\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{w}_{k}\|\leq\delta_{k}
, and

\mathbf{u}_{k}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
with
\|\mathbf{w}_{k}-\mathbf{u}_{k}\|\leq\eta_{k}
, respectively. Therefore, by the triangle inequality,

\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|\leq\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|+\|\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{w}_{k}\|+\|\mathbf{w}_{k}-\mathbf{u}_{k}\|\leq\delta+\delta_{k}+\eta_{k}.

Since
\mathbf{u}_{k}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
, it follows that
\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))}\|\mathbf{u}\|\leq\|\mathbf{u}_{k}\|\leq\delta+\delta_{k}+\eta_{k}
. This proves the claim.
∎

Remark 1

The discrete-gradient approximation property used in
(
2.5
) and in Proposition
2
is
essential. It is not implied by local Lipschitz continuity alone. In the
discrete-gradient literature, such approximation results are obtained
under additional regularity conditions
[
8
,
27
]
, for example semismoothness together
with a uniform small-o condition on the discrete-gradient construction.

Remark 2

(Asymptotic consistency)

Assume that
\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\subset\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})+B(0,\rho_{k})
as
\rho_{k}\to 0
, and
\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\subset\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))+B(0,\eta_{k})
as
\eta_{k}\to 0
. If
\mathbf{x}_{k}
is a sequence of

(\tau_{k},\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
discrete-gradient Goldstein stationary points,
with
\tau_{k}\to 0
, then

\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))}\|\mathbf{u}\|\leq\tau_{k}+\rho_{k}+\eta_{k}\to 0.

Moreover,
\mathop{\rm dist}\bigl(0,\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\bigr)\leq\tau_{k}+\rho_{k}\to 0
.

2.4
Discrete-gradient Construction

In this subsection, we describe the construction of the discrete gradient following the approach introduced in
[
8
,
27
]
. We denote the set of unit directions by

D:=\{\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\|\mathbf{d}\|=1\}.

For a given direction
\mathbf{d}\in D
, we define the index

i_{\max}:=\mathop{\rm argmax}_{j=1,\dots,n}|d_{j}|.

(2.8)

Since
\|\mathbf{d}\|=1
, it follows that
|d_{i_{\max}}|\geq 1/\sqrt{n}
and hence
d_{i_{\max}}\neq 0
, which guarantees that the construction below is well-defined. The set of vertices of the unit hypercube

G:=\{{\bf e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid{\bf e}=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})^{\top},\ |e_{j}|=1,\ j=1,2,...,n\},

and the class of positive infinitesimal functions

P:=\left\{z:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)\ \middle|\ \frac{z(\lambda)}{\lambda}\to 0\text{ as }\lambda\to 0^{+}\right\}

are defined. Given parameters
\lambda>0
,
\omega\in(0,1]
,
{\bf e}\in G
, and
z\in P
, the sequence of points

\mathbf{x}_{0}:=\mathbf{x}+\lambda\mathbf{d},\qquad\mathbf{x}_{j}:=\mathbf{x}_{0}+z(\lambda){\bf e}_{j}(\omega),\qquad j=1,\dots,n,

(2.9)

is defined, where

{\bf e}_{1}(\omega):=\begin{pmatrix}\omega e_{1}\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad{\bf e}_{2}(\omega):=\begin{pmatrix}\omega e_{1}\\
\omega^{2}e_{2}\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\ldots,\quad{\bf e}_{n}(\omega):=\begin{pmatrix}\omega e_{1}\\
\omega^{2}e_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\omega^{n}e_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

In practical implementations, a specific choice of
z(\lambda)
must be made. Following the implementation commonly used in
[
8
,
27
]
, we select the quadratic perturbation

z(\lambda)=\lambda^{2},

(2.10)

which satisfies
z(\lambda)/\lambda\to 0
as
\lambda\to 0
and yields a stable numerical approximation.

The discrete gradient associated with index
i_{\max}
satisfying (
2.8
) is defined as

\mathbf{g}_{i_{\max}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{d},{\bf e},z,\lambda,\omega):=(g_{i_{\max}1},\dots,g_{i_{\max}n})^{\top},

with components

g_{i_{\max}j}:=\frac{f(\mathbf{x}_{j})-f(\mathbf{x}_{j-1})}{z(\lambda)\omega^{j}e_{j}},\qquad j\neq i_{\max},

and

g_{i_{\max}i_{\max}}:=\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+\lambda\mathbf{d})-f(\mathbf{x})-\lambda\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i_{\max}}g_{i_{\max}j}d_{j}}{\lambda d_{i_{\max}}}.

Since
z(\lambda)>0
,
\omega>0
, and
e_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
,
j=1,2,...,n
, all denominators are nonzero and the above expressions are well-defined. By construction, the identity

f(\mathbf{x}+\lambda\mathbf{d})-f(\mathbf{x})=\lambda\mathbf{d}^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{i_{\max}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{d},{\bf e},z,\lambda,\omega)

holds exactly. Each discrete gradient requires at most
n+2
function evaluations (or
n+1
additional evaluations if
f(\mathbf{x})
is already available).

2.5
Discrete-Gradient Sets and Finite Subdifferential Approximation

Discrete gradient set.

For a fixed scalar
\lambda>0
, we define

V(\mathbf{x},\lambda):=\mathop{\rm cl}\mathop{\rm conv}\left\{\mathbf{g}_{i_{\max}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{d},{\bf e},z,\lambda,\omega)\mid\mathbf{d}\in D,\,{\bf e}\in G,\,z\in P,\,\omega\in(0,1]\right\}.

The set
V(\mathbf{x},\lambda)
coincides with the discrete-gradient
set
D_{0}(\mathbf{x},\lambda)
introduced in
[
8
]
,
up to notation. By Proposition 5.1 in
[
8
]
, if
f
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L
, then

\|\mathbf{g}_{i_{\max}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{d},{\bf e},z,\lambda,\omega)\|\leq C(n)L,

(2.11)

with the dimension-dependent constant
C(n)>0
, and consequently,

V(\mathbf{x},\lambda)\subset B(0,C(n)L).

(2.12)

Approximation of the Clarke subdifferential.

By Corollary 5.1 in
[
8
]
, under the additional regularity
conditions used there, for each fixed
\mathbf{x}
and every sufficiently small scalar

\lambda>0
,

V(\mathbf{x},\lambda)\subset\partial f(\mathbf{x})+B(0,\delta(\lambda)),\qquad\delta(\lambda)\to 0\text{ as }\lambda\to 0.

(2.13)

This is a pointwise approximation result. For convergence arguments
involving points in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}
, we impose Assumption (A2),
which is the neighborhood version used in
[
8
, Assumption 5.1]
. Moreover, if
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is a local minimizer, then by Proposition 5.3 in
[
8
]
, there exists
\lambda_{0}>0
such that

0\in V(\mathbf{x}^{*},\lambda)\qquad\text{for all }\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0}).

(2.14)

Approximation of the Goldstein subdifferential.

For
\varepsilon>0
, we define

V_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\lambda):=\mathop{\rm conv}\left(\bigcup_{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|\leq\varepsilon}V(\mathbf{y},\lambda)\right).

If the pointwise approximation (
2.13
) holds uniformly for
all
\mathbf{y}
satisfying
\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|\leq\varepsilon
, then combining it with
the definition of
\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x})
yields

V_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\lambda)\subset\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x})+B(0,\delta(\lambda)).

(2.15)

Under the corresponding uniform version of the pointwise approximation
(
2.13
) over the ball

\{\mathbf{y}\mid\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|\leq\varepsilon\}
, the ideal neighborhood-based
discrete-gradient set satisfies

V_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\lambda)\subset\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x})+B(0,\delta(\lambda)),\qquad\delta(\lambda)\to 0.

(2.16)

Thus, as
\lambda\to 0
, the ideal discrete-gradient model is
asymptotically consistent with the Goldstein subdifferential.

Standing Assumptions.

We collect here all assumptions required for the convergence analysis.

(A1)

The function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is locally Lipschitz.

(A2)

For every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
\delta>0
, there exist

\lambda_{0}>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

V(\mathbf{y},\lambda)\subseteq\partial f(\mathbf{x}+\overline{B}(0,\delta))+B(0,\delta)\qquad\forall\mathbf{y}\in B(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon),\quad\forall\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0}),

where

\partial f(\mathbf{x}+\overline{B}(0,\delta)):=\bigcup_{\mathbf{z}\in\overline{B}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})}\partial f(\mathbf{z}),\qquad\overline{B}(\mathbf{x},\delta):=\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\|\leq\delta\}.

(A3)

The initial level set

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_{0}):=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(\mathbf{x})\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{0})\}

is bounded.

(A4)

The function
f
is weakly upper semismooth in the relevant
search directions. Moreover, the discrete gradients generated along any
LiS
direction satisfy the following directional consistency
condition: for any direction
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, any

z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, any infinite index set
J\subset\mathbb{N}
, any sequence

\{h_{i}\}_{i\in J}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
h_{i}\downarrow 0
as
i\to\infty

along
J
, and any sequence of discrete gradients
\{\mathbf{g}_{i}\}_{i\in J}

generated at the points
z+h_{i}\mathbf{d}
, one has

\limsup_{i\in J,\ i\to\infty}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{d}\geq\liminf_{i\in J,\ i\to\infty}\frac{f(z+h_{i}\mathbf{d})-f(z)}{h_{i}}.

(2.17)

(A5)

At every iteration where the local Armijo admissibility result
is invoked, the current model vector

\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and the corrected direction

\mathbf{d}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
satisfy the directional model-error bound

\left|f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x}_{k};\mathbf{d}_{k})-\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{d}_{k}\right|\leq\xi_{k}\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|,

(2.18)

where
\xi_{k}\geq 0
. Moreover, there exists
\theta\in(0,1)
such that the relative directional model-error condition

\xi_{k}\leq\theta\varrho\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|

(2.19)

holds. This assumption is used only to guarantee local Armijo admissibility of
the corrected direction. It is not required for the finite termination of
Algorithm
1
, which is proved under (A4).

Assumption (A2) is the discrete-gradient approximation assumption used in

[
8
, Assumption 5.1]
. It is not a consequence of local
Lipschitz continuity alone; it is imposed to ensure that discrete gradients
computed at nearby points and sufficiently small discretization parameters
are contained in a small enlargement of the Clarke subdifferential over a
neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}
.

Remark 3

(Clarke subdifferential properties used below)

Under (A1), the Clarke subdifferential
\partial f(\mathbf{x})
is nonempty,
convex, and compact for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, the set-valued
mapping
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\partial f(\mathbf{x})
is outer semicontinuous and locally
bounded. In particular, if
\mathbf{x}_{j}\to\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{g}_{j}\in\partial f(\mathbf{x}_{j})
, and

\mathbf{g}_{j}\to\mathbf{g}
, then
\mathbf{g}\in\partial f(\mathbf{x})
. Also, for every bounded set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists
M>0
such that

\|\mathbf{g}\|\leq M,\qquad\forall\mathbf{g}\in\partial f(\mathbf{x}),\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in C.

These standard facts will be used in the Clarke-stationarity convergence
argument; see, e.g.,
[
10
]
.

Finite approximation via sampling.

In practice, the full sets
V(\mathbf{x},\lambda)
and
V_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\lambda)
are unavailable. We construct a finite approximation by selecting directions
\{\mathbf{d}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{d}_{m}\}\subset D
and sampling points
\mathbf{y}_{\ell}
such that
\|\mathbf{y}_{\ell}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}
:

\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}):=\left\{\mathbf{g}_{i_{\max}}\left(\mathbf{y}_{\ell},\mathbf{d}_{\ell},{\bf e}_{\ell},z_{k},\lambda_{k},\omega\right)\right\}_{\ell=1}^{m}.

(2.20)

Then,

\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\subset V_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\lambda_{k}).

(2.21)

Indeed,
\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
provides a computable finite sampled
model whose elements are controlled through the discrete-gradient
approximation property. It should not be interpreted as an outer
approximation of
\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
in the set-inclusion
sense. Combining (
2.15
) and (
2.21
)
yields the following finite-sample consistency result.

Proposition 2

(Finite-sample consistency of discrete gradients)

Assume that the pointwise discrete-gradient approximation property
(
2.13
) holds at each sampling point
\mathbf{y}_{\ell}
.
Let
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
satisfy
\lambda_{k}\to 0
and
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
. Let
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
be a finite set of discrete gradients constructed
at sampling points
\{\mathbf{y}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{m_{k}}
satisfying

\|\mathbf{y}_{\ell}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|\leq\varepsilon_{k},\qquad\ell=1,\dots,m_{k},

using discretization parameter
\lambda_{k}
. Suppose that the corresponding
pointwise errors are bounded by
\delta_{k}\to 0
, i.e.,

V(\mathbf{y}_{\ell},\lambda_{k})\subset\partial f(\mathbf{y}_{\ell})+B(0,\delta_{k}),\qquad\ell=1,\dots,m_{k}.

Then

\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\subset\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})+B(0,\delta_{k}).

(2.22)

Proof.

Each element
\mathbf{g}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
is a discrete gradient computed at a point
\mathbf{y}_{\ell}
with
\|\mathbf{y}_{\ell}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}
, and a parameter
\lambda_{k}
. By the discrete-gradient approximation property (
2.13
), for each
\ell
there exists
\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\in\partial f(\mathbf{y}_{\ell})
such that
\|\mathbf{g}_{\ell}-\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\|\leq\delta_{k}
. Hence,

\mathbf{g}_{\ell}\in\partial f(\mathbf{y}_{\ell})+B(0,\delta_{k}).

Taking convex combinations, for any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
, we can write

\mathbf{v}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{k}}p_{\ell}\mathbf{g}_{\ell},\qquad\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{k}}p_{\ell}=1,\qquad p_{\ell}\geq 0.

For each
\ell
, there exists
\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\in\partial f(\mathbf{y}_{\ell})
such that

\|\mathbf{g}_{\ell}-\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\|\leq\delta_{k}
, hence
\mathbf{g}_{\ell}=\mathbf{w}_{\ell}+\beta_{\ell}

with
\beta_{\ell}\in B(0,\delta_{k})
. Therefore,

\mathbf{v}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{k}}p_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{k}}p_{\ell}\beta_{\ell}.

Since
\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\in\partial f(\mathbf{y}_{\ell})
for each
\ell
, it follows that

\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{k}}p_{\ell}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\in\mathop{\rm conv}\Big(\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m_{k}}\partial f(\mathbf{y}_{\ell})\Big),

and since
B(0,\delta_{k})
is convex,
\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{k}}p_{\ell}\beta_{\ell}\in B(0,\delta_{k})
. Hence,

\mathbf{v}\in\mathop{\rm conv}\Big(\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m_{k}}\partial f(\mathbf{y}_{\ell})\Big)+B(0,\delta_{k}),

which implies
\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\subset\mathop{\rm conv}\Big(\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m_{k}}\partial f(\mathbf{y}_{\ell})\Big)+B(0,\delta_{k})
. Since
\|\mathbf{y}_{\ell}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}
, it follows that

\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m_{k}}\partial f(\mathbf{y}_{\ell})\subset\bigcup_{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}}\partial f(\mathbf{y}).

Therefore,

\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\subset\mathop{\rm cl}\mathop{\rm conv}\Big(\bigcup_{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}}\partial f(\mathbf{y})\Big)+B(0,\delta_{k})\overset{\eqref{e.Goldsteinsubb}}{=}\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})+B(0,\delta_{k}),

so that (
2.22
) is obtained.
∎

Remark 4

(Role of discrete-gradient properties)

The inclusion (
2.13
) is the key property used in the proof above. It ensures that discrete gradients are contained in a controlled neighborhood of the Clarke subdifferential.

The optimality condition (
2.14
), which guarantees that
0\in V(\mathbf{x}^{*},\lambda)
at local minimizers, is not required for the present approximation result, but will play a role later in the stationarity and convergence analysis.

Discrete-gradient model error and sources of approximation.

Assumption (A2) provides the basic consistency property of the
discrete-gradient construction. In particular, discrete gradients computed at
points sufficiently close to a reference point, and with sufficiently small
discretization parameter, belong to a small enlargement of the Clarke
subdifferential over a neighborhood of that reference point. Therefore, the
error appearing in the discrete-gradient approximation is not noise in the
objective values. Rather, it is a deterministic model error caused by replacing
exact Clarke subgradient information by computable discrete gradients. In the
present framework, this approximation is governed by three quantities: the
Goldstein radius
\varepsilon_{k}
, the discretization error
\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{disc}}:=\delta(\lambda_{k})
, arising from the discrete-gradient construction, and the sampling/modeling
error
\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{samp}}
, accounting for possible finite-sampling,
inexact construction, or model-reduction effects in the finite set

\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
. Accordingly, the total approximation error is
\bar{\delta}_{k}:=\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{disc}}+\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{samp}}
.

The discretization error controls the ideal neighborhood-based discrete-gradient
set in the sense that

V_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})\subset\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})+B(0,\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{disc}}),

provided that the pointwise discrete-gradient approximation error is uniform over
the ball
\{\mathbf{y}:\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}\}
. The finite sampled model is
assumed to satisfy

\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\subset V_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})+B(0,\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{samp}}).

In the exact finite-sampling case considered in (
2.21
), this
relation holds with
\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{samp}}=0
. A positive

\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{samp}}
allows for additional finite-sampling, inexact
construction, or model-reduction errors. Combining the two relations yields
the finite-sample approximation property

\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\subset\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})+B(0,\bar{\delta}_{k}).

(2.23)

Thus,
\bar{\delta}_{k}
should be interpreted as the total model approximation
error between the finite computable discrete-gradient model

\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
and the Goldstein subdifferential model

\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
.

The overall approximation quality is governed by

\varepsilon_{k}
,
\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{disc}}
, and

\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{samp}}
. We assume that

\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{disc}}\to 0,\quad\text{and}\quad\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{samp}}\to 0,

as
\lambda_{k}\to 0
and the sampling/model construction becomes sufficiently
accurate. In statements involving the finite sampled set
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
,
we use the total error
\bar{\delta}_{k}
, while results concerning the ideal
neighborhood-based discrete-gradient construction

V_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})
involve only the discretization error

\delta_{k}^{\mathrm{disc}}
.

Consequently, if
\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))}\|\mathbf{v}\|\to 0
,
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
,
\bar{\delta}_{k}\to 0
, then
\mathop{\rm dist}(0,\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\to 0
. Indeed, by (
2.23
), for every

\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
there exists

\mathbf{w}_{k}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
such that

\|\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{w}_{k}\|\leq\bar{\delta}_{k}.

Hence,

\mathop{\rm dist}(0,\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\leq\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|+\bar{\delta}_{k}.

(2.24)

Taking
\mathbf{v}_{k}
as a minimal-norm element of

\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
gives the assertion. Therefore, if there exists a subsequence
K\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

\mathbf{x}_{k}\to\mathbf{x}^{\ast},\quad\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))}\|\mathbf{v}\|\to 0,\quad\varepsilon_{k}\to 0,\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\delta}_{k}\to 0\quad(k\in K),

then the standard outer-semicontinuity argument for the Clarke
subdifferential yields
0\in\partial f(\mathbf{x}^{\ast})
, so that
\mathbf{x}^{\ast}
is a Clarke stationary. If, instead, only a uniform bound
\bar{\delta}_{k}\leq\bar{\delta}
is available, then exact Clarke stationarity cannot be concluded in general.
The conclusion is only approximate Goldstein–Clarke stationarity, with a
stationarity residual controlled by
\bar{\delta}
.

2.6
Minimal-Norm Approximation and Quadratic Programming Formulation

Approximate minimal-norm problem.

In view of the inclusion (
2.22
), we consider the problem of computing the minimal-norm element
of
\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
as a computable surrogate of the
minimal-norm element of
\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
.

Minimal-norm element.

Let
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})=\{\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{m}\}
and define the matrix

V_{k}:=\big[\mathbf{g}_{1}\ \mathbf{g}_{2}\ \cdots\ \mathbf{g}_{m}\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}.

Any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
can be written as
\mathbf{v}=V_{k}{\bf p}
, where
{\bf p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfies

\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}p_{\ell}=1,\qquad p_{\ell}\geq 0.

The vector
\mathbf{v}_{k}
is the projection of the origin onto
\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
:

\mathbf{v}_{k}=\operatorname{proj}_{\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))}(0),

(2.25)

and
\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|
represents the distance from the origin to the sampled approximation of the subdifferential. Since
\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
is a nonempty compact convex set, the
projection of the origin (
2.25
) onto this set exists and is unique. Hence, the problem

\mathbf{v}_{k}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))}\|\mathbf{v}\|

(2.26)

admits a unique solution
\mathbf{v}_{k}=V_{k}{\bf p}^{\ast}
. However, the coefficient vector
{\bf p}^{\ast}
solving the simplex-constrained quadratic program need not be unique when the columns of
V_{k}
are linearly dependent.

Finding the approximate minimal-norm element (
2.26
) is equivalent to the convex quadratic program

\min_{{\bf p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\frac{1}{2}\|V_{k}{\bf p}\|^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}p_{\ell}=1,\qquad p_{\ell}\geq 0.

(2.27)

Using
\|V_{k}{\bf p}\|_{2}^{2}={\bf p}^{\top}V_{k}^{\top}V_{k}{\bf p}
, we define the Gram matrix

G_{k}:=V_{k}^{\top}V_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}.

Equivalently, with this
G_{k}
, the problem (
2.27
) can be written as

\min_{{\bf p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\frac{1}{2}{\bf p}^{\top}G_{k}{\bf p}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}p_{\ell}=1,\quad p_{\ell}\geq 0,

which is a convex quadratic program on the probability simplex. Let
{\bf p}^{\ast}
denote an optimal solution of (
2.27
). The approximate minimal-norm element is
\mathbf{v}_{k}=V_{k}{\bf p}^{\ast}
. By (
2.22
),
\mathbf{v}_{k}
can be interpreted as an approximate minimal-norm element of
\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
, up to an error of order
\delta_{k}
.

2.7
Approximate Descent Directions and Existing Approaches

Three-layer structure.

The proposed framework can be decomposed into three conceptual layers:

(i)

Sampling layer:
discrete gradients are computed along directions
\mathbf{d}\in D
with
\|\mathbf{d}\|=1
to construct a finite set
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
;

(ii)

Model layer:
the convex hull
\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
is used as a computable finite sampled model associated with the Goldstein subdifferential
\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
;

(iii)

Optimization layer:
a minimal-norm stationarity vector
\mathbf{v}_{k}
is computed by solving (
2.26
).

The normalization constraint
\|\mathbf{d}\|=1
is required only in the sampling layer and does not restrict the optimization layer.

Approximate descent directions.

For a locally Lipschitz function
f
, the computation of a descent direction can be formulated via the minimal-norm element of the Goldstein
\varepsilon
-subdifferential:

\widehat{\mathbf{v}}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\mathbf{v}\in\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x})}\|\mathbf{v}\|.

(2.28)

If
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}\neq 0
, the corresponding
\varepsilon
-steepest descent direction is
\mathbf{d}_{s}:=-\widehat{\mathbf{v}}/\|\widehat{\mathbf{v}}\|
. However, solving (
2.28
) is generally impractical in derivative-free settings, since it requires full knowledge of
\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x})
. To overcome this limitation, discrete approximations can be employed. Let
V(\mathbf{x},\lambda)
denote a discrete-gradient set. It is known (see
[
8
, Proposition 5.4]
) that if

\mathbf{v}^{\lambda}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\mathbf{v}\in V(\mathbf{x},\lambda)}\|\mathbf{v}\|\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{v}^{\lambda}\neq 0,

then the normalized direction
\mathbf{d}^{\lambda}:=-\mathbf{v}^{\lambda}/\|\mathbf{v}^{\lambda}\|
is a descent direction at
\mathbf{x}
. This guarantee relies on access to the full set
V(\mathbf{x},\lambda)
.

In practice, only a finite subset is available, and the descent property may be degraded due to approximation errors.

A related strategy based on subgradient sampling has been proposed by Maleknia and Soleimani–Damaneh
[
30
]
. They construct an inner approximation
\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}))\subset\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x})
, and compute

\widehat{\mathbf{u}}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}))}\|\mathbf{u}\|.

(2.29)

The direction
\overline{\mathbf{d}}_{s}:=-\widehat{\mathbf{u}}/\|\widehat{\mathbf{u}}\|
approximates the
\varepsilon
-steepest descent direction and admits a guaranteed enrichment mechanism based on exact subgradients.

Discrete-gradient-based directions.

In the present work, we adopt a similar minimal-norm framework, but replace exact subgradients by discrete-gradient approximations. Specifically, we construct a finite set
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
using only function evaluations and compute
\mathbf{v}_{k}
by (
2.26
).

A natural reference direction associated with the minimal-norm model is
-\mathbf{v}_{k}
. Indeed, since

\mathbf{v}_{k}
is computed by (
2.26
), the vector
-\mathbf{v}_{k}
may be interpreted as a computable approximation of an
\varepsilon
-steepest descent direction induced by the sampled
subdifferential model. Due to the approximation property (
2.23
), that is

\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\subset\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})+B(0,\bar{\delta}_{k}),

the vector
\mathbf{v}_{k}
provides approximate first-order information about
the local behavior of
f
. In particular, when the approximation error

\bar{\delta}_{k}
is sufficiently small, one heuristically expects
descent-oriented behavior along directions sufficiently aligned with

-\mathbf{v}_{k}
.

However, in the proposed framework, the vector
-\mathbf{v}_{k}
is not imposed
as the actual search direction. Instead,
\mathbf{v}_{k}
primarily serves as a
computable stationarity and orientation measure derived from the
approximate subdifferential model. The search direction may then be
generated using additional algorithmic mechanisms that exploit
information accumulated across iterations, while preserving suitable
descent properties relative to
\mathbf{v}_{k}
.

This separation between the approximation model and the direction
generation mechanism is fundamental in the derivative-free setting.
It allows the incorporation of memory-based or curvature-enhanced
search directions without modifying the underlying stationarity
framework. In particular, Section
3
introduces matrix
conjugate subgradient directions constructed from the vectors

\{\mathbf{v}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
together with previous search information.

3
Matrix Conjugate Subgradient Method

In this section, we extend the matrix parametric approach
(described in the supplemental material of the present paper
[
31
, Section 1]
) to a nonsmooth setting by employing
discrete-gradient approximations of the Goldstein

\varepsilon
-subdifferential
[
8
,
27
,
30
]
. The resulting framework
is embedded into a globalization mechanism designed to promote stability
and convergence toward Clarke stationary points while remaining entirely
Hessian-free
[
5
,
15
,
17
]
.

In pursuit of flexibility, diversity, and generality in optimization algorithms, we here employ a matrix-based formulation of the
CG
parameter in our algorithmic framework, as opposed to the traditional scalar parameter settings. This extended approach enhances a conjugacy factor, called the degree of conjugacy, by integrating additional recent search directions into the method, while ensuring global convergence via suitable safeguard mechanisms.

As is well-known from classical literature, to solve the unconstrained optimization problem (
2.1
),
CG
iterations are recursively updated by

\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\mathbf{x}_{k+1}:=\mathbf{x}_{k}+\mathbf{s}_{k},\qquad\mathbf{s}_{k}:=t_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\texttt{CG}},

(3.1)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, in which
t_{k}\in(0,+\infty)
is the step size computed via a
LiS
technique along the (descent) direction
\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\texttt{CG}}
, successively generated by

\mathbf{d}_{0}^{\texttt{CG}}:=-\nabla f_{0},\qquad\mathbf{d}_{k+1}^{\texttt{CG}}:=-\nabla f_{k+1}+\beta_{k}^{\texttt{CG}}\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\texttt{CG}},\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(3.2)

with
\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
referred to as the
CG
parameter.

To the best of our knowledge, the computational performance of
CG
methods is strongly influenced by the particular choice of the parameter
\beta_{k}

[
2
]
. Existing analytical developments aimed at improving the theoretical properties or numerical performance of classical
CG
algorithms have largely been based on scalar parameterizations
[
6
,
20
]
, or, at most, on vector-based extensions
[
36
]
, which may be regarded as “one-dimensional” improvement strategies. In this sense, the current literature reveals a notable lack of analytical motivation for multidimensional generalizations. As previously mentioned, such extensions can offer several advantages, including increased flexibility of
CG
algorithms, the incorporation of richer theoretical structures, and, ultimately, improved robustness of the resulting methods.

Here, we present a general formulation for parameterizing the
CG
direction (
3.2
) in a matrix-based framework. We begin by formally replacing the scalar parameter
\beta_{k}
with
\beta_{k}\mathbf{I}
, where
\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denotes the identity matrix. To further enhance the dimensionality of the parametric adjustment—and consequently increase the flexibility of the algorithm—we extend this formulation by introducing a matrix parameter
\bm{\ss}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
in place of
\beta_{k}\mathbf{I}
. Accordingly, the search direction (
3.2
) is reformulated as

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{0}^{\texttt{CG}}:=-\nabla f_{0},\qquad\mathbf{d}_{k+1}^{\texttt{CG}}:=-\nabla f_{k+1}+\bm{\ss}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\texttt{CG}},\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(3.3)

It is worth noting, however, that employing a dense matrix
\bm{\ss}_{k}
in (
3.3
) may incur significant computational overhead, particularly for large-scale problems. To maintain computational efficiency, it is therefore natural and practical to impose sparsity on
\bm{\ss}_{k}
, or alternatively, to adopt a limited-memory form of this matrix.

To ensure the global convergence of the corresponding
CG
algorithm, it is necessary to impose appropriate safeguards on the search direction formula given by (
3.3
). These include preserving the SDC and ensuring that the sequence
\{\|\bm{\ss}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}\|\}_{k\geq 0}
remains uniformly bounded
[
34
]
. Such conditions can, for instance, be enforced through suitable restart schemes
[
13
]
. On the other hand, determining an appropriate sparsity structure for the matrix
\bm{\ss}_{k}
, as well as devising an effective strategy for computing its entries based on key properties of limited-memory algorithms, is of significant importance. In this regard, one possible approach is outlined in
[
31
, Section 1]
, where
\bm{\ss}_{k}
is first modeled as a banded tridiagonal matrix, and its entries are subsequently determined by exploiting more recent search directions to enhance conjugacy.

Now, we discuss how the proposed
CG
direction can be incorporated into our conjugate subgradient algorithm. To proceed, first by replacing
\nabla f_{k}
in (
3.3
) by
\mathbf{v}_{k}
defined by solving (
2.26
), we define the
MatCSG
direction as

\mathbf{d}_{0}^{\texttt{MCS}}:=-\mathbf{v}_{0},\qquad\mathbf{d}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}:=-\mathbf{v}_{k+1}+\bm{\ss}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}},

(3.4)

where
\bm{\ss}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a sparse matrix parameter as already introduced (see also Section 1 in
[
31
]
). Accordingly, we define the subgradient displacement vector as follows by

\mathbf{q}_{k}:=\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k}.

Unlike classical
CG
methods, where
\nabla f_{k}
represents exact first-order information, the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{k}
here are approximate subgradient models constructed from sampled discrete gradients. Consequently, the term
\bm{\ss}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}
should be interpreted as a heuristic memory mechanism that enriches the search direction using past information, rather than enforcing exact conjugacy.

Since
f
is locally Lipschitz, the exact first-order directional behavior is characterized by the Clarke directional derivative

f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})=\max_{\mathbf{g}\in\partial f(\mathbf{x})}\mathbf{g}^{\top}\mathbf{d}.

However, in the present framework, the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{k}
are computed from sampled discrete gradients and therefore only provide approximate first-order information. Consequently, the directional quantity

\mathbf{v}_{k+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}

cannot, in general, be identified with the exact directional derivative. To establish sufficient decrease, it is therefore necessary to relate the exact Clarke directional derivative to the approximate directional model induced by
\mathbf{v}_{k+1}
. This is achieved through the directional consistency estimate introduced below, which bounds the discrepancy between the exact subdifferential model and the approximate discrete-gradient model along the search direction.

The following result is a direct Euclidean analogue of

[
32
, Proposition 3.1]
. Its proof follows the same algebraic
argument after identifying
g_{I}
with
\mathbf{v}_{k+1}
,
q_{I}
with

\mathbf{d}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}
,
B_{II}
with the identity matrix, and
\delta

with
\varrho
.

Theorem 1

(Angle enforcement and descent property of the
MatCSG
direction)

Let
\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\neq 0
be defined by
(
2.26
), where
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})
is the discrete-gradient sample defined by (
2.20
). Let
\mathbf{d}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
be a search direction and define

\omega_{1,k+1}:=\|\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\|^{2},\qquad\omega_{2,k+1}:=\|\mathbf{d}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}\|^{2},\qquad\omega_{k+1}:=\mathbf{v}_{k+1}^{\top}\mathbf{d}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}.

(3.5)

Moreover, let
\varrho\in(0,1)
and define

c_{k+1}:=\frac{\omega_{k+1}}{\sqrt{\omega_{1,k+1}\omega_{2,k+1}}}\in[-1,1],\qquad w_{k+1}:=\frac{\omega_{1,k+1}\omega_{2,k+1}(1-c_{k+1}^{2})}{1-\varrho^{2}}\geq 0.

If
\omega_{k+1}\leq-\varrho\sqrt{\omega_{1,k+1}\omega_{2,k+1}}
, then
\mathbf{d}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}

already satisfies the bounded angle condition and no correction is needed.
Otherwise, the corrected direction

\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}:=\mathbf{d}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}-\varpi_{k+1}\mathbf{v}_{k+1}

(3.6)

is defined, where

\varpi_{k+1}:=\frac{\omega_{k+1}+\varrho\sqrt{w_{k+1}}}{\omega_{1,k+1}}.

(3.7)

Then, the corrected direction satisfies the exact bounded angle condition

\frac{\mathbf{v}_{k+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}}{\|\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\|\,\|\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}\|}=-\varrho<0,

(3.8)

and therefore is a descent direction relative to the approximate first-order model induced by
\mathbf{v}_{k+1}
. Moreover, since

\mathbf{d}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}=-\mathbf{v}_{k+1}+\bm{\ss}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}},

the corrected direction
\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}
may be interpreted as a
safeguarded version of the memory-enhanced direction

\mathbf{d}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}
, obtained by shifting it toward the approximate descent
direction
-\mathbf{v}_{k+1}
until the prescribed bounded angle condition holds.

4
Two-Point Descent Procedure via Discrete Gradients

Throughout this section,
k
denotes the iteration counter of the main
loop of Algorithm
2
, which is introduced in Section
5
. At each outer iteration

2

invokes the two-point
LiS
procedure (Algorithm

The bounded angle condition established in
Theorem

ensures that the corrected direction

\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}
satisfies
\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}<0
, so that
\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}
is descent-oriented with respect to the
approximate subgradient model generated by
\mathbf{v}_{k}
.

Before introducing the two-point
LiS
procedure, we record a
sufficient condition under which the bounded-angle corrected direction
admits an Armijo step. This result clarifies the role of the directional
model error assumption (A5). It is not needed for the finite termination of
Algorithm
1
, which is proved later under the weak upper
semismoothness and directional consistency assumption (A4).

Proposition 3

(Armijo-type decrease for the corrected
MatCSG
direction)

Assume that (A1) and (A5) hold. Let

\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and

\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\neq 0
satisfy the bounded angle condition

\mathbf{v}_{k+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}\leq-\varrho\|\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\|\,\|\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}\|<0,

for some
\varrho\in(0,1)
. Then, for every

\mu_{1}\in(0,1-\theta)
, there exists
t_{k}>0
such that

f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}+t_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}})\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})+\mu_{1}\,t_{k}\,\mathbf{v}_{k+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}.

Proof.

For brevity, set
\mathbf{x}:=\mathbf{x}_{k+1}
,
\mathbf{d}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}
,
\mathbf{v}:=\mathbf{v}_{k+1}
, and
\xi:=\xi_{k+1}
. By (A5), applied with
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{k+1}
,
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{k+1}
, and
\mathbf{d}=\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}
, the directional model error conditions
(
2.18
) and (
2.19
) hold. Hence,
by (
2.19
) and the bounded angle condition, we have

\xi\|\mathbf{d}\|\leq\theta\varrho\|\mathbf{v}\|\,\|\mathbf{d}\|\leq-\theta\,\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}.

Consequently, using the directional error bound (
2.18
), we get

f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})\leq\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}+\xi\|\mathbf{d}\|\leq\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}-\theta\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}=(1-\theta)\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}<0.

Choosing any
\mu_{1}\in(0,1-\theta)
and since
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}<0
, we get

f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})\leq(1-\theta)\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}<\mu_{1}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d},

so that
f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})<\mu_{1}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}
. By the definition of the Clarke directional derivative,

\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{d})-f(\mathbf{x})}{t}\leq f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d}).

Therefore, there exists
\bar{t}>0
such that, for all
t\in(0,\bar{t}]
,

\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{d})-f(\mathbf{x})}{t}\leq f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})+\frac{\gamma}{2}<\mu_{1}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d},

where
\gamma:=\mu_{1}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}-f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})>0
. Multiplying by
t>0
gives

f(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{d})<f(\mathbf{x})+\mu_{1}t\,\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}.

Taking any
t\in(0,\bar{t}]
proves the claim.
∎

Remark 5

(On the error condition for Armijo decrease)

The directional model error condition (
2.18
) together with (
2.19
) ensures that the directional approximation error is sufficiently small
relative to the predicted descent along the corrected direction

\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}
. Indeed, in view of the bounded angle condition (
3.8
), we have

|\mathbf{v}_{k+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}|\geq\varrho\|\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\|\,\|\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}\|.

Therefore, (
2.19
) implies
\xi_{k+1}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}\|\leq\theta|\mathbf{v}_{k+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}|
. Thus, the true Clarke directional derivative remains negative and is
controlled by the predicted model decrease.

The admissible directional error level scales proportionally with

\|\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\|
. In particular, as
\|\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\|\to 0
, the allowable
directional error must also vanish, ensuring that the model accuracy
increases near stationary points.

From a computational perspective, this requirement is consistent with the
discrete-gradient framework, since the directional model error can be
reduced by refining the discretization parameter, enriching the direction
set, or improving the finite sampled model.

Moreover, within the two-point
LiS
procedure, failure of the
sufficient decrease condition triggers the generation of additional
discrete gradients. This potentially improves the directional approximation
quality and facilitates satisfaction of the above condition.

Corollary 1

(Descent implies Armijo admissibility)

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1
, let
\mathbf{v}_{k+1}

be defined by (
2.26
). Assume that the directional
model error satisfies (
2.18
), where
\xi_{k+1}\geq 0
. Suppose that there exists
\theta\in(0,1)
such
that (
2.19
) holds. Then, for every
\mu_{1}\in(0,1-\theta)
, the corrected direction

\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}
admits a step size
t_{k}>0
such that

f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}+t_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}})\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})+\mu_{1}\,t_{k}\,\mathbf{v}_{k+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}.

Proof.

By Theorem
1
, the corrected direction

\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k+1}^{\texttt{MCS}}
satisfies the bounded angle condition (
3.8
). The directional model error bound (
2.18
) and the relative directional model-error condition (
2.19
) are precisely the remaining assumptions of
Proposition

. Therefore, the claimed
Armijo-type decrease follows directly from
Proposition
3
.
∎

The descent subgradient method of Maleknia and Soleimani–Damaneh

[
30
]
employs a two-point Mifflin
LiS
procedure, called
DG-TPLiS
, that either produces a serious step satisfying a sufficient decrease
condition or generates a new subgradient that enriches the approximation
of the Goldstein
\varepsilon
-subdifferential. The key feature of that
procedure is the use of two step lengths:

•

t_{i}
, which is kept inside
(0,\varepsilon)
and is used to generate
new subgradient information;

•

\bar{t}_{i}
, which is used to test whether a serious step satisfying
sufficient decrease can be accepted.

In the present derivative-free setting, exact subgradients are unavailable.
Therefore, they are replaced by approximate subgradients computed by the
discrete-gradient construction. Moreover, instead of using the normalized
minimal-norm subgradient direction, the search direction is generated by
the
MatCSG
formula (
3.4
) and then corrected by
(
3.6
)–(
3.7
) to enforce a bounded angle condition.

The
DG-TPLiS
mechanism below follows the two-point structure of

[
30
]
. The only modification in the interval update is that
the next trial point
t_{i+1}
is selected as the geometric mean of the two
contracted endpoints. This is a particular admissible interior choice and
does not affect the interval contraction argument.

The sufficient decrease condition used in the
DG-TPLiS
algorithm is

f(\mathbf{x}+t\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}})-f(\mathbf{x})\leq\mu_{1}t\,\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}},

where
\mu_{1}\in(0,1)
and

\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}<0
. The enrichment condition is

\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}\geq\mu_{2}\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}},\qquad 0<\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<1.

Since
\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}<0
, the enrichment condition identifies
a discrete gradient that is less aligned with the current descent model
than
\mathbf{v}_{k}
and therefore provides new information for improving the
convex approximation of the Goldstein subdifferential.

Normalization and practical scaling.

Throughout Algorithm
1
, the corrected
MatCSG

direction is normalized before it is passed to the
DG-TPLiS
. More
precisely, after the correction step (
3.6
)–(
3.7
), we
use the direction

\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}:=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}\|}.

(4.1)

Since the bounded angle condition is invariant under positive scaling,
(
4.1
) preserves the descent orientation:

\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}\leq-\varrho\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|.

For practical implementations, one may instead use a positively scaled
direction

\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}:=\alpha\,\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}},\qquad\alpha>0,

(4.2)

where
\alpha
is fixed independently of
k
. This scaling does not change
the angle, since

\frac{\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}}{\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|\,\|\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}\|}=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}}{\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|\,\|\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}\|}.

Moreover, if the
DG-TPLiS
accepts only steps satisfying

t_{k}\geq\underline{t}>0,

(4.3)

then the accepted displacement satisfies

t_{k}\|\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}\|=\alpha t_{k}\geq\alpha\underline{t}.

Thus, in the scaled implementation, every successful step has displacement
bounded below by the positive constant
\alpha\underline{t}
. Consequently,
the finite-termination arguments remain valid with
\underline{\tau}=\alpha\underline{t}
. More generally, the same conclusion holds for variable scalings
\alpha_{k}

provided that

0<\alpha_{\min}\leq\alpha_{k}\leq\alpha_{\max}<\infty.

In the theoretical statements below, we use the normalized convention
(
4.1
); the scaled version
(
4.2

Lemma 1

(Interval contraction and limit properties)

t\mapsto f(\mathbf{x}_{k}+t\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}})
is continuous. Passing to the limit along

gives

f(\mathbf{x}_{k}+t^{\ast}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\leq\mu_{1}t^{\ast}\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}.

Thus
t^{\ast}\in T
.

(iii)

Let
\mathcal{I}:=\{\,i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:t_{i+1}^{u}=t_{i}\,\}
. We first prove that
\mathcal{I}\neq\emptyset
. Suppose, by contradiction,
that
\mathcal{I}=\emptyset
. Then the upper endpoint is never updated,
and therefore the sufficient decrease condition at
t_{i}
holds for all

i\geq 0
. In particular,

f(\mathbf{x}_{k}+t_{0}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\leq\mu_{1}t_{0}\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}.

By the definition of the auxiliary sequence,

\bar{t}_{p}=\left[\exp\left(\frac{\log t_{0}}{p}\right)\right]^{p}=t_{0}.

Since
t_{0}>\underline{t}
, the successful-step test is satisfied at
iteration
p
, which contradicts the assumption that the algorithm does
not terminate. Hence
\mathcal{I}\neq\emptyset
.

Now suppose that
\mathcal{I}
is finite. Since upper endpoint updates occur
only finitely many times, there exists
\bar{i}
such that
t_{i}^{u}
is
constant for all
i\geq\bar{i}
. By part (ii),
t_{i}^{u}\downarrow t^{\ast}
; therefore this constant must be
t^{\ast}
, and hence
t_{i}^{u}=t^{\ast}
, for all
i\geq\bar{i}
. Because
\mathcal{I}\neq\emptyset
and is finite, let
j:=\max\mathcal{I}
. Then, by the definition of
\mathcal{I}
,
t_{j+1}^{u}=t_{j}
. Since no upper endpoint updates occur after index
j
, the sequence

\{t_{i}^{u}\}
is constant for all
i\geq j+1
. By part (ii), we also have
t_{i}^{u}\downarrow t^{\ast}
. Therefore, this constant value must be
t^{\ast}
, and hence
t_{j}=t_{j+1}^{u}=t^{\ast}
.
Moreover, because the upper endpoint was updated at index
j
, the
sufficient decrease condition failed at
t_{j}
, namely,

f(\mathbf{x}_{k}+t_{j}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})>\mu_{1}t_{j}\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}.

Since
t_{j}=t^{\ast}
, this contradicts
t^{\ast}\in T
. Therefore

\mathcal{I}
must be infinite.
∎

The previous lemma is purely algorithmic: it describes the contraction of
the trial interval and the limiting behavior of the two-point
DG-TPLiS

sequence under the assumption of nontermination. We now use this structure,
together with the directional consistency condition in (A4), to prove finite
termination of Algorithm
1
. Notice that the Armijo
admissibility result in Proposition
3
is not
used in this proof. That proposition gives a sufficient local condition for
existence of an Armijo step under the directional model error assumption
(A5), whereas the finite termination result below relies instead on the
enrichment mechanism and the weak upper semismoothness/directional
consistency condition (A4).

Theorem 2

(Finite termination of
DG-TPLiS
)

Suppose that (A1) and (A4) hold.
Assume that Algorithm
1
is applied with

\varepsilon\in(0,1),\qquad\underline{t}\in(0,\varepsilon),\qquad p\in\mathbb{N},\qquad 0<\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<1.

Let
\mathbf{d}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}
,
q:=\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{d}<0
,
I=I_{k}
,
\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{s}_{k}
,
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{k}
, and
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{k}
Then, Algorithm
1
terminates after finitely many inner iterations. More precisely, it returns either

I=1\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{s}=\mathbf{x}+\bar{t}_{i}\mathbf{d}\quad\text{with}\quad f(\mathbf{x}+\bar{t}_{i}\mathbf{d})-f(\mathbf{x})\leq\mu_{1}\bar{t}_{i}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d},\qquad\bar{t}_{i}\geq\underline{t},

or it returns
I=0
and
\mathfrak{s}=\mathbf{g}_{i}
with
\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{d}\geq\mu_{2}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}
.

Proof.

Assume, by contradiction, that Algorithm
1
does not
terminate. Let
\mathcal{I}
be the infinite index set from
Lemma
1
(iii). For every
i\in\mathcal{I}
, the upper
endpoint is updated, and hence the sufficient decrease condition at

t_{i}
fails:

f(\mathbf{x}+t_{i}\mathbf{d})-f(\mathbf{x})>\mu_{1}t_{i}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}.

By Lemma
1
(ii),
t_{i}\to t^{\ast}
and
t^{\ast}\in T
, so
f(\mathbf{x}_{k}+t^{\ast}\mathbf{d})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\leq\mu_{1}t^{\ast}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}
. Combining the last two inequalities gives, for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
,

f(\mathbf{x}+t_{i}\mathbf{d})-f(\mathbf{x}+t^{\ast}\mathbf{d})>\mu_{1}(t_{i}-t^{\ast})\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}.

Since
i\in\mathcal{I}
implies
t_{i}=t_{i+1}^{u}
, and since

t_{i+1}^{u}>t^{\ast}
for every finite nonterminal index, we have
t_{i}>t^{\ast}
. Define
h_{i}:=t_{i}-t^{\ast}>0
and
z:=\mathbf{x}+t^{\ast}\mathbf{d}
. Then
h_{i}\downarrow 0
along
\mathcal{I}
, and

\frac{f(z+h_{i}\mathbf{d})-f(z)}{h_{i}}>\mu_{1}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}=\mu_{1}q,\qquad i\in\mathcal{I}.

Therefore,
\displaystyle\liminf_{i\in\mathcal{I},\ i\to\infty}\frac{f(z+h_{i}\mathbf{d})-f(z)}{h_{i}}\geq\mu_{1}q
. By the directional consistency condition (
2.17
) in (A4),

\limsup_{i\in\mathcal{I},\ i\to\infty}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{d}\geq\mu_{1}q.

(4.4)

On the other hand, since the algorithm does not terminate through the
enrichment condition, we have
\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{d}<\mu_{2}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{d}=\mu_{2}q
for all
i
. Hence,
\displaystyle\limsup_{i\in\mathcal{I},\ i\to\infty}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{d}\leq\mu_{2}q

and
0<\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<1

. Thus,

\mu_{1}q\overset{\eqref{eq:dgtplis_limsup_gtd_bound}}{\leq}\limsup_{i\in\mathcal{I},\ i\to\infty}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{d}\leq\mu_{2}q<\mu_{1}q,

which is impossible. Therefore, Algorithm

must terminate
after finitely many inner iterations.
∎

Theorem

establishes finite termination of the
inner
DG-TPLiS
procedure. Finite termination of the outer

DG-MatCSG

of the minimal-norm element of the convex hull after enrichment.

5
Computation of an Approximate Goldstein Stationary Point

In this section, we adapt the descent subgradient framework of
Maleknia and Soleimani–Damaneh
[
30
]
to the discrete
gradient setting. The objective is to compute a

(\delta,\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
approximate Goldstein stationary point in the
sense of (
2.6
) using only function
evaluations.

At each iteration
k
, we maintain a finite set
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, consisting of discrete gradients computed at
\mathbf{x}_{k}
and along trial
points generated by the
DG-TPLiS
procedure.

We distinguish between:

•

the
base discrete gradient

\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
used in the
LiS
;

•

the
minimal-norm element

\mathbf{v}_{k}
defined by (
2.26
).

By construction,
\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|\leq\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|
. The complete version of the method is summarized in
Algorithm
2
. Starting from an initial point
\mathbf{x}_{0}
,
a discrete gradient
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{0}
is computed and used to initialize the set

\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, and we set
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{0}):=\{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{0}\}
. At iteration
k
, we compute
\mathbf{v}_{k}
by (
2.26
). If

\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|\leq\delta
, then, by Definition
2.6
, the point

\mathbf{x}_{k}
is a
(\delta,\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
approximate Goldstein stationary point, and the procedure terminates.

Moreover, under the approximation properties
(
2.5
)–(
2.7
), Proposition
1

implies that
\mathbf{x}_{k}
is also an approximate Goldstein stationary point
with respect to
\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
.

Otherwise, a search direction is computed using the
MatCSG

construction (
3.4
). Since the
LiS
uses the scalar product

\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{d}_{k}
, the correction step
(
3.6
)–(
3.7
) is applied with respect to the current
base vector
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
in order to ensure descent with respect to

\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
.

A modified two-point
LiS
procedure,
DG-TPLiS
, is
then applied along
\mathbf{d}_{k}
using
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
. This procedure either
produces a trial point satisfying a sufficient decrease condition or returns an additional discrete gradient that enriches the set

\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
.

If
DG-TPLiS
produces a new point
\mathfrak{s}_{k}

(I_{k}=1)
, we set
\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathfrak{s}_{k}
. A new base discrete gradient
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k+1}
is computed at

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}
, and the new set is initialized as
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})=\{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k+1}\}
. The minimal-norm vector
\mathbf{v}_{k+1}
is then computed from this set at the
next iteration.

Otherwise
(I_{k}=0)
, the current point remains unchanged and the returned
vector is added to the set
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{x}_{k}
,
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})=\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\cup\{\mathfrak{s}_{k}\}
, and
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k+1}:=\mathfrak{s}_{k}
. The iteration counter is then increased and the procedure repeats.

During enrichment iterations
(I_{k}=0)
, the base point remains unchanged
and the search direction is not recomputed. Instead, the current direction
is reused while the set
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
is enriched by additional
discrete gradients. The reused direction is assumed to preserve the bounded angle condition
with respect to all enrichment vectors generated while the base point
remains unchanged.

The enrichment vectors returned by Algorithm
1

are interpreted as approximate subgradients associated with the current
base point through the local approximation properties of the discrete
gradient construction. More precisely, they are computed at trial points
associated with the fixed base point and the current search direction.

In Algorithm
1
, the symbol
\mathbf{v}_{k}
denotes the base vector
passed to
DG-TPLiS
. When the procedure is invoked inside
Algorithm

\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
, not necessarily as the minimal-norm element of

\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
.

Lemma 2

(Finiteness of successful iterations in DG–MatCSG)

Assume that (A1) and (A3) hold. Suppose that the
LiS
directions
satisfy the normalization convention (
4.1
), i.e.,
\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|=1
holds and that every successful step returned by Algorithm

satisfies
t_{k}\geq\underline{t}>0
. Assume that the bounded angle condition holds at every successful
iteration, namely,

\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{d}_{k}\leq-\varrho\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|\,\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|,\qquad\varrho\in(0,1).

Let
A:=\{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:I_{k}=1\}
. If Algorithm

is finite.

Assume by contradiction that

k
, and hence
\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|>\delta
for all

. For each

Using the bounded angle condition and the normalization

\|\mathbf{d}_{k}\|=1
, we obtain

\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{d}_{k}\leq-\varrho\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|.

Moreover, by the successful-step lower bound,
t_{k}\geq\underline{t}>0
. Therefore, for every
k\in A
,

f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\leq-\mu_{1}\varrho\,\underline{t}\,\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|.

Since
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\subset\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
and
\mathbf{v}_{k}
is the minimal-norm element of

\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
, we have
\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|\leq\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|
. Hence, because the algorithm has not terminated,
\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|\geq\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|>\delta
. Consequently, for every
k\in A
,
f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\leq-\mu_{1}\varrho\,\underline{t}\,\delta
. Thus, each successful iteration decreases the objective value by at least
the fixed positive amount
\mu_{1}\varrho\,\underline{t}\,\delta>0
. For enrichment iterations, the base point is unchanged, and therefore
f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})=f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
. If

f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\to-\infty
. On the other hand, all iterates remain in the level set
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
. By (A3), this level set is bounded. Since
f
is locally Lipschitz by
(A1), it is continuous; hence
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
is closed and compact.

is bounded below on
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, which contradicts

f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\to-\infty
. Therefore

must be finite.
∎

(Finite termination of

\|\bar{d}\|=1

terminates in a finite number of

Assume by contradiction that Algorithm

does not

, and hence
\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|>\delta
for all
k
.

2
, the set of successful

\delta_{\nu}\to 0
and
\varepsilon_{\nu}\to 0
. Assume moreover that the generated sequence remains in the initial level
set, that is,
\mathbf{x}_{\nu}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
for all
\nu\geq 0
. Then any accumulation point
\mathbf{x}^{\ast}
of the shifted sequence

\{\mathbf{x}_{\nu+1}\}_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
generated by
Algorithm
3
is a Clarke stationary point of
f
, i.e.,

0\in\partial f(\mathbf{x}^{\ast})
. Consequently, the same conclusion holds for
the accumulation points of
\{\mathbf{x}_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{x}_{\nu}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
for all
\nu\geq 0
and

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
is bounded by (A3), the sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
admits
accumulation points. Let
\mathbf{x}^{\ast}
be an accumulation point of the shifted
sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{\nu+1}\}_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, and let
\mathbf{x}_{\nu_{j}+1}\to\mathbf{x}^{\ast}
. By construction, for each
\nu_{j}
there exists

\mathbf{v}_{\nu_{j}+1}\in\mathop{\rm conv}(\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{x}_{\nu_{j}+1}))

such that
\|\mathbf{v}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|\leq\delta_{\nu_{j}}
. Using the consistency inclusion (
6.1
), there
exists

\mathbf{w}_{\nu_{j}+1}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{\nu_{j}}}f(\mathbf{x}_{\nu_{j}+1})

such that
\|\mathbf{v}_{\nu_{j}+1}-\mathbf{w}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|\leq\bar{\delta}_{\nu_{j}}
. Hence

\|\mathbf{w}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|\leq\|\mathbf{v}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|+\|\mathbf{v}_{\nu_{j}+1}-\mathbf{w}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|\leq\delta_{\nu_{j}}+\bar{\delta}_{\nu_{j}}\to 0.

Since
\mathbf{w}_{\nu_{j}+1}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{\nu_{j}}}f(\mathbf{x}_{\nu_{j}+1})
, by the definition of the Goldstein subdifferential,

\mathbf{w}_{\nu_{j}+1}\in\mathop{\rm cl}\mathop{\rm conv}\left(\bigcup_{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|\leq\varepsilon_{\nu_{j}}}\partial f(\mathbf{y})\right).

Using the closure in the definition of

\partial_{\varepsilon_{\nu_{j}}}f(\mathbf{x}_{\nu_{j}+1})
, for each
j
we may
choose vectors

\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{\nu_{j}+1}\in\mathop{\rm conv}\left(\bigcup_{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|\leq\varepsilon_{\nu_{j}}}\partial f(\mathbf{y})\right)

such that
\|\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{\nu_{j}+1}-\mathbf{w}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|\leq 1/j
. Then

\|\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|\leq\|\mathbf{w}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|+\|\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{\nu_{j}+1}-\mathbf{w}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|\to 0.

By Carathéodory’s theorem, there exist coefficients

\lambda_{i}^{(j)}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{(j)}=1,

points
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(j)}
with
\|\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(j)}-\mathbf{x}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|\leq\varepsilon_{\nu_{j}}
, and subgradients
\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(j)}\in\partial f(\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(j)})
such that

\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{\nu_{j}+1}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{(j)}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(j)}.

Since
\mathbf{x}_{\nu_{j}+1}\to\mathbf{x}^{\ast}
as

\varepsilon_{\nu_{j}}\to 0
, we have
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(j)}\to\mathbf{x}^{\ast}
for each
i=0,\dots,n
. By local boundedness of the Clarke subdifferential near
\mathbf{x}^{\ast}
, the
sequences
\{\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(j)}\}_{j}
are bounded. Passing to a subsequence if
necessary, we may assume

\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(j)}\to\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\ast},\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{i}^{(j)}\to\lambda_{i}^{\ast},

with
\lambda_{i}^{\ast}\geq 0
and

\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{\ast}=1
. By outer semicontinuity of the Clarke subdifferential,

\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\ast}\in\partial f(\mathbf{x}^{\ast}),\qquad i=0,\dots,n.

Passing to the limit in
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{\nu_{j}+1}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{(j)}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(j)}
, and using
\|\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{\nu_{j}+1}\|\to 0
, we obtain
0=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{\ast}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{\ast}
. Since
\partial f(\mathbf{x}^{\ast})
is convex, the convex combination on the
right-hand side belongs to
\partial f(\mathbf{x}^{\ast})
. Hence,
0\in\partial f(\mathbf{x}^{\ast})
; this proves the claim.
∎

7.1
Stopping criteria

To measure the progress of each solver
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we use the relative objective reduction

q_{s}:=\frac{f_{s}-f_{\mathop{\rm opt}}}{f_{0}-f_{\mathop{\rm opt}}},

(7.1)

where
\mathcal{S}
is the set of solvers being compared. Here,
f_{s}
denotes the best objective value produced by solver
s
,
f_{0}
is the objective value at the common initial point, and
f_{\mathop{\rm opt}}
is the best known objective value for the problem. The value
f_{\mathop{\rm opt}}
is typically obtained from the best point found by a collection of local and global derivative-free methods and proposed solvers in the present paper, and may correspond to a global minimizer or to a high-quality local minimizer. Thus,
q_{s}
is used only as a benchmarking metric; in practical applications,
f_{\mathop{\rm opt}}
is usually not available.

Solver
s
is said to solve a problem if

q_{s}\leq\varepsilon

before either of the prescribed computational limits is reached, namely the maximum number of function evaluations
nfmax
or the maximum CPU time
secmax
. If this condition is not satisfied within these limits, the problem is classified as unsolved for that solver.

The parameters
\varepsilon
,
nfmax
, and
secmax
are selected to make the comparison informative: they are chosen so that the strongest solver solves at least half of the test set, except when the noise level is sufficiently large that increasing the evaluation or time budget no longer yields a meaningful improvement in robustness or efficiency. In the numerical experiments, we used

\texttt{secmax}=600\penalty\ \text{sec},\quad\texttt{nfmax}=2\times 10^{6},\quad\varepsilon\in\{10^{-6},10^{-4},10^{-2}\},\quad\text{and}\quad 2\leq n\leq 10^{3}.

7.2
Diagonal scaling from previously accepted best points

We use a bounded diagonal scaling vector computed from recently obtained
best points. Let
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}_{k}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}^{(r_{k})}
with
r_{k}\leq m_{s}
denote the stored best points, ordered according to their objective values,
with
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{(1)}
being the best one. Here
m_{s}
is the prescribed
memory size used for the scaling archive;
m_{s}=3
is used for the current comparison. If fewer than two best points are
available, we set
\mathbf{s}_{k}=\mathbf{1}
. Otherwise, for each coordinate

j=1,\ldots,n
, we compute

\bar{s}_{k,j}=\displaystyle\mathop{\rm median}_{\ell=2,\ldots,r_{k}}\left|x_{k,j}^{(\ell)}-x_{k,j}^{(1)}\right|.

Nonfinite and zero entries are replaced by one. The scaling vector is then
clipped componentwise as

s_{k,j}=\min\left\{s_{\max},\max\left\{s_{\min},\bar{s}_{k,j}\right\}\right\},\qquad j=1,\ldots,n,

where
0<s_{\min}\leq s_{\max}<\infty
. Thus, with
S_{k}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_{k})
, the matrix
S_{k}
is uniformly
bounded and uniformly nonsingular.

In the implementation, after computing the
MatCSG
direction

\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}
by (
3.4
), we apply the diagonal scaling before the
bounded-angle correction. More precisely,
S_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\texttt{MCS}}
is used as the
input direction in the correction step (
3.6
)–(
3.7
).
The resulting corrected direction is then normalized according to
(
4.1
) before being passed to the two-point
line-search procedure.

This order is essential. Unlike scalar positive scaling, diagonal scaling can
change the angle between the search direction and
\mathbf{v}_{k}
. Therefore, the
scaling is applied before the bounded-angle correction, while the final
direction used in the line search still satisfies the bounded-angle condition
required in the analysis. Consequently, the line-search and finite-termination
arguments remain unchanged.

8
Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed a derivative-free matrix conjugate-subgradient framework for unconstrained nonsmooth optimization of locally Lipschitz functions. The method uses discrete gradients to construct a finite sampled model of the Goldstein subdifferential and computes a minimal-norm element of the convex hull of these sampled vectors. This vector provides both a practical stationarity measure and a reference direction for constructing descent-oriented search directions. The main algorithmic novelty is the introduction of a derivative-free matrix conjugate-subgradient direction.

This direction extends the basic discrete-gradient steepest-descent direction by incorporating memory from previous iterations through a structured matrix correction. Since the available first-order information is only approximate, several safeguards were introduced, including coefficient damping, dominance control, diagonal scaling, matrix-stability checks, and bounded-angle correction. These mechanisms make the memory-enhanced direction more stable and robust in a fully derivative-free nonsmooth setting.

We also adapted exact-subgradient sampling and enrichment ideas to the approximate-subgradient setting by replacing exact subgradients with discrete-gradient approximations computed only from function values. The two-point line-search procedure was designed to either accept a serious step or enrich the local sampled model, thereby improving robustness without requiring analytical derivatives or active-index information. Under the stated consistency and directional assumptions, the finite sampled model is consistent with Goldstein-type stationarity, and the safeguarded search directions satisfy the required descent-oriented properties.

The numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed variants are more robust
than the benchmark DFON solvers on the tested problem
collections. For the lower and medium accuracy requirements, both proposed
variants solve the largest fraction of problems within the prescribed
function-evaluation and time budgets. Under the strictest tolerance,

\varepsilon=10^{-6}
, the advantage of the matrix conjugate-subgradient variant
becomes more pronounced:
DG-Clarke-MatCSGdir
remains the most reliable
solver, whereas the steepest-descent variant and the benchmark solvers plateau
at substantially lower solved fractions. Overall, the results show that the
matrix memory mechanism improves robustness as the accuracy requirement becomes
more demanding.

Future work will focus on reducing the cost of discrete-gradient sampling for large-scale problems, developing adaptive rules for locality and sampling parameters, and extending the proposed framework to constrained nonsmooth optimization problems.
