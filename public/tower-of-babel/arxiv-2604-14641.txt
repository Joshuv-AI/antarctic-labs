Learning to Draw ASCII Improves Spatial Reasoning in Language Models
Shiyuan Huang, Li Liu, Jincheng He, Leilani H. Gilpin

Abstract
When faced with complex spatial problems, humans naturally sketch layouts to organize their thinking, and the act of drawing further sharpens their understanding. In this work, we ask whether a similar principle holds for Large Language Models (LLMs): can learning to construct explicit visual layouts from spatial descriptions instill genuine spatial understanding? We introduce Text2Space , a dataset that pairs natural language descriptions with ground-truth ASCII grid layouts and spatial QA pairs, enabling us to separate failures in constructing spatial representations from failures in reasoning over them. We adopt ASCII because it is human-readable, operates entirely within the token space of language models, and encodes spatial relations in a structurally verifiable form. Our evaluation reveals a pronounced “Read-Write Asymmetry”: LLMs interpret ASCII representations effectively but struggle to produce them from text, and these construction errors propagate to incorrect answers downstream. To address this limitation, we train models on layout construction (Text → \rightarrow ASCII) and find that it significantly improves spatial reasoning from text alone, even without producing any ASCII at inference time. Combining construction with comprehension training further amplifies these gains. Crucially, these improvements transfer to three external spatial reasoning benchmarks, demonstrating that, much as sketching sharpens human spatial thinking, learning to construct explicit layouts instills spatial understanding that generalizes beyond the training format.

Spatial reasoning

However, existing benchmarks are limited to evaluating final answers

More critically, even correct answers may reflect surface-level pattern matching rather than robust spatial understanding. A model can exploit lexical cues in simple configurations yet fail unpredictably as spatial complexity grows. Prior work on faithful reasoning has shown that correct answers produced through unreliable intermediate steps offer no guarantee of consistent behavior on novel problems

To open this black box, we use ASCII grid layouts as an explicit, structured text that makes the reasoning process itself observable. The ASCII representation is well suited as this diagnostic lens because it lives within the model’s own token space, is immediately interpretable by humans, and can be formally verified against ground truth. We introduce
Text2Space
, a benchmark of diverse spatial configurations paired with natural language descriptions, images, and three styles of ASCII grids. Each instance includes a ground-truth layout, providing verifiable supervision for both diagnosing representation failures and training models toward robust spatial understanding.

Figure
1
outlines our study design, which explores two processes between natural language and ASCII: construction turns text into layouts, and comprehension reads spatial relations from them. Using
Text2Space
, we contribute three investigations. First, we
benchmark
language models on ASCII comprehension, generation, and reasoning. Second, we examine the
reasoning process
by assessing whether ASCII representations faithfully support model reasoning. Most importantly, we investigate whether the spatial understanding acquired through ASCII-based learning
generalizes
to purely linguistic spatial reasoning tasks.

Our evaluation yields three principal findings that progressively identify a representation bottleneck (what we call the
construction gap
) and show how to mitigate it.
Finding 1: Models can understand spatial layouts but struggle to generate them.

Across all models, comprehending ASCII diagrams is substantially easier than constructing them from text, identifying construction as the primary constraint.

Finding 2: This construction gap directly harms reasoning.

When models sketch layouts as an intermediate step, their construction errors propagate to the final answer. Yet when accurate ASCII is provided instead, reasoning improves substantially over text alone, showing that the bottleneck is not reasoning itself but the quality of the representation it operates over.

Finding 3: Training on building construction reduces the asymmetry and the benefits generalize.
Training models on layout construction improves spatial reasoning from text alone, even without producing any ASCII at inference time. Comprehension training alone does not consistently improve performance, but it leads to gains when combined with construction, producing the strongest overall results. These gains transfer to three external benchmarks, confirming that construction training builds transferable spatial understanding rather than format-specific heuristics.

We present three contributions: (1) a benchmark of spatial configurations paired with ground-truth ASCII layouts, enabling evaluation that separates representation failures from reasoning failures; (2) identification of the
construction gap
, showing that models comprehend spatial layouts more reliably than they construct them and that this asymmetry propagates into downstream reasoning; and (3) empirical evidence that learning to construct spatial layouts fosters transferable internal spatial understanding, validated on three external benchmarks.

Related Work

Linguistic Spatial Reasoning Benchmarks.

Early work on spatial language processing focused on extracting formal representations from natural language text
(
17
)
. Subsequent benchmarks like bAbI
(
46
)
introduced positional reasoning, while
StepGame

(
36
)
and
SpartQA

(
28
)
advanced the field with more complicated reasoning and broader linguistic coverage. However, a critical limitation remains across these benchmarks: they rely exclusively on final-answer accuracy. This "black-box" evaluation obscures the underlying inference process, making it impossible to distinguish between failures in representation and failures in reasoning. Our work addresses this methodological gap by introducing ground-truth intermediate representations, enabling precise diagnosis of spatial reasoning errors.

While integrating visual modalities (VLMs) offers a potential remedy for spatial understanding, recent studies reveal that VLMs face their own challenges in visual perception
(
22
;
23
;
37
;
16
)
and spatial reasoning
(
5
;
52
;
30
)
. Rather than relying on a separate visual encoder, we use ASCII grids to represent spatial layouts entirely within the language model’s native token space. These text-native grids serve a dual purpose: they act not only as a reasoning source within the context but also as a verifiable representation of all spatial relations.

ASCII and Spatial Representation.

The utility of intermediate reasoning steps is well-established through Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting
(
44
)
and its extensions, such as Tree-of-Thoughts
(
51
)
and latent reasoning
(
11
)
. Beyond purely linguistic traces, prior work has shown that symbolic, text-native spatial representations can effectively support spatial reasoning in language models, including grid-based worlds and discrete relational layouts
(
8
)
. Visualization-of-Thought
(
47
;
19
)
draws on human mental imagery to generate symbolic or pixel-level visualizations for spatial reasoning. While we share the intuition that explicit spatial construction aids reasoning, we focus on a different question: when and why does this process fail, and can training on it improve performance? We use ASCII as a diagnostic medium because, unlike natural language, its spatial outputs are structurally verifiable against ground truth.

Generalization of Spatial Reasoning.

Recent research demonstrates that enhancing specific capabilities in LLMs often yields transfer benefits to other domains. For instance,
35
indicate that training on mathematics improves general reasoning benchmarks, not just quantitative tasks. Prior work shows that spatial training benefits visual reasoning tasks
(
45
;
10
;
42
;
13
)
, typically through training on perception or comprehension objectives. We study a complementary mechanism: whether learning to construct spatial layouts can strengthen spatial understanding in language models, yielding transferable gains in spatial reasoning.

3
Text2Space
Dataset

We introduce
Text2Space
, a dataset pairing each natural language description with a ground-truth ASCII layout, a rendered image, and a query-answer pair, all derived from one spatial graph. The layout serves as a verifiable reference for construction, comprehension, and downstream reasoning. Figure
2
shows an example.

3.1
Data Generation

The dataset is constructed from connected spatial graphs where entities are linked via eight directional relationships:
above, below, left, right, upper-left, upper-right, lower-left, lower-right
. All relations adhere to a unified coordinate system, serving as the ground truth for both ASCII diagrams and rendered images. Instances are generated using a breadth-first search (BFS) procedure to guarantee connectivity. We enforce strict validation rules: self-loops and duplicate edges are prohibited, and any configuration conflicting with existing spatial constraints is rejected. These safeguards ensure every instance represents a valid, internally consistent topology. A detailed data generation process is presented in the Appendix
F
.

We vary complexity from simple two-component structures to intricate graphs with up to eight components and twelve relations, capturing configurations that require transitive reasoning across multiple inference steps.
Each instance includes natural language descriptions synthesized from the spatial graph, using diverse synonyms to ensure linguistic variation. Corresponding queries are designed so that target object pairs admit unique inferability, guaranteeing a single unambiguous spatial relation.

3.2
Multi-Format Representations

ASCII Visualization:
We include three rendering styles to test robustness against formatting variations. The
simple format
uses minimal spacing without borders for clarity. The
grid format
introduces structure using
+
,
-
, and
|
characters. The
panel format
employs box-drawing characters for a visually distinct layout.

Image Representation:
The spatial arrangement is also rendered in a standardized image format with configurable grid lines and directional arrows, serving as both a multimodal evaluation target and a human-readable reference. Further dataset details and data examples are provided in Appendix
A
.

4
Experiments

We split
Text2Space
into training and test sets, with the test set consisting of 1,000 samples used in the following experiments. We choose the ASCII
grid format
throughout our experiments; details of this format are provided in Appendix
B
. Our experiments address three questions in sequence.
Benchmarking:
Can models both read and draw spatial layouts, and where does the gap lie?
Reasoning process:
When models sketch a layout as an intermediate step, does the quality of that sketch help or hurt downstream reasoning?
Generalization:
Does training on layout construction instill spatial understanding that transfers to text-only reasoning and external benchmarks, even when no layout is produced at inference time?

4.2
Task Setups

Since ASCII is a unique representation that encodes two-dimensional spatial structure within a one-dimensional token sequence, we first evaluate language models’ ability to understand and generate ASCII representations. Specifically, we assess two complementary capabilities: generating an ASCII representation from a textual description (
Desc
\rightarrow
ASCII
) and generating a natural-language description from an ASCII representation (
ASCII
\rightarrow
Desc
).

We then evaluate spatial reasoning tasks, denoted
\mathcal{R}
, in which the model is given a reasoning source and asked to answer queries about spatial relationships.
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc}}
refers to reasoning from textual descriptions alone, while
\mathcal{R}_{\text{ASCII}}
refers to reasoning from ASCII representations alone.
Beyond reasoning from a single source, we investigate the role of ASCII as an intermediate step alongside descriptions through two generation orders. In
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc:(Ans, ASCII)}}
, the model first produces the answer and then generates an ASCII representation. In
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc:(ASCII, Ans)}}
, the model first generates an ASCII representation and then uses it to answer the query.
For both orders, we consider
one-turn generation
, where the model produces both outputs within a single response, and
two-turn generation
, where the second output is elicited via a follow-up prompt. The two generation orders mirror established self-explanation paradigms
(
14
;
25
)
: we compare two generation orders: predict-then-explain versus explain-then-predict
(
4
)
.

Finally, we evaluate
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc+ASCII}}
. In this phase, we substitute the model-generated ASCII with the ground-truth ASCII before the model answers the query. This replacement strategy simulates an ideal reasoning process, allowing us to assess the upper-bound performance of the model when guided by an ideal spatial representation.

4.3
Evaluation Methods

Our evaluation protocol targets three key aspects of the model’s performance:
QA Correctness:
For the spatial reasoning QA tasks, we validate the prediction against a predefined set of relation aliases.

Representation Alignment:
For the
ASCII
\leftrightarrow
Desc
conversion tasks, we measure the correspondence between converted representations. For
ASCII
\rightarrow
Desc
, the ASCII diagram is treated as the reference. We evaluate, across all generated descriptions, the percentage that can be correctly inferred from the given ASCII representation. For
Desc
\rightarrow
ASCII
, we evaluate the generated ASCII diagrams,
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc:(Ans, ASCII)}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc:(ASCII, Ans)}}
, by taking the description as the reference and measuring the proportion of the description that is correctly reflected in the ASCII output.

Reasoning Consistency:
For the joint generation settings, we introduce two complementary metrics to analyze internal coherence. Consistency(Cons): Measures the alignment between the generated ASCII and the generated Answer. This determines if the model’s intermediate output supports its final prediction. Consistency Accuracy(C_Acc): Measures answer accuracy strictly on instances where the ASCII and Answer are consistent. By controlling for internal coherence, this isolates the model’s reasoning capability, helping distinguish whether errors arise from failures in constructing spatial representations or from limitations in reasoning over them.

4.4
Learning Spatial Relations with ASCII

We propose that ASCII layouts function as a structured reasoning intermediate: a verifiable “cognitive map” that makes spatial relationships explicit. We hypothesize that learning to construct such maps
(
21
;
53
)
consolidates spatial logic during training. If so, models trained on layout construction should improve on spatial reasoning tasks even without producing any layout at inference time, and the benefit should transfer beyond the training format.
To test and disentangle the understanding and generation of ASCII representations, we design three configurations.
Learning to Construct
(
Desc
\rightarrow
ASCII
) forces the model to actively construct spatial layouts from text (performing “mental mapping”).
Learning to Comprehend
(
ASCII
\rightarrow
Desc
) focuses on parsing and verbalizing ASCII representations.
Bidirectional Alignment
(
Desc
\leftrightarrow
ASCII
) combines both tasks simultaneously.

We fine-tuned
Qwen3-30B-A3B
on 4x NVIDIA A40 GPUs using the SFTTrainer from the HuggingFace TRL library
(
40
)
. We employed Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
(
12
;
27
)
for parameter-efficient training, utilizing a split of 4,000 training and 500 validation examples. We adhered to the default library configurations for all other settings; full training details are provided in Appendix
C
.

We evaluate performance on the Spatial Reasoning Task (
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc}}
), where models answer spatial relations solely from natural language descriptions without explicit ASCII input. To assess whether the benefits extend beyond our benchmark, we further evaluate on three external tasks that each target a different facet of spatial cognition:
StepGame

(
36
;
34
)
examines compositional directional navigation,
bAbI
task 19
(
46
)
tests route planning through connected locations, and
SpartQA

(
28
)
challenges models with complex spatial questions in rich natural language. None of these tasks involve ASCII, and we evaluate the model’s performance with zero-shot prompting setting. If construction training instills generalizable spatial understanding, gains should appear on these tasks despite the format mismatch.

(
4

14

(
44
)

flawed construction
rather than treating it as a black-box effect. Models can reason over spatial layouts when provided, but cannot yet produce them reliably enough to support their own reasoning.

This diagnosis points to a concrete path forward: since the deficiency lies in representation construction, the next step is to examine whether training on this capability can strengthen the model’s spatial understanding. We first analyze whether the model’s generated layouts are internally coherent with its answers, using consistency as an unsupervised signal of reasoning reliability (Section
5.2
). We then investigate whether training on layout construction can instill the spatial understanding needed to bridge this discrepancy (Section
5.3
).

5.2
Consistency Analysis in Spatial Reasoning

Table
3
shows the alignment between ASCII representations and textual answers across both output orders and turn settings. We report three key metrics: (1) Answer Accuracy measures overall task performance; (2) Consistency quantifies the alignment between the generated ASCII and the textual answer; and (3) Consistent Accuracy evaluates correctness specifically on instances where the representation and answer correspond.

In the
one-turn
generation setting, producing the ASCII representation before the answer does not improve consistency. Similar consistency across output orders suggests that the model does not clearly use the ASCII as a reasoning scaffold, with both outputs likely reflecting the same underlying representation.
In the
two-turn
generation setting, we observed an improved consistency rate, where the model first generates either the answer or the ASCII, and is then prompted to produce the other. The consistency gap between
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc:(Ans, ASCII)}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc:(ASCII, Ans)}}
indicates that the response to the follow-up query is strongly influenced by the previously generated output. Despite this notable improvement in consistency, answer accuracy increases only marginally. This limited gain may partly reflect to the suboptimal quality of the generated ASCII representations, which correlates with lower answer accuracy (Table
2
).

Nevertheless, consistency remains a valuable indicator, particularly as it requires no ground-truth annotations for either layout or the answer. While prior work has applied Natural Language Inference (NLI) to assess reasoning via consistency
(
43
;
24
;
18
)
, our approach benefits from the explicit verifiability of the generated artifact. Unlike unstructured text, ASCII representations enforce strict spatial constraints and encode the full spatial configuration, not just the queried pair, allowing us to objectively assess the alignment between the representation and the answer. We observe that when the model’s ASCII representation and its textual answer agree, the answer is far more likely to be correct. This consistency check requires no ground-truth labels: the ASCII itself is structurally verifiable, so agreement between the two outputs serves as an unsupervised reliability signal.

Our results show
“ASCII as a Verifier”
as a promising direction. The structural constraints imposed by ASCII suggest that cross-representation consistency may provide a more reliable signal of reasoning validity than text-only self-consistency. Future work may explore integrating such structured verification signals into reasoning pipelines, both as an error-detection mechanism and as a guiding objective for improving intermediate representation construction.

We now evaluate whether these improvements transfer to the downstream spatial reasoning task (
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc}}
), which does not generate ASCII at inference time but requires answering spatial queries from text alone, a task format entirely absent from fine-tuning. Table
2
shows that construction training (FT
{}_{\text{D}\rightarrow\text{A}}
) improves accuracy by over 7 percentage points, indicating that learning to construct spatial layouts strengthens spatial reasoning from text alone. In contrast, comprehension training (FT
{}_{\text{D}\leftarrow\text{A}}
) fails to improve performance and falls slightly below the baseline, suggesting that improved comprehension without a reliable spatial representation as its foundation is not just insufficient but counterproductive. Since both conditions expose the model to identical spatial configurations, the divergence isolates
construction
as the active ingredient, not mere exposure to spatial data.

Fine-tuning on layout construction substantially improves ASCII accuracy for
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc:(Ans, ASCII)}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc:(ASCII, Ans)}}
, reversing the pattern from Section
5.1
: accurate sketches now scaffold reasoning rather than mislead. The bottleneck was not the representation format but
sketch quality
: even after fine-tuning, ground-truth ASCII (
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Desc+ASCII}}
) still outperforms model-generated sketches, confirming that further improvements in construction will yield additional reasoning gains.

The persistent advantage of externally provided layouts, even after construction training, parallels human spatial cognition. In a preliminary human validation (Appendix
I
), participants achieve substantially higher accuracy with access to ASCII layouts than with text descriptions alone. Mentally reconstructing spatial structure from sequential text is inherently more demanding than reading a provided layout for both humans and models.

The consistency analysis (Table
3
) tells a complementary story. Construction training improves consistency between the model’s sketch and its answer far more than comprehension training does. The mechanism is intuitive: when the sketch is accurate, it reinforces the textual answer; when it remains flawed, better comprehension only makes the model more aware of the conflict between its noisy sketch and the description. Construction training thus both improves consistency and ensures that consistent instances are more likely to be correct.
The confusion matrices (Appendix, Figure

6
Conclusion

We investigate whether training language models to construct explicit spatial layouts can instill genuine spatial understanding. Using ASCII grids as a diagnostic instrument that is text-native, human-readable, and structurally verifiable, we introduce
Text2Space
, a benchmark to separate construction failures from reasoning failures.

Our results reveal a clear read-write asymmetry. Models can interpret spatial layouts effectively but struggle to construct them from text, and these construction errors propagate to downstream reasoning. Consistency analysis further shows that when a model’s ASCII representation agrees with its textual answer, answer accuracy is substantially higher. This agreement serves as an unsupervised reliability signal. Training models to construct layouts addresses this bottleneck directly: spatial reasoning from text improves even without active sketching at inference time, and the benefit transfers to three external benchmarks (
StepGame
,
bAbI
,
SpartQA
) that require spatial understanding. Only configurations that include construction training generalize across all three benchmarks, with the combination of construction and comprehension yielding the strongest gains. These results indicate that the spatial understanding gained through layout construction is not format-specific but foundational, enabling models to reason more effectively across diverse task formulations.

Limitations

ASCII grids are one instantiation of our approach, chosen for diagnostic precision and structural verifiability. This scope entails several boundaries: we do not explore alternative formats such as coordinate lists or scene graphs, nor spatial problems involving continuous geometry, pixel-level perception, or 3D environments. As a discrete abstraction, ASCII does not capture the richness of continuous spatial perception, and our synthetic benchmark may not fully represent real-world noise and ambiguity. Future work could examine whether construction training transfers to other model families and whether richer formats scale to more complex spatial environments.

Our evaluation relies on answer accuracy and consistency between generated diagrams and predictions. These metrics provide observable, verifiable signals of reasoning behavior but do not directly reveal the model’s internal representations. While ASCII’s structure offers a foundation for iterative verification and self-correction, this work does not explore such mechanisms.
