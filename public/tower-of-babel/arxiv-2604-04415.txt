Structured Causal Video Reasoning via Multi-Objective Alignment
Zinuo Li, Yongxin Guo, Jun Liu, Jiawei Zhan, Xi Jiang, Chengjie Wang, Mohammed Bennamoun, Farid Boussaid, Feng Zheng, Qiuhong Ke

Abstract
Human understanding of video dynamics is typically grounded in a structured mental representation of entities, actions, and temporal relations, rather than relying solely on immediate deductive reasoning. In contrast, existing Video-LLMs largely depend on unstructured video reasoning, where critical visual evidence is embedded in verbose textual descriptions and temporal causality is often weakly modeled. This leads to inefficient processes and fragile causal inference. To bridge this cognitive gap, we propose constructing a compact representation of salient events and their causal relationships, which we name Structured Event Facts , prior to the reasoning stage. This structured prior serves as an explicit constraint to promote concise and causally grounded reasoning, while also making intermediate evidence easier to verify. To effectively train models on such structured facts, we introduce CausalFact-60K and a four-stage training pipeline comprising facts alignment, format warm-start, thinking warm-start, and reinforcement learning-based post-training. During RL stage, we find that this framework introduces competing objectives, as structural completeness and causal fidelity must be balanced against reasoning length, making it difficult to optimize. We address this challenge by formulating the optimization as a Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL) problem and explicitly optimizing toward the Pareto-Frontier to balance these trade-offs. As a result, we introduce Factum-4B , which yields more reliable reasoning and delivers stronger performance on challenging video understanding tasks requiring fine-grained temporal inference.

Introduction

Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) have achieved remarkable success in interpreting static images by adapting the architectural paradigms of Large Language Models (LLMs)
(
26
;
1
)
. Recently, this progress has extended to the video domain, aiming to capture dynamic temporal information
(
51
;
24
)
. To enhance the reasoning capabilities of these models, the research community has predominantly adopted the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) paradigm
(
44
)
in Large Language Models, which encourages models to generate intermediate reasoning steps before deriving a final answer. Later on, CoT has also been proven effective to multimodal tasks
(
2
)
.

Figure 1
:
Compared with existing video reasoning approaches, our model first extracts facts event information from videos. It then applies a thinking process strictly constrained by causal relationships, which is specifically optimized for video data. This method clarifies critical information and enhances interpretability while focusing on the temporal dimension of videos. Video is sampled from ActivityNet-Captions
(
21
)
.

Unlike text, video data is characterized by dense spatio-temporal redundancy. When models simply employ unstructured CoT like text-only tasks, the resulting generation often devolves into verbose, non-linear narratives where pivotal visual cues are drowned in irrelevant tokens.
Empirically, we observe that reasoning-enhanced Thinking models often degrade in performance compared to their Instruction-tuned counterparts

(
2
)
. They suffer from reasoning drift, losing focus on the user query, and frequently reduce the temporal dimension to isolated frame retrieval rather than analyzing causal relationships. This behavior contrasts sharply with human cognitive processes. Psychological research suggests that human perception relies on forming a structured mental sketch, which registers entities, actions and event boundaries before engaging in abstract reasoning
(
50
)
.

Motivated by this cognitive mechanism, we propose a
Structure-First
paradigm, a module generated prior to the reasoning phase. As shown in
Figure

1
, we enforce the model to synthesize a high-density schema containing time, object, human action, scene, and causal event caption before the reasoning process begins. This structured prior serves as an explicit constraint, anchoring the subsequent reasoning to be concise, salient, and causally grounded.

However, there is often a trade-off between maintaining structural strictness and ensuring reasoning length. We find that this multi-stage reasoning process presents a non-trivial optimization challenge during Reinforcement Learning (RL) stage. In resource-constrained settings with limited length budgets, maximizing facts density can inadvertently truncate reasoning depth, and vice versa. Standard RL strategies
(
7
)
, typically optimized via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
36
)
, or Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
37
)
struggle to balance these conflicting rewards, leading to training instability, where the advantages calculation cannot distinguish optimization direction.

To avoid collapsing multiple rewards into a single scalar, which can obscure critical trade-offs, we treat multi-dimensional reward vectors as distinct signals for policy updates. Unlike standard GRPO, we treat each reward component as an independent objective to preserve task-specific gradients. Inspired by the Multiple Gradient Descent Algorithm (MGDA)
(
11
)
, we propose Pareto-Frontier guided Advantage Balancing (P-FAB) algorithm to effectively resolve rewards conflicts and dynamically balance distinct objectives. Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We identify the limitations of unstructured CoT in video understanding. To address this, we propose
CausalFact-60K
, a dataset that empowers the model to precisely output facts information and utilize causal relationships for video reasoning with dense captions.

•

We reveal that the standard GRPO advantage calculation may lead to conflicts among multi-objective rewards and ambiguous gradient directions. Motivated by the pareto-frontier, we propose
Pareto-Frontier guided Advantage Balancing (P-FAB)
algorithm. This algorithm dynamically balances multi-objective advantages based on pareto-optimal during the advantage calculation process.

•

Based on aforementioned contributions, we train a small but strong model:
Factum-4B
. Extensive experiments on standard benchmarks demonstrate that Factum-4B significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, validating the effectiveness of structured reasoning process and P-FAB algorithm.

Related Work

Video understanding LVLMs.

Following the success of image-to-text LVLMs
(
26
;
10
)
, early video LLMs treated videos as sequences of static frames and employed token compression to fit high-dimensional spatio-temporal signals into limited LLM token budgets
(
29
;
51
)
.
However, this frame-level approach overlooks core challenges in video understanding, particularly temporal grounding and causal reasoning
(
28
;
4
)
. To address this, recent works have introduced time-aware architectures
(
35
;
17
;
16
;
15
)
to better capture temporal dynamics, or incorporated memory and tool-augmented mechanisms for handling long-form, untrimmed videos
(
38
;
34
;
41
)
.
Parallel efforts focus on improving training data quality and scale
(
54
)
and establishing reliable benchmarks for evaluating long-horizon temporal and causal reasoning
(
30
;
13
)
.
Yet, despite architectural advances, current video LLMs often rely on sparse frame heuristics rather than modeling true event dynamics
(
30
;
13
;
45
)
, limiting their performance in complex real-world scenarios. Our method addresses this by learning an explicit structured prior that summarizes key events and their causal relationships before free-form reasoning, effectively enhancing temporal grounding and reducing dependence on brittle frame-selection shortcuts.

Reasoning and chain-of-thought in LVLMs.

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting
(
44
)
improves LLM reasoning by eliciting intermediate steps, and Multimodal-CoT extends this to vision-language models by conditioning reasoning traces on visual features
(
55
)
.
However, visual inputs are often both redundant, containing many irrelevant frames, also underspecified with fine-grained temporal and causal cues easily missed. As a result, unconstrained vision Chain of Thought tends to become verbose, drift off-topic, or hallucinate unsupported details, especially when intermediate steps lack grounding in verifiable evidence
(
5
)
.
Prior approaches address this through tool-augmented verification (e.g., ReAct/MM-ReAct)
(
49
;
47
)
, exploration of multiple reasoning paths (e.g., Tree-of-Thoughts, self-consistency)
(
48
;
42
)
. In contrast, we generate an internal
Structured Event Facts
, a compact, high-density schema encoding key events, entities, and causal relationships, which explicitly constrains subsequent CoT reasoning to be concise, salient, and firmly grounded in extracted video facts.

Reinforcement learning in LVLMs

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) aligns LLMs with human preferences using comparison-based feedback
(
33
;
40
)
, typically via scalar rewards optimized with PPO
(
36
)
. Recent work proposes GRPO
(
37
)
, a more efficient alternative that updates policies using group-wise relative comparisons, reducing reliance on value-function estimation while enabling rule-based rewards modeling.
For LVLMs, existing approaches directly apply GRPO to diverse video understanding tasks such as reasoning, temporal grounding, and counting
(
12
;
43
)
.
However, these methods overlook the inherently multi-objective nature of video understanding, where models must balance objectives including structural constraints, reasoning capability, final accuracy, and computational budgets, etc. In this paper, rather than naively averaging rewards, we optimize reward weights by solving a Multi-Gradient Descent Algorithm (MGDA) objective to approximate the Pareto-Frontier
(
20
;
25
)
. This approach ensures a fair, stable, and balanced optimization across all objectives.

3
Methodology

3.1
Overview

To reformulate complex video thinking, a straightforward approach would typically involve a two-stage process:
Thinking Warm-start
to establish causal logic, followed by
Reinforcement Learning
for alignment. However, the unique integration of
Structured Event Facts
within our framework necessitates a specialized orchestration of the training steps.

We empirically find that this facts-first dependency introduces an optimization barrier. Directly optimizing accurate factual description and complex causal reasoning through these two simple stages overwhelms the model, leading to hallucinated facts or collapsed reasoning structures. To resolve this conflict, we decouple the learning process into four progressive stages:
Facts Training (Stage 1), Format Warm-Start (Stage 1.5), Thinking Warm-Start (Stage 2), and RL-based Post-training (Stage 3)
.

Stage 1 trains the model to produce high-quality facts outputs with accurate descriptions of person, action, scene, etc. Stage 2 focuses on training the model’s causal reasoning ability. These two stages are both trained through instruction tuning. In Stage 1, we ask the model to output facts information from the video, while in Stage 2, we ask real QA questions such as temporal grounding and visual reasoning.
Detailed prompts are shown in the Appendix
B.4
.

Notably, to bridge the gap between pure factual description (Stage 1) and complex reasoning (Stage 2), we introduce an intermediate Stage 1.5. In this stage, we ask for the same facts as in Stage 1 but require the model to strictly adhere to the thinking format (e.g.,
<thinking>...<answering>
), thereby warming up the structural format. Otherwise, the model hallucinates bad structure. Finally, Stage 3 employs our proposed Pareto-Frontier guided Advantage Balancing (P-FAB) to resolve the objective conflicts in multi-objective reinforcement learning. Since each stage requires distinct supervision signals, we construct a comprehensive data curation pipeline, detailed in
Section

3.2
.

3.2
Data Curation: Facts and Thinking

3.2.1
Motivation

Although video reasoning has raised researchers’ attention, existing video understanding models often reason without structure
(
43
;
2
)
, where critical clues are submerged in redundant text. Conventional Chain-of-Thought (CoT) can become verbose and unfocused on long videos, introducing invalid steps while under-emphasizing temporal causal logic in favor of discrete time points.

This motivates us to propose a thinking process with clearer clues, better interpretability, and stronger causality. Thus, we propose to model the video understanding problem as follows:
Facts, Thinking, Answering
, where a clear reasoning process with strong causality can be established.

Specifically, we force the model to output
Structured Event Facts
as the primary step of our methodology to enhance structured information extraction, aiming to provide precise, compact, and query-relevant clues that establish a reliable semantic foundation for subsequent reasoning. In this stage, the model is required to describe the major events in the video at reasonable intervals, spanning approximately 10 to 30 seconds, and extract the key cues within each event, such as salient characters, human actions, scene context, and camera position, and then provide a detailed, time-ordered description of each event in chronological sequence, as illustrated
Figure

2
.

Building upon the resulting Structured Event Facts, we aim to steer the model toward better causal reasoning. Leveraging the salient clues extracted in the facts phase, we enforce a structured causal reasoning process with three tightly coupled stages: 1. Global Search & Localization, 2. Causal Verification, and 3. Final Alignment.

As shown in
Figure

2
. The first phase
Global Search & Localization
requires the model to retrieve query-relevant clues from the structured facts list to quickly narrow attention to a small set of highly relevant time windows, providing a reliable starting point for downstream reasoning. In
Causal Verification
stage, it examines temporal coherence by analyzing the preceding and succeeding events around the hypothesized interval, and validates the localization through cause, which effects consistency rather than shallow pattern matching. To avoid over-reliance on textual priors and keep the decision grounded in visual evidence, the model performs a mandatory visual verification on the inferred interval using vision tokens sent with textual prompts.

In
Final Alignment
stage, the model performs a global consistency check to ensure the inferred temporal interval is fully contained within the observed activity sequences, improving the overall reliability of the reasoning process.

Figure 3
:
Overview of the two-stage pipeline for constructing from VTG datasets. Stage 1 performs video filtering and gap filling, generates structured facts captions, and applies an automatic quality judge with random human inspection; low-quality samples are rejected and iteratively refined. Stage 2 produces causally grounded reasoning traces, followed by a second quality-judging and human spot-checking step, yielding the final curated causal–facts set.

3.2.2
Data Construction Pipeline

As illustrated in
Figure

3
, to enable high-quality facts and Thinking generation, we build a comprehensive data construction pipeline.

For pre-processing, we adopt a strict selection pipeline to distill informative samples from high-quality human-annotated Video Temporal Grounding datasets
(
14
;
21
)
.
More video source details are shown in the Appendix
B
.
We prioritize videos with high event density, assuming an ideal event segment spans 20–30 seconds: for a video of duration
T
, we retain it only if event number
N\geq T/30
, i.e., a 150-second video requires at least 5 events. For retained videos, we further apply gap filling to improve continuity: unlabeled intervals between adjacent annotated events (e.g., unlabeled
[25,38]
between annotated
[15,25]
and
[38,50]
) are treated as candidate event segments for the model to describe, increasing coverage and encouraging global spatiotemporal modeling. Videos with
N\textless T/30
are excluded from facts training to avoid weakening structured signals, but are retained for the subsequent RL stage to enhance robustness and generalization.

Next, we feed the filtered dense timestamps into a facts Generator to produce the event facts descriptions. Since facts are fine-grained structured signals that require strong instruction following to achieve high data quality, we employ two complementary models: Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct
(
2
)
and Gemini2.5- Pro
(
9
)
. To prevent specific model preferences from compromising data quality, the two models alternate roles: when one serves as the generator, the other acts as a quality judge. The two strong models mutually critique and filter outputs, enabling facts to capture detailed visual cues. The data produced in this stage is served for Stage 1 and Stage 1.5.

After obtaining the facts, we generate challenging questions and prompt the LLM to produce explicit thinking traces conditioned on the question and the associated facts information. Since thinking data demands a higher quality than facts itself, we use Gemini2.5-Pro exclusively as the generator in this stage, while Qwen3-VL serves as the quality judge. The data produced in this stage is served for Stage 2.

To ensure data quality, we conduct a
human evaluation
by sampling 50% of the total 60K annotations. Specifically, we partition the generated data into batches of 1,000 instances and randomly select 500 samples from each batch for manual inspection.

3.3
Pareto-Frontier guided Advantage Balancing

Motivations.

After the first three stages, the model acquires structured reasoning capabilities. In Stage 3, we introduce reinforcement learning to align the model with real-world problem-solving. However, the integration of facts, thinking, and answering components naturally elongates the chain-of-thought, transforming the training into a complex Multi-Objective Optimization problem, where two problems may occur in standard Group Relative
Policy Optimization (GRPO):

(1)
Inherent Conflicts
: There is a natural Pareto trade-off between comprehensive fact extraction and token efficiency, which static weights cannot dynamically navigate; and (2)
Directional Ambiguity
: Standard GRPO cannot distinguish update differences between multiple objectives.
As illustrated in Figure
4
, GRPO produces identical advantage values for candidates with the same average rewards, thereby masking the underlying distribution and potential imbalances among different reward components.

Figure 4
:
GRPO vs P-FAB advantage comparison. P-FAB dynamically adjusts weights by solving a minimum-norm problem in the standardized reward space, ensuring that rare but critical signals are not overwhelmed by high-variance conflicting objectives.

Pitfalls of traditional policy gradient optimization.

In policy gradient optimization, updates follow the direction
A\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}\mid q_{i})
, where the advantage
A
is derived from a weighted reward
R_{\text{final}}=\sum w_{i}r_{i}
. To manage conflicting objectives, a common practice is to tune the coefficients
w_{i}
to prioritize specific goals. However, since these weights remain static during training, they often exacerbate trade-offs rather than resolving the underlying conflicts.

P-FAB as a solution.

We reformulate this interaction by defining a group-wide reward vector
\mathbf{v}_{m}=[r_{1,m},\dots,r_{G,m}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{G}
for each objective
m
, where
G
is the number of responses per prompt. We interpret each
\mathbf{v}_{m}
as a
“directional preference vector”
, in essence a
“vote”
from objective
m
on which samples to promote or suppress. By stacking these update signals into a matrix
\Delta
and solving for the minimum-norm combination within its convex hull, we derive a compromise update that is maximally compatible with all objectives. Inspired by MGDA
(
11
)
, we propose
Pareto-Frontier guided Advantage Balancing (P-FAB)
algorithm, as described in

Methodology Details.

Following the group-relative approach in GRPO
(
37
)
, we first isolate the relative quality of each completion within its prompt group. Consider a batch
\mathcal{B}
containing
N
prompts, where each prompt
q
generates a group of
G
responses
\{o_{1},\dots,o_{G}\}
. For each objective
m
, the centered reward
\delta_{i,m}
for response
o_{i}
is computed by subtracting the group mean:

\delta_{i,m}=r_{m}(q,o_{i})-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r_{m}(q,o_{j}).

(1)

This normalization ensures that
\delta_{i,m}
indicates whether response
o_{i}
outperforms the group average for objective
m
.

Solving Common Direction.

The theoretical foundation of our approach stems from MGDA. In multi-objective optimization, the goal is to find a parameter update that simultaneously improves all objectives
\{L_{1}(\omega),\ldots,L_{M}(\omega)\}
. MGDA achieves this by seeking a convex combination of task gradients that forms a common descent direction. A key result from MGDA theory shows that finding such a direction is equivalent to solving the following dual problem:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta_{M}}\left\|\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}\nabla L_{m}(\omega)\right\|^{2}.

(2)

When the optimal norm is zero, a Pareto-stationary point is reached, otherwise, the minimizing combination yields a descent direction that benefits all tasks. In our preference optimization setting, the centered reward
\delta_{i,m}
defined in Eq. (
1
) plays an analogous role to task gradients: it represents the per-sample, per-objective signal indicating how the policy should be updated. This analogy allows us to adapt the MGDA principle to our setting: by minimizing the norm of the weighted combination
\sum_{m}\alpha_{m}\delta_{i,m}
, we seek a consensus direction that balances all reward objectives.

However, a primary challenge in applying MGDA directly to
\delta_{i,m}
is scale sensitivity: objectives with high variance can dominate the norm minimization, suppressing signals from other tasks. To ensure scale invariance, P-FAB computes optimal weights in a standardized space. For a prompt group
q
, let
\mathbf{D}_{q}
be the matrix with entries
\delta_{i,m}
. We construct the standardized matrix
\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}
by scaling each column by its standard deviation
\sigma_{q,m}
, ignoring objectives where
\sigma_{q,m}<\tau=1e-6
to maintain numerical stability.

Next, we seek the weight vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{q}
by employing the Frank-Wolfe algorithm
(detailed in Appendix
D.2
)
, which is highly efficient for quadratic optimization to minimize the norm of the aggregate direction within this standardized space:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{q}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta_{M}}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\|^{2}.

\displaystyle s.t.\;

\displaystyle\Delta_{M}=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\mid\sum\alpha_{m}=1,\alpha_{m}\geq 0\}

(3)

Advantage scalarization.

Once the Pareto-optimal weights
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}_{q}
are obtained, we apply them to the centered rewards
\mathbf{D}_{q}
to preserve the original signal magnitude fidelity, followed by a final group-wise normalization:

A_{i}^{\text{raw}}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha^{*}_{q_{i},m}\,\delta_{i,m},\quad\quad A_{i}=\frac{A_{i}^{\text{raw}}-\mu_{A}}{\sigma_{A}+\epsilon}.

(4)

Finally, the scalarized advantage
A_{i}
is substituted into the standard GRPO objective. Let
\rho_{i}(\theta)=\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q_{i})/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i}|q_{i})
be the probability ratio. The policy is optimized by minimizing:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\min\left(\rho_{i}(\theta)A_{i},\;\text{clip}(\rho_{i}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)A_{i}\right)-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\right]

(5)

where
\varepsilon
is the clipping parameter to prevent excessive policy updates, and
\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}
is the KL-divergence penalty ensuring the policy stays close to the reference model.

Discussion on P-FAB.

As illustrated in
Figure

4
, P-FAB 1) naturally amplifies
sparse and hard-to-satisfy
objectives while reducing the weights of rewards that are more easily obtained. Consequently, the resulting advantage values are more discriminative, prioritizing samples that satisfy critical, rare criteria over those that merely perform well on simpler tasks.
2) We consider the model to have reached a local
Pareto-Frontier
when the objectives achieve
Pareto stationarity
. In this state, the convex hull of the task gradients contains the origin (
\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|\sum\alpha_{m}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{m}\|\approx 0
), implying that no common descent direction exists. At this point, any further improvement in one objective necessitates a degradation in another, signifying that the model has fully navigated the inherent trade-offs of the task.

4.1
Implementation Details

We use
Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct
as our base model. Since the 4B model is more susceptible to general-capability forgetting, we adopt stage-specific hyperparameter settings across our three training stages. For Stage 1, Stage 1.5, and Stage 2, we fine-tune the model with LoRA, but with different hyperparameters. In Stage 1 and Stage 1.5, we use LoRA with rank
64
and
\alpha=128
, a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-5}
, and weight decay of
1\times 10^{-4}
to mitigate catastrophic forgetting of general knowledge. In Stage 2, we keep the same LoRA setup but increase the learning rate to
1\times 10^{-4}
and weight decay to
2\times 10^{-4}
.

For the RL Stage 3, we use 4 different rewards:
Format, Linear IoU, Multi-choice Accuracy, and Length
, where Length reward makes the conflicts.
Detailed reward designs are shown in the Appendix
D.1
.
We perform full-parameter updates with a learning rate of
5\times 10^{-6}
,
\mathrm{num\_generation=8}
, video max tokens set to

On MLVU, our model maintains top-tier performance in Temporal Reasoning and Ego-centric understanding, effectively bridging the gap between open-source specialized models and multi-billion parameter proprietary systems. These combined results underscore that by enabling causal reasoning, Factum-4B achieves a more robust and granular understanding of complex video dynamics.

Discussion and Weaknesses.

A consistent trend across benchmarks is that
unstructured
thinking does not enable better video understanding ability:
Qwen3-VL-4B-Thinking consistently underperforms Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct on nearly all benchmarks
, aligning with our motivation that verbose reasoning can introduce evidence drift and weaken temporal alignment. In contrast, our framework explicitly organizes important clues into a compact format, leading to stronger performance and improved precision under stricter temporal thresholds. However, our current training data is still limited, there’s a need to
scale up
the dataset to enable the model to achieve strong performance across all video understanding tasks in the future.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we present Factum-4B, a Video-LLM that bridges the cognitive gap between human video understanding and current models by introducing Structured Event Facts, serving as an explicit constraint that guides the model towards interpretable causal video reasoning. We develop a four-stage training pipeline that progressively equips the model with facts sketching and causal reasoning towards real-world task. To handle multi-objectives trade-offs under constrained length budgets during RL, we introduce Pareto-Frontier guided Advantage Balancing (P-FAB) algorithm. Empirically, Factum-4B yields more stable and reliable reasoning, delivering stronger performance on challenging video understanding tasks that demand temporal localization and causal inference. We hope this work provides a promising direction for scalable, evidence-grounded video reasoning systems.

B.4
Data Curation Prompts

This section describes the prompts used in our data curation pipeline. We employ three specialized prompts for different stages: Facts Generation, Quality Judge, and Thinking Generation. The complete prompts are illustrated in Figure
9
, Figure
10
, and Figure
11
, respectively.

Facts Generation Prompt.

The Facts Generation prompt (Figure
9
) instructs the model to analyze video content and produce structured summaries for pre-defined time segments. Given a list of timestamps as input, the model generates a theme summary and event factuals for each segment. The prompt enforces a strict schema with six required components per segment:
[person]
,
[human_action]
,
[scene]
,
[object]
,
[camera]
, and
[casual_event_caption]
. A critical dependency rule ensures logical consistency: if no person is present in the scene, the human action field must be marked as “None”. The prompt also specifies that timestamps must be preserved in their original seconds format without conversion to MM:SS, and all output must be enclosed within
<facts></facts>
tags.

Quality Judge Prompt.

The Quality Judge prompt (Figure
10
) defines a strict evaluation protocol for validating generated facts. It takes two inputs: the target timestamps that must be present and the model-generated structured text. The evaluation criteria are organized into four categories: (1)
Format Adherence
ensures proper tag structure, required headers, and all six mandatory fields in every line; (2)
Timestamp Alignment
verifies one-to-one correspondence between output lines and input segments, rejecting any missing or hallucinated timestamps; (3)
Logical Consistency
enforces the person-action dependency rule, ensuring that “None” person entries always correspond to “None” actions; and (4)
Content Quality
assesses completeness, fluency of captions, and specificity of descriptions. The judge outputs a structured JSON containing a numeric score (0-10), a regeneration flag, a list of critical errors, and qualitative feedback.

Thinking Generation Prompt.

The Thinking Generation prompt (Figure
11
) guides the model to produce evidence-based reasoning chains for video question answering. Given a user question and extracted factuals, the model must derive answers through a two-stage structured reasoning process. In
Stage 1: Global Search & Localization
, the model scans factual descriptions for keywords related to the question, identifies candidate time segments based solely on textual evidence, and explicitly states which factual IDs are selected as candidates. In
Stage 2: Causal Verification
, the model validates candidates using antecedent-action-consequence logic:
[Antecedent]
establishes the pre-condition contrast,
[Verifying Key Visual Actions]
provides textual proof of the event, and
[Consequence]
confirms the post-condition result. The output follows a
<thinking>...</thinking><answer>...</answer>
format, ensuring that reasoning is grounded in explicit factual evidence rather than implicit assumptions.

Appendix C
Instruction Tuning Data Format

To effectively train the model to reason over structured evidence, we design a progressive prompt engineering strategy that guides the generation of training data across three distinct stages. This multi-stage approach ensures the model first learns to extract high-density visual facts, then adapts to a strict structural format, and finally integrates these skills into a coherent reasoning chain.

In the initial stage, the objective is to align the model’s visual perception with our structured event schema, as displayed in
Figure

12
. We explicitly instruct the model to localize activity events and generate a detailed report containing a global theme and detailed event factuals. We enforce a rigid output format for each temporal segment, requiring specific fields such as time, person, human action, scene, object, camera, and a casual event caption. This design forces the model to decompose complex video dynamics into discrete, verifiable atomic elements rather than generating generic captions, effectively transforming raw video data into the structured representation used throughout the pipeline.

Before tackling complex reasoning tasks, the model must internalize the specific XML-like tag structure required for our framework. We therefore introduce a format warm-up stage, as shown in
Figure

13
. The system prompt defines the interaction protocol: the assistant must first output the factual block, followed by a thinking block, and conclude with the answering block. Since the task here is simply to reproduce video facts, the thinking block is populated with a trivial placeholder. This step ensures the model adheres to the syntactic structure without the cognitive load of complex problem-solving.

The final stage focuses on generating high-quality chain-of-thought data, as depicted in
Figure

14
. We combine the structured facts from the first stage with challenging QA pairs. The prompt requires the model to first recall the structured factual context. Crucially, the subsequent thinking block is no longer trivial; it contains a detailed derivation process where the model performs global search, causal verification, and alignment based on the provided facts. This stage produces the gold-standard thinking data used to warm-start the policy model, ensuring that the reasoning process is explicitly grounded in the extracted evidence.

Appendix D
More details of Reinforcement Learning

D.1
Detailed Rewards Design

To guide the model toward generating structured, causally grounded, and concise reasoning chains, we design a composite reward function consisting of three distinct components:
Format Reward, Linear IoU Reward, Multi-choice Accuracy Reward, and Length Reward
.

Causal Thinking Format Reward (
r_{\text{fmt}}
).

To enforce the structured reasoning schema proposed in our method, we design a rule-based format reward. This reward evaluates whether the model strictly follows the
<factual>
,
<thinking>
, and
<answering>
XML structure and whether the
<thinking>
block explicitly traverses the required causal reasoning steps.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be the set of mandatory causal keywords defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=\{

\displaystyle\textit{``Global Search'', ``Causal Verification'', ``Final Alignment'',}

(6)

\displaystyle\textit{``Antecedent'', ``Visual Verification'', ``Consequence''}\}

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\text{basic}}
denote the satisfaction of the basic regex pattern,
\mathcal{T}_{\text{valid}}
denote the constraint that all XML tags appear exactly once, and
\mathcal{C}_{\text{think}}
represent the content extracted from the
<thinking>
block. The format reward
r_{\text{fmt}}
is strictly defined as:

r_{\text{fmt}}=\begin{cases}1.0&\text{if }\mathcal{P}_{\text{basic}}\land\mathcal{T}_{\text{valid}}\land(\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\text{think}})\\
0.5&\text{if }\mathcal{P}_{\text{basic}}\land[\neg\mathcal{T}_{\text{valid}}\lor(\mathcal{K}\not\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\text{think}})]\\
0.0&\text{otherwise (pattern mismatch)}\end{cases}

(7)

This reward scheme assigns a baseline score of 0.5 for adhering to the basic format structure, grants a full score of 1.0 only when the reasoning chain is structurally complete and causally rigorous, and assigns 0.0 for format violations.

Task-Specific Performance Rewards (
r_{\text{task}}
).

Depending on the downstream task type, we apply different performance metrics as rewards:

Linear IoU Reward):
For temporal grounding tasks, we compute the Temporal Intersection over Union (TIoU) between the predicted time segments
S_{pred}
and ground truth segments
S_{gt}
. To handle ambiguity where a single continuous prediction covers multiple disjoint ground truth segments, we implement a hybrid scoring mechanism. Specifically, if
|S_{pred}|=1
and
|S_{gt}|>1
, the reward is defined as
\max(\text{Coverage Ratio},\text{Span IoU})
, ensuring the model is rewarded for correctly identifying the overall event span. Otherwise, we compute the average IoU across all segment pairs.

Multi-choice Accuracy Reward:
For QA tasks, we strictly parse the predicted option (A/B/C/D) from the
<answering>
block. The reward is a binary indicator function:
r_{\text{acc}}=\mathbb{I}(\text{Pred}=\text{GT})
.

Length Reward (
r_{\text{len}}
).

To prevent the “verbosity tax” often associated with Chain-of-Thought reasoning, we introduce a soft length constraint. Unlike hard truncation, we employ a piecewise linear decay function to gently discourage excessive token generation beyond a target budget.

Let
L
be the generated response length,
L_{max}
be the hard maximum length, and
L_{buffer}
be a soft buffer zone. The target length is defined as
L_{target}=L_{max}-L_{buffer}
. The efficiency reward is calculated as:

r_{\text{len}}(L)=\begin{cases}1.0&\text{if }L\leq L_{target}\\
1.0-\frac{L-L_{target}}{L_{buffer}}&\text{if }L_{target}<L\leq L_{max}\\
0.0&\text{if }L>L_{max}\end{cases}

(8)

This design provides a full reward for concise answers within the target budget and applies a linear penalty only when the model enters the buffer zone, preventing the optimization instability caused by abrupt reward cliffs.

D.2
Frank-Wolfe Algorithm for Solving the Minimum-Norm Problem

To solve the minimum-norm optimization problem in Eq. (
3.3
), we employ the Frank-Wolfe algorithm, also known as the conditional gradient method
(
19
)
, which is particularly well-suited for constrained convex optimization over polytopes such as the probability simplex.

Problem Formulation.

Recall that our objective is to find the optimal weight vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}
that minimizes the squared norm of the aggregated direction in the standardized space:

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{*}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta_{M}}f(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\quad\text{where}\quad f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\left\|\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\|^{2}.

(9)

This is a convex quadratic program over the simplex
\Delta_{M}=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\mid\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}=1,\alpha_{m}\geq 0\}
.

Algorithm Description.

Starting from a uniform initialization
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{1}_{M}
, each iteration
t
proceeds as follows:

Step 1: Gradient Computation.
We compute the gradient of the objective at the current iterate:

\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)})=2\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}^{\top}(\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)}).

(10)

Step 2: Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO).
We solve a linear minimization problem over the simplex:

\mathbf{s}^{(t)}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{s}\in\Delta_{M}}\langle\mathbf{s},\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)})\rangle.

(11)

Since
\Delta_{M}
is a simplex, the solution is simply a one-hot vector placing all mass on the coordinate with the smallest gradient component:

\mathbf{s}^{(t)}=\mathbf{e}_{k^{*}},\quad\text{where}\quad k^{*}=\arg\min_{m}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)})]_{m}.

(12)

Step 3: Exact Line Search.
We compute the update direction
\mathbf{d}^{(t)}=\mathbf{s}^{(t)}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)}
and determine the optimal step size via exact line search. For the quadratic objective, this admits a closed-form solution:

\gamma^{(t)}=\max\left(0,\min\left(1,\frac{-\langle\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)},\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\mathbf{d}^{(t)}\rangle}{\|\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\mathbf{d}^{(t)}\|^{2}}\right)\right).

(13)

If
\|\hat{\mathbf{D}}_{q}\mathbf{d}^{(t)}\|^{2}<\epsilon
(we use
\epsilon=10^{-12}
), we terminate early to avoid numerical instability.

Step 4: Weight Update.
We update the weights via:

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t+1)}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)}+\gamma^{(t)}\mathbf{d}^{(t)}.

(14)

Termination Criteria.

The algorithm terminates when the change in weights falls below a tolerance threshold
\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t+1)}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(t)}\|<\tau
(we use
\tau=10^{-6}
), or when the maximum number of iterations is reached (we use
T_{\max}=50
).

Convergence Guarantees.

The exact line search ensures monotonic decrease of the objective value at every iteration, guaranteeing convergence to the global optimum of this convex problem. In the context of multi-objective optimization, the solution corresponds to a Pareto-stationary point.
