Unique Winning Opening Move in Three-Row Chomp
Erez Sheiner

Abstract
Chomp was introduced by Gale in 1974 [ 4 ] . In the same paper, Gale reported that the 3 × n 3\times n games had been completely analyzed for n ≤ 100 n\leq 100 , with a unique winning first move in every case, and asked whether winning first moves are unique in general. Although the general uniqueness statement is false [ 1 , Section 7.1] , we prove that the three-row uniqueness phenomenon suggested by Gale’s computations holds for all n n : every 3 × n 3\times n Chomp rectangle has exactly one winning opening move. This settles the three-row case of Gale’s 52-year-old first-move uniqueness question. The proof is carried out in the two-variable recurrence introduced by Brouwer, Horváth, Molnár-Sáska, and Szabó [ 1 ] for the function f ⁡ ( q , r ) f(q,r) whose values encode the P P -positions. The main local ingredient is a rightmost-hole principle: if a value p p is absent from the set C ⁡ ( q , r ) C(q,r) but belongs to all corresponding sets C ⁡ ( t , r ) C(t,r) for q < t < p q<t<p , then all intermediate values q + 1 , … , p − 1 q+1,\ldots,p-1 are forced to belong to C ⁡ ( q , r ) C(q,r) . This separates the diagonal values from the starts of constant rows, and yields a partition of the positive integers into the two possible types of winning opening moves.



1
Introduction

Chomp, introduced by Gale
[
4
]
, is played on a finite

n\times m
binary matrix
A
. The game starts with
A_{ij}=1
for all
i,j
,
and a move
(p,q)
is allowed if
A_{pq}=1
. The move
(p,q)
sets

A_{ij}=0
for every
i\geq p
,
j\geq q
. The player forced to make the move

(1,1)
loses. In the three-row case we write a position as

[p,q,r],\qquad p\geq q\geq r\geq 0,

where
p,q,r
are the row lengths in nonincreasing order. A
P
-position is a
position from which the previous player wins, and an
N
-position is a
position from which the next player wins.

A move reducing the second row of
[p,q,r]
to length
a<q
gives

[p,a,\min(a,r)]
; in particular, if
a<r
, the resulting position is

[p,a,a]
. Similarly, reducing the first row to length
a<p
gives

[a,\min(a,q),\min(a,r)]
.

The three-row case has been studied through recurrences, computation, and
renormalization methods. Zeilberger gave an early recurrence-based analysis of
three-row Chomp
[
5
]
. Brouwer, Horváth, Molnár-Sáska,
and Szabó introduced the two-variable function used below and developed the
associated table of
P
-positions
[
1
]
. Friedman
and Landsberg studied the same structure from a nonlinear-dynamics and
renormalization viewpoint
[
2
]
. A recent preprint of
Garg on
4\times n
Chomp also places the three-row case in this context: it
records the computational evidence for uniqueness in
3\times n
Chomp up to

n\leq 100{,}000
and states that a proof had remained out of reach

[
3
]
. The present note provides such a proof directly from the
recurrence.

Theorem 1.1

(Unique opening move)
.

For every positive integer
n
, the initial three-row Chomp rectangle

[n,n,n]

has exactly one winning opening move.

The rest of this note proves the theorem.

2
The recurrence

The following elementary lemma explains the meaning of the function used in
the recurrence.

Lemma 2.1

(Existence and uniqueness of the encoded
P
-position)
.

For every
q\geq r\geq 0
, there is a unique integer
p>r
such that one of the
following holds:

(i)

r<p<q
and
[p,p,r]
is a
P
-position;

(ii)

p\geq q
and
[p,q,r]
is a
P
-position.

In either case, the corresponding
P
-position is obtained from any sufficiently
long position
[k,q,r]
by reducing the first row to length
p
.

Proof.

First observe uniqueness. Suppose
p_{1}<p_{2}
are two integers satisfying the
lemma. If
p_{i}<q
, the position corresponding to
p_{i}
is
[p_{i},p_{i},r]
; if

p_{i}\geq q
, it is
[p_{i},q,r]
.

In all cases, the position corresponding to
p_{1}
is obtained from the
position corresponding to
p_{2}
by reducing the first row to length
p_{1}
.
This is a legal move from one
P
-position to another, impossible.

It remains to prove existence. We argue by induction on
q+r
. The case

q=r=0
is clear: among the one-row positions
[p,0,0]
, the unique

P
-position is
[1,0,0]
.

Now let
q\geq r\geq 0
, and assume the assertion has already been proved for
all pairs
(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
with
q^{\prime}\geq r^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime}+r^{\prime}<q+r
.

Consider first the values of
p
for which
[p,q,r]
can move to a

P
-position by reducing the second row. If the second row is reduced to

a<q
, the resulting position is

[p,a,\min(a,r)].

The lower pair
(a,\min(a,r))
has smaller sum than
(q,r)
. Hence, by the
induction hypothesis, there is at most one value of
p
for which this
resulting position is a
P
-position.

Similarly, if the third row is reduced to
b<r
, the resulting position is

[p,q,b],

and the lower pair
(q,b)
has smaller sum than
(q,r)
. Hence, again by the
induction hypothesis, there is at most one value of
p
for which this
resulting position is a
P
-position.

Thus only finitely many values of
p
are blocked by moves reducing the
second or third row. Choose an integer
p\geq q
which is not one of these
blocked values.

If reducing the first row of
[p,q,r]
to some length
k
with
r<k<p

gives a
P
-position, then existence is proved, since this
k
satisfies the
lemma. Otherwise, no move reducing the first row to a length greater than

r
leads to a
P
-position. A move reducing the first row to a length

1\leq k\leq r
gives the rectangle
[k,k,k]
, which is an
N
-position by the
standard strategy-stealing argument, and the move to length
0
is the losing
move that takes the poisoned square.

By the choice of
p
, no move reducing the second or third row of
[p,q,r]

leads to a
P
-position. Therefore
[p,q,r]
has no move to a
P
-position,
and hence it is itself a
P
-position. Since
p\geq q
, this is alternative
(ii).
∎

We denote the unique integer in Lemma
2.1
by

f(q,r)
. Thus, if

r<f(q,r)<q,

then

[f(q,r),f(q,r),r]

is a
P
-position, while if

f(q,r)\geq q,

then

[f(q,r),q,r]

is a
P
-position. Conversely, every three-row
P
-position is encoded in
this way.

The recurrence introduced by Brouwer et al.
[
1
]
is based
on the following blocked values. When computing
f(q,r)
, a value

p
is blocked if choosing it would give a position with a move to an already
encoded
P
-position. We separate the blocked values according to whether
the move reduces the second row or the third row.

If the second row is reduced to some length
a<q
, then the lower two row
lengths become

a,\min(a,r).

The corresponding encoded
P
-position is therefore given by

f(a,\min(a,r))
. Thus the values blocked by reducing the second row are

R(q,r)=\{f(a,\min(a,r)):0\leq a<q\}.

Equivalently,

R(q,r)=\{f(a,a):0\leq a<r\}\cup\{f(a,r):r\leq a<q\}.

If the third row is reduced to some length
b<r
, then the lower two row
lengths become
q,b
. Hence the values blocked by reducing the third row are

C(q,r)=\{f(q,b):0\leq b<r\}.

Finally, let

B(q,r)=R(q,r)\cup C(q,r).

Thus
B(q,r)
is the set of all values already blocked when computing

f(q,r)
.

The recurrence of Brouwer et al. is

f(q,r)=\begin{cases}f(q-1,r),&q>r\ \text{and}\ f(q-1,r)<q,\\
\operatorname{mex}B(q,r),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1)

where
\operatorname{mex}
denotes the minimum excluded value

\operatorname{mex}(A)=\min\{k>0:k\notin A\}.

We shall call
(q,r)
a mex cell when the second line of
(
1
) applies.

The first line of the recurrence is needed because
B(q,r)
records only
the values blocked by moves reducing the second or third row. It does not
record possible moves reducing the first row. If

k=f(q-1,r)<q,

then
k
already encodes the
P
-position
[k,k,r]
. Hence any position

[p,q,r]
with
p\geq q
has a legal move to this
P
-position by reducing
the first row to length
k
. In such a case recomputing a mex from
B(q,r)

alone may give a value whose corresponding position is actually an

N
-position, because it can still move to
[k,k,r]
. Therefore the
recurrence first checks whether such an old encoded
P
-position is already
reachable by reducing the first row. If so, the value remains

f(q-1,r)
; only otherwise do we compute the new value by the mex rule.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
0\leq r\leq q
and
f(q,r)>q
, then
(q,r)
is a mex cell and

\{1,2,\ldots,q\}\subseteq B(q,r).

Proof.

If the first line of (
1
) applied, then

f(q,r)=f(q-1,r)<q
, contrary to
f(q,r)>q
. Hence

f(q,r)=\operatorname{mex}B(q,r)
. Since this mex is larger than
q
, every positive
integer at most
q
lies in
B(q,r)
.
∎

We shall also use the following elementary consequences of the fact that no
move from a
P
-position can lead to another
P
-position.

Lemma 2.3
.

The following hold.

(a)

For fixed
q
, the values
f(q,0),f(q,1),\ldots,f(q,q)
are distinct.

(b)

If
s<t_{1}<t_{2}<p
and
f(t_{1},s)=p
, then
f(t_{2},s)\neq p
.

(c)

If
f(p,r)=p
, then
p\notin R(q,r)
for every
q<p
.

Proof.

We use only the defining property that no option of a
P
-position is again a

P
-position.

First, by Lemma
2.1
,
f(q,b)>b
for every
b\leq q
.

For (a), suppose
b_{1}<b_{2}\leq q
and
f(q,b_{1})=f(q,b_{2})=p
. Since
p>b_{2}
,
the two encoded
P
-positions differ only by reducing the third row from

b_{2}
to
b_{1}
. This would give a legal move from one
P
-position to another.

For (b), since
p>t_{2}
, the equalities
f(t_{1},s)=p
and
f(t_{2},s)=p
encode
the
P
-positions
[p,t_{1},s]
and
[p,t_{2},s]
. The first is obtained from the
second by reducing the second row. Again this would be a legal move from one

P
-position to another.

For (c), suppose that
f(p,r)=p
. We show that no value contributing to

R(q,r)
, with
q<p
, can be equal to
p
.

If
r\leq a<q<p
and
f(a,r)=p
, then the
P
-position
[p,a,r]
is obtained
from the
P
-position
[p,p,r]
by reducing the second row. This is
impossible.

If
0\leq a<r
and
f(a,a)=p
, then the
P
-position
[p,a,a]
is obtained
from the
P
-position
[p,p,r]
by reducing the second row to length
a
.
This is again impossible. Therefore
p\notin R(q,r)
for every
q<p
.
∎

3
A local blocking principle

The following lemma is the local blocking step used later. Recall that

C(q,r)=\{f(q,b):0\leq b<r\},

so membership in
C(q,r)
records values blocked by reducing the third row.

Lemma 3.1

(Interval blocking)
.

Let
0\leq r<q<p
. Suppose

p\notin C(q,r)

and

p\in C(t,r)\qquad\text{for every }q<t<p.

Then

\{q+1,q+2,\ldots,p-1\}\subseteq C(q,r).

Proof.

Fix
t
with
q<t<p
. Since
p\in C(t,r)
, there is a unique
s_{t}<r
such
that

f(t,s_{t})=p.

The uniqueness follows from Lemma
2.3
(a). Moreover, if

t_{1}<t_{2}
and
s_{t_{1}}=s_{t_{2}}
, then Lemma
2.3
(b) is
contradicted. Hence the rows
s_{t}
are distinct as
t
varies over

q+1,\ldots,p-1
.

For each such
t
, put

u_{t}=f(q,s_{t}).

We claim that

q<u_{t}<p.

(2)

Indeed, consider the
P
-position
[p,t,s_{t}]
. Reducing the second row from

t
to
q
gives the option
[p,q,s_{t}]
. Since this position is an option of a

P
-position, it is an
N
-position; because the game is finite, it therefore
has a move to a
P
-position.

That winning reply cannot be obtained by reducing the second row of

[p,q,s_{t}]
, since the same resulting position would already be reachable from

[p,t,s_{t}]
. It also cannot be obtained by reducing the third row, since that
would give a
P
-position of the form
[p,q,b]
with
b<s_{t}<r
, i.e.

f(q,b)=p
, contradicting
p\notin C(q,r)
.

Therefore the winning reply from
[p,q,s_{t}]
is obtained by reducing the first
row. If that reduction made the first row at most
q
, then the same resulting
position would already be reachable directly from
[p,t,s_{t}]
by reducing the
first row, impossible. Hence the resulting
P
-position has the form

[u,q,s_{t}]
with
q<u<p
. By the definition of
f
, this
u
must be

f(q,s_{t})=u_{t}
. Thus
q<u_{t}<p
, proving (
2
).

The values
u_{t}
are distinct, since the rows
s_{t}
are distinct and the
values
f(q,0),f(q,1),\ldots,f(q,q)
are distinct by
Lemma
2.3
(a). Hence

t\mapsto u_{t}
is an injection from
\{q+1,\ldots,p-1\}
into itself. It is
therefore a bijection, and every value
q+1,\ldots,p-1
occurs as
f(q,s_{t})

for some
s_{t}<r
. This is exactly the asserted inclusion in
C(q,r)
.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Rightmost-hole lemma)
.

Let
0\leq r<q<p
. Suppose

p\notin C(q,r)

and

p\in C(t,r)\qquad\text{for every }q<t<p.

If

f(q,r)>q,

then

f(q,r)\geq p.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, the values
q+1,q+2,\ldots,p-1
lie in

C(q,r)
and hence in
B(q,r)
. By Lemma
2.2
, the hypothesis

f(q,r)>q
implies that
1,2,\ldots,q
also lie in
B(q,r)
, and that

f(q,r)=\operatorname{mex}B(q,r)
. Thus every value
1,2,\ldots,p-1
lies in
B(q,r)
, so
the mex is at least
p
.
∎

4
Separating diagonal values and row-starts

We next prove the following diagonal maximality property.

Lemma 4.1

(Maximum at the diagonal for fixed
q
)
.

For every
q\geq 0
, the diagonal value
f(q,q)
is the largest among the
values

f(q,0),f(q,1),\ldots,f(q,q).

Equivalently,

f(q,q)=\max_{0\leq r\leq q}f(q,r).

In particular,

f(q,q)>q.

Proof.

Suppose, to the contrary, that
f(q,q)
is not the largest among

f(q,0),\ldots,f(q,q)
. Let
f(q,r)
, with
r<q
, be the largest
of these values. By Lemma
2.3
(a), the
q+1
values
are distinct positive integers, so their maximum is greater than
q
.
Thus
f(q,r)>q
. Lemma
2.2
therefore implies that

(q,r)
is a mex cell and

f(q,r)=\operatorname{mex}B(q,r).

For each
s
with
r<s\leq q
, we have
f(q,s)<f(q,r)
. Since

f(q,r)=\operatorname{mex}B(q,r)
, this implies
f(q,s)\in B(q,r)
. It cannot occur in

C(q,r)
, by distinctness for fixed
q
, Lemma
2.3
(a).

It also cannot occur among the values
f(t,t)
with
0\leq t\leq r
. Indeed, if

f(t,t)=f(q,s)=m
for some
t\leq r<s
, then
m>s>t
. The
P
-position encoded
by
f(q,s)=m
has second row of length at least
s
, and reducing that second
row to
t
gives the
P
-position
[m,t,t]
, encoded by
f(t,t)=m
. This is
impossible.

Hence each of the
q-r
values
f(q,r+1),\ldots,f(q,q)
must occur at a
position
f(t,r)
with
r<t<q
. There are only
q-r-1
such positions, a
contradiction. Thus the diagonal entry is maximal. Since the values

f(q,0),f(q,1),\ldots,f(q,q)
are
q+1
distinct positive integers, their
maximum is at least
q+1
, and therefore

f(q,q)>q
.
∎

The following propagation lemma is the main exclusion step.

Lemma 4.2

(Row-start propagation)
.

Assume

f(p,r)=p.

Then, for every
q
with

r<q<p,

one has

p\in C(q,r).

Proof.

Suppose not, and choose the largest
q
with
r<q<p
such that

p\notin C(q,r)
. By maximality,

p\in C(t,r)\qquad\text{for every }q<t<p.

We first show that

f(q,r)>q.

(3)

If
f(q,r)<q
, then the recurrence enters the constant branch at the next
step, since
f(q,r)<q+1
, and so
f(q+1,r)=f(q,r)
. Inductively, the value remains constant as the first argument increases up to

p
, which contradicts
f(p,r)=p
. If

f(q,r)=q
, then again
f(q,r)<q+1
, so the value becomes constant from first argument
q+1
onward, now with
value
q
, and again
f(p,r)\neq p
. Hence
(
3
) holds.

The rightmost-hole lemma now gives

f(q,r)\geq p.

(4)

On the other hand, the value
p
is absent from
B(q,r)
. It is absent from

C(q,r)
by the choice of
q
, and it is absent from
R(q,r)
by
Lemma
2.3
(c), since
q<p
and
f(p,r)=p
.

Since (
3
) makes
(q,r)
a mex cell,
f(q,r)=\operatorname{mex}B(q,r)
. A
mex cannot skip over an absent value
p
; together with (
4
),
this forces

f(q,r)=p.

Since
r<q<p
, this equality encodes the
P
-position
[p,q,r]
. But

f(p,r)=p
encodes the
P
-position
[p,p,r]
. The position
[p,q,r]
is
obtained from
[p,p,r]
by reducing the second row from
p
to
q
, a legal
move from one
P
-position to another. This is impossible. Therefore no such

q
exists.
∎

Define

D=\{f(a,a):a\geq 0\}

and

S=\{p\geq 1:\text{ there exists }r<p\text{ such that }f(p,r)=p\}.

The elements of
D
are the first coordinates of diagonal
P
-positions. The
elements of
S
are the first coordinates at which some row starts to be
constant.

Proposition 4.3
.

The sets
D
and
S
are disjoint.

Proof.

Assume, for a contradiction, that

p=f(a,a)

and also

f(p,r)=p

for some
r<p
.

By Lemma
4.1
,
p=f(a,a)>a
. If
r\geq a
, then the

P
-position
[p,a,a]
is obtained from the
P
-position
[p,p,r]
by reducing
the second row to
a
, impossible. Hence

r<a<p.

Apply Lemma
4.2
with
q=a
. It gives

p\in C(a,r),

so
p=f(a,b)
for some
b<r<a
. But also
p=f(a,a)
, contradicting distinctness for fixed
a
, Lemma
2.3
(a).
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Every positive integer belongs to exactly one of
D
and
S
.

Proof.

Let
p\geq 1
. The rectangle
[p,p,p]
is an
N
-position by the standard
strategy-stealing argument. A winning move from it cannot be a move that cuts
all three rows to a smaller rectangle
[m,m,m]
with
m\geq 1
, since that is
again an
N
-position; the move that takes the poisoned square is losing.
Therefore a winning opening move has one of the following two forms:

[p,a,a]\qquad(a<p),

or

[p,p,r]\qquad(r<p).

In the first case
p=f(a,a)
, so
p\in D
. In the second case
f(p,r)=p
, so

p\in S
. Thus every positive integer belongs to
D\cup S
.

By Proposition
4.3
, the two sets are disjoint. Hence every
positive integer belongs to exactly one of them.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

A move from
[n,n,n]
is winning if and only if it moves to a
P
-position. By
Proposition
4.4
, exactly one of the following alternatives
holds:

(i)

n=f(a,a)
for some
a<n
;

(ii)

f(n,r)=n
for some
r<n
.

The value
a
in (i) is unique: if
n=f(a,a)=f(b,b)
with
a<b
, then
n>b

by Lemma
4.1
, and the
P
-position
[n,a,a]
is obtained
from
[n,b,b]
by reducing the second row. This is impossible. The value
r

in (ii) is unique by distinctness for fixed
n
, Lemma
2.3
(a).

In case (i), the unique winning opening move is to
[n,a,a]
. In case (ii),
the unique winning opening move is to
[n,n,r]
. Therefore
[n,n,n]
has
exactly one winning opening move.
∎
