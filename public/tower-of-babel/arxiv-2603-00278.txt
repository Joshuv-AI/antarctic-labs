Abstract




Every great magic trick consists of three parts or acts. The first part is called “The Pledge”. The magician shows you something ordinary…Perhaps he asks you to inspect it to see if it is indeed real, unaltered, normal. But of course… it probably isn’t. The second act is called “The Turn”. The magician takes the ordinary something and makes it do something extraordinary…But you wouldn’t clap yet. Because making something disappear isn’t enough; you have to bring it back. That’s why every magic trick has a third act, the hardest part, the part we call “The Prestige”.

— Cutter (Michael Caine) in Christopher Nolan’s “The Prestige”

1
The Pledge

Let
m
and
n
be positive integers.
Suppose you have a deck consisting of
mn
different cards numbered
0,1,\ldots,{mn-1}
from top to bottom.
Consider the following two ways of dealing the cards into an
m\times n
rectangular grid:

(R)
:
Row deal.

Deal the cards by rows, dealing the
m
rows from top to bottom and the
n
cards within each row from left to right.

Figure 1:
Example of a row deal with
m=3
and
n=5

(C)
:
Column deal.

Deal the cards by columns, dealing the
n
columns from left to right and the
m
cards within each column from top to bottom.

Figure 2:
Example of a column deal with
m=3
and
n=5

We label the rows of the grid by
0,1\ldots,m-1
and the columns by
0,1,\ldots,n-1
.
The map which takes (R) to (C) yields a permutation
\gamma
of the set
S=\{0,1,\ldots,mn-1\}
of cards by replacing the card in position
(i,j)
of (R) with the card in position
(i,j)
of (C).
(We can think of
\gamma
as first picking up the cards by rows and then dealing them out by columns.)

Now for a combinatorial puzzle: what is the
sign
of the permutation
\gamma
?
(If you’d like to think about it before seeing the answer, stop reading now!)

The answer is that

\operatorname{sign}(\gamma)=(-1)^{\binom{m}{2}\cdot\binom{n}{2}}.

(1)

To prove (
1
), recall that the sign of a permutation
\sigma
of a totally ordered finite set
X

is equal to
(-1)^{\iota({\sigma})}
, where
\iota(\sigma)
is the number of
inversions
of
\sigma
.
(An inversion is an ordered pair
(x,y)\in X^{2}
with
x<y
and
\sigma(x)>\sigma(y)
.)

Exercise
.

Show that a pair
(x,y)
of cards in (R) form an inversion of
\gamma
if and only if
y
lies below and to the left of
x
.

The number of inversions of
\gamma
is therefore
\binom{m}{2}\cdot\binom{n}{2}
,
since each pair consisting of a
2
-element subset
\{i,i^{\prime}\}
of
\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
and a 2-element subset
\{j,j^{\prime}\}
of
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
gives rise
to a unique inversion
(x,x^{\prime})
, where
x=(i,j)
and
x^{\prime}=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
, by ordering the elements so that
i<i^{\prime}
and
j>j^{\prime}
.

If
m
and
n
are both odd, then (
1
) simplifies: in this case, we obtain

\operatorname{sign}(\gamma)=(-1)^{\frac{(m-1)(n-1)}{4}}.

(2)

In other words,
\operatorname{sign}(\gamma)=1
if either
m\equiv 1\pmod{4}
or
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
,
and
\operatorname{sign}(\gamma)=-1
if
m\equiv n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.

Formula (
2
) may bring to mind Gauss’s Law of Quadratic Reciprocity.
Is this just a coincidence? Continue on, dear reader…

2
The Turn

We now assume that
m
and
n
are
relatively prime
, in addition to being odd and positive.
This gives us a third way of dealing the cards into an
m\times n
rectangular grid:

(D)
:
Diagonal deal.

Starting in the top left corner, deal the cards diagonally down and to the right, wrapping around from bottom to top or right to left whenever necessary.

1

1

For the topologists in the audience, we are actually dealing the cards onto a torus.

Figure 3:
Example of a diagonal deal with
m=3
and
n=5

The map which takes (R) to (D) yields a permutation
\alpha
of
S
.
(We can think of
\alpha
as first picking up the cards by rows and then dealing them out diagonally.)

Now for another combinatorial puzzle: what is the sign of
\alpha
?
(Again, if you’d like to think about it before seeing the answer, stop reading now!)

We claim that
\operatorname{sign}(\alpha)
is equal to the sign of the permutation of
\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
induced by multiplication by
n
.
In other words, writing
{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{n}{m}}

for the sign of this permutation, we are claiming that

\operatorname{sign}(\alpha)={\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{n}{m}}.

(3)

To prove (
3
), we begin with the following observations:

Exercise
.

1.

Show that the card in position
(i,j)
of (D) is the unique card congruent to
i
modulo
m
and to
j
modulo
n
.

2.

Show that the card in position
(i,j)
of (R) is
ni+j
.

3.

Show that
\alpha
permutes each individual column of (R), and
\alpha^{-1}
restricted to column
j
sends the card in row
i
to the unique card congruent to
ni+j
modulo
m
.

It follows that
\alpha^{-1}
is a product of
n
permutations, each of which can be expressed as a composition of multiplication by
n
modulo
m
and translation by
j
modulo
m
.
Since
m
is odd and translation by
j
modulo
m
is a product of
k
-cycles with
k
dividing
m
, it follows that translation by
j
modulo
m
is an even permutation for every
j
.
Therefore, since the sign of a permutation is a homomorphism, we have
\operatorname{sign}(\alpha)=\operatorname{sign}(\alpha^{-1})={\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{n}{m}}^{n}
. Since
n
is also odd, this establishes (
3
).

We now swap the roles of rows and columns in the previous argument.
The map which takes (D) to (C) yields a permutation
\beta
of
S
.
Since
\operatorname{sign}(\beta)=\operatorname{sign}(\beta^{-1})
and
\beta^{-1}
can be obtained from
\alpha
by swapping the roles of
m
and
n
, it follows by symmetry that

\operatorname{sign}(\beta)={\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{m}{n}}
.
And since
\beta\circ\alpha=\gamma
, we have

\operatorname{sign}(\beta)\cdot\operatorname{sign}(\alpha)=\operatorname{sign}(\gamma),

i.e.,

{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{m}{n}}\cdot{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{n}{m}}=(-1)^{\frac{(m-1)(n-1)}{4}}.

(4)

Formula (
4
) is even
more
reminiscent of the Law of Quadratic Reciprocity!
But wait…what is the connection to perfect squares modulo primes?

Now that you are hooked, dear reader, you have no choice but to continue…

3
The Prestige

Let
p
be an odd prime, and let
a
be a positive integer not divisible by
p
. Recall that the
Legendre symbol

{\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{a}{p}}

is defined by

{\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{a}{p}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $a$ is a square modulo $p$}\\
-1&\text{if $a$ is not a square modulo $p$}.\\
\end{cases}

The connection between the “Zolotarev symbol”
{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{a}{p}}
and the Legendre symbol
{\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{a}{p}}
is given by:

Zolotarev’s Lemma
.

If
p
is an odd prime and
a
is a positive integer not divisible by
p
, then

{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{a}{p}}={\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{a}{p}}.

To prove Zolotarev’s Lemma, we first recall Gauss’s
primitive root theorem
: if
p
is an odd prime, the multiplicative group
(\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z})^{\times}
is
cyclic
.
It follows that
{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{\cdot}{p}}
is a surjective homomorphism from the multiplicative group
(\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z})^{\times}
to
\{\pm 1\}
;
surjectivity follows from the fact that if
g
is a primitive root mod
p
(i.e., a cyclic generator of
(\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z})^{\times}
) then

{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{g}{p}}
is a
(p-1)
-cycle and thus has signature
-1
.
The kernel of
{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{\cdot}{p}}
is therefore a subgroup of
(\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z})^{\times}
of index
2
,
but the only such subgroup is the group of quadratic residues. Thus

{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{\cdot}{p}}
coincides with the Legendre symbol
{\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{\cdot}{p}}
.

Combining Zolotarev’s Lemma with (
4
) yields:

Law of Quadratic Reciprocity
.

If
p
and
q
are distinct odd primes, then

\boxed{\displaystyle{\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{p}{q}}\cdot{\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{q}{p}}=(-1)^{\frac{(p-1)(q-1)}{4}}.}

Closing Credits

Gauss found eight different proofs of the Law of Quadratic Reciprocity over the course of his life.
There are (at the present moment) 345 different proofs listed in Franz Lemmermeyer’s database
[
2
]
.
The proof I’ve given here is essentially a creative reimagining and dealgebraization of a beautiful 1872 proof due to Egor Ivanovich Zolotarev (1847–1878)
[
3
]
, which is not as well-known as it ought to be. My “card dealing”
interpretation of Zolotarev’s proof first appeared on my blog in 2013
[
1
]
.

Director’s Commentary

1.

Zolotarev defended his doctoral thesis in 1874 and was appointed as an “extraordinary professor” by the Russian Academy of Sciences in 1876, but his meteoric rise in the math world ended abruptly when he was run over by a train at the age of 31.

2.

If we identify the set
\{0,1,2,\ldots,mn-1\}
with the set
\mathbf{Z}/mn\mathbf{Z}
of integers modulo
mn
, the map
\gamma
is just the canonical ring isomorphism from
\mathbf{Z}/mn\mathbf{Z}
to
\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}\times\mathbf{Z}/n\mathbf{Z}
afforded by the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

3.

Zolotarev’s Lemma generalizes to the statement that if
m,n
are relatively prime odd positive integers then
{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{m}{n}}
is equal to the Jacobi symbol
{\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{m}{n}}
, and the proof above then gives quadratic reciprocity for the Jacobi symbol (which is used for rapid computation of Legendre symbols).

Director’s Cut Bonus Footage

We show how to use similar arguments to obtain the two “supplements” to the Law of Quadratic Reciprocity, which give formulas for
{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{2}{n}}
and
{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{-1}{n}}
, respectively.

Let
n\geq 3
be an odd integer and put
m=\frac{n-1}{2}
. We now work with a deck of

n-1
cards labeled
1,2,\dots,n-1
, which we deal into a
2\times m
rectangular array in three different ways as follows:

(R)
:
Row deal.
Deal the cards by rows, as before.

(C)
:
Column deal.
Deal the cards by columns, as before.

(Z)
:
Zigzag deal.
Deal the first card into the lower-left corner, then alternate between moving up and moving down and to the right.
Observe that (Z) can be obtained from (C) by simply swapping the two entries in each column.

Let
\gamma:(R)\to(C)
as before, and let
\alpha:(R)\to(Z)
,
\beta:(Z)\to(C)
.
Then
\gamma=\beta\circ\alpha
, hence

\operatorname{sign}(\gamma)=\operatorname{sign}(\beta)\cdot\operatorname{sign}(\alpha).

By our previous calculation (
1
), we have

\operatorname{sign}(\gamma)=(-1)^{\binom{2}{2}\binom{m}{2}}=(-1)^{\binom{m}{2}}.

Since
(Z)
is obtained from
(C)
by swapping the entries in each of the
m
columns,

\beta
is a product of
m
transpositions and thus

\operatorname{sign}(\beta)=(-1)^{m}.

By direct inspection, we see that
\alpha
corresponds
to multiplication by
2
modulo
n
on the set
\{1,2,\dots,n-1\}
.
Thus
\operatorname{sign}(\alpha)={\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{2}{n}}
.

Combining the above identities gives

{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{2}{n}}=\operatorname{sign}(\alpha)=\operatorname{sign}(\beta^{-1})\operatorname{sign}(\gamma)=\operatorname{sign}(\beta)\operatorname{sign}(\gamma)=(-1)^{m+\binom{m}{2}}=(-1)^{\frac{m(m+1)}{2}}=(-1)^{\frac{n^{2}-1}{8}}.

By Zolotarev’s lemma, if
n=p
is an odd prime then
{\genfrac{[}{]}{}{}{2}{p}}={\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{2}{p}}
,
so we recover the classical supplemental law:

\boxed{\displaystyle{\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{2}{p}}=(-1)^{\frac{p^{2}-1}{8}}.}
