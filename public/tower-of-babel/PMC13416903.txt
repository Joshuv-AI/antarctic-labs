Angiogenic biomarker concentrations and their relation to coronary atherosclerosis in women with a history of preeclampsia: Findings from the CPH‐PRECIOUS study
Authors: Signe Højstrup Bessing, Maria Guldbrandt Hauge, Jesper James Linde, Marianne Johansen, Anne Ersbøll, Klaus Fuglsang Kofoed, Andreas Fuchs, Per Ejlstrup Sigvardsen, Finn Gustafsson, Mille Kirk, Line Rode, Peter Damm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPreeclampsia is known to be associated with a two‐ to fourfold elevated risk of developing cardiovascular disease in the first 15 years following pregnancy. In this cross‐sectional study of 892 women with prior preeclampsia, we aimed to assess differences in circulating angiogenic biomarker concentrations according to CT‐assessed coronary atherosclerosis in women up to 28 years after an index pregnancy complicated by preeclampsia.Material and MethodsWomen were identified from clinical databases consisting of data from all deliveries within the Capital Region of Denmark from 1995 onwards. Women with prior preeclampsia and a current age between 35 and 55 years were included from the CPH‐PRECIOUS cohort. The primary outcome was differences in circulating angiogenic biomarkers according to CT‐assessed coronary atherosclerosis.ResultsThe median time since the pregnancy complicated by preeclampsia was 12.5 years (range: 0–28 years). PlGF concentrations were significantly higher in women with coronary atherosclerosis (9.6 pg/mL [IQR 4.9–12.6] vs. 7.4 pg/mL [IQR 3.9–11.3], p = 0.001), and subsequently a lower calculated sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio (4.2 [IQR 3.1–6.3] vs. 5.0 [IQR 3.6–6.9], p = 0.001) was found. No differences in sFlt‐1 concentrations were observed. Higher PlGF concentrations were significantly associated with coronary atherosclerosis; however, associations were attenuated in adjusted analysis.ConclusionsThis is the first large study to investigate long‐term differences in circulating angiogenic biomarker concentrations after preeclampsia, highlighting the complex relationship between angiogenic biomarkers, preeclampsia, and later atherosclerotic burden.

INTRODUCTION

Preeclampsia is a major contributor to pregnancy‐associated morbidity and mortality 1 , 2 and is furthermore associated with a two‐ to fourfold elevated risk of developing cardiovascular disease (CVD) in the first 15 years following pregnancy. 3 This increased risk persists independently of other common cardiovascular risk factors. 4 , 5 Preeclampsia and CVD share risk factors and pathophysiological pathways characterized by inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. However, the underlying mechanisms linking preeclampsia to CVD remain poorly understood. 6

Systemic endothelial dysfunction is a prominent component in preeclampsia, 7 especially in early‐onset cases, driven by imbalances between circulating antiangiogenic factors, such as soluble fms‐like tyrosine kinase‐1 (sFlt‐1) and proangiogenic factors as placental growth factor (PlGF). 8 Changes in these angiogenic biomarkers have been extensively studied during pregnancy. In women with preeclampsia, second and third trimester concentrations of plasma sFlt‐1 are elevated, binding and reducing plasma concentrations of PlGF, and the ratio sFlt‐1/PlGF is now used clinically as a predictor of preeclampsia when elevated and a clinical rule‐out tool when the ratio is low. 9 , 10 Additionally, sFlt‐1 and PlGF play a role in atherogenesis, and several studies have reported higher concentrations of circulating sFlt‐1 and PlGF across various cohorts of non‐pregnant patients with cardiovascular disease, and associations with adverse cardiovascular outcomes have been described. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

Prior studies investigating postpartum changes in circulating concentrations of sFlt‐1 and PlGF following a pregnancy complicated by preeclampsia report alterations within the first year following pregnancy. 15 , 16 However, the potential long‐term alterations and associations with CVD are yet to be investigated.

We hypothesized that an angiogenic imbalance persists in women with prior preeclampsia, especially in cases of early‐onset preeclampsia, and that this is associated with the development of coronary atherosclerosis. In this present study, the objective was to investigate concentrations of sFlt‐1 and PlGF in addition to the sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio, according to the presence or absence of CT‐assessed coronary atherosclerosis, in women up to 28 years after a pregnancy complicated by preeclampsia. Additionally, we explored differences in circulating angiogenic biomarker concentrations according to the onset of preeclampsia, potentially revealing insight into the underlying pathophysiology.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design

This is a cross‐sectional study based on data from the CPH‐PRECIOUS (the Copenhagen Preeclampsia and Cardiovascular Disease) study. 4 Women with prior preeclampsia were included with a median time interval of 12.5 (range 0–28) years following the index pregnancy complicated by preeclampsia. Clinical variables, blood samples, and coronary CT angiography (CTA) were collected and performed at the time of study inclusion. We investigated concentrations of circulating angiogenic biomarkers in women with prior preeclampsia, stratified by the presence of coronary CTA assessed atherosclerosis, defined as any coronary plaque on coronary CTA or a coronary artery calcium (CAC) score >0 in the case of a nondiagnostic coronary CTA.

Study population

Women with prior preeclampsia and current age between 35 and 55 years were initially identified in a hospital‐based, validated clinical database consisting of data from all deliveries within the Capital Region of Denmark from 1995 onwards, 17 comprising approximately one‐third of all deliveries in Denmark. Women were identified using the World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes assigned on hospitalization (Appendix S1). Study participants were randomly selected and invited by letter, with an acceptance rate of 29.2%. The invitation process and the obtainment of the study cohort are thoroughly described elsewhere. 4 , 18 In this sub‐study, patients without measurement of angiogenic biomarkers were excluded (Figure 1 and Table S1). All participants (n = 892) were examined between May 2019 and March 2020 (Figure 1).

Flowchart displaying how the study population was derived after the invitation process was completed for the CPH‐PRECIOUS study cohort. PIGF, placental growth factor; sFlt‐1, soluble fms‐like tyrosine kinase 1.

Clinical variables

Study participants underwent clinical examination including measurements of height, weight, hip and waist circumferences, blood pressure and electrocardiogram. Non‐fasting venous blood samples were collected, and a coronary CTA was performed. Additionally, comprehensive questionnaires were completed, including information on maternal characteristics during pregnancy, ethnicity, educational level, menopause status, parity, and cardiovascular risk factors. All information was collected at the time of the coronary CTA. Hypertension was defined as self‐reported treatment with antihypertensive medication or systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg on the day of examination.

Subtypes of preeclampsia were defined according to ICD‐10 codes assigned upon hospitalization (Appendix S1). Onset of preeclampsia was defined according to gestational age at delivery, with early‐onset defined as <34 + 0 weeks and late‐onset as ≥34 + 0 weeks. Severe preeclampsia, including HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets), was defined as ICD‐10: O14.1, O14.2, O15.0, and Z358Q, and mild–moderate preeclampsia as ICD‐10: 014.0 (Appendix S1).

Laboratory results including plasma concentrations of low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, total cholesterol, hemoglobin A1c level (HbA1c), plasma N‐terminal pro B‐type natriuretic peptide (proBNP), plasma creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urine albumin‐creatinine ratio (UACR) were obtained on the same day per standard hospital protocol. Hypercholesterolemia was defined as self‐reported treatment with lipid‐lowering medication or plasma LDL concentration >3.0 mmol/L on the day of examination.

Angiogenic biomarkers: PlGF, sFlt‐1 and sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio

Angiogenic biomarkers included PlGF, sFlt‐1, and the sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio. Blood samples were collected on the day of clinical examination. Serum was stored at −80°C, and concentrations of PlGF and sFlt‐1 were measured in thawed samples in August to September 2024 using the BRAHMS PlGF and BRAHMS sFlt‐1 KRYPTOR plus KRYPTOR automated immunofluorescent homogeneous assays (ThermoFisher Scientific GmbH, Hennigsdorf, Germany) on a BRAHMS KRYPTOR compact PLUS platform (ThermoFisher Scientific GmbH). The BRAHMS PlGF KRYPTOR assay is calibrated against an internal reference standard prepared from recombinant human PlGF. The assay has a limit of detection (LOD) of 3.6 pg/mL, a limit of quantification (LOQ) of 6.9 pg/mL, and the measuring range is 3.6–7000 pg/mL. CVs were 17% at ∼13 pg/mL and 6% at ∼27 pg/mL. The BRAHMS sFlt‐1 KRYPTOR assay is calibrated against an internal reference standard prepared from recombinant human sFlt‐1. The assay has a LOD of 22 pg/mL, a LOQ of 29 pg/mL, and the measuring range is 22–90 000 pg/mL. Interassay coefficients of variation (CV) were 8% at ∼45 pg/mL. All measurements below the LOD were displayed as LOD/2.

Cardiac CT image protocol and analysis

Cardiac CT imaging protocol

The coronary CTA was performed using a 320‐multislice CT scanner (Acquilion One, Canon). Oral metoprolol was administered for any patient 60–90 min prior to scan if heart rate >55 beats per minute and systolic blood pressure >110 mmHg and no contraindications, according to a standardized protocol. Immediately before image acquisition, 0.8 mg sublingual nitroglycerin was administered. Firstly, non‐contrast images were obtained to assess the CAC score. Then, during acquisition, 50–120 mL of contrast agent (Visipaque, GE Healthcare) was injected, followed by a saline flush. Helical scan data were obtained with prospective electrocardiography gating. All images were transferred to an external workstation (Vitrea 6.9, Vital Images Inc).

Cardiac CT analysis

CAC score was quantified by means of the Agatston scoring method over the entire coronary tree (17 segments). The contrast‐enhanced coronary CTA was analyzed for the presence of atherosclerotic plaques, and the extent of luminal diameter stenosis was reported according to guidelines from the Society of Cardiac Computed Tomography. 19 A nondiagnostic coronary CTA scan was defined as two nondiagnostic proximal segments or five nondiagnostic segments in total. All CT images were analyzed blinded as previously described. 4

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are tested by Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test and presented as mean (± standard deviation (SD)) or median [interquartile range] according to baseline distribution. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (%). To address the potential correlation between prior preeclampsia and coronary atherosclerosis, we performed stepwise multivariable logistic regression models, adjusted for age (Model 1) and additional potential confounders (Model 2), including diabetes, hypertension, smoking, body mass index (BMI) and eGFR. Potential confounders were chosen a priori based on existing evidence. Sensitivity analysis was performed according to the 90‐ and 75‐percentile to investigate high vs. low concentrations. Subgroup analysis was performed according to the onset of preeclampsia. In 197 women (22.1%), the PlGF concentration was below the LOD value of 3.6 pg/mL. In 81 women (9.1%), the sFlt‐1 concentration was below the LOD value of 22.0 pg/mL. All measurements <LOD were set at the value of LOD/2, implicitly assuming a uniform distribution between 0 and the LOD. 20 To accommodate for this right‐skewed distribution when performing regression models, women were divided into quartiles according to angiogenic biomarker concentrations. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R studio (R version 4.1.3, Inc., Boston, MA).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics and coronary CTA findings

The study population consisted of 892 women, all with a history of preeclampsia. Baseline demographics at the time of preeclampsia are shown in Table 1. Median age at the time of delivery was 32.0 years [IQR 29.0–35.0]. In total, 44.6% had suffered from mild–moderate preeclampsia, 30.0% from severe preeclampsia, and 5% from HELLP. In 20.3% of the cases, preeclampsia was unspecified. Overall, 15.2% had early‐onset preeclampsia. The burden of potentially modifiable lifestyle risk factors was overall low at the time of delivery (Table 1). Baseline characteristics from the time of study inclusion, a median of 12.5 [IQR 8.0–18.0] years after preeclampsia, are shown in Table 2 stratified by the presence of coronary atherosclerosis, which was identified in 207 (23.2%) of the women. Women with coronary atherosclerosis were older compared to women without coronary atherosclerosis (48.0 [IQR 43.0–52.0] vs. 44.0 [IQR 39.0–48.0] years, p < 0.001). They also had a higher burden of cardiovascular risk factors, with more hypertension (48.8% vs. 34.6%, p < 0.001), hypercholesterolemia (57.1% vs. 41.1%, p < 0.001) and higher BMI (27 [IQR 23–31] vs. 25 [IQR 23–30] kg/m2, p < 0.05). There were no differences in the presence of diabetes, smoking or physical exercise.

Concentrations of circulating angiogenic biomarker: sFlt‐1, PlGF and sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio

PlGF concentrations were higher in women with coronary atherosclerosis, compared to women without coronary atherosclerosis (9.6 pg/mL [IQR 4.9–12.6] vs. 7.4 pg/mL [IQR 3.9–11.3], p = 0.001), whereas sFlt‐1 concentrations were similar in the two groups (Table 2 and Figure 2). The sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio was lower in women with vs. without coronary atherosclerosis (4.2 [IQR 3.1–6.3] vs. 5.0 [IQR 3.6–6.9], p = 0.001).

Concentrations of angiogenic biomarkers according to presence (blue) or absence (yellow) of coronary atherosclerosis (n = 892). p‐values are displayed on the figure. PIGF, placental growth factor; sFlt‐1, soluble fms‐like tyrosine kinase 1.

Higher PlGF concentrations (highest vs. lowest quartile) were associated with coronary atherosclerosis in crude models with an OR of 1.88, 95% Cl 1.21–2.95, p < 0.01. The association was attenuated and not statistically significant in the adjusted analysis. No associations were found for sFlt‐1 concentrations in a crude, age‐adjusted (Model 1) and multivariable‐adjusted (Model 2) logistic regression model (all p > 0.05) (Table 3).

To further investigate the relation of high versus low angiogenic biomarker concentrations, sensitivity analyses comparing the >75‐ versus ≤75‐percentile, and the >90‐ versus ≤90‐percentile, respectively, were performed (Table 3). Higher versus lower PlGF concentrations were associated with coronary atherosclerosis with an OR of 1.59, 95% Cl 1.12–2.23, p < 0.001 and 1.67, 95% Cl 1.03–2.65, p < 0.01, respectively. However, the associations were attenuated in age‐ and multivariable‐adjusted analysis (all p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Additionally, compared to the reference (lowest quartile), higher quartiles of the sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio were significantly associated with reduced odds of coronary atherosclerosis in crude, age‐ (Model 1) and multivariable‐adjusted model (Model 2). Results remained significant in a sensitivity analysis comparing the >25‐ versus ≤25‐percentile (Table 3).

Coronary atherosclerosis, angiogenic biomarker concentrations and onset of preeclampsia

A stratified analysis was performed to explore potential relations with the onset of preeclampsia (Table 4). Among the total of 135 women with early‐onset of preeclampsia, PlGF concentrations were significantly higher in women with coronary atherosclerosis (n = 40) compared to women without coronary atherosclerosis (n = 95) (10.5 pg/mL [IQR 6.3–13.5] vs. 7.4 [IQR 3.8–10.8], p = 0.008). No alterations were observed in sFlt‐1 concentrations (p < 0.059). A similar finding was observed in women with late‐onset preeclampsia with PlGF concentrations of 8.8 pg/mL [IQR 4.8–12.2] in n = 167 women with coronary atherosclerosis versus 7.4 pg/mL [IQR 3.9–11.4] in n = 588 women without coronary atherosclerosis, p = 0.017.

A subgroup analysis of women with early‐onset preeclampsia (n = 135, Table S2) was performed to explore potential associations between higher versus lower concentrations of angiogenic biomarkers and coronary atherosclerosis. The PlGF >75‐ versus ≤75‐percentile concentration was significantly associated with coronary atherosclerosis in crude and age‐adjusted analysis (OR 2.85, 95% CI 1.26–8.48, p < 0.05 and OR 2.45, 95% Cl 1.07–6.02), p < 0.05. No association was found in the multivariable‐adjusted model, or in the sensitivity analysis comparing >90‐ versus ≤90‐percentile. No association was found for sFlt‐1 and sFlt1‐/PlGF ratio.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first large clinical cohort study to evaluate concentrations of circulating angiogenic biomarkers several years following preeclampsia. We report that PlGF concentrations were significantly higher in women with CT‐assessed coronary atherosclerosis following preeclampsia. This finding was consistent irrespective of the time of onset of preeclampsia. Additionally, we report higher vs. lower quartiles of the sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio to be associated with lower odds of coronary atherosclerosis. These findings extend the relationship between angiogenic markers, preeclampsia, and later atherosclerotic burden.

In preeclampsia, second‐ and third‐trimester sFlt‐1 concentrations are usually abnormally elevated, and PlGF reduced, leading to a high sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio. 10 These alterations have been found to persist within the first year following preeclampsia. 15 , 16

PlGF stimulates angiogenesis, 10 is highly expressed in pathological conditions with tissue ischemia and inflammation, 21 and increased concentrations have been reported to be associated with both increased short‐ and long‐term risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including cardiac death, in a cohort of patients with acute coronary syndrome (n = 3369), including both men and women. 22 Prior studies in asymptomatic women (n = 32 826) report elevated concentrations of PlGF to be associated with chronic coronary syndromes during a follow‐up period of 14 years. 23

Taken together, these findings support an interplay between PlGF and inflammation. PlGF may modulate inflammatory responses and vascular remodeling, but it may also primarily reflect underlying inflammatory and atherogenic activity. Thus, elevated PlGF could be both a marker of vascular inflammation and a potential contributor to early atherosclerotic plaque development. We extend on prior findings by reporting that, in this population of women with prior preeclampsia, PlGF concentrations were significantly higher among those with the presence vs. absence of coronary atherosclerosis. In a subgroup analysis, results remained consistent, with elevated concentrations of PlGF in women with the presence of coronary atherosclerosis when stratifying according to the time of onset of preeclampsia. In our analysis, differences in PlGF concentrations were observed for both continuous and dichotomic crude analysis. However, results were attenuated in multivariable‐adjusted analysis (adjusted for age, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, BMI, and eGFR at follow‐up), likely reflecting that other biological factors confound or modulate the potential association between pregnancy‐associated imbalances in PlGF concentrations and later cardiovascular conditions. One could further hypothesize that unmeasured comorbidities may influence the PlGF concentrations, as PlGF is expressed in various types of cells, for example, heart, lung, thyroid, liver, skeletal muscle and bone. Additionally, we report that higher vs. the lowest quartiles of the sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio are associated with lower odds of coronary atherosclerosis, also in multivariable analysis. One likely explanation of these findings is the higher PlGF concentrations, causing alterations in the sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio.

It is well established that sFlt‐1 is implicated in the development of atherosclerosis, 24 , 25 and it is consistently reported to be associated with major adverse cardiovascular events and all‐cause mortality when elevated, across various cohorts of non‐pregnant patients with chronic coronary syndromes, 10 , 26 acute coronary syndrome 27 and heart failure, 10 , 13 , 28 , 29 respectively.

Few studies have investigated the role of sFlt‐1 in women outside of pregnancy. One large population‐based cohort study (n = 3292) reported higher concentrations of sFlt‐1 to be associated with subclinical aortic atherosclerosis and an increased risk of cardiovascular events, including cardiovascular death, during a 12‐year follow‐up. However, this association was not significant in the sensitivity analysis comprising only women, and information on preeclampsia was not available. 11 Smaller observational studies (n = 34 and n = 84, respectively) comparing women with and without prior preeclampsia reported no alterations in sFlt‐1 concentrations 7–9 years postpartum. 30 , 31 These studies did not report on any long‐term clinical outcomes. Similarly, we did not observe differences in concentrations of sFlt‐1 in this large cohort of women with prior preeclampsia when comparing groups according to absence or presence of coronary atherosclerosis. This could reflect how circulating concentrations of sFlt‐1 relatively quickly return to index levels following preeclampsia, and subsequently, that sFlt‐1 does not seem to be involved in the potential long‐term vascular dysfunction following preeclampsia but rather reflects more acute or transient physiological processes rather than long‐term post‐pregnancy changes.

Given these complexities, preeclampsia may identify women with an underlying susceptibility in angiogenic/vascular pathways. Later‐life PlGF, and maybe not to the same extent sFlt‐1, concentrations might represent the chronic expression of these shared pathways, which are not unique to pregnancy, but may have been “unmasked” or stressed during the preeclamptic pregnancy.

This large clinical cohort study is the first to investigate concentrations of angiogenic biomarkers and their relation to coronary atherosclerosis up to 28 years following preeclampsia. Data were collected prospectively with a high overall completeness. Further, measurements of coronary atherosclerosis were evaluated by coronary CTA, providing high sensitivity. However, several limitations must be considered when results are interpreted. The design of the study precludes causal interference and a control group without preeclampsia was not available. The acceptance rate for study participants for the CPH‐PRECIOUS cohort was 29.2%, introducing selection bias due to non‐participation limiting external validation. Further data only comprised single‐time measurements of angiogenic biomarker concentrations. Data on hormone therapy were not available, as information on menopause status was determined by questionnaire. Menopause status is strongly correlated with age and to avoid potential multicollinearity, this was not included as a potential confounder.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that women with prior preeclampsia had significantly higher concentrations of PlGF when CT‐assessed coronary atherosclerosis was present years following the index pregnancy, whereas no difference was found for concentrations of sFlt‐1. Hence, a higher ratio of sFlt‐1/PlGF was associated with lower odds for coronary atherosclerosis. Our findings add information to the complex interplay between preeclampsia and later coronary atherosclerosis, providing insights into the underlying pathophysiology. With this being the first study to investigate changes in circulating angiogenic biomarker concentrations up to 28 years after an index pregnancy complicated by preeclampsia, results should be confirmed and expanded to studies preferably with serial measurements of angiogenic biomarkers over time.
