 1. OR-SAGE TOOL BACKGROUND

The OR-SAGE tool is designed to use industry-accepted practices in screening sites and then employ the
proper array of data sources through the considerable computational capabilities of GIS technology
available at ORNL. The tool was developed to screen the potential for NPP siting on a national and
regional basis. However, because of the tool granularity, it is often focused specifically on the immediate
area around user sites of interest. If data center siting parameters can be added to OR-SAGE, the ability to
evaluate data center siting on a localized scale will be beneficial.1
More than 60 data sets have been collected and processed by ORNL to develop exclusionary, avoidance,
and suitability criteria for screening sites for a variety of power generation types, including nuclear power
plants. Available site evaluation parameters include population density, slope, seismic activity, proximity
to cooling-water sources, proximity to hazard facilities, avoidance of protected lands and floodplains,
susceptibility to landslide hazards, and many others. All siting parameters should be considered as flags to
inform siting decisions and should not be used to rule in or rule out any NPP site. Once data center siting
parameters are identified, appropriate data sets will be collected and processed.
The OR-SAGE process is very versatile. Essentially, OR-SAGE is a visual, relational database. The
database partitions the contiguous United States, a total of 720 million hectares (~1.8 billion acres), into
100-m by 100-m (1 hectare or ~2.5 acre) cells. The database is tracking just under 700 million individual
land cells. Successive suitability criterion is applied to each cell in the database. User-specified thresholds
can be applied to each siting parameter data layer. In this manner, a variety of scenarios can be quickly
and thoroughly evaluated. Data can be added and/or revised within OR-SAGE to address user interests.
Siting security assessment capability is currently being added to OR-SAGE. Security is expected to be of
concern at data centers whether it is collocated with a nuclear power generating technology or not. If data
center is collocated with a nuclear power generating source, the security threat attractiveness level of both
will likely increase. It will be of additional benefit if a potential data center site is also assessed for
security vulnerability.

1.1 Methodology

OR-SAGE is essentially a dynamic visualization database that has matured with support from the Electric
Power Research Institute (EPRI) and the US Department of Energy (DOE) Office of Nuclear Energy
(DOE-NE). Specifically, the DOE-NE Systems Analysis and Integration Campaign enabled additional
functionality and supported a broad application of OR-SAGE to the potential for backfit of advanced
reactors at aging coal plants [4]. Similarly, the DOE Gateway for Accelerated Innovation in Nuclear
(GAIN) supported a broad application of OR-SAGE to the industry through the GAIN Vouchers. This
DOE report demonstrated the versatility of OR-SAGE and serves as a good reference for the OR-SAGE
methodology.
Power reactor siting in the United States is based on limiting dose to individuals on the site exclusion area
boundary and on the boundary of a low-population zone as defined in Title 10 to the Code of Federal
Regulations, Part 100 (10 CFR 100). There is also well-defined US Nuclear Regulatory Commission
(NRC) guidance [5] for siting a nuclear power plant (NPP) in the US in NRC Regulatory Guide 4.7 (RG
4.7), “General Site Suitability Criteria for Nuclear Power Stations.” Furthermore, the EPRI siting guide
[6] for Nuclear Energy Generation Facilities provides siting criteria for consideration and is updated
periodically. Approximately 50 potential site selection evaluation parameters are identified in the various
sources related to public health and safety, environment, socioeconomic, and engineering factors. The

 3. DATA CENTER PARAMETER RESEARCH

Numerous sources provided insight on parameters to consider for data center siting. This section includes
all data center siting considerations identified across the various available sources. Power and cooling
energy requirements were consistent across all sources. Some recommended considerations will be more
amenable to OR-SAGE and available data sources than others. It should be noted that:
 Companies that handle US federal government data are held to more stringent IT
 infrastructure standards, established with the Federal Information Security Management
 Act (FISMA). The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) provides
 technical and operational details that can help fulfill the FISMA directive. This often
 impacts decisions like facility location, levels of data security, data access, frequency of
 monitoring and reporting [7].

3.1 Power

Data centers are estimated to consume between 3-4 percent of the world’s electricity, requiring robust
electricity usage to power similarly robust processing and computing needs [7]. The power required to
operate one data center can equal the power required to sustain tens of thousands of households [1].
Therefore, reliable power at a reasonable cost is a primary factor in data center site selection. Primary
power can be delivered from the grid via an agreement with a utility or power can be supplied behind the
meter from an onsite power source. The primary power source can be backed up by a secondary onsite
source such as a diesel generator. Power considerations include power capacity, quality, redundancy (grid
diversity), regional power infrastructure, scalability, cost, efficiency, and uninterruptable power supply
availability [1, 7, 8].
Except for small data center projects, a new data center using power provided by a utility will require an
onsite substation. Developers of very large data centers, or hyperscale data centers, may want to invest in
clean, reliable onsite power resources [9]. Small, advanced NPPs could fill this role.

3.1.1 Data Center Types

Available literature suggests that there are 4 to 6 different types of data centers [24, 25, 26]. On one end
of the spectrum are small, onsite data centers supporting a specific entity. Such enterprise data centers are
an integral part of the entity complex and are not feasible for site modeling using GIS techniques.
Middle-sized data centers include core data centers, collocation data centers, managed data centers, and
edge data centers. These service providers are separate from the entity and are differentiated by the
specific service they are offering. While the footprint for such data centers can be much smaller, a
reasonable GIS bounding value is 40 acres, as noted in Section 3.4. According to the 2024 LBNL data
center report [18], a typical high-end 8-processor server will draw an average of 1.2 kW. A 2017 LBNL
report [27] suggests that a mid-sized data center could have up to 500 servers. This would suggest a
power requirement for the servers of 0.6 MW. Even with a PUE of 1.5, a mid-sized data center would be
bound by a power requirement of 1 MW. Such a data center would necessitate a local water source
capable of providing at least 50 gpm to cool the data center while adhering to the recommended 10%
demand limit on the available water supply.
Hyperscale data centers support extremely large data service providers. Companies with such needs
include Amazon, Apple, ByteDance, Google, Meta, and Microsoft. Hyperscale data centers represent a

 9

significant capability increase above middle-sized data centers. These data centers have a much larger
footprint, exist on their own campus, and generally fit within an upper bound of 200 acres including all
support buildings. Hyperscale data centers have at least 5,000 servers [24, 26]. Current building trends are
toward more hyperscale data centers. If it is assumed that economies of scale also apply, including room
for center expansion, it is not unreasonable to anticipate hyperscale data centers with 50,000 servers. At
1.2 kW per server and a PUE of 1.5, this would suggest a power requirement of 90 MW. For bounding
purposes, a smaller hyperscale at 5,000 servers would be bound at 10 MW and an extremely large
hyperscale data center with 50,000 servers should be bound at 100 MW. The cooling water requirements
would be 500 gpm and 5,000 gpm, respectively.
Beyond individual hyperscale data centers, developers are envisioning gigawatt scale campuses housing
multiple hyperscale facilities [28]. Two-thousand-acre campuses are proposed requiring as much as
10 GW. Projects at this scale would require multiple generators for support.

3.1.2 Energy Storage

Data centers and the customers they support cannot afford to experience service disruptions resulting
from power outages or power spikes. Onsite renewable energy sources and a means to store the energy
could be advantageous. This would allow the data center to continue operation until a backup power
supply can be brought online. This could be in the form of batteries, or the heat stored in solar salts.
Certain advanced reactor technologies, such as the Natrium concept, could provide the primary power
source and simultaneously provide for thermal energy storage.

3.1.3 Energy Efficiency

A metric of data center energy efficiency is power usage effectiveness (PUE). The data center PUE is
important to consider because the energy source providing power to the data center requires a cooling
source which is typically water. This is identified as the indirect water footprint for a data center.
PUE = Total power supplied to the data center / Power consumed by the server equipment [10].
A PUE value close to 1.0 is ideal because it signifies that most or all energy consumed by a data center is
used to power the computer server devices. However, a PUE of 1.0 is unrealistic because the energy used
for non-computing elements, such as lighting and cooling, increases the PUE above 1.0. Still, a data
center’s PUE tends to be inversely proportionate to its size based on the economy of scale [11].

3.2 Cooling

Data center power consumption is significantly impacted by the need to keep servers and equipment cool.
Because of the amount of heat generated by the server equipment, water is a key resource in cooling the
equipment, aided by air cooling. A 2019 United Nations world water development report indicates that:
 Water scarcity is a problem that will worsen. Water usage worldwide has increased by
 about 1% every year since the 1980s. It’s predicted to keep rising at that rate until 2050 –
 resulting in a 20 to 30% increase from today. Combined with an expected decrease in
 water supply due to climate change, the United Nations expects water demand to outpace
 supply by almost 40% as soon as 2030. [12]
Therefore, data center site selection is highly dependent on the continued availability of cooling water.
More temperate climates may be preferred because they enhance cooling capacity and reduce the cooling
water requirements. Environments that require greater mechanical cooling are less desirable because of
the costs associated with keeping cooling equipment running. [7, 8].

 10

Data center cooling requirements depend on the proposed size of the project. A 2024 LBNL study
projected direct data center cooling requirements at 0.48 L/kWh. This translates to 0.0021 gallons per
minute (gpm) for each kW of demand. Under this consideration, a large 100 MW data center would
require 210 gpm of purified freshwater cooling. If this were limited to no more than 10% of the available
water source, then a source providing at least 2,100 gpm would be necessary. This would be added to the
water-cooling requirements of a collocated advanced NPP and subject to the same 10% limitation.
Alternatively, reports from 2016 put the data center makeup water at 12.8 gpm/MW. A 100 MW data
center at this value would require 12,800 gpm in cooling with the local water source capable of providing
at least 128,000 gpm. New data centers certainly trend toward the lower water demand. As such, a
reasonable bound on new data center makeup water is 5 gpm/MW from a local water source capable of
providing at least 50 gpm/MW. Therefore, a 100 MW facility would need a local water source capable of
providing at least 5,000 gpm just for data center cooling to adhere to the recommended 10% limit on
available water.
To address the need for the amount of cooling water required, a site may decide to source the water on
site via borehole or artificial lake rather than relying on stream or river. This variation can also be
modeled in OR-SAGE, although a water source to account for evaporation and drought must be
considered. As a result, most data center operators rely on local potable water resources because access to
rainfall, grey water and surface water is seen as unreliable [23].

3.2.1   Climate

Weather and climate can significantly impact the energy efficiency and operational costs for a data center.
One simple metric to measure climate is evaluating the average temperatures in a region of interest. This
will affect the data center cooling efficiency over the course of a year [13]. Building facilities in cool,
low-humidity regions provides cooling technology options that may not be feasible in warmer climates.
This will lower energy costs, improve cooling efficiency and reduce environmental impact [3].
Within a region of interest, locations at a higher altitude may be preferable to a location in a lower
altitude.

3.2.2 Indirect and Direct Water Use

The US is a global leader in the data center industry, with more than 5,300 facilities, according to
information from Statista.com, a global data and business intelligence platform. The round-the-clock
operation of these data centers makes the digital world possible. In the process, data centers consume
enormous amounts of resources, especially water. That water is used in two primary ways: indirectly, to
generate the electricity to run the computer server equipment, and directly, as the medium to dissipate the
heat generated by the servers and other data center equipment [14].
Within a data center, water is used for cooling server equipment rooms. Most data centers use a chilled
water circuit to absorb the heat generated by the server equipment. The water is chilled by cooling units
powered by electricity or, sometimes, by cool air from the outside using an air-cooled system [15]. When
air cooled systems can be used in place of water-cooled systems, during periods of colder weather for
example, water-cooled technologies become more efficient [16]. In some locations, water usage only
commences when external temperatures rise above a certain threshold, increasing the need for cooling.
Under such conditions, some data centers will start a system that sprays droplets of water (adiabatic
cooling) to cool down the air flow by a few additional degrees before switching to a water chiller system
[15].
A metric of data center water requirements is the water usage effectiveness (WUE). The WUE is the ratio
between water used at the data center and electricity delivered to the server hardware [12]. Specifically:
WUE = Annual water usage (L) / Server equipment energy (kWh) [16]
 11

According to a 2016 Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) report, it takes about 7.6 liters of
water on average to generate 1kWh of energy in the US, while an average data center has a WUE of 1.8
L/kWh (7.9 gpm/MW). The result is US data centers consume billions of liters of water each year and the
amount of water usage is growing [10]. For example, it was reported2 that Google data centers in The
Dalles, Oregon consumed more than 355 million gallons of water in 2021 — an amount that had tripled
since 2016 — representing more than one-quarter of the town’s annual water consumption. Two more
data centers are slated to come online in the future, adding to the regional water demand [14].
Another study in 2016 by the Uptime Institute [22] put data center water usage at 12.8 gpm/MW for a
standard water chiller system and cooling tower, which translates to a WUE of 2.9 L/kWh. Cooling
towers require makeup water because of evaporation, blow down, and drift. The report noted that there
are techniques, technologies, and temperature tolerances that can reduce the cooling water requirement
per MW of data center load.
Since 2016, data centers have committed to better tracking of water usage and improvements in
efficiency. In addition, more water-neutral renewable energy sources have come online. An updated 2024
LBNL report indicates that data centers in the US have a WUE of 0.36 L/kWh (1.6 gpm/MW) through
2023. After 2023, the average data center WUE is projected to rise to a value of 0.45 to 0.48 L/kWh
(2.1 gpm/MW), reflecting increasing construction of hyperscale and Collocation data centers [17].
The 2024 LBNL report notes that US data centers consumed approximately 176 TWh in 2023 using
nearly 800 billion liters of water for electricity generation based on the regional electricity grid mix for
data center locations. This translates to a national average of 4.52 L/kWh (20 gpm/MW) of indirect water
consumption for data centers [17]. This indicates as much as 5.0 L/kWh (22 gpm/MW) total direct and
indirect water consumption for new data centers. A collocated small, advanced NPP to directly power the
data center can project the cooling water requirements of the NPP and the projected needs of a hyperscale
or collection of data centers.
Direct data center water consumption occurs close to the data center site while the associated indirect
water consumption is distributed more broadly geographically. Therefore, data center site selection should
consider the potential stress on the local water basin associated with the potential site [11].
Data center cooling sources could be freshwater lakes and streams, freshwater aquafers, local water
distribution systems, or reprocessed water (municipal gray water). Onsite purification will likely be
required because of the ultrapure need for data center cooling.

3.3 Access to Fiber or Connectivity

Fiber infrastructure is becoming widespread in developed areas such as urban and suburban locations.
Rural siting of a data center may lack easy access to fiber optic infrastructure. This would require
planning and right-of-way to bring fiber to the site. Good connectivity to the internet and other data
centers ensures that data can be transferred quickly and predictably. Redundancy through multiple service
providers or through a content delivery network (CDN) can help prevent interruptions to operations.
Therefore, proximity to an internet exchange point where multiple large networks integrate with one
another should be considered. Sites that do not possess adequate telecommunication infrastructure
including the presence of multiple carriers should be eliminated from the selection process. [1, 7, 8, 11].
Data maps of fiber lines should be used to show the proximity of such lines. This will provide an
indication of the distance and diversity of fiber lines available. Rules of thumb for cost and distance can
be generated for ranking suitable sites.

3.4 Space

According to Bohler [2], data centers generally need at least two buildings on a site to optimize utilities
and security. Bohler estimates a minimum of 40 acres for a small data center. Hyperscale data center
developers will require larger tracts of land. For example, a very large data center, or hyperscale data
center, will require hundreds of acres with considerable energy resources [9]. Some developers are
proposing hyperscale data center parks on several thousand acres [28]. Middle-sized data centers require a
footprint as small as 40 acres [2] while a hyperscale data center may require up to 200 acres [9].
Therefore, 200 acres seems to be a reasonable bounding value for the data center alone. However,
additional land will be required to accommodate a collocated advanced NPP. Fifty acres or less will
typically accommodate the advanced NPP. However, a data base site may want to seek more than 250
acres combined to allow for additional separation. Of course, if security is to be combined for the
facilities, then a smaller footprint is more efficient. The NRC has additional siting requirements regarding
NPP proximity to population centers that collocated facilities will need to consider. These are
incorporated in the OR-SAGE site requirements for advanced NPPs.

3.4.1 Community Pushback

Public concern about any construction project is inevitable. Data center construction will generate
concerns over:
• Noise
• Environmental Impact
• Visual Appeal
• Loss of land for other purposes
• Traffic
• Impact on price of local electricity supply

3.4.2 Real Estate Cost

Important factors for data center siting include the cost of rent or purchase, utilities, taxes and other
operating expenses because they substantially affect the cost of providing cloud services [1]. However, in
early 2022, hyperscale operators scooped up much of the available real estate in primary data center
markets [3], effectively shutting out smaller data center operators. Therefore, evaluation of secondary
markets may be more fruitful for future siting considerations.
One real estate caveat is that building in regions with a high potential for hurricanes, earthquakes,
wildfires and other natural disasters puts your data and equipment at risk. These regions are often more
expensive to build in due to added insurance and construction regulations, as well as improved structural
hardening to offset risks, so there may be a point at which an area may not be worth the additional risk
and expense [3, 7].

3.5 Security

Investment risk to the developer of the data center will require some attention to both cyber security and
physical site security. This could be in the form of fences, barriers, 24/7 guarded site access, onsite
security teams, and automated security. The site should be in a safe and secure area, away from high
crime areas [1]. In addition, buildings and support equipment should be protected from fire and other
natural disaster risks. Risk
As noted, when considering the real estate cost, the risk of natural and man-made hazards to the site must
be evaluated. A loss of the data center or a loss of access to the data center could negatively impact the

 13

user base or force the operator to rely on a backup data center. By choosing a secure location, businesses
can mitigate the risk of data loss and ensure the continuity of their operations [18]. Risks could include:
• Commercial airports
• Chemical or energy facilities (fire, missile, or toxic gas)
• Seismic zones
• Faults
• Volcanic activity
• High water (including climatic impacts)
• High wind (including climatic impacts)
• Fire threat (including climatic impacts)

These and other identified risks should be flagged if they are within a certain distance of the proposed site
or if they engulf a proposed site so that the siting decision is better informed.
Data center siting should also consider any planned inter-connectivity with other data centers. An
organization that offers a nationwide network of data centers can promise more reliability than one that
has sites all within a short distance of one another. The ability of a datacenter to failover to another
location after a disaster is an important siting consideration. From the user perspective, if an organization
is choosing to house data in more than one data center, choosing facilities that are far enough apart means
selecting centers that are not prone to the same risks. While there’s no hard or fast requirement, 100 miles
is a good rule of thumb for data center separation for one organization [19].
All these parameters serve as flags in the siting decision process. Airports, volcanic activity, chemical,
and energy facilities, not including the collocated NPP, could be flagged if located within 5 miles of the
proposed data center site. These risk factors are currently not included in this analysis. A typical building
code limit for seismic activity is to build for 0.75g, so values above this limit can be flagged; this value is
used in this analysis for assessing suitable sites. A conservative flag for faults would be to use the same
standoff distance as for the collocated advanced NPP. Parameter flags for water, wind, fire, and any other
identified threats to a proposed site will need to be developed for secondary analysis or to rank suitable
sites depending on data availability and the perceived threat.

3.6 Accessibility

The selected site should be near adequate roads and within proximity of a transportation hub, such as an
airport, for ease of shipping data center hardware. Transportation will be a factor to consider for
employees, visitors, and hardware upkeep whether an urban, suburban, or rural site is selected.
Remote locations may be less expensive, but they can also be difficult to reach. Before constructing a new
facility, consider the logistics of building, maintaining and staffing a data center that isn’t easily
accessible [3]. Road infrastructure can be overlaid on any data center siting parameter results. This can
inform the cost to make a proposed site accessible.

3.7 Labor Pool

The skill level and technical knowledge of the local labor pool should be part of the evaluation because
data centers require educated, skilled professionals to support the computer equipment, the associated
support equipment, and provide for facility and infrastructure maintenance. Therefore, the site selection
also impacts the ability attract and retain employees with appropriate skill sets [3, 7, 20]. Locating a data
center near a university or a computer training school may be a siting consideration that helps to ensure
the availability of staff with appropriate skills [21]. Pending available data, multiple data sets could
provide college/training availability, economic diversity, labor rates, etc. While none of these factors will

 14

directly influence how adequate a proposed site might be, they will provide good information to
differentiate between proposed sites.

3.8 Zoning and Tax Incentives

A secondary site decision could include zoning and other ordinances. Once the siting options are
narrowed to a small list, site zoning restrictions could indicate that some locations are more favorable
than others. Zoning restrictions could affect the type, size, or height of the planned facility. In addition,
periodic operations such as running diesel generators for backup power may be restricted [21]. Obtaining
a zoning variance or rezoning a property can be a time intensive process and may ultimately not be
possible.
Many states and communities want to attract employers and jobs to their region. This often takes the form
of tax incentives to locate a facility in the region. Tax incentives will vary by state and county depending
on the industry, the number of associated jobs, and the payroll. As with zoning, this is a parameter that
may be more pertinent to a comparison of available sites when a developer is weighing options.
Remote and secondary markets may be more likely to offer incentives and tax breaks to attract new data
center construction projects and jobs to their communities [3].
Zoning and tax incentives are likely to be significant drivers in a data center siting decision. However,
data to visually influence a decision using GIS technology may be limited. For example, there may be
some zoning information that could be used to flag residential areas, but this may be difficult to translate
into areas that could be rezoned for commercial plus nuclear use. Likewise, tax incentives to lure business
to an area are not routinely tracked in a database. However, proprietary data available to a potential data
center builder may be available for visualization.

4.1.1 Montana State

The overall siting results for large light water reactor (LWR) technology in the state of Montana can be
seen in Table M1. It is immediately apparent from the composite map that far west reaches of the state are
the most restrictive with regard to siting. Looking to the constituent layers below, this is due to a series of
factors. The primary restrictions seem to be the series of steep mountain ranges, national forests, Native
American reservations, and other protected regions that span from Glacier National Park in the northwest
to Park and Sweet Grass counties near the northern edge of Yellowstone National Park. To a lesser extent,
the active fault lines and associated seismic activity that can sometimes coincide with mountain ranges
present additional barriers to siting.

In contrast, the predominant barrier to siting in the eastern portion of Montana is access to cooling water,
for which the large LWR has the greatest requirement. The combination of protected regions and
mountain ranges in the west with limited streamflow in the east means that the vast majority of baseline
suitable land in Montana is found in the riparian zones of the Missouri and Yellowstone Rivers.
Consequently, the counties with the greatest amount of suitable land are Treasure (85.7%), Richland
(75.5%), Chouteau (74.3%), Prairie (70.1%) and Hill (68.3%). In total, only 11 of Montana’s 56 counties
exceed 50% suitability, with four having no suitable land: Carter, Daniels, Powder River, and Wheatland.

These dynamics largely hold true for the small modular reactor siting results in Table M2. While the
population, slope, and cooling water data layers have differing setbacks and thresholds from the large
LWR, most of the meaningful changes take place in the western portion of the state. Consequently, the
additional siting restrictions that do not change between technology configurations significantly limit the
impact of the relaxed constraints in the small modular reactor siting regime. Looking to the raw
percentages by county, however, it is immediately evident that many do gain some amount of suitable
land. The most suitable counties gain between 5 and 10% of additional suitable land, with the exception
of Prairie County, which is supplanted by Teton County at 72.9% suitability.

Looking now to the siting results for a dry-cooled SMR in Table M3, it seems the eastern half of the state
opens up significantly for potential suitable land, primarily due to the removal of the cooling water
requirement. One exception lies in Roosevelt County, which is almost entirely occupied by the Fort Peck
Indian Reservation. Similarly, the Crow and Northern Cheyenne Reservations preclude most of Big Horn
County. These instances aside, the removal of the cooling water requirement means that 32 Montana
counties have greater than 50% suitability, with 8 of those reaching 90% or more.

Given the significantly relaxed siting criteria for data center technology, it is little surprise that Table M4
shows most of the state as suitable, with the notable exception of Mineral County, which finds itself
tucked into one of the most topologically diverse regions in the state. What is less evident is the rationale
behind the locations of existing fiber optic lines and data centers when so much of the state should be
suitable. While there are no apparent missing criteria, the siting parameters do not account for incentives.
In this case, most of the major fiber optic intersections and some data center locations coincide with the

The impact of this phenomenon on the opportunities for collocation can be seen in Table M5, which
shows that the primary intersection of available fiber optic networks with potential locations for nuclear
power are in the southeast quadrant of the state surrounding the Yellowstone River. Two counties that lay
within this corridor are Rosebud and Treasure County, which are further analyzed in the next section.

4.1.2 Utah State

From the data package in Table U1, it is clear that large light water reactors face immense siting
challenges within the state of Utah. While there is still some level of viability in the state, there is very
little in the way of contiguous suitable land, and no counties appear to exceed 40% viability. In fact, most
counties in Utah do not exceed even 20% viability. The constraints here are primarily logistical in nature.
Utah contains portions of both the Wasatch and Uinta Mountain ranges, which preclude large portions of
the state due to steep terrain. Much of these lands also have protected status, and include a number of
national forests, parks, and monuments, for which the state is quite famous. The other logistical concern is
of course that of cooling water. Though the Colorado River does flow through the eastern portion of the
state, much of the rest does not have appropriate streamflow resources to support a large light water
reactor. Additionally, the state of Utah contains a significant number of active fault lines, though the
greatest risk of seismic activity is concentrated along the Wasatch range in the north. This same area also
contains one of the few major population centers and areas of open water, both situated around the Great
Salt Lake. Utah has two major airports, and several military installations around Tooele and Box Elder
counties. All of these considerations amount to a patchwork of suitable regions distributed in a few
counties.

The story is much the same for the fresh-water cooled SMR configuration, the siting maps for which can
be seen in Table U2. This is because the most restrictive criteria do not differ for this configuration. There
does however appear to be an increase in viable land for Box Elder, Uintah, and Grand County. For
Uintah, this is due to the reduced cooling water requirement, whereas Grand and Box Elder seem to
benefit primarily from the relaxed slope constraint.

As expected, the removal of the cooling water requirement from the SMR siting maps in Table U3
considerably expands suitability, particularly in the seven counties that flank the western edge of the state.
Here still the prevalence of fault lines, steep terrain, and protected regions means that finding contiguous
tracts of suitable land will not be a trivial process.

In Table U4, the most restrictive criteria are removed for siting data centers, and because of this the vast
majority of the state has considerable viability for the technology. The only exceptions include Salt Lake,
David, and Weber counties. These fall into a small region of the state impacted by floodplains and high
seismic activity. All three also share portions of the Great Salt Lake, further reducing suitability.

As previously established, suitability for reactors in the state of Utah is for the most part patchy, and this
is duly reflected in the collocation maps in Table U5. Here it is more evident that the higher slope
tolerance for the water-cooled SMR technology allows for more contiguous regions of collocation

4.1.3.2 Routt County

The last region in the greenfield analysis is that of Routt County Colorado. The potential for large water
reactor development in the area seems to be extremely limited, as evidence in Table C.R1. Nestled in the
midst of the Rocky Mountains, the vast majority of Routt County exceeds the slope criteria for LWR
technology. On its own this criterion makes siting prohibitively difficult. Routt County also has several
parks and national forests that dominate its north and eastern boundaries, a sizeable landslide hazard in its
center, and a regional airport in the west.

While the reduced slope restriction in Table C.R2 does allow for some suitability near Routt County’s
southern boundary, most of the rest of the area is not suitable for water-cooled SMR development. Even
the removal of the water requirements for dry-cooled SMRs in Table C.R3 only allows for siting in some
sparse regions to the north. Even then, the lower tolerance for slope required for this technology mitigates
the potential improvements to viability. While many regions in this analysis see significant improvements
in viability as siting constraints are relaxed, this is not the case in Routt County due primarily to the fact
that the most restrictive criteria do not change between technology configurations.

While Table C.R4 does show a much greater potential for data center siting in Routt County, the landslide
risk still precludes the greater portion of the center of the area.

The overall impact of these various constraints are evident in the collocation analysis in Table C.R5,
where potential regions of collocation are significantly limited all throughout the county. Though limited,
it seems the most viable technology is actually the water-cooled SMR, due primarily to the relaxed slope
criteria in the south and some portions of Central Routt County.
