Prevalence of e-cigarette use and its association with knowledge and attitudes among palestinian medical students and physicians: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Maaweya Jabareen, Mousa Humeedat, Wasef Alhroub, Ahmad Fasfoos, Hisham AlBakri, Marah Ghassan Shareef, Dana Hussam Nairoukh, Raghad Majed Maraqa, Lara Naser Said, Ahmad Salameh, Mohammad Zatari, Farid K Abu Sham’a, Abdallah A Qawasmeh

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectronic cigarette (e-cigarette) use has increased globally, particularly among adolescents and young adults, despite growing evidence regarding potential health risks. Healthcare professionals play a critical role in tobacco control and smoking cessation; however, limited evidence exists regarding knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) toward e-cigarettes among medical students and physicians in Palestine.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between November and December 2025 among medical students and physicians from major universities and teaching hospitals across the West Bank, Palestine. Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire adapted from a validated instrument. The questionnaire assessed sociodemographic characteristics, smoking-related behaviors, e-cigarette knowledge, attitudes, and use patterns. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors independently associated with high knowledge levels, positive attitudes, and current e-cigarette use. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.ResultsA total of 433 participants were included, with a mean age of 25.55 ± 7.69 years; 55.4% were female. Overall, 34.2% reported current use of at least one tobacco or nicotine product, and 11.8% were current e-cigarette users. Current e-cigarette use was substantially higher among males than females (20.7% vs. 4.6%, p < 0.001). High knowledge level was observed in 73.0% of participants, while 24.7% had positive attitudes toward e-cigarettes. Positive attitude was inversely associated with high knowledge level (aOR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.16–0.44), while current e-cigarette use was associated with positive attitude (aOR = 4.32, 95% CI: 1.61–11.59). In the final parsimonious model, current e-cigarette use was independently associated with positive attitude toward e-cigarettes (aOR = 7.45, 95% CI: 3.66–15.18), having close friends who currently smoked (aOR = 3.63, 95% CI: 1.06–12.40), and spending more than five hours per day at college (aOR = 2.68, 95% CI: 1.02–6.99). Among current e-cigarette users, 51.0% also used at least one combustible tobacco product. Self-reported symptoms after switching from conventional cigarettes to e-cigarettes were described as exploratory findings based on a small subgroup.ConclusionsE-cigarette use is relatively common among medical students and physicians in Palestine and frequently coexists with other tobacco products. Although overall knowledge was generally high, important gaps remain, and positive attitudes toward e-cigarettes were strongly associated with current use. These findings highlight the need for enhanced education on emerging nicotine products within medical curricula, strengthened tobacco-control policies, and targeted interventions addressing social influences and misconceptions regarding e-cigarettes among current and future healthcare professionals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28348-3.Keywords: E-cigarettes, Vaping, Medical students, Physicians, Tobacco use

Background

Electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) and similar devices have changed patterns of tobacco use worldwide, particularly among adolescents and young adults. An e-cigarette is typically a battery-operated device designed to deliver nicotine, user-selected flavorings, and other chemicals such as propylene glycol or glycerol through aerosolization [1, 2]. These products are often marketed as safer, cleaner, and more socially acceptable alternatives to conventional cigarettes, contributing to their rapid uptake. As a result, e-cigarettes have become the most commonly used tobacco product among young people in many countries [3].

Despite their widespread popularity and perceived safety, growing evidence has raised concerns regarding their potential health effects. Several flavoring chemicals, while considered safe for oral consumption, are not intended for inhalation and have been associated with airway epithelial injury, impaired mucociliary clearance, and inflammatory lung disease, including e-cigarette or vaping-associated lung injury (EVALI) [4]. Collectively, emerging evidence demonstrates that e-cigarettes are not risk-free and may adversely affect cardiovascular and respiratory health, promote nicotine addiction, and facilitate concurrent use with conventional cigarettes [5, 6].

Several systematic reviews indicate that e-cigarette use is widespread among young people and adults, with a pooled global prevalence of around 22.6% among students and a current use prevalence of about 11% in general populations [7, 8]. Additionally, global estimates suggest that more than 100 million people worldwide are current e-cigarette users, including approximately 15 million adolescents aged 13–15 (World Health Organization 2025). Healthcare professionals play a pivotal role in tobacco control through health education, risk communication, and patient counseling. Nevertheless, available evidence indicates that knowledge and attitudes toward e-cigarettes among healthcare providers and medical trainees are heterogeneous and context-dependent [9].

Evidence from several countries, including Saudi Arabia report that a notable proportion of medical and health-science students use e-cigarettes, with prevalence estimates ranging from approximately 10% to 31% and frequent dual use alongside conventional cigarettes or waterpipes, indicating that medical education alone does not fully protect against vaping behaviors [1, 10–12]. In Palestine, e-cigarette use is also increasingly reported among both university students and the general population, with studies reporting prevalence rates of approximately 18–20% among students and 15% among adults. Despite growing use, misconceptions regarding the safety, addictiveness, and smoking cessation benefits of e-cigarettes remain common, with many individuals perceiving them as safer and more cost-effective than conventional cigarettes [13–15].

Healthcare professionals play a key role in smoking cessation and shaping public perceptions, making their knowledge and attitudes toward e-cigarettes essential to assess. Many medical trainees also report limited preparedness and confidence in counseling patients about e-cigarette use, reflecting insufficient formal education on emerging tobacco products [16]. Similarly, regional evidence has identified ongoing misconceptions and measurable e-cigarette use among medical students despite medical training [17]. In the Palestinian context, evidence on medical students and physicians remains limited. Therefore, this study aims to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding e-cigarettes among medical students and physicians in Palestine and to identify sociodemographic, lifestyle, and smoking-related factors associated with knowledge levels, attitudes, and current e-cigarette use.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted among medical students and physicians in Palestine between 10 November and 5 December 2025.

Data were collected from multiple academic and healthcare institutions across the West Bank only. The participating academic institutions included An-Najah National University in the northern region, Al-Quds University in the central region, Hebron University, and Palestine Polytechnic University in the southern region. These institutions represent major medical education centers distributed across different geographic areas of the West Bank, enhancing the geographic coverage and diversity of the study sample.

In addition, data were collected from affiliated teaching hospitals across the West Bank, with two hospitals representing each of the northern, central, and southern regions. This distribution ensured inclusion of participants from key clinical training environments across different geographic areas of the West Bank.

Overall, the combination of academic institutions and teaching hospitals provided broad coverage of both educational and clinical settings, strengthening the representativeness of the study population within the West Bank context.

Participants and sampling

The target population included undergraduate medical students, medical doctors, and medical specialists involved in medical education or clinical practice during the study period.

A non-probability convenience sampling approach was adopted due to the absence of a comprehensive national registry of medical students and physicians in Palestine. As a result, a complete sampling frame could not be established, and participants were recruited based on accessibility and willingness to participate from academic institutions and healthcare facilities.

Inclusion criteria were being a medical student or practicing physician at the time of the study and providing informed consent to participate. Exclusion criteria included individuals who were not medical students or physicians or who declined to participate.

The minimum required sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula for a single population proportion, assuming an unknown population size. A 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), an expected proportion of 0.50 (to ensure maximum sample size), and a margin of error of 5% (d = 0.05) were applied. The calculated minimum sample size was 384 participants, rounded to 385. To account for potential non-response or incomplete questionnaires, a 10% adjustment was applied, resulting in a target sample size of at least 424 participants. The final sample included 433 respondents, exceeding the minimum required sample size.

Because recruitment was conducted through multiple open channels without a defined sampling frame, the total number of individuals exposed to the survey could not be determined; therefore, a formal response rate could not be calculated.

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this study.

Ethical considerations

This observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Hebron University; therefore, clinical trial registration was not required. Participation was voluntary, with electronic informed consent obtained from all respondents. No personal identifiers were collected, and data were kept anonymous and securely stored for research purposes only.

Data collection tool

Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire adapted from a previously validated instrument assessing prevalence, knowledge, and attitudes toward e-cigarette use among university students [13]. The questionnaire was modified to ensure suitability for a medical population while maintaining its original three-section structure.

The first section collected sociodemographic characteristics and lifestyle and smoking-related variables, including age, gender, professional status, place of residence, monthly income, physical activity, dietary habits, coffee consumption, and smoking history. Smoking variables included current and past use of conventional cigarettes, e-cigarettes, and waterpipe smoking, as well as exposure to smoking within the household and among parents and close friends. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), a smoker was defined as an individual who had smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and was currently smoking at the time of the study. A current regular e-cigarette user was defined as a participant who had used e-cigarettes for at least 30 days, including use within the past 7 days [18]. The second section assessed knowledge regarding e-cigarettes using 13 items covering health risks, addiction potential, cardiovascular and respiratory effects, fertility-related risks, passive exposure, and nicotine content. Responses were recorded as “yes,” “no,” or “do not know.” Correct responses were scored as 2 points, incorrect responses as 0 points, and “do not know” responses as 1 point, with reverse scoring applied where appropriate. The total knowledge score ranged from 0 to 26. A cut-off point of 75% (≥ 20) was used to classify participants as having high knowledge, while scores below 20 indicated low knowledge.

The third section assessed attitudes toward e-cigarettes using 13 statements rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree, with no neutral option to reduce central tendency bias. Positive statements were scored from 4 (strongly agree) to 1 (strongly disagree), while negative statements were reverse-scored. The total attitude score ranged from 13 to 52, with a cut-off point of 75% (≥ 39) used to classify participants as having a positive attitude, while scores below 39 indicated a negative attitude.

Three additional sections were included to explore patterns of e-cigarette use, reasons for use, and self-reported symptoms after switching from conventional cigarettes to e-cigarettes. These sections were administered primarily to current e-cigarette users and were included for descriptive and exploratory purposes without implying causal inference.

The questionnaire was originally developed in English and translated into Arabic, followed by backward translation to ensure linguistic equivalence. Content validity was assessed by an expert panel, and items were refined based on consensus feedback. A pilot study was conducted on 50 participants from the target population to assess clarity and completion time, with minor wording adjustments implemented accordingly. Internal consistency reliability was acceptable, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.859 for the knowledge scale and 0.792 for the attitude scale.

Data collection process

Data were collected using both online and face-to-face administration methods. Participants completed the questionnaire either electronically via a secure online survey platform or in person using an electronic version of the same instrument.

Recruitment was conducted across multiple academic institutions and hospitals in the northern, central, and southern regions of the West Bank. Recruitment strategies included institutional electronic dissemination (emails and online survey links), classroom-based distribution, and direct approach within hospital settings. Because data collection relied on multiple open recruitment channels without a defined sampling frame, the total number of individuals exposed to the survey could not be determined; therefore, a formal response rate could not be calculated.

Participation was voluntary, anonymity was ensured, and no identifying information was collected.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed before group comparisons. Because age was not normally distributed, age comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test and presented as mean ± standard deviation for descriptive consistency. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Bivariate analyses were first conducted to examine differences in participant characteristics according to knowledge level, attitude level, and current e-cigarette use status. Full bivariate results were presented in the supplementary tables to reduce redundancy in the main manuscript. Variables were considered for multivariable logistic regression based on theoretical relevance, bivariate findings, reviewer concerns, and model stability.

Separate multivariable logistic regression models were used to identify factors associated with high knowledge level, positive attitude toward e-cigarettes, and current e-cigarette use. For the current e-cigarette use model, a parsimonious final model was constructed to reduce overfitting and improve interpretability, given the relatively small number of current e-cigarette users. The composite current smoking variable was excluded from the adjusted model because it overlapped conceptually and statistically with the outcome variable and with product-specific tobacco-use variables. Product-specific tobacco variables, including conventional cigarette smoking and waterpipe use, were retained. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 433 participants were included in the analysis, with a mean age of 25.55 ± 7.69 years. More than half of the participants were female (55.4%), and slightly more than half were medical doctors (54.5%). Most participants reported living with their families during university, and the majority had low monthly income. Regarding lifestyle and social smoking exposure, 32.8% reported regular exercise, 34.9% reported following a healthy diet, and 58.4% reported having close friends who currently smoked. Overall, 34.2% of participants reported current use of at least one tobacco or nicotine product. Specifically, 14.8% currently smoked conventional cigarettes, 11.8% were current e-cigarette users, and 15.9% reported current waterpipe use.

Sex-specific prevalence estimates showed a marked gender difference in tobacco and nicotine product use. Any current tobacco or nicotine product use was reported by 59.6% of males compared with 13.8% of females. Current e-cigarette use was also substantially higher among males than females (20.7% vs. 4.6%, p < 0.001). Similar male predominance was observed for conventional cigarette smoking (31.1% vs. 1.7%, p < 0.001) and waterpipe use (22.8% vs. 10.4%, p < 0.001). These findings show that tobacco and e-cigarette use were substantially more common among male participants (Table 1).

Bivariate analyses

The bivariate analyses are presented before the multivariable analyses to follow the analytical sequence used in this study. Supplementary Tables 2–4 provide the complete bivariate comparisons for the three main outcomes: high knowledge level, positive attitude toward e-cigarettes, and current e-cigarette use. These bivariate analyses describe the unadjusted associations and provide the empirical context for the adjusted multivariable models presented in the main manuscript.

In the bivariate analyses, high knowledge level was significantly associated with regular exercise, father’s current smoking status, and attitude toward e-cigarettes, while most other sociodemographic and smoking-related variables were not significantly associated with knowledge level (Supplementary Table 2). Positive attitude toward e-cigarettes was significantly associated with older age, male gender, job title, current smoking status, conventional cigarette smoking, current e-cigarette use, waterpipe use, monthly income, number of smokers living in the same household, close friends’ current smoking status, time spent at college per day, and knowledge level (Supplementary Table 3). Current e-cigarette use was significantly associated with age, male gender, waterpipe use, coffee consumption, monthly income, number of smokers living in the same household, close friends’ current smoking status, time spent at college per day, and attitude toward e-cigarettes (Supplementary Table 4).

Multivariable analyses

In the multivariable model for high knowledge level about e-cigarettes, participants who reported regular exercise had lower odds of high knowledge compared with those who did not exercise regularly (aOR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.30–0.81, p = 0.005). Coffee consumption 1–6 times per week was associated with higher odds of high knowledge compared with no coffee consumption (aOR = 2.10, 95% CI: 1.12–3.95, p = 0.021), while daily coffee consumption showed a borderline association. Positive attitude toward e-cigarettes was inversely associated with high knowledge level (aOR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.16–0.44, p < 0.001), indicating that participants with more favorable attitudes toward e-cigarettes were less likely to have high knowledge. Other variables entered into the model were not independently associated with knowledge level (Table 2).

Among current e-cigarette users (n = 51), patterns of use indicated that e-cigarette initiation most commonly occurred during university years, reported by 27 participants (52.9%). A further 16 participants (31.4%) reported initiating e-cigarette use after graduation, while 7 participants (13.7%) started before university and only 1 participant (2.0%) started during internship. Regarding duration of use, 15 current users (29.4%) had used e-cigarettes for less than one year, 13 (25.5%) for 1–2 years, 13 (25.5%) for 3–5 years, and 10 (19.6%) for 5–10 years. Most current users also reported using e-cigarettes during periods of academic or work-related stress, with 45 participants (88.2%) reporting stress-related use.

Dual use was common among current e-cigarette users. Of the 51 current e-cigarette users, 11 participants (21.6%) also smoked conventional cigarettes, and 17 participants (33.3%) also used waterpipe. Overall, 26 current e-cigarette users (51.0%) used at least one combustible tobacco product in addition to e-cigarettes, indicating that e-cigarette use frequently coexisted with other forms of tobacco use rather than occurring as an isolated behavior. Triple use of e-cigarettes, conventional cigarettes, and waterpipe was reported by 2 participants (3.9%). In the full sample, dual use of e-cigarettes and conventional cigarettes was reported by 11 participants (2.5%), dual use of e-cigarettes and waterpipe by 17 participants (3.9%), dual use of e-cigarettes with any combustible tobacco product by 26 participants (6.0%), and triple use by 2 participants (0.5%).

Reported reasons for e-cigarette use suggested that economic, practical, and social factors were more prominent than cessation-related motives. The most frequently reported reason was that e-cigarettes were perceived as cheaper than other tobacco products, reported by 28 current users (54.9%). The ability to use e-cigarettes in places where conventional cigarettes are banned was reported by 25 participants (49.0%), while ease of access and availability was reported by 17 participants (33.3%). Social influence was also evident, as 17 participants (33.3%) reported that peers or colleagues used e-cigarettes, which they perceived as influencing their own use. Image-related reasons were less frequent, with 9 participants (17.6%) perceiving e-cigarettes as more modern or sophisticated. Only 9 participants (17.6%) reported using e-cigarettes as a method to quit combustible tobacco, and 7 participants (13.7%) reported using them because they perceived e-cigarettes as less harmful than combustible tobacco products. Media or celebrity influence was rarely reported, by only 2 participants (3.9%), while 13 participants (25.5%) reported other reasons.

Self-reported symptoms after switching from conventional cigarettes to e-cigarettes were assessed among a smaller subgroup of former conventional smokers who reported switching to e-cigarettes (n = 22). These findings should be interpreted as exploratory, self-perceived experiences rather than clinically verified outcomes. Within this subgroup, cardiovascular complaints, including increased heart rate and/or irregular blood pressure, were reported by 11 participants (50.0%). Respiratory symptoms, such as cough or shortness of breath, were also reported by 11 participants (50.0%). Upper aerodigestive and mucosal symptoms, particularly persistent dryness of the throat, mouth, or lips, were reported by 9 participants (40.9%). Gastrointestinal symptoms, including nausea or other gastrointestinal complaints, were reported by 7 participants (31.8%), while sleep-related symptoms, including insomnia or disturbed sleep, were reported by 6 participants (27.3%). Less commonly reported symptoms included dizziness or imbalance among 3 participants (13.6%), other neurological symptoms among 2 participants (9.1%), and urinary or genital symptoms among 1 participant (4.5%).

Given the descriptive nature of these findings, the small subgroup size for symptom reporting, and the absence of clinical verification or a comparison group, the symptom-related results should not be interpreted as evidence of causality or confirmed adverse clinical outcomes. Instead, they provide preliminary, hypothesis-generating information on user-perceived experiences after switching from conventional cigarettes to e-cigarettes.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that e-cigarette use among medical students and physicians in Palestine represents an important public health concern, particularly given the role of healthcare professionals as current and future providers of tobacco prevention and cessation counseling. Although the prevalence of e-cigarette use observed in this study was lower than that reported among medical students at Umm Al Qura University (31.8%) [12], and healthcare providers in Saudi Arabia (22.9%) [10], it exceeded estimates from Cairo University (7.3%) [17], and Turkey (4.0%) [19]. The overall prevalence of tobacco use remained high despite participants’ medical background and was only slightly lower than national estimates reported by the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics [20], and findings among university students in the West Bank [13]. These findings are concerning because healthcare professionals and trainees play a critical role in tobacco prevention and cessation efforts, and their own tobacco use may undermine their effectiveness as health advocates.

The findings underscore the importance of social and environmental influences on e-cigarette use. Spending longer periods at university was associated with increased use, consistent with previous studies identifying peer networks and social interactions as key drivers of vaping behavior among medical students [19, 21]. Similarly, having friends who smoke was associated with increased use, in line with studies identifying peer smoking as a key determinant of e-cigarette uptake [19, 22, 23]. In addition, male gender more frequently associated with e-cigarette use in our study, which aligns with broader literature. The DEBRA study and YUPESS survey both reported male sex as a risk variables [24, 25], and similar associations have been observed in large medical student populations in the United States and Brazil [26]. Overall, these findings reinforce the role of both peer influence and gender as key associated factors of e-cigarette use across populations. Conversely, practicing physicians were less likely to use e-cigarettes than medical students, supporting evidence that healthcare professionals generally maintain more cautious views and lower engagement with e-cigarettes [27]. Overall, these findings suggest that social context, professional identity, and stage of training influence susceptibility to e-cigarette use.

Participants reported using e-cigarettes primarily for reasons related to convenience, affordability, social influence, and use in smoke-free settings rather than smoking cessation. Similar motivations have been documented among university and medical students, where curiosity, peer influence, and accessibility were prominent drivers of use [19]. In contrast, studies involving adult smokers and young adults have more frequently identified smoking reduction, nicotine dependence management, and perceived harm reduction as primary motivations [28, 29]. These differences highlight the need for interventions targeting the social normalization of e-cigarette use within academic environments.

Despite generally high knowledge levels, important gaps remained regarding the health risks, constituents, and regulation of e-cigarettes. Similar findings have been reported among healthcare students and professionals, whose knowledge of e-cigarette health effects, contents, and safety is often incomplete [19, 30]. Participants demonstrated uncertainty about long-term harms, chemical exposure, and safety profiles, consistent with evidence that many healthcare trainees and practitioners lack sufficient knowledge or confidence to counsel patients about e-cigarette use [31]. These findings suggest that knowledge of emerging nicotine products may remain inconsistent even among individuals with formal medical education [30, 31].

In multivariable analysis, lower knowledge was associated with more positive attitudes toward e-cigarettes and regular physical activity. The relationship between physical activity and knowledge remains unclear and inconsistent with previous evidence, which reports mixed findings across studies, with some showing higher knowledge among physically active students [32], and others reporting the opposite [33]. Overall, these findings suggest that associations between lifestyle factors, attitudes, and knowledge are likely context-dependent rather than causal.

Overall, participants’ predominantly cautious attitudes toward e-cigarettes indicate a generally critical perception of their safety and effectiveness, with strong support for regulatory restrictions in public and workplace settings. When interpreted in a broader context, these findings diverge from more permissive attitudes reported in some international studies, including a multicountry survey showing substantial variation in perceived risk across settings [9]. Differences observed across countries likely reflect variations in regulatory environments, professional training, and public health messaging rather than uniform perceptions of e-cigarettes. Similar cautious attitudes have been documented among healthcare professionals in England [34], whereas studies from the United States have reported more permissive clinical recommendations [35], or predominantly negative perceptions due to insufficient evidence [36, 37]. Higher knowledge levels were associated with less favorable attitudes toward e-cigarettes, whereas positive attitudes were strongly associated with current use. However, given the cross-sectional design, this relationship should be interpreted cautiously because attitudes may influence use, while previous use may also shape attitudes. This finding aligns with a previous study among university students in Palestine [13] and studies among medical students in other settings [16, 17]. This discrepancy may reflect methodological and contextual differences, as well as changes in attitudes after initiation or continued use despite awareness of harm.

Patterns of tobacco use indicated substantial overlap between e-cigarettes and other tobacco products, consistent with previous reports of dual use involving conventional cigarettes and waterpipes [19, 38, 39]. However, the absence of independent associations after adjustment suggests that co-use may reflect shared social and behavioral determinants rather than direct relationships between specific tobacco products. Similarly, although participants reported symptoms resembling those described in previous experimental and observational studies [2, 40–42], the subjective nature of symptom reporting and the cross-sectional design preclude any conclusions regarding causality.

These findings have important implications for tobacco control policy in Palestine. Existing legislation, including Anti-Smoking Law No. 25 of 2005, does not address e-cigarettes or other emerging nicotine products and is further limited by weak implementation and enforcement [43]. These regulatory gaps exist within a broader context characterized by high tobacco use, economic instability, psychological stress, illicit tobacco trade, and limited cessation services [44]. Strengthening tobacco control efforts will require legislative reform to incorporate emerging nicotine products, improved enforcement mechanisms, expansion of cessation services, and measures to address illicit tobacco markets. Alignment with WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control standards and the development of a comprehensive national tobacco control strategy could support more effective prevention and control efforts.

This study has several strengths, including being among the first in Palestine to comprehensively assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding e-cigarette use among both medical students and physicians, a key population in tobacco control and patient counseling. The inclusion of participants from multiple universities and healthcare settings enhances sample diversity, while the use of validated instruments and multivariable analysis strengthens the methodological rigor. From a practical and clinical perspective, the findings highlight the importance of routinely assessing e-cigarette use in tobacco history taking and providing balanced, evidence-based counseling that addresses both short-term symptoms and long-term uncertainties. They also underscore the need to strengthen medical education on emerging nicotine products to improve knowledge consistency and counseling confidence among future healthcare professionals.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal or temporal inferences regarding the associations between e-cigarette use, knowledge, attitudes, and reported symptoms; therefore, the findings are limited to identifying correlations only. In addition, the data were self-reported and may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly given the medical background of the participants.

Furthermore, the use of a convenience sampling approach, along with the absence of a comprehensive national sampling frame for medical students and physicians in Palestine, may limit the representativeness of the sample and reduce the generalizability of the findings. The recruitment process, which relied on multiple open channels across academic and hospital settings, may also have introduced selection bias and influenced participant characteristics.

Another limitation is the lack of clinical verification of reported symptoms; thus, these should be interpreted as subjective self-reported experiences rather than confirmed clinical outcomes. Finally, detailed information regarding e-cigarette device type, nicotine concentration, frequency, and duration of use was not collected, which may introduce residual confounding in the assessment of symptom patterns and related risk factors.

Conclusion

E-cigarette and tobacco/nicotine product use were common among medical students and physicians in Palestine, with current e-cigarette use reported by 11.8% of participants and substantially higher prevalence among males than females. Dual use was also frequent; approximately half of current e-cigarette users also used at least one combustible tobacco product, suggesting that e-cigarette use often coexists with broader patterns of tobacco and nicotine consumption rather than occurring as an isolated behavior.

Although most participants had a high level of knowledge regarding e-cigarettes, important knowledge gaps remained, and positive attitudes toward e-cigarettes were observed in nearly one-quarter of the sample. Higher knowledge was inversely associated with positive attitudes, while current e-cigarette use was associated with more favorable attitudes toward e-cigarettes. In the final adjusted model, positive attitude toward e-cigarettes, having close friends who currently smoked, and spending more than five hours per day at college were independently associated with current e-cigarette use.

The self-reported symptoms described by a small subgroup of former conventional smokers who switched to e-cigarettes should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory, self-perceived experiences rather than clinically confirmed outcomes. Overall, these findings provide baseline evidence on e-cigarette-related knowledge, attitudes, and use among Palestinian medical students and physicians. They highlight the need for structured, evidence-based education on emerging tobacco and nicotine products within medical curricula and continuing professional development, alongside stronger tobacco-control and prevention efforts targeting healthcare populations.
