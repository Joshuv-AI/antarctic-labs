 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This document provides a high-level summary of the potential human and environmental impacts
associated with carbon dioxide (CO2) pipeline transport, injection, and storage activities. It also
reviews lessons learned from natural and industrial analogs of CO 2 storage as well as case
studies of notable accidental CO2 releases. The analysis is structured into several key sections,
each addressing specific resource impacts and potential consequences.

Chapter 1: Introduction and Purpose

 1.1 Description of CO2 Pipeline and CO2 Storage Infrastructure Projects: This section
 provides a brief overview of the extensive network of CO2 pipelines in the United States,
 primarily used for enhanced oil recovery (EOR). It describes the types of geologic formations
 suitable for CO2 storage, including saline aquifers, depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs, and the
 mechanisms of CO2 trapping.

 1.2 Potential Storage Locations: The U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) identifies regions
 with significant CO2 storage potential, and discusses current and proposed projects, including
 the location of projects with Underground Injection Control (UIC) Class VI well permits
 issued by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA).

Chapter 2: Potential Routine and CO2 Specific Impacts

This chapter describes the nature of the possible impacts, which can be routine or accident-
related.

 2.1 Routine Impacts: Routine construction and operations impacts include environmental
 disturbances from pipeline and infrastructure development. These impacts would be similar
 to pipeline and well development activity for other industries and are not unique to CO 2
 transport and storage. Regulatory requirements and best management practices are in place to
 mitigate these impacts.

 2.2 Accident-Related Impacts: Potential accidental impacts include CO2 releases, induced
 seismicity, surface deformation, and contaminant mobilization. This section also describes
 the regulatory frameworks designed to prevent and manage such incidents.

Chapter 3: Resource Impacts

 3.1 Air Quality: Potential impacts include emissions of criteria pollutants from construction
 equipment and accidental releases of hazardous air pollutants (HAPs) during CO 2 releases.

 3.2 Geology: Discusses the risks of induced seismicity and surface deformation due to
 pressurized CO2 injection, with historical data indicating mostly microseismic events.

 3.3 Soil Resources: Highlights potential soil acidification and contamination from CO2
 releases and accidental releases of drilling fluids.

 vii

 3.4 Water Resources: Addresses the risk of underground sources of drinking water (USDW)
 and surface water contamination from CO2 and brine migration, emphasizing the importance
 of geological characterization to prevent such occurrences.

 3.5 Vegetation: Identifies potential impacts, including physiological stress and mortality in
 plants due to CO2 releases, with potential effects on species composition. The effects of CO2
 on plants likely will vary significantly by species.

 3.6 Wildlife: Discusses habitat loss and fragmentation from construction, and physiological
 impacts from CO2 exposure. The effects of CO2 on wildlife likely will vary significantly by
 animal type due to differences in physiological tolerance to CO2 exposure.

 3.7 Aquatic Biological Resources: Discusses potential impacts on aquatic species from
 changes in water quality due to CO2 and contaminant releases.

 3.8 Human Health and Safety: Evaluates risks from job site accidents, CO2 releases, and
 exposure to other hazardous gases, with a focus on acute and chronic health effects.

 3.9 Land Use, Access, and Recreation Resources: Examines potential restrictions on land
 use and access due to infrastructure placement and CO2 releases.

 3.10 Socioeconomics: Considers economic impacts from accidents related to CO2 pipeline
 transport and storage, including emergency response costs and infrastructure repairs.

 3.11 Infrastructure: Describes potential damage to infrastructure from CO2 releases,
 induced seismicity, and surface deformation.

Chapter 4: Case Studies

This chapter outlines notable natural and industrial CO2 releases and summarizes the lessons
learned.

 4.1 Natural CO2 Releases

 This section explores the insights gained from studying naturally occurring CO 2 releases and
 CO2 domes, which serve as analogs for understanding potential impacts from CO 2 storage
 projects.

 4.1.1 Human Impacts: Natural CO2 releases have been associated with significant human
 fatalities, particularly in volcanic regions. Notable incidents include the catastrophic releases
 from volcanic crater lakes in Cameroon, which resulted in over 1,700 fatalities; and a
 volcanic CO2 release in Indonesia that caused 149 deaths. In Italy, diffuse CO2 degassing
 from dormant volcanic systems has led to several fatalities and numerous health incidents.
 These cases highlight the potential dangers of CO2 accumulation in low-lying areas and
 enclosed spaces, emphasizing the importance of monitoring and public awareness in regions
 with CO2 storage projects.

 viii

 4.1.2 Environmental Impacts: Natural CO2 releases have caused extensive environmental
 damage, including vegetation die-offs and wildlife mortality. For instance, the Mammoth
 Mountain release in California led to significant tree kills over 170 acres. Such events
 illustrate the potential for CO2 releases to disrupt ecosystems, affecting plant and animal
 communities. The lack of systematic studies on wildlife impacts during natural CO 2 releases
 suggests a need for more research to understand these effects fully.

 4.2 Industrial CO2 Case Studies

 This section examines incidents involving CO2 releases from industrial activities, providing
 insights into the risks and management of CO2 storage and transport.

 4.2.1 Natural CO2 Storage Reservoirs: Naturally occurring CO2 reservoirs, such as those in
 the United States and Europe, offer valuable lessons on the geological conditions necessary
 for safe CO2 storage. These reservoirs demonstrate that CO2 can be contained underground
 for extended periods, although some subsurface movement of CO2 from the formation is
 common. While uncommon, a few notable incidents exist, including well blowouts at Sheep
 Mountain, Colorado, and Torre Alfina, Italy, which released significant amounts of CO 2 but
 resulted in no fatalities. These cases underscore the importance of proper well construction
 and monitoring to prevent accidental releases.

 4.2.2 CO2 Pipeline and Storage Case Studies: Recent CO2 pipeline releases, such as those
 operated by Denbury Gulf Coast Pipelines, LLC, highlight the potential for CO2 releases and
 their impacts on local communities. The 2020 Satartia, Mississippi, incident led to
 evacuations and hospitalizations due to CO2 exposure, emphasizing the need for effective
 emergency response plans and public education. The Illinois Basin-Decatur Project (IBDP)
 and the Archer Daniels Midland (ADM) Carbon Storage Facility provide examples of
 successful CO2 storage projects, although the ADM facility experienced an incident in 2024
 due to a corroded monitoring well. These cases demonstrate the importance of rigorous site
 characterization, well integrity, and continuous monitoring to ensure the safety and
 effectiveness of CO2 storage operations.

Lessons Learned: The case studies in Chapter 4 highlight several key lessons for CO 2 storage
and transport projects:

 • Natural CO2 releases illustrate the potential risks of CO2 accumulation in low-lying areas
 and enclosed spaces, underscoring the need for monitoring and public awareness.

 • Industrial incidents emphasize the importance of proper well construction, monitoring,
 and emergency response planning to prevent and manage CO2 releases.

 • The successful containment of CO2 in natural reservoirs and industrial projects
 demonstrates that CO2 can be safely stored underground with appropriate geological
 conditions and regulatory oversight.

 1 INTRODUCTION AND PURPOSE

1.1 DESCRIPTION OF CO2 PIPELINE AND CO2 STORAGE INFRASTRUCTURE
 PROJECTS

The transport of CO2 through pipelines has been practiced for decades and the United States
currently has a network of over 5,300 miles of pipelines transporting millions of tons of CO2
annually (Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016, Bielka, et al. 2024, Folga, et al. 2025).
Thousands of additional pipelines are proposed to transport CO2 in the United States (CRS
2023). Historically, these pipelines typically have been constructed in support of enhanced oil
recovery (EOR), where CO2 is injected into oil and gas formations to facilitate the extraction of
these resources. Since the 1970s, hundreds of millions of tons of CO2 have been injected for the
purposes of enhanced oil recovery, or EOR (Duncan, Nicot and Won Choi 2009). In addition to
EOR, interest exists in constructing new pipelines or repurposing existing pipelines for CO 2
transport in service of the long-term sequestration of CO2 underground in geologic storage
formations.

Underground geologic storage formations used for CO2 injection and storage can be saline
aquifers (the formation with the most storage potential), depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs,
underground coal layers, porous basalt rock, and salt caverns (Benson, et al. 2002, Koornneef,
Ramirez and Turkenburg 2012). To be suitable for CO2 storage, these formations must have
sufficient storage capacity, geology, and structure to allow injection and stable containment of
CO2 (Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016). After injection, CO2 is trapped in the storage
formation by several potential mechanisms that primarily depend on the physical and chemical
properties of the formation (IPCC 2005). One mechanism is structural trapping (or buoyant
trapping) in which there is a physical barrier over the formation (such as an impermeable
caprock layer) that physically prevents the movement of CO2 out of the formation (Anderson
2016). Another physical retention mechanism is residual trapping (or hydraulic trapping) where
CO2 is effectively retained in the pore spaces of the formation. The effectiveness of residual
trapping is dependent on the permeability and porosity of the injection zone. Two other trapping
mechanisms are based on chemical interactions in the formation. Solubility trapping occurs when
CO2 is retained by dissolving in brines (highly saline liquid). In addition, CO2 can react with the
formation rock to form stable carbonate minerals (mineral trapping) (Liu and Mercedes Maroto-
Valer 2011). While both chemical trapping mechanisms take place over a long period of time,
they are the most effective for long-term trapping (Liu and Mercedes Maroto-Valer 2011).

The Department of Energy (DOE) anticipates a substantial increase in environmental reviews of
CO2 pipelines and geologic CO2 storage infrastructure. Therefore, the purpose of this document
is to provide brief summaries of the potential causes and impacts to humans and the environment
resulting from CO2 pipeline transport and storage activities. The potential impacts are addressed
under broad resource categories typical of National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA)
programmatic assessments. While the resource evaluations are limited due to the lack of project-
and location-specific details, this document can be used to inform future programmatic NEPA
evaluations. The document also reviews the lessons learned from natural and industrial analogs
of CO2 storage as well as case studies of existing accidental releases of CO 2 from pipelines and
storage formations. A comparative analysis of CO2 transport and storage operations and oil and

gas operations is beyond the scope of this document, but a comparison of pipeline incidents for
the transport of CO2 and refined petroleum products can be found in Volume 1 of this report
series (Folga, et al. 2025).

1.2 POTENTIAL STORAGE LOCATIONS

In 2012, the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) completed an assessment of the technically
accessible storage resources for geologic CO2 storage in the United States based on current
geologic and hydrologic knowledge and current engineering practices (USGS 2012). Regions
with suitable CO2 storage locations exist throughout the United States. However, the Coastal
Plains region has the greatest storage potential by far (65% of potential storage), followed by
Alaska and the Rocky Mountains and Northern Great Plains (both 9%), Eastern Mid-Continent
(8%), and the Western Mid-Continent (5%).

Globally, there have been multiple prior and ongoing CO2 storage projects (generally combined
with EOR), including some in the United States, as reviewed in Yang et al. (2023). As of June
2025, the U.S. EPA has issued four final Underground Injection Control (UIC) Class VI well
permits (wells used to inject CO2 into deep rock formations) with the permits covering a total of
eight wells. The projects are located in Illinois, Indiana, Texas, and California (EPA n.d.-a).
Nationwide as of June 2025, the EPA is reviewing 49 CO2 storage applications, primarily in
Texas, California, and Illinois (EPA n.d.-a). Notably, not all CO2 storage operations are
regulated by the EPA as the EPA has granted Wyoming, North Dakota, Louisiana, Arizona, and
West Virginia “primacy”—that is, authority to regulate and permit UIC Class VI injection
wells—and nine additional states have begun the application process for primacy.

2.1 ROUTINE IMPACTS

Routine construction impacts would potentially result from the environmental disturbance
associated with construction of pipelines, the well site, and support infrastructure. Other
construction impacts may result from equipment emissions, increased traffic and noise, and some
of these impacts would continue to a lesser degree through the operational life of the project due
to normal operations, maintenance, and repair activities (Table 2-1). The significance of these
impacts would depend on project-specific details to be evaluated during the project permitting
process. Projects that use existing pipeline routes and well fields would likely have fewer
impacts than projects requiring new land clearing. Construction-related impacts for CO2 pipeline
and storage projects are not unique as they are similar to those of past construction activities for
the oil and gas industry. Consequently, these routine construction impacts are well characterized.
Regulatory requirements, currently in place, to avoid and minimize such impacts would be a
condition of project permitting. In addition, well-established best management practices exist
that can be incorporated into project permits. For example, the Federal Energy Regulatory
Commission (FERC), which regulates the interstate natural gas pipelines, has developed
comprehensive guidance for minimizing impacts from pipeline construction and operations
(FERC n.d.).

CO2 releases. CO2 releases can occur from such events as cracked or ruptured pipelines, well
blowouts, damaged well casings, and from the geologic storage formation after injection occurs.
Pipeline releases can occur when the physical structure of the pipeline is compromised by
pipeline corrosion, faulty valves, human error, sabotage, and natural disasters (landslides;
earthquakes) (Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016). Among the factors determining the
amount and duration of CO2 released are pipeline pressure, size and type of damage (crack vs
rupture), the amount of time to detect the release, spacing of valve stations, and the efficacy of
pipeline shutdown procedures (Brown, et al. 2014, Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016).
The area affected by the pipeline release will also be determined by geographic terrain, soil, and
weather conditions. For example, CO2 is heavier than air and will tend to settle in low-lying
areas, while strong winds and open areas may disperse and dilute CO2 quickly upon release. In
addition, for buried pipelines, soil conditions, along with leak and rupture dynamics, would
strongly influence the amount of CO2 reaching the atmosphere (Vitali, et al. 2021).

Releases from CO2 pipeline infrastructure are rare, especially considering the thousands of miles
of pipelines and the decades of pipeline transport activity. Between 2010 and 2024, a total of
76 CO2 pipeline incidents were reported to the PHMSA database (Folga, et al. 2025).
Furthermore, based on data from existing CO2 and natural gas pipelines, releases are of short
duration because they are usually detected quickly by pipeline sensors and pipeline systems are
equipped with emergency shutoff valves that can stop CO2 release (Duncan, Nicot and Won
Choi 2009).

Accidental CO2 releases from the well or storage formation can move to non-target formations
(e.g., non-permitted areas such as USDWs) belowground and/or move vertically to surface soils
and then enter the atmosphere. Accidental CO2 release during injection and storage can take
several forms (Pearce, et al. 2014, Koornneef, Ramirez and Turkenburg 2012) :

 • CO2 releases to the atmosphere from a point source like an injection well blowout,
 improperly sealed injection or monitoring well casings, or old poorly sealed wells from
 earlier oil and gas operations that penetrate the caprock.

 4

 • CO2 release to the atmosphere from multiple sites along faults and fractures that are pre-
 existing or created or expanded by CO2 injection.

 • Diffuse CO2 release to the atmosphere through permeable soils over a wide area due to
 caprock permeability that allows the vertical movement of CO2 through the caprock.

 • Subsurface lateral or vertical CO2 movement outside of its target storage formation due to
 formation failure (e.g., existing or pressure induced faults, permeability of the caprock) or
 subsurface well failure.

The release of CO2 through well blowouts and well casing failures are the primary release
pathway (Roberts and Stalker 2020). While new wells are constructed using CO2-resistant
cements and steel alloys (as stipulated under the EPA UIC Class VI well requirements), CO2
release may also occur from old, abandoned wells used for prior oil and gas activities. Well
releases may last hours to weeks and can be corrected using well-established methods derived
from EOR experience. Other release pathways like caprock failure or release through faults and
fractures may result in CO2 releases of greater temporal duration (Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and
Hahn 2016). Several methods have been identified to stop CO2 movement from geologic
formations through faults and fractures. Simply ceasing CO2 injection and/or removing CO2
using extraction wells to allow the formation pressure to decrease would be one measure
(Esposito and Benson 2011). Various methods, evaluated through experimental, field, and
modeling studies, have also been proposed to stop releases using polymer and silica-based gels,
microcements and nanoparticles to seal movement pathways (Tongwa, et al. 2013, Brunner and
Neelea 2017). In addition, these materials can be used to divert flow from the CO2 storage
reservoir to an adjacent reservoir (Brunner and Neelea 2017).

Projected CO2 release from the target geologic storage formations will vary by project site and
acceptable levels of release have not been established, although the IPCC (2005) proposed a
storage retention of at least 99% of CO2 over a 1,000-year period (Roberts and Stalker 2020).
The CO2 storage formations will be up to thousands of meters underground and natural CO2
trapping processes can reduce the migration of CO2 to the atmosphere if it does escape from the
storage formation and these trapping mechanisms will continue to decrease the annual
probability of atmospheric release over time. Documented evidence reveals that CO2 storage
sites can be successfully identified using the appropriate geologic studies (Koornneef, Ramirez
and Turkenburg 2012, Roberts and Stalker 2020, Pearce, et al. 2014, Yang, et al. 2023, Xiao, et
al. 2024). This evidence includes:

 • Geologic formations with natural CO2 accumulations that have not exhibited harmful
 levels of atmospheric release.

 • Laboratory experiments and numerical simulations that provide structural tolerances of
 caprock integrity to CO2 injection.

 • Existing data from EOR projects and industrial scale CO2 storage projects (e.g.,
 Weyburn, In Salah, Illinois Basin – Decatur Project) that suggest environmentally
 significant long-term CO2 release from a storage formation is unlikely.

 5

 • Multiple field-based CO2 storage studies conducted under DOE’s Regional Carbon
 Sequestration Partnerships, which stored more than 12 million metric tons of CO2 and
 found no human health or environmental impacts (NETL n.d.-a).

In addition, the DOE has funded CO2 storage feasibility assessments under the Carbon Storage
Assurance Facility Enterprise (CarbonSAFE) initiative and the development of a series of best
practices for carbon storage manuals by the National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL
n.d.-b).

Releases of other gases. Along with CO2, the CO2 capture process may introduce gaseous
halogens, sulfur, and nitrogen compounds into CO2 transport and injection activities (Hillebrand,
Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016). In addition, other potentially harmful gases naturally occurring in
the storage formation (e.g., hydrogen sulfide [H2S]) can be mobilized during CO2 releases
depending on the geological characteristics of the formation. Therefore, CO2 released from
pipelines or geologic storage may also co-release other gases into the atmosphere or aquifers
(Koornneef, Ramirez and Turkenburg 2012, Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016). As
described in the individual resource sections, these gases are more harmful than CO2 and can
affect a variety of resources.

Induced seismicity. The injection of CO2 would increase pressure in the geologic formation and
the surrounding area to the degree that it could induce seismic events (Xiao, et al. 2024).
Increased seismic activity may also increase permeability of the storage formation resulting in
migration of CO2 out of the formation. As discussed in Section 3.2, only minor seismic events
have been documented at existing CO2 injection projects, with most events undetectable to
humans (Xiao, et al. 2024).

Avoiding known faults would be important in minimizing induced seismicity. To reduce the
probability of induced seismicity, the EPA’s regulations for UIC Class VI wells require studies
to evaluate the potential for induced seismicity when evaluating potential CO2 storage
formations. Also required is a post-well installation formation monitoring and risk mitigation
plan for seismic activity (Xiao, et al. 2024).

Surface deformation. Due to the pressure induced by CO2 injection into the geologic storage
formation, the overlying geologic layers may undergo deformation that is transmitted all the way
to the earth’s surface (Soong, et al. 2023). While modeling and field studies of CO2 storage
projects suggest the annual ground uplift is only in millimeters, there is the potential for damage
to infrastructure (Section 3.11).

Mobilization of contaminants. Surface water and potable groundwater (in many cases, USDW)
contamination are primary concerns associated with CO2 storage. If CO2 were to escape from the
storage formation and migrate to potable aquifers it could acidify the groundwater and mobilize
arsenic and other metals that naturally occur in the aquifer due to its mineralogy (Hillebrand,
Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016). In addition, the injection of CO2 into the storage formation may
displace existing brines from the formation, which could subsequently enter groundwater
aquifers. In addition to the high salinity, brines may contain concentrations of metals harmful to
humans and the environment. Such events could contaminate water sources used for drinking
water, industrial, or agricultural uses as well as surface water used by plants, humans, and

 6

wildlife (IPCC 2005). The type of contaminants potentially mobilized can vary with the injection
reservoir type (e.g., saline aquifer; depleted oil and gas reservoir) as well as the local geology of
the storage formation, leakage pathway, and aquifer (IPCC 2005, Zheng, et al. 2021, Wei, et al.
2023). The introduction or mobilization of contaminants represents a potentially longer-term
challenge, as contaminants may remain in the area of release for longer than CO 2 due to natural
chemical and biological retention processes.

The primary method to minimize the potential for CO2 or brine intrusion into USDW resources
is appropriate characterization of subsurface geology to ensure containment of CO 2 and that
there is a sufficient physical separation between the CO 2 injection formation and potable aquifers
(IPCC 2005).

Regulatory programs are in place to minimize the potential for the CO2 pipeline and storage
impacts described above. The U.S. Department of Transportation's Pipeline and Hazardous
Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA) regulates the safety of CO 2 pipelines. This includes
PHMSA's Office of Hazardous Materials Safety regulations to minimize threats to life, property,
and the environment due to hazardous materials-related incidents and the Office of Pipeline
Safety, which ensures safety in the design, construction, operation, maintenance, and spill
response planning (PHMSA 2021).

CO2 injection wells and storage areas are regulated under the EPA UIC Class VI well permits.
Requirements for well permits are extensive, one of the many goals of these requirements is to
prevent accidents (EPA n.d.-b). Key safety requirements are listed below, and they would reduce
the potential for the resource specific impacts described in Section 4:

 • Site characterization requirements to ensure containment of CO 2 within the injection zone
 and that induced seismicity is not a concern.

 • Modeling the projected extent of the injected CO2 plume and geologic pressure during
 pre-project evaluations.

 • Well construction safety requirements to prevent CO 2 or formation fluids from moving
 outside of the injection zone.

 • Testing, monitoring, and reporting requirements for wells, groundwater quality, and the
 CO2 plume and pressure throughout project operations and after CO2 injection has ended.

 • Operational safety and emergency and remedial response plans to ensure safe injection
 activity.

 • Safety requirements to avoid endangering USDWs or human health and to plug the
 injection well in a manner that will not allow fluid movement that endangers USDWs.

 • Requirements for the operator maintain funding to cover accident remediation costs,
 plugging the injection well, post-injection site care.

3.1 AIR QUALITY

For NEPA analysis, air quality impacts are evaluated in terms of construction- and operational
project- related emissions of criteria air pollutants to ensure the project would not result in
exceedances of the National Ambient Air Quality Standards. Primary and secondary ambient air
quality standards exist for six criteria pollutants: sulfur dioxide, carbon monoxide, ozone,
nitrogen dioxide, lead, and particulate matter (EPA n.d.-c). Equipment operations (trucks,
excavators, generators, pumps, etc.) necessary for CO2 pipeline transport and storage projects
would potentially increase the atmospheric concentration of criteria pollutants in the immediate
area of the emission sources. The greatest amount of pollutant emissions would likely occur
during the construction phase of the project. Sources would include emissions from construction
equipment, worker vehicles, and delivery trucks as well as fugitive dust resulting from
excavation, soil storage, and handling. During operations, some periodic increase in the local
baseline pollution emissions may occur due to pipeline pumps and compressors, facility
maintenance activities, well workovers, and diesel generators.

The impacts of these releases would depend on a number of factors, including equipment used
and the frequency of use, existing pollution levels, land use, the duration of construction, and the
project location among other factors. These air quality impacts would be evaluated at the project-
specific level during the permitting process to ensure compliance with the Clean Air Act.

In addition, the EPA regulates emissions of HAPs under the Clean Air Act. Of the 188 HAPs, the
movement of carcinogenic radon (a naturally occurring radioactive gas) from the soil to the
atmosphere during an accidental leak of injected CO 2 has received the most attention for its
potential human health effects (Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016, Koornneef, Ramirez
and Turkenburg 2012) (Table 3-1). See Section 3.8 for a discussion of radon release as pertains
human health.

CO2 itself is not a criteria air pollutant or HAP, but it has well documented effects on human
health. The potential impacts of CO2 and associated air pollutants on humans and the
environment are evaluated in resource specific sections of Chapter 3.

3.2 GEOLOGY

Potential impacts on geology from CO2 storage could result from increasing pressure in
subsurface geology to a degree that may generate earthquakes and changes in the ground surface
topography (Table 3-2).

Table 3-2. Potential Impacts on Geologic Resources Resulting from CO2 Transport, Injection, and

Induced seismicity. Pressure changes following CO2 injection could radiate from the injection
site for tens of miles, eventually diminishing to background levels with distance from the
injection wells (Zhou, et al. 2010). These pressure changes could activate existing faults,
producing seismic events ranging from low-intensity, microseismic activity (too small to be felt
by humans) to more powerful seismic events. The greatest potential for induced seismic activity
would be in areas already prone to earthquakes (DOE 2013). Another key factor is the size of
existing faults, as there is a positive relationship between fault length and the magnitude of a
potential seismic event (Cheng, et al. 2023). Furthermore, if faults that ran through the CO2
storage reservoir caprock were activated by CO2 injection, the integrity of the reservoir could be
compromised.

Experience gained and data collected throughout CO 2 injection for EOR operations provides
information on potential injection related seismic activity, although it is important to note that
injection volumes and well construction standards differ between EOR wells (which adhere to
UIC Class II standards) and UIC Class VI wells used for the permanent geologic storage of CO2.
At only one project site, an EOR-related CO2 injection at the Cogdell oil field in Texas, was CO2
injection potentially associated with a large seismic event (magnitude 4.4) as well as 18 events
that were of a magnitude of 3 or greater (Xiao et al. 2024). The amount of CO2 injected for EOR
is less than that proposed for CO2 storage, suggesting that CO2 storage may carry a higher
seismic risk (White and Foxall 2016). However, recent reviews of global CO2 storage projects
indicate only microseismic events (magnitude < 3) at most following CO2 injection, many with
magnitudes less than 1, which would not be felt on the ground (Cheng, et al. 2023). This was true
despite the differences between projects with respect to storage formation, depth, and injection
volumes. In addition, seismic monitoring at the Illinois Basin Decatur CO2 storage project
detected approximately 20,000 microseismic events with a magnitude of less than 1.2 since
injection and storage began in 2011 (see Section 4.2.2). Similarly, microseismic activity was
found at a CO2 storage project in Salah, Algeria (Xiao, et al. 2024). Federal regulations and
existing industry best management practices are expected to minimize geologic impacts. These
include geologic studies of the site to evaluate the location of existing faults and the potential for
seismic activity following CO2 injection.

 9

Surface deformation. Subsurface pressure associated with natural CO2 accumulations have been
documented to swell the ground surface, suggesting a similar potential for CO 2 storage projects
(Viveiros and Silva 2024). Another potential cause of surface deformation is well blowouts,
which have been documented to cause craters during geothermal and oil and gas drilling. Based
on data from existing CO2 injection sites, CO2 injection has the potential to cause the earth
surface to rise for some distance around the injection site. For example, at the In Salah CO2
injection site in Algeria, several years of CO2 injection resulted in a surface uplift rate of up to
0.64 cm/year with swelling around the injection well of up to 1.27 cm/year and total uplift of up
to approximately 2.5 cm. Ground swelling extended up to 6.2 miles from the injection site
(Onuma and Okinawa 2009, Soong, et al. 2023). However, the effects were temporary at some
locations, as the injection volume declined over time.

Routine impacts. The construction of pipelines, wells, and support facilities would result in soil
disturbance by pipeline placement and construction equipment. In addition, underground pipeline
placement may require trenching and excavation within the right of way (ROW). Soil
disturbance from pipeline placement can significantly impact physical, chemical, and biological
soil properties, as reviewed in Brehm and Culman (2022). While these impacts are not found
consistently, the documented effects to soils in the disturbed ROW can include decreased water

 10

infiltration, soil moisture, stability, and an increase in compaction, temperature, erodibility, and
rock fragments. Pipeline placement is also documented to reduce soil organic carbon and affect
various chemical and nutrient soil constituents in complex ways. In addition, pipeline-related soil
disturbance has been found to decrease microbial biomass in the soil. Such impacts may be
reduced if existing pipeline ROWs are used.

Soil contamination from accidental spillage of fuels and drilling fluids is also possible. Such
impacts are typical of any pipeline and drilling project and would be mitigated by best
management practices and spill cleanup plans.

CO2-specific impacts. CO2 release from underground pipelines may affect the chemical,
physical, and biological characteristics of the soil around the release. Studies of CO 2 dispersion
in soil following experimental pipeline breakage indicate that CO2 can spread laterally and
vertically and that the soil CO2 concentrations following injection are highly variable spatially,
depending on pipeline burial depth and size, size of the pipeline rupture, release direction, and
duration as well as the physical properties of the soil, such as soil composition (sand, gravel,
clay) and soil temperature (Yan, et al. 2016, Liu and Mercedes Maroto-Valer 2011, Yu, et al.
2024, Uilhoorn 2013). Weather is also important, with the highest CO2 flux from the soil to the
surface during dry conditions and periods of low atmospheric pressure.

Pipeline ruptures can affect soils by explosive releases of pressurized CO 2 that physically
remove soils and damage soil structure. In addition, following a pipeline fracture, high-pressure
CO2 in a liquid or gaseous state would undergo rapid depressurization and cooling (the Joule-
Thomson effect), resulting in a drop in soil temperature and, potentially, the formation of ice
hydrates in the soil (Uilhoorn, 2013; Liu et al. 2019). In the case of small, millimeter-sized
pipeline fractures, ice spheres and temperature changes may be less than a few meters from the
source (Yan et al. 2016; Yu et al. 2024). However, larger pipeline fractures could potentially
affect a greater soil area, as was the case with the 2020 Denbury Gulf Coast Pipelines, LLC
pipeline failure in Satartia, Mississippi, which created an estimated 12-meter-deep crater and ice
cover for several meters around the fracture site (USDOT 2022).

If a longer-term (weeks to months) release of CO2 from the storage formation or from a faulty
well were to occur, the movement of CO2 to surface soils could increase CO2 levels in soil,
resulting in soil acidification, low oxygen levels, and changes in soil permeability and mineral
content (Patil, Colls and Steven 2010). In addition, soil acidification can mobilize heavy metals
that may adversely affect soil ecosystem processes (Gupta and Yadav 2020). Several
experimental long-term CO2 soil injection studies have been conducted to better understand the
fate and effects of CO2 released during a potential leak from a CO2 storage formation. These
studies indicate that atmospheric flux and natural chemical transformation in the soil would
return the soil to baseline conditions over time after the CO2 release is stopped soil (Gupta and
Yadav 2020, Roberts and Stalker 2020). The effects of soil CO2 injections lasted hours to days in
the case of soil CO2 concentration and soil chemistry (Roberts and Stalker 2020). As with
experimental pipeline releases, the movement of injected CO 2 through soil and its flux to the
atmosphere is influenced by factors such as soil composition, moisture, and temperature as well
as atmospheric conditions.

 11

Experimental CO2 injection studies also have found impacts to soil microbes within days of CO2
injection, if soil gas CO2 concentrations reached 10% (Roberts and Stalker 2020). Studies of
natural CO2 degassing areas indicate that CO2-saturated soils have distinct, often low-density soil
microbial communities compared to unsaturated soils, suggesting industrial CO2 releases would
also result in changes in soil microbial species composition and activity (Pearce, et al. 2014,
Gupta and Yadav 2020, Viveiros and Silva 2024). However, these changes are expected to be
temporary (up to a few months) (Roberts and Stalker 2020).

The injection and vertical migration of CO2 could introduce displaced brines, organic and
inorganic contaminants, and radon into the upper soil layers (Gupta and Yadav 2020, Viveiros
and Silva 2024). For example, radon, a known carcinogen, is a radioactive elemental gas that can
be found in naturally high concentration in certain soil types and is often associated with areas of
high naturally occurring CO2 release. Depending on their concentration in the soil, these
introduced contaminants may reach levels that are harmful to the soil ecosystem.

Groundwater (USDW). A USDW is an aquifer or a part of an aquifer that is currently used as a
drinking water source or has the potential to be a drinking water source in the future. CO2 could
reach USDW aquifers by existing or induced faults, permeability in the caprock, or by failure of

 12

an injection well that passes through the aquifer (Xiao, et al. 2024). Consequently, the potential
for USDW contamination would be determined by factors such as long-term storage reservoir
integrity and CO2 trapping capacity and proper well installation and construction. If CO2 reaches
USDW resources, it may acidify the groundwater and potentially mobilize potentially toxic
amounts of metals and organic compounds originating in both storage reservoirs that are brine-
filled and USDWs containing freshwater. The potential for such an event depends on whether the
rock layers of the formations would be a significant source of contaminants (Hillebrand et al.
2016; Gupta and Yadav 2020). Methods proposed for remediating CO2 intrusion into aquifers
include horizontal or vertical extraction wells to remove the CO2; injecting water to dissolve the
gaseous CO2, increasing capillary trapping; or a combination of these methods (Esposito and
Benson 2011).

Deep saline aquifers (porous rock formations containing water with high concentrations of
dissolved salts) are considered to have high potential for CO2 injection due to their lack of
suitability for human use and their high storage capacity (Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn
2016). However, injecting CO2 into these formations may displace the brine into aquifers with
potable water (Koornneef, Ramirez and Turkenburg 2012). These brines contain high
concentrations of dissolved salts and may also contain toxic concentrations of metals and organic
compounds that may result in long-term contamination if they were to enter the aquifer
(Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016, Xiao, et al. 2024).

Finally, CO2 injection has the potential to alter the flow and elevation of groundwater (Gupta and
Yadav 2020). For example, modeling studies of the Gulf Coast Basin of the United States found
that injecting 50 million tons of CO2 per year for 50 years reversed the direction of groundwater
flow and increased the water table elevation by ~1m. The modeled increase in elevation resulted
in greater groundwater evaporation and at higher injection rates would potentially increase
surface water flow in streams that receive inputs from the aquifer. Gupta and Yadav (2020)
provide a comprehensive review of the effects of CO2 injection on groundwater.

EPA UIC Class VI well permits have requirements to mitigate the potential for new CO2
injection projects to contaminate USDWs. These include the identification of USDW aquifers
that may be affected by the project, risk assessment for USDW contamination, and the
development of mitigation plans (Xiao, et al. 2024). Though data are limited, no documented
instances exist of CO2 storage projects contaminating USDWs.

Surface water. Potential impacts on surface water resources from CO2 pipeline transport and
injection may occur during construction and operation activities. During pipeline construction,
impacts on surface water would be similar to any pipeline construction operation. Such impacts
include potential runoff of sediment into stream crossings and disturbance to streambeds during
pipeline placement. In some cases, streams may be excavated and the pipeline buried. In many
cases, disturbance to streams could be minimized by aboveground pipelines or pipeline
placement using horizontal drilling beneath the streambed to avoid damage to the stream. While
an unlikely event, a CO2 injection well blowout may release formation fluids (e.g., brine) to an
adjacent surface waterbody that could adversely affect water quality (DOE 2013). Similarly, well
drilling for CO2 injection would generate drilling wastes that could accidentally be released into
surface waters. However, existing regulations and best management practices would minimize
the potential for such events.

 13

The potential USDW impact scenarios described above are also a potential source of surface
water contamination if contaminated groundwater were to discharge into surface water (Gupta
and Yadav 2020). Depending on the degree of contamination, the surface waters connected to the
contaminated USDW may become unusable as a source of drinking water, recreation, or
irrigation. However, no studies indicate that CO2 storage projects have introduced CO2 or
contaminants into surface water.

Routine impacts. As with any pipeline construction project, vegetation within the pipeline
corridor would be removed unless an existing pipeline corridor is used. Similarly, vegetation
clearing at the CO2 injection well site would be necessary for developing new well sites and
support roads and operational facilities. For belowground pipelines, revegetation would occur

productivity for some crops has also been reported (Brehm and Culman 2022). In natural plant

revegetation would occur following project decommissioning, construction equipment used for
pipeline placement has the potential to introduce invasive plant species, which may result in

and richness compared to undisturbed areas (Brehm and Culman 2022). Also, protected species
listed under the Endangered Species Act (ESA) or state species lists could be affected by

infrastructure development but impacts to listed species can be minimized or avoided during
project specific consultation.

CO2-specific impacts. If large quantities of CO2 reach the soil due to movement from the storage
reservoir or well casing, there is the potential for soil freezing, soil acidification, and low oxygen
soil conditions to occur, with resulting impacts on plant physiology, growth, seed production
and, ultimately, species density and composition in the area affected (IPCC 2005, Gupta and
Yadav 2020). CO2 is well documented to affect plant communities. For example, rapid exposure
to toxic levels of CO2 is documented to cause large plant die offs and high levels of naturally
occurring soil CO2 can prevent vegetation growth (Pearce, et al. 2014, Viveiros and Silva 2024).
Plants show stress responses to CO2 at about 5% to 20% soil gas concentration with death
occurring after a few weeks of continued exposure (Benson, et al. 2002, Pearce, et al. 2014,
IPCC 2005). Such exposure could affect natural plant communities as well as agricultural crops
(Viveiros and Silva 2024). However, the effects will vary with season, plant species, and existing
plant adaptation to CO2 exposure. For example, inter-specific physiological differences exist in
trees versus grasses, with grasses being less influenced by CO2 levels (Benson, et al. 2002).
Therefore, grasses may be favored by high CO2 conditions, which could change the existing
vegetation communities in the area affected by the release (Benson, et al. 2002). If the source of
CO2 release is stopped, soil conditions should return to baseline over time through CO 2 flux to
the atmosphere and chemical transformation in the soil at which time plant communities could
recover.

As described in Section 3.4, CO2 injection could introduce saline brines, radon, or potentially
toxic organic or inorganic pollutants into USDWs. Therefore, another potential impact to plants
could result from gases or contaminated groundwater entering soil or surface water where it
could be taken up by plants. Many contaminants may be retained in the soil and so any harmful
effects on natural plant communities as well as agricultural production would likely be more
long-term than soil saturation by CO2.

Routine impacts. Potential construction impacts on wildlife include habitat loss and

CO2-specific impacts. If CO2 were to reach wildlife habitat following a release, there is the
potential for wildlife to be exposed to toxic levels of CO2. In a review of naturally occurring CO2
releases, several instances of animal mortality ranging from insects to large mammals, including
cattle, were reported (Viveiros and Silva 2024). As with humans, the potential effects of CO2 on
wildlife will depend on the amount released and the location, geography, and weather conditions
as these factors affect CO2 gas concentration and dispersion. The IPCC (2005) notes that ground-
dwelling animals may be subjected to the highest concentrations of CO 2 as CO2 is diluted as it
moves up in the atmosphere. In addition, the impacts of CO2 exposure on wildlife will vary by
animal taxa. For example, insects have much higher tolerance to CO2 than vertebrates (Benson,
et al. 2002). Studies of soil invertebrates found CO2 induced mortality for several species
occurred between 11% and 15% with non-lethal effects at concentrations as low as 2%, as
reviewed in Benson, et al. (2002). Due to their respiratory physiology and habitat adaptations,
many reptiles, especially turtles, are often more tolerant to elevated CO2 than are mammals.
Burrowing mammals like gophers and rodents are also generally more tolerant of CO 2 due to the

 16

natural variability of CO2 concentration in soil and these species can tolerate CO2 concentrations
greater than 10% (Benson, et al. 2002).

While large natural CO2 releases are documented to kill animals and the vegetation they use as
habitat, no such events have been detected in association with industrial CO2 injections, likely
because of the small size of the CO2 releases and the ability of industrial operation to detect and
stop the release (Lewicki, Birkholzer and Tsang 2007).

and Keister 2022, Folga, et al. 2025). Assuming large-scale CO2 storage occurred by 2050 (over
93,000 miles of pipelines; 500 well sites at 450 installations with 5,000–15,000 workers), Duong
and Loisel (2011) estimated annual worker fatalities between 4 and 15 per year for CO2 pipeline
transport, injection, and storage operations alone. This estimate was based on oil and gas analogs
and existing CO2-related industrial activities. The authors noted significant data limitations in
developing the estimates and that they were likely to change with new CO 2 accident data and
improvements in industrial safety practices. Based on existing data, fatalities associated with
CO2 pipeline transport, injection and storage were no greater than for the oil and gas industry,
and lower than other industrial extraction activities like mining (Duong and Loisel 2011).

Accidental CO2 release. Pipelines often run through or near areas of mixed use and ownership
potentially putting the public at risk from a pipeline incident. Therefore, pipeline incidents
occurring in populated areas could initially harm individuals exposed to the explosive force of
the pipeline break and the extreme cold temperatures of the released CO 2. The primary concern
for human health related to CO2 pipeline transport and storage activities is toxicity following the
release and dangerous levels of concentration of CO2 and other potentially life-threatening gases
such as H2S, sulfur dioxide (SO2), and sulfur trioxide (SO3) (Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn
2016). CO2 releases could occur during pipeline transport, well injection, and post-injection if
CO2 releases from the storage formation and reaches the surface. CO 2 toxicity in humans can
result from both acute and chronic exposure. Acute exposure would occur if large quantities of
CO2 were to be rapidly released into the atmosphere, likely through pipeline breakage or by
upward movement to the surface from the storage formation through a large fault or through the
injection well or an old, abandoned well. Although acute exposures may be of short duration
(seconds to hours), higher CO2 exposure concentrations (2%–30% CO2) can have significant
physiological effects up to mortality (Boada, et al. 2024). The Occupational Safety and Health
Administration (OSHA) considers a time-weighted average of 0.5% atmospheric CO2 over an 8-
hour period as a permissible exposure limit for CO2 (OSHA 1996). Early symptoms of acute
exposure in humans include blood acidification (acidosis), headaches, nausea, and dizziness
(Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016). The acidosis can trigger rapid breathing, lethargy,
cardiac arrhythmia, and seizures, and mortality in the case of asphyxiation and severe acidosis.
The severity of the effects increases with exposure time and CO 2 concentration. For example,
atmospheric CO2 from 1 to 3% begins to reduce blood pH and increase breathing rate (de Larry,
et al. 2012). Exposure to 6% CO2 can induce headaches and difficulty breathing in less than a
few hours, while unconsciousness can occur within several minutes at concentrations above
10%. Concentrations of atmospheric CO2 of 15% to 30% can result in mortality within minutes
of exposure (de Larry, et al. 2012, Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016).

A chronic exposure could occur if there is a longer-term, undetected flux of CO2 to the
atmosphere or the accumulation of CO2 in homes and buildings from CO2 saturated soils
(Viveiros and Silva 2024). The slow but longer-term release of CO2 can accumulate in buildings,
potentially making them uninhabitable. Indoor CO2 is particularly problematic due to the
confined space and the odorless and colorless nature of CO 2, which may not be initially
recognized by humans. Consequently, exposure may be of longer duration, especially if the
release is not detected for an extended period. Long-term chronic exposure to low levels of CO2,
is less well studied, but case studies from areas with naturally high CO2 emissions have
documented multiple health effects such as increases in headache, fatigue, obesity, diabetes,

 19

bronchitis, and other respiratory disorders. The CO2 levels associated with these health effects
range as low as 0.05% to 0.3% (Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016, Boada, et al. 2024).

If a well blowout or pipeline rupture occurred, individuals could be affected by both the physical
force of the event as well as the subsequent release of CO 2 (Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn
2016). Based on the PHMSA database, there have been 113 incidents of CO2 pipeline releases
between 1994 and 2021 and an average of 5.11 incidents per year between 2004 and 2021
(Bielka, et al. 2024). No fatalities were associated with the reported incidents. The low casualty
rate was likely due to the non-flammable nature of CO2 releases, the small volumes released, and
the remote, sparsely populated location of the pipelines. See Section 4.2.2 for a discussion of
case studies involving CO2 pipeline releases.

There are little quantitative data on accidental industrial CO2 releases, therefore most risk
assessments use quantitative models to predict the potential atmospheric concentrations of CO 2
and the effects on humans present in the area. Following pipeline failure, CO2 enters the
atmosphere under high pressure and forms a dense CO2 gas cloud that initially rises in the
atmosphere (Hu, et al. 2022). However, CO2 is denser than air, which slows the upward
movement of the cloud, and it eventually begins to sink even as it is being diluted by ambient air.
Eventually the diluted cloud is dispersed by wind in open areas but may also settle in low-lying
areas. There have been modeling and field-based experimental studies in which CO2 was
released from pipelines to understand how far potentially toxic levels of CO 2 could extend from
the release point, as reviewed in Hillebrand et al. (2016), Vitali et al. (2021), and Bielka et al.
(2024). The results of these studies indicate CO2 dispersion from pipeline releases is highly
variable. The maximum CO2 release rate from a pipeline is estimated to range between 0.001 and
22 tons/second depending mainly on the diameter of the pipeline, pipeline pressure, and the size
of the fracture (Koornneef, Ramirez and Turkenburg 2012).

Similarly, modeling studies indicated that the distance from the pipeline release point where CO2
concentrations are no longer hazardous varied from <1 m to 7.2 km. The large range in distances
produced by these modeling studies indicates the importance of site condition and release
scenario in assessing potential harms from pipeline release incidents. Many studies found CO2
levels decreased rapidly with time and distance from the release point due to mixing with the
atmosphere, with toxic CO2 levels limited to within a few hundred meters of the release
(Koornneef, Ramirez and Turkenburg 2012, Wang, et al. 2021, Hu, et al. 2022). For example, in
a recent evaluation of multiple above and below-ground CO2 pipeline release scenarios of
various sizes, the downwind area in which the probability of fatalities is greater than 50% ranged
from 1.4 m to 239 m from the release point for aboveground pipelines and <0.1 m to 118.4 m for
buried pipelines (Bielka, et al. 2024). In addition to the amount and duration of CO2 released,
local conditions such as land cover, temperature, humidity, wind speed, and topography are
critical factors in both CO2 dispersal distance and atmospheric concentration.

As with pipeline releases, the potential for human health impacts from storage and injection
releases would increase with population density and proximity to sources of CO 2 releases. While
acute exposures from well blowouts have occurred, no long-term chronic human CO2 exposures
have been documented for industrial CO2 injection and storage. Furthermore, the potential for a
long-term release can be minimized by the extensive monitoring of pressure and CO 2 movement
in the formation, which would provide an early warning of CO2 movement from the formation. If

 20

releases from wells or faults or fractures were detected, remediation measures can stop well
releases and prevent the vertical migration of CO2 from the storage formation to the surface
(Section 2.2).

Other gas releases. CO2 releases can contain other gases harmful to humans, including those
originating from CO2 capture and injection (sulfur and nitrogen compounds, halogens) as well as
contaminants found in the local geology that can be mobilized during CO2 release (Hillebrand,
Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016, Koornneef, Ramirez and Turkenburg 2012). For example, as the
references show, H2S is an asphyxiant that can be co-transported and co-injected with CO2 and
could therefore be released along with CO2 during a release. Human exposure to gaseous H2S
could occur outdoors or through accumulation in buildings. H2S is far more toxic than CO2, and
life-threatening exposure levels are orders of magnitude lower than that of CO2 (Hillebrand,
Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016). Radon gas is another concern as CO2 movement to the ground
surface can mobilize radon into soils or the atmosphere (Koornneef, Ramirez and Turkenburg
2012, Boada, et al. 2024). Radon gas is radioactive, and at concentrations that vary by geology,
is documented to cause cancer and respiratory disorders, as documented in areas with natural
CO2 degassing. Human exposure to radon could occur outdoors and from soil releases that
contaminate buildings and homes.

USDW and surface water contamination. CO2 dissolved in water is not necessarily harmful, as
humans use CO2-rich spring waters for drinking water and recreation (Pearce, et al. 2014).
However, examples exist of human fatalities from inhaling toxic amounts of CO2 that degassed
from CO2 saturated surface waters (Lewicki, Birkholzer and Tsang 2007). In addition, as
described in Section 3.4, CO2 injection and movement from the storage formation can mobilize
potentially toxic organic and inorganic compounds that could enter USDWs and ultimately
surface water used for municipal drinking water or irrigation, resulting in the potential for human
exposure to harmful contaminants (Hillebrand, Pflugmacher and Hahn 2016).

3.10 SOCIOECONOMICS

The socioeconomic impacts of CO2 pipeline transport and storage can be defined in terms of 1)
the routine effects of project construction and operations on local communities, economies, and
employment; and 2) impacts from accidents associated with CO2 transport and storage that may
create economic costs to the local economies (Table 3-10).

Routine impacts. Examples of routine impacts include increases in noise, traffic, and emissions
during construction and maintenance activities that may affect local communities. Another
impact common to all pipeline construction is the potential re-introduction of rocks to the soil
surface during the excavation of soil for the purpose of laying underground pipelines. These
rocks may damage farm equipment, prevent crop harvest, reduce crop yield, and require re-
seeding or other remedies. Typically, however, the pipeline company would be responsible for
reimbursing farmers for costs related to these impacts. The significance of such routine impacts
would depend on specific details of the project as well as the characteristics of the local
economies and communities affected and would be evaluated during project permitting.

CO2-specific impacts. Duguid et al. (2022) evaluated the financial costs of CO2 pipeline releases.
The analysis indicated that CO2 releases are uncommon compared to other pipeline accidents and
that the financial costs associated with accidental CO2 pipeline releases are significantly less than
for pipelines carrying other liquids or gases. Examination of state-level CO2 incident data in the

United States indicates that the per-incident property damage is similar for most states and, in
general, is less than $40,000 per incident. This average property damage cost is skewed by the
2020 CO2 pipeline rupture in Satartia, Mississippi (Folga, et al. 2025). This is in comparison
with a per-incident cost of $1 million for natural gas transmission and gathering pipelines (Folga,
et al. 2025).

seismic events and surface deformation unlikely.

 4 CASE STUDIES

4.1 NATURAL CO2 RELEASES

Naturally occurring CO2 releases have been studied to gain insight on the potential impacts from
CO2 storage (Benson, et al. 2002, Lewicki, Birkholzer and Tsang 2007, Duncan, Nicot and Won
Choi 2009, Gal, et al. 2012, Cheng, et al. 2023). There are limitations to this approach because
the amount of CO2 in natural geologic reservoirs is lower than the higher volume, pressurized
injection of CO2 that would occur under geologic storage (Lewicki, Birkholzer and Tsang 2007).
However, natural CO2 releases are useful in providing information on the scale of potential
hazards associated with rapid release of CO2 as well as other potentially harmful gases. In
addition, natural analogs do provide the ability to study the subsurface movement of CO2, soil
CO2 flux, the appropriate geologic conditions for long-term containment of CO2, and the
physical and chemical interactions between injected CO 2 and the reservoir geology (Benson, et
al. 2002).

The CO2 releases that occur naturally are commonly associated with volcanic activity, natural
faults, and outgassing from lakes and are documented to have caused a number of ecosystem and
public health effects (Lewicki, Birkholzer and Tsang 2007). What follows is a summary of some
of the well-documented and consequential CO2 releases.

4.1.1 Human Impacts

Natural CO2 releases have been implicated in hundreds of human fatalities, the largest being
from volcanic crater lakes in Cameroon in 1984 and 1986 (1700+ fatalities) and from the release
of a CO2 gas cloud from the shallow subsurface of a volcano in Indonesia in 1979 (149 fatalities
and 1,000 casualties) (Boada, et al. 2024). Several other incidents with fatalities have occurred in
western Europe, Japan, Ecuador, and the Democratic republic of Congo that were caused by the
release of CO2 of volcanic origin. In the rare event CO2 was measured, CO2 concentrations
during these incidents rose to greater than 10% (Viveiros and Silva 2024).

Many of the most well documented natural CO2 releases have occurred in Italy. Most of the
fatalities recorded there were associated with diffuse CO2 degassing from dormant volcanic
systems, which can be found in both rural and urban areas (Viveiros and Silva 2024). Roberts
et al. (2011) reviewed human and environmental impacts from the large number of natural CO 2
seeps in Italy, including Sicily. Incidents of human fatalities were greatest in higher population
areas and for individuals who were engaging in activities close to the ground or surface waters
that were saturated with CO2. When indoor measurements of CO2 were taken, the highest
measurements occurred in basements and the ground-floor level of buildings (Viveiros and Silva
2024). Scientists have found CO2 seeps killed 19 humans and hundreds of animals over the past
50 years, with the greatest individual loss of human life occurring in 1990 when three adults died
at Mefite D’Ansanto. Other examples of CO2 releases with human impacts include:

 • Albani Hills, Italy. The volcanically formed Albani Hills contain pressurized subsurface
 CO2 reservoirs that can release CO2 through faults, with gas reaching the atmosphere
 through soils, wells, and vents. Two large CO2 releases were documented in 1995 and

 27

 1999 (Lewicki, Birkholzer and Tsang 2007). In 1995, a sudden release of CO2 from the
 soil and shallow water wells caused unsafe accumulations of CO 2 in building basements,
 which prevented their use for a day. Field surveys also found a well in the town of
 Ciampino that was degassing CO2 at lethal levels. In September 1999, a CO2 degassing
 event resulted in the death of 30 cows due to asphyxiation. Structural damage to
 buildings and roads were also reported from the ground swelling due to CO2
 accumulation. The CO2 release appeared to be caused by seismic activity that initiated
 CO2 migration to the atmosphere through surface vents (Viveiros and Silva 2024).

 • Vulcano Island, Italy. Vulcano Island has two active volcanic centers, La Fossa and
 Vulcanello (Gurrieri, et al. 2023). While no recent volcanic eruptions have been recorded,
 the sites undergo periodic increases in CO2 degassing from the craters. These events led
 to at least two human fatalities and the death of several animals during the 1980s. In a
 December 2005 event, scientists estimated a CO2 flux of 1,350 tons/day over the
 1.66 km2 area in which measurements were taken. Due to the large number of tourists, a
 CO2 gas monitoring program was instituted in 2021 to provide an early warning for
 potentially harmful CO2 degassing events (Gurrieri, et al. 2023).

Natural CO2 seeps like those in Italy are more relevant natural analogs for CO2 storage than
volcanic releases because low-temperature releases from seeps would in many cases be similar to
CO2 releases from CO2 storage projects (Roberts, Wood and Haszeldine 2011). The overall
fatality rate from natural CO2 seep releases is low. Using a database of the 286 natural release
incidents in Italy, the rate of accidental human death was calculated to be only 1 in 36 million
per year for the exposed population: a low risk, compared to other well-known hazards such as
fatalities from lightning strikes and accidental carbon monoxide poisoning (Roberts, Wood and
Haszeldine 2011).

Lessons learned. The degassing of CO through soils or surface waters generally occurs
continuously and harmlessly all over the world, with the gas intermixing with the atmosphere
(Benson, et al. 2002). Single incidents with large numbers of human fatalities (>20) have been
documented for large, rapid CO2 releases. However, such incidents were associated with
volcanic and magmatic activity and such releases are not likely to be similar to an accidental CO 2
storage or pipeline release (Benson, et al. 2002).

The risk of human fatalities associated with natural CO 2 seeps is very low. Furthermore, key
differences exist between CO2 storage and natural CO2 releases that suggest CO2 storage is less
likely to result in human casualties compared to natural releases:

 • CO2 pipelines and wells are highly monitored, and any CO2 releases are likely to be
 detected and fixed quickly.

 • Public access to CO2 injection wells would be restricted, unlike many natural CO2 seeps.

 • Local communities can be notified of the CO2 pipeline and injection activities so that
 they are more likely be aware of the dangers of CO2 releases, how to recognize a release,
 and precautions to take.

 28

Natural CO2 releases also demonstrate that the impacts of CO2 depend not only on the volume
released but also on such weather conditions as wind, barometric pressure, and soil water
content. These variables should be evaluated during project-specific risk assessments (Viveiros
and Silva 2024). In addition, pre-project site characterization should evaluate the potential for
CO2 releases to facilitate the release of other gases, such as H2S and radon. Natural CO2 releases
also indicate that the odorless and colorless nature of CO2 increases the chances of toxic
exposures in homes and buildings. Consequently, CO2 detectors could reduce the risk of harmful
exposure.

4.1.2 Environmental Impacts

Natural CO2 releases are documented to have numerous environmental effects on wildlife and
their habitat. One primary impact is the absence of vegetation in areas with high levels of CO2
venting as well as the death of existing vegetation at sites where a sudden release of large
quantities of CO2 occurred from volcanos or existing vents and faults (USGS 2012, Viveiros and
Silva 2024). Such vegetation impacts have been documented in areas with naturally occurring
CO2 concentration between 30% and 50% (Viveiros and Silva 2024). For example, a large
magmatic CO2 gas release at Mammoth Mountain, California, resulted in a flux of CO2 from the
soil greater than 1,200 tons of CO2 per day between 1990 and 1995. During this period, large tree
kills were observed, affecting up to 170 acres of forest (Benson, et al. 2002).

In addition, in a number of cases the release of CO2 gases have caused wildlife mortality in
invertebrates, birds, reptiles, and small and large mammals throughout the world, including in
the Azores, Portugal, Italy, the United States, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and Hakkoda,
Japan. These incidents are reviewed in Lewicki et al. (2007) and Viveiros and Silva (2024).
However, observations of animal mortality were incidental and no systematic studies exist of
wildlife mortality and atmospheric concentration of natural CO2 releases.

Lessons learned. It is clear from natural CO2 releases that accidental CO2 releases from pipelines
and storage have the potential to harm or kill vegetation, wildlife, and aquatic organisms.
Unfortunately, there are few measurements of atmospheric CO2 concentrations during natural
CO2 release events, and ecosystem impacts from exposure to elevated concentrations of CO2 are
poorly documented (Benson et al. 2002; Vivieros and Silva 2024).

4.2 INDUSTRIAL CO2 CASE STUDIES

Nature itself offers compelling evidence that CO₂ can remain securely stored underground for
millions of years. Across the United States, numerous naturally occurring CO₂ reservoirs have
been identified, providing valuable insights into the geologic conditions that enable long-term
containment. The following case studies examine these natural reservoirs, as well as incidents
associated with CO₂ pipelines and storage operations, to identify lessons learned that can inform
the safe development of future carbon storage and transport projects.

 29

4.2.1 Natural CO2 Storage Reservoirs

Naturally occurring CO2 reservoirs in the United States include the Jackson Dome (Mississippi);
Sheep Mountain Dome, McElmo Dome, and Doe Canyon (all in Colorado); Bravo Dome (New
Mexico); Farnham Dome (Utah); Springerville (Arizona); and Big Piney-LaBarge (Wyoming)
(Allis, et al. 2001, Benson, et al. 2002). The CO2 from these domes is extracted and transported
by pipeline to oil fields like the Permian Basin (spanning west Texas and New Mexico), where it
is used for EOR. In addition, several of these CO2 reservoirs are considered potentially suitable
for CO2 storage. The domes are useful for understanding the geologic characteristic required for
successful CO2 storage as well as the long-term effects of underground CO2 storage (Allis, et al.
2001).

The belowground depth of naturally occurring CO2 reservoirs in the United States can vary
considerably from several hundred meters to thousands of meters. Most CO2 domes consist of
sandstone and dolomite reservoir formations overlain by mudstone and anhydrite rock that act to
seal the formation (Allis, et al. 2001). Most CO2 reservoirs show some degree of deep,
subsurface movement from the formation. For example, some reservoirs are stacked, suggesting
CO2 has migrated upward to reservoirs closer to the surface (Benson, et al. 2002). In addition,
evidence of natural CO2 movement to the surface in eastern Utah around the Farnham Dome
suggests that surface releases are more likely to be associated with CO2 reservoirs closer to the
ground surface (Allis, et al. 2001). Despite the large number of these domes and the evidence of
surface releases, no reports of hazards surface CO2 accumulations are associated with these CO2
reservoirs. However, virtually no studies of natural surface releases have been conducted, so
more studies in this region would be valuable for characterizing leakage volumes from known
CO2 fields.

While the extraction of CO2 from natural CO2 reservoirs is the opposite of CO2 injection, it still
provides information on the frequency and effects of CO2 releases from well blowouts.
Following is a summary of some global examples of CO2 incidents associated with CO2
extraction and other industrial applications.

 Sheep Mountain, Colorado. CO2 extraction from Sheep Mountain began in 1975. The CO2
 reservoir is located at 1,000 m to 1,800 m depth and the extracted CO2 is taken by pipeline to
 the Permian Basin (Allis et al. 2001). During its time in operation, the Sheep Mountain well
 has had only one blowout, which occurred in March 1982 and resulted in CO2 release from
 the well and ground fractures along the slope of Little Sheep Mountain. Using mud and brine
 injections, the well release was sealed by April (Lewicki, Birkholzer and Tsang 2007).

 Florina Basin, Greece. The only commercially exploited natural CO2 reservoir in Greece,
 the Florina CO2 field has been in production for several decades. Its only accidental CO2
 releases occurred in 1990, when a mineral water exploration well was drilled to 559 m depth.
 The CO2 release from the ground surface was detected near the exploration well. In addition,
 a ~25 m2 depression occurred around the well, causing the drill rig to collapse. Groundwater
 began discharging into the depression and created a pool. A swimmer died due to CO2
 asphyxiation when the pool was open for public swimming; the pool was subsequently
 closed. While groundwater continued to flow into the pool, it was dry by 2003 as the
 reservoir pressure declined (Lewicki, Birkholzer and Tsang 2007).

 30

 Torre Alfina, Italy. The Torre Alfina geothermal field consists of water overlaid by a CO2
 gas cap (Lewicki, Birkholzer and Tsang 2007). In 1973, a 663 m well was drilled through the
 cap rock, resulting in a well blowout that released ~25,000 t of CO2 into the atmosphere as
 well as numerous CO2 releases from the ground along fracture pathways in the caprock.
 Atmospheric CO2 concentrations of up to ~50% were detected within a 250-m area around
 the Alfina 1 well with the highest values found near the ground surface, in topographic
 depressions, and during periods of low wind speed. The well was completely cemented but
 attempts to use drilling to focus subsurface CO2 flow to less hazardous areas were only
 partially successful (Lewicki, Birkholzer and Tsang 2007). Despite the large amount of CO2
 released, no injuries or fatalities were reported.

Lessons learned. The existence of stable, naturally occurring CO2 reservoirs suggests that CO2
can be safely sequestered underground under the appropriate geologic conditions. However,
some level of belowground movement may occur in which CO2 migrates to a non-target
formation. The potential for such movement for any proposed CO2 storage site would have to be
evaluated during site evaluation at the project level. In addition, well blowouts are the most
common mechanisms for CO2 release associated with CO2 extraction and they could also occur
during CO2 injection for long-term storage. Therefore, proper well construction and operation are
critical to preventing accidental CO2 releases.

4.2.2 CO2 Pipeline and Storage Case Studies

Denbury Gulf Coast Pipelines, LLC pipeline Incident. Two recent CO2 release incidents have
been reported from pipelines operated by Denbury Gulf Coast Pipelines, LLC (Denbury). On
February 22, 2020, a landslide ruptured a 24-inch CO2 pipeline near Satartia, Mississippi,
releasing liquid CO2 that vaporized with exposure to the atmosphere (Bielka, et al. 2024). The
pipeline rupture was detected remotely by the Denbury operations control room and pipeline
shutdown procedures were initiated. In total, approximately 31,405 barrels of CO2 were released
between the time of rupture at 7:06 pm and complete closure of the pipeline at 10:25 pm.
Examples of human health effects documented after the incident include drivers passing out and
two residents living near the pipeline fainting while investigating the CO2 cloud. Forty-five
people were taken to the hospital following vapor cloud exposure, but no information on injuries
was reported. While no fatalities were reported, the rupture resulted in the closure of local
highways and the evacuation of 200 people. Evacuees were allowed to return by 8:00 am the
next day and air quality monitoring stopped at 11:24 am. The pipeline was shut down for 246
days until operations resumed on October 26, 2020 (USDOT 2022).

After community evacuations, outdoor CO₂ concentrations ranged from 0.01% to 2.6% and five
detections of CO2 exceeded 0.5%, which is OSHA’s eight-hour exposure toxicity threshold.
Outdoor CO2 levels fell to 0.5% or lower within hours after the rupture (USDOT 2022). The CO2
measurements in nearby buildings ranged from 0.02% to 2.8% CO2, with six detections
exceeding 0.5%. Within hours after opening doors and windows of these structures, CO 2
readings were below 0.35%. No H2S was detected during indoor and outdoor monitoring. The
accident investigation report indicated that existing weather conditions delayed dissipation of the
CO2 vapor cloud (USDOT 2022). Furthermore, Denbury did not notify local officials of the
rupture; therefore, first responders were unaware of the nature of the gas cloud.

 31

In April 2024, a release incident from a CO2 pipeline operated by Denbury Gulf Coast Pipelines,
LLC near Sulphur, Louisiana, was reported (Iowa Capital Dispatch 2024). According to the
initial incident report from the U.S. Coast Guard National Response Center, 2,548 barrels of CO2
were released (USCG n.d.). No injuries, property damage, or evacuations were reported.
Remedial action included shutting down the pump station and closing a main line valve. The
National Response Center incident data are preliminary and no final report has been issued by a
federal or state response agency.

Lessons learned. The Denbury pipeline incidents indicate that pipeline ruptures are capable of
causing large CO2 releases that result in evacuations, highway closures, and human health
effects. However, these incidents also show that companies have the ability to quickly detect a
rupture and shut down the pipeline to prevent further release. Furthermore, both outdoor and
indoor CO2 levels will rapidly return to baseline levels after the release is stopped. An accident
investigation report by the U.S. Department of Transportation (USDOT) made several
recommendations for CO2 pipelines (USDOT 2022). First, emergency response planning should
account for impacts on local populations, in this case the Village of Satartia and surrounding
areas. Second, the report concluded that pipeline operators need to better address climate and
geologic hazards (i.e., landslides) posed to pipelines. Finally, companies should better educate
local populations and officials about CO2 release scenarios and work with them to develop
effective emergency response plans. The Satartia incident also illustrates the importance of leak
detection systems and emergency shutdown valves for limiting the amount of CO2 released in
case of a pipeline fracture, especially in densely populated areas (Bielka, et al. 2024).

Illinois Basin-Decatur Project (IBDP). The IBDP was a commercial‐scale demonstration CO2
sequestration project in Decatur, Illinois. Between 2011 and 2014, over 1 million metric ton of
CO2 was injected in the Mt. Simon sandstone formation at a depth of about 2,100 m (Bukar, et
al. 2025). Baseline information on seismicity, air, soil, groundwater, and geologic conditions was
collected before and after injection. Among the findings were the following:

 • While 4,848 microseismic events were detected after injection, their average magnitude
 was only -0.9, and 89% of the events measured less than magnitude 0; 19 events were in
 the low 1s in magnitude (Bauer, et al. 2022). Seismic events of this magnitude would not
 be perceptible on the ground.

 • Monitoring at the IBDP site indicated no migration of the injected CO 2 outside of the
 storage formation (Iranmanesh, et al. 2024). Rather the CO2 plume migrated along major
 faults in the formation and entered upper units of the reservoir where it was trapped
       (Bukar, et al. 2025).

 • Soil monitoring found high natural variability in soil gases and there was no indication
 that CO2 had increased in soil due to CO2 injection (Shao, et al. 2019).

ADM Carbon Storage Facility Incident. ADM operates a carbon storage facility in Decatur,
Illinois, where CO2 produced during ethanol production is captured and injected into a storage
formation. ADM was the first American company to be granted a UIC Class VI well permit by
the EPA and has been successfully operating CO2 injection wells in Decatur for more than a

 32

decade. The facility is the first CO2 storage operation in the United States and has sequestered
approximately 4.5 million metric tons of CO2 (ADM n.d.).

ADM’s UIC Class VI Permit authorizes the injection of CO2 into the Mt. Simon formation at
depths between 1,692 m and 2,147 m below ground surface. In 2024, ADM detected potential
movement of CO2 and brine out of the storage formation at a depth of 1,524 m. It was found that
corrosion in the tubing of a monitoring well allowed the movement of fluid from Mt. Simon into
the Ironton–Galesville formation, which is not authorized by the permit (EPA 2025, Douglas
2024). ADM also noted that the fluid quality in the Ironton–Galesville formation in the vicinity
of the monitoring well changed significantly. Consequently, on August 14, 2024, EPA issued a
Notice of Violation and Opportunity to Confer to ADM. On September 19, 2024, the EPA issued
a proposed enforcement order to ADM for alleged violations of the Safe Drinking Water Act
because the migration of injected fluid into an unauthorized formation violated the UIC Class VI
injection permit (EPA 2024). The EPA also stated there was no risk to surface or USDWs or to
public health at the time of the incident, and that injection was paused to allow ADM to conduct
additional tests. No official incident investigation report has been finalized for this incident and
its causes as of this writing.

No public health impacts have been reported due to the ADM incident and there is no indication
that drinking water could be contaminated because the CO2 movement occurred almost a mile
below existing USDWs (EPA 2024). Under the EPA enforcement order, ADM will be required
to identify the extent of fluid migration and implement remedial actions to address the alleged
violations. The latest information indicates that ADM has plugged the monitoring well to stop
additional CO2 movement.

Lessons learned. Along with several other global CO2 storage projects, the IBDP project
suggests that CO2 can be safely stored within deep geologic storage formations. Data collected
from the IBDP indicates the importance of the comprehensive pre-injection characterization of
the proposed CO2 storage formation and surrounding geology, especially the identification of
faults within potential reservoirs, redundant reservoir seals, and ability of the formation to
withstand pressurized injection. It is critical that project studies should evaluate the existing
caprock seal, pre-existing faults, and fractures as well as seismic activity as unsealed fractures
can act as conduits for the vertical movement of CO2 (Lewicki, Birkholzer and Tsang 2007,
Bukar, et al. 2025).

As demonstrated by the ADM CO2 storage project and other CO2 projects throughout the world,
well failure is the most likely route of CO2 release associated with injection and storage. Such
releases can occur belowground or from the surface. As demonstrated by the ADM project, well
design, construction, and monitoring are of critical importance where robust permitting and
monitoring requirements allowed the early detection and reporting of the leak, followed by
mitigative measures undertaken by ADM.
