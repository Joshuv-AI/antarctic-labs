Error estimates for tamed Euler and Randomized Euler schemes for SDEs with locally Lipschitz drift with applications to non-logconcave sampling and optimization
Iosif Lytras †, Angelos Ntousis ∗

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the numerical discretization of stochastic differential equations with locally Lipschitz, super-linearly growing drift, and the resulting implications for sampling from non-log-concave distributions satisfying a logarithmic Sobolev inequality. In this regime, the classical Euler–Maruyama scheme underlying the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA) is known to be unstable. We analyze the KL-accelerated tamed unadjusted Langevin algorithm (kTULA) and introduce a new tamed randomized midpoint scheme, termed tRLMC. Building on the shifted-composition approach of [ 5 ] , we develop two new local-error frameworks that yield finite-time, non-asymptotic error estimates against the underlying SDE—in KL divergence for kTULA, and in total variation for tRLMC —valid for general locally Lipschitz drift. Specializing these frameworks to the sampling problem under a logarithmic Sobolev inequality, we obtain a near-optimal O ~ ( ε − 1 / 2 ) \widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1/2}) iteration complexity for kTULA in KL divergence, with corresponding guarantees in total variation and Wasserstein distance. We further establish, for the first time, a non-asymptotic guarantee in total variation for a tamed randomized Langevin scheme under super-linear drift growth, together with the corresponding Wasserstein-distance bound, both with O ~ ​ ( ε − 1 ) \widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}) complexity for tRLMC. As a consequence, both schemes yield non-asymptotic bounds for a non-convex excess-risk optimization problem.



1.
Introduction

The numerical discretization of stochastic differential equations (SDEs) with super-linearly growing drift has attracted considerable attention in the numerical analysis of stochastic dynamics. Beyond its intrinsic interest, this analysis underpins a wide range of sampling algorithms used in computational statistics, Bayesian inference, and large-scale optimization. The general SDE of interest in this work takes the form

(SDE)

dX_{t}=-h(X_{t})\,dt+\sigma\,dB_{t},

where
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a locally Lipschitz drift that may grow super-linearly at infinity,
\sigma>0
is a constant volatility, and
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a canonical Wiener process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with unit covariance. A canonical application consists of sampling from a target distribution
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, typically expressed in Gibbs form as
\pi(x)\propto e^{-\beta u(x)}
for some potential function
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. Under suitable assumptions on
u
, this distribution arises as the invariant measure of the Langevin SDE

(LSDE)

dX_{t}=-\nabla u(X_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\beta}}\,dB_{t},

which is precisely (
SDE
) specialized to
h:=\nabla u
and
\sigma:=\sqrt{2/\beta}
.
Motivated by this fundamental property of (
LSDE
), one of the most widely used
algorithmic schemes for sampling from
\pi
is the

unadjusted Langevin algorithm
(ULA), given recursively by

(ULA)

\theta^{\mathrm{ULA}}_{0}:=\theta_{0},\qquad\theta^{\mathrm{ULA}}_{n+1}=\theta^{\mathrm{ULA}}_{n}-\lambda\,h\!\left(\theta^{\mathrm{ULA}}_{n}\right)+\sqrt{\tfrac{2\lambda}{\beta}}\,\xi_{n+1},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where
\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
n=1,2,\ldots
, denotes the state variable of the algorithm,

(\xi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sequence of
standard
d
-dimensional random vectors with unit covariance,

\lambda>0
is a step-size parameter, and
h:=\nabla u
denotes the gradient of the
potential
u
.
The guiding idea behind (
ULA
) is that it may be viewed as an Euler–Maruyama
discretization of (
LSDE
), so that, for sufficiently large
n
and sufficiently small

\lambda
,
\theta^{\mathrm{ULA}}_{n}
is distributed approximately according to the invariant measure of (
LSDE
), which is precisely the target
distribution
\pi
.

This connection has motivated a substantial literature devoted to establishing
non-asymptotic convergence guarantees for (
ULA
) in various probability metrics,
most notably Wasserstein distances, total variation, and information-theoretic divergences
such as the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence.
Much of this literature focuses on the case where
\pi
is log-concave and the gradient

\nabla u
is globally Lipschitz, corresponding respectively to convexity and smoothness
of the potential
u
; see, e.g.,

[
8
,
9
,
10
]
and references therein.

Beyond the globally Lipschitz log-concave setting, a substantial body of work has sought to relax convexity and smoothness assumptions on
u
, notably through dissipativity, convexity-at-infinity, and related structural conditions; see, for instance,

[
7
,
24
,
6
,
27
]
.
A complementary line of research, initiated by the insights of Vempala and Wibisono
[
31
]
, establishes convergence under isoperimetric inequalities, such as Poincaré and logarithmic Sobolev inequalities; see

[
11
,
12
,
6
,
25
]
.

This work examines the numerical discretization of (
SDE
) in a regime that lies at the intersection of two well-studied directions: relaxing global Lipschitz assumptions on the drift, and—in the sampling specialization—weakening convexity via isoperimetric inequalities. While each of these settings has been analyzed extensively in isolation, rigorous non-asymptotic guarantees in their combination—particularly in the presence of super-linear drift growth—remain limited in the literature. Crucially, the numerical-analytic obstacles arising from super-linear drift are not specific to the gradient case
h=\nabla u
: explosion of moments, divergence of Euler–Maruyama in mean-square sense, and breakdown of standard local-error frameworks all occur at the level of (
SDE
), prompting a treatment that is general at the level of the SDE and only later specialized to sampling. This motivates the following two-fold question:

How does one derive finite-time error estimates for numerical discretizations of SDEs with super-linearly growing locally Lipschitz drift, and what guarantees can such estimates yield for sampling and optimization in the absence of log-concavity and global smoothness?

This scenario presents substantial challenges. As noted in several prior studies, both (
ULA
)
and its stochastic variants, such as SGLD, may become unstable in such regimes. In particular, when
h

grows super-linearly, the associated Euler–Maruyama scheme—which forms the backbone of
(
ULA
)—can fail dramatically. A pivotal result in
[
12
]
showed that, in such cases,
the numerical approximation may diverge from the true SDE solution in mean-square sense, even over
finite time horizons. This phenomenon is directly linked to the explosion of moments in the discretized
process, highlighting why standard schemes may break down when applied to approximate (
SDE
)
with super-linearly growing drift
h
.

Addressing this issue requires a fundamentally different approach—one that revisits the way in which
(
ULA
) is designed as a numerical discretization of (
LSDE
), and leverages insights from the
theory of numerical methods for SDEs. In this vein, a promising class of techniques—known as tamed Euler
schemes—emerged in
[
13
,
28
,
29
]
. These methods modify the drift
h
in order to ensure
the stability of (
ULA
) even under super-linear growth. Specifically, they replace the original
coefficient
h
with a modified version
h_{\lambda}
, depending on the stepsize
\lambda
, which is constructed
to satisfy two essential properties:

(P1)

The tamed coefficient
h_{\lambda}
grows at most linearly, i.e.,

h_{\lambda}(\theta)=O(\|\theta\|)
as
\|\theta\|\to\infty
.

(P2)

h_{\lambda}
converges pointwise to the original coefficient
h
as
\lambda\to 0
.

These properties ensure both the stability of the numerical scheme and consistency with the original
dynamics as the stepsize vanishes.

Tamed Euler algorithms.

In the context of Langevin-based sampling, taming techniques have been applied under strong
dissipativity or convexity assumptions
[
3
,
15
,
21
]
, in
stochastic-gradient settings
[
19
,
17
]
, under a “convexity at infinity”
assumption
[
26
]
, and more recently under functional inequalities

[
22
,
20
,
23
]
. In parallel, truncated or
projected schemes have been examined under logarithmic Sobolev assumptions

[
34
]
.

In this work, we build on the KL-accelerated tamed unadjusted Langevin algorithm (kTULA) introduced in
[
23
]
, which can be viewed as a modification of TULA designed to ensure stability under super-linear drift growth. The key idea underlying our analysis is that taming induces state-dependent perturbations in the dynamics, which fall outside the scope of existing KL frameworks. By explicitly controlling these perturbations, we establish non-asymptotic finite-time error estimates for
kTULA
in KL divergence under polynomial smoothness and dissipativity of the drift; when specialized to the sampling setting
h=\nabla u
under a logarithmic Sobolev inequality, this yields improved convergence rates toward
\pi_{\beta}
even in the presence of super-linear gradient growth.

Tamed randomized Euler algorithms.

At the same time, an alternative line of work has focused on randomized discretization schemes for Langevin dynamics, where randomness is introduced directly at the level of the numerical integrator, for instance through randomized time steps or intermediate evaluations of the drift. Such methods have been shown to improve stability properties, reduce discretization bias, and, in certain regimes, achieve improved convergence rates compared to their deterministic counterparts.

A representative example, in the Langevin case
h=\nabla u
, is the randomized midpoint Langevin algorithm, given by

\displaystyle Y_{n+1}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=Y_{n}-\tau_{n+1}\nabla u(Y_{n})\,\lambda+\sqrt{2}\,\Delta W_{n+1}^{\tau},\qquad Y_{0}=x_{0},

\displaystyle Y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=Y_{n}-\nabla u(Y_{n+1}^{\tau})\,\lambda+\sqrt{2}\,\Delta W_{n+1},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where
(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are i.i.d. uniform random variables on
[0,1]
.

Originally introduced as numerical methods for SDEs with irregular coefficients, such randomized schemes were later adapted to sampling settings in
[
30
]
and further developed in

[
4
,
36
,
5
,
16
]
.
In the context of sampling, randomized discretizations are particularly appealing, as they can better capture the underlying stochastic dynamics while mitigating the accumulation of discretization error.

However, existing analyses of these methods rely crucially on global Lipschitz assumptions on the gradient. In particular, it remains unclear whether the stability and bias-reduction properties of randomized schemes persist when the drift exhibits super-linear growth, as is typical in non-log-concave and high-dimensional learning problems.

Motivated by the developments in both the randomized Euler and tamed Euler literature, it is natural to investigate whether the stabilizing effect of taming can be combined with the robustness properties of randomized discretizations. This raises the question of whether one can construct discretization schemes that remain stable under super-linear drift growth while retaining the favorable bias properties of randomized methods.

In this work, we address this question by introducing and analyzing a tamed randomized Langevin Monte Carlo algorithm (tRLMC). We establish non-asymptotic finite-time error estimates for this method in total variation against the underlying SDE, and, in the sampling specialization, derive non-asymptotic convergence guarantees in non-log-concave regimes, including explicit bounds in Wasserstein distance and total variation, with further implications for optimization.

Our contributions.
Our results are organized in two layers: finite-time error estimates against the underlying (
SDE
), valid for general locally Lipschitz drift, and their specialization to the sampling problem under
h=\nabla u
and a logarithmic Sobolev inequality. The two finite-time frameworks address complementary settings: the KL framework applies to the deterministic tamed Euler scheme, where a sharp cross-regularity estimate is available, whereas the TV framework applies to the randomized scheme, for which such a cross-regularity estimate is currently out of reach due to the randomization of the integration time.

(i)

Finite-time KL error estimates for SDEs with locally Lipschitz drift.

Building on
[
5
]
, we develop a new local-error framework for controlling the KL divergence between a discretization scheme and the (
SDE
) when the drift grows super-linearly. Applied to (
kTULA
), the framework yields a finite-time estimate of the form

\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\mu\widehat{P}^{N}\,\big\|\,\nu P^{N}\bigr)\;\lesssim\;\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\,\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\;+\;\bigl((N\lambda)\vee\log N\bigr)\,\lambda^{2},

valid for general locally Lipschitz drift; see Theorem
1
.

(ii)

Finite-time TV error estimates for randomized schemes under super-linear drift.

By combining the taming methodology with a randomized midpoint method, we introduce a new discretization, termed
tRLMC
, and develop a corresponding total variation local-error framework. This yields the first finite-time error estimate in total variation for a randomized scheme applied to SDEs with super-linearly growing locally Lipschitz drift; see Theorem
2
.

(iii)

Near-optimal sampling guarantees for tamed Euler schemes in
\mathrm{KL}
divergence.

Specializing the KL framework to the Langevin case
h=\nabla u
under a logarithmic Sobolev inequality, we derive an explicit
\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}})
complexity bound for
kTULA
to reach accuracy
\epsilon
in KL divergence toward
\pi_{\beta}
. This further yields improved guarantees in Wasserstein and total variation distances.

(iv)

Sampling guarantees for tamed randomized schemes.

Specializing the TV framework to the Langevin case, we show that
tRLMC
achieves
\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
complexity for sampling toward
\pi_{\beta}
in
W_{2}
and total variation distance under a logarithmic Sobolev inequality.

(v)

Connection with optimization.

We apply our Wasserstein convergence results to obtain explicit non-asymptotic excess-risk bounds for tamed Langevin schemes in the non-log-concave setting. More precisely, we decompose the excess risk as:

\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[u(\theta_{n})\bigr]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\beta}}\!\bigl[u(x)\bigr]}_{\text{sampling error}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\beta}}\!\bigl[u(x)\bigr]-u^{*}}_{\text{concentration for large }\beta},

where
\theta_{n}
denotes the
n
-th iterate of the algorithm. Thus, a solution to the sampling problem yields corresponding optimization guarantees for sufficiently large
\beta
.

2.
Preliminaries and Blanket Assumptions

In this section, we provide the necessary groundwork for stating the proposed algorithmic
schemes and our main results. We adopt throughout a two-layer convention: structural assumptions and notation are formulated at the level of the general (
SDE
) with locally Lipschitz drift
h
, while functional inequalities and the target measure
\pi_{\beta}
are introduced only in the sampling specialization
h=\nabla u
.

2.1.
Notation

Throughout,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
its associated inner product. For a sufficiently smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we write
\nabla f
,
\nabla^{2}f
and
\Delta f

for its gradient, Hessian matrix, and Laplacian respectively. For a continuously differentiable map
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
J(F)
for its Jacobian. In the gradient case
F=\nabla u
, one has
J(\nabla u)=\nabla^{2}u
.
For a measurable map
T
, we write
\Law(T)
for its induced distribution.
For a probability measure
\mu
and Markov kernel
P
, the pushforward measure
\mu P
is

(\mu P)(A):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P(x,A)\,\mu(dx),\qquad\mu P^{n}:=\mu\underbrace{P\cdots P}_{n\ \text{times}}.

SDE semigroup and one-step kernel.

Let
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the Markov semigroup of (
SDE
), i.e.,

P_{t}(x,\cdot)=\Law(X_{t}^{x})
where
X_{0}^{x}=x
.
Fix a step size
\lambda>0
and define the one-step diffusion kernel

P:=P_{\lambda}.

In the sampling specialization
h=\nabla u
,
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
coincides with the Langevin semigroup of (
LSDE
).

2-Wasserstein distance.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the space of probability measures with finite second moment.
For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the
2
-Wasserstein distance is defined by

(1)

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu):=\left(\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|^{2}\,\gamma(dx,dy)\right)^{1/2},

where
\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
, that is, probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
. Equivalently,
(X,Y)
is a coupling of
\mu
and
\nu
if
\Law(X)=\mu
and
\Law(Y)=\nu
.

Kullback–Leibler divergence.

Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the Kullback–Leibler divergence is defined by

(2)

\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\!\left(\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}\right)\,d\mu,

whenever
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
, and
\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu):=+\infty
otherwise.

We rely on the standard chain rule and data processing inequality for relative entropy, which yield the marginal bound
\mathrm{KL}(\mu^{Y}\|\nu^{Y})\leq\mathrm{KL}(\mu^{X,Y}\|\nu^{X,Y})
. A key refinement for our purposes is the
shifted chain rule

[
5
]
. By introducing an auxiliary variable
X^{\prime}
, it provides the flexible bound

(3)

\mathrm{KL}(\mu^{Y}\|\nu^{Y})\leq\mathrm{KL}(\mu^{X^{\prime}}\|\nu^{X})+\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\mu^{X},\mu^{X^{\prime}})}\int\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\mu^{Y|X=x}\,\big\|\,\nu^{Y|X=x^{\prime}}\bigr)\,\gamma(dx,dx^{\prime}).

This freedom to couple
X
and
X^{\prime}
is central to the shifted comparison arguments developed in Section
5
.

Rényi divergence.

The Rényi divergence of order
q>1
between two probability measures
\mu,\nu
is defined by

(4)

\mathsf{R}_{q}(\mu\|\nu):=\frac{1}{q-1}\log\int\left(\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}\right)^{q}\,d\nu,

whenever
\mu\ll\nu
, and
+\infty
otherwise. In the limit as
q\to 1
, this recovers the Kullback–Leibler divergence, while the case
q=2
yields
\mathsf{R}_{2}=\log(1+\chi^{2})
, where
\chi^{2}
is the chi-squared divergence.

Infinitesimal generator.

We denote by
\mathcal{L}
the infinitesimal generator of (
SDE
),

\mathcal{L}f\;:=\;\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\,\Delta f\;-\;\langle h,\nabla f\rangle,

which, in the sampling specialization
h=\nabla u
and
\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}
, reduces to the Langevin generator
\mathcal{L}f=\tfrac{1}{\beta}\Delta f-\langle\nabla u,\nabla f\rangle
.

2.2.
Assumptions on the drift

We impose the following structural conditions on the drift
h
of (
SDE
).

(A1)

Polynomial Jacobian growth.

There exist constants
L>0
and
\ell\geq 0
such that

\max\bigl\{\|h(x)\|,\ \|J(h)(x)\|\bigr\}\leq L\bigl(1+\|x\|^{2\ell}\bigr),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(A2)

Polynomial Lipschitz continuity.

There exist constants
L^{\prime}>0
and
\ell^{\prime}\geq 0
such that

\|h(x)-h(y)\|\leq L^{\prime}\bigl(1+\|x\|+\|y\|\bigr)^{\ell^{\prime}}\|x-y\|,\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(A3)

One-sided Lipschitz continuity.

There exists a constant
K^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\langle h(x)-h(y),\,x-y\rangle\geq-K^{\prime}\|x-y\|^{2},\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(A4)

Dissipativity.

There exist constants
a,b>0
such that

\langle h(x),x\rangle\geq a\|x\|^{2}-b,\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(A5)

Polynomial Jacobian Lipschitz continuity.

There exist constants
L^{\prime\prime}>0
and
\ell^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
such that

\|J(h)(x)-J(h)(y)\|\leq L^{\prime\prime}(1+\|x\|+\|y\|)^{\ell^{\prime\prime}}\|x-y\|,\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Assumption
(A1)
allows the drift and its Jacobian to grow polynomially at infinity, thereby encompassing a wide class of drift coefficients with super-linear growth that fall outside the scope of the standard global Lipschitz framework. In the sampling specialization
h=\nabla u
, it covers non-convex potentials for which classical convergence analyses of (
ULA
) are not directly applicable.

Assumption
(A2)
ensures that the drift is locally Lipschitz continuous,
with a Lipschitz modulus that may itself grow polynomially.
This condition is substantially weaker than global Lipschitz continuity
and is compatible with super-linear drifts,
while still being sufficient to establish well-posedness of (
SDE
)
and stability of the numerical schemes considered in this work.

Assumption
(A3)
is a one-sided Lipschitz condition, which plays a key role in controlling
the propagation of errors between coupled trajectories of (
SDE
).
It is strictly weaker than monotonicity or strong convexity,
and is particularly well suited to the analysis of non-convex dynamics.

Assumption
(A4)
is a dissipativity condition that ensures coercive behavior of the drift
at infinity.
This assumption is central to our analysis, as it allows us to derive uniform-in-time
moment bounds for both (
SDE
) and its discretizations, and is a natural coercivity-type condition in applications.

Assumption
(A5)
is imposed only in the analysis of the (
kTULA
)
scheme. It provides the additional Jacobian regularity—namely, local Lipschitz continuity of
J(h)
with polynomial modulus—required to obtain the improved weak error rate underlying our KL convergence bounds. In the gradient case
h=\nabla u
, since
J(h)=\nabla^{2}u
, this reduces to a polynomial-Lipschitz Hessian condition on the potential. The assumption is not needed for the randomized scheme (
tRLMC
), whose
bias-reduction mechanism does not rely on second-order smoothness of the drift.

2.3.
Sampling and functional inequalities

We now turn to the specialization of (
SDE
) that underlies the sampling application. Throughout this subsection and whenever sampling guarantees are stated, we assume that
h=\nabla u
for some
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta u(x)}\,dx<\infty
for any
\beta>0
, and we set
\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}
. Under this specialization, (
SDE
) reduces to (
LSDE
), which admits as invariant measure the Gibbs distribution

\pi_{\beta}(A)\;:=\;\frac{\int_{A}e^{-\beta u(x)}\,dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta u(x)}\,dx},\qquad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

In addition to the structural assumptions
(A1)
–
(A5)
on
h=\nabla u
, we impose a functional inequality on
\pi_{\beta}
.

(A6)

Logarithmic Sobolev inequality (LSI).

The target distribution
\pi_{\beta}
satisfies a logarithmic Sobolev inequality with constant
C_{\mathrm{LSI}}>0
,
meaning that for every probability measure
\nu\ll\pi_{\beta}
with density
f:=\frac{d\nu}{d\pi_{\beta}}
,

(LSI)

\mathrm{KL}(\nu\|\pi_{\beta})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\log f\,d\pi_{\beta}\leq\frac{1}{2C_{\mathrm{LSI}}}\,I_{\pi_{\beta}}(\nu),

where
I_{\pi_{\beta}}(\nu)
denotes the
relative Fisher information
of
\nu
with respect to
\pi_{\beta}
.

Assumption
(A6)
is widely used in the analysis of Langevin dynamics since it implies exponential
ergodicity in relative entropy. In particular, by the Bakry–Émery theorem
[
1
]
, (
LSI
) holds for
strongly convex potentials and is stable under bounded perturbations, Lipschitz mappings, and
convolutions.

Finally, (
LSI
) implies Talagrand’s transportation-cost inequality: for every
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(TI)

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\pi_{\beta})\;\leq\;\sqrt{\frac{2}{C_{\mathrm{LSI}}}\,\mathrm{KL}(\nu\|\pi_{\beta})}.

3.
Tamed schemes and main results

This section introduces the two tamed discretization schemes analyzed in this work—(
kTULA
) and (
tRLMC
)—and presents the corresponding main results. The results in this section are organized into two distinct layers. First, we establish finite-time error estimates against the underlying (
SDE

(A5)

3.1.
Tamed discretizations

Throughout this subsection, we fix constants
a>0
and
\ell\geq 0
, and define, for each
\lambda>0
, the tamed drift
h_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
associated with
h
by

(5)

h_{\lambda}(x):=ax+\frac{h(x)-ax}{\bigl(1+\lambda\|x\|^{2(\ell+1)}\bigr)^{1/2}}.

The construction (
5
) is well defined for any locally Lipschitz drift
h
and does not require
h

The kTULA scheme.

The KL-accelerated tamed unadjusted Langevin algorithm is given by

(kTULA)

\widehat{X}_{0}^{\lambda}:=X_{0},\qquad\widehat{X}_{n+1}^{\lambda}=\widehat{X}_{n}^{\lambda}-\lambda\,h_{\lambda}(\widehat{X}_{n}^{\lambda})+\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda}{\beta}}\,\xi_{n+1},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where
X_{0}
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random variable,
\lambda>0
is the stepsize,
\beta>0
is the inverse temperature, and

(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are i.i.d. standard
d
-dimensional Gaussian random vectors. We denote by
\pi_{n}^{\lambda}
the density of
\Law(\widehat{X}_{n}^{\lambda})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Remark 1
.

The design of the tamed coefficient
h_{\lambda}
in (
5
) follows from that of
mTULA
[
26

3.3.
Comparison with related literature

We compare our results with the existing literature on Langevin-based sampling under non-convexity and local Lipschitz continuity of the gradient. We emphasize that Theorems
1
and
2
are valid for general locally Lipschitz drift on (
SDE
) and are, to the best of our knowledge, the first finite-time error estimates of their kind under super-linear drift growth.

Comparison in Wasserstein distance.

Several recent works on taming and projection schemes provide convergence rates of
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
in Wasserstein distance under super-linear drift growth
[
34
,
2
]
. Our analysis of (
kTULA
) matches this rate under the same or weaker assumptions, and extends the analysis to KL divergence and total variation, both of which are stronger metrics.

Comparison in KL divergence.

For tamed schemes in KL divergence, the closest comparison is with
[
22
,
20
,
23
]
, of which
[
23
]
provides the strongest known guarantees prior to the present work. The current article provides the best known complexity for convergence to the invariant measure under these assumptions. More specifically, ignoring logarithmic factors in
\varepsilon
, our algorithm converges in KL divergence after

n\;\geq\;\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\bigl(d^{3(\ell+1)/2}\,\varepsilon^{-1/2}\bigr).

On the other hand,
[
23
]
achieves a general rate of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\bigl(d^{\frac{\ell+1}{\epsilon_{h}}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2-\epsilon_{h}}}\bigr)
for
\epsilon_{h}\in\bigl(0,\frac{1}{2}\bigr]
, which explodes in the dimension as
\epsilon_{h}\to 0
. Specializing their framework to
\epsilon_{h}=\frac{1}{2}
corresponding to our setting, that work achieves a complexity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\bigl(d^{2(\ell+1)}\,\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{3}}\bigr)
for KL accuracy
\varepsilon
.

Comparison for randomized schemes.

To the best of our knowledge, the only existing analysis of randomized Langevin schemes under super-linear drift growth is
[
33
]
, which establishes Wasserstein convergence. Our analysis of (
tRLMC
) matches the Wasserstein rate of
[
33
]
and additionally provides, for the first time in this setting, a non-asymptotic convergence guarantee in total variation, obtained through a new total variation local-error framework.

[-4,4]

5.
Proof outline and technical innovations

The convergence analyses underlying our main results share a common
technical core, which we develop in detail in this section. The goal is
to bound the divergence between two stochastic processes driven by

different
Markov kernels: the discrete scheme
kTULA
or

tRLMC
on the one hand, and the (
SDE
) on the other. Classical coupling arguments are
tailored to the Wasserstein metric and do not transfer verbatim to
information-theoretic divergences such as Kullback–Leibler or
total variation. The shifted composition methodology of
[
5
]
provides a principled remedy by
introducing an auxiliary, third process that interpolates between the
two processes of interest. We adapt this methodology to the present
setting, where the super-linear growth of the drift induces a
state-dependent coupling increment that does not appear
in
[
5
]
. This is the only point at which the abstract
machinery genuinely departs from
[
5
]
.

Following the two-layer structure of the paper, the local-error frameworks developed in this section—both the KL framework of §
5.1
–§
5.5
and the TV framework of §
5.6
—are formulated at the level of the (
SDE
) with general locally Lipschitz drift
h
, requiring no gradient structure. The Wasserstein convergence argument of §
5.7
additionally invokes the sampling specialization
h=\nabla u
and Assumption
(A6)
, as it relies on the exponential contraction of
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
toward
\pi_{\beta}
.

5.1.
Setup, kernels, and one-step local errors

Fix a step size
\lambda\in(0,1]
. Let
P=P_{\lambda}
denote the Markov
kernel of (
SDE
) run for time
\lambda
,
and let
\widehat{P}
denote the one-step transition kernel of
(
kTULA
). For any
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, set
\widehat{\mu}_{n}:=\mu\,\widehat{P}^{\,n}
and

\nu_{n}:=\nu\,P^{n}
.
The objective of the framework is to control

\mathrm{KL}(\widehat{\mu}_{N}\,\|\,\nu_{N})
in terms of
one-step
, i.e. local,
quantities computed at a single iteration.

We work with three measurable functions encoding the one-step
discrepancy between
\widehat{P}
and
P
:

E_{\mathrm{strong}},E_{\mathrm{weak}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty),\qquad\Gamma\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty).

Here
E_{\mathrm{strong}}(x)
controls the
L^{2}
-distance between
one step of
\widehat{P}
and one step of
P
from the same point
x
;

E_{\mathrm{weak}}(x)
is the corresponding bias, which exploits
cancellations and is therefore typically smaller than

E_{\mathrm{strong}}(x)
; and
\Gamma(x,y,\lambda)
is a

coupling increment
controlling the synchronous-coupling
distortion between two trajectories of (
SDE
) started at
x
and
y
.
For convenience we set

(15)

a_{0}(x):=E_{\mathrm{strong}}(x),\qquad a_{1}(x,y):=E_{\mathrm{weak}}(x)+\Gamma(x,y,\lambda)\,E_{\mathrm{strong}}(x).

The framework is fed by the following one-step Wasserstein bound,
verified for
kTULA
in Lemma
9
: there
exists
L>0
such that, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(16)

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\!\bigl(\delta_{x}\widehat{P},\delta_{y}P\bigr)\;\leq\;L^{2}\,\|x-y\|^{2}+2\,a_{1}(x,y)\,\|x-y\|+a_{0}(x)^{2}.

Remark 3

(State-dependence of
a_{1}
)
.

In
[
5
]
, the coupling increment
\gamma
is a constant. In the present
super-linear setting, the polynomial Lipschitz
condition
(A2)
forces
\Gamma
to depend on both endpoints,
namely
\Gamma(x,y,\lambda)=C_{\mathrm{cpl}}\lambda(1+\|x\|^{\ell^{\prime}}+\|y\|^{\ell^{\prime}})
.
This is the only structural difference between our framework
and
[
5
]
; as we shall see, it is benign provided
uniform moments along the auxiliary process introduced
in §
5.2
are established.

5.2.
The auxiliary interpolating process

The principal obstacle in bounding
\mathrm{KL}(\widehat{\mu}_{N}\,\|\,\nu_{N})

directly is that
\widehat{\mu}_{N}
and
\nu_{N}
are produced by

different
Markov kernels: any one-step KL bound therefore
encodes both the discretization bias and the contraction of the
SDE semigroup, and the two effects are difficult to separate.
The shifted composition strategy circumvents this obstacle by
introducing a third process
(Y_{n}^{\prime})_{n=0}^{N}
that
(i) starts at
\nu_{0}
, (ii) terminates at
\widehat{\mu}_{N}
, and
(iii) evolves at intermediate steps via the SDE kernel
P
, so
that intermediate one-step KL terms only involve
P
and are amenable
to standard regularity estimates of the semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

Construction.

Let
(\widehat{X}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
denote
the
kTULA
chain and the chain of one-step SDE updates with
\widehat{X}_{0}\sim\mu
and

Y_{0}\sim\nu
. Choose shift coefficients

(\eta_{n})_{n=0}^{N-1}\subset[0,1]
with
\eta_{N-1}=1
, and define

(17)

\widetilde{Y}_{n}:=(1-\eta_{n})\,Y_{n}^{\prime}+\eta_{n}\,\widehat{X}_{n},\qquad n=0,1,\dots,N-1.

Geometrically,
\widetilde{Y}_{n}
lies on the segment between
Y_{n}^{\prime}

and
\widehat{X}_{n}
; the parameter
\eta_{n}
measures how far the
auxiliary process is pushed toward the
kTULA
chain at step

n
. Define

(18)

Q_{n}:=\begin{cases}P,&0\leq n\leq N-2,\\[2.0pt]
\widehat{P},&n=N-1,\end{cases}

and update the auxiliary process by

(19)

Y_{n+1}^{\prime}\sim Q_{n}\bigl(\widetilde{Y}_{n},\cdot\bigr),\qquad n=0,1,\dots,N-1,\qquad Y_{0}^{\prime}=Y_{0}.

We write
\nu_{n}^{\prime}:=\Law(Y_{n}^{\prime})
.

The terminal condition
\eta_{N-1}=1
together with the choice

Q_{N-1}=\widehat{P}
guarantees the interpolation property

(20)

\nu_{0}^{\prime}\;=\;\nu_{0},\qquad\nu_{N}^{\prime}\;=\;\widehat{\mu}_{N},

since
\widetilde{Y}_{N-1}=\widehat{X}_{N-1}
and the last update applies

\widehat{P}
to
\widehat{X}_{N-1}
. Figure
5

illustrates the construction. The freedom to choose
\eta_{n}\in[0,1]

is what enables the framework to exploit
both
the contraction
of
P
and the local discretization errors of
\widehat{P}
: the
bounds, determined in Proposition
2
,
dictate the final iteration complexity.

\eta_{N-1}=1

5.3.
KL telescoping via the shifted chain rule

The bridge between the auxiliary process and the KL divergence is
the shifted chain rule (
3
) introduced in
Section
2
. Recall that the auxiliary variable

X^{\prime}
allows comparison of Markov updates from
different

starting points, which is precisely what is needed when the
underlying kernels disagree.

We assume the following two one-step KL bounds:

(21)

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\delta_{x}P\,\|\,\delta_{y}P\bigr)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;c\,\|x-y\|^{2},

(22)

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\delta_{x}\widehat{P}\,\|\,\delta_{y}P\bigr)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;c^{\prime}\,\|x-y\|^{2}+b(x)^{2},

for constants
c,c^{\prime}\geq 0
and a measurable function

b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
. We refer to (
21
) as the

regularity
of the SDE kernel and
to (
22
) as the
cross-regularity
of
the scheme. For
kTULA
, both are established in
Propositions
5
–
8
.

Lemma 1

(KL telescoping bound)
.

Let
(Y_{n}^{\prime})_{n=0}^{N}
and
(\widetilde{Y}_{n})_{n=0}^{N-1}
be defined
by (
17
)–(
19
). Then

(23)

\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\widehat{\mu}_{N}\,\|\,\nu_{N}\bigr)\;\leq\;\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(Q_{n}(\widetilde{Y}_{n},\cdot)\,\big\|\,P(Y_{n}^{\prime},\cdot)\bigr)\right].

If (
21
)–(
22
) hold, then

(24)

\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\widehat{\mu}_{N}\,\|\,\nu_{N}\bigr)\;\leq\;c\,\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\eta_{n}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{X}_{n}-Y_{n}^{\prime}\|^{2}+c^{\prime}\,\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{X}_{N-1}-Y_{N-1}^{\prime}\|^{2}+\bar{b}^{\,2},

where
\bar{b}^{\,2}:=\|b\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{N-1})}^{2}
.

5.4.
Shifted Wasserstein recursion

The right-hand side of (
24
) is controlled entirely
by the squared coupling distances

(25)

d_{n}^{\,2}\;:=\;\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{X}_{n}-Y_{n}^{\prime}\|^{2},\qquad n=0,1,\dots,N-1,

which propagate forward in time according to the next lemma. This is
the only place in the analysis where the state-dependent coefficient

a_{1}
enters.

Lemma 2

(Shifted Wasserstein recursion)
.

Assume (
16
). For all
0\leq n\leq N-2
,

(26)

d_{n+1}^{\,2}\;\leq\;L^{2}(1-\eta_{n})^{2}\,d_{n}^{\,2}+2\,(1-\eta_{n})\,\overline{a}_{1}\,d_{n}+\overline{a}_{0}^{\,2},

where

\overline{a}_{0}:=\sup_{0\leq k<N}\|a_{0}(\widehat{X}_{k})\|_{L^{2}}

and

\overline{a}_{1}:=\sup_{0\leq k<N}\|a_{1}(\widehat{X}_{k},\widetilde{Y}_{k})\|_{L^{2}}
.

Proof.

By the Wasserstein lift of the coupling
and (
16
),

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{n+1},\nu_{n+1}^{\prime})\;\leq\;L^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{X}_{n}-\widetilde{Y}_{n}\|^{2}+2\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[a_{1}(\widehat{X}_{n},\widetilde{Y}_{n})\,\|\widehat{X}_{n}-\widetilde{Y}_{n}\|\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[a_{0}(\widehat{X}_{n})^{2}\right].

Since
\widehat{X}_{n}-\widetilde{Y}_{n}=(1-\eta_{n})(\widehat{X}_{n}-Y_{n}^{\prime})
, the
first term equals
L^{2}(1-\eta_{n})^{2}d_{n}^{2}
, Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the
cross term by
2(1-\eta_{n})\overline{a}_{1}d_{n}
, and the third term
by
\overline{a}_{0}^{\,2}
. Choosing the synchronous Wasserstein coupling
on the next step yields (
26
).
∎

Remark 4

(Effect of state-dependence on
\overline{a}_{1}
)
.

The supremum defining
\overline{a}_{1}
involves the joint law of

(\widehat{X}_{k},\widetilde{Y}_{k})
, hence requires uniform moment bounds on
both
(\widehat{X}_{k})
and the shifted process
(\widetilde{Y}_{k})
. Since

\widetilde{Y}_{k}
is a convex combination of
Y_{k}^{\prime}
and
\widehat{X}_{k}
,
controlling its moments reduces to controlling those of
Y_{k}^{\prime}
, which
we establish in
Lemma
10
via the dissipativity condition
(A4)
on the drift of (
SDE
). This is the only place where the
polynomial-Lipschitz nature of the drift plays a non-trivial role at
the level of the framework.

The recursion (
26
) is purely deterministic once

\overline{a}_{0},\overline{a}_{1},L
have been fixed. Its analysis is
identical to that of the corresponding recursion
in
[
5
, Lemma B.5]
, and yields the following optimal
control of the shifted distances.

Proposition 2

(Deterministic control of the shifted distances)
.

Let
L\in[\tfrac{1}{2},2]
,
\overline{a}_{0},\overline{a}_{1}\geq 0
, and
\bar{N}:=N\wedge 1/(1-L)_{+}
.
There exists a sequence

(\eta_{n})_{n=0}^{N-2}\subset[0,1]
with
\eta_{N-1}=1
such that any
nonnegative sequence
(d_{n})_{n=0}^{N-1}

satisfying (
26
) and
d_{0}=\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)
obeys

(27)

\displaystyle d_{N-1}^{\,2}

\displaystyle\;\lesssim\;\tfrac{L^{-1}-1}{L^{-N}-1}\,d_{0}^{\,2}+\bigl(((L-1)N)\vee\log\bar{N}\bigr)\,\overline{a}_{0}^{\,2}+\bar{N}\,\overline{a}_{1}^{\,2},

(28)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\eta_{n}^{2}\,d_{n}^{\,2}

\displaystyle\;\lesssim\;\tfrac{L^{-1}-1}{L^{-N}-1}\,d_{0}^{\,2}+\bigl(((L-1)N)\vee\log\bar{N}\bigr)\,\overline{a}_{0}^{\,2}+\bar{N}\,\overline{a}_{1}^{\,2}.

Proof.

The recursion (
26
) has the same form as the
recursion analyzed in
[
5
, Appendix B.2]
, with
deterministic coefficients
\overline{a}_{0},\overline{a}_{1}
in place of
the local errors used there. The optimal shift sequence and the dynamic
programming argument of
[
5
, Lemmas B.4–B.6]
apply
verbatim and yield (
27
)–(
28
).
∎

5.5.
The KL local-error bound for kTULA

Combining Lemma
1
with
Proposition
2
yields the main KL bound of the
framework.

Theorem 4

(KL local-error framework)
.

Assume (
16
), (
21
), and
(
22
), and let
L\in[\tfrac{1}{2},2]
. With

\overline{a}_{0},\overline{a}_{1}
as in Lemma
2
,

\bar{b}:=\max_{0\leq n<N}\|b\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{n})}

\bar{N}=N\wedge 1/(1-L)_{+}

(29)

\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\mu\widehat{P}^{N}\,\|\,\nu P^{N}\bigr)\;\lesssim\;(c+c^{\prime})\!\left[\tfrac{L^{-1}-1}{L^{-N}-1}\,\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)+\bigl(((L-1)N)\vee\log\bar{N}\bigr)\,\overline{a}_{0}^{\,2}+\bar{N}\,\overline{a}_{1}^{\,2}\right]+\bar{b}^{\,2}.

We emphasize that the bound (
29
) compares the discretization
\mu\widehat{P}^{N}
against the law
\nu P^{N}
of the (

.

5.6.
A TV bound for the randomized scheme tRLMC

For the tamed randomized midpoint scheme
tRLMC
, the KL
framework above is not directly applicable: the randomization of the
integration time prevents the natural construction of an adapted
continuous-time interpolation, and consequently a sharp cross-regularity
estimate of the form (
22
) is not currently
available. We therefore work in the weaker total variation metric,
where a direct one-step estimate is at hand.

Let
\widetilde{P}
denote the one-step kernel
of
tRLMC
, and write

\widetilde{\mu}_{n}:=\mu\widetilde{P}^{\,n}
. We assume, in addition to
the regularity (
21
), the existence of a one-step
TV local error: there exists a measurable function

C_{\mathrm{TV}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
such that, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(30)

TV\!\bigl(\delta_{x}\widetilde{P},\delta_{x}P\bigr)\;\leq\;C_{\mathrm{TV}}(x)\,\lambda.

For
tRLMC
, this estimate is established in
Proposition
11
.

The auxiliary process construction of §
5.2

remains valid with
\widehat{P}
replaced by
\widetilde{P}
throughout.

Lemma 3

(TV bound via the shifted construction)
.

Assume (
21
) and (
30
), and let

d_{n}^{\,2}:=\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{X}_{n}-Y_{n}^{\prime}\|^{2}
. Writing

\overline{C}_{\mathrm{TV}}:=\sup_{0\leq n<N}\|C_{\mathrm{TV}}(x)\|_{L^{2}(\widetilde{\mu}_{n})}
,

(31)

TV\!\bigl(\widetilde{\mu}_{N},\nu_{N}\bigr)\;\leq\;\overline{C}_{\mathrm{TV}}\,\lambda+\sqrt{\tfrac{c}{2}}\,d_{N-1}+\sqrt{\tfrac{c}{2}\,\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\eta_{n}^{2}\,d_{n}^{\,2}}.

Theorem 5

(TV local-error framework)
.

Assume (
16
), (
21
), and
(
30
), and let
L\in[\tfrac{1}{2},2]
. With

\overline{a}_{0},\overline{a}_{1}
as in

(with
\widehat{P}
replaced by

\widetilde{P}
) and
\bar{N}=N\wedge 1/(1-L)_{+}
,

(32)

TV\!\bigl(\mu\widetilde{P}^{N},\nu P^{N}\bigr)\;\lesssim\;\overline{C}_{\mathrm{TV}}\,\lambda+\sqrt{\tfrac{L^{-1}-1}{L^{-N}-1}\,\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)+\bigl(((L-1)N)\vee\log\bar{N}\bigr)\,\overline{a}_{0}^{\,2}+\bar{N}\,\overline{a}_{1}^{\,2}}.

The proof combines Lemma
3
with

. The KL and TV frameworks rest

5.7.
Wasserstein convergence of tRLMC

Wasserstein convergence for
tRLMC
avoids the auxiliary process
construction and proceeds via a more direct route based on the
contraction of the underlying semigroup toward
\pi_{\beta}
. Unlike the local-error frameworks of §
5.1
–§
5.6
, this argument is genuinely sampling-specific: throughout the present subsection we therefore assume the specialization
h=\nabla u
,
\sigma=\sqrt{2/\beta}
of (
SDE
) to the (
LSDE
), and we additionally impose Assumption
(A6)
.

Under this specialization, the local Lipschitz property of
the drift, combined with the one-sided Lipschitz
condition
(A3)
, yields a one-step Wasserstein bound of the
form (
16
) with

\Gamma(x,y,\lambda)=C_{\mathrm{cpl}}\lambda(1+\|x\|^{2\ell}+\|y\|^{2\ell})
.
Iterating this estimate over
n_{0}
steps and exploiting the uniform
moment bounds of
Lemma
12
furnishes the long-time
discretization-error estimate

(33)

\mathcal{W}_{2}\!\left(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{\,n},\;\bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{\,n-n_{0}}\bigr)P^{n_{0}}\right)\;\leq\;C\,e^{c\lambda n_{0}}\,\lambda,\qquad n\geq n_{0}\geq 0,

where
C,c
depend polynomially on the dimension. In parallel, the
combination of the logarithmic Sobolev inequality
(A6)
and the one-sided
Lipschitz condition
(A3)
implies the contraction

(34)

\mathcal{W}_{2}\!\bigl(\nu P^{t},\pi_{\beta}\bigr)\;\leq\;C_{\mathrm{contr}}\,e^{-\dot{c}\,t}\,\mathcal{W}_{2}\!\bigl(\nu,\pi_{\beta}\bigr),\qquad t\geq 0,

for some
\dot{c}>0
depending on
C_{\mathrm{LSI}}
. Combining
(
33
) and (
34
) via the
triangle inequality

\mathcal{W}_{2}\!\bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{\,n},\pi_{\beta}\bigr)\;\leq\;\mathcal{W}_{2}\!\Bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{\,n},\,\bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{\,n-n_{0}}\bigr)P^{n_{0}}\Bigr)+\mathcal{W}_{2}\!\Bigl(\bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{\,n-n_{0}}\bigr)P^{n_{0}},\pi_{\beta}\Bigr),

and balancing the two contributions by an appropriate choice of
n_{0}

yields the announced Wasserstein convergence estimate of
Theorem
3
.

C.7.
Wasserstein convergence for tRLMC

Throughout this subsection, we work in the sampling specialization
h=\nabla u
,
\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}
, under Assumption
(A6)
. We denote by
P
the one-step Langevin kernel of (

), and by

the one-step kernel of (

Lemma 16
.

For any
n,n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
,

W_{2}\!\bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{n},\,\bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{\,n-n_{0}}\bigr)P^{n_{0}}\bigr)\;\leq\;C_{d}\,e^{\frac{3}{2}K^{\prime}\lambda n_{0}}\,\lambda,

where
C_{d}=\mathcal{O}(d^{2\ell+2})
.

Proof.

Recall the one-step Wasserstein estimate (
16
):

W_{2}^{2}(\delta_{x}\widetilde{P},\delta_{y}P)\leq L^{2}\|x-y\|^{2}+2a_{1}(x,y)\|x-y\|+a_{0}(x)^{2}.

Applying Young’s inequality yields

W_{2}^{2}(\delta_{x}\widetilde{P},\delta_{y}P)\leq L^{3}\|x-y\|^{2}+\frac{a_{1}(x,y)^{2}}{(L-1)L^{2}}+a_{0}(x)^{2}.

By convexity of the Wasserstein distance,

W_{2}^{2}(\mu\widetilde{P},\nu P)\leq L^{3}W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)+\frac{1}{(L-1)L^{2}}\int a_{1}(x,y)^{2}\,d\mu(x)\,d\nu(y)+\int a_{0}(x)^{2}\,d\mu(x).

Define

\bar{a}_{0}^{2}:=\sup_{0\leq k<n_{0}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[a_{0}(\bar{Y}_{k})^{2}\bigr],\qquad\bar{a}_{1}^{2}:=\sup_{0\leq k<n_{0}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[a_{1}(\bar{Y}_{k},Y_{k})^{2}\bigr],

where
(\bar{Y}_{k})
are the (
tRLMC
) iterates and
(Y_{k})
the corresponding SDE updates.
Iterating the above inequality yields

W_{2}^{2}\!\bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{n},\,\bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{\,n-n_{0}}\bigr)P^{n_{0}}\bigr)\leq L^{3n_{0}}\Bigl(\frac{\bar{a}_{1}^{2}}{(L-1)^{2}}+\frac{\bar{a}_{0}^{2}}{L-1}\Bigr).

Since
L=e^{K^{\prime}\lambda}
, one has
(L-1)^{-1}=\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})
.
Using the moment bounds from Lemma
12
, together with the
local error estimates from Propositions
9
–
10
, we obtain

\bar{a}_{0}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(d^{2\ell+2}\lambda^{3}),\qquad\bar{a}_{1}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(d^{4\ell}\lambda^{2}).

Substituting these bounds yields the result.
∎

(Contraction)
.

Proposition 12

(Wasserstein convergence)
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

W_{2}(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{n},\pi_{\beta})\;\leq\;\frac{C^{*}C_{d}}{1-e^{-1}}\,\lambda\;+\;C^{\prime}e^{-c_{0}\lambda n},

where
C_{d},C^{*},C^{\prime},c_{0}>0
are given explicitly in the proof. This estimate yields Theorem
3
.

Proof.

Let
n_{0}=\left\lceil\frac{\log C_{\mathrm{contr}}+1}{\dot{c}\,\lambda}\right\rceil
, so that
C_{\mathrm{contr}}e^{-\dot{c}\lambda n_{0}}\leq e^{-1}
, and set
C^{*}:=e^{\frac{3}{2}K^{\prime}\lambda n_{0}}
.

By Lemma
16
and Lemma
17
, for
n\geq n_{0}
,

\displaystyle W_{2}(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{n},\pi_{\beta})

\displaystyle=W_{2}\bigl(\bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{n-n_{0}}\bigr)\widetilde{P}^{n_{0}},\pi_{\beta}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq W_{2}\bigl(\bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{n-n_{0}}\bigr)\widetilde{P}^{n_{0}},\,\bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{n-n_{0}}\bigr)P^{n_{0}}\bigr)+W_{2}\bigl(\bigl(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{n-n_{0}}\bigr)P^{n_{0}},\pi_{\beta}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}e^{\frac{3}{2}K^{\prime}\lambda n_{0}}\lambda+C_{\mathrm{contr}}e^{-\dot{c}\lambda n_{0}}\,W_{2}(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{n-n_{0}},\pi_{\beta})

\displaystyle\leq C^{*}C_{d}\lambda+\frac{1}{e}\,W_{2}(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{n-n_{0}},\pi_{\beta}).

By
[
35
, Lemma 3.18]
,

W_{2}(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{n},\pi_{\beta})\leq\frac{C^{*}C_{d}}{1-e^{-1}}\,\lambda+\sup_{N\geq 0}W_{2}(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{N},\pi_{\beta})\,e^{-\frac{n}{n_{0}}+1}.

From the uniform moment bounds in Lemma
12
,

W_{2}(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{N},\pi_{\beta})\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{N}}\|x\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\beta}}\|x\|^{2}}\leq C_{\mathrm{mom}},

where
C_{\mathrm{mom}}=\mathcal{O}(d)
. Hence, defining
C^{\prime}:=C_{\mathrm{mom}}\,e
and
c_{0}=\frac{\dot{c}}{\log C_{\mathrm{contr}}+1}
,

W_{2}(\mu_{0}\widetilde{P}^{n},\pi_{\beta})\leq\frac{C^{*}C_{d}}{1-e^{-1}}\,\lambda+C^{\prime}\,e^{-c_{0}\lambda n},

as claimed.
∎

Proof.

For the second term, applying Pinsker’s inequality and the regularity condition (
21
) almost surely yields
\mathrm{TV}\bigl(P(\widetilde{Y}_{N-1},\cdot),P(Y^{\prime}_{N-1},\cdot)\bigr)\leq\sqrt{\frac{c}{2}\|\widetilde{Y}_{N-1}-Y^{\prime}_{N-1}\|^{2}}
. Taking the expectation and applying Jensen’s inequality provides

I_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{c}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{Y}_{N-1}-Y^{\prime}_{N-1}\|^{2}}.

Since
\eta_{N-1}=1
, the identity
\widetilde{Y}_{N-1}=\widehat{X}_{N-1}
holds exactly, bounding the second term by
I_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{c}{2}}\,d_{N-1}
.

For the third term, the contraction of total variation under Markov kernels implies
I_{3}\leq\mathrm{TV}(\nu^{\prime}_{N-1},\nu_{N-1})
. Applying Pinsker’s inequality and telescoping the shifted chain rule up to time
N-1
provides the relative entropy bound

\mathrm{KL}(\nu^{\prime}_{N-1}\|\nu_{N-1})\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{KL}\bigl(P(\widetilde{Y}_{n},\cdot)\,\big\|\,P(Y_{n}^{\prime},\cdot)\bigr)\Bigr].

Invoking (
21
) and the interpolation identity
\widetilde{Y}_{n}-Y_{n}^{\prime}=\eta_{n}(\widehat{X}_{n}-Y_{n}^{\prime})
evaluates the sum as

\mathrm{KL}(\nu^{\prime}_{N-1}\|\nu_{N-1})\leq c\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{Y}_{n}-Y_{n}^{\prime}\|^{2}=c\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\eta_{n}^{2}\,d_{n}^{2}.

Substituting this into the Pinsker bound yields
I_{3}\leq\sqrt{\frac{c}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\eta_{n}^{2}\,d_{n}^{2}}
.

Combining the bounds for
I_{1},I_{2}
, and
I_{3}
establishes (
35
).
∎

D.5.1.
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

By Propositions
3
,
5
,
and
8
, together with the one-step weak and strong

4

satisfy, uniformly over
0\leq n<N

\overline{a}_{0}^{\,2}=O(\lambda^{3}),\qquad\overline{a}_{1}^{\,2}=O(\lambda^{4}),\qquad c=O(\lambda^{-1}),\qquad c^{\prime}=O(\lambda^{-1}).

,
\bar{N}:=N\wedge(1-L)_{+}^{-1}

\bar{b}:=\sup_{0\leq n<N}\|b\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{n})}

in both regimes:
\bar{N}=N
when

\bar{N}\leq(1-L)^{-1}\leq N
when
L<1
. For

Wasserstein coefficient satisfies

\frac{L^{-1}-1}{L^{-N}-1}=\frac{L-1}{L^{N}-1}\in[0,1],

the bound holding when
L\geq 1

L^{N}-1\geq L-1\geq 0
, and when

L\leq 1
, then
L^{N}-1\leq L-1<0
, and the case
L=1
following by continuity.

Applying the estimate (
29
) and bounding the
Wasserstein coefficient by
1

\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\mu\widehat{P}^{N}\,\big\|\,\nu P^{N}\bigr)\;\lesssim\;(c+c^{\prime})\Bigl[W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)+\bigl((L-1)N\vee\log\bar{N}\bigr)\overline{a}_{0}^{\,2}+\bar{N}\,\overline{a}_{1}^{\,2}\Bigr]+\bar{b}^{\,2}.

We bound the bracketed terms. Since
L\in[\tfrac{1}{2},2]
, the mean value theorem
gives

|L-1|=|e^{K^{\prime}\lambda}-1|\leq e^{|K^{\prime}|\lambda}|K^{\prime}|\lambda\leq 2|K^{\prime}|\lambda=O(\lambda),

so, together with
\log\bar{N}\leq\log N
and
\overline{a}_{0}^{\,2}=O(\lambda^{3})

the second bracketed term is
O\bigl(((N\lambda)\vee\log N)\lambda^{3}\bigr)

\bar{N}\,\overline{a}_{1}^{\,2}\leq N\,\overline{a}_{1}^{\,2}=O(N\lambda^{4})

Multiplying the bracket by
(c+c^{\prime})=O(\lambda^{-1})
therefore contributes

O(\lambda^{-1})\,W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)+O\bigl(((N\lambda)\vee\log N)\lambda^{2}\bigr)+O(N\lambda^{3}).

For the remaining term, Proposition
8

the last bound by Lemma

Collecting these contributions,

\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\mu\widehat{P}^{N}\,\big\|\,\nu P^{N}\bigr)\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\lambda}\,W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)+C\bigl((N\lambda)\vee\log N\bigr)\lambda^{2}+C\,N\lambda^{3}+C\,\lambda^{2}.

\lambda^{2}

and
N\lambda^{3}=(N\lambda)\lambda^{2}
are dominated by

\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\mu\widehat{P}^{N}\,\big\|\,\nu P^{N}\bigr)\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\lambda}\,W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)+C\bigl((N\lambda)\vee\log N\bigr)\lambda^{2},

\lambda

∎

D.5.6.
Proof of Corollary
4

Proof.

(57)

\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[u(\widehat{X}_{N})\bigr]-\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(\theta)=\Bigl(\mathbb{E}[u(\widehat{X}_{N})]-\mathbb{E}[u(Z_{\infty})]\Bigr)+\Bigl(\mathbb{E}[u(Z_{\infty})]-\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(\theta)\Bigr).

We first estimate the difference

Using Assumption

\displaystyle|u(x)-u(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\|h\bigl(tx+(1-t)y\bigr)\bigr\|\,\|x-y\|\,dt

(58)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[u(\widehat{X}_{N})]-\mathbb{E}[u(Z_{\infty})]

\displaystyle\leq C_{h}\Bigl(1+\bigl(\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{X}_{N}\|^{4\ell}\bigr)^{1/2}+\bigl(\mathbb{E}\|Z_{\infty}\|^{4\ell}\bigr)^{1/2}\Bigr)

\sup_{N\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{X}_{N}\|^{4\ell}<\infty,
