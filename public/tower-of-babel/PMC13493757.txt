Achieving Canadian Treat-to-Target Criteria in Moderate-to-Severe Atopic Dermatitis: A Retrospective Analysis of Outcomes from Real-World Canadian Practice
Authors: Jensen Yeung, Parbeer Grewal, Mark Legault, Jason Stacey, Irina Turchin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTreat-to-target (T2T) is a clinical paradigm intended to improve disease outcomes using measurement-based goals. Various groups of dermatologists have made T2T recommendations for managing moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis (AD). These are based primarily on expert opinion, and their relevance for real-world outcomes has received limited attention. The objective of the present analysis was to assess the relevance of Canadian T2T criteria for moderate-to-severe AD in real-world practice by describing the agreement between achieving criteria and treatment decisions.MethodsCanadian dermatologists conducted retrospective chart reviews of adult patients with moderate-to-severe AD treated with an advanced systemic therapy (AST) in clinical practice. T2T criteria were applied to patient outcomes and treatment decisions were reported.ResultsCharts were reviewed for 297 patients who were assessed a combined 503 times over up to 1 year of treatment. Treatment modifications were made in less than 10% (38/425, 9%) of cases where patients met target and more than half (41/78, 53%) where they missed target. For patients missing target, switching AST was the most common modification.ConclusionsTreatment decisions were generally aligned with the retrospectively applied Canadian T2T criteria. While these results require further validation, they suggest that the T2T criteria represent valid thresholds for clinical decision-making.

Introduction

The use of advanced systemic therapies (ASTs), including biologics and oral JAK inhibitors (JAKis), has significantly improved outcomes for patients with moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis (AD) [1]. However, some patients do not experience adequate improvements with their current AST [2]. Although modifying treatment may lead to improvement, there is also a risk of worse outcomes or adverse events with the modification. Benchmarks for acceptable levels of improvement over time could guide treatment decisions.

Treat-to-target (T2T) is a clinical paradigm intended to improve the outcomes of chronic diseases by using validated measures at specific time points to track progress toward treatment targets and provide guidance on when to modify treatment [3, 4]. In dermatology, various T2T recommendations have been proposed for plaque psoriasis [4–6], and, more recently, for AD [7–11]. These recommendations are based largely on clinical experience, expert opinion, and consensus. While T2T criteria have been applied to data from phase 3 trials supporting the efficacy of ASTs [12, 13], their validity in real-world practice has received limited attention [14].

In 2023, a group of 11 Canadian dermatologists published T2T criteria for moderate-to-severe AD [9]. The purpose of the present study was to take a first step toward validating these criteria by applying them retrospectively to the outcomes of patients who were being treated with an AST for moderate-to-severe AD in real-world Canadian dermatology practice.

Methods

Study Design

This was a Canadian noninterventional retrospective chart review. Participating dermatologists who were familiar with the Canadian T2T recommendations for moderate-to-severe AD [9] agreed to review the charts of close to 10 adult patients treated with an AST for AD in clinical practice; patients treated as part of a clinical trial were excluded. Patients had to have been initiated on the AST between June 2022 and February 2024, a period during which three classes of ASTs were available in Canada: interleukin (IL)-4/IL-13 inhibitors (dupilumab), IL-13 inhibitors (tralokinumab), and JAKis (upadacitinib or abrocitinib). The start date for this period was selected because 1 June 2022 was the date after which all these ASTs were available in Canada. The end date was selected because it allowed for patients to have been on treatment for at least 6 months before data collection began in September 2024. A minimum of two and a maximum of four patients treated with each class of systemic treatment could be included by each dermatologist.

The protocol for this study was reviewed and approved by an institutional review board whose membership complies with the requirements defined in Health Canada regulations and International Council on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines. A waiver of consent was granted for the review of medical records.

Data Collection

Data were entered into a password-protected online database. Deidentified patient demographics and treatment history were collected along with disease severity scores at baseline (defined as the visit at which the AST was prescribed) and at least one follow-up visit.

Outcomes

Target criteria were applied retrospectively. Target status (met or missed) and treatment decisions (continue or modify) were the key outcomes recorded as available at each follow-up visit. The Canadian target criteria have been described elsewhere and are summarized in Fig. 1 [9]. Briefly, the criteria include meeting a threshold score on one of two clinician-rated measures, the Eczema Area Severity Index (EASI) or the Physician’s Global Assessment (PGA), as well as a threshold score on at least one of three patient-rated scales, the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (Pruritus NRS), or Patient Oriented Eczema Measure (POEM). The criteria include three assessment time points during the first year of treatment. Results

Participating Dermatologists

There were 29 participating dermatologists from across Canada, including 16 from Ontario, 7 from Alberta, 2 from Quebec, and 1 each from British Columbia, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, and New Brunswick. The participating dermatologists had a broad range of clinical experience including management of moderate-to-severe AD, with 7% having less than 5 years, 48% having 5–10 years, 31% having 11–20 years, and 14% having more than 20 years of experience in clinical practice.

Baseline Characteristics

Patient Demographics and Disease Characteristics

Charts were reviewed for 297 patients treated with dupilumab (n = 108), tralokinumab (n = 92), or a JAK inhibitor (n = 97). Previous topical treatments included topical corticosteroids (88%), topical calcineurin inhibitors (80%), and topical PDE4 inhibitors (16%). Previous systemic treatments included corticosteroids (48%), methotrexate (40%), phototherapy (46%), dupilumab (17%), cyclosporine (6%), JAK inhibitor (5%), and tralokinumab (2%). Patients were evenly split between male and female with a mean age of 42 years; 15% of patients were aged over 65 years. Most patients had AD with involvement of the trunk and limbs; head (face and scalp), neck, and hands were also commonly involved. The most commonly reported comorbid conditions were asthma (25%), allergic rhinitis (24%), and chronic hand eczema (16%).

Disposition and Evaluations

Of the 297 patients, 203 remained on the AST for 1 year or longer. The other 94 patients were either on treatment for less than 1 year (n = 59) or discontinued treatment with the AST after less than 1 year (n = 35). The most common reasons for discontinuation were lack of efficacy (n = 15) and adverse events (n = 10). Three patients discontinued because they achieved “clear skin” or wanted a “drug holiday”, and two patients discontinued owing to cost or loss of insurance.

Target Criteria and Clinical Decisions

Over the three assessment time points, there were 503 assessments at which target was met 425 times (84%) and missed 78 times (16%). Target criteria were met at least once by 256 patients (86%), while 62 patients (21%) missed target at least once (because patients could have both hit and missed target at different assessments, the total percentage is greater than 100%; Table 2). There were nine patients who remained on treatment for at least 1 year who never met target.

When target criteria were met, treatment modifications were made less than 10% of the time over the three assessment points (38/425, 9%). For these patients, the most common modification was to decrease dose (seven patients treated with a JAKi) or dosing frequency (four patients treated with a biologic and two patients treated with a JAKi). AST dose or dosing frequency was increased (up-dosed) for six patients. These six patients were reported to have a special site (or hard to treat area) involvement including face, scalp, hands, or genitals, although it is not known whether this was the reason for up-dosing.

When target was missed, treatment modifications were made more than half the time (41/78, 53%). The most common modification was switching to another AST (20/41, 49%), followed by adding another systemic treatment (8/41) and increasing dose or dose frequency (7/41). Increasing dose was more common for patients treated with a JAKi while increasing the dose frequency was more common for patients receiving a biologic.

At the first assessment time point (12–16 weeks), 187 (85%) of 219 patients met target. For the 32 patients who missed target, treatment modifications were made for 13 (41%) patients, including 2 patients for whom the dose was increased, 3 for whom another systemic therapy was added, 6 who switched to another AST, and 2 who discontinued the AST without the next therapy being specified.

At 6–8 months, 28 (17%) of 163 patients missed the target criteria. Among these, nine were assessed for the first time at this time point while ten had missed target and nine had met target at 12–16 weeks. Modifications to treatment were made for 21 (75%) of these patients. The most common modifications were switching to another AST (n = 9), adding another systemic treatment (n = 4), and modifying the dose or dose frequency (n = 4). For the 120 patients assessed at the 1-year time point, 18 (15%) missed the target criteria. Treatment was modified for seven (39%) of these patients, with five switching to another AST.

Discussion

Published T2T recommendations for AD are largely based on the consensus of expert dermatologists, sometimes with input from other stakeholders such as nurses and patients [8–10]. To date, achievement rates of T2T criteria have been applied to phase 3 data to retrospectively support the efficacy of newer ASTs [12, 13] and have only recently been applied in real-world practice to assess the frequency of meeting specific criteria [14]. While these approaches are useful, other aspects of validating target criteria have not been explored. The present study takes a first step toward validating the Canadian T2T recommendations for moderate-to-severe AD by describing the relationship between target status and real-world treatment decisions.

Within the context of this retrospective study, treatment modifications should have occurred more frequently for patients who missed target than for patients who met target. This was the case with about half of patients who missed target having their treatment modified compared with one in ten patients who met target. Overall, these results indicate agreement between the T2T criteria and treatment decisions. As might be expected, the types of treatment modifications were different for those who met versus missed target. For patients missing target, the Canadian T2T recommendations suggest optimizing the current AST (increase dose or dose frequency), adding another systemic treatment, or switching treatments. In the present study, about half of treatment modifications for patients missing target was to switch ASTs rather than optimization or adding another systemic treatment. The rationale for deciding between treatment modifications was not captured in this study but warrants investigation in future prospective studies. For patients who met target, dose modifications were infrequent with reduction of dose or dosing frequency being most common. Dosing strategies after achieving target are not usually addressed in T2T recommendations and may also be worthy of future study.

Agreement between missing target and treatment modification was not perfect, with only half of the patients missing target having their treatment modified. On the surface, this might be interpreted as suboptimal treatment for some patients. However, several factors should be considered. The data recorded in this study were limited by the constraints of the questionnaire and the information recorded in patient charts. If, through shared decision-making, the clinician and patient decided against modifying treatment, this information may not have been captured. Staying the course of treatment could have been a viable option for several reasons. For instance, at early assessment points, reminding patients about optimal skin care and use of topical treatments may have been considered prior to making any treatment modifications. It is also possible that participating dermatologists wanted to allow more time for improvements to accrue beyond the initial 16 weeks. When assessed at that time point, modest improvements might have encouraged the patient and clinician to stay the course given the risk of losing improvements by changing treatment. This approach might be reflected in the fact that modifications were less frequent at the 12–16 week assessment than at the 6–8 month assessment (41% versus 75% for patients not meeting target). Finally, the reality in Canada is that switching between costly ASTs can involve lengthy insurance paperwork, varied access to advanced therapeutics, and a risk of not being insured, which might deter physicians and patients from considering treatment changes. With that in mind, it is noteworthy that switching ASTs was the most common modification strategy, being chosen more than twice as frequently as increasing the dose or dosing frequency.

In addition to assessing the Canadian T2T criteria, this study provides insight into real-world Canadian clinical practice. Participants were representative of Canadian dermatologists in terms of geography, experience, and type of practice. There was considerable ethnic and racial diversity among the patients, also suggesting a broad representation of Canada’s population.

A limitation to this study is that it is a retrospective chart analysis. While rating scale scores, assessment points, and treatment decisions could not be biased by foreknowledge of the T2T criteria, there are other inherent biases in this design. For instance, charts could only be included if they contained at least one of each of the physician-rated and patient-reported outcomes included in the T2T criteria. This requirement imposes a bias on participating dermatologists who regularly use and document these outcomes. There is also the possibility of recall bias in selecting patient charts. In an effort to avoid this, participants were asked to screen for eligible charts beginning from the start date (in June 2022) moving forward until 10 eligible charts were identified. However, the methodology used by each dermatologist was not monitored so the selection of certain memorable patients cannot be ruled out. This may account for the high rate (about 85%) of achieving target.

Owing to this study’s sample size, retrospective nature, and similarity to other real-world noninterventional studies, there are gaps in the data. Not all patients who remained on their AST were assessed at each of the three time points. When the T2T criteria were created, it was not expected that all patients treated in real-world practice would be assessed at each time point. For example, if a patient was assessed at 16 weeks and achieved almost complete clearance, they might not need to be assessed at the 6–8 month time point.

Conclusions

This retrospective study is among the first to assess the relationship between meeting recommended treatment targets and clinical decisions. While the study design and limitations prevent definitive conclusions, the results suggest that the Canadian T2T criteria are aligned with real-world practice and may represent valid thresholds for making clinical decisions. This study represents a starting point for more formal evaluation and validation of the Canadian T2T recommendations.
