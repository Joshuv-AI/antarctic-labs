An energy-decreasing algorithm for the finite element approximation of ferronematic equilibrium states
Alexandre Ern Ruma R. Maity

Abstract
We develop an energy-decreasing algorithm for the finite element approximation of two-dimensional ferronematic equilibrium states. The problem is formulated as the minimization of the harmonic energy of two two-dimensional vector fields, both with prescribed length, together with an additional nonlinear relation on the orientation of the two vectors. The finite element setting is based on piecewise continuous finite elements on a weakly acute triangulation. The computational realization of the energy-decreasing algorithm employs a decomposition-coordination framework and a Uzawa-like iteration. Numerical experiments are presented to illustrate the computational performances of the algorithm.



Keywords:
Liquid crystals, ferronematics, harmonic map, finite element method, energy-decreasing iterations, decomposition-coordination method

Introduction

Nematic liquid crystals are a physical state of a material where the constituent molecules exhibit long-range orientational ordering but no long-range positional ordering
[
15
]
.
The study of composite systems consisting of magnetic nanoparticles dispersed in a nematic liquid crystal is commonly referred to as ferronematics in the literature
[
12
,
21
]
.
The nemato-magnetic coupling enhances magnetic susceptibility, enabling low-field control of material behavior with potential applications in optics, sensors, and telecommunications
[
24
,
18
]
.
Ferronematic equilibrium states are sought as minimizers of a suitable free energy density of the system, which generally consists of the Landau–de Gennes free energy density for the nematic liquid crystal, plus the Ginzburg–Landau free energy density for the magnetization, and the nemato-magnetic coupling energy density (see, e.g.,

[
9
]
and the references cited therein). We focus here on a two-dimensional setting, where the ferronematic system occupies an open, bounded, connected, Lipschitz set (domain)
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In this case, the two variables describing the state of the system are: (i) The nematic liquid crystal tensor,
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
, a mapping from
\Omega
to the space of symmetric, trace-free matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
; this is the so-called order parameter that describes the orientational ordering; (ii) The magnetization vector,
{\boldsymbol{M}}
, a mapping from
\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, represents the spatially averaged magnetic moment of the suspended nanoparticles. Owing to the two-dimensional setting, the order parameter
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
can be viewed as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as well. Altogether, the dependent variables are collected in the
\mathbb{R}^{4}
-valued vector
\Psi:=({\boldsymbol{Q}},{\boldsymbol{M}})^{\mathrm{T}}
.

We are particularly interested in the practically important case where the ratio (
\ell
) between the material-dependent length scale and the computational domain length scale takes very small values.
In this situation, equilibrium states can still be explored numerically using the above free energy density, but this becomes particularly demanding computationally as the Euler–Lagrange minimization conditions lead to a system of four coupled nonlinear PDEs. Examples can be found in
[
9
,
20
,
14
]
. An alternative approach is to formally let the parameter
\ell
approach zero and tackle directly the free energy density minimization under the additional constraint resulting from the above limit. Specifically, one enforces length constraints on the two-dimensional vectors
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
and
{\boldsymbol{M}}
, and a nonlinear relation expressing
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
in terms of
{\boldsymbol{M}}
. Altogether, this means that the values of
\Psi
are prescribed to lie in
\mathcal{N}
, a connected, compact, one-dimensional smooth submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. This is the approach followed herein, where ferronematic equilibrium states in the above asymptotic limit are modeled by minimizing the harmonic energy functional
E:H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{4})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that (see Section
2
for more details on the notation)

E(\Psi):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}{\lvert\kern-1.07639pt\lvert\nabla\Psi\rvert\kern-1.07639pt\rvert}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}\,{\rm dx}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}{\lvert\kern-1.07639pt\lvert\nabla{\boldsymbol{Q}}\rvert\kern-1.07639pt\rvert}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}\,{\rm dx}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}{\lvert\kern-1.07639pt\lvert\nabla{\boldsymbol{M}}\rvert\kern-1.07639pt\rvert}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}\,{\rm dx},

(1.1)

subjected to the constraint that
\Psi
takes values in
\mathcal{N}
. Moreover, boundary values are prescribed on
\partial\Omega
that are compatible with the above constraint.
We assume the existence of a minimizer. Sufficient conditions for the Ginzburg–Landau problem are discussed in
[
8
]
based on the notion of winding numbers for the Dirichlet boundary datum.

The main goal of the paper is to analyze the finite element approximation of the above constrained minimization problem of the harmonic energy and to propose an energy-decreasing iterative algorithm with reasonable computational costs.
We consider piecewise affine, continuous finite elements on a weakly acute triangulation
\mathcal{T}

[
25
,
16
,
11
]
. Weakly acute triangulations are commonly used in the context of finite element approximations of harmonic energy minimization under length constraints. In the present setting, this assumption allows us to write the discrete harmonic energy as a sum of local positive contributions indexed by pairs of mesh nodes sharing a mesh edge. Furthermore, the constraint of taking values in
\mathcal{N}
is enforced at the mesh nodes. Incidentally, this means that we are considering a nonconforming approximation as the discrete functions
\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4}
do not take values in
\mathcal{N}
everywhere in
\Omega
(but only at the mesh nodes).

We first study how to devise energy-decreasing iterations at the continuous level. To this purpose, we leverage a representation of every element in the constraint manifold
\mathcal{N}
by a unit vector
{\boldsymbol{n}}
from the unit circle
{\boldsymbol{S}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The key relation to ensure the energy-decreasing property is the identity (
2.8
) below, relating the (spatial) gradient of
\Psi
to the (spatial) gradient of
{\boldsymbol{n}}
.
Unfortunately, it is not possible to extend this result to the discrete level. The first key
idea forward, exploiting the weakly acute property of the mesh, is to replace the identity (
2.8
) at the continuous level by the identity (
3.6
), which evaluates the harmonic energy of a discrete field
\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}
by a finite sum of positive contributions resulting from local increments of unit vectors associated with pairs of mesh nodes sharing a mesh edge. However, two unit vectors per mesh node need to be considered (and not just one), the two being related by a nonlinear mapping from
{\boldsymbol{S}}
onto itself. The second key idea forward is achieved by Lemma
3.2
which uncovers a subtle geometric property of the constraint manifold
\mathcal{N}
. The result of Lemma
3.2
brings the central design condition that guarantees the energy-decreasing property of the present iterative algorithm.

To enhance the computational efficiency of the energy-decreasing iterative algorithm, we adopt a decomposition-coordination approach in the spirit of Fortin and Glowinski’s framework for constrained optimization with separable objective functions and coupled constraints
[
17
]
. The constraint is incorporated using a Lagrange multiplier within an augmented Lagrangian approach
[
6
,
7
]
. A critical point of the augmented Lagrangian is sought using an Uzawa-type iterative algorithm
[
2
,
17
]
. As the primal minimization problem remains nonlinear, a further simplification is introduced by linearizing the constraint at each step of the Uzawa algorithm.

The minimization of the harmonic energy under prescribed length constraints is already well explored in the literature. The simplest setting is the Oseen–Frank model which minimizes the harmonic energy of a unit direction vector
[
1
,
13
,
19
]
, see also
[
23
]
for further computational enhancements,
[
3
]
for the stability and convergence analysis and
[
4
]
for the implicit time discretization of harmonic map flows into spheres. More complex models have been addressed as well, including the Eriksen model where the dependent variable is the tensor
{\boldsymbol{Q}}=s({\boldsymbol{n}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{n}}-d^{-1}\mathbb{I})
, where the order parameters are the scalar-valued function
s
and the field
{\boldsymbol{n}}
taking values in the unit sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
d
is the ambient space dimension
[
5
,
22
,
10
]
. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, numerical methods addressing the present ferronematic equilibrium problem have not yet been analyzed.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the admissible constraint manifold
\mathcal{N}
and formulates the energy minimization problem. This section also outlines the continuous energy-decreasing algorithm. The main result of this section is Theorem
2.4
. Section
3
develops the finite element approximation, leading to the discrete energy-decreasing algorithm. The main results of this section are Lemma
3.2
and Theorem
3.3
.
Section
4
describes important computational aspects, including the decomposition-coordination approach and the Uzawa-like iterations. Finally, Section
5
reports numerical experiments on two examples featuring nonzero smooth Dirichlet boundary conditions on a square domain, thereby illustrating the computational performance of the proposed algorithms.

2
Continuous setting

For positive integer numbers
p,q
,
\|\bullet\|_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}
denotes the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
and
\|\bullet\|_{\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}}
denotes the Frobenius norm in
\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
.
The Cartesian components of a vector
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
are denoted
(\Psi_{i})_{1\leq i\leq q}
.

2.1
Admissible manifold

Let
{\boldsymbol{S}}
be the unit sphere (circle) in
\mathbb{R}^{2},

{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}:=(1,0)^{\mathrm{T}}
the first vector of the Cartesian basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
\mathbb{I}
the
2\times 2
identity matrix. We consider the (nonlinear) map

{\boldsymbol{R}}:{\boldsymbol{S}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{S}},\quad{\boldsymbol{n}}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{n}}):=(2{\boldsymbol{n}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{n}}-\mathbb{I}){\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}.

(2.1)

An elementary computation shows that, if
{\boldsymbol{n}}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}
is parametrized as
{\boldsymbol{n}}:=(\cos(\varphi),\sin(\varphi))^{\mathrm{T}}
, then
{\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{n}})=(\cos(2\varphi),\sin(2\varphi))^{\mathrm{T}}
. Given two positive real numbers
Q_{c}
and
M_{c}
, we
define the (nonlinear) map

\mathcal{G}:{\boldsymbol{S}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4},\quad{\boldsymbol{n}}\mapsto\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{n}}):=(Q_{c}{\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{n}}),M_{c}{\boldsymbol{n}})^{\mathrm{T}}.

(2.2)

It is readily seen that
\mathcal{G}:{\boldsymbol{S}}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
is a smooth diffeomorphism, where

\mathcal{N}:=\{\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{n}})\;|\;{\boldsymbol{n}}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}.

(2.3)

We observe that
\mathcal{N}
is a connected, compact, one-dimensional smooth submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. For all
{\boldsymbol{n}}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}
, let
T_{{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\boldsymbol{S}}
denote the tangent space of
{\boldsymbol{S}}
at
{\boldsymbol{n}}
, i.e.,
T_{{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\boldsymbol{S}}=\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;|\;{\boldsymbol{v}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{n}}=0\}
. Similarly, let
T_{\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{n}})}\mathcal{N}
denote the tangent space of
\mathcal{N}
at
\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{n}})
. The differential of
\mathcal{G}
at
{\boldsymbol{n}}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}
,
d\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{n}}):T_{{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\boldsymbol{S}}\rightarrow T_{\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{n}})}\mathcal{N}
is given by, for all
{\boldsymbol{n}}=(n_{1},n_{2})^{\mathrm{T}}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}
and all
{\boldsymbol{v}}=(v_{1},v_{2})^{\mathrm{T}}\in T_{{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\boldsymbol{S}}
,

\langle d\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{n}}),{\boldsymbol{v}}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}2Q_{c}(v_{1}{\boldsymbol{n}}+n_{1}{\boldsymbol{v}})\\
M_{c}{\boldsymbol{v}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4Q_{c}n_{1}v_{1}\\
2Q_{c}(n_{1}v_{2}+n_{2}v_{1})\\
M_{c}v_{1}\\
M_{c}v_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.4)

Lemma 2.1

(Norm)
.

We have
\|\langle d\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{n}}),{\boldsymbol{v}}\rangle\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}^{2}=(4Q_{c}^{2}+M_{c}^{2})\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}
.

Proof.

Since
v_{1}n_{1}+v_{2}n_{2}=0
, the first component of
\langle d\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{n}}),{\boldsymbol{v}}\rangle

rewrites
2Q_{c}(n_{1}v_{1}-n_{2}v_{2})
. Since
(n_{1}v_{1}-n_{2}v_{2})^{2}+(n_{1}v_{2}+n_{2}v_{1})^{2}=(n_{1}^{2}+n_{2}^{2})(v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2})=\|{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}=\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}
, the conclusion is straightforward.
∎

2.2
Minimization problem

Let
\Omega
be an open, bounded, connected, Lipschitz set (domain) in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Given some Dirichlet boundary data
\Psi^{b}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{4})
taking values in
\mathcal{N}
a.e. in
\partial\Omega
, we consider the functional space

W:=\{\Phi\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{4})\;|\;\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}})\in\mathcal{N}\;\text{a.e.\penalty\ ${\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega$}\;\wedge\;\Phi|_{\partial\Omega}=\Psi^{b}\},

(2.5)

and the (Dirichlet) energy functional

E:H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{4})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad\Phi\mapsto E(\Phi):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}{\lvert\kern-1.07639pt\lvert\nabla\Phi\rvert\kern-1.07639pt\rvert}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}\,{\rm dx}.

(2.6)

We assume that there exists
\Psi^{*}\in W
. Sufficient conditions for the Ginzburg–Landau problem are discussed in
[
8
]
based on the notion of winding numbers for
\Psi^{b}.

Our goal is to solve the following constrained minimization problem:

\min_{\Phi\in W}E(\Phi).

(2.7)

As
E
is strongly convex and
W
is a nonempty, closed (but nonconvex) set, the model problem (
2.7
) admits at least one solution in
W
.

Lemma 2.2

(Composition using
\mathcal{G}
)
.

(i)
Let
{\boldsymbol{n}}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
take values in
{\boldsymbol{S}}
a.e. in
\Omega
; then
\Psi:=\mathcal{G}\circ{\boldsymbol{n}}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{4})
and takes values in
\mathcal{N}
a.e. in
\Omega
.

(ii)
Let
\Psi\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{4})
take values in
\mathcal{N}
a.e. in
\Omega
;
then,
{\boldsymbol{n}}:=\mathcal{G}^{-1}\circ\Psi\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
and takes values in
{\boldsymbol{S}}
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Proof.

(1) Proof of (i). By construction,
\Psi
takes values in
\mathcal{N}
a.e. in
\Omega
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is bounded and
\Omega
is a bounded set,
\Psi\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{4})
. Letting
\partial_{k}
denote the partial derivative in the
k
-th Cartesian direction, for all
k\in\{1,2\}
, we observe that

\partial_{k}\Psi=\langle d\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{n}}),\partial_{k}{\boldsymbol{n}}\rangle.

Owing to Lemma
2.1
, we infer that

\|\partial_{k}\Psi\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}^{2}=(4Q_{c}^{2}+M_{c}^{2})\|\partial_{k}{\boldsymbol{n}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}\quad\forall k\in\{1,2\}.

(2.8)

Since
{\boldsymbol{n}}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
by assumption, this shows that
\Psi\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{4})
.

(2) Proof of (ii). The proof is straightforward since
{\boldsymbol{n}}:=\mathcal{G}^{-1}\circ\Psi=M_{c}^{-1}(\Psi_{3},\Psi_{4})^{\mathrm{T}}
.
∎

2.3
Energy-decreasing algorithm

Before presenting the algorithm, we recall some elementary properties of the
projection
P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}
from
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{*}:=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
onto
{\boldsymbol{S}}
, i.e.,

P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\frac{1}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}}{\boldsymbol{x}}
for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{*}
.
Set

\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 1}:=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;|\;\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\geq 1\}.

(2.9)

Lemma 2.3

(Projection onto sphere)
.

(i)

\|P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})-P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{y}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}
for all
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 1}
.

(ii)
For all
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 1}
,
P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{v}})\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
and takes values in
{\boldsymbol{S}}
; moreover,
\|\nabla P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{v}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}\leq\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}
.

Proof.

The statement (i) is just a consequence of the fact that, when acting on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 1}
,
P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}
is the projection onto
{\boldsymbol{B}}^{1}
, the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which is a closed convex set. The statement (ii) follows from the same arguments, see also

[
1
, Prop. 1]
.
∎

Given an initial guess
\Psi^{0}\in W
, our goal is to generate a sequence
(\Psi^{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
in
W
that is energy-decreasing, i.e.,

E(\Psi^{j+1})\leq E(\Psi^{j}),\quad\forall j\geq 0.

(2.10)

At iteration
j\geq 0
, given
\Psi^{j}\in W
,
the algorithm proceeds in four steps:

1.

Set
{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}:=\mathcal{G}^{-1}\circ\Psi^{j}
. Owing to Lemma
2.2
(ii),
{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
and takes values in
{\boldsymbol{S}}
a.e. in
\Omega
.

2.

Define the linear space
{\boldsymbol{K}}^{j}:=\{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})\;|\;{\boldsymbol{w}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\in T_{{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})}{\boldsymbol{S}}\;\text{a.e. ${\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega$}\}
and solve the constrained minimization problem

{\boldsymbol{w}}^{j}:=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in{\boldsymbol{K}}^{j}}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}-{\boldsymbol{w}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}.

(2.11)

This is a well-posed problem since the functional is strongly convex in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
and the minimizing set is a closed convex subset of
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Moreover, since
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\cdot}{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})=0
and
\|{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}=1
a.e. in
\Omega
, we infer that
{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}-{\boldsymbol{w}}^{j}
takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 1}
a.e. in
\Omega
(observe that
\|{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})-{\boldsymbol{w}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}=1+\|{\boldsymbol{w}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}\geq 1
).

3.

Set
{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}:=P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}\circ({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}-{\boldsymbol{w}}^{j})
. Owing to Lemma
2.3
,
{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
and takes values in
{\boldsymbol{S}}
.

4.

Set
\Psi^{j+1}:=\mathcal{G}\circ{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}
. Owing to Lemma
2.2
(i),
\Psi^{j+1}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{4})
and takes values in
\mathcal{N}
a.e. in
\Omega
. Moreover, for a.e.
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\partial\Omega
, we have
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})={\boldsymbol{0}}
so that
{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{x}})=P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{x}}))={\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})
; hence,
\Psi^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{G}^{-1}(\Psi^{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})))=\Psi^{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\Psi^{b}({\boldsymbol{x}})
. In conclusion, we have
\Psi^{j+1}\in W
as claimed.

Theorem 2.4

(Energy decay)
.

The sequence
(\Psi^{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
satisfies (
2.10
).

Proof.

Owing to (
2.8
), we infer that

\|\nabla\Psi^{m}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}^{2}=(4Q_{c}^{2}+M_{c}^{2})\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{n}}^{m}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}^{2}\quad\forall m\in\{j,j+1\}.

Moreover, owing to Lemma
2.3
(ii), we infer that

\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}\leq\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}-{\boldsymbol{w}}^{j})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}\leq\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx},

where the second bound follows from (
2.11
) and the observation that
{\boldsymbol{0}}\in{\boldsymbol{K}}^{j}
. Putting everything together gives

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\Psi^{j+1}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}

\displaystyle=(4Q_{c}^{2}+M_{c}^{2})\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(4Q_{c}^{2}+M_{c}^{2})\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}=\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\Psi^{j}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}.

This completes the proof.
∎

3
Finite element approximation

3.1
Discrete setting

We assume that
\Omega
is a polygonal domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and consider a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
that covers
\Omega
exactly. The collection of mesh vertices is partitioned as
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}:=\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\circ}\cup\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\partial}
, where
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\circ}
(resp.,
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\partial}
) denotes the collection of interior (resp., boundary) mesh vertices. The standard hat (Courant) basis functions are denoted as
\{\rho_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}
and satisfy the partition-of-unity property
\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}\rho_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}=1
over
\Omega
. We set

k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\rho_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}{\cdot}\nabla\rho_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}\,{\rm dx}\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}},

(3.1)

and observe that
k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}=k_{{\boldsymbol{b}}{\boldsymbol{a}}}
as well as

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}=0

owing to the partition-of-unity property.
In what follows, we assume that the triangulation
\mathcal{T}
is weakly acute
[
25
,
16
,
11
]
so that

k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}\leq 0\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}},\;{\boldsymbol{a}}\neq{\boldsymbol{b}}.

(3.2)

Recall that this condition is satisfied if and only if the sum of the two angles opposite any edge connecting a pair of mesh vertices is less than or equal to
\pi
. This is a reasonable condition in 2D.

To approximate the minimization problem (
2.7
), we consider the discrete minimizing set

\displaystyle W_{\mathcal{T}}:=\{\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{4})\;|\;

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(T;\mathbb{R}^{4}),\,\forall T\in\mathcal{T}\;;\;

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{a}})\in\mathcal{N},\,\forall{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\circ};\;;\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{a}})=\Psi^{b}({\boldsymbol{a}}),\,\forall{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\partial}\},

(3.3)

where
\mathbb{P}^{1}(T;\mathbb{R}^{4})
is composed of divariate,
\mathbb{R}^{4}
-valued, affine polynomials restricted to
T\in\mathcal{T}
.
We notice that every
\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}\in W_{\mathcal{T}}
can be expanded as follows:

\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{a}})\rho_{\boldsymbol{a}}.

(3.4)

An important observation is the nonconforming nature of the approximation provided by the discrete minimizing set, i.e., we have
W_{\mathcal{T}}\not\subset W
. Indeed, we have
\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\in\mathcal{N}
if
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
, but this is not guaranteed if
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega\setminus\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
Altogether, the discrete minimization problem reads as follows:

\min_{\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}\in W_{\mathcal{T}}}E(\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}),

(3.5)

with the (Dirichlet) energy functional still defined in (
2.6
).

3.2
Preliminary notation and results

Recalling (
2.2
), given any
\Psi=(\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2},\Psi_{3},\Psi_{4})^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathcal{N}
,
we write
{\boldsymbol{n}}:=M_{c}^{-1}(\Psi_{3},\Psi_{4})^{\mathrm{T}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:=Q_{c}^{-1}(\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2})^{\mathrm{T}}
,
so that
{\boldsymbol{n}}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}={\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{n}})\in{\boldsymbol{S}}
as well. Consistently with this notation,
for all
\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}\in W_{\mathcal{T}}
and all
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
, we define the two vectors

{\boldsymbol{n}}({\boldsymbol{a}})
and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{a}})
, both in
{\boldsymbol{S}}
and such that
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{a}})={\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{n}}({\boldsymbol{a}}))
.

Lemma 3.1

(Energy evaluation)
.

The following holds for all
\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}\in W_{\mathcal{T}}
:

\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Big\{\,Q_{c}^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{a}})-{\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{b}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}+M_{c}^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{n}}({\boldsymbol{a}})-{\boldsymbol{n}}({\boldsymbol{b}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}\,\Big\}.

(3.6)

Proof.

Using the expansion (
3.4
), the definition (
3.1
) of
k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}
and the properties
\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}=0
,
we infer that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{a}}){\cdot}\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{b}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{a}}){\cdot}(\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{b}})-\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{a}}))+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{b}}){\cdot}(\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{a}})-\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{b}}))

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}\|\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{a}})-\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{b}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}^{2},

and the claim follows from

\|\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{a}})-\Phi_{\mathcal{T}}({\boldsymbol{b}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}^{2}=Q_{c}^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{a}})-{\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{b}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}+M_{c}^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{n}}({\boldsymbol{a}})-{\boldsymbol{n}}({\boldsymbol{b}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}
.
∎

Our second preliminary result is related to the geometry of the admissible set
\mathcal{N}
.
We define the function
\varphi:(-1,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\varphi(r):=\frac{2r}{1-r^{2}}\quad\forall r\in(-1,1).

(3.7)

Lemma 3.2

(Geometry of
\mathcal{N}
)
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{n}}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}
and let
{\boldsymbol{t}}\in T_{{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\boldsymbol{S}}
be the unit vector
obtained from
{\boldsymbol{n}}
by a rotation
of
\frac{\pi}{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Set
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:={\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{n}})\in{\boldsymbol{S}}
and let

{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\in T_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}{\boldsymbol{S}}
be the unit vector obtained from
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
by a rotation
of
\frac{\pi}{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The following holds for all
r\in(-1,1)
:

({\boldsymbol{R}}\circ P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}})({\boldsymbol{n}}+r{\boldsymbol{t}})=P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}+\varphi(r){\boldsymbol{\tau}}),

(3.8)

with the function
\varphi:(-1,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined in (
3.7
).
In other words, we have

\big(Q_{c}P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}+\varphi(r){\boldsymbol{\tau}}),M_{c}P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{n}}+r{\boldsymbol{t}})\big)^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathcal{N}.

(3.9)

Proof.

We first observe that
{\boldsymbol{n}}+r{\boldsymbol{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}+\varphi(r){\boldsymbol{\tau}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 1}
, so that both projections
P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{n}}+r{\boldsymbol{t}})
and

P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}+\varphi(r){\boldsymbol{\tau}})
are well-defined. There are many ways of
verifying (
3.8
). We use here a representation by complex numbers.
We represent
{\boldsymbol{n}}
as
e^{\mathsf{i}\alpha}
with
\mathsf{i}^{2}=-1
,
{\boldsymbol{n}}+r{\boldsymbol{t}}
as
(1+\mathsf{i}r)e^{\mathsf{i}\alpha}
, and
P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{n}}+r{\boldsymbol{t}})
as
z_{1}:=(1+r^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+\mathsf{i}r)e^{\mathsf{i}\alpha}
. Similarly,
we represent
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
by
e^{2\mathsf{i}\alpha}
and
P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}+\varphi(r){\boldsymbol{\tau}})
by

z_{2}:=(1+\varphi(r)^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+\mathsf{i}\varphi(r))e^{2\mathsf{i}\alpha}
.
Since
|z_{1}|=|z_{2}|=1
, the identity (
3.8
) amounts to
z_{1}^{2}=z_{2}
. We infer that

\displaystyle z_{1}^{2}=z_{2}

\displaystyle\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\frac{(1+\mathsf{i}r)^{2}}{1+r^{2}}=\frac{1+\mathsf{i}\varphi(r)}{\sqrt{1+\varphi(r)^{2}}}

\displaystyle\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\frac{1-r^{2}}{1+r^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\varphi(r)^{2}}}\;\wedge\;\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}=\frac{\varphi(r)}{\sqrt{1+\varphi(r)^{2}}}

\displaystyle\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\varphi(r):=\frac{2r}{1-r^{2}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.3
Discrete energy-decreasing algorithm

Given an initial guess
\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{0}\in W_{\mathcal{T}}
, our goal is to generate a sequence
(\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
in
W_{\mathcal{T}}
that is energy-decreasing, i.e.,

E(\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j+1})\leq E(\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j}).

(3.10)

At iteration
j\geq 0
, given
\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\in W_{\mathcal{T}}
,
we write
\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})=(Q_{c}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}}),M_{c}{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}}))^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathcal{N}
with

{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})={\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}}))
, for all
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
, and we let
{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})

(resp.,
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})
) be the unit vector obtained from
{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})
(resp.,

{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})
) by a rotation of
\frac{\pi}{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Set
M_{\mathcal{T}}:=\mathbb{R}^{\#\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}

where
\#\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
denotes the cardinality of the set
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
We define the closed convex subset

K_{\mathcal{T}}:=\big\{\,r_{\mathcal{T}}:=(r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}})_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}\in M_{\mathcal{T}}\;|\;r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\in[-\gamma,\gamma]\;\forall{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\circ};\;r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}=0\;\forall{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\partial}\,\big\},

(3.11)

where
\gamma\in(0,1)
is a user-defined parameter. Then, we define the (nonconvex)
functional
F^{j}:K_{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
r_{\mathcal{T}}:=(r_{\boldsymbol{a}})_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}
,

\displaystyle F^{j}(r_{\mathcal{T}}):=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Big\{\,

\displaystyle Q_{c}^{2}\|({\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})+\varphi(r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}){\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}}))-({\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}})+\varphi(r_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}){\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}}))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+{}

\displaystyle M_{c}^{2}\|({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})+r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}}))-({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}})+r_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}}))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}\,\Big\}.

(3.12)

This functional is well-defined since
\gamma\in(0,1)
.
The algorithm proceeds in two steps:

1.

We solve the minimization problem

r_{\mathcal{T}}^{*}:=(r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}})_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}\in\argmin_{r_{\mathcal{T}}\in K_{\mathcal{T}}}F^{j}(r_{\mathcal{T}}).

(3.13)

We refer the reader to Section
4
for algorithmic aspects of solving this problem.

2.

We set, for all
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
,

{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{a}}):=P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})+r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})),\quad\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{a}}):=\big(Q_{c}{\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{a}})),M_{c}{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{a}})\big)^{\mathrm{T}}.

(3.14)

(Observe that
{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})+r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 1}
.)
By construction,
\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{a}})\in\mathcal{N}
for all
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
. Moreover, since
r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}=0
for all
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\partial}
, we infer that
{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{a}})={\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})
, so that
\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{a}})=\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})=\Psi^{b}({\boldsymbol{a}})
for all
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\partial}
. In conclusion, we have
\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j+1}:=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{a}})\rho_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\in W_{\mathcal{T}}
as claimed.

Theorem 3.3

(Energy decay)
.

Assume that the triangulation is strictly acute so that (
3.2
) holds true.
Then the sequence
(\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
satisfies (
3.10
).

Proof.

Owing to Lemma
3.2
, we infer that

{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{a}}):={\boldsymbol{R}}({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{a}}))=({\boldsymbol{R}}\circ P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}})({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})+r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}}))=P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})+\varphi(r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}){\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})).

Invoking Lemma
3.1
gives

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j+1}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Big\{\,Q_{c}^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{a}})-{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{b}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}+M_{c}^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{a}})-{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j+1}({\boldsymbol{b}})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}\,\Big\}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Big\{\,Q_{c}^{2}\|P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})+\varphi(r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}){\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}}))-P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}})+\varphi(r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}){\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}}))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+M_{c}^{2}\|P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})+r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}}))-P_{{\boldsymbol{S}}}({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}})+r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}}))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}\,\Big\}.

Invoking Lemma
2.3
and crucially that
k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}\leq 0
(see (
3.2
)),
we infer that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j+1}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Big\{\,Q_{c}^{2}\|({\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})+\varphi(r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}){\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}}))-({\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}})+\varphi(r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}){\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}}))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+M_{c}^{2}\|({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})+r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}}))-({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}})+r^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}}))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2}\,\Big\}

Recalling (
3.12
), this proves that

\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j+1}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}\leq F^{j}(r_{\mathcal{T}}^{*}).

Observing that
\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}=F^{j}(0)
,
we conclude that

\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j+1}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}^{2}\,{\rm dx}\leq F^{j}(r_{\mathcal{T}}^{*})\leq F^{j}(0)=\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\Psi_{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}}^{2}.

This proves the claim.
∎

4
Algorithmic aspects

In this section, we provide some details on the computational algorithm for solving
the minimization problem (
3.13
), followed by an
overall description of the energy-decreasing iterative procedure.

4.1
Decomposition-coordination principle using augmented Lagrangian

To tackle the minimization problem (
3.13
), we
employ a decomposition-coordination strategy based on an augmented Lagrangian framework.
The idea is to introduce the auxiliary variable
p_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}=\varphi(r_{\boldsymbol{a}})

for all
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
, which we rewrite in more compact form as

p_{\mathcal{T}}=\varphi(r_{\mathcal{T}})\in M_{\mathcal{T}}
. The idea is then to consider a minimization problem
in the two variables
r_{\mathcal{T}}:=(r_{\boldsymbol{a}})_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}\in K_{\mathcal{T}}
and

p_{\mathcal{T}}:=(p_{\boldsymbol{a}})_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}\in M_{\mathcal{T}}
(decomposition step),
and to enforce that
p_{\mathcal{T}}=\varphi(r_{\mathcal{T}})

by means of a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}=(\lambda_{\boldsymbol{a}})_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}\in M_{\mathcal{T}}

and an augmented Lagrangian (coordination step).

In the first step, recalling the cost functional
F^{j}
in (
3.12
), we set

\displaystyle F^{j}_{1}(r_{\mathcal{T}})

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}M_{c}^{2}\|({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})+r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}}))-({\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}})+r_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}}))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2},

(4.1a)

\displaystyle F^{j}_{2}(p_{\mathcal{T}})

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}Q_{c}^{2}\|({\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})+p_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}}))-({\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}})+p_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}}))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}^{2},

(4.1b)

so that
F^{j}_{1}(r_{\mathcal{T}})+F^{j}_{2}(p_{\mathcal{T}})=F^{j}(r_{\mathcal{T}})
whenever
p_{\mathcal{T}}=\varphi(r_{\mathcal{T}})
.
For later use, we record the expressions for the gradients of the above two functionals: For all
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}F^{j}_{1}({\tilde{r}}_{\mathcal{T}})

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}M_{c}^{2}\big({\tilde{r}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}-{\tilde{r}}_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}})-{\boldsymbol{t}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}})\big),

(4.2a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{p_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}F^{j}_{2}({\tilde{p}}_{\mathcal{T}})

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}Q_{c}^{2}\big({\tilde{p}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}-{\tilde{p}}_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}})-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{a}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{j}({\boldsymbol{b}})\big).

(4.2b)

Then the decomposed version of the minimization problem (
3.13
) reads as

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}(r_{\mathcal{T}},p_{\mathcal{T}})\in{K_{\mathcal{T}}}\times{M_{\mathcal{T}}}\\
p_{\mathcal{T}}=\varphi(r_{\mathcal{T}})\end{subarray}}\Big\{F^{j}_{1}(r_{\mathcal{T}})+F^{j}_{2}(p_{\mathcal{T}})\Big\}.

(4.3)

In the second step, the constraint in (
4.3
) is enforced using a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}\in M_{\mathcal{T}}
. We define the Lagrangian on
K_{\mathcal{T}}\times M_{\mathcal{T}}\times M_{\mathcal{T}}
such that

{L}^{j}(r_{\mathcal{T}},p_{\mathcal{T}},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}})=F^{j}_{1}(r_{\mathcal{T}})+F^{j}_{2}(p_{\mathcal{T}})+\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{T}}D(p_{\mathcal{T}}-\varphi(r_{\mathcal{T}})).

(4.4)

Here,
D
is a diagonal matrix with entries
(d_{\boldsymbol{a}})_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}
that is expected to scale as the entries
k_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}
of the stiffness matrix so as to balance dimensionally all the terms on the above right-hand side. In the present two-dimensional setting, a reasonable choice is
d_{\boldsymbol{a}}:=h^{d-2}=1
for all
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
, where
h
denotes the mesh size (the largest element diameter in
\mathcal{T}
).
We work with an augmented Lagrangian formulation. Given the augmentation parameter
\zeta>0
, we set

\displaystyle{L}^{j}_{\zeta}(r_{\mathcal{T}},p_{\mathcal{T}},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}})=

\displaystyle F^{j}_{1}(r_{\mathcal{T}})+F^{j}_{2}(p_{\mathcal{T}})+\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{T}}D(p_{\mathcal{T}}-\varphi(r_{\mathcal{T}}))+\frac{\zeta}{2}(p_{\mathcal{T}}-\varphi(r_{\mathcal{T}}))^{\mathrm{T}}D(p_{\mathcal{T}}-\varphi(r_{\mathcal{T}})).

(4.5)

Set
u_{\mathcal{T}}:=(r_{\mathcal{T}},p_{\mathcal{T}})\in K_{\mathcal{T}}\times M_{\mathcal{T}}.
A couple
(u_{\mathcal{T}},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}})
is a saddle-point of the augmented Lagrangian
{L}^{j}_{\zeta}
if and only if

\displaystyle{L}^{j}_{\zeta}(u_{\mathcal{T}},\theta_{\mathcal{T}})\leq{L}^{j}_{\zeta}(u_{\mathcal{T}},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}})\leq{L}^{j}_{\zeta}(v_{\mathcal{T}},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}})\quad\forall\theta_{\mathcal{T}}\in M_{\mathcal{T}},\forall v_{\mathcal{T}}\in K_{\mathcal{T}}\times M_{\mathcal{T}}.

(4.6)

Our goal is now to find a saddle-point of
L^{j}_{\zeta}
. We do so by using an alternating-direction Uzawa-like iterative method that we describe next.

4.2
Alternating-direction Uzawa-like iterations

We find a saddle-point of
L^{j}_{\zeta}
by means of the Uzawa/Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)
[
2
,
17
]
.
The iterative method is initialized by specifying
p_{\mathcal{T}}^{0}\in M_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{0}\in M_{\mathcal{T}}

(see Remark
4.1
below). Then, at iteration
n\geq 0
, assuming
p_{\mathcal{T}}^{n},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}
known from the initialization or the previous iteration, we calculate
r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1}\in K_{\mathcal{T}},
then
p_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1}\in M_{\mathcal{T}}
and finally
\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1}\in M_{\mathcal{T}}
in the following three steps:

\displaystyle r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\text{arg}\min_{r_{\mathcal{T}}\in K_{\mathcal{T}}}{L}^{j}_{\zeta}(r_{\mathcal{T}},p_{\mathcal{T}}^{n},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}),

(4.7a)

\displaystyle p_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\text{arg}\min_{p_{\mathcal{T}}\in M_{\mathcal{T}}}{L}^{j}_{\zeta}(r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1},p_{\mathcal{T}},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}),

(4.7b)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}+\rho\nabla_{\lambda}{L}^{j}_{\zeta}(r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1},p_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}),

(4.7c)

where
\rho>0
is a user-dependent parameter.

Let us briefly describe the three steps in (
4.7
), starting with the second step (
4.7b
) which simply amounts to a linear solve: Find
p_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1}\in M_{\mathcal{T}}
so that

\nabla_{p_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}{L}^{j}_{\zeta}(r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1},p_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{n})=\nabla_{p_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}F_{2}^{j}(p_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1})+d_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\lambda_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n}+\zeta d_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}(p_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n+1}-\varphi(r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n+1}))=0,\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}},

(4.8)

where the gradient of
F_{2}^{j}
is given by (
4.2b
).
The third step, (
4.7c
), in turn amounts to a simple componentwise
update on the Lagrange multiplier by setting

\lambda_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n+1}=\lambda_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}+\rho d_{\boldsymbol{a}}(p_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n+1}-\varphi(r_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n+1})),\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}.

(4.9)

Finally, the first step, (
4.7a
), is more intricate owing to the nonlinearity produced by the function
\varphi
. The idea to enhance computational efficiency is then to replace, only in the first step (
4.7a
), the nonlinear function
\varphi
by its linearization at
r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}
given by
\varphi_{\textsc{l}}^{n}(r_{\mathcal{T}}):=\varphi(r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n})+\varphi^{\prime}(r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n})(r_{\mathcal{T}}-r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n})
. With the same goal in mind, we also relax the constraint of seeking a minimizer in
K_{\mathcal{T}}
, and just seek a minimizer in
M_{\mathcal{T}}
(this is possible since the linearized function
\varphi_{\textsc{l}}
is defined over the whole real line). Our numerical experiments below indicate that the obtained minimizer anyway lies in
K_{\mathcal{T}}
(see Remark
4.2
below for further discussion). Altogether, the first step (
4.7a
) is replaced by

r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1}\leftarrow\text{arg}\min_{r_{\mathcal{T}}\in M_{\mathcal{T}}}{L}^{j,n}_{\textsc{l},\zeta}(r_{\mathcal{T}},p_{\mathcal{T}}^{n},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{n}),

(4.10)

with the linearized augmented Lagrangian

{L}^{j,n}_{\textsc{l},\zeta}(r_{\mathcal{T}},p_{\mathcal{T}},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}})=F^{j}_{1}(r_{\mathcal{T}})+F^{j}_{2}(p_{\mathcal{T}})+\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{T}}D(p_{\mathcal{T}}-\varphi_{\textsc{l}}^{n}(r_{\mathcal{T}}))+\frac{\zeta}{2}(p_{\mathcal{T}}-\varphi_{\textsc{l}}^{n}(r_{\mathcal{T}}))^{\mathrm{T}}D(p_{\mathcal{T}}-\varphi_{\textsc{l}}^{n}(r_{\mathcal{T}})).

(4.11)

A straightforward calculation shows that (
4.10
) amounts to the following linear solve: Find
r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1}\in M_{\mathcal{T}}
so that, for all
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}{L}^{j,n}_{\textsc{l},\zeta}(r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1},p_{\mathcal{T}}^{n},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{n})={}

\displaystyle\nabla_{r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}F^{j}_{1}(r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1})-d_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\lambda_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n}\varphi^{\prime}(r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n})

\displaystyle-\zeta d_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\big(p_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n}-\varphi(r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n})-\varphi^{\prime}(r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n})(r^{n+1}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}-r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n})\big)\varphi^{\prime}(r_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n})=0,

(4.12)

where the gradient of
F^{j}_{1}
is given by (
4.2a
). The linearization in (
4.10
) is consistent since
{L}^{j,n}_{\textsc{l},\zeta}\rightarrow{L}^{j}_{\zeta}
in the limit
n\rightarrow\infty.

We emphasize that the nonlinear function
\varphi
is still used in (
4.7b
) and in (
4.7c
). In particular, if the Uzawa-like iteration converges, (
4.9
) guarantees that
p_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n+1}=\varphi(r_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n+1})
, which is the critical condition required to ensure an energy decrease. We notice in passing that one consequence of the above linearization is the need to provide a value for the initial primal variable,
r_{\mathcal{T}}^{0}
.

Remark 4.1

(Initialization)
.

We initialize the Uzawa/ADMM iterations in a nested fashion by exploiting the outer energy-decreasing loop (index
j
). At iteration
j
, we set
r_{\mathcal{T}}^{0}:=r_{\mathcal{T}}^{j-1,*}
,
p_{\mathcal{T}}^{0}:=p_{\mathcal{T}}^{j-1,*}
and
\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{0}:=\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{j-1,*}
, where the right-hand sides are the values obtained at convergence of the Uzawa/ADMM algorithm at the previous iteration
(j-1)
. For
j=0
, we simply set
r_{\mathcal{T}}^{0}:=0
,
p_{\mathcal{T}}^{0}:=0
and
\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{0}:=0
.

Remark 4.2

(Domain of
r_{\mathcal{T}}
)
.

The above alternating-direction Uzawa-like iteration does not guarantee that the discrete variable
r_{\mathcal{T}}
lies within the admissible interval
(-1,1)
. It turns out to be the case in all our numerical experiments. We notice that the variable
r_{\mathcal{T}}
is related to the step-size in the energy-decreasing algorithm, so that it is expected to tend to zero if convergence does indeed occur. Therefore, possible difficulties with the domain of
r_{\mathcal{T}}
are to be expected, if any, at the first iterations. It is possible to add an energy barrier to the Lagrangian so as to prevent
r_{\mathcal{T}}
from stepping out of its admissible domain. One possibility is to consider the Lagrangian (we omit the augmentation term for brevity)

{L}^{j}(r_{\mathcal{T}},p_{\mathcal{T}},\lambda_{\mathcal{T}})=F^{j}_{1}(r_{\mathcal{T}})+F^{j}_{2}(p_{\mathcal{T}})+\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{T}}D(p_{\mathcal{T}}-\varphi(r_{\mathcal{T}}))+\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}s_{\boldsymbol{a}}\log\left\lvert 1-r_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{2}\right\rvert,

where
(s_{\boldsymbol{a}})_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}
is a collection of user-dependent weights. As the introduction of the energy barrier in the Lagrangian makes the first step, (
4.7a
), of the Uzawa-like algorithm nonlinear, the linearization of the coupling function
\varphi
is no longer relevant. The nonlinear minimization problem to evaluate
r_{\mathcal{T}}^{n+1}
can be implemented using a gradient-descent iterative scheme combined, e.g., with Armijo’s rule for line-search at each step. Details are omitted for brevity as this algorithm is classical.

4.3
Overall computational algorithm

The pseudocode in Algorithm
1

describes the computational workflow.
The method employs a nested iterative structure, where the outer loop (index
j
) performs energy-descent updates, whereas the inner loop (index
n
) solves the associated constrained minimization problem using the alternating-direction Uzawa-like iteration described in the previous section. The stopping criterion for the outer energy-decreasing loop is

-\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{\text{outer}}\leq E(\Psi^{j}_{\mathcal{T}})-E(\Psi^{j+1}_{\mathcal{T}})\leq\varepsilon_{\text{outer}}

(4.13)

with tolerance
\varepsilon_{\text{outer}}:=10^{-6}
. We allow for a slightly negative lower bound in (
4.13
) with
\varepsilon_{0}:=10^{-3}
in order to avoid pursuing further iterations when the energy essentially stagnates.
The stopping criterion for the inner Uzawa/ADMM loop is based on the satisfaction of the coupling condition
p_{\mathcal{T}}=\varphi(r_{\mathcal{T}})
and reads as follows:

\displaystyle\Bigg(\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{T}}}|\varphi(r_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n+1})-p_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n+1}|^{2}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\varepsilon_{\text{pri}}.

(4.14)

The tolerance
\varepsilon_{\text{pri}}
can vary across the numerical experiments and is specified in Section
5
.
All the linear systems arising in the Uzawa/ADMM loop are solved by a direct method using QR decomposition.

5
Numerical results

This section presents computational experiments on two test cases with known analytical solution. The goal is to illustrate the performances of the present methodology in terms of efficiency and accuracy.
In all cases, we consider the square domain
\Omega:=(0,1)^{2}
and discretize it using four successively refined, unstructured triangulations of size

(the diameter of the largest triangle in each triangulation). These triangulations are, respectively, called

,
\mathcal{T}_{3}
, and

The ferronematic parameters are set to

]

{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega

(and to compute the errors). We denote by

the converged solution on the mesh

,

.
