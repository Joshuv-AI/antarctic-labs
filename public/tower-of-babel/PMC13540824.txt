Gender-specific age inflection points for hypertension, elevated fasting blood glucose, and dyslipidemia in a Northern Chinese cohort: a comorbidity-based analysis
Authors: Li Xiaonan, Hou Zhiyong, Chen Meihan, Hou Dan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to characterize age inflection points for hypertension, elevated fasting blood glucose, and dyslipidemia in a Chinese physical examination population, analyze gender differences and the impact of comorbid conditions on these inflection points, and inform the development of gender-specific precision screening strategies. MethodsData from 263,824 health examination records collected at the General Hospital of the Northern Theater Command between January 2020 and May 2025 were included. The maximum Youden index was used to determine age inflection points for single diseases and comorbid conditions. Chi-square tests were employed to compare disease prevalence before and after the inflection points, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.ResultsSignificant gender differences were observed in inflection points for single diseases. The overall inflection point for hypertension was 40.5 years (38.5 years in males, 43.5 years in females). After the inflection point, prevalence increased from 7.7% to 23.1% in males (OR = 3.59, 95%CI: 3.46–3.72) and from 2.9% to 11.2% in females (OR = 4.24, 95%CI: 4.00-4.50). The overall inflection point for elevated fasting blood glucose was 44.5 years (43.5 years in males, 50.5 years in females). After the inflection point, prevalence increased from 4.6% to 23.6% in males (OR = 6.39, 95%CI: 6.13–6.66) and from 3.1% to 14.5% in females (OR = 5.36, 95%CI: 5.08–5.66). The overall inflection point for dyslipidemia was 40.5 years (31.5 years in males, 45.5 years in females). After the inflection point, prevalence increased from 36.7% to 46.6% in males (OR = 1.50, 95%CI: 1.45–1.56) and from 14.4% to 36.9% in females (OR = 3.48, 95%CI: 3.37–3.60). All differences were statistically significant (P < 0.001). Compared to single-disease models, comorbid conditions showed an average increase of 0.12 in area under the curve (AUC). The combination of elevated fasting blood glucose and hypertension in females had the highest AUC (0.75, 95%CI: 0.74–0.76), with sensitivity of 69.1%, specificity of 69.5%, and Youden index of 0.39. The combination of dyslipidemia and elevated fasting blood glucose achieved the highest Youden index (0.41), with significantly higher AUC in females (0.76, 95%CI: 0.75–0.77) than in males (0.72, 95%CI: 0.71–0.72). For triple comorbidity, AUC was 0.75 (95%CI: 0.73–0.76) in females versus 0.70 (95%CI: 0.69–0.71) in males. Female inflection points for comorbidities were consistently later than those in males, and AUC values were uniformly higher in females, with the most pronounced gender difference observed for the combination of dyslipidemia and hypertension (AUC = 0.73 in females vs. 0.64 in males).ConclusionThis study identified gender-specific age inflection points for hypertension, elevated fasting blood glucose, and dyslipidemia, and further characterized these thresholds for comorbid conditions. Comorbidity models showed improved predictive performance over single-disease models. A consistent gender pattern was observed: female inflection points occurred later than those in males but converged around age 50 for all comorbid conditions, while the discriminative power of age for male dyslipidemia was limited. These findings may inform the development of gender-specific, comorbidity-focused screening strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28129-y.Keywords: Inflection point age, Hypertension, Elevated fasting blood glucose, Dyslipidemia, Gender differences, Comorbidity

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) have become the leading health threat in China. According to the Report on Nutrition and Chronic Disease Status of Chinese Residents (2020) [1], NCDs account for 88.5% of total deaths, with cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and chronic respiratory diseases contributing to 80.7% of these fatalities. These conditions are closely associated with three major metabolic disorders: hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Nationally, the prevalence of hypertension among adults aged ≥ 18 years is 27.5%, while diabetes and hypercholesterolemia affect 11.9% and 8.2% of this population, respectively [1]. The burden is particularly pronounced in middle-aged and older adults: dyslipidemia affects 52.4% of individuals aged 45–60 years undergoing health examinations [2], and the 10-year cardiovascular disease risk after age 45 is 1.8-fold higher than in those aged 35–44 years (95%CI: 1.5–2.1) [3].

Of greater concern, the co-existence of multiple metabolic disorders—defined as the presence of two or more conditions—has emerged as a major global public health challenge [4, 5]. Comorbidity not only complicates disease management but also exerts synergistic and amplified effects on target organ damage. Specifically, the coexistence of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease increases all-cause mortality risk by 2.56-fold [6], while concurrent hypertension and diabetes elevate the risk of microvascular complications by 3.8-fold. The clustering of all three conditions—hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia—further exacerbates cardiovascular mortality. The age-dependent nature of this comorbidity burden is particularly noteworthy. Among individuals aged ≥ 55 years, the prevalence of coexisting hypertension and diabetes reaches 32.7%, representing a 130% increase compared to those aged 45–54 years [7].

Age is widely recognized as the strongest predictor of chronic diseases. However, most previous studies have treated age as a continuous variable or stratified by fixed quintiles, an approach that fails to capture the “turning points” where disease risk accelerates sharply. Identifying such age-related turning points is crucial for developing precise, time-specific screening strategies. Nevertheless, existing evidence remains limited. Most studies focus on turning points for individual diseases, while the characteristics, gender differences, and predictive value of comorbidity-specific turning points have not been systematically described. In fact, even within the broader literature on multimorbidity, precise, data-driven turning point thresholds for specific disease combinations remain largely in the exploratory stage. For example, a recent study by Gilson et al. employed a data-driven approach to identify turning points in the life course for multimorbidity burden, with over half of these turning points occurring between the ages of 20 and 40—a demographic traditionally considered homogeneous in clinical settings [8]. This study provides valuable insights into the dynamic changes of multimorbidity throughout the life cycle, yet its focus remains on identifying overall turning points rather than extending to gender-specific analysis of metabolic comorbidity combinations or determining optimal screening cutoff values using ROC methods. These knowledge gaps significantly hinder the development of precise early-intervention strategies in clinical practice.

To address this gap, the present study leverages a large-scale health examination cohort to precisely delineate gender-specific inflection points for hypertension, elevated fasting blood glucose, and dyslipidemia—both as single diseases and as comorbid conditions. By quantifying the risk differentials before and after these inflection points, we aim to provide evidence-based guidance for age- and gender-stratified precision screening strategies.

Method

Data collection

This study included 263,824 records from annual health examinations conducted at the Health Management Center of the General Hospital of the Northern Theater Command between January 1, 2020 and May 1, 2025. Individuals who attended multiple annual examinations during this period contributed multiple records, each reflecting a distinct yearly check-up rather than an ad-hoc visit. Extracted data encompassed demographic characteristics (sex, age) and clinical measurements, including blood pressure, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C).Participants of all ages, including those under 18 and over 18 years old, were included in the primary analysis to comprehensively capture age-related disease turning points in the real-world study cohort. (Ethics approval number: Y[2025]396).

Variable definitions

All blood samples were collected via venipuncture after an overnight fast of at least 8 h.

Hypertension

Hypertension was diagnosed according to the 2024 Revised Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension [9]. Blood pressure was measured using a validated upper-arm electronic sphygmomanometer with participants in a resting state, wearing light clothing, and with the upper arm exposed. The measurement protocol consisted of two consecutive readings taken at 1-minute intervals. If the difference between the two readings for systolic blood pressure (SBP) or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) exceeded 5 mmHg, a third measurement was performed. The final blood pressure value was calculated as the mean of all measurements. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, or a prior diagnosis of hypertension.While clinical guidelines require hypertension diagnosis based on sustained blood pressure elevation across multiple visits, this study used the average of two or three readings from a single health examination visit as a pragmatic proxy, which is a recognized approach in large-scale epidemiological studies using health check-up data.

Elevated fasting blood glucose

Elevated fasting blood glucose was determined based on the 2024 Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Diabetes [10]. Plasma glucose was measured using the glucose oxidase method. Elevated fasting blood glucose was defined as fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 6.1 mmol/L.

Dyslipidemia

Dyslipidemia was assessed according to the 2023 Chinese Guidelines for Lipid Management [11]. Lipid profiles included total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). Dyslipidemia was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: TC ≥ 6.2 mmol/L, TG ≥ 2.3 mmol/L, LDL-C ≥ 4.1 mmol/L, or HDL-C < 1.0 mmol/L.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For descriptive statistics, continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages [n (%)].

To maximize statistical power and minimize selection bias due to case deletion, missing values for all independent variables were imputed using predictive mean matching. The missing rate for all analytical variables was below 10%, with no missing values for age or gender (Table S2). The imputation model included all analyzed variables (sex, age, SBP, DBP, FPG, TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C) as well as body mass index (BMI). Given the cross-sectional design of this study, comorbidity was defined as the coexistence of two or more conditions meeting the diagnostic criteria within the same health examination cycle. Sensitivity analyses were performed using multivariable logistic regression models to reassess the risk differences before and after the inflection points for each disease and comorbidity after adjusting for sex and BMI (i.e., adjusted odds ratios), with results presented in the Supplementary materials.

To further evaluate the robustness of the primary findings, internal validation was conducted using the bootstrap method with 1,000 resamples (Table S3). For single diseases and comorbidity, ROC curves were constructed with age as the continuous predictor variable and the presence of hypertension, elevated fasting blood glucose, or dyslipidemia as the outcome variable. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify the predictive value of age for each disease. The optimal inflection point for each condition was determined as the age corresponding to the maximum Youden index, representing an optimal threshold for discriminating between those with and without the condition based on age alone. Chi-square (χ²) tests were used to compare disease prevalence before and after the identified prevalence points. It should be clarified that since the AUC value is partially influenced by the cohort’s age distribution and disease prevalence, this study constitutes a threshold identification exercise rather than an assessment of diagnostic accuracy. The use of age as the sole predictor is based on its universality and non-invasiveness to determine the optimal risk stratification threshold.

For comorbidity analyses, we further constructed ROC curves for pairwise combinations of the three diseases and for the coexistence of all three conditions, defined as meeting the diagnostic criteria for two or three diseases simultaneously. Inflection points for each comorbidity state were identified, and the predictive performance of age for different disease combinations was compared with that for single diseases. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Study population

A total of 263,824 health examination records were included in the study, comprising 134,639 females and 129,185 males, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 0.96:1. Participants ranged in age from 4 to 101 years, with a mean age of 42.9 ± 14.7 years. The age distribution was broadly comparable between sexes, with the peak frequency of examinations occurring at age 34 years (Fig. 1). The study population exhibited a significant burden of metabolic risk factors, with hypertension, elevated fasting blood glucose, and dyslipidemia present in 11.4%, 9.6%, and 33.2% of participants, respectively. All single and comorbid cardiometabolic conditions, along with mean BMI, were significantly more prevalent in males than in females (all P < 0.001) (Table 1 ).

ROC curves for predicting the occurrence of hypertension, hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia by age

For hypertension (overall prevalence 11.4%; 15.6% in males, 6.7% in females), the overall inflection point was 40.5 years, with distinct gender-specific thresholds (38.5 years in males vs. 43.5 years in females). Age demonstrated moderate predictive performance for hypertension (AUC: 0.671 overall), with better discrimination in females (AUC = 0.708) than in males (AUC = 0.668).

To assess the robustness of these findings, we performed sensitivity analyses using multivariable logistic regression adjusting for BMI (Table S1). All associations remained highly significant after adjustment (all P < 0.001). The adjusted ORs were 3.52 (95%CI: 3.42–3.62) for hypertension, 6.08 (95%CI: 5.90–6.27) for elevated fasting blood glucose, and 1.98 (95%CI: 1.95–2.02) for dyslipidemia, consistent with the unadjusted estimates. These results confirm that the observed age inflection points are independent of BMI.

Age inflection points for comorbidities

Compared with single-disease models, age demonstrated superior predictive performance for comorbid conditions; however, the extent of this improvement was not uniform. Gains in AUC were most pronounced when the baseline single-disease predictive performance was low—most notably in males, where the AUC for dyslipidemia rose from 0.54 to the 0.64–0.72 range when combined with other conditions. In contrast, for single-disease indicators that already possessed strong predictive power (e.g., elevated fasting blood glucose in females, AUC = 0.77), the addition of comorbid conditions yielded no further improvement in AUC. Although relative gains were larger in certain male subgroups, the absolute AUC values for comorbidities remained consistently higher in females than in males. (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Discussion

The high prevalence of comorbid chronic metabolic diseases—including hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia—has become a major global public health challenge. The combined burden of these conditions substantially exceeds that of any single disease, leading to significantly increased risks of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality [12]. Using data from 263,824 health examination records, the present study systematically constructed a gender-comorbidity dual-dimension inflection point atlas for hypertension, elevated fasting blood glucose, and dyslipidemia. We found that the predictive value of age inflection points for comorbid conditions was significantly superior to that for single diseases, with a marked gender-specific pattern: the predictive performance of age for comorbidities was consistently higher in females than in males. These findings provide evidence-based support for tiered prevention and management of metabolic diseases.

The overall inflection point for hypertension was identified at 40.5 years, with marked gender disparity (38.5 years in males vs. 43.5 years in females). These inflection ages are consistent with the epidemiological trend of increasingly younger onset of hypertension documented in Chinese populations over the past two decades, a trend corroborated by the 2024 Chinese hypertension guidelines [9]. The steep escalation in risk following the inflection points underscores their clinical significance: the odds of hypertension increased 3.6-fold in males after 38.5 years and 4.2-fold in females after 43.5 years (both P < 0.001). These findings pinpoint optimal time windows for gender-specific primary prevention, such as initiating intensified low-sodium dietary interventions and ambulatory blood pressure monitoring at age 38 in men and 43 in women.

The difference in turning point ages between men and women may be related to estrogen’s role in delaying the onset of hypertension by regulating the sympathetic nervous system, the RAAS, and endothelial function [13]. A survey revealed that the prevalence of hypertension is 36.8% in men and 26.3% in women, with the prevalence among young adults aged 25–34 doubling over the past decade, particularly in North China and Northeast China [14]. These data suggest that although the widespread high salt intake and salt-sensitive genetic background in northern populations may collectively contribute to an earlier overall turning point, differences in hormonal profiles between genders may determine the distinct temporal trajectories of accelerated risk between men and women. The clinical significance of these inflection points is underscored by the sharp risk escalation thereafter: the odds of hypertension increased 3.6-fold in males after 38.5 years and 4.2-fold in females after 43.5 years (both P < 0.001). These findings pinpoint optimal time windows for gender-specific primary prevention, such as intensified low-sodium dietary interventions and ambulatory blood pressure monitoring starting at age 38 in men and 43 in women.

The inflection points for elevated fasting blood glucose and dyslipidemia exhibited even more pronounced gender differences, highlighting the critical role of sex hormones in metabolic regulation. In this study, the inflection age for elevated fasting blood glucose was 43.5 years in males and 50.5 years in females, a gender gap of 7 years. This seven-year gender disparity coincides precisely with the menopausal transition period. In the “National Women’s Health Study” (SWAN)—a longitudinal cohort study of middle-aged women—the menopausal transition was independently associated with progressive deterioration of fasting blood glucose and insulin resistance, and this association remained significant after adjustment for age and BMI [15]. The prevalence of metabolic syndrome increased significantly from premenopausal to postmenopausal stages in the SWAN study [16], primarily driven by impaired glucose metabolism. These prospective data indicate that it is the menopausal transition itself, rather than mere aging, that accelerates the deterioration of glucose homeostasis, providing a mechanistic framework for the observed inflection point in elevated female fasting blood glucose levels at 50.5 years of age. The gender disparity in dyslipidemia was even more pronounced, with inflection ages of 31.5 years in males and 45.5 years in females—a difference of 14 years. This result aligns with epidemiological patterns. A meta-analysis [17] revealed that the prevalence rates among adult males and females were 47.3% vs. 38.8%. Another age-and gender-stratified survey indicated that the prevalence of dyslipidemia surged sharply to a peak of 48.2% in the 30–39 age group among males, whereas the prevalence among females increased gradually with age, reaching its peak after 60 years—consistent with our finding of a turning point at age 31.5 in males [18]. Meanwhile, high endogenous testosterone levels are associated with a more favorable lipid profile in males, while obesity-induced secondary gonadal dysfunction reduces testosterone levels and worsens lipid profiles [19]. These findings suggest that the early turning point in males is primarily driven by lifestyle-related factors and obesity, with limited independent predictive power of age in this study (AUC = 0.542), further supporting this interpretation.

These gender-specific metabolic trajectories are highly consistent with epidemiological patterns of cardiovascular outcomes. Studies have confirmed that the risk of acute myocardial infarction in males under 45 years is significantly higher than in females [20], and the association between hypercholesterolemia and myocardial infarction is substantially stronger in males than in females [21]. Conversely, females are more susceptible to the effects of hypertension, diabetes, and pregnancy-related complications [22]. Collectively, this evidence suggests that early prevention strategies should be tailored by gender: males should initiate lipid monitoring and lifestyle interventions after age 30 (i.e., before the dyslipidemia inflection point), while the intervention window for females can be deferred to approximately age 45. However, for high-risk females with hypertension, diabetes, or a history of pregnancy-related complications, risk assessment should be initiated earlier [22].

Building upon the validation of inflection points for individual diseases, this study further constructed comorbidity models by analyzing pairwise and triple combinations of the three conditions. We confirmed that age demonstrates significantly superior predictive performance for metabolic comorbidities compared to single diseases, with this advantage being more pronounced in females—offering a novel perspective for comorbidity prevention and management. The AUC values for comorbid conditions showed an average increase of 0.12 over single diseases, with the highest AUC observed for the combination of elevated fasting blood glucose and hypertension in females (AUC = 0.75, Youden index = 0.39), indicating optimal discriminative ability for comorbidity risk. This superiority can be attributed to the synergistic amplification effects of metabolic dysregulation. When hypertension and hyperglycemia coexist, a positive feedback loop may be established through overactivation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and insulin resistance [23]. Hypertension increases glomerular filtration pressure, reducing renal insulin clearance, while hyperinsulinemia directly induces vascular smooth muscle cell hypertrophy, intracellular calcium overload, and elevated oxidative stress—promoting medial thickening and luminal narrowing, thereby further exacerbating blood pressure elevation [24]. Additionally, the interplay between RAAS activation and insulin resistance may accelerate vascular endothelial damage and target organ injury (including heart, brain, and kidneys) through mechanisms such as mTOR signaling pathway dysregulation and endothelial dysfunction [23]. The co-occurrence of dyslipidemia and hyperglycemia deteriorates β-cell function through lipotoxic mechanisms. Accumulation of free fatty acids in the pancreas activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to β-cell apoptosis and increasing comorbidity risk by 37% compared to single diseases [25]. Notably, females exhibited higher AUC values than males across all comorbidity combinations, with the underlying mechanism potentially related to a unique metabolic compensatory pattern in females. Premenopausal estrogen may delay comorbidity onset by activating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ), maintaining adipocyte differentiation and lipid storage balance. However, this compensatory mechanism may mask underlying metabolic damage, allowing pathological factors to accumulate over time [26]. Following menopause around age 50, the loss of estrogen’s protective effects is associated with a concentrated manifestation of accumulated metabolic damage, which is associated with a sharp increase in comorbidity risk over a short period. Specifically, the risk of triple comorbidity in females at 51.5 years was 4.7-fold higher than at 50.5 years. This steep risk trajectory enhances the predictive signal of age for comorbidities, reflected in higher Youden indices.

In terms of screening implications, the inflection points identified in this study point toward a gender-specific ‘three-tiered screening framework,’ which is proposed here as a direction for further exploration. For males, 38–43 years represents a high-yield window for detecting hypertension and elevated fasting blood glucose: the inflection point for hypertension was 38.5 years (AUC = 0.668, OR = 3.59), and that for elevated fasting blood glucose was 43.5 years (AUC = 0.763, OR = 6.39). Both conditions demonstrated robust age-dependent escalation in prevalence, providing quantitative thresholds for initiating blood pressure and glucose monitoring in these age bands. The earliest inflection point, observed for dyslipidemia at 31.5 years, must be interpreted with caution: the model’s discriminative ability was near chance (AUC = 0.542, Youden index = 0.100), and the OR after adjusting for BMI dropped sharply from 3.48 to 3.59 to 1.98, indicating substantial residual confounding. This inflection point is therefore regarded as a preliminary observation and should not be used as a standalone basis for clinical action. For females, inflection points for all single diseases and comorbid conditions clustered within the 45–55 year range (hypertension 43.5 years, AUC = 0.708, OR = 4.24; dyslipidemia 45.5 years, AUC = 0.670, OR = 3.48; elevated fasting blood glucose 50.5 years, AUC = 0.768, OR = 5.36), with AUC values consistently outperforming those in males. This clustering establishes the menopausal transition as a critical window for comprehensive metabolic assessment, and the sex-specific thresholds reported here may directly inform the timing of such screening. For individuals at elevated risk of comorbidity, the optimal predictive performance was observed at 42.5 years in males and 50.5 years in females, with comorbidity models consistently outperforming single-disease models (AUC improvement averaging 0.12). These ages represent empirically derived time points for integrated risk assessment; their clinical utility awaits further validation in prospective studies.

The findings should be interpreted considering several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design cannot infer causality or the temporal order of disease onset; the identified inflection points represent cross-sectional thresholds, and repeated records from individuals with multiple visits may introduce data dependency. Their prospective predictive value requires cohort validation. Second, although adjusted for sex and BMI, unmeasured lifestyle confounders (e.g., smoking, diet, physical activity, may lead to slight overestimation of the age effect. Third, the inflection points identified in this study need to be further validated in broader geographic and multi-center populations in the future. In addition, as part of the data were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022), the potential influence of this exceptional period may need to be considered when interpreting the findings.

In summary, as a large-sample cross-sectional analysis based on real-world health examination data, this study systematically delineates, for the first time, a gender-specific age inflection point atlas for metabolic comorbidities. Despite the aforementioned limitations, these findings provide critical initial hypotheses and quantitative benchmarks for subsequent prospective validation, multifactorial adjustment, and multicenter comparisons.

Conclusion

In this large health-examination cohort from northern China, we identified gender-specific age inflection points for hypertension, elevated fasting blood glucose, and dyslipidemia—alone and in comorbidity—and showed that disease prevalence was substantially higher after these thresholds, with notable gender-related differences in the age profiles. The three-tiered prevention and screening model proposed based on these inflection point characteristics—with targeted screening for hypertension and elevated fasting blood glucose at 38–43 years in males, comprehensive metabolic assessment during the menopausal transition (45–55 years) in females, and integrated comorbidity screening at approximately 42.5 years for males and 50.5 years for females—not only supports the strategic shift from treatment-centered to prevention-centered care under the Healthy China 2030 initiative, but also offers a referable gender-specific, threshold-based screening paradigm for other low- and middle-income countries facing similar challenges of population aging.
