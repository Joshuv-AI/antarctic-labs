Adherence to 14-day primaquine treatment for radical cure of Plasmodium vivax and mixed malaria patients treated at health centers in Yilmana Densa district, Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Eshetie Alelign Tsega, Ayenew Molla Lakew, Habtamu Alebachew Tegegne, Gizachew Abeza Getahun, Ejigu Gebeye Zeleke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimaquine is the right drug for the radical cure of malaria. The Ethiopian National malaria guideline recommends a 14-day course of primaquine for vivax and mixed malaria patients. Adherence to the full course of primaquine is crucial to achieve a radical cure. However, the recurrence of malaria is still high. Therefore, this study aimed to assess adherence status and identify factors affecting adherence to primaquine treatment among malaria patients treated at health centers in Yilmana Densa district, Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Yilmana Densa district from December 15, 2024 to January 30, 2025. A total of 594 malaria patients were selected using systematic random sampling. Data were collected through house-to-house visits by using an interviewer-administered questionnaire and observation. Self-report, followed by a pill count, was used to ascertain adherence status. The data were entered with EpiData 4.7 and analyzed using Stata version 17. Binary logistic regression was used. Variables with a P-value < 0.25 in bivariable analysis were entered into multivariable analysis. Variables with a P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.ResultsAmong the 594 study participants, 545 (91.8%) were interviewed. The overall adherence to primaquine treatment was 282 (51.7%), with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 47.4% to 56.0%. Female sex (AOR = 1.64, 95%CI = 1.11–2.43), having knowledge that malaria can relapse (AOR = 1.69, 95%CI = 1.11–2.58), and a prior history of malaria infection (AOR = 1.67, 95%CI = 1.14–2.44) were positively associated with probably adherent. Whereas experiencing side effects was negatively associated (AOR = 0.49, 95%CI = 0.33–0.72).ConclusionsThe primaquine treatment adherence status is low in the Yilmana Densa district. Being female, having knowledge that malaria can relapse, and having a prior malaria history were positively associated with adherence. Conversely, side effect has negatively associated. Hence, health education focusing on the risk of malaria relapse shall be promoted, giving due attention to males, and counselling about the expected side effects proactively.

Background

Malaria is a protozoan infectious disease caused by Plasmodium parasites [1, 2]. It remains a major global public health issue, including in Ethiopia [3]. Plasmodium vivax (PV) malaria is caused by PV, while the coexistence of PV and Plasmodium falciparum is referred to as mixed malaria. Recurrent malaria refers to the reappearance of clinical signs and symptoms of malaria after a variable symptom-free period following PV or mixed malaria infection [4]. This remains one of the key challenges in malaria elimination programs because PV can cause recurrence through its dormant liver stage, known as the hypnozoite. [5, 6] Approximately 40% of the world’s population is at risk of PV malaria, with an estimated five to fifteen million cases reported annually [7, 8]. Plasmodium vivax is also of major public health importance in Ethiopia [9]. In Ethiopia, 30–35% malaria cases are caused by PV [3], and about 61.8% of vivax malaria patients experience relapse episodes [10].

Primaquine (PQ) is the right drug for the radical cure of malaria [11–13]. Radical cure of PV and mixed malaria is defined as completion of the full 14-day course of PQ treatment according to the prescribed dose and schedule [3]. World Health Organization (WHO) currently recommends three PQ dosing regimens for radical cure: (i) 0.25 mg for each kilogram per day for 14 days (the standard), (ii) 0.5 mg for each kilogram per day for 14 days (high standard), and (iii) 0.75 mg for each kilogram once per week for eight weeks [14]. Ethiopia currently uses the first WHO-recommended regimen, which consists of 0.25 mg for each kilogram per day for 14 days. According to the current Ethiopian national malaria treatment guideline, chloroquine (CQ) is given for three days together with PQ 0.25 mg per kilogram per day for 14 days for PV infection, whereas Artemether-Lumefantrine (AL) is given for three days plus PQ 0.25 mg per kilogram per day for 14 days for mixed infections. The purpose of the 14-day PQ regimen is to eliminate the dormant liver stage of the parasite and achieve a radical cure [3].

Ethiopia has planned to eliminate malaria by 2030 [6]. However, since 2020, the incidence of malaria has shown an increasing trend [15], with recurrent malaria contributing substantially to this increase [16]. Adherence to PQ treatment is essential for the radical cure of PV malaria and plays a critical role in malaria elimination efforts [17]. Evidence from the literature indicates that poor adherence to PQ treatment is a significant barrier to effective radical cure. Malaria recurrence is reported to be 2.3 times higher among patients with poor adherence compared with those who complete the treatment regimen. In addition, relapse of vivax malaria can be reduced by 90% when PQ radical cure treatment is fully implemented [18]. A systematic review conducted across 18 countries on adherence to PQ treatment reported that only 59.7% studies demonstrated complete adherence, while the remaining studies reported poor adherence [8]. The review identified several factors associated with PQ treatment adherence, including older age, secondary level education [12], knowledge about malaria transmission [19, 20], absence of malaria signs and symptoms after treatment initiation [21, 22], forgetfulness, alcohol consumption [23, 24], duration of PQ treatment [22, 25, 26], side effects [24, 26, 27], presence of comorbidity [28], type of instruction provided regarding PQ dosage and schedule (verbal or written) [24], types of malaria species [22], and prior history of malaria [29].

Adherence to PQ treatment provides benefits at the individual, community, and programmatic levels. At the individual level, adherence ensures radical cure, optimal therapeutic response, prevention of disease progression to severe forms, and reduction of the risk of drug resistance. At the community level, good adherence reduces the infectious reservoir, prevents reinfection, and limits the emergence and spread of drug resistance [30]. At the programmatic level, improved adherence decreases the malaria burden by interrupting ongoing community transmission and preventing relapse, thereby reducing health service demand, lowering the costs associated with repeated malaria episodes, and improving economic productivity [31].

However, studies conducted in Ethiopia on anti-malaria treatment have mainly focused on drug safety and effectiveness, while evidence regarding treatment adherence, particularly adherence to PQ treatment, remains limited [8]. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the status of PQ treatment adherence and to identify associated factors among patients with PV and mixed malaria in Yilmana Densa district, Ethiopia. The findings of this study will help inform the development and implementation of tailored interventions aimed at improving PQ treatment adherence and supporting the national goal of malaria elimination strategy.

Methods

Study design and period

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Yilmana Densa district, Ethiopia, from December 15, 2024 to January 30, 2025.

Study area

The study was conducted in Yilmana Densa district, Northwest Ethiopia. Yilmana Densa is one of the malaria-endemic districts in the North Gojjam Zone of the Amhara Region, Ethiopia. It is located 43 km southwest of Bahir Dar, the capital city of the Amhara Region. In 2024, the district had a catchment population of 304,934 (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Map of study area of primaquine treatment adherence among Plasmodium vivax and mixed malaria patients treated at health centers in Yilmana Densa district, Ethiopia, from December 15, 2024, to January 30, 2025. The district has 11 health centers (HCs) and one primary hospital. The climatic conditions of the district are broadly categorized into three agroecological zones: Kolla (24%), Woyna Dega (64%), and Dega (12%). The annual temperature ranges from 8.8 C0 to 25.2 C0, with an average annual rainfall of 1270 mm.

Source populations

The source population comprised all patients with PV and mixed malaria infections who received a 14-day course of PQ treatment at HCs in Yilmana Densa district.

Study populations

The study population was all patients with PV and mixed malaria who received a 14-day course of PQ treatment at the five selected HCS.

Inclusion criteria

All patients with PV and mixed malaria infections who received a 14-day course of PQ treatment at the five selected HCs between December 15, 2024 and January 30, 2025, were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with PV and mixed malaria who received a 14-day course of PQ treatment but were transferred out of the district or referred to a higher-level health facility from the five selected HCs between December 15, 2024, and January 30, 2025, were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size was calculated using the single-population proportion formula, based on an estimated adherence proportion of 62.4% from a previous study conducted in Indonesia [19]. A 95%CI and 5% marginal error were used. After adding a 10% non-response rate and a 1.5 design effect, the final sample size was calculated to be 594. Among the eleven HCs in the district, five (45%) were selected randomly. The total sample size was then allocated proportionally to each selected HCs based on the number of PV and mixed malaria cases reported during the same season in the previous year (2023) (Fig. 2). A tracking tool (register) containing patient addresses and the start and end dates of PQ treatment was prepared. All PV and mixed malaria patients who received the 14-day PQ treatment were registered by pharmacy professionals working at the dispensing unit. Finally, study participants were selected using a systematic random sampling technique. The list of selected study units was distributed to trained health extension workers (WEWs), who are community-based health workers providing primary health care services, according to their respective kebeles (the lowest administrative unit in Ethiopia).Fig. 2Diagram showing the allocation of samples for the study of primaquine treatment adherence among Plasmodium vivax and mixed malaria patients treated at health centers in Yilmana Densa district, Ethiopia, from December 15, 2024, to January 30, 2025

Study variables

Dependent variable Primaquine treatment adherence status (probably adherent and probably non-adherent).

Independent variables

Socio-demographic factors: age, sex, marital status, religion, educational level, and residency.

Knowledge and behavioral-related factors: knowledge on malaria (heard about malaria before), its transmission, prevention method, relapse, radical cure, relapse prevention, the use of 14-day PQ treatment, alcohol use, and use of insecticide-treated net (ITN).

Socio-economic factors: occupation, possession of community-based health insurance (CBHI).

Drug-related and other factors: a prior history of malaria, side effects, species type, instruction type provided about the dosage and schedule, and comorbidity.

Adherence definition

Probably adherent: If the patient reported that he/she has taken all doses (14 days) of PQ treatment per prescription and had no remaining pill(s), if he/she showed the blister pack, or if the patient reported that he/she has taken all doses (14 days) of PQ but failed to show the blister pack.

Probably non-adherent: If the patient reported that they have not taken the full course (14 days) of PQ treatment per prescription but failed to show remaining pill(s), or those reported that they have taken all doses but displayed leftover pill(s) [8, 21, 24].

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire adapted from the literature [21], and observations were also used to count remaining pill(s). The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, translated into Amharic, and then back-translated into English to check its consistency. Trained HEWs received the list of participants (tracking tool) every week, preferably every Friday, from their respective HCs. Health extension workers collected the data through house-to-house visits using the already-filled tracking tool address. Self-report followed by pill count was used to ascertain the outcome variable. Each participant was visited and interviewed after 14 days of PQ treatment initiation. A second visit was conducted for participants who were not found during the first visit.

Data quality assurance

One-day on-site training was provided to pharmacy professionals working at the dispensing units, data collectors, and supervisors on data collection procedures and data confidentiality. The questionnaire was pre-tested on 30 participants (5% of the total sample) in a non-selected catchment area. Data collectors were closely supervised throughout the data collection process by supervisors.

Data processing and analysis

After checking the data for completeness and consistency, the data were coded and entered into EpiData 4.7 and analyzed using Stata version 17. Descriptive analysis was performed to compute frequencies and percentages for variables. The distribution of the continuous variables was assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and the median with interquartile range was reported. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with an average VIF of 2.14. The Phi coefficient (φ) was used to assess associations among dichotomous predictors. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between the dependent and independent variables. Variables with a P-value < 0.25 in bivariable analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model analysis to identify factors associated with PQ treatment adherence. Variables with a P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (P-value = 0.1465). The strength of associations between the outcome and independent variables was expressed using odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI.

Primaquine treatment adherence status

Among the 545 respondents, 282 (51.7%) with a 95%CI of 47.4%, 56.0% were classified as probably adherent. Of the total participants, 341 (62.6%) self-reported that they had taken all their pills. Among these self-reported participants, 110 (32.3%) were able to show their blister packs, of which 51(46.4%) were empty. Factors associated with primaquine treatment adherence

The multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that female sex, knowing that malaria can relapse, and having a history of malaria were significantly positively associated with being probably adherent. Female participants had 64% higher odds of being adherent compared with males (AOR = 1.64, 95%CI = 1.11–2.43). Participants who knew malaria can relapse were 1.69 times more likely to be adherent than those who did not have this knowledge (AOR = 1.69, 95%CI = 1.11–2.58). Similarly, participants with a prior history of malaria were 1.67 times more likely to be adherent compared with those without such a history (AOR = 1.67, 95%CI = 1.14–2.44). In contrast, experiencing side effects was negatively associated with probably adherent. Participants who experienced side effects had 51% lower odds of being adherent compared with those who did not (AOR = 0.49, 95%CI = 0.33–0.72) (Table 4). Discussion

Malaria is a significant public health problem worldwide, including in Ethiopia. Achieving radical cure of vivax malaria is crucial for malaria control and elimination and requires a 14-day course of PQ treatment[6]. This study aimed to determine adherence to PQ treatment and identify factors affecting its adherence.

This study found that 51.7% of malaria patients were probably adherent to 14-days of PQ treatment regimen. This indicates that nearly half of the patients did not complete the full course of PQ medication and, therefore, may not have achieved a radical cure. The finding was in line with studies conducted in Myanmar, 55% [29], and Ghana, 50.1%[30]. It was also consistent with the Worldwide Antimalarial Resistance Network systematic review meta-analysis, which reported that 40.3% patients had poor adherence (< 50%) and 50.9% had complete adherence to PQ treatment (> 50%) [8].

Our finding was lower compared with studies conducted in other study settings, including Gambia, where (67.9%) adherence was reported using direct observation therapy [32]; Cambodia, where (78%) adherence was reported based on self-reported data [18]; Brazil (93.3%) [33]; Indonesia (62.4%), which used a mixed methods approach [19]; and Thailand (79.86%). The PQ adherence status identified in our study was also lower than the adherence standard required by the WHO and Ethiopian Ministry of Health malaria treatment guideline, which recommended completing the standard PQ dose (0.25 mg/kg per day for 14 days) to achieve radical cure [3, 7]. Overall, the adherence status observed in this study was lower than that reported in many previous studies.

This low level of adherence can be explained by several factors. First, a study conducted in Cambodia used self-report as the sole method for an outcome ascertainment mechanism [18], whereas our study employed both self-report and pill count. Relying on self-report alone may lead to overestimation of adherence level. Second, the studies conducted in Myanmar-Thailand and Brazil [29, 33] used a follow-up design, where patients had scheduled appointments after initiating the treatment. Such follow-up mechanisms may have encouraged patients or caregivers to complete the full course of treatment and adhere more strictly. Third, the study conducted in Brazil implemented short message service reminders through mobile health interventions [33], which helped patients remember to take their PQ medication on time. Lastly, since we are comparing our findings with studies conducted outside Ethiopia and even outside Africa, differences in population characteristics and contexts may also account for the observed variation in adherence levels.

This low level of adherence implies that nearly half of the malaria patients did not complete the full course of PQ treatment. Consequently, these patients may fail to achieve a radical cure, making malaria relapse more likely. Moreover, non-adherence may sustain malaria transmission within the community, as non-adherent individuals may serve as parasite reservoirs. In the long term, this poses an additional challenge of potential drug resistance. As a result, the overall burden of malaria may persist, ultimately undermining malaria elimination programs.

Several reasons were reported for the discontinuation of PQ treatment. The most frequently reported reason was feeling well or disappearance of symptoms (35.4%). This has also been commonly reported in previous studies as a major reason for not completing the full treatment course [21, 34]. The chief complaint that prompts patients to seek treatment often subsides within three to four days of initiation of therapy, because of the concomitant use of other anti-malaria drugs prescribed alongside the 14-day PQ regimen. As a result, patients may pay less attention to continuing PQ and prematurely discontinue it. This finding suggests inadequate awareness regarding the purpose of radical cure and the misconception that treatment is no longer necessary once symptoms resolve. Therefore, adequate counselling at the initiation of PQ treatment is essential to improve adherence. Another commonly reported reason for the discontinuation of the PQ treatment was the long duration of treatment, mentioned by (17.2%) of non-adherent participants[27]. These findings highlight the need for program planners to consider shorter treatment regimens or implement follow-up mechanisms to support adherence. Forgetfulness was also reported as a reason for non-adherence (16.7%), consistent with findings from previous studies[30]. The treatment requires daily attention and commitment; patients may become less motivated to continue taking the medication once symptoms subside. Experiencing side effects was another reason for discontinuation of PQ treatment (15.2%), consistent with findings from other studies [24, 30, 32]. Primaquine is known to cause various side effects, and patients may not be adequately informed that these side effects are expected and manageable. Therefore, counselling before initiation of PQ treatment should include information about possible side effects, their management, and when to seek medical attention. Such counselling may help build patients’ confidence and improve commitment to completing the full course of the treatment.

Significant predictors of probable adherence were: sex, knowing malaria can relapse, and a prior history of malaria infection, which were statistically significant positive associations with probable adherence. However, experiencing side effects showed a statistically significant negative association with probably adherent.

In the present study, females were 1.64 times more adherent than males. This finding was supported by a study conducted in Brazil, which reported that females were more adherent than males [35], and another study done in Bangladesh, which found that males were less adherent than females [21]. This difference may be explained by the fact that females often have more frequent contact with the health service due to maternal healthcare needs, which may provide additional opportunities for health counselling than males, and which may help them to adhere to the PQ medication. Furthermore, females may spend more time at home because of socially defined gender roles, which may facilitate better medication adherence.

In our study, participants who knew malaria could relapse were 1.69 times more likely to adhere to PQ treatment than those who did not have such knowledge. A study done in Indonesia and Thailand supports this finding [21, 27, 34]. Having awareness about the relapsing nature of malaria could create a fear of getting sick again and repeated suffering, which could prompt them to take the full course of medication to be cured radically. Furthermore, individuals with such awareness may have better health literacy, which could contribute to better medication adherence.

Participants with a history of malaria infection were 1.67 times more likely to adhere to PQ treatment than those without such a history. This finding was supported by a study conducted in Myanmar[29]. This may be explained by the fact that individuals with a previous malaria episode may have had opportunities to receive advice and health education regarding the treatment schedules and the importance of completing the full course of medication while seeking care at health facilities. Such experiences may help them to develop positive treatment-related behaviours that promote adherence. Additionally, previous experience with malaria may increase fear of relapse, motivating patients to adhere to treatment to avoid future suffering.

The occurrence of side effects has reduced the likelihood of adherence by 51%. This finding is supported by studies conducted in Thailand, Brazil, Gambia, and Ghana [24, 27, 30, 32, 33]. The experience of side effects or any discomfort related to the medication may discourage patients from completing the treatment course. Furthermore, PQ treatment continues after the completion of short-course anti-malarial drugs such as AL or CQ, by which time the signs ands and symptoms of malaria may already have resolved. As a result, patients who feel clinically cured may be less motivated to continue taking PQ, particularly when experiencing side effects. In addition, experiencing unexpected side effects may create mistrust or concern about the medication, especially if patients were not adequately informed about the possible side effects before treatment initiation. This may further encourage discontinuation of the medication.

Unlike previous studies, which reported that older age, instruction methods regarding dosage and treatment schedule (verbal instruction only), knowledge about the cause of malaria, malaria transmission, malaria prevention, and ITN use were associated with better adherence [20, 24, 27], our study did not find a statistically significant association between these factors and adherence status.

Limitations of the study

Although this study provides meaningful findings on PQ treatment adherence, some limitations should be considered while interpreting the results. First, only participants who received services at HCs were included in the study. It would have been better to also include patients who received services from health posts (HPs), private clinics, and the hospital. Therefore, the findings may not fully represent all patients receiving PQ treatment and may limit the generalizability of the results to other health care settings in the study area. Second, there may have been social desirability bias, particularly among participants who failed to present the blister pack during the assessment. This could have resulted in overestimation of the actual adherence status. Third, a qualitative component was not included in the study. Incorporating qualitative methods could have provided deeper insight into patients' perspectives and reasons for non-adherence within the study context.

Conclusions

The adherence status to PQ treatment is low in the Yilmana Densa district. Factors positively associated with adherence included being female, having a previous history of malaria, and awareness that malaria can relapse. Conversely, experiencing side effects was negatively associated with adherence. These findings underscore the importance of strengthening patient education and counselling regarding malaria relapse and providing adequate support for the management of side effects. Hence, counselling that emphasizes the risk of malaria relapse and the importance of completing the full course of PQ treatment shall be promoted. Special attention should be given to male patients to address the lower adherence observed among them. In addition, patients should be encouraged to report side effects rather than discontinuing treatment. Future researchers are recommended to include patients receiving services from HPs, private clinics, and the hospital to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of PQ treatment adherence. Additionally, qualitative methods shall be considered to explore in-depth perspectives on PQ treatment adherence.
