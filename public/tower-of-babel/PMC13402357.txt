Minimizing galvanic corrosion for durable anode-less aqueous zinc batteries
Authors: Yunxiang Zhao, Xiaotan Zhang, Rui Yao, Haojie Zhu, Feiyu Kang, Chunyi Zhi, Cheng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anode-less aqueous zinc batteries offer a promising route to energy-dense and intrinsically safe energy storage, yet their practical deployment is hindered by poor reversibility under constrained zinc inventory. Here, we identify galvanic corrosion between deposited zinc and current collectors as a critical but previously underappreciated degradation pathway in anode-less aqueous zinc batteries. A hybrid passivation layer, comprising an electrically insulating polymer matrix embedded with high-permittivity metal fluorides, is designed to mitigate parasitic electron transfer and limit current collector exposure. The resulting dielectric polarization homogenizes the interfacial electric field, promoting planar and dense zinc deposition that isolates the current collector and suppresses corrosion. Consequently, irreversible zinc loss during both cycling and calendar ageing is reduced, enabling stable zinc plating/stripping for over 900 h at 5 mA cm−2/3 mAh cm−2 with 51% depth of discharge. Ampere-hour-scale anode-less full cells deliver 89% capacity retention after 130 cycles at 0.5 A g−1, with minimal self-discharge during calendar ageing, and initially anode-free pouch cells achieve device-level energy densities exceeding 90 Wh L−1. This work advances the mechanistic understanding of corrosion processes and motivates corrosion-conscious engineering of heterogeneous interfaces across broader metal battery systems.

Introduction

Rechargeable batteries based on zinc (Zn) chemistry are in-principle promising for next-generation sustainable energy storage, owing to Zn’s high theoretical capacity, compatibility with nonflammable aqueous electrolytes, and natural abundance1–3. However, the specific energy of state-of-the-art aqueous Zn batteries (AZBs) remains uncompetitive4, typically below 50 Wh kg−1 based on the total mass of active materials. This stems from the requirement for substantial Zn excess, often an order of magnitude greater than the actual cycled amount5,6, to compensate for irreversible Zn loss during prolonged cycling (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Note 1). Anode-less configuration7, which strictly limits Zn excess by controlling the negative-to-positive capacity ratio (N/P ratio), has been proposed to balance specific energy and cycle life (Supplementary Notes 2 and 3). Reducing the N/P ratio below 4 is estimated to yield specific energy exceeding 100 Wh kg−1. This value could potentially be doubled when adopting an initially anode-free configuration where the Zn negative electrode is entirely replaced with a bare current collector, thereby maximizing negative electrode utilization8. Despite these advantages, the finite supply of active Zn ultimately determines the cell lifespan. Limiting Zn excess remains challenging because metallic Zn tends to form dendritic deposits with high interfacial reactivity toward the electrolyte, leading to inactive Zn species formation and a consequent decline in Coulombic efficiency (CE)9. While dendrite-induced short circuits have long been regarded as the primary failure mode, growing evidence suggests that Zn depletion-induced polarization dominates under constrained Zn conditions10,11. Irreversible Zn loss generally consists of corroded Zn and electronically isolated Zn (or “dead” Zn)12,13. Notably, recent studies indicate that the isolated fraction can be partially reactivated in subsequent cycles14,15, whereas no evidence to date supports the recoverability of corroded Zn. In practical energy storage scenarios, cells are subjected to complex electrochemical protocols involving intermittent cycling and resting phases16. During rest periods, Zn loss is primarily governed by spontaneous corrosion reactions, which contribute to calendar ageing17. Despite its significance, corrosion-driven degradation in anode-less AZBs remains insufficiently understood.

Corrosion refers to the deterioration of materials, particularly metals, through chemical or electrochemical interactions with their environment18, typically involving metal oxidation into ionic species accompanied by electron transfer (Supplementary Note 4). Since Zn negative electrodes operate beyond the electrochemical stability window of aqueous electrolytes, spontaneous corrosion is thermodynamically inevitable19. The resulting interfacial corrosion products, often described as solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)-like layer, offer limited passivation and can scarcely prevent further Zn dissolution20,21. Consequently, corrosion readily occurs in AZBs unless effective kinetic inhibition is introduced, and this issue has attracted increasing attention in the battery community17,19. To mitigate Zn corrosion, a wide range of strategies have been explored, including interface reconstruction11,22,23, crystallographic engineering24–26 and alloying approaches27–29. Despite these extensive efforts, the underlying corrosion mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Moreover, repeated Zn plating/stripping, especially under high depth of discharge (DOD), inevitably leads to electrode pulverization and the formation of inactive Zn fragments (Fig. 1b). The continuous exposure of freshly formed Zn surfaces to aggressive electrolytes further exacerbates interfacial degradation30. Recent efforts incorporating current collectors have shown promise in preserving Zn layer integrity (Fig. 1c). In particular, metallic substrates with moderate interfacial interactions can promote compact Zn deposition31, thereby mitigating corrosion by minimizing surface exposure. However, direct contact between dissimilar metals in electrolytes can initiate electrochemical reactions driven by their intrinsic potential differences, giving rise to galvanic corrosion32,33. During cell operation and storage, Zn negative electrodes therefore undergo both chemical and electrochemical corrosion, which accelerates the calendar ageing of AZBs34. Such a dilemma largely relates to the insufficient understanding of corrosion mechanisms, appealing for further expeditions to elucidate the correlation between dynamic Zn corrosion and interfacial evolution, and to develop rational interface designs for minimizing Zn corrosion (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Herein, we delve into the failure mechanisms of Zn negative electrodes under limited Zn excess and identify galvanic corrosion as a critical degradation pathway in anode-less AZBs. Specifically, spontaneous galvanic coupling between deposited Zn and the exposed current collector accounts for ~78% of irreversible Zn loss in practical Zn negative electrodes. To address this issue, we construct a hybrid passivation layer consisting of an electrically insulating polymer matrix embedded with high-permittivity metal fluorides, designed to suppress parasitic electron transfer and limit current collector exposure to the electrolyte (Fig. 1d). This interfacial design promotes subsurface, compact Zn deposition that nearly fully covers the current collector and mitigates Zn corrosion, thereby reducing irreversible Zn loss by ~60% and enabling a high Zn plating/stripping CE of 99.85% over 8000 cycles. Consequently, prototype anode-less and even initially anode-free pouch cells exhibit prolonged cycling lifespans, low self-discharge, and competitive energy densities exceeding 90 Wh L−1. These findings reveal a previously underappreciated degradation pathway in practical Zn negative electrodes, providing a crucial complement to the prevailing understanding of corrosion mechanisms in AZBs.

Results

Failure mechanisms in aqueous Zn batteries with limited Zn excess

Understanding the electrochemical behavior and degradation pathways of Zn negative electrodes under restricted Zn supply is essential for advancing energy-dense AZBs. To ensure practical relevance, rigorous testing protocols were adopted, including moderate charge/discharge rates (0.2–2 C), realistic areal capacities (≥3 mAh cm−2), and high DOD (≥25%)5,7. Thin Zn foils (10 μm) were first cycled in half cells at 5 mA cm−2/3 mAh cm−2, corresponding to 51% DOD (Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast to conventional thick Zn (100 μm), which fail abruptly after ~190 h due to dendrite-induced short circuits35, thin Zn shows clear voltage polarization after ~130 h, indicating insufficient residual Zn to sustain Zn plating/stripping9,10. Post-mortem analysis reveals severe pulverization, leaving behind a damaged surface rich in electrochemically inaccessible Zn. Similar Zn depletion-induced polarization was also observed in full cells (Supplementary Fig. 2). Replacing the depleted Zn foil after polarization failure (~140 cycles) restores capacity and enables an additional ~100 stable cycles. These findings provide initial evidence that negative electrode degradation governs capacity fading in anode-less AZBs.

The introduction of inert substrates enhances electronic continuity across the electrode, thereby improving Zn utilization. In particular, zincophilic metallic substrates can trigger underpotential Zn deposition via interfacial alloying36, allowing self-repair of damaged Zn regions (Supplementary Fig. 3). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, integrating a copper (Cu) substrate nearly doubles the half-cell lifetime (~240 h). Nevertheless, Zn depletion remains inevitable. Post-mortem analysis reveals a rough surface with residual Zn trapped in insulating corrosion products, further aggravating deposition non-uniformity10. While Zn protrusions have not evolved into hazardous dendrites, root dissolution leaves them electrically isolated, contributing to irreversible Zn loss. Notably, full cells with pre-deposited Zn exhibit increasing voltage hysteresis, in contrast to the stable profiles seen with thin Zn metal until complete depletion (Supplementary Fig. 5). This partly contradicts conventional expectations, as pulverized Zn with a larger exposed surface area is typically considered more prone to corrosion and impedance rise37. The intensified corrosion in pre-deposited Zn negative electrodes likely originates from additional pathways associated with dissimilar metal interfaces.

The corrosion behavior of Cu, Zn, Cu+Zn (Zn attached to Cu), and Cu@Zn (Zn electrodeposited on Cu) in a typical ZnSO4 electrolyte was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping and focused ion beam (FIB) milling (Supplementary Figs. 6–8). After 8 days of ageing, Cu remains essentially inert with negligible corrosion, whereas Zn undergoes modest and relatively uniform chemical corrosion associated with spontaneous hydrogen evolution. In contrast, electrical coupling between Zn and a Cu substrate leads to markedly enhanced corrosion. Localized corrosion pits appear in the Zn region of Cu+Zn, whereas Cu@Zn exhibits severe Zn dissolution with a porous morphology. In both cases, extensive corrosion products accumulate on the Cu regions, indicating the activation of an additional electrochemically driven corrosion pathway. Mechanistically, direct electrical contact between Zn and Cu establishes a galvanic couple in the electrolyte, in which Zn serves as the sacrificial anode and undergoes accelerated dissolution (Supplementary Fig. 9). Meanwhile, the cathodic hydrogen evolution reaction preferentially proceeds on the Cu surface, increasing the local alkalinity and promoting the accumulation of corrosion products. To quantify this contribution, a galvanic cell was constructed using spatially separated Zn and Cu electrodes electrically coupled via a potentiostat38, while holding the Cu electrode at 0 V vs. Zn2+/Zn (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Note 5). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 11, the corrosion current initially peaks due to capacitive effects, then rapidly declines to the μA cm−2 range. Even after tens of hours, the current remains nonzero and stabilizes at a relatively fixed value, evidencing sustained galvanic corrosion. Notably, by integrating this current over time and comparing it with CE analysis, the galvanic corrosion accounts for 78% of the total Zn loss, underscoring its critical role in AZB degradation. Overall, the failure of anode-less AZBs can be reasonably attributed to depletion of the limited Zn reservoir, arising from continuous chemical and electrochemical corrosion, together with electrical isolation caused by uncontrolled Zn plating/stripping. In practical architectures where Zn is integrated onto current collectors, galvanic corrosion is identified as the primary source of irreversible Zn loss. Therefore, a stable Zn/electrolyte interface should be deliberately engineered to mitigate corrosion and ensure long-term cycling stability in anode-less AZBs.

Design of a passivation layer against galvanic corrosion

Galvanic corrosion poses a critical challenge for practical Zn negative electrodes, necessitating strategies that suppress parasitic electron transfer and minimize exposure of the conductive current collector to the electrolyte during both cycling and resting38. This requires: (i) interfacial passivation with low electronic conductivity, limited electrolyte permeability, and sufficient ionic accessibility; (ii) promotion of planar and dense Zn deposition to physically isolate the current collector; and (iii) long-term mechanical robustness to sustain these protective effects (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). Only when these criteria are fulfilled concurrently can galvanic corrosion be effectively suppressed. From a materials perspective, integrating electronic insulation, electrolyte blocking, regulated Zn deposition, and mechanical durability within a single interfacial layer is inherently challenging (Supplementary Table 3). This motivates the design of a hybrid interfacial layer comprising organic poly(vinylidene fluoride) and inorganic metal fluorides (PVDF/MFx). Both electronically insulating components suppress parasitic electron transfer associated with galvanic coupling while limiting direct electrolyte access to the current collector. The PVDF matrix, owing to its intrinsic flexibility and affinity for polar species39, maintains mechanical integrity during repeated Zn plating/stripping. In parallel, the embedded MFx modulates the interfacial physicochemical environment through its M-F bonding characteristics40–42, thereby promoting compact and uniform Zn deposition and enhancing interfacial stability.

Representative MFx candidates were screened and categorized as either reactive or non-reactive, based on the redox potential of Mx+/M relative to Zn2+/Zn (Fig. 2a). Reactive MFx are expected to undergo initial reduction, yielding zincophilic alloys that direct Zn nucleation and growth, whereas non-reactive MFx remain thermodynamically stable, functioning primarily as inert barriers to homogenize interfacial electric fields and ionic fluxes. The basic properties of selected MFx are presented in Supplementary Fig. 14. The hybrid passivation layer was fabricated via a scalable solution-casting method. The optimized MFx content was determined to be 90 wt.%, and the coating thickness was controlled at approximately 2 μm, based on a combined consideration of Zn deposition thermodynamics, interfacial transport kinetics, and mechanical stability of the passivation layer (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16). Owing to its low material price at scale and compatibility with established manufacturing processes, this strategy remains economically viable for large-scale implementation (Supplementary Table 4). Cross-sectional SEM images and EDS elemental mapping reveal a conformal coating tightly adhered to the Cu substrate, while its pronounced hydrophobicity is expected to effectively block electrolyte infiltration and mitigate Zn corrosion (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). To probe the impact of different MFx on Zn plating/stripping reversibility, CE measurements were conducted (Supplementary Fig. 20). Neither pure PVDF nor SnF2-containing layers improve Zn plating/stripping stability, both yielding broad CE distributions. In contrast, non-reactive MFx layers produce narrower CE distributions and prolonged cycle life. Nevertheless, the ZnF2- and AlF3-based systems exhibit significant increases in voltage hysteresis, whereas the increase for CeF3 is limited to just 14 mV, indicating improved interfacial stability (Supplementary Fig. 21). Although passivation inherently introduces serial resistance and raises nucleation overpotentials, the growth overpotentials remain largely unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 22). In some cases, a slight decrease is even noted, likely due to improved deposition kinetics from favorable Zn morphologies. Optical and SEM imaging reveal that bare Cu induces rough, uneven Zn deposits intertwined with flake-like byproducts, forming protrusions and leaving large areas uncovered, which aggravates galvanic corrosion (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24). Applying an electrically insulating PVDF coating reduces corrosion, yet the limited spatial confinement yields planar but morphologically non-uniform deposits. Incorporating SnF2 further complicates deposition, as its in situ reduction to metallic Sn triggers direct Zn plating on the coating surface, ultimately causing entanglement with the separator (Supplementary Fig. 25). This behavior explains the reduced CE, since such deposition readily leads to Zn orphaning. Conversely, passivation layers incorporating non-reactive MFx produce Zn deposits with a metallic luster at the macroscopic scale. While Zn nearly covers the entire substrate in all cases, ZnF2- and AlF3-based layers generate loosely packed morphologies with spherical and block-like structures, respectively, exposing large surface areas. Notably, CeF3 promotes compact Zn deposition, with hexagonal flakes aligned parallel to the substrate and gradually merging into a dense layer as the capacity increases. This planar and dense morphology helps minimize Zn corrosion and prevent Zn isolation, accounting for the enhanced Zn plating/stripping performance (Supplementary Fig. 26). Control experiments rule out the influence of substrate type and current density, confirming the critical role of passivation layer design (Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28).

The intrinsic electrical insulation of the polymer matrix, together with the favorable Zn deposition enabled by embedded high-permittivity MFx, holds promise for minimizing galvanic corrosion. With passivation, the galvanic current rapidly stabilizes at ~1 μA cm−2, only one-third of that observed on bare Cu (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 34). The minute residual current likely arises from microcracks or pores that locally increase electrolyte permeability and ion transport. Time-integration of the galvanic current reveals a 68% reduction in cumulative corrosion charge, corresponding to 0.33 mAh cm−2 of active Zn loss after 240 h, demonstrating effective corrosion inhibition (inset of Fig. 2f). SEM images and corresponding EDS elemental mapping after the galvanic corrosion test further confirm the interfacial stability (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 35). The passivated Cu exhibits only faint signals of corrosion species, and the coupled Zn surface remains largely intact. In contrast, the bare Cu surface is covered with extensive flaky zinc hydroxysulfate (Zn4SO4(OH)6·xH2O, ZHS), while the counter Zn surface displays a porous morphology with widespread corrosion pits. To further validate the proposed interfacial design principle, barium titanate, a non-fluoride and low-cost alternative with high permittivity, low electronic conductivity, and low electrolyte permeability, was introduced. The resulting hybrid layer promotes compact Zn deposition and effectively mitigates galvanic corrosion, confirming that the passivation mechanism is not intrinsically dependent on rare-earth or fluoride chemistry (Supplementary Fig. 36). The effectiveness of this hybrid interfacial design was also demonstrated across multiple current collector materials beyond the Zn-Cu couple, highlighting its applicability to diverse galvanic couples (Supplementary Figs. 37 and 38).

Based on these findings, Fig. 2h schematically summarizes the galvanic corrosion mechanism and its mitigation through hybrid interfacial engineering. In aqueous electrolytes, direct electrical contact between deposited Zn and a more noble current collector, such as Cu, establishes a galvanic couple. In this couple, Zn undergoes anodic dissolution, while cathodic electrolyte reduction proceeds on the Cu surface, accompanied by corrosion byproduct accumulation. This process accelerates localized corrosion and destabilizes the Zn negative electrode interface, posing a critical challenge to the calendar life of anode-less AZBs. To address this issue, a hybrid interfacial layer consisting of a dense polymer matrix embedded with electrically insulating MFx is introduced, which suppresses electrolyte permeation and parasitic electron transport. Moreover, dielectric polarization from high-permittivity MFx homogenizes the interfacial electric field and promotes compact Zn deposition that nearly fully covers the current collector, thereby minimizing its electrochemical exposure. As a result, galvanic corrosion is substantially mitigated, enabling a stable and durable Zn negative electrode interface.

Enhanced interfacial stability and mitigated Zn loss

To evaluate the effectiveness of the tailored passivation layer in enhancing interfacial stability, we systematically examined the corrosion behavior and reversibility of Zn negative electrodes under both cycling and ageing conditions. Morphological analysis was first performed to establish the correlation between Zn deposit compactness and corrosion susceptibility. After Zn plating/stripping, the passivation layer was removed to simultaneously assess subsurface Zn compactness and surface Zn morphology. As shown in Fig. 3a (left and middle), Zn deposited on bare Cu at a practical areal capacity of 3 mAh cm−2 exhibits a loose, dendritic structure, with mossy Zn extending into the separator. In contrast, the passivated surface yields a planar, dendrite-free Zn layer composed of densely stacked hexagonal platelets spanning several hundred micrometers, indicating well-regulated Zn nucleation and growth. The surface Zn coverage was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 39, surface Zn coverage increases from 46.5% on bare Cu to nearly 100% on passivated Cu, substantially reducing current collector exposure to the electrolyte and thereby mitigating galvanic corrosion. FIB-SEM further reveals that Zn deposits beneath the passivation layer are compact and nearly pore-free, whereas those on bare Cu possess a highly porous architecture with interconnected voids (right of Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 40). Consistently, compact Zn deposition beneath the passivation layer is observed across multiple plated areal capacities (Supplementary Fig. 41). As the areal capacity increases from 1 to 5 mAh cm−2, the average Zn thicknesses on the passivated Cu are 1.8, 5.2 and 8.7 μm, respectively, compared with 4.0, 10.6 and 17.0 μm on bare Cu (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 42–44). These values correspond to a porosity of <5% for the passivated samples, reducing surface exposure and thereby mitigating corrosion and electrical isolation. The consistent Zn morphology across all capacities confirms that dielectric regulation by the passivation layer remains effective throughout plating. The capability in corrosion inhibition was evidenced by Tafel polarization measurements50, which reveals a lower corrosion current density for Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn (0.80 mA cm−2) than for Cu@Zn (2.00 mA cm−2) (Supplementary Fig. 45). Under mildly acidic conditions, Zn corrosion is generally cathodically controlled and dominated by the hydrogen evolution reaction51. Since deposition morphology and corrosion susceptibility directly affect Zn reversibility, the average CE was quantified using a modified Aurbach method52. After stabilization, Cu/PVDF/CeF3 exhibits an average CE of 99.46%, higher than the 98.69% of bare Cu (Supplementary Fig. 47), and this advantage is maintained during prolonged cycles (Supplementary Fig. 48). Post-mortem analysis of fully stripped electrodes identifies two major sources of irreversible Zn loss, namely electrically isolated Zn (inactive Zn0) and Zn-containing byproducts (ZHS-Zn2+) (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 49). Their respective contributions were separated by titration gas chromatography (TGC)53,54, where inactive Zn0 is quantified from the volume of H2 evolved upon acid titration, while ZHS-Zn2+ is obtained by subtracting the inactive Zn0 contribution from the total irreversible Zn loss determined by CE analysis (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 50). As summarized in Fig. 3e, for bare Cu and Cu/PVDF/CeF3, 0.54% (0.37 mAh cm−2) and 0.35% (0.24 mAh cm−2) of the total capacity, respectively, are consumed in forming isolated Zn0, whereas 0.79% (0.54 mAh cm−2) and 0.44% (0.30 mAh cm−2) are lost to ZHS-Zn2+ accumulation. Corrosion therefore constitutes the dominant source of irreversible Zn loss, corroborating it as the major degradation pathway in anode-less AZBs. Crucially, the passivation layer decreases the cumulative irreversible capacity loss from 1.09 to 0.54 mAh cm−2, and from 0.82 to 0.34 mAh cm−2 after excluding the first-cycle loss.

In practical scenarios involving calendar ageing, irreversible Zn loss arises predominantly from corrosion17,38. The corrosion evolution of Zn negative electrodes was visualized using an in situ optical setup based on light-scattering principles. Hydrogen bubbles generated by corrosion reactions on the Zn surface enhance near-surface light scattering, and the resulting light intensity variation serves as a semi-quantitative indicator of hydrogen evolution. Time-dependent light intensity maps reveal discernible hydrogen bubble formation on Cu@Zn after 6 h of ageing, with bubble sizes exceeding 100 μm after 24 h, indicative of severe corrosion (Supplementary Fig. 51). In contrast, Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn exhibits only sparse bubble formation even after prolonged ageing, reflecting improved interfacial stability against corrosion. The ageing process was further tracked by time-resolved electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). Progressive ageing leads to the accumulation of electrically insulating corrosion products on the electrode surface (Supplementary Figs. 52 and 53 and Supplementary Table 5), resulting in a steady increase in interfacial resistance55. To quantify the degradation kinetics, the interfacial resistance evolution was fitted using a power law relationship (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{{{{\rm{interface}}}}}\propto a{t}^{b}$$\end{document}Rinterface∝atb), a model commonly used to describe SEI-like interphase growth56. As shown in Fig. 3f, Zn||Zn symmetric cells with Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn (0.31) exhibit a lower scaling exponent than those with Cu@Zn (0.42), indicating slower interfacial degradation. This difference becomes more apparent when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{{{{\rm{interface}}}}}(t)$$\end{document}Rinterface(t) is normalized to its initial value (Fig. 3g). Despite comparable initial interfacial resistances of ~8 Ω cm2, Cu@Zn cells exhibit an 18-fold increase after 8 days of ageing to ~170 Ω cm2, whereas Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn cells show only a 4-fold rise to ~37 Ω cm2. Persistent galvanic corrosion in Cu@Zn is also accompanied by continuous hydrogen evolution, causing pronounced cell swelling of 15.0% (Supplementary Fig. 54). Such gas accumulation may be more pronounced in less constrained pouch cells and thus poses practical safety concerns. In contrast, cells incorporating Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn exhibit minimal swelling of 1.2%, and the Zn layer remains intact with negligible corrosion after calendar ageing. Apart from the inherently sluggish solid-state diffusion process, charge transfer dominates the electrochemical response. Notably, in Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn cells, the relaxation times of all processes remain nearly constant, and the increase in the relaxation distribution function is markedly suppressed, indicating that the passivation layer mitigates interfacial degradation during calendar ageing.

The corrosivity of deposited Zn was further quantified by CE tests after controlled shelving. Comparable capacity losses across different areal capacities suggest that the corrosion process is primarily time-dependent (Supplementary Fig. 56). At a practical areal capacity of 3 mAh cm−2, extending the rest period from 1 to 8 days leads to a 15.3% decrease in CE for bare Cu, whereas Cu/PVDF/CeF3 shows only a 6.4% decrease (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 57). Even after 32 days, the CE for Cu/PVDF/CeF3 remains above 73%, in sharp contrast to 34% on bare Cu. Although CE continues to decay during prolonged shelving, the apparent corrosion rate decreases over time (Fig. 3j). Within the first 8 days, the average corrosion rate for Zn on Cu/PVDF/CeF3 is only 0.94 μAh cm−2 h−1, less than half that on bare Cu (2.08 μAh cm−2 h−1). This rate is lower than those reported for advanced concentrated or hydrated organic electrolytes in AZBs17, and is even comparable to typical levels in lithium metal battery systems56,59. These results position Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn in the regime of weak Zn corrosivity and uniform, compact Zn morphology, highlighting its potential for highly reversible Zn plating/stripping (Supplementary Fig. 58).

Reversible Zn plating/stripping

To validate the merits of the tailored passivation layer in improving Zn reversibility, Zn plating/stripping tests were performed. In Zn||Cu asymmetric cells employing Cu/PVDF/CeF3, Zn plating begins with a lattice matching stage, after which the CE stabilizes above 99.85% for 8000 cycles, corresponding to a cumulative capacity of 8 Ah cm−2 (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 59). This represents one of the longest cycle lives and highest cumulative capacities reported under such high CE and current density conditions. Notably, the average CE reaches 99.92% from the 1000th to the 6000th cycle, indicating effective suppression of parasitic reactions. Even at 3 mAh cm−2, Cu/PVDF/CeF3 maintains an average CE of 99.67% over 1200 cycles. The voltage profiles remain stable throughout Zn plating/stripping, with hysteresis increasing by only ~17 μV per cycle (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 60). Consistently, Zn deposited beneath the passivation layer remains compact without discernible voiding, and almost no residual irreversible Zn is detected in the fully stripped state, reflecting enhanced electrochemical reversibility and accounting for the improved CE (Supplementary Fig. 61). CE and cyclability were also evaluated across a wide range of current densities and areal capacities to assess practical relevance (Supplementary Fig. 62). By comparison, bare Cu exhibits inferior Zn plating/stripping performance, with rapid CE decay and severe voltage hysteresis buildup under all tested conditions. Operando DRT analysis further reveals the enhanced interfacial stability imparted by the passivation layer, as evidenced by nearly symmetric DRT responses throughout the full Zn plating/stripping cycle (Supplementary Fig. 63). Favorable Zn deposition also contributes to shorter relaxation times and lower distribution intensities, indicating accelerated reaction kinetics. After 20 activation cycles, the DRT spectra converge with consistently low relaxation values, supporting stabilized interfacial processes (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 64). Notably, the tailored passivation layer maintains mechanical integrity without observable cracking and continues to protect the Cu substrate after prolonged cycling and ageing (Supplementary Fig. 65). Ion chromatography (IC) detects only trace-level fluoride release, indicating negligible interaction with the electrolyte and excluding any meaningful involvement of fluoride species in interphase chemistry (Supplementary Fig. 66). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) after Ar+ sputtering, together with XRD analysis, confirms that the embedded CeF3 retains its chemical and structural integrity, demonstrating sustained interfacial functionality without electrochemical degradation (Supplementary Figs. 52, 67, and 68). This stability is also supported by unchanged redox features in cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 69). Zn reversibility was further examined in Zn||Zn symmetric cells with limited Zn excess, in which 5.85 mAh cm−2 of Zn was pre-deposited, equivalent to a 10 μm-thick Zn foil (Supplementary Fig. 71). At a high DOD of 51%, both bare Zn and Cu@Zn exhibit a sharp increase in voltage polarization after ~100 and ~200 h, respectively, reflecting rapid Zn loss caused by uncontrolled corrosion and electrical isolation (Fig. 4d). Post-cycling morphology confirms this degradation, with bare Zn showing severe pulverization and Cu@Zn displaying extensive byproduct accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 72). In contrast, Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn maintains stable Zn plating/stripping for over 900 h, with a planar and compact Zn morphology that underpins the consistently low voltage hysteresis (inset of Fig. 4d and Supplementary Figs. 73 and 74). Similar stability is retained at a higher DOD of 75% and a current density of 10 mA cm−2 (Supplementary Figs. 75 and 76). Rate performance at a fixed capacity of 3 mAh cm−2 reveals that, across current densities from 1 to 20 mA cm−2, Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn consistently delivers lower and more stable voltage hysteresis than bare Zn and Cu@Zn, verifying enhanced Zn plating/stripping kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 77). Overall, the high electrochemical reversibility of Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn derives from combined benefits of favorable Zn deposition and effective corrosion resistance enabled by the hybrid interfacial design. Across four key performance metrics, the results reported here are comparable to, or surpass, those of state-of-the-art strategies, including separator modification, electrolyte optimization, negative electrode structural design, and current collector enhancement (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 6). These results establish Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn as a promising alternative to conventional thin Zn metal and directly pre-deposited Zn negative electrodes for realizing highly reversible anode-less AZBs.

Energy-dense aqueous Zn batteries

Achieving high energy density in AZBs requires stringent control over Zn excess, thus driving the development of anode-less and even initially anode-free configurations (Fig. 5a). Motivated by the improved Zn reversibility of Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn, we next evaluated its practical performance in full cells paired with a high-capacity NaV3O8·nH2O positive electrode. Benefiting from favorable Zn deposition with reduced interfacial resistance, the Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn||NaV3O8·nH2O coin cell exhibits lower voltage hysteresis and improved capacity retention (Supplementary Figs. 78 and 79 and Supplementary Table 7), consistent with the half-cell behavior. To investigate the influence of the passivation layer on calendar ageing and time-dependent degradation, self-discharge tests were conducted (Supplementary Fig. 80). Persistent Zn corrosion during storage consumes active Zn and accumulates insulating by-products, leading to increased interfacial impedance, amplified voltage hysteresis and accelerated capacity fade56. After sequential rest periods of 24, 48, and 96 h, the Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn cell shows hysteresis increases of only 17, 31, and 36 mV, respectively, much lower than those of the Cu@Zn cell (65, 94, and 134 mV), highlighting the efficacy of the passivation layer in suppressing self-discharge and preserving capacity. For long-term cycling, the anode-less Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn||NaV3O8·nH2O cell with an N/P ratio of ~1.9 retains 80% of its capacity after 720 cycles, corresponding to nearly 900 h of operation (Fig. 5b). The stable median discharge voltage throughout cycling indicates limited interfacial impedance growth, consistent with the uniform and compact Zn morphology after cycling (inset of Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 81). In contrast, full cells with bare Zn and Cu@Zn fail after only 115 and 227 cycles, respectively, due to the rapid depletion of active Zn (Supplementary Fig. 82). Under even more challenging conditions, an initially anode-free Cu||ZnxNaV3O8·nH2O cell with an N/P ratio of 0 was constructed, where Zn was sourced exclusively from the pre-zincated positive electrode (Supplementary Fig. 83). As shown in Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 84, the Cu/PVDF/CeF3||ZnxNaV3O8·nH2O cell delivers an areal capacity of ~3 mAh cm−2 and maintains 90% of its capacity after 158 cycles. To further demonstrate the practical viability of the proposed hybrid interfacial design, pouch cells were fabricated. Key cell parameters, including a realistic areal capacity ( > 3 mAh cm−2), high DOD (N/P < 2), lean electrolyte (E/C < 10 g Ah−1), moderate cycling rates ( < 2 C), and low external pressure (0.5 MPa), were carefully implemented to approximate practical energy storage scenarios (Supplementary Fig. 85). The self-discharge rate, a critical metric for large-scale energy storage applications, was first evaluated. As shown in Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 8, the single-layer Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn||NaV3O8·nH2O pouch cell exhibits a monthly capacity loss of only 12%, outperforming several representative aqueous battery schemes, and approaching commercial aqueous technologies such as lead acid, nickel-metal hydride, and nickel-cadmium batteries. The electrolyte volume dependence suggests that reducing the electrolyte amount may help mitigate ageing-induced capacity fading (Supplementary Fig. 86). The long-term cycling performance is shown in Supplementary Fig. 87, where the single-layer anode-less pouch cell maintains stable operation for 274 cycles at 0.5 A g−1, despite a high DOD exceeding 50%. Guided by these design principles, the ampere-hour-scale Cu/PVDF/CeF3@Zn||NaV3O8·nH2O pouch cell delivers a high specific energy of 144 Wh kg−1 (based on total active material), and an energy efficiency of 74.5% (Supplementary Fig. 88 and Supplementary Table 9). The cell also maintains stable cycling over 130 cycles, with an average CE of 99.93%, indicating limited active Zn loss (Fig. 5e). With a capacity decay rate of only 0.086% per cycle, the multi-layer pouch cell retains over 80% of its initial capacity after 234 cycles.

To date, few studies have successfully reconciled high energy density with long cycling stability in initially anode-free AZBs, particularly in practical pouch cell configurations. This demanding architecture imposes stringent requirements on Zn reversibility and interfacial stability, serving as a rigorous benchmark for evaluating the effectiveness of the tailored passivation layer. Notably, the initially anode-free Cu/PVDF/CeF3||ZnxNaV3O8·nH2O pouch cell, with a nominal capacity of 240 mAh, delivers a specific energy of 157 Wh kg−1 (based on total active material) and retains 80% of its capacity after 147 cycles (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Fig. 89 and Supplementary Table 10). This performance is competitive among recent initially anode-free AZBs, demonstrating balanced specific energy and cycling stability (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Table 11). The ultrathin interfacial layer also reduces the required Zn excess, thereby decreasing cell thickness and increasing the overall energy density. As shown in Fig. 5h and Supplementary Table 12, the fabricated anode-less and initially anode-free pouch cells reach device-level energy densities exceeding 90 Wh L−1, comparable to several advanced aqueous Zn batteries12,60,61, and approaching the level of commercial lead-acid batteries (50–90 Wh L−1)12,62, supporting their potential for practical deployment. While further engineering efforts are required to minimize inactive components and maximize packing efficiency, our results establish a clear pathway toward practical, energy-dense AZBs.

Discussion

In conclusion, we disclose galvanic corrosion between deposited Zn and current collectors as the primary degradation pathway in practical Zn negative electrodes, whose mitigation is essential for durable anode-less AZBs. Suppressing this process requires limiting parasitic electron transfer and minimizing current collector exposure to the electrolyte. As a proof of concept, we design a hybrid passivation layer comprising an electrically insulating PVDF matrix embedded with high-permittivity CeF3, which reduces irreversible Zn loss by ~60%. Combined theoretical simulations and experimental characterizations reveal that dielectric polarization from CeF3 homogenizes the interfacial electric field, promoting nearly full-density Zn deposition that shields the current collector and mitigates corrosion. As a result, the engineered interface supports reversible Zn plating/stripping for over 900 h under deep cycling conditions. Leveraging this interfacial stability, ampere-hour-scale Zn||NaV3O8·nH2O full cells exhibit stable cycling performance (0.086% capacity decay per cycle) and resistance to calendar ageing (12% monthly loss) under practical conditions. Notably, initially anode-free pouch cells deliver a device-level energy density of 90 Wh L−1, approaching that of commercial lead-acid batteries. This work provides a framework for advancing other anode-less battery chemistries and demonstrates the potential of Zn-based systems to complement conventional energy storage technologies.

Methods

Fabrication of PVDF/MFx passivation layers

Organic-inorganic hybrid coatings comprising poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF, Aladdin) and metal fluorides (MFx, where M = Sn, Zn, Al or Ce) were prepared by a scalable solution-casting method. SnF2 (Aladdin, 99%), ZnF2 (Aladdin, 99%), AlF3 (Aladdin, 99.9%) and CeF3 (Aladdin, 99.9%) powders were separately dry ball-milled in a planetary ball mill (MITR YXQM) at 400 rpm for 4 h, dispersed in ethanol (Aladdin, 99.5%), and centrifuged using a centrifuge (BIORIDGE TG16.5) at 27 × g for 10 min to remove large agglomerates. The purified powders were then mixed with PVDF and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, Aladdin, 99.5%) at a weight ratio of 9:1:20 to form a homogeneous slurry. The slurry was cast onto the current collector using a doctor blade (MTI MSK-SZQ-F20) and vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 6 h to yield the final coating.

Synthesis of NaV3O8·nH2O nanobelts

NaV3O8·nH2O nanobelts were synthesized following a modified procedure63. Typically, 11 mmol V2O5 (Aladdin, 99.5%) and 60 mmol NaCl (Aladdin, 99.8%) were dispersed in 30 mL of deionized water and magnetically stirred at 600 rpm using a magnetic stirrer (DLAB MS-H380-Pro) at 30 °C for 96 h. The resulting product was collected by centrifugation at 5200 × g for 10 min, thoroughly washed with deionized water, and freeze-dried using a freeze dryer (EYELA FDU-2110) at −80 °C and 10 Pa for 12 h.

Preparation of electrodes

Pre-deposited Zn negative electrodes were prepared by galvanostatic Zn deposition on current collectors at 5 mA cm−2 to an areal capacity of 5.85 mAh cm−2, corresponding to a Zn thickness of 10 μm, unless stated otherwise. Positive electrodes were prepared through a dry-processing route by blending the as-synthesized NaV3O8·nH2O active material, Super P (Canrd, 99.5%), and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, Aladdin, 60 wt.% in water) at a weight ratio of 6:3:1. Ethanol was added during manual grinding in a mortar until a cohesive, dough-like mixture was formed. The mixture was then roll-pressed onto a stainless-steel mesh current collector (400 mesh, pore size of 0.033 mm) using a roll press (MTI MSK-2150) to improve mechanical integrity and interfacial adhesion. The positive electrodes were vacuum-dried in a vacuum oven (YIHENG DZF-6030A) at 60 °C for 12 h before use. The active material loading was ~10 mg cm−2.

Materials characterizations

For ex situ material characterization, freshly prepared samples were immediately stored in an Ar-filled glovebox after preparation and kept there until measurement to preserve their original interfacial chemistry and minimize artefacts caused by prolonged air exposure. After cycling or ageing, cells were disassembled at the specified electrochemical states in ambient air at 25 °C. The electrodes were carefully harvested with tweezers, gently rinsed with deionized water and ethanol to remove residual electrolyte, and dried under vacuum at 25 °C for 1 h. Relative permittivities of passivation layers were measured using broadband dielectric spectroscopy (BDS, Novocontrol Concept 40) over a frequency range from 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz under an applied AC voltage. To ensure reliable electrical contact, both sides of the pellet samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold before measurement. X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance, Cu Kα radiation) was employed to identify crystalline phases and track compositional changes in electrodes after cycling or ageing. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50) in attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode was performed to analyze chemical bonding characteristics. Morphology and microstructure were examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU8010) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Oxford Ultim Max 65) for elemental analysis. For post-cycling analyses of the electrodes, the passivation layer was removed after cell disassembly to expose the underlying Zn deposits. When the layer remained firmly adhered after limited cycling, it was removed by immersing the electrode in NMP until complete detachment. After extended cycling, when the adhesion was weakened, the layer was gently peeled off intact using tweezers. Cross-sectional imaging was performed by manually cleaving the electrode to obtain large-area sections, while internal structural features were observed using focused ion beam scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM, Thermo Scientific Helios 5 DualBeam). Ion milling was conducted at 30 kV with a beam current of 9.3 nA for initial material removal, followed by cleaning at 0.79 nA to obtain a smooth cross-sectional surface. Contact angle measurements were conducted using a contact angle goniometer (KRUSS DSA30) to assess electrolyte wettability and probe interfacial permeability. Anion concentrations, with a focus on fluoride, were analyzed by ion chromatography (IC, Thermo Scientific Dionex Aquion). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI 5000 VersaProbe II) was employed to analyze surface chemical composition and electronic states. Spectra were collected using monochromatic Al Kα radiation after mild Ar+ sputtering at 2 kV for 10 min, with a sputtering rate of 5 nm min−1 calibrated using a SiO2 standard. Binding energies were calibrated to the C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV. Peak fitting was conducted using XPSPeak software with a Shirley background and a mixed Gaussian-Lorentzian line shape containing 80% Gaussian and 20% Lorentzian components. Titration gas chromatography (TGC) was employed to quantify irreversible Zn loss, including inactive Zn0 and ZHS-Zn2+, following a previously reported protocol54. Inactive Zn0 was quantified from the hydrogen gas evolved during acid titration using gas chromatography (GC, Thermo Scientific Trace 1300 ISQ). The amount of ZHS-Zn2+ was subsequently deduced in combination with CE analysis. A schematic illustration of the TGC workflow is provided in Supplementary Fig. 50.

Electrochemical measurements

All electrochemical tests were conducted at 25 °C in temperature-controlled environmental chambers (YH LRH-150). Unless otherwise specified, measurements were performed using 2032-type coin cells with stainless-steel cases and springs, and assembled under ambient conditions. Each coin cell contained one positive electrode and one negative electrode separated by one glass microfiber filter separator (Whatman GF/D, 19 mm in diameter, 675 μm thick without compression, 2.7 μm pore size), with 80 μL of 3 M ZnSO4 electrolyte prepared from ZnSO4·7H2O (Aladdin, 99.5%). The positive and negative electrodes were cut into discs with a diameter of 15 mm using an electrode cutter (MSK-T10). Galvanostatic cycling was recorded using multichannel battery testers (LANHE CT3001A, LANBTS BT2013A and NEWARE CT-4008). Zn||Zn symmetric cells were assembled with Zn foils (Canrd, 99.999%, 10 or 100 μm thick) or pre-deposited Zn as both the working and counter electrodes. Zn||Cu asymmetric cells employed bare Cu foils (Canrd, 99.95%, 10 μm thick) or passivated Cu foils (12 μm thick) as the working electrodes, with a stripping cutoff voltage of 0.5 V vs. Zn2+/Zn. Full cells were cycled between 0.4–1.4 V vs. Zn2+/Zn, with discharge-first and charge-first protocols for anode-less and initially anode-free cells, respectively. Pouch cells were assembled using 9 × 9 cm2 electrodes and glass microfiber filter separators (Whatman GF/A, 260 μm thick without compression, 1.6 μm pore size), with 1.5 mL electrolyte per separator sheet. The electrolyte was injected before sealing, followed by gas release and final heat sealing of the pouch bag. The cells were cycled under an external pressure of ~0.5 MPa using a customized clamping jig with silicone pads. Detailed calculations of energy density and specific energy are provided in Supplementary Tables 9 and 10, including the measured capacity and energy, component mass, number of stacking layers, N/P ratio and E/C ratio. Electrochemical techniques, including chronoamperometry (CA), Tafel analysis, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), cyclic voltammetry (CV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were conducted using an electrochemical workstation (Bio-Logic VMP-3). Tafel and LSV tests were conducted in a three-electrode setup with either the current collector or Zn negative electrode as the working electrode, a platinum foil as the counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode. EIS was measured in potentiostatic mode at the open-circuit voltage after a 10 min stabilization period, over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 10 mHz with a perturbation amplitude of 10 mV and 6 points collected per decade. Distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis was carried out using DRTtools64.
