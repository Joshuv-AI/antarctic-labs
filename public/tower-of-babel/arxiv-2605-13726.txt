Euclidean distance degree defect of singular projective varieties
Laurenţiu G. Maxim, Jose Israel Rodriguez, Botong Wang

Abstract
The unit Euclidean distance degree and the generic Euclidean distance degree are two well-studied invariants of projective varieties. These quantities measure the algebraic complexity of nearest-point problems on a variety, and in many examples arising in optimization, engineering, statistics, and data science, there is a significant gap between them. We refer to this difference as the defect of the Euclidean distance (ED) degree. In this paper, we provide a constructible enhancement and a topological formula for the defect of the ED degree of an arbitrary complex projective variety, extending our previous results from the smooth setting. Since the generic Euclidean distance degree is typically more tractable, our approach offers a new method for computing ED degrees in broad generality.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background on ED degrees

The notion of Euclidean distance degree was introduced in
[
7
]
as a measure of the algebraic complexity for
nearest
point problems of an algebraic variety.
It counts the number of complex critical points of the squared Euclidean distance function from a generic data point to a given variety.

A natural
generalization arises when one considers weighted least squares optimization as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X
be an irreducible closed subvariety of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
For a given weight
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
, the

\mathbf{w}
-weighted Euclidean distance degree
of
X
is the
number of complex critical points of

d_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(x_{i}-u_{i})^{2}

on the smooth locus
X_{\reg}
of
X
, for generic data
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
We denote this degree by
\EDdeg_{\mathbf{w}}(X)
.

Many models in applications are invariant under rescaling
and are therefore naturally viewed as projective varieties.
The notion of
\mathbf{w}
-weighted ED degree can be defined for these models as well, using affine cones.

Definition 1.2
.

If X is an irreducible closed subvariety of the complex projective space
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, we define the (projective)
\mathbf{w}
-weighted Euclidean distance degree of
X
by

\EDdeg_{\mathbf{w}}(X):=\EDdeg_{\mathbf{w}}(C(X)),

where
C(X)
is the affine cone of
X
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
.

When the weight vector
\mathbf{w}
is generic, we refer to
\EDdeg_{\mathbf{w}}(X)
as the
generic ED degree
and denote it by
\GED(X)
. When
\mathbf{w}=\bf{1}
, the all-ones vector, we obtain the
unit ED degree
, denoted
\UED(X)
. While these notions can be defined in both affine and projective settings, in this paper we focus on the projective setting.

It was proved in
[
16
, Theorem 1.3]
(see also
[
1
, Theorem 8.1]
for the smooth case) that the unit ED degree
of a projective variety can be computed as an Euler characteristic weighted by a certain constructible function. More precisely, one has the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be an irreducible closed subvariety. Then

\UED(X)=(-1)^{\dim(X)}\chi({\rm Eu}_{X}|_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus(Q\cup H)}),

(1.1)

where
{\rm Eu}_{X}
is MacPherson’s local Euler obstruction function on
X
,

Q
is the isotropic quadric

Q:=\left\{[x_{0}:\dots:x_{n}]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\mid x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}\cdots+x_{n}^{2}=0\right\},

H
is a general hyperplane in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, and
\chi(-)
denotes the Euler characteristic of a constructible function. In particular, if
X
is smooth, then

\UED(X)=(-1)^{\dim(X)}\chi(X\setminus(Q\cup H)).

(1.2)

Moreover, as already noted in
[
15
]
, the proof of Theorem
1.3
can be extended to the computation of
\EDdeg_{\mathbf{w}}(X)
, for an arbitrary weight
\mathbf{w}\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n+1}
. Indeed, if one replaces
Q
by
the quadric

Q_{\mathbf{w}}=\left\{[x_{0}:\dots:x_{n}]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\mid w_{0}x_{0}^{2}+w_{1}x_{1}^{2}\cdots+w_{n}x_{n}^{2}=0\right\},

the following holds.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be an irreducible closed subvariety. Then,

\EDdeg_{\mathbf{w}}(X)=(-1)^{\dim(X)}\chi({\rm Eu}_{X}|_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\setminus(Q_{\mathbf{w}}\cup H)}),

(1.3)

with
H
a general hyperplane.
In particular, if
X
is smooth, one has the equality

\EDdeg_{\mathbf{w}}(X)=(-1)^{\dim(X)}\chi(X\setminus(Q_{\mathbf{w}}\cup H)).

(1.4)

These formulas give a topological description of the weighted ED degree allowing
techniques from topology, algebraic geometry, as well as singularity theory, to be applied to their computation.

The unit ED degree
is in general difficult to compute even if
X
is smooth, since the isotropic quadric
Q
may intersect
X
non-transversally.
On the other hand, for generic weight
\mathbf{w}
, the quadric
Q_{\mathbf{w}}
intersects
X
transversally, and the computation of
\GED(X)
is more manageable, e.g., (see
[
7
]
,
[
21
]
).

1.2
ED degree defect and statements of results

In this paper, we evaluate the difference between generic and unit ED degree in terms of invariants of the singularities of

X\cap Q
. This leads to formulas for the unit ED degree that may otherwise be difficult to obtain directly.
Of course, if the difference is zero then the unit ED degree equals the more amenable generic ED degree.
That is,
we analyze the difference

\DED(X)\coloneqq\GED(X)-\UED(X),

which in
[
15
]
was referred to as the
defect
of the Euclidean distance degree. It is known that
\DED(X)
is non-negative, and for many varieties appearing in optimization, engineering, statistics, and data science, the defect is substantial.

A pencil of quadric hypersurfaces

Let

be an irreducible complex projective variety in

on

The special member of the pencil corresponds to

Incidence variety of the pencil and the associated vanishing cycles

We fix a Whitney stratification
\mathscr{X}
of

For a generic choice of the weight
\mathbf{w}

Consider the incidence variety of the pencil, that is,

\pi:\widetilde{X}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptp:\widetilde{X}\to X.

We define a regular function

For

as above,
the nearby cycle functor

\psi_{f}:CF(X\setminus X_{\infty})\to CF(X_{0}\setminus X_{\infty})

(1.7)

is defined on the groups of constructible functions

and valued on

For
\alpha\in CF(X\setminus X_{\infty})

\psi_{f}(\alpha)(x):=\chi(\alpha|_{F_{x}}),

Definition 1.5
.

With the notation above, the

of

\varphi_{f}:CF(X\setminus X_{\infty})\to CF(X_{0}\setminus X_{\infty}),\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha\mapsto\psi_{f}(\alpha)-\alpha|_{f^{-1}(0)}.

The definition can be restated
in terms of the quadrics

\varphi_{f}:CF(X\setminus Q_{\mathbf{w}})\to CF((X\cap Q)\setminus Q_{\mathbf{w}}),\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha\mapsto\psi_{f}(\alpha)-\alpha|_{X\cap Q\setminus Q_{\mathbf{w}}}.

When

is the local Euler obstruction function of

,
we have a constructible function

\varphi_{f}(\Eu_{X\setminus Q_{\mathbf{w}}}):(X\cap Q)\setminus Q_{\mathbf{w}}\to\mathbb{Z}.

(1.8)

Next,
for
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}

let
H_{\mathbf{u}}

This constructible function appears in our main result on the ED degree defect when

is a generic hyperplane.
Our result is formulated in terms of the Euler characteristic of a constructible function; we now review its definition.

Fix
\alpha\in CF(Z)
a constructible function on a variety
Z

let
\mathscr{Z}
be a Whitney stratification of
Z
such that

Then, the

of

\chi(\alpha):=\sum_{V\in\mathscr{Z}}\chi(V)\cdot\alpha(V),

where

ED degree defect as a topological invariant

A topological interpretation of the ED degree defect in terms of invariants of singularities of
X\cap Q
was given in
[
15
]
in the case when
X
is a smooth irreducible complex projective variety in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, by using the above pencil of quadrics on

and the associated vanishing cycles. In this paper we extend the results of
[
15
]
to an arbitrary complex projective variety
X
.
A main motivator of this extension is because many models in applications are singular.
As already noticed in
[
15
]
, the ED degree defect can in general be computed much easier than computing
\GED(X)
and
\UED(X)
individually.

With the above assumptions and notations, our first result, generalizing
[

.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be an irreducible complex projective variety, and let
\mathbf{w}
be a generic weight.
Let

Then,

\DED(X)=(-1)^{\dim(X)-1}\chi\big((\varphi_{f}(\Eu_{X\setminus Q_{\mathbf{w}}}))|_{X_{0}\setminus(Q_{\mathbf{w}}\cup H)}\big)

Substituting the identities (

\begin{split}\UED(X)-\GED(X)&=(-1)^{\dim(X)}\left[\chi(\varphi_{f}(\Eu_{X\setminus X_{\infty}}))-\chi(\varphi_{f}(\Eu_{X\setminus X_{\infty}})|_{H})\right]\\
&=(-1)^{\dim(X)}\chi\left(\varphi_{f}(\Eu_{X\setminus X_{\infty}})|_{X_{0}\setminus(X_{\infty}\cup X^{H})}\right),\end{split}

where the last equality uses the fact that

is generically chosen.
∎

Proof of Theorem

This is similar to the proof given in the smooth case in

of (

B_{1}:=\{{\rm Eu}_{{\overline{V}}\setminus(X_{\infty}\cup H)}\mid V\in\mathscr{X}_{0}\}

of constructible functions with support on

Recall that
\mu

for
\mathscr{X}
the Whitney stratification of
X
as before, and we denoted by

which are contained in

For each

Furthermore, by applying Theorem

By combining Theorem

\begin{split}\DED(X)&=(-1)^{\dim(X)-1}\chi\big(\mu\big)\\

(2.7)

In order to find the desired formula for

B_{2}:=\{{1}_{V\setminus(X_{\infty}\cup H)}\mid V\in\mathscr{X}_{0}\}

3
Examples

In this section, we apply the main results
Theorem

1.6
and

to a pair of concrete examples.

Example 3.1
.

Let
X
be the complex projective surface

X=\left\{[x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2}:x_{3}]\in\mathbb{P}^{3}:x_{1}x_{2}+\sqrt{-1}x_{0}x_{2}=x_{3}^{2}\right\}.

The projective variety
X
has an isolated singularity at

p=[1:\sqrt{-1}:0:0],

which lies
on the isotropic quadric.
The intersection of
X
and the isotropic quadric
Q
is transversal everywhere except at
p
.
Thus, we will compute
\DED(X)
using formula (
2.17
).

First, we dehomogenize and consider the affine chart
x_{0}\neq 0
. The affine equation of
X
is

(z_{1}+\sqrt{-1})z_{2}=z_{3}^{2}.

On this affine chart,

f=\frac{1+z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}}{a_{0}+a_{1}z_{1}^{2}+a_{2}z_{2}^{2}+a_{3}z_{3}^{2}}

for some general
a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
with
0\leq i\leq 3
.

Define the map
\theta:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to X
by

\theta:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to X,\hskip 10.00002pt(u,v)\mapsto[1:u^{2}-\sqrt{-1}:v^{2}:uv].

This is a double cover of the above affine open set of
X
, which is only ramified at the singular point

p
.
Thus, to compute the Euler characteristics of the local analytic sets
f^{-1}({{\bf s}})\cap B_{\epsilon}(p)
and
l^{-1}(\delta)\cap B_{\epsilon^{\prime}}(p)
as in formula (
2.17
), we can compute the Euler characteristic of their preimages in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
via
\theta
, and then divide by
2
. Note that none of these two sets contains the singular point
p
, and hence their preimages in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
are unramified double covers.

Since
\theta^{-1}(B_{\epsilon}(p))
is a small regular neighborhood of the origin of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, we see that
\theta^{-1}(f^{-1}({{\bf s}})\cap B_{\epsilon}(p))
is equal to the local Milnor fiber of
f\circ\theta
at the origin. Since

\displaystyle f\circ\theta(u,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+(u^{2}-\sqrt{-1})^{2}+(v^{2})^{2}+(uv)^{2}}{a_{0}+a_{1}(u^{2}-\sqrt{-1})^{2}+a_{2}(v^{2})^{2}+a_{3}(uv)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{u^{4}-2\sqrt{-1}u^{2}+v^{4}+u^{2}v^{2}}{a_{0}+a_{1}(u^{2}-\sqrt{-1})^{2}+a_{2}(v^{2})^{2}+a_{3}(uv)^{2}},

and

\frac{1}{a_{0}+a_{1}(u^{2}-\sqrt{-1})^{2}+a_{2}(v^{2})^{2}+a_{3}(uv)^{2}}

is an invertible function near
p
, the Milnor number of
f\circ\theta
at the origin can be computed algebraically and is equal to
3
. Therefore, the Milnor fiber of
f\circ\theta
at the origin is homotopy equivalent to the wedge of 3 circles. Therefore,

\chi\big(\theta^{-1}\big(f^{-1}({{\bf s}})\cap B_{\epsilon}(p)\big)\big)=-2,

and

\chi\big(f^{-1}({{\bf s}})\cap B_{\epsilon}(p)\big)=-1.

Similarly, we can compute

\chi\big(l^{-1}(\delta)\cap B_{\epsilon^{\prime}}(p)\big)=0.

Therefore, by (
2.17
),

\DED(X)=1.

For this example we find

\GED(X)=10,\hskip 10.00002pt\UED(X)=9.

One way to compute these numbers using symbolic computation over
\mathbb{Q}
is to consider the product of the conjugates

x_{0}^{2}x_{2}^{4}+x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{4}-2x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}^{2}x_{3}^{2}+x_{0}^{2}x_{3}^{4}.

The GED and UED of this new hypersurface is
20
and
18
, respectively. Since the ED degree is additive over irreducible components, the result follows.

\diamond

Example 3.2
.

Let us consider the surface defined by

X=\left\{[x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2}:x_{3}]\in\mathbb{P}^{3}:(x_{1}+\sqrt{-1}x_{0})^{2}x_{0}=x_{2}^{2}x_{3}\right\}.

\displaystyle\DED(X)

\displaystyle=-\varphi_{f}(\Eu_{X\setminus Q_{\mathbf{w}}})(p_{1})

Denote by
X^{\mathrm{aff}}
the subset of

4.1
Structured low rank matrices

We consider examples of
m\times n
structured low rank matrices. Define

X_{m,n,r}\subset\mathbb{P}^{mn-1}
to be the projective variety of matrices of rank at most
r
:

X_{m,n,r}:=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}x_{11}&\dots&x_{1n}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
x_{m1}&\dots&x_{mn}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{P}^{mn-1}:\rank\left([x_{i,j}]_{m\times n}\right)\leq r\right\}.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{P}^{mn-1}
be a coordinate linear subspace, i.e., a linear space defined by setting a subset of the coordinates equal to zero. Then the intersection
\mathcal{L}\cap X_{m,n,r}
consists of matrices of rank at most
r

with a prescribed sparsity pattern given by
\mathcal{L}
.
In general, such intersections need not be irreducible.

When there is no sparsity constraint,
the Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem computes
the unit ED degree of
X_{m,n,r}
by the following closed form formula:

\UED(X_{m,n,r})=\binom{\min\{m,n\}}{r}.

This is presented in
[
7
]
along
with a connection to singular value decomposition (SVD)
and a discussion of
\GED(X_{m,n,r})
. In this setting, SVD also provides the global optimal solution to the ED optimization problem:

\min_{x\in X_{m,n,r}\cap\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}\|x-u\|_{F}^{2},\hskip 10.00002ptu\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},

where
\|x-u\|_{F}:=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(x_{ij}-u_{ij})^{2}}

denotes the Frobenius norm on matrices.
However, once the sparsity constraint is introduced, minimizing the distance function on
\mathcal{L}\cap X_{m,n,r}

is well-known to be difficult
[
12
,
21
]
.
We report computations of the GED, UED, and ED degree defect for such examples.

Example 4.1
.

Let
X_{1}
be the subvariety of
\mathbb{P}^{8}
given by the determinant of

\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}&x_{1}&x_{2}\\
x_{3}&x_{4}&x_{5}\\
x_{6}&x_{7}&x_{8}\end{bmatrix}
.
Next, let
X_{2}=X_{1}\cap V(x_{0})
. That is, we are considering rank at most two matrices where one of the entries is zero.
Then

The singular locus of

is of degree seven in
\mathbb{P}^{8}
.
It consists of three components.
The first two are obtained by intersecting a component of the singular locus of

with

. Namely, the first two components are

V(x_{6},x_{3},x_{0},x_{5}x_{7}-x_{4}x_{8},x_{2}x_{7}-x_{1}x_{8},x_{2}x_{4}-x_{1}x_{5}),\\
V(x_{2},x_{1},x_{0},x_{5}x_{7}-x_{4}x_{8},x_{5}x_{6}-x_{3}x_{8},x_{4}x_{6}-x_{3}x_{7}).

The third irreducible component in the singular locus of

is the linear space

.
Despite knowing this,
applying the formulas in
Section

1.2
remains a challenge. The main difficulty is
understanding how the isotropic quadric intersects

This motivates the study of computing Whitney stratifications of projective varieties intersected with isotropic quadrics, as well as computing the values of the local Euler obstruction along the strata.

\diamond

4.2
Intersecting toric hypersurfaces and the sphere

Toric varieties appear across applications
[
20
, Chapter 8]
such as phylogenetics, reaction networks, and algebraic statistics. One reason for their prevalence is because many models are given by monomial maps
naturally leading to a toric variety.

In this section we consider examples of ED degrees of toric hypersurfaces and their restrictions to the sphere.
One way that these varieties arise is by homogenizing an affine toric variety and then restricting to the sphere.
A known result concerns the ED degree of hypersurfaces defined by a sparse polynomial (i.e., with prescribed monomial support and generic coefficients)
[
5
]
:
in this setting the ED degree is given by the mixed volume of the associated Lagrange multiplier system.
When an irreducible hypersurface is defined by a
binomial, it is a toric hypersurface.
