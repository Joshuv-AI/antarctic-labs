Prenatal metabolic adversity reprograms insulin-responsive transcription in the developing nucleus accumbens
Authors: Bonnie Alberry, Barbara Barth, Aashita Batra, Marcio Bonesso Alves, Patricia Maidana Miguel, Nicholas O'Toole, Sachin Patel, Derek Lupinsky, Tie Yuan Zhang, Xianglan Wen, Danusa Mar Arcego, Daniela Pereira Laureano, Irina Pokhvisneva, Roberta Dalle Molle, Patricia Pelufo Silveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrenatal metabolic adversity, including fetal growth restriction (FR), programs long-term alterations in systemic and neural insulin sensitivity, yet its impact on insulin signaling within reward-circuit plasticity across development remains poorly understood. Using a rodent model of gestational FR, we examined how early metabolic stress alters insulin regulation of mesolimbic reward circuits using in vivo chronoamperometry to measure nucleus accumbens (NAc) dopamine (DA) release during palatable food exposure, with and without peripheral insulin. We assessed longitudinal consumption behavior and conducted transcriptomic profiling (RNA-Seq) at birth (P0), weaning (P21), and adulthood (P90) following saline or insulin administration. FR blunted immediate NAc DA release in response to palatable food, a deficit specifically reversed by peripheral insulin, indicating altered insulin sensitivity of mesolimbic reward circuits. FR animals also display accelerated initial palatable food consumption. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that FR reprograms the NAc's molecular response to insulin. Across development and sex, only 2–9% of insulin-responsive genes overlap between FR and controls. FR generated condition-specific and frequently inverted transcriptional signatures, affecting genes linked to synaptic plasticity (Cplx3, Rab3b) and neurodevelopment (Ccn3). These findings demonstrate that prenatal adversity reconfigures the NAc by altering its molecular and neurochemical responsiveness to insulin. This developmental reprogramming reveals how early metabolic stress reshapes insulin sensitivity within reward circuitry, a mechanism that may contribute to both metabolic and psychiatric disease vulnerability.Keywords: Growth restriction, Nucleus accumbens, Dopamine, Insulin signaling, Transcriptomics, Animal model

Introduction

Globally, nearly one in four newborns experience significant prenatal adversity, placing them at heightened risk for long-term health challenges regardless of a country's economic status [1,2]. Prenatal adversity frequently impairs fetal growth, an indicator of poor prenatal environmental quality, and is associated with persistent increases in morbimortality across the life course [2]. Prenatal metabolic adversity, particularly fetal growth restriction (FR), is a major driver of developmental programming of metabolic disease. Individuals born small for gestational age have early alterations in insulin sensitivity that may precede later metabolic dysfunction [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Exposure to prenatal adversity increases susceptibility to psychiatric disorders [7] and chronic diseases in adulthood [8], including type 2 diabetes (T2D) [9], as well as their comorbidity [10]. Prenatal adversity contributes to over 18% of T2D prevalence in aging men [11], with postnatal catch-up growth further compounding metabolic risk in both sexes [12,13]. Despite these well-established associations, the underlying biological mechanisms linking early adversity to later neuropsychiatric and metabolic outcomes remain poorly understood.

One emerging pathway involves the temporal evolution of insulin sensitivity. Newborns born small for gestational age (SGA) have poor pancreatic development, lower insulin secretion, and increased insulin sensitivity at birth [3], which induces rapid catch-up growth [4] and early insulin resistance [3,5]. These changes occur during a sensitive period of development, inducing long-lasting neuroadaptations. Brain insulin signaling influences synaptogenesis [14], plasticity [15], neuroprotection [16], cognition [17], and behaviors related to energy balance and food intake [[18], [19], [20]].

As insulin sensitivity deteriorates over time in SGA individuals, parallel behavioral changes emerge. Gestational food restriction is linked to altered sucrose taste reactivity at birth [21], impulsivity towards sweet treats in infancy [22], and a preference for cheaper, less nutritious foods in adolescence [23], as well as differential brain activation in response to palatable food images [24]. These tendencies culminate in a preference for fats and sugars later in life [[25], [26], [27]]. Though subtle, these behavioral shifts can have compounding metabolic consequences, suggesting a feed-forward mechanism where altered insulin sensitivity influences neurodevelopment and eating behavior, which in turn exacerbates the metabolic disarrangement and further impacts brain function [10,28].

Brain insulin signaling may mediate these effects [29], yet its molecular dynamics over the lifespan in FR individuals remain unclear. The nucleus accumbens (NAc), a critical part of the mesolimbic dopamine (DA) reward pathway [30], plays a central role in processing the hedonic aspects of food. Peripheral insulin modulates NAc activity, influencing food-related behaviors, impulsivity, and mood [31]. NAc insulin affects DA transporter availability [31] and astrocytic insulin receptors regulate DA release, with disruptions linked to anxiety- and depressive-like behavior in mice [32]. In FR rodent models, altered NAc DA neurotransmission influences palatable food intake [33]. Similar to humans born SGA, FR rodents show an increased preference for sweet rewards [34], have altered hedonic taste reactivity to sweet foods [35], and increased palatable food intake [36]. NAc DA release in response to sweet food is delayed in FR animals, and normalized by peripheral insulin administration [37], providing direct evidence for the interplay between peripheral insulin and NAc responsivity in the context of food reward.

Despite these insights, the immediate behavioral and neurochemical responses to palatable food and insulin in FR individuals are empirically lacking. Altered insulin sensitivity may reshape NAc reward circuitry, driving both metabolic and behavioral disruptions in an amplifying, self-reinforcing cascade. To address this gap, we aim to: (1) characterize second-by-second behavioral and neurochemical responses to palatable food in FR animals; (2) assess immediate NAc DA response to peripheral insulin administration in FR individuals facing palatable food; (3) investigate transcriptomic changes in the NAc across key developmental stages (birth (P0), weaning (P21), and adulthood (P90)) following FR and insulin administration. We hypothesize that insulin critically modulates NAc development and function, shaping eating behavior trajectories in animals exposed to fetal growth restriction. To test this hypothesis, we used a combination of in vivo neurochemistry, behavioral phenotyping, and longitudinal transcriptomic profiling.

Materials and methods

Animals

Adult Sprague Dawley rats were obtained from Charles River Canada (St. Constant, QC) or CEMIB Laboratory Animal Reference Center (Campinas, SP, Brazil) and housed two per cage in standard conditions (12h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, ad libitum food). After a two-week acclimation, ∼75-day-old primiparous females were time-mated, with sperm-positive vaginal smears confirming gestational day 1 (G1). Pregnant females were individually housed and randomly assigned on G10 to either a control group with ad libitum food (Adlib) or a food-restricted group (FR) receiving 50% of normal caloric intake until term [38]. To determine normal intake, food was weighed daily in a cohort of seven pregnant females, and the FR group was provided 50% of this amount by mass each day. Within 24 h of birth, all litters were culled to eight pups (four males, four females) to standardize litter size. All litters were cross-fostered to Adlib dams to isolate gestational food restriction effects. Offspring from both groups were used in three independent experiments (Figure 1A): 1) DA release in the NAc during palatable food consumption following insulin injection (N = 17 litters) (Figure 1B); 2) palatable food consumption behavior (N = 25 litters); 3) gene expression in the NAc at birth (P0), postnatal day 21 (P21), and adulthood (P90) (N = 41 litters). Each animal participated in only one experiment to avoid cross-study confounds, with no more than two per sex per litter in each experiment to minimize potential litter effects. All procedures adhered to national guidelines (Canada, Brazil) and were approved by the Douglas Mental Health University Institute (AUP DOUG-8018) or the Research Ethics Committee of the Hospital de Clínicas de Porto Alegre (GPPG/HCPA, project numbers 13–0264, 120353).

Chronoamperometry

At P21, rats were weaned, sex-separated, and housed four per cage. Only males were used for this experiment. The surgical demands of the protocol (stereotaxic electrode implantation, multi-day post-operative recovery, and multi-day voltammetry recording sessions) constrained feasible sample sizes. Dividing the chronoamperometry cohort by sex may have produced an underpowered experiment. Prioritizing males for the neurochemical experiment allowed us to achieve a statistically interpretable sample size within these constraints. Electrochemical probes comprised three 30 μm carbon fibers (Textron Systems, Wilmington, MA) extending 50–100 μm beyond a pulled glass capillary tip (0.5 mm outer diameter), coated with 5% Nafion® (Sigma–Aldrich, 274704). Electrodes were calibrated in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4) for DA sensitivity and selectivity, yielding a DA:ascorbic acid ratio of 1619:1 ± 133:1 (mean ± SEM) [37].

Between P80-120, rats underwent stereotaxic surgery under ketamine (40 mg/kg, i.p.), xylazine (5 mg/kg, i.p.), and isoflurane anesthesia. Tramadol (5 mg/kg pre-op; 2.5 mg/kg post-operation (post-op), i.p.) and cetoprofen (5 mg/kg daily for two days post-op, i.p.) were administered for analgesia. Bupivacaine (40 μl) with epinephrine (0.2 mg) was injected subcutaneously at the surgical site. Probes were implanted in the right NAc shell (1.2 mm anterior to bregma, 0.8 mm lateral, 7.0 mm ventral) [39], with an Ag/AgCl reference electrode in the contralateral parietal cortex. Electrodes were soldered to miniature pin connectors (9-pin ABS Plug, Ginger Scientific, GS09PLG-220) and secured with dental acrylic and four stainless steel cranial screws [37,39]. Animals recovered on a heating pad (2–5 h) before returning to their cages.

DA detection used a high-speed chronoamperometric apparatus (Quanteon, Lexington, KY). An oxidation potential of +0.55 mV was applied at 2 Hz for 100 ms. Oxidation current was integrated during the last 80 ms of each pulse. Every 10 cycles were summed and converted to DA concentration (μM) using in vitro calibration. Reduction currents at 0 V (100 ms) were digitized to identify electroactive species, with DA-induced reduction typically 60–80% of the oxidation signal [39,40].

To reduce food neophobia, rats received 6 Froot Loops® pellets in their home cage one day post-op (Figure 1B). The next day, they received 6 pellets in a voltammetry cabinet (60 × 60 × 60 cm) with a cable port and glass front, followed by 1 h habituation. On day 3, rats were recorded for 2 h to stabilize DA signals, then given 30 g of chow. After food removal, they remained in the cabinet for 1 h before receiving 15 Froot Loops® pellets for 30 min, with an additional hour of recording. On day 4, rats acclimated for 2 h, received a saline injection (100 μL, i.p.), then 15 pellets after 30 min, and were recorded for 30 min post-food removal. On day 5, the same protocol was followed with insulin (5UI/kg in saline, 100 μL total volume, i.p.) instead of saline. On day 6, electrode placement was histologically validated via lesion (+4.5 DC), followed by decapitation and brain extraction (reported in [37]). Brains were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde, 24 h), cryoprotected (30% sucrose), frozen in isopentane, sectioned (50 μm; cryostat (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), and stained with hematoxylin-eosin. Rats with incorrect placements were excluded (n = 5), yielding final group sizes of n = 5 (Adlib) and n = 4 (FR).

Statistical analysis was performed in R [41]. Mean and SEM were calculated per time point and group. A linear mixed model with random intercepts for animal assessed effects of FR, insulin, and time on DA response, including interactions. Pairwise estimated marginal means contrasts assessed the FR-insulin interaction, with significance at p < 0.05.

Palatable food consumption

At ∼ P80, rats (n = 30) were individually housed in cages equipped with BioDAQ® monitoring systems (Research Diets), featuring two food hoppers mounted on strain gauge-based load cells. Hopper weights were sampled 50 times per second (50 Hz; consistent with BioDAQ® system specifications), with mean and SD calculated per second, as previously described [36,37,42,43]. Feeding events were categorized as bouts (weight change >2000 mg, ending after 5 s of inactivity) or meals (>0.1 g intake, separated by > 15 min) [44,45].

From day 0 (light phase), rats had ad libitum access to standard chow (2.95 kcal/g; 15% protein, 12% fat, 73% carbohydrate; NUVILAB) and water during a 4-day habituation period. Rats that did not eat on any habituation day were excluded (n = 3). On day 4, animals were offered a choice between standard chow and a highly palatable diet (4.59 kcal/g, 25% protein, 23% fat, 47% carbohydrate (20% from sucrose); Prag Soluções, SP, Brazil). To reduce neophobia, small amounts of the palatable diet were introduced two days prior.

Linear models explored the effect of sex and group, with interaction, on body-weight-adjusted total consumption and eating rate in the first 30 min. We followed significant interactions with pairwise estimated marginal means contrasts. To further characterize consumption patterns, a k-means clustering approach for longitudinal data (KmL) [46] was applied to cumulative palatable food intake during the first 30 min in R. Three animals were excluded for non-consumption. Five models (2–6 clusters) were run twenty times each. A two-cluster solution was selected for its stability, balanced size, and interpretability (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Cluster distributions were analyzed using Pearson's Chi-square tests to assess Group (Adlib vs. FR) and Sex (Male vs. Female) effects (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0.1.

RNA extraction and RNA-sequencing library preparation

Brains were collected at three points: P0, P21, and P90. At P0, litters were standardized to 4 males and 4 females, with culled pups used for tissue collection. Subjects received either saline (1 mL/kg) or insulin (5 IU/kg in saline) i.p. injections and were sacrificed 30 min later by decapitation. Brains were rapidly removed, the striatum dissected on ice, and frozen at −80 °C. A subset of pups remained until P21, then received the same treatment (saline or insulin, i.p.), followed by sacrifice after 30 min. Brains were flash-frozen in isopentane and stored at −80 °C. NAc coronal sections (200 μm) were collected using a 0.8 mm tissue punch. Another cohort was weaned at P21, sex-separated, and housed in standard conditions until P90. At P90, they received either saline or insulin (i.p.) and were sacrificed 30 min later. Brains were flash-frozen in isopentane and stored at −80 °C. NAc punches (200 μm, 0.8 mm diameter) were collected as above.

Total RNA was isolated using Qiazol reagent and purified with RNeasy Micro kits (Qiagen) with on-column DNase I digestion. RNA quality was assessed via NanoPhotometer P-Class (Implen GmbH, Munich, Germany), with all samples showing 260/280 ratios >1.9. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using Illumina TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Gold (48 Samples)-Illumina (Cat#20020598). Ten samples per group across all 24 groups (2 sexes x 3 developmental timepoints x 4 treatment conditions) were sent for whole-transcriptome sequencing at the Genome Quebec Innovation Centre using an Illumina HiSeq 4000 PE100 sequencer.

RNA-seq differential gene expression

RNA-seq reads were aligned to the Rattus norvegicus rn6 genome using STAR, with gene-level counts generated using the “quantMode GeneCounts” option. Principal component analysis did not identify any outliers to exclude. Normalization and differential expression analysis were performed using edgeR across three comparisons: FR + Saline vs Adlib + Saline (FR), Adlib + Insulin vs Adlib + Saline (INS), FR + Insulin vs FR + Saline (FRINS) in males and females separately at three time points, totalling 18 comparisons. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as p < 0.05 to capture broad expression patterns, with a fold-change threshold >30%. Analyses were limited to protein-coding genes. Cell type enrichment was performed using gene lists, where a gene was considered cell-type-specific if its average expression exceeded 10x the background expression across all other cell types in the same region [47]. Enrichment was quantified via odds ratio using GeneOverlap [48]. Gene ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analyses for DEGs were conducted using MetaCore® (Clarivate; https://portal.genego.com).

Rank–rank hypergeometric overlap (RRHO)

Unfiltered expression lists were ranked by degree of differential expression (DDE), where DDE represents the -log10(p-value) multiplied by the fold change sign. RRHO maps were produced for each sex and time point to compare FR-saline vs. Adlib-saline, compared to Adlib-insulin vs Adlib-saline, and FR-insulin vs FR-saline, compared to Adlib-insulin vs. Adlib-saline.

Results

Prenatal growth restriction blunts NAc DA release in response to palatable food, which is reversed by insulin

Similar to findings in humans, FR in Sprague–Dawley rats induces peripheral insulin resistance [37,49] and increases preference for palatable foods in adulthood [36,37]. As systemic insulin resistance alters brain responsivity to food cues [20,50], and modulates DA neurotransmission [51], we investigated how FR affects DA release in response to palatable food presentation under basal metabolic conditions and following peripheral insulin administration (Figure 1A). Adult rats previously exposed to FR or Adlib controls received insulin or saline i.p. injections 30 min before in vivo chronoamperometry (Figure 1B). DA concentrations were recorded at the sub-second resolution following palatable food presentation. FR significantly blunted the immediate DA release in response to palatable food, but this reduction was reversed by insulin administration (Figure 1C). A linear mixed model with a random intercept for subject revealed a significant main effect of time (F(1,85733) = 27132.15, p < 2.20 x 10−16), but no significant main effects of FR or insulin alone. However, there were significant interactions between FR and insulin (F(1,85733) = 22.78, p = 1.82 x 10−6), FR and time (F(1,85733) = 2287.13, p < 2.20 x 10−16), insulin and time (F(1,85734) = 6714.47, p < 2.20 x 10−16), and a three-way interaction between FR, insulin, and time (F(1,85734) = 4998.96, p < 2.20 x 10−16). In FR animals, the effect of insulin on NAc DA release was substantially larger in magnitude than in Adlib controls (FR: estimate = 13.6 μM, SE = 0.09, p < 2.20 × 10−16; Adlib: estimate = 1.6 μM, SE = 0.07, p < 2.20 × 10−16), consistent with a more pronounced sensitivity of the FR NAc to peripheral insulin when dopamine release has been blunted under basal conditions. Under insulin treatment, there was no significant difference between FR and Adlib animals (estimate = 1.32 μM, SE = 5.85, p = 0.82), although Adlib animals tended to have higher DA response than FR under saline, this difference did not reach significance (estimate = 10.66 μM, SE = 5.85, p = 0.068). These findings confirm that FR blunts the NAc DA release in response to palatable food, consistent with previous reports of delayed DA peak [37], and reduced DA turnover following prenatal stress [52]. Importantly, insulin restored DA release in FR animals, supporting the well-known role of peripheral insulin in modulating DA neurotransmission [53]. Additional parameters characterising the temporal dynamics of the DA response to palatable food in this model, including delayed peak DA release in FR animals, have been reported previously using the same electrode protocol and animal model [37], and are consistent with the blunted immediate release we describe here.

Prenatal growth restriction increases palatable food intake immediately after presentation

We have previously shown that FR rats consume more calories from palatable foods than standard rat chow over four days [36]. Here, we investigated the second-by-second eating behavior during the first 30 min of palatable food presentation (Figure 1D). Total consumption (body weight-adjusted) did not differ by sex or group (Supplementary Fig. 1a). However, the eating rate showed a significant main effect of sex (F(3,25) = 11.02, p = 1.89 x 10−5) and a sex-by-group interaction (p = 0.0498; Supplementary Fig. 1b). Females ate faster than males in both Adlib (p = 5.80 x 10−5) and FR groups (p = 0.035). To explore individual consumption patterns, we clustered trajectories into high (Cluster A) and low (Cluster B) consumption groups (Supplementary Fig. 1c). FR animals were more likely to be in Cluster A, though this trend did not reach statistical significance (χ2(1, N = 29) = 4.44, p = 0.06; Supplementary Fig. 1d). Within Cluster A, 64.7% were FR and 35.3% were Adlib; in Cluster B, it was 25% FR and 75% Adlib. A separate Chi-square test showed no significant sex difference in cluster membership (χ2(1, N = 29) = 0.024, p = 1; Cluster A: 47.1% Female vs. 52.9% Male; Cluster B: 50% Female vs. 50% Male). Adjusted for body weight, Cluster A animals consumed significantly more than Cluster B (p = 3.47 x 10−5, Supplementary Fig. 1e), though eating rate did not differ between clusters (p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 1f). These findings reinforce previous descriptions that FR increases palatable food consumption [36], and this effect is sex specific.

Insulin-associated transcriptomic changes in the NAc depend on prenatal growth restriction

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying these behavioural and dopaminergic changes, we examined transcriptional changes in the NAc following FR and insulin administration at three developmental stages: birth (P0), weaning (P21), and adulthood (P90)(Supplementary Data 1). Thirty minutes before brain collection, animals received either insulin or saline i.p. injections, followed by bulk RNA-sequencing of the NAc. DEGs were identified using a nominal p < 0.05 and a |logFC| > 0.3785, corresponding to a fold change >30% [54]. Across all time points and sexes, only 2–9% of insulin-responsive DEGs overlapped between FRINS and INS comparisons (Figure 2A,D, I, L, Q, T), indicating that insulin exerts distinct and non-overlapping transcriptional effects in FR animals compared to controls. To investigate cell-type specificity, we conducted enrichment analysis using brain-region-specific cell marker lists [47]. This revealed spiny neuron gene enrichment in P0 female FR and INS DEGs, but not in FRINS, again suggesting a differential molecular response to insulin in FR and Adlib animals. Additionally, microglia-specific gene enrichment was observed in P21 male FRINS and P90 female FR (Figure 2B,C, J, K, R, S). These findings align with prior evidence that insulin receptor activation in NAc spiny neurons influences local neurotransmission [55] and that insulin acutely regulates gene expression in different NAc cell types, including microglia [56].

We used two-sided RRHO analysis to assess genome-wide transcriptional overlap between the FR, INS, and FRINS at each sex and age [57]. FR and INS responses showed directional overlap, with the strongest co-expression alignment observed at P0 in females (Figure 2E,F, M, N, U, V). However, the FRINS diverged from INS, showing distinct and unrelated directionality. Union heatmaps sorted by FR-regulated fold changes confirmed this pattern: FR and insulin share directional similarities, but FRINS is reversed across time points (Figure 2G,H, O, P, W, X). These results indicate that FR alters the transcriptional response to insulin in the NAc in a sex- and age-specific manner.

Analysis of the most statistically significant gene expression changes revealed that they occurred at P0 and P21 in females following FR (Supplementary Fig. 2). At P0, FR females had upregulation of a few genes (Figure 3A), including Gcgr (glucagon receptor), whose deletion in mice improves glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity [58], suggesting its upregulation may reflect insulin dysregulation in FR females. A larger set of genes was downregulated, including Gabra3 (Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid Type A Receptor Subunit Alpha3), Galr1 (Galanin Receptor 1), and Rab3b (Ras-Related Protein Rab-3B). Gabra3 knockout reduces GABA-induced currents in midbrain DA neurons [59]. Galanin enhances NAc DA release, fat consumption, and the rewarding aspects of its consumption [60]. Rab3b is a monomeric GTPase highly enriched in synaptic vesicles, involved in vesicle trafficking and synaptic transmission [61], and has been shown to enhance DA storage when overexpressed [62]. These most significant DEGs (FDR q < 0.05, |logFC| > 0.3875) in FR females at P0 were enriched for “Transport_RAB3B regulation” pathway (Figure 3B,C) and GO terms including “response to glucagon” and “regulation of short-term neuronal synaptic plasticity” (Figure 3D), aligning with reduced DA release observed in FR animals. The convergent downregulation of Rab3b, Gabra3, and Galr1 in P0 FR females is mechanistically coherent with the blunted adult DA release we observe by chronoamperometry. Rab3b loss would impair synaptic vesicle trafficking and reduce dopamine storage capacity; Gabra3 downregulation would reduce GABAergic inhibition of midbrain dopamine neurons, potentially altering tonic DA tone; and Galr1 reduction would attenuate galanin-mediated potentiation of NAc DA release. Together, these early transcriptional changes may constitute a developmental substrate for the neurochemical phenotype observed in adulthood, suggesting that the molecular programming of reward circuitry begins before birth in FR animals.

At P21, FR females had downregulated Car3 (carbonic anhydrase III, Supplementary Fig. 2), a gene involved in neuronal excitability and previously implicated in prenatal adversity [63]. To assess transcriptomic dynamics, we examined DEG directionality across P0, P21, and P90 (Figure 4). Few genes were consistently differentially expressed (Figure 4A), indicating age- and sex-specific responses. In FR females, genes unchanged at P21 remain stable into adulthood, while in FR males, many became upregulated by P90. Insulin-treated animals (INS and FRINS) showed minimal changes at P0, but greater dysregulation emerged by P21, with further shifts by P90. DEG overlap between sexes was limited at each age and treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3), highlighting the sex-specific transcriptional responses to FR and insulin.

DEG overlap across treatment combinations and age of assessment. (a) Number of DEGs either increased or decreased following each treatment across the age of evaluation. (b) Two genes, Ccn3 and Cplx3, are repeatedly differentially expressed across treatments and ages in both males and females.

To identify temporally unstable genes, we examined overlap across all 18 group comparisons and found Ccn3 (cellular communication network factor 3) and Cplx3 (Complexin 3) to be the most frequently differentially expressed (Figure 4B). Ccn3 (also known as IGFBP-9) was downregulated by FR at P0 (females) and P21 (males), and by INS and FRINS at P90 (males), but upregulated by FR and INS at P21 (females), and by FR at P90 in both sexes. Cplx3 had a similar bidirectional profile, downregulated by FR at P21 (males) and FRINS at P90 (males), but upregulated under multiple conditions at P21 and by FR at P90 in both sexes. These patterns suggest both genes are sensitive to early-life metabolic disruption. While not a classical IGFBP, IGFBP-9 (Ccn3) is developmentally regulated in the rat CNS [64] and linked to insulin resistance in childhood obesity [65]. Cplx3 regulates SNARE-mediated synaptic vesicle fusion [66], crucial for neurotransmitter release.

Prenatal growth restriction primes a subset of genes for altered response to insulin

To assess interactions between FR and insulin response, we created four broad categories that describe whether gene expression changes are driven by FR alone, insulin alone, either treatment, or interactions between them (Supplementary Data 2). Categories 1 (FR) and 2 (Insulin) capture simple, single-treatment effects. Category 3 captures shared sensitivity, or genes that respond in the same way to FR or Insulin. Finally, category 4 characterizes synergistic or opposing effects when both treatments are combined. Together, they map the full landscape of FR and Insulin response in this data.

FR not only alters overall gene expression but also reprograms the NAc's response to peripheral insulin across development. This priming effect is characterized by subsets of insulin-responsive genes with reversed expression in FR animals, or genes typically upregulated by insulin in controls are downregulated in FR animals, and vice versa.

To identify these genes, we focused on those differentially expressed in Adlib following insulin (INS DEGs), and with opposite changes in FR animals after insulin (FRINS DEGs), excluding genes altered by FR alone (FR DEGs). These genes fall into two categories (Supplementary Fig. 4): genes typically upregulated by insulin but downregulated in FR + insulin (4G), and those downregulated by insulin but upregulated in FR + insulin (4H). While such reversals occurred at all developmental stages, there was no consistent overlap across the timepoints or sexes.

At birth (P0), FR males show reversed expression of genes involved in gonadotropin release (p = 2.25 × 10−3), including Jun and Fst (Supplementary Fig. 5). In FR females, affected genes are associated with growth hormone-mediated development (p = 1.95 × 10−4), insulin-like growth factor signaling (p = 6.41 × 10−5), and direct insulin response (p = 2.70 × 10−4), including Gh1, Ghrhr, and Gal (Supplementary Fig. 6).

At weaning (P21), FR males have reversals in genes central to T2D (p = 3.39 × 10−6), growth (p = 3.18 × 10−17), and obesity (p = 3.88 × 10−15), including Gcg, Igfbp6, Bdkrb2, and Gdf15 (Supplementary Fig. 7). In FR females, reversed genes are linked to glucocorticoid receptor signaling (p = 3.39 × 10−5) and obesity (p = 3.07 × 10−4), including Cebpb and Fas (Supplementary Fig. 8).

In adulthood (P90), FR males show reversals in genes involved in T2D and metabolic syndrome treatment (p = 6.90 × 10−4) and obesity (p = 8.59 × 10−4), such as Nr1h3 and Pparg, along with neuropeptide signaling genes (p = 1.88 × 10−4; Gal, Ccr7, Adcyap1) (Supplementary Fig. 9). In FR females, reversed genes are enriched for immune response (p = 2.61 × 10−6) and inflammation (p = 2.50 × 10−6), including Creb3l4, Birc3, Cyp19a1, Pglyrp1, and Pla2g5 (Supplementary Fig. 10).

FR primes the NAc for a reversed transcriptional response to insulin. This reprogramming is dynamic, unique to each sex and developmental stage, and has direct implications for lifelong metabolic and inflammatory processes.

Together, these three experimental approaches provide convergent evidence for a coherent mechanistic sequence. The chronoamperometry data demonstrate that FR blunts the immediate dopaminergic response to palatable food in adulthood, a deficit rescued by peripheral insulin. The consumption data reveal that FR accelerates initial palatable food intake, a pattern consistent with diminished hedonic DA signaling driving compensatory overconsumption. The transcriptomic data provide the molecular substrate underlying both: FR fundamentally reprograms the NAc's insulin-responsive gene expression from birth onward, generating a shifted molecular state in which normal insulin-DA interactions are replaced by condition-specific and frequently inverted transcriptional patterns. Although the three experiments were conducted in separate animals, the convergence of their findings supports a unifying model in which prenatal metabolic adversity reconfigures NAc circuitry through transcriptional reprogramming, with functional consequences detectable across neurochemical, behavioral, and molecular levels of analysis.

Discussion

This study tests the hypothesis that prenatal metabolic adversity via gestational food restriction disrupts insulin signaling within the mesolimbic reward circuit, producing durable alterations in the neurochemical and transcriptional responsiveness of the nucleus accumbens across development. We propose a mechanistic sequence in which FR-induced insulin dysregulation in the NAc blunts the dopaminergic response to palatable food, driving compensatory increases in palatable food consumption. In parallel, FR reprograms the molecular machinery supporting insulin-DA interactions from the earliest postnatal stages, generating a fundamentally reconfigured signaling landscape that persists into adulthood in a sex- and age-specific manner. Our findings support each element of this sequence and reveal that the molecular reprogramming precedes adult behavioral and neurochemical phenotypes, establishing early transcriptional programming as a plausible mechanism through which prenatal adversity confers lasting vulnerability to metabolic and psychiatric disease.

FR blunts immediate NAc DA release in response to palatable food, a deficit reversed by peripheral insulin, supporting prior evidence of delayed DA peaks after palatable food [37] and reduced turnover [52], while reinforcing insulin's role in modulating DA transmission [53]. FR also increases immediate palatable food consumption, consistent with previous findings [36]. This behavioral change is consistent with disrupted hedonic processing and altered DA signaling in the NAc, and convergently supports the neurochemical and transcriptomic findings in independent cohorts: NAc in which DA release is blunted and insulin-responsive transcription is reprogrammed from birth is expected to also have diminished reward signaling in response to palatable food, potentially driving compensatory increases in consumption. It should be noted that the consumption experiment was conducted without concurrent insulin manipulation, meaning individual consumption patterns cannot be directly linked to insulin signaling status or to the transcriptomic profiles in the current dataset; a within-animal assessment of insulin's effect on feeding behavior in FR animals represents an important direction for future work. These results support the concept that the brain undergoes developmental programming of insulin sensitivity. While insulin resistance in peripheral tissues such as liver, muscle, and adipose tissue has been extensively characterized [67], comparatively little is known about how early metabolic stress shapes insulin responsiveness within neural circuits that regulate reward and feeding behaviour. The NAc is increasingly recognized as a metabolically responsive structure in which insulin modulates dopamine transmission and reward-related behaviour [68]. Our results suggest that prenatal metabolic stress establishes a long-lasting shift in insulin sensitivity within the NAc, reflected in altered dopamine response and transcriptional reprogramming.

Transcriptomic analyses reveal that FR reprograms the NAc's insulin response, producing distinct and often reversed transcriptional patterns compared to controls. Only a small fraction of insulin-responsive genes overlaps between FR and Adlib animals, indicating a fundamental shift in cellular response. These changes are highly sex- and age-specific, with minimal overlap across timepoints or between sexes. Cell-type enrichment analyses support this divergence, with spiny neuron gene enrichment in P0 female FR and insulin response, and microglia-specific gene enrichment in P21 male FRINS and P90 female FR. These findings align with previous work identifying spiny neuron insulin receptor activation in the NAc [55], as well as distinct gene regulation by peripheral insulin in neurons and microglia [56].

The pattern of inverted, rather than absent, transcriptional responses to insulin in FR animals warrants mechanistic consideration. Classical peripheral insulin resistance is characterised primarily by attenuated downstream signaling and might be expected to produce fewer insulin-responsive genes. Our RRHO analyses reveal that the transcriptional profile of FR-saline animals partially overlaps with that of Adlib-insulin animals, suggesting that FR may have already established a chronically insulin-like molecular state. Under this interpretation, the inverted response to acute insulin challenge in FR animals may reflect a compensatory reversal in a system that has adapted to sustained insulin-like signaling pressure during the postnatal catch-up growth period and not a simple failure of insulin responsiveness. We characterise our findings as evidence of central insulin dysregulation rather than resistance specifically.

The observation that females carry the highest transcriptional burden at birth, with the largest number of FR-associated DEGs at P0, appears to contradict the view that males are more vulnerable to prenatal adversity [69]. This framing obscures an important distinction that vulnerability at the level of phenotypic outcome is not equivalent to vulnerability at the level of early transcriptional response. The epidemiological and preclinical evidence for increased male vulnerability to prenatal adversity derives predominantly from postnatal phenotypic outcomes, including physical performance [70], cardiovascular reactivity [71], HPA axis reactivity [72], and behavioral dysregulation [72], rather than from transcriptome-level responses at birth. A larger early transcriptional response in females may represent an adaptive, compensatory reaction to FR rather than evidence of greater pathological susceptibility. This interpretation is consistent with the observation that females show greater transcriptional plasticity in response to early adversity in the hippocampus and frontal cortex [73], and that this early molecular mobilization may partially protect against later behavioral and metabolic phenotypes, although prenatal stress can cause still permanent alterations in HPA axis reactivity in female rats [74]. Conversely, males with a comparatively muted transcriptional response at P0 may achieve greater phenotypic disruption through a smaller set of highly penetrant gene changes, a pattern consistent with the male-biased neurodevelopmental vulnerability hypothesis [75]. Sex differences in the timing and character of transcriptional response to early adversity likely reflect distinct regulatory strategies, rather than a difference in vulnerability.

Gene-level changes offer mechanistic insight into how FR reprograms NAc function from the earliest developmental stages. In P0 FR females, upregulation of Gcgr (glucagon receptor) may signal early insulin dysregulation, as glucagon in the brain suppresses food intake like central insulin [76], and GCGR is expressed in cortical and subcortical regions where impaired signaling is associated with diminished cognitive function [77]. Downregulation of Gabra3 and the Rab3b pathway provides a mechanistically coherent substrate for the blunted adult DA release observed by chronoamperometry. Loss of Gabra3, which encodes a GABA-A receptor subunit, impairs GABAergic currents in midbrain dopamine neurons [59], and would alter tonic inhibitory regulation of dopaminergic output. Rab3b downregulation would reduce the vesicle trafficking capacity required for DA storage and release, given its role as a monomeric GTPase enriched in synaptic vesicles and its ability to enhance DA storage when overexpressed [62]. Downregulation of Galr1 further predisposes FR females to altered hedonic processing: galanin acting through GALR1 potentiates NAc DA release and enhances the rewarding aspects of palatable food consumption [60], so its loss would be expected to attenuate the motivational salience of food reward. The convergent downregulation of these three genes at birth thus anticipates, and may mechanistically contribute to, the blunted immediate DA response and accelerated palatable food consumption observed in adult FR animals, representing a prenatal molecular fingerprint of the adult neurochemical and behavioral phenotype.

At P21, Car3 downregulation in FR females suggests altered neuronal excitability. Across development, FR animals have reversed insulin responses in genes critical for T2D, growth, obesity, and neuropeptide signaling pathways were reversed in their insulin response. In FR females, reversed insulin responses included genes related to growth hormone-mediated development, insulin-like growth factor signaling (P0), metabolism via glucocorticoid receptor signaling (P21), and immune response and inflammation (P90). This demonstrates that FR primes the NAc for altered insulin sensitivity, with direct implications for lifelong metabolic and behavioral regulation.

Two genes, Ccn3 and Cplx3, emerged as the most temporally unstable, repeatedly differentially expressed across stages and treatments. Their bidirectional, context-dependent expression across development suggests they function as molecular sensors of the metabolic state established by early adversity rather than as unidirectionally dysregulated targets. Ccn3 (NOV/IGFBP-9) is a matricellular protein linked to insulin resistance in childhood obesity [65] and adipose tissue insulin signaling [78] and is developmentally regulated in the rat central nervous system [64]. Its repeated differential expression across FR, INS, and FRINS conditions at multiple ages suggests it may function as a molecular bridge between the metabolic and neuromodulatory consequences of prenatal adversity. Cplx3, encoding Complexin 3, regulates SNARE-mediated synaptic vesicle fusion [66] and is essential for neurotransmitter release [79]. Its repeated dysregulation across developmental stages and treatment conditions in both sexes positions it as a candidate mediator of the altered synaptic function that underlies the FR neurochemical phenotype.

While we provide an extensive dataset in this study, limitations include the use of bulk RNA-sequencing, which may obscure cell-type-specific changes. Future work using single-nucleus RNA-sequencing could provide greater resolution. While the FR rat model captures many human phenotypes, validation in human cohorts is needed to confirm the translational relevance. Additionally, the bulk RNA-seq findings reported here have not been independently validated in a separate animal cohort. Future studies employing targeted qRT-PCR or orthogonal sequencing approaches in new litters will be important to validate the most significant gene-level findings. The current study cannot determine whether the transcriptional changes we report are specific to the NAc or reflect broader gene expression alterations across the brain. Although the enrichment of NAc-relevant pathway terms and cell-type-specific markers in our DEG sets supports regional relevance, parallel transcriptomic profiling of other brain regions, including the prefrontal cortex [80], is required and ongoing to establish the anatomical specificity of FR-induced insulin transcriptional reprogramming. The restriction of the chronoamperometry experiment to male animals is a further limitation. Given the sex-specific transcriptional findings of greater DEG burden in P0 females and the distinct insulin response inversion patterns across sexes, the neurochemical consequences of FR and insulin in females remain to be characterised. The sex-specific transcriptional data generate clear directional predictions about female NAc DA dynamics. Testing these predictions with in vivo electrochemistry is a priority for future work. High-speed chronoamperometry measures extracellular electroactive species and therefore does not provide definitive chemical identification of the recorded signal. Nevertheless, the Nafion-coated electrodes used here have been extensively validated for catecholamine recordings and substantially reduce interference from other electroactive compounds [81]. Given the dense dopaminergic innervation of the nucleus accumbens shell and the relatively limited noradrenergic input to this region, the observed signal is interpreted as predominantly reflecting dopamine dynamics. However, a contribution from norepinephrine or other electroactive species cannot be entirely excluded.

This study is the first to integrate immediate behavioral and neurochemical responses to palatable food with developmental transcriptomic profiling of the NAc following FR and insulin exposure. It highlights brain insulin signaling as a mediator of the effects of prenatal growth restriction on neurodevelopment and behaviour, with the potential for long-term consequences on physical and mental health [29]. Importantly, we identify sex-specific and stage-dependent patterns of molecular programming, suggesting discrete developmental windows during which interventions may be most effective. The identification of genes primed for a reversed insulin response provides a potential mechanistic framework for understanding metabolic reprogramming after FR and may help guide strategies aimed at reducing the risk of insulin dysregulation, disordered eating, and related mental health outcomes in at-risk populations.

Altered insulin sensitivity within reward circuits may have important implications in overeating, obesity, and metabolic disease. Insulin action in the mesolimbic system contributes to the regulation of food motivation and reward-driven eating, processes that influence energy intake and body weight [82]. The translational relevance of these findings is supported by evidence linking prenatal metabolic adversity in humans to a progressive sequence of reward- and feeding-related behavioral changes across the lifespan. Individuals born small for gestational age show altered hedonic responses to sweet taste as neonates [21], impulsivity toward sweet treats in infancy [22], a preference for less nutritious foods in adolescence [23], and increased intake of fats and sugars in adulthood [[25], [26], [27]]. This developmental trajectory mirrors the progression of insulin dysregulation in SGA individuals, from early hyperinsulinemia and increased insulin sensitivity at birth, through accelerating insulin resistance across childhood and adolescence [3], and is consistent with the model that insulin-mediated reconfiguration of NAc reward circuits contributes to these behavioral shifts at each developmental stage. The sex-specific transcriptional patterns we identify, with females showing earlier and more extensive molecular reprogramming at birth, and males demonstrating progressive upregulation into adulthood, may partially explain well-documented sex differences in the relationship between prenatal adversity and subsequent metabolic and psychiatric outcomes in human cohort studies [[83], [84], [85]]. Convergent human evidence comes from a co-expression-based polygenic score reflecting individual variation in the striatal dopamine transporter gene network, where constituent genes are enriched for insulin signaling, and it significantly moderates the effect of low birth weight on psychiatric and cardiometabolic comorbidity risk in both adult and adolescent cohorts [86]. Together, these observations suggest that the NAc transcriptional signatures identified here may reflect conserved mechanisms of developmental metabolic programming, with the striatal insulin-dopamine axis representing a candidate pathway through which prenatal adversity confers lasting vulnerability to comorbid metabolic and psychiatric disease.

By demonstrating that prenatal metabolic stress fundamentally reprograms insulin-responsive transcription within the nucleus accumbens, our findings suggest that early developmental events may contribute to later metabolic vulnerability by altering the neural regulation of food reward. This supports the concept that the brain, like peripheral tissues, undergoes developmental programming of insulin sensitivity. While insulin resistance in liver, muscle, and adipose tissue has been extensively characterized, comparatively little is known about how early metabolic stress shapes insulin responsiveness within neural circuits that regulate reward and feeding behaviour. Our results suggest that FR establishes a long-lasting shift in insulin sensitivity within the NAc, reflected in altered dopamine responses and transcriptional reprogramming.
