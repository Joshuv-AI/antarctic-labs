EXECUTER-mediated singlet oxygen signaling in plants: Mechanisms, evolution, and functional diversification
Authors: Kaiwei Liu, Wangpin Wu, Chanhong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSinglet oxygen (1O2) is a highly reactive, chloroplast-derived reactive oxygen species that functions not only as a damaging oxidant but also as a signaling molecule regulating gene expression, stress responses, and development. The EXECUTER (EX) family, comprising EX1 and its paralog EX2, is central to 1O2-triggered retrograde signaling in Arabidopsis thaliana. Recent mechanistic advances have revealed that EX1 undergoes post-translational oxidation and proteolytic turnover and that EX1-derived small peptides interact with TOC/TIC translocons to activate signaling cascades. EX1 also has unexpected functions in darkness, during which it modulates auxin-responsive gene networks. Comparative genomic analyses further show that EX-like proteins containing the conserved singlet oxygen sensor domain are present in green algae. By contrast, the complete EX architecture, including both the singlet oxygen sensor and UvrBC-like domains, emerged in charophytes and subsequently diversified across major plant lineages. In this review, we present classical and emerging insights into EX-mediated signaling, highlight evolutionary patterns of EX proteins across the plant kingdom, and discuss how understanding EX1/EX2 dynamics may inform strategies to engineer reactive oxygen species perception, stress tolerance, and crop growth. We propose a unified model of EX-mediated 1O2 sensing and outline key unresolved questions to guide future research.Key words: chloroplast, ROS, singlet oxygen, retrograde signaling, EXECUTER, TOC/TIC translocons, auxin, salicylic acid

Introduction: From toxic 1O2 to a plastidial signal

tSinglet oxygen (1O2) is produced within photosystem II (PSII) when excited chlorophyll transfers excess energy to molecular oxygen, particularly under conditions in which absorbed light exceeds the capacity for photochemical conversion (Asada, 2006; Pospisil and Prasad, 2014; Li and Kim, 2022; Lee and Kim, 2024). Traditionally considered a toxic byproduct of photosynthesis, 1O2 has been associated with oxidative damage, photoinhibition, and cell death (Triantaphylides et al., 2008; Koh et al., 2016).

This view was challenged by the characterization of the Arabidopsis fluorescent in blue light (flu) mutant. This conditional mutant accumulates free protochlorophyllide (Pchlide), a chlorophyllide precursor, in the dark. Upon illumination, the accumulated Pchlide rapidly transfers excitation energy to molecular oxygen, generating 1O2 (Meskauskiene et al., 2001; Wagner et al., 2004). Although flu seedlings die rapidly following 1O2 release, genetically identified suppressor mutants can survive despite comparable 1O2 accumulation. This breakthrough, first reported in 2004 (Wagner et al., 2004), established that 1O2 is not merely cytotoxic but also functions as a signaling molecule. Specifically, 1O2 can regulate nuclear gene expression through chloroplast-to-nucleus retrograde signaling from chloroplasts to the nucleus, and coordinate stress responses such as growth inhibition and programmed cell death (Lee et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2012; Page et al., 2017).

The discovery of flu and its suppressors spurred intensive research into 1O2 biology, uncovering multiple distinct signaling pathways that rely on spatially separated components within and beyond the chloroplast. For instance, β-carotene functions as a localized sensor within PSII (Ramel et al., 2013; Shumbe et al., 2016; D'Alessandro et al., 2018), SAFEGUARD1 associates with non-appressed grana regions (Wang et al., 2020), and the E3 ubiquitin ligase PUB4 operates in the cytosol (Woodson et al., 2015). β-cyclocitral, a volatile oxidation product of β-carotene generated in the grana core, functions as a diffusible signal that promotes acclimatory gene expression and stress tolerance (Ramel et al., 2012, 2013). This pathway is mediated in part by the zinc-finger protein METHYLENE BLUE SENSITIVITY 1, which acts downstream of β-cyclocitral to regulate a subset of 1O2-responsive nuclear genes (Shumbe et al., 2017). These findings highlight the compartmentalized nature of 1O2 signaling and illustrate how plants integrate localized reactive oxygen species (ROS) production into global cellular responses.

Genetic mapping of flu suppressors identified 15 independent mutations in EXECUTER1 (EX1), which encodes a nuclear-encoded, chloroplast-localized protein (Wagner et al., 2004). Missense or nonsense mutations in EX1 disrupt 1O2 signaling from the chloroplast to the nucleus, rendering flu ex1 double mutants largely insensitive to 1O2 upon a dark-to-light transition. In light-grown plants, EX1 modulates the expression of stress-related genes, particularly those responsive to salicylic acid (SA), jasmonate (JA), and ROS (op den Camp et al., 2003; Lee et al., 2007). However, the molecular players acting downstream of EX1 remained elusive for more than a decade. Notably, β-cyclocitral-mediated signaling operates independently of EX1, highlighting the existence of parallel 1O2 signaling pathways.

Collectively, these findings indicate that distinct suborganellar sources of 1O2 engage mechanistically separable signaling pathways. EX1-dependent signaling is frequently associated with programmed cell death, whereas β-cyclocitral-METHYLENE BLUE SENSITIVITY 1 signaling is more closely linked to acclimation responses (Shumbe et al., 2017). Together, these pathways underscore the complexity of 1O2 signaling and suggest that chloroplasts deploy multiple mechanisms to modulate nuclear gene expression in response to photooxidative stress. In this review, we focus primarily on the roles of EX1 and its paralog EX2 in retrograde signaling, while acknowledging that other 1O2 signaling pathways contribute to the broader signaling network (Woodson et al., 2015; Tano et al., 2023; Lemke et al., 2024).

Mechanistic advances in EX1/EX2-mediated 1O2 signaling

EX1 in the light: Photodamage, proteolysis, and 1O2 signaling

The Arabidopsis EX1 and EX2 proteins contain a highly conserved C-terminal singlet oxygen sensor (SOS) domain (previously annotated as DUF3506) and a shorter N-terminal UvrBC domain in their mature forms (Figure 1). Recent studies have revealed that EX1-mediated 1O2 signaling depends on oxidative post-translational modification of tryptophan 643 (Trp643) within the SOS domain, followed by proteolytic turnover by the thylakoid FtsH protease complex (Wang et al., 2016; Dogra et al., 2019), which is also central to chloroplast biogenesis and PSII repair (Figure 2). More broadly, mass spectrometry analyses of intact chloroplasts have shown that PSII core proteins are highly susceptible to oxidation, most likely as a consequence of 1O2 generation. In particular, oxidation of the N-terminal Trp14 residue of the D1 protein was recently shown to promote its FtsH-dependent degradation under high-light stress, establishing a direct mechanistic link between Trp oxidation and the PSII repair cycle (Kato et al., 2023). These findings indicate that the oxidative modification of specific tryptophan residues can serve as a molecular tag for FtsH-mediated turnover, coupling protein quality control with stress signaling and photosystem repair.

Light-dependent, EX1-mediated 1O2 sensing, proteolytic processing, and retrograde signaling.

Under light conditions, 1O2 is generated within chloroplast thylakoid membranes, particularly in the flu mutant, in which excess protochlorophyllide absorbs light energy and transfers it to molecular oxygen. EX1 and its paralog EX2 localize to the grana margins and mediate 1O2-triggered retrograde signaling. EX1 senses 1O2 through oxidation of its conserved Trp643 residue within the C-terminal SOS domain, triggering its proteolytic degradation by the thylakoid FtsH metalloprotease complex. EX2 is similarly oxidized at Trp530 and undergoes rapid turnover, functioning as a modulator or decoy that fine-tunes the intensity of EX1-dependent signaling. Both EX1 and EX2 associate with the stromal ClpC1/2 chaperone-protease system, suggesting a broader role for chloroplast proteostasis in signal initiation. Proteolytic processing of EX1 releases its UvrBC domain, which interacts with TOC33 and TIC110 in the TOC–TIC translocon machinery. TOC33 facilitates the export of this domain to the cytosol, after which the domain translocates to the nucleus and associates with WRKY18/40 transcription factors to activate stress- and defense-associated gene expression. Whether the EX2 UvrBC domain contributes to this pathway remains unresolved. This figure was created with BioRender.

In addition to the FtsH protease, the chloroplast stromal caseinolytic protease (Clp) chaperone–protease system may contribute to EX1-mediated 1O2 signaling. Within this system, ClpC1 and ClpC2 function as ATP-dependent chaperones that recognize and deliver protein substrates to the proteolytic Clp core for degradation (Annis et al., 2025). In vivo substrate-trapping experiments have provided evidence that ClpC1 interacts with EX1 and its paralog EX2: ClpC1-TRAP, a mutant ClpC1 variant that binds but cannot release its substrates because of defective ATP hydrolysis, captured both EX1 and EX2, either directly or as part of associated complexes (Montandon et al., 2019; Rei Liao et al., 2022). Consistent with these findings, previous co-immunoprecipitation analyses showed that EX1 interacts with both the FtsH protease and ClpC1/2 chaperones (Dogra et al., 2019). The accessibility of thylakoid-bound EX proteins to the stromal Clp protease suggests a potential regulatory mechanism: the Clp system may degrade stromal pools of full-length EX1 and EX2 to optimize precursor homeostasis and control their sorting to the thylakoid membrane, where they function. At present, it remains unclear whether EX1 and EX2 regulate ClpC1/2 chaperone activity or are themselves substrates of the Clp system in the stroma.

Phylogenetic and biochemical analyses suggest that EX1 and EX2 arose from a common EX ancestor and diverged in seed plants and that EX2 also contains an oxidation-prone Trp residue at the position corresponding to that in EX1 (Dogra et al., 2022) (Figure 1). Consistent with this shared molecular architecture, EX2, like EX1, undergoes rapid degradation upon 1O2 release, either during dark-to-light transitions in the flu mutant or under high-light stress in wild-type plants. Protein interaction studies further show that EX1 and EX2 physically associate on the stromal side of the thylakoid membrane, particularly at the grana margin (Figure 2). Functionally, however, their relationship extends well beyond a simple sensor-regulator module. In the canonical flu mutant system, EX1 acts as the primary executor of 1O2 signaling, with EX1 degradation essential for initiating nuclear gene reprogramming, whereas EX2 turnover functions as a protective “decoy” that buffers EX1 activity and prevents excessive signaling.

Notably, this functional interplay is further modulated in the Arabidopsis genomes uncoupled 1 (gun1) mutant. GUN1 functions as a central integrator of biogenic retrograde signaling during early chloroplast development (Susek et al., 1993; Koussevitzky et al., 2007; Shimizu and Masuda, 2021). Recent work has demonstrated that GUN1 is indispensable for sustaining thermotolerant chloroplast biogenesis during early seedling establishment, as the loss of GUN1 leads to cotyledon bleaching, ROS accumulation in chloroplasts, and impaired photomorphogenic growth under heat stress (Qi et al., 2026). Remarkably, in the gun1 mutant background, EX1 and EX2 exert opposing effects on stress responses, revealing an additional layer of context-dependent regulation that links EX-mediated 1O2 signaling to GUN1-controlled chloroplast biogenesis under heat stress. Specifically, the loss of EX1 partially alleviates the heat sensitivity of gun1, whereas the loss of EX2 markedly exacerbates this phenotype. Strikingly, this functional hierarchy is not fixed but can be reversed under cold stress in a distinct mutant background. In the cold-stressed leaf-variegation mutant var1, which lacks the PSII repair protease FtsH5, the loss of EX2 suppresses chlorosis far more effectively than does the loss of EX1 (Wang and Wu, 2025), in sharp contrast to the EX1-dominant phenotype observed in flu. This finding indicates that EX2 has acquired functions that are at least partially independent of EX1, including the promotion of SA biosynthesis and the repression of photosynthesis-associated nuclear gene expression (Wang and Wu, 2025). Such evolutionary functional diversification substantially expands the roles of EX proteins beyond 1O2 sensing. Collectively, the dynamic and context-specific interplay between EX1 and EX2 enables plants to fine-tune their stress signaling responses.

The UvrBC domain is a short, approximately 35-amino-acid structural motif derived from the bacterial UvrB and UvrC endonucleases, which are involved in nucleotide excision repair. Rather than conferring enzymatic nuclease activity, this domain primarily mediates protein–protein interactions, driving UvrB–UvrC dimerization through direct head-to-head contacts (Moolenaar et al., 1995, 1997; Singh et al., 2002; Seck et al., 2023). A recent study revealed that the UvrBC domain of EX1 interacts with the plastid translocon components TOC33 and TIC110 (Zhao et al., 2025). Although translocon at the outer envelope membrane of chloroplasts (TOC)–translocon at the inner envelope membrane of chloroplasts (TIC) complexes are primarily known for importing nucleus-encoded proteins into chloroplasts, their association with EX1 suggests an additional potential role in exporting stress-derived retrograde signals. This idea is supported by the identification of flu suppressors harboring independent loss-of-function mutations in TOC33, directly linking chloroplast protein translocon function to 1O2 signaling (Figure 2). Notably, although the loss of TOC33 impairs 1O2 signaling in flu, expression of the EX1 UvrBC domain in the cytosol can bypass the requirement for TOC33 and still trigger stress responses. Cell fractionation and co-immunoprecipitation analyses further revealed that the UvrBC domain localizes to the nucleus and interacts with the transcription factors WRKY18 and WRKY40 (hereafter referred to as WRKY18/40) (Figure 2). However, the precise function of the UvrBC domain in regulating nuclear gene expression remains to be defined, and the effect of loss of WRKY18/40 on the expression of 1O2-responsive nuclear genes also requires further investigation. In addition, the precise mechanism by which TOC33 mediates retro-translocation of the UvrBC domain from the plastid to the cytosol remains to be elucidated.

Professor Klaus Apel’s laboratory at ETH Zurich was the first to identify the Arabidopsis flu mutant and perform a forward genetic screen to isolate flu suppressors (Meskauskiene et al., 2001; Goslings et al., 2004). At that time, only plants exhibiting wild-type-like greening under continuous light were selected, as paler plants were assumed to accumulate less 1O2 because of lower Pchlide levels. Surprisingly, although the toc33 mutant appeared pale green, the Pchlide levels accumulated in the dark in the flu toc33 double mutant were comparable to those in the flu single mutant (Zhao et al., 2025). Consistent with this observation, the levels of 1O2 generated upon reillumination were also similar in the flu toc33 double and flu single mutants.

Taken together, the proteolytic degradation of EX1 not only removes oxidized EX1 but is also proposed to generate a chloroplast-derived danger signal, most likely in the form of one or more small peptides that are transmitted to the nucleus via the TOC/TIC translocon machinery. It is important to note, however, that TOC/TIC involvement is inferred primarily from genetic evidence, and the mechanistic details of this retro-translocation process remain unresolved. The proposed involvement of the TOC/TIC machinery in signal and/or peptide export raises important biophysical questions regarding directionality and substrate specificity. One possibility is that chloroplast stress induces partial remodeling or destabilization of the TOC complex, permitting the transient reverse translocation of signaling components. Alternatively, retrograde signaling may not involve direct passage through the canonical import channel but instead rely on cytosolic extraction systems. In this context, the cell division cycle 48 AAA+ ATPase–SUPPRESSOR OF PPI1 LOCUS 2 machinery, which is central to the chloroplast-associated protein degradation pathway and mediates the ubiquitin-dependent removal of chloroplast proteins (Ling et al., 2019; Li et al., 2022; Li and Jarvis, 2024), could facilitate the extraction of signal-competent factors from the chloroplast surface. A third scenario is that TOC33 functions primarily as a regulatory node that senses perturbations in protein import or envelope homeostasis and transduces this information to downstream signaling pathways without serving as a physical conduit. It also remains possible that 1O2-induced damage compromises chloroplast envelope integrity (Kim et al., 2012; Woodson et al., 2015), allowing signaling molecules to escape independently of a dedicated export route, while TOC33 participates in downstream signaling, potentially through interactions with EX1-associated components such as the UvrBC domain. Distinguishing among these models will require direct biochemical and structural analyses under defined stress conditions. Resolving whether TOC33 operates as an export channel, a remodeling platform, or an indirect signaling regulator remains a key step toward achieving a mechanistic understanding of chloroplast-to-nucleus communication. Additionally, because EX2 also undergoes turnover upon 1O2 release (Dogra et al., 2022), it will be important to examine whether its UvrBC domain contributes to signal modulation or antagonism and how its function compares with that of EX1 in the retrograde signaling pathway.

Collectively, these plastid-derived signals activate a nuclear transcriptional program enriched in stress- and defense-related genes, including those responsive to stress hormones such as SA and JA, as well as to ROS (op den Camp et al., 2003; Lee et al., 2007). With respect to SA and JA, it remains unclear whether these hormones function solely as downstream signaling components or whether 1O2 can also trigger their rapid accumulation independently of the transcriptional activation of their biosynthetic pathways. Therefore, rather than operating as independent linear pathways, chloroplast-derived 1O2 signals appear to engage an interconnected signaling network. EX1-dependent signaling, β-cyclocitral-mediated responses, and hormone pathways may be differentially activated depending on the suborganellar origin, intensity, and temporal dynamics of 1O2 production. For example, EX1-mediated signaling is often associated with genetically controlled cell death programs, whereas β-cyclocitral primarily promotes acclimatory gene expression; these outputs can be further shaped by interactions with SA- and JA-dependent transcriptional networks (Ramel et al., 2012; Lv et al., 2015; Bali et al., 2023). Crosstalk among these pathways likely determines both the amplitude and the spatial propagation of stress responses, enabling cells to fine-tune outcomes ranging from acclimation to localized or systemic cell death.

EX1 in darkness: growth regulation and the defense–growth trade-off

In addition to its role in light-grown plants, EX1 mediates 1O2 signaling in etiolated seedlings. In this context, SA-induced lipid peroxidation generates plastidial 1O2 bursts that activate EX1-dependent retrograde signaling (Lee and Kim, 2024; Li et al., 2025). Whereas EX1 primarily induces stress- and defense-related genes under light conditions, it modulates the expression of auxin-related genes in darkness, including members of the SMALL AUXIN UP-REGULATED RNA and EXPANSIN families (Figure 3) (Li et al., 2025).

EX1 functions in darkness to integrate SA-induced 1O2 bursts with auxin-responsive growth regulation.

In darkness, EX1 performs a distinct regulatory function that is independent of photodamage-associated 1O2 signaling. SA induces lipid peroxidation and generates transient 1O2 bursts within etioplasts, activating an EX1-dependent retrograde signaling pathway. In this context, EX1 represses auxin-responsive genes, including members of the SMALL AUXIN UP-REGULATED RNA (SAUR) and EXPANSIN families, thereby modulating skotomorphogenesis and restricting hypocotyl elongation. The SA receptor NPR1 maintains expression of auxin-responsive genes, but EX1-mediated 1O2 signaling antagonizes NPR1 function, leading to reduced SAUR accumulation. Lower SAUR activity relieves the inhibition of PP2C clade D (PP2C.D) phosphatases, altering the phosphorylation of plasma membrane H+-ATPases and reducing cell expansion. The contribution of EX2 to dark-induced 1O2 signaling remains to be determined. This figure was created with BioRender.

NONEXPRESSER OF PR GENES 1 (NPR1) is required to sustain the expression of these auxin-related genes in the presence of SA in the dark. By contrast, 1O2-triggered, EX1-mediated retrograde signaling represses their expression. Consequently, the loss of EX1 significantly rescues the hypocotyl phenotype of npr1 mutants, restoring the expression of SMALL AUXIN UP-REGULATED RNA and EXPANSIN and promoting elongation through cell wall acidification (Park et al., 2010; Spartz et al., 2014).

These findings position EX1 as a molecular switch that balances growth and defense. Under light conditions, EX1 turnover leads to the release of danger signals that prioritize stress adaptation; in darkness, EX1 channels 1O2-derived signals toward developmental pathways, thereby modulating hypocotyl elongation. EX2 may also influence these outcomes, highlighting the dynamic, environmentally dependent interplay between EX1 and its paralog; this possibility warrants further investigation.

It is worth noting that although EX1 and NPR1 respond to SA in etiolated seedlings to modulate hypocotyl growth (Li et al., 2025), recent genetic evidence obtained under cold stress suggests a distinct regulatory configuration. Mutations in the SA signaling components ENHANCED DISEASE SUSCEPTIBILITY 1 and PHYTOALEXIN DEFICIENT 4 suppress the chlorotic phenotype of var1, whereas the loss of NPR1 does not, indicating that 1O2-associated responses can be mediated through ENHANCED DISEASE SUSCEPTIBILITY 1/PHYTOALEXIN DEFICIENT 4-dependent but NPR1-independent pathways (Wang and Wu, 2025). These observations suggest that chloroplast-derived 1O2 signals engage different branches of the SA signaling network depending on the environmental context, such as darkness or cold stress. This conditional wiring highlights the flexibility of retrograde signaling and suggests that distinct downstream modules are selectively recruited to shape stress-specific physiological outcomes.

Evolutionary insights: conservation, divergence, and functional innovation

To elucidate the evolutionary trajectory of EX-mediated 1O2 perception pathways, we conducted a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of 120 EX-like proteins from 52 Viridiplantae species (Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). Phylogenetic reconstruction using a maximum-likelihood method revealed that EX-like proteins can be traced to chlorophytes, including Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, and possess a conserved SOS domain that is present in all descendant lineages examined (Supplemental Figure 1; Supplemental Table 3). The ancestral EX gene likely existed as a single copy before the emergence of land plants. The earliest clear gene duplication event was observed in mosses, as evidenced by the presence of four copies in Physcomitrella patens (Figure 4A; Supplemental Table 2). At least two EX gene copies were retained in seed plant genomes, forming distinct EX1 and EX2 clades in the unrooted phylogenetic tree (Figure 4B). Although the SOS domain and a conserved oxidation-prone tryptophan residue within it are ubiquitous across Viridiplantae, the UvrBC domain shows a more variable distribution among EX1 and EX2 proteins (Figures 4A and 4C). The diversification of EX genes and their domain architectures coincided with the increasing complexity of stress signaling and desiccation tolerance in land plants. The emergence of distinct EX1 and EX2 paralogs may have facilitated the finer integration of oxidative stress cues with downstream nuclear gene regulation.

The complete EX architecture, harboring either a canonical UvrBC domain or a less conserved UvrBC-like region, first emerged within Charophyceae, including the multicellular aquatic alga Chara braunii, and was subsequently distributed across diverse land plant lineages (Figure 4A; Supplemental Table 4). The absence of, or low sequence similarity within, the UvrBC domain in chlorophytes may render these ancestral EX proteins incapable of mediating 1O2 signaling in the same manner as their land plant counterparts. This evolutionary divergence may explain why EX has not been identified as a component of the 1O2 retrograde signaling pathway in the unicellular alga C. reinhardtii. In contrast to the universally conserved SOS domain in green plants, the UvrBC-related region displays greater sequence divergence and evolutionary differentiation. When this divergence is pronounced, sequence similarity may fall below the statistical threshold (E-value cutoff) required to identify a canonical UvrBC or UvrBC-like domain. This pattern is observed in most monocots, including EX orthologs in rice (Figures 4A and 4C; Supplemental Table 4). However, it remains unclear whether the evolutionary divergence of the UvrBC region in monocots, characterized by low similarity to the canonical UvrBC domain rather than the complete absence of this region, affects the overall role of EX-like proteins in 1O2 signaling. Alternatively, the monocot UvrBC-like region may have evolved lineage-specific functions, such as more efficient chloroplast retrograde signal export or differential binding to downstream signaling partners. Such divergence suggests that the UvrBC domain may function as an accessory module within EX pathways, conferring lineage-specific advantages that potentially facilitate adaptation to particular developmental cues or stress-related selection pressures.

Forward-looking perspectives: translational potential of EXECUTER signaling

Recent advances redefine EX-mediated 1O2 signaling not as a binary cell-death switch but as a tunable regulatory module that integrates oxidative stress perception with regulated protein turnover and nuclear gene expression. This conceptual shift opens potential avenues for crop improvement aimed at enhancing stress resilience while minimizing growth penalties.

Tuning EXECUTER sensitivity and signaling amplitude

At the core of EX signaling is the oxidation of a conserved tryptophan residue within the SOS domain, followed by FtsH-dependent proteolysis. This well-defined molecular trigger represents a tractable target for precision breeding or genome editing. Modest attenuation of EX1 oxidation sensitivity, for example, through amino acid substitutions that reduce but do not abolish 1O2 responsiveness, could prevent unnecessary activation of retrograde stress signaling under mild or transient stress conditions, thereby limiting yield losses. Conversely, enhanced signaling responsiveness may be beneficial under conditions of frequent photooxidative stress, such as field environments characterized by fluctuating light (Figure 5A).

The oxidation-sensitive Trp643 residue can be substituted with 1O2-insensitive residues, such as leucine (L) or alanine (A), or with an oxidation-mimicking residue, phenylalanine (F), to modulate the rate of EX1 turnover (Dogra et al., 2022; Kato et al., 2023).

(B) Adjusting the buffering capacity of EX2 or the EX1:EX2 stoichiometry to fine-tune 1O2 signaling strength and mitigate excessive growth inhibition.

(C) Conceptual breeding and gene-editing pipeline that leverages natural EX1/EX2 allelic variation and CRISPR-based engineering to generate climate-resilient crop varieties adapted to fluctuating or concurrent environmental stresses.

In parallel, EX2 provides an endogenous buffering mechanism that constrains EX1 activity. Modulating EX2 abundance or stability offers a second, potentially more targeted strategy to dampen excessive signaling without eliminating stress perception (Figure 5B). Natural variation in EX genes, including differences in protein stability or domain composition, likely already encodes such tuning capacity and represents a practical reservoir for crop improvement through allele mining and introgression (Figure 5C). Such allelic variation may influence the threshold, amplitude, or duration of 1O2-induced responses, thereby shaping differences in photooxidative stress tolerance, acclimation capacity, and cell-death sensitivity among accessions and species. Importantly, candidate EX alleles should be evaluated not only for stress resistance but also for their effects on growth, development, and yield stability, as altered retrograde signaling may shift the balance between acclimation and growth inhibition.

Translational implications of EXECUTER divergence across plant lineages

The evolutionary distribution of EX proteins raises important questions about the conservation of 1O2 signaling mechanisms across plant lineages (Figure 4A). In Arabidopsis and other dicots, the presence of an N-terminal UvrBC or UvrBC-like region has been associated with downstream signaling functions, whereas this region shows reduced sequence conservation in most monocots and earlier-diverging photosynthetic lineages. This pattern suggests several non-mutually exclusive evolutionary scenarios. First, the UvrBC-related region may have emerged or become functionally specialized during land plant evolution to provide additional regulatory capacity. Alternatively, this region may have undergone lineage-specific sequence diversification in monocots while retaining related signaling functions. Finally, monocots may use distinct protein modules that fulfill analogous signaling roles.

Importantly, divergence of the UvrBC-related region does not necessarily imply a loss of EX function but may instead reflect mechanistic diversification in the transmission of chloroplast-derived 1O2 signals. This possibility has important translational implications, as signaling mechanisms defined in Arabidopsis may not be fully conserved in major crop species such as rice, maize, and wheat. Comparative functional analyses across species will therefore be essential to determine whether EX-dependent signaling relies primarily on conserved core mechanisms or lineage-specific adaptations.

To address this question, cross-species complementation experiments using ex mutants from Arabidopsis and rice, combined with domain-swapping approaches involving divergent EX UvrBC-related regions, could provide a tractable framework for directly assessing the contribution of this region to 1O2 signaling outputs.

Managing growth–defense trade-offs

EX signaling intersects with multiple hormone-signaling pathways, including those mediated by SA, JA, and auxin, positioning it as a key node in growth–defense coordination. Although the activation of EX1 enhances stress resistance, it often coincides with the repression of growth-related programs. In agricultural contexts, especially in controlled or semi-controlled systems, this trade-off may be unnecessarily costly. Targeted uncoupling of EX-dependent stress signaling from downstream growth repression, for instance, through cis-regulatory editing of auxin-responsive genes, offers a potential strategy to preserve biomass accumulation while maintaining oxidative stress sensing (Figure 5D).

Outlook

Rather than pursuing wholesale activation or suppression, future translational efforts should aim to fine-tune EX signaling strength, duration, and context dependence. Achieving this will require deeper insight into EX1/EX2 stoichiometry, the dynamics of FtsH-mediated proteolysis, and the transcriptional outputs of EX turnover. Integrating genetics, comparative genomics, and in vivo proteostasis analyses will be essential to identify the molecular signals released upon EX degradation and define how they are interpreted by the nucleus. Ultimately, such knowledge will enable the rational deployment of EX pathways as adjustable redox rheostats, helping crops balance stress resilience with growth under increasingly variable environmental conditions.
