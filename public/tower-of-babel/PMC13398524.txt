ELF3 links kidney function GWAS loci to maladaptive epithelial inflammation
Authors: Yanjuan Hou, Konstantin Kloetzer, Bernhard Dumoulin, Eunji Ha, Hongbo Liu, Dhanunjay Mukhi, Magaiver Andrade-Silva, Xuemei Hu, Siyu Pan, Katalin Susztak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have uncovered over a thousand loci associated with kidney function, but the effector genes and mechanisms remain largely unknown. Here, we demonstrate that ELF3 is the effector gene at a kidney function GWAS locus and acts as an epithelial proinflammatory amplifier of the reprogramming of injured proximal tubules (iPTs). E74-like factor 3 (ELF3) expression is induced in iPTs in both mouse models and human chronic kidney disease (CKD), where it defines transitional epithelial states enriched for inflammatory gene programs and surrounded by leukocyte infiltrates. Conditional deletion of Elf3 in mice after injury reduced iPT accumulation, cytokine production, and immune cell recruitment. Mechanistically, ELF3 directly bound and activated components of the noncanonical nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathway (Nfκb2, Map3k14, and Il6r) and was required for NFκB2 nuclear translocation and cytokine induction. Spatial transcriptomics and immunofluorescence of human CKD kidneys confirmed that ELF3 expression correlates with epithelial inflammation, disease progression, and loss of kidney function. Together, these findings establish ELF3 as a genetically validated effector gene that drives inflammatory reprogramming in iPTs, nominating it as a therapeutic target to blunt maladaptive inflammation in CKD.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a global health challenge, affecting nearly 10% of the adult population and driving substantial morbidity, mortality, and health care costs (1). Despite its prevalence, the molecular mechanisms that govern CKD onset and progression remain poorly defined, limiting the development of effective targeted therapies. A growing body of work highlights tubular epithelial injury and persistent inflammation as central drivers of disease progression (2–4).

Human genetics provides a powerful entry point into disease biology. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified over 1000 loci associated with kidney function and disease risk (5–8). Genes supported by human genetic evidence are significantly more likely to yield successful therapeutics, yet the translational impact of GWAS has been blunted by the fact that most signals (>95%) map to noncoding regions (6, 9). Pinpointing the effector genes and molecular pathways underlying these associations remains one of the greatest unmet needs in nephrology.

Human and mouse kidney disease single-cell transcriptomic studies have revealed an injury-induced population of proximal tubule (PT) cells—termed injured proximal tubule (iPT) cells—that emerge in response to acute or chronic insults (10–13). Unlike healthy epithelia, iPT cells adopt a maladaptive state characterized by dedifferentiation, metabolic stress, and persistent proinflammatory signaling (14–16). Instead of restoring homeostasis, these cells secrete cytokines and chemokines that recruit and activate immune cells, amplifying local inflammation (14, 17). Their persistence strongly correlates with CKD severity and adverse clinical outcomes (18, 19). However, the transcriptional regulators that establish and sustain this maladaptive epithelial state remain poorly understood, posing a key obstacle for therapeutic intervention.

A fundamental gap remains and genetic studies have not helped to understand iPT development: How does inherited genetic risk converge on transcriptional programs of epithelial injury and inflammation? Inflammation is a hallmark of progressive CKD (2, 3), but the identity of transcription factors that integrate injury signals with proinflammatory gene expression in tubular epithelia remains unknown. Bridging this gap would not only provide mechanistic insight but also nominate genetically validated therapeutic targets.

E74-like factor 3 (ELF3) is an epithelial-specific member of the E-twenty-six (ETS) transcription factor family with well-established roles in epithelial differentiation and immune regulation across barrier tissues (20). In airway disease, ELF3 mediates mucosal remodeling and allergic inflammation (21); in cancer, it is activated by inflammatory signals such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), where it sustains chronic proinflammatory transcriptional loops (22, 23). ELF3 also regulates antiviral responses, amplifying type I interferon signaling (24). Although recent multiomics studies have linked ELF3 activity to injured human tubular epithelial cells (25, 26), its regulation, causal function, and mechanistic role in kidney injury and CKD progression remain largely undefined.

Here, we identify ELF3 as the effector gene at a kidney function–associated GWAS locus and demonstrate that it is a transcriptional driver of epithelial inflammation in CKD. Through integrated genetic, transcriptomic, and epigenomic analyses—including single-cell and spatial profiling, in vivo mouse models, and CUT&RUN—we show that ELF3 is necessary to sustain the maladaptive proinflammatory state of iPT cells via direct regulation of the noncanonical NF-κB pathway. To our knowledge, this work provides the first evidence that a transcription factor can serve as a causal effector gene at a kidney GWAS locus, linking human genetics to epithelial cell biology and uncovering a targetable mechanism in progressive CKD.

RESULTS

ELF3 is a kidney disease candidate gene identified by GWAS

GWAS analyzing over 2 million subjects have identified a locus on chromosome 1, where the lead single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) rs10920308 was significantly associated with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR; P = 2.48 × 10−11), a key measure of kidney function (5). The sentinel variants at this locus overlap both the gene body and flanking regulatory elements of ELF3.

To understand whether ELF3 is the target of kidney function–associated variants, we integrated kidney-specific methylation quantitative trait locus (mQTL) data with the GWAS signal. The mQTL signal at the ELF3 locus colocalized with the GWAS haplotype driven by the lead SNP rs10920308 and was mapped to the annotated regulatory domain of ELF3, with a high posterior probability of colocalization (PP4 = 0.996) (Fig. 1A). Within this region, three CpG sites (cg04125586, cg18218190, and cg20275771) exhibited GWAS risk allele-dependent methylation (P = 1.3 × 10−9, 1.0 × 10−7, and 2.5 × 10−9, respectively; all false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted P < 0.05), suggesting that germline GWAS variation alters the local chromatin environment at the ELF3 locus.

We next evaluated expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) effects in microdissected human kidney tissues (27). The lead eGFR GWAS SNP rs10920308 was associated with ELF3 transcript levels in glomerular samples (P = 0.0042; Fig. 1B). These findings suggest that kidney function GWAS variants modulate the methylation of this locus and downstream expression of ELF3.

To investigate the cell type–specific chromatin context, we analyzed a single-nucleus assay for transposase-accessible chromatin (ATAC)-seq atlas of over 150,000 nuclei from human kidneys (5). We observed chromatin accessibility at the ELF3 promoter and upstream enhancers across multiple epithelial subtypes, including proximal tubule segments (PT S1–S3), thick ascending limb, distal convoluted tubule, and both principal and intercalated collecting duct cells. In contrast, mesangial, endothelial, and immune cell types displayed minimal accessibility at these regulatory elements.

To further prioritize ELF3 as the causal gene, we applied three orthogonal gene-mapping frameworks: Open4Gene, Activity-by-Contact (ABC), and the combined SNP-to-gene (cS2G) strategy. Each method integrates genetic and epigenomic data to infer gene-variant relationships. Integrative genetic prioritization analyses identified ELF3 as the top (sole) candidate target gene at the eGFR-associated locus (table S1). Figure 1C provides functional support for this prioritization by illustrating kidney cell type–specific chromatin accessibility and predicted regulatory interactions linking eGFR-associated variants to the ELF3 locus. In summary, these studies nominate ELF3 as a kidney disease risk gene based on eGFR GWAS and multiomics integration.

ELF3 is induced in injured tubule cells and enriched in transitional epithelial states in kidney injury models

As human genetic and epigenetic evidence supported ELF3 as a candidate effector gene at an eGFR-associated GWAS locus, we next assessed whether ELF3 expression is altered in response to kidney injury. We analyzed bulk kidney RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets from three mechanistically distinct murine models of kidney disease: unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) (28), folic acid–induced nephropathy (FA) (29), and apolipoprotein L1 (APOL1) risk variant expression (30). Across all models, ELF3 transcript levels were significantly elevated in diseased kidneys compared to strain-matched controls (FDR < 0.01 for each model) (Fig. 1D). In parallel, Havcr1 (hepatitis A virus cellular receptor 1), Vcam1 (vascular cell adhesion molecule 1), and Ccl2 (C-C motif chemokine ligand 2) were also increased at these terminal time points, indicating that ELF3 up-regulation aligns with established injury and inflammatory responses (Fig. 1D). The consistent up-regulation across diverse injury paradigms suggests that ELF3 activation is a common transcriptional response to kidney damage.

To understand the cell type expression of ELF3, we performed RNA in situ hybridization (ISH) and coimmunofluorescence staining on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded kidney sections from control and adenine-fed mice. In healthy kidneys, Elf3 signal was confined primarily to the nuclei of cortical and medullary tubular epithelial cells, with no observable expression in glomeruli or the interstitium (fig. S2A). Following 4 weeks of adenine-induced injury, Elf3 expression was markedly higher (fig. S2A) and showed strong colocalization with multiple injury markers, including KIM1 (kidney injury molecule 1), VCAM1, SOX9 (SRY-box transcription factor 9), and PCNA (proliferating cell nuclear antigen), with colocalization most frequently observed with SOX9 and PCNA, highlighting ELF3’s preferential association with iPT-like injured epithelial states (Fig. 1E and fig. S2B).

To further identify the nephron segments expressing ELF3, we performed coimmunofluorescence staining using segment-specific markers. A minority of ELF3+ cells (∼15%) colocalized with aquaporin 2, marking principal cells in the collecting duct. In contrast, ELF3 showed limited or no detectable colocalization with markers of other tubular epithelial segments, including thick ascending limb, distal convoluted tubule (solute carrier family 12 member 3, SLC12A3), or intercalated cells (SLC26A4) (fig. S2C). These findings suggest that ELF3 is induced in injured tubules and preferentially enriched in atypical or transitional epithelial states that emerge during kidney injury.

Single-cell analysis identifies ELF3 as a marker of proinflammatory epithelial states in iPTs

To define the expression of ELF3 in human kidney tissue, we analyzed previously generated large human kidney single-cell RNA-seq data (31). This resource includes samples from multiple sources including the Susztak laboratory (11) and Kidney Precision Medicine Project (KPMP) (10), comprising a total of 150 patients and over 700,000 cells from multiple kidney cohorts, disease types, and disease stages. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analysis resolved all major kidney cell populations and clearly demarcated the iPT compartment, which could be further subdivided into five distinct subclusters (C0–C4) (Fig. 2A).

We next assessed the expression of ELF3 across kidney cell types. Similar to the mouse models, ELF3 expression was negligible in stromal and immune cells. Among epithelial segments, ELF3 was detected in collecting duct principal cells, but was also present in several injured tubular populations, including injured thick ascending limb (TAL) and descending thin limb (DTL) and ascending thin limb (ATL) cells. We also observed strong ELF3 expression in iPT subclusters coexpressing canonical injury markers such as HAVCR1 and VCAM1 (Fig. 2B) (32, 33). Notably, the broader ELF3 transcript distribution observed in the human CKD dataset differs from the adenine-treated mouse kidney in fig. S2C, where ELF3 protein showed little detectable signal in distal convoluted tubule (DCT) or TAL segments, likely reflecting the more heterogeneous and multistage tubular injury present in human CKD biopsies.

To further resolve the heterogeneity within the iPT compartment, we analyzed subclusters C0–C4 and found that the iPT_4 cluster exhibited the highest ELF3 expression. This ELF3-positive iPT subcluster was characterized by strong expression of inflammation-associated genes, including IL18, NFKB1, ITGB8 (integrin subunit beta 8), and CCL2, as well as the iPT marker SOX9 (Fig. 2C) (34).

To identify genes associated with ELF3 expression in iPT_4 cells, we compared ELF3-expressing iPT_4 cells to other iPT cells that lack ELF3 expression. Differential expression analysis revealed markedly increased expression of proinflammatory genes in iPT_4, including NFKBIZ, TNFR10B (TNF receptor superfamily member 10B), ICAM1 (intercellular adhesion molecule 1), and CXCL1 (C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1) (Fig. 2D).

We validated these findings at the protein level using immunofluorescence staining in control and 4 weeks of adenine-injured mouse kidneys. These ELF3+ tubules frequently colocalized with the chemokine CCL2 and were surrounded by F4/80+ macrophages and CD45+ leukocytes, reinforcing their identity as inflammation-associated epithelial cells (Fig. 2E and fig. S2D). Together, these findings demonstrate that ELF3 marks a transcriptionally distinct injured tubular population characterized by proinflammatory gene expression and association with immune cells and fibrotic changes.

Ablation of ELF3 following kidney injury reduces iPT accumulation and renal inflammation in CKD

To investigate the temporal pattern of ELF3 activation during tubular injury, we performed a detailed time-course analysis in the adenine-induced CKD model (fig. S3, A to E). Elf3 mRNA was significantly increased as early as 12 hours after adenine exposure, coinciding with the induction of canonical early injury markers such as Havcr1 (KIM1 protein) and Sox9, and remained elevated throughout the injury course (Fig. 3A and fig. S3B). Notably, this increase coincided with chronic injury markers [Col1α1, Col3, Vcam1, and Csf1 (colony stimulating factor 1)] and worsened kidney function (fig. S3, C and D). ISH revealed induction of Elf3 transcripts at day 4 (fig. S3F), whereas Western blotting and immunofluorescence demonstrated a corresponding increase in ELF3 protein expression around day 7 (fig. S3, E and G).

We next generated mice with conditional inducible deletion of ELF3 by crossing the Pax8-rtTA; TRE-Cre; Elf3flox/flox mice. In these animals, doxycycline administration resulted in tubular epithelial-specific ELF3 deletion (35). Adenine diet–induced injury did not activate Cre in the absence of doxycycline, ensuring that ELF3 deletion is strictly doxycycline-dependent (fig. S4A). To target the established injury phase, mice were maintained on adenine diet for 1 week and then switched to adenine/doxycycline food on day 7 (Fig. 3B). At this 1-week baseline time point, Elf3 mRNA levels were comparable between genotypes, with no differences in serum injury markers or tubular injury gene expression (fig. S4, B and C). Efficient deletion of ELF3 was confirmed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and Western blotting and further validated by immunofluorescence showing loss of ELF3 signal in tubular epithelial cells (fig. S4, D to F).

Postinjury ELF3 ablation led to a markedly lower mRNA expression of tubular injury markers Havcr1 and Lcn2 (lipocalin 2; neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin, Ngal) in whole kidney samples (Fig. 3C). Immunofluorescence studies indicated a reduction in KIM1+, Vcam1+ epithelial cells in ELF3-deficient kidneys, indicating the role of ELF3 in expansion of the iPT population (Fig. 3D). These findings were supported by qPCR and immunoblotting, which showed decreased expression of iPT markers including KIM1, Vcam1, and Icam1, and reduced protein levels of Vcam1 and KIM1 (Fig. 3, H and I, and fig. S5C).

Consistent with reduced epithelial injury, histological and immunofluorescence analyses showed attenuated renal inflammation. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed less tubular dilation and inflammatory infiltration in ELF3-deleted kidneys (Fig. 3, E and F), while CD45 immunostaining confirmed a substantial reduction in leukocyte infiltration (Fig. 3, E and G). Together, these data indicate that postinjury ELF3 deletion limits the expansion of the iPT population and is associated with reduced renal inflammation.

The ELF3 targets NFκB2 modulating the inflammatory reprogramming of injured renal epithelium

To define the direct transcriptional targets of ELF3, we performed CUT&RUN profiling. To mimic iPT cells, we treated mouse primary proximal tubular epithelial cells (PTECs) with tumor necrosis factor–α (TNFα) and interferon-γ (IFNγ) (Fig. 4A). As reported previously, this cytokine treatment has been shown to reprogram cells into an injured phenotype, characterized by the induction of markers such as Vcam1, Ccl2, and Icam1 (14, 36, 37). Consistent with these reports, our transcriptomic profiling further demonstrated that this in vitro cytokine–induced program closely parallels the iPT transcriptional signature observed in vivo in single-cell datasets (fig. S6, A to D). In parallel, comparison of multiple kidney injury stimuli shows that ELF3 was most strongly induced by TNFα + IFNγ, exhibiting the highest levels of both transcript and protein expression (fig. S7, B and C). These data support the use of this condition as an optimal in vitro model for mapping ELF3-dependent regulatory elements.

The CUT&RUN analysis identified 4536 high-confidence ELF3 binding sites in the genome of “iPT-like” cells (fig. S9A). To prioritize targets with in vivo relevance, we intersected ELF3-bound genes with disease-induced differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from injured tubular epithelial cells (injTECs) in human CKD single-cell datasets. This yielded a core set of 859 conserved genes, with not only ELF3 binding but also changes observed in patients with CKD. The conserved targets showed enrichment for inflammatory and stress-response pathways including TNF signaling, Rap1 signaling, and apoptosis (Fig. 4A and fig. S9B).

To validate the direct regulatory relationship between ELF3 and components of the TNFα/NF-κB signaling cascade, we visualized ELF3 CUT&RUN peaks at representative loci using genome browser tracks. ELF3 binding was detected at promoter and enhancer regions of several core inflammatory genes, including NFκBIA, NFκB2, MAP3K14 (mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 14), and IL6R, which are key mediators of NF-κB activation and proinflammatory signaling (Fig. 4B).

To elucidate the mechanism by which ELF3 promotes inflammation in injured tubular cells, we examined the interaction between ELF3 and NF-κB signaling components. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed that NFκB2 expression was enriched in ELF3+ iPT populations and closely associated with the expression of injury markers VCAM1 and HAVCR1 (fig. S10A). Consistent with these transcriptional patterns, immunofluorescence staining revealed robust accumulation of NFκB2 in adenine-injured kidneys, which colocalized with ELF3+ tubular regions (Fig. 4C and fig. S10B). To assess whether ELF3 is functionally required for the proinflammatory phenotype, we analyzed kidneys from mice with injury-specific tubular-specific ELF3 deletion. Immunostaining revealed that ELF3-deficient kidneys exhibited reduced expression of NFκB2 and NIK (NF-κB-inducing kinase), along with diminished expression of the injury-associated marker Vcam1 (Fig. 4D), suggesting that ELF3 is necessary for the proinflammatory iPT phenotypic transition in vivo. Consistent with the role of NIK as an upstream regulator of noncanonical NFκB2 activation, its reduction further supports impaired NFκB2 signaling in ELF3-deficient kidneys. These findings were corroborated by qPCR analysis, which showed significantly lower expression of inflammatory genes including Nfkb2, RelA, RelB, and Map3k14 in mice with ELF3 deletion (Fig. 4E). Immunoblotting further confirmed lower NF-κB signaling activity, as evidenced by decreased p-p65 (RelA Ser536)/total RelA and NFκB2 levels in Pax8Cre-ELF3fl/fl mice (Fig. 4F and fig. S10C).

To define the overlap between ELF3 and NFκB2 regulatory programs, we next performed CUT&RUN profiling for NFκB2 in primary tubular epithelial cells. Genomic intersection analysis revealed that 2651 of 2731 NFκB2 target genes were also directly bound by ELF3 (Fig. 4G). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that the shared targets were enriched for inflammatory and interferon-related pathways, including viral defense and IFNγ signaling. Genome browser tracks confirmed cobinding of ELF3 and NFκB2 at promoters of Stat1(signal transducer and activator of transcription 1), Cxcl10, and Cxcl11, all of which were down-regulated in ELF3-deficient kidneys (38) (Fig. 4H and fig. S10, D and E).

To functionally validate the proinflammatory role of ELF3, we quantified cytokine and chemokine expression in kidneys of healthy and diseased ELF3-deficient and wild-type (WT) mice. Loss of ELF3 lowered the injury-induced expression of Tnf, Il6, Il1β, Csf1, Ccl2, Ccl5, Cxcl10, and Cxcl12 (Fig. 5A). To understand the role of NFκB2 in ELF3-mediated effects in kidney tubule cells, we cultured primary tubular epithelial cells from Elf3fl/fl mice and deleted ELF3 by transduction with Cre-expressing adenovirus (fig. S7C). Treatment of cells with TNFα and IFNγ triggered NFκB2 nuclear translocation, which was sensitive to the NIK inhibitor SMI1, consistent with the essential role of NIK in activating the noncanonical NFκB2 pathway. Notably, ELF3-deficient cells exhibited impaired NFκB2 nuclear localization, suggesting that ELF3 acts upstream of or cooperates with the noncanonical NFκB2 pathway (Fig. 5B and fig. S7, D and E).

To examine the synergy between ELF3 and cytokine signaling in tubular epithelial cells, we performed bulk RNA-seq in primary mouse tubular epithelial cells following ELF3 deletion and stimulation with TNFα + IFNγ. Bulk transcriptomic analysis revealed clear genotype-dependent separation, as principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated distinct clustering of WT (CTR) and ELF3KO (KO) samples under cytokine treatment (fig. S11A). Differential expression analysis identified widespread transcriptional changes, with 1622 genes up-regulated and 1626 genes down-regulated in ELF3KO compared with WT cells following TNFα + IFNγ stimulation (fig. S11B). Enrichment analysis showed significant alterations associated with ELF3 deficiency under inflammatory conditions (fig. S11C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs further revealed significant down-regulation of multiple inflammatory pathways in ELF3KO cells, including the TNF signaling pathway, leukocyte transendothelial migration, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, and NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 5C). Consistent with these pathway-level changes, heatmap visualization demonstrated reduced expression of key injury- and inflammation-associated genes in ELF3KO cells following TNFα + IFNγ treatment, including Lcn2, Havcr1, Vcam1, Cxcl16, Cxcl1, Icam1, and Cxcl10, all of which exhibited lower transcript levels compared with WT controls (Fig. 5D).

To validate the transcriptomic findings, we next quantified inflammatory gene expression by qPCR under cytokine stimulation and pathway inhibition. Loss of ELF3 blunted the iPT phenotype, as reflected by reduced expression of Vcam1 and Icam1. Similarly, ELF3 deletion or NIK inhibition reduced the expression of Il6, Ccl2, Ccl5, Cxcl9, and Cxcl10. While pharmacologic NIK inhibition further reduced Vcam1 levels in ELF3-deficient cells, other inflammatory targets were not observably affected, suggesting that ELF3 operates in part independently of NIK (Fig. 5, E and F). In addition, inhibition of canonical NF-κB signaling using the RelA nuclear-translocation inhibitor JSH23 modestly reduced a subset of TNFα/IFNγ-responsive inflammatory genes in Elf3-deficient cells (Fig. 5, E and F), suggesting that while ELF3 primarily engages the noncanonical NFκB2 pathway, canonical NF-κB components can contribute in a gene-specific and cooperative manner. Together, these findings demonstrate that ELF3 cooperates with NFκB2 and directly regulates the iPT phenotype and broad inflammatory gene program in tubular epithelial cells.

ELF3 expression defines inflammatory epithelial phenotypes in human CKD kidneys

Our mouse genetic knockouts and cell biological studies defined the role of ELF3 and downstream NFκB2 as the key transcription factor regulating the inflammatory iPT phenotype in mice. To investigate ELF3 in human kidney disease, we analyzed spatial transcriptomic data from patients with CKD spanning stages G2 to G4 (11). ELF3 expression was minimal in CKD G2 kidneys but progressively increased in CKD G3 and G4 samples, paralleling the expansion of iPT signatures (Fig. 6A). Regions with high ELF3 expression also exhibited higher expression of NFκB2, Vcam1, and Icam1, indicating colocalization with inflammatory epithelial states (Fig. 6A). To validate these transcriptomic observations at the protein level, we performed coimmunofluorescence staining on human CKD biopsy sections. ELF3 was expressed in Vcam1-positive and NFκB2-positive iPT cells across inflamed regions. Partial overlap between ELF3 and SOX9 was observed in damaged tubules, while ELF3+ epithelial cells were frequently located near CD45+ immune infiltrates (Fig. 6B and fig. S12A). To further evaluate the association between ELF3 expression and kidney disease severity, we analyzed 95 microdissected human CKD kidney samples (39). ELF3 transcript levels showed a significant inverse correlation with eGFR (r = −0.253, P = 1.28 × 10−10) and a positive correlation with tissue histological damage, such as tissue fibrosis level (r = 0.4, P < 2 × 10−16) (Fig. 6C). Moreover, correlation analysis revealed strong associations between ELF3 and key injury- and inflammation-related genes, including SOX9, TNFRSF12A (encoding Fn14), IRF1, MAP3K14, NFκB2, and ICAM1 (Fig. 6D and fig. S13, A and B), reinforcing the link between ELF3 and inflammatory epithelial states. Together, these findings suggest that ELF3 marks a conserved, inflammation-associated epithelial program that expands with disease progression.

DISCUSSION

GWAS have identified hundreds of loci associated with kidney function, but for most, the causal genes and underlying mechanisms remain unknown (40–43). In this study, we identify ELF3 as the effector gene at a kidney function–associated GWAS locus, providing a direct molecular link between inherited genetic risk and epithelial injury responses (5). By integrating genetic, epigenomic, transcriptomic, and functional data, we establish ELF3 as a central transcriptional regulator that connects genetic susceptibility to tubular inflammation and epithelial-immune cross-talk—two key drivers of CKD progression.

Tubular epithelial cells are now recognized as active participants in kidney injury, capable of sensing environmental stress, initiating inflammatory cascades, and orchestrating tissue remodeling (2–4, 10–16). Under acute conditions, these responses promote repair and homeostasis. However, under chronic or unresolved injury, the same pathways become maladaptive, sustaining inflammation and driving fibrosis—hallmarks of the transition from acute kidney injury to CKD (16, 18, 25, 44, 45). While many downstream inflammatory mediators have been characterized, the upstream transcriptional regulators that orchestrate these changes—especially those genetically linked to disease susceptibility—are poorly defined (46–49).

In this context, ELF3 emerges as a genetically supported and functionally validated transcription factor that reprograms epithelial responses to injury (10, 25, 50, 51). Using fine-mapping approaches that combined GWAS, kidney-specific eQTL and mQTL data, and single-cell chromatin accessibility profiling, we nominate ELF3 as the most likely causal gene at a GWAS locus associated with declining kidney function (5). Notably, ELF3 is the only gene in the locus that demonstrates enhancer accessibility in PT cells, compartment-specific eQTL regulation, and consistent injury-induced expression across mouse and human datasets. These observations are further supported by large-scale epigenomic analyses of human kidney injury. A recent KPMP epigenomic atlas (25) independently prioritized ELF3 as a candidate regulator of iPT cell states (fig. S14). Together, these findings establish ELF3 as a transcription factor that is both genetically associated with CKD risk and mechanistically involved in disease pathogenesis.

Functionally, we show that ELF3 is necessary to drive a proinflammatory epithelial state. ELF3 expression is induced early in response to injury and remains elevated during chronic disease, where it defines a distinct subpopulation of iPT cells marked by sustained dedifferentiation, cytokine production, and immune cell recruitment (10, 20). Transcriptomic and CUT&RUN analyses revealed that ELF3 regulates a broad gene network enriched for TNF, interferon, and apoptosis pathways, and cooperates with the noncanonical NF-κB signaling axis. Specifically, ELF3 directly binds and activates key regulators of the NFκB2 pathway, including Nfkb2, Nfkbia, Map3k14, and Il6r (52, 53). ELF3 and NFκB2 share extensive co-occupancy at inflammatory gene promoters, including Stat1, Irf1, and Cxcl10, suggesting that ELF3 functions not only as an initiator of injury signaling but as a coregulator that amplifies sustained transcriptional activation.

Genetic ablation of ELF3 once kidney injury is present attenuated key features of tubular inflammation: iPT expansion, leukocyte infiltration, and expression of inflammatory mediators such as Vcam1, Icam1, and NF-κB signaling components. These findings suggest that ELF3 primarily governs inflammatory reprogramming in tubular epithelial cells. Blunting the epithelial-immune axis through ELF3 inhibition may reduce chronic inflammation and thereby slow disease progression.

Mechanistically, ELF3 acts upstream of or in cooperation with the noncanonical NFκB2. ELF3 deficiency impaired NFκB2 nuclear translocation in response to TNFα and IFNγ, and pharmacologic inhibition of NIK had a limited additional suppressive effect in ELF3-deficient cells. These data suggest that ELF3 is required to activate or maintain the chromatin landscape that enables NFκB2–driven transcription. This model is supported by our CUT&RUN intersection analysis, which showed that over 97% of NFκB2-bound genes are also directly bound by ELF3. In this way, ELF3 may function as a lineage-specific epigenomic gatekeeper that drives immune-responsive programs in the chronically injured tubule.

Beyond its role in epithelial-intrinsic signaling, ELF3 appears to mediate epithelial-immune cross-talk by regulating chemokine production (21–23). In both mouse and human PT cells, ELF3 was required for optimal induction of proinflammatory mediators including CCL2, CCL5, CXCL9, and CXCL10 in response to TNFα and IFNγ. Knockout of ELF3 suppressed cytokine responses even in the presence of NIK inhibition, suggesting that ELF3 exerts both NIK-dependent and NIK-independent regulatory control. This capacity to integrate multiple inflammatory cues places ELF3 at a central node of chronic kidney inflammation.

To validate the relevance of these findings in human disease, we analyzed ELF3 expression in kidneys from patients with CKD using spatial transcriptomics and multiplexed immunostaining (11). ELF3 expression was spatially confined to iPT regions coexpressing injury and inflammatory markers such as Sox9, Vcam1, Icam1, and NFκB2, and its abundance strongly correlated with decreased eGFR and increased fibrosis score. Across independent cohorts, ELF3 expression correlated with key inflammatory and profibrotic genes, including TNFRSF12A, MAP3K14, and STAT1, mirroring the regulatory network defined in our mechanistic studies. These data support ELF3’s role not only as a mechanistic effector of injury, but also as a potential biomarker of disease activity and epithelial remodeling in CKD.

While our study provides integrated genetic and mechanistic evidence supporting ELF3 as a regulator of epithelial inflammatory reprogramming, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our in vivo loss-of-function experiments were conducted using late-stage tubular deletion in an established adenine nephropathy model. Therefore, although ELF3 deletion attenuated epithelial inflammatory responses and immune cell recruitment, it did not demonstrate reversal of established fibrotic remodeling or recovery of global renal function at the examined time point (fig. S5, A and B). Although pathway enrichment analyses suggested enrichment of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-Akt, extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), cytoskeleton-associated, and cell cycle-associated programs in Elf3-deficient tubular cells, the specific genes and functional mechanisms underlying these pathways were not further dissected in the current study. These observations raise the possibility that, in addition to promoting inflammatory epithelial reprogramming, ELF3 may also participate in epithelial adaptation or prosurvival responses during kidney injury, which could partially explain the limited effects of late-stage ELF3 deletion on established fibrosis and global renal function in the adenine-induced CKD model. Second, our analyses focused on tubular epithelial ELF3, and we cannot exclude additional roles for ELF3 in other epithelial segments or injury contexts, particularly in heterogeneous human CKD where ELF3 transcripts are observed across multiple injured tubular populations. Third, although genetic fine-mapping and multiomic integration strongly nominate ELF3 as the effector gene at the eGFR-associated locus, we did not perform allele-specific perturbation of the causal regulatory variants; thus, the precise variant-to-regulatory-mechanism relationship remains to be defined. Last, CUT&RUN profiling was performed in cytokine-stimulated primary tubular epithelial cells, which model an iPT-like inflammatory state but may not fully capture the spectrum of in vivo chromatin states across different phases of kidney injury. In addition, although ELF3 functions as a transcription factor, immunostaining analyses predominantly detected perinuclear/cytoplasmic ELF3 signal in injTECs in vivo, which may reflect context-dependent regulation of ELF3 localization during kidney injury. This observation suggests that ELF3 transcriptional activity may be dynamically regulated by injury-associated cellular states, although the precise mechanisms controlling ELF3 subcellular localization in vivo remain incompletely understood. These considerations define the scope of our conclusions, which position ELF3 as a genetically supported transcriptional amplifier of maladaptive epithelial inflammation rather than a proven determinant of fibrosis regression.

Together, our findings identify ELF3 as a genetically anchored, transcriptionally active driver of proinflammatory epithelial states in kidney disease. By linking GWAS-identified risk variants to cell-intrinsic epithelial stress responses and epithelial-immune communication, ELF3 bridges the gap between inherited susceptibility and molecular pathology. Targeting ELF3 or its downstream epigenomic circuitry offers a promising strategy to selectively disrupt maladaptive tubular inflammation while preserving essential repair functions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Visualization of GWAS, mQTL, and eQTL signals at the ELF3 locus

Genomic regions of interest on chromosome 1 encompassing the ELF3 locus were visualized using LocusZoom for kidney function GWAS and mQTL signals, as previously described (5, 27). GWAS and mQTL summary statistics were obtained from previously published kidney function genetic studies. The scorecard annotations summarize integrated genetic evidence at the ELF3 locus, including GWAS, methylation QTL, and Open4Gene, as described in our previous study. eQTL results were derived from microdissected human kidney compartments, including glomerular and tubular fractions, and box plots show the relationship between genotype and normalized ELF3 expression levels, as described in the original study (27).

Human single-nucleus ATAC-seq datasets

Single-nucleus ATAC-seq data were obtained from a previously published study from our laboratory (5), as detailed in table S3. All data processing and analyses were performed as described in the original publication.

Human single-cell RNA-seq datasets

Previously published human kidney single-cell RNA-seq datasets (31), including those generated by our laboratory (11) and other research groups (10), were obtained from public repositories or directly from the original authors. A complete list of datasets and references is provided in table S3. Processed .h5ad files were imported into Scanpy for analysis. Low-quality cells were filtered based on gene counts and mitochondrial transcript percentage, followed by log-normalization and identification of highly variable genes. PCA, neighborhood graph construction, UMAP, and Leiden clustering were performed using default Scanpy workflows. PT cells were subsetted and reclustered to define iPT states. Differential expression between iPT subclusters was computed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

Human FFPE (formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded) Visium spatial transcriptomics

Human kidney FFPE Visium data were generated previously and described in detail (11). In brief, RNA was extracted from FFPE blocks using the RNeasy FFPE kit (QIAGEN, 73504), and samples meeting quality thresholds (DV200 > 50%) based on Agilent Bioanalyzer assessment were processed. Five-micrometer sections were mounted on Visium Spatial Gene Expression Slides and processed following the manufacturer’s FFPE workflow. Raw FASTQ files and Space Ranger–processed count matrices from this dataset were obtained and reanalyzed in the present study using our downstream computational pipeline described below.

Human CKD bulk transcriptomic datasets and correlation analysis

Correlation analyses were performed using transcriptomic data from 95 human CKD samples as previously described (39). Gene expression values (transcripts per million, TPM) were log2-transformed, and pairwise correlations between ELF3 and clinical traits (eGFR, fibrosis score) or selected injury/inflammatory markers were quantified using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Statistical analyses and visualization were conducted in R using the stats and ggplot2 packages.

Mouse kidney bulk transcriptomic datasets

Bulk kidney transcriptomic data were obtained from previously published studies involving mouse models of kidney disease. Specifically, datasets from the UUO model, FA-induced kidney injury model, and APOL1 transgenic mouse model were derived from previously published studies (28–30). Detailed information regarding animal models, sample sizes, disease severity, and experimental procedures is provided in the original publications.

Mouse models and kidney injury protocols

All mouse studies were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Pennsylvania. Pax8-rtTA (JAX no. 007176), TetO-Cre (TRECre) (stock no. 00623), and Elf3fl/fl mice (JAX no. 030056) were intercrossed to generate doxycycline-inducible, tubular epithelial-specific knockout mice (Pax8-rtTA; TetO-Cre; Elf3fl/fl) (35). Elf3fl/fl littermates lacking Cre were used as controls. All mice were male and 6 to 8 weeks old at the start of experiments, and were housed under specific pathogen–free conditions with food and water provided ad libitum.

Chronic kidney injury was induced using a 0.2% adenine-containing diet (TD.160020, Inotiv) (54). Mice were first fed a standard adenine diet for 7 days, followed by doxycycline-adenine chow for the remaining 3 weeks. This design allowed for ELF3 ablation during the progression phase of injury while preserving its expression during the initial response. Mice were euthanized at designated time points for tissue collection and downstream analyses, including histology, immunostaining, and RNA and protein extraction.

Histological analysis

Kidneys were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4-μm thickness. Sections were stained with H&E using standard protocols. For immunofluorescence staining, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval, blocked in 5% donkey serum, and incubated with primary antibodies listed in table S2. The specificity of the ELF3 and NFκB2 antibodies used in this study was validated experimentally and is shown in fig. S1. Detection was performed using fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies, and nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). RNA ISH was conducted on paraffin sections using RNAscope 2.5 HD Duplex Detection Kit (bio-techne) following the manufacturers’ original protocol. Probe information is provided in table S2. Images were captured using a Leica fluorescence or bright-field microscope and analyzed using ImageJ.

Tubular injury was evaluated on H&E-stained kidney sections. Cortical fields were randomly selected and scored in a blinded manner. Tubular injury was defined by the presence of tubular dilation, epithelial cell flattening, luminal cast formation, and loss of brush border. Injury severity was graded using a semiquantitative scale based on the percentage of affected tubules: 0, none; 1, <10%; 2, 10 to 25%; 3, 26 to 50%; and 4, >50%. Scores from multiple fields per kidney were averaged to obtain a single value per animal.

Colocalization analysis was performed using Fiji/ImageJ. ELF3-positive cells were identified within tubular regions based on nuclear morphology and ELF3 fluorescence signal. Marker-positive cells were defined using uniform threshold settings applied across all samples. The proportion of marker-positive cells within the ELF3-positive cell population was quantified. Quantification was conducted on randomly selected cortical fields in a blinded manner. A minimum of five fields per section and six to eight biological replicates per group were analyzed. Data are expressed as the percentage of marker-positive cells among ELF3-positive cells.

Immune cell infiltration was quantified on immunofluorescence-stained kidney sections. F4/80+ macrophages and CD45+ leukocytes were identified using uniform threshold settings in Fiji/ImageJ. Positive cells were counted within randomly selected cortical fields and normalized to tissue area. Data are expressed as the number of positive cells per square millimeter. Quantification was performed in a blinded manner using at least five fields per section and six to eight biological replicates per group.

Primary renal tubular epithelial cell isolation

Primary renal tubular epithelial cells were isolated from 2.5-week-old mouse kidneys. Kidneys were harvested, decapsulated, and minced into small fragments in cold phosphate-buffered saline. Tissues were digested with collagenase type I (1 mg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C for 30 min with gentle agitation, and digestion was terminated with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cell suspension was sequentially filtered through 100-, 70-, and 40-μm strainers, followed by centrifugation at 1000g for 10 min. Red blood cells (RBCs) were removed using RBC lysis buffer (Zenbio). Cells were resuspended and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium (ITS) under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). All experiments were performed using early-passage cultures (P1) to preserve tubular epithelial identity. Cell purity was validated by immunofluorescence staining for tubular epithelial markers and by assessing the absence of fibroblast markers (fig. S7A).

To achieve ELF3 deletion in primary tubular cells, renal tubular epithelial cells isolated from Elf3flox/flox mice were transduced with either green fluorescent protein (GFP)–Cre adenovirus or GFP adenovirus as a control (University of Iowa Viral Vector Core). Following a 48-hour incubation, cells were subjected to the specified experimental treatments. Cells collected at 3 hours were processed for RNA analysis, whereas those collected at 24 hours were used for immunofluorescence staining and protein quantification.

Human renal PT epithelial cell culture

Immortalized human proximal tubule epithelial cells [hTERT-RPTECs; American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) CRL-4031] were maintained according to the supplier’s recommendations. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/F12 basal medium supplemented with the hTERT RPTEC Growth Kit and 0.5% FBS, and incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were passaged at ∼90% confluence using trypsin-EDTA (ATCC PCS-999-003), followed by neutralization with soybean trypsin inhibitor, and replated at the appropriate density for downstream experiments.

CUT&RUN

Primary renal tubular epithelial cells were isolated from mouse kidneys and cultured under standard conditions. Cells were stimulated with recombinant TNFα (20 ng/ml) and IFNγ (20 ng/ml) for 3 hours prior to CUT&RUN. CUT&RUN was performed using the CUTANA CUT&RUN Assay Kit (EpiCypher, catalog no. 14-1048) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were immobilized on concanavalin A–coated magnetic beads, permeabilized, and incubated with anti-ELF3 or anti-NFκB2 antibody (listed in table S2) overnight at 4°C. After incubation with pA-MNase, chromatin digestion was activated with calcium, and released fragments were collected. Released DNA fragments were purified using the CUT&RUN DNA Purification Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog no. 86654) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, catalog no. E7645) and sequenced on an Illumina platform [paired-end, 50–base pair (bp) reads].

Library quality and fragment-size distribution were assessed using an Agilent TapeStation, demonstrating characteristic mono- and subnucleosomal peaks consistent with high-quality CUT&RUN libraries (fig. S8, A and B). Signal-to-noise characteristics of CUT&RUN data were further evaluated using fingerprint plots (fig. S8C). The ELF3 and NFκB2 antibodies used for CUT&RUN were validated by Western blotting and immunofluorescence (fig. S1). Antibody specificity for chromatin binding was further supported by enrichment of canonical ETS-family motifs (for ELF3) and NF-κB motifs (for NFκB2), as well as enrichment of CUT&RUN signal at peak centers (fig. S8, D to F).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells or kidney tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined by NanoDrop spectrophotometry. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 2 μg of total RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad) on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Gene expression was normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh), and relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in table S3.

Western blotting

Cells or kidney tissues were lysed directly in 3× SDS Blue Loading Buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog no. 56036S) and heated at 95°C for 5 min to denature proteins. Lysates were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBS-T) for 1 hour at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies and developed using ECL detection reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were captured using a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Primary antibodies are listed in table S2. Densitometric quantification was performed using ImageJ, and protein levels were normalized to GAPDH.

Bulk RNA-seq of in vitro tubular epithelial cells

Total RNA was extracted from cytokine-treated RPTECs and virus-transduced mouse primary tubular epithelial cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN). RNA integrity was confirmed with the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100, and only samples with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) > 7 were used for library preparation. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit, and paired-end 150-bp sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq platform (Novogene). Raw sequence quality was assessed with FastQC (v0.11.9), and adapter/low-quality reads were trimmed with TrimGalore (v0.4.5). Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38, Gencode annotation) or mouse reference genome (mm10/GRCm38, Gencode annotation) using STAR (v2.4.1d), and transcript quantification was performed with RSEM (v1.3.0). DEGs were identified using DESeq2 (v1.32.0) with cutoff |log2FC| > 1 and FDR < 0.05.

CUT&RUN data analysis

Raw sequencing reads were trimmed using TrimGalore (v0.6.6) and aligned to the mouse genome (mm10) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.4) with the parameters --very-sensitive --no-mixed --no-discordant. Duplicate reads and mitochondrial reads were removed. Peaks were called using MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) with --nomodel --shift -75 --extsize 150 parameters. Only peaks with q value < 0.01 were retained. Peak annotation and visualization were performed using ChIPseeker and deepTools, respectively. Motif enrichment analysis was conducted using HOMER (v4.11) with default parameters.

Statistics

Data are presented as means ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test was applied. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
