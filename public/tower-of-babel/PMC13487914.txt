Fluid management and outcomes of dengue fever in Japan: single-center descriptive study in a non-endemic country
Authors: Tetsuya Suzuki, Kei Yamamoto, Kozue Takahashi, Sho Saito, Kayoko Hayakawa, Norio Ohmagari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue fever (DF) is endemic in tropical and subtropical regions. Nonetheless, sporadic cases of autochthonous dengue have also been reported in non-endemic countries. Although preparedness for dengue outbreaks in these settings is essential, evidence describing clinical presentation of patients and fluid management remains limited. This single-center retrospective descriptive study was conducted to examine the clinical management of DF in Japan, with a particular focus on fluid management.MethodsThis study included patients diagnosed with DF from January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2020. The inclusion criterion was a body temperature of ≥ 37.0 °C. Epidemiological, demographic, and clinically relevant data were obtained through data extraction and chart review.ResultsOverall, 162 patients (115 inpatients and 47 outpatients) were included. The median age was 32 years (interquartile range [IQR], 24–42 years), and 59.3% were male. Six patients (3.7%) reported a history of DF. The median time from symptom onset to initial presentation was 3 days (IQR, 1–5 days), and 38 patients (23.5%) presented with warning signs (WS) at initial presentation. Among hospitalized patients, intravenous fluids were administered to 92.2%, with most receiving extracellular fluids. The median fluid volumes administered within 24 h, 72 h, and 7 days after diagnosis were 2569, 6769, and 10,011 mL, respectively, in severe disease; 1734, 5103, and 7855 mL in non-severe disease with WS; and 1500, 3650, and 5500 mL in non-severe disease without WS, respectively. These volumes were lower than those recommended by the World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Nevertheless, no deaths or intensive care unit admissions occurred, and all inpatients were discharged without major sequelae.ConclusionThis study described the clinical management of DF in Japan. During hospitalization, effective fluid administration may be achievable with volumes lower than conventional recommendations. Further studies are warranted to determine optimal fluid administration strategies and volumes for adult patients with DF in high-resource, non-endemic settings, including Japan.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-01001-w.Keywords: Dengue fever, Fluid therapy, Non-endemic countries, Hospitalization, Adult, Japan

Background

Dengue fever (DF) is a mosquito-borne viral infection. An estimated 96 million people worldwide developed symptomatic infections in 2010 [1]. To date, vaccines have been introduced in several countries [2]. Nonetheless, no antiviral drugs are available, and fluid replacement therapy remains the mainstay of treatment. Appropriate management of plasma leakage is essential, and the World Health Organization (WHO) and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) have issued guidelines on recommended fluid replacement volumes for clinical management [3, 4]. Randomized controlled trials [5, 6] in children from endemic countries have examined appropriate fluid volumes for the treatment of dengue hemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome, whereas studies involving adults or patients with non-severe DF remain limited. Furthermore, evidence regarding treatment in non-endemic countries is limited.

Recently, cases of autochthonous DF have been reported in non-endemic countries, including the United States [7], France [8], and Italy [9]. Japan is also a non-endemic country for DF. Nonetheless, an outbreak caused by autochthonous cases was reported [10] in 2014, and concerns remain regarding future DF outbreaks associated with climate change [11]. Therefore, non-endemic countries, including Japan, should be prepared for possible dengue outbreaks. However, data on the treatment of dengue patients in these countries, particularly regarding fluid administration, remain limited. Therefore, we retrospectively summarized fluid administration practices in patients with DF to provide insights into clinical management in non-endemic countries. The aim of this study was to describe clinical management and fluid administration practices of patients with DF at a designated medical institution for specified infectious disease in Japan.

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

This single-center, retrospective, descriptive study was conducted at the National Center for Global Health and Medicine, a Designated Medical Institution for Specified Infectious Diseases in Shinjuku, Tokyo. Patients who visited the Department of Infectious Diseases between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2020, were included if they were diagnosed with DF and had a body temperature of ≥ 37.0 °C at the initial visit. This body temperature criterion was established to include patients in the acute phase and to exclude patients diagnosed with DF overseas who presented for post-recovery health evaluations. The body temperature threshold was set lower than usual to account for patients who had taken antipyretics before presentation. DF was diagnosed primarily using rapid diagnostic testing for NS-1 antigen or IgM antibodies. In Japan, health insurance coverage for rapid diagnostic tests for DF is limited to medical institutions capable of admitting patients; however, other medical institutions may also perform these tests as administrative tests through public health centers. Although the study site did not establish explicit criteria for admission, treatment, or discharge, hospital admission was strongly encouraged for patients with inadequate oral fluid intake, warning signs, or poor general condition that could hinder frequent hospital visits. In the outpatient settings, patients were advised to undergo frequent follow-up assessments and were instructed to maintain adequate oral fluid intake, avoid NSAIDs use, and monitor for signs of bleeding, although strict monitoring was often difficult. After hospitalization, clinicians decided whether to initiate intravenous fluid therapy based on the patients’ oral fluid intake, warning signs, and anticipated clinical course. Discharge was typically considered when patients were able to maintain adequate oral intake, their general conditions had improved, they were afebrile, and their thrombocytopenia had begun to resolve. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Japan Institute of Health Security (JIHS-S-004348). The requirement for individual informed consent was waived; however, patients were provided the opportunity to opt out.

Data collection

Patients were screened using the department’s patient database, and epidemiological, demographic, and relevant clinical data were collected via data extraction and chart review for those meeting the inclusion criteria. Data on medical expenses were unavailable for patients whose payments were made outside the Japanese health insurance system (e.g., short-term foreign visitors). Based on WHO guidelines [3], patients with any of the following were classified as having “DF with warning signs” (WS): abdominal pain or tenderness, persistent vomiting, clinical fluid accumulation (ascites or pleural effusion), mucosal bleeding, lethargy or restlessness, hepatomegaly > 2 cm, or an increase in hematocrit accompanied by a rapid decrease in platelet count. Patients with any of the following were classified as having “severe dengue”: severe plasma leakage leading to shock or fluid accumulation with respiratory distress, severe bleeding (as assessed by clinicians), or severe organ involvement, including aspartate aminotransferase or alanine aminotransferase levels ≥ 1000 U/L, impaired consciousness, or organ failure.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and categorical variables as percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Because of the small number of severe dengue cases, these patients were excluded from the severity-based analyses, and statistical comparisons were limited to patients with non-severe dengue with warning signs and those without warning signs. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study flow and patient characteristics

Overall, 230 patients were screened, of whom 68 were excluded. The remaining 162 were included in the analysis. Among them, 115 were hospitalized, and 47 were managed as outpatients. The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. The median age was 32 years (IQR, 24–42 years); 3.7% were aged < 16 years and 1.9% were aged ≥ 65 years. Women and men accounted for 40.7% and 59.3% of patients, respectively. Almost all patients were in good health. Only three patients (1.9%) had diabetes and five (3.1%) had hypertension. None had chronic heart failure or kidney disease. Although six patients (3.7%) reported a history of DF, no supporting medical records or documentation were available. Rapid diagnostic test indicated that two patients were negative for both IgM and IgG, two were negative for IgM and positive for IgG, and the remaining two were positive for both IgM and IgG. Overall, 18 patients (15.0%) tested positive for IgG on rapid testing, including four (2.5%) who were positive for both IgM and IgG. Among all patients, 119 (73.5%) were estimated to have acquired the infection in Southeast Asia and 12 (7.4%) in Japan. Although DF is not endemic in Japan, an outbreak of autochthonous dengue occurred in 2014, and all cases originated from that outbreak. The median time from symptom onset to the first medical visit was 3 days (IQR, 1–5 days). All patients except one presented within 10 days of symptom onset. One patient presented 20 days after symptom onset; however, the NS1 antigen rapid diagnostic test result was positive. Therefore, all included patients were considered to be in the acute phase of dengue infection. At the initial visit, 38 patients (23.5%) exhibited warning signs. Clinical course, treatment, and outcomes by severity

No deaths or intensive care unit admissions occurred among hospitalized patients, and all inpatients were discharged without major sequelae. The median length of hospital stay was 7 days (IQR, 5–8 days), the median duration of fever was 6 days (IQR, 5–7 days), and the median time from symptom onset to discharge was 9 days (IQR, 8–10 days). Among non-severe patients, only the minimum platelet count and fever duration were significantly lower and longer, respectively, in those with warning signs. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) were used in only three patients (2.6%) in the non-severe dengue without WS group. Of these patients, one received NSAIDs as an antipyretic, one as both an analgesic and antipyretic, and one as an analgesic. Transfusions were required in only two severe patients (1.7%), both of whom received platelet transfusions. Details are presented in Table 2.

Intravenous fluids were administered to 106 of 115 hospitalized patients (92.2%). Among the nine patients who did not receive intravenous fluids, three were classified as having non-severe dengue with WS and six as non-severe dengue without WS. Only one patient in the non-severe dengue without WS group received exclusively hypotonic fluids. The median total fluid volumes administered at 24 h, 72 h, and 7 days after diagnosis were severe 2569, 6769, and 10,011 mL in patients with severe dengue; 1734, 5103, and 7855 mL in patients with non-severe dengue with WS; and 1500, 3650, and 5500 mL in patients with non-severe dengue without WS, respectively. Clinical course, treatment, and outcomes by admission timing

Hospitalized patients were categorized according to admission timing: those admitted at the initial visit (i.e., hospitalized on the day of their first visit to our hospital) and those admitted at a subsequent visit (i.e., hospitalized during a later scheduled or unscheduled visit after initial outpatient management). Patients admitted at the initial visit had a longer interval between symptom onset and their first visit than those admitted at a subsequent visit. This suggests that patients admitted at the initial visit tended to delay seeking medical care until their condition had progressed, resulting in immediate hospitalization upon presentation. In contrast, patients who sought care earlier in the course of illness were clinically stable at the initial visit and were initially managed as outpatients before being admitted at a subsequent visit. Hospital stay was longer among patients hospitalized at the initial visit. However, no significant differences were observed in fever duration (defined as the number of days from symptom onset to defervescence) or in the time from symptom onset to discharge. This finding likely reflects that many patients admitted at a subsequent visit had already experienced part of their febrile course before hospitalization. The median total fluid volumes administered within 24 h, 72 h, and 7 days after diagnosis were 2000, 5300, and 8050 mL, respectively among patients admitted at the initial visit compared with 0, 1000, and 4000 mL among those admitted at a subsequent visit. Healthcare cost

Medical cost data were unavailable for patients who were not covered by Japan’s public health insurance system (e.g., short-term foreign visitors). Consequently, cost data were available for only 66% of the study population. Among patients managed exclusively as outpatients, the median medical cost was 26,875 yen (IQR 21,605–36,315 yen), equivalent to approximately 169 US dollars (IQR, 136–228 US dollars), based on an approximate exchange rate of \159 per US$1in April 2026; the same exchange rate was applied throughout the analysis. For inpatients, the median medical cost according to disease severity was 409,630 yen (2,576 US dollars; IQR not applicable, n = 1) for severe dengue, 287,675 yen (IQR, 216,160–354,905 yen; 1806 US dollars [IQR, 1359–2232 US dollars]) for non-severe dengue with warning signs, and 270,820 yen (IQR, 219,010–314,265 yen; 1703 US dollars [IQR, 1377–1977 US dollars]) for non-severe dengue without warning signs.

When stratified by admission timing, the median medical cost was 294,260 yen (IQR, 232,470–352,295 yen; 1851 US dollars [IQR, 1462–2216 US dollars]) among patients admitted at the initial visit and 219,085 yen (IQR, 192,772–255,310 yen; 1378 US dollars [IQR, 1212–1606 US dollars]) among those admitted at a subsequent visit.

Discussion

This single-center study described fluid administration volumes and clinical outcomes among patients with DF in Japan, a non-endemic country. Studies addressing the clinical management of DF in non-endemic, high-resource countries remain scarce. Although conducted at a single center, this study examined patient outcomes in relation to fluid administration volumes. A previous study from Japan using data from the National Database of Health Insurance Claims [12] identified potential deviations from international guidelines, including NSAID use in 13.2% of DF cases. Conversely, NSAID use in this study was 2.6%. NSAID use may have been restricted because of guideline adherence at the Designated Medical Institution for Specified Infectious Diseases; nonetheless, a key strength of this study was the use of actual chart reviews, enabling accurate confirmation of diagnoses, clinical courses, and treatment details.

Patients in this study sought medical care approximately 3 days after symptom onset, and 71.0% (115 patients) were ultimately hospitalized. The median hospital stay was 7 days. A previous study [13] from Singapore reported a median interval of 6 days between symptom onset and medical consultation. Another report [14] from Singapore indicated that the hospitalization rate has remained approximately 30% since 2013, and the median length of hospitalization was 3 days. Relative to these data, patients in Japan appear to seek care at tertiary care facilities earlier after symptom onset; hospitalization appears to be more frequent, and hospital stays tend to be longer. Along with institutional constraints in Japan, where insurance coverage for rapid diagnostic tests for dengue is limited to medical facilities capable of admitting patients, easier access to tertiary care facilities likely influenced these results. Furthermore, because Japan is a non-endemic country, DF may be perceived as a rare infectious disease, which could contribute to the high hospitalization rate and prolonged length of stay.

The duration of fever and the time from onset to discharge were comparable between patients admitted at the initial visit and those admitted at a subsequent visit. Some hospitalized patients may have derived limited additional benefit from intravenous fluid therapy and may not have required hospitalization. However, because admission criteria and length of stay are influenced by factors such as healthcare system, hospital capacity, and public attitudes and expectations regarding medical care, direct comparisons of the appropriateness of admission criteria across countries are difficult. Nevertheless, outpatient management is associated with lower medical costs than hospitalization, and earlier hospitalization results in higher healthcare expenditures. In Singapore, 97.3% of DF cases were hospitalized in 2003; however, revision of the admission criteria reportedly reduced the hospitalization rate without worsening patient outcomes [13]. Even in non-endemic countries such as Japan, establishing practical admission criteria may be important in preparation for future increase in dengue cases. In this context, it is important to consider that most DF patients in Japan may be assumed to have a primary infection, as in our study. Many patients in our cohort were hospitalized until 2016. In Japan, a domestic DF outbreak occurred in 2014, and its social impact may have influenced patient management policies. Since 2017, the number of hospitalized patients has decreased, and outpatient follow-up based on each patient’s condition has become more common.

Regarding intravenous fluids, hypotonic solutions were rarely administered in this study, whereas isotonic fluids were administered in most cases. This pattern was consistent with the recommendations of the WHO [3] and CDC [4]. The median volume of fluid administered within the first 24 h to patients with non-severe dengue and warning signs in our study was 1734 mL. The CDC Pocket Guide [4] recommends administering 10 mL/kg during the first hour, followed by 5–7 mL/kg/h for 2–4 h if the patient stabilizes, then 3–5 mL/kg/h for an additional 2–4 h, and subsequently 2–4 mL/kg/h. Based on these recommendations, a 60-kg patient receiving fluid therapy for 24 h after diagnosis would receive approximately 3840–6600 mL of fluid. Similarly, calculations based on the WHO guidelines3 yield a fluid volume of approximately 3180–4920 mL for a 60-kg patient. The intravenous fluid volumes administered in our study were lower than those recommended by the WHO and CDC and were closer to the total fluid volume of approximately 2500 mL (total amount of oral intake and intravenous fluids) recommended in the Sri Lankan guidelines [15], one of the few national guidelines that provide explicit quantitative recommendations for adult dengue patients. The fluid volumes recommended by the WHO are primarily based on studies involving pediatric patients, and one study [16] suggested the limitations of extrapolating these findings to adults. The lower fluid volumes observed in our study may also reflect the fact that patients presented at varying times after symptom onset and were therefore in different clinical phase at the time of diagnosis. Consequently, the study population may not fully correspond to the patient populations assumed by the WHO and CDC recommendations. Nevertheless, a key study [17] from Sri Lanka verifying fluid administration in adults with dengue hemorrhagic fever reported mean fluid volumes of 1395.4 mL during the first 12 h, and 1401.2 mL during the subsequent 12 h, corresponding to a total of approximately 2800 mL over the first 24 h. This fluid volume was comparable to the pattern observed in this study. These findings suggest that adult patients with DF in the acute phase may often be managed with volumes below those recommended by the WHO or CDC. Because excessive fluid administration in non-severe DF has been reported to worsen prognosis [18], and further research is needed to determine appropriate fluid administration volumes for adult patients.

This study has some limitations. As a retrospective descriptive study, it describes actual fluid administration practices in patients with DF; these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that the administered fluid volumes were optimal. Because the data reflect the clinical practices of hospitals in Japan specializing in infectious disease care, generalizability is limited. Nonetheless, by clearly documenting current clinical practice in Japan, this study is expected to provide valuable insights for clinical settings across the country. Finally, because medical cost data were incomplete, cost-related findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

This study described the clinical management of DF at a specialized medical institution in Japan. In countries such as Japan, where access to tertiary care is readily available, hospitalization may occur at a relatively low threshold; however, some patients may derive limited benefit from early admission. Additionally, during hospitalization, patients may be managed with more conservative fluid volumes than those traditionally recommended by international guidelines. If domestic outbreaks occur because of climate change, a rise in hospitalized patients would increase medical costs and the associated financial burden. Further research is needed to establish practical admission criteria and fluid administration strategies for patients with dengue in high-resource, non-endemic settings, including Japan.
