Role of spectral CT in quantitative evaluation of pathologic response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma: a pilot study
Authors: Huanhuan Zheng, Yan Zhu, MiaoMiao Li, Qinglei Zhang, Xiaolei Wei, Xingbiao Chen, Wei Ren, Song Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo find a useful non-invasive biomarker for evaluating treatment response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (nICT) in patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), using spectral computed tomography (CT).MethodsSpectral CT-derived parameters including the 40 keV virtual monoenergetic imaging (VMI), iodine concentration (IC), and normalized iodine concentration (NIC) were obtained before and after nICT in 87 patients with ESCC. Independent samples t-test (normality) or Mann-Whitney U test (non-normality) was used to compare the differences of spectral CT-derived parameters between responders and non-responders. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictive factors for responders. Diagnostic performance of parameters in predicting response was tested with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsThe post-area, post-40 keV VMI, post-IC, and post-NIC of the responders were significantly lower than those of the non-responders (p < 0.001, < 0.001, < 0.001, and 0.002, respectively). Post-area (odds ratio [OR], 0.980, 95% confidence interval [95% CI], 0.970–0.990; p < 0.001) and post-NIC (OR, 0.824, 95% CI, 0.723–0.938; p = 0.003) were independent post-treatment predictors of therapeutic efficacy. A predictive model combining post-NIC and post-area showed the most favorable diagnostic performance with an area under the curve of 0.844.ConclusionsPost-NIC and post-area of tumor might serve as independent monitoring biomarkers for assessing pathological response to nICT in ESCC. The model combining post-NIC and post-area may provide preliminary imaging evidence for individualized clinical decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08443-0.Keywords: Esophageal neoplasms, Spectral CT, Neoadjuvant therapy, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is the 6th leading cause of cancer-related death globally [1]. In China, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is the predominant histological type. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors have achieved remarkable progress in the treatment of various malignancies and have been recommended for the management of advanced esophageal cancer [2, 3]. Chemotherapy and immunotherapy exert synergistic antitumor effects, as chemotherapeutic agents can augment the immunogenicity of tumor cells and modulate the activity of immune cells [4]. However, the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (nICT) for locally advanced esophageal cancer remain controversial. Responders achieved better survival outcomes, while non‑responders may face unnecessary treatment-related toxicity and missed opportunities for effective disease control [5, 6]. Tumor regression grade (TRG), based on residual tumor cells, fibrosis, and inflammation in surgical specimens, is currently the pathological indicator that directly reflects the biological response of tumors to neoadjuvant therapy [7]. However, TRG assessment relies on invasive surgical tissue sampling and yields delayed results. Thus, developing a noninvasive preoperative biomarker to identify patients likely to benefit from nICT is crucial for optimizing individualized management of ESCC.

Currently, computed tomography (CT) is a widely utilized imaging modality for the assessment of esophageal cancer. The advancement of CT has promoted the clinical application of spectral CT, extending its utility beyond conventional anatomical imaging to functional evaluation. Through material decomposition and quantitative analysis, spectral CT provides key information on tumor characterization and activity, which is essential for understanding the biological behavior of lesions [8]. Previous studies have demonstrated that spectral CT is valuable for the evaluation of gastrointestinal tumors. Compared with standard linearly-blended reconstructions, 40 keV virtual monoenergetic imaging (VMI) improves the reliability of size measurements and diagnostic accuracy for colorectal liver metastases [9]. Moreover, iodine material decomposition maps can be applied to determine histological subtypes of gastric adenocarcinoma [10], differentiate between benign and malignant gastric lesions [11], identify colorectal cancers with microsatellite instability [12], predict lymph node metastasis [13] and evaluate the response to chemotherapy and immunotherapy in both gastric and colorectal cancers [14, 15]. Additionally, spectral CT can acquire high-quality images at low radiation doses, which significantly reduces patient radiation exposure risk and offers a safer imaging evaluation method for patients requiring long-term follow-up.

However, its value in predicting the response to nICT in esophageal cancer patients remains unclear. Previous studies have reported the application of spectral CT in the diagnosis and prognostic monitoring of esophageal cancer [16–18]. For example, Wang J et al. reported that a combined model incorporating spectral CT-derived parameters and clinical features could help predict the response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in ESCC patients [18]. Nevertheless, its utility in forecasting treatment response to nICT in ESCC has not been sufficiently investigated. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the value of spectral CT-derived parameters as a non-invasive biomarker in the preoperative individualized prediction of treatment response to nICT in patients with ESCC.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study was approved by our institutional review board (IRB, 2024-081-02) and followed the ethical standards of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki). Informed consent was waived for patients. The data of patients with ESCC who underwent spectral CT examination and nICT between April 2022 and April 2026 were collected. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of esophageal cancer confirmed by endoscopic biopsy; (2) received nICT and radical esophagectomy; (3) underwent chest contrast-enhanced spectral CT scans before and after nICT. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) poor spectral CT image quality due to artifacts, and (2) local or systematic treatment before spectral CT scan. Finally, 87 patients (70 men and 17 women; mean age, 65.89 ± 8.88 years; range, 27–78 years) formed the study cohort (Fig. 1).

Spectral CT protocols

Before and after nICT, chest contrast-enhanced spectral CT scans were performed using a 128-row dual-energy CT (IQon Spectral CT, Philips Healthcare). Patients underwent scanning in the supine position with the following scan parameters: tube voltage of 120 kV, tube current using automatic tube current modulation, 64 mm × 0.625 mm collimation, 0.5 s rotation time, 1.016 pitch, matrix size of 512 × 512, slice thickness 5 mm, and reconstruction slice thickness 1 mm. Using a high-pressure injector, 80 ml of iodinated contrast agent was administered via the median cubital vein at 3 ml/s, followed by a 20 ml saline flush at the same rate. Arterial phase images were acquired 30–40 s after injection completion.

Image analysis

All spectral CT images were independently reviewed by a radiologist (Z.H.H.) with 9 years of experience in diagnosing gastrointestinal tumors. A second radiologist (L.M.M.) with 10 years of experience independently analyzed the images of 30 cases for assessment of inter-observer agreement. Both readers were blinded to the clinicopathological information of the patients. In an effort to strengthen methodological rigor and clarity, we revised the description of the spectral CT image review procedure in the Image Analysis subsection.

The spectral CT images were uploaded to the Philips ISP image workstation (IntelliSpace Portal V 10, Philips Healthcare, the Netherlands), and CT 40 keV VMI, and iodine concentration (IC) maps were subsequently generated (Fig. 2). The region of interest (ROI) was used for two purposes: extracting spectral CT quantitative parameters and measuring tumor area. Based on the 40 keV VMI, a free-hand ROI was placed on the largest cross-section of the tumor, aiming to include as much tumor tissue as possible while excluding the esophageal lumen and adjacent structures. If the lesion had largely shrunk after chemotherapy and immunotherapy, based on the previous CT spectral images, the ROI was placed in the same area of the tumor as before the treatment. After ROI delineation, the post-processing workstation automatically calculated the tumor area within the ROI (i.e., the cross-sectional area of the ROI, in mm²) and simultaneously extracted the 40 keV VMI and IC values within the ROI. In addition, the iodine concentration value of the aorta at the corresponding level (IC-A) was obtained. The normalized iodine concentration (NIC) was calculated using the following formula: NIC = IC / IC-A×100%. In order to conduct a comparison of measurements made by different observers, 30 patients (15 responders and 15 non-responders) were randomly selected for consistency assessment.

Treatment options and tumor response evaluation

Patients received 1 to 4 cycles of nICT before the surgery, with a cycle interval of 21–30 days. The chemotherapy consisted of albumin-bound paclitaxel and carboplatin, and immunotherapy was delivered using anti-programmed cell death protein 1 antibodies, including camrelizumab, tislelizumab, sintilimab, and nivolumab. The radical esophageal cancer surgery was performed 4 to 6 weeks after the end of the treatment of nICT.

The postoperative pathological tumor response and regression evaluation criteria for ESCC after nICT are as follows: no residual viable tumor cells are defined as TRG 0; residual tumor cells accounting for less than 10% are defined as TRG 1; residual tumor cells ranging from 10% to 50% are defined as TRG 2; and residual tumor cells exceeding 50% are defined as TRG 3. Responders are classified as TRG 0 and TRG 1, while non-responders are classified as TRG 2 and TRG 3.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the normality assumption for all parameters, with results shown in Supplemental Table 1. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to evaluate the differences in spectral CT-derived parameters among the TRG. Independent samples t-test (normality) or Mann-Whitney U test (non-normality) was used to compare differences in continuous variables between responders and non-responders. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. To determine the independent predictors of the responders, variables with p < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were finally included in the binary logistic regression analysis with a stepwise backward method. Prior to binary logistic regression analysis, collinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF; VIF < 5 indicates acceptable collinearity, while VIF > 10 suggests severe multicollinearity). Following the EPV ≥ 10 rule to prevent overfitting, we restricted the number of independent variables in the multivariable logistic regression model to ≤ 1/10 of the total positive events. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was performed to evaluate model fit, with a p value > 0.05 indicating good calibration. The diagnostic performance of the parameters in predicting treatment response was tested with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and DeLong’s test. Inter-observer agreement in measurement of parameters was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) which indicated satisfactory agreement (ICC > 0.800). Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (v.22.0 for Microsoft Windows x64, SPSS, Chicago, IL). ROC analysis was performed using MedCalc Statistical Software version 19 (MedCalc Software bvba, Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2019). A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

No statistically significant difference was observed in quantitative spectral CT-derived parameters between pathological responders and non-responders prior to nICT (all p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Quantitative spectral CT-derived parameters after nICT

At the end of the nICT cycle, the spectral CT parameters of the tumor increased with the degree of TRG (all p < 0.05) (Supplemental Table 2). Compared to the pathological non-responder group, the responder group showed significantly lower values in the post-area, post-40 keV VMI, post-IC, and post-NIC (p < 0.001, < 0.001, < 0.001, and 0.002, respectively) (Table 1) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Unlike conventional CT, which relies on single-energy spectra for anatomical visualization, spectral CT uses multi-energy spectrum scanning to generate quantitative parameter maps. With a single scan, it can simultaneously achieve structural and functional imaging. In this study, spectral CT-derived parameters including the 40 keV VMI, IC, and NIC were evaluated. The findings showed that spectral CT after treatment may have potential for the non-invasive assessment of the therapeutic response to nICT in ESCC patients, with the post-treatment predictive model combining post-NIC and post-area yielding the most favorable diagnostic outcome.

IC is a quantitative parameter derived from spectral CT material decomposition, which reflects the absolute iodine concentration of tumor tissues. NIC is generated by normalizing tumor IC to the iodine concentration of the aorta or adjacent large vessels, effectively minimizing inter-individual variations in cardiac output and circulatory transit time. Although studies investigating the utility of CT iodine maps to predict treatment response in esophageal cancer remain limited, existing evidence confirms significant differences in IC and NIC among patients with different neoadjuvant therapy outcomes [18, 19]. IC and NIC represent imaging biomarkers of tumor vascularization and permeability [20]. Previous studies have demonstrated that IC correlates with CD34‑labeled microvascular density (MVD), microvascular area, and microvascular grade, and NIC exhibits better performance than unnormalized IC [21]. Both IC and NIC are strongly correlated with CT perfusion parameters, including blood flow, blood volume, and permeability, serving as surrogate markers for tumor perfusion [22]. Our results revealed that post-IC and post-NIC values were significantly lower in responders than in non-responders. Theoretically, effective immunochemotherapy induced tumor and vascular destruction, fibrosis, reduced MVD, and compression of the extracellular space, leading to decreased IC and NIC of responders. Conversely, insufficient immune activation in non-responders results in abundant vascularization within viable tumor tissues and maintains contrast agent accumulation, thereby elevating IC and NIC levels. Our findings are consistent with the previous results reported by Ge X et al. [23]. However, the value of pre-IC and pre-NIC of the tumor in the neoadjuvant treatment of esophageal cancer has not yet reached a consensus. Baseline IC and NIC reflect pre‑treatment functional vascular density and perfusion status. In our cohort, no significant differences in baseline values were observed between responders and non‑responders, indicating balanced baseline vascularization. Wang J, et al. reported that higher baseline IC and NIC were observed in the effective group than in the ineffective group [18], which is contrary to our results. Given the lack of predictive power of baseline IC and NIC, post-treatment spectral CT parameters may potentially become an effective tool for monitoring response to nICT in future studies.

In this study, 40 keV VMI was selected for the assessment of ESCC based on physical principles and disease imaging characteristics. Positioned immediately above and closest to the iodine K‑edge (approximately 33.2 keV), 40 keV VMI maximizes iodine attenuation, thereby providing the best contrast for iodine‑containing tissues [24]. Previous studies have confirmed that 40 keV VMI provides a higher signal-to-noise ratio and contrast-to-noise ratio than higher energy-level monochromatic images in patients with esophageal cancer [17, 25]. Despite increased noise at 40 keV VMI, dedicated spectral CT algorithms (e.g., anti-correlation filtering, structure propagation, constrained noise reduction) control noise while enhancing contrast, improving lesion conspicuity [26]. The 40 keV VMI attenuation value essentially reflects a physically amplified projection of the total iodine pool within the tumor. We found that post-40 keV VMI values showed a significant difference between the responders and non-responders after nICT. This is mainly due to the reduction of tumor vascularity and fibrosis formation following treatment, which leads to a decrease in iodine uptake, resulting in a lower attenuation value at 40 keV VMI.

Furthermore, we found that post-40 keV VMI, post-IC, and post-NIC values gradually increased from TRG 0 to TRG 3. This trend effectively reflects the pathological progression of lesions following nICT, presenting a histological transition from hypovascular fibrous tissue to hypervascular residual viable tumor. Mechanistically, a higher TRG indicates a larger volume of residual tumor, which leads to increased lesion vascularization and enhanced iodine uptake capacity. Consistent with our observations, Djuric Stefanovic et al. demonstrated that conventional CT attenuation of esophageal cancer was positively correlated with TRG, with a greater amount of residual tumor corresponding to higher CT attenuation values [27]. This might indicate that a better therapeutic response is associated with less residual tumor and reduced tumor vascular activity.

Since immune cells do not inherently contain iodine, spectral CT does not directly quantify immune cell infiltration. However, changes in IC, NIC, and 40 keV VMI values before and after treatment reflect alterations in tumor vascularization status, given the reciprocal regulatory interplay between vascular structure and the immune microenvironment. Aberrant tumor vasculature can induce hypoxia and immunosuppression, whereas treatment-induced vascular normalization helps improve local perfusion, reverse immunosuppression, and promote immune cell infiltration. Therefore, spectral CT may provide a valuable approach for indirectly evaluating immune microenvironment remodeling.

We also evaluated conventional tumor-related parameters. The results showed that there was no statistically significant difference in tumor area between the responder and non-responder groups before nICT. Similarly, the tumor area in the responder group was significantly smaller after nICT, which was consistent with the findings reported by Ruan Y. et al. [28]. Adachi M et al. identified the area of residual tumor as an independent predictor of recurrence-free survival in patients with ESCC treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy [29]. Although post-IC and post-40 keV VMI showed higher univariate AUCs than post-NIC, severe multicollinearity between them (VIF > 10) necessitated their exclusion from the multivariate logistic regression model to ensure stability, without negating their predictive potential. In contrast, post-area and post-NIC demonstrated acceptable collinearity and were retained in the final model, with OR of 0.980 and 0.824, respectively. Thus, the exclusion of post-IC and post-40 keV VMI was driven by statistical considerations rather than inferior performance, and post-NIC provided a stable, independent predictive contribution after multivariate adjustment. The predictive model integrating post-NIC and post-area yielded an AUC of 0.844, and DeLong’s test confirmed that its diagnostic performance was significantly superior to that of post-NIC alone.

Notably, baseline spectral CT parameters failed to predict pathological response or TRG, whereas preoperative parameters at post-nICT demonstrated satisfactory discriminatory ability. This highlights that spectral CT might be more appropriately considered a tool for post-treatment response monitoring than a pre-treatment predictive biomarker. Our findings indicate that post-treatment spectral CT may facilitate guiding the optimization of surgical plans, such as a watch-and-wait strategy, the design of postoperative adjuvant therapy, and doctor‑patient communication. Furthermore, these preoperative parameters may serve as a supplement for identifying patients with major or near-complete pathological response, which helps screen potential candidates eligible for an active surveillance or deferred surgery. Nevertheless, this clinical application remains exploratory at present and requires further validation through prospective studies with pathological confirmation and long-term follow-up. Since all the significant imaging parameters were obtained after the completion of nICT, the current data do not directly support the mid-treatment adjustment of the treatment plan during nICT. However, the strong correlation observed in this study implies that future studies should explore serial spectral CT monitoring during nICT (e.g., after each cycle) to achieve early intra‑treatment response assessment and timely adjustment of therapeutic regimens.

However, our study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size is an important limitation of the present study, as it inevitably limits the generalizability of our findings despite acceptable model performance (EPV = 26.5; Hosmer‑Lemeshow p = 0.151; AUC = 0.844). Additionally, the lack of external validation restricts the assessment of model robustness, and the use of a scanner from a single manufacturer may limit the applicability of our results to other devices. Future studies with larger samples, multicenter internal and external validation are needed. Second, increased noise at 40 keV VMI is non‑negligible. To achieve a better balance between noise and contrast, further studies are needed to explore the clinical feasibility of 40 keV VMI combined with noise-reduction techniques in the diagnosis of ESCC. Third, intratumoral heterogeneity in ESCC may not be fully captured by a single two-dimensional slice. We used a largest‑slice two-dimensional ROI for clinical feasibility and reproducibility [30, 31], but three-dimensional volumetric analysis should be pursued in future studies to better reflect whole‑tumor heterogeneity. Finally, multiple PD-1 inhibitors were not stratified by drug type in this study, which may introduce a certain degree of drug heterogeneity. Future subgroup analyses specific to individual drugs are warranted.

Conclusions

Generally, baseline spectral CT parameters before treatment failed to predict pathological response to nICT in ESCC. In contrast, post‑NIC and post-area of tumor might serve as independent monitoring biomarkers for assessing pathological response. The model combining post-NIC and post-area is likely to offer assistance in preoperative surgical planning and potentially help identify patients who may be suitable for a watch-and-wait strategy or deferred surgery. Given that this is merely an initial exploration, further prospective validation is required. The findings of this study may provide preliminary imaging evidence for individualized clinical decisions.
