Prophets Inequalities with Uncertain Acceptance
Emile Martinez, Felipe Garrido-Lucero, Umberto Grandi, Sebastián Pérez-Salazar

Abstract
We introduce the prophet inequality with uncertain acceptance model, in which a decision maker sequentially observes a sequence of independent options, each characterized by a value x i x_{i} and an acceptance probability p i p_{i} , both sampled from a known joint distribution. At time i i , the decision maker observes the value x i x_{i} and must irrevocably and immediately decide whether to attempt to select it or to continue to the next time step. If the option is selected, the process terminates with probability p i p_{i} and the decision maker obtains x i x_{i} ; otherwise, she continues searching. In this setting, two natural benchmarks arise: the value-aware decision-maker , who knows all value realizations in advance but not the acceptance outcomes, and the full-knowledge prophet , who knows all realizations beforehand and can choose the best option among those that will be accepted. We characterize the worst-case competitive ratios between our defined agents and show that all these values equal 1 / 2 1/2 . In addition, we provide sufficient conditions under which the value-aware decision-maker surpasses the 1 / 2 1/2 -barrier against the more informed prophet. This demonstrates the (crucial) interest for the decision maker to improve her knowledge over the values rather than over the acceptances, and is obtained via a more general result that reduces the value-aware decision-maker’s problem to a classical prophet inequality with scaled Bernoulli distributions, followed by a sequence of transformations that further reduce the problem to a unique optimization problem.

Introduction

Consider a student searching for an flat in a highly competitive rental market. Flats are visited sequentially, and hesitation is a luxury she cannot afford: after each visit, the student must make an immediate and irreversible decision about whether to apply. Although advertisements may provide prior information about an apartment’s quality, its true value is revealed only upon inspection. Passing on an apartment means losing it forever, while accepting it terminates the search. The student thus faces a fundamental tension induced by the online nature of the problem, namely the trade-off between accepting a currently observed option and forgoing it in the hope of encountering a better apartment in the future. The online selection literature has devoted substantial effort to understanding and alleviating this tension across a wide range of applications, including classical online selection problems

The goal is to design an algorithm that maximizes the expected payoff. The performance of such an algorithm is usually measured by its
competitive ratio

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\right]

While the online tension faced by the decision maker is well understood in this setting, there is a less explored source of uncertainty that introduces an additional layer of complexity. In a competitive rental market, submitting an application does not guarantee acceptance. In our introductory example, rejections may occur with positive probability, forcing the student (modeled by the decision maker in this situation) to continue the search. Moreover, the probability of rejection may be correlated with the quality of the apartment, as better flats typically require stronger guarantees from the tenant. This introduces a new trade-off: an option with modest quality but a high probability of acceptance may be preferable to waiting for a higher-quality apartment that is unlikely to accept the student’s offer. Motivated by this observation, we introduce the
prophet inequalities with uncertain acceptance
(
\mathrm{PI}\text{-}\mathrm{UA}
), which extends the prophet inequality framework to account for the possibility of rejection. Informally, upon observing
X_{i}
, if the decision maker selects the value, the payoff is obtained with some probability, and otherwise the process continues. A formal description of the model is provided in the next subsection.

Interestingly, due to the information revealed in two steps,
\mathrm{PI}\text{-}\mathrm{UA}
naturally induce an intermediate agent between the decision-maker and the prophet, an agent that would be aware to the values but blind to acceptance: the
value-aware-decision-maker

is stronger than the decision maker and weaker than the prophet. However, its existence raises natural research questions:
How

performs? Can the decision maker perform poorly against
\mathrm{VA\text{-}DM}
and
\mathrm{VA\text{-}DM}
performs poorly against the prophet? Does the decision-maker’s error compared to
\mathrm{VA\text{-}DM}

\par\par\par\noindent{Are $\mathrm{PI}\text{-}\mathrm{UA}$ actually harder than classic prophet inequalities?} Comparing worst-case guarantees in $\mathrm{PI}\text{-}\mathrm{UA}$ and $\mathrm{PI}$ does not yield any insight into establishing which of the two problems is actually harder. Therefore, we perform a more refined analysis by making instance-by-instance comparisons. We consider three different ways of mapping a $\mathrm{PI}\text{-}\mathrm{UA}$ instance into a $\mathrm{PI}$ instance and check, for each of these transformations, how the competitive ratios $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ compare against the one of the decision maker and the classical prophet. In general, our results show that prophet inequalities with uncertain acceptance appear to be largely orthogonal to classical prophet inequalities, with no clear hierarchy between the two problems.
\par\par\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{Related Literature}
\par\noindent{Prophet inequalities.} Prophet inequalities were introduced by Krengel and Sucheston\penalty\ \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{krengel1977semiamarts}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}. Samuel-Cahn showed that a competitive ratio of $1/2$ can be achieved using a single-threshold algorithm, and that this guarantee is the best possible\penalty\ \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{samuel1984comparison}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}. Much of the current interest in prophet inequality problems within mechanism design is driven by their connection to posted-price mechanisms, as reservation prices can be translated into threshold-based solutions for prophet inequality problems (see, e.g.,\penalty\ \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{chawla2010multi,correa2019pricing,hajiaghayi2007automated}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}). Closer to our work is \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{assaf1998statistical}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}, which considers a prophet inequality with an observable online value $X_{i}$ and an unknown value $A_{i}$. Unlike our setting, once a selection is made in their model, a payoff $f(X_{i},A_{i})$ is earned. In contrast, in our framework $A_{i}$ signals whether the selection is successful, and an unsuccessful selection allows the decision-maker to continue searching. Our setting also offers a richer informational structure, as the equivalent of our three possible competitive ratios $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$, three are $0$ in the worst case -namely $\beta$ and $\gamma$, diminishing the interest of studying the equivalent of the prophet in their setting.
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\par\noindent{Uncertain acceptance.} There is a large body of work exploring uncertain offer acceptance in hiring problems, starting with the secretary problem\penalty\ \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{smith1975secretary,tamaki1991secretary}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}. More recently,\penalty\ \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{perez2025robust}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}} considers a competitive framework similar to ours; however, a key difference is that they assume adversarially chosen values revealed in {random order}, whereas we only assume distributional information about values. Moreover, in this line of work the probability of acceptance is assumed to be {homogeneous}, $p\in(0,1)$, and independent of the observed value. In contrast, our model does not impose such independence, allowing us to capture richer settings (e.g., high-value offers may be less likely to be accepted). Recently, Xu\penalty\ \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{xu2025online}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}} studies an online selection problem in which acceptance does not end the process and the reward is the sum of the values of the selected items; stopping occurs when a selection is unsuccessful (i.e., $A_{i}=0$).
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\par\noindent{Uncertain acceptance as a result of competition}. It is natural to interpret uncertain acceptance as arising from competition, where other agents may select an item before you do. Competitive versions of online selection problems have garnered increasing interest, particularly in the secretary problem (\cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{immorlica2006secretary, karlin2015competitive, ramsey2024stackelberg}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}).
\par A few studies have examined competitive versions of the prophet inequality. \cite[citet]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{ezra2021prophet}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}} considered a setting where $k$ agents compete on the same prophet inequality instance. If an agent claims a value but another agent secures it, the game continues for her. They analyzed two tie-breaking rules when multiple agents claim the same value: either a random agent is selected, or the selection follows a pre-established order. They established in both cases insightful bounds. In this line, \cite[citet]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{gensbittel2024competition}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}} specifically studied the case of two competing agents, allowing the selection of any previous value as long as the other agent has not taken it.
\par In such settings, competition could be modeled, for each agent individually, as a prophet inequality with uncertain acceptance. Nevertheless, in our case, we consider that the acceptance must be independent at each step, which is not the case here as a prior refusal implies that another agent was accepted, reducing competition and increasing the likelihood of future acceptance. To see more on the reason of this hypothesis, see Section \ref{sec:independent_acceptance}.
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\par\noindent{Mathematical programming in prophet inequalities.}
There is a long line of work applying structured mathematical programs to prophet inequality problems\penalty\ \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{alaei2014bayesian,niazadeh2018prophet,jiang2022tight,brustle2025splitting,perez2025iid}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}. Closest to our work is\penalty\ \cite[cite]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{ekbatani2024prophet}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}, which studies prophet inequalities with the possibility of buying back previously selected values. Through a sequence of reductions, they show that it suffices to analyze {scaled Bernoulli distributions}, enabling the problem to be solved via a linear program. We employ a similar approach in the proof of Theorem\penalty\ \ref{thm:CR_bernoulli}.

\par In this article, we introduced the prophet inequality with uncertain acceptance model, extending the classical prophet inequality model by allowing the selection of an option to fail with some positive probability after observing its realized value. In this setting, we analyzed the competitive ratio of the optimal online algorithm with respect to two more informed benchmarks: $\mathrm{VA\text{-}DM}$, who knows all value realizations in advance but not the acceptance outcomes, and the prophet, who knows all realizations beforehand. Our findings show that $\mathrm{VA\text{-}DM}$ can surpass the classical worst-case $\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}$-barrier, and that the two settings\textemdash with and without uncertain acceptance\textemdash are non-trivially related, as no clear hierarchy can be established between them.
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\par We conclude this article by discussing some extra properties of our setting and possible future research directions.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{The Importance of Independent Acceptances}
\par In examples such as the search for an apartment in competitive rental markets, assuming correlated acceptance decisions is natural. Indeed, when a student is rejected for a given flat, she is likely to be rejected for similar or more attractive flats, as students may not know their true acceptance probabilities in advance and instead learn about them through rejections, as is common for individuals looking for a flat in a new city for the first time. Moreover, in small markets, being rejected meaning that someone else has been accepted reduces the competition and thus increases the likelihood of being accepted later.
\par Nevertheless, we choose to model the random variables $(A_{1},...,A_{n})$ as independent for two reasons. First, in large markets, the sets of applicants competing for two different flats rarely coincide. Additionally, when a student is rejected, the applicant who obtained the flat exits the market, so posterior applications should be independent from this rejection.
\par The second reason is technical. \cite[citet]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{hill1981ratio}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}} showed that introducing correlation among the random variables in the $\mathrm{PI}$ model significantly increases the difficulty of the problem for the decision maker, who can no longer guarantee a constant competitive ratio against the Prophet. Indeed, for $p\in(0,1)$ and $j\in\{0,...,n-2\}$, considering the instance $definedbythedistributions\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{P}\left((X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=(p^{0},p^{-1},\dots,p^{-j},0\dots,0)\right)=p^{j}-p^{j+1},\\
&\mathbb{P}\left((X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=(p^{0},p^{-1},\dots,p^{-(n-1)})\right)=p^{n-1},\end{aligned}\cite[citeauthor]{\@@bibref{Authors}{hill1981ratio}{}{}}provedthecompetitiveratiobetweenthedecisionmakerandtheprophetisoforder$\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{n(1-p)})$.Moreover,thisresultcanbeextendedtooursettingbyconsidering(deterministic)randomvariables$(X_1,...,X_n)$,with$X_i = p^{i-1}$foreach$i \in[n]$,andcorrelatedrandomvariables$(A_1,...,A_n)$givenby,\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{P}\left((A_{1},\dots,A_{n})=(1,1,\dots,1,0\dots,0)\right)=p^{j}-p^{j+1},\\
&\mathbb{P}\left((A_{1},\dots,A_{n})=(1,1,\dots,1)\right)=p^{n-1}.\end{aligned}Therefore,inordertoperformworst-caseanalysiswithpositiveresults,theindependenceoftherandomvariables$(A_1,...,A_n)$isacrucialhypothesis.\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Absence of Monotonicity}
\par Intuitively, the informational advantage of $\mathrm{VA\text{-}DM}$ diminishes when acceptation becomes likely. More formally, we could suspect some monotonic behavior when a specific probability of acceptance diminish - some $\mathbb{P}\left(A_{i}=1\mid X_{i}=x_{i}\right)$ for some $i\in[n]$ and $x_{i}$ realization of $X_{i}$, all other things being equal. We could suspect this monotonic behavior to be on the performance of $\mathrm{VA\text{-}DM}$ -i.e. on $\beta$, on the position of $\mathrm{VA\text{-}DM}$ between the decision-maker and the prophet -i.e. $\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{\beta}}$, etc. Nevertheless, the results are inconclusive, as all competitive ratios display multiple regimes as $p$ increases. This behavior is illustrated in Figure\penalty\ \ref{fig:no_monotonicity} for an instance with two random vectors $(Y_{1},Y_{2})$, where $X_{1}=1$, $A_{1}=1$, $X_{2}=10\cdot\mathrm{Ber}(1/2)$, and $Y_{2}=\mathrm{Ber}(p)$.

\par Several research directions are considered based on the results of this article. First, although we have characterized the instances where $\mathrm{VA\text{-}DM}$ achieves a good competitive ratio against the prophet, the question of characterizing the instances where the decision maker performs well against $\mathrm{VA\text{-}DM}$ is still open. One possible idea, following the work of \cite[citet]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1YearPhrase2}{kennedy1997prophet}{\@@citephrase{(}}{\@@citephrase{)}}}, is to look at the variance of the random variables as a possible complexity measure, as worst-case instances typically present {long-shot random variables}.
