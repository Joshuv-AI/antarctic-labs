Syntactic Separation Implies Computational Indistinguishability: An Abstract Obstruction Theorem
Fabio Francesco Gabriele Buono

Abstract
We prove that syntactic separation implies computational indistinguishability. A local syntactic system ℛ \mathcal{R} acts on terms within radius r 0 r_{0} without consulting any model; when two Skolem functions are syntactically separated in ℛ \mathcal{R} , no derivation can prove their equivalence (Case 1), and any sound local extension requires Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(n) steps, improving to Ω ⁡ ( 2 n ) \Omega(2^{n}) under clause-per-configuration encoding (Case 2). Both bounds are new: the derivation-length lower bound does not appear in prior work on Skolemization or saturation proving, and the cryptographic reading, syntactic separation as ciphertext indistinguishability, derivation cost as negligible advantage, is original. The same obstruction, as formal instances of Case 1 and Case 2, governs the Natural Proofs barrier of Razborov and Rudich, the Type Omitting Theorem, and the unconditional 𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{AC}^{0} barrier of Loff et al. [ 14 ] .



1
Introduction

1.1
The phenomenon

A syntactic system acts on the shape of expressions.
A semantic property concerns what those expressions mean in a model.
The two can be in conflict: a property that is visible to the model may
be completely invisible to the syntactic system, even when every
derivation the system can perform passes through terms in which the
property is present.

This paper proves that this conflict is not an accident of specific
systems but a structural phenomenon: whenever a property is

protected
from the rules of a syntactic system, no derivation
can cross the barrier, and any extension of the system that tries to do
so pays an exponential price.

The phenomenon appears across mathematics in forms that look unrelated:

•

In
proof theory
, the Skolem functions witnessing two
different proofs of
\forall x\,\exists y.\,\phi(x,y)
are
semantically equal but a saturation-based prover cannot derive
their equality, the relevant information is locked inside
Skolem constants the rules cannot touch.

•

In
cryptography
, a ciphertext produced by the
Mixed-Radix One-Time Pad
[
7
]
achieves Shannon
perfect secrecy because the decomposition basis
B
encodes a
numerical fact invisible to any syntactic test on the ciphertext;
the same structure underlies the original construction
of
[
12
]
.

•

In
type theory
, the Type Omitting Theorem says that a
theory omits a type
\Phi(x)
if and only if
\Phi
is not
principal, no syntactic formula pins it down.
A constructive type system is blind to extensional properties
of the functions it types: it sees the proof term, not the
function computed
(Typological Invariance Principle, Remark
6.2
).

•

In
circuit complexity
, the Natural Proofs barrier of
Razborov and Rudich shows that no proof technique that is both
useful (holds for random functions) and constructive (checkable in
polynomial time) can establish a super-polynomial circuit lower
bound, the semantic invariant of circuit complexity is
protected from every local syntactic inspection of the circuit
graph.

These are not analogies.
They are instances of a single structural theorem, proved here in its
abstract form and applied to each domain in Section
6
.

1.2
The result

This paper proves an abstract theorem: whenever a local syntactic
system
\mathcal{R}
acts on a term structure in which a semantic invariant is
protected from all rules, no derivation in
\mathcal{R}
can reach that
invariant (Case 1), and any sound extension that overcomes this
barrier requires
\Omega(n)
derivation steps, growing with
the number of independent witnesses, and improving to

\Omega(2^{n})
under clause-per-configuration encoding (Case 2).
The proof-theoretic structure of Case 2 is precisely the structure of
computational hiding in cryptography: syntactic separation between two
Skolem functions is ciphertext indistinguishability, and the derivation
cost lower bound is the adversary’s negligible advantage.

The starting point is
[
1
]
, which isolates the mechanism
for a specific calculus.
That work resolves an open question of
[
2
]
,
the incomparability of open induction (
\mathsf{OI}
) and clause set cycles
(
\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{CSC}}
), by observing that the addition rules fire only when the
first argument of
+
is
0
or a successor, so a Skolem constant
is permanently unreachable.
From this observation,
[
1
]
extracts the
Syntactic
Invariance Principle
(
\mathsf{SIP}
, Lemma 5 of
[
1
]
).
The present paper takes
\mathsf{SIP}
as its structural starting point and proves
something strictly stronger: a two-case theorem parametric in the
signature, locality radius, and model, which applies to any rewriting
calculus and yields the cryptographic and complexity-theoretic
consequences described above.

The formal framework for the structural blindness argument is the
observational hierarchy of
[
10
]
:
\mathcal{R}
defines a
constrained observer
O_{\mathcal{R}}\prec O_{\top}
blind to positions in

\mathcal{F}
.
Proposition 9.6 of
[
10
]
shows observer-constrained
classes collapse unconditionally: under the profile observer

(which maps a string to its symbol-count vector),

\mathbf{P}_{O_{\mathrm{prof}}}=\mathbf{NP}_{O_{\mathrm{prof}}}\subsetneq\mathbf{P}
.
Theorem
3.1
is an instance of this collapse in the
setting of term rewriting and Skolemization; whether it follows
as a formal corollary of Proposition 9.6 is Question (Q6).

2
Definitions

2.1
Terms and positions

Definition 2.1

(Signature and terms)
.

A
signature

\Sigma
is a finite set of function symbols, each
with a fixed arity.
Let
V
be a countable set of variables disjoint from
\Sigma
.
The set
\mathcal{T}(\Sigma,V)
of
terms
over
\Sigma
and
V
is defined
inductively:

•

every
x\in V
is a term;

•

if
f\in\Sigma
has arity
k\geq 0
and

t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in\mathcal{T}(\Sigma,V)
, then
f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
is a term
(constants are symbols of arity
0
).

Definition 2.2

(Positions)
.

For a term
t\in\mathcal{T}(\Sigma,V)
, the set
\mathrm{Pos}(t)\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{*}
of

positions
is defined by:

•

\varepsilon\in\mathrm{Pos}(t)
(the root position);

•

if
t=f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
and
p\in\mathrm{Pos}(t_{i})
, then

i{\cdot}p\in\mathrm{Pos}(t)
.

The
subterm
of
t
at position
p
is written
t|_{p}
.
The term obtained from
t
by replacing
t|_{p}
with
s
is
written
t[s]_{p}
.
The
tree distance

d(p,q)
between two positions
p,q\in\mathrm{Pos}(t)

is the number of edges in the unique path between them in the tree
structure of
t
.

Definition 2.3

(Context of radius
r
)
.

For
t\in\mathcal{T}(\Sigma,V)
,
p\in\mathrm{Pos}(t)
(Definition
2.2
),
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
, the

context of radius
r
around
p
in
t
is the partial function

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{r}(p)(t)\;:=

\displaystyle\bigl(\,q\;\mapsto\;t|_{p\cdot q}\,\bigr)_{q\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\,p{\cdot}q\in\mathrm{Pos}(t),\,d(p,p{\cdot}q)\leq r},

\displaystyle\text{(equivalently, }|q|\leq r\text{, where }|q|\text{ is the number of steps from }p\text{ to }p{\cdot}q)

mapping each extension
q
of
p
within distance
r
to the
corresponding subterm
t|_{p\cdot q}
.
Two terms
t
and
t^{\prime}
have
equal contexts of radius
r
at
p

if
\mathcal{B}_{r}(p)(t)
and
\mathcal{B}_{r}(p)(t^{\prime})
have the same domain and
agree as functions on that domain
(i.e., they are equal as partial functions, including having the
same set of defined positions).

Example 2.4

(Contexts)
.

Let
t=s(a+b)
and
p=1
(the position of
a{+}b
).
Then
\mathcal{B}_{1}(p)(t)
maps:
\varepsilon\mapsto a{+}b
(the subterm at
p

itself, i.e.,
q=\varepsilon
);
1\mapsto a
(left child,
q=1
);

2\mapsto b
(right child,
q=2
).
The context does not include the parent
s(a{+}b)
, since the parent
is a prefix of
p
, not an extension: Definition
2.3

ranges over extensions
q
with
p{\cdot}q\in\mathrm{Pos}(t)
, i.e.,
descendants of
p
, not ancestors.

\mathcal{B}_{0}(p)(t)
maps only
\varepsilon\mapsto a{+}b
.

2.2
Local syntactic systems

Definition 2.5

(Local syntactic system)
.

A
local syntactic system
is a tuple

\mathcal{R}=(\Sigma,V,\mathit{Rules},r_{0})
where
\Sigma
and
V
are as
in Definition
2.1
,
r_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
is the
locality
radius
, and
\mathit{Rules}
is a finite set of
rewriting rules
.

Each rule
\rho\in\mathit{Rules}
is specified by a finite

left-hand side
pattern
\ell_{\rho}\in\mathcal{T}(\Sigma,V)
and a finite

right-hand side

r_{\rho}\in\mathcal{T}(\Sigma,V)
, and defines a partial
function on pairs
(t,p)
as follows.
Rule
\rho
is
applicable
at position
p
in term
t
if there
exists a substitution
\sigma
(a map
V\to\mathcal{T}(\Sigma,V)
) such that

\sigma(\ell_{\rho})=t|_{p}
; when applicable, it produces

\rho(t,p)\;:=\;t[\sigma(r_{\rho})]_{p}.

We require the following two conditions.

(i)

Locality.
The applicability of
\rho
at
(t,p)
depends
only on
\mathcal{B}_{r_{0}}(p)(t)
(Definition
2.3
).
That is, if
\mathcal{B}_{r_{0}}(p)(t)=\mathcal{B}_{r_{0}}(p)(t^{\prime})
as partial
functions, then
\rho
is applicable at
p
in
t
if and only
if it is applicable at
p
in
t^{\prime}
.

(ii)

Model independence.
The applicability of
\rho
at

(t,p)
depends only on the syntactic form of
\mathcal{B}_{r_{0}}(p)(t)
,
not on the truth value of any formula in any model.

A
derivation
in
\mathcal{R}
from a clause set
S
is a finite or infinite
sequence
S=S_{0},S_{1},S_{2},\ldots
where each
S_{i+1}
is obtained
from
S_{i}
by selecting a term
t
appearing in some literal of
some clause
C\in S_{i}
, choosing a position
p\in\mathrm{Pos}(t)
, and
applying rule
\rho\in\mathit{Rules}
at
p
in
t
; the literal
containing
t
is updated by replacing
t
with
\rho(t,p)
,
producing a new clause
C^{\prime}
, and setting
S_{i+1}=S_{i}\cup\{C^{\prime}\}
.
The
length
of a finite derivation is the number of rule
applications.

Example 2.6

(Superposition calculus as a local system)
.

The superposition calculus over the signature

\Sigma=\{0,s,+\}
with rules

\displaystyle 0+x

\displaystyle=x

(A1)

\displaystyle s(x)+y

\displaystyle=s(x+y)

(A2)

is a local syntactic system with
r_{0}=1
.
Each rule inspects the outermost function symbol of the first argument
of
+
: this symbol lies within distance
1
of the application
position
p
in
t
(i.e., within
\mathcal{B}_{1}(p)(t)
).
Both left-hand sides are finite; applicability does not consult any model.

Definition 2.7

(Soundness)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a first-order
\Sigma
-structure.
A local syntactic system
\mathcal{R}
is
sound with respect to
\mathcal{M}
if
every clause derivable by
\mathcal{R}
from a set of clauses that are valid in
\mathcal{M}

is also valid in
\mathcal{M}

(a clause is
valid in
\mathcal{M}
if every ground instance of its
disjunction is satisfied by
\mathcal{M}
).

Remark 2.8

(Theory-relative soundness)
.

Alternatively, one may fix a theory
\mathcal{T}
and require that every clause
derivable by
\mathcal{R}
from axioms of
\mathcal{T}
is valid in every model of
\mathcal{T}
.
This is equivalent to soundness with respect to every model of
\mathcal{T}
.
In what follows we work with a fixed model
\mathcal{M}
; the theory-relative
version is formally analogous and all results carry over.

2.3
Protected positions

Definition 2.9

(Protected set)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}=(\Sigma,V,\mathit{Rules},r_{0})
be a local syntactic system.
A set of positions
\mathcal{F}
is
protected with respect to
\mathcal{R}

if it satisfies the following two conditions for every term
t
and every

p\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathrm{Pos}(t)
:

(i)

No rewriting at
p
.

No rule
\rho\in\mathit{Rules}
is applicable at position
p

in
t
.

(ii)

No rewriting inside
p
.

No rule
\rho\in\mathit{Rules}
is applicable at any position

q\in\mathrm{Pos}(t)
such that
q
is a proper extension of
p

(i.e.,
q=p\cdot q^{\prime}
for some nonempty
q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
),
meaning no rule rewrites inside the subterm
t|_{p}
.

In particular,
\mathcal{F}
is not a fixed set of strings in
\mathbb{N}^{*}
;
it is a
predicate
on pairs
(t,p)
with
p\in\mathrm{Pos}(t)
,
which must hold uniformly across all terms appearing in any
derivation from
\mathcal{R}
.
Concretely, one writes
p\in\mathcal{F}(t)
to mean that position
p

in term
t
is protected.

Remark 2.10

(Protected sets are a property of
\mathcal{R}
, not an axiom)
.

Definition
2.9
is a property that can be verified by
inspecting the left-hand sides of the rules in
\mathit{Rules}
.
It is not an additional axiom imposed on
\mathcal{R}
.
In concrete systems, one verifies it by checking that certain
unification problems have no solution (e.g., first-symbol clashes;
see Section
4
).

Example 2.11

(Protected positions in the superposition calculus)
.

In Example
2.6
, let
a
and
b
be fresh Skolem
constants (distinct from
0
and from every term of the form
s(t)
).
The set
\mathcal{F}
of positions headed by
a
or
b
is protected with
respect to
\mathcal{R}
.
This is verified in Lemma
4.1
below.

2.4
Syntactic invariants

Definition 2.12

(Syntactic invariant)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}=(\Sigma,V,\mathit{Rules},r_{0})
be a local syntactic system
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a protected set with respect to
\mathcal{R}
.
A property
\mathsf{Inv}
of terms is a
syntactic invariant for
\mathcal{R}

anchored to
\mathcal{F}
if the following five conditions hold.

(i)

Local checkability.

Whether
\mathsf{Inv}(t)
holds depends only on the subterms of
t
at
positions within distance
r_{0}
of some fixed finite set of
reference positions.

(ii)

Initialization.

\mathsf{Inv}(t)
holds for every term
t
in the initial clause set.

(iii)

Preservation.

For every
t
with
\mathsf{Inv}(t)
and every rule
\rho\in\mathit{Rules}

applicable at some position
p
in
t
, we have

\mathsf{Inv}(\rho(t,p))
.

(iv)

Anchorage.

If
\mathsf{Inv}(t)
holds and
\mathsf{Inv}(t[s]_{p})
fails for some term
s

and position
p
, then
p\in\mathcal{F}
.

(v)

Coherence.

There exist two designated function symbols
f_{0},f_{1}\in\Sigma

such that: no literal of any clause
C
with
\mathsf{Inv}
holding on
all its terms contains both
f_{0}(\cdot)
and
f_{1}(\cdot)

as subterms at comparable positions
(i.e., positions syntactically equated by the literal,
such as the two sides of an equation
f_{0}(t_{1})=f_{1}(t_{2})
).
(
\mathsf{Inv}
is applied term-by-term to the terms in each literal.
In the application to Skolemization,
f_{0}=f
and
f_{1}=g
,
and the excluded literal is
f(x)=g(x)
.)

Remark 2.13

(Role of anchorage)
.

Condition (iv) ensures that
\mathsf{Inv}
is not merely preserved by
\mathcal{R}
for
trivial reasons (e.g., because
\mathcal{R}
never produces any new terms at all),
but that its violation is structurally tied to the protected set
\mathcal{F}
.
Without (iv), one could take
\mathsf{Inv}(t)=\top
(always true), which
satisfies (i)–(iii) trivially (the conclusion is always true)
but carries no information.

2.5
Skolem functions and syntactic separation

Definition 2.14

(Skolem functions and semantic equivalence)
.

Let
\psi=\forall x\,\exists y.\,\phi(x,y)
be a first-order formula.
A
Skolemization
of
\psi
is a formula

\psi_{f}=\forall x.\,\phi(x,f(x))

where
f
is a fresh function symbol, the
Skolem function
for
the existential quantifier.

Two Skolem functions
f
and
g
(arising from two Skolemizations of

\psi
) are
semantically equivalent in
\mathcal{M}
if

f(x)=g(x)
holds in
\mathcal{M}
for all
x
in the domain of
\mathcal{M}
.
We write
f\equiv_{\mathcal{M}}g
as shorthand for this condition, and
drop the subscript when
\mathcal{M}
is clear from context.

Remark 2.15

(Relation to
[
3
]
)
.

The interaction between Skolem functions and induction in
saturation-based theorem provers is studied in depth
in
[
3
]
, where Skolem symbols are shown to take the
role of induction parameters.
The present definition of syntactic separation
(Definition
2.16
below) makes precise the structural
condition under which two such parameters cannot be compared by
the rewriting system.

Definition 2.16

(Syntactic separation)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a local syntactic system with protected predicates

\mathcal{F}_{f}
and
\mathcal{F}_{g}
for
f
and
g
respectively.
Skolem functions
f
and
g
are
syntactically separated in

\mathcal{R}
if the following four conditions hold:

(i)

every occurrence of
f
in the initial clause set appears at a
position in
\mathcal{F}_{f}
;

(ii)

every occurrence of
g
in the initial clause set appears at a
position in
\mathcal{F}_{g}
;

(iii)

\mathcal{F}_{f}
and
\mathcal{F}_{g}
are both non-empty;

(iv)

\mathcal{F}_{f}
and
\mathcal{F}_{g}
are disjoint: no position

(t,p)
belongs to both.

Remark 2.17

(On condition (iii) of Definition
2.16
)
.

Condition (iii) requires only that the protected sets are non-empty.
The stronger property, that no derivation in
\mathcal{R}
can produce a clause
containing a literal of the form
f(t_{1})=g(t_{2})
, is not
assumed but proved in Case 1 of Theorem
3.1
, using the
syntactic invariant
\mathsf{Inv}
and the disjointness of
\mathcal{F}_{f}
and

\mathcal{F}_{g}
.

Main Theorem

With all definitions in place, we can state the central result.

Theorem 3.1

(Local Syntactic Obstruction)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}=(\Sigma,V,\mathit{Rules},r_{0})
be a local syntactic system,
sound with respect to a model
\mathcal{M}

(Definitions
2.5
and
2.7
).
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a set of positions protected with respect to
\mathcal{R}

(Definition
2.9
), and let
\mathsf{Inv}
be a syntactic invariant
for
\mathcal{R}

Proof of Case 2.

The proof proceeds in four steps.

Step 1: Derivation trees.

Fix

and the instance

with

global configurations

A derivation in

that correctly proves

on

must produce a correct conclusion for every configuration

.
We represent the derivation as a tree

: internal nodes are

\neg(f(x)=g(x))
is resolved for some specific input
(i.e., some

Each branch of

handles a disjoint subset of configurations.

Step 2: Capacity of local rules.

Each rule

is applicable based on

for
the relevant position

Since any two distinct configurations

and

are

(hypothesis of Case 2), a single

partitions the

is the number of distinct local contexts of radius

that

can observe, equivalently, the maximum number of

By the finiteness of patterns (Definition

(i)),

is finite and independent of

Step 3: Branching argument via syntactic distinguishability.

We work with the specific family

from

(constructed and verified in

Recall that

is built from

and

.
A global configuration

from each gadget

.

We say two configurations

and

-distinguished

Claim 3.2
.

For each gadget index

and each pair of

(i.e.,

), every correct derivation of

on

must contain at least one rule

-distinguishes them.

Proof of Claim

Suppose for contradiction that a derivation

of

never applies any rule at a

and

Then, by the opacity of

applies the same rule applications at the same positions

and

nor

By Lemma

contributes the clause

-distinguishes the two configurations.
Since

Step 4: Derivation tree branching argument.

We count the distinct rule applications in the derivation
\mathcal{D}

that are required to resolve each gadget.

To see this, observe that the

from

.
By spatial separation (Lemma

Therefore the resolution of gadget

branches independently of the

We use a per-configuration trace argument.
For each configuration

, define its

gadget-

as the subsequence of rule applications in
\mathcal{D}

that act at a position overlapping with gadget

in that configuration.

By Claim
3.2
, for every gadget
i
and every pair of
configurations
(C,C^{\prime})
differing only at gadget
i
, the derivation

\mathcal{D}
must contain at least one rule application that
i
-distinguishes

By spatial separation (Appendix

-distinguishing

The

Since any rule overlapping gadget

sees only the local context of

Therefore

∎

Remark 3.3

(Stronger lower bound via explicit clause encoding)
.

The bound
L\geq\Omega(n)
proved above follows from the gadget argument.
A stronger bound
L\geq\Omega(2^{n})
holds under a modified construction
of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
in which the instance contains
one clause per global configuration:

\mathcal{I}_{n}=\{E_{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})}\}_{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}}
,
where
E_{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})}
encodes the equivalence condition for the
specific configuration
C_{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})}
.
Each such clause
E_{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})}
encodes a distinct refutation goal
(the negation of
f(x)=g(x)
for the specific constants
of configuration
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
),
and since
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
is refutation-based (as required by Case 2 of
Theorem
3.1
),
each such goal requires at least one dedicated rule application to resolve.
Since there are
2^{n}
clauses and each requires at least one step,
the total derivation length satisfies
L\geq 2^{n}=\Omega(2^{n})
.
The trade-off is that
|\mathcal{I}_{n}|=2^{n}
(the instance itself is exponential),
so the lower bound holds for the derivation length relative to the

number of clauses
, not relative to
n
directly.
Whether a superlinear-in-
n
bound holds for instances of polynomial size
remains open (see Question (Q3) of Section
8
).

Remark 3.4

(Unconditional versus conditional lower bound)
.

The lower bound in Case 2 is unconditional: it does not assume

\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}
or any other unproven hypothesis.
It holds for any local syntactic system
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
satisfying the stated hypotheses,
provided the family
\{\mathcal{I}_{n}\}
with
2^{n}
locally
indistinguishable configurations exists.
The existence of such a family is established constructively in
Lemma
4.5
(Appendix
A
).

Remark 3.5

(Superpolynomial versus exponential)
.

Under weaker hypotheses on the family
\{\mathcal{I}_{n}\}
(e.g.,

N(n)
superpolynomial but sub-exponential), the clause-per-configuration
encoding (Remark
3.3
) yields
L\geq N(n)=\Omega(N(n))
,
which is superpolynomial in
n
.
The exponential bound
L=\Omega(2^{n})
holds specifically when

N(n)=2^{n}
, as guaranteed by the gadget construction.

5
Cryptographic Connection

5.1
Formal correspondence

We establish a formal correspondence between the structure of
Theorem
3.1
and the structure of cryptographic
indistinguishability.

Definition 5.1

(Syntactic adversary)
.

A
syntactic adversary
for
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F})
is any deterministic
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that operates by applying rules from a local
extension
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{R}
(with the same locality radius
r_{0}
) to terms
presented as inputs.
We distinguish two classes:

•

A
perfectly-constrained adversary
cannot apply any rule
at positions in
\mathcal{F}
; this corresponds to Case 1 (base
system
\mathcal{R}
).

•

A
computationally-bounded adversary
operates via a local
extension
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{R}
(Definition
2.5
) that may
include rules applicable at positions in
\mathcal{F}
, subject to a
polynomial bound on total rule applications (steps in a
deterministic RAM model with unit-cost arithmetic); this
corresponds to Case 2.

Each configuration
C_{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})}
is parametrized by a bit
vector
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
(Definition
2.16
).
The
advantage
of
\mathcal{A}
in identifying this bit vector on
instance
\mathcal{I}_{n}
is

\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A},n)\;:=\;\Pr_{i\sim\mathrm{Unif}[N]}\bigl[\mathcal{A}(C_{i})\text{ correctly
outputs which Skolem function is encoded in }C_{i}\bigr]-\tfrac{1}{2},

where the probability is uniform over the
N=2^{n}
configurations

C_{1},\ldots,C_{N}
of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
.
Here ”correctly outputs which Skolem function is encoded in
C_{i}
”
means: given
C_{i}=C_{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})}
,
\mathcal{A}
outputs the bit
vector
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
that determines which constants
a_{j}^{b_{j}}

are selected in each gadget (equivalently, which disjunct of each
gadget clause must be refuted).
Both
f
and
g
appear in every
C_{i}
; the task is to identify
the configuration, not to distinguish which function is semantically
present (both are, by semantic equivalence).
Note that
\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A},n)
may in principle be negative
(if
\mathcal{A}
is systematically wrong); the bound
Proposition
5.3
applies to the signed quantity,
and the corollary Corollary
5.4
concerns adversaries
achieving positive advantage.

Proposition 5.2

(Case 1 implies zero advantage)
.

Under the hypotheses of Case 1 of Theorem
3.1
,

\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A},n)=0
for every syntactic adversary

\mathcal{A}
and every
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

By Case 1, no derivation in
\mathcal{R}
produces any clause of the form

f(t_{1})=g(t_{2})
that would reveal which configuration

(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
is active.
Since
\mathcal{A}
is constrained to apply rules from
\mathcal{R}
(or a local
extension with the same protected set), it produces the same
output on every configuration,
and therefore cannot identify the active bit vector
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})

with any advantage.
Therefore
\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A},n)=0
.
∎

Proposition 5.3

(Case 2 implies negligible per-step advantage)
.

Under the hypotheses of Case 2 of Theorem
3.1
, any syntactic
adversary
\mathcal{A}
running for
L
steps has

\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A},n)\;\leq\;\frac{L\cdot c}{2^{n}}.

In particular, to achieve
\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A},n)\geq\delta

for any constant
\delta>0
, the adversary must run for at least

L\geq\delta\cdot 2^{n}/c=\Omega(2^{n})
steps.

Proof.

We use the computationally-bounded adversary model
(Definition
5.1
).
Each step of
\mathcal{A}
applies one rule of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
of locality
radius
r_{0}
.
By the indistinguishability hypothesis (Lemma
4.5
(ii)),
any rule application that does not act at a position occupied by some

a_{i}^{b_{i}}
produces the same output on all
2^{n}
configurations.
A rule application that does act at such a position can alter the
local context for at most
c
configurations (those that share the
same local pattern at that position).

Let
D(L)
denote the set of configurations that
\mathcal{A}
has

distinguished
after
L
steps, i.e., configurations for which

\mathcal{A}
has applied at least one rule that reads a gadget-specific
constant.
Each step adds to
D(L)
at most
c
configurations
(those sharing the same local context at the position acted on);
by induction on
L
,
|D(L)|\leq L\cdot c
.

For configurations not in
D(L)
,
\mathcal{A}
has applied only rules
that are blind to the gadget constants; its output on these configurations
is independent of which configuration
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
is active.
Since
\mathcal{A}
’s output is the same for all
configurations not in
D(L)
, it must output the same bit vector
for all of them; since each of the
2^{n}
configurations is equally
likely by the uniform distribution, the probability that this
fixed output is the correct bit vector is exactly
\frac{1}{2}
.

The advantage of
\mathcal{A}
is thus bounded by the fraction of
configurations in
D(L)
:

\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A},n)\;\leq\;\frac{|D(L)|}{2^{n}}\;\leq\;\frac{L\cdot c}{2^{n}}.

For
\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A},n)\geq\delta>0
we need

L\geq\delta\cdot 2^{n}/c=\Omega(2^{n})
.
∎

Corollary 5.4

(Computational hiding from Case 2)
.

Under the hypotheses of Case 2 of Theorem
3.1
,
the gadget family

is computationally hiding: no syntactic

is active with non-negligible advantage.
Specifically, any adversary achieving advantage
\geq\delta>0

requires
\Omega(2^{n})
rule applications, which is super-polynomial
in
n
.

Proof.

Immediate from Proposition
5.3
with
L=

polynomial in
n
: for any polynomial

,

L(n)\cdot c/2^{n}\to 0
as

,
so the advantage is negligible.
∎

Section

domains, type theory and circuit complexity, not because these
are analogies to cryptography, but because they have the same
mathematical structure: a constrained observer that cannot see a

6.1
Type theory and the Type Omitting Theorem

Background.

The Type Omitting Theorem (Henkin–Orey, 1956) states: a countable
consistent theory
T
omits a type
\Phi(x)
, a set of formulas in
one free variable, if and only if
\Phi
is
not principal
,
meaning no single formula
\theta(x)
is consistent with
T
and
implies every formula in
\Phi
.

Via the Curry–Howard correspondence, a type is a proposition and a
term of that type is a proof.
A type system
\mathcal{T}
(Martin-Löf type theory, the Calculus of
Constructions, or a dependent type system such as Coq or Agda) acts as
a syntactic system: it inspects the
syntactic structure
of terms
and decides type membership based on formation rules alone.

Extensional properties of functions, what a function computes rather
than how it is written, are semantically defined.
Rice’s theorem is the limiting case: no non-trivial extensional property
of a computable function is decidable by any syntactic inspection of its
code.
Within a constructive type system, this takes the following form: a type

\Phi
expressing a semantic property of functions (e.g. “this term
computes the Fibonacci sequence”) is
semantically isolated
if
its membership depends on the function computed, not on the proof term,
and
syntactically dense
if every proof term of a related type
passes through subterms that look locally like
\Phi
-witnesses.

Remark 6.2

(Typological Invariance Principle)
.

Corollary

the syntactic machine (the type) is structurally blind to the semantic

7
Conclusions

The central observation of this paper is that impossibility results and
lower bounds in proof theory, cryptography, type theory, and circuit
complexity share a single structure: a constrained observer cannot
reach a semantic invariant that lies above its observational level.
Theorem
3.1
formalizes this as an abstract obstruction with
two faces, impossibility (Case 1) and a cost lower bound (Case 2),
and the cross-domain connections of Section
6
show
that the translation is not metaphorical but exact.

These three components of the paper, the proof-theoretic analysis,
the cryptographic connection, and the cross-domain connections, are not separable.
The exponential lower bound of Case 2 is explained by the cryptographic
hiding structure (Section
5
): without it, the

\Omega(2^{n})
bound would appear as an artifact of the gadget
construction rather than as a structural necessity.
The cross-domain connections (Section
6
) are
accessible only because Section
5
has already identified
computational hiding as the correct interpretation of syntactic
separation: Type Theory and Natural Proofs barriers are then
recognizable as instances of the same hiding phenomenon, not as
separate results requiring separate arguments.

The cryptographic content is not peripheral.
The structure of Case 2 is the structure of computational hiding:
protected positions are commitment schemes, syntactic separation is
ciphertext indistinguishability, and the
\Omega(2^{n})
derivation cost
is the adversary’s negligible advantage.
The proof-theoretic and cryptographic frameworks are the same framework,
seen from different sides of the same invariant.

For circuit complexity, the framework provides two things.
First, an unconditional
\Omega(n)
lower bound on the inspection cost
of any large and constructive technique, without pseudo-random generators.
Second, a structural explanation: the Natural Proofs barrier is not a
consequence of computational hardness but of observational blindness,
and it holds in every world of Impagliazzo, including Algorithmica.
The result of
[
14
]
is the first
quantitative confirmation of this: its
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
-natural-proofs
barrier lives on the observational axis, not the computational one.

Open Questions

The following questions arise from Theorem
3.1
and the
surrounding framework but are not answered in this paper.

(Q1)

Decidability of protected sets.

For which classes of local syntactic systems is the existence
of a non-trivial protected set
\mathcal{F}
decidable in time
polynomial in the clause set?
Remark
4.2
shows that linear-time
decidability holds for ground atoms under first-symbol clash.
The general case — patterns with deep nesting or variable
overlap — is open.

(Q2)

Formal reduction between Cases 1 and 2.

Does the existence of a syntactic invariant
\mathsf{Inv}
in Case 1
imply the existence of a gadget family witnessing the Case 2
lower bound for some extension
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
?
A positive answer would unify the two cases into a single
combinatorial criterion.

(Q3)

Instance complexity of the lower bound.

The gadget family
\{\mathcal{I}_{n}\}
grows with
n
.
Is there a single clause set
\mathcal{I}
of fixed size
M
such that
any local extension
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
requires derivation length
super-polynomial in
M
to prove
f\equiv g
on
\mathcal{I}
?
This is the Case 2 analogue of the single witness
W

(commutativity) in Case 1.

(Q4)

Commitment scheme construction.

Corollary
5.4
shows that Case 2 implies
computational hiding.
Does every instance of Case 2 also yield an explicit binding
property, making it a commitment scheme in the standard
(hiding and binding) sense?
Specifically, is there a polynomial-time reduction from
breaking the binding to solving a derivation problem in
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
?

(Q5)

Extensions to non-local systems.

The results assume a fixed locality radius
r_{0}
.
Can analogous obstruction theorems be proved for systems with
unbounded inspection depth, or for global rules such as
resolution?
Corollary
6.3
(i) shows unconditionally that
no natural proof technique can establish circuit lower bounds;
any extension to global systems that overcomes the syntactic
barrier must therefore not be a natural proof in the sense of
Razborov–Rudich
[
13
]
.

(Q6)

Formal embedding into the observational hierarchy.

Remark
5.8
identifies
\mathcal{R}
as a constrained
observer
O_{\mathcal{R}}\prec O_{\top}
.
Remark
6.4
carries this out concretely for

\mathsf{AC}^{0}
-natural proofs:
O_{\mathsf{AC}^{0}_{d}}\prec O_{\top}

with the collapse
\mathbf{P}_{O_{\mathsf{AC}^{0}_{d}}}=\mathbf{NP}_{O_{\mathsf{AC}^{0}_{d}}}\subsetneq\mathbf{P}
.
The general case requires: (i) embedding
(t,p)\mapsto\mathcal{B}_{r_{0}}(p)(t)
into the string-based formalism
of
[
10
]
; (ii) identifying the canonical observer
level of
O_{\mathcal{R}}
; (iii) deriving Theorem
3.1
as a
corollary of Proposition 9.6 of
[
10
]
.
The mixed-radix decomposition of
[
9
]

provides a concrete observational invariant whose embedding
may serve as a test case for step (i).

(Q7)

Obfuscation.

The VBB impossibility
[
16
]
states that
no algorithm (not necessarily syntactic) can obfuscate
all circuits to black-box equivalence.
This matches Case 1 structurally: the semantic invariant (the
function computed) is protected from any local syntactic observer.
Does Theorem
3.1
formally subsume VBB impossibility,
and does the Case 2 bound correspond to the computational gap
between iO and VBB?

(Q8)

Algebrization.

The algebrization barrier
[
17
]

shows that arithmetization-based techniques cannot resolve
complexity separations.
Arithmetization acts locally on the syntactic representation
of a circuit, and the semantic invariant (complexity class
membership) is protected from this local action.
Does the algebrization barrier fit Theorem
3.1

with
r_{0}
equal to the degree of the polynomial extension?
