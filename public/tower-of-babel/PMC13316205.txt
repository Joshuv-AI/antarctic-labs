Language network reorganization in temporal lobe epilepsy: insights from task-based functional MRI
Authors: Tamjid Imtiaz, Alfredo Lucas, Eli Cornblath, Mariam Josyula, Nina Petillo, Emily Zhang, Kathy A Lawler, Daniel J Zhou, Joel M Stein, Sandhitsu Das, Kathryn A Davis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), recurrent seizures can cause structural and functional disruptions within the temporal lobe and nearby language regions, resulting in neural reorganization. In this study, we leverage task-based functional magnetic resonance imaging (tb-fMRI) to understand how this language reorganization process varies based on the seizure location, the timing of seizure onset, and the chronicity of epileptic activity.Method84 drug-resistant TLE patients who completed a tb-fMRI sentence completion task were included in this study. Analysis included group comparison of activation and comparison of the extent of reorganization in the whole brain and standard language regions of interest (ROI) level. We also measured the impact of the age of onset and the duration of epilepsy in the reorganization process.ResultsA significantly higher degree of language network reorganization is observed in left (L) TLE than in right (R) TLE at the whole brain level. At the regional level, a higher degree of reorganization is observed in temporal and frontal lobe ROIs in LTLE compared to RTLE, especially in the middle frontal gyrus and posterior temporal gyrus. The effects of age of onset and epilepsy duration were prominent in LTLE subjects at both the whole-brain and ROI levels.ConclusionPatients with LTLE show greater changes in the middle frontal and posterior temporal regions, particularly in those with an earlier age of onset and longer disease duration. Identifying these patterns may assist in surgical planning and personalizing treatment to protect cognitive function.Keywords: Language network reorganization, Temporal lobe epilepsy, Magnetic resonance imaging, Neuropsychological assessment, Language lateralization

Introduction

Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), the most common form of focal epilepsy (Jehi et al., 2015; Yardi et al., 2026), is characterized by recurrent seizure activity within the temporal lobe and adjacent regions critical for higher cognitive functions such as language processing (Ives-Deliperi and Butler, 2021). In previous studies, structural factors like lesion characteristics have been shown to influence language network patterns (Foesleitner et al., 2020). Beyond structural effects, functional factors such as interictal discharges and seizure propagation may further modulate language organization (Martino et al., 2025; Rosenberger et al., 2009). Consequently, the language reorganization process is multifactorial and remains challenging to characterize at an individualized, patient-specific level (Hamberger and Cole, 2011). Understanding the process and correlating the extent of this reorganization with patient-specific clinical phenotypes is crucial for preoperative surgical planning to minimize the risk of postoperative cognitive impairments.

Task-based fMRI (tb-fMRI) is clinically used to noninvasively lateralize (Szaflarski et al., 2017) and localize (Benjamin et al., 2018) language function presurgically and has been shown to be concordant with gold standard methods such as direct cortical stimulation and the WADA test (Bizzi et al., 2008; Binder et al., 1996). This noninvasive technique permits both the localization and lateralization of function, while the WADA test is invasive and can only provide lateralization information (Wang et al., 2012; Massot-Tarrús and Mirsattari, 2022). Moreover, prior studies have shown that language lateralization derived from tb-fMRI serves as an indirect marker of verbal memory lateralization, while Wada memory testing is often insufficiently reliable or lacks material specificity to accurately localize verbal memory processes (Binder, 2011; Binder et al., 2008). Despite the superior sensitivity and specificity of task-based fMRI for localizing and lateralizing language function, robust statistical evidence of language network reorganization in temporal lobe epilepsy remains limited (Zhang, 2025; Eickhoff et al., 2009). This gap is largely driven by small sample sizes, insufficient multiscale and regionally extended analyses of hemispheric language systems, and the substantial clinical and pathological heterogeneity across patients.

In TLE, a high incidence of language alteration can be attributed to disruptions in nearby pathways and regions supporting language in the dominant hemisphere, particularly Broca's (in the frontal operculum) and Wernicke's area (in the posterior superior temporal lobe) (He et al., 2018). Although the degree of reorganization is patient-specific, the most common reorganization processes include intra-hemispheric (compensation in the ipsilateral side of the network) and inter-hemispheric (recruitment of contralateral homologous regions) reorganization (Kim et al., 2010; Brázdil et al., 2005). Prior research has shown altered activity in typical language regions including the temporal and frontal lobe in TLE patients compared to healthy controls (Banjac et al., 2021; Karpychev et al., 2023). Nevertheless, only a limited number of studies have examined language-network reorganization across TLE subtypes using task-based activation profiles. Furthermore, prior work described reorganization within a specific language atlas (Rolinski et al., 2020; Ankeeta et al., 2025), which may obscure intra-hemispheric spatial patterns of reorganization that do not necessarily conform to anatomical boundaries. There is converging evidence that atypical language lateralization is more prevalent in individuals with early, as opposed to late seizure onset (Prentice et al., 2025), and that the duration of epilepsy also contributes to such alterations (Marcelle et al., 2022). However, the extent to which these factors influence whole-brain and regional patterns of language-network reorganization across TLE groups remains unclear. Elucidating how seizure onset age and disease duration relate to the reorganization process, therefore represents an important area for further investigation.

In this study, we aim to understand the reorganization of language and verbal memory networks using tb-fMRI. We hypothesized that the typically language-dominant left hemisphere is more vulnerable to seizure-induced neural disruption in left temporal lobe epilepsy (LTLE) compared to right TLE (RTLE), resulting in greater language network reorganization at both intra- and interhemispheric levels. To test this, we evaluated reorganization patterns at both the whole-brain level and within established language-related regions of interest (ROIs) across multiple atlases at varying parcellation sizes. We also examined whether the degree of network reorganization is modulated by the age of seizure onset and disease duration, which may reflect compensatory mechanisms supporting functional recovery. Finally, we hypothesized that disease-related reorganization may contribute to maintaining language function and assessed this by examining correlations between language performance and reorganization measures.

Methods

Subject demographics

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pennsylvania, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients with drug-resistant unilateral temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) who underwent neuroimaging as part of presurgical evaluation between 2015 and 2024 at our Level 4 epilepsy center were included. Only patients with seizures originating from the temporal lobe were included in the study, as determined by a multidisciplinary epilepsy surgical conference. Seizure lateralization of each of the patients were determined using a multidisciplinary approach incorporating semiology, brain MRI, FDG-PET, MEG, scalp EEG, and intracranial EEG. All subjects underwent tb-fMRI with a single-session sentence completion task. Alongside the sentence completion task, an object naming task was conducted to verify eye openness and visual cortex activation, ensuring that subjects remained alert and actively performed the task. Patients who did not show visual cortex activation during the object naming task were excluded. A total of 84 patients were included in the analysis. Image acquisition and fMRI task paradigm

Both tb-fMRI and T1-weighted (T1w) structural images at 3 T were acquired on a Siemens PRISMA fit system. Whole brain Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (BOLD) tb-fMRI images were captured with the following parameters: a 2.0 mm isotropic voxel size, echo time (TE) = 35 ms, repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, flip angle = 70 degrees, and multi-band acceleration factor = 4. Whole brain 3D T1w MPRAGE structural images were acquired with the following parameters: 1 mm isotropic voxel size, TR =1760 ms, TE = 3.15 ms, flip angle = 9 degrees, resolution = 192 × 256 × 192. A sentence-completion paradigm was used to assess language function, and an object-naming paradigm was used as a positive control task to assess the participant's engagement. During the sentence-completion task, participants were presented with alternating blocks of fill-in-the-blank sentences. The active blocks consisted of intelligible sentences and participants were instructed to silently think of a word to complete each sentence. The control blocks consisted of gibberish sentences for which participants were instructed to look at each sentence without trying to complete it. The paradigm consisted of six 20-s blocks alternating between rest and task conditions. During the Object naming task, participants were presented with pictures of objects and instructed to think of the name of each object. For control blocks, participants were presented with a crosshair to look at without thinking of any objects. The paradigm consisted of 12-s blocks alternating between eight rest and seven task condition blocks. Sentence completion task is generally used for language localization and hemispheric lateralization for identifying the primary language cortex. This is a semantic language paradigm which can activate both the superior temporal gyrus in the Wernicke area and the Broca area in the dominant hemisphere (Black et al., 2017). The object naming task is also used clinically for language lateralization but the general consensus is that it provides less effective hemispheric language lateralization than other tasks though it is easier to perform, especially for cognitively impaired patients (Pillai and Zaca, 2011). Thus, as mentioned previously, we used it here only as a positive control task and excluded patients without activation on object naming.

Functional imaging processing and voxel-based statistics

The overall functional image processing pipeline is summarized in Fig. 1. First, we used fMRIPrep (Esteban et al., 2019) to perform brain extraction and segmentation of individual T1w images, registration of task fMRI BOLD volumes to individual T1w and Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI152NLin2009cAsym) template space, and time-series confound estimation. We also spatially smoothed the resulting BOLD images with a 5 × 5 × 5 mm3 full width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. Complete details of the pre-processing pipeline used in this study are included in Supplementary Methods.

Methods outline: tb-fMRI data were processed with fMRIprep and task activation maps were derived via first level generalized linear model. Time-series confounders generated by fMRIPrep were included as nuisance regressors, while task-related confounders were used as task regressors to construct the design matrix for the GLM analysis.

Voxel-based statistics and group level analysis

To calculate the voxel-level statistics, we derived individual-level condition-specific effects via a first-level generalized linear model (GLM). We used time series confounders from fMRIprep as the nuisance regressor, and the confounders associated with the task as the task regressor for creating the design matrix. We used a total of 18 nuisance regressors for GLM, such as average signal within brain mask, white matter mask, CSF mask, their corresponding time derivatives, as well as six motion parameters and their time derivatives. We used Glover (Glover, 1999) hemodynamic response function and high-pass filtering with a Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) basis (cut-off freq = 0.01 Hz) as the drift model to remove low-frequency fluctuations in the BOLD time series. We modeled the task condition as 20s blocks and convolved with the hemodynamic response function. Finally, we extracted activation contrasts associated with the sentence completion task by subtracting the resting blocks from the task blocks.

Language dominance

We calculated laterality indices (LI) to provide a quantitative measure of language dominance (Adcock et al., 2003) and an indirect estimate of hemispheric reorganization associated with plasticity (Esteves et al., 2021). We calculated the LIs over the whole brain as well as within regions of interest (ROI) to indicate hemispheric dominance. LI was calculated according to the formula:

LI=L−R/L+R; where L and R are the number of activated voxels in the left and right hemisphere, respectively. A positive LI indicates left hemispheric dominance, and a negative value indicates right hemispheric dominance. According to the standard practice in language fMRI research, (LI > +0.2) is defined as left, (−0.2 ≤ LI ≤ +0.2) as bilateral and (LI < −0.2) as right hemisphere dominant (Trimmel et al., 2021; Yildirim et al., 2025).

Whole brain and ROI analysis based on LI

To understand language plasticity across LTLE and RTLE patients, we conducted a LI–based analysis at both the whole-brain and language-related ROI levels. At the whole-brain level, we calculated the LI for each subject individually using their task activation map. For ROI-level analyses, we used two separate groups of ROIs to capture network reorganization. In the first approach, we focused on the core language regions, which we refer to as the core language network. To extract the regions corresponding to this network, we utilized an extensively validated language atlas derived from 806 individuals (Lipkin et al., 2022). This atlas includes three frontal parcels (the inferior frontal gyrus, its orbital portion, and the middle frontal gyrus) and three temporal/parietal parcels (the anterior temporal, posterior temporal, and angular gyrus) in each hemisphere, totaling 12 parcels. In the second approach, we focused on regions that are more task-relevant (sentence completion in our case), which we refer to as the task-related network. To define these ROIs, we queried Neurosynth (Yarkoni et al., 2011) using “sentence comprehension” as the search term, which identified 77 fMRI studies and an activation map. Inside this task activation map, we extracted seven homologous ROIs in each hemisphere using the Harvard–Oxford (HO) cortical atlas (Frazier et al., 2005), which includes the middle frontal gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus (triangular and opercular parts), superior temporal gyrus (posterior), middle temporal gyrus (posterior and temporo-occipital parts), and frontal orbital cortex. As expected, there were some overlapping ROIs between the two atlases. The rationale for employing two distinct atlases is twofold. First, the core language atlas provides a probabilistic representation restricted to canonical language regions. In contrast, the task-derived atlas encompasses a broader set of language-related areas, including lower-level processing regions with extended frontal coverage. Using multiple atlases permits different spatial patterns of the ROIs of the language network, allowing us to better characterize the network's spatial extent.

Neuropsychological testing

A standard semantic verbal fluency test was administered in a subgroup of patients who completed the task-based fMRI protocol. Raw scores were converted to standardized T-scores using normative data to allow consistent comparisons across individuals (Heaton et al., 2004). Semantic verbal fluency was chosen as the behavioral measure of language proficiency because it reflects the ability to generate meaningful word associations (Massol et al., 2021), a process closely related to producing coherent sentence completions in the fMRI task. This alignment ensures consistency between the behavioral and imaging measures of our study.

Statistical analysis

In the individual-level activation map, we report peak-level activations at a threshold of p < 0.01, corrected for false discovery rate (FDR) across the whole brain. We used a cluster-level threshold to remove any activated cluster with fewer than 50 voxels. To assess the robustness of our findings with respect to threshold selection, a sensitivity analysis was performed by recalculating LI values at three FDR-corrected statistical thresholds: p < 0.001, p < 0.01, and p < 0.05. Across all tested thresholds, the between-group difference in LI between L-TLE and R-TLE patients remained statistically significant, confirming the stability of language lateralization patterns regardless of threshold choice (Fig. S8). As expected, a progressive reduction in LI magnitude was observed with increasingly lenient thresholds in both groups, attributable to the greater inclusion of bilateral voxels at lower thresholds. LI values were adjusted for age, handedness, age at seizure onset, and epilepsy duration by extracting residuals from a linear regression model. The covariate-adjusted LI residuals were subsequently compared between L-TLE and R-TLE groups using an independent samples t-test. To derive the group level activation, we applied a voxel-wise analysis using Threshold Free Cluster Enhancement (Smith and Nichols, 2009) with FSL randomise (Jenkinson et al., 2012) in a two-sample t-test. We used 5000 permutations and a threshold of p < 0.05 to determine statistical significance. For two-sample voxel-wise comparisons between subgroups, we included age and handedness as covariates in the design matrix. We also measured the absolute value of Cohen's d between the two groups to quantify effect sizes (Cohen, 2013). We considered Cohen's d values above 0.5 to represent a moderate effect size, and above 0.8 to represent a large effect size. For all analyses, statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05, and Bonferroni correction was used to correct for multiple comparisons where specified (pFWER). In addition, to determine the correlation between language laterality and the semantic verbal fluency test, we calculated a partial correlation between the LIs and the verbal fluency test scores, including age, age of onset and duration of epilepsy as additional covariates. We used Pearson correlation coefficient as the measure of degree of correlation. To determine the impact of age of onset as well as the duration of epilepsy on the functional reorganization across the groups, we calculate a partial correlation between the LIs and the impact variables and include age as an additional covariate. We used Pearson correlation coefficient as the measure of degree of correlation.

Results

Language network is more lateralized in RTLE than LTLE

We measured the group-level activation maps from the tb-fMRI acquired from LTLE and RTLE subjects, respectively. More spatially distributed (number of language-activated voxels, LTLE: 29389, RTLE: 25416) and attenuated left-sided activation was observed in LTLE subjects compared to RTLE subjects (Fig. 2A–B). This was also the case when we measured the group-level difference between these two groups, where we found strong residual left-sided activation (Fig. 2C). We also measured LIs from each individual's activation map. Although the left lateralized language network is observed in both groups, the language lateralization was significantly higher in the RTLE subjects compared to the LTLE (Cohen's d = 0.51, p = 0.0101) (Fig. 2D), showing that the reorganization process was mostly observed in the LTLE subjects compared to the RTLE. We also conducted the same analysis for both groups, restricting it to the right handed patients only and found that the difference remained significant between the groups (Fig. S9).

An earlier age of onset is associated with greater reorganization at both the whole-brain and ROI levels in LTLE

To determine if the early age of onset has any impact on the reorganization of language networks in LTLE and RTLE groups, we measured the correlation between the age of onset and language LI of individual subjects at both whole brain and ROI levels. At a whole-brain level, LTLE subjects show a positive correlation indicating the impact of early age of seizure onset in the reorganization process (Pearson r = 0.433, p = 0.002) (Fig. 4A). Similar impact of early age of onset on reorganization process was observed in previously found ROIs with strong effect size between groups: middle frontal gyrus (Pearson r = 0.413, p = 0.003), posterior temporal gyrus (Pearson r = 0.372, p = 0.01) (Fig. 5A), posterior portion of the superior temporal gyrus (Pearson r = 0.423, p = 0.002), and temporo-occipital segment of the medial temporal gyrus (Pearson r = 0.314, p = 0.02) (Fig. S3A). No significant correlation was found for RTLE subjects at the whole brain or ROI level. In a secondary analysis, we also examined the reorganization trajectories of early- and late-onset patients within the LTLE and RTLE groups. Each group was divided into two subgroups: patients with an age of seizure onset below 6 years were classified as early onset, whereas those with onset after 6 years were classified as late onset. The rationale for this criterion is based on prior work (Gaillard et al., 2007; Berl et al., 2014) suggesting that there are differences in language dominance depending on whether seizure onset occurs early in development (i.e., before 6 years old) or later. The results indicated that the early-onset subgroups showed a numerically stronger negative association between language reorganization and epilepsy duration, with Spearman correlations of r = −0.612 (p = 0.06) in LTLE and r = −0.754 (p = 0.0835) in RTLE, relative to the late-onset subgroups (LTLE: r = −0.114, p = 0.4820; RTLE: r = 0.277, p = 0.1615) (Fig. S4). Differences in correlations between early and late-onset groups in LTLE and RTLE were assessed using Fisher's r-to-z transformation. A significant difference was observed only in the RTLE group (Z = −2.06, one-tailed p = 0.0192).

Longer duration of epilepsy modulates the extent of functional reorganization across both whole-brain and ROI level in LTLE

To evaluate the influence of epilepsy duration on language network reorganization in LTLE and RTLE groups, we examined the correlation between epilepsy duration and language LI across both whole-brain and ROI levels. At the whole-brain level, LTLE subjects exhibited a significant negative correlation (Pearson's r = −0.429, p = 0.002), indicating that longer epilepsy duration is associated with greater functional reorganization (Fig. 4B). Consistent effects were observed at the ROI level, where longer duration of epilepsy showed significant associations with reorganization in previously identified regions, including the middle frontal gyrus (r = −0.421, p = 0.003), posterior temporal gyrus (r = −0.387, p = 0.007) (Fig. 5B), posterior portion of the superior temporal gyrus (r = −0.435, p = 0.002), and temporo-occipital segment of the middle temporal gyrus (r = −0.327, p = 0.022) (Fig. S3B). In contrast, no significant correlations were detected in RTLE subjects at either the whole-brain or ROI levels. We further assessed the association of structural pathologies and disease severity with language network reorganization. We examined the presence of focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic seizures (FBTCS) and lesions as two important factors that might influence language network reorganization (Figs. S7 and S9). After analyzing the groups based on their laterality index, we found no significant differences, suggesting that lesion type and the presence of FBTCS do not substantially modulate the observed patterns of functional network reorganization in this cohort.

Impact of language network reorganization in preserving language functionality

To assess whether language network reorganization associated with epileptic activity might serve as a compensatory mechanism for preserving language function, we examined the correlation between semantic performance and language lateralization at both the whole-brain and ROI levels. Although the association in LTLE was not statistically significant (r = −0.259, p = 0.1383), the negative trend supports our hypothesis that intra- and inter-hemispheric reorganization may contribute to maintaining language function in this group (Fig. S5). In contrast, no significant relationship was observed in the RTLE cohort. ROI-level analyses revealed similar patterns to those observed at the whole-brain level for both LTLE and RTLE (Fig. S6). It is important to note that this analysis was conducted on a subset of the original cohort, with comparable sample sizes across groups (LTLE = 37; RTLE = 27).

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to characterize language network reorganization in individuals with temporal lobe epilepsy. Overall, our results are consistent with greater reorganization in LTLE compared to RTLE. At both the whole-brain level and within language-related regions, LTLE patients demonstrated increased reorganization, highlighting the influence of recurrent seizure activity on the typically left-lateralized language system. Moreover, several language-associated regions appeared particularly sensitive to reorganization in LTLE, indicating that certain nodes of the network may serve as focal points of plasticity. Together, these findings provide new insight into the regions that are most susceptible to reorganization and offer a clearer understanding of language network alterations in LTLE (Binding et al., 2022), which may help inform surgical decision-making.

Previous studies of language lateralization in both neurologically healthy individuals and patients with epilepsy have consistently shown that language function is predominantly left-lateralized, and that atypical language organization is characterized by reduced lateralization (Hamberger and Cole, 2011; Mbwana et al., 2008). This has led to a prevailing hypothesis that reorganization of the language network reflects a compensatory mechanism aimed at preserving language function in the presence of neurological insult. In our study, we demonstrate that LTLE is associated with greater language network reorganization by using larger and relatively balanced LTLE and RTLE group sizes, compared to prior work. This effect was observed both intra-hemispherically (i.e., compensatory recruitment of adjacent ipsilateral regions) and inter-hemispherically (i.e., recruitment of contralateral homologous regions). Moreover, all imaging data were acquired on a single scanner, eliminating the need for inter-scanner harmonization and minimizing variability related to differences in acquisition protocols.

There is growing evidence that left and right TLE are neurologically distinct phenotypes. Recent work using intracranial EEG demonstrates that the lateralization of seizures can be differentiated by lateralized interictal network signatures, which reflect asymmetric large-scale network disruption (Conrad et al., 2024). Similarly, resting-state functional gradient analyses have revealed distinct subcortical connectivity alterations between LTLE and RTLE, particularly within hippocampal and association networks (Lucas et al., 2023). These findings, along with other neuroimaging studies (Voets et al., 2011; Lemkaddem et al., 2014) indicate that seizure laterality differentially impacts brain network organization. In this context, our observation provides convergent evidence that left-hemisphere seizure pathology induces more profound reorganization of language systems. Our results thus support the view that LTLE and RTLE are distinct network disorders with differential consequences for language networks.

We found strong evidence in the LTLE group that both the age of onset and the duration of epilepsy significantly influence language network reorganization. Specifically, earlier onset and longer duration of epileptogenic activity in the left hemisphere were associated with greater degrees of reorganization. In contrast, no significant effects were observed in the RTLE group. Nonetheless, it remains plausible that seizures originating in either hemisphere can disrupt the typical developmental trajectory of the language network when epileptogenic activity begins during critical language acquisition periods. Supporting this idea, even within our small subgroup of patients with early age of onset, we observed stronger reorganization over time in both LTLE and RTLE subjects. This pattern aligns with established evidence showing that the right hemisphere plays a more prominent role in early language development, whereas the left hemisphere maintains consistently strong involvement throughout development (Olulade et al., 2020).

An important question is whether language reorganization in response to epileptogenic activity serves as a compensatory mechanism to preserve cognitive performance (Nenning et al., 2026). To explore this, we examined the relationship between verbal semantic scores and language lateralization in both LTLE and RTLE groups. Our underlying hypothesis was that disruption of canonical language regions, particularly within the temporal lobe, would drive the recruitment of adjacent ipsilateral areas as well as contralateral homologues, with a stronger compensatory effect expected in LTLE. Although we did not find strong statistical evidence supporting this compensatory relationship, a modest trend was present that inspires further investigation in larger, well-characterized cohorts. Overall, understanding how language reorganization relates to cognitive outcomes has important clinical implications for individualized surgical planning and prognostication.

Our study has several limitations. First, a notable limitation of the present study is the absence of a healthy control group, which restricts the extent to which definitive conclusions regarding language reorganization can be drawn, particularly in R-TLE patients. Establishing true language reorganization requires a normative reference baseline against which atypical lateralization patterns can be objectively compared. However, it is worth noting that prior literature consistently demonstrates that R-TLE patients largely maintain typical left-hemispheric language dominance, suggesting that R-TLE patients closely mirror the normative lateralization pattern (Thivard et al., 2005). Nonetheless, future studies incorporating matched healthy control groups will be essential to establish definitive normative thresholds and to better characterize the full spectrum of language network alterations in both L-TLE and R-TLE populations. Another challenge was verifying whether participants were fully engaged and performing the language task as intended, given the absence of real-time verbal confirmation. To address this, we incorporated an additional object-naming task to ensure subjects were awake and attentive. Second, although we examined the relationship between language performance and lateralization using available neuropsychological scores, not all patients who completed the task-fMRI protocol had corresponding verbal semantic scores. Nonetheless, we were able to include a well-balanced subset of participants for this analysis. Future research would benefit from larger cohorts that integrate both task-based fMRI and diffusion imaging to characterize the language network from complementary structural and functional perspectives. Additionally, an important direction for future work is the use of resting-state functional networks to examine language organization without requiring subjects to perform explicit tasks, which may be particularly valuable in certain clinical populations (e.g., pediatric drug-resistant epilepsy).

Conclusion

We found greater language network reorganization in LTLE compared to RTLE across both whole-brain analyses and traditional language regions, as quantified by the laterality index. Our results further demonstrated distinct patterns of intra- and inter-hemispheric reorganization across patient groups stratified by seizure age of onset and epilepsy duration. LTLE patients consistently showed more extensive reorganization than RTLE patients, reflecting the greater vulnerability of the left hemisphere's dominant language system to seizure-related disruption. Additionally, we observed increased whole-brain and regional-level reorganization among individuals whose seizures began earlier during language development. Finally, although the association between the degree of language reorganization and behavioral measures of language performance did not reach statistical significance, a tendency toward this relationship was observed, highlighting the need for larger cohort studies to formally establish its clinical relevance for understanding patient-specific brain plasticity and informing surgical decision-making.
