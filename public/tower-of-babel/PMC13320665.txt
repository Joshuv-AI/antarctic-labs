Reply to Borst et al.: Acupuncture and the placebo effect
Authors: Lynne Peeples, Gene Russo
Published: 2026

The news feature (1), written in collaboration with PNAS Front Matter editors, was not intended to advocate for acupuncture as a practice. Rather, we set out to examine the scientific efforts underway to understand the potential physiological mechanisms underlying this ancient technique. In particular, the article explored emerging research on connective tissue and the interstitium—interconnected fluid-filled spaces found throughout much of the body—and the possibility that this network could help explain some observations associated with acupuncture (and the efficacy that many patients report). The article explicitly discusses both the power of placebo responses and the longstanding challenge of disentangling them from other potential physiological effects (for example see page 2, paragraph 5, and page 3, paragraph 2). As noted in the article, recent studies suggest that the apparent benefits of “sham” acupuncture may not reflect placebo effects alone (2, 3). Superficial or off-target needling could also activate biological pathways, including through sensory nerves and connective tissue. Also, for context, the quote highlighted in the letter by Borst et al. (4) opens the story, describing traditional beliefs surrounding acupuncture; the article then shifts to modern scientific investigations. Regardless of where the evidence ultimately lands, millions of people around the world routinely turn to acupuncture, particularly for pain relief. We hope this article sheds light on the current state of the science behind a practice that continues to attract substantial interest from scientists and the public.
