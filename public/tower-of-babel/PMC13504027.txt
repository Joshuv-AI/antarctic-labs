The potential utility of in-silico approach in identifying phytochemicals against various targets for the management of lung cancer
Authors: Rupali Saini, Anis Ahmad Chaudhary, Richa Mishra, Saurabh Gupta, Jibrin Dauda Nggada, Mohamed A. M. Ali, Sanjay Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Research on novel treatment approaches is crucial for lung cancer, because it is one of the most common and aggressive malignancies with high mortality in the world. The potential advantages of using in silico methods to find phytochemicals for lung cancer treatment have been summarized in this review. This also highlights the various computational tools, such as ADMET profiling, network pharmacology, molecular docking, and machine learning algorithms, involved in the identification of potential phyto-constituents for lung cancer therapy. Key molecular targets that were assessed against a range of phytochemicals that showed multi-target binding and promising pharmacokinetic properties included EGFR, KRAS, ALK, and PD-L1. Prominent compounds such as quercetin, curcumin, and luteolin showed notable interactions with carcinogenic pathways, tumor microenvironment modulators, and apoptosis inducers. Mechanistic understanding, high-throughput screening, and economical drug development were made possible by this computational approach. This review demonstrates the potential utility of in silico techniques to bridge the gap between precision oncology and traditional medicine, as well as the intriguing function of phytochemicals as supplemental medicines in lung cancer therapy.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the most prevalent and leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 2.48 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths in 2022 [1]. Lung cancer poses substantial clinical challenges, as most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, characterized by widespread metastatic potential and distinct molecular heterogeneity [2]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) denotes the major subtype of lung cancer, revealing diverse responses to offered treatment modalities due to its significant molecular heterogeneity and involved biological characteristics [3].

Current therapeutic approaches including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, have improved patient outcomes to some extent [4]. The treatment methods face multiple challenges, which include the development of drug resistance, systemic toxicity, and excessive expenses of treatment, with variable success rates between different patient groups [5]. The need for new treatment developments arises from existing subtypes, which still lack effective targeted treatment options [6].

Phytochemicals, which are bioactive compounds that come from medicinal plants, have gained attention as potential cancer treatment solutions during the past few years [7]. The naturally occurring compounds exhibit multiple drug effects which include antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-proliferative, and pro-apoptotic processes [8]. The treatments utilizing phytochemicals offer multiple action pathways which result in low harmful effects to patients, thus making them suitable as stand-alone treatments or as supplements to standard medical procedures [9].

The drug discovery process has undergone a fundamental transformation through the use of computational methods to discover and enhance prospective medicinal compounds. The use of in-silico methods, which include molecular docking, virtual screening, quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) analysis, pharmacophore modelling, and network pharmacology, permits a quick and inexpensive assessment of the drug properties of phytochemicals that interact with their specific molecular targets.

One of the most significant areas of research in the context of cancer is the development of targeted therapies that can selectively affect cancerous tissues without damaging normal tissues. It has been shown that natural products and their derivatives targets various immune cells, including target T cells, macrophages, B cells, NKs, regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, immunogenic cell death, and immune checkpoints. Furthermore, these natural compounds inactivate several key pathways, including NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, MAPK, and JAK/STAT pathways [10]. Current research focuses on the exploration of a combination T-cell therapeutic approach that can specifically target cancer-related targets, such as antigens related to cancer stem cells, instead of normal cell antigens [11]. Within the same context, the use of in silico techniques, such as network pharmacology, molecular docking, etc., has been prominent in the process of identifying phytochemicals that can bind cancer-related proteins/targets [12].

Methodology

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using major scientific databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, covering publications from 2020 to 2026. Relevant studies were identified using keywords to identify relevant studies that included phytochemicals, anticancer mechanisms, bioavailability, drug delivery systems, and clinical efficacy, together with their various combinations. The inclusion criteria focused on peer-reviewed articles that documented both the anticancer mechanisms of phytochemicals and their molecular targets, and their progress in extraction and delivery systems, and their clinical or preclinical effectiveness. Studies that did not focus on lung cancer or phytochemical-based therapeutic approaches were eliminated from the research.

Pathogenesis of lung cancer

The Pathophysiology of lung cancer explain the cellular and molecular mechanisms leading to malignant transformation of the lung tissue. There are various factors involved in lung cancer pathogenesis, such as tobacco smoke, which is responsible for over 85% of lung malignancies [13],. The carcinogens found in tobacco smoke induce genetic and epigenetic changes in the lung epithelial cells, ultimately resulting in dysplasia and malignancy. Other factors such as radon exposure, air contamination, and asbestos exposure are also important contributing in lung cancer development [14].

Genetic mutation, pre-neoplastic alterations, and field cancerization embody the multistage development of lung carcinoma [15] Fig. 1. The chronic DNA damage that accumulates can cause misrepair mutations that allows lung cancer to grow through the repairing ability of the tissues or the induction of programmed cell death within compromised cells [16]. Genetic alterations have a significant role in the pathophysiology of lung cancer, and some mutations have been found among different subtypes of lung cancer [17]. For example, EGFR, KRAS, and p16 mutations are common in NSCLC, while MYC, BCL2, and TP53 mutations are common in SCLC [18]. Research has shown that about 10% of SCLC results from inherited DNA repair gene mutations such as RAD51D, BRCA1, and BRCA2 [19]. Additionally, it has been observed that the mutations also affect the patient’s chemotherapy response. Furthermore, almost 90% of SCLC patients have mutations in the RB1 and TP53 genes, and as the disease advances, new subclonal mutations in crucial genes affect the disease process [18].

Epigenetic dysregulation in lung cancer involves DNA methylation, histone changes, and non-coding RNA expression are some of the epigenetic pathways that contribute to the development of lung cancer [20]. Lung cancer cells show aberrant histone H4 changes such as hyperacetylation of H4K5 and H4K8 and hypoacetylation of H4K12 and H4K16, which could be a sign of the development of a more aggressive type of the disease [21]. Researchers are testing the effectiveness of epigenetic therapy, i.e., DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitors like azacytidine and decitabine, in the treatment of lung cancer [22] (Fig. 1).

Premalignant lesions and field cancerization represent early alterations in lung tissue that may grow to invasive cancer [23]. These contain squamous dysplasia, atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH), and diffuse idiopathic pulmonary neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia, which are associated with different histological subtypes of lung cancer. Figure 1 In lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), disease progression usually follows a sequence from hyperplasia to carcinoma in situ (CIS) [24] Considering these early molecular and cellular alterations is important, as they specify potential targets for therapeutic intervention and can be studied using in silico approaches for identifying bioactive phytochemicals.

Additional risk factors include advanced age (over 60 years), asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and previous tuberculosis infection because these factors increase lung cancer risk independent of smoking status [25]. Radon gas is the second leading cause of lung cancer in the United States and the leading cause among non-smokers. Air pollution also contributes to lung cancer risk, albeit to a lesser extent [26]. Genetic predisposition plays a role, with a personal or family history of lung cancer increasing susceptibility [18]. The pathogenesis of lung cancer is a multifaceted process influenced by environmental exposures, genetic mutations, and epigenetic modifications [19]. It is essential to understand these mechanisms to decrease mortality rates from lung cancer, since it becomes possible to use some therapeutic and preventive measures [27] (Fig. 1).

Molecular oncology research has discovered vital cancer-causing genetic elements and biological pathways that can be used as potential targets for new drug design for lung cancer [29]. The targets control vital cellular functions, which include cell growth and programmed cell death, and blood vessel formation and defence against immune system attacks. Important molecular targets involved in various pathways in Lung Cancer

Collectively, these molecular targets stand for critical drivers of lung cancer progression and act as important points of therapeutic intervention. Significantly, various of these targets have been broadly studied in computational drug discovery studies for screening potential inhibitors. In this context, naturally derived phytochemicals have expanded attention due to their ability to cooperate with many oncogenic proteins and controldiverse signalling pathways.

One of the most recognized therapeutic targets is the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), a tyrosine-kinase receptor involved in regulating cellular proliferation and survival of NSCLC cells [49]. Activating EGFR mutations markedly exons 19 deletions and exon 21 L858R substitution, have been frequently observed in NSCLC patients, especially among Asians and nonsmokers [50]. Several phytochemicals including kaempferol, morin, and isorhamnetin have demonstrated EGFR-modulating activity in preclinical studies [30]. However First-generation EGFR inhibitors showed their clinical efficacy through their initial results, yet patients developed resistance mutations, which restricted their effectiveness. To address this problem, scientists created second-generation and third-generation EGFR inhibitors [51] (Table 1).

One of the most frequent oncogenic drivers in lung cancer, particularly among smokers, is the KRAS mutation [52]. Although KRAS was previously considered “undruggable,” recent advances have led to the development of the natural KRAS inhibitor Ginkgolide A which have shown promising results, provided a new therapeutic alternative for patients with KRAS-mutant NSCLC [35]. Similarly, BRAF mutations, particularly the V600E variant, contribute to lung cancer pathogenesis. BRAF inhibitors such as Curcumin, and Apigenin have demonstrated significant effectiveness in patients with BRAF-mutant NSCLC [36] (Table 1).

Additionally, mutations in lung cancer frequently activate the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) pathway, making PI3K inhibitors a viable therapeutic strategy [53]. These agents may exhibit enhanced efficacy and help to overcome resistance, when it was used in combination with other targeted therapies [54]. Furthermore, HER2-targeted therapies have demonstrated encouraging clinical outcomes in patients with HER2-positive lung cancer [54].

Immune checkpoint proteins have emerged as valuable therapeutic targets in lung cancer, alongside oncogenic driver mutations [55]. Lung cancer cells frequently overexpress programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), enabling tumors to evade immune surveillance by engaging PD-1 mediated immune suppression [56]. Immune checkpoint natural inhibitors such as EGCG (Epigallocatechin gallate), resveratrol, apigenin, and luteolin block the PD-1/PD-L1axis, thereby restoring antitumor immune responses, enhancing tumor cell killing, and improving survival outcomes [37] (Table 1).

Various tools applied in in-silico method

Since, lung cancer requires innovative, effective treatments with low toxic side. Targeting key molecular processes in tumor development, computational biology, and in-silico techniques have emerged as robust tools for speeding the phytochemical therapeutic discovery process. The efforts made in screening phytochemicals against multi-target proteins, recent studies have demonstrated combined network pharmacology, molecular docking, machine learning (ML), and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to find potential drugs [57] (Fig. 3). For example, phytochemicals in Annona muricata and Mangifera indica plants, such as quercetin, kaempferol, and rutin, were screened by network pharmacology-based research to show notable interaction with glycoproteins such as EGFR, AKT1, and MMP9, which play crucial roles in lung cancer metastasis and drug resistance [58, 59]. These compounds were ranked for drug-likeness and oral bioavailability via pharmacokinetic (ADMETox) profiling and subsequently validated with molecular docking software such as AutoDock Vina and PyRx [60]. Using in-silico screening of Terminalia arjuna phytoconstitents, luteolin and oleanolic acid were identified as lead compounds against ALK2, BRAF, and VEGFR2 with binding energies similar to FDA-approved drugs such as alectinib and are therefore considered as potential multi-target drugs [61].

Advanced ML models, such as random forest and support vector machines, have further enhanced predictive accuracy by analyzing chemical fingerprints (e.g., MACCS, Morgan2) to classify phytochemicals with anti-lung cancer activity [62]. A 2024 study employed these ML algorithms on a dataset of 649 phytomolecules, coupled with docking against mutant EGFR, to shortlist indolocarbazole scaffolds as promising inhibitors, later validated by 100-ns MD simulations for binding stability [30]. These scaffolds, structurally analogous to staurosporine, showed high affinity for EGFR mutants, addressing resistance mechanisms common in tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapies [63]. An integrated phytochemical-based drug discovery strategy combining in silico screening with experimental validation for lung cancer is presented in Fig. 3.

The development of phytochemicals towards anti-cancer agents is the strategic use of combination therapies, leveraging synergistic effects that are often achieved between a multitude of phytochemicals or in conjunction with the conventional measures of cancer treatment [64]. The Synergistic effect of these Phytochemicals enhances anticancer effectiveness while reducing toxicity levels and helps combat drug resistance. The combination of phytochemicals with either chemotherapeutic drugs or targeted therapies has demonstrated promising outcomes by improving outcomes with fewer associated side effects during preclinical and clinical studies [65]. The research studies created the concept of pharmacogenomic synergy through their findings, which identify specific phytochemicals that affect essential components of drug metabolism, drug transport, and tumor microenvironmental conditions to enhance the effectiveness of standard cancer treatments. This method establishes the foundation for future advancements in personalized medicine, which will enable doctors to develop customized treatment plans based on their patients’ molecular testing results and other individual characteristics [66].

Another computational pipeline used STITCH database mining with permutation tests to discover that lapatinib and dacomitinib function as established NSCLC inhibitors that served as reference materials during chemical-protein interaction network studies to determine candidate priority [67]. The combination of docking tools (PyRx) and ML-based virtual screening and MD simulations (GROMACS) enables scientists to quickly discover phytochemicals that interfere with glycosylation enzymes (e.g., MGAT5, FUT8) and oncogenic signalling pathways [68]. For example, pictilisib, an identified PIK3 inhibitor, has been utilized as a benchmark compound inglycomics-driven glycoproteomics studies to validate computational predictions targetinginhibiting glycosyltransferases required in unusuallinkages aberrant N-glycan synthesis in lung cancer cells [67]. These reference compounds offer a comparative framework for evaluating the binding-affinity relevance of phytochemicals identified through in-silico approaches.

Furthermore, The ADMET prediction tools SwissADME and pkCSM verify the safety and bioavailability standards of chosen compounds which helps to decrease late-stage product failures [69]. The drug discovery process benefits from these methods because they provide both operational efficiency and help to understand how high-affinity phytochemicals block the KEAP1-NFE2L2 pathway, which protects against immunotherapy-resistant tumors [70]. By bridging ethno-pharmacological knowledge with modern bioinformatics, computational methods offer a cost-effective, high-throughput paradigm to repurpose plant-derived compounds, such as ellagic acid and 14,16-dianhydrogitoxigenin, into precision therapies targeting lung cancer’s genetic and epigenetic vulnerabilities [71]. Collectively, these improvements demonstrate the transformative potential of in silico techniques in overcoming traditional drug development hurdles, paving the door for clinically effective phytochemical-based therapies with minimal off-target effects [72].

Phytochemicals

Even though conventional treatments such as chemotherapy, radiation therapy, surgery, and targeted therapy have made significant improvements in lung cancer, there appears to be a growing trend within the scientific and medicinal communities regarding the use of nature-derived compounds for cancer prevention and treatment [73]. Of all these nature-derived compounds, one particular class of compounds, known as phytochemicals, which come from plants and exhibit biological activity, appears to generate considerable attention within the scientific and medicinal communities due to its diversified biological properties [74] (Table 2). An effective complement to conventional therapies, these molecules suppress the growth of tumors and also trigger apoptosis, suppress metastasis, and offer antioxidant protection.

Induction of apoptosis and cell cycle arrest are major pathways through which phytochemicals display anticancer action [75]. Programmed cell death, or apoptosis, is an essential mechanism that stops unchecked cell proliferation, which is the root cause of cancer [76]. Certain phytochemicals, such as allicin, and apigenin, cause lung cancer cells to undergo apoptosis [77]. Allicin, for instance, has been shown to increase the activity of pro-apoptotic proteins like Bax and inhibit the activity of anti-apoptotic proteins like Bcl-2, resulting in death [78]. By inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest at various stages, phytochemicals can inhibit the growth of cancer. For instance, apigenin has been shown to suppress the cell growth of cancer. For instance, apigenin has been shown to suppress cell growth by causing G2/M phase arrest in lung cancer cells [79] (Table 2).

One of the most significant mechanisms of lung cancer progression is tumor proliferation and metastasis, which can be severely suppressed by phytochemicals [74]. The phytochemicals suppress some of the most critical signal transduction pathways essential for oncogenesis and thereby suppress tumor adaptability and proliferation [37]. Of special interest in this regard is ginger’s 6-shogaol, which has been reported to suppress the Akt survival and proliferation necessary signaling pathway of cancer cells [80]. Murine models of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) showed that 6-shogaol also significantly inhibits the onset of tumors [81]. In addition, andrographolide, another bioactive compound, was seen to suppress tumor adaptability to hypoxia, which is essential for cancer cell survival and metastasis [82]. Andrographolide inhibits tumor growth by intervening in this edition, which also enhances the value of other anticancer therapies [76].

Phytochemicals possess strong antioxidant activities alongside their direct cytotoxic effect on lung cancer cells, which are cardinal in the prevention of cancer of the lungs [83]. The prevalence of free radicals causes oxidative stress, leading to DNA damage and the induction of mutations that may lead to cancer development [84]. By quenching the free radicals and lowering oxidative damage, the risk is minimized [85]. The risk for lung cancer has been associated with the intake of phytochemical-rich food such as cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, kale), legumes, whole grains, nuts, seeds, berries, and citrus fruits [86]. Table 2 Synergistic intake of these foods provides a rich diversity of phytochemicals that complement their anti-cancer property [87]. Although encouraging findings have been reported from preclinical trials, the application of phytochemicals as antitumor agents in lung cancer therapy is premature, with only a few human trials [88]. Laboratory testing, however, has invariably delineated their ability to act as adjuvant therapy to conventional lung cancer therapy [89]. The ability of phytochemicals to cause apoptosis, inhibit tumor growth, and exert antioxidant protection presents them as lead drugs for clinical trials [90]. Their integration with nutritional treatment and therapeutic care enables doctors to develop complete treatment methods that combine traditional medical practices with alternative treatment options for lung cancer patients [84].

It is expected that the role of phytochemicals in lung cancer prevention and treatment will grow stronger as more studies uncover their full potential [91]. We can potentially successfully incorporate these natural chemicals in clinical practice if we continue to explore their molecular mechanism, appropriate dosages, and synergistic effects with existing medications [7]. While traditional drugs remain the mainstay of lung cancer therapy, the application of phytochemicals offers a promising avenue towards improving patient outcomes and decreasing the disease burden worldwide [92]. A phytochemical-based diet and further research into phytochemical-based diet and further research into phytochemical- based drugs may improve the treatment and prevention of lung cancer, ultimately enhancing the prognosis and quality of life for those suffering. Limitations of phytochemicals as drug in lung cancer therapy

Despite the promising anti-cancer effects, the use of phytochemicals in the therapy of lung cancer has certain key challenges in the clinic [145]. A significant portion of phytochemicals is poorly soluble in aqueous media, has low bioavailability, is metabolized quickly, or has low systemic availability, and thus reduces the therapeutic effectiveness in vivo [74]. Also, the use of phytochemicals from plant sources has issues in terms of variability in plant sources, preparation processes, and the level of purity of the compounds, which adds to the difficulties in standardization of the preparation for reproducible results in pre-clinical trials [7]. Moreover, the phytochemicals do not necessarily show specific actions in biological systems, thus having higher tendencies for unwanted biological actions, especially in combination treatments with the commonly used chemotherapeutic drugs [91]. The most significant part of the data on phytochemical-based anti-cancer actions has progressed in pre-clinical trials only and lacks proper, well-planned trials to validate the safety, dosages, and long-term effectiveness of the drugs [92]. Also, the differences in the DNA variability of individuals, variability in the nature of the cancer, as well as variability in the gut microflora’s in the host, could affect the responses to the phytochemical treatments [146].

Conclusion

This review underscores that phytochemicals such as quercetin, curcumin, luteolin, 6-shogaol, thymoquinone, and related compounds exhibit promising anti-lung cancer potential by modulating key oncogenic drivers (EGFR, KRAS, ALK, MET), survival pathways (PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK/ERK, NF-κB, JAK/STAT), and various cancer hallmarks including proliferation, angiogenesis, metastasis, oxidative stress, and immune evasion. By integrating network pharmacology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, ADMET profiling, and machine learning, in silico approaches enable the efficient prioritization of multi-target phytochemicals that interact with various essential molecular targets involved in different pathways including PD-1/PD-L1 immune evasion, RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways required for lung cancer pathogenesis. Current research evidence, which exists mainly from in silico studies, demonstrates that phytochemical- can be utilized as complementary treatments with reduced side effects against lung cancer. Mostly studies on phytochemicals remain in preclinical development because researchers have not yet confirmed them through human clinical trials. Therefore, more clinical research is needed the claims about how phytochemicals help lung cancer patients by decreasing treatment resistance, boosting immunotherapy, and improving their survival and quality of life.

Future prospectives

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have significantly advanced bioinformatics by enabling the analysis of large-scale multi-omics datasets, biomarker discovery, disease classification, and drug-target prediction, thereby accelerating precision medicine and cancer research [147]. Recent applications in lung cancer and genomics have demonstrated the potential of AI-driven approaches to identify molecular signatures, predict therapeutic responses, and support next-generation sequencing data analysis [148]. However, the reliability of these computational models is often limited by challenges associated with publicly available datasets, including small sample sizes, batch effects, missing clinical information, class imbalance, and population-specific biases, which can reduce model generalizability across diverse cohorts [149]. Reproducibility also remains a major concern, as variations in data preprocessing, feature selection methods, and algorithmic architectures can lead to inconsistent outcomes across studies [150]. Furthermore, many advanced AI models operate as “black-box” systems, limiting biological interpretability and clinical trustworthiness, thereby emphasizing the need for explainable AI frameworks [151]. Algorithmic bias represents another critical challenge, as models may inadvertently learn technical, demographic, or sampling biases rather than true biological patterns, potentially affecting prediction accuracy and healthcare equity [152]. Therefore, AI- and ML-based findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating tools that require rigorous validation using independent datasets as well as experimental confirmation through in vitro, in vivo, and clinical investigations before translation into clinical practice [153].

The validation of in-silico results through experimental and clinical methods represents a crucial research path. The translation of computational insights into successful clinical outcomes requires extensive testing through in vitro studies, in vivo studies, and properly conducted human clinical trials. Future studies should validate the predicted multi-target effects of phytochemicals on essential signaling pathways, which include PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK/ERK and immune checkpoints PD-1/PD-L1.

The research study on phytochemical combination therapies with standard chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy shows considerable potential for drug development. The combination treatment methods will enable better treatment results for lung cancer while decreasing treatment side effects and solving the problem of drug resistance. Future research needs to apply precision medicine as a framework to develop phytochemical treatments that match specific genetic and molecular characteristics of individual patients. The clinical application of phytochemicals requires researchers to establish standardized methods for phytochemical source identification, dosage determination, toxicity assessment, and regulatory requirements. The full therapeutic potential of phytochemicals for lung cancer treatment requires scientists to establish pathways that connect computational predictions with clinical applications.
