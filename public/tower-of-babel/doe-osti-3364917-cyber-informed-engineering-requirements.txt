Executive Summary
The increasing digitalization of critical infrastructure introduces new cyber-physical vulnerabilities
that can compromise the safety, reliability, and operational integrity of engineered systems.
Traditional cybersecurity approaches often focus on protecting digital assets through software,
network, or procedural defenses but frequently overlook how engineering design decisions can
inherently reduce or amplify cyber consequences. Cyber-informed engineering (CIE) addresses
this gap by embedding cybersecurity awareness directly into the engineering process,
emphasizing consequence-focused design and system-level resilience. However, the practical
application of CIE principles in real engineering workflows remains underdeveloped, limiting its
impact on infrastructure modernization projects.

This paper aims to operationalize the CIE concept through the application of a structured CIE
requirements framework with two representative use cases: a battery energy storage system
(BESS) and a supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) improvement program within a
public water utility. The objective is to demonstrate how CIE can be integrated into the
requirements development phase of engineering projects to proactively identify, prioritize, and
mitigate cyber-physical risks before system deployment. The approach leverages a structured,
repeatable framework for classifying and prioritizing functional and nonfunctional requirements
based on digital impact, categorized as direct, indirect, or no digital impact. The framework was
applied across each system’s engineering life cycle, including requirement elicitation, scoping and
deconfliction, specification, validation, and management.

The application of the framework yielded several key results. For the BESS use case, CIE
principles guided the identification of high-impact digital pathways, enabling the development of
engineering requirements that reduce dependency on vulnerable digital controls and enhance
system resilience under cyber-compromised conditions. For the SCADA improvement program,
the framework facilitated the identification of CIE-driven design enhancements for instrumentation
and control subsystems such as disinfectant residual analyzers, chemical dosing pumps, and
ultrasonic-level transmitters—focusing on secure control logic, data validation, and consequence
isolation. Across both cases, the results demonstrated systematically integrating CIE during the
requirements phase leads to improved visibility of cyber-physical dependencies, clearer
prioritization of protective design actions, and enhanced alignment between cybersecurity
objectives and operational performance goals.

The findings confirm the CIE requirements framework provides a practical pathway for embedding
cyber resilience into traditional engineering workflows without introducing complexity or
conflicting with performance requirements. By integrating consequence-focused analysis with
structured requirement management, this approach ensures cybersecurity becomes an inherent
aspect of engineering design rather than an external layer of protection. The paper concludes
applying CIE principles early in the system life cycle can materially reduce digital consequence
exposure, strengthen system reliability, and inform future modernization efforts across diverse
critical infrastructure sectors.

1. Introduction
The convergence of operational technology (OT) and information technology (IT) has transformed
how modern critical infrastructures are designed, operated, and secured. As these systems
become increasingly digitalized, their exposure to cyber-enabled disruptions grows, introducing
new vulnerabilities that can lead to real-world physical consequences. Traditional cybersecurity
approaches, which often focus on software or network defenses, are insufficient to address these
challenges at the engineering level. Cyber-informed engineering (CIE) bridges this gap by
embedding cybersecurity awareness and consequence-focused design thinking directly into the
engineering life cycle.

CIE integrates cybersecurity considerations directly into the conception, design, development,
and operation of cyber-physical systems, particularly critical infrastructure such as energy
systems [1]. Its objective is to mitigate or eliminate cyber-enabled attack risks by embedding
cybersecurity—a foundational element in engineering decisions and controls—from the earliest
stages of a system’s life cycle [2]. This approach enables engineers to “engineer out” cyber risks
during the initial design phases, achieving greater security and cost efficiency than adding
protections later.

CIE expands traditional engineering practices to include defense against digital consequences by
equipping engineers to understand and address cyber risks as part of their core responsibilities.
It applies engineering tools and controls to protect critical infrastructure prioritizing the most
severe threats that could lead to physical damage or operational disruption. CIE is founded on a
set of core engineering and cybersecurity principles that promote shared standards, educational
initiatives, and collaboration between engineering and cybersecurity disciplines. By integrating
these principles, CIE enables the design of critical infrastructure systems that are inherently
resilient to cyber threats through holistic and multidisciplinary engineering approaches, moving
beyond reliance on traditional IT-based cybersecurity measures.

To introduce CIE at the earlier stages of the system engineering life cycle, a requirements
framework for CIE was developed: Requirements Framework for Cyber-Informed Engineering. 1
The requirements framework for CIE [3] embeds CIE principles within the traditional engineering
requirements life cycle, ensuring security is treated as a foundational element across system
design, operations, and maintenance. Specifically, the framework adapts the conventional
requirements process to focus on consequence-focused design, the integration of engineering
mitigations, and the alignment of cybersecurity measures with critical system functions.

Rather than creating a new requirements methodology, the CIE framework complements existing
systems engineering processes by embedding CIE considerations early and continuously. It also
emphasizes collaboration among engineers, cybersecurity experts, and other stakeholders to
ensure a holistic and resilient security posture. Through this approach, CIE promotes a proactive,
security-centric mindset that ensures cybersecurity is systematically addressed not as an
afterthought but as an integral component of each phase of system development. Ultimately, this

framework enables organizations to design, specify, and validate resilient systems that maintain
operational trustworthiness and mission assurance in the face of evolving cyber threats.

The key components of the CIE requirements framework are shown in Figure 1 and are as follows:
1) Requirement Elicitation: Focuses on identifying and documenting cybersecurity-related
 needs and concerns along with system requirements from all stakeholders through methods
 such as interviews, document reviews, and operational analysis. This ensures engineering
 protections, digital safeguards, and defensive design features are recognized early in system
 development .
2) Requirement Scoping and Deconfliction: Assesses, categorizes, and prioritizes
 requirements, distinguishing between functional (direct system operations) and nonfunctional
 (e.g., resilience, integrity, culture) cybersecurity requirements. This phase also classifies each
 requirement based on its digital impact—direct, indirect, or no digital impact—to guide the
 appropriate application of CIE principles.
 a) Direct digital impact refers to systems or components that can be directly influenced by
 cyber compromise or digital manipulation, such as the battery management system (BMS)
 in a battery energy storage system (BESS), where digital control directly affects
 operational safety and performance.
 b) Indirect digital impact refers to systems that may be affected through interdependence
 or secondary cyber effects, where a cyber incident in one subsystem indirectly impacts
 another. For example, a cyberattack on the cooling system could cause improper thermal
 regulation, which in turn degrades the overall performance, efficiency, or safety of the
 BESS—even though the compromised subsystem is not the primary control system.
 c) No digital impact includes components or requirements that have no cyber interface or
 digital dependency, such as the physical dimensions or weight of a BESS enclosure.
 This classification helps engineers focus their attention where it matters most by applying CIE-
 driven mitigations proportionally to functions with the highest potential for digital
 consequences while maintaining alignment with system performance, safety, and operational
 objectives.
3) Requirement Specification: Defines requirements with precision, ensuring they are clear,
 measurable, and verifiable. Requirement modeling is used to represent and validate both
 system behaviors and cybersecurity interactions, improving traceability and reducing
 ambiguity in requirement interpretation.
4) Requirement Validation: Conducts formal reviews, prototype evaluations, and model
 checking to confirm all requirements are implementable, testable, and aligned with CIE
 principles. Each requirement is accompanied by a validation plan specifying how the
 corresponding security objectives will be evaluated and verified.
5) Requirement Management: Provides ongoing governance of requirements throughout the
 system life cycle. This includes change tracking, threat reassessment, and continuous
 alignment with cybersecurity and operational resilience goals as system conditions,
 technologies, and threat environments evolve.

Despite significant progress in cybersecurity and industrial control system protection, there
remains a fundamental disconnect between cybersecurity strategies and engineering design
practices. Many system specifications, procurement documents, and modernization projects fail
to account for cyber-physical dependencies and digital consequence pathways during early
design stages. The lack of structured methods to integrate CIE principles into requirements
development contributes to this gap. This paper addresses this shortfall by applying the CIE
Requirements Framework with two distinct yet representative use cases, BESS and a supervisory
control and data acquisition (SCADA) improvement program, to demonstrate how CIE can
systematically enhance system resilience through consequence-focused engineering decisions.

The methodology adopted in this paper leverages the CIE Requirements Framework developed in
prior research, which defines a structured process for eliciting, analyzing, and prioritizing
engineering requirements that account for digital consequences. This framework is applied across
the system engineering life cycle with two real-world contexts. The framework is used to identify
CIE-driven requirements for BESS and SCADA use cases. Each use case follows the steps of
requirement validation of the existing requirements, requirement elicitation for the gaps found in
the validation, scoping and deconfliction, specification, and management—demonstrating how
CIE principles can be operationalized in engineering practice.

The objective of this paper is to demonstrate how the CIE Requirements Framework can be
effectively applied to improve the security and resilience of critical infrastructure systems by
aligning cybersecurity considerations with core engineering processes. Through the application
of CIE to real-world use cases in BESS and SCADA systems, this work illustrates how engineering-
based mitigations can minimize or eliminate digital consequences that lead to operational
disruptions. Ultimately, this paper seeks to show CIE does not replace existing engineering
methodologies but rather enhances them, enabling engineers to systematically integrate security,
resilience, and consequence awareness into every phase of design, implementation, and
operation.

2. BESS Use Case
The BESS use case examined in this study is based on a real-world deployment initiated through
a request for proposals (RFP) process at the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR). The RFP
serves as a formal solicitation mechanism through which a project owner defines technical
specifications, performance criteria, and compliance requirements, inviting qualified vendors or
system integrators to submit proposals. The selected vendor is then responsible for the design,
engineering, procurement, and commissioning of the BESS in accordance with these
specifications.

However, although the RFP process ensures conformance to performance, safety, and
interoperability standards, it traditionally lacks structured guidance for embedding CIE principles
during requirements development. As a result, cybersecurity often remains a secondary
consideration, introduced later in the system life cycle rather than being engineered into the
foundational design. This use case provides a representative scenario to evaluate how the CIE
Requirements Framework can augment existing RFP-based engineering processes to produce a
more secure, resilient, and cyber-aware BESS deployment.

The requirements framework (as shown in Figure 1) starts with requirement elicitation followed by
requirement scoping and deconflicting, requirement specifications, requirement validation, and
then requirement management. Because the RFP specifies all the requirements for BESS, the
following use case will start with a review of these specifications in Section 2.1, corresponding to
the middle box in Figure 1. Section 2.2 will then proceed to validation (moving to the right in Figure
1); once the validation shows the RFP is not well aligned with the CIE principles, the use case will
jump back to the requirement elicitations in Section 2.3 (the leftmost box in Figure 1), followed by
requirement scoping and deconflicting in Section 2.4, followed by requirement specification
(again) in Section 2.5. Once the requirement specifications are aligned with CIE principles, Section
2.6 incorporates those into requirement management (the rightmost box in Figure 1).

2.2. Cyber-Informed Engineering Requirement Validation
The validation of requirements in this use case is conducted through a structured review process
[4]. The primary objective of this review is to evaluate the completeness, clarity, and relevance of
each requirement. Within the context of CIE, the review emphasizes assessing how well the
requirements align with key CIE principles ensuring cybersecurity is systematically integrated into
the engineering process rather than treated as an external overlay [5]. During this process, each
requirement is examined to verify that it accurately reflects security needs and that potential gaps
are identified for remediation [6], [7], [8].

The RFP demonstrates strong coverage of electrical, performance, and safety engineering details,
including specifications for power, dynamic response, environmental tolerance, and fault ride-
through capabilities. However, the RFP is notably weak or silent on many CIE-centric elements—
particularly those that connect design requirements to CIE principles. The following principle-by-
principle evaluation summarizes the key findings from the review.

CONSEQUENCE-FOCUSED DESIGN
The RFP defines functional performance criteria such as power output, ramp rates, response
times, and grid-forming or ride-through capabilities. Although these implicitly address some
operational consequences such as loss of capacity or grid instability, there is no explicit mapping
from high-consequence outcomes (e.g., uncontrolled deep discharge leading to thermal events

                                                                                      Page 11 of 60

or inadvertent islanding with safety implications) to prioritized requirements that mitigate those
risks. Moreover, the RFP does not distinguish or prioritize requirements based on consequence
type (e.g., safety, regulatory, or commercial loss), which limits the ability to ensure high-impact
events receive the strongest engineering safeguards.

ENGINEERED CONTROLS
The RFP includes numerous physical safety features, such as spill protection, fire detection, door
interlocks, and enclosure environmental ratings. Cooling specifications and internal temperature
ranges are also defined. However, explicit nondigital fail-safe mechanisms that operate
independently of the battery management system or power conversion system (PCS) such as
mechanical or analog overcurrent trips, independent thermal cutouts, or mechanical stops for
charging are absent. The RFP allows deep discharge conditions but does not prescribe hardware-
level constraints to prevent misuse if digital control systems are compromised. This absence
weakens physical resilience under degraded digital conditions.

SECURE INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE
The automation section specifies communication protocols (OPC, EtherCAT, Modbus) and two-
way data exchange with NLR at 1-kilohertz bandwidth. However, it lacks network zoning or
segmentation, authentication, encryption, and protocol hardening measures. There are no
constraints on remote vendor access, no requirements for demilitarized zones (DMZs) or jump
hosts, and no specifications defining read-only versus write permissions. In addition, there are no
requirements for secure time synchronization, replay protection, or secure key management. As
written, the RFP defines communication but not communication security.

DESIGN SIMPLIFICATION
The RFP effectively specifies essential energy-to-power and power electronics performance
criteria; however, it does not include requirements to minimize unnecessary complexity in control
or human-machine interface (HMI) systems. There is also no mention of disabling unused services
or interfaces on HMIs and engineering workstations to reduce the attack surface. The omission of
such requirements increases the potential for latent vulnerabilities and operational uncertainty.

LAYERED DEFENSE
Although the RFP defines several physical and safety layers such as fire sensors, spill protection,
and door interlocks, it does not establish multiple independent defensive layers for critical control
functions. For example, there is no explicit requirement for redundant protection across BMS
commands, physical breakers, and PCS watchdog functions. Likewise, no provisions exist for
overlapping monitoring, such as using an independent analog temperature sensor to cross-check
pack overtemperature conditions. The absence of such redundancy creates single points of failure
that could allow undetected faults or malicious commands to propagate.

ACTIVE DEFENSE
The RFP includes alarm systems for fire and gas detection but lacks requirements in operator-
executable active-defense actions that are physical and independent of networked control. These
include a clearly identified manual safe-shutdown pathway that allows operators/technicians to
quickly transition critical functions to a predefined safe condition during suspected abnormal
control behavior. These include verifiable procedures and interfaces that enable human

                                                                                       Page 12 of 60

intervention to contain consequences in real time (e.g., local isolation of specific subsystems,
manual override to conservative setpoints, and confirmation indicators that the system has entered
the intended safe state). Without these, the system’s ability to detect, contain, and investigate cyber
incidents is severely limited.

INTERDEPENDENCY EVALUATION
The RFP provides detailed specifications for subsystems including the converter, BMS, cooling
system, and enclosure and outlines their interfaces to the grid and NLR’s SCADA environment.
However, it does not include explicit mapping of subsystem interdependencies, such as how BMS,
TMS (thermal management system), and PCS interactions might influence power-plant-level
outcomes. Furthermore, there are no requirements for cross-verification among subsystems (e.g.,
requiring the PCS to validate the BMS-reported state of charge for consistency). Without such
mapping, the potential for cascading or unintended consequences across subsystems remains
unaddressed.

DIGITAL ASSET AWARENESS
Although the RFP requests engineering drawings, it does not include a requirement for a digital
asset inventory or configuration baseline. There is no mandate for maintaining an asset register
(hardware, firmware, software versions, and serial numbers), a software bill of materials (SBOM),
or a defined process to track firmware updates and patch states. This gap limits life cycle
cybersecurity management and situational awareness for maintenance and response.

CYBER-SECURE SUPPLY CHAIN CONTROLS
Although the RFP implicitly references manufacturer identification and component labeling
through technical drawings and specifications, it lacks explicit procurement-level cybersecurity
requirements. There are no provisions for firmware signing, vendor vetting, origin assurance,
vulnerability disclosure obligations, or update cadence requirements. The absence of these
measures weakens supply chain integrity and reduces the traceability of components critical to
safe operation.

PLANNED RESILIENCE
The RFP specifies requirements for stand-alone and remote operation, and it defines some
degraded behaviors (e.g., stand-alone mode using the latest stored parameters). However, it omits
explicit degraded modes for suspected cyber compromise such as safe default setpoints, reduced
ramp rates, or limited charge/discharge conditions and lacks a recovery procedure for
compromised firmware. As a result, operational resilience under adversarial or uncertain digital
conditions remains underdefined.

ENGINEERING INFORMATION CONTROL
Although the RFP mandates the provision of manuals and drawings, it lacks controls governing
the distribution of sensitive design information such as network diagrams, firmware, and
programmable logic controller (PLC) logic. There is no requirement for controlled access,
nondisclosure agreements (NDAs), or data classification for engineering artifacts. Unrestricted
information sharing of this nature increases the risk of sensitive design exposure to unauthorized
parties.

ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE
The RFP includes general expectations for operations and maintenance but does not establish
requirements for cybersecurity training, incident response planning, vendor support protocols, or
defined roles and responsibilities during cyber events. Without clear organizational and procedural
integration of cybersecurity, even well-engineered systems may remain operationally vulnerable
[9].

2.3. Cyber-Informed Engineering Requirement Elicitation
The CIE requirement validation process identified that the reviewed specifications were not fully
aligned with CIE principles. Several requirements could be refined or expanded to better reflect
consequence-focused, engineering-driven cybersecurity practices. To address these gaps, a
systematic requirement elicitation process was conducted.

The elicitation process extends beyond validation to actively uncover missing or insufficient
cybersecurity requirements within the RFP specifications. By applying each principle of cyber-
informed engineering, the elicitation identifies gaps, formulates guiding questions, and ensures
engineering, operational, and cybersecurity considerations are aligned throughout the system’s
life cycle. The following subsections describe the principle-by-principle elicitation process used
for the BESS deployment.

CONSEQUENCE-FOCUSED DESIGN
The elicitation process begins by identifying the system’s most critical outcomes [10], [11], [12].
Engineers and system owners are prompted to define high-consequence events by asking, “What
are the unacceptable end states safety—environmental, regulatory, service continuity, or
reputational—if BESS subsystems fail or are manipulated?” Once these end states are
established, each potential failure is mapped to its corresponding consequence through the
prompt, “For each high consequence, what failure modes, both digital and physical, could lead to
it?” To quantify the severity and business impact of these consequences, the discussion extends
to, “What business or mission metrics (e.g., injuries, downtime hours, regulatory fines, lost capacity
in MW) best capture the consequence magnitude?” Supporting data such as incident history,
regulatory limits, operational thresholds (e.g., temperature or state-of-charge limits), and
emergency response procedures are collected to ensure consequence evaluation is grounded in
measurable and operationally relevant terms.

ENGINEERED CONTROLS
The elicitation focuses on reinforcing the physical layer of defense by identifying independent,
hardware-based protections. Design engineers are prompted to consider, “What hardware,
analog, or mechanical protections exist that act independently of BMS or PCS control logic (e.g.,
hardware breakers, thermal cutouts, analog current sensors)?” In addition, the elicitation explores
failure scenarios through questions such as, “How do these devices behave when the controller
is offline or compromised?” and “What are the allowable thermal and current envelopes before
mechanical trips are required?” This line of inquiry ensures physical safeguards retain their
effectiveness even when digital control functions are degraded or unavailable.

                                                                                        Page 14 of 60

SECURE INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE
This elicitation emphasizes the need for robust and clearly defined communication and data flow
architectures. IT and OT architects are asked, “What is the intended zone-and-conduit
architecture, and who will have read/write rights to which data tags?” Procurement teams are
further prompted to clarify, “What are the access rules for vendor remote maintenance, jump
hosts, and external support connections?” From a cybersecurity standpoint, the discussion
centers on “Which communication protocols are required, and what secure variants are supported
(e.g., OPC UA, TLS, or certificate-based authentication)?” These questions aim to ensure system
connectivity is both functional and defensible against unauthorized access or data compromise
[13].

DESIGN SIMPLIFICATION
The elicitation under this principle aims to reduce unnecessary complexity that could introduce
hidden vulnerabilities. System integrators are prompted to evaluate, “What optional services,
ports, or web interfaces are enabled by default on delivered devices?” Operational personnel are
then asked, “Which HMI or diagnostic features are essential for operations versus those that serve
only as convenience functions?” From a security perspective, we consider, “Can any control
device be delivered with a pre-hardened image to minimize configuration exposure?” These
prompts help eliminate digital excess, simplify system configurations, and enhance the overall
resilience of the deployed BESS system.

LAYERED DEFENSE
This elicitation promotes redundancy and independence across protective mechanisms.
Engineering and operations teams are encouraged to document multiple layers of defense by
answering, “For each critical function, what two or more independent controls—physical and
digital—are in place to prevent unacceptable outcomes?” Designers are further asked, “Are
duplicate sensors or relays of different technologies implemented for critical detections?” Through
these structured questions, the process ensures each essential function is protected by multiple,
diverse defense mechanisms that prevent single points of failure from escalating into high-
consequence events

ACTIVE DEFENSE
The elicitation for active defense focuses on ensuring continuous monitoring, detection, and
response readiness. Security and Security Information and Event Management (SIEM)
administrators are asked, “What telemetry and logs must be exported, where are they retained,
and who is responsible for responding to detections?” Vendors are also prompted with, “Can the
BMS or PCS log all setpoint changes, firmware updates, and remote access sessions and export
tamper-evident logs?” This structured inquiry ensures visibility across operational layers and
supports the rapid identification of anomalies or unauthorized activities within the system.

INTERDEPENDENCY EVALUATION
Interdependency evaluation seeks to map and understand how failures can propagate across
subsystems. System engineers are prompted to create interdependency diagrams through
questions such as, “How do BMS, PCS, TMS, cooling, fire suppression, and grid interfaces
interact, and where can a fault propagate between them?” Operations teams are then asked,

                                                                                     Page 15 of 60

“What manual overrides or cross-checks exist across subsystems to prevent cascading failures?”
This approach enables engineers to visualize systemic coupling and ensures protective measures
account for both direct and indirect digital impacts across interconnected systems.

DIGITAL ASSET AWARENESS
This elicitation emphasizes maintaining a complete and up-to-date inventory of digital assets.
Asset managers are prompted with, “Provide a full inventory including device type, model, serial
number, firmware version, network address, and function. Who maintains this inventory, and how
often is it updated?” Procurement teams are asked to supply, “Software Bills of Materials (SBOMs)
and firmware component lists for all digital assets.” These inquiries promote traceability and
visibility across the BESS ecosystem, enabling faster identification of vulnerabilities, version
mismatches, and supply-chain risks.

CYBER-SECURE SUPPLY CHAIN CONTROLS
The elicitation for this principle focuses on securing vendor and component provenance.
Procurement and security teams are asked, “What supply-chain attestations are required for
components and firmware, and are firmware images digitally signed?” Vendors are prompted to
explain, “What is your patch cadence, code-signing process, and vulnerability disclosure policy?”
These questions establish accountability and transparency throughout the supply chain, ensuring
external dependencies do not introduce hidden cyber risks into the operational environment.

PLANNED RESILIENCE
Planned resilience elicitation ensures the system can maintain essential functions under degraded
conditions. Operations personnel are prompted to define, “What degraded operational modes are
acceptable, such as reduced power output or conservative setpoints, and how are they invoked
automatically or manually?” Design engineers are then asked, “Where are golden images and
offline backups stored, and what are the defined recovery time and recovery point objectives?”
These discussions guide the establishment of clear recovery pathways, enabling the system to
sustain operation or quickly recover after a disruption.

ENGINEERING INFORMATION CONTROL
The elicitation under this principle focuses on securing sensitive engineering information and
documentation. Document owners are asked, “Where are wiring diagrams, network schematics,
PLC logic, and configuration files stored, and who has access permissions?” Legal and
compliance teams are prompted to consider, “Are nondisclosure agreements and least-privilege
controls enforced for all external contractors and vendors?” These measures ensure critical
design information is protected against unauthorized access, tampering, or unintentional
exposure, reducing potential attack surfaces.

ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE
The final elicitation principle addresses the human and procedural aspects of cybersecurity
integration. Human resources and operations teams are asked, “What training programs exist for
operators and engineers on OT cybersecurity and incident response, and how frequently are they
updated?” Leadership is then prompted with, “How are cyber roles, responsibilities, and budgets
formally assigned and maintained within the organization?” By establishing structured governance

                                                                                    Page 16 of 60

and regular training, this principle ensures cybersecurity awareness and accountability become
ingrained within the operational culture rather than treated as an afterthought [14].

2.4. Cyber-Informed Engineering Requirement Scoping and Deconflicting
Following the elicitation phase, where foundational system and cybersecurity requirements were
gathered through structured interviews and scenario-based questions, the next step in the
process is requirement scoping and deconflicting. This phase ensures the elicited requirements
are correctly categorized, aligned with digital system impacts, and assessed against the 12 CIE
principles to prioritize engineering efforts where they matter most.

The objective of this phase is twofold:
 • To distinguish between functional and nonfunctional requirements, clarifying which directly
 influence system operation and which contribute to resilience, integrity, or organizational
 culture
 • To evaluate each requirement’s digital impact—whether direct, indirect, or none—so
 engineers can proportionally apply CIE mitigations to those system functions that present
 the highest cyber-physical consequence potential.

By classifying, scoring, and justifying each requirement’s relationship to CIE principles, this phase
bridges raw elicitation data with actionable specifications. It reveals where traditional
cybersecurity measures may be insufficient and where engineered safeguards such as hardware
interlocks, control independence, or system segmentation should be emphasized. The scoring
also helps identify overlaps and conflicts between requirements, enabling harmonized
implementation that supports both safety and secure operability.

 • Table 3 evaluates each requirement against the 12 CIE principles using a quantitative 1–5

 • Table 4 explains the rationale behind each score, illustrating why certain requirements lack
 alignment and where engineering or procedural mitigations are needed.

This structured approach transforms the elicited requirements into a traceable, consequence-
focused framework that sets the stage for formal specification and refinement in the next phase.

2.6. Cyber-Informed Engineering Requirement Management
Cyber-informed engineering requirement management will serve as the central mechanism for
maintaining alignment between engineering, cybersecurity, and operational safety throughout the
project life cycle. Each specification—particularly those refined through the CIE framework—will
be managed in a requirement management repository that links every item to the corresponding
CIE principle. As operational data and validation results from testing (FAT/SAT) become available,
these requirements will be updated to reflect real-world performance and identified gaps.

For example, if validation testing reveals that thermal management interlocks do not adequately
respond under degraded network conditions, the associated engineered control requirement will
be revised to enforce independent analog fail-safes. Similarly, when communication testing shows
latency or protocol weaknesses, secure information architecture requirements will be updated to
specify hardened communication pathways and protocol enforcement. The requirement
management process will use structured traceability to ensure every change such as updating
vendor firmware signing procedures or modifying the BMS-to-PCS data exchange frequency is
explicitly linked to both CIE mitigation (e.g., physical consequence containment, function isolation,
redundancy) and the supporting cybersecurity mechanism (e.g., authentication, telemetry
validation).

This CIE-driven management approach ensures updates are not made solely for compliance or
security hardening but to directly reduce digital pathways to high-consequence events identified
during consequence mapping. By continuously aligning requirements to field performance,
vendor updates, and operational lessons learned, the CIE requirement management process
maintains a living specification that evolves to support safe, resilient, and cyber-informed BESS
operation throughout its deployment life cycle.

3. SCADA Improvement Use Case
The SCADA improvement use case represents a real-world modernization effort that upgrades
operational technology across multiple water system facilities of a public water utility to improve
reliability, automation, maintainability, and control of access to critical systems. As part of the

This use case is intentionally scoped to the contractor-facing technical specifications used to
execute field implementation. The design documents and construction drawings referenced in this
report are treated as representative examples of contractor deliverables and are not attributed to
a specific utility, project, or site. In practice, contractor drawings and specifications are only one
component of a broader modernization program. Parallel engineering deliverables define system
architecture, SCADA standards, network zoning, control logic, and acceptance criteria that remain
the responsibility of the Owner and Engineer. The CIE requirements framework is applied here to
strengthen contractor-facing requirements that materially influence cyber-physical exposure while
making explicit which decisions and risks cannot be delegated through procurement language.
This framing is deliberate—responsibilities should be assigned intentionally, information sharing
should be controlled to the minimum necessary for correct installation and verification, and
although tasks may be delegated, operational risk ultimately remains with the owner.

3.2. Cyber-Informed Engineering Requirement Validation
The validation of requirements in this use case is conducted through a structured review process.
The primary objective of this review is to evaluate the completeness, clarity, and relevance of each
requirement. Within the context of CIE, the review emphasizes assessing how well the
requirements align with key CIE principles, ensuring cybersecurity is systematically integrated into
the engineering process rather than treated as an external overlay. During this process, each

                                                                                       Page 34 of 60

requirement is examined to verify that it accurately reflects security needs and that potential gaps
are identified for remediation.

The reviewed contractor-facing technical specifications provide a robust engineering foundation
in electrical, mechanical, and installation workmanship that aligns well with conventional SCADA
engineering best practices. Most CIE elements that are information centric or systems level (e.g.,
consequence mapping and prioritization [18], secure information architecture constraints
[segmentation, authentication, encryption, DMZ/jump host controls], digital asset registers and
SBOM requirements, and contractual supply chain and engineering information controls) are
intentionally not expressed in these contractor-facing drawings and specifications because they
are controlled by the Owner/Engineer and implemented by utility staff through separate processes
and controlled artifacts. One exception visible in this design package is that some critical function
assurance features and engineered controls are explicitly specified, which provides a meaningful
basis for CIE evaluation at the physical and control-integration layer. The following principle-by-
principle evaluation summarizes the key findings from the review.

CONSEQUENCE-FOCUSED DESIGN
The reviewed specifications establish a strong electrical and mechanical baseline such as UL-
508A panels, short-circuit current ratings (SSCRs), conduit and grounding practices, and detailed
wiring preservation that implicitly support safe operation and equipment survivability. However,
the specification set does not require an explicit consequence mapping that ties these engineering
items to prioritized, stakeholder-defined unacceptable end states (safety, environmental, service
continuity, regulatory). Consequence-focused design analysis and prioritization are typically
performed during the Owner/Engineer design process and guided by client risk-management
decisions; as a result, the associated consequence documentation is generally not carried in
contractor-facing bid drawings and specifications. Accordingly, the drawings and specifications
do not provide a consequence matrix or explicit traceability from high-consequence outcomes to
specific engineered controls and acceptance tests, which limits the ability of this contractor-facing
package (on its own) to demonstrate that protections are prioritized by consequence severity.

ENGINEERED CONTROLS
The reviewed specifications include many important physical and electrical protections such as
mechanical spill containment for chemical skids, door interlocks, enclosure NEMA ratings,
specified liquid-tight flexible metal conduit limits, and explicit bonding/grounding practices—all of
which contribute to baseline robustness. Independent, nondigital fail-safes that remain effective if
PLC/remote terminal unit (RTU) logic, panel firmware, or networked control are unavailable or
compromised are implemented within the contractor package primarily through the design
drawings and associated control details, consistent with the convention that drawings and
specifications are interdependent and avoid duplicative statements. Accordingly, interposing
relays, redundant DC modules, and other engineered protection elements are treated as “where
shown” design features rather than uniformly restated as stand-alone specification clauses. From
a CIE validation perspective, the residual gap is less the absence of engineered controls in the
design package and more the lack of an explicit, consequence-traceable statement of intent and
coverage (e.g., which critical functions are protected by independent nondigital safeguards, under
which degraded conditions, and how that coverage will be verified during acceptance testing).

                                                                                       Page 35 of 60

SECURE INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE
Physically, the drawings and port/ducting standards (dedicated conduits, separated power vs.
communications, labeled ports) are well-constructed to enable secure zoning. That said, the
specification stops at the physical layer and delegates logical network architecture to the Owner
and Engineer; it does not require in-scope architectural constraints such as mandatory
segmentation, defined zone/conduit mapping, minimum authentication or encryption standards
for field protocols, DMZ/jump host controls for remote vendor access, or requirements for secure
time synchronization and replay protection. Because PLC programming and network configuration
are outside the contractor’s deliverables, the specification would benefit from bounding
statements that ensure 1) the physical topology does not preclude a secure logical architecture
and 2) explicit handoff requirements (e.g., protected port labeling, demarcation points) are
enforced.

DESIGN SIMPLIFICATION
The reviewed deliverables emphasize maintainability and correctness (preservation of continuous
runs, two-tier terminal blocks for transitions, spare conduits and pull cords), which supports clarity.
However, there is no explicit requirement to minimize unnecessary digital services or optional
interfaces on delivered HMIs, panel PCs, or engineering workstations. The BOM and Owner-
furnished equipment provisions increase functional capability but also risk introducing unneeded
attack surface in the absence of controls to disable or harden unused services. A CIE-oriented
specification would require delivery of hardened images or a documented minimal feature set for
panel PCs and a verification step that nonessential services are disabled.

LAYERED DEFENSES
The reviewed specifications provide multiple defensive elements across physical, electrical, and
mechanical domains such as redundant DC supplies, surge suppression, rated enclosures,
interlocks, and fire/gas alarm interfaces, forming a credible layered posture. Nonetheless, the
specification does not require diverse, overlapping sensing or control technologies that would
materially improve resilience, such as independent analog temperature sensing to cross-verify
digital measurements or an analog watchdog that can isolate charging if discrepancies occur. The
lack of mandated sensing diversity and explicit cross-subsystem veto logic leaves single points of
failure where a single compromised control chain could produce unsafe outcomes.

ACTIVE DEFENSE
The technical documents require standard commissioning artifacts (CAT6/fiber test reports,
FAT/SAT support, I/O termination lists) and provide space for Owner-installed logging devices, but
the contractor-facing requirements do not explicitly define operator-executable response actions
(e.g., local safe-shutdown, manual isolation of specific control zones, or switching to predefined
conservative local control modes), nor do they require clear indications/feedback that these
actions have successfully driven equipment to the intended safe state. Active defense includes
physical and operational mechanisms for rapid consequence containment during abnormal or
suspicious system behavior, independent of network visibility or cyber tooling. As a result, the
installed system may lack the telemetry and auditability needed to detect, investigate, and contain
cyber-physical incidents without additional Owner engineering.

                                                                                         Page 36 of 60

INTER-DEPENDENCY EVALUATION
The drawings and wiring schedules clearly document the physical interconnections among PLCs,
engine panels, chemical skids, analyzers, and field instruments, which is a necessary foundation
for interdependency analysis. However, the specification does not require a formal
interdependency deliverable such as a system interaction diagram or fault-propagation analysis,
nor does it require cross-verification tests that validate how failure in one subsystem (e.g., engine
panel or analyzer) affects downstream logic and safety functions. Without contractual
interdependency mapping and acceptance tests that exercise propagation scenarios, cascading
failures or unintended emergent behaviors remain a significant residual concern.

DIGITAL ASSET AWARENESS
The specification requires nameplates, as-built drawings, and panel I/O lists, which support
manual asset identification and maintenance. However, a requirement for a living, machine-
readable digital asset register that records device identities, firmware versions, serial numbers,
MAC/IP addresses, and responsible contacts or a software bill of materials for embedded
components is missing. The absence of these artifacts reduces the project’s ability to rapidly
assess exposed assets during maintenance, vulnerability disclosure, or incident response and
places additional burden on the Owner to construct asset visibility post-installation.

CYBER-SECURE SUPPLY CHAIN CONTROLS
The BOM and part callouts clarify responsibilities and component origins but stop short of
procurement-level cybersecurity clauses. Notably, several key networked components are Owner-
furnished specifically to control access, configuration authority, and chain of custody, which
partially mitigates supply chain exposure for those elements. There are limited explicit
requirements within the contractor-facing package for signed firmware, vendor security
attestations, vulnerability disclosure, and patch service-level agreements or provenance
documentation for critical networked modules. Given that several delivered panels, embedded
modules, and field-connected devices include firmware and embedded components, the lack of
supply chain security provisions remains a gap in ensuring the long-term trustworthiness and
maintainability of supplied equipment—particularly for contractor-supplied subsystems and any
embedded firmware outside the Owner-furnished boundary.

PLANNED RESILIENCE
The specification sensibly requires temporary power, preservation of hardwired logic for critical
analyzers, and as-built documentation elements that support recovery and operational continuity.
It does not, however, define engineered degraded modes and validated recovery procedures for
suspected digital compromise (for example, safe default limits, constrained operational envelopes,
or procedures to restore known good firmware images) within the contractor-facing
drawing/specification package. These resilience behaviors are typically engineered and governed
by the Engineer and Owner and may therefore be deliberately excluded from bid documents and
contractor deliverables. In addition, there is no explicit requirement in the contractor package for
golden images, offline configuration backups, or contractual recovery time objective/recovery
point objective targets. As a result, from the perspective of applying the CIE requirements
framework to the contractor-visible specifications, recovery expectations cannot be verified or
traced to testable acceptance criteria without supplemental Owner/Engineer requirements.

                                                                                       Page 37 of 60

ENGINEERING INFORMATION CONTROL
The specification mandates delivery of detailed engineering artifacts (drawings, I/O lists, wiring
diagrams), but it does not impose controls on their storage, distribution, or integrity within the
contractor-facing scope. This absence should not be interpreted as a gap in the overall program;
rather, it may reflect engineering information control being applied by the Owner/Engineer through
controlled release of design information and by managing sensitive repositories, access approvals,
and disclosure constraints outside the contractor’s scope of work. There are no requirements in
the contractor package for secure document repositories, role-based access control to sensitive
schematics, NDAs for external contractors, or integrity protections (e.g., signed deliverables).
Consequently, when applying the CIE requirements framework strictly to the contractor-visible
specifications, information-control mechanisms are not directly verifiable or traceable to
acceptance criteria without supplemental Owner/Engineer governance artifacts.

ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE
The project documentation specifies turnover, FAT/SAT support, and basic training expectations;
however, it does not require cybersecurity-specific curricula for operators or engineering teams,
nor does it define roles and responsibilities for incident detection, escalation, or recovery within
the contractor-facing technical specification set. This absence should not be interpreted as a lack
of organizational requirements overall; rather, these organizational measures are typically
developed and governed by the Owner and Engineer and can be embedded in internal operating
procedures, training plans, and incident-response playbooks that are intentionally excluded from
bid documents. Consistent with that model, the design and construction process may itself
function as a continuous improvement mechanism, where lessons learned during design
development, installation, and acceptance testing feed back into updated internal processes,
requirements, and training. As a result, when the CIE framework is applied strictly to contractor-
visible documents, organizational culture controls are not directly auditable without referencing
the Owner’s internal governance artifacts.

3.3. Cyber-Informed Engineering Requirement Elicitation
The requirement elicitation process builds directly upon the gaps identified during the CIE
requirement validation of the SCADA technical specifications. Although the validation revealed
that several engineering and control-system elements were not fully aligned with CIE principles—
particularly in consequence definition, elimination of high-risk pathways, and integration of
cybersecurity into engineering decisions—the elicitation phase expands the analysis by
systematically generating the missing requirements. This transition from validation to elicitation
ensures the SCADA design evolves from an implicit set of operational assumptions to an explicit
set of consequence-focused engineering requirements while recognizing that many intent-level,
risk management, and governance artifacts are typically developed and managed by the Owner
and Engineer and are intentionally not carried in contract bid documents. By applying each CIE
principle to the specific contexts of specifications—including PLC upgrades, communication
backhaul, reservoir and pump controls, HMI/SCADA architecture, and integration with the
enterprise OT environment—the elicitation provides a structured foundation for embedding
cybersecurity into engineering decisions throughout the life cycle of the project. The following

                                                                                      Page 38 of 60

subsections describe the principle-by-principle elicitation process used for the SCADA
deployment.

CONSEQUENCE-FOCUSED DESIGN
The elicitation process begins by identifying the SCADA system’s most critical outcomes.
Engineers, system owners, operators, and safety leads are prompted to define high-consequence
events by asking, “What are the unacceptable end states with respect to safety, environmental
release, regulatory noncompliance, service interruption (potable water outage), or severe
reputational impact if key SCADA controlled subsystems fail or are manipulated?” Once these end
states are established, each potential failure is mapped to its corresponding consequence through
the prompt, “For each unacceptable end state, what failure modes (both digital [for example, PLC
logic tampering, spoofed telemetry, and remote command injection] and physical [for example,
valve failure or pump mechanical fault]) could lead to it?” To quantify consequence magnitude,
elicitors ask, “What operational or business metrics (e.g., interruption hours, volume of untreated
water discharged, regulatory exceedance levels, number of affected customers) best capture the
impact?” Supporting artifacts to collect include incident history logs, worst-case scenario
calculations, regulatory thresholds for water quality and chemical dosing, and existing standard
operating procedures for emergency response. Because this consequence mapping is typically a
design and risk management activity rather than a contractor deliverable, the elicitation explicitly
captures it as an Owner and Engineer controlled artifact that can be selectively referenced in
contractor-facing requirements only where needed to enforce critical function assurance.

ENGINEERED CONTROLS
The elicitation process focuses on identifying hardware and analog protections that operate
independently of PLCs and SCADA to prevent or limit unacceptable consequences. Design and
maintenance teams are asked, “What hardware, analog, or mechanical protections exist that act
independently of PLC/SCADA control logic (for example, local mechanical pump shutoffs,
hardwired disinfectant residual dosing limiters, analog float switches, or independent thermal
cutouts)?” and “How do these devices behave when PLCs or SCADA are offline, unreachable, or
demonstrably compromised?” To validate these answers, gather the relevant drawings details that
specify interlocks, hardwired logic preservation, and field-level protective behaviors, along with
equipment datasheets and acceptance test procedures. The elicitation outcome is an explicit
inventory of independent engineered controls and the associated acceptance tests demonstrating
that critical functions remain bounded even when networked control is degraded.

SECURE INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE
The elicitation identifies how the physical conduit and port topology in drawings must map to a
secure logical architecture. Network architects and OT/IT stakeholders are asked, “What is the
intended zone and conduit architecture, and which protocols and encryption/authentication
schemes are allowed across each conduit?” and “Who will have read versus write privileges to
tags and control points, and where are the Owner/Contractor demarcations for network handoff?”
Supporting artifacts to collect include port standards, conduit and port labeling conventions,
internal zoning policies, and remote access governance that may be maintained outside contractor
documents. The elicitation produces a zoning and protocol baseline that is Owner and Engineer

                                                                                      Page 39 of 60

controlled, plus a constrained set of contractor-facing installation and labeling requirements that
preserve the ability to implement that architecture during commissioning.

DESIGN SIMPLIFICATION
The elicitation targets reduction of unnecessary features that would otherwise increase attack
surface on panel PCs, radios, and HMIs. Integrators and operations are asked, “Which services
and interfaces are operationally essential versus convenience, and which default services, ports,
or accounts can be disabled or removed on delivered devices?” and “Can control devices be
delivered with a minimal feature baseline that is testable at acceptance?” Evidence to collect
includes vendor default configuration lists, device hardening guidance, and the BOM assignments
that distinguish Owner-furnished versus contractor-installed components. The elicitation outcome
is a minimal feature baseline and acceptance verification approach that is compatible with the
project’s division of responsibilities, so the contractor supports physical provisioning while the
Owner and Engineer enforce configuration intent where they retain programming and network
control.

LAYERED DEFENSE
The elicitation ensures critical SCADA functions have independent, overlapping protections.
System engineers and operations staff are prompted, “For each critical function, such as remote
pump start stop, emergency chemical dosing shutdown, or high-level protection, what at least two
independent controls from different domains exist, for example, mechanical, analog sensing, or
independent cutout, and what still prevents the unacceptable consequence if one control fails?”
To substantiate responses, collect the relevant interlock schematics, hardwired logic paths, and
field device specifications referenced by drawings. The elicitation outcome is a layered defense
specification with acceptance tests demonstrating that diverse controls remain effective when any
single layer is disabled, failed, or bypassed.

ACTIVE DEFENSE
The elicitation establishes what telemetry and logging are required for detection, correlation, and
investigation of anomalous events. Security operations, SCADA engineers, and vendor
representatives are asked, “Which events must be logged at the device or system level, such as
logic downloads, firmware updates, setpoint changes, and remote sessions, what minimum time
stamping and integrity properties are required, and where should log aggregation occur?”
Supporting material includes sample logs from representative devices, historian and alarm tag
lists, and the Owner’s retention and monitoring policies. The elicitation produces controlled
logging and telemetry requirements that are typically implemented by Owner and Engineer
configuration activities while specifying any contractor support needs such as panel space, wiring
provisions, and demarcation points for secure log export hardware.

INTERDEPENDENCY EVALUATION
The elicitation converts the physical and wiring topology into an operational interdependency
model to reveal propagation channels. System engineers and operators are asked, “Provide an
interdependency diagram showing how PLC programs, engine control panels, chemical dosing
skids, ultrasonic-level sensors, and communications interact, and identify where faults or malicious
actions could propagate across those boundaries.” In practice, this evaluation is typically
performed by the Engineer as an intrinsic part of the design process, with the Owner defining

                                                                                      Page 40 of 60

operational requirements and providing feedback on system-level impacts across local and
distributed operations. Supporting data include I/O lists, junction box entry rules, and wiring
dependencies shown in drawings, supplemented by Owner and Engineer knowledge of control
logic and operational modes that may not be present in contractor-facing drawings. The elicitation
therefore focuses on making the interdependency evaluation explicit and traceable as a design
assurance artifact and on capturing the Owner’s operational assumptions and acceptance criteria
for managing propagation and cascading effects. The deliverable is an interdependency diagram
and fault propagation assessment with test scenarios that can be exercised during acceptance
testing to verify containment and safe behavior across subsystem boundaries.

DIGITAL ASSET AWARENESS
The elicitation ensures a machine-readable, operational asset register is produced for the SCADA
landscape. Asset managers and procurement are asked to “Provide a complete inventory (device,
model, serial, firmware version, network address or demarcation, physical location, and function)
and specify who will maintain and update this registry and at what cadence” and “Where feasible,
provide an SBOM or firmware provenance statement for embedded software in PLCs, radios, and
panel PCs.” Supporting materials include the panel nameplate schedules and Owner-furnished
equipment lists. The elicitation outcome is a living asset register and provenance package,
recognizing that the contractor may supply identification data whereas the Owner governs the
authoritative baseline after turnover.

CYBER-SECURE SUPPLY CHAIN CONTROLS
The elicitation establishes how supply chain risk is managed in a way that matches the project’s
responsibility split. Procurement, engineering, and vendors are asked, “Which components are
Owner furnished specifically to control chain of custody, and for vendor-supplied components
what provenance, secure update practices, and vulnerability disclosure obligations are required?”
Evidence to collect includes vendor security documentation, firmware signing capabilities, and
support life cycle commitments. The elicitation outcome is a set of procurement clauses and
acceptance criteria focused on consequence containment and maintainable trust while
accounting for the fact that key networked elements may already be controlled through Owner
furnishing rather than contractor procurement.

PLANNED RESILIENCE
The elicitation defines engineered degraded modes and recovery expectations appropriate for a
water utility SCADA environment while acknowledging that these are often developed by the
Engineer and Owner staff and excluded from contractor bid documents. Operations and control
engineers are prompted, “What degraded operating envelopes are acceptable and how are they
invoked, and what manual local control capabilities must remain available when supervisory
control is impaired?” and “What restoration process exists for known good configurations,
including backups and recovery objectives?” Supporting evidence includes contingency
procedures, acceptance test plans, and configuration management practices maintained by the
Owner. The elicitation outcome is a resilience and recovery package that can be selectively
translated into contractor-facing provisions where physical installation must support recovery,
such as demarcation, labeling, and access to panels and field devices.

The elicitation clarifies how sensitive engineering artifacts are controlled across the life cycle,
recognizing that these controls are typically governed outside the contractor’s scope and that the
absence of such controls in bid documents can be an intentional application of engineering
information control. Document owners, legal teams, and configuration managers are asked,
“Where will wiring/network schematics, PLC logic, and configuration files be stored, who may

controlled repositories, access governance, and turnover packaging while limiting contractor-
facing disclosure only to what is necessary to correctly install and verify the physical system.

ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE
The elicitation aligns people and processes to the engineered system but treats these measures
as Owner and Engineer governance artifacts rather than contractor deliverables. Operations
leadership and engineering managers are asked, “What training, role definitions, and escalation
paths are required to operate and maintain the upgraded SCADA assets safely, including how to
respond to abnormal control behavior or suspected compromise?” and “How are lessons learned
from design development and acceptance testing fed back into updated procedures and
training?” The elicitation outcome is a set of internal training and operational readiness artifacts,
such as role assignments and exercise objectives, that can be validated through Owner-managed
processes without expanding contractor-facing documents beyond their intended scope.

3.4. Cyber-Informed Engineering Requirement Scoping and Deconflicting
Following the elicitation phase, where foundational system and cybersecurity requirements were
gathered through structured interviews and scenario-based questions, the next step in the
process is requirement scoping and deconflicting. This phase ensures the elicited requirements
are correctly categorized, aligned with digital system impacts, and assessed against the 12 CIE
principles to prioritize engineering efforts where they matter most.

The objective of this phase is twofold:
 • To distinguish between functional and nonfunctional requirements to clarify which directly
 influence system operation and which contribute to resilience, integrity, or organizational
 culture.
 • To evaluate each requirement’s digital impact—whether direct, indirect, or none—so
 engineers can proportionally apply CIE mitigations to those system functions that present
 the highest cyber-physical consequence potential.

By classifying, scoring, and justifying each requirement’s relationship to CIE principles, this phase
bridges raw elicitation data with actionable specifications. It reveals where traditional
cybersecurity measures may be insufficient and where engineered safeguards such as hardware
interlocks, control independence, or system segmentation should be emphasized. The scoring
also helps identify overlaps and conflicts between requirements, enabling harmonized
implementation that supports both safety and secure operability.

The purpose of this stage is to translate the analytical outcomes of the CIE scoping, rating, and

implements the physical and installation controls and delivers objective evidence whereas the

3.6. Cyber-Informed Engineering Requirement Management
Cyber-informed engineering requirement management will serve as the central mechanism for
maintaining alignment between engineering, cybersecurity, and operational safety throughout the
SCADA modernization life cycle associated with the contractor-facing specification set. Each
specification, particularly those refined or added through the CIE framework, will be managed in
a structured requirements repository that links every requirement to its associated CIE principle,
applicable subsystem (panel wiring, instrumentation, networks, controls), and expected
consequence mitigations. As operational evidence accumulates during panel fabrication, field
installation, network turn-up, and FAT/SAT validation, these requirements will be iteratively
updated to reflect real-world system behavior and newly identified digital-physical interactions
while preserving the boundary that configuration, policy, and operational risk decisions remain
Owner and Engineer responsibilities.

For example, if SAT testing reveals that pump or well-control interlocks do not maintain safe-state
behavior under degraded telemetry or SCADA polling delays, the associated engineered-control
requirement will be revised to strengthen hardware independent protections and local defaults
that are implementable within the contractor deliverables, rather than relying solely on remote
supervisory commands. Similarly, if communication testing identifies issues such as jitter-sensitive
protocols, excessive retries on cellular backhaul, or unexpected broadcast traffic across process
networks, secure information architecture requirements will be updated to tighten physical
demarcations, port labeling, and handoff constraints so the Owner and Engineer can enforce
deterministic pathways, protocol whitelisting, and zoning during configuration. The requirement
management process will use strict traceability so every update—such as modifying panel
intrusion switch logging behavior, adding tamper-evident wiring documentation, refining fiber
OTDR deliverables, or adjusting PLC-to-instrument signal validation—is explicitly tied to both a
CIE mitigation and the supporting cybersecurity mechanism, with clear attribution of who
implements each change (contractor versus Owner and Engineer).

This CIE-driven management approach ensures updates are not introduced merely as generic
security hardening but are directly targeted to reduce digital pathways to high-consequence
events identified in Owner and Engineer consequence mapping and risk decisions such as loss
of chemical dosing control, uncontrolled pump operation, reservoir overfill, or failure of critical
instrumentation. By continuously aligning requirements with field performance, vendor component
updates, engineering discoveries, and site-specific operational lessons, the CIE requirement

                                                                                      Page 58 of 60

management process maintains a living, technically grounded specification that evolves to support
safe, resilient, and cyber-informed SCADA deployment across the entire program life cycle.

4. Conclusion
This report demonstrates the practical application of the CIE Requirements Framework to two
representative use cases—a BESS and a SCADA improvement program—to illustrate how
engineering processes can be intentionally structured to reduce cyber-physical risks. By
integrating cybersecurity considerations directly into the requirements phase, the framework
transforms cybersecurity from a reactive layer of protection into a proactive element of system
design. The structured classification of requirements by functional type and digital impact (direct,
indirect, or no digital impact) enables a disciplined prioritization of engineering controls where
digital compromise could have the most severe operational consequences.

The BESS use case highlights how CIE principles can identify high-consequence digital
dependencies leading to engineering design decisions that minimize unsafe failure modes and
maintain operational integrity under degraded or compromised conditions. Similarly, the SCADA
improvement program demonstrates the value of CIE-driven analysis in guiding refinements such
as improved isolation of digital pathways, validated signal processing, and engineered fail-safe
configurations. These results confirm applying the CIE Requirements Framework produces
tangible engineering benefits, including reduced attack surface, improved consequence visibility,
and stronger alignment between cyber resilience and operational performance.

Overall, the findings affirm CIE provides a repeatable, scalable methodology for embedding
cybersecurity awareness into the engineering life cycle of critical infrastructure. By treating cyber
risk as an engineering design parameter, organizations can systematically “engineer out”
vulnerabilities before systems are built, tested, and deployed—resulting in more resilient and
secure operations. The work further reinforces the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration
between engineers, operators, and cybersecurity professionals to ensure CIE principles are not
only conceptually understood but practically implemented.
