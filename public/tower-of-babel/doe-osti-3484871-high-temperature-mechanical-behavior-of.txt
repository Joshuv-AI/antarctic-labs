Abstract
This report is a Milestone 3 deliverable in FY2026, under work package MT-26AN090211 to
support research and qualification activities supported by the Advanced Materials and
Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program. It provides an update on the work done on the
laser powder bed fusion based additive manufacturing of steels for nuclear applications, conducted
by Argonne National Laboratory. Building upon previous years’ efforts, the focus of FY26 includes
continued optimization of process parameters for the alloys: A709, G91 & G92. The work package
also includes fabricating test samples to conduct a thorough microstructural analysis and perform
preliminary mechanical testing. The major accomplishments of this work package are summarized
below.
 • Process Optimization and Sample Fabrication: Leveraging optimized process
 parameters developed in FY2024, larger material blocks of A709 alloys were successfully
 fabricated for creep samples. Optimization was complete to determine the sensitivity of
 process parameters on ferrite/martensite ratio and subsequent effect on mechanical
 performance of G91/G92.
 • A709 Austenitic Stainless-Steel Investigation: Creep testing of both SSJ3 and ASTM-
 size specimens showed that solution-annealed (SA) material exhibited shorter creep
 rupture lives than the as-deposited (AD) and precipitation-treated (PT) conditions.
 Fractographic examination identified preferential crack initiation along cellular boundaries
 enriched in Cr and, for ASTM-size specimens, Cr, Mo, and C. Ion-irradiation experiments
 demonstrated that the AD condition exhibited greater resistance to irradiation-induced void
 formation than the SA and PT conditions. Furthermore, all investigated LPBF A709
 conditions showed improved resistance to void swelling relative to LPBF 316H. Under
 simultaneous irradiation and constant mechanical loading, void formation increased
 substantially, with void density exhibiting an exponential dependence on applied load,
 demonstrating a strong interaction between stress and irradiation-induced defect evolution.
 Complementary Thermo-Calc®-based CALPHAD calculations were used to explore
 compositional strategies for improving phase stability. The simulations indicate that alloy
 chemistry can be optimized to minimize undesirable σ-phase precipitation while
 controlling the volume fraction of M23C6 carbides. Collectively, these results demonstrate

 1

 that processing condition, applied stress, irradiation, and alloy chemistry strongly influence
 the mechanical and microstructural stability of LPBF A709.
 • G92 Ferritic/Martensitic Steel Investigation: Microstructural evolution and mechanical
 behavior of additively manufactured G91 and G92 ferritic–martensitic steels was
 compared. In the as-deposited condition, both alloys exhibited comparable microstructures,
 with no significant differences observed at the investigated length scales. Following heat
 treatment, precipitation became more pronounced in both materials; however, G92
 exhibited a greater extent of carbide precipitation and the formation of Laves phase
 compared with G91. These differences highlight the influence of alloy chemistry on phase
 evolution during post-deposition heat treatment. Mechanical testing further revealed clear
 differences between the two alloys. G92 consistently exhibited significantly higher strength
 than G91 across the investigated temperature range and processing conditions. Despite this
 increase in strength, G92 retained ductility comparable to G91, with only a relatively small
 reduction in elongation. Overall, the results indicate that G92 provides an advantageous
 combination of enhanced strength and retained ductility, while its greater tendency for
 secondary-phase precipitation may influence long-term microstructural stability.

 Chapter 1: Introduction
Additive Manufacturing is a process of fabricating objects via a layer-by-layer deposition from a
3d geometry model into complex near net shape components, thus reducing the need for post
process machining, welding, and brazing[1]. Recently, there has been a push to explore the
feasibility of using additive manufacturing techniques for the fabrication of components in energy
industries. Of particular interest for the current project, based on the AMMT roadmap, is the
incorporation of current reactor materials into advanced manufacturing workflows for the
fabrication, qualification, and eventual deployment of nuclear reactor components[2]. These alloys
are the austenitic stainless steel: A709, and ferritic/martensitic steels: Grade 91 and Grade 92
(henceforth referred to as G91 & G92). Through the DOE-NE Advanced Reactor Technology
(ART) down-selection process in prior years, Alloy 709 (A709) has been designated for sodium-
cooled fast reactor (SFR) applications because of its superior high-temperature performance
compared to that of conventional 316H stainless steel[3]. Grade 92 (G-92) steel represents a third-
generation creep-resistant ferritic/martensitic (F/M) steel with tungsten and boron additions that
enhance performance beyond that of Grade 91 (G-91) steel. Despite the potential for superior creep
resistance at temperatures exceeding 600°C, G92 remains unqualified under nuclear codes with
much less documented AM research[4]. As part of the work, to understand the feasibility of using
advanced manufacturing techniques, these three alloys were manufactured using Laser Powder
Bed Fusion (LPBF) Argonne National Laboratory.
The current M3 report summarizes the initial findings of high temperature mechanical behavior
including tensile and creep properties of these three alloys (in as deposited as well as heat treated
conditions) and provides a comparison of the microstructure and mechanical properties of the
alloys. Further, we also report on the ion-irradiation behavior of alloy A709. Finally, CALPHAD
based insights into the microstructural evolution of all three alloys are also reported.
Chapter 2 details the target alloys evaluated in this study, while Chapter 3 outlines the experimental
methods used for creep testing, irradiation testing, and CALPHAD software modeling. Chapter 4
presents findings for laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) processed alloys A709, G91, and G92. The
final chapters summarize key conclusions, discuss implications for high-temperature performance,
and offer recommendations for future research and process optimization.

 Chapter 2: Literature Review

2.1 Austenitic Stainless Steels

2.1.1 A709(NF709)
First developed by the British in 1950s, the 20Cr25Ni/Nb stainless steel has been used as a fuel
cladding material for the British Advanced Gas-cooled Reactor (AGR) fleet since 1962[5].
Approximately 90,000 fuel pins were present in each AGR plant, and there were 14 plants
constructed and operated. While the creep strength of 20Cr25Ni/Nb was relatively low, it was
sufficient for its intended application. Based on this, in the 1980s, the Nippon Steel Corporation
modified the base chemical composition by adding B, Mo, and Ti to 20Cr25Ni/Nb in order to
strengthen the creep resistance for ultra-supercritical boilers. The resulting austenitic stainless steel
was trademarked NF 709[3], [6]. The design criteria for developing the composition of NF 709
were to have a stable austenite devoid of the sigma and other intermetallic phases under long-term
elevated-temperature service conditions, and creep-strengthened by carbonitride M(CN)
precipitated in a stable, fine dispersion. Through a DOE-NE ART material down-selection and
intermediate term testing program, Alloy 709, an advanced austenitic stainless-steel alloy, was
recommended as a Class A structural material for the SFR because of its overall superior structural
strength advantage. While extensive work has been performed on A709 in the wrought form to
have it code-qualified, there is no available literature on the additive manufacturing aspect.

2.2 Ferritic/Martensitic Steels

2.2.1 Grade 91
G91 steel was developed as a 2nd generation creep resistant alloy. ORNL modified the composition
by adding small amounts of V and Nb to 9Cr–1Mo to form fine carbo-nitrides[4]. Used in fossil
and nuclear power plants in components operating at temperatures up to ~650°C, it is the only
alloy being studied in the current work package which is included in ASME Boiler and Pressure
Vessel (B&PV) Code Section III Division 5. Having a Cr concentration of 9 wt. % gives very good
creep strength while having a minimal increase in the DBTT. Significant work has been performed
recently on the additive manufacturing of G91 steel[7], [8], [9], [10]. Blown powder technique,
wire DED, and LPBF based techniques have all been used to fabricate this alloy to understand the
microstructural evolution, mechanical behavior, corrosion, and in some cases even irradiation
behavior[11]. In the case of LPBF sample, as-deposited additively manufactured G91 steel had a

 7

microstructure of lower bainitic regions surrounded by martensite. The as-deposited additively
manufactured material had excellent tensile mechanical properties with greater strength than the
wrought material at room and elevated temperatures, showing excellent promise for nuclear
applications. Retention of strength at 300 and 600 °C for the as-deposited additively manufactured
material was attributed to transitional carbides in the lower bainitic regions.

2.2.2 Grade 92
To improve the creep properties of 2nd generation G91 alloy, G92 steel was developed as a 3rd
generation creep-resistant F/M steel with the addition of tungsten and minute amounts of boron.
While it has been reported to have better creep properties relative to G91, it has not been code
qualified to Section III, Division 5[4], [12], [13]. In practice, the microstructure is made up of
tempered martensitic lath structure which is stabilized by Cr rich M23C6 carbide, intra-lath MX
type of Nb and V carbonitrides, martensite phase transformation induced high dislocation density,
and solid solution strengthening from tungsten[14], [15] The presence of tungsten in the M23C6
precipitate decreases growth rate of the precipitate during creep exposure, which in turn increases
the stability of the martensitic lath structure of the steel on creep exposure. Currently, there is no
available research on the additive manufacturing of G92 steel.

 Chapter 3: Materials and Methods

3.1 Fabrication Technique

A Renishaw AM400 LPBF machine equipped with a Yb-Fiber pulsed laser with a maximum power
of 400W and a beam diameter of 70 microns was used to fabricate all the samples. For smaller
builds of A709, G91, and G92, a reduced build volume feature available within the Renishaw setup
was used. These builds were 40mm*40mm*10mm in size. For ASTM sized creep samples, 8 larger
blocks were printed with sizes of 4”*4”*1”. These are shown in Figure 1.

 Figure 1: Full Scale Builds of Alloy A709

3.2 Ion Irradiation

Ex-situ ion irradiation experiments were performed using the Tandem accelerator at the ex-situ
beam lines of IVEM facility. More information regarding these tests was reported by Chen et
al[16].

3.3 Mechanical Testing

3.3.1 Tensile Testing
Dog bone shaped tensile samples were also extracted from the two blocks, both perpendicular and
parallel to the build direction. The tensile samples were SS-3 type with gage length of 7.62mm,
gage width and thickness of 1.52mm and 0.76 mm respectively. Tensile tests were performed on

 9

an Instron Model 5980 Materials Testing Systems with the Instron Bluehill 3 testing software for
control and data acquisition. The tester is equipped with a three-zone furnace for air testing up to
1100°C. To begin a test, a specimen was installed into a grip that was designed for shoulder
loading. The tips of two thermal couples were placed in close vicinity to the top and bottom region
of the gauge section of the specimen to reflect the actual temperature of the specimen and to serve
as the input of the furnace control. The temperature was brought up to the target gradually and a
soaking period of 1 hour was applied to ensure a thermal equilibrium of the loading train. All tests
were conducted with a strain rate of 0.001/s until rupture. Tensile tests were conducted at 25oC and
550oC.

3.3.2 Creep Testing
Round-type creep specimens per ASTM E8/E8M specification, with nominal gauge lengths of
31.75 mm and gauge diameters of 6.35 mm, were machined from the as-built and heat-treated
materials. The schematic of the sample is shown here. The creep tests were performed with ATS
model 2320-MM lever arm testers in air until rupture[17]. Testing conditions along with samples
tested are presented in Table 1

3.4 Characterization Techniques

Standard metallography techniques were employed to polish the samples for optical and electron
microscopes. Samples were polished to 4000 grit SiC abrasive paper, followed by diamond
suspension (3 μm and 1 μm), and by a 0.05 μm colloidal silica suspension final polish in a Buehler

 10

Vibromet. A JEOL 7500 SEM was used for electron microscopy, EBSD, and EDS. Scanning
transmission electron microscopy (STEM) in high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) and bright
field (BF) imaging modes were performed with a FEI Talos F200X TEM/STEM at the Center for
Nanoscale Materials (CNM) in ANL to study the microstructures of selected specimens. EDS was
used to map the chemical composition of selected areas of interest. The EDS intensity maps were
converted to Quantitative Hyperspectral X-ray Maps (Q-maps) for analysis. Specimens were
mechanically polished down to 100 μm in thickness, and 3 mm TEM disks were punched out. The
disks were then jet-polished until electron transparent with an electrolyte of 5% perchloric acid
and 95% methanol at −30 °C with a Struers TenuPol-5 jet polisher. The samples for post-irradiation
experiments were made using Helios 5 CX Focused Ion Beam machine.

3.5 Thermodynamic and Kinetics Simulation

The thermodynamics-and-kinetics simulation was conducted based on the Equilibrium and
Precipitation modules built in the Thermo-Calc software. The TCFE13 and MOBFE8 databases
were adopted. The input parameters referred to the experimental conditions, including
composition, temperature, and annealing time.

4.1.2 ASTM Sized Creep Data
Creep tests were conducted in accordance with ASTM Standard E139-11, “Standard Test Methods
for Conducting Creep, Creep-Rupture, and Stress-Rupture Tests on Metallic Materials.” Once the
furnace reached the target temperature, the specimen was soaked for 1 hour before applying the
load stepwise until full loading was reached within 2 to 5 minutes. A load cell positioned directly
above the dead weight monitored the force applied at each increment, and data was logged using
WinCCS II software. Following specimen fracture, the furnace was air-cooled and the sample
removed along with the test rig. Overall length and minimum diameter were then measured by
carefully rejoining the broken pieces to calculate elongation and reduction of area. Table 1 lists the
conditions initiated in FY25-26. A total of nine tests were initiated out of which six ruptured.

4.1.3 Ion Irradiation Data
The ion-irradiation was performed at the IVEM-Tandem facility. All the samples were irradiated
at 600oC and 10 dpa. To maintain a concise presentation and avoid repetition of similar
characterization results, the SA condition is presented here as a representative example of the post-
irradiation characterization. Figure 10 shows a representative FIB lift-out from the SA sample
following irradiation. As expected, MX precipitates are distributed throughout the microstructure.
Within the irradiated region, extending approximately 1000 nm from the surface, only a limited
number of irradiation-induced voids were observed, indicating relatively low void formation under
the investigated irradiation conditions. Evidence of radiation-induced segregation (RIS) is also
apparent, particularly in the Ni EDS elemental maps, where localized variations in Ni
concentration can be observed.

 Figure 9: Post Irradiation Analysis of SA sample after 600oC/10DPA

Figure 10 compares the post-ion-irradiation microstructures of the three A709 conditions: AD, SA,
and PT. TEM images were acquired under both over-focused and under-focused conditions to
facilitate the identification of irradiation-induced voids and distinguish them from precipitates and
other microstructural features. Comparison of the images reveals a clear difference in void
formation among the three material conditions. The PT condition exhibits the highest density of
irradiation-induced voids, whereas very few to none clearly identifiable voids are observed in the
SA and AD conditions. As expected, these observations indicate that the initial processing and

 17

heat-treatment condition significantly influences the susceptibility of A709 to irradiation-induced
void formation.

 Figure 10: Post Irradiation Comparison of AD, SA, PT samples after testing at 600oC/10DPA

To place the irradiation response of alloy A709 in direct comparison with 316H, the two heat-
treatment conditions of the AM materials are compared in Figure 11. Substantial differences in
irradiation-induced void formation are observed between the two alloys in both the AD and SA
conditions. In the AD condition, 316H exhibits a significantly higher density of irradiation-induced
voids, whereas no clearly identifiable voids are observed in A709. Following solution annealing,
however, the two alloys exhibit contrasting trends. In 316H, solution annealing appears to reduce
the overall number of irradiation-induced voids, while in A709, an increase in void formation is
observed relative to the AD condition. These contrasting responses suggest that both alloy
chemistry and pre-irradiation heat treatment play important roles in determining susceptibility to
irradiation-induced void formation. Two key questions therefore emerge and will be addressed in
future work: (i) whether the reduced void formation in AD A709 relative to AD 316H is associated
with differences in Ni content or other compositional effects, and (ii) why solution annealing
appears to promote void formation in A709 while suppressing it in 316H. Further quantitative

 18

characterization of void size and number density, together with analysis of radiation-induced
segregation and precipitate evolution, will be required to establish the mechanisms responsible for
these contrasting behaviors.

4.1.4 Ion Irradiation Data with Load
Following the creep and ion-irradiation studies, the combined effects of ion irradiation and applied
mechanical loading were investigated under constant-load conditions. This section evaluates the
material response during simultaneous irradiation and loading, providing insight into the
interaction between irradiation-induced microstructural evolution and stress-assisted deformation
in the investigated alloys. The tests were done at the IVEM Tandem facility. Figure 12 shows the
load setup used along with the thermal camera imaging, used to measure the temperature during
the testing.

 Figure 12: Ion Irradiation with Load Setup in IVEM at Argonne National Laboratory

Similar to the ion-irradiation experimentation, to maintain a concise presentation and avoid
repetition of similar characterization results, the SA condition is presented here as a representative
example of the post-irradiation characterization. Figure 13 presents a schematic of the
experimental setup together with SEM images obtained from both sides of the dog-bone specimen,
corresponding to (i) the load-only region and (ii) the region subjected to simultaneous loading and
ion irradiation. A pronounced difference in the surface microstructure is observed between the two
regions. On the load-only side, extensive deformation is evident, with crack-like features
developing along boundaries throughout the examined region. In contrast, these deformation
features are largely absent from the region exposed to both irradiation and applied load. The
reduced surface deformation on the irradiated side is consistent with irradiation-induced
hardening, which restricts plastic deformation through the accumulation of irradiation-induced
defects that impede dislocation motion. To further investigate the microstructural mechanisms
underlying this contrasting response, additional characterization was performed using TEM. In
particular, a FIB lift-out was prepared from the simultaneously loaded and irradiated region to
examine whether the application of stress influences irradiation-induced void formation and other
microstructural features within the damaged zone. This analysis enables a more direct assessment
 20

of the interaction between applied mechanical loading and irradiation-induced defect evolution.
The corresponding TEM observations, including the effect of applied load on void formation, are
presented and discussed in Figure 14.

 Figure 13: Post Irradiation Analysis of SA Sample with and without Load

Examination of the SA sample under simultaneous ion- irradiation and constant applied load
reveals a clear increase in the number of irradiation-induced voids compared with the
corresponding irradiation condition without applied load. This observation suggests that the
presence of mechanical stress influences the irradiation-induced microstructural response and may
promote void nucleation and/or growth. Another significant feature observed in the irradiated
region is radiation-induced segregation (RIS) of Ni. The Ni elemental distribution shows localized
compositional variations associated with irradiation, indicating redistribution of Ni within the
damaged region. The simultaneous observation of enhanced void formation and Ni RIS under
constant load raises important questions regarding the interaction between applied stress,
irradiation-induced point-defect evolution, and elemental redistribution. Further quantitative
analysis of void size and number density, together with the magnitude and spatial distribution of
Ni segregation, will be required to determine how the applied load modifies these irradiation-
induced processes and whether the observed RIS is directly associated with the enhanced void
formation.

4.2 Ferritic/Martensitic Steels: G91 & G92

For FY2026, research efforts were directed towards optimization to determine the sensitivity of
process parameters on ferrite/martensite ratio and subsequent effect on mechanical performance
of G91 and G92. A secondary objective of the FY2026 program was the comparison of the two
alloys in terms of microstructure evolution and mechanical behavior. CALPHAD simulations are
also presented in this chapter comparing both the alloy systems.

4.2.1 SEM Comparison
Figure 20 and Figure 21 show G91 and G92 SEM images respectively showing as-deposited and
heat-treated conditions. For G91, as deposited condition has a matrix of ferrite and martensite with
some theta precipitates in the ferrite phase. When normalized (1070C/15min), the whole matrix is
converted into martensite phase. Compared to this, when tempered (770C/45min) the
microstructure changes to tempered martensite + ferrite + carbides. At the scale, theta precipitates
do not appear to be present. Finally, when there is 2 step heat treatment (normalized + tempered)
is done, the microstructure is fully tempered martensite + carbides. The ferrite phase appears to
have fully dissolved.

4.2.2 TEM Comparison
Since the SEM characterization revealed only limited differences between the as-deposited G91
and G92 microstructures, further investigation was performed using TEM to determine whether
differences could be resolved at a finer length scale. Representative TEM micrographs are
presented in Figure 22. Consistent with the SEM observations, no substantial microstructural
differences are evident between the two alloys in the as-deposited condition. Both materials exhibit
a predominantly ferritic/martensitic matrix, with a limited population of theta (θ) precipitates
observed within the ferritic regions. The similarity between the as-deposited microstructures is not
entirely unexpected, as one of the principal compositional differences between G91 and G92 is the
addition of tungsten (W) in G92. In the as-deposited condition, W is expected to remain largely in
solid solution within the matrix rather than producing readily distinguishable microstructural

features at this scale. Consequently, its contribution is primarily associated with solid-solution
strengthening, and the presence of W cannot be directly identified from the TEM images alone.
More pronounced differences between G91 and G92 are therefore expected to emerge following
subsequent heat treatment and associated precipitation evolution.

 Chapter 5: Conclusions

5.1 Conclusions

This report advances the fundamental understanding of additive manufacturing for nuclear-grade
materials, building upon FY 2025 investigations with comprehensive process-structure-property
correlations for Fe-based alloys.
 1. G92 Ferritic/Martensitic Steel Optimization:
 • Microstructure: G91 v G92
 The as-deposited G91 and G92 specimens showed comparable microstructures with no
 significant differences. Heat treatment promoted precipitation in both alloys, with G92
 displaying a greater extent of carbide and Laves phase formation than G91.
 • Mechanical Behavior: G91 v G92
 G92 exhibited significantly higher strength than G91 across the temperature range
 evaluated, while maintaining comparable ductility with only a minimal reduction in
 elongation.
 2. A709 Austenitic Stainless-Steel Process-Property Relationships:
 • Mechanical Behavior
 SSJ3 Creep: Solution Annealed (SA) samples exhibited shorter creep rupture lives than As
 Deposited(AD) or Precipitation Treated (PT) samples. Fractographic analysis of ruptured
 specimens indicates that crack initiation predominantly occurs along Cr-rich cellular
 boundaries.
 ASTM Size Creep: Solution Annealed (SA) samples exhibited shorter creep rupture lives
 than As Deposited(AD) or Precipitation Treated (PT) samples. Fractographic analysis of
 ruptured specimens indicates that crack initiation predominantly occurs along Cr, Mo, C-
 rich cellular boundaries.
 • Irradiation Behavior
 Irradiation: LPBF as-built (AD) specimens exhibited greater resistance to irradiation-
 induced void formation compared with Solution Annealed (SA) and Precipitation Treated
 (PT) specimens. Overall, all A709 conditions demonstrated improved resistance to void
 swelling relative to LPBF 316H.

 39

 Irradiation with Constant Load: A pronounced effect of applied load on void formation was
 observed. The application of constant load significantly accelerated void formation in
 LPBF A709 specimens, with void density increasing exponentially with load.
 • CALPHAD
 Thermo-Calc®-based CALPHAD simulations indicate that optimized alloy chemistries
 can be developed to minimize σ-phase precipitation while enabling precise control over
 the volume fraction of M23C6 carbides.

This comprehensive investigation conclusively demonstrates the technical viability of additive
manufacturing for nuclear-grade Fe-based alloys, with both G92 ferritic/martensitic steel and A709
austenitic stainless steel achieving mechanical properties that meet or exceed conventional
processing benchmarks. The successful production of complex geometries with tailored
microstructures through controlled thermal histories validates AM as a transformative
manufacturing technology for nuclear applications.
