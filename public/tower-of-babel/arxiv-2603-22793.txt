Reliable Classroom AI via Neuro-Symbolic Multimodal Reasoning
Sina Bagheri Nezhad

Abstract
Classroom AI is rapidly expanding from low-level perception toward higher-level judgments about engagement, confusion, collaboration, and instructional quality. Yet classrooms are among the hardest real-world settings for multimodal vision: they are multi-party, noisy, privacy-sensitive, pedagogically diverse, and often multilingual. In this paper, we argue that classroom AI should be treated as a critical domain , where raw predictive accuracy is insufficient unless predictions are accompanied by verifiable evidence, calibrated uncertainty, and explicit deployment guardrails. We introduce NSCR , a neuro-symbolic framework that decomposes classroom analytics into four layers: perceptual grounding, symbolic abstraction, executable reasoning, and governance. NSCR adapts recent ideas from symbolic fact extraction and verifiable code generation to multimodal educational settings, enabling classroom observations from video, audio, ASR, and contextual metadata to be converted into typed facts and then composed by executable rules, programs, and policy constraints. Beyond the system design, we contribute a benchmark and evaluation protocol organized around five tasks: classroom state inference, discourse-grounded event linking, temporal early warning, collaboration analysis, and multilingual classroom reasoning. We further specify reliability metrics centered on abstention, calibration, robustness, construct alignment, and human usefulness. The paper does not report new empirical results; its contribution is a concrete framework and evaluation agenda intended to support more interpretable, privacy-aware, and pedagogically grounded multimodal AI for classrooms.

Introduction

Computer vision and multimodal foundation models are moving into educational settings with the promise of helping teachers notice participation patterns, summarize class discussions, identify confusion, and reflect on instructional practice. The appeal is obvious: classrooms are rich multimodal environments in which video, audio, language, gesture, gaze, and temporal interaction all convey pedagogically meaningful information. This is precisely why classroom AI has become a growing topic within multimodal learning analytics (MMLA) and classroom observation research

At the same time, classrooms are one of the least forgiving environments in which to deploy black-box multimodal systems. They contain many people, substantial occlusion, shifting seating layouts, overlapping speech, varied activity structures, and strong distribution shift across schools, cultures, age groups, and subjects. More importantly, educational targets such as engagement, confusion, self-regulation, participation opportunity, or collaboration quality are not directly visible in the way that object categories are visible. They are theory-laden constructs inferred from partial evidence, shaped by pedagogy and context, and often contested across stakeholders

;

The dominant modeling pattern in the area still tends to couple low-level detection with direct label prediction: estimate gaze, posture, speech, or facial activity and map those signals to a downstream classroom judgment. This has produced important advances in gaze-following and classroom observation

signals

remains under-specified. When the system concludes that a learner is confused or that a group is collaborating well, it often cannot say which evidence mattered, which assumptions were invoked, how uncertainty propagated across modalities, or when it should abstain and defer to a teacher.

A parallel line of work in language reasoning suggests a promising alternative. Recent neuro-symbolic methods combine structured fact extraction with executable code or logical reasoning, improving interpretability and compositional reliability over purely prose-based generation

Problem Formulation

We consider a classroom episode as a multimodal stream

X=\{X^{v}_{1:T},X^{a}_{1:T},X^{\ell}_{1:T},X^{c}\},

The goal is to predict, summarize, or answer a classroom query about a target variable
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{Y}
may correspond to student-, group-, or class-level outputs such as confusion hypotheses, participation summaries, collaboration episodes, or responses to structured classroom questions. Unlike direct end-to-end models,
NSCR
introduces explicit intermediate objects:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{m\in\mathcal{M}}g_{m}(X),

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\mathcal{O},X^{c}),

(3)

\displaystyle(\hat{y},\mathcal{E},s)

\displaystyle=R(\mathcal{F},X^{c},\mathcal{P}),

(4)

where

are perceptual grounding modules,
\Gamma
maps their outputs into symbolic facts, and
R
is an executable reasoner that returns a prediction
\hat{y}
, an evidence trace
\mathcal{E}
, and a confidence or support score
s

\mathcal{P}

Typed facts.

Each fact
f\in\mathcal{F}
is a tuple

f=(p,a,v,\tau,c,\pi),

(5)

where

are arguments,

is confidence, and

Abstention.

Because educational decisions are risk-sensitive, the system may abstain. Let
s(\hat{y})
denote the support score of the top hypothesis and
\Delta
the margin to the runner-up. Then

\text{output}=\begin{cases}\hat{y},&\text{if }s(\hat{y})\geq\tau_{s}\text{ and }\Delta\geq\tau_{\Delta},\\
\texttt{DEFER},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(6)

The option to abstain is essential in classroom settings, where a wrong alert can be more harmful than no alert at all.

Construct alignment.

A central requirement is that the symbolic layer reflect educational constructs instead of collapsing them into arbitrary detector outputs. In other words, predicates should encode hypotheses about classroom activity at the right level of abstraction:
gaze target
,
help request
,
speaking opportunity
,
shared attention
, or
teacher prompt
, rather than only raw pixel motion. This echoes the MMLA view that the path from signals to knowledge must be theoretically justified

4.1
Perceptual Grounding

The perceptual layer can use any off-the-shelf or task-specific modules: pose estimation, body orientation, gaze estimation, hand-raise detection, speaker diarization, ASR, discourse parsing, OCR over slides or boards, object/activity recognition, or audio-prosodic analysis. Representative components already exist for robust speech recognition and diarization
25
;
7
, while classroom-oriented sensing platforms demonstrate the feasibility of integrating visual and audio streams in authentic learning environments
1
.
NSCR
does not prescribe a particular detector architecture; instead, it requires a standardized output interface so that detectors produce
candidate observations
rather than opaque feature vectors. A candidate observation includes: the event or attribute type, the affected entity or entities, the time span, a confidence estimate, and the provenance needed for later audit.

This interface is important because many classroom signals are approximate and contested. For example, ASR may be wrong in the presence of overlapping speech or code-switching, gaze estimates may be degraded by pose or occlusion, and a group activity detector may fail when students are clustered tightly. If such uncertainty is hidden, downstream judgments become impossible to interpret.

4.2
Symbolic Abstraction

Grounded observations are mapped into a limited but expressive vocabulary of classroom facts. We recommend a schema organized around five predicate families:

•

OBS(entity, attribute, value, time, conf)
for low-level observable properties;

•

EVENT(actor, action, target, interval, conf)
for discrete events or actions;

•

REL(entity_1, relation, entity_2, interval, conf)
for interaction or spatial relations;

•

CONTEXT(key, value, interval)
for pedagogical phase, activity structure, or lesson metadata; and

•

POLICY(id, condition, consequence)
for deployment rules, abstention logic, or retention constraints.

Two design choices matter. First, symbolic facts should remain close enough to detector outputs to be grounded, but far enough from raw signals to be pedagogically meaningful. Second, the vocabulary should be
construct aware
: “help request” and “speaking opportunity” are better building blocks for classroom reasoning than unconstrained latent embeddings because they can be tied to educational theory, annotation protocols, and teacher interpretation.

4.3
Executable Reasoning over Classroom Facts

Once facts are created, higher-level classroom inference is delegated to an executable reasoning layer. We envision three complementary mechanisms.

Rule-based composition.

Some classroom constructs can be expressed as compositional patterns. For example, a
confusion candidate
may be supported by a recent teacher question, a failed attempt, a help request, and sustained attention on the task artifact. A
participation opportunity
may require that a student was oriented to the interaction, the floor was open, and the student was not already speaking. A
collaboration episode
may combine mutual orientation, balanced turns, and references to a shared artifact. These patterns need not be universally fixed; they can be encoded as templates to be refined with domain experts.

Programmatic aggregation.

Other queries are easier to express in executable code: counting how many distinct students asked for help after a specific demonstration, identifying who contributed during a discussion phase, or ranking groups by balance of turns. This follows the spirit of program-aided reasoning
12
and symbolic fact extraction
3
.

LLM-assisted program synthesis.

For more complex or open-ended queries, an LLM may propose a reasoning program from the symbolic facts and the user query. Here, the key contribution of
SymCode
-style design is that the generated program becomes the reasoning artifact itself and can be executed, checked, and possibly debugged using interpreter feedback
4
. In classroom settings, program synthesis should be constrained by a schema, a whitelist of operators, and policy checks.

A generic support function for a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
can be written as

s(h)=\sum_{f\in\mathrm{supp}(h)}w_{f}\log c_{f}-\lambda_{v}V(h)-\lambda_{p}P(h),

(7)

where
c_{f}
is the confidence of fact
f
,
V(h)
counts violated logical or temporal constraints, and
P(h)
counts policy violations such as attempting to issue a student-level alert from a single uncertain modality. This form is intentionally abstract; the important point is that support depends on explicit facts and constraints rather than on uninspectable activations.

4.4
Uncertainty, Abstention, and Governance

Classroom systems should not equate low confidence with low stakes. We therefore make governance explicit. Policies can encode deployment rules such as:
never issue a student-level alert unless two modalities agree
;
when ASR confidence is below threshold, downgrade all discourse-derived claims
;
retain only symbolic traces for routine analytics
; or
require teacher confirmation before any student-specific recommendation is shown
. These rules belong in the executable layer so that they are inspectable and machine-checkable.

In practice, a governance module uses confidence estimates, contradiction checks, and task-specific risk thresholds to decide whether to emit a label, return a structured explanation, request additional evidence, or abstain. This makes
NSCR
particularly suitable for selective prediction settings, where coverage and risk must be traded off deliberately.

4.5
Privacy-Aware Data Minimization

A further advantage of symbolic reasoning is that it enables stronger data minimization. Many classroom uses do not require long-term storage of raw video once grounded events have been extracted. A deployment can therefore separate three retention layers: (1) short-lived raw buffers used for on-device or near-device perception, (2) symbolic traces with timestamps, uncertainty, and provenance, and (3) aggregate reports for teachers or researchers. This structure is aligned with established privacy principles in learning analytics
24
and gives system designers a clearer handle on consent, retention, and audit than end-to-end embeddings alone.

T1: Classroom state inference.

This task concerns hypotheses about individual, group, or whole-class states such as confusion candidates, participation opportunity, or off-task behavior. We intentionally use the term
candidate
for many targets because educational constructs are rarely directly observable. Ground-truth annotation should therefore include both target labels and coder rationales or supporting evidence spans where possible.

T2: Discourse-grounded event linking.

Here the system answers structured questions such as:
Which students requested help after the teacher introduced the denominator example?
;
Which group responded to a prompt with a peer explanation rather than a teacher-directed reply?
This task directly tests the ability to link language, time, video events, and instructional context.

T3: Temporal early warning.

Educationally meaningful patterns often unfold over minutes rather than frames. A useful benchmark should therefore evaluate early-warning logic for confusion, disengagement, or collaborative breakdown, while also measuring whether the system knows when not to raise an alert.

T4: Collaboration analysis.

Small-group classrooms require reasoning over turn balance, mutual orientation, shared artifact references, and role shifts. Because collaboration quality is context-sensitive, the benchmark should include both structured indicators and human ratings with clear rubrics.

T5: Multilingual classroom reasoning.

Many real classrooms are multilingual or code-switched. This task extends
NSAR
-style symbolic reasoning to settings where transcript evidence, retrieval, translation, and classroom events must be integrated across languages. It is especially relevant for educational equity because ASR and discourse models often fail disproportionately in such settings.

Benchmark dimensions.

Across all tasks, benchmark splits should vary systematically by classroom layout, grade band, subject, activity type, number of cameras, audio quality, missing modalities, and language configuration. This is essential for measuring generalization beyond the lab.

6
Evaluation Protocols Beyond Accuracy

A central thesis of this paper is that classroom AI should be evaluated at the level of reasoning and governance, not only low-level detection. We recommend a five-level protocol.

Level 1: Perception quality.

Standard metrics such as mAP, event F1, WER, DER, or temporal localization remain necessary, but they should be treated as upstream diagnostics rather than end goals.

Level 2: Grounding fidelity.

The symbolic abstraction should be evaluated directly: did the system extract the right facts, time spans, and relations from the perceptual layer? Metrics can include fact-level precision/recall, argument accuracy, and provenance correctness.

Level 3: Reasoning correctness and faithfulness.

Executable outputs should be assessed with exact match, execution success, contradiction rate, and evidence sufficiency. Importantly, a system should be penalized if it returns the right answer for the wrong reasons or if the explanation omits decisive evidence.

Level 4: Reliability under abstention.

Because
NSCR
can defer, selective metrics become crucial. Let
A_{\tau}
be the event that the system accepts rather than abstains at threshold
\tau
. The
coverage
and
selective risk
are

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\Pr(A_{\tau}),

(8)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Risk}_{\mathrm{sel}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\Pr(\hat{y}\neq y\mid A_{\tau}).

(9)

These should be reported alongside calibration error and out-of-distribution robustness across classrooms, camera layouts, and language conditions. In practice, this means supplementing task accuracy with calibration analyses
15
, uncertainty baselines such as deep ensembles
18
, simple OOD detectors
16
, selective prediction risk–coverage curves
13
, and WILDS-style shift-aware evaluation splits
17
.

Level 5: Human usefulness and policy compliance.

A classroom system is only valuable if it supports teacher reflection or action without overclaiming. We therefore recommend task-based human evaluation with educators, measuring usefulness, perceived trust, cognitive load, and policy compliance. Example questions include: Did the explanation reveal enough evidence to be actionable? Did abstentions occur when evidence was weak? Would the teacher prefer this output to a raw confidence score? This emphasis on actionable explanation is consistent with model-agnostic explanation work and with the broader view that high-stakes domains should prefer inherently interpretable reasoning processes when possible
26
;
27
.

7.2
Participation Opportunity vs. Non-Participation

A classroom dashboard that simply counts speaking turns can misclassify quiet students as disengaged even when they never had a speaking opportunity. In
NSCR
, participation opportunity is a separate reasoning target: the system checks whether the interaction floor was open, whether the student was oriented to the discussion, whether overlapping speech blocked entry, and whether the activity phase expected individual speaking. This prevents a common fairness failure in which absence of speech is conflated with absence of opportunity.

7.3
Multilingual Classroom Query Answering

Consider a bilingual classroom in which the query is posed in English but relevant discourse is partly in Spanish and partly in English. A teacher might ask:
Who requested help after the fraction example?
An
NSAR
-style pipeline can extract symbolic facts from ASR transcripts, align them with visual entities and time spans, and compute the answer through executable code. This use case links the multilingual reasoning strengths of neuro-symbolic fact extraction with the classroom setting’s need for grounded event linkage.

8
Discussion, Limitations, and Ethics

The main strength of
NSCR
is not that it eliminates ambiguity, but that it localizes ambiguity in inspectable places: detector outputs, symbolic abstractions, reasoning rules, and governance thresholds. This is valuable for debugging and for participatory design with teachers and learning scientists.

Still, several limitations remain. First, a symbolic schema can be transparent and yet pedagogically invalid. If the chosen predicates do not correspond to meaningful educational constructs, the system will produce neat but misleading explanations. Second, rule-based components can be brittle, whereas LLM-generated code can still be wrong or socially inappropriate. Third, annotation cost is substantial: construct-aligned datasets require richer labels than simple detection benchmarks. Fourth, neuro-symbolic pipelines do not remove surveillance risk. Symbolic traces may be safer than persistent raw video, but they still encode potentially sensitive information about minors and classroom practice.

These limitations reinforce the need for ethics-aware deployment. Educational datasets involving human subjects may require IRB review, consent procedures, retention limits, and careful subgroup analysis. Reviewers should be able to inspect not only model performance but also the policies encoded in the system, including anonymisation and retention choices
29
. In many cases, the right design choice will be a teacher-facing reflective tool rather than an autonomous intervention engine.

9
Conclusion

We presented
NSCR
, a neuro-symbolic framework for classroom AI that separates multimodal perception from pedagogically meaningful reasoning. By grounding classroom events into typed symbolic facts and composing them through executable rules, programs, and policies,
NSCR
offers a path toward more interpretable, uncertainty-aware, and privacy-conscious learning analytics. We also proposed a benchmark and evaluation agenda centered on reasoning quality, abstention, construct alignment, and human usefulness. We hope this paper helps shift classroom AI from black-box label prediction toward verifiable multimodal inference suitable for real educational settings.
