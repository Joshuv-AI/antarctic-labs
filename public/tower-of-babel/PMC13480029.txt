Neurological outcomes after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest due to aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Vincent Legros, Florine Darty, Jessica cateura, Marie Werner, Guillaume Besch, Camille Bonny, Hervé Floch, Alexis Raynaud, Julien Pottecher, Benjamin Chousterman, Paer-Selim Abback, Mathilde Holleville, Félix Porée, Jean-Denis Moyer, Alice Jacquens, Pierre-Antoine Seube-remy, Nicolas Mongardon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOut-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) due to aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is rare but associated with extremely poor prognosis. Data on neurological outcomes and prognostic factors are limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective multicenter cohort study across 12 French neuro-ICUs including all adult patients admitted alive after OHCA-aSAH between 2014 and 2024. Demographics, cardiac arrest characteristics, aSAH severity, ICU management, and 6-month neurological outcomes (modified Rankin Scale, mRS) were collected. Firth penalized logistic regression was applied to explore predictors of favorable outcome.ResultsAmong 15,907 SAH admissions, 164 patients had OHCA-aSAH. Median age was 54 [43–62] years; 47.5% were male. Only 8 patients (4.9%) achieved favorable outcome (mRS ≤ 3) while 156 (95.1%) had poor outcome (mRS ≥ 4), including 154 deaths (mRS 6). Low-flow duration tended to be shorter, epinephrine use lower, and pupillary abnormalities less frequent among survivors, although no independent statistical association could be confirmed. All patients receiving pre-hospital antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy (n = 14, 8.7%) had poor outcome. External ventricular drainage, ICP monitoring, and endovascular aneurysm treatment were more frequent, and ICU and hospital length of stay were longer, in survivors. One hundred and eight patients (65.8%) progressed to brain death within 1 [1–2] day. Penalized regression did not identify independent predictors of good outcome, though pupillary abnormalities and epinephrine use were associated with progression toward brain death.ConclusionsOHCA-aSAH carries a dismal prognosis, with < 5% achieving favorable neurological recovery. Selected patients may benefit from aggressive neurocritical care, and the high rate of brain death emphasizes the importance of organ donation pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-026-06114-6.

Introduction

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) accounts for less than 5% of all strokes [1–4]. Despite its rarity, it disproportionately affects young or middle-aged adults with significant burden in terms of mortality and socioeconomic consequences. Mortality reaches 50% in the most severe cases, and among survivors, nearly two-thirds exhibit persistent neurological disabilities [5]. This high morbidity reflects not only the severity of the initial hemorrhage but also the complex cascade of secondary brain injuries and systemic complications triggered by the aneurysmal rupture.

Indeed, simultaneously to aneurysm rupture, extracerebral dysfunctions—particularly cardiopulmonary, endocrine, renal, and metabolic disturbances—are frequent and severe, reflecting the profound neurogenic stress response induced by aneurysm rupture. Among these systemic manifestations, cardiovascular complications are among the most frequent and clinically impactful, encompassing a broad spectrum from transient myocardial dysfunction and electrocardiographic abnormalities to severe arrhythmias and shock.

Within this spectrum, cardiac arrest is the most dramatic manifestation. It is estimated that initial cardiac arrest occurs in approximately 2% of patients with aSAH, identifying aneurysmal rupture as the leading cause of neurogenic cardiac arrest [6–11]. The prognosis is particularly grim, combining the consequences of the primary cerebral insult with the global hypoxic–ischemic injury inherent to cardiac arrest. Prognostic assessment in these cases is therefore highly challenging [9].

Beyond cardiac complications, the initial severity of the aSAH remains a cornerstone of prognostication. Clinical grading based on the World Federation of Neurosurgeons (WFNS) scale and radiological assessment using the modified Fisher scale provide essential information on cerebral insult and risk of complications [12–14]. In the specific scenario of cardiac arrest, these assessment tools are even more crucial, as they help distinguish potentially salvageable cases from those with catastrophic brain injury. However, the discriminative power of Glasgow Coma Scale within the WFNS scale may be questioned in the case of aSAH-induced cardiac arrest as GCS3 merely reflects the absence of cerebral perfusion than intrinsic primary brain injury.

Despite the clinical importance of this entity, recent data remain limited regarding out-of-hospital cardiac arrest secondary to aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (OHCA-aSAH). Most existing studies rely on heterogeneous populations, dated registries, or small cohorts, making it difficult to generalize prognostic estimates or identify which patients may benefit from aggressive neurocritical care strategies [6].

Given these gaps, the primary objective of our study was to evaluate the 6-month neurological prognosis of patients with OHCA-aSAH. Secondary objective was to identify prognostic factors.

Methods

Study design

We conducted an observational retrospective cohort study using data from 12 University hospitals in France between January 1, 2014, and December, 31, 2024.

This study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines and a checklist is provided in Supplementary Material 1 [15]. This study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the French Society of Anesthesiology and Critical Care (SFAR) (CERAR 00010254-2024-116).

Participants

This study included all consecutive patients aged 18 years or older who were admitted alive to the hospital following an OHCA-aSAH. Patients for whom the neurological prognosis at six months was unavailable were excluded.

All patients were managed in highly specialized referral centers with expertise in SAH and post–cardiac arrest care. These centers provided interventional neuroradiology (including endovascular coiling), neurosurgery (aneurysm clipping, decompressive craniectomy, and external ventricular drainage (EVD)), and coronary angiography with the capacity for urgent percutaneous coronary intervention. Care was delivered by multidisciplinary teams including neurointensivists, neurosurgeons, interventional neuroradiologists, and cardiologists [16, 17].

Data source and collection

For all patients, demographic data were collected such age, sex, main comorbidities, anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy.

We also collected data relating to OHCA, such as location, onset of prodromes, cardiopulmonary resuscitation by bystander, duration of no and low flow, initial cardiac rhythm, epinephrine injection and defibrillation.

In patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), we recorded treatment modalities (endovascular treatment, neurosurgical intervention, decompressive craniectomy, external ventricular drainage, and intracranial pressure monitoring) and CT scan characteristics, including modified Fisher scale [14] classification and aneurysm localization.

Important elements of the ICU course were recorded, such as the occurrence of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), the need for renal replacement therapy (RRT) and septic shock, as well as the duration of mechanical ventilation and ICU and hospital lengths of stay.

Decisions regarding withdrawal or withholding of life-sustaining therapies (WLST) were center-specific but were made in accordance with the recommendations of the French Society of Anesthesia and Intensive Care Medicine (SFAR) [18–20].

Brain death was diagnosed according to the national recommendations of the French Biomedicine Agency (Agence de la Biomédecine) in all participating centers throughout the study period. These recommendations standardized the clinical assessment and confirmatory investigations required for brain death determination, and were not modified during the study period, thereby limiting inter-center variability in diagnostic practices [21].

For neurological prognosis, modified Rankin Scale (mRS) [22, 23] scores were assessed at 6 months through ICU, hospital or rehabilitation care follow-up. Good neurologic outcome was considered as mRS 0–3 based on previous publications [24].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean (standard deviation) or median [interquartile range], according to data distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages). Comparisons between groups were performed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables, as appropriate.

Due to the limited number of patients with favorable neurological outcome, a penalized logistic regression model using Firth’s correction was applied to explore associations with selected clinically relevant variables. Automatic variable selection procedures were avoided to prevent overfitting. Results were expressed as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

All analyses were performed using SAS. version 9.4 (SAS Institute. Inc. Cary. NC. USA).

Results

Baseline characteristic

Between 2014 and 2024, 15,907 patients were admitted to the 12 participating neuro-ICUs for SAH. After application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 164 patients had OHCA-aSAH and were included in the final cohort (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The age of the population was 54 years [43–62], and 78 patients (47.5%) were male. At 6 months, only eight patients (4.9%) achieved a good neurological (mRS ≤ 3). However, 0, 1, 4, and 3 patients were mRS 0, 1, 2 and 3, respectively. Conversely, 156 patients (95.1%) had a poor outcome (mRS 4–6), including 154 deaths (mRS 6) (Fig. 1).

Bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation was performed in 71.8% of cases. No-flow duration did not differ between groups, with 0 [0–5] minute (0 [0–0] vs. 0 [0–5], p = 0.07). In contrast, low-flow duration was significantly shorter in patients who achieved good neurological recovery (8 [1–11] vs. 20 [10–30] minutes, p = 0.001).

Both the use of epinephrine (12.5% vs. 81.4%, p < 0.001) and the cumulative administered dose (0 [0–0] mg vs. 2 [1–3] mg, p = 0.005) during resuscitation were lower in the good outcome group.

Shockable rhythm was observed in 19 patients (12.1%) overall, with no cases in the good outcome group (0%) and 19 cases (12.2%) in the poor outcome group. A summary of the rhythm types is described in Supplementary Table S2.

All patients receiving pre-hospital antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy (n = 14, 8.7%) none had a good outcome (p = 0.01).

Subarachnoid hemorrhage characteristics and management

Pupillary abnormalities (Bilateral mydriasis or anisocoria, both non-reactive to light) were present in 71.4% of the overall cohort and were significantly less frequent in patients with good outcomes (12.5% vs. 74.4%, p = 0.0002). Most patients (93.2%) were classified as modified Fisher scale 4, without significant differences between outcome groups. Anterior circulation aneurysms accounted for 62.8% of cases.

External ventricular drain placement (87.5% vs. 25.6%, p = 0.0002) and intracranial pressure monitoring (62.5% vs. 28.2%, p = 0.04) were significantly more frequent among patients with favorable outcomes. Similarly, regarding aneurysm treatment, all patients with good outcomes underwent endovascular coiling, whereas this strategy was used in only 18.6% of patients with poor outcomes (p = 0.0005). The use of targeted temperature management did not differ between groups (Table 3).

ICU course and in-hospital management

Patients who ultimately achieved a good neurological outcome experienced longer and more complex ICU courses, requiring longer durations of mechanical ventilation ICU and hospital lengths of stay.

Among ICU complications, only acute respiratory distress syndrome occurred more frequently in patients with good outcomes (37.5% vs. 6.4%, p = 0.001) (Table 3).

However, none of the evaluated variables -including demographic characteristics, OHCA-related variables, and SAH severity markers - reached statistical significance, and no independent prognostic factor could be reliably identified in this cohort (Table 4).

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort conducted across 12 French neuroICUs over a 10-year period, we report the largest individual series specifically focusing on OHCA-aSAH. Among more than 15,000 screened patients, only 164 had this dreadful combination, underlining the rarity of this extreme situation. Despite advances in neurocritical care and resuscitation strategies over the past decades, the prognosis of OHCA-aSAH remained extremely poor, with only 4.9% of patients achieving a good neurological outcome at 6 months, and among them, only 1 patient was mRS 0–1, after very long ICU and hospital stays.

Burden and clinical severity of aSAH complicated by cardiac arrest

The epidemiology and pathophysiology of aSAH have been extensively described [1, 2]. However, the subgroup of patients presenting with OHCA secondary to SAH constitutes a particularly severe phenotype, combining both primary neurological injury and global ischemia–reperfusion injury.

Previous studies have consistently reported extremely low survival and favorable outcome rates in this population. Old series were obtained before major improvement in CA management, mainly driven by target temperature management and refinements on systemic physiological goals. Keeping these improvements in mind, a multicenter French cohort reported a survival rate of 0% in a series of 73 OHCA-aSAH admitted to medical ICU. The bias was that none of the 3 center was a neuro-ICU, suggesting that less severe cases may have been admitted to neuro-ICU, whereas the most severe cases may have been recused by neurosurgical teams and admitted thereafter in medical ICU [6]. Other recent series focusing specifically on OHCA-aSAH have confirmed these findings, with favorable neurological outcomes ranging between 0% and 10% [7–10]. Our results are therefore fully consistent with the existing literature and further emphasize the dramatic prognosis of this entity. It is of utmost importance to note that, despite being larger at first sight, these series [7, 9] were based on large-scale database without individual details (for instance, no Utstein style data reporting, no data on pupillary abnormalities). So, our series is the largest contemporary cohort with individual granularity.

Importantly, the very high proportion of patients presenting with severe radiological and clinical features in our cohort, including modified Fisher scale 4 hemorrhage in more than 90% of cases and a high prevalence of pupillary abnormalities, highlights the overwhelming burden of primary brain injury. These findings are in line with the pathophysiological framework of early brain injury after aSAH, which encompasses hyperacute intracranial hypertension, transient cerebral circulatory arrest, and widespread cortical depolarization [5, 25].

Mechanisms linking aSAH and cardiac arrest

The mechanisms responsible for cardiac arrest in the setting of aSAH are complex and multifactorial. Acute intracranial hypertension, brainstem compression, and massive sympathetic discharge have all been implicated [10, 26]. Experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that abrupt catecholamine release following acute brain injury can induce severe myocardial stunning, arrhythmias, and profound hemodynamic instability [26].

Electrocardiographic abnormalities such as QT prolongation, giant T waves, and ST-segment changes have long been described after aSAH [27, 28]. These abnormalities are strongly associated with elevated circulating catecholamine levels and autonomic dysfunction [28, 29]. In severe cases, this neurogenic cardiac injury may be sufficient to precipitate ventricular arrhythmias or pulseless electrical activity, ultimately leading to cardiac arrest. Our cohort, characterized by a very high proportion of neurological prodromes prior to arrest and a predominance of non-shockable rhythms, supports this pathophysiological hypothesis and mirrors previous observations [6, 9, 10].

Cardiac arrest characteristics and resuscitation profiles

In the present study, most cardiac arrests occurred at home, and bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation was performed in nearly three-quarters of cases. Importantly, patients with good outcomes had significantly shorter low-flow durations and were far less likely to receive epinephrine during resuscitation. These findings are consistent with the concept that rapid restoration of spontaneous circulation and lower cumulative ischemic burden remain essential determinants of neurological recovery, even in the context of catastrophic primary brain injury. Our results underline an association between epinephrine use and dosing and poorer OHCA outcomes; however, no causal inference can be drawn from these findings [30].

However, in contrast to cardiac arrest of cardiac origin, the prognostic value of classical resuscitation parameters appears more limited in OHCA-aSAH. In previous studies, no-flow and low-flow durations were inconsistently associated with outcome, and shockable rhythms were rare and poorly discriminative [6–10]. Our multivariable analysis did not confirm a significant association between no-flow/low-flow durations or initial rhythm and favorable neurological outcome, likely because of limited statistical power related to the sample size and the low number of patients with favorable outcomes.

Neurological severity and early neurocritical care management

Among all variables explored, absence of pupillary reactivity to light showed a trend toward an association with poor outcome, although this association did not reach statistical significance in the penalized regression model. This finding is clinically intuitive and strongly supported by prior literature, as pupillary abnormalities reflect early brainstem involvement, transtentorial herniation, or severe global cerebral ischemia. In cohorts of severe aSAH without cardiac arrest, pupillary reactivity has repeatedly been identified as a robust predictor of mortality and poor neurological recovery [12, 13].

Unfortunately, no information was available regarding rebleeding in these patients, which is a factor known to strongly worsen the prognosis of SAH [31].

All survivors received a more invasive neurocritical care approach, including specialized neurointensive care management, intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring, and/or external ventricular drainage (EVD). These findings may suggest that an aggressive neurocritical care strategy, combined with early endovascular aneurysm occlusion, is associated with improved neurological outcomes, possibly through earlier detection of intracranial hypertension and more targeted physiological management.

Nevertheless, this association should be interpreted with great caution. First, there is a substantial indication bias: patients who did not receive invasive management were likely those with the most severe initial neurological injury, a greater burden of comorbidities, or those in whom an unfavorable prognosis led clinicians to limit therapeutic intensity early in the course of care [32]. Therefore, the absence of invasive monitoring or neurosurgical procedures probably does not reflect lower efficacy of these strategies, but rather an early clinical selection process.

This selection phenomenon, already described in severe stroke and neurocritical care populations, tends to favor the use of ICP monitoring and invasive therapies in younger patients with fewer comorbidities and an initial neurological condition perceived as potentially reversible [33]. Consequently, in an observational study, it remains difficult to disentangle the intrinsic benefit of neurocritical care interventions from the effect of a prior selection of patients with the greatest perceived potential for recovery.

Accordingly, and consistent with recent propensity-adjusted data in severe stroke cohorts [34], the apparent association between ICP monitoring and outcome is likely explained by confounding by indication rather than an independent protective effect. These mechanisms are well recognized in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage and acute brain injury and contribute to self-fulfilling prophecy bias in neurocritical care [5, 35].

Nevertheless, our data suggest that reasonable neurological recovery remains possible provided that aggressive and timely neuroprotective strategies can be implemented in highly selected cases (short low flow duration, shockable rhythm, low dose epinephrine and preserved pupil reactivity).

This observation is in line with recent guideline recommendations emphasizing that early withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy should be avoided in the acute phase of aSAH whenever prognostic uncertainty persists [5].

Extracerebral complications and systemic burden

Patients with favorable neurological outcome experienced significantly longer ICU and hospital stays and were more frequently affected by acute respiratory distress syndrome, due to the well-known survivorship bias.

Extracerebral organ dysfunction following aSAH is increasingly recognized as a major determinant of morbidity and resource utilization [25, 35, 36]. Acute lung injury, cardiac dysfunction, metabolic and endocrine disturbances are common and may further complicate the management of these patients. In our cohort, however, the incidence of septic shock and renal replacement therapy did not differ between outcome groups, suggesting that systemic complications, although frequent, were not the primary drivers of neurological prognosis in this extremely severe population.

Causes of death and organ donation

Neurological causes of death largely predominated, confirming that refractory brain injury remains the leading mechanism of death in OHCA-aSAH. This observation is consistent with previous multicenter studies reporting that most deaths occur following progression to brain death or following decisions to withhold or withdraw life-sustaining therapies because of devastating neurological prognosis [6, 8, 10].

Notably, two thirds of patients progressed to brain death, within a very short delay of 1 day. This rapid evolution suggests that the period of continued life-sustaining therapy before the diagnosis of brain death is generally short. Severe SAH is a well-known cause of brain death among potential organ donors, and patients presenting with associated cardiac arrest appear to be at particularly high risk of this progression. These findings support ICU admission for all patients for maintaining initial resuscitative management and avoiding very early decisions to withhold or withdraw life-sustaining therapies, as brain death may occur rapidly and allow organ donation.

However, the potential impact of self-fulfilling prophecy bias must be carefully considered when interpreting these findings. Early prognostic pessimism in patients presenting with extremely severe neurological injury may have influenced decisions regarding therapeutic intensity, limitation of invasive neurocritical care strategies, or withdrawal of life-sustaining therapies. In turn, these early decisions may themselves have contributed to poor outcomes, making it difficult to distinguish irreversible neurological injury from outcomes partially influenced by treatment limitation practices. This issue is particularly relevant in OHCA-aSAH, where reliable early neurological prognostication remains challenging during the first hours after resuscitation [37–39].

Prognostic modeling and limitations of prediction

Despite extensive exploration of clinical, resuscitation, and neurological variables, our penalized regression model failed to identify any independent predictors of good outcome with conventional statistical significance. This is explained by the extremely small number of favorable outcomes and the resulting limited statistical power. Similar difficulties have been reported in previous studies, where multivariable models were often unstable or unable to reliably discriminate survivors with good neurological recovery [7–10].

Interestingly, although the discrimination of our model was excellent, this apparent performance must be interpreted cautiously, as it is likely influenced by overfitting and the very unbalanced outcome distribution. The application of Firth penalized regression was therefore essential to mitigate small-sample bias and separation issues, but it cannot fully overcome the intrinsic limitations of the dataset.

Our additional descriptive analyses, including the survival curve, stacked outcome distributions, and causes-of-death visualization, provide complementary insights into the temporal dynamics and heterogeneity of outcome trajectories.

Clinical implications

From a clinical standpoint, our findings reinforce several important messages. First, resuscitation of aSAH complicated by cardiac arrest should not systematically be considered as futile, as a very small but meaningful proportion of patients can recover with reasonable neurological function. Second, early neurological severity—particularly brainstem involvement—appears to be a more relevant prognostic signal than traditional cardiac arrest variables. Third, aggressive neurocritical care and timely aneurysm treatment may be justified in carefully selected patients, in accordance with contemporary guideline recommendations [5].

At the same time, the extremely low rate of favorable outcomes highlights the need for realistic and transparent discussions with proxies regarding prognosis, goals of care, and potential organ donation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design exposes it to missing data and unmeasured confounding. Among the notable missing data were the absence of information regarding rebleeding events, the lack of data on the delay between admission and aneurysm securing — a major component of management — and insufficient information regarding the target temperature used for targeted temperature management (TTM), which evolved during the study period [40].

The very small number of favorable outcomes limits statistical power and prevents robust identification of independent prognostic factors. Selection bias related to early treatment decisions and withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy is unavoidable and may have influenced both management strategies and outcomes.

Furthermore, participating centers were all specialized neuro-ICUs, which may limit generalizability to less specialized settings. In addition, the prognosis reported in this study may have been overestimated. Survival was null in a previous medical series of 73 SAH (Arnaout CCM 2015). First, the proportion of patients who died outside the hospital following cardiac arrest secondary to SAH is unknown. Second, we also lack information regarding patients who were not transferred to tertiary centers because of extremely poor prognostic features, leading clinicians to consider aggressive neurocritical care management futile. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and their generalizability to the overall population of patients with SAH-related cardiac arrest may be limited.

Finally, although this study followed current reporting standards for observational research, external validation in independent and larger cohorts is required before any prognostic model can be proposed for routine clinical use. A prospective registry with standardized data collection, particularly regarding prehospital management, aneurysm treatment timelines, neurocritical care strategies, and causes of treatment limitation, would likely help better clarify prognostic factors and reduce some of the selection biases observed in the present study.

Conclusion

In this large multicenter cohort of patients with OHCA-related aSAH, 6-month neurological outcomes were overwhelmingly poor, with fewer than 5% of patients achieving favorable recovery. Although the prognosis of this condition remains extremely severe, some selected patients may still survive with acceptable neurological outcomes following aggressive neurocritical care and early aneurysm treatment. At the same time, a substantial proportion of patients progressed to brain death, highlighting the potential importance of organ donation considerations in this population. Given the important methodological limitations and selection biases inherent to this study, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Further prospective collaborative registries are needed to better characterize this population, refine prognostic assessment, and support clinical decision-making.
