Robotics and Computer Navigation Do Not Offer Notable Benefits Compared with Manual Techniques for Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Alec Lachniet, Sara E. Strecker, Caitlin McCracken, Mark Shekhman, Dan Witmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Computer navigation and robotics have been introduced to total knee arthroplasty (TKA) to improve precision in component positioning and soft-tissue balancing. Both technologies have been heavily marketed to both surgeon and patients alike.Materials and Methods:This pragmatic, retrospective, single-center study compared outcomes for patients who underwent a primary unilateral TKA for osteoarthritis between January 2020 and December 2023. Surgical technique and implant selection were left to surgeon choice. All TKAs were performed using a medial parapatellar approach, with the majority being cemented. Overall, 4273 patients met eligibility criteria. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were collected using an electronic patient engagement platform preoperatively and up to 1 year. Linear and logistic regression modeling were used to assess potential confounders.Results:The use of computer navigation increased over the time frame assessed, whereas the use of robotics decreased. The majority (87%) of patients underwent a TKA using manual techniques. Noncemented fixation predominated in computer navigation and robotics; demographics reflected a younger, healthier, male patient population. No difference was noted in PROMs or complications among techniques. Costs differed markedly, with the robotic group being an average of $1500 more expensive, even when controlling for confounders.Conclusions:Robotics, but not computer-navigation, increased overall costs of TKA surgery without differences noted in complication rates or PROMs compared with standard instrumentation. This study is notable in that it is pragmatic, and no surgeon involved had industry ties. The best technique for modern TKA remains the one an individual surgeon is most comfortable with.

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is the most effective treatment for multicompartment end-stage osteoarthritis, with more than one million cases expected to be performed by 2030.1-3 Patient satisfaction for the TKA procedure itself is variable, with rates ranging from 70% to 90%4-6 and is highly dependent on the presence of pain or perceived instability.4,5 To improve patient satisfaction and reduce early TKA failures, like aseptic loosening, robotic arm–assisted technologies and computer-guided navigation systems have been introduced.3,5 These systems are designed to improve precision in implant component positioning and soft-tissue balancing,3,5 which is essential for implant longevity and subsequent patient satisfaction.1 With up to 19% of patients being dissatisfied with their TKA outcome, optimal alignment and balance is essential in maximizing patient success.1

There are a variety of alignment and balance philosophies in modern TKA.7,8 Whichever one of these the surgeon chooses, targets for bony resection and balance must be set. Manual instrumentation uses a surgeons optical and tactile feedback in combination with bony jigs to set the cuts during the procedure. Computer-assisted navigation techniques use intraoperatively placed landmarks to achieve this goal but do not measure ligament balance. Robotic-assisted platforms use active robotic arms under a surgeon's direction to set the cuts of the procedure typically based on measuring preresection ligament tension. Robotic platforms may use image-based or imageless technology, which may affect the accuracy of preresection planning between the systems.9

Robotic-assisted TKA and computer-navigated TKA may offer benefits over standard TKA techniques to minimize human error and subsequently increase patient outcomes and recovery by reducing alignment outliers, while improving balancing and knee kinematics.5,6 However, controversy surrounds the relative benefits of each, as few benefits have been seen over conventional TKA in terms of patient functional outcomes or satisfaction rates despite improved radiographic accuracy.10,11 Like robotic TKA, computer-navigated TKA is associated with higher cost and longer operating time.12,13 In addition, many of the studies reporting beneficial outcomes of robotic-assisted or computer-navigated TKA are done by investigators with financial ties to the companies making and/or marketing these systems.14

The purpose of this study is to evaluate surgical outcomes, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), complications, and costs associated with the use of computer navigation or robotics at a high-volume orthopedic center, which does not receive funding from any manufacturer. We hypothesize that outcomes and complications will remain similar, although costs are increased in the computer-navigated or robotic use group.

Methods

This was a retrospective, single-center cohort study, of patients who underwent a TKA using computer navigation, or robotic techniques at our institution. Surgeries were performed by nine fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeons at a high-volume urban center between January 2020 and December 2023. This was a pragmatic study representative of a large practice, with all TKAs being performed through a medial parapatellar approach using three major implant manufacturers and a variety of polyethylene insert designs. Most manual TKAs were cemented, and a larger percentage of navigated and robotic TKAs used noncemented fixation. The robotic system used was the Mako system (Stryker), and the computer navigation system used was Intellijoint (Intellijoint Surgical). If the surgeon performed less than 10 surgeries using a particular technique, the patient’s data were excluded from the analysis. All surgeons used manual techniques, two of the nine also used computer navigation, and six of the nine also used robotics. Tourniquet use varied by surgeon and technique. All surgeries were performed under the same spinal anesthesia and regional nerve block protocol unless contraindicated, with the use of computer navigation or robotics up to the discretion of the surgeon. All patients followed the same postoperative physical therapy and multimodal pain management protocol. Patient-reported outcomes were collected using FORCE therapeutics patient engagement app (FORCE Therapeutics). This study was approved by our institutional review board (HHC—2024 -0223).

Patients included in this study were between 18 and 89 years of age who underwent a unilateral TKA for primary or posttraumatic osteoarthritis. Revision or conversion TKAs were excluded, as were patients who had a TKA due to fracture or cancer. Patients with osteonecrosis or inflammatory arthritis were excluded. Patients were also excluded if they were on long-term opiates for pain control, as morphine milligram equivalents while inpatient were calculated. The first 25 surgeries that a surgeon did with a new technique were also excluded to account for any learning curve. Overall, there were 4273 patients who met eligibility criteria. All patients were optimized before surgery.

The Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement, PROM Information System, and the Forgotten Joint Score were assessed preoperatively, at 12 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively, as is standard of care, through the use of a patient engagement platform, FORCE Therapeutics. Patient data, including surgical and implant information, as well as the presence of a complication or emergency department (ED) visit within the first 90 days postsurgery, were collected using the patient electronic medical record, EPIC (EPIC Systems).

Categorical variables, like sex, race/ethnicity, skilled nursing facility (SNF) discharge, tourniquet usage, the use of a noncemented construct, or the presence of a complication, were compared using chi-squared tests or Fisher exact tests. Continuous variables, like ambulation distance or other physical therapy parameters, length of stay, surgical time (incision to close), total cost, or PROMs, were compared using large one-way analysis of variances. Costs were adjusted to account for the year of surgery. Total hospital cost was rounded to the nearest $500. Implant and supply costs were rounded to the nearest $250. Univariate logistic and linear regression models were used to assess confounders in the data. Generalized linear modeling was used to determine factors that influenced PROMs and what influenced hospital billing costs. All analyses were done using Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation) or Stata version 17 (StataCorp), and results with a P < 0.05 were determined to be statistically significant.

Results

Between 2020 and 2023, approximately 1000 patients were treated per year, as seen in Table 1. An increase in the use of computer navigation from 1.6% in 2020 to 11.7% in 2023 was observed. The use of robotics decreased in that time frame from 10% in 2020 to 6.2% in 2023. Overall, 3709 patients underwent a TKA using manual techniques, 251 underwent a TKA using computer navigation, and 313 underwent a TKA using robotics.

TKA patients in the computer navigation group reported higher pain both at rest and with activity than patients in the other two groups, as seen in Table 4. When controlling for confounding variables, like the use of cement, and tourniquet use through linear regression modeling, inpatient pain with activity was not statistically significantly different between the groups, although inpatient pain at rest remained statistically significantly higher in the computer navigation group. Younger age and male sex were the primary drivers for higher pain. Although pain was higher overall in the computer navigation and robotic groups; all physical therapy parameters assessed, like ambulation distance, timed up and go test, and the 30-second sit to stand test were all equivalent. Patients in the computer navigation group were 1.8× more likely (95% CI, 1.07 to 3.00) to go to a SNF; however, there were striking differences in RAPT scores in patients who went to a SNF (6.06) versus those who did not (9.37) indicative of more comorbidities in the computer navigation group. Even with this variability in preoperative RAPT scores, no differences were found in return to the ED, readmission, or revision surgery rates among cohorts.

The total average billed costs of the patient's hospital stay, implant costs, and supply costs can be seen in Table 6. Cemented implants were also compared with noncemented implants. Overall, there were no differences >$250 in the implant cost for patients operated on with manual techniques as compared with patients where computer navigation or robotics were used. Supply cost was approximately doubled for both computer navigation and robotics cases. Generalized linear modeling showed that the type of surgery (manual, computer navigated, or robotic), the surgical time, patient age, patient sex, and length of stay were markedly associated with the total billed costs, whereas the surgeon, the use of cement, or the RAPT score were not notable factors influencing the total billed cost.

Discussion

Multiple studies have found no differences in outcomes between manual, computer navigation, and robotic techniques, especially among high-volume arthroplasty surgeons.9,15 Robotics has been heavily marketed to both the surgeon and the consumer but costs more.16 Much of the current literature is heavily biased due to financial relationships with industry from the senior authors. This is a pragmatic study with multiple brands and types of TKAs used, allowing for generalization. This broad distribution of brands and implantation techniques is because no surgeon in our cohort has financial relationships with industry, and the surgeons are actively testing new systems to see what works best for our patient population.

When adjusted for age, sex, and tourniquet usage, no differences were observed in 1-year outcomes between the manual, computer-navigated, and robotic-assisted groups. Computer navigation and robotics were more likely to be used on younger, male patients. These groups had noncemented implants used more often, as is typical for younger age groups with modern TKA.17 No differences were seen in ED visits, all causes readmissions, or revision surgeries between these groups.

Patients in the computer-navigated group were 1.8 times more likely to be discharged to a SNF, although regression modeling indicates that this is likely due to the unique makeup of this population and for the propensity for the surgeons who used computer navigation to take on sicker patients who have a low RAPT score. Costs for manual techniques and computer navigation were similar, likely driven by the reduced use of cement in the computer navigation group, as the authors believe that the cost of the cement is offsetting the cost of the disposables used for computer navigation. Robotic surgeries were approximately $1000 more expensive than computer navigation and $1500 more expensive compared with a manual technique, and robotic surgeries had a markedly longer surgical time, which was consistent across multiple surgeons. Our data, showing no differences in patient outcomes, call into question the value of robotics in a high-volume setting, especially as payors begin to tie reimbursement of outcome scores.

Our study has potential limitations. This was a retrospective review of a large population where all patients came from the same institution. The population was predominately non-Hispanic White and older than 65 years. In addition, a smaller number of computer-navigated and robotic-assisted surgeries were reported compared with manual techniques. Furthermore, the study design did not allow for accurately determining technique setup time between computer navigation, robotic, and manual techniques, as surgical time was from incision to close. Patients were followed for 1 year post TKA for the purposes of this study. Longer-term follow-up may show differences in techniques in terms of implant survivability.

In conclusion, even in the hands of multiple surgeons, no differences were observed in outcomes between patients who had a TKA using a manual technique, a computer-navigated technique, or with the assistance of robotics. Companies continue to push out patient facing documents highlighting the benefits of having surgeons use their specific technology, and surgeons face increasing pressure to improve patient satisfaction, but the best technique likely varies between surgeons due a number of factors, including experience, training, and comfort with new technology. Robotics, while an exciting new frontier, appears to add notable cost without a benefit to PROMs or complications rates, especially in the context of high-volume hospitals.
