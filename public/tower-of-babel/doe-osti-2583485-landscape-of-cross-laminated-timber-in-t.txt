1 Introduction
Mass timber construction is emerging as a cornerstone for sustainable and innovative
construction practices in the United States. Mass timber is a renewable option to mainstream
construction practices given its ability to store carbon throughout its use. Through the passage of
the International Building Code, the use of mass timber beyond low-rise residential construction
has expanded to mid- and high- rise multi-story buildings.

A popular type of mass timber construction, cross-laminated timber (CLT), is a wood panel
system that is manufactured by gluing alternating lumber board layers at right angles. Because of
this structure, CLT panels have high strength, dimensional stability, and the ability to resist high
compressive forces. They are considered a sustainable alternative to reinforced concrete
construction because of the renewable nature of the primary wood material in CLT panels.

Despite its environmental and structural benefits, the adoption rate of CLT remains low. This
review investigates the current landscape of CLT use in buildings, providing information on the
types of building that use CLT, how those buildings are used, and in what U.S. regions CLT is
manufactured and used. This information helps identify potential barriers to adoption to inform
ways to reduce these barriers. This review synthesizes insights from recent studies to offer a
detailed perspective on the state of CLT adoption, its barriers, and the evolving landscape of
opportunities that may facilitate its broader acceptance and use.

2.2.1 Timber Requirements for CLT Production
Assuming a demand of 180,600 m3 CLT by 2030 in the northwestern United States, current CLT
production capacity of 212,000 m3 is already 20% higher in this region than the projected
demand. Increased and sustained CLT demand is thus required to operate the current CLT
facilities at their 2-day shift capacity. However, this demand can only be established when CLT
manufacturers provide robust supply chains. There is a fine balance between growing CLT
demand and the CLT manufacturing industry while preventing supply issues or oversaturation.
Vertically integrated CLT companies, which can execute activities at multiple steps of the CLT
chain, including timber sourcing, CLT manufacturing, and installation in CLT buildings, have
been suggested as a possible solution to this issue because they can forecast and satisfy their own
timber and CLT panel requirements (Brandt et al. 2021).

Softwood lumber is currently used for structural-grade CLT products. There are some major
differences in softwood, which is sourced from gymnosperm trees, and hardwood, which is
sourced from angiosperm trees. In general, softwood is easier to process into products, has a
lower density, lower cost, grows at a relatively faster rate, and has a lower resistance to fire.
Yew, cedar, pine, Douglas fir, and spruce are commonly known softwood species. Hardwood, on
the other hand, has a higher density, cost, and resistance to fire, but grows at a slower rate and is
difficult to process. Common species of hardwood include ash, oak, walnut, maple, mahogany,
and alder (Goto 2024). Sawmills in the states east of the Rocky Mountains produce more than

 6

6,000 million board feet of hardwood, while those on the west side produce less than 500 million
board feet of hardwood annually (Lang 2023).

According to the ANSI/APA PRG-320 standard, softwood species with specific gravity higher
than 0.35 are acceptable for use in structural CLT panels. Most hardwood species have a higher
specific gravity and also satisfy the moisture content requirement for CLT, but they are not an
accepted structural CLT raw material according to the standard (Quesada 2019). Some reasons
for this include the irregular width in hardwood production, additional pressure and processing
time requirements, naturally occurring chemicals in the wood that could interfere with the glue,
higher weight of hardwood CLT panels, or requirements of different tools or energy for
machining. It could also be difficult for a 50,000 m3 CLT planned building to establish a reliable
and steady supply of enough hardwood lumber (Quesada 2019). However, based on recent
research, the use of hardwood lumber in structural CLT is a possibility, and CLT manufacturers
are optimistic about the prospect (Adhikari et al. 2020).

The northwestern United States produces about 3.4 million m3 of lumber annually, much more
than the CLT production volume in this region. However, even today in the Unites States,
softwood production is less than consumption, and softwood must be imported. Thus, when this
demand rises, lumber demand might constrain the growth of CLT. Conversely, domestic
hardwood production is more than domestic consumption (Adhikari et al., 2020).

2.3 Barriers to CLT Manufacturing
2.3.1 Technical and Production Challenges
CLT manufacturing requires precision manufacturing and stringent quality control, making the
production process complex and cost-intensive. Brandner et al. (2015) emphasize the need for
harmonized global standards as the next decade brings increasing production volume for CLT.
Especially in countries such as Japan, Canada, and the United States, developing standardization
processes will help bolster CLT’s role as a structural material regardless of regional variations in
the sourcing and manufacturing capabilities. For load-bearing systems specifically, Brandner et
al. emphasize a need to establish a CLT strength class system for better consistency in its
characteristic properties. In their engineering guide for wood frame construction, the Canadian
Wood Council (2014) discuss the complexities involved in the lamination process, which
requires advanced machinery and skilled operators to ensure that the panels meet safety and
performance standards. Because CLT requires a larger volume of wood material than traditional
stick frame construction, its cost is higher and its performance relies on proper adhesion during a
more complex manufacturing process than other building materials.

2.3.2 Economic and Market Dynamics
Economic factors such as fluctuating timber prices significantly impact CLT manufacturing.
Timber is a primary raw material for CLT, and its price volatility can affect final production
costs unpredictably. Brandt et al. (2019) provide a detailed analysis using modeled CLT mills of
various scales to demonstrate how variations in lumber prices influence the economics of CLT
production. This analysis notes that higher raw material costs can make CLT less competitive
against traditional building materials and that these costs often depend on the manufacturing
facility’s location. Typically, the minimum selling price of CLT is dictated by asset utilization,
capital costs, discount rates, and lumber costs for production facilities. Financially, running the

 7

facilities at full production volume for 24/7 shifts on the shortest possible press cycles is ideal
(Brandt et al. 2019). For example, choosing the lowest cost lumber (southern pine) and locating
the facility in the southeast can drop the minimum selling price of CLT from $536/m3 to
$518/m3. Furthermore, the market for CLT is also challenged by established preferences for
concrete and steel, especially in regions without strong timber industry support (Mallo and
Espinoza 2014).

2.3.3 Supply Chain and Resource Availability
Securing a consistent and quality supply of timber is crucial for CLT manufacturers. Hassler et
al. (2022) highlight the competition for high-quality wood, not only from other wood product
sectors but also from bioenergy industries, which can strain the supply chain and elevate costs.
The supply chain dynamics for softwoods are generally more favorable for CLT production.
Softwood plantations, particularly those involving species such as pine, spruce, and Douglas fir,
have been a staple in the timber industry because of their rapid growth and consistent quality.
These species are commonly used in CLT manufacturing, with spruce-pine-fir and Douglas fir-
larch lumber being the most typical choices for CLT panels because of their strength and
availability (He et al. 2018). Softwoods such as spruce and pine are easier to process and bond,
simplifying manufacturing logistics. Plantation softwood, particularly pine, is considered the
future timber material resource for building and construction, with significant volumes of sawn
softwood available for structural applications (Cherry et al. 2019). However, even lower-grade
softwoods, such as out-of-grade pine, are being considered for CLT production, which could
maximize yield from existing plantations and offer new opportunities for using timber that was
previously sold at a loss (Cherry et al. 2019). Other challenges remain, including the need to
standardize the quality and dimensions of softwood boards and to optimize the supply chain to
meet the increasing demand for softwood CLT panels (Skjerstad et al. 2021).

For hardwoods to be used in the CLT industry, significant changes in the supply chain are
needed. One of the primary issues is that many hardwood sawmills lack gang saws capable of
producing the fixed-width boards necessary for CLT production, leading to inconsistencies in the
dimensions of hardwood boards. This lack of uniformity makes it difficult to assemble CLT
panels that meet the required structural standards. Unlike softwoods, hardwoods require precise,
consistent dimensions for effective bonding and assembly, and without this standardization,
manufacturing becomes inefficient (Hassler et al. 2022). Additionally, hardwoods pose further
challenges because of their more complex bonding requirements, which often necessitate
specialized adhesives that are not widely available or optimized for CLT production. These
bonding difficulties add another layer of complexity to hardwood CLT manufacturing (Hassler et
al. 2022).

Moreover, logistical challenges such as transportation and handling of large timber sections add
complexity to the supply chain, affecting the overall sustainability of CLT manufacturing
operations (Espinoza et al. 2015).

2.3.4 Labor Market and Workforce Development
The specialized nature of CLT manufacturing requires a skilled workforce, proficient in modern
woodworking techniques and familiar with CLT’s unique properties. A study by De Araujo and
Christoforo (2023) highlights that the lack of trained professionals is a significant barrier to the
growth of the CLT industry, particularly in regions without established educational programs for

 8

advanced timber technologies. They emphasize the importance of increasing training and
education to support the industry’s expansion globally (De Araujo and Christoforo 2023). These
educational programs need implementation at all levels of the CLT labor market, from design
engineers to construction workers.

In university settings, particularly, increasing the knowledge of CLT manufacturing and
construction among faculty can build an educated and experienced student cohort. A program
developed by Manrique and Haglund (2019) in the Pacific Northwest highlights how
incorporating CLT-focused education in architecture programs enables students to design and
execute CLT projects. As these individuals move into professional organizations such as
architectural and engineering firms, they share collective knowledge on building and executing
successful CLT projects, contributing to the industry’s growth (Manrique and Haglund 2019).
Amplifying the successes of CLT construction projects to a wider audience will also increase the
market’s familiarity with CLT (Harris et al. 2022).

Anecdotally, CLT installation can take a third of the time of an installation with traditional
materials such as light-gauge steel (Hanes 2019), offering the potential for CLT to substantially
increase construction labor productivity. However, there is limited information in the literature
on how CLT impacts the construction labor market directly. Forsythe and Sepasgozar (2019)
suggest that prefabricated timber systems such as CLT improve installation productivity because
of mechanized processes, such as crane use, allowing faster onsite assembly compared to
traditional methods. However, the training and development of a skilled workforce remain
critical for scaling up CLT production and construction without compromising quality (Forsythe
and Sepasgozar 2019).

2.3.5 Regional Economic Support
The level of support from local economies can greatly influence the success of CLT
manufacturing. Supportive policies, such as subsidies for sustainable construction materials or
tax incentives for using local timber, can enhance the viability of CLT projects. Chen et al.
(2019) discuss how regional support in the Pacific Northwest, a region with a robust forestry
sector, has facilitated the growth of CLT through targeted educational and infrastructural
investments. For example, Washington used a state-funded pilot project to build three schools
out of CLT for roughly $1 million each (Washington Forest Protection Association 2017). This
program showcased the advantage of using CLT panels, where a four-classroom school could be
erected in a single day. Southern Oregon has also led the development of large-scale CLT
construction, building and investing in the first certified U.S. manufacturing facility for CLT
panels with the help of the U.S. Economic Development Administration (2023). This region,
with its forestry industry and innovative pilot programs, has taken the lead in large-scale CLT
construction in the United States.

3 Building Construction
3.1 CLT as a Construction Material
CLT is emerging as a leading construction material in modern architecture because of its
exceptional structural properties, sustainability, and cost-effectiveness. As the demand for more
ecofriendly, efficient, and resilient construction methods rises, CLT is being increasingly
adopted for projects ranging from residential to commercial and institutional buildings. This
section explores the key reasons why CLT is chosen as a construction material, emphasizing its
structural advantages, environmental benefits, and economic potential.

3.1.1 Construction and Structural Advantages of CLT
CLT is increasingly chosen for construction projects because of its remarkable structural
benefits. Among the primary reasons for its selection are its high strength-to-weight ratio and its
ability to provide enhanced seismic resistance, which is particularly valuable in earthquake-prone
regions. The inherent stiffness and in-plane strength of CLT panels, coupled with the ductility of
their connections, afford buildings a superior performance under seismic conditions, as noted by
Izzi et al. (2018) and Sandoli et al. (2021). The seismic design methodologies and innovations in
connections for CLT structures have also evolved, ensuring better resilience and safety during
earthquakes (Pei et al. 2013, Tannert et al. 2018). Additionally, Brandner et al. (2015) highlight
that CLT buildings can be constructed more rapidly than those using traditional materials, which
is valuable in reconstruction in regions prone to earthquakes. Connections between panels,
however, play a crucial role in maintaining structural integrity, particularly in regions prone to
seismic activity. Therefore, designers must carefully select and implement connection systems
when using CLT (Mohammad et al. 2012). The challenges in ensuring the seismic performance
of CLT structures remain significant despite advances in design and connection technologies.
For instance, conventional steel connections, such as hold-downs and angle brackets, continue to
pose risks because of their susceptibility to brittle failures under seismic loads. This has led to
the development of innovative connection systems with better ductility and energy dissipation
properties, improving seismic resilience (Li and Tsavdaridis 2023). However, these new designs
introduce complexity in manufacturing, raising concerns about scalability and cost-efficiency.

Moreover, experimental studies have highlighted the need for more comprehensive design
strategies that account for both axial and shear interactions in connections, which are essential
for mitigating lateral displacements during seismic events. These interactions, often overlooked
in traditional designs, are now recognized as crucial for ensuring structural integrity under
earthquake conditions (Pozza et al. 2018).

Additionally, research on the out-of-plane behavior of CLT connections, which is critical in
multihazard environments, has shown gaps in knowledge. Connections must be designed to
transfer loads effectively, yet this remains a challenge, particularly in high-rise constructions or
regions prone to earthquakes, hurricanes, or other natural hazards (Salgado and Guner 2021).

Thus, while the adoption of CLT can be driven by increased global standardization and design
provisions, overcoming these persistent connection challenges is key to broader application and
cost-effectiveness (Tannert et al. 2018).

3.1.2 Demand Driven by Carbon Reduction Goals
The adoption of CLT is also motivated by the increasing consumer demand for sustainable and
low-carbon building solutions. Liu et al. (2016) demonstrate that using CLT can reduce the
energy consumption and CO2 emissions of buildings, supporting the sustainability objectives of
various stakeholders including builders, investors, and end users who are committed to
environmental stewardship. In this study, they used life cycle energy analysis to determine the
energy consumption of buildings built with CLT compared to those built out of other materials
such as concrete. Their results demonstrate that buildings built with CLT had more than 30%
lower life cycle primary energy consumption than equivalent buildings using concrete. One of
the main reasons this occurs is because of the material production, transportation, and onsite
erection stages. Timber requires less energy to extract compared to other materials. Additionally,
in the operation stage, it was found that the buildings with CLT have better opaque envelope
performance (better insulation) compared to the others; therefore, they need less energy for
heating. Finally, in the end-of-life stage, the authors assume that parts of the CLT buildings
could be recycled and therefore offset carbon emissions (Liu et al. 2016).

CLT’s ability to sequester carbon can contribute to reducing the embodied carbon of buildings,
another factor of interest in the sustainable building sector (Lehmann 2012). For example, a
comparative life cycle assessment of a 12-story mass timber building in Portland, Oregon, used
the EN 15978 framework to evaluate environmental impacts for stages A1–A5 (product to
construction), B2, B4, and B6 (use), C1–C4 (end of life), and D (beyond the building life). The
analysis demonstrated an 18% reduction in global warming potential compared to a similar
concrete building (Chen et al. 2020). Similarly, another study that surveyed similar life cycle
stages indicated that low-energy versions of CLT buildings can reduce life cycle carbon
emissions by up to 9% (Dodoo et al. 2014).

The environmental benefits are further supported by dynamic life cycle modeling frameworks,
which account for forest management and manufacturing variations. These models show that
CLT can achieve significant net greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions reductions, ranging from 954
to 1,445 metric tons CO2 equivalent per hectare in high-productivity scenarios (Lan et al. 2020).
Moreover, if forests are managed sustainably, wood and timber products are often classified as
sustainable materials from their renewability and less energy-intensive processing during
manufacturing (Rasmussen et al. 2021).

In Jayalath et al. (2020), they investigate the life cycle GHG emissions and life cycle cost of a
mid-to-high rise building of CLT construction located in three major cities in Australia. The
GHG emissions of the CLT building in the cradle-to-gate phases are 50% lower than those of the
equivalent concrete building. The operational GHG emissions of the CLT building depended on
location, being less than those of the concrete building in most locations and more in some
locations. Ultimately, this reduction in GHG emissions for buildings of CLT construction can be
a pathway to greener buildings, aligning with new stakeholder values.

A life cycle assessment analysis by Atnoorkar et al. (2024) in the United States compared the
energy use and emissions of CLT buildings in the United States to reinforced concrete
construction. The authors concluded that CLT buildings can reduce the cradle-to-gate energy use
and emissions compared to reinforced concrete construction. However, if CLT supply chains
span long distances (over 6,000 kilometers (km)), the resulting transportation emissions can

 11

increase emissions of the CLT buildings beyond that of reinforced concrete buildings. These
results show that both the siting and the CLT material sourcing for new construction can inform
the ultimate GHG reduction potential of CLT construction.

3.1.3 Cost Effectiveness of CLT Construction
The rapid construction capabilities of CLT, because of its prefabrication, significantly reduce
onsite building time, which can lead to cost savings in labor and project management (Hindman
and Bouldin 2015, Li and Tsavdaridis 2023). Many studies showcase the rapid onsite
construction of buildings using CLT materials. Construction may be as short as 3–4 days per
story, taking as little as 3–4 months total for buildings of up to nine stories. This is half the time
compared to traditional construction materials, such as concrete, which may take up to 28 days
per story (Mallo and Espinoza 2015). Moreover, in another study conducted by Mallo and
Espinoza (2016), it was found that the construction cost of CLT was generally lower at $5–
$20/ft2 compared to traditional materials such as concrete at $14–$40/ft2. However, more recent
studies indicate that this may no longer be universally true. Ahmed and Arocho (2021) found that
the construction cost of mass timber buildings, including CLT, was approximately 6.43% higher
than that of comparable concrete buildings. Similarly, a study by Gu et al. (2020) reported that
the front-end construction costs for a mass timber building were 26% higher than those of a
reinforced concrete alternative. It is worth noting that despite the higher initial costs, the same
study showed that CLT buildings could have a 2.4% lower total life cycle cost because of their
longer lifespan and higher end-of-life salvage value. This indicates that while CLT may offer
long-term financial benefits and sustainability advantages, the upfront construction costs are
often higher compared to more traditional materials. This is backed up by other studies such as
Clemans et al. (2017) that argue that the reduced construction time and lower labor requirements
can offset the higher upfront costs, rendering CLT a cost-effective solution in the long term.
Moreover, Jayalath et al. (2020) provide a life cycle cost analysis showing that CLT buildings
can be more economical over their lifespan because of lower life cycle GHG emissions and
maintenance costs. In the life cycle cost analysis of the study, it was found that most elements of
the CLT building had room for cost savings, leading to the concrete building being 8%–10%
more costly during initial construction. Furthermore, during operation, the CLT cost savings
were dependent on location but were found to be 13%–16% higher than the concrete building.
The study highlights that the energy performance of these buildings is directly related to the
outdoor weather conditions of each location, which in turn affect the operational energy
consumption. This is particularly evident in the comparison of heating and cooling demands,
which vary based on the climate of each city. However, it was identified that the recycling cost
of CLT compared to the concrete building was more favorable because of the lighter weight of
CLT. Overall, the total life cycle cost of the CLT building is 0.9%–13% lower than the concrete
building (Jayalath et. al. 2020).

To effectively manage the initial material and construction costs of CLT, it is crucial to integrate
CLT considerations early in the design process. Significant cost savings can be realized when the
design is optimized specifically for CLT, rather than treating mass timber as a direct one-to-one
replacement for traditional materials.

3.2 CLT and Environmental Product Declarations
3.2.1 Summary of EPD Information
Environmental Product Declarations (EPDs) are standardized documents providing data on the
environmental impacts of products. EPDs can cover various stages of a product’s life cycle,
including raw material extraction, manufacturing, transportation, and sometimes end-of-life
scenarios, depending on the product category rule that governs that product. EPDs are developed
following international standards such as ISO 14025 environmental labels and declarations and
EN 15804 Sustainability of construction works, Environmental product declarations, Core rules
for the product category of construction products. However, product category rules are
developed uniquely for different products and may include different life cycle stages for different
products, making it difficult to compare EPD results across products (Gelowitz and McArthur
2017). EN 15804 was introduced in 2012 and provides stricter guidance for comparison,
focusing specifically on construction products and offering detailed rules for scope, allocation,
and impact categories (Durão et al. 2020). The stricter regulations under EN 15804 aim to
address some of the comparability issues seen with ISO 14025, enhancing the consistency of
EPDs across the construction industry (Fet and Skaar 2006). Since the implementation of EN
15804, the number of available EPDs has significantly grown, reflecting its influence in
standardizing environmental assessments for construction materials (Achenbach et al. 2016).

3.2.2 Prevalence of EPDs for CLT and Mass Timber Products and Their Impact
Many CLT and mass timber products have EPDs, providing detailed information on their carbon
footprint and other environmental impacts. EPDs for these products often cover the cradle-to-
gate life cycle stages, which include raw material extraction, transportation, and manufacturing
processes. Some studies have expanded to cradle-to-grave assessments, considering the entire
life cycle of the building (Liu et al. 2016).

A comprehensive review of 81 third-party verified EPDs for CLT and other mass timber
products (e.g., glulam and laminated veneer lumber) highlights the variability in reporting and
results among different products. The analysis includes the production phase, construction
process phase, the end-of-life phase, and the benefits and loads of the various materials. The
study found that the global warming potential varies significantly among products with median
global warming potential values for CLT, glulam, and timber at 0.26, 0.24, and 0.17 kilograms
CO2e per kilogram of product, respectively, because of differences in density and end-of-life
scenarios (Rasmussen et al. 2021).

EPDs have become essential tools for designers and architects, enabling them to make informed
decisions during the early stages of building design. This integration of sustainability from the
outset helps ensure that environmental considerations are embedded throughout the project
lifecycle (Pichette et al. 2023). The use of EPDs in green building rating systems, such as LEED,
has further promoted their adoption, because they provide a standardized approach to assessing
and comparing the environmental performance of construction materials (Gelowitz and
McArthur 2016).

 13

3.2.3 Challenges of EPDs
While EPDs provide valuable information, they are not without limitations. The variability in
EPD data, stemming from differences in third-party verification processes and the assumptions
used in life cycle assessments, can make it difficult to compare products directly. For example,
different EPDs may use varying end-of-life scenarios, leading to inconsistencies in reported
impacts (Del Rosario et al. 2021). EPDs for CLT are highly sensitive to regional differences
because of variations in forestry practices, manufacturing processes, and transportation logistics.
These regional factors can significantly influence the environmental performance of CLT,
particularly in terms of GHG emissions. Additionally, the validity period of an EPD, typically 5
years, may limit its usefulness over time, especially as production processes and environmental
impacts evolve (Božiček et al. 2021).

Results from life cycle cradle-to-gate assessment studies of CLT production underscore the
necessity of incorporating region-specific data into EPDs to provide accurate assessments of
environmental impacts. A life cycle assessment study of CLT production in the southeastern
United States demonstrated that higher forest productivity in the region leads to lower net GHG
emissions over time. The study also showed that the heat source used for lumber drying and
efficient management of wood wastes are both critical to minimizing the environmental impact
of CLT production (Lan et al. 2020). Similarly, a life cycle assessment of CLT production in
western Washington revealed that optimizing transportation routes and using a mix of locally
sourced wood species can reduce the global warming potential of CLT (Chen et al. 2019).
However Atnoorkar et al. (2024) show that if transportation distances exceed certain limits,
emissions from CLT buildings can be more than that of comparable reinforced concrete
buildings. Furthermore, a comprehensive review of 81 EPDs for structural wood products,
including CLT, across different regions showed that variations in global warming potential are
often influenced by regional production practices and the end-of-life scenarios applied. The
study found that in regions with well-established regulations, such as Canada, the United States,
Australia, and New Zealand, the guidance provided by EN 15804 is particularly useful.
However, even with standardized frameworks, the need for region-specific data remains critical
to ensure accurate and reliable environmental assessments (Rasmussen et al. 2021).

Finally, the availability of EPDs varies significantly among products, with some materials having
extensive documentation while others have little to none. This inconsistency can hinder the
broader adoption of EPDs in the construction industry, particularly in regions where EPDs are
less prevalent (Passer et al. 2015). As the industry continues to evolve, there is a growing need
for harmonization and standardization of EPDs to enhance their reliability and comparability
across different products and regions.

3.3 Barriers to the Widespread Use of CLT
The current state of CLT adoption in the United States is influenced by its market maturity,
cultural and educational barriers, and economic factors. The construction industry’s conservative
nature and the initial costs associated with transitioning to CLT-based construction are
significant deterrents. Key challenges to CLT adoption in the United States span knowledge and
information gaps, economic and manufacturing barriers, and regulatory and standardization
issues. Recent studies have added dimensions to these challenges, including acoustic
performance considerations in CLT structures (Islam and Chen 2023). The importance of

 14

incorporating CLT into U.S. seismic design codes (Furley 2023, van de Lindt, Koliou, and
Bahmani 2023) in addition to the exploration of hybrid CLT structures, which layer hardwoods
and softwoods to improve CLT strength and stiffness (Satir 2023), further complicate the
adoption landscape and underscore the need for multifaceted solutions.

Section 2.1 showed that the adoption of CLT buildings has differed by regions across the United
States. The CLT industry in the United States is growing but remains a small contributor to new
construction. O’Neill (2023) pointed to barriers such as a lack of technical information,
unavailability of CLT panels in the market, high construction cost, and construction industry
economics, which can prevent CLT adoption in a regional market. This section analyzes
challenges and barriers to CLT adoption in the United States interspersed with regional
implications.

3.3.1 Regulatory Barriers
Building codes and regulations in the United States have been slow to fully accommodate the use
of CLT, particularly in high-rise construction. The 2021 International Building Code has only
recently begun to include provisions for mass timber construction—it has allowed three new
construction types to use CLT in buildings with 9–18 floors. Even though it is a step forward,
only some states and cities have adopted this code (“Status of Building Code Allowances for Tall
Mass Timber in the IBC,” February 24). CLT is not approved for use in seismic U.S. regions and
can require individual approval for buildings and certified testing of connections, making it less
competitive compared to traditional construction materials (van de Lindt et al. 2023). The
variability in code adoption across states and municipalities complicates the design and approval
process for CLT projects.

3.3.2 Supply Chain Limitations
The nascent state of the CLT supply chain in the United States poses significant challenges to its
adoption. Limited manufacturing capacity and the concentration of suppliers in certain
geographic areas contribute to higher costs and longer procurement times (Clemans et al. 2017,
Karacabeyli and Douglas 2013). Expanding the domestic production of CLT and developing a
robust supply chain are essential for reducing costs and ensuring the material’s competitiveness
with traditional construction options (Clemans et al. 2017, Karacabeyli and Douglas 2013).
Because CLT construction is highly dependent on a robust CLT manufacturing industry, barriers
faced by the CLT manufacturing industry are also important to analyze. Thus, the relationship
between the CLT manufacturing and construction industry is highly circular—a strong demand
for CLT buildings translates to a stronger CLT manufacturing industry and vice versa, and
regions that encourage CLT adoption could see the development of a robust CLT industry and
market.

3.3.3 Fire Safety Concerns
Fire safety concerns remain a significant barrier to the adoption of CLT, especially for high-rise
buildings. While research has demonstrated CLT’s inherent fire resistance and the potential for
protective char to insulate and preserve the structural integrity of timber during a fire, these
findings have yet to fully alleviate regulatory and public safety concerns (National Fire Research
Laboratory 2019). Further research and development of fire protection strategies for CLT
buildings are crucial for addressing these concerns. It was found that the acceptance of CLT as a

 15

fire-resistant material in the European market faces challenges, particularly because of concerns
about its fire performance among professionals. (Espinoza et al 2015).

3.3.4 Industry Education and Standards Development
The lack of familiarity with CLT among architects, engineers, and builders, as well as building
code officials, limits its adoption. There is a pressing need for comprehensive educational
programs and the development of detailed design and construction standards to facilitate the
broader use of CLT. These efforts should aim to demonstrate the material’s versatility,
performance, and benefits, thereby increasing confidence in CLT as a viable construction
material (Karacabeyli and Gagnon 2019). A survey of European CLT manufacturers also
identified a low awareness of CLT among architects and engineers as a primary barrier to
adoption, driving weak demand for CLT (Quesada et al. 2018).

3.3.5 Environmental Impact and Sustainability Questions
The environmental and sustainability benefits of CLT, particularly its potential for carbon
sequestration, are well documented. However, concerns about the long-term ecological impacts
of increased timber harvesting for mass timber construction, including deforestation and
biodiversity loss, warrant careful consideration and management. Sustainable forest management
practices and life cycle assessment studies are critical for ensuring that the environmental
benefits of CLT are realized (Brandt et al. 2019).

3.3.6 Regional Economics, Timber Supply, and Climate
Regional factors can affect the CLT manufacturing and construction industries. For example, the
northwestern states of Oregon and Washington produce more than 25% of the U.S. softwood
lumber (Cloughesy 2016), and this presence of the timber industry could have given a particular
impetus to CLT manufacturing and use in the region. Surveyed CLT manufacturers identified
inadequate reliable timber sources, getting CLT products certified, lack of trained and CLT-
aware local staff, and transporting CLT products outside the primary market as important
regional barriers in addition to a robust CLT market demand (Quesada et al. 2018).

Location-related factors play a role in the viability and ease of use of CLT as a construction
material. For example, CLT lends itself well to just-in-time delivery, and thus can reduce
construction time and costs in dense urban areas (Hanes 2019). However, CLT projects can be
hampered by seismicity of a region or water and humidity concerns. Water and moisture damage
can also lead to warping, rotting, and mold buildup in wood materials. This issue can be
addressed effectively by the appropriate methods, even in high humidity regions. Even then,
humidity and moisture considerations could influence where CLT buildings are built.

The integration of CLT into U.S. design codes, particularly for affordable housing (Koliou and
Bahmani 2023), and the development of hybrid CLT materials (Satir 2023) present opportunities
for CLT’s broader adoption. The ongoing research into CLT’s acoustic and seismic performance
(Islam and Chen 2023, Hayes, Koliou, and van de Lindt 2023) highlights the industry’s efforts to
address technical challenges. These efforts, coupled with targeted educational and advocacy
initiatives, comprehensive risk assessment data for building insurance underwriting, and a carbon
footprint standard that accounts for sequestered biogenic carbon, could significantly enhance the
acceptance and use of CLT in sustainable construction practices.

5 Conclusion
CLT is an innovative building construction material and is considered a sustainable alternative to
reinforced concrete construction. Despite its environmental and structural benefits, the adoption
rate of CLT remains limited because of several barriers related to insurance and regulatory
considerations, regional supply chain limitations, and industry workforce education. To further
CLT development, tax incentives, research and development, and infrastructure improvements
were identified as opportunities that would enable the wider use of CLT as a sustainable building
material.
