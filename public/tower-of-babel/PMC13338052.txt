Redefining bladder sympathetic Innervation with three-dimensional image reconstruction
Authors: Xiaoji Zhou, Mingming Xu, Bin Chen, Tao Sui, Peng Tang, Zijiao Zhang, Xudong Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe primary goals of functional bladder reconstruction following spinal cord injury are to restore voiding power and reduce voiding resistance. The sympathetic nerve is one of the critical constraints within the reduce voiding resistance. In this paper, we sought to generate a three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of bladder-innervating sympathetic nervous tissue, utilizing histopathological samples derived from male and female cadavers. Bladder tissue samples were collected from four formalin-fixed cadavers (two male, two female). After preparing axial cross-sections of the bladder at 4 mm intervals and staining them with Tyrosine-hydroxylase, the distance between the sympathetic nerve site and lumen surface of the bladder, the number of sympathetic nerve sites, and the sympathetic nerve density were measured. The densest sympathetic innervation was observed at the bladder neck (female specimen) and the prostatic urethra (male specimen). For both sexes, nerve density at all levels of the proximal urethra and bladder neck was significantly greater in the posterior region compared to the anterior: in females, 2.51 ± 0.95 vs 1.11 ± 0.21 nerves per mm2 (p < 0.001); in males, 2.88 ± 0.81 vs 1.49 ± 0.56 nerves per mm2 (p < 0.001). The study found no statistically significant differences in nerve-to-lumen distances across multiple anatomical regions in either males or females, despite some numerical variations. In conclusion, novel 3D reconstruction of the bladder sympathetic nerve distribution is feasible, and this approach may refine our current understanding of human bladder innervation. In addition, it presents a sympathetic nerve map of the bladder, which may serve as a preliminary anatomical basis to explore the potential of nerve-targeting radiofrequency therapies for bladder dysfunction following SCI.Subject terms: Anatomy, Neuroscience, Urology

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) affects the quality of life by damaging the central and peripheral nervous systems1,2. Among several comorbidities, the spastic bladder remains intractable, which is characterized by detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia (DSD) and impairs both urinary storage and voiding function. This condition elevates intravesical pressure, rendering patients susceptible to urinary tract infections and renal failure—with the latter being a leading cause of late-stage mortality among individuals with SCI3,4. Accordingly, the recovery of voiding function is of paramount importance for individuals with SCI5. Reconstruction of bladder innervation for the purpose of improving the urinating function has been highlighted in many studies6–8. Since 2010, our team also have conducted a series of studies on bladder nerve reinnervation9–14. While we observed improvements in detrusor contractility and voiding function, these parameters were not fully restored to ideal levels. We considered that the reason might be that previous research might have focused predominantly on enhancing voiding power while overlooking the significance of voiding resistance—a factor of comparable importance.

The sympathetic nervous system is a well-recognized regulator of smooth muscle tone at the bladder neck and proximal urethra, thereby contributing to urethral resistance15,16. After SCI, especially suprasacral SCI, the primary mechanism of impaired voiding is detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia, in which the external urethral sphincter contracts involuntarily during detrusor contraction17,18. Superimposed on this somatic dyscoordination, the vesicourethral region also exhibits sympathetic hyperresponsiveness, leading to heightened smooth muscle tone that further elevates outlet resistance19–21. Consequently, management strategies targeting the sympathetic nervous system utilize sympathetic inhibitors or invasive therapeutic interventions to reduce voiding resistance22–24. However, limitations such as inconsistent efficacy, prolonged treatment duration, transient therapeutic effects, and surgical complications highlight the urgent need for alternative approaches that are safer, more effective, minimally invasive, and long-lasting.

Inspired by the success of sympathetic radiofrequency ablation in cardiac and renal interventions, the present study maps the spatial distribution of sympathetic innervation in the urethrovesical region through computational modeling. This anatomical map may provide a foundational reference for the future development of targeted radiofrequency therapies to modulate aberrant sympathetic activity in the bladder, with the goal of reducing voiding resistance in patients with SCI.

Materials and methods

Anatomical dissection

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Two male and two female formalin-fixed cadavers (age range: 50–79 years; mean age: 69 years), donated to the Department of Anatomy at Nanjing Medical University, were used in this study. Written informed consent was obtained from all donors prior to donation, and ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Huishan District People’s Hospital, China (Approval No.: 0001256). All specimens underwent meticulous screening to exclude a history of prior surgical interventions or urological pathologies. An experienced anatomist dissected intact urethral and bladder specimens together with their adjacent organs: the prostate and seminal vesicles in males, and the vagina, cervix, and uterus in females. Transverse sections were prepared sequentially at 4-mm intervals, spanning from the distal urethra to the bladder dome. All specimens were then transferred to standard tissue cassettes containing 10% buffered formalin, followed by paraffin embedding.

Histological evaluation

Specimen preparation was overseen by an experienced genitourinary pathologist, who also conducted all histopathological assessments. Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)—a well-recognized marker for sympathetic neurons—was utilized to localize sympathetic nerve tissue25. Transverse sections were obtained using a paraffin microtome (Leica RM 2235; Leica Microsystems Nussloch GmbH, Nussloch, Germany), with a uniform thickness of 5 μm. We prepared the histological sections by baking at 37℃ overnight. Paraffin was removed by 3 wash cycles of xylene. Sections were rehydrated through two washes of 100% alcohol for 3 min each, one wash of 95% alcohol for 3 min, and a final rinse in purified water for 5 min. We performed endoperoxidase block in 70% methanol/50% hydrogen peroxide solution for 10 min. After thorough rinsing with purified water, we pretreated the slides using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 25 min at 90℃ in a steamer. The sections were then allowed to cool at room temperature for 20 min. After washing the slides with phosphate-buffered saline and blocking non-specific binding with 1% bovine serum albumin, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) monoclonal antibody (No.GB11181, Servicebio) diluted 1:500 with casein. Next, the sections were washed with PBS and incubated for 20 min at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (No. G1302, Servicebio). We again rinsed the slides with phosphate buffered saline-Tween. Anti-rabbit antibodies were detected with a DAB Chromogen Kit (No. G1212, Servicebio). Finally, the sections were rinsed with distilled water, counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared with xylene, and mounted with coverslips.

Sympathetic nerve distribution

Histological images were acquired using a light microscope coupled with a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (Olympus BX50/DP70; Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed via Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, MD, USA). Sympathetic nerve tissue was identified based on positive TH immunostaining. Manual delineation of nerve profiles was performed specifically within the bladder substructures (i.e., bladder body, bladder neck, and proximal urethra), with nerves in adjacent organs (e.g., vagina) excluded from the analysis to avoid confounding results.

The acquired images were imported into AutoCAD software and subdivided into four anatomical quadrants: posterior, left, right, and anterior. For each slide, the following measurements and calculations were conducted: counting the number of demarcated nerve sites (defined as discrete TH-positive nerve fascicles or fiber clusters visible under light microscopy) in each quadrant; measuring the area (in mm2) of each quadrant using AutoCAD software; calculating nerve density as the ratio of the number of nerve sites to the corresponding histology slide area (expressed as profiles/mm2). Finally, AutoCAD software was used to measure the distance from each nerve site to the lumen surface of the urethra or bladder.

3D image reconstruction

High-resolution histological images were imported into Blender (a 3D modeling and animation software) for structural visualization. Serial 2D sections from male and female bladder specimens were meticulously aligned at a consistent interval of 4 mm—matching the slicing interval used in specimen preparation to ensure anatomical fidelity. Cylindrical tracings were employed to connect sympathetic nerve markers across adjacent sections, with the nerve structures labeled in red. Large nerve bundles were connected when they appeared at the best-matched positions in at least two consecutive slides; the same rule was applied to small fascicles. However, obliquely coursing fibers could not be reliably tracked because their cross-sectional profiles and positions differed substantially between adjacent slides, and they were therefore excluded from the tracing process. Concurrently, the epithelial boundaries of the urethra, bladder, and (in females) vagina were delineated to generate virtual 3D reconstructions of the target organs. High-resolution 3D models illustrating the distribution of sympathetic nerves were then rendered using Blender. Subsequently, a color-coded heat-map was constructed within the software to visualize spatial heterogeneity in sympathetic nerve density across the entire bladder, enabling intuitive identification of regions with high or low nerve abundance.

Data analysis

IBM SPSS statistics version 22.0 software (IBM Inc., Chicago, IL) was adopted for statistics. Baseline continuous variables were presented as the mean values and standard deviation (SD). The data analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0 software with the Mann–Whitney U test and a 1-way analysis of variance, and p < 0.05 was considered to be significant.

Results

Histological analysis

We identified an average of 1,488 sympathetic nerve tissue sites in male cadavers and 1,361 sites in female cadavers. It was observed on cross-sectional histology that nerve tissue sites were significantly more prevalent in the posterior than in the anterior regions of the bladder neck and body, in both males and females (Fig. 1).

In male cadavers, the highest nerve density was observed at the prostatic urethra (1.88 ± 0.91 nerves/mm2), which was significantly greater than that at the bladder neck (0.71 ± 0.29 nerves/mm2) and the bladder body (0.27 ± 0.12 nerves/mm2) (F = 140.18, p < 0.001; all pairwise comparisons p < 0.001). Nerve density in the male prostatic urethra was significantly higher posteriorly (2.88 ± 0.81) nerves/mm2 than anteriorly (1.49 ± 0.56) nerves/mm2 (U = 1.00, Z = − 4.79, p < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was found between the left and right sides (U = 103, Z = − 0.94, p = 0.35). The difference in the distance between nerve tissue and lumen was not statistically significant (F = 2.36, p = 0.09), although the measurement in the prostatic urethra (10.51 ± 1.95 mm) was numerically higher than those at the bladder neck and body (9.07 ± 1.29 mm, 9.11 ± 1.89 mm, respectively). In the prostatic urethra, no statistically significant differences in nerve distances were detected between the posterior and anterior regions, or between the left and right regions. The same was true for the bladder neck and bladder body (Table 1).

In female cadavers, nerve density was highest at the bladder neck (1.96 ± 0.88 nerves/mm2), significantly exceeding the values at the proximal urethra (1.09 ± 0.21 nerves/mm2) and the bladder body (0.50 ± 0.12 nerves/mm2) (F = 69.56, p < 0.001; all pairwise comparisons p < 0.001). A significant difference in nerve density was found between the posterior and anterior regions of the female bladder neck (posterior: 2.51 ± 0.95 nerves/mm2 vs. anterior: 1.11 ± 0.21 nerves/mm2; U = 0.01, Z = − 3.37, p = 0.001), but no significant difference was detected between the left and right sides (U = 16, Z = − 1.68, p = 0.09). The distance between nerve tissue and the bladder lumen at the level of the bladder neck (3.29 ± 1.21 mm) was lower than distances at the urethra and bladder body levels (3.99 ± 1.22 mm and 4.41 ± 1.98 mm, respectively). However, this difference did not achieve statistical significance (F = 2.66, p = 0.07). No statistically significant differences in nerve distances were observed between the posterior and anterior regions or between the left and right regions within the bladder neck. The same held true for the proximal urethra and the bladder body (Table 2).

3-dimensional reconstruction for mapping nerve distribution

The 3D reconstructions of sympathetic nerve tracings and the color-coded nerve density maps of the sympathetic nerves were successfully generated using computer aided design software. These reconstructions provided a spatially integrated view of the nerve distribution that is not readily apparent from individual two-dimensional sections. Consistent with the quantitative findings, the three-dimensional models visually confirmed that sympathetic innervation was concentrated in the bladder neck and proximal urethra in both sexes, with a clear posterior predominance. Accordingly, Fig. 2 specifically presents a magnified, intuitive view of the predominant sympathetic nerve distribution within the bladder neck and proximal urethra—the regions of highest nerve density. The corresponding color-coded nerve density maps for the entire bladder are shown in Figs. 3 and 4. We observed that, in female specimens, sympathetic nerve density was higher in the posterior bladder neck and posterior proximal urethra, whereas the anterior region was only sparsely innervated. In male specimens, a moderate degree of sympathetic innervation was also present in the anterior bladder neck and anterior proximal urethra, though density was still markedly higher posteriorly.

Discussion

The primary physiological function of the bladder is the storage and expulsion of urine. Micturition serves as a major pathway for eliminating metabolic waste, thereby maintaining optimal physiological homeostasis and fluid balance. The autonomic (sympathetic and parasympathetic) and somatic (pudendal nerve) nerve pathways originating from the spinal cord that are responsible for micturition have been previously well described15,16. However, little is known about the distribution of the autonomic innervation of the bladder at the microscopic level, particularly regarding its sympathetic component. In 1966, Badawi conducted the first histochemical studies of bladder autonomic innervation, which revealed a higher density of adrenergic and muscarinic innervation in the bladder base compared to the body26. Similarly, by employing electron microscopy in 1974, Gosling and colleagues demonstrated a higher prevalence of autonomic nerves in the bladder neck27. Recently, Spradling’s histopathological analysis of human cadavers further detailed autonomic nerve distribution in the bladder, revealing concentrated innervation in the posterior proximal urethra and bladder neck28. Nevertheless, it is unclear what the precise microanatomical distribution of sympathetic nerves in the bladder. Nordling conducted the first morphological investigation of human bladder sympathetic innervation, showing a higher prevalence of sympathetic nerves at the bladder neck and trigone—findings later corroborated by qualitative studies from other groups29–31. While we concur with these observations, quantitative data and detailed spatial mapping of sympathetic nerves at the microanatomical level remain limited. As presented in the aforementioned results, we found that in female specimens, the highest sympathetic nerve density was localized to the posterior aspect of the bladder neck. In male specimens, the peak density was situated posterior to the prostatic urethra, which contrasted with the distribution pattern (bladder neck) observed in females. These results confirm sex-based differences in sympathetic nerve density at the bladder neck level. Our findings not only align with prior descriptions of sympathetic innervation regions but also provide a critical supplement to the microanatomical understanding of the precise spatial distribution of sympathetic nerves in the human bladder.

Recent advances in the treatment of neurogenic bladder (NB), such as bladder reinnervation and sacral root electrostimulation, have demonstrated efficacy in cases refractory to conservative management6,32. In our previous work, our team also performed relevant anatomical research and clinical research with the aim of simplifying surgical procedures and advancing minimally invasive approaches9,12–14. Although postoperative electrophysiological assessments revealed enhanced detrusor contractility and voiding motility, voiding efficiency remained unsatisfactory. This observation may stem from therapeutic strategies that focus on improving voiding propulsion while overlooking voiding resistance—despite the essential need for coordination between both. In patients with SCI, heightened sympathetic activity contributes to increased smooth muscle tone at the bladder neck and proximal urethra, thereby further elevating outlet resistance in addition to the primary obstruction caused by detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia18–20. Current strategies to reduce voiding resistance—including adrenergic alpha-receptor blockers (which inhibit sympathetic tone at the internal sphincter) and invasive surgical interventions (e.g., external sphincterotomy)—are limited by side effects, inconsistent efficacy, and short-lived benefits22,24,33. As a promising neuromodulation strategy, bladder-directed radiofrequency therapy works to modulate aberrant neural signaling and restore bladder function34. One example is the NewUro device for overactive bladder, which is currently undergoing clinical evaluation35. Building on these insights, establishing a precise 3D microanatomical map of bladder sympathetic nerves could facilitate novel targeted therapies and optimize existing neuromodulatory interventions. Specifically, based on the data from this study, the identification of intravesical regions with high-density sympathetic nerves—with the posterior segment of the prostatic urethra in males and the posterior segment of the bladder neck in females serving as such regions—may enable the implementation of targeted treatments (e.g., radiofrequency ablation). Radiofrequency ablation aimed at reducing sympathetic-mediated smooth muscle tone at the bladder neck and proximal urethra may lower baseline outlet resistance and potentially improve voiding efficiency, particularly when combined with other treatments (e.g., botulinum toxin injection to the external sphincter or pharmacologic management of DSD). The efficacy of this therapeutic strategy, however, requires validation through subsequent animal experiments and clinical trials.

Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction using histological and immunohistochemical techniques represents a novel approach that has been successfully applied in prior research36. In the field of bladder and urethral innervation mapping, 3D modeling of bladder nerve distribution offers distinct advantages over conventional two-dimensional histology—most notably, its potential to contribute to the refinement of surgical techniques. For instance, Alsaid et al. demonstrated this utility by meticulously reconstructing the neurovascular bundle architecture at the level of the prostate and urethral sphincter using 3D technology; this methodology holds implications for improving post-prostatectomy outcomes by guiding nerve-sparing strategies37. Similarly, precise 3D mapping of female bladder innervation could advance nerve-preserving techniques in radical hysterectomy and other pelvic surgeries, reducing the risk of iatrogenic urinary dysfunction38. Computational 3D nerve maps not only minimize iatrogenic tissue injury during surgery but also optimize targeted neuromodulation therapies—all by fostering a more sophisticated understanding of regional neuroanatomy. A case in point is Purves’s work: their identification of concentrated autonomic nerves in the bladder neck and trigone informed the selection of optimal botulinum toxin injection sites, ultimately enhancing therapeutic efficacy for overactive bladder39. In the current study we used similar methodology to create a 3D model of the sympathetic nerve tissue of the bladder. Our results demonstrate that 3D image reconstruction using cadaver histopathology is a viable method to quantitate nerve density in different regions of the bladder and create tools to visualize areas with the highest nerve concentrations. However, given the complexity and larger number of sympathetic nerves innervating the bladder, it is challenging to precisely track the courses of individual nerves. Therefore, while our 3D nerve reconstructions reveal important findings regarding nerve location and density in general, they do not currently provide a precise depiction of the path of individual nerve tracts as they travel in the bladder. This is largely due to the 4 mm interslice spacing and the simplified connectivity assumptions of cylindrical tracings, which cannot reliably match specific entry sites across levels. The 3D maps are thus best regarded as a topographic overview rather than a true fiber-level reconstruction. It is therefore crucial to conduct further large-sample studies using true volumetric 3D imaging—such as continuous serial sectioning or tissue clearing—to accurately resolve fiber trajectories and validate the present findings in the future.

The current study also has other limitations. First, all measurements were performed on formalin-embalmed cadaveric specimens. Owing to variations in formalin fixation duration and staining protocols, these measurements may not fully replicate the characteristics of in vivo samples; thus, their interpretation should be exercised with caution. Second, the availability of cadavers was limited, which constrained our sample size. Future studies must include a larger cohort of specimens to improve statistical power and enhance the generalizability of findings. Finally, the distribution of sympathetic nerves within the bladder may vary significantly with factors such as age and ethnicity. Therefore, it is also crucial to conduct further research using a substantial number of bladder specimens to validate and substantiate our preliminary observations.

Conclusions

Three-dimensional reconstruction of the bladder innervation using histopathological imaging of human cadaveric specimens is feasible, and this approach may refine our current understanding of human bladder sympathetic innervation. Across both male and female bladders, sympathetic innervation exhibits a distinct concentration in two key regions: the proximal urethra and the bladder neck. Specifically, the highest density of sympathetic nerve tissue is localized to the posterior region of bladder neck in the female bladder, whereas in the male bladder, this peak concentration was found in the posterior aspect of the prostatic urethra. It is possible that this work could contribute to the anatomical understanding needed for developing novel nerve-targeted radiofrequency therapies for bladder dysfunction following SCI in the future.
