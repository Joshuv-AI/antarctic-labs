Loneliness and social support in cardiovascular aging: a closer look at the reported positive correlation
Authors: Arman Abroumand Gholami, Karimova Mavluda, Dilovar Karshiyeva, Oybek Eshmamatov, Gulnoz Khasanova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis correspondence offers a methodological reflection on the cross-sectional study by Darabi and colleagues, which examines loneliness and social support in older Iranian adults with cardiovascular disease. The study addresses an important gap in Middle Eastern aging research. However, the reported positive correlation between loneliness and social support invites closer methodological consideration. We invite readers to consider four methodological aspects: whether shared method variance may have influenced the observed correlation, why subgroup findings do not follow the reported trends, whether the instruments perform similarly in a low-literacy population, and what the cross-sectional design can, and cannot, tell us about directionality. These considerations suggest the observed association might benefit from further investigation with longitudinal designs and objective measures. We hope these reflections support the continued study of social health in aging populations.Keywords: Loneliness, Social support, Cardiovascular disease, Common method bias

We read with great interest the article by Darabi and colleagues, recently published in Aging Clinical and Experimental Research [1]. The authors uniquely examine three distinct social health constructs, loneliness, social isolation, and perceived social support, simultaneously in a single cohort of older Iranian adults with cardiovascular disease. Notably, they provide, to our knowledge, one of the first characterizations of these interrelationships in a Middle Eastern population, where cultural norms around family support and social integration may differ substantially from Western settings. However, the paradoxical positive correlation they report between loneliness and social support raises several methodological concerns that we wish to bring to the attention of readers.

The artifact of common method variance

The exclusive reliance on self-report instruments completed by the same respondents introduces common method bias. According to Podsakoff et al. [2], when different constructs of interest share the same measurement method and respondent, common method bias can artificially inflate observed correlations by 0.20–0.40 [2]. A distressed participant may report high loneliness (“I feel alone”) and concurrently high perceived support (“I have people who help me”) without any true positive association between these constructs. Therefore, the paradoxical positive correlation (reported in Fig. 2 [1] as RS = + 0.635, p < 0.001) may reflect shared method variance rather than a genuine psychosocial phenomenon. Objective measures of social interaction (e.g., observed contact frequency) or temporal separation of measurements would be necessary to validate this finding. The authors employed neither remedy.

Internal inconsistency between subgroup findings and reported correlations

The authors use NYHA classification to group patients by heart failure severity. While this is appropriate for descriptive purposes, the data reveal an internal inconsistency that challenges the robustness of their reported correlations. As shown in Table 2 [1], Class III patients report the highest loneliness (37.88) but the lowest social isolation (12.69). If loneliness and social isolation are inversely correlated (RS = -0.689, p < 0.001), the group with the highest loneliness should also have the highest, not the lowest, social isolation. This is not observed. Similarly, if loneliness and social support are positively correlated (RS = + 0.635, p < 0.001), Class III patients should report the highest social support. However, Class II patients report substantially higher social support (33.11) than Class III (26.14). The authors offer a post-hoc explanation that dependency increases loneliness despite family involvement. However, this explanation does not account for why Class IV patients, who are the most functionally dependent, report lower loneliness (30.39) than Class III (37.88). These inconsistencies suggest that the reported correlations (RS = -0.689 and RS = + 0.635) may not be robust across all subgroups. Alternatively, the small sample size in Class III (n = 49) compared to Class IV (n = 190) may have contributed to unstable estimates. Either way, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Threats to measurement equivalence

The authors report Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the SELSA (0.91), LSNS (0.90), and MSPSS (0.88) in their sample, demonstrating acceptable internal consistency. However, methodological research indicates that while Cronbach’s alpha measures internal consistency, it “does not establish validity” [3]. Establishing validity in a new population requires demonstrating that an instrument measures the intended construct, not just that its items correlate with each other. Furthermore, these Persian validations were conducted in general populations, not in severely ill, low-literacy CVD patients. This distinction is critical for two reasons. First, psychometric instruments validated in literate populations may exhibit differential item functioning (DIF) when administered to individuals with limited literacy. DIF occurs when the relationship between an item and the underlying construct differs across groups, undermining the validity of comparisons. Even well-designed instruments like PROMIS have shown DIF across health literacy groups, meaning items function differently depending on the respondent’s literacy level [4]. Given that 81.5% of participants in this study were illiterate, the validity of these instruments in this specific population remains unestablished.

Second, the administration mode differed from the validated format. Questionnaires were interviewer-administered (read aloud) for illiterate participants, whereas the Persian validations were conducted using self-report. Mode of administration can affect psychometric properties [5]; the authors did not re-validate the instruments for interviewer administration. For an instrument to be considered valid in a new population additional psychometric evidence is required, including: [1] confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to verify the original factor structure holds; [2] DIF analysis to ensure items perform similarly across literacy levels; and [3] convergent validity with other validated instruments [6]. The authors provided none of these. Consequently, the observed correlations may reflect measurement artifacts rather than genuine psychosocial phenomena.

The direction of association remains unresolved

The authors interpret their findings using causal language, stating that loneliness “may influence” cardiovascular outcomes and that social isolation “is associated with increased risk”. In a cross-sectional study, two opposing causal directions are equally plausible: loneliness may contribute to worse cardiovascular outcomes, or severe cardiovascular disease may cause functional limitations that increase loneliness. Given that 43.9% of participants had NYHA Class IV heart failure, the most severe category, the latter explanation is at least as plausible as the former. The authors offer no empirical evidence to favor one direction over the other. They performed no sensitivity analyses (e.g., age stratification, exclusion of severe cases) that might shed light on temporal ordering. Therefore, despite the causal language in their Discussion, the direction of the reported associations remains unestablished. Longitudinal follow-up data would be required to determine whether loneliness precedes cardiovascular decline or vice versa.

In conclusion, the study by Darabi and colleagues (2026) have provided a valuable hypothesis-generating contribution to an understudied population. However, for the reasons outlined above, their paradoxical finding of a positive correlation between loneliness and social support should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence of a genuine psychosocial phenomenon. We recommend that future longitudinal studies employ objective measures of social interaction, validate instruments for low-literacy populations, and formally test for reverse causation before accepting this counterintuitive association.
