BCP-YOLO: a lightweight and interpretable YOLOv8-based framework for real-time colorectal polyp detection
Authors: Xiaoyan Fei, Yongyue Bao, Caiyan Dai, Kongfa Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors worldwide. Early screening relies on accurate polyp detection during colonoscopy. Polyps in colonoscopic images exhibit diverse morphologies, indistinct boundaries, and low contrast. Specular reflections, fold occlusions, and imaging artifacts further complicate detection, which fail to meet the requirements of real-time clinical assistance.MethodsTo address these challenges, we propose BCP-YOLO (You Only Look Once), a high-precision, relatively lightweight polyp detection framework built upon an improved YOLOv8 architecture, designed to achieve a well-balanced trade-off between detection accuracy and computational efficiency. First, to mitigate complex background interference and improve small polyp detection, a BiFormer module is integrated into the backbone network to enhance focus on salient polyp regions while suppressing noise. To alleviate boundary ambiguity, the CARAFE content-aware upsampling operator is incorporated into the feature fusion stage, to refine lesion boundaries and spatial details. PConv module is employed to optimize network efficiency, reducing computational cost while maintaining detection performance.ResultsExperimental results on the Kvasir-SEG and CVC-ClinicDB datasets demonstrate that BCP-YOLO achieves a mean average precision (mAP0.5) of 88.5% on Kvasir-SEG, representing a 3.4% improvement over the YOLOv8 baseline. Precision and recall increase by 5.5% and 1.3%, respectively. The model contains 11.7 M parameters and achieves an inference speed of 104.1 frames per second (FPS). Five-fold cross-validation on both datasets validates its strong generalization capability and robustness.ConclusionThe method provides a high-accuracy and deployable solution for computer-aided diagnosis in real-time colonoscopy, offering significant potential to improve the reliability and efficiency of early colorectal cancer screening.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is a malignant tumor arising in the colon or rectum and is one of the three most prevalent cancers worldwide [1]. It ranks second in mortality among all malignant tumors and exhibits high incidence and mortality rates globally [2]. Most colorectal cancers originate from precancerous lesions or adenomatous polyps [3]. Colorectal polyps are abnormal growths on the intestinal mucosal surface, characterized by high concealment, indistinct boundaries, and morphological similarity to normal mucosal folds [4]. Early-stage patients are typically asymptomatic, making polyps difficult to detect during routine screening [5]. When clinical symptoms appear, polyps often have progressed to a larger size with increased malignant potential. Therefore, early and accurate detection of colorectal polyps is essential for colorectal cancer prevention and has become a major focus in medical image analysis research [6].

Currently, colonoscopy remains the primary clinical modality for polyp screening [7]. A flexible endoscope equipped with a micro-camera is inserted through the anus to obtain real-time images of the intestinal lumen. Conventional polyp identification relies heavily on visual inspection by endoscopists and is susceptible to variations in clinical experience, fatigue, and subjective judgment, thereby increasing the risk of false positives and false negatives [8]. Residual fecal matter or undigested food in the lumen may further reduce visibility and increase the likelihood of missed lesions [9]. Although Computed Tomography (CT) colonography provides three-dimensional visualization of the colon, its detection rate for diminutive polyps (<6 mm) is approximately 44% [10]. Moreover, the complex folding structure and tortuous anatomy of the colon may lead to incomplete beam penetration and imaging artifacts. Other modalities such as CT colonography and capsule endoscopy have also been explored, but their diagnostic accuracy and clinical practicality remain limited [11].

Existing computer-aided polyp detection methods can be broadly categorized into two groups. The first group comprises traditional machine learning approaches, which construct detection models based on manually designed features combined with techniques such as threshold segmentation [12], morphological operations [13], clustering algorithms [14], and support vector machines [15]. These methods primarily depend on handcrafted low-level visual features with limited representation capacity, resulting in insufficient robustness under complex intestinal environments, illumination variations, and diverse polyp morphologies. Consequently, they fail to meet the clinical requirements for detection accuracy and stability [16]. The second group consists of deep learning approaches, which automatically learn high-level semantic representations without manual feature engineering [17]. These methods demonstrate stronger environmental adaptability, generalization capability, and real-time inference performance [18]. In numerous studies, deep learning-based models have achieved superior detection accuracy and efficiency, showing substantial potential in colorectal polyp detection research [19]. Recent studies have further pushed these boundaries, showcasing the unique advantages of advanced deep learning architectures in executing precise lesion classification and clinical stratification under low-contrast, multi-scale, and heterogeneous medical imaging environments [20].

Deep learning methods have been widely applied in medical image detection due to their capability for automatic feature extraction [21]. These approaches can effectively capture local image features such as edges, textures, and shapes, and progressively learn higher-level abstract representations through stacked convolutional operations. For instance, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) primarily learn local visual patterns through multiple convolutional layers, gradually enhancing the model’s ability to recognize polyp boundaries and morphological characteristics [22]. To overcome the limitations of convolution-based models in capturing global contextual information, the Transformer architecture introduces a global modeling mechanism [23] that alleviates the difficulty of distinguishing polyps with similar visual appearances [24]. For example, Selvaraj et al. proposed the CRP-ViT model, which integrates Vision Transformer (ViT) to enhance polyp classification performance. By leveraging global attention modeling, this architecture improves the ability to differentiate visually similar polyp types [25]. However, pure Transformer-based models typically require large-scale annotated datasets for effective training [26] and involve high computational complexity, leading to increased deployment cost and limited practicality in resource-constrained clinical environments [27]. In another study, Sun et al. proposed an improved EP-YOLO model based on YOLOv10, introducing an SE_SPPF module and an additional small-object detection layer to address the large computational overhead and weak small-object detection capability of conventional YOLO models [28]. Nevertheless, the model exhibits reduced accuracy when processing dynamically blurred endoscopic images in the dataset, and it is prone to bounding-box overlap and misclassification when multiple polyps are densely distributed. Although deep learning has achieved promising progress in polyp detection, challenges such as tiny polyp detection, image noise interference, blurred boundaries, and significant morphological variability remain unresolved [29]. These issues continue to hinder the reliable real-time clinical deployment of automated detection systems [30].

Among the You Only Look Once (YOLO) series models, YOLOv8 achieves a favorable balance among lightweight design, detection accuracy, and real-time inference performance, making it particularly suitable for clinical real-time detection and resource-constrained deployment scenarios [31]. In such settings, achieving an effective balance between computational complexity, inference latency, and detection accuracy is essential. In contrast, YOLOv9 introduces increased model complexity and computational cost [32], while YOLOv10 demonstrates reduced robustness in detecting small and low-contrast targets [33]. Therefore, YOLOv8 is selected as the baseline model in this study.

To address the aforementioned clinical bottlenecks, we propose BCP-YOLO based on the YOLOv8 architecture, establishing a unified Local Global Perception and Feature Reassembly Principle to bridge macro-contextual awareness, micro-boundary reconstruction, and structural sparsity within a coherent paradigm. This principled design systematically targets three critical challenges in colonoscopic imaging: limited semantic discrimination under low-contrast conditions, structural ambiguity from blurred lesion boundaries, and operational constraints during real-time deployment. Specifically, to enhance low-contrast feature discrimination, Bi-Level Routing Attention (BiFormer) [34] is incorporated into the backbone to execute coarse-grained, region-level routing, dynamically purging uninformative mucosal fields and specular artifacts to focus on subtle, occult polyps. Second, to counteract semantic diffusion and missed detections of tiny targets, the Content-Aware Reassembly of Features (CARAFE) upsampling operators [35] integrated into the feature-fusion phase. Through content-aware kernel prediction and non-uniform pixel aggregation, CARAFE achieves exact pixel-level semantic alignment under an expanded receptive field, precisely reconstructing fine morphological details and micro-boundary gradients. Finally, to satisfy the stringent throughput demands of intraoperative deployment, a Pinwheel-shaped Convolution (PConv) module [36] introduces an anisotropic, directional sampling topology. By constructing asymmetric convolutional pathways via directional padding and specialized kernels, PConv broadens the effective receptive field for complex geometric structures while systematically eliminating computational redundancy, thereby lowering inference latency and memory access costs without compromising localization accuracy. By unifying global contextual perception, fine-grained structural representation, and lightweight computation, this collaborative framework shifts the positioning of computer-aided polyposis screening from empirical engineering to a theoretically grounded and clinically applicable architectural design.

Material and methods

Dataset and preprocessing

This study employs Kvasir-SEG [https://datasets.simula.no/kvasir-seg/] as the primary experimental dataset [37]. The dataset contains 1000 colonoscopic polyp images covering different anatomical locations and diverse polyp morphologies, with significant variations in size, shape, color, and texture. Each image is accompanied by a pixel-level segmentation mask. To construct annotations required for the detection task, the segmentation masks were used as the basis for label generation. Specifically, the OpenCV contour detection algorithm was employed to extract object boundaries, and the minimum axis-aligned bounding boxes were generated using extreme point calculations. The annotations were then formatted according to the COCO format and finally converted into a format compatible with the YOLO detection framework to support model training and evaluation. In the experimental design, Kvasir-SEG was used for both ablation studies and comparative experiments to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed model architecture and the introduced modules in colorectal polyp detection. The dataset was divided into training and validation sets at a ratio of 4:1, and a K-fold cross-validation strategy was adopted to mitigate overfitting and ensure the robustness of the experimental results.

To further assess the generalization capability of the proposed model, the external dataset CVC-ClinicDB [http://www.cvc.uab.es/CVC-Colon/index.php/databases/cvc-clinicdb/] was introduced. This dataset consists of 612 static images extracted from high-quality colonoscopy videos and includes polyp presentations at different clinical stages. The same annotation construction and preprocessing pipeline used for Kvasir-SEG was applied to this dataset, which was then utilized as an independent external test set. In this setting, the model trained on Kvasir-SEG was directly evaluated on CVC-ClinicDB to assess cross-dataset generalization. Additionally, an independent five-fold cross-validation experiment was conducted on CVC-ClinicDB to further evaluate the robustness of the proposed architecture under a different data distribution. Through comprehensive evaluation across the two datasets, the detection performance, robustness, and generalization ability of the proposed model were systematically validated under varying clinical scenarios and imaging conditions.

BCP-YOLO architecture

In colonoscopic images, complex anatomical structures and variable imaging conditions pose significant challenges to automatic polyp detection. Specifically, severe visual noise from mucosal folds, uneven illumination, and specular reflections obscures lesion textures, increasing the risk of false positives. Furthermore, polyps often share highly similar color and texture with surrounding normal mucosa and possess indistinct boundaries. Finally, early-stage tiny polyps occupy minimal pixels, leading to weak spatial representation and potential missed detections. To address these challenges, this study introduces a targeted network architecture. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the Backbone integrates the BiFormer module, utilizing a BiFormer mechanism to focus on lesion regions and suppress background noise. In the multi-scale Head, the CARAFE module refines geometric details through adaptive feature reassembly, significantly improving tiny polyp detection. Meanwhile, the PConv operator in the connection layers exploits channel redundancy to reduce computational overhead. This design ensures high-precision, stable, and real-time full-scale polyp detection.Fig. 1Overall architecture of the proposed BCP-YOLO framework

Overall architecture of the proposed BCP-YOLO framework

The proposed BCP-YOLO framework takes a colonoscopic image as input and sequentially performs backbone feature extraction, multi-scale feature fusion, and detection prediction. The backbone progressively compresses spatial information through convolution and downsampling operations, generating hierarchical features for multi-scale lesion representation. To improve lesion discrimination under complex endoscopic conditions, BiFormer blocks are introduced to strengthen long-range contextual modeling via BiFormer. C2f and PConv modules are alternately incorporated to enhance feature interaction while reducing redundant computation, whereas the SPPF module further enriches contextual perception through multi-receptive-field aggregation. The extracted multi-scale features are subsequently fused in the neck through cross-scale aggregation. During feature fusion, CARAFE facilitates adaptive feature fusion during multi-scale aggregation. Finally, the fused features are forwarded to the decoupled detection head for parallel classification and bounding-box regression to generate the final predicted bounding boxes and confidence scores.

In the backbone stage, the BiFormer module is introduced to enhance feature representation under complex colonoscopic imaging conditions. BiFormer performs joint modeling of long-range contextual dependencies and local detailed information through a BiFormer mechanism, enabling the network to integrate semantic relationships between the polyp region and surrounding tissue structures over a larger receptive field. This design allows the model to focus more precisely on clinically relevant suspicious lesion regions. Meanwhile, the BRA mechanism effectively suppresses non-lesion interference caused by mucosal folds, specular reflections, residual fluid highlights, and intestinal contents, thereby reducing the risk of false detections.

Furthermore, to guarantee fast and stable inference under the limited computational resources of clinical endoscopic systems, the PConv module is integrated into selected convolutional layers. PConv performs feature extraction through four directional convolution branches with asymmetric padding configurations. The resulting directional responses are aggregated to enhance spatial perception and capture geometric structures of colorectal polyps from multiple orientations. This elegant design maintains global feature continuity and semantic consistency while drastically stripping away redundant computations. As a result, both the parameter scale and computational complexity are minimized without compromising feature representation, making the model highly suitable for real-time deployment in resource-constrained environments.

During the multi-scale feature fusion stage, the CARAFE module is adopted to replace conventional fixed-interpolation upsampling methods. Since early-stage adenomatous polyps are typically minute, accurate localization relies heavily on recovering fine-grained spatial details from high-resolution feature maps. CARAFE addresses this by dynamically generating adaptive reassembly kernels based on local semantics. This allows low-resolution features to be reconstructed adaptively during the feature pyramid construction, precisely restoring high-resolution spatial structures. Consequently, this content-aware upsampling strategy effectively alleviates boundary blurring caused by semantic mismatches, significantly boosting the detection rate of small polyps while preserving strict contour integrity in boundary regions.

By collaboratively integrating YOLOv8 with the BiFormer, CARAFE, and PConv modules at different stages of the network, the proposed BCP-YOLO framework achieves coordinated improvements in feature representation capability, multi-scale feature fusion quality, and computational efficiency. The overall architecture is well aligned with the imaging characteristics of colorectal polyps in colonoscopic images. By enhancing lesion saliency and reducing the risk of missed detections while maintaining both detection accuracy and real-time performance, the proposed method provides a high-precision, stable, and efficient computer-aided diagnosis solution with strong potential for practical clinical deployment in colorectal polyp detection.

BRA-driven feature extraction

To address the subtle characteristics of colorectal polyps, this study introduces the BiFormer module [34] based on Bi-Level Routing Attention (BRA) into the deep layers of the backbone network and integrates it into the BCP-YOLO framework. Conventional self-attention mechanisms typically perform equal-weight modeling across the entire spatial domain, which may introduce redundant features and noise, thereby weakening the representation capability of critical regions. BiFormer addresses this limitation by employing a content-aware BRA mechanism that enables region-level adaptive selection. Specifically, BRA employs a two-stage hierarchical routing strategy, first identifying candidate regions at a coarse-grained level and then refining attention within these regions, preserving a global receptive field while suppressing background interference. This design enhances feature representation and improves robustness for detecting subtle lesions in complex colonoscopic environments. CARAFE-based multi-scale fusion

Intestinal polyp lesions exhibit high spatial heterogeneity and multi-scale characteristics, and are susceptible to dynamic interference from mucus, specular reflections, and mucosal folds. Conventional upsampling operators such as bilinear interpolation [38] and transposed convolution [39] employ spatially content-agnostic weights, which often cause semantic diffusion, loss of critical details, or severe edge artifacts, thereby limiting performance in high-precision localization of occult polyps. To mitigate these limitations, the Content-Aware Reassembly of Features (CARAFE) [35] operator is integrated into the feature fusion path. CARAFE enhances feature representation by predicting a reassembly kernel for each target location and performing a content-aware weighted summation of local features. In complex endoscopic scenarios, CARAFE is particularly effective during feature upsampling. For occult polyps with blurred boundaries, its content-aware design adaptively generates reassembly kernels from local texture gradients, suppressing information diffusion during cross-scale transmission and improving lesion boundary delineation. Through adaptive feature reassembly, CARAFE fuses deep semantic information with shallow spatial details while reducing aliasing artifacts typical of static upsampling, thereby enhancing robustness to specular reflections and mucus occlusion and yielding more discriminative representations for the detection head. CARAFE maintains low computational overhead through channel compression that decouples kernel prediction from input channels, reducing the complexity of content encoding, and through a lightweight local dot-product aggregation in the reassembly stage. In summary, CARAFE through its precise feature reconstruction mechanism, significantly enhances the model’s detection accuracy and generalization capability for occult and multi-scale polyp targets in complex environments, all while ensuring computational efficiency.

Settings for Ablation experiments and comparative experiments

In the ablation study, this work systematically evaluates the independent and joint contributions of each proposed module, including the BiFormer backbone with BRA, the CARAFE content-aware feature reassembly module, and the PConv operator. Taking the original YOLOv8 as the baseline, six ablation configurations are designed. Single-module variants (Ablations 1–3) are used to isolate the effect of each component, while dual-module combinations (Ablations 4–6) are employed to assess potential synergistic interactions between modules. The final model, BCP-YOLOv8, integrates all three modules to verify the necessity of their joint contribution to overall performance improvement. For the comparative experiments, the proposed BCP-YOLOv8 is evaluated against several mainstream object detection algorithms to comprehensively assess its effectiveness in clinical-assisted diagnosis. These include one-stage detectors (SSD, YOLOv8, YOLOv9, YOLOv10, YOLOX, and PP-YOLO), two-stage detection framework Faster R-CNN and Transformer-based end-to-end detection frameworks such as DETR. The selected models cover diverse detection paradigms and computational complexities, enabling a comprehensive assessment of the relative advantages of the proposed method for clinical lesion detection.

Discussion

This study introduces BCP-YOLO, an algorithmic paradigm optimized from a YOLOv8 baseline to mitigate systemic diagnostic impediments in colorectal endoscopic screening, specifically polypoid macromorphology, poorly demarcated lesion margins, and low contrast intraluminal backgrounds. The architecture secures enhanced discriminative capacity via three targeted structural interventions: a backbone-integrated BiFormer attention mechanism to capture long-range contextual dependencies while suppressing specular artifacts, a CARAFE operator within the feature-fusion network to reconstruct fine edge details for diminutive lesions, and a pinwheel convolution (PConv) strategy to capture omnidirectional topological features under minimal computational overhead. Evaluated on the public dataset Kvasir-SEG, BCP-YOLO yields 88.5% mAP0.5 at an inference speed of 104.1 FPS with a parameterized volume of 11.7 M, demonstrating a balance between detection accuracy and efficiency throughput required for real-time intraoperative deployment.

To establish that these localized performance increments originate from intrinsic structural attributes rather than statistical anomalies in data partitioning, rigorous two-tailed paired t-tests and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were evaluated using the cross-validation metrics. Compared against representative deep learning architectures, BCP-YOLO demonstrates robust statistical superiority across both Kvasir-SEG and CVC-ClinicDB dataset. Crucially, the proposed model achieves highly significant improvements across all comparisons, maintaining p < 0.01 on the dataset against YOLOv8 and YOLOv10, and demonstrating p < 0.05 when evaluated against RT-DETR under cross-dataset domain shifts. All corresponding 95% CIs strictly exclude zero, verifying the robust generalization capacity of the individual components. Furthermore, ablation experiments confirm the isolated empirical utility of each module, while Grad-CAM spatial mappings demonstrate that the attentional focus of the network correlates with the diagnostic trajectories of expert endoscopists, confirming the interpretability of the model for clinical decision support systems. Nevertheless, real-world clinical translation still faces operational challenges such as transient motion blur, mucosal occlusions, and highly obscured planar lesions with negligible texturing contrast. Mitigating these environmental domain shifts remains a critical bottleneck for deterministic deployment.

From an operational perspective, these validated statistical attributes indicate that the algorithm provides reproducible lesion boundary delineation during screening. By mitigating diagnostic variance typically introduced by shifting illumination, variable camera angulation, and mucosal reflections, the architecture offers objective, observer-independent diagnostic assistance. To facilitate reproducibility and further academic development, the detection demo has been made deployed online at [https://polyp-detection.streamlit.app/]. Future work will extend this framework to concurrent polyp segmentation and histopathological classification, alongside multi-center prospective validation to evaluate its diagnostic utility in real-world gastroenterology.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed BCP-YOLO, an optimized object detection framework designed to address the persistent clinical challenges of automated colorectal polyp detection, specifically targeting flat lesions, ill-defined boundaries, and confounding intraluminal noise. By establishing a unified architectural strategy that balances localized geometric precision with global spatial context, the proposed network successfully integrates bi-routing sparse attention for contextual filtering, content-aware upsampling for boundary reassembly, and pinwheel-shaped directional convolutions for lightweight feature reinforcement. Rigorous cross-dataset validation and statistical significance testing demonstrate that BCP-YOLO consistently achieves superior diagnostic fidelity, securing a benchmark mAP0.5 of 88.5% while maintaining an intraoperative processing throughput of 104.1 frames per second. Although translating this architecture into live clinical environments will require ongoing refinement to counter transient motion blur and mucosal occlusions, the open-source deployment of this highly discriminative, observer-independent paradigm provides a clinically viable baseline capable of reducing lesion omission rates during early colorectal cancer screenings.
