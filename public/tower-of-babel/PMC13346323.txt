Strategies and successes of smoking cessation methods in head and neck cancer patients: a systematic review
Authors: Vincent Vander Poorten, Justine Van Rossum, Malene Roosens, Sandra Nuyts, Carol. R. Bradford, Juan Pablo Rodrigo, Remco De Bree, Karthik N. Rao, Orlando Guntinas-Lichius, Nabil F. Saba, Alfio Ferlito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tobacco use and especially smoking tobacco is a key risk factor in the development and the progression of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Continued smoking after diagnosis worsens treatment outcomes, survival rates, and quality of life significantly. This systematic review evaluates the effectiveness of smoking cessation interventions in HNSCC patients, aiming to provide an evidence-based foundation supporting smoking cessation efforts. A comprehensive search of five databases yielded 2492 studies, of which 11 met the inclusion criteria (total patient number = 1593). These covered a range of interventions, including behavioral counseling, pharmacotherapy (nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), bupropion, and varenicline), and digital or structural support tools. Reported cessation rates ranged from 23.9 to 74% depending on the intervention type, intensity, and timing. Interventions combining counseling with pharmacological support and structured follow-up were generally more effective, particularly when initiated shortly after diagnosis. The review also highlights the potential benefits of digital tools, integrated care approaches, and opt-out systems. Despite the substantial heterogeneity across studies, which prohibited a formal meta-analysis, the findings underscore the importance of a tailored, multimodal approach to smoking cessation in HNSCC patients. Implementation barriers such as limited resources and organizational constraints persist, and further research is needed to determine the optimal follow-up strategies and address comorbidity-inducing behavioral factors.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10555-026-10348-9.

Introduction

Globally, tobacco use is the leading cause of preventable mortality, affecting smokers but also those exposed to second-hand smoke, contributing significantly to a wide range of diseases [1]. Among these are tobacco-related head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC), with 70–80% of newly diagnosed cases attributable to the combined use of alcohol and tobacco [1–3]. Chronic alcohol use and smoking are independent risk factors for HNSCC, but when combined, there is a multiplicative risk [4–8]. Head and neck tumor histology varies by location, but 90% are squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC), a large proportion of which are tobacco-related and involve the oral cavity, paranasal sinuses, nasopharynx, oropharynx, larynx, hypopharynx, and salivary glands [9].

While smoking cessation is no longer a form of primary prevention once HNSCC has developed, it remains a critical form of tertiary prevention. Continued smoking after diagnosis implies a greater likelihood of developing a second primary tumor [10] and is associated with increased surgical complications, reduced effectiveness and more treatment-related adverse effects of radiotherapy, and a higher mortality [5, 8, 11–15].

Smoking cessation in HNSCC patients is thus essential, yet with significant challenges. Typical barriers in tobacco-related cancer patients are cessation urgency, fatalistic beliefs, psychosocial distress, treatment-related stress, and socioeconomic vulnerability [16–20]. Nonetheless, the cancer diagnosis can act as a powerful “teaching moment”: the vast majority of smokers, when confronted with a tobacco-induced cancer, are motivated to quit smoking [21, 22]. This moment is also a valuable opportunity for primary prevention among family members, reducing smoking and alcohol use among relatives [16, 21].

Smoking cessation relies on both pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic interventions to support abstinence. Furthermore, addressing the underlying stress factors maximizes the likelihood of smoking and alcohol cessation, as this stress is a driver of risky behavior pre- but also postdiagnosis [22, 23].

Considerable research has assessed the efficacy of smoking cessation interventions (SCI) in the broad population as well as in cancer patients in general. The studies focusing directly on the effectiveness of SCI in HNSCC patients are less frequent and are the subject of this systematic review, in which we aim to synthesize the evidence on how to optimally leverage the HNSCC diagnosis as “teachable moment” with effective, evidence-based interventions, to support smoking cessation efforts.

Methods

Search strategy

A systematic search for studies assessing the effectiveness of SCI in patients with HNSCC was performed in the following electronic databases and registers: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science Core Collection, Scopus and Cochrane Library. The used search terms, listed in Supplementary Table I, were restricted to articles published in English and applied up to August 2nd 2024, in a first search, and later expanded to March 28th 2026. In addition, the reference lists of relevant systematic reviews were manually screened for potential additional studies.

Study selection

For study selection, we used the PRISMA guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) [24] with inclusion and exclusion criteria structured according to the PICO framework. The population (P) included HNSCC patients (≥ 18 years) who were either current smokers or had quit within the past 12 months. Practically, because of the high risk of relapse in this critical period, the latter patients can be considered active smokers. This cut-off is commonly used in the literature and is supported by the guidelines of the NCCN [25, 26]. According to studies in cancer patients in general, the highest smoking relapse rate is in the first six months after smoking cessation [21, 27, 28]. Eligible cancer types included malignancies of the oral cavity, nasopharynx, oropharynx, larynx, and hypopharynx. Studies focusing only on precancerous lesions or involving a mixed cancer population without HNSCC specific data were excluded, with an exception for De Bruin-Visser et al. [29], who did not report on HNSCC patients separately, but was included for their focus on smoking-related cancers in a cohort consisting of a majority (66%) of HNSCC and a minority of lung cancer patients. The intervention (I) comprised pharmacological and nonpharmacological behavioral tobacco cessation or reduction strategies and their combination. Studies focusing exclusively on nonsmoking forms of tobacco use (e.g., chewing tobacco, snuff, snus, gutkha, or betel quid) or offering only a single counseling session without follow-up were excluded. The comparator (C) group included placebo, no intervention, usual care, or alternative smoking cessation strategies. The primary outcome (O) focused on smoking cessation rates. Both self-reported and biochemically verified abstinence were considered valid outcome measures. Studies that did not provide data on the effectiveness of the intervention were excluded. To obtain a comprehensive overview of the current literature, study design on itself (except for reviews) was not an exclusion criterion. The search strategy yielded 2492 studies; the manual reference list search resulted in 3 additional studies, after removing duplicate records using Rayyan [30]. Then, 1828 studies remained for assessing relevance using titles and abstracts . Two independent reviewers (JVR and MR) then assessed eligibility according to the predefined criteria, and the assessment of one reviewer was blinded for the other during the selection process. In case of disagreement after unblinding, a full-text review resulted in consensus by discussion. Eleven trials studying 1593 patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data extraction

Data on study characteristics (title, first author, publication year, country, study design, study setting, and study population), cancer type, number of included patients, description of the intervention, description of the control group, relevant outcome measure(s), follow-up duration, and relevant results were systematically extracted by JVR for each included study. A risk of bias assessment was performed, using the ROBINS-I V2 tool for nonrandomized interventional studies (Fig. 2) and the RoB 2 tool for the randomized controlled trials (Fig. 3) [32–34]. For the cross-sectional study by McQueen et al., we did not perform a formal risk of bias assessment due to the inherently high risk of bias in cross-sectional studies. Data analysis

Due to a substantial heterogeneity in study designs, interventions and control conditions, outcome measure(s), and outcome measure methods, a meta-analysis could not be performed.

Results

Study characteristics

A wide range of study designs was encountered, and included studies had been performed in several countries, with the majority from the United States of America. Trials, from a period between 1993 and 2022, examined the effectiveness of SCI in adults with a current or recent smoking history and a diagnosis of HNSCC, except for and as stated above, the study by De Bruin-Visser et al. [29], which also included patients with lung cancer, another tobacco-related malignancy. Rettig et al. studied patients with other cancer types but reported separate results for HNSCC and was included as well [35]. McQueen et al. did not specify the use of conventional cigarettes versus other tobacco products but was also included [36]. Patient recruitment and the start of the intervention program took place in a hospital setting, usually associated with an oncology center or a university hospital. Follow-up was either face-to-face or by telephone, depending on the study protocol. Characteristics of interventions

The type and intensity of SCI varied widely between the included studies. Three main types of SCI were observed: behavioral counseling, pharmacological support, and (in some cases) additional psychosocial support.

Behavioral counseling

Nearly all studies incorporated behavioral counseling, ranging from brief counseling sessions to structured programs with intensive follow-up. Several interventions (Rettig et al. [35], Duffy et al. [38], and Sharp et al. [42]) implemented intensive counseling based on motivational interviewing cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT). De Bruin-Visser et al. [29] used theoretical models (Bandura [44] and Prochaska and DiClemente [45]) within a nursing-led program. Smaily et al. combined a brief perioperative intervention with supplementary materials such as a motivational document, smartphone apps, and an emergency line [43].

Pharmacological support

The majority of interventions offered nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), either systematically [35] or based on clinical indication [29, 38]. NRT included nicotine patches, chewing gum, sublingual tablets, or a combination. A number of studies also incorporated bupropion, a norepinephrine/dopamine reuptake inhibitor (NDRI) [46], or varenicline, a partial agonist of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR) [47]. In several studies, patients were permitted to select from a range of alternative nicotine products (ANP), including Swedish snus [20, 35, 42].

Additional psychosocial support

Some authors incorporated SCI within broader care frameworks that simultaneously addressed comorbidities such as alcohol use and depressive symptoms [35, 38]. Other studies collected data on concurrent substance use but did not implement targeted interventions [36].

Additional approaches

A subset of interventions integrated digital tools or structural support mechanisms. Rettig et al. gave patients access to the “smokefreetxt” program via SMS, financial incentives, and tailored support, including combination NRT and individualized counseling [35]. Ghosh et al. investigated the effect of financial incentives, namely, monetary rewards per session with a negative CO test result, in a small sample population [40].

Effectiveness of interventions

Effectiveness of interventions in HNSCC patients

Reported smoking abstinence rates varied considerably between studies. Day et al. observed 42% abstinence at nine months using a personalized program combining counseling, pharmacotherapy, and psychological support [37]. Following a nurse-led behavioral intervention with optional pharmacological support, De Bruin-Visser et al. observed 40% abstinence at six months, and 33% at 12 months [29]. Duffy et al., combining CBT with pharmacotherapy, achieved a six-month abstinence of 47% compared with a quite high 31% in the control group (p = 0.048) [38]. Rettig et al. used intensive counseling with NRT, motivational interviewing, financial incentives, and a regular follow-up and reported an abstinence rate of 74% at eight weeks in the intervention group, compared with 30% in the control group (p = 0.046) [29]. Although the difference in cessation remained at 12 months (31% versus 0%), it was no longer statistically significant because of small numbers remaining (19 intervention and 10 control participants) [35]. Smaily et al. found no significant difference at 12 months between a standardized short-term perioperative intervention using nicotine patches (20.8%) and usual care (33.3%) [43]. Santi et al. reported a stable cessation rate of 23.9% following an intensive clinical program including behavioral counseling and optional pharmacotherapy [20]. Finally, Sharp et al. achieved a one-year abstinence of 68% using a nurse-led intervention with systematic follow-up and ANPs [42].

Timing of intervention

Garces et al. identified the association between timing of intervention and effectiveness as a 47% cessation when the intervention was started within three months of diagnosis, compared to 22% between three months and five years, and to 19% for later interventions (p = 0.021) [35]. Other studies also implemented interventions shortly after diagnosis or at the start of radiotherapy, in line with the “teaching moment” concept [43, 48].

Use of nicotine replacement therapy

The type of NRT used and whether or not NRT was used in combination with other means varied among studies. Rettig et al. offered combination NRT (e.g., patch + gum or patch + spray) as a standard [35]. Smaily et al. administered nicotine patches for eight weeks, with doses adjusted according to the Fagerström Nicotine Dependence Test [43, 49]. De Bruin-Visser et al. and Duffy et al. provided NRT “when indicated” [29, 38]. Sharp et al. allowed participants to choose between different NRT and ANP; 91% used nicotine patches, often combined with another product [42]. Santi et al. provided behavioral counseling and optional pharmacotherapy and saw 51.2% quit without any pharmacotherapy (“cold turkey”), while in successful quitters 14% had used NRT and 34.9% varenicline. Also, in the study by McQueen et al. [36], 83% had chosen a “cold turkey” approach [20, 29, 35–43].

E-cigarettes as a smoking cessation therapy

McQueen et al. assessed e-cigarette use among HNSCC patients via a cross-sectional survey and found that 21.7% used e-cigarettes as part of their smoking cessation strategy. It did not prove very efficacious: the abstinence rate among HNSCC patients who were using e-cigarettes as part of their smoking cessation strategy was 39%, compared to 72% among those with a strategy without e-cigarettes (p = 0.0057) [36].

Smoking cessation combined with treatment of comorbidities

Duffy et al. evaluated an integrated intervention targeting smoking, alcohol use, and depressive symptoms in HNSCC patients [38]. While the control group received basic advice and referrals, the intervention group received CBT combined with 9 to 11 telephone sessions and optional pharmacological support (NRT, bupropion, and antidepressants). At six months, smoking cessation was 47% in the intervention group versus 31% in the control group (p = 0.048). Both groups exhibited reductions in alcohol use and depressive symptoms, with no statistically significant differences observed between the groups (p = 0.853 and p = 0.668, respectively). McQueen et al. surveyed 106 HNSCC patients and found that smokers were significantly more likely to report frequent alcohol use than former smokers (p = 0.014). Problematic alcohol use as defined by The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT-C) was reported by 30% of smokers versus 14% of nonsmokers, but this difference was not statistically significant [36].

Financial incentives

The study by Ghosh et al. did not provide conclusive evidence whether financial incentives provide additional benefit [40].

Discussion

The existing literature indicates that combining behavioral counseling with pharmacotherapy is often the most effective approach in the general oncology population. This review focuses on HNSCC patients and confirms this observation [35]. Several of the included studies systematically used pharmacotherapy, usually in combination with counseling [35, 38, 42]. Although the length of follow-up varied, abstinence rates of around 30% at twelve months seem to be a realistically obtainable goal [35, 43]. A higher rate up to 68% has been reported, but the discrepancy may be partly due to the inclusion of ANPs such as snus, which were also counted as cessation tools in the study that reported this high cessation rate, suggesting that aiming for a “smoke-free” goal, rather than “nicotine-free,” may lead to higher abstinence rates [42]. This distinction could be important for future research. In the literature, one RCT with only intensive counseling versus a control group also finds a 63.8% one year cessation rate for the intervention group, but the outlier observation is an even higher (77%) cessation rate in the control group (that had less intensive counseling, i.e. regular smoking cessation advice) in that study [41]. A potential source of contamination in the control group arises from the fact that the same healthcare providers delivered smoking cessation advice to participants in both the intervention and control arms of the study. This overlap could have led to inadvertent delivery of intervention-related messages to the control group, thereby diluting the differences between groups. In addition, the trial had broad inclusion criteria, accepting patients who had smoked up to one year before enrollment, and one fifth of the sample had undergone total laryngectomy, a procedure that physically prevents smoking. These factors may have confounded the results.

The use of e-cigarettes is gaining popularity, both as an independent cessation strategy and as a replacement for traditional cigarettes. To date, the available literature has not demonstrated improvements in cessation or reduction rates: Rettig et al. found no increase in abstinence and only a nonsignificant reduction in smoking intensity [35], and McQueen et al. reported a significantly negative effect on smoking cessation [36]. E-cigarettes may provide a false sense of safety, making users less likely to quit completely. The modest reduction in cigarette consumption that was observed was not statistically significant [36].

Integrating SCI in a more comprehensive strategy of also targeting alcohol and depression has been compared to usual care in a randomized trial in HNSCC patients [38]. This combined approach seemed effective for the 6 months smoking cessation (47% quitting compared to 31% in usual care; p < 0.05), but reductions in 6 month depression and alcohol outcomes did not differ significantly between both groups. The authors in retrospect noted that a higher dose of bupropion—effective in treating depression at higher doses—would have been warranted and might have produced stronger antidepressive effects. While these and others’ findings underscore the importance of such an integrated care strategy, practical implementation faces time constraints, limited resources, and organizational barriers [35, 36].

Once successful, the risk of relapse is real and highest during the first six months, so it is important to assess the long-term effectiveness of interventions. Furthermore, prolonging the intervention by increasing the frequency of follow-up sessions, ideally conducted face-to-face, seems beneficial. Interventions with regular and longer follow-up resulted in better outcomes [35, 38, 43]. In one study, patients cited a lack of motivation and personal contact as the main reasons for relapse, and 66% believed that additional follow-up would have helped them maintain abstinence [43]. Since relapse is an inherent part of the cessation process, we should view smoking cessation not as an on–off attempt, but as an ongoing process in which repeated counseling and follow-up are indispensable, hence the importance of further research into the optimal length and frequency of follow-up in this population.

Even an effective treatment will not work if the appropriate target population is not reached. Therefore, it is important to consider not only the efficacy but also whether patients can be motivated to be treated. Rettig et al. showed that only a minority of patients reported having previously received advice on smoking cessation [35]. In this respect, opt-out systems, automatically including patients in smoking cessation programs unless they refuse to participate, seem promising [20], but only if there is sufficient funding and specialized expertise [50]. This opting-out strategy has been assessed in a RCT by Richter et al., however not in an HNSCC-specific population, and showed higher participation in the cessation treatment, but did not prove higher quit rates [51]. The risk of this approach may be that patients feel forced to participate when they are not yet ready to quit.

This review did not specifically focus on predictors of effective and sustained smoking cessation. De Bruin-Visser et al. identified previous cessation attempts as a positive predictor of success [29]. Other studies found higher abstinence rates in patients who underwent surgery, especially those who had a total laryngectomy, likely due to the practical limitations imposed by the treatment [14]. Additionally, this review did not focus on social determinants that may aid the cessation process, such as friends and family support. De Bruin-Visser et al. found no significant relation between the support of friends or family and cessation rates [29]. However, the NCCN guidelines state that having other household members who smoke negatively impacts the cessation attempt [26]. A prospective study conducted within a population of HNC patients by Kashigar et al. seems to support this guideline, and states that “Spousal smoking, peer smoking, smoke exposure at home, and seeing a family physician were strongly and consistently associated with smoking cessation and time to quitting after a HNC diagnosis.” [46]. Finally, the moment of HNSCC diagnosis can serve as a powerful trigger for smoking cessation, a “teaching moment” and healthcare providers should make use of this momentum by offering the right support [14, 20, 39]. In this respect, interventions initiated within the first few months after diagnosis are more effective. Also, other health conditions, such as cardiac or respiratory problems, may be strong motivators for quitting [36]. Conversely, patients with earlier stage disease and thus undergoing less intense therapy are more likely to fail their smoking cessation effort [52].

The key point is that, regardless of the specific trigger, many patients are willing to quit smoking. Santi et al. report that, when confronted with a HNSCC diagnosis, 70% of current smokers expressed motivation to quit, and this level of motivation remained high even after 12 months. This finding highlights that, while the desire to quit is present, sustained support is essential to help translate intention into long-term abstinence [20].

Due to the considerable heterogeneity of the studies, it is not possible to identify a single optimal intervention. It was also frequently not possible to determine the relative contribution of individual components (e.g., counseling, pharmacotherapy, and follow-up) to the overall effectiveness. Nevertheless, this review confirms several key factors, previously identified in the broader oncology population, which also appear to be relevant for patients with HNSCC. This is a patient group often considered difficult to reach due to factors such as increased vulnerability, nicotine dependence, and psychosocial stress. In addition, from this review, new elements have emerged that appear to be particularly relevant to HNSCC patients, including the importance of early intervention after diagnosis, regular and long-term follow-up (especially during the first six months), and the integration with comorbidity management. Incorporating these elements into the smoking cessation strategies for HNSCC patients may lead to more tailored and ultimately more effective interventions, thereby increasing the likelihood of sustained abstinence. Health insurance companies, e.g., in Germany since August 2025, stimulate this multifaceted approach to smoking cessation, by coupling reimbursement of the pharmacotherapy (NRT and varenicline) to the condition of its use within a comprehensive smoking cessation program.

Limitations

A key limitation was the heterogeneity among studies regarding interventions, control conditions, follow-up duration, and outcome measures. In addition, the definitions of smoking cessation varied, and not all studies used biochemical verification of smoking status. None of the studies assessed outcomes beyond one year. Furthermore, studies were included that started interventions at the time of diagnosis as well as at later stages in the disease process. This raises the possibility that patients who had already quit smoking independently may have dropped out, resulting in an overrepresentation of more resistant, ‘hard core’ smokers. This selection bias could potentially lead to an underestimation of the effectiveness of the studied interventions. On the other side, considering patients who stopped smoking for 6 months as ‘still smokers’ may result in an overestimation of effectiveness. Due to the limited literature on this topic, we have implemented an extended study period which introduces potential era bias.

Conclusion

An intensive, multimodal approach, combining behavioral counseling, pharmacotherapy, and structured follow-up, seems to be best-supported, particularly when initiated shortly after diagnosis. However, implementation in clinical practice remains challenging due to time constraints, limited resources, and a lack of structural continuity of care. To support successful cessation in this vulnerable group, interventions seem to need to be timely and accessible and should probably be fitted in a multimodal prehabilitation program. These interventions should also be tailored to the specific needs of HNSCC patients, including a long-term follow-up with frequent interval visits. The emergence of innovative and digital strategies, such as mobile applications, text message support, or online coaching, may offer promising opportunities to improve the accessibility and efficiency of follow-up. Further research is needed to better understand the long-term effects of these interventions and to explore the role of additional behavioral factors in maintaining abstinence over time.
