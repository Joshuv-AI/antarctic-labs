One-Step Thermal
Treatment Route to Obtain ZnO Nanospheres
from ZnAlEu LDH Nanotubes

Thaina Calaça Futamata, Dimy Nanclares, Alexandre Candido Teixeira, Karina Torre da Fonseca, Danilo Mustafa

Abstract

This study presents a novel synthesis route for producing
ZnO nanospheres
via the controlled calcination of nanotubular layered double hydroxide
(LDH) precursors. The thermal treatment promotes the transformation
of the nanotubular LDH precursor into ZnO nanospheres through a restructuring
process associated with LDH decomposition and oxide formation. Comprehensive
characterization using XRD, SEM, HRTEM, and XRF mapping confirmed
the formation of highly crystalline wurtzite ZnO-spheres. Although
no distinct Eu2O3 reflections were initially
observed by XRD, XRF mapping and XANES analyses revealed a heterogeneous
distribution of europium within the calcined material. Eu-rich domains
coexist with regions dominated by crystalline ZnO nanospheres, suggesting
partial segregation of europium during the thermal transformation
process. XANES analysis verified europium predominantly in the +3
oxidation state. The photocatalytic efficiency of the resulting material
was evaluated through the degradation of Rhodamine 6G (R6G) under
UV irradiation. The ZnO-spheres achieving ∼90% R6G degradation
after 300 min, following pseudo-first-order kinetics (k
app= 0.601 h–1). This significant activity
is attributed to the synergistic effect of the spherical morphology,
which provides a surface area and improved light-harvesting efficiency,
and the synergistic role of the heterostructure formed between the
ZnO-spheres and the Eu3+ rich domains, which facilitates
spatial charge carrier separation at the interface, reducing electron–hole
recombination. This work underscores the potential of LDHs as sacrificial
templates for creating morphologically controlled mixed oxide photocatalysts
and demonstrates the ZnO-spheres as a highly promising candidate for
environmental remediation applications.

Introduction

Rhodamine 6G (R6G) is
a xanthene-based dye widely employed as a
model pollutant in photocatalytic degradation studies due to its well-defined
molecular structure, intense visible absorption, and high photostability. It is frequently detected in wastewater from
textile, printing, and chemical industries, posing significant environmental
risks due to its persistence, potential toxicity, and low biodegradability.
,
 The aromatic rings and nitrogen-containing functional groups in
its structure confer high resistance to biodegradation, underscoring
the need for advanced oxidative treatment methods.
,
 Furthermore,
its strong absorption band at approximately 526 nm enables straightforward
monitoring of degradation kinetics via UV–Vis spectroscopy. These features make R6G an ideal probe molecule
for assessing the efficiency of novel photocatalytic materials under
controlled laboratory conditions.

Zinc oxide (ZnO) is a wide-bandgap
semiconductor with a large exciton
binding energy, long recognized for its
versatility in applications ranging from electronic devices and sensors
to photocatalysis. In particular, ZnO
has attracted increasing attention for the degradation of persistent
organic pollutants (POPs). For solar-driven
applications, owing to its advantages over benchmark TiO2, including a broader absorption spectrum in the solar light, low
cost, and nontoxicity. However, the photocatalytic
performance of pristine ZnO is limited by its relatively wide bandgap,
which requires high-energy photons that represent only a small fraction
of the solar spectrum. One strategy to improve its efficiency involves
increasing the surface area and tailoring its morphology to enhance
light harvesting and charge separation efficiency.

materials with the general formula [MII

 Isomorphic substitution of divalent cations in otherwise neutral

 Self-supported Zn/Al/Eu LDH nanotubes

 serve as ideal sacrificial templates

While numerous studies
have reported on Eu doped ZnO in various
morphologies, such as nanoparticles or
thin films, the use of nanotubular LDHs
as sacrificial precursors to engineer well-defined ZnO-spheres remains
largely unexplored. Such a morphological transformation, from ordered
nanotubular precursors to spherical architectures, is expected to
provide enhanced light harvesting, higher surface-to-volume ratios,
and improved dispersion of Eu species. These features highlight the
potential of LDH-derived nanostructures as versatile platforms for
designing advanced ZnO-based photocatalysts.

This work, therefore,
introduces a novel morphology transformation
route in which self-supported ZnAl with Eu3+ layered double
hydroxide (LDH) nanotubes are converted into ZnO-spheres through controlled
thermal decomposition. While LDHs have been widely explored as precursors
for metal oxides, reports on the preservation of a well-defined, templated
nanostructure during the transition from a tubular to a spherical
morphology remain scarce. By combining a nanostructured soft-templating
synthesis with calcination-induced restructuring, this study provides
new insights into the relationship between precursor architecture
and final oxide properties. The resulting nanospheres were thoroughly
characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), thermogravimetric analysis
(TGA), X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES), X-ray excited
optical luminescence (XEOL), scanning and transmission electron microscopy
(SEM/TEM), and their photocatalytic performance was assessed through
the degradation of Rhodamine 6G under UV irradiation, serving as a
proof-of-concept application to demonstrate the functional potential
of the new nanosphere morphology. The findings offer valuable guidelines
for tailoring ZnO-based nanostructures for environmental and optoelectronic
applications. Rather than focusing on band gap optimization, the present
study aims to establish a structure–morphology–function
relationship by demonstrating how the thermal transformation of LDH
nanotubes into ZnO nanospheres influences the photocatalytic response.
The originality of this approach lies in the conversion of luminescent
LDH nanotubes into mixed-oxide nanospheres while preserving complex
hierarchical features inherited from the precursor architecture.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate
(Zn­(NO3)2·6H2O, 96%) and aluminum
nitrate nonahydrate (Al­(NO3)3·9H2O, 98%) were obtained from LabSynth, while sodium hydroxide (NaOH,
97%) was supplied by Vetec. Europium­(III) nitrate hexahydrate (Eu­(NO3)3·6H2O) was synthesized in house
by dissolving a stoichiometric amount of europium­(III) oxide (Eu2O3, 99%, CSTARM) in dilute nitric acid under reflux
conditions, following a reported procedure. The organic anion benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylic acid (BTC) and the
triblock copolymer Pluronic P-123 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Synthesis

The sample, denoted as
LDH ZnAlEu15-Tubes, with nominal composition [Zn2A0.85Eu0.15(OH)6]·(BTC3–)­0.33·pH2O, was synthesized via coprecipitation at
constant pH under ambient pressure, following a modified procedure
based on previous report.
−

 First, a micellar solution was prepared by dissolving
Pluronic P-123 (0.15 wt %) and benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylic acid (BTC,
11.5 mmol L–1) in 200 mL of deionized water. The
pH was adjusted to 8 using NaOH. The solution was heated to 60 °C
to ensure full dissolution of components and then cooled to room temperature.
A second heating cycle at 60 °C was applied to promote micellization.

Subsequently, 10 mL of a 1 mol L–1 aqueous solution
containing the metal nitrates Zn­(NO3)2·6H2O, Al­(NO3)3·9H2O, and
Eu­(NO3)3·6H2O in a molar ratio
of 2:0.85:0.15 was added dropwise (∼10 mL h–1) to the micellar solution under continuous stirring. The pH was
maintained at 8 throughout the addition using a Metrohm 848 Titrino
Plus automatic titrator. After complete addition, the resulting suspension
was aged statically at 60 °C for 48 h. The solid product was
collected by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with deionized water,
and then subjected to two washing steps with methanol under sonication
(15 min each) to ensure complete removal of the residual Pluronic
P-123 template. Finally, the material was dried in an oven at 60 °C
for 72 h.

Calcination

The as-synthesized LDH
ZnAlEu15-Tubes was calcined in a static-air muffle furnace. The temperature
was ramped from room temperature to 550 °C at a rate of 5 °C
min–1 and held at the target temperature for 48
h.

Although the thermal decomposition of the LDH is completed
at substantially lower temperatures, the extended calcination time
was intentionally selected to ensure that the material reached a stable
final morphology prior to structural, optical, and photocatalytic
characterization. The objective of the present study was to investigate
the properties of the fully transformed ZnO nanospheres rather than
the kinetics of the morphology evolution.

Rhodamine 6G Photocatalytic Degradation

Rhodamine 6G (R6G, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as the model dye. A
5 μM aqueous solution was prepared at neutral pH (pH 6.5). In
a typical experiment, 20 mL of the R6G solution was mixed with 20
mg of the catalyst (the ZnO-spheres derived from LDH calcination)
and sonicated for 20 min to ensure uniform dispersion. Photocatalytic
degradation was performed using a monochromatic excitation source
at 352 nm provided by an FS5 spectrofluorometer (Edinburgh Instruments,
UK) operating in continuous-output mode. The dye–catalyst suspension
was kept in a sealed beaker and irradiated for 5 h. For reproducibility
and comparative analysis, the photocatalytic experiments were independently
repeated four times for both the ZnO nanospheres and the precursor
LDH nanotubes under identical experimental conditions. The reported
degradation curves correspond to the average values, and the error
bars represent the standard error of the mean (SEM). Aliquots of 1.5
mL were withdrawn at regular intervals (0, 60, 120, 180, 240, and
300 min), centrifuged to remove the catalyst, and the absorbance of
the supernatant was recorded at 526 nm to monitor degradation.

Characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction
(XRD) patterns were recorded in Bragg–Brentano geometry using
a D8 Discover diffractometer (Bruker) with Cu Kα radiation (λ
= 1.5418 Å, 40 kV, 30 mA) and a Lynxeye detector. Data were collected
over a 2θ range of 4° to 70°, with a step size of
0.02°, an integration time of 1 s per step, and sample rotation
at 15 rpm. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on a TGA
Q500 instrument (TA Instruments) under an air flow of 60 mL·min–1, heating from room temperature to 800 °C at
a rate of 5 °C·min–1. Transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) images were obtained with a JEOL JEM-2100 microscope
operated at 200 kV, equipped with a lanthanum hexaboride (LaB6) electron source, providing a spatial resolution of up to
0.2 nm. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses were performed
using a JEOL JSM-7401F field emission scanning electron microscope
(FESEM) operated at 5.0 kV with a magnification of 50,000×. Samples
were gold coated prior to analysis. Photoluminescence excitation and
emission spectra were recorded at room temperature using an FS5 spectrofluorometer
(Edinburgh Instruments) equipped with a 150 W xenon arc lamp. Measurements
were performed in solid-state using front-face geometry, with spectral
correction applied to both excitation and emission channels. The synchrotron-based
studies were conducted at the Carnaúba beamline from Sirius, the fourth generation synchrotron
accelerator from the Brazilian Synchrotron Light Laboratory (LNLS)
that is part of the Brazilian Center for Research in Energy and Materials
(CNPEM), a private nonprofit under the Brazilian Ministry of Science,
Technology and Innovations (MCTI). The studies were conducted at room
temperature and pressure, at the Tarumã endstation, using a
submicrometric X-ray beam. STXM and XRF images were recorded with
excitation energy at 9725 eV, in which is found the highest beamline
flux. The powder sample sustained in carbon tape was raster scanned
in areas of 200 μm × 200 μm, with pixel size 2 μm,
and 50 μm × 50 μm, with pixel size 0.5 μm.
STXM signal was recorded using a Photodiode Alibava model AS04–105A,
while XRF measurements were taken using two silicon drift detectors
(SDD) model Vortex, 4 elements, from Hitachi. These images were analyzed
using the software PyMCA. XANES and XEOL
studies were performed simultaneously in energy scans around the Eu
LIII and Zn K-edge. XANES signal was recorded in fluorescence mode
using both the Vortex detectors, and the data analyzed using the software
Athena from the suíte Demeter. XEOL total yield was acquired using a charge-coupled device (CCD)
model QE-Pro-ABS (Ocean Optics), which covers the range from 200–950
nm. XEOL emission spectrum was acquired with the same detector at
fixed energy of 7000 eV.

Results and Discussion

Structural Characterization

The diffraction
pattern of the as-synthesized precursor () exhibits broad reflections typical of nanocrystalline
layered double hydroxide (LDH) phase based on ZnAlEu (LDH ZnAlEu15-Tubes),
with peaks matching the characteristic reflections previously reported
for nanotubular LDHs intercalated with benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylate
(BTC) anions. No additional crystalline
phases or impurities are detected, confirming the purity and structural
integrity of the precursor prior to thermal treatment.

Upon calcination (resulting in the sample denoted
as ZnO-spheres),
the XRD profile undergoes a complete transformation. Reflections associated
with the LDH phase disappear entirely, indicating complete phase transformation.
The diffractogram displays the peaks marked in blue (2θ ≈
31.8°, 34.7°, 36.2°, 46.8°, 56.7°, 61.7°,
65.9°, 68.2°; see Supporting Information, Table S1), are indexed to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110),
(103), (200), and (112) planes of hexagonal wurtzite ZnO, in agreement
with Crystallographic Open Database (COD) card 1010339. The interplanar
distances (dhkl) and lattice parameters (a = b = 0.323
nm, c = 0.514 nm) calculated from the pattern are consistent with
literature values, confirming the presence of ZnO as a major phase.
,

After calcination, the aluminum and europium initially present
in the LDH layers are not detected as a separate crystalline phase.
This behavior is consistent with previous reports on ZnAl LDHs, where
the decomposition leads to the formation of ZnO nanocrystals homogeneously
dispersed within an amorphous Al–O matrix, rather than to the
crystallization of separate alumina phases. The absence of diffraction peaks related to europium oxides indicates
that Eu3+ species are finely distributed in the amorphous
Al–O matrix or partially incorporated into the ZnO lattice,
in both cases at levels below the detection threshold of XRD.

Thermal Transformation

Thermogravimetric
analysis (TGA) of the LDH ZnAlEu15-Tubes precursor reveals three well-defined
mass loss steps (Figure S1). The initial
mass loss event, centered at ≈90 °C, attributed to the
desorption of physisorbed water molecules from the external surface
and interlayer galleries, held by weak hydrogen bonding.

A second, more pronounced mass loss step
occurs at ≈220 °C. This event is associated with (i) the
removal of strongly coordinated interlayer water molecules, (ii) the
dehydroxylation of the brucite-like layers themselves, and (iii) the
condensation of hydroxyl groups.
,,
 These processes collectively initiate the collapse of the characteristic
layered LDH structure.

The third
and final mass loss step, observed between 300 and 620
°C, is assigned to the further dehydroxylation of the lattice,
the progressive breakdown of the residual hydroxide structure, and
the complete combustion and release of the intercalated BTC anions.
,
 The broad temperature range of this step is governed by the dense,
poorly ordered packing of the collapsed layers, which kinetically
hinders the diffusion and escape of the volatile decomposition products
(CO2, H2O).

The total mass loss recorded at 600 °C is in good agreement
with the theoretical value calculated for the complete dehydroxylation
and combustion of the LDH, confirming its full transformation into
a mixed metal oxide. This oxide intermediate is the direct precursor
for the formation of the final ZnO-spheres.

In summary, the
TGA profile corroborates the successful formation
of a thermally stable LDH structure and maps its stepwise decomposition
pathway. The analysis confirms that a calcination temperature of 550
°C, as employed in this study, is sufficient to promote the extensive
decomposition of the layered precursor and the formation of the mixed
metal oxide phase. Minor remnants of the original lamellar framework
may, however, persist locally due to kinetic stabilization during
thermal treatment. This thermally induced transformation represents
the fundamental step that enables the morphological evolution from
the initial nanotubular architecture to the predominant nanospheric
morphology.

Morphological Evolution

Scanning
Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis () reveals the coexistence of two distinct
morphological features: (i) micrometric aggregates predominantly composed
of highly crystalline ZnO nanospheres and (ii) residual plate-like
structures inherited from the original LDH precursor. Although the
material exhibits intrinsic morphological heterogeneity after calcination,
the spherical nanoparticle aggregates clearly constitute the predominant
morphology observed throughout the analyzed regions, while the lamellar
domains remain restricted to localized residual areas. Although these
lamellar-like structures preserve aspects of the precursor morphology,
the absence of characteristic LDH reflections in the XRD pattern indicates
that the original layered crystallographic order was lost during calcination.
This observation indicates that, although the thermal treatment largely
decomposed the lamellar precursor, localized regions of the initial
morphology persisted, likely stabilized by slower diffusion of decomposition
gases or local compositional heterogeneity.

Crucially, high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) of individual
nanospheres
reveals well-resolved lattice fringes with a dominant interplanar
spacing of 0.28 ± 0.01 nm, obtained from FFT analysis calibrated
by the 10 nm scale bar. This value matches the (100) planes of wurtzite
ZnO (d100 = 0.279 nm), and the corresponding reciprocal-space
spots appear as a sharp pair aligned close to the horizontal axis,
consistent with a nearly single-crystalline domain. The Fast Fourier
Transform (FFT) pattern derived from the HRTEM image () further confirms the high
structural quality of the nanoparticle, with reflections readily indexed
to the wurtzite structure of ZnO, in full agreement with the XRD results.

X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) mapping elucidates the chemical distribution
of Eu and Zn within the ZnO-spheres sample (a–c). Maps were generated for the
(a) Eu L-alpha and (b) Zn K-alpha emission lines, with a composite
image (c) showing their correlative distribution. The analysis reveals
a clear chemical segregation: intense Eu signals (red) are highly
localized within large, massive microparticles, consistent with residual
LDH phases. In stark contrast, the Zn signal (blue) is strong and
ubiquitously distributed throughout the sample, aligning with the
abundant nanoparticle agglomerates observed by SEM and TEM. The composite
image confirms the prevalence of the Zn signal and shows that the
intense Eu hotspots are confined to specific micron-sized domains.

The spatial resolution of the XRF maps (2 μm
pixel size)
exceeds the dimensions of individual ZnO nanospheres by more than
2 orders of magnitude, and therefore the elemental maps primarily
reflect mesoscale chemical heterogeneities between agglomerated domains
rather than atomic-scale Eu distribution within the ZnO lattice. To
address the question of Eu incorporation at the local structural level,
the optical spectroscopy data provide complementary and more sensitive
information. The dominance of the 5D0 → 7F2 electric dipole transition over the 5D0 → 7F1 magnetic dipole
transition in both the XEOL and PL emission spectra (d and S3) is a well-established indicator that Eu3+ ions
occupy sites devoid of inversion symmetry. This observation is consistent
with partial incorporation of Eu3+ into the wurtzite ZnO
lattice (space group P63mc, noncentrosymmetric),
where Zn2+ sites lack a center of inversion, although incorporation
into an amorphous Al–O or Eu-oxide matrix with similarly low
local symmetry cannot be excluded. The absence of detectable Eu2O3 reflections in the XRD pattern further indicates
that any Eu-rich domains formed during calcination are either amorphous,
nanodispersed below the XRD coherence length, or present in quantities
below the detection threshold. Collectively, XRD, XANES, XEOL, and
PL data converge to a picture in which europium is distributed across
two environments: a fraction segregated into amorphous or nanocrystalline
Eu-rich clusters visible as mesoscale heterogeneities in XRF maps,
and a fraction incorporated into the ZnO lattice at the atomic scale,
the latter evidenced by the noncentrosymmetric optical signature of
the Eu3+ emitter.

Scanning Transmission X-ray Microscopy (STXM) was
employed to investigate
mesoscale density variations (d). Images acquired at an excitation energy of 9725
eV using absorption contrast show a heterogeneous material distribution.
Warmer-colored regions (red) correspond to areas of higher X-ray absorption,
indicating denser and/or thicker material agglomerates. Cooler-colored
regions (light blue to green) indicate areas of lower mass thickness.
This distribution is consistent with the SEM and TEM observations,
where the dense agglomerates correlate with the ZnO-spheres and the
less dense regions with the residual LDH platelets or thinner areas.

The local electronic structure and luminescence properties of the
ZnO-spheres composite were probed by X-ray absorption and luminescence
spectroscopy. X-ray Absorption Near Edge Structure (XANES) at the
Eu LIII-edge was employed to determine the oxidation state
of europium, a critical parameter for understanding the optical properties
of the micro- and nanoparticles present in the composite. The XANES
spectrum (Figure SI-2a) is consistent with
the predominance of Eu3+ species, as evidenced by the intense
white-line feature and its energy position, which closely resembles
previously reported spectra of Eu2O3 and Eu3+ -containing compounds available in the literature.

The luminescence response was investigated
via X-ray Excited Optical
Luminescence (XEOL) measurements, conducted simultaneously with XANES.
The XANES/XEOL yield spectrum (b) shows that the correlation between XEOL intensity
and X-ray absorption suggests that a significant fraction of the absorbed
energy is released via radiative decay channels upon excitation at
both the Eu LIII-edge and the Zn K-edge (c).

The deconvoluted
XEOL emission spectrum, obtained under 7000 eV
excitation (d), displays the characteristic sharp-line transitions of Eu3+, ascribed to the 5D0 → 7Fn (n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4) transitions in the orange-red
region. A broader emission band centered at approximately 380 nm is
also observed and attributed to the near-band-edge emission of ZnO.
,
 This confirms the dual nature of the composite’s luminescence,
originating from both the Eu3+ dopant ions and the ZnO
host matrix.

Additional photoluminescence (PL) measurements
were performed to
further investigate the optical response of the ZnO nanospheres (Figure S2). The excitation and emission spectra
display the characteristic transitions of Eu3+ ions, confirming
the preservation of the luminescent properties after thermal transformation
of the LDH precursor. The emission spectrum is dominated by the 5D0 → 7F2 transition
in the red spectral region, indicative of Eu3+ ions located
in noncentrosymmetric environments. These results are fully consistent
with the XEOL measurements and further corroborate the coexistence
of luminescent Eu3+ species within the ZnO composite system.

In summary, the multitechnique characterization encompassing SEM,
HRTEM, STXM, XRF, XANES, and XEOL provides a comprehensive portrait
of the composite material. Electron microscopy (SEM/HRTEM) revealed
the core morphology of crystalline ZnO-spheres coexisting with residual
LDH microparticles, a heterogeneity corroborated by STXM and XRF mapping,
which highlighted the correlation between dense Zn-rich agglomerates
and segregated, Eu-rich domains. The XANES analysis definitively established
the trivalent state of europium (Eu3+), while XEOL spectroscopy
uncovered the efficient radiative relaxation of the system and its
dual luminescence origin: sharp lines from Eu3+ emitters
and a broad band from the ZnO matrix. Collectively, these findings
confirm the efficacy of the reported one-step thermal treatment of
luminescent LDH nanotubes. Despite the heterogeneous precursor, the
process successfully yields well-defined ZnO-spheres with high crystallinity.
The method produces a complex composite system where these highly
crystalline nanospheres coexist with residual LDH-derived microparticles,
resulting in functional morphologies and attractive optical properties
for both the ZnO matrix and the Eu3+ ions. These findings
demonstrate that the reported thermal transformation is not merely
a phase conversion process, but rather a morphology-driven restructuring
route capable of generating multifunctional oxide nanostructures with
coupled structural, optical, and photocatalytic properties.

These findings further demonstrate that the ZnAlEu LDH nanotubes
function as sacrificial templates, in which the precursor architecture
actively guides the structural reorganization occurring during calcination.
In addition to supplying the constituent metal species, the nanotubular
framework guides the formation of a distinct ZnO nanosphere morphology,
illustrating how the structural characteristics of the precursor can
be exploited to engineer oxide materials with tailored morphologies.

Rhodamine Degradation

The photocatalytic
performance of ZnO-spheres was evaluated through the degradation of
Rhodamine 6G (R6G) under UV irradiation at 352 nm. Figure S3 shows the time-dependent UV–vis absorption
spectra of R6G in the presence of the catalyst. The characteristic
absorption band of R6G gradually decreases with increasing irradiation
time, indicating progressive degradation of the dye. After 300 min
of irradiation, the intensity of the main absorption band decreased
by approximately 90%, indicating the extensive degradation of Rhodamine
6G. Control experiments performed under dark conditions for 60 min
showed negligible degradation, confirming that the observed activity
originates from the photocatalytic process mediated by ZnO-spheres.
To evaluate the influence of morphology on the photocatalytic response,
identical experiments were also conducted using the precursor LDH
nanotubes. All measurements were repeated four times to ensure reproducibility
and statistical consistency.

The temporal evolution of the relative
concentration C0/C for both ZnO nanospheres and precursor
LDH nanotubes is shown in a. The nanospheres exhibited significantly enhanced
photocatalytic activity, achieving approximately 90% degradation after
300 min of UV irradiation, whereas the nanotubular precursor displayed
only minor degradation under the same conditions. The degradation
curves represent the average of four independent experiments, with
error bars corresponding to the standard error of the mean (SEM).

The markedly superior performance of the nanospheres
demonstrates
that the thermal transformation from nanotubular LDH precursors into
ZnO spherical oxide structures plays a decisive role in enhancing
photocatalytic efficiency. This improvement can be attributed to the
increased accessible surface area, enhanced light scattering associated
with the spherical morphology.

As shown in b, the plot of ln­(C/C0) versus irradiation time, confirms
that the degradation follows pseudo-first-order kinetics. The calculated
rate constant for the ZnO-spheres is k
app = 0.601 h–1.

This kinetic performance demonstrates
the beneficial role of morphology
transformation and Eu incorporation in improving photocatalytic activity, underscoring the beneficial role of the unique
nanosphere morphology and europium incorporation. Although the absolute
degradation efficiency is moderate, these results are significant
as a proof of concept, confirming that the LDH derived ZnO-spheres
are catalytically active. The study primarily highlights the role
of morphology transformation rather than the optimization of photocatalytic
performance. The statistically significant enhancement observed after
calcination demonstrates that the transformation from nanotubular
LDH precursors into ZnO nanospheres plays a decisive role in promoting
photocatalytic activity.

Under UV excitation, ZnO absorbs photons
and generates electron–hole
pairs. In the absence of dopants, rapid recombination of these charge
carriers typically limits photocatalytic efficiency. The enhanced
photocatalytic efficiency can be rationalized by the presence of Eu-rich
domains distributed within the ZnO-based composite, which may act
as electron trapping sites and promote charge separation ().

Under UV
excitation, the ZnO semiconductor generates electron–hole
pairs. Due to the band alignment at the interface between these distinct
phases (ZnO and the Eu-rich residual domains), the photogenerated
charge carriers can be spatially separated. A plausible mechanism
involves the transfer of photogenerated electrons from the ZnO conduction
band toward interfacial Eu-rich domains. Whether this process occurs
through direct electron trapping by Eu containing species or is mediated
by interface states and structural defects cannot be determined from
the present results. Nevertheless, the heterogeneous distribution
of Eu-rich domains suggests that these regions may act as charge-separation
centers, suppressing electron–hole recombination and facilitating
the generation of reactive oxygen species.

This process inhibits
direct recombination and leads to a more
efficient charge separation process. The trapped electrons can subsequently
transfer to molecular oxygen adsorbed on the catalyst surface, producing
superoxide radicals (•O2
–), while
the photogenerated holes oxidize surface hydroxyl groups or water
molecules to yield hydroxyl radicals (•OH). Both reactive oxygen species play a decisive role in the
degradation of R6G by attacking the xanthene ring and breaking down
the conjugated chromophore system. Thus, the synergistic effect of
enhanced charge separation and the increased generation of •OH
and •O2
– radicals explains the
superior photocatalytic performance of ZnO-spheres compared to undoped
ZnO.

Among the possible photocatalytic pathways, the most plausible
mechanism involves a continuous electron transfer process until the
Fermi level of the ZnO composite shifts toward a more negative potential.
This band alignment promotes the transfer of photogenerated electrons
to the Eu-rich phase, from which they can be transferred to adsorbed
oxygen species, enhancing the formation of •O2
– radicals. Simultaneously, photogenerated holes in
the valence band of ZnO promote the production of hydroxyl radicals
(•OH) from surface hydroxyl groups and water molecules. These •OH species are recognized as the
dominant oxidative agents responsible for the cleavage of the xanthene
ring in R6G, ultimately driving its mineralization.

Overall,
these findings demonstrate that the ZnO-spheres derived
from LDH precursors combine morphological and electronic advantages
that act synergistically to enhance photocatalytic efficiency. The
hollow spherical architecture promotes effective light harvesting
and provides a high density of active surface sites, while the formation
of a heterostructure with the Eu3+ rich domains promotes
efficient interfacial charge separation, which modulates carrier lifetimes,
hinders recombination processes, and facilitates the generation of
reactive oxygen species. The calculated apparent rate constant of

Conclusion

This work successfully demonstrates
a novel and effective strategy
for the synthesis of heterostructured ZnO-spheres through the controlled
thermal decomposition of specially designed ZnAlEu-LDH nanotube precursors.
The key achievement lies in the morphological transformation from
one-dimensional nanotubes to three-dimensional nanospheres, a process
driven by the decomposition and recrystallization of the LDH framework.

The multitechnique characterization encompassing XRD, SEM, HRTEM,
STXM, XRF, XANES, and XEOL provided a comprehensive portrait of the
resulting composite. It confirmed the predominant formation of highly
crystalline wurtzite ZnO-spheres, as evidenced by well-defined lattice
fringes and sharp FFT patterns in HRTEM, coexisting with minor Eu-rich
residual domains derived from the LDH precursor. This chemical heterogeneity
and the stable trivalent state of europium (Eu3+) were
further elucidated by XRF mapping and XANES, respectively. Furthermore,
the material exhibited a dual luminescence mechanism, emitting from
both the ZnO matrix and the Eu3+ ions, a property uncovered
by XEOL spectroscopy.

As a proof of concept, the photocatalytic
performance was evaluated
through the degradation of Rhodamine 6G. The ZnO-spheres demonstrated
significantly enhanced photocatalytic activity compared to the precursor
LDH nanotubes, achieving approximately 90% degradation of Rhodamine
6G under UV irradiation after 300 min. Kinetic analysis revealed pseudo-first-order
behavior with an apparent rate constant of 0.601 h–1, compared with 0.003 h–1 for the precursor LDH
nanotubes under identical experimental conditions.

In summary,
this study provides valuable insights into the relationship
between precursor morphology and the final properties of mixed metal
oxides. The presented synthesis route is a versatile platform for
designing advanced photocatalysts. The ZnO-spheres emerge as a highly
promising material for environmental remediation applications, combining
the benefits of a surface area morphology with improved charge separation
efficiency. Future work will focus on optimizing the Eu loading and
evaluating the catalyst’s performance under visible light to
assess its practical applicability. Therefore, the photocatalytic
experiments validate the concept that the nanosphere morphology obtained
from LDH precursors is functional for pollutant degradation. This
establishes a basis for future optimization and application of such
nanostructures in environmental catalysis.
