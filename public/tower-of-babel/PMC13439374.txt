An autonomous lab for data-driven homogeneous catalysis
Authors: J A Bennett, N Orouji, A Velayati, Eamon F Reynolds, Matthew E Porowski, M Saha, D Billodeaux, Alexander J M Miller, M Abolhasani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe discovery and optimization of homogeneous catalysts remain bottlenecks in the development of efficient and selective chemical transformations. Here we report Flex-Cat, a closed-loop autonomous catalysis platform that couples parallel miniaturized batch reactors for pressurized gas–liquid chemistry with a hierarchical, plate-constrained Bayesian optimization framework for mixed discrete (ligand identity) and continuous (process) variables. Using rhodium-catalyzed hydroformylation of propylene and a chemically diverse library of phosphorus-based ligands, Flex-Cat conducts 680 experiments across three multi-objective optimization campaigns targeting branched, linear, and tunable aldehyde regioselectivity. Across three campaigns targeting branched, linear, and regioselectivity flexibility, Flex-Cat identified ligand–reaction condition regions that achieved over 2.5-fold improvements in turnover frequency and expanded the accessible regioselectivity range. Notably, Flex-Cat autonomously identified ligands exhibiting condition-programmable selectivity inversion (flexible ligands) across the same catalytic system. Data-driven structure–performance trends are extracted from the autonomously generated dataset, and top candidates are validated by translation to a 20 mL reactor format (10 × volume). Flex-Cat establishes a scalable and generalizable approach for autonomous catalyst development, linking discovery-scale experiments to process-relevant outcomes.Subject terms: Chemical engineering, Homogeneous catalysis

Introduction

The discovery and development of high-performance catalysts, especially metal-ligand coordination complexes, play a critical role in the chemical and pharmaceutical industries, facilitating reactions that would otherwise be inefficient or impossible1–4. Despite substantial advancements5–8 over the past few decades, the process of identifying optimal catalysts remains challenging due to the vast and complex parameter space inherent to homogeneous catalysis8,9. This space spans both discrete variables (e.g., the chemical identity of the ligand) and continuous variables (e.g., temperature, pressure, reactant concentration, and catalyst loading) associated with a given catalyst-ligand system. The combinatorial explosion of these parameters makes exhaustive experimental screening impractical, as it demands significant time, resources, and generates substantial chemical waste.

Traditionally, catalyst and ligand discovery have followed a ‘human-centered’, closed-loop approach10. In this method, researchers rely heavily on prior knowledge and intuition to suggest a new ligand for a given catalytic transformation. A small set of reaction conditions is then tested, guided by empirical experience or literature precedents. The resulting data inform the next round of ligand or reaction condition selection, typically using a one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) approach11. While this method has yielded important discoveries, it is inherently time-consuming, resource-intensive, and constrained by the limits of human cognitive and experimental throughput12.

To address these inherent limitations, high-throughput experimentation (HTE) emerged for the systematic exploration of extensive reaction parameter spaces, including catalysts, ligands, solvents, temperatures, and additives12–14. Commonly employed screening strategies within HTE include OFAT and design of experiment (DOE) methods15,16. However, conventional HTE methods face persistent challenges. Even moderately sized ligand libraries can rapidly generate vast combinatorial reaction spaces, complicating data interpretation and significantly increasing analytical burdens17. Moreover, traditional HTE lacks inherent predictive capabilities, primarily relying on empirical trial-and-error methods that risk overlooking unintuitive yet highly effective catalytic conditions. Recognizing these critical limitations, recent research efforts have increasingly integrated artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning techniques into HTE workflows10,18, paving the way toward autonomous, data-driven experimental approaches. Such integration has enabled the rise of autonomous laboratories19, capable of intelligently navigating complex chemical spaces, predicting optimal reaction conditions, and adaptively refining experimental strategies in real-time.

Recent advancements in autonomous laboratories20–32, which leverage close collaboration between humans, AI, and robotic HTE, offer a promising strategy to efficiently navigate these vast ligand design spaces33 using data-driven closed-loop experimentation34,35. Autonomous laboratories automatically perform experiments, analyze data, and iteratively refine subsequent tests, reducing both the number of required experiments and the associated costs. To harness their full catalytic potential, however, autonomous laboratories must overcome several technological hurdles, particularly in reactor design, experimental orchestration, and scheduling36–40.

A reaction that exemplifies these challenges and opportunities is the homogeneous rhodium (Rh)-catalyzed hydroformylation of olefins41–47, widely recognized as one of the most commercially successful applications of homogeneous catalysis. It underpins large-scale aldehyde manufacturing that feeds major value chains for plasticizers, surfactants, solvents, and related intermediates. Beyond industrial relevance, hydroformylation provides a stringent benchmark for autonomous catalysis because regioselectivity arises from strong ligand–condition interactions under pressurized syngas–liquid conditions. Optimizing hydroformylation processes can significantly enhance economic efficiency, improve product selectivity, and reduce chemical waste. Even this well-studied catalytic reaction still faces challenges in precisely controlling the regioselectivity of the aldehyde product (i.e., linear or branched). Regioselectivity in the hydroformylation reaction depends on both the ligand structure (discrete parameter) and reaction conditions (continuous parameters), such as syngas pressure, syngas composition, temperature, catalyst, and ligand loading48–50. Consequently, identifying the optimal combinations of ligands and reaction conditions to achieve the desired regioselectivity and productivity is a challenging, experimentally demanding task.

Moreover, Rh-catalyzed hydroformylation reactions are typically performed under high-pressure and high-temperature conditions in the presence of reactive gases (carbon monoxide [CO] and hydrogen [H2])3,51. These conditions introduce critical safety, reproducibility, and throughput concerns in an automated setting52. For example, an automated reactor for hydroformylation of olefins must be robustly sealed and equipped with appropriate pressure and temperature control as well as relief mechanisms. Coordinating these demanding reaction conditions with high-throughput screening objectives requires careful hardware design, integration of control software, and real-time safety monitoring to prevent failures or inconsistencies during extended autonomous operation.

A key barrier to autonomous optimization in pressurized gas–liquid HTE is that critical reactor setpoints (e.g., temperature, total pressure, and gas composition) are shared across all wells within a plate/reactor block. This physical coupling constrains experimental degrees of freedom and makes standard independent-well optimization strategies inapplicable. Accordingly, efficient autonomous discovery in this regime requires a decision policy that explicitly coordinates plate-level global setpoints with well-level formulation choices.

In this work, we present Flex-Cat, which is a flexible ligand and reaction exploration platform for data-driven CATalysis. Flex-Cat is an advanced autonomous laboratory platform designed for the accelerated discovery and optimization of homogeneous catalysts and ligands, demonstrated here through the closed-loop exploration of phosphorus-based ligands in Rh-catalyzed hydroformylation reactions. Flex-Cat integrates a hardware digital twin that orchestrates and schedules all experimental steps, from liquid handling to robotic reactor management, enabling robust and reproducible autonomous catalysis. Specifically, we constructed and explored a diverse ligand library of 16 phosphorus-based ligands using Flex-Cat. Using this autonomous laboratory, we executed multiple data-driven closed-loop optimization campaigns targeting distinct aldehyde regioselectivity outcomes, including linear, branched, and maximally flexible aldehyde production profiles. This approach demonstrates the power of autonomous robotic experimentation to rapidly optimize catalytic reactions across diverse objectives, while significantly reducing experimental overhead and accelerating discovery timelines.

In prior work34, we utilized a flow-based automated reactor to rapidly map a Pareto front primarily across continuous reactor variables for fixed catalyst–ligand identity in olefin hydroformylation. While flow systems excel at rapidly changing continuous conditions, efficiently exploring discrete variables (e.g., ligand identity) typically requires extensive washing and crossover mitigation, making it less amenable to initial library screening. Flex-Cat addresses this complementary bottleneck by combining parallelized batch reactors with hierarchical, plate-constrained decision-making, enabling closed-loop optimization across mixed discrete (ligand identity) and continuous variables under pressurized CO/H₂ gas–liquid constraints. In this way, batch screening supports rapid down-selection to promising ligand–condition basins, which can subsequently be refined using continuous-variable tools (including flow platforms).

By combining autonomous operation, digital twin-driven orchestration, and expanded discrete design spaces, this work advances the frontier of catalyst discovery in homogeneous catalysis. The ability to execute multi-objective, regiodivergent optimization campaigns within a unified autonomous laboratory framework sets the stage for broader adoption of AI- and robotics-driven experimentation in chemical synthesis.

Results and discussion

Flex-Cat operates by integrating a physical robotic experimentation platform with a digital autonomous decision-making agent. The physical component comprises a modular, glovebox-integrated robotic workstation capable of fully automated reagent handling, reaction setup, and execution under inert and high-pressure/temperature conditions. Complementing the physical components is Flex-Cat’s digital infrastructure, which is a bespoke AI algorithm designed to autonomously navigate complex reaction spaces by generating experimental hypotheses, interpreting experimental outcomes, and refining its internal probabilistic belief model. This AI algorithm leverages Bayesian Optimization (BO) to balance exploration and exploitation across large, multidimensional design spaces, enabling data-efficient discovery of optimal ligands and reaction conditions for homogeneous catalysis. The following sections detail the architecture and capabilities of Flex-Cat’s robotic hardware, including its modular workflow and digital twin–enabled orchestration. We then present the validation and benchmarking of the robotic system to ensure reliable data generation for AI-driven robotic experimentation. Next, we describe Flex-Cat’s AI initialization strategy, followed by its transition into a multi-objective optimization mode.

Flex-Cat’s hardware

The Flex-Cat platform (Fig. 1a) is a fully integrated robotic chemical workstation designed for high-throughput optimization and discovery of homogeneous catalytic systems under inert and high-pressure/temperature reaction conditions. The core robotic infrastructure consists of a programmable XYZA gantry arm enclosed within a custom inert-atmosphere enclosure, equipped with a modular tool-exchange system. The gantry arm can access all operational zones on the deck and interfaces with task-specific hardware using a suite of interchangeable end-effectors, including an eccentric vial gripper, a well-plate gripper for multi-reactor formats, and a screw capping module for automated vial capping and uncapping. (Fig. 1b) Reagent delivery is facilitated by a multi-channel syringe-based liquid handling system. Solid dispensing is performed using a gravimetric powder dosing unit equipped with an auger-driven feed mechanism and feedback-controlled mass tracking.

Temperature-controlled storage modules for solids, liquids, and well plates support long-term reagent management. Flex-Cat’s hardware incorporates three reaction platforms: (i) a heated orbital shaker module with resistive heating for stock preparation, (ii) a high-pressure parallel miniaturized reactor (microtiter plate [MTP] reactor block) for batch screening at the 2 mL scale (up to 40 different reaction vials per plate), and (iii) a 20 mL scale-up plate (up to 8 different reaction vials per plate). All pressurized reactors are connected to digitally regulated gas delivery lines (CO, H₂, nitrogen [N₂,], and propylene [C₃H₆] mixture) and externally heated via a closed-loop circulating thermostat.

Flex-Cat’s modular workflow (Fig. 1b) consists of a (i) stock preparation module, (ii) reaction setup and transfer module, (iii) reaction module, which is a miniaturized parallel batch reactor module (MTP unit) with multiple plates of reaction vial volumes from 2 to 20 mL, and (iv) reaction characterization module (gas chromatography-flame ionization detector, GC-FID).

In the stock preparation module, the system first uncaps 20 mL vials using the screw capper, followed by solid dosing of ligand precursors using the automated solid-dispensing tool. Solvent addition is performed via the appropriate liquid-handling channel, after which vials are recapped using the screw capper tool and transferred with the eccentric gripper to the heater/shaker platform to ensure complete dissolution and homogenization of the stock solution.

Flex-Cat workflow is modularized into digitally orchestrated sequences controlled by a bespoke hardware digital twin (HDT) architecture, illustrated in Fig. 2, which manages experimental execution, hardware coordination, and real-time scheduling of the robotic chemical workstation. The HDT continuously monitors and records dispensed mass and liquid volume, updating the digital inventory and ensuring tracking and compliance with target molarities.

The reaction setup and transfer module is designed to assemble parallel reaction batches in 2 mL GC vials arranged in high-density well plates. The gantry arm initiates vial loading by dispensing the required solvents, ligands, and Rh catalyst solutions using different syringe modules as dictated by volumes and precision requirements. Upon completion of reagent addition, the gantry arm retrieves the MTP gripper and loads the fully prepared plate into the high-pressure reactor block.

The MTP reactor module operates at elevated temperatures (up to 110 °C), gas pressures (up to 300 psig), and is fully sealed during operation. The HDT automatically initiates pre-run cycles, including inert gas purging, evacuation, and sequential pressurization with C3H6, CO, and H2, regulated by digital pressure controllers. After achieving the desired pressure and thermal setpoints, the miniaturized parallel batch reactions proceed under constant agitation for 2 h, followed by active cooling to 0 °C and post-run purging cycles switching the MTP atmosphere to N2 to ensure glovebox safety before opening the MTP reactor module.

Following each autonomous catalyst optimization campaign, the identified reaction conditions and corresponding champion ligand are transferred to the 20 mL scale-up MTP reactor plate to evaluate the Flex-Cat’s knowledge transfer and scale-up capabilities. All process parameters are controlled through a single digital infrastructure (HDT), enabling seamless translation of reaction protocols from discovery to scale-up without manual intervention. This systematic bridging of scales enables Flex-Cat to assess the fidelity of catalyst performance trends and product regioselectivity at higher volumes, reinforcing the platform’s utility for accelerated ligand discovery and for validating synthetic outcomes under process-relevant conditions within a unified, autonomous framework.

The characterization of experimental outcomes is performed using a GC-FID unit. This unit is used to characterize the products of reactions automatically executed by Flex-Cat’s hardware. The characterization module quantifies aldehyde products and determines regioisomeric distributions for each reaction condition. The resulting experimental data, including turnover frequency (TOF) and aldehyde regioselectivity (SN), are captured and logged by the HDT and immediately used to update Flex-Cat’s probabilistic belief model of the catalyst–reaction landscape. This closed-loop integration between autonomous robotic experimentation and real-time data-driven learning enables Flex-Cat to iteratively refine its experimental strategy, prioritize high-performing ligand candidates, and efficiently navigate large chemical parameter spaces. All of these steps, including prediction, reagent preparation, reaction execution, product characterization, and belief model update, are followed in the same sequence for every new set of Flex-Cat’s reaction predictions, ensuring reproducibility, continuity, and full automation of the reaction optimization process.

Flex-Cat’s model initialization

Each autonomous catalyst exploration campaign conducted by Flex-Cat begins with training its AI model using in-house-generated experimental data to establish a holistic understanding of the catalytic system under investigation. In the case of propylene hydroformylation with 16 chemically diverse phosphorus-based ligands, we initiated the closed-loop campaigns with a Latin hypercube sampling (LHS, designed by a human scientist) dataset (320 experiments) to provide broad coverage of the feasible operating space. Flex-Cat then transitioned to autonomous, model-guided optimization, where subsequent experiments were proposed by the hierarchical BO policy and executed by the platform. This initial dataset provided broad coverage of the ligand–reaction parameter space, which was then used to transition into a multi-objective optimization mode guided by a bespoke BO algorithm. The LHS experiments consisted of ten sets of MTP reactions with 32 different reactions per plate (i.e., ten unique T, P, gas compositions, and 20 unique ligand and Rh concentrations for each ligand in the library (Fig. 3), 2 per ligand per plate). The phosphorus-based ligand library (Fig. 3) was selected to represent a combination of functionalized triphenyl phosphines, phosphites, and related molecules, exploring a range of steric and electronic effects through structural isomers and functional groups. The functionalized triphenyls include both strong and weak electron-donating and -withdrawing groups, as well as adjusting the steric bulk of the ligand through positioning of the functional groups across ortho-, meta-, and para- substitution relative to the phosphine. Additionally included is an industrially relevant bulky cyclic fluorophosphite (L1) reported in prior work34,53. The selected ligand library is intended to be a class of related ligands for probing the importance of steric and electronic effects in the performance of Rh-catalyzed hydroformylation of propylene.

Data-driven reaction digital twin

To test and validate the robustness of Flex-Cat’s AI algorithm and optimize its digital architecture, we constructed a data-driven machine learning model as a digital twin of the hydroformylation reaction using an ensemble of neural networks (ENN) trained on the initial LHS dataset of 320 experimental conditions. The data-driven hydroformylation reaction digital twin differs from Flex-Cat’s HDT: the data-driven model predicts reaction outcomes for a given set of inputs based on the entire available dataset, whereas the HDT captures the current physical state of the robotic hardware. The data-driven digital twin is used as a virtual benchmarking environment to test acquisition strategies, evaluate reaction-optimization trajectories, and tune Flex-Cat’s AI algorithm hyperparameters (SI: Figs. S4 and S5) before deployment on the physical platform. The data-driven reaction digital twin enables rapid iteration and ensures the autonomous platform operates efficiently and reliably. Specifically, we investigated the impact of the number of reactions per MTP run on the mapped area of the reaction Pareto front (SI: Model/Algorithm Validation). Model training loss and prediction parity plots are given in SI: Figs. S6 and S7. A total of 320 sampled reaction data points from the data-driven reaction digital twin using the LHS method were used to initialize the virtual BO frameworks’ surrogate model (i.e., a Gaussian Process [GP] model), while the number of reactions per MTP run was varied from 10 to 40, using a constant total BO experimental budget of 480 reactions. The results, presented in SI: Fig. S8, indicate that 10 reactions per MTP run enabled Flex-Cat’s AI algorithm to explore a larger average area of the Rh-catalyzed hydroformylation of propylene Pareto front within five rounds of virtual BO cycles.

Flex-Cat’s AI algorithm

To autonomously explore high-dimensional catalytic reaction spaces, Flex-Cat incorporates a bespoke AI algorithm that serves as the core decision-making engine in its closed-loop optimization framework. The AI algorithm interprets experimental results, updates a probabilistic surrogate model of the catalytic landscape, and proposes new experimental conditions for execution by the robotic platform. By tightly coupling machine learning with automated experimentation, the AI algorithm enables data-efficient discovery and optimization in complex mixed-variable design spaces.

Following hyperparameter tuning and benchmarking studies using the data-driven reaction digital twin, a batch size of 10 experiments per well-plate was selected for Flex-Cat’s BO campaigns. To effectively explore the mixed discrete–continuous parameter space associated with Rh-catalyzed hydroformylation reaction, we developed a hierarchical two-stage BO framework tailored to parallel batch experimentation (Fig. 5).

Following hyperparameter tuning and batch-size benchmarking using the data-driven reaction digital twin, a batch size of ten experiments per well-plate was selected for Flex-Cat’s BO campaigns. This choice balances end-to-end learning cadence (reaction execution and analytical throughput) with the need to query a broader range of plate-level regimes (temperature, pressure, and gas composition) within a fixed total experimental budget. Because these plate-level setpoints are shared across all wells on a plate, increasing the number of wells per cycle trades off against the number of distinct reactor-level regimes that can be explored. Additional batch-size benchmarking results (10–40 reactions per cycle under equal total budgets) are provided in the SI (Model/Algorithm Validation, Fig. S8). Although the MTP supports up to 40 vials per plate, filling all wells in every cycle is not necessarily optimal for closed-loop learning when end-to-end cadence is constrained by reaction time and analysis throughput; smaller batches enable exploration of more plate-level regimes within a given budget and time.

Optimization benchmarking

To validate the selection of the sampling strategy and acquisition function, a digital benchmarking campaign was performed using the digital twin model as the ground truth. Flex-Cat’s algorithm was compared with fully random sampling, factorial LHS sampling with the same number of samples per plate as the initialization, as well as BO with two acquisition functions, an equal-weighted linear combination of TOF and selectivity, and the same combination with a boost to selectivity further from 0.5. All benchmarks were given the same experimental budget of 688. The bespoke BO with selectivity boosted acquisition performed the best in the maximum observed selectivity metric (SN, 0.82 over 0.74) while remaining competitive in TOF as compared to the unboosted acquisition (0.46 vs. 0.52 normalized TOF) (SI Model/Algorithm Validation, Fig. S9–S11).

The physical constraints of the MTP reactor block, including uniform temperature, total pressure, and syngas composition across all wells on a given plate, necessitate a structured search strategy (SI: Fig. S12). In the first stage, Flex-Cat’s AI algorithm selects plate-level operating setpoints shared across the well-plate, including reaction temperature, total pressure, and partial pressures of CO and H₂. In the second stage, well-level formulation conditional optimization is performed within each well (reaction) by selecting ligand identity and tuning the concentrations of catalyst and ligand precursors. This hierarchical optimization approach allows Flex-Cat to balance exploration and exploitation across the discrete ligand library and continuous reaction parameters while operating within the physical realities of parallelized experimental execution.

To identify promising plate-level condition sets, we initialize a set of intermediate GP models by randomly generating 128 candidate combinations of reaction temperature, total pressure, and syngas composition. For each combination, 512 local variations are sampled by changing ligand identity and reagent concentrations. This sampling method results in a virtual screening of 65,536 total reaction conditions. SN is the aldehyde product regioselectivity (i.e., the fraction of linear or branched product, for either linear or branched campaign, respectively, to total product), and TOF is the normalized TOF. The first two terms, weight regioselectivity and activity equally, while the rectified linear unit (ReLU(SN − 0.5)) adds an increasing benefit with higher regioselectivity as selectivities further from 0.5 are more difficult to achieve. This formula thus favors condition sets that not only balance high activity and regioselectivity, but that push regioselectivity past the 50% threshold. The two trained GP models predict the objective function for each combination, and the average Z score (Eq. 2) across local conditions is used to evaluate each plate-level condition set.

A third GP model is then trained using the plate-level reaction parameters as inputs and the upper confidence bound (UCB) of Z scores as outputs. This model is queried using an expected improvement (EI) acquisition function, which prioritizes global regions with a high probability of improving upon the current best result. EI incorporates both the mean prediction and associated uncertainty to balance exploitation of regions with high mean scores and exploration of regions of high uncertainty.

Once a new plate-based reaction condition is selected, a constrained optimization step determines the top local experimental candidates under these fixed settings. Ligand identity, encoded using a one-hot scheme, is selected based on the highest predicted utility. To introduce diversity and avoid repetition, we supplement deterministic reaction selections in each BO cycle with candidates from alternative acquisition strategies, such as UCB and noisy expected improvement (NEI). This automated reaction selection process is repeated iteratively. After each MTP design is executed, new experimental data are used to retrain Flex-Cat’s surrogate model, and the optimization continues. To ensure effective navigation of the reaction landscape, every three iterations of EI-based optimization are interspersed with one iteration focused purely on exploitation (EPLT) by maximizing the objective function (Z). In alignment with the exploration campaigns, the UCB acquisition function is used with α values below 0.5 to mitigate candidate selection repetition.

To perform a well-balanced mapping of the hydroformylation reaction Pareto front, we carried out three different BO campaigns: linear, branched, and flexibility. These three parallel campaigns allow autonomous discovery of the highest-performing ligand (discrete parameter) and the most optimal reaction conditions (continuous parameters) for producing both linear and branched aldehydes, and to identify the most flexible ligand with the greatest l/b to b/l variation. We define a flexible ligand as one that can produce both linear-favored and branched-favored aldehydes at TOF values exceeding 300 h−1 under different reaction conditions. All three campaigns use the same GP model, trained on the same dataset, to avoid introducing bias between the campaigns.

While the ligand set and allowable operating ranges were specified a priori for feasibility and safety, the BO policy did not incorporate ligand descriptors, pre-trained ligand predictors, or literature-derived performance priors to steer experiment selection. Ligand choice and continuous-variable proposals were updated solely from the posterior learned from Flex-Cat-generated measurements (subject to predefined feasibility and safety bounds). This enabled autonomous identification of flexible ligands whose regioselectivity can be programmatically shifted toward branched- or linear-enriched outcomes by changing reaction conditions, without prior labeling of tunability. Thus, the autonomy demonstrated here should be interpreted as closed-loop optimization and ligand–condition mapping within a predefined, safety-bounded design space, rather than unconstrained discovery across an open-ended ligand universe.

While GP surrogates are widely used in BO, the key methodological contribution here is the constraint-aware hierarchical policy that couples plate-level shared setpoints with well-level formulations in a physically grounded way. We therefore evaluated search efficiency under identical budgets by benchmarking against random sampling and continued LHS and by isolating the impact of hierarchical constraints and objective-shaping terms (SI: Model/Algorithm Validation Figs. S9–S11). The released codebase is modular, enabling substitution of alternative categorical–continuous BO strategies and ligand-prior models.

Autonomy scope

Variable bounds, feasibility constraints, and safety envelopes were specified a priori for pressurized CO/H₂ and propylene operation, and the ligand set and initialization strategy were defined by the human scientist. Within these constraints, Flex-Cat autonomously selected plate-level operating setpoints (temperature, total pressure, gas composition) and well-level formulations (ligand identity and concentrations), executed experiments, analyzed products, updated surrogate models, and proposed subsequent experiments in a closed loop. Because the operation involves toxic and flammable gases under pressure, experiments were run with engineered interlocks and safety supervision.

Autonomous hydroformylation with Flex-Cat

To demonstrate the capabilities of Flex-Cat for accelerated data-driven catalyst discovery and optimization, we applied the developed autonomous catalysis platform to the Rh-catalyzed hydroformylation of propylene using a diverse library of phosphorus-based ligands. The campaign encompassed three multi-objective optimization tracks, each targeting distinct performance metrics: branched regioselectivity, linear regioselectivity, and regioselective flexibility. In each case, Flex-Cat’s AI algorithm guided the selection of ligand–condition combinations through a bespoke BO, while the robotic system executed all experimental steps in a closed-loop workflow with minimal human intervention. Each optimization campaign consisted of three batches of exploitation and exploration cycles (3 × [3 × EI + 1 × EPLT]), totaling 360 autonomously executed reaction conditions.

Substantial performance enhancements were achieved across all three optimization campaigns. The maximum observed TOF (TOFₘₐₓ) increased from 2900 h⁻¹ during the initial LHS phase to 7500 h⁻¹ following BO-driven exploration (Fig. 6a). Simultaneously, both ends of the aldehyde regioselectivity spectrum were expanded. For example, in the branched-selective campaign, Flex-Cat’s BO runs improved from an initial l/b = 0.71 and TOF = 850 h⁻¹ to l/b = 0.73 and TOF = 7500 h⁻¹ (Fig. 6b). In the linear-selective campaign, high-performing conditions emerged, such as l/b = 5.33 with TOF = 150 h⁻¹, l/b = 2.51 with TOF = 500 h⁻¹, and l/b = 1.51 with TOF = 2,770 h⁻¹ (Fig. 6c). These results underscore Flex-Cat’s ability to efficiently traverse complex reaction parameter spaces and identify conditions yielding both enhanced activity and tunable product regioselectivity.

To quantify the sample-efficiency benefit of Flex-Cat’s hierarchical, plate-constrained BO policy, we performed budget-matched in-silico benchmarking against (i) random sampling and (ii) continued LHS under identical feasibility bounds and plate constraints. For each baseline, we report distributions across multiple random seeds and compare Pareto/selection-envelope expansion under equal total budgets (SI: SI:Model/Algorithm Validation Figs. S9–S11). Because all policies were evaluated with the same total number of experiments and identical plate-coupled execution constraints (shared temperature/pressure/gas composition per plate), differences reflect decision policy rather than feasible action space. Under these controlled conditions, the closed-loop policy achieved earlier and broader discovery of high-performing ligand–condition basins than non-adaptive baselines.

The discrete and continuous decision-making capabilities of Flex-Cat’s AI algorithm were evaluated using two heatmaps. The first heatmap (Fig. 7a) summarizes ligand selection frequency across all BO cycles. In the linear campaign, ligands L3, L11, L14, and L16 were preferentially selected, whereas ligands L2, L6, L9, and L12 were predominantly chosen in the branched-selective campaign. In the flexibility campaign, ligands L3, L7, L8, L11, and L14 were most frequently selected, suggesting these candidates offered adaptive behavior across a broad hydroformylation reaction envelope.

To summarize structure–performance trends revealed by Flex-Cat’s autonomously generated dataset, we examined steric and electronic descriptors available for ligands L2–L15 in the Kraken organophosphorus(III) descriptor library33. For each LHS condition, we correlated the measured regioselectivity (SN) with 190 candidate descriptors and identified descriptors that repeatedly emerged as strong correlates across conditions (SI: Ligand Parametrization, Fig. S48 and Table S1). Among these, the octant buried volume (OvBur) was frequently top-ranked and was therefore selected as a compact steric axis for visualizing outcomes observed across the closed-loop campaigns.

Within this library, ligands with larger OvBur values (e.g., L2, L6, L9, and L12; OvBur ≈ 18–21 Å³) were associated with the most branched-selective outcomes observed (SN ≈ 0.4; l/b ≈ 0.67). These ligands all have ortho-substitution on the aryl groups of the phosphorus. Ligands L1 and L4 are in the same range of OvBur values, but are less branched-selective, which we attribute to substantial differences in the electron donor ability of these ligands. The flexibility campaign highlighted ligands capable of modulating regioselectivity based on selected reaction conditions. Ligands with intermediate steric profiles (OvBur ≈ 14 Å³) were among the most flexible ligands (e.g., L3, L7, L8, L11, and L14) that accessed both linear- and branched-enriched regimes under different conditions. While they are sterically similar, they span a wide range of electron-donating strength, as evidenced by their CO stretching frequencies. Within this ligand set and descriptor space, flexibility co-varied more strongly with OvBur than with νCO across the explored conditions. Hydroformylation catalysis is mechanistically complex. For example, possible catalyst structures feature a varied number of supporting ligands with multiple accessible isomeric structures. Additionally, changes in reaction conditions may alter the turnover-limiting elementary step(s)54–58. While this complexity makes it challenging to connect the descriptors to specific mechanistic features, identifying the distinct steric and electronic regions in which high branched selectivity or high flexibility can be achieved provides valuable insight into future ligand design.

Following the optimization campaigns, Flex-Cat selected top-performing ligand–condition pairs for validation in its 20 mL reactor format (Table 1). This translation from the 2 mL discovery format to a 20 mL format demonstrates initial knowledge transfer across an order of magnitude in volume within the same automated execution framework. Both formats were executed on the same MTP shaker module using the same control recipe (gas charging sequence, agitation frequency, and temperature program), ensuring consistent execution for comparative evaluation. The decrease in TOF observed upon translation from the 2 mL discovery format to the 20 mL format may reflect differences in mixing and gas–liquid mass transfer, as well as other scale-dependent factors. Quantitative characterization of mass transfer coefficients and transport–kinetic deconvolution would be needed for future scale-translation studies. The ability to autonomously map and exploit structure–function relationships across a chemically diverse ligand space underscores Flex-Cat’s potential as a general platform for catalyst discovery, process optimization, and fully autonomous translational scale-up.

Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive demonstration of Flex-Cat, an autonomous catalysis platform that integrates parallel batch reactor automation with a bespoke AI-driven optimization framework to enable accelerated exploration and optimization of complex catalytic systems. Using Rh-catalyzed hydroformylation of propylene as a representative testbed, Flex-Cat autonomously identified high-performing ligand–condition combinations for linear-selective, branched-selective, and tunable regioselective aldehyde production. Importantly, the platform achieved this without human intervention, showcasing the potential of autonomous laboratories to reshape traditional catalyst discovery workflows.

The contribution of Flex-Cat is not a single record-setting catalyst, but a reproducible demonstration of autonomous, constraint-aware discovery in pressurized CO/H₂ gas–liquid homogeneous catalysis where plate-level shared setpoints couple experimental degrees of freedom. By autonomously identifying flexible ligands and mapping operating regions that tune regioselectivity and activity, Flex-Cat produces actionable design knowledge that is difficult to obtain with conventional HTE under comparable safety and execution constraints. The key output is a data-backed map of tunable operating basins and ligand classes, enabling rapid down-selection for subsequent process development.

Flex-Cat’s architecture, featuring a miniaturized high-throughput (MTP) reactor array, a centralized data management system, and a modular AI algorithm, enables high-density experimentation in chemically and thermally demanding homogeneous catalysis environments. The use of a digital twin to define parameter constraints and reaction boundaries ensured safe and interpretable operation throughout the optimization process. Importantly, Flex-Cat’s two-stage BO algorithm accounted for both discrete (ligand identity) and continuous (reaction condition) variables within the physical constraints of the experimental setup, allowing the system to reason effectively in a mixed-variable space. This design mirrors real-world decision-making in catalysis development, where discrete structural changes (e.g., ligand substitution) must be evaluated alongside tunable thermodynamic or kinetic parameters.

Initial training via LHS (320 experimental data) provided Flex-Cat’s AI algorithm with a foundation for constructing surrogate models. The 320-experiment initialization reflects the combination of a 16-ligand library, plate-coupled reactor variables, and the deliberate exclusion of descriptor-informed priors in this study. The LHS size selected in this study should not be interpreted as a fixed requirement for adapting Flex-Cat to other reaction systems, where smaller libraries, narrower operating windows, transfer learning, or descriptor-informed priors could reduce the initialization burden. Flex-Cat’s surrogate models were iteratively refined through batch BO, guided by well-defined acquisition functions aligned with specific catalytic objectives. Flex-Cat’s closed-loop workflow generated over 360 experimental data points across the three multi-objective campaigns. Throughout these cycles, Flex-Cat automatically conducted experiments, characterized outcomes using GC-FID analysis, and updated its internal models to reflect new structure–performance relationships. This tight integration of physical experimentation with computational learning improved search efficiency and also facilitated the autonomous generation of chemical insight.

The progression of each optimization campaign reflected clear structure–function trends in the phosphorus-based ligand library. For example, ligands with bulky ortho-substituted aryl groups (e.g., L6, L9) promoted branched regioselectivity, consistent with their influence on insertion pathways in Rh–H complexes. Conversely, linear-selective ligands from the Flex-Cat ligand library (e.g., L14, L16) were characterized by lower steric demand and π-acidic character. Quantitative analysis of the steric parameters computed for each ligand showed that the octant-based buried-volume steric parameter correlated well with regioselectivity. In conjunction with computed (L)RhH(CO)3 CO stretching frequencies, a steric/electronic region of high flexibility (condition-dependent regioselectivity) was identified, suggesting that tunability arises from access to multiple catalytic regimes rather than from a fixed bias. An important outcome of this analysis is that the autonomous optimization can be intuitively understood by chemists using well-defined structural and electronic parameters, providing useful insights that can guide future catalyst designs.

These observations underscore Flex-Cat’s capacity not just to optimize performance metrics, but to autonomously infer and exploit mechanistic sensitivities of a catalytic system. The ligand selection frequencies, heatmaps of reaction condition preferences, and trajectories of product regioselectivity scores all reinforce the AI algorithm’s ability to navigate non-trivial design spaces that are typically inaccessible to brute-force or intuition-driven strategies. Furthermore, Flex-Cat’s generalizable optimization architecture enabled the system to identify reaction conditions that decoupled regioselectivity from activity (a long-standing challenge in catalysis), leading to the discovery of condition-dependent selectivity inversion across multiple ligand classes.

An essential feature of Flex-Cat is its capacity for knowledge transfer across scales. The transition from the 2 mL discovery-scale to a 20 mL reactor enabled validation of AI-selected reaction conditions under industrially relevant settings. This scale-up step demonstrated that the structure–performance relationships and optimal operating conditions identified through miniaturized autonomous experimentation were preserved at larger scales, providing a critical bridge between discovery and process development. The robustness of this transfer affirms the utility of Flex-Cat as both a tool for discovery and a foundation for the end-to-end development of catalytic processes.

This study explored a finite library of 16 ligands within predefined feasibility and safety bounds; thus, the findings primarily support discovery and mapping of ligand–condition regions within that design space, rather than universal optimality and descriptor analyses were presented as within-library correlations. In addition, translation from the 2 mL discovery format to the 20 mL format may reflect scale-dependent mixing and gas–liquid transport effects, which warrant dedicated study during subsequent process development.

More broadly, the Flex-Cat platform establishes a reproducible, scalable, and modular paradigm for closed-loop catalyst discovery. The design principles, including parallelized experimentation, physically grounded AI models, digital twin enforcement, and autonomous scale-up validation, form a blueprint for generalizing autonomous robotic experimentation across diverse reaction classes and chemical domains.

In conclusion, this work demonstrates that combining parallel robotic experimentation with purpose-built AI optimization accelerates catalyst discovery and generates new mechanistic understanding and scalable process insights. Flex-Cat’s success in uncovering high-performing catalysts and conditions for Rh-catalyzed hydroformylation establishes a transformative methodology for autonomous chemical research and provides a framework for realizing the vision of intelligent, self-improving laboratories.

Methods

Flex-Cat’s automation

The automation of the Flex-Cat system involves integrating software and hardware controls. Individual tool operations were programmed in Chemspeed’s AutoSuite for low-level hardware control (e.g., axis movements, dispense tasks, capping/uncapping, transport, and reactor operation). Higher-level automation and scheduling tasks were performed by the HDT in ArkSuite. The HDT is responsible for tracking the locations and amounts of all materials in the Flex-Cat’s inventory, as well as ordering and executing the individual low-level operations for a given full experimental sequence. An individual batch experiment involves preparing a stock solution, setting up an MTP plate, transferring the MTP plate to the reactor block, and executing the reaction. After the reactions are characterized with GC-FID for TOF and regioselectivity (SN) analysis, the data is passed to Flex-Cat’s AI algorithm to update its belief model and obtain the next set of experimental conditions to be automatically conducted by Flex-Cat’s robotic hardware. Since operation involves pressurized CO/H₂ and propylene, experiments were conducted with safety supervision and engineered interlocks.

Stock prep

Stock solutions are prepared inside the Flex-Cat glovebox by first uncapping standard 20 mL vials using the screw capper tool. Ligands are then dispensed into the vials using the GDU-P tool, followed by the addition of solvent. Downstream steps (e.g., solvent filling) can be dynamically adjusted in dispense amount based on the measured dispense values in prior steps (e.g., solid dosing) to ensure repeatable stock concentrations across preparations. The fully prepared stock solutions are then repositioned into designated storage zones on the deck using the gantry arm and the appropriate gripper for downstream use. The vials are subsequently capped and transferred to the heater-shaker plate for thorough mixing and ligand dissolution. Once the stock solutions are prepared, they are positioned in their designated zones on the deck. The standard stock concentrations typically range from 2 to 6 mg/mL, depending on the molecular weight of the ligand and its solubility in the chosen solvent.

MTP plate loading

GC vials with slit caps are placed in a well plate for loading with the reaction mixture. The process begins with dispensing solvent into the vials using the 10 mL syringe, followed by adding ligands using the 1 mL syringe. Between each dosing operation, a programmed rinsing cycle involving solvent aspiration with internal and external needle flushing is performed to eliminate cross-contamination and maintain volumetric precision. The Rh stock solution is then added to the vials using the same 1 mL syringe, completing the loading process. Finally, the robotic arm retrieves the MTP plate gripper tool and transfers the well plate into the MTP reactor block to initiate the reaction.

MTP batch

Once the reaction plate is placed inside the reactor block, a series of pre-run purge cycles is initiated. The reactor headspace is purged with nitrogen for six cycles, followed by evacuation of the block under vacuum. After purging, the block temperature is set to the target setpoint using the cryostat. As the MTP reactor is heated using a cryostat, the calibration between the cryostat setpoint and the temperature reached in the well-plate is required to account for heat losses from the reactor block and the heat transfer fluid lines connecting the system to the cryostat. The MTP reactor block was sealed at a given setpoint and allowed to reach steady-state temperature; the average temperature across the reaction plate was used. (SI: Experimental setup - Reactors) The MTP reactor unit is then sequentially filled with reactive gases in the order of propylene, CO, and H2. A wait time of 4 min is introduced between each gas fill to minimize pressure fluctuations and allow stabilization. Gas pressures are precisely regulated using three digital pressure controllers (Alicat). Following gas loading, the reaction begins, and the MTP reactor unit is agitated with the orbital shaker at 5 Hz (300 rpm) continuously for 2 h to ensure thorough mixing and optimal reaction conditions. Upon completion, the reactor block is actively cooled to 0 °C via the cryostat while maintaining agitation for an additional 1 h to halt the reaction and lower the vapor pressure of the reaction vials. To prevent exposure of reactive gases to the glovebox environment, the block is purged again with high pressure nitrogen for six cycles (25 to 2.5 bar for 10 × dilution per cycle). Finally, the drawer valve is opened, and the reaction plate is transferred back to the loading deck.

Reactor scale-up

Reaction scale-up was performed in a custom MTP reactor plate designed for 8 × 20 mL sample vials. Vial septa were pierced with 1.12 mm ID glass capillary tubing to facilitate gas transfer and mitigate evaporation, analogous to the slit cap GC vials in the screening MPT reactor plates. The plate preparation and MTP reactor running procedures are directly transferable from the 1 mL scale, with the HDT tracking dispensed into new well positions.

Safety engineering

Hydroformylation uses toxic and flammable gases (CO and H₂) under pressure; accordingly, Flex-Cat incorporates layered engineered controls for incident-free operation. Continuous gas monitoring is coupled with an alarm and a fail-safe pneumatic isolation system that automatically shuts off the reactive-gas supply under fault conditions (e.g., gas detection, power loss or loss of exhaust pressure). Each reactor module includes overpressure protection (rupture disk) plumbed to the primary exhaust. To prevent release of reactive gases during any operation that could expose the glovebox environment (e.g., plate transfer), the MTP block executes an automated high-pressure inert-gas purge protocol prior to opening the reactor drawer, using repeated nitrogen pressure-swing cycles to dilute residual headspace gases to below defined safe thresholds. Platform operational procedures were designed so that pressurized operations occur only in sealed states with monitored pressure and temperature traces, and that fault events trigger automated safe-state transitions. Because hazardous-gas operations inherently carry risk, the system is intended to run unattended only in accordance with approved facility protocols; safety supervision is required during pressurized gas operations.

Analysis

After the reaction is complete, the plate is removed from the Chemspeed platform for subsequent GC sample preparation. A robust GC analysis method tailored for propylene hydroformylation reaction mixtures has been developed and optimized. The column used for analysis is a Shimadzu SH-I-5Sil MS (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm). 18.8 cm/s column flow at 59.3 kPa with an injection of 1 µL at 250 °C and a split ratio of 51.9. Column temperature was held at 35 °C for 3 min then raised to 260 °C at 20 °C/min with the FID held at 265 °C. Prior to sample analysis, a calibration procedure is performed to ensure accurate quantification. For calibration, 0.5 M stock solutions of n-butyraldehyde and iso-butyraldehyde are first prepared. Five calibration standards are then formulated by combining 1 mL of toluene, 100 µL of an internal standard solution (0.05 M 1,3,5-trimethoxybenzene in toluene), and 100 µL of 0.5 M aldehyde mixtures in toluene with varying ratios: pure linear aldehyde, 3:1 linear-to-branched, equimolar (1:1), 1:3 linear-to-branched, and pure branched aldehyde. The concentrations of these aldehyde standards are plotted against the normalized area of aldehyde peaks (aldehyde peak area divided by internal standard peak area). This linear relationship is subsequently employed to determine the concentrations of aldehydes in unknown reaction mixtures.

For reaction mixture analysis, the samples are prepared analogously to the calibration standards. Each reaction mixture (100 µL) is combined with 1 mL of toluene and 100 µL of the internal standard. Prepared samples are then transferred to a Shimadzu 2014 gas chromatograph equipped with an automatic sampler. Upon analysis, aldehyde concentrations obtained from GC measurements are used to calculate the TOF and SN. These calculated metrics are then integrated into the modeling framework for further analysis and optimization.
