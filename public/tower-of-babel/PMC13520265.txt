Background and introduction

Societies today navigate a complex landscape where the physical and virtual worlds increasingly collide through Artificial Intelligence (AI) and the “Society 5.0” paradigm [1]. While digital innovation promises efficiency, it risks distancing healthcare from the core values of human connection and compassionate care. To safeguard humanity in this automated era, we should turn to inclusive ethics.

The dominant global gold standards of research ethics, such as the Helsinki Declaration, are rooted in Western principles of individualism. However, African bioethics scholars—notably Godfrey B. Tangwa, Werdie van Staden, and Samuel J. Ujewe—have long established that these principles must be integrated with African ethical canons to be truly representative. This paper argues that the philosophy of Ubuntu and specific Afrocentric canons (ujamaa, utulivu, kujitoa, uhaki, and ukweli) provide the necessary counterbalance to Western positivism, ensuring that as technology advances, research remains human-centred and communally responsible.

The philosophy of Ubuntu provides a framework for fostering social togetherness and promoting inclusive behaviours that honour the intrinsic value of humanity [2]. Ubuntu, derived from the Nguni term meaning ‘humanity’, embodies a profound philosophical concept deeply rooted in African culture [3]. Translated as ‘I am because we are’ or ‘humanity towards others’, Ubuntu offers a counterbalance to the shift to the increased use of artificial, rather than human, intelligence and to the overall decrease in human interaction. Instead of emphasizing individualism and positivism, Ubuntu underscores a holistic approach, acknowledging the interconnectedness within the ecosystem of people, planet, and place [3, 4]. The essence of Ubuntu is: ‘A person with Ubuntu is open and available to others, affirming of others, does not feel threatened that others are able and good, for he or she has a proper self-assurance that comes from knowing that he or she belongs in a greater whole and is diminished when others are humiliated or diminished, when others are tortured or oppressed’ [5].

A human-centred ethic challenges the notions of Eurocentrism, classism, and isolationism [6]. This paradigm shift to a collectivist, instead of individualistic, view serves as both a social ethic and a code of conduct, endorsing modes of interaction that prioritize communal well-being and respect for others. The Ubuntu philosophy finds relevance across all the domains of healthcare, including clinical practice such as nursing sciences and medicine, and research related to these fields.

Theoretical foundations of African bioethics

Research ethics committees commonly derive their principles from the Helsinki Declaration on Ethical Principles for Medical Research [7], which are considered the global gold standard of research ethics. Recently, there has been increasing discussion about the inclusion of Afrocentric pedagogies and paradigms in education and research in Africa [4, 8–10], which, by extension, includes the reconsideration of the exclusive use of the Western or Eurocentric code of ethics regarding research practices across the continent.

While this paper seeks to integrate African research canons into modern research ethics, it does so by building upon a rich legacy of established African bioethics scholarship.

Applied African bioethics: the San Code

The San Code of Research Ethics serves as a vital practical precedent for this paper. Developed by the San people of South Africa, this code was created specifically to prevent the exploitation of indigenous knowledge by external researchers. It explicitly demands respect, honesty, justice, and care—virtues that mirror the Swahili canons of uhaki (justice) and ukweli (truth). The existence of the San Code proves that African-centred ethics frameworks are not just theoretical; they are functional, applied standards that protect the dignity of African participants [14].

Objective

This conceptual paper aims to establish a case for integrating African research canons—ujamaa, utulivu, kujitoa, uhaki, and ukweli—into bioethical discourse to create a more inclusive, culturally relevant framework. As a conceptual review, this paper analyses existing Afrocentric paradigms and uses the Bachelor of Clinical Medical Practice (BCMP) curriculum as an illustrative case study of decolonised ethics in practice.

A shift away from colonisation

The process of decolonisation, defined as the political independence of a previously colonised country [15], extends to education, where ‘a nation must become independent with regards to the acquisition of knowledge, skills, values, beliefs and habits [16]. While the terms ‘transformation’ and ‘decolonisation’ are sometimes used interchangeably in South Africa, it is important to recognise that decolonisation does not imply an attack on white academics by black academics [17]. Instead, it signifies the need to expand texts and work by white or European scholars with contributions from diverse voices. Decolonisation should not be limited to any racial group; both white and black academics have a crucial role in driving authentic research-driven curriculum decolonisation.

Makhele, proposes an Africanised and decolonised curriculum based on principles such as African humanism, cooperation and reciprocity, mutual assistance and association, ecological considerations, and independent and personal spiritual understanding [18]. Irrespective of how one chooses to define decolonisation, the authors agree with Le Grange that it cannot be a once-off event, but a process, and one that is not necessarily easy to achieve [19]. Few curricula in health sciences have been developed since the change to a democratic government in South Africa in 1994. As a result, there has been little opportunity to design a decolonised curriculum, and most changes have been revisions to existing curricula.

To illustrate the practical application of a decolonised ethics framework, we look to the Bachelor of Clinical Medical Practice (BCMP) at the University of Pretoria. The health system in South Africa faces persistent challenges due to shortages of healthcare workers, and their maldistribution between the public and private sectors in urban and rural areas [20]. The BCMP programme aims to increase the number of skilled mid-level healthcare providers in underserved rural areas in South Africa [21, 22]. It can be argued that this curriculum embodies the 4Rs that are central to an indigenous paradigm as proposed by Le Grange [19]. The 4Rs are relational accountability, respectful representation, reciprocal appropriation, and rights and regulation – referring to observing ethical protocols that accord ownership of knowledge (where appropriate) to indigenous peoples of the world.

Relational accountability is when all parts of the curriculum are connected, as is the case in the BCMP curriculum. This curriculum not only incorporates the core competencies developed by the Health Professions Council of South Africa (HPCSA), but also the six Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) that describe the work of a health professional who is trusted to carry out tasks safely and efficiently [23]. These EPAs are incorporated as learning outcomes to measure student competence [21]. The modules are integrated and applied in the clinical component, based on interaction with rural communities during work-integrated learning (WIL) at clinics where these students spend the majority of their time.

Respectful representation relates to how the curriculum acknowledges and creates space for the voice and knowledge of indigenous people. The students in the BCMP programme, called Clinical Associate students (ClinAs), have the opportunity to work closely with community health workers who live in the community and have lived experience regarding the needs and challenges of the specific population that they serve.

Reciprocal appropriation ensures that the benefits of knowledge produced is shared by both the communities and the university. During their final year of study, ClinAs complete a health systems assignment at their local clinical learning centre (CLC) with the specific goal to investigate, analyse and understand how a specific health service is managed, e.g., the dispensing of prescribed medication for out-patients at the hospital or clinic. The conclusion of this assignment includes specific recommendations and suggestions on how a specific service can be improved. The findings are discussed in the student group with the facilitators (lecturers responsible for the BCMP programme) and then presented to the health service providers and hospital and/or clinic management. (In the BCMP programme, lecturers are referred to as facilitators, reflecting the program’s emphasis on self-regulated learning. Instead of traditional lectures, learning occurs in an authentic clinical setting, where facilitators assist students). The purpose of this knowledge building is that all role players will benefit: the students, the facilitators (lecturers), health service providers as well as the end-users of the service (the patients).

Rights and regulations refer to developing and adhering to ethics protocols that accord ownership of knowledge (where appropriate) to indigenous peoples of the world. This concept is not relevant to this study because the term “community”, as used in this context, pertains to the students and staff members rather than a specific indigenous group.

By showcasing a decolonised curriculum in health professions education, this illustrative example prompts consideration of how other fields could also embrace an indigenous paradigm. Research ethics was therefore identified as a suitable example due to its value base.

A shift to Afrocentricity

Most scholars agree on the principles of autonomy, beneficence, and justice for clinical ethics, with some adjustments in how these principles are applied in the research setting [24–28]. However, these principles are not universally acknowledged. Some authors argue that these principles can be challenged in terms of their cross-cultural plausibility and are less relevant in populations where community, rather than individual values, are stressed [29, 30].

When broadening our ethics perspectives, it is of value to explore paradigms such as Ubuntu. Ubuntu is a Nguni term meaning ‘humanity’. It is sometimes translated as ‘I am because we are’ (also ‘I am because you are’), or ‘humanity towards others’ (isiZulu – umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu). In isiXhosa, the latter term is used but is often meant in a more philosophical sense to mean the belief in a universal bond of sharing that connects all humanity [3]. These paradigms challenge the prevailing Western-Eurocentric viewpoint, which often overly emphasises positivism, Eurocentrism, classism, and individualism. Instead, Ubuntu offers a more human-centred and holistic approach that acknowledges the interdependencies within the ecosystem of people, planet, and place. Embracing Ubuntu implies understanding that our actions have ripple effects, not only on other individuals but also on the environment and the larger community. It emphasises the importance of recognising and respecting the interdependence among people, the planet, and the places we inhabit. This holistic approach helps us cultivate a deeper sense of responsibility and promotes sustainable practices that contribute to the well-being of all.

The philosophy of Afrocentricity was not only the philosophical paradigm of the study, but also a framework for understanding the lived experience of participants, who are African. This approach is in line with the work of Molefi Kete Asante, who argues that Afrocentricity is not an anti-European view but rather a framework for understanding phenomena from an African perspective [31]. In Mazama’s Afrocentric Research Design (ARD), seven key characters are identified and were used to inform our study [32]. Firstly, the African experience is seen as a crucial foundation for understanding and exploring any subject matter. Secondly, spirituality holds significant importance and should be given proper attention. The third key character describes the necessity for immersion in the subject matter, emphasizing the need for deep engagement and understanding. Fourthly, a holistic approach is essential, recognizing the interconnectedness of various aspects of African culture and society. Fifthly, intuition is considered a valid source of information, acknowledging the importance of intuitive knowledge. The sixth character claims not everything that matters can be measured, challenging the notion that only quantifiable data is valuable. Finally, the knowledge generated from the research should be liberating, empowering individuals and communities. The ARD’s focus is on the process of learning and becoming, as well as the humanising engagement with the research subjects. These guidelines are further expounded on in five canons: ujamaa, utulivu, kujitoa, uhaki and ukweli.

A convergence and expansion of our ethics horizons

Asante and Mazama [31, 32] propose five research canons derived from Swahili words, representing the fundamental African virtues of truth, justice, rightness, propriety, harmony, and balance. These canons, namely ujamaa (familyhood/community), utulivu (justice/peacefulness), kujitoa (dedication/commitment), uhaki (harmony/justice), and ukweli (truth), form the basis of Afrocentric research methodology. By applying these canons, researchers can ensure the rigor, credibility, and trustworthiness of their work, aligning it with an African perspective and enhancing the research process [6].

Four ethics principles are discussed by relating them to the African canons [6]. These canons were used in conjunction with the traditional ethics principles in recent studies [29, 30]. The first ethics principle, explored by Pitsoe and Letseka [6], is social responsibility, which necessitates that researchers acknowledge their obligation to address issues within local or global communities. The focus of our study was on a specific South African problem, empowering ClinA students with the requisite life skills to become effective and ethically responsible healthcare providers, who are able to provide services in underserved areas [33]. This principle of social responsibility aligns with the concept of ujamaa, which denotes community or familyhood in Swahili. Ujamaa necessitates that researchers recognize the needs of the community, transcend the boundaries between researcher and participant, and allow themselves to be guided and informed by the community’s aspirations and interests.

While the principles of social responsibility and ujamaa exhibit similarities, they are not identical. Social responsibility, in a Western context, is distant and lacks the intimate and familial essence embodied by ujamaa. In our study, the term ‘community’ refers to the collective comprising the ClinAs and BCMP staff members. The insights and perspectives provided by this community were invaluable as they were regarded as experts or authorities on the subject matter being researched by us.

In the context of research ethics, justice is the second fundamental principle that researchers should uphold. Justice entails accepting the responsibility to ensure equitable treatment of all individuals and organisations involved in the research process. We achieved the principle of justice through transparent communication, informed consent, and voluntarily participation. The concept of justice finds resonance in the Swahili word utulivu, which embodies the essence of fairness. For an Afrocentric researcher, embracing the principle of utulivu entails actively working towards fostering harmony within the community and avoiding any form of exaggeration, cheating, or favouritism among or within groups. This guiding principle of utulivu (fostering harmony) transcended beyond the realm of ethical considerations of individual justice as it emphasises group harmony. This African canon could be expressed in the Nominal Group Technique which relies on active participation and expression of all participants’ viewpoints [34]. This method ensured an equitable, inclusive and respectful approach, leading to the attainment of consensus through the systematic prioritization of ideas and suggestions [35]. Throughout the study, each participant was treated with equal regard and fairness, or Ubuntu.

The third ethics principle is non-maleficence, often referred to as the principle of ‘first, do no harm’. It is crucial for researchers not only to protect participants from harm but also to ensure and promote the overall well-being of all those engaging in the research process. In the context of Afrocentric research, the principle of non-maleficence aligns with the concept of kujitoa, which emphasises commitment and a researcher’s positionality. This concept encourages researchers to engage with the community and openly share their perspectives in a reciprocal manner, rather than adopting a value-free stance. To adhere to the principle of non- maleficence, researchers must approach the structure and use of knowledge in the research process with consciousness and sensitivity. Furthermore, researchers should make their underlying assumptions explicit and subject them to continuous self-reflection and self-criticism to enhance objectivity. By critically examining their own perspectives and biases, researchers can minimize the potential for harm and maximize the benefits of the research process.

I, as the principal researcher actively engaged in self-reflection, recognising the essential role played and the influence on the findings. To facilitate this process, a self-reflection journal was maintained, acknowledging my positionality as a white, Afrikaans-speaking woman. Through this introspection, I moved beyond mere tolerance of diversity, striving to cultivate an open heart and mind that embraced different viewpoints and cultures. By immersing oneself in the subject matter and actively listening to the participants’ stories and viewpoints, a deeper understanding of their world and lived experiences were gained. This approach allowed me to embrace the concept of kujitoa, which involves acknowledging one’s positionality and sharing personal perspectives. Consequently, this deepened my relationships with the research participants, establishing connections that went beyond simply ‘doing no harm’.

The fourth ethics principle in research is the principle of respect for the individual, which highlights the critical interaction between researchers and all individuals encountered throughout the research process. Researchers have a responsibility to acknowledge and uphold the dignity and autonomy of every individual, ensuring the preservation of humanity and freedom of choice in all circumstances. In order to participate in research, individuals and their families must receive comprehensive information regarding all pertinent aspects of the study. This ensures that they possess the freedom to withdraw from the study at any point without facing adverse consequences. By upholding the principle of respect for autonomy in research, a culture of empowerment, trust, and ethical conduct is fostered, enabling individuals to actively engage in the research process while safeguarding their rights and dignity. To ensure fairness for all participants in this study, stringent measures were taken to protect privacy and confidentiality. For example, making the research public by means of this article, no names or identifiable information that could compromise the anonymity of the participants are included. Furthermore, participants were given the option to withdraw their participation at any time, without fear of any consequences.

Within the Afrocentric context, the principle of respect for the individual aligns with the concept of uhaki, which encompasses the promotion of human rights, dignity, and social justice among African people. The uhaki principle emphasises the significance of fostering and preserving harmonious relationships between researchers and participants. While there are similarities between the Western principle of respect for the individual and the concept of uhaki, the promotion of social justice extends beyond individual respect to all African people.

The final Afrocentric principle, ukweli, encompasses all the ethics considerations discussed. The term ukweli translates to ‘truth’ in Swahili. Within the research context, it is defined as the ‘groundedness of research in the experiences of the community being studied’ [6]. This principle emphasises a shift from objectivity to truth, honesty, and fairness. The task of the researcher task is to ascertain whether the results and conclusions accurately represent the experiences and perspectives of the research participants or the community they have investigated, rather than their own opinions and views. In this particular study, this was achieved by actively engaging the community, seeking their needs, experiences, and views. I, as the principal researcher listened to their stories without passing judgment. To confirm the truth of the findings, the traditional conventions associated with qualitative research were followed such as member checking and using a constructivist grounded theory approach to respect the voices of the participants and respect the truth of the reported results. This humanising engagement with the research subjects added value and depth to the data as their input and experiences was authentic African.

Conclusion

Expanding our ethics horizons requires more than just a supplementary nod to African values; it demands a fundamental shift in how we conceive of the researcher-participant relationship. This approach stands in purposeful contrast to the Cartesian “I think, therefore I am,” which has long underpinned Western individualistic ethics. Instead, an Afrocentric research paradigm, rooted in Ubuntu, recognises that “I am because we are,” placing communal well-being at the heart of the ethical process.

In the rapidly evolving landscape of Society 5.0, where automation and AI threaten to reduce human interaction to data points, these African canons provide a vital humanizing counterbalance. By synthesising the virtues of ujamaa (community), utulivu (harmony), kujitoa (commitment), uhaki (justice), and ukweli (truth), researchers can navigate the complexities of digital innovation without losing sight of the patient-provider connection. This integrated framework ensures that technological progress enhances, rather than detracts from, the delivery of holistic and culturally relevant healthcare.

Ultimately, embracing these canons—supported by the established scholarship and modelled by applied frameworks —enables a more just, inclusive, and compassionate global bioethics. This paradigm shift does not just serve the African continent; it offers a blueprint for a balanced and harmonious coexistence for all current and future generations.
