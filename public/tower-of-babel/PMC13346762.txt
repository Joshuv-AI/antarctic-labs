Physics-guided self-supervised learning for high-fidelity reconstruction in live-cell imaging
Authors: Ge Xu, Xinyang Li, Xingye Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-term live-cell fluorescence imaging requires low excitation intensity or short exposure times to reduce phototoxicity, which leads to insufficient signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) in the acquired data. Under such conditions, existing reconstruction methods struggle to balance noise reduction and fine structure preservation, often resulting in distorted reconstructions or severe artifacts. Here, we present HiFi-DeconvFormer, a physics-guided self-supervised reconstruction framework for super-resolution deconvolution imaging. By combining a spatial-redundancy-based training strategy with a network architecture that incorporates physical imaging models, our approach enables robust self-supervised learning without requiring ground truth data. Leveraging windowed Transformers and multi-modal regularization, the framework effectively captures long-range biological continuity and high-frequency edge features, ensuring accurate recovery of weak signals. Extensive benchmarks show that HiFi-DeconvFormer outperforms state-of-the-art methods under low-SNR conditions, achieving high-fidelity, artifact-free reconstruction of dynamic subcellular processes.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Live-cell imaging is indispensable for observing dynamic biological processes, such as organelle interactions and protein diffusion. However, an inherent trade-off exists: achieving high spatiotemporal resolution typically necessitates increased excitation intensity or exposure duration, which inevitably induces phototoxicity and photobleaching, thereby compromising cell viability1,2. Furthermore, restricted by labeling efficiency or the inherently low endogenous expression levels of certain biomarkers, target structures often fail to generate high-intensity fluorescence signals. These constraints collectively compel researchers to operate within a “photon-starved” imaging condition, yielding images with low SNR. While advancements in fluorescent probes (e.g., PK Mito Probes3 and StayGold4) and detection hardware (e.g., Lattice Light-Sheet5, 3-Photon6, ISM7, SPIM8, and PALM9) have expanded the performance boundaries from an acquisition perspective, computational reconstruction emerges as a versatile and cost-effective avenue at the processing level. It maximizes information retrieval under a limited photon budget, thereby transcending hardware-imposed limitations. The critical challenge lies in recovering weak, fine-scale, and authentic biological signals from noise-dominated data while strictly ensuring structural fidelity10,11.

Numerous classic algorithms address the degradation and reconstruction of fluorescence images. Deconvolution algorithms, such as Richardson-Lucy and Wiener filtering12,13, aim to invert the point spread function (PSF) to recover high-frequency details. However, given the mathematically ill-posed nature of this inverse problem, these methods significantly amplify noise under photon-starved conditions, as they lack effective mechanisms to handle stochastic noise. Conversely, general-purpose denoising techniques (e.g., wavelet transform or BM3D) show limited robustness against signal-dependent noise. Moreover, lacking optical diffraction modeling, they cannot resolve inherent blur, leading to inevitable structural over-smoothing. Consequently, simply cascading such denoising steps with deconvolution is insufficient to resolve the intrinsic conflict between noise suppression and resolution enhancement, failing to adequately address the challenges of fluorescence image reconstruction.

A critical limitation of these traditional algorithms lies in their reliance on rigid mathematical assumptions to process diverse cellular imaging data, leaving them incapable of extracting complex, high-dimensional statistical priors from large-scale datasets. In contrast, deep learning (DL) has emerged as a transformative solution. Supervised approaches have demonstrated exceptional performance in both general image restoration (e.g., RCAN14, EDSR15) and fluorescence microscopy (e.g., CARE16, DFCAN17), utilizing neural networks to model high-dimensional statistical complexity. However, these frameworks are fundamentally constrained by the strict requirement for paired “noisy-clean” training data, which is often experimentally infeasible for dynamic live specimens. To overcome this limitation, unsupervised strategies (e.g., Noise2Noise18, Neighbor2Neighbor19, and recent methods leveraging temporal or spatial redundancy20–22) have introduced new paradigms by leveraging internal noise statistical properties. However, these “black-box” data-driven models frequently overlook the fundamental physical laws of imaging. Under such photon-starved conditions, this disconnection from physical optics leads to severe reliability issues, including the generation of high-frequency structural artifacts and the “hallucination” of non-existent biological features10.

To bridge the chasm between physical reliability and data-driven performance, we propose HiFi-DeconvFormer, a physics-guided self-supervised framework tailored for extreme low-SNR regimes. Distinct from purely data-driven approaches, our method models the fluorescence image degradation process by explicitly decoupling it into noise injection and deterministic optical blurring, while embedding the physical PSF directly into the optimization loop to effectively balance noise suppression and detail recovery. We also introduce a spatial-redundancy-based strategy that enables self-supervised learning from as little as a single noisy frame (one-shot), effectively eliminating the need for ground truth data. Furthermore, by leveraging universal structural priors inherent to fluorescence microscopy, we integrate a windowed Transformer architecture with multi-scale analysis tools to accurately retrieve authentic biological information from noise-dominated signals23,24,25. We demonstrate that HiFi-DeconvFormer achieves robust, artifact-free reconstruction across diverse biological structures—from microtubules to mitochondria—using minimal training data (a single 512 × 512 image). By fundamentally expanding the spatial receptive field to link discontinuous signals, it pushes the performance boundaries down to extremely low-light conditions (~3.91 dB SNR), establishing it as a practical tool for long-term live-cell imaging.

Results

The HiFi-DeconvFormer architecture and strategy

To overcome the fundamental limitation of ground-truth inaccessibility and achieve high-fidelity reconstruction in dynamic live-cell imaging, we established HiFi-DeconvFormer, a physics-guided self-supervised learning framework. Unlike traditional end-to-end approaches that treat image reconstruction as a “black-box” process, our framework explicitly decouples the restoration workflow into two physically distinct tasks: stochastic noise suppression and resolution enhancement (optical deconvolution). To this end, we designed a cascaded dual-network architecture (Fig. 1a, b).

Schematic of the HiFi-DeconvFormer framework

a Self-supervised training pipeline. The framework explicitly decouples stochastic denoising from deterministic physics-guided deconvolution, optimized via cycle-consistency constraints, physics-compliant fidelity loss, and multi-modal regularization. b Inference workflow. The trained model processes a single noisy frame to yield a high-fidelity reconstruction. c Data generation strategy leveraging spatial redundancy. Statistically independent training pairs are constructed from single frames via parity-based global downsampling and spatial alignment, eliminating the need for temporal data or ground truth.

To address the challenge of training data scarcity, we adopted a self-supervised strategy based on Spatial Redundancy. By performing global downsampling on a single noisy frame, we generate statistically independent training pairs (Fig. 1c). This strategy leverages the inherent local correlation of biological structures between adjacent pixels and the pixel-wise independence of noise, enabling the network to learn structural invariance while effectively suppressing noise25. Building upon this, we introduced a Cycle-Consistency Constraint during the denoising stage. By mandating consistency between the denoised predictions of self-supervised pairs, this constraint significantly enhances the network’s robustness in extracting weak signals under ultra-low-SNR conditions26. In the optical deconvolution stage, we embed the optical imaging model directly into the optimization loop. Following the pre-denoising stage, the post-deconvolution module is explicitly guided by the physical PSF of the imaging system. We formulated a physics-compliant fidelity loss that validates the reconstructed super-resolution image through a backward degradation model, ensuring strict adherence to the physical constraints of fluorescence imaging. Furthermore, we incorporated a specially designed multi-modal regularization term to further elevate reconstruction quality. This synergistic “Physics & Data” dual-drive mechanism ensures that the algorithm robustly enhances resolution while suppressing noise in photon-starved regimes, maintaining structural fidelity and mitigating artifacts commonly associated with purely data-driven methods.

Performance benchmark

To evaluate the performance of HiFi-DeconvFormer under various imaging conditions, we conducted a comprehensive benchmark against state-of-the-art techniques—including the physics-based iterative algorithm MRA27 and the self-supervised DL method ZSDeconvNet28—using the public BioSR dataset. We specifically selected Microtubules (characterized by simple, continuous structures) and F-actin (exhibiting complex geometric features) to assess performance. Since our primary objective is to push the boundaries of reconstruction under photon-starved regimes, the visual comparisons in Fig. 2 specifically showcase low-SNR conditions as defined in the BioSR dataset (Microtubules: SNR Level 01; F-actin: SNR Level 02). Nevertheless, to ensure a rigorous and comprehensive evaluation, our quantitative analyses (Fig. 2c, i) encompass a broader series of SNR levels from the dataset, systematically validating the algorithm’s robustness against varying noise intensities.

In the Microtubule reconstruction task, the primary challenge lies in overcoming noise interference in regions with sparse fluorophores. Authentic fluorescence microscopy images exhibit high heterogeneity even within a single field of view—characterized by uneven fluorophore distribution and diverse structural types—due to disparities in underlying biological structural density, stochastic variations in fluorophore labeling efficiency, and non-uniform illumination. In regions with simple structures and dense fluorophores, all three algorithms demonstrated comparable reconstruction performance (Fig. 2a). However, in regions with sparse fluorophores, the differences between methods became pronounced (Fig. 2b). MRA tends to misinterpret stochastic noise as high-frequency structural details, leading to obvious artifacts and discontinuities in the image. Conversely, ZSDeconvNet, due to the sensitivity of its “Recorrupt-to-Recorrupt” denoising strategy to noise control hyperparameters29, often sacrifices weak signals to suppress noise. This results in over-smoothing, where subtle edge information is lost. In contrast, HiFi-DeconvFormer is specifically optimized for such weak signals and complex backgrounds under extreme SNR conditions. Benefiting from optical physical constraints and our multi-modal regularization, HiFi-DeconvFormer achieves optimal noise-signal separation, precisely capturing the weak cytoskeletal backbone even with extremely sparse fluorophores (Fig. 2d).

To further evaluate the reconstruction performance from a quantitative perspective, we conducted a comprehensive resolution and fidelity analysis on the Microtubule samples at the lowest signal-to-noise ratio (SNR Level 01) (Fig. 2e, f). First, through Fourier Ring Correlation (FRC) measurements (Fig. 2e), our method achieved an exceptional resolution of 130.068 nm, significantly outperforming ZSDeconvNet (139.817 nm) and MRA (288.261 nm), and successfully breaking the theoretical diffraction limit of approximately 255 nm. In contrast, neither the raw noisy image (367.515 nm) nor the clear wide-field reference (Clear WF, 281.657 nm) could provide comparable structural details. Furthermore, the Mean Squared Error (MSE) heatmaps (Fig. 2f) intuitively illustrate the intensity deviations of different methods during weak signal recovery. The experimental results demonstrate that HiFi-DeconvFormer produces the fewest structural artifacts and achieves an optimal balance between background noise suppression and the reconstruction of ultra-weak topologies. Its MSE metrics significantly surpass those of the baseline algorithms, statistically verifying its high-fidelity characteristics in extreme low-light scenarios.

In the F-actin reconstruction task, the crux lies in preserving disordered textures within complex geometries. We further evaluated the methods’ ability to recover complex structures using this more challenging sample. In regions characterized by low structural complexity and dense fluorophores (Fig. 2g top row), MRA achieved decent sharpness for fine structures, albeit with residual noise. Conversely, DL-based methods effectively suppressed noise but suffered from resolution degradation, as neural networks tend to output an “average solution” (regression to the mean) when mapping from low to high resolution30. However, in highly complex regions with similarly dense fluorophores (Fig. 2g bottom row), MRA began to fail in distinguishing fine features from noise, introducing numerous “dark artifacts” that severely disrupted structural continuity. Simultaneously, the over-smoothing phenomenon in ZSDeconvNet became more pronounced. As we shift our focus to regions with sparse fluorophores (Fig. 2h), these performance gaps widened significantly. Compared to the distorted results of MRA and ZSDeconvNet, HiFi-DeconvFormer not only accurately recovered the complex cytoskeletal backbone but, more critically, successfully suppressed the smoothing effect (Fig. 2j). Thanks to our introduced edge-sensitive tight-framelet regularization, it reconstructed precise yet disordered textural details, striking an optimal balance between weak signal preservation and noise suppression.

In quantitative evaluation, we quantified reconstruction fidelity using Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) and Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM)31 metrics (Fig. 2c, i). To ensure equitable comparison, all samples underwent rigorous normalization and intensity calibration. Statistical analysis demonstrates that our method consistently outperforms both MRA and ZSDeconvNet across all noise levels, with the performance differential notably widening as SNR decreases—a trend consistent with our design emphasis on photon-starved live-cell scenarios. Specifically, for Microtubules at the lowest SNR (Level 01), HiFi-DeconvFormer achieved substantial gains, improving SSIM by 12.7% and PSNR by 6.73% compared to MRA, and by 9.03% and 4.00% respectively compared to ZSDeconvNet. Similarly, in the challenging F-actin reconstruction (Level 02), our method yielded improvements of 4.3% (SSIM) and 4.5% (PSNR) over MRA, and 2.67% (SSIM) and 2.31% (PSNR) over ZSDeconvNet. These quantitative findings robustly validate the effectiveness of synergizing a physics-guided framework with multi-modal regularization to break through the reconstruction limits inherent to fluorescence microscopy in low-photon regimes.

Robust performance in data-starved scenarios

In practical biological research, acquiring large-scale, high-quality training datasets sometimes is impractical. To validate the data efficiency of HiFi-DeconvFormer, we evaluated its reconstruction performance on Microtubules samples at the lowest SNR level (Level 01) by progressively reducing the training sample size from 55 down to a single image. Leveraging our self-supervised data generation strategy based on spatial redundancy, we effectively expanded the training set via tailored data augmentation, even from limited inputs (Fig. 1b). Experimental results indicate that reconstruction quality remains virtually constant as the training sample size is reduced from 55 to just 2 images. Remarkably, even under a 1-shot training regime (using a single noisy image), our method achieves high-quality reconstruction for general structures (Fig. 3a), although a minor compromise in weak signal recovery is observed in regions with extremely complex textures. Quantitatively, the performance degradation is minimal: the mean PSNR decreased marginally from 32.049 to 31.639 dB, and the mean SSIM from 0.905 to 0.874—representing a decline of less than 5% in both metrics. This exceptional data efficiency confirms that HiFi-DeconvFormer relies not on the “memorization effect” typical of large-scale datasets, but rather on learning the underlying physical imaging laws and statistical characteristics. This capability renders it particularly suitable for biological scenarios involving harsh imaging conditions or extremely rare specimens.

It is crucial to clarify the distinction between the 1-shot generalization demonstrated above and pure zero-shot (or instance-specific optimization) deployment. The former involves training the network on a single image and performing inference across an entire test batch, demonstrating remarkable structural generalization. However, in practical deployment scenarios, users may wish to completely eliminate additional data collection. To validate this pure zero-shot capability, we trained and inferred on the exact same single noisy image of F-actin under challenging low-SNR conditions (SNR Level 02). As shown in Fig. 3b, relying solely on the single target input without any external data, our method successfully recovered the complex, interwoven branches of F-actin with high visual fidelity. This confirms that our framework does not implicitly overfit to simple microtubule structures, but rather relies on a single training sample to adapt to fundamentally different morphological phenotypes. Furthermore, regarding the potential of leveraging large datasets, our framework exhibits ultra-high data efficiency. Because the network is strongly guided by explicit optical physics (PSF) and mathematical priors (Framelet sparsity), it converges rapidly to an exceptionally high reconstruction ceiling with minimal data. While scaling the training data from 1 to 5 images yields robust global priors, further expanding the dataset results in diminishing marginal returns (as seen in Fig. 3a). Nevertheless, in extreme scenarios where the signal-to-noise ratio drops to limits where intra-image information is fundamentally scarce, utilizing larger datasets may still empower the network to learn broader statistical priors, thereby pushing the reconstruction limits even further.

High-fidelity imaging of long-term live-cell dynamics

To demonstrate the practical value of HiFi-DeconvFormer in biological applications, we extended our benchmark to the highly challenging scenario of long-term live-cell imaging. In this context, excitation intensity must be strictly limited to minimize phototoxicity, resulting in extremely low SNRs that deteriorate further over time.

We initiated our evaluation by observing the dynamics of lysosomes labeled with Lamp1 protein (Fig. 3c). While all methods recovered the characteristic ring-like morphology to some extent, significant discrepancies in detail emerged. MRA was severely affected by noise interference, leading to a loss of structural continuity; the ring shapes appeared distorted and were marred by artifacts arising from unclean backgrounds. ZSDeconvNet, limited by a denoising strategy reliant on manual parameter tuning, struggled to find an optimal operating point due to its high hyperparameter sensitivity. Consequently, most results retained substantial residual noise and dot-like artifacts, causing image distortion. In contrast, HiFi-DeconvFormer maximized the preservation of biological authenticity for Lamp1, clearly presenting complete ring structures with only a negligible compromise in edge resolution. Notably, our method is hyperparameter-free in both the denoising and deconvolution stages—relying only on empirically consistent hyperparameters for the regularization term—thereby demonstrating superior robustness across different structures and SNR levels.

Following the assessment of lysosome structures, we investigated the stability of our method in resolving dynamic membrane architectures, specifically mitochondrial membranes (Fig. 3d). In the analysis of membrane structures, the performance discrepancies between methods regarding mitochondrial membrane stability widened further. As SNR decreased, the reconstruction quality of MRA degraded rapidly, overwhelmed by severe noise corruption. While ZSDeconvNet effectively suppressed noise, it failed when encountering densely stacked membranes, resulting in severe smoothing of fine structures. In contrast, our method not only maintained stability during observation but also precisely captured the edges of mitochondrial membranes, successfully resolving complex morphological features throughout the dynamic process.

Finally, to test performance on dense, reticular geometries, we conducted long-term observations of the F-actin cytoskeleton in COS-7 cells labeled with mEmerald-lifeact (Fig. 3e). In this scenario, our method exhibited exceptional capability in extracting weak signals and complex structures. Facing the noise overfitting of MRA and the confusion of high-frequency details by ZSDeconvNet, HiFi-DeconvFormer accurately separated weak, complex reticular fiber signals from ultra-low-SNR backgrounds, clearly resolving interwoven cytoskeletal structures.

Collectively, these results confirm the effectiveness of our proposed framework in long-term imaging: it achieves an optimal balance among noise robustness, structural fidelity, and hyperparameter stability under low SNR, providing a reliable tool for the precise capture of complex biological dynamics.

Discussion

In this work, we presented HiFi-DeconvFormer, a physics-guided self-supervised framework constructed upon the physical degradation chain of fluorescence imaging. Our primary objective was to achieve high-fidelity reconstruction under extreme low-SNR conditions. By integrating a spatial-redundancy-based training strategy with a dual-network architecture that embeds physical imaging models, our method enables the robust, artifact-free recovery of dynamic subcellular structures from single noisy frames without requiring ground-truth data.

The central paradox of live-cell imaging lies in acquiring high-fidelity dynamic information under extremely limited photon flux. While mathematically advanced iterative deconvolution algorithms, such as MRA, excel in high-SNR regimes by recovering resolution without loss, they falter in low-SNR live-cell scenarios where the coupling of noise and blur leads to algorithm failure and severe artifacts. Consequently, a “pre-denoising” strategy that aligns with the inverse process of the optical degradation chain becomes inevitable. Although DL demonstrates exceptional performance in denoising, its “black-box” nature lacks physical constraints, making it prone to generating spurious structures. The logical core of HiFi-DeconvFormer is the symbiotic integration of physics and data: we leverage the powerful statistical fitting capabilities of DL for pre-denoising while embedding a rigorous optical imaging model (PSF) into the optimization loop to guide deconvolution. This architecture ensures that while stochastic noise is removed, the reconstruction strictly adheres to the laws of optical diffraction, thereby maximizing fidelity under extreme conditions.

Crucially, the stability of the denoising stage serves as the cornerstone for subsequent deconvolution. The “Recorrupt-to-Recorrupt” strategy employed by ZSDeconvNet is highly sensitive to noise hyperparameters, which predisposes the method to performance fluctuations in dynamic imaging. This instability was further validated by our hyperparameter sensitivity analysis, which revealed significant variability in reconstruction quality. In contrast, our self-supervised strategy demonstrates superior robustness, fundamentally deriving from the inherent spatial redundancy of fluorescence microscopy images. Since microscopy systems typically adhere to the Nyquist sampling theorem, high structural correlation exists between adjacent pixels. This pixel-level redundancy allows us to preserve complete biological structural information during global downsampling for independent training pair construction, without significant information loss. By eliminating reliance on manual assumptions of noise model parameters, this strategy not only ensures reconstruction stability across varying structures and SNRs but also delivers exceptional data efficiency.

Beyond providing a stable foundation, we further exploited the intrinsic characteristics of fluorescence images to enhance reconstruction quality. Since fluorescent probes target specific biological structures (e.g., microtubules, nuclear membranes) via antigen-antibody binding or selective targeting, their spatial distribution is strictly regulated by organelle morphology, creating long-range topological associations. This specificity imparts a continuity characteristic to the image content, while the enrichment of probes solely at target structures provides high-contrast edges against the background32. To capture this structural continuity, we introduced a Transformer-based architecture, utilizing its ability to model long-range dependencies to accurately recover continuous biological backbones from noise-submerged backgrounds. Furthermore, to address the high-contrast edge features, we employed the tight-framelet transform to capture sparsity. Through sparse regularization, we precisely preserved weak textural details while suppressing smoothing effects33–35. The efficacy of this mechanism was visually verified in our Results section: facing extremely weak F-actin textures, only the method based on sparse priors successfully avoided the over-smoothing observed in ZSDeconvNet, reproducing clear, disordered textures.

Nevertheless, certain limitations remain. While our method shows robustness to PSF errors and does not require absolutely perfect PSF measurements, reconstruction quality may be compromised in deep-tissue imaging where severe aberrations exist. Future work will explore “blind deconvolution” strategies integrated with adaptive optics. Additionally, the current computational burden of frame-by-frame reconstruction precludes support for real-time online imaging; subsequent efforts will focus on network lightweighting and inference acceleration. Finally, given the inherently three-dimensional nature of biological processes, extending this framework to isotropic 3D reconstruction is a primary objective for our next phase. Our future efforts will focus on overcoming the remaining technical hurdles, specifically reconciling the axial-lateral resolution disparity, refining 3D PSF modeling for deep tissues, and optimizing the computational efficiency of 3D Transformer architectures.

Self-supervised scheme and data generation

In live-cell imaging, acquiring high-quality “clean-noisy” paired data is prohibitively challenging. Traditional self-supervised strategies like Noise2Noise (N2N)18 and variants exploiting temporal redundancy20,21 rely on temporally adjacent frames, but rapid dynamic changes in living cells—such as mitochondrial motility—violate the static scene assumption. While alternative single-frame approaches like “Recorrupt-to-Recorrupt29 ” (R2R) avoid temporal dependency, they typically require manual estimation of noise parameters. Our hyperparameter sensitivity experiments (Fig. 4c) revealed that R2R-based methods are highly susceptible to parameter mismatch, leading to unstable reconstruction performance. To overcome these limitations, we propose a parameter-free self-supervised strategy leveraging spatial redundancy. We designed an efficient and stable data generation strategy to construct training pairs that satisfy the statistical independence assumption without requiring manual parameter tuning or temporal data. For a single noisy image, we perform global downsampling based on the parity of pixel row/column indices to yield four sub-images. A spatial alignment calibration step is then applied to center-align each sub-image, ensuring structural coherence. To address potential concerns regarding the loss of high-frequency structural details due to this downsampling operation, it is crucial to consider the inherent physical properties of SR microscopy. Routinely, the spatial sampling rate of the detector is configured to be substantially finer than the Nyquist sampling limit, generating inherent spatial redundancy. More importantly, the highest resolvable frequency is physically governed by the PSF, whose spatial footprint intrinsically spans an area larger than a 2 × 2 pixel grid. Consequently, underlying biological signals are highly correlated across adjacent pixels, whereas stochastic sensor noise remains strictly independent pixel-by-pixel. Therefore, separating adjacent pixels via parity indices, coupled with our spatial alignment calibration, successfully decouples the independent noise distributions without sacrificing valuable high-frequency information. Unlike traditional random subsampling, which risks disrupting pixel spatial correlations and eroding structural edge information, this systematic spatial decomposition uniformly preserves frequency components, effectively eliminating sampling bias. Crucially, the generated four sub-images exhibit strict spatial non-overlap, thereby satisfying the requirement for pixel-wise noise independence. Each sub-image is subsequently restored to its original dimensions via nearest-neighbor interpolation. This step prevents scale-induced structural artifacts that might otherwise arise from resolution discrepancies between training and testing data. To validate the superiority of this self-supervised data generation strategy under extreme low-SNR conditions, we conducted a control-variable ablation study on endoplasmic reticulum (ER) samples: while keeping the downstream deconvolution module fixed, we substituted the upstream denoising strategy with classical self-supervised mechanisms (e.g., N2V, R2R, and Ne2Ne). Experimental results (Fig. 4b) reveal that compared to processing the raw noisy images directly (PSNR: 24.787 dB), incorporating N2V (26.347 dB), R2R (28.061 dB), and Ne2Ne (28.303 dB) strategies as pre-processing provides varying degrees of performance improvement. However, our proposed strategy—combining parity downsampling with spatial alignment calibration—achieves the highest final restoration fidelity (PSNR: 28.581 dB). This quantitatively demonstrates that while effectively stripping away pixel-wise stochastic noise, our strategy provides the optimal feature baseline for the downstream physical deconvolution, thereby more comprehensively preserving the structural continuity and high-frequency features of weak biological signals.

Network architecture

Predicated on the decoupled degradation model, we constructed the HiFi-DeconvFormer, a cascaded dual-network architecture (Fig. 1a). Fluorescence microscopy images exhibit inherent spatial continuity; however, the intrinsic limitation of local receptive fields in traditional Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) hinders the capture of global long-range dependencies39. Yet, preserving these long-range correlations within weak signals is critical for interpreting cellular functions. Furthermore, fluorophore distribution is highly heterogeneous, manifesting as distinct disparities between dense and sparse regions within a single field of view. The weight-sharing mechanism of CNNs—where fixed parameters are applied across all spatial locations—results in “indiscriminate processing,” rendering them ill-equipped to adapt to such spatial heterogeneity. Motivated by these properties, we adopted the Transformer architecture as the fundamental building block of our network40. In contrast to CNNs, the attention mechanism inherent to Transformers dynamically assigns weights based on feature importance at specific locations, effectively overcoming the challenges posed by uneven fluorophore distribution. The core component is the LeWin Transformer Block24 (Fig. 4d), derived from the Swin-Transformer, which incorporates the Window-based Multi-head Self-Attention (W-MSA) mechanism41. Specifically, while traditional convolutional architectures (e.g., U-Net) rely on 3 × 3 kernels with a restricted local receptive field of only 9 pixels, our W-MSA mechanism utilizes a 20 × 20 window. This expands the single-layer receptive field to 400 pixels, enabling the self-attention mechanism to dynamically calculate similarities between spatially distant pixels and assign high weights to morphologically homologous weak signals. This quantitative leap in the receptive field allows the network to effectively bridge noise-induced breakpoints and maintain long-range biological continuity, even under the extreme low-SNR conditions where local kernels typically fail. The block additionally employs a simple Multi-Layer Perceptron as the feed-forward network and integrates a multi-scale restoration modulator to refine multi-level features within the decoder. As demonstrated in the comparison with a traditional CNN architecture (U-Net42) in Fig. 4e, HiFi-DeconvFormer exhibits remarkable performance in reconstructing continuous weak signals. Furthermore, quantitative performance metrics averaged across diverse biological scenarios and SNR levels are presented.

HiFi-DeconvFormer employs a 4-level U-shaped Transformer backbone with a central bottleneck. The number of Transformer blocks for the encoder and decoder is configured as [2, 2, 2, 2], with 2 blocks for the bottleneck; the corresponding channel numbers are [32, 64, 128, 256], and 512 for the bottleneck. The number of attention heads increases from refs. 1,2,4,8 in the encoder to 16 in the bottleneck, with the window size fixed at 20 × 20 pixels. To meet the super-resolution requirement, the deconvolution stage concludes with a specific upsampling module (1-16-128-16-1 channels). The total number of trainable parameters for this end-to-end joint framework is 43.07 M. Evaluated on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU with an input resolution of 128 × 128 pixels, the computational complexity (FLOPs) is 35.40 G, the inference time is only 63.71 ms per frame, and the peak memory usage during inference is 0.48 GB. This efficient forward-pass speed renders it highly suitable for practical biological imaging workflows.

To further validate the indispensability of introducing Framelet regularization under extremely low-SNR conditions, we visually compared it with Total Variation (TV) and spatial L1 sparse regularization, which are commonly adopted in the field of image restoration (rightmost in Fig. 4h). Standard TV regularization, governed by its “piecewise constant” mathematical assumption, enforces indiscriminate spatial smoothing. In extreme low-light scenarios, this inevitably generates severe “staircase effects” and blocky artifacts, irreversibly flattening continuous biological filaments. Conversely, global spatial L1 regularization directly penalizes absolute pixel intensity. When authentic fluorescence signals are already weaker than the noise floor, the network fails to distinguish between them, leading to the indiscriminate suppression of weak signals and an overall darkening of the image. In contrast, the Framelet transform leverages directional and scale-specific filters to abandon global spatial penalties. By applying weighted constraints across different sub-bands, it precisely strips away high-frequency isotropic shot noise while losslessly preserving the structural continuity—a capability that simple TV or spatial L1 regularization fundamentally cannot achieve.

The necessity and incremental contribution of this tailored deconvolution design are further corroborated by a rigorous control-variable cross-evaluation. When the high-quality denoised output from our method was utilized as a uniform input across different deconvolution back-ends, significant performance disparities emerged (Fig. 4i). The traditional Richardson-Lucy (RL) algorithm, owing to its sensitivity to non-Poisson statistical characteristics, generated severe artifacts (PSNR: 29.531 dB; FRC: 137.171 nm). While the ZSDeconvNet back-end exhibited improvement (PSNR: 31.542 dB), its capacity to resolve weak signals remained constrained (FRC: 156.671 nm). In contrast, the complete HiFi-DeconvFormer framework—integrating our multi-modal regularization and the Transformer backbone—achieved global optimal performance (PSNR: 32.083 dB; FRC: 130.068 nm). These findings demonstrate that although robust pre-denoising forms an essential foundation, the targeted, physics-guided deconvolution design yields an irreplaceable, independent incremental value, which is crucial for breaking reconstruction limits and securing artifact-free, high-resolution outputs.

Datasets and evaluation metrics

The datasets utilized in this study were primarily derived from the publicly available BioSR dataset17 and the open-source repository provided by Qiao et al.28. The static benchmarking data, comprising Microtubule and F-actin images (Figs. 2 and 3), were acquired using Total Internal Reflection Fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy. These static samples were imaged with a high-numerical-aperture objective (NA = 1.49), resulting in a calibrated physical pixel size of approximately 63−65nm. For the long-term dynamic sequences (Fig. 4), capturing lysosomal motility and cytoskeletal reorganization, to minimize phototoxicity during sustained volumetric or high-speed imaging, these sequences were captured under optimized low-light conditions with the excitation numerical aperture adjusted to 1.35–1.41. Detailed acquisition protocols can be found in the original publications. While the raw acquired images possess a physical pixel size of ~63nm, our HiFi-DeconvFormer is designed to achieve 2× super-resolution. Therefore, in our physics-guided degradation model (Eq. 1), we discretized the PSF and the latent clean image x on a finer grid with a pixel size of 31.3nm (0.5× the raw pixel size). This oversampling strategy ensures precise modeling of high-frequency diffraction features and aligns the network’s output space with the target super-resolution domain.

The raw high-resolution Structured Illumination Microscopy (SIM) images were not directly utilized as ground truth (GT). Instead, they were subjected to degradation via Gaussian blurring to align their spatial resolution with the theoretical resolution limit of wide-field super-resolution reconstruction, thereby serving as the standardized GT. Complementing visual assessment, we employed PSNR and SSIM as core metrics to quantify reconstruction quality. To ensure evaluation fairness and physical validity, prior to quantitative analysis, all reconstructed samples underwent normalization followed by linear calibration. This process precisely aligned the dynamic range of the predicted images with that of the GT, effectively eliminating interference from non-structural intensity discrepancies during metric calculation. Based on these rigorous evaluation criteria, we compared the performance of different methods.

Implementation details

The HiFi-DeconvFormer framework was implemented in Python 3.12.3 using the PyTorch 2.5.1 library, accelerated by CUDA 11.8. All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA A100 (40 GB) GPU. Network parameters were optimized using the Adam optimizer with set hyperparameters (β1=0.5,β2=0.999), and a constant learning rate of 10−5. The training data were processed using a batch size of 4, with input images randomly cropped into patches of 128 × 128 pixels. For the loss function configuration, the weights for the regularization terms (Eq. 9) were empirically calibrated to λ1=0.01 for Hessian regularization and λ2=10−7 for Framelet regularization. To accurately balance noise suppression with texture preservation, we adopted a frequency weighting scheme for the Framelet coefficients (Eq. 13): higher penalty weights (μk=0.3) were assigned to some high-frequency directional sub-bands (indices k∈{6,8,9}), while other weights (μk=0.015) were applied to the remaining sub-bands.

For practical deployment, training the model to full convergence on a single input image under the pure zero-shot (single-image instance optimization) paradigm takes approximately 4 h on a single NVIDIA A100 (40 GB) GPU. Considering that this paradigm completely circumvents the immense costs associated with acquiring and processing large-scale paired ground-truth images, this one-time optimization duration is highly acceptable for routine microscopy tasks.
