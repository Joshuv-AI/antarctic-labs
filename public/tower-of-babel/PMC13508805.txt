Evaluation and Comparison of Machine Learning Methods for Type 2 Diabetes Classification and Associated Factors
Authors: Masoumeh Dadashpour, Mohammad Yousefi, Sohrab Effati, Somayeh Ghiasi Hafezi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a prevalent chronic metabolic disorder associated with serious complications, including nephropathy, cardiovascular disease, retinopathy, and neuropathy. Given its increasing incidence and the complexity of associated factors—such as obesity, metabolic syndrome, and sedentary lifestyle—accurate identification is essential. This study aimed to evaluate and compare the performance of several machine learning algorithms to identify key associated factors and detect individuals with T2DM within this dataset.MethodsA publicly available dataset from Kaggle, comprising health records of 99,982 individuals, was used. Five supervised machine learning models were evaluated: Bayesian ridge regression, logistic regression, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), artificial neural networks, and random forest. Each model was trained and evaluated to assess classification performance. Performance was measured using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC–ROC) and accuracy. SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values were used to interpret model outputs and identify the most influential features.ResultsAmong the five models, XGBoost demonstrated the highest performance, achieving an accuracy of 96% and an AUC–ROC of 0.98. SHAP analysis identified hemoglobin A1c, blood glucose, age, body mass index, and sex as the most influential predictors of T2DM. Conclusion: XGBoost was the most effective algorithm for identifying individuals with T2DM in this dataset. It also provided insights into the relative importance of clinical features, supporting more precise classification. However, results should be interpreted with caution until validated in independent cohorts.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia. In 2019, it affected approximately 463 million adults and caused 4.2 million deaths. Its global prevalence is increasing rapidly and is projected to reach 700 million by 2045. Beyond elevated blood glucose levels, diabetes involves complex metabolic dysregulation and is associated with serious complications. Obesity—defined as a body mass index (BMI) exceeding 25.0 kg/m2 in Asian countries and over 30.0 kg/m2 in Western countries—is a major risk factor for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [1]. Additionally, metabolic syndrome, which includes central obesity, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, substantially increases this risk. Diabetes-related complications may be present at diagnosis or develop over time, affecting the kidneys, heart, eyes, and nervous system [2].

Machine learning (ML) is a rapidly evolving field with a wide range of applications across academic research and industry. ML algorithms are highly effective for identifying complex patterns in data and leveraging these patterns to inform predictions on new data, making them valuable for solving real-world problems. Accordingly, ML has diverse applications across multiple domains [3,4].

Currently, ML is considered a powerful tool for disease classification and has improved the accuracy and efficiency of diagnostic processes in healthcare. ML systems analyze large datasets to identify patterns and risk factors associated with specific diseases, enabling more timely interventions. Diseases such as diabetes are commonly detected using algorithms such as logistic regression (LR) and support vector machines, which can process complex medical data [5].

Various ML techniques have demonstrated utility in identifying individuals with T2DM. LR and decision trees have achieved accuracies of 83% and 82%, respectively, on the Pima Indian dataset, while support vector machines and k-nearest neighbors have also shown effectiveness. Notably, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) demonstrated strong performance, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC–ROC) of 0.9 in a decade-long T2DM classification study using UK Biobank data, highlighting its potential for identifying high-risk individuals [6].

Given the rapid advances in ML for disease classification, including T2DM, evaluating and comparing different models is essential to identify the most appropriate approach. This study assessed Bayesian ridge regression, LR, XGBoost, neural networks, and random forest models for T2DM classification. SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis was used to interpret model outputs and identify key features, including hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), blood glucose, age, BMI, and sex. The objective of this study was to address gaps in the literature and enhance the clinical applicability of ML models to improve the identification of individuals with T2DM and support personalized care.

Methods

Dataset

The dataset used in this study was obtained from the Kaggle platform and included information on 99,982 individuals. Among these participants, 8,500 (8.5%) had T2DM, whereas 91,482 (91.5%) did not. The dataset included demographic, clinical, and lifestyle variables relevant to T2DM classification, including age, sex, BMI, blood glucose, HbA1c, and smoking history. This study relied on a single publicly available dataset, and no external validation was performed, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using Python and SPSS software. A significance level of 0.001 was applied. Continuous variables with normal distributions are presented as means ± standard deviations and were analyzed using the two-sample t-test. Continuous variables with non-normal distributions are presented as medians (Q1, Q3) and were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are reported as counts (percentages) and were analyzed using the chi-square test.

Data Preprocessing

Categorical variables, including smoking history, sex, and other string-based variables, were converted into binary format using one-hot or binary encoding, as appropriate. The dataset was then split into training and testing sets at an 80:20 ratio. Continuous variables were standardized to ensure comparability across features. Stratified sampling was used to preserve class distribution in both sets. Rows with missing values were removed, and all analyses were conducted on the remaining data.

Evaluation Metrics and Class Imbalance Handling

To comprehensively assess model performance, multiple evaluation metrics were used, including accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC–ROC. Accuracy provides an overall measure of correct classifications, whereas balanced accuracy and F1-score are particularly informative for imbalanced datasets because they ensure appropriate consideration of the minority class (T2DM). AUC–ROC evaluates the ability of the models to discriminate between positive and negative cases across a range of thresholds. To address class imbalance (8.5% T2DM vs. 91.5% non-T2DM), oversampling techniques such as the synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) and/or class weighting were applied during model training to reduce bias toward the majority class.

Machine Learning Models

1) Bayesian ridge modeling

Bayesian ridge modeling applies Bayesian theory and regularization by treating model parameters as random variables. It combines prior information with observed data to estimate parameter distributions, which may improve classification performance. In complex settings, simulation and sampling methods can be used to approximate these distributions efficiently [7].

2) Logistic regression modeling

LR is one of the most widely used methods for analyzing binary outcomes. This approach is valued for its simplicity, its ability to extend to multiclass classification, and its use of optimization procedures such as iterative reweighted least squares. For imbalanced data and rare events, adjustments such as prior weighting may improve classification performance. These features make LR an effective and flexible tool for data mining [8].

3) XGBoost modeling

XGBoost is a gradient-boosting algorithm that builds scalable and efficient models using decision trees while minimizing a loss function. By tuning parameters such as tree depth and learning rate, it controls model complexity and helps reduce overfitting. Randomization techniques, including row and feature sampling, can increase training efficiency and improve classification performance. In addition, its column-based data structure and use of percentiles to identify optimal splits improve computational efficiency and reduce runtime [4].

4) Neural network modeling

In neural network models, inputs are combined through weighted connections and processed nonlinearly. These models function in a manner analogous to biological neurons, which integrate complex information from multiple inputs and transform it into simplified decisions. Classification is achieved by learning patterns in the input data. Artificial neural networks consist of units that combine multiple inputs and generate a single output [9].

5) Random forest modeling

Random forest is a widely used ML algorithm for classification and regression tasks. It improves classification accuracy and model stability by combining multiple decision trees constructed independently with randomization. Each tree is trained on different samples and subsets of features, and the final prediction is determined by averaging or majority voting. Random forest is particularly useful for modeling complex relationships because it reduces overfitting and is relatively robust to noise. In addition, this algorithm uses two forms of randomization: random sampling of observations and random selection of features at each split. These properties improve classification performance and reduce unnecessary tree complexity.

Evaluation

Several advanced techniques were used to enhance model performance and address classification challenges. All models were evaluated using 10-fold stratified cross-validation to ensure balanced representation of classes within each fold. To address class imbalance, Bayesian ridge and random forest used SMOTE to oversample the minority class, whereas the artificial neural network used class weights during training. For hyperparameter optimization, Bayesian ridge and random forest used GridSearchCV to identify optimal parameters within each fold. LR and XGBoost used predefined parameters or limited tuning to enhance reproducibility. Regularization techniques were applied to reduce overfitting, including Bayesian regularization in Bayesian ridge, dropout and L2 penalties in the artificial neural network, and parameter constraints in tree-based models. Model performance was assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, balanced accuracy, and AUC–ROC. The best-performing fold for each model was selected on the basis of the highest AUC–ROC value, or F1-score for random forest, and the corresponding training and testing confusion matrices were recorded. Feature importance and model interpretability were assessed using SHAP, which identified key predictors such as HbA1c, blood glucose, age, BMI, and sex. Optimal hyperparameter values for all models are provided in Supplementary Table S1. This evaluation framework provides a robust, reproducible, and interpretable assessment of performance across the ML algorithms used in this study.

Confusion Matrix

A confusion matrix is a standard evaluation tool for ML models that enables the assessment of model performance beyond a simple classification of predictions as correct or incorrect. A confusion matrix consists of two rows and two columns, and each cell corresponds to one of the four possible outcomes: true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN) (Figure 1).

SHAP Beeswarm

SHAP beeswarm plot is a visualization tool that shows the impact of each feature on model predictions. The x-axis represents SHAP values, where positive values reflect an increased diabetes probability and negative values correspond to lower probability. The y-axis lists features by importance from top to bottom, and each point represents a sample colored by feature value (blue for low values and red for high values). The horizontal spread of points indicates the variability of each feature’s impact across different samples.

Results

Demographic Profile of the Study Cohort

The study population comprised 99,982 individuals. No sex-stratified subgroup analyses were conducted. Among the participants, 8,500 (8.5%) had diabetes, whereas 91,482 (91.5%) did not. The mean age of the participants was 42 years. In addition, the mean values for BMI, HbA1c, and blood glucose were 27.32 kg/m2, 5.52, and 138.05 mg/dL, respectively. The numbers of individuals with hypertension and heart disease were 7,845 (7.84%) and 3,249 (3.94%), respectively. Best Model

Based on the evaluation results presented in Table 2, XGBoost was identified as the best-performing model. Feature Impact Analysis based on SHAP

The XGBoost model demonstrated the best classification performance on the basis of the AUC–ROC metric and the confusion matrices (Figure S1). High HbA1c values (red) had a strong positive effect on the model’s classification output, substantially increasing the predicted probability of diabetes. Similarly, high blood glucose values positively influenced classification, whereas lower values had a weaker effect and, in some cases, decreased the predicted probability of diabetes. Older age (red) contributed positively to the prediction of diabetes, whereas younger age (blue) generally had a negative effect. Higher BMI values also tended to increase model output, although their effects were more variable. The influence of sex was limited and dispersed, whereas the impact of smoking history varied in direction depending on the combination of other features. Finally, hypertension and heart disease had less overall influence, but their presence (red) generally increased the model-predicted probability of diabetes.

Discussion

This study evaluated five ML algorithms to identify the most suitable model for T2DM classification. The results indicated that XGBoost was the most effective model for identifying individuals with T2DM in this dataset. Using SHAP, we found that elevated HbA1c, elevated blood glucose, age, BMI, and sex were the features most strongly associated with T2DM. These findings suggest that the proposed framework may support research efforts to improve the identification of individuals with T2DM; however, its applicability in clinical settings should be interpreted cautiously pending further validation.

ML models have increasingly been used to study T2DM, and this study presents an approach that uses the interpretability of advanced algorithms such as XGBoost to clarify key predictors and their interactions. Previous studies have shown that random forest models and Bayesian networks can identify important factors such as age, genetic predisposition, and lifestyle [10,11]. Artificial neural networks have also demonstrated strong performance, although this often comes at the expense of interpretability [12,13]. In this context, the present study shows that XGBoost can provide strong classification performance while also offering interpretable insights. By combining predictive performance with clearer feature-level interpretation, this approach may help bridge the gap between technical performance and clinical relevance when analyzing healthcare datasets. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of balancing predictive capability with interpretability and provide insights into feature behavior in medical data.

XGBoost also shows potential for application in real-world research settings, as recent studies have explored the integration of ML models, particularly XGBoost, into clinical research workflows for various purposes. For example, XGBoost has shown promise in early sepsis detection, demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity when deployed within a medical record system [7]. Another advantage of XGBoost is its ability to support personalized and accurate classification-based assessments [8,14].

Furthermore, XGBoost has shown promising performance in identifying diabetic retinopathy among individuals with T2DM [15]. Such models can effectively identify and prioritize influential factors, thereby offering potentially useful insights for clinical decision-making. In addition, interpreting model predictions with techniques such as SHAP improves transparency and supports interpretability [9].

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study relied on a single publicly available dataset without external validation, which may limit generalizability. In addition, because the dataset was obtained from a public source, uncertainties related to data collection procedures and reporting accuracy should be considered. To improve generalizability and practical applicability, future studies should validate the trained models using independent external datasets, such as other public diabetes cohorts or real-world clinical data. Incorporating additional datasets may also help reduce dataset-specific bias, improve population diversity, and strengthen confidence in model performance in real-world settings.

Second, the cross-sectional nature of the data precludes assessment of temporal relationships; therefore, prospective studies are needed to evaluate predictive performance for incident diabetes. Third, class imbalance (diabetes cases: 8.5%) may have influenced model performance; although SMOTE was applied, synthetic samples may introduce bias. Fourth, important variables such as genetic information, medication use, and socioeconomic factors were unavailable, and the real-world clinical utility of the model was not assessed. In addition, hyperparameter tuning for complex models such as XGBoost and neural networks is computationally intensive and carries a risk of overfitting despite regularization strategies, and model complexity may limit interpretability even when explainability methods are used.

To improve reliability, future studies could explore ensemble strategies or cost-sensitive learning to further address class imbalance, incorporate additional datasets to enhance population diversity, and validate models in independent cohorts. Further research may also focus on improving model transparency without compromising predictive performance, optimizing hyperparameter-tuning procedures, and improving data quality through more rigorous data collection and preprocessing strategies.

Conclusion

In this study, five distinct ML algorithms were evaluated to identify the most suitable model for T2DM classification. Among the five models, XGBoost showed the best performance for identifying individuals with T2DM in this dataset. SHAP analysis indicated that elevated HbA1c, elevated blood glucose, age, BMI, and sex were the most influential predictors of T2DM. The proposed framework shows promise for identifying individuals with T2DM, but prospective validation in clinical settings is needed before broader application.
