A rare presentation of pericardial cavernous hemangioma: case report
Authors: Mohammad Abu Baker, Amro Al-Aqra, Abdalkareem Sawalha, Moath Nairat, Hamza A. Abdul-Hafez, Sajed Majadla, Noor Sawalha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPericardial hemangiomas are exceedingly rare benign vascular cardiac tumors that may remain asymptomatic for years but can cause compression, arrhythmia, effusion, or heart failure when large. Early recognition and definitive management are important to avoid morbidity.Case presentationA 56-year-old woman with well-controlled hypertension and diabetes presented with two months of progressive retrosternal chest heaviness and dyspnea. She reported a known intrapericardial mass monitored for 10 years. CT angiography revealed a well-circumscribed, vascularized intrapericardial mass (5.5 × 6.2 × 4.0 cm) compressing the left atrium with a central hypodense area; transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular function (EF 60%). The patient underwent median sternotomy and complete surgical excision; intraoperative dissection identified vascular supply from the left circumflex artery. Histopathology confirmed a cavernous hemangioma (resected specimen 7.5 × 4.5 × 3.0 cm). The postoperative course was uneventful and she was discharged on postoperative day 4.DiscussionAlthough uncommon, pericardial hemangiomas should be considered when imaging demonstrates vascular cardiac masses. Multimodality imaging aids planning; symptomatic lesions are best managed by complete surgical resection to relieve compression and prevent complications.ConclusionThis case illustrates successful surgical management of a symptomatic pericardial cavernous hemangioma and highlight the value of timely diagnosis and resection for optimal outcomes.

Introduction

The term hemangioma refers to benign, non-reactive vascular anomalies resulting from cellular proliferation, composed of disproportionally growing capillaries. These vascular anomalies are categorized as either capillary or cavernous. Cardiac hemangiomas are uncommon benign tumors. They are vascular neoplasms of the heart [1]. Pericardial hemangioma is a rarer type of primary cardiac neoplasms, comprising about 2% to 5% of benign cardiac tumors [2, 3]. Symptoms arise from either compression of heart tissues or obstruction of outflow pathways, rendering this type of tumor challenging when it comes to diagnosis and treatment. Our study aims to present another case of cardiac hemangioma and analyze the numerous facets of this condition by reviewing cases documented in the literature.

Case report

We present a case of a 56-year-old female patient, who was referred to a cardiologist due to a complaint of chest pain that started two months ago. The pain was retrosternal, described as heaviness in nature, associated with shortness of breath and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, with no relieving or aggravating factors. The patient rated it 4/10, she denied any history of palpitations, nausea, vomiting, cough, sputum production, fever, or recent chest trauma. According to the patient, there is a history of an intrapericardial mass attached to the wall of the left heart for the past 10 years, which required regular follow-up. She is a non-smoking patient with a past medical history of hypertension and diabetes mellitus of 5 years duration, both well controlled with medications.

On admission, her blood pressure was 130/90 mmHg, with a pulse rate of 70/min, respiratory rate of 16/min and SpO2 of 97% at room air. On examination, her heart sounds were normal with no murmurs, and the respiratory system examination revealed normal vesicular breathing sounds bilaterally, with no added sounds. Both the central nervous system and abdominal examination were unremarkable. The computed tomography angiography (CTA) showed a well-defined left retro-atrio-ventricular and intrapericardial vascularized mass, measuring 5.5 × 6.2 × 4 cm in maximum (Fig. 1), containing a central hypodense area mostly due to necrosis with minimal pericardial effusion. Echocardiogram showed an ejection fraction of 60%, while the electrocardiogram (ECG) showed regular sinus rhythm, with no ST-segment changes.

Recently her symptoms persisted and worsened, which led her to seek medical advice at our hospital. Based on her new medical findings, our multidisciplinary team, represented by the cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, and oncology departments, it was recommended that the patient undergo surgery for intrapericardial mass resection, as it was compressing the left atrium.

The operation was performed under general anesthesia through a median sternotomy. The procedure was performed under cardiopulmonary bypass. Cardioplegia was not used, as the heart was not arrested. Instead, the cardiac chambers were decompressed to improve surgical exposure and facilitate complete tumor resection. Pericardiotomy showed normocardia, a heart of regular size and systolic function, with a visible solid, oval, clearly circumscribed pericardial tumor mass (Fig. 2) identified over the posterior surface of the heart, extending from the appendages of the left atrium to the mid of the posterior surface, and stretching down to the atrioventricular (AV) groove (Fig. 2). There was no extension of the mass into any cardiac chamber. The mass was carefully dissected from its attachment over the heart with identification of collaterals from the left circumflex artery. Histological examination reported a single encapsulated oval mass (Fig. 2) measuring 7.5 × 4.5 × 3.0 cm, diagnosed as cavernous hemangioma without evidence of malignancy (Fig. 3).

The postoperative period was uneventful, and no complications were seen. Regular physiotherapy and antibiotics were prescribed under the follow-up of the infection control team. She was kept in the cardiogenic care unit for 4 days after the operation for close monitoring and evaluation and was discharged on the fourth postoperative day in good general condition.

Discussion

Cardiac tumors are considered rare [1]. These tumors are predominantly ventricular in origin, but may also arise from the atria, as they develop from the endocardium, myocardium, or epicardium/pericardium regions [2]. In the present case, the pericardial tumor originated from the left atrium, extending to the mid of the posterior surface and down to the AV groove.

The location and size of the hemangioma are the main determinants of the clinical presentation. Hemangiomas are linked to a wide range of symptoms, including dysrhythmia, chest discomfort, blood vessel obstruction, heart failure, effusion, in addition to respiratory symptoms such as cough, dysphagia or dyspnea. Since pericardial hemangiomas are clinically inconsequential, they are typically incidentally discovered when patients present with other problems [4]. Whereas the presentation of constrictive pericarditis with consumption coagulopathy is less frequently reported [5]. This case presented with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) symptoms, characterized by persistent chest pain and breathlessness.

Even though pericardial tumors are rare, their recognition has increased recently due to the growing use of radiographic techniques in clinical practice, allowing for early identification and treatment. As far as we know, 14 cases over the last 50 years were found in the literature [6]. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), CT, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or cardiac catheterization showing tumor blush are used to make the diagnosis. Echocardiography has little use in diagnosing hemangioma, even though it can identify the tumor. In comparison, MRI is a better qualitative diagnostic tool due to its better contrast resolution [7]. In the present case, the pericardial mass was discovered accidentally by CTA imaging investigations.

Histological analysis of the dissected mass revealed a pericardial hemangioma. Based on histological findings, pericardial hemangiomas are divided into three types: cavernous (which are made up of many dilated, thin-walled vascular spaces lined by flattened endothelium), arteriovenous (which are made up of dysplastic, malformed arteries and veins) or capillary (which are made up of capillary-like vessels and lined by plump endothelial cells) [1]. In this case, the patient’s tumor was cavernous and originated in the pericardium. Pericardial hemangioma’s development rates might fluctuate between spontaneous regression, dormancy and rapid expansion [8]. As part of the treatment, the tumor is surgically removed for diagnostic reasons and to avoid the physical manifestations of its growth.

Despite cardiac cavernous hemangiomas being extremely rare and an easily tolerated tumor, they need to be treated quickly and surgically as soon as they are discovered to prevent future problems that might endanger the patient’s prognosis.
