Relational Dualities and Bisimulation
Piotr Kozicki, G. A. Kavvos

Abstract
The Kripke semantics of various logics arises via categorical dualities between a category of relational frames and their maps, and a category of algebras and logical homomorphisms. When the relational frames are considered as computational systems (e.g. the states of a machine), the corresponding algebra is one of logical predicates on these systems (e.g. predicates on these states, i.e. program logics). Our aim is to extend this phenomenon to relations, putting well-behaved relations between systems (e.g. bisimulations) in correspondence with relations between predicates. This is achieved by constructing particular relational extensions of Tarski duality (for infinitary classical propositional logic) and Thomason duality (for infinitary classical modal logic). We sketch how these dualities give rise to a proof system that relates formulae between different systems.

Introduction

It is a well-known secret that relational semantics (e.g. Kripke semantics)
arises from
dualities
, i.e. categorical equivalences of the form
F:\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\mathcal{D}
. The idea is that
\mathcal{C}
is a ‘spatial’ category whose
objects are
frames
, and
\mathcal{D}
is an ‘algebraic’ category whose objects
are
algebras of predicates
, which allow reasoning about the elements of
the frames.

For example, truth table semantics for infinitary classical propositional logic
arises from the
Tarski duality

\mathbf{Set}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\mathbf{CABA}
between the
category of sets and the category of
complete atomic Boolean algebras

(CABAs)
[
22
, §1]
.

The semantics of
finitary
classical
propositional logic arises from the duality

\mathbf{Set}_{\text{fin}}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\mathbf{Bool}_{\text{fin}}

between finite sets and finite Boolean algebras. In
fact, we can arrive at the Tarski duality
\mathbf{Set}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\mathbf{CABA}
by taking
the Pro-completion of this duality
[
27
]
.

The Kripke semantics of infinitary classical modal logic

[
23
,
24
]
arises from the
Thomason duality

\mathbf{Frm}_{\mathrm{open}}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\mathbf{CABAO}
between the category of Kripke frames and the category
of CABAs with operators (i.e. modalities)
[
42
]

[
22
, §2.4]
. The Kripke semantics of infinitary intuitionistic logic

[
25
]
arises from the duality
\textbf{Pos}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\textbf{PrAlgLatt}

between posets and prime algebraic lattices
[
34
,
21
]
.
Famous dualities of this form include Stone duality (for Boolean algebras)

[

]
, Jónsson-Tarski duality

[

,

,

]
(for Boolean algebras with
operators), and Esakia duality
[
9
]
(for Heyting algebras). See
the chapter by Kishida
[

]
for further references.

The overwhelming majority of work in this area has not paid much attention to
the logical rôle of
morphisms
of these categories.

2

One
exception is the work of Abramsky
[
1
]
on domain theory in
logical form.
In many cases the ‘frame’ morphisms
f:W\to W^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{C}
can
be thought of as ‘truth-preserving’: they map worlds to worlds in a way that
preserves and reflects truth, i.e.
w\vDash\varphi
iff
f(w)\vDash\varphi
. The corresponding morphism
{f}^{*}:F(W^{\prime})\to F(W)
of
\mathcal{D}
then
preserves the logical structure, e.g.
{f}^{*}(\varphi\land\psi)={f}^{*}(\varphi)\land{f}^{*}(\psi)
. Thus
{f}^{*}
can be considered as
mapping predicates over
W^{\prime}
to predicates over
W
, in a manner that
preserves logical connectives.

Obtaining this property in the case of intuitionistic and modal logics requires
strong properties on frame morphisms. For example, in the Kripke semantics of
modal logic the induced map
{f}^{*}:\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}\to\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}

preserves the modality
\Box
iff
f:W\to W^{\prime}
is an
open map
, i.e. a
transition-preserving function whose graph is a
(functional) bisimulation

[
22
, §2.4]
.
3
3

3

Open maps are also called

p-morphisms

[
5
, §2.3]
or
bounded morphisms

[
4
, §2.1]
.

However, functionality is incidental to the idea of bisimulation. It is natural
to wonder what happens if we relax the morphisms of
\mathcal{C}
, requiring them to
merely be
relations
instead of functions. Answering this question would
allow us to have categories
\mathcal{C}
consisting of frames and well-behaved
relations, e.g. bisimulations. A duality of this form would then put
bisimulations in correspondence with a different kind of morphism between
algebras, enabling reasoning ‘across’ a bisimulation.

Such results, which may be called
relational dualities
, can already be found
in the literature. However, most of them put relations between frames in
correspondence with
hemimorphisms
, i.e. morphisms that preserve
most—but not all!—logical connectives. For example, a relation
R\subseteq W\times W^{\prime}
uniquely corresponds to a function
W\to\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}
, which uniquely
corresponds to a function
{R}^{*}:\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}\to\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}
that
preserves all joins (but not necessarily all meets)
[
20
]

[
22
, §2.3]
.

This is unsatisfactory from a computational perspective. Traditional dualities
allow us to compute the ‘action’ of a morphism of frames
f:W\to W^{\prime}
on the

syntax
of a formula. Given a formula
\varphi
(over
W^{\prime}
) we can define
a formula
{f}^{*}(\varphi)
(over
W
) by recursion. For example, in Tarski
duality this would be computed by defining
{f}^{*}(\varphi\land\psi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}{f}^{*}(\varphi)\land{f}^{*}(\psi)
and so on. Thus,
{f}^{*}
can be pushed
through all connectives until it reaches a propositional variable
p
, where it
returns a predicate
f^{-1}(p)
which is true at
w
exactly when
p
is true at

f(w)
. Hence, a function
f:W\to W^{\prime}
allows us to map predicates on
W^{\prime}
to
predicates on
W
in a logic-preserving manner. As hemimorphisms do
not

preserve all logical connectives, it is impossible to adapt this definition to a
relation
R\subseteq W\times W^{\prime}
.

To tackle this difficulty this paper develops a different type of duality, viz.
one that puts relations
R\subseteq W\times W^{\prime}
between frames in
correspondence with relations
\mathcal{F}(R)\subseteq F(W)\times F(W^{\prime})

between predicates. This allows us to view
\mathcal{F}(R)
as a

relational judgment
between formulae pertaining to different systems,
with
R
playing the rôle of a ‘background theory’ which specifies how
propositions over the two systems are related. The ultimate goal is to develop a
formal system for synthetically reasoning about the
relation between
formulae over different frames
. It is not difficult to imagine that this might
have applications in program logics.

If the relation
R\subseteq W\times W^{\prime}
is also a
bisimulation
between
frames, it can be thought of as a notion of equivalence between the states of
two systems, with
w\mathbin{R}w^{\prime}
meaning that
w
and
w^{\prime}
have the same
behaviours. Viewed as a judgment, the induced relation
\mathcal{F}(R)\subseteq F(W)\times F(W^{\prime})
then satisfies ‘inference rules,’ which we wish to identify.

As we are interested in bisimulations, we take a hint from the relevant
literature on coalgebra
[
41
,
30
,
18
]
and base all the
results in this paper on the
lower lifting
, which maps a relation
R\subseteq W\times W^{\prime}
to the
lower relation

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}\times\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}
, defined by

S\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}T\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny def}}}}{{\equiv}}\ \forall s\in S.\ \exists t\in T.\ s\mathbin{R}t.

Famously, this construction is
not
a functor
\mathbf{Rel}\longrightarrow\mathbf{Rel}
, as it does
not preserve identities. However, we can define an appropriate category

\mathbf{CABARel}
with CABAs as objects, so that it becomes a functor
\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}}:\mathbf{Rel}\longrightarrow\mathbf{CABARel}
. Surprisingly, this functor is an equivalence. Moreover,
\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}}

‘extends’ Tarski duality, in the sense that the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Set}^{\mathsf{op}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Rel}^{\mathsf{op}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{CABA}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{CABARel}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}}\circ(-)^{\dagger}}

commutes, where
{(-)}^{\dagger}:\mathbf{Rel}^{\mathsf{op}}\cong\mathbf{Rel}
is the obvious formal
duality that reverses a relation. This follows a known pattern wherein
relational dualities are not really ‘dualities’ per se, but become so only after
composition with a formal duality
[
22
, §2.3]

[
26
, Remark
4.1]
. In this light the
(-)^{\mathsf{op}}
is merely an artifact of
functionality.

After recalling some definitions to fix notation
(
\cref
section:preliminaries), we briefly recap the elements of the Tarski
duality
\mathbf{Set}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\mathbf{CABA}
(
\cref
section:tarski). Then, we show that the
lower lifting given above can be used to extend it to a
relational Tarski
duality
(
\cref
section:relational-tarski). We achieve this by precisely
characterising the relations between CABAs that are obtained as lower liftings,
and hence establish an equivalence. Furthermore, we show that this is an
extension of the usual Tarski duality, and sketch the formal system that this
duality induces.

Following this, we show how the same lifting gives rise to a relational version
of the Thomason duality
\mathbf{Frm}_{\mathrm{open}}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\mathbf{CABAO}

(
\cref
section:relational-thomason). This is somewhat more challenging: while
the Tarski duality acts on arbitrary functions, the Thomason duality associates
open maps (i.e. functional bisimulations) with modal complete Boolean
homomorphisms. We show that this has an analogue on the relational level,
putting (bi)simulations in unique correspondence with what we call

(bi)simulatory relations
between CABAs. We show that this

relational Thomason duality
extends the duality originally given by
Thomason, and again sketch the formal system that this duality induces.
We close with a discussion of related work (
\cref
section:related-work).

Preliminaries

A
relation

R:A\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow B
from a set
A
to a set
B
is a subset
R\subseteq A\times B
. We write
a\mathbin{R}b
to mean
(a,b)\in R
. If
R:A\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow B
and
S:B\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow C
their composition
R\mathbin{;}S:A\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow C
is
defined in the usual manner, i.e.
a\mathbin{\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R\mathbin{;}S$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R\mathbin{;}S$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R\mathbin{;}S$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R\mathbin{;}S$}\mathclose{}{)}}}c
just if there exists
some

with
a\mathbin{R}b\mathbin{S}c
. Note that we write the composition
of relations using diagrammatic order. Given a relation
R:A\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow B
we
define its
opposite

{R}^{\dagger}:B\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow A
by

iff
a\mathbin{R}b
. Sets and relations form a category
\mathbf{Rel}
under composition, with
the identity
\textsf{Id}_{A}:A\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow A
given by
a_{1}\mathbin{\textsf{Id}_{A}}a_{2}
iff
a_{1}=a_{2}
. Moreover, the opposite construction extends to a functor
{(-)}^{\dagger}:\mathbf{Rel}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathbf{Rel}
which constitutes a formal duality
\mathbf{Rel}^{\mathsf{op}}\cong\mathbf{Rel}

given by taking the opposite. This shows that
\mathbf{Rel}
is a
dagger
category
, i.e. equipped with an involutive contravariant functor
[
14
, §2.3.1]
.

A partial order
(P,\sqsubseteq_{P})
is a set
P
equipped with a relation

\mathord{\sqsubseteq_{P}}:P\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow P
which is reflexive, transitive, and
anti-symmetric. We will commonly refer to partial orders by their carriers (
P
,

Q
, …) and often elide the subscript of
\sqsubseteq
. A function
f:P\to Q
is
monotonic
just if
x\sqsubseteq_{P}y
implies

. A

is a partial order that has all finite
joins and meets. We will use
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\ldots
to denote
lattices. A
complete lattice

\mathcal{L}
is a partial order that has
all joins, which also implies that it has all meets. A pair of monotonic
functions
f:P\to Q
and
g:Q\to P
is an
adjunction
(which in this
setting is also called a

) just if
f(x)\sqsubseteq_{Q}y
iff
x\sqsubseteq_{P}g(y)
. This is often denoted by
f\dashv g
. It is a
consequence that
f
preserves any joins that exist in
P
and
g
preserves any
meets that exist in
Q
. The
adjoint functor theorem

[
7
, §7.34]

[
19
, §I.4.2]
says that if
f:P\to Q
is
monotonic,
P
is a complete lattice, and
f
preserves all joins, then
f
has
a right adjoint
g:Q\to P
. Dually, if
g:Q\to P
is monotonic,
Q
is
a complete lattice, and
g
preserves all meets, then
g
has a left adjoint

f:P\to Q
. We refer the reader to the book by Davey and Priestley

[
7
]
for background on orders, and to the books by Mac Lane,
Awodey, and Riehl
[
31
,
2
,
36
]
for category
theory.

A Primer on Tarski Duality

We recap the Tarski duality
\mathbf{Set}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\mathbf{CABA}
, and show how it induces an
infinitary classical propositional logic. Recall that a
Boolean algebra

is a distributive lattice
\mathcal{B}
in which every element
x\in\mathcal{B}
has a
complement

\lnot x\in\mathcal{B}
for which
x\lor\lnot x=\top
and
x\land\lnot x=\bot

[
19
]
.

For any set
X
, its powerset
\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}
is a Boolean algebra
[
7
, §5.2]
. In fact, it is a
complete
Boolean algebra, i.e. a complete
lattice with joins and meets of
(S_{i})_{i\in I}
given by
\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}
and

\bigcap_{i\in I}S_{i}
respectively. Moreover,
\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}
is
atomic
, in the
following sense.

Definition 1

(Atom)
.

Let
(P,\sqsubseteq)
be a partial order with a bottom element
\bot
. An
element
a\in P
is an
atom
of
P
if
a\neq\bot
and
x\sqsubseteq a
implies that either
x=\bot
or
x=a
. We write
\textsf{At}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle P$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle P$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle P$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle P$}\mathclose{}{)}}
for the set of
atoms of
P
.

Definition 2

(Atomic complete lattice)
.

A complete lattice
\mathcal{L}
is
atomic
just if every element is
equal to the join of atoms below it, i.e. for every
x\in\mathcal{L}
we
have
x=\bigsqcup\left\{a\in\textsf{At}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{L}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle\mathcal{L}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}$}\mathclose{}{)}}\mid a\sqsubseteq x\right\}
.

A
complete atomic Boolean algebra (CABA)
is then a complete Boolean
algebra
\mathcal{B}
which is moreover atomic. For any set
X
, the powerset
\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}

is a CABA: its atoms are exactly the singleton sets
\{x\}
for
x\in X
:
every
S\subseteq X
can be reconstructed as
S=\bigcup_{x\in S}\{x\}
.

A
morphism of CABAs

{f}^{*}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is
a monotonic function that preserves all meets and joins. This
definition has some significant implications. First, as
\mathcal{B}

and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
are Boolean algebras, every such

{f}^{*}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is a
complete Boolean
homomorphism
, i.e. preserves
all
Boolean connectives,
including negation. Second, by the adjoint functor theorem the map

{f}^{*}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
has both a left and a right
adjoint:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{!}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{*}}

\dashv

\dashv

CABAs and their morphisms form a category
\mathbf{CABA}
.

Given any function
f:X\to Y
we can define a complete Boolean homomorphism

{f}^{*}:\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}\to\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}

,

,
22
]
. By the adjoint

has both a left and a right adjoint, respectively

\displaystyle f_{{!}}(A)

\displaystyle=\left\{y\in Y\mid\exists x\in X.\ f(x)=y\land x\in A\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{f(x)\mid x\in A\right\}

\displaystyle f_{{*}}(A)

.

Lemma 3
.

We can thus define a functor

, which maps a CABA

, and use

\Cref

{f}^{*}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
to the restriction

f_{{!}}|_{\textsf{At}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle\mathcal{B}^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mathcal{B}^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{B}^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}}:\textsf{At}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle\mathcal{B}^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mathcal{B}^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{B}^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}\to\textsf{At}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle\mathcal{B}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle\mathcal{B}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mathcal{B}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{B}$}\mathclose{}{)}}
of its
left adjoint to atoms.

This is a pseudo-inverse to
\mathcal{P}
, and we obtain a categorical
equivalence.

Theorem 4

(Tarski duality)
.

\mathbf{Set}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\mathbf{CABA}.

The gist is that every CABA
\mathcal{B}
is isomorphic to the powerset

A Relational Tarski Duality

If instead of a function
f:W\to W^{\prime}
we were to have an arbitrary

relation

R:W\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow W^{\prime}
, what would the corresponding morphism between

\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle W$}\mathclose{}{)}}
and
\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle W^{\prime}$}\mathclose{}{)}}
be?

It is possible to obtain such a duality by enlarging
\mathbf{CABA}
to
\mathbf{CABA}_{\smash{\bigvee}}
,
whose morphisms only preserve joins, and then show that
\mathbf{Rel}\simeq\mathbf{CABA}_{\smash{\bigvee}}

[
20
,
39
]

[
22
, §2.3]
. Indeed, if

preserves all joins, then—by writing any set
as a union of singletons—we see that

is completely determined by a
function
X\to\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}
, i.e. a relation

. Hofmann and Nora

[
16
, §4.5]
show that this duality is a special case of a
general one. However, these more general morphisms do
not
preserve the
logical structure of predicates. For example it may be that
h(\varphi\land\psi)\neq h(\varphi)\land h(\psi)
. We have thereby lost the benefit of being
able to recursively compute

on the syntax of formulae.

Instead, we would like a duality whose logical side consists of predicates and

relations
. To construct one we consider a simple way of
lifting

relations to powersets
[
28
]
.

Definition 5

(Lower Relation)
.

Given a relation
R:X\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow Y
, the associated
lower relation

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}:\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}
is defined by

S\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}T\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny def}}}}{{\equiv}}\ \forall s\in S.\ \exists t\in T.\ s\mathbin{R}t.

This lifting has a long history. It is one half of the
Egli-Milner
lifting
, which first appeared in the powerdomain literature

[
35
,
13
]
. It then resurfaced in the coalgebra literature

[
41
]
, where post-fixed-points amount to

between

[

, §3]
. Intuitively,
S\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}T

means every state of
S
can be ‘
R
-simulated’ by a state of
T
.

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}}
maps a relation between sets to a relation between CABAs. However, it
is
not
a functor
\mathbf{Rel}\longrightarrow\mathbf{Rel}
, as it maps the identity relation
\textsf{id}:X\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow X
to the containment relation
\mathbin{\subseteq}:\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}
. We will define a category
\mathbf{CABARel}
so that

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}}
becomes a functor
\mathbf{Rel}\longrightarrow\mathbf{CABARel}
. The relations between CABAs
will be characterised as follows.

Definition 6

(Directionally atomic relation)
.

Let
P,Q
be partial orders,
\mathcal{L}
be a complete lattice, and

\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be CABAs.

1.

A relation
R:P\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow Q
is a
bimodule
just when
p^{\prime}\sqsubseteq p\mathbin{R}q\sqsubseteq q^{\prime}
implies
p^{\prime}\mathbin{R}q^{\prime}
.

2.

A relation
R:\mathcal{L}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow Q
is
left-disjunctive

just if
a_{i}\mathbin{R}b
for all
i\in I
implies
\left(\bigsqcup_{i\in I}a_{i}\right)\mathbin{R}b
.

3.

A relation
R:\mathcal{B}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is

atomic-founded
when for any atom
a\in\mathcal{B}
, if
a\mathbin{R}b
then there exists an atom
b^{\prime}\sqsubseteq b
with
a\mathbin{R}b^{\prime}
.

4.

A relation
R:\mathcal{B}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is

directionally atomic
if it is left-disjunctive,
atomic-founded, and a bimodule.

It is simple to show that directionally atomic relations compose, and that

\sqsubseteq
is the identity.

Proposition 7
.

If
R:\mathcal{B}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
and

S:\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}
are directionally atomic, then so is
their composition
R\mathbin{;}S:\mathcal{B}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}
.

Proposition 8
.

\mathbin{\sqsubseteq}:\mathcal{B}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
is directionally
atomic, and is the identity for composition of directionally atomic relations.

We can therefore define a category
\mathbf{CABARel}
with CABAs as objects, and
directionally atomic relations as morphisms. We can then construct a functor:

Lemma 9
.

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}:\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}

.

Lemma 10
.

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}}
is a functor
\mathbf{Rel}\longrightarrow\mathbf{CABARel}
.

Proof.

By
\Cref

.

First, we show that

Second, we show that

Lemma 11
.

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}}:\mathbf{Rel}\longrightarrow\mathbf{CABARel}

Proof.

Suppose

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}=\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle S$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle S$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle S$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle S$}\mathclose{}{)}}}
. Then

Lemma 12
.

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}}:\mathbf{Rel}\longrightarrow\mathbf{CABARel}
is full. In other words, any

is

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle S$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle S$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle S$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle S$}\mathclose{}{)}}}
for some relation
S:X\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow Y
.

Proof.

We will prove that
R=\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle S_{R}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle S_{R}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle S_{R}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle S_{R}$}\mathclose{}{)}}}
where

is given by

.

Suppose that

. Then for any

, as

\left\{a\right\}\mathbin{R}\left\{b\right\}

Suppose now that

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}}:\mathbf{Rel}\longrightarrow\mathbf{CABARel}

Atomic-foundedness of

\blacklozenge A\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}}B
as
\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}}
is a bimodule.

Finally, to show (iii) implies (i), suppose the premise of (iii) and
that
x\mathbin{Q}y
for some
x\in X
and
y\in Y
, equally

\left\{x\right\}\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}}\left\{y\right\}
. Note that

\left\{y\right\}\subseteq\Box\blacklozenge\left\{y\right\}
and
\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}}

is a bimodule, therefore

\left\{x\right\}\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}}\Box\blacklozenge\left\{y\right\}
, then by (iii)

\blacklozenge\left\{x\right\}\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle Q$}\mathclose{}{)}}}\blacklozenge\left\{y\right\}
. So for
any
x^{\prime}\in X
for which
x\to_{R}x^{\prime}
, there exists
some
y^{\prime}\in Y
for which
y\to_{R}y^{\prime}
and

x^{\prime}\mathbin{Q}y^{\prime}
, i.e.
Q
is a simulation.
∎

Definition 24
.

A
simulatory relation

Q:(\mathcal{B},\Box_{\mathcal{B}})\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow(\mathcal{B}^{\prime},\Box_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}})
between CABAOs is a directionally atomic

for which the condition of

\Cref
lemma:simulatory holds, i.e.

A\mathbin{Q}\Box_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}B\ \Longrightarrow\ \blacklozenge_{\mathcal{B}}A\mathbin{Q}B.

Proposition 25
.

If

R:(\mathcal{B},\Box_{\mathcal{B}})\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow(\mathcal{B}^{\prime},\Box_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}})
and

S:(\mathcal{B}^{\prime},\Box_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}})\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow(\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime},\Box_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}})
are simulatory relations, then their relational
composition is a simulatory relation

R\mathbin{;}S:(\mathcal{B},\Box_{\mathcal{B}})\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow(\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime},\Box_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}})
.

Define
\mathbf{CABAOSim}
to have CABAs with operators

as objects and simulatory relations

as morphisms. We will often write these
morphisms simply as

and omit
subscripts. Using the preceding proposition, this is a category, with

\sqsubseteq
as an identity morphism.

Lemma 26
.

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}}
is a functor
\mathbf{FrmSim}\longrightarrow\mathbf{CABAOSim}
.

.

\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}}:\mathbf{FrmSim}\longrightarrow\mathbf{CABAOSim}

In summary, we obtain an equivalence

Theorem 30
.

\mathbf{FrmSim}\simeq\mathbf{CABAOSim}
.

As in our previous relational duality, the directionality disappears in the
relational case. We could turn this result into a duality by composing it with a formal
duality between
\mathbf{FrmSim}
and the category of
co
simulations. However,
this is somewhat awkward.

Our final objective is to restrict this equivalence by analogy to the
restriction of
\mathbf{Frm}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\mathbf{CABAO}^{-}
to
\mathbf{Frm}_{\mathrm{open}}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\mathbf{CABAO}
,
where the maps in
\mathbf{Frm}_{\mathrm{open}}^{\mathsf{op}}
are open, and correspondingly the maps in

\mathbf{CABAO}
preserve all logical connectives. In our relational duality the
morphisms are no longer functional, but merely simulations. Thus, openness will
turn them into
bisimulations
.

Unfortunately, expressing bisimulations using our current vocabulary is not
immediately possible. For that we will need the following ‘dual’ relational
lifting.

Definition 31

(Upper Relation)
.

Given a relation
R:X\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow Y
, the associated upper relation
\mathbin{\mathfrak{U}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}:\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle X$}\mathclose{}{)}}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow\mathcal{P}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle Y$}\mathclose{}{)}}
is defined by

S\mathbin{\mathfrak{U}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny def}}}}{{\equiv}}\forall t\in T.\ \exists s\in S.\ s\mathbin{R}t.

It is easy to see that
\mathbin{\mathfrak{U}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}=\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R^{\dagger}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R^{\dagger}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R^{\dagger}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R^{\dagger}$}\mathclose{}{)}}}^{\dagger}
for any
R:X\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow Y
.
This makes the theory dual, in the sense that
\mathbin{\mathfrak{U}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}
is

opdirectionally atomic
(i.e. right-disjunctive, atomic op-founded, and an
opbimodule). To avoid this proliferation of concepts we will simply work with
the opposite relation
\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle{R}^{\dagger}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle{R}^{\dagger}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{R}^{\dagger}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}^{\dagger}$}\mathclose{}{)}}}
. We can construct this relation directly
on relations between CABAs.

Definition 32
.

For a relation
R:\mathcal{B}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
between CABAs we define
its
variant relation

\mathbin{{R}^{\bullet}}:\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow\mathcal{B}

to be

b^{\prime}\mathbin{\mathbin{{R}^{\bullet}}}b\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\mbox{\tiny def}}}}{{\equiv}}\forall\text{atom~}a^{\prime}\sqsubseteq b^{\prime}.\ \exists\text{atom~}a\sqsubseteq b.\ a\mathbin{R}a^{\prime}.

This acts in the expected way between lifted relations.

Lemma 33
.

\mathbin{{\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}}^{\bullet}}=\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle{R}^{\dagger}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle{R}^{\dagger}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{R}^{\dagger}$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}^{\dagger}$}\mathclose{}{)}}}
for any

, and

\mathbin{\mathfrak{U}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}={\mathbin{{\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}}^{\bullet}}}^{\dagger}

Let
R:X\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow Y
be a relation between sets. Then for any

and

5.2
A Worked Example: Bisimulation Between Buffer Systems

We will now show how to use these rules to reason over a particular
bisimulation. The two frames both model a
buffer
holding a single natural
number. However, the two buffers have
internal branching
: the first has
two different ‘ways’ of holding a number (the left and right slots), while the
second one has three (slots A, B, and C). This might happen if the buffer is
provided by a distributed cluster of machines whose internal structure is not
observable.

Define
(X,\to_{X})
by letting

X=\left\{\textsf{empty}_{X}\right\}\cup\left\{\textsf{L}(n),\textsf{R}(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

with

\textsf{empty}_{X}\to_{X}\textsf{L}(n)\qquad\textsf{empty}_{X}\to_{X}\textsf{R}(n)\qquad\textsf{L}(n)\to_{X}\textsf{empty}_{X}\qquad\textsf{R}(n)\to_{X}\textsf{empty}_{X}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Similarly, define
(Y,\to_{Y})
by

Y=\left\{\textsf{empty}_{Y}\right\}\cup\left\{\textsf{A}(n),\textsf{B}(n),\textsf{C}(n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

with

\textsf{empty}_{Y}\to_{Y}\textsf{A}(n)\qquad\textsf{empty}_{Y}\to_{Y}\textsf{B}(n)\qquad\textsf{empty}_{Y}\to_{Y}\textsf{C}(n)

\textsf{A}(n)\to_{Y}\textsf{empty}_{Y}\qquad\textsf{B}(n)\to_{Y}\textsf{empty}_{Y}\qquad\textsf{C}(n)\to_{Y}\textsf{empty}_{Y}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. There is a bisimulation
Q:X\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow Y
between
these frames given by

Q=\left\{(\textsf{empty}_{X},\textsf{empty}_{Y})\right\}\cup\left\{(w,v)\mid n\in\mathbb{N},\ w\in F_{X}(n),\ v\in F_{Y}(n)\right\}

where
F_{X}(n)=\left\{\textsf{L}(n),\textsf{R}(n)\right\}
and
F_{Y}(n)=\left\{\textsf{A}(n),\textsf{B}(n),\textsf{C}(n)\right\}
. In other words, full states
holding the same value are related, and so are empty states. This is evidently a
non-functional bisimulation.

We define the following predicates, parametrised by
n\in\mathbb{N}
. On

X
let
\mathit{empty}_{X}=\left\{\textsf{empty}_{X}\right\}
,
L_{n}=\left\{\textsf{L}(n)\right\}
, and
R_{n}=\left\{\textsf{R}(n)\right\}
. On
Y
let

\mathit{empty}_{Y}=\left\{\textsf{empty}_{Y}\right\}
.

We will use the following facts as the ‘background theory’:

\displaystyle\blacklozenge\,\mathit{empty}_{X}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\bigvee_{n}F_{X}(n)

\displaystyle\Box\,\mathit{empty}_{X}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\bigvee_{n}F_{X}(n)

\displaystyle L_{n}\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle Q}}{\searrow}F_{Y}(n)

\displaystyle\blacklozenge\,\mathit{empty}_{Y}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\bigvee_{n}F_{Y}(n)

\displaystyle\Box\,\mathit{empty}_{Y}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\bigvee_{n}F_{Y}(n)

\displaystyle R_{n}\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle Q}}{\searrow}F_{Y}(n)

The first two columns arise by direct observation. The last column holds because
every left/right state is simulated by a full state holding the same value.

We derive the fact that if the previous state in the first buffer was a left or right one holding
n
, then the current state is simulated by an empty state in the second buffer:

(\blacklozenge\,L_{n}\lor\blacklozenge\,R_{n})\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle Q}}{\searrow}\mathit{empty}_{Y}

The derivation proceeds in two symmetric branches, combined by disjunction:

\frac{\frac{\frac{L_{n}\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle Q}}{\searrow}F_{Y}(n)\hskip 7.3612ptF_{Y}(n)\vdash\Box\,\mathit{empty}_{Y}}{L_{n}\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle Q}}{\searrow}\Box\,\mathit{empty}_{Y}}}{\blacklozenge L_{n}\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle Q}}{\searrow}\mathit{empty}_{Y}}\quad\frac{\frac{R_{n}\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle Q}}{\searrow}F_{Y}(n)\hskip 7.3612ptF_{Y}(n)\vdash\Box\,\mathit{empty}_{Y}}{R_{n}\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle Q}}{\searrow}\Box\,\mathit{empty}_{Y}}}{\blacklozenge R_{n}\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle Q}}{\searrow}\mathit{empty}_{Y}}}{(\blacklozenge L_{n}\lor\blacklozenge R_{n})\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle Q}}{\searrow}\mathit{empty}_{Y}}

6
Related Work

A few relational dualities have been previously described in the literature.
Most are of the form
\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{op}}\simeq\mathcal{D}
where
\mathcal{C}
is a category whose
morphisms are relations, whereas
\mathcal{D}
is a category of algebras with some form
of
hemimorphism
, i.e. a morphism preserving most—but not all!—of
the logical structure. The earliest duality of this form is
\mathbf{Rel}\simeq\mathbf{CABA}_{\smash{\bigvee}}
.
This duality has been rediscovered multiple times, but is likely due to
Jónsson
[
20
]
. Kishida
[
22
, §2.3]
argues that
this extends to a ‘2-duality,’ as both of these are (strict) 2-categories.
Halmos
[
11
]
extended it to the continuous case, i.e. a duality
between Stone spaces with continuous relations on the one hand, and Boolean
algebras with hemimorphisms on the other. Cignoli et al.
[
6
]
do
something similar for Priestley spaces and continuous monotone relations. Hofmann
and Nora
[
16
]
have proposed a general framework for such
dualities, obtaining the relational side as the Kleisli category of a suitable
monad. In later work they extended such dualities to metric structures and
quantale-enriched categories
[
17
]
.

Kurz, Moshier, and Jung
[
26
]
present dualities that are
much closer to the flavour we employ in this paper. They achieve this
by working in an order-enriched setting, where relations can be
presented as both spans and cospans in a 2-categorical manner. They
use this to extend (well-behaved) dualities to dualities between
categories of relations, and even adjunctions of categories to
adjunctions between framed bicategories
[
40
]
. For
example, if their recipe is applied to the category
Pos
of posets
and monotone functions it lifts a bimodule
R:X\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow Y
to the
relation
\mathbb{2}^{R}:[X,\mathbb{2}]\relbar\joinrel\mapstochar\joinrel\rightarrow[Y,\mathbb{2}]

between upper sets that is defined by letting
A\mathbin{\mathbb{2}^{R}}B

just if
x\in A
and
x\mathbin{R}y
implies
y\in B
. The type of
lifting they obtain is very different: as pointed out by one of our
reviewers,
A\mathbin{\mathbb{2}^{R}}B
just if
R[A]\subseteq B
,
whereas
A\mathbin{\mathfrak{L}\mathchoice{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\displaystyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\textstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}{\mathopen{}{(}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}\mathclose{}{)}}}B
just if
A\subseteq R^{-1}[B]
.

Birkmann, Urbat, and Milius
[
3
]
present extensions of
categorical dualities through monoidal adjunctions and apply them to algebraic
language theories. As a corollary they obtain some relational dualities, e.g. between well-behaved relations of profinite ordered monoids and natural
morphisms of residuation algebras.

Malacaria
[
32
]
presents the Thomason duality in a new light,
and shows how it can be used to give bisimulation an algebraic meaning. In
particular, the main theorem shows that two Kripke frames are bisimilar just if
their associated (dual) algebras have an isomorphic subalgebra.
