Protocol for the Southeast Asian Healthy Habits, Healthy Kids (SEAHK) study, a culturally tailored family-based pilot randomized controlled trial to improve dietary quality
Authors: Akilah Dulin, Alison Tovar, Shira Dunsiger, Kate B. Carey, Kim M. Gans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSoutheast Asian (SEA) children are burdened by high rates of diet-related chronic conditions and diseases. These burdens partly result from socioeconomic disadvantages and refugee migration trajectories. Yet, to our knowledge, no dietary interventions exist for this population. This protocol paper describes the rationale, study design, intervention and study evaluation for the SEA Healthy Habits, Healthy Kids Study.MethodsThe study enrollment goal was 75 parent–child dyads recruited in Providence, Rhode Island, in the United States. A SEA parent/guardian and one of their 6-to-11-year-old children were randomized to (1) a monthly financial incentive only group; (2) an enhanced financial incentive group that receives additional interventions including family-based group nutrition education, three motivational interviewing (MI) phone calls, and weekly text messages on healthy eating norms and other nutrition topics; or (3) a structurally equivalent attention-matched control group. The primary study goals were to assess feasibility and acceptability, conduct process evaluation to determine implementation fidelity and intervention dose, and determine preliminary efficacy. Feasibility was assessed based on attendance (primary) and secondary measures, recruitment, enrollment, intervention dose, and retention at 6 months. Acceptability was measured using satisfaction questionnaires on study favorability. Preliminary efficacy (i.e., comparing changes in children’s diet quality (Healthy Eating Index and dermal carotenoids)) will be determined via longitudinal mixed effects models. Regression models will investigate the relationship between dose, implementation measures and study outcomes.ResultsThis intervention received Institutional Review Board approval on February 16, 2023. We conducted intervention development from July 2022 to August 2022 with 62 SEA parents and children. For the intervention, we began participant recruitment in April 2023, which concluded January 2025.DiscussionThis study will demonstrate whether a culturally tailored healthy eating intervention for SEA families is feasible and acceptable and whether it leads to changes on key efficacy indicators. The intervention is informed by theories and frameworks relevant for the population and utilizes a family-centered approach. If this innovative pilot intervention achieves the study objectives, then we will refine it and pursue a larger randomized control trial.Trial registrationThis study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under registration number NCT05817838. The trial was registered on April 17, 2023. The trial registry record is available at https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05817838.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40814-026-01863-1.

Background

Healthy dietary quality in childhood is a health protective factor across the lifespan [1]. Yet, in the United States (US), national estimates suggest that children’s dietary quality remains suboptimal, which puts them at risk of diet-related chronic diseases [2]. A diet aligning with the US Dietary Guidelines as measured by the Healthy Eating Index (HEI), reduces mortality, type 2 diabetes, cancer and cardiovascular disease [3, 4]. However, HEI scores for children ages 6 to 11 years are low (52.6 out of 100 for children ages 5 to 8 years and 50.1 out of 100 for children ages 9 to 13 years) [3]. Poor diet quality is of particular concern for Southeast Asian (SEA) children (e.g., Hmong Cambodian and Vietnamese) in the US, especially those whose families came as refugees [5–7]. SEA children experience more adverse health outcomes relative to other groups, such as obesity, elevated blood pressure and insulin resistance [8, 9]. These diet and health-related disparities suggest that interventions for SEA children are warranted.

Although diet-related disparities are partly shaped by behavioral factors and cultural dietary norms, exposure to adverse social determinants of health also contributes to these disparities. For instance, the intergenerational transmission of refugee-related traumas and cultural norms can contribute to feelings of guilt among adults when regulating children’s dietary behaviors [10–13]. Also, some SEA cultural dietary norms associate unhealthy Western dietary patterns with better health and greater height [10–13]. Another contributing factor is that some SEA families live in poverty (e.g., 11% among Laotian people and 17% among Hmong people), which is associated with high levels of food insecurity, low nutrition-related knowledge and increased access to inexpensive, energy-dense nutrient-poor foods [14–16]. The adverse impact of these behavioral and social determinants may persist due to the lack of culturally relevant dietary interventions for SEA children and families.

While several dietary interventions in the US focus on Asian children, the majority of these have been designed for Chinese children, and to our knowledge none have been developed for SEA families [17–20]. Tailored, multilevel, multicomponent interventions for SEA families are warranted given their routes of immigration to the US, cultural norms and values, dietary preferences, and exposure to adverse social determinants of health [21]. Specifically, moderate-intensity (i.e., 26 h over 6 months) dietary interventions that include group- and family-based nutrition education, community health workers (CHWs) and motivational interviewing (MI), have demonstrated efficacy and may be relevant dietary intervention approaches for SEA families [21–25]. However, research is needed to determine the feasibility, acceptability, and efficacy of these approaches for this population. Additional intervention approaches, such as incorporating text messaging, dietary norms, and financial incentives also demonstrate promise, but have been underutilized in family-based interventions or demonstrated mixed results [26, 27]. Dietary norms messaging interventions have improved adults’, parents’ and children’s healthy dietary intentions and behaviors, especially among those with poor diet quality [28–30]. However, additional dietary norms research and intervention strategies remain underexplored. For instance, the impact of dietary norms on behaviors is moderated by the proximity and strength of affiliation with the norms reference group, yet most dietary norms interventions do not account for these factors [31, 32]. Financial incentives also demonstrate promise for improving diets and health, but research findings highlight persistent gaps in how to implement financial-incentive interventions that lead to sustained dietary changes [33–36]. Furthermore, interventions using text messaging, dietary norms, and financial interventions to improve dietary behavior have not been studied with SEA populations.

Objectives and hypotheses

This study addresses critical gaps in the literature by describing the study design, intervention, and evaluation methods for a pilot dietary intervention tailored for SEA families with children ages 6 to 11. The main goals of this small RCT were to test feasibility and acceptability of the intervention which aimed to improve children and families’ diet quality through family-based nutrition education, MI, descriptive dietary norms text messaging, and healthy eating financial incentives. Another main goal was to conduct process evaluation to determine implementation fidelity and intervention dose. The secondary goal was to explore preliminary efficacy of the intervention on children’s diet quality (primary), anthropometrics and HbA1c (exploratory) and parent outcomes (secondary). We hypothesized that: (1) the study methods will be feasible and acceptable for SEA families and (2) participants in the multicomponent, multilevel intervention will demonstrate a trend towards greater improvements in primary, exploratory and secondary outcomes compared to participants in the structurally equivalent attention-matched control or financial-incentive-only arms.

Methods

Overall study design and setting

The Southeast Asian Healthy Habits, Healthy Kids (SEAHK) study was a culturally tailored pilot randomized control trial (RCT) with SEA families with children ages 6 to 11 years. This pilot RCT used a parallel group design, random block allocation stratified by ethnic subgroup (i.e., Hmong, Cambodian, Laotian and Vietnamese) and a superiority framework conducted in three cohorts. Families were randomized to one of three study arms, financial incentive only, a financial incentive and group-based nutrition education, MI and dietary norms text messaging (hereafter referred to enhanced financial incentive intervention) and a structurally equivalent attention matched control group that was matched to the enhanced financial incentive intervention. We selected the financial incentive only arm because it will answer gaps in financial incentives research, and the enhanced financial incentive intervention arm to test if this strategy is more effective than financial incentives alone, as multicomponent interventions may be more effective [21, 22]. Last, we selected the structurally equivalent attention matched control group because the efficacy of the SEAHK enhanced financial incentive intervention had not been tested before, and our community partner preferred this over a wait-list or no intervention control arm. All interventions lasted 6 months. Research staff conducted all study enrollment and 6-month data collection activities at one SEA social service, community-based organization in Providence, Rhode Island in the US. SEAHK study community health workers conducted all intervention activities at the above-mentioned location. Interpreters were available to assist with SEAHK study activities as needed.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol received approval from the Brown University Institutional Review Board and is registered at Clinical Trials.gov under registration number, NCT05817838. Since the community partner organization was involved in human subjects activities, we established an Organizational Alliance Agreement whereby Brown University’s IRB provided oversight and review of the community organization’s research activities. This study received IRB approval under study protocol 2022003499 on February 16, 2023 and the Organizational Alliance Agreement for this protocol was approved on March 3, 2023. All research staff (both academic and community) completed the basic CITI human subjects and good clinical practice certification and were trained on all study protocols, including confidentiality, and received periodic refresher trainings. Community health workers and Brown University research staff performed all informed consent and assent procedures. Community health workers obtained verbal informed consent from adults prior to the initial phone screening that determined initial study eligibility for the adult and their child. Prior to study enrollment, Brown University research staff explained study procedures and obtained written informed consent and assent forms from parents and children, respectively, who agreed to participate in the study.

Regarding confidentiality, research staff attempted to minimize loss of confidentiality in the group-based sessions for the enhanced financial incentive and structurally equivalent attention-matched control groups by asking that study participants and community health workers not share who was participating in the study and to keep all content discussed within the group confidential. Confidentiality of data and identifying information was maintained by numerically coding all data, disguising identifying information, keeping all data in locked file drawers and/or on secure servers and using Brown University’s Qualtrics software application, which uses encryption procedures. Within 5 years after study completion, all study-related data will be destroyed. None of the principal investigators have any financial conflicts of interest as it pertains to the overall trial. However, one of the principal investigators, Dr. Dulin, is a board member at the center and provides in-kind services and one of the study co-investigators is the executive director of the center.

Participant eligibility criteria

Parent/legal guardian and child eligibility were determined in two phases, via an initial phone screening and a subsequent, in-person visit. All visits were conducted in English or with an interpreter.

Phase 1 initial phone screening: The adult completed the initial phone screening eligibility on behalf of themselves and their child. The adult inclusion criteria were (a) Hmong, Cambodian, Laotian or Vietnamese ethnicity; (b) the child’s legal guardian; (c) live with the child; (d) age 18 or older; (e) knowledgeable about the child’s diet; (f) responsible for household food preparation; (g) read/speak English, Hmong, Khmer, Vietnamese, and/or Lao; (h) own a smartphone; (i) be willing to shop at the partner SEA grocery store; (j) no participation in weight-related studies in the past 12 months because of the potential for selection bias and study contamination; and k) no hospitalization due to type 2 diabetes in the past year. The child inclusion criteria were a) Hmong, Cambodian, Laotian or Vietnamese; (b) age 6 to 11 years; (c) read/speak English; (d) no conditions affecting the child’s ability to eat fruits, vegetables or whole grains; (e) hospitalization due to type 2 diabetes in the past year; and (f) no behaviors that would affect a participant’s ability to engage in a general healthy eating program.

Phase 2 in-person screening: Adults and children who met initial eligibility criteria were invited to an in-person visit. Staff confirmed eligibility by having the child complete the Veggie Meter, a non-invasive measure of dermal carotenoids that is highly correlated with serum carotenoids and captures carotenoid-rich fruit and vegetable intake over a 2-month period [37, 38]. If this final eligibility criterion (i.e., child’s Veggie Meter score ≤ 400) was met, the family was invited to complete measures and enroll in the study. The participants completed study measures, anthropometrics and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c). A physician reviewed HbA1c values indicative of prediabetes or diabetes (i.e., ≥ 5.7%); individuals with HbA1c values ≥ 6.5% needed to receive clearance to participate (e.g., letter) from their primary provider.

Participant recruitment

Community partner organization staff recruited participants at cultural festivals and SEA-owned businesses (e.g., ethnic food markets, nail salons), religious institutions and from the center’s client-base. We also relied on participants’ direct referrals and engaged in more passive recruitment activities by posting study information on the center’s Facebook page and the personal social media sites of the center’s staff.

Assignment of interventions

The study biostatistician uses a permuted random block procedure with small randomly sized blocks to generate the randomization schemes. Randomization is stratified by SEA ethnic subgroup. The biostatistician places the randomization assignments in sealed envelopes which are sent to the research staff; the study biostatistician is not involved in any human subjects activities. The research staff open the sealed envelope once participants complete all in-person screening, informed consent, assent and data collection activities. While research staff were not blinded to participant conditions, the biostatistician was blinded to participants’ assignments and was provided only deidentified data.

Development of the SEAHK intervention

The Community Energy Balance (CEB) and Family-Centered Action Model of Intervention Layout and Implementation (FAMILI) frameworks, behavioral economics, social norms theory and popular education informed intervention components, structure and delivery [21, 39–42]. The CEB and FAMILI frameworks propose that for ethnic minority groups, multilevel, multicomponent, multigenerational interventions may be the most effective for changing and sustaining behavior change. While CEB describes cultural-contextual influences (e.g., food environments), intervention settings (e.g., community-based), agents (e.g., multigenerational) and intervention approaches (e.g., multilevel), the FAMILI framework focuses on children’s needs while intervening within the family system [21, 39]. Behavioral economics suggests that financial incentives compensate individuals for the physical and mental costs of changing behavior [40]. Social norms theory suggests that individuals’ behaviors are shaped by their misperceptions of how their peers behave [41]. Popular education philosophy uses an empowerment model that centers on participants sharing experiences and evidence-based behavior change techniques [42].Thus, these frameworks informed the intervention’s cultural tailoring, focus on home and ethnic food environments, the community-based setting of the family-based, group nutrition education sessions, MI phone calls, financial incentives, and social norms text messages.

In addition to the conceptual frameworks, direct input from SEA families and prior, community-engaged research to develop nutrition education content informed the SEAHK culturally tailored intervention [42]. Input from SEA families was obtained via our initial concept mapping study to identify risk and protective factors for childhood obesity among SEA [13], our prior pilot dietary intervention with 9 SEA families and initial focus groups that we held as part of the current intervention study. We held initial focus groups with 21 Southeast Asian parents/guardians and 18 children. Adults completed a descriptive dietary norms survey which informed development of dietary norms text messages. Adults in these sessions also refined children’s nutrition education content; they did not refine the content for adults as our prior development study indicated high acceptability of the adults’ content. Adults also provided insights about participant recruitment and engagement strategies, acceptability of the planned multigenerational intervention and acceptable approaches to obtain HbA1c measures from children and adults. Adults also indicated their preferred number of times they would like to receive weekly text messages and preferred content. The children’s exploratory focus groups used an age-appropriate, novel child-centered approach, “Write, draw, show and tell” [43]. Children provided drawings of unhealthy and healthy foods and discussed suggested strategies about how they would talk to other children to get them to eat healthier. Focus group facilitators completed written summaries within 48 h of each focus group to inform the study design. The study team, including all community partners and research staff, reviewed the results to inform adaptation, refinement, and cultural tailoring.

Although the focus group results will be detailed elsewhere, results from written summaries indicated that parents and children preferred interactive nutrition sessions that incorporated culture, pride and comfort with SEA foods, focused on portion sizes, and increasing fruit, vegetable and water intakes. As it pertains to the social norms text messages, parents preferred to receive two to three text messages each week for 6 months. They expressed that in addition to the dietary norms text messages, they would also prefer to receive general health coaching, videos, recipes, and short messages from dietary experts. Parents also identified strategies to obtain HbA1c measures from children, such as parents modeling the ease of assessment by going first. Also, based on their knowledge of the proposed study and its purpose, parents suggested that we inform enrolled participants that the HbA1c assessment was optional. We incorporated these procedures into our study protocol. Last, parents discussed logistical difficulties with recruiting and including multigenerational family members (e.g., grandparents) in nutrition education group sessions and did not prefer this proposed multigenerational approach.

We held separate confirmatory focus groups with 13 SEA adults and 10 children to determine acceptability of sample intervention materials. We used a voting system; when ≥ 40% voted to improve content, discussion ensued about how to change the materials [44]. Families received a $30 incentive for their participation in the initial and confirmatory focus groups, respectively.

Study intervention arms

Based on the above development activities, we created the following study arms.

Financial incentive only: Participants received four $15 coupons (US dollars) mailed to their home each month for 6 months; coupons expired 4 weeks after receipt. The coupons were printed on security paper and were stamped with the Brown University seal for authenticity. The coupons were restricted to the purchase of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and water at a partnering Southeast Asian grocery store. Overall, participants received the equivalent of $360 in financial-incentive coupons. To determine fidelity to the coupon purchases, the research staff collected the coupons from the grocery store each month and provided the store with the financial equivalent of the coupons used. The research staff member recorded coupon use in an Excel file. Also, participants received weekly text message reminders to use their smartphones to take photos of their purchases and upload them to the SEAHK study’s text message platform. In turn, participants who uploaded their photos were entered into a monthly $25 raffle for participation in the photo verification. Participants received weekly text message reminders to upload their photos.

Enhanced financial incentive: Participants received the same intervention described in the Financial Incentive only arm but participants received their coupons at the in-person group nutrition education sessions rather than by mail. The financial incentives were supplemented with both in-person group nutrition education sessions and out-of-session MI phone calls, healthy eating text messages and photo verification reminders. Two SEA community health workers led twice monthly group-based, 1-h nutrition education sessions; adults and children attended separate sessions. The community health worker followed a general structure for each session as detailed below. For the first nutrition education session, the community health worker introduced and reviewed session goals, engaged in interactive nutrition education content and concluded with goal setting. Subsequent sessions followed the same structure but began by reviewing prior session content, checking in on adults’ progress on goals and discussion of text messages received. Children’s sessions included an introduction and review of session goals, interactive nutrition education content, brief activity breaks, and concluded with goal setting. At these sessions, children and adults viewed infographics of SEA and non-SEA children and families engaging in healthy eating behaviors to support a perceived healthy eating norm through this educational visual. In addition to the nutrition education group sessions, the adult participants received MI phone calls in months 1, 3, and 5 from trained community health workers. These calls addressed participants’ thoughts about the nutrition education sessions and text messages, barriers to behavior change, and plans to improve dietary practices and the home food environment. The community health workers used Motivational Enhancement, which is a brief form of MI [45]. In addition, adults received two to three weekly text messages that communicated both positive dietary norms and evidence-based behavior change techniques such as social support, normative information, instruction on how to perform a behavior, identification of self as role model, and restructuring the physical environment [46]. The purpose of the normative information was to establish healthy practices through relevant models and relatable community data. To ensure that adults received dietary norms messages from their preferred reference group, they were matched with one of three groups (i.e., dietary norms of mostly SEA parents, of mostly non-SEA parents, or equally SEA and non-SEA parents). Some of the text messages were interactive and included a series of messages while others were not interactive. Examples of the text messages are included in Table 2. Structurally equivalent attention-matched school engagement (control group): These participants received an identical intervention structure as the enhanced financial incentive group, except instead of nutrition content, they received school engagement content to improve children’s academic outcomes. The rationale for this content was that the SEA community-based organization previously led a school engagement intervention for SEA families with high retention (84%). Additionally, the team identified that this content was timely and relevant because the State of Rhode Island took control over the Providence Public School District in 2019 (ongoing) due in part, to poor performance on student metrics [47]. The education sessions included activities and topics such as visiting a library to learn about resources, discussing connections with teachers, homework support, navigating language barriers and cultural norms. At these sessions, children and adults viewed infographics of SEA and non-SEA children and families engaging in activities to improve educational outcomes. As in the enhanced financial incentive group, to increase attendance, adults were entered into a monthly attendance raffle. In months 1, 3 and 5, adults received MI phone calls from trained community health workers. The calls covered the school engagement curriculum, goal setting, barriers and progress on goals. The community health workers used a Motivational Enhancement protocol. Enrolled adults received two to three interactive text messages aligned with the curriculum content. Strategies to improve adherence

Study incentives

Enrolled participants received $50 for data collected both at study enrollment and 6 months and $20 for completion of each of the two, 24-h dietary recalls at both timepoints for a total of $180 USD. Children received small toys at study enrollment and 6-month visits to thank them for participating. Participants in the control group also received the cash equivalent of the financial incentive coupon amount ($360 US dollars) at 6-month follow-up.

Criteria for discontinuation and fidelity monitoring

Participants in all study arms could withdraw from the study voluntarily. Participants in the enhanced financial incentive and school engagement arms were withdrawn administratively if they enrolled in the study but never attended any of the in-person sessions. To increase adherence to the SEAHK study protocols, community health workers completed content fidelity and participant engagement checklists at the end of each group-based education session. These checklists included about 20 questions that inquired as to whether the community health worker reviewed goals and objectives for the session, if they reviewed prior session activities and linked them to the current session and reviewed the study text messages that participants received in the prior week. The questions then asked about whether participants were interested in or engaged in these activities. Subsequently, the checklist asked about whether specific content for the current session was discussed and whether participants asked questions and seemed engaged. The last set of questions asked about the helpfulness of the sessions on increasing participants’ knowledge, suggestions to improve the activity and open-ended feedback. The research team randomly visited two classes per cohort using a fidelity checklist to assess adherence to the intervention curriculum. For the MI phone calls, community health workers used record-keeping forms to track the number of phone contact attempts and completed fidelity checklists. Calls were audiotaped and research staff randomly monitored 20% of calls. Based on reviews of the fidelity checklists and in-person monitoring, refresher trainings were offered to community health workers as needed. Research staff also ran weekly text messaging reports to ensure that participants received the messages as intended. Research staff also collected the financial incentive coupons from the partner store each month and downloaded participants’ photos of their food purchases to document coupon use and compliance with healthy food purchases in a study database.

Reporting adverse events

We created a Data and Safety Monitoring Plan for the SEAHK intervention. This plan was required by the funding agency, the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases, to protect the health and safety of participants and the validity and integrity of the study [48]. The proposed trial was considered a low-risk behavioral intervention. The multiple principal investigators, Drs. Gans and Keita, provided continuous internal monitoring of the intervention. Included in the Data and Safety Monitoring Plan were routine, weekly reviews of participant recruitment, retention, adherence to the protocol, adverse event rates, and compliance to the evaluation protocol. If there were any adverse events, they would have been reported in accordance with the Brown University IRB protocol and the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (i.e., fatalities reported within 24 h, other serious adverse events reported within seven working days).

Data collection, management, and analysis

Outcomes and data collection instruments

The main goals of this study were to assess feasibility including attendance (primary) and recruitment, enrollment, intervention dose, and retention (secondary), acceptability of intervention components, and conduct process evaluation to determine implementation fidelity and intervention dose.

Feasibility: The primary feasibility measure was attendance. Secondary measures included recruitment, enrollment, intervention dose, and retention. Attendance was measured using participant attendance logs for the enhanced financial incentive and control arms in-person group sessions. Recruitment was measured by documenting how participants learned of the study and participant yield from each recruitment site. Enrollment was assessed by the percent of eligible participants who consented to participate. We also developed intervention dose variables (e.g., number of in-person group nutrition education sessions attended, MI calls completed, and financial incentive verification photos submitted) summarizing intervention exposure for each participant overall and by intervention component. Because the goal was to deliver a moderately intensive behavioral intervention (i.e., 26 h or more), intervention dose was converted to hours (e.g., 45 min per in-person session (9 h total); homework (1 h total) 15 s to read and respond to each norms interactive text message series (50 min total); 15 min per MI call (45 min total); and up to 24 grocery trips (based on 2018 American Time Use Survey, the average grocery store trip in the United States takes 46 min (18.4 h total)) [49]. Retention was assessed by the percent of enrolled adults and children who completed follow-up surveys, dietary recalls, anthropometrics and HbA1c measures.

Acceptability: Adult participants, community health workers and the grocery store owner completed acceptability measures at the 6-month follow-up. Adults completed 6-month satisfaction questions (e.g., helpfulness of the intervention components, overall satisfaction). The community health workers reported helpfulness of the intervention activities for changing participants’ knowledge and behaviors and open-ended responses about acceptability of the curriculum. We also conducted semi-structured qualitative interviews with the grocery store owner and community health workers to identify barriers to and facilitators of intervention implementation fidelity and acceptability of trainings and interventions [49].

Preliminary efficacy: Preliminary efficacy of the intervention was assessed by a trend towards clinically meaningful changes in children’s diet quality, anthropometrics, and HbA1c from study enrollment to 6-month follow-up, comparing study arms as described in the Objectives and Hypotheses section.

Children’s diet quality via the Healthy Eating Index (HEI) score: Children’s diet quality was assessed via two 24-h dietary recalls. Enrolled adults assisted children with recall reporting. We used the double pass method and a portion size booklet [50]. Recall data were entered into the Nutrition Data System for Research (Minneapolis, Minnesota), which calculates HEI total score (range 0 to 100) and subcomponent scores (total fruit, whole fruit, total vegetable, greens and beans, whole grain, refined grains and added sugars) as primary outcomes [3]. We will use the HEI-2020 [3]. We expect that our intervention will demonstrate a trend towards improving total HEI by 5 units, which is clinically and statistically meaningful [51]. A reduction of 5 HEI units predicts a 4–6% decrease in overall mortality and a 15% decrease in obesity; 5 HEI units is statistically meaningful as it is approximately 0.5 of the standard deviation of HEI; and 5 HEI units is a reasonable expectation for a moderately intensive intervention [52–54]. We also included the Veggie Meter scores, described in eligibility criteria section, as indicators of children’s intake of carotenoid rich fruits and vegetables.

Children’s body mass index (BMI): Body weight was measured with a Tanita Professional Digital Scale (Model BF350) and height was determined via portable stadiometer (Seca 214). With these measures, we calculated the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s BMI age/sex-specific percentiles for children. For children, no change in weight or a reduction in zBMI of ≥ 0.25 over the 6 months will be considered clinically meaningful because it is associated with improvements in type 2 diabetes risk markers [23, 25, 55].

Children’s and adult’s waist circumference: Child and adult waist circumference was measured with a flexible tape and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey waist circumference protocols [56]. We classified waist circumference using Adult Treatment Panel III (adults) and age-sex percentiles (children) [57, 58].

Children and adults’ HbA1c was derived from blood: HbA1c was obtained from trained research staff who conducted finger sticks. HbA1c, which provides an average of 2 to 3 months of blood glucose values, was measured via DCA Vantage Analyzer, a valid and reliable measure used with young children and in community settings [59, 60]. A clinically meaningful HbA1c reduction is 0.5% [61].

Adults’ dietary outcome measures: Adult outcomes include the 16-item Townsend Food Behaviors Checklist which is a valid, reliable measure of diet quality [62, 63] and a 16-item beverage intake questionnaire (BEV-Q) which is a reliable and valid measure of habitual intake.

Parents completed the following surveys and measures, completion date because season is a confounder of dermal carotenoids and demographics of the adult and child age, sex, SEA subgroup (i.e., Laotian, Cambodian, Vietnamese, or Hmong), marital status, education, household size and income. They also completed child feeding, diet self-efficacy and healthy eating change strategies questionnaires [64–66]. Parents also reported food security status using the United States Department of Agriculture’s 6-item module [67] and Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and Women, Infants, and Children program participation. Parents also reported family history of type 2 or gestational diabetes, primary care provider information and cultural group identification. The cultural identification measure was adapted from prior studies [68, 69] and was used to match participants to the cultural reference group (SEA parents only, both SEA and non-SEA parents or non-SEA parents only) for the dietary norms and healthy eating text messages.

Participants

Sample size determination

The sample size for this pilot study is 75 adult–child dyads. We determined the sample size using attendance and pilot results from our preliminary study with 9 families and from effect sizes. We used an attendance rate of 50%, a conservative standard deviation of 25% and a sample size of 25 dyads per intervention arm, a one-sided 90% confidence interval with 50% as the lower bound is obtained from an observed value of 54% (54–1.282[25/√75] = 50).

Data management and analysis

Multiple Principal Investigator Dr. Dulin was responsible for data management and creation of study codebooks. Dr. Dulin conducted periodic data quality checks (e.g., range checks for data values) and worked with the biostatistician and the study program coordinators to develop codebooks and databases that the biostatistician will use to complete analyses.

Statistical analysis plan

As a preliminary step, we will assess potential between-group differences in baseline characteristics (demographics, dietary intake, diet quality, anthropometrics and HbA1c of children and enrolled adults) using graphical methods, non-parametric and parametric tests as appropriate. Any variables not balanced by randomization will be controlled for as covariates in subsequent analyses if they are correlated with the predictor and outcome under consideration at a modest p < 0.10 level. The distribution of each study outcome will be assessed using parametric and graphical methods and log transformed as necessary.

Feasibility and acceptability

Feasibility will be examined based on the primary feasibility outcome of attendance (% of sessions attended for the enhanced financial incentive and control group arms). The intervention will be considered feasible if 70% of families attend in-person sessions. Secondary measures will be considered feasible if 7 SEA families are recruited and enrolled on average over 12 months and if 80% of enrolled families are retained at 6 months. The intervention overall and individual intervention components will be considered “acceptable” if at least 70% of participants rate the component as “favorable” or “very favorable” on the measure of acceptability.

Preliminary efficacy

As this is a pilot study, all analyses related to preliminary efficacy are considered exploratory. We will estimate preliminary efficacy of the intervention on study outcomes using a series of longitudinal mixed effects models. Outcomes at 6 months will be regressed on baseline value, intervention component, as well as potential confounders identified as part of the preliminary analysis. We do not expect to find statistically significant differences between study arms for this small pilot study, instead interest is in estimating effect sizes (and corresponding 95% confidence intervals) for future studies, particularly as it relates to differences between the two active intervention arms (enhanced financial incentive vs. financial incentive only). Models will include a subject-specific intercept and adjust for clustering by community health worker. Models will include sex (of enrolled adult and child) as a covariate. We will explore whether sex moderates the effect of intervention on outcomes. A similar approach will be used to examine whether cultural identification is a moderator. Regression models will investigate the relationship between intervention dose, implementation measures and study outcomes. In all cases, interest is in effect estimates (with corresponding 95% confidence intervals) rather than strict statistical hypothesis testing.

If an enrolled adult or child dropped out of the intervention, we attempted to gather follow-up information. If a participant refused to be contacted or lost contact with the study, we will censor data at baseline. Our analysis will focus on the intent-to-treat sample, meaning that all randomized families will be included in analysis, regardless of the amount of data they contributed. Analysis will use estimating equations and maximum likelihood to produce estimates of the regression parameters. This approach makes use of all available data without requiring imputation of missing values [70]. We will run a sensitivity analysis to explore the robustness of our findings to other assumptions of the missing data.

Discussion

This study describes the protocol for a randomized control pilot intervention trial designed to test feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary efficacy of a multilevel, multicomponent, family-based, dietary intervention with SEA families. SEA families and children are burdened by poor diet quality, adverse social determinants of health, histories of trauma, and adverse health outcomes, yet culturally tailored dietary interventions are lacking for this population [10–13]. This intervention addressed these gaps by using appropriate frameworks and intervention components informed by CEB, FAMILI, behavioral economics, popular education and social norms theory [21, 39–42]. Based on these approaches, the intervention included a novel combination of established and underutilized intervention components including family-based nutrition education, MI calls, descriptive dietary norms text messaging, and healthy eating financial incentives.

Strengths and limitations

This study has a number of strengths in that it is responsive to the cultural and social contexts of an underserved population (SEA families). The study design is informed by relevant frameworks and theories, utilized well-established and novel intervention components, and was multilevel, including individual, interpersonal and environmental components. Importantly, this intervention was conducted in partnership with our community organization partner, with whom we have a 13-year relationship and input directly from SEA families. This longstanding partnership and collaboration ensured cultural relevance and underscored the importance of dietary interventions for SEA families. Although there are many strengths, this study is not without some limitations. For instance, participant attendance and retention were challenging, so we altered the original study protocol by implementing additional strategies to improve attendance (e.g., attendance raffles and dinner for those attending in-person classes). Because we anticipated that measuring redemption of financial incentive coupons might be a challenge, we considered having the SEA grocery store monitor the purchases. However, the SEA stores lacked stock-keeping unit and scanning equipment, and owners in the pilot interviews were uncomfortable monitoring purchases with visual verification. Also, previous studies cited difficulties with tracking purchases and accessing sales data from grocery stores [71, 72]. Thus, we opted to use the photo verification system using participants’ cell phones. Because it was likely that participants may have forgotten to send verification photos, we used a text message reminder system. We also adapted our protocol to include a raffle to incentivize participants’ uploading photos of the items purchased with the coupons.

Conclusions

Results from this feasibility trial will inform a fully powered randomized control trial. We will assess study results in the context of the eight domains of feasibility studies as described elsewhere [73] to inform the future fully powered trial.
