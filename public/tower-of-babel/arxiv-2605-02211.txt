Many Hamiltonians Are Sparsifiable
Arpon Basu arpon.basu@princeton.edu Princeton University Joshua Brakensiek josh.brakensiek@berkeley.edu UC Berkeley Aaron Putterman aputterman@g.harvard.edu Harvard University

Abstract
We study the problem of Hamiltonian sparsification: given a parameter ε ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \varepsilon\in(0,1) and an n n -qubit Hamiltonian H H which is the sum of r r -local positive semi-definite (PSD) terms H 1 , … ​ H m H_{1},\dots H_{m} , our goal is to compute a sparse set L ⊆ [ m ] L\subseteq[m] , along with weights w : L → ℝ ⩾ 0 w:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0} such that for every state | ψ ⟩ ∈ ℂ 2 n \lvert\psi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}} , ∑ i ∈ L w ⁡ ( i ) ⋅ ⟨ ψ | H i | ψ ⟩ ∈ ( 1 ± ε ) ⋅ ∑ i = 1 m ⟨ ψ ​ | H i | ​ ψ ⟩ . \sum_{i\in L}w(i)\cdot\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle. When the set L L is significantly smaller than m m , this reduces the number of terms in the underlying system, while still ensuring that the behavior of the system is essentially unchanged. Perhaps surprisingly, despite the strength of this approximation condition, we show that many Hamiltonians indeed are sparsifiable to a number of terms much smaller than n r n^{r} , including: (a) Hamiltonians where each term is an r r -local Pauli string, (b) Hamiltonians where each term is an r r -local random operator of rank R R , for R ⩾ 2 r − 1 + 1 R\geqslant 2^{r-1}+1 , and (c) Hamiltonians where each term is an arbitrary r r -local operator of rank ⩾ 2 r − 1 \geqslant 2^{r}-1 (a.k.a. Quantum SAT). Taken together, our results show that the sparsifiability of Hamiltonians is a robust phenomenon, contrary to prevailing belief (see for instance, Aharonov-Zhou ITCS 2019, QIP 2019). Our results find applications, for instance, to better (semi-)streaming algorithms for quantum Max-Cut, answering a question left open by Kallaugher and Parekh (FOCS 2022). In fact, our results even codify that quantum systems are often easier to sparsify than their classical counterparts. On a technical level, our results rely on an extensive analysis of a quantity that we call the “non-redundancy” of a Hamiltonian. Roughly speaking, this measures the largest set of terms where each can be “uniquely satisfied”: i.e., such that for each term H i H_{i} in the set, there is a state | ψ ⟩ \lvert\psi\rangle which gives term H i H_{i} non-zero energy, and all other terms H ≠ i H_{\neq i} zero energy. This quantity is inherently related to the sparsifiability of the Hamiltonian, but is often easier to work with, as it is governed entirely by the manner in which the kernels of the Hamiltonian terms interact.

This more general understanding of CSP sparsification motivates a natural question about Hamiltonian sparsification:

Is Hamiltonian sparsification always impossible? Or are there natural classes of Hamiltonians for which significant sparsification is possible?

In what follows, we give a strongly affirmative answer to the second question.

1.1
Our Work

As our first result, we study the sparsifiability of Hamiltonians specified by
Pauli strings
(we call these
Pauli Hamiltonians
). In this setting, we consider a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
where each local term is of the form
P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{n}
, where
P_{j}\in\{\operatorname{Id},X,Y,Z\}
acts on the
j^{\mathrm{th}}

r
-local, this is equivalent to requiring that
\#\{j:P_{j}\neq\operatorname{Id}\}\leqslant r
. Note that arbitrary Pauli strings define operators which have eigenvalues that are
\pm 1

H_{i}=P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{n}+\operatorname{Id}
.
2
2

2

Our results also apply to Pauli strings of the form
\operatorname{Id}-P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{n}

Such Hamiltonians naturally arise in the analysis of quantum stabilizer codes, with each Pauli string corresponding to a stabilizer term of the code
[
4

. For this class of Hamiltonians, we show the following:

Theorem 1.1

(Sparsifying Pauli Hamiltonians)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an
r
-local Pauli Hamiltonian with
m

\varepsilon>0

\varepsilon

w:L\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}

\lvert\psi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}

\sum_{i\in L}w(i)\cdot\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle.

Furthermore, there is a randomized (classical)
\mathrm{poly}(n,m)

An arbitrary Pauli Hamiltonian can have

r

As an aside, it is tempting here to say that

As our second main result however, we show that this sparsification phenomenon extends far beyond just Pauli strings. Indeed, we study the case of

1.2
A Quantum Theory of Non-Redundancy

Underlying our improved sparsifier constructions is an extensive study of a quantity that we call the
non-redundancy
of Hamiltonians, which generalizes the classical theory of CSP non-redundancy
[
12
,
11
,
19
,
16
,
13
,
38
,
15
]
. More formally, given a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
with PSD terms
H_{1},\dots H_{m}
, we say that
\mathcal{H}
is
non-redundant
if, for every
i\in[m]
, there exists a state
\lvert\psi\rangle
such that

\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle\neq 0,

yet for every
j\neq i\in[m]
,

\langle\psi{|}H_{j}{|}\psi\rangle=0.

It turns out that any non-redundant Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is an immediate barrier to sparsification: indeed, consider a sparsifier
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
(with terms
L\subseteq[m]
,
w:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
) which is missing some term
H_{i}
that was present in
\mathcal{H}
. Then, by the definition of
\mathcal{H}
being non-redundant, there must exist a state
\lvert\psi\rangle
such that
\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle\neq 0
. Since
\langle\psi{|}H_{j}{|}\psi\rangle=0
for every
j\in[m]\setminus\{i\}
, we have that
\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}{|}\psi\rangle\neq 0
. However
\langle\psi{|}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}{|}\psi\rangle=0
because
H_{i}
is
not
in the sparsifier
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
. Thus, for this state
\lvert\psi\rangle
, the sparsification condition is violated, as

0=\langle\psi{|}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}{|}\psi\rangle\notin(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}{|}\psi\rangle.

In fact, sparsification is obstructed in an even stronger sense here: the above shows that the
space of ground-states of the sparsifier
is strictly larger than the ground-space of the original Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
.

In general, when a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is redundant (i.e., not non-redundant), we define its non-redundancy to be the size of the largest
subset
of terms which is non-redundant.

Given the ample success non-redundancy has had in characterizing the sparsifiability of various classical CSPs (in particular,
[
16
]
), we believe that a strong theory of Hamiltonian non-redundancy is necessary toward the further construction of Hamiltonian sparsifiers.

To understand the non-redundancy of a Hamiltonian, one must reason about the manner in which the the ground states of the various terms of
\mathcal{H}
interact. For example, as we elaborate upon in
Lemma

1.8
, the crux of proving
Theorem

1.2
is to study the interactions between the kernels of two random
r
-local Hamiltonians which share a single qubit. More precisely, we show that if the rank of these individual Hamiltonian terms is sufficiently large, then the ground states of these two local Hamiltonians are entirely
disjoint
! This disjointness condition is critical for us getting lower bounds on the spectrum of our Hamiltonian, leading to a near-optimal sparsifier–this is discussed in much more detail in
Section

1.4.2
. Similar observations are used in the proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

However, our contributions to the study of non-redundancy extend far beyond the technical concerns of these sparsifier constructions. In particular, one of our primary contributions is a more systematic understanding of non-redundancy in
translation invariant
Hamiltonian systems where each
r
-local Hamiltonian is the same PSD predicate matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
. We let
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)
denote the size of the largest non-redundant Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
all of whose terms are copies of
M
applied to different
r
-qubit subsets (i.e., each term is of the form
M{|}_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T}
).

This is the natural Hamiltonian analogue to the study of the non-redundancy (or sparsifiability) of classical CSPs. In fact, we show in
Proposition

2.9
that the study of non-redundancy in Hamiltonians is
strictly
more general than in the CSP world, as one can represent any classical predicate as a
diagonal
matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
with
\{0,1\}
entries. Thus, any classical result on the sparsifiability or non-redundancy of constraint satisfaction problems
[
36
,
24
,
31
,
18
,
20
,
39
,
21
,
19
,
34
,
35
,
16
,
13
]
immediately translates to understanding Hamiltonians with
diagonal
operators
M
.

Obviously, the class of
r
-local Hamiltonians is much richer than the space of diagonal matrices. As such, we require novel tools for studying the non-redundancy of
r
-local Hamiltonians.

As our first contribution, we give a criterion for which sparsification is
impossible
. In particular, if our predicate matrix
M
can be expressed as the tensor product of rank
1
matrices, then we can construct
r
-local non-redundant Hamiltonians with
\Omega_{r}(n^{r})
terms. This result can be viewed as a quantum analogue of known lower bounds for the
\and_{r}
predicate
[

,
18
,
19
,
35
]
as well as a vast generalization of “Example A” in
[

]

Theorem 1.5

(Tensor Lower Bound)
.

Let
M=M_{1}\otimes M_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes M_{r}
be a predicate matrix where
M_{1},\ldots,M_{r}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
are non-zero rank

\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)\geqslant(\lfloor n/r\rfloor)^{r}\geqslant\Omega_{r}(n^{r})
.

As mentioned above,
Theorem

1.5
implies immediate, stronger lower bounds for Hamiltonian sparsification, both in the sense of preserving the energy on every state
\lvert\psi\rangle
, and also for the weaker task of only preserving the spectral gap.

Our second contribution is a systematic understanding of predicate matrices
M
acting on
2
-qubits (analogous to the arity-
2

[
24
,
18
,
11
]
). In particular, we show that the setting of
Theorem

1.5
is the
only
situation in which
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)
is superlinear!

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}
be a nonzero predicate matrix. Then
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)=\Theta(n)
unless
M=M_{1}\otimes M_{2}
where unary predicate matrices
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
have rank one, in which case
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)=\Theta(n^{2})
.

Given a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
whose terms are each of the form
M{|}_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T}

of the ground state of
\mathcal{H}
. In particular, we isolate any two terms
H_{a},H_{b}
in our Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
which share a single qubit. Say the first term acts on the first two qubits
\{1,2\}
and the second term acts on the first and third qubits
\{1,3\}
. Roughly speaking, we show that for any state in the common ground of both of these two terms, the state
must be
invariant under swapping the second and third qubits (see
Lemma

8.3
). Crucially, the automorphisms of a quantum state can be composed, so from just a handful of these automorphisms we can deduce a large family of additional ones. As such, we can deduce that whenever the underlying interaction graph of Hamiltonians forms certain (bipartite) cycles, the Hamiltonian itself must be redundant (see
Lemma

8.4
)! From here, a simple linear bound on the non-redundancy quickly follows. See
Section

8
for further details.

Our final contribution to the study of NRD is a further understanding of the non-redundancy of generic
r
-local Hamiltonians. In particular, while
Theorem

1.2
applies to
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
of rank at least
2^{r-1}+1
, we can adapt the techniques of
Theorem

1.6
to improve our understanding of translation-invariant Hamiltonians of slightly
lower rank
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
be a random predicate matrix of rank
R\geqslant 2^{r-1}-1
. Then, with probability
1
over
M
,
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)\leqslant\widetilde{O}(n)
.

The key technical lemma needed to prove
Theorem

1.7
is that two terms which share a single qubit again induce an automorphism in the ground state. However the automorphism which is created is now a composition of
r-1
transpositions rather than a single one (see
Lemma

9.2
). This makes the structure of the automorphism group of the overall ground state a bit more complex, but we can get a sharp bound on the non-redundancy by combining two facts: (1) each introduced automorphism increases the size of the overall automorphism group by a factor of at least 2, and (2) the total size of the automorphism group is at most
{|}S_{n}{|}=n!
. Thus, there can be at most
2\log_{2}(n!)=\widetilde{O}(n)
non-redundant constraints.

1.3
Broader Context and Comparison to Related Work.

We do remark that our setting differs slightly from that of
[
3
]
. In their work, Aharonov and Zhou primarily study the weaker problem of
gap-simulation
mentioned above, which requires preserving the ground states along with the spectral gap of the Hamiltonian (and places no stronger requirements on the higher end of the spectrum).
[
3
]
studies this task through the lens of
degree-reduction
(reducing the maximum number of terms that any qubit participates in) and
dilution
(reducing the total number of terms). Our setting aligns with this goal of dilution.

Of particular note however, is the fact that the negative results in
[
3
]
rely on an inability to increase the
interaction strength
between any two qubits, which is often important for building physical quantum systems. In our stronger setting which asks for full-spectrum simulation, reducing the interaction strength between qubits is not possible, as this inherently reduces the energy on the upper end of the spectrum. This setting where increased interaction strength is permitted is also studied in
[
3
]
, where it is shown that
degree reduction
is possible, albeit, by introducing
\mathrm{poly}(n)
extra auxiliary qubits (a commonly allowed operation, see
[
22
,
52
]
). However, for the task we study in this paper of
dilution
(reducing the number of terms in the Hamiltonian),
[
3
]
prove no analogous results, instead showing only impossibility results for certain classes of Hamiltonians. Our results hold
without
introducing extra qubits; the only modification to the underlying Hamiltonian is the removal of certain terms, and the re-weighting of others. Indeed, even the underlying operators in the individual terms are unchanged (up to scaling).

Nevertheless, the problem we study of Hamiltonian sparsification (or sometimes called “full spectrum simulation”) is still natural in many contexts. Indeed, as discussed above, many problems like quantum MAX-CUT (i.e., see
[
43
,
25
,
5
,
42
,
33
,
30
,
44
]
) or in general, the maximization of the energy of a stoquastic Hamiltonian (see
[
32
]
) require sparsifiers that do preserve the upper end of the Hamiltonian’s spectrum, while others require the preservation of the lower end of the spectrum
[
45
,
53
]
. We thus view our full-spectrum sparsification requirement as a natural
algorithmic
question, as it serves as an important preprocessing step for downstream quantum algorithms. Indeed, by reducing the number of distinct terms (i.e, performing sparsification), downstream algorithms can operate more efficiently on Hamiltonians with smaller descriptive complexity and fewer terms.

At a broader level, our results point to an ability to perform general size reduction in certain classes of Hamiltonians. Such procedures have been widely studied in the context of the Quantum PCP conjecture previously; see, for instance,
[
6
,
29
,
2
,
3
]
. However, our results are lacking in two regards: first, as mentioned above, the sparsification can increase the
interaction strengths
of the remaining terms, and second, our sparsification result
does not
hold for every Hamiltonian. Nevertheless, we view it as a promising open question whether our techniques can be repurposed for this setting. In particular, our primary technical insight of studying
Hamiltonian non-redundancy
deals with this same fundamental objective of trying to preserve the ground state of a Hamiltonian while reducing the number of terms (and thus degree) as much as possible.

Finally, we note that the study of quantum non-redundancy is closely related to the recent study of conditions for the sparsifiability of general families of PSD matrices. Indeed, while the study of CSP sparsification (as described above) using the notion of non-redundancy has been very fruitful, the techniques used to prove these results are highly combinatorial, thus preventing their use in more general contexts. However,
[
8
]
generalizes the notion of non-redundancy in a more quantitative manner to define the quantity known as
connectivity threshold
, which allows them to prove sparsification results about families of PSD matrices even when there might not be any underlying combinatorial structure.
[
8
]
analyzes a simple “importance sampling sparsification” algorithm (á la
[
48
]
) and shows that just like non-redundancy is the appropriate notion of sparsification in CSP sparsification, connectivity thresholds are the appropriate notion of sparsification in
PSD sparsification
. Our results can be interpreted as developing a framework towards rigorously understanding what these “connectivity thresholds” look like in Hamiltonians.

1.4
Technical Overview

To give the reader a sample of the technical methods used in this paper, we give proof sketches of two of our main results:
Theorem

1.1
on sparsifying Pauli Strings and
Theorem

1.2
on sparsifying generic Hamiltonians.

1.4.1
Pauli String Sparsification

We now give a brief overview of the proof of
Theorem

1.1
. In this setting, we are given some local Pauli strings (each term shifted by identity so that the minimum eigenvalue becomes
0
). Before we describe how we prove
Theorem

1.1
, we invite the reader to appreciate why Hamiltonian sparsification is in general a harder problem than classical CSP sparsification. When we frame classical CSP sparsification in terms of Hamiltonians, the corresponding Hamiltonians are diagonal (in the computational basis
\{\lvert 0\rangle,\lvert 1\rangle\}^{\otimes n}
), and in particular it suffices to reason about the energies of the basis vectors alone, which gives the problem a very combinatorial flavor.

However, given two local Hamiltonians
M_{T}=M|_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T}}
and
M_{T^{\prime}}
, where
T,T^{\prime}
intersect,
M_{T},M_{T^{\prime}}
no longer commute, and the usual combinatorial tools fail.

Nevertheless, for the case of Pauli strings, we can
restore commutativity
by partitioning the underlying hypergraph into
partite
pieces. More formally, suppose the non-identity terms in our Pauli string are
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}
. Further suppose the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
underlying our Hamiltonian was
r
-partite, i.e. the qubits can be partitioned into sets
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
such that
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\bigtimes_{i=1}^{r}V_{i}
. Then notice that when the Pauli string
P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{r}
(padded appropriately with
\operatorname{Id}
) acts on
\mathcal{H}
,
P_{1}
only acts on qubits from
V_{1}
,
P_{2}
only acts on qubits from
V_{2}
, and so on. In particular, even if two hyperedges
T,T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
share some qubits, the respective Hamiltonians on
T,T^{\prime}
still commute, thanks to the tensor product structure of Pauli strings!

Thus from a non-commuting family of Pauli Hamiltonians, we can extract a commuting family of Hamiltonians by passing on to a partite sub-instance. Now, because the Hamiltonians commute in a partite instance, they are simultaneously diagonalizable. But notice that a diagonal (in some rotated basis) system of Hamiltonians is equivalent to a classical system of CSPs, which means we can use tools from the theory of classical CSP sparsification
[
34
,
35
,
16
]
to resolve the sparsifiability of this family of Pauli Hamiltonians! In fact, the eigenvalues of a partite family of PSD Pauli Hamiltonians behave exactly like the assignment values of a corresponding instance of
\mathbf{XOR}
, and using classical CSP sparsification results on
\mathbf{XOR}
then yields the near-linear sparsifiability of Pauli Hamiltonians as in
Theorem

1.1
.

Finally, it remains to show that any hypergraph can be partitioned into (not too many) partite instances. For the technical overview, we will just explain the proof of this for graphs, i.e., we show that any graph can be decomposed into
\widetilde{O}(1)
many bipartite graphs: Indeed, we know that the MAX-CUT of any graph contains
\geqslant 1/2
the edges of the graph, and furthermore, a cut containing
\geqslant 1/2
fraction of the edges can be found very easily, for instance, by choosing every vertex to be in the cut at random with probability
1/2
.
5
5

5

This random algorithm can also be derandomized without too much effort using a simple “potential function” argument to keep track of which vertices to include in the cut depending on which side of the cut it sends more edges to
Consequently, peeling off the MAX-CUT reduces the number of edges in the graph by at least a factor of
2
, showing that this process terminates in
O(\log n)\leqslant\widetilde{O}(1)
many rounds. This proof sketch can be generalized to hypergraphs, see
Proposition

2.3
for further details.

1.4.2
Generic Sparsification

We now provide some intuition for
Theorem

1.2
, which relies more heavily on our non-redundancy machinery. Recall that in this setting, we are given a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
with
r
-local terms
H_{1},\dots H_{m}
. Notationally, each
H_{i}=(M_{i}){|}_{T_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{i}}}
, where
M_{i}
is a
random
PSD matrix of rank
R\geqslant 2^{r-1}+1
. For now, we take random to mean that
\operatorname{span}(M)=\operatorname{span}(v_{1},\dots v_{R})
, where each
v_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}}
and is chosen
at random
among unit norm vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}}
. As we shall see however, the exact manner in which these vectors is chosen is not important, as sparsifiability of these systems proves to be a robust phenomenon.

As discussed above, our key technique towards understanding the sparsifiability of a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is in understanding the maximal
non-redundant sub-instances
. In this direction, it is natural to then consider when, for a state
\lvert\psi\rangle
, it is the case that there are many terms
H_{i}:i\in[m]
such that
\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle=0
.

Understanding the Non-Redundancy of Generic Hamiltonians

In fact, in this setting with random operators
M_{i}
, it turns out that non-redundancy is relatively well-behaved. The key insight here is that whenever two terms
H_{i},H_{j}

intersect
(in the sense that they operate on a shared qubit), this imposes a very powerful constraint on which states
\lvert\psi\rangle
can be in the kernel of
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
. Formally, we show the following:

Lemma 1.8
.

Let
H_{i}=(M_{i}){|}_{T_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{i}}}
and
H_{j}=(M_{j}){|}_{T_{j}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{j}}}
, where
T_{i},T_{j}\subseteq[n]
, and
T_{i}\cap T_{j}\neq\varnothing
. Then, with probability
1
over
M_{i},M_{j}
(as defined above),
\ker(H_{i})\cap\ker(H_{j})=0\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
.

That is to say, the above lemma implies that there are
no
states of norm
1
which are in the kernels of both
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
. With
Lemma

1.8
in hand, we can now bound the size of any non-redundant Hamiltonian of the above form by
n
: indeed, let us suppose for the sake of contradiction that a Hamiltonian of the above form has
\geqslant n+1
terms. Then, it must be the case that there is some qubit which is shared between two terms. Call these terms
H_{a}
and
H_{b}
. Now, let us consider any other term
H_{c}
.

In order for
\mathcal{H}
to be a non-redundant Hamiltonian, it must be the case that there exists a state
\lvert\psi\rangle
such that

\langle\psi{|}H_{a}{|}\psi\rangle=\langle\psi{|}H_{b}{|}\psi\rangle=0,

while
\langle\psi{|}H_{c}{|}\psi\rangle>0
. But, this implies that
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\ker(H_{a})\cap\ker(H_{b})
, and so by
Lemma

1.8
,
\lvert\psi\rangle
must be trivial. But then
\langle\psi{|}H_{c}{|}\psi\rangle=0
as well, which is a contradiction.

Proving
Lemma

1.8
is more subtle; the intuition here is that a constraint of the form
\langle\psi{|}H_{a}{|}\psi\rangle=0
is imposing
2^{n-r}\cdot R
many linear constraints on the state
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
. The factor of
R
comes from the rank of the matrix
M_{a}
, and the factor of
2^{n-r}
comes from the tensor product with
\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{a}}}
. Thus, when we have two terms
H_{a}
and
H_{b}
, any state
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\ker(H_{a})\cap\ker(H_{b})
must obey a set of
2\cdot 2^{n-r}\cdot R
many linear constraints. Proving that there is no non-trivial state
\lvert\psi\rangle
which satisfies these constraints is then equivalent to showing that the
dimension
spanned by these combined constraints is
2^{n}
.

Unfortunately, it turns out that if
H_{a}
and
H_{b}
are
disjoint
(i.e., operating on non-intersecting groups of qubits), then the combined constraints have dimension
<2^{n}
and so there are non-trivial states
\lvert\psi\rangle
in
\ker(H_{a})\cap\ker(H_{b})
. Our argument in
Section

4.1
shows however that, when
H_{a}
and
H_{b}

do
share qubits, and satisfy a non-degeneracy condition among their spans which we call “genericity” (which holds with probability
1
for random operators), then in fact the combination of their implied linear constraints
does
span the entire
2^{n}
dimensional space.

We omit the technical analysis here, but our argument relies on carefully casting this span condition as the determinant of a specific
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
sub-matrix of these
2\cdot 2^{n-r}\cdot R
many linear constraints. If this determinant is non-zero, then this immediately implies that their span is
2^{n}
dimensional. Importantly, we show that when we treat the entries of the matrices
M_{a},M_{b}
as variables, this determinant polynomial is
not
the zero polynomial: thus, when the entries are chosen at random, the determinant is non-zero with probability
1
.

Building Sparsifiers For Generic Hamiltonians

Lemma

1.8
also has immediate applications in building sparsifiers. Indeed, consider the case when we have two terms,
T_{a},T_{b}
such that
T_{a}\cap T_{b}\neq\varnothing
. By
Lemma

1.8
, we know that
\ker(H_{a}+H_{b})
is trivial, as there is no other non-trivial state which can give both
H_{a}
and
H_{b}
zero energy.

Even more importantly, we know that
H_{a}+H_{b}
can be written as a tensor product of some matrix
(M_{a,b}){|}_{T_{a}\cup T_{b}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-(T_{a}\cup T_{b})}
, where
M_{a,b}
operates on
\leqslant 2r-1
many qubits. Thus, the eigenvalues of
H_{a}+H_{b}
, are exactly the eigenvalues of this matrix
M_{a,b}
(with multiplicity). Here is where we make use of an additional key observation: the matrix
M_{a,b}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{O(r)}\times 2^{O(r)}}
, where
r
is a
constant
in our setting. Thus, we have a constant size PSD matrix, with an empty null-space. So, we can then say that all eigenvalues of
M_{a,b}
are at least
\Omega_{r}(1)
(we use the subscript of
r
to denote that there may be (even exponential) dependence on the values in the matrices
M_{a},M_{b}
or the constant
r
). We formalize this reasoning in
Section

4.1
; the key intuition is that these matrices are sampled from a distribution parameterized only by
r
, thus
independent
of
n
, the number of qubits.

Continuing, suppose we are able to find many “disjoint pairs of intersecting terms”; call these
H^{(j)}_{a},H^{(j)}_{b}:j\in[C]
, where
H^{(j)}_{a}
operates on qubits
T_{a}^{(j)}\subseteq[m]
and
H^{(j)}_{b}
operates on qubits
T_{b}^{(j)}\subseteq[m]
such that
T_{a}^{(j)}\cap T_{b}^{(j)}\neq\varnothing
. Then, we know that the smallest eigenvalue of
H
must scale with the number of these disjoint pairs that we find, and so must be at least
C\cdot\Omega_{r}(1)
. But, we now can provide a tight bound on the so-called
importance score
of each term
H_{i}\in H
. Indeed, we see that:

\mathrm{Importance}(H_{i}):=\max_{\lvert\psi\rangle}\frac{\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi{|}H{|}\psi\rangle}\leqslant\max_{\lvert\psi\rangle}\frac{\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle}{\sum_{j\in[C]}\langle\psi{|}(H^{(j)}_{a}+H^{(j)}_{b}){|}\psi\rangle}

\leqslant\frac{\lambda_{\max}(H_{i})\cdot\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(\sum_{j\in[C]}H^{(j)}_{a}+H^{(j)}_{b})\cdot\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}}\leqslant O_{r}\left(\frac{1}{C}\right).

The key observation is that once
C
is large, say
\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}
, then this provides a very strong bound on the “importance score” of all constituent terms of the Hamiltonian. In fact, this bound is sufficiently strong such that we can
directly
invoke Matrix Chernoff to argue that if we randomly sample the terms of
H
at rate
\approx\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}C}
, and give the sampled terms weight
\approx\frac{\varepsilon^{2}C}{n}
(to yield a new, sparser, Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}
), then it is the case that, with high probability, for every state
\lvert\psi\rangle
,

\langle\psi{|}\widetilde{H}{|}\psi\rangle\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\langle\psi{|}H{|}\psi\rangle.

The argument then concludes by observing that whenever the number of terms
m
is larger than (say)
10n
, we can indeed find
\geqslant\frac{m}{4}
disjoint pairs of intersecting terms. Thus, in the above,
C=\frac{m}{4}
, and so the resulting sample retains only
\approx m\cdot\frac{n}{C\varepsilon^{2}}\leqslant O(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
many terms.

A more careful version of this argument is used to ultimately prove
Theorem

1.2
.

\mathcal{H}

2.1

2.2
Comparison to Classical CSP Sparsification.

We say that a predicate matrix

Given any

-

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{H}}w(T)\mathbf{1}[x{|}_{T}\in R]\in[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\cdot\sum_{T\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbf{1}[x{|}_{T}\in R]

(5)

We now show when

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an

-uniform hypergraph on vertex set

. Let

be a classical predicate with corresponding relation

. Given any map

(1)

\varepsilon
-sparsifier of

(2)

\varepsilon
-sparsifier of

with respect to

.

Proof.

First we show that

1
implies

. Assume

is an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of

with respect to
M
. Let
\psi
be a state of the form
{|}x\rangle
for some

. For every

, we have that

{\mathcal{E}}_{T,M}(\psi)=\langle\psi,M_{T}\psi\rangle=\langle\psi{|}_{T},M{|}_{T}\psi_{T}\rangle=M_{x{|}_{T},x{|}_{T}}=\mathbf{1}[x{|}_{T}\in R].

Hence,

Therefore, the fact that
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},w}(\psi)\in[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\psi)
(see

4
) implies

, as desired.

We now show that

1

\varepsilon
-sparsifier of

with respect to

for some

. Now, for an arbitrary state

and an arbitrary hyperedge

, observe that since

(and thus

) is a diagonal matrix, we have that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{T,M}(\psi)=\langle\psi,M_{T}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{|}\psi(x){|}^{2}(M_{T})_{x,x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{|}\psi(x){|}^{2}\mathbf{1}[x{|}_{T}\in R]

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]}w(i)\mathbf{1}[(Gx)_{i}=1]\in[1-\varepsilon/3,1+\varepsilon/3]\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}\nu(i)\mathbf{1}[(Gx)_{i}=1].

(6)

Returning to our
\neg\mathbf{XOR}
instance, any assignment
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
can be viewed as a vector
y:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},1)\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n+1}
. Now for all
i\in[m]
, we have that

(Gy)_{i}=1\iff\bigoplus_{j\in T_{i}}x_{j}\oplus 1=0\iff x\text{ satisfies }(\neg\mathbf{XOR})|_{T_{i}}.

Thus,
Eq.

6
implies
w
is an
\varepsilon/3
-sparsifier of our
\neg\mathbf{XOR}
instance.

To make this sparsifier unbiased, consider
y=(0,\ldots,0,1)\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n+1}
and note
Gy=(1,\ldots,1)
. Thus, (
6
) implies that

\sum_{i\in[m]}w(i)\in[1-\varepsilon/3,1+\varepsilon/3]\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}\nu(i).

Now for all
i\in[m]
define
w^{\prime}(i):=w_{i}\cdot\frac{\sum_{i\in[m]}\nu(i)}{\sum_{i\in[m]}w(i)}\in[1-\varepsilon/3,1+\varepsilon/3]
. We have that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w^{\prime}(i)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu(i)
, and for all
y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n+1}
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]}w^{\prime}(i)\mathbf{1}[(Gx)_{i}=1]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in[m]}\nu_{i}}{\sum_{i\in[m]}w(i)}\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}w_{i}\mathbf{1}[(Gx)_{i}=1]

\displaystyle\in[1-\varepsilon/3,1+\varepsilon/3]\cdot[1-\varepsilon/3,1+\varepsilon/3]\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}\nu_{i}\mathbf{1}[(Gx)_{i}=1]

\displaystyle\subseteq[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}\nu_{i}\mathbf{1}[(Gx)_{i}=1],

as desired. Therefore, the weights
w^{\prime}(1),\ldots,w^{\prime}(m)
form our unbiased
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of our
\neg\mathbf{XOR}
instance.
∎

Concentration Inequalities.

Fact 2.16

(Matrix Bernstein Inequality (
[
50
]
))
.

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be independently random mean-zero Hermitian matrices such that

with probability
1
for some

\sigma:=\left\|{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}^{2}\right\|_{\operatorname{op}}^{1/2}
,
Y=\sum_{i\in[m]}Y_{i}
. Then
{\|}Y{\|}_{\operatorname{op}}\leqslant O(R\log N+\sigma\sqrt{\log N})
with probability
\geqslant 1-1/N^{10}
.

Fact 2.17

(Matrix Chernoff)
.

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be independent, random Hermitian matrices. Let
Y=\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}
and let
Z={\mathbb{E}}[Y]
. Suppose that

with probability
1
, where

is an arbitrary scalar, for every

. Then, for all

,

\Pr\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}\preccurlyeq(1-\varepsilon)Z\right]\leqslant N\cdot\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}/2R)

\Pr\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}\succcurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)Z\right]\leqslant N\cdot\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}/3R).

2.5
Continuous Distributions and Boundedness Assumptions

We will often make use of the following fact about evaluating non-zero polynomials:

Claim 2.18

(Corollary 10 in
[
28
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{C}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}
be a multivariate polynomial on
k
variables which is
not
identically zero. Let
Z(f):=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k}:f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=0\}
. Then
Z(f)
has zero Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{C}^{k}
. In particular, for any absolutely continuous distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{C}^{k}
,

\Pr_{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\sim\mu}(f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=0)=0\,.

We also need to make some analytical assumptions. Fix any
r\geqslant 2
. Throughout the paper we will be dealing with arbitrarily large families of PSD matrices
\{M_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
, and we will need them to satisfy some “boundedness” conditions, which we state below. Before we state our conditions, we denote, for any non-zero Hermitian PSD matrix
A
, by
\lambda_{\min,\neq 0}(A)
the minimum non-zero eigenvalue of
A
.

Our first boundedness criterion restricts the maximum eigenvalue of our collection of PSD matrices.

Definition 2.19

(
C
-boundedness)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}:=\{M_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
be a collection of non-zero PSD matrices, where
r\geqslant 2
. For some
C\in(0,\infty)
we say
\mathcal{M}
is
C
-bounded if
\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}{\|}M{\|}_{\operatorname{op}}=\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\lambda_{\max}(M)\leqslant C
.

Again, we can see that there are absolutely continuous distributions of PSD matrices which are
C
bounded, for
C\leqslant O_{r}(1)
.

Example 2.20
.

Consider the collection of matrices
\mathcal{M}
which contains all matrices
M
which can be written as

M=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{r}}v_{i}v_{i}^{\dagger},

where each
v_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}}
is a vector with norm
\leqslant 1
.

Our second criterion also restricts some minimum nonzero eigenvalues.

Definition 2.21

(
(C,\ell_{0})
-boundedness)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}:=\{M_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
be a family of non-zero PSD matrices, where
r\geqslant 2
. For some
C\in(0,\infty),\ell_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
we say
\mathcal{M}
is
(C,\ell_{0})
-bounded if:

(1)

\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}{\|}M{\|}_{\operatorname{op}}=\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\lambda_{\max}(M)\leqslant C
, and,

(2)

Fix any non-empty
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{M}
with
{|}\mathcal{M}^{\prime}{|}:=\ell\leqslant\ell_{0}
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be any
r
-uniform hypergraph with
\ell
hyperedges, and write
V(\mathcal{H}):=\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{H}}T
. Let
\pi:\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{H}
be any bijection. Then

\lambda_{\min,\neq 0}\left(\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}M|_{\pi(M)}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{V(\mathcal{H})\setminus\pi(M)}\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{C}\,.

Example 2.22
.

To see that this definition makes sense, consider the following example of a bounded matrix family: Fix any non-zero PSD
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
, and let
M_{i}=M
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Also fix any
\ell_{0}:=\ell_{0}(r)
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(M,\ell_{0})
such
\mathcal{M}:=\{M_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is
(C,\ell_{0})
-bounded: Indeed, there are finitely many
r
-uniform hypergraph configurations with
\leqslant\ell_{0}
hyperedges, and since a finite set is compact, we can choose
C>0
so that
\mathcal{M}
is
(C,\ell_{0})
-bounded. Note that
C
is a function of
r,\ell_{0},M
.

3
Sparsifying Pauli Hamiltonians

3.1
General Framework

We begin with some definitions and notation:

Definition 3.1

(Pauli Matrices)
.

Define the Pauli matrices

X=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},Y=\begin{bmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix},Z=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}.

The Pauli matrices are unitary, and their eigenvalues are
\pm 1
. For
P\in\{X,Y,Z\}
, fix
\{u^{(-1)}_{P},u^{(1)}_{P}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2}
to be an orthonormal eigenbasis for
P
, with
Pu^{(\lambda)}_{P}=\lambda u^{(\lambda)}_{P}
for all
\lambda\in\{-1,1\}
.

We now take tensor products of Pauli operators to build the so-called
Pauli strings
:

Definition 3.2

(Pauli Strings)
.

A unitary operator
\operatorname{U}
acting on
\mathbb{C}^{\{0,1\}^{[n]}}
of the form
P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{n}
, where
P_{i}\in\{\operatorname{Id},X,Y,Z\}
acts on the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
qubit, is called a
Pauli string
. Furthermore, if
\#\{i:P_{i}\neq\operatorname{Id}\}\leqslant r
, then we call
\operatorname{U}
a
r
-local operator.

Note that if
\operatorname{U}
is a Pauli string, then all eigenvalues of
\operatorname{U}
are
\pm 1
. Furthermore, if
\operatorname{U}
is a
r
-local Pauli string, then
\operatorname{U}=W|_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]\setminus T}
for some
T\in[n]^{r}
. To fit Pauli strings into our framework of PSD matrices, we simply add the identity matrix to the entire system. This makes all the eigenvalues non-negative, and the resulting system PSD. Thus, we give the following definition:

Definition 3.3

(Pauli PSD Matrices)
.

Let
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}\in\{X,Y,Z,-X,-Y,-Z\}
be Pauli matrices (and their negations). We call the matrix
M:=P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{r}+\operatorname{Id}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
a
Pauli PSD matrix
. Equivalently, one could constrain
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}\in\{X,Y,Z\}
and specify that
\operatorname{Id}\pm P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{r}
is a Pauli PSD matrix.

We also define
Pauli Hamiltonians
:

Definition 3.4

(Pauli Hamiltonians)
.

If we have a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}=\{(T_{i},M_{i})\}_{i\in[m]}
where each
M_{i}
is a Pauli PSD matrix, then we call
\mathcal{H}
a
Pauli Hamiltonian
. In general, we can also have a
weighted Pauli Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}=(\{(T_{i},M_{i})\},\nu)
, where
\nu
is a function mapping each index
i
to a weight in
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
. Note that a weighted Pauli Hamiltonian is naturally associated to the PSD matrix
\sum_{i}\nu(i)\cdot(M_{i})|_{T_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{i}}}
.

Theorem 3.5

(Sparsifying Pauli Hamiltonians)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a
r
-local weighted Pauli Hamiltonian with
m
terms on
n
qubits. Then there exists a
\varepsilon
-sparsifier
w:[m]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
of size
\leqslant\widetilde{O}_{r}(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
, i.e. for any
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
,

\sum_{i}w(i){\mathcal{E}}_{T_{i},M_{i}}(\psi)\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\sum_{i}\nu(i){\mathcal{E}}_{T_{i},M_{i}}(\psi)\,.

Furthermore,
\sum_{i}w(i)=\sum_{i}\nu(i)
and there exists a randomized classical
\mathrm{poly}(m,n,O_{r}(1))
-time algorithm to compute such a sparsifier.

Proof.

Firstly, note that there are
3^{r}
choices for a Pauli PSD matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
. Thus we can split the hypergraph underlying
\mathcal{H}
into
k\leqslant 3^{r}\leqslant O_{r}(1)
hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{H}_{k}
corresponding to the different choices for a Pauli PSD matrix. Note that we can take the union of
\varepsilon
-sparsifiers of each of these sub-hamiltonians to get an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier for
\mathcal{H}
, and thus
\operatorname{SPR}(\mathcal{H},\varepsilon)\leqslant\sum_{j}\operatorname{SPR}(\mathcal{H}_{j},\varepsilon)
.

Thus fix a Pauli PSD matrix
W=\operatorname{Id}+P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{r}
and consider the
W
-Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{W}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, i.e.
\mathcal{H}_{W}
is the collection of all hyperedges in
\mathcal{H}
which are being acted upon by
W
. Note that it suffices to prove the claim for
\mathcal{H}_{W}
, i.e. show that
\operatorname{SPR}(\mathcal{H}_{W},\varepsilon)\leqslant\widetilde{O}_{r}(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
, and such a sparsifier can be found efficiently.

By
Proposition

2.3
, we can compute (in
\mathrm{poly}(n^{r})
time) a partition
\mathcal{H}_{W}=\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{H}_{W,a}
such that
{|}\mathcal{I}{|}\leqslant O_{r}(\log n)
, and
\mathcal{H}_{W,a}
is
r
-partite. If we show that
\operatorname{SPR}(\mathcal{H}_{W,a},\varepsilon)\leqslant\widetilde{O}_{r}(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
, then we obtain
\operatorname{SPR}(\mathcal{H}_{W},\varepsilon)\leqslant{|}\mathcal{I}{|}\cdot\widetilde{O}_{r}(\varepsilon^{-2}n)\leqslant\widetilde{O}_{r}(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
, and we’re done.

Thus for the remaining proof assume we have a
r
-partite
W
-Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
on
n
qubits,
8
8

8

if some
r
-partite subhypergraphs have fewer than
n
qubits that are acted non-trivally upon, we can simply shrink our space of qubits
where
W=P_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{r}
. Let
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
be the parts of
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\bigtimes_{k=1}^{r}V_{k}
. Thus for any
T\in\mathcal{H}
, we can write
T=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})
, where
t_{k}\in V_{k}
for all
k\in[r]
.

Now, consider the orthonormal basis
9
9

9

note that if
\mathcal{B}_{1}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\},\mathcal{B}_{2}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell}\}
are two orthonormal bases of vector spaces
V_{1},V_{2}
, then
\mathcal{B}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{B}_{2}=\{u_{i}\otimes v_{j}\}_{i\in[k],j\in[\ell]}
is an orthonormal basis for
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}

\mathcal{B}_{W}
of
\mathbb{C}^{\{0,1\}^{[n]}}
given by

\mathcal{B}_{W}:=\left\{\bigotimes_{k=1}^{r}\bigotimes_{v\in V_{k}}u^{(b_{v})}_{P_{k}}\right\}_{b\in\{-1,1\}^{[n]}}\,.

We claim that
\mathcal{B}_{W}
diagonalizes
W_{T}=W|_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T}}
for any
T=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})\in\mathcal{H}
. Firstly, note that it suffices to show that
\mathcal{B}_{W}
diagonalizes
\operatorname{U}_{T}:=(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{r}P_{k})\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T}}
, where
P_{k}
acts on
t_{k}
, since
W_{T}=\operatorname{U}_{T}+\operatorname{Id}
. But note that since
P_{k}
acts on
t_{k}
, and since every vector in
\mathcal{B}_{W}
has either
u^{(-1)}_{P_{k}}
or
u^{(1)}_{P_{k}}
in the
t_{k}^{\mathrm{th}}
position in the tensor product, we obtain that every vector in
\mathcal{B}_{W}
is an eigenvector of
W_{T}
for any
T\in\mathcal{H}
. Furthermore, for any
b\in\{\pm 1\}^{[n]}
, we have

\operatorname{U}_{T}\cdot\bigotimes_{k=1}^{r}\bigotimes_{v\in V_{k}}u^{(b_{v})}_{P_{k}}=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{r}b_{t_{k}}\right)\cdot\bigotimes_{k=1}^{r}\bigotimes_{v\in V_{k}}u^{(b_{v})}_{P_{k}}\implies W_{T}\cdot\bigotimes_{k=1}^{r}\bigotimes_{v\in V_{k}}u^{(b_{v})}_{P_{k}}=\left(1+\prod_{k=1}^{r}b_{t_{k}}\right)\cdot\bigotimes_{k=1}^{r}\bigotimes_{v\in V_{k}}u^{(b_{v})}_{P_{k}}\,.

Writing
b_{v}=(-1)^{\tau_{v}}
(for
\tau_{v}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
), we obtain

W_{T}\cdot\bigotimes_{k=1}^{r}\bigotimes_{v\in V_{k}}u^{(b_{v})}_{P_{k}}=2\cdot\left(1-\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}\tau_{t_{k}}\right)\cdot\bigotimes_{k=1}^{r}\bigotimes_{v\in V_{k}}u^{(b_{v})}_{P_{k}}\,,

where
\oplus
is taken over the field
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
. Consequently, in the basis
\mathcal{B}_{W}
,
W_{T}/2
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal describes the truth-table of
\neg\mathbf{XOR}(\tau_{t_{1}},\ldots,\tau_{t_{r}})
! Since quadratic forms are invariant under an orthonormal change of basis (from
(\lvert 0\rangle+\lvert 1\rangle)^{\otimes n}
to
\mathcal{B}_{W}
), sparsifying the (weighted)
W
-Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
reduces to sparsifying the (weighted) classical CSP
\neg\mathbf{XOR}
on the same hypergraph by
Proposition

2.9
. At this point, we can conclude by
Corollary

2.15
.
∎

Note that by
Remark

2.14
, the bound on the sparsifier size in
Theorem

3.5
does
not
depend on the weights in
\mathcal{H}
.

3.2
Sparsifying Pauli String Decompositions

We can also prove a somewhat stronger version of
Theorem

3.5
. To motivate this extension, let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
be a PSD predicate matrix which can be be written as a sum
M:=\eta(1)M_{1}+\cdots+\eta(k)M_{k}
, where each of
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}
is a Pauli PSD matrix. Now consider an
M
-Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}=\{(T_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
, with weights
\nu:[m]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
. Given our Pauli decomposition of
M
, we can decompose
\mathcal{H}
as
\mathcal{H}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{H}_{k}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{j}:=(\{(T_{i},M_{j}\},\eta(j)\nu)
. Applying
Theorem

3.5
, we can compute sparsifiers
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}
of
\mathcal{H}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{H}_{k}
which each has size
\widetilde{O}_{r}(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
. Since
k\leqslant O_{r}(1)
, the aggregate size of these sparsifiers is also
\widetilde{O}_{r}(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
.
A priori
, the resulting sparsifier is not a true
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}
as each term
M_{T_{i}}
is decomposed into
\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}(i)(M_{j})_{T_{i}}
, which may not be a scalar multiple of
M_{T_{i}}
. However, careful inspection of the proof of
Theorem

3.5
shows that these
k
different
\mathcal{H}_{j}
’s reduce to sparsifying the
same
classical
\neg\mathbf{XOR}
instances! In other words, the
\varepsilon
-sparsifier weights for any one of these
\mathcal{H}_{i}
’s works as an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier for any of the others and thus of
\mathcal{H}
itself! We formalize this observation in the following corollary.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
be a PSD predicate matrix which can be be written as a sum
M:=\eta(1)M_{1}+\cdots+\eta(k)M_{k}
, where each of
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}
is a Pauli PSD matrix. Given an
M
-Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}=\{(T_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
with weights
\nu:[m]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
, one can in randomized
\mathrm{poly}(n^{r})
-time construct an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\{(T_{i})\}_{i\in[m]}
with weights
w:[m]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
with support size at most
\widetilde{O}_{r}(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
. And furthermore,
\sum_{i}w(i)=\sum_{i}\nu(i)
.

As many details of the proof of
Corollary

3.6
are identical to
Theorem

3.5
, we only describe the novel aspects of the proof.

Proof.

Using
Proposition

2.3
, we may assume without loss of generality that our instance
\mathcal{H}
is
r
-bipartite. That is, the
n
-qubits have a partition
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
such that
T_{i}\subseteq V_{1}\times\cdots\times V_{r}
for all
i\in[m]
.

Since
M=\eta(1)M_{1}+\cdots+\eta(k)M_{k}
, we may construct weighted Pauli Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}_{j}:=(\{(T_{i},M_{j}\}_{i\in[m]},\eta(j)\nu)
such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}_{j}}
. By the proof of
Theorem

3.5
, since each
\mathcal{H}_{j}
is bipartite, an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}_{j}
is equivalent to an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of the classical CSP
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{j}:=\{T_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
of
\neg\mathbf{XOR}
with weights
\eta(j)\nu:[m]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
. Let
w^{(1)}:[m]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
be an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{1}
(which is also an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
). Now observe for any
j\in[k]
, we have that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{j}
is
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{1}
scaled by
\eta(j)/\eta(1)
. Thus,
\frac{\eta(j)}{\eta(1)}\cdot w^{(1)}
is an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{j}
and
\mathcal{H}_{j}
. Now define
w:=\frac{w^{(1)}}{\eta(1)}
. We claim that
w
is an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}
. To see why, for any state
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}w(i){\mathcal{E}}_{T_{i},M}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{k}w(i)\eta(j){\mathcal{E}}_{T_{i},M_{j}}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\eta(j)w^{(1)}(i)}{\eta(i)}{\mathcal{E}}_{T_{i},M_{j}}(\psi)

\displaystyle\in[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\eta(j)\nu(i){\mathcal{E}}_{T_{i},M_{j}}(\psi)

\displaystyle\subseteq[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu(i){\mathcal{E}}_{T_{i},M}(\psi),

as desired. Finally, observe that by
Theorem

3.5
, we have that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w^{(1)}(i)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\eta(1)\nu(i).
Thus,
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w(i)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{w^{(1)}(i)}{\eta(1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu(i)
, as desired.
∎

4
Sparsifying Random Hamiltonians

In this section, we will study the sparsifiability of Hamiltonians using
random
predicate matrices
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
.

Remark 4.1

(Distribution assumptions)
.

Throughout this section, we will consider matrices
M
which have rank
R\geqslant 2^{r-1}+1
, and whose span can be written as
\mathrm{span}(M)=\mathrm{span}(v_{1},\dots v_{R})
, where the vectors
v_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}}
are chosen at
random
from a distribution with absolutely continuous joint support, and uniform boundedness (see
Definition

2.19
,
2.18
). Furthermore, we suppose that this distribution is fixed
only as a function of

r
. We then let the number
n
of qubits grow
independently
of this distribution over predicate matrices.

With this notation in place, our main theorem is the following:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
be a random predicate matrix of rank
R\geqslant 2^{r-1}+1
drawn from a distribution in accordance with
Remark

4.1
and let
\mathcal{H}=\{(T_{i},M):{i\in[m]}\}
be an arbitrary Hamiltonian over
n
variables and
m
terms. Then, with probabilty
1-o_{r}(1)
over the choice of
M
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is a randomized algorithm running in time
\mathrm{poly}(n,m)
which computes a weight function
w:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
such that
w
is an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}
and the size of
w
is bounded by
\widetilde{O}_{r}(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
.

Remark 4.3
.

Note that we use
O_{r}(\cdot)
to denote that the leading constant of the sparsifier size has a constant which depends on the arity
r
(in particular, on the exact non-zero eigenvalues of the predicate matrix
M
). Importantly, the arity
r
and the predicate matrix
M
are fixed
independently
of the number of qubits
n
, and so as the number of qubits
n
increases, the size of the sparsifier scales only linearly in
n
(and the error parameter
\varepsilon
).

We can also prove a version of
Theorem

4.2
which holds when the individual predicate matrices are
different
. Formally, we show:

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\{(T_{i},M_{i}):{i\in[m]}\}
be an arbitrary Hamiltonian over
n
variables and
m
terms, where each predicate matrix
M_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
is random of rank
R\geqslant 2^{r-1}+1
, independently drawn from a distribution in accordance with
Remark

4.1
.

Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is a randomized algorithm running in time
\mathrm{poly}(n,m)
which computes a weight function
w:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
such that
w
is an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}
and the size of
w
is bounded by
\widetilde{O}_{r}(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
over the randomness of the
M_{i}
’s and the algorithm.

Remark 4.5
.

Going forward, when we describe matrices as being “random”, we mean exactly that they are drawn from a distribution as described in
Remark

4.1
. For brevity of the statements, we omit this qualifier.

Additionally, going forward, we will use
Proposition

2.3
to assume that the underlying Hamiltonian is
r
-partite WLOG, which leads to extra logarithmic factors in the sparsifier size.

4.1
Analyzing Intersecting Terms

Towards proving
Theorem

4.2
, our first key insight is to look at the structure of the underlying terms in the Hamiltonian. In particular, we focus our attention on any terms
T_{i}\subseteq[n]
and
T_{j}\subseteq[n]
such that
T_{i}\cap T_{j}\neq\varnothing
. For such terms, we have the following lemma:

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
be a random predicate matrix of rank
R\geqslant 2^{r-1}+1
, and let
T,T^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
,
{|}T{|}=r
denote two local constraints. Suppose that
T\cap T^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
. Then, with probability
1
over
M
, for any state
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{T,M}(\psi)={\mathcal{E}}_{T^{\prime},M}(\psi)=0
, it must be the case that for any
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
that
\psi_{z}=0
.

Remark 4.7
.

Note that when a predicate matrix
M
satisfies the above condition, we say that
M
is
generic
.

Proof.

We suppose WLOG that
R=2^{r-1}+1
, as we shall see below that having higher rank introduces
more constraints
on the kernel, which only makes the proof simpler.

Indeed, let us consider two terms
T
and
T^{\prime}
such that
T\cap T^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
. We let
U=T\cup T^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
denote the union of these two terms, and observe that
{|}U{|}\leqslant 2r-1
. For now, we focus on the case where
{|}U{|}=2r-1
(so
T
and
T^{\prime}
intersect in exactly one qubit).

Now, let
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
be any state such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{T,M}(\psi)={\mathcal{E}}_{T^{\prime},M}(\psi)=0
. In particular, this means that for any choice of
y\in\{0,1\}^{[n]-U}
, it must be that

\langle\psi_{y},M_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{U-T}\psi_{y}\rangle=\langle\psi_{y},M_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{U-T^{\prime}}\psi_{y}\rangle=0,

where
\psi_{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{{|}U{|}}}
is the resulting “sub-vector” of
\psi
which is achieved by keeping only those indices of
\psi
which equal
y
in the positions
[n]-U
(see
Definition

2.1
). We will show that, in fact, for every
y^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{U}
that
\psi_{y\circ y^{\prime}}=0
, thereby showing that every entry of
\psi
is
0
.

To see this, recall that
\mathrm{span}(M)=\mathrm{span}(v_{1},\dots v_{R})
, where each
v_{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}}
is chosen at
random
. Thus, a single constraint of the form

\langle\psi_{y},M_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{U-T}\psi_{y}\rangle=0

is imposing several constraints; indeed, for each choice of
\widehat{y}\in\{0,1\}^{U-T}
, it must be the case that
\langle\psi_{y\circ\widehat{y}},M\psi_{y\circ\widehat{y}}\rangle=0
, which in turn means that
\langle\psi_{y\circ\widehat{y}},v_{j}\rangle=0
, for every
j\in[R]
. We denote this set of constraints by
A_{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{(2^{{|}U{|}-T}\cdot R)\times 2^{{|}U{|}}}
. The fact that all these linear constraints must equal
0
implies that
A_{T}\psi_{y}=0
.

Likewise, we derive analogous constraints involving
T^{\prime}
, and denote their constraint matrix by
A_{T^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{C}^{(2^{{|}U{|}-T}\cdot R)\times 2^{{|}U{|}}}
. Thus, our set of constraints is given by
A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{T}\\
A_{T^{\prime}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\cdot(2^{{|}U{|}-T}\cdot R)\times 2^{{|}U{|}}}

Recall that
\psi_{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{{|}U{|}}}
, while the number of linear equations that we have is

2\cdot R\cdot 2^{{|}U{|}-r}\geqslant(2^{r-1}+1)\cdot 2^{{|}U{|}-r+1}>2^{{|}U{|}}.

Our goal now will be to show that, with high probability over the randomness of the vectors
v_{1},\dots v_{R}
, that this collection of constraints
A
has rank
2^{{|}U{|}}
. To do this, we first show that
there exists
a choice of
v_{1},\dots v_{R}
such that
A
has rank
2^{{|}U{|}}
:

Claim 4.8
.

Let
T,T^{\prime}
be such that
{|}T\cap T^{\prime}{|}=1
. Then, letting
A\in\mathbb{C}^{2\cdot(2^{{|}U{|}-T}\cdot R)\times 2^{{|}U{|}}}
denote the constraint matrix above, there exists a choice of
v_{1},\dots v_{R}
such that
A
has rank
2^{{|}U{|}}
.

Proof of
4.8
.

For ease of notation, we assume WLOG that
T=\{1,2,3,\dots r\}
and that
T^{\prime}=\{1,r+1,r+2,\dots 2r-1\}
. To define our vectors
v_{i}:i\in[R]
, we index the entries of
v_{i}
as a tuple
(a,u)\in\{0,1\}^{r}
where
a\in\{0,1\}
and
u\in\{0,1\}^{r-1}
, using
(v_{i})_{(a,\cdot)}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r-1}}
to refer to the vector which is achieved by considering only indices which start with symbol
a
. Likewise, for a value of
u\in\{0,1\}^{r-1}
we let
e_{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r-1}}
denote the standard basis vector which has a
1
only in coordinate
u
. Equivalently, for an integer
\ell\in 2^{r-1},
we use
e_{\ell}
to refer to
e_{u}
where
u
is the binary representation of
\ell
.

Under this notation we have:

(1)

(v_{1})_{(0,\cdot)}=e_{0}
and
(v_{1})_{(1,\cdot)}=0
.

(2)

For
i\in\{2,3,\dots,R-1\}
,
(v_{i})_{(0,\cdot)}=e_{i-1}
and
(v_{i})_{(1,\cdot)}=e_{i-2}
.

(3)

(v_{R})_{(0,\cdot)}=0
and
(v_{R})_{1,\cdot}=e_{R-2}

To show that
A
has full rank, we will show that
\ker(A)=0
. So, for any vector
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{{|}U{|}}}
, we construct two matrices
M_{0},M_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r-1}\times 2^{r-1}}
. The entries of
M_{0},M_{1}
are indexed by bit strings of length
r-1
, where
M_{0}(a,b)=\psi_{0\circ a\circ b}
and
M_{1}({a,b})=\psi_{1\circ a\circ b}
. For the vectors
v_{i}:i\in[R]
, and the term
T
, the requirement that for each choice of
\widehat{y}\in\{0,1\}^{U-T}

\langle\psi_{\widehat{y}},v_{i}\rangle=0
is equivalent to enforcing that for each column
b\in\{0,1\}^{r-1}
:

(1)

M_{0}(0,b)=0
.

(2)

M_{0}(\ell,b)+M_{1}(\ell-1,b)=0
for
\ell\in\{1,2,\dots R-2\}
. Equivalently,
M_{0}(\ell,b)=-M_{1}(\ell-1,b)
.

(3)

M_{1}(R-2,b)=0
.

Likewise, the constraints from the term
T^{\prime}
imply that, for every row
a\in\{0,1\}^{r-1}
that:

(1)

M_{0}(a,0)=0
.

(2)

M_{0}(a,\ell)+M_{1}(a,\ell-1)=0
for
\ell\in\{1,2,\dots R-2\}
. Equivalently,
M_{0}(a,\ell)=-M_{1}(a,\ell-1)
.

(3)

M_{1}(a,R-2)=0
.

Importantly, together the above relations imply that, for any
a\in\{0,1,\dots 2^{r-1}-1\}
and
b\in\{0,1,\dots 2^{r-1}-1\}
that
M_{1}(a,b)=-M_{0}(a+1,b)
and that
M_{1}(a,b)=-M_{0}(a,b+1)
. Together, this means that
M_{0}(a,b+1)=M_{0}(a+1,b)
, i.e., that the diagonals of
M_{0}
must all be equal.

Finally, we then see that the conditions above imply that, in
M_{0}
, all entries of the form
M(a,0)=M(0,b)=0
, and likewise in
M_{1}
that
M_{1}(a,R-2)=M_{1}(R-2,b)=0
(the right and bottom sides of the matrix
M_{1}
). This, together with the propagation condition, then imply that
M_{0}=M_{1}=0
.

Because the only possible choice of
\psi
such that
M_{0},M_{1}
satisfy the conditions imposed by the constraints
\langle\psi_{\widehat{y}},v_{i}\rangle=0
is
\psi=0^{2^{{|}U{|}}}
, it must be the case that the constraint matrix
A
is rank
2^{{|}U{|}}
(otherwise it would have a non-trivial nullspace). This concludes the claim.
∎

From
4.8
, we know that there exists a collection of vectors
v_{1},\dots v_{R}
such that the constraint matrix
A
has rank
2^{{|}U{|}}
. In particular, for this choice of vectors
v_{1},\dots v_{R}
, there must be some
2^{{|}U{|}}\times 2^{{|}U{|}}
minor of
A
which has non-zero determinant. We denote this minor by
A_{L_{1}\times\left[2^{{|}U{|}}\right]}
, where
L_{1}\subseteq[2\cdot R\cdot 2^{{|}U{|}-r}]
,
{|}L_{1}{|}=2^{{|}U{|}}
.

Now, we can observe that
\det(A_{L_{1}\times\left[2^{{|}U{|}}\right]})
is a polynomial in the entries of the vectors
v_{1},\dots v_{R}
(since each entry in
A
is simply an entry from one of these vectors). The above shows that, as a polynomial,

\det(A_{L_{1}\times\left[2^{{|}U{|}}\right]})\neq 0.

Now, we are free to invoke
2.18
, as each entry of the vectors
v_{i}
is sampled uniformly at random from a continuous distribution. This implies that with probability
1
over the vectors
v_{1},\dots v_{R}
that
\det(A_{L_{1}\times\left[2^{{|}U{|}}\right]})\neq 0
, and so
\operatorname{rank}(A)=2^{{|}U{|}}
, and
\ker(A)=0
. Thus, every entry of
\psi
must be
0
.

Finally, it remains to consider the case when
{|}T\cap T^{\prime}{|}>1
. We let
U=T\cup T^{\prime}
, we let
\ell=T\cap T^{\prime}
, and we assume WLOG that
T=\{1,2,3,\dots\ell,\ell+1,\dots r\}
and
T^{\prime}=\{1,2,\dots\ell,r+\ell,\dots 2r-1\}
.

In this case, as before, we know that for any choice of
y\in\{0,1\}^{[n]-U}
, our requirement that
\psi
gives
0
energy enforces that

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{y},M_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{U-T}\psi_{y}\rangle=\langle\psi_{y},M_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{U-T^{\prime}}\psi_{y}\rangle=0,

(7)

where
\psi_{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{{|}U{|}}}
. For clarity, we let
\phi=\psi_{y}
.

Now, suppose for the sake of contradiction that there existed a non-zero vector
\phi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{{|}U{|}}}
such that
Eq.

7
holds. We now lift
\phi
to a new vector
\widehat{\phi}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{2r-1}}
, such that, for any entry of
\widehat{\phi}
, indexed by
b\in\{0,1\}^{2r-1}
, we have:

\widehat{\phi}_{b}=\begin{cases}0\text{ if }\exists i\in\{2,3,4,\dots\ell\}:b_{i}\neq b_{i+r-1}.\\
\phi_{b_{1:r}\circ b_{r+\ell:2r-1}}.\end{cases}

At the same time, we consider a different Hamiltonian, with constraints
S=\{1,2,3,\dots r\}
and
S^{\prime}=\{1,r+1,r+2,\dots 2r-1\}
. The key observation is that, by construction,

\langle\phi,M_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{U-T}\phi\rangle=\langle\widehat{\phi},M_{S}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[2r-1]-S}\widehat{\phi}\rangle

and

\langle\phi,M_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{U-T^{\prime}}\phi\rangle=\langle\widehat{\phi},M_{S^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[2r-1]-S^{\prime}}\widehat{\phi}\rangle.

But, for these terms
S,S^{\prime}
, we see that
{|}S\cap S^{\prime}{|}=1
, and thus the only state
\widehat{\phi}
which gives both
S
and
S^{\prime}
zero energy is when every entry of
\widehat{\phi}
is
0
. But, this then implies that every entry of
\phi
itself is also
0
. This concludes the claim.
∎

Note that by repeating the above argument, we can also prove a version of
Lemma

4.6
where
T
and
T^{\prime}
have
different
random operators
M,M^{\prime}
. The same argument goes through as we can re-use the proof from
Lemma

4.6
to show that when
M=M^{\prime}
, the determinant polynomial is non-zero (and thus, when
M,M^{\prime}
are distinct, the determinant polynomial is still non-zero). Thus, we formally get the following corollary:

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
M,M^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
be random predicate matrices of rank
R\geqslant 2^{r-1}+1
, and let
T,T^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
,
{|}T{|}=r
denote two local constraints. Suppose that
T\cap T^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
. Then, with probability
1
over
M,M^{\prime}
, for any state
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{T,M}(\psi)={\mathcal{E}}_{T^{\prime},M}(\psi)=0
, it must be the case that for any
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
that
\psi_{z}=0
.

Now, as a consequence of
Lemma

4.6
, we also have the following corollary:

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
be a random predicate matrix of rank
R\geqslant 2^{r-1}+1
, and let
T,T^{\prime},T^{\prime\prime}\subseteq[n]
,
{|}T{|}={|}T^{\prime}{|}=r
denote two
r
-local constraints. Suppose that
T\cap T^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
. Then, with probability
1-o_{r}(1)
over
M
,

\lambda_{\min}(M{|}_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T}+M{|}_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T^{\prime}})\geqslant\Omega_{r}(1),

and

\lambda_{\max}(M{|}_{T^{\prime\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T^{\prime\prime}})\leqslant O_{r}(1).

Proof.

To see the second point above, we can simply observe that

\lambda_{\max}(M{|}_{T^{\prime\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T^{\prime\prime}})=\lambda_{\max}(M)\leqslant O_{r}(1),

by our assumption on boundedness (see
Definition

2.19
,
Remark

4.1
).

For the first point, we let
U=T\cup T^{\prime}
. We observe that we can view
M{|}_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T}+M{|}_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T^{\prime}}
as a local operator on
U=T\cup T^{\prime}
. Thus, there is a matrix
\widehat{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{{|}U{|}}\times 2^{{|}U{|}}}
such that

M{|}_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T}+M{|}_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T^{\prime}}=\widehat{M}_{U}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-U}.

Indeed, in this formulation, we see that
\widehat{M}=M{|}_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{U-T}+M{|}_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{U-T^{\prime}}
. Thus the eigenvalues of
\widehat{M}
depend only on the entries of
M
, which in turn depends only on the distribution from which
M
is sampled and the parameter
r
. Likewise, via
Lemma

4.6
, we know that all eigenvalues of
\widehat{M}=M{|}_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{U-T}+M{|}_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{U-T^{\prime}}
are positive. Thus, we can see that, with probability (say)
1-o_{r}(1)
,

\lambda_{\min}(M{|}_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T}+M{|}_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T^{\prime}})\geqslant\Omega_{r}(1),

as this matrix
\widehat{M}
is drawn from a distribution whose parameters depend only on
r
. Importantly, as discussed in
Remark

4.1
, the parameters of this distribution are
independent
of
n
.
∎

Invoking the same argument (but instead using
Corollary

4.9
), we can also show the following:

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
M,M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
be random predicate matrices of rank
R\geqslant 2^{r-1}+1
, and let
T,T^{\prime},T^{\prime\prime}\subseteq[n]
,
{|}T{|}={|}T^{\prime}{|}=r
denote three
r
-local constraints, using
M,M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime}
respectively. Then:

1.

With probability
1
over
M^{\prime\prime}
,

\lambda_{\max}(M^{\prime\prime}{|}_{T^{\prime\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T^{\prime\prime}})\leqslant O_{r}(1).

2.

If
T\cap T^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
, then with probability
\geqslant 1/2
over
M,M^{\prime}
,

\lambda_{\min}(M{|}_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T}+M^{\prime}{|}_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T^{\prime}})\geqslant\Omega_{r}(1).

Proof.

The first point follows from our assumption on the boundedness of our matrices (see
Definition

2.19
).

For the second point, we can again observe that, letting
U=T\cup T^{\prime}
, we can write

M{|}_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T}+M^{\prime}{|}_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T^{\prime}}=\widehat{M}{|}_{U}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-U}.

Here,
U\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{O(r)}\times 2^{O(r)}}
, and
U
’s entries depend only on the entries of
M^{\prime},M
. From
Corollary

4.9
, we know that with probability
1
over
M,M^{\prime}
that
\lambda_{\min}(M{|}_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T}+M^{\prime}{|}_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T^{\prime}})>0
.

Importantly, because the distribution from which
M,M^{\prime}
are drawn is only parameterized by
r
(see
Remark

4.1
), the statistics of this distribution also depend only on
r
. Thus, we can then say that there exists some constant
\gamma_{r}\geqslant\Omega_{r}(1)
such that with probability
1/2
,

\lambda_{\min}(M{|}_{T}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T}+M^{\prime}{|}_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-T^{\prime}})=\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{M})\geqslant\gamma_{r}\geqslant\Omega_{r}(1).

∎

In the above, we use
\Omega_{r}(\cdot)
and
O_{r}(\cdot)
to hide (potentially large) constant dependences on a function of
r
. Note that it may be slightly counter-intuitive in
Corollary

4.11
that we only use a weak property that with probability
1/2
the smallest eigenvalue of the sum of terms is
\Omega_{r}(1)
. As we shall we see later, this is because when the number of terms increases (scaling with
n
, the number of qubits, independent of
r
), it is possible that certain “low probability” tail events may occur which could yield eigenvalues that scale with
n
as opposed to
r
.
Corollary

4.11
provides a very basic, weak guarantee which nevertheless still suffices for our sparsifiers by using parameters of the distribution of predicate matrices that are
independent
of
n
.

4.2
Building Sparsifiers

Sparsifiers for the Shared Predicate Case

With
Corollary

4.10
now established, we proceed to our algorithm for building sparsifiers. Roughly speaking, the intuition is that once there are sufficiently many terms which operate on
intersecting
sets of qubits, then this naturally provides a stronger lower bound on the minimum eigenvalue of the entire Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
. Towards proving this intuition formally, we first make the following claim:

Claim 4.12
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hamiltonian with terms
H_{i}=(M,T_{i}):i\in[m]
. Provided
m\geqslant 4n
, then there must exist
\geqslant\frac{m}{4}
disjoint pairs of terms
(a_{j},b_{j}):j\in[\frac{m}{2}],a_{j},b_{j}\in[m]
such that
T_{a_{j}}\cap T_{b_{j}}\neq\varnothing
.

Proof.

Indeed, consider the following basic algorithm: let
S=[m]
denote the starting set of all terms in the Hamiltonian, and let
j=1
. Now, while there exists a pair of disjoint terms
(a_{j},b_{j}),a_{j},b_{j}\in[m]
such that
T_{a_{j}}\cap T_{b_{j}}\neq\varnothing
, we remove this pair of terms (i.e.,
S\leftarrow S\setminus\{a_{j},b_{j}\}
), and increment
j
by
1
.

We claim that in this process, the final value of
j
will be at least
\frac{m}{2}+1
(meaning that at least
\frac{m}{2}
many pairs of intersecting terms are identified). Indeed, to see why, suppose that
{|}S{|}>\frac{n}{r}
. Then, we can see that the total sum of the degrees of the underlying qubits is
>\frac{n}{r}\cdot r=n
. Thus, there must be some qubit whose degree is
\geqslant 2
, which means that there must be some two terms that intersect.

Thus, in essence, while
{|}S{|}>\frac{n}{r}
, we can find a pair of intersecting terms. Thus, we can identify

\geqslant\frac{m-\frac{n}{r}}{2}\geqslant\frac{m}{4}

many intersecting terms in the Hamiltonian.
∎

With the above claim, we now make the following definition of
importance scores
:

Definition 4.13
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hamiltonian with terms
H_{i}=(M_{i},T_{i}):i\in[m]
. For each term
i\in[m]
, we define the
importance score
of
H_{i}
as:

\mathrm{Importance}(H_{i})=\max_{\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}}\frac{\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}{|}\psi\rangle}.

Importantly, we have the following claim regarding importance scores:

Claim 4.14
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hamiltonian with
r
-local terms
H_{i}=(M_{i},T_{i}):i\in[m]
on
n
qubits, and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Suppose that for every term
i\in[m]
,

\mathrm{Importance}(H_{i})\leqslant p.

Let
C=\frac{100n}{\varepsilon^{2}}
. Now, let
L\subseteq[m]
be a random sample of terms, where
i
is sampled in
L
independently with probability
p\cdot C
. Then, with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
over the sample
L

that

Furthermore, with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
, the number of sampled terms is bounded by
O(\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot m\cdot p\cdot n).

Proof.

We use Matrix Chernoff to prove this (see
2.17
). For each

, we let

Y_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{p\cdot C}\cdot H_{i}\text{ w.p. }p\cdot C\\
0\quad\quad\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Letting

, we can then immediately see that
Z=\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{i}=\mathcal{H}
. Likewise, because

\mathrm{Importance}(H_{i})\leqslant p,

we then also know that for every

that

\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle\leqslant p\cdot\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}{|}\psi\rangle=p\cdot\langle\psi{|}Z{|}\psi\rangle.

Because
Y_{i}\preccurlyeq\frac{1}{p\cdot C}\cdot H_{i}
, we then obtain that

Y_{i}\preccurlyeq\frac{1}{p\cdot C}\cdot p\cdot Z=\frac{1}{C}\cdot Z.

Thus, by using the statement of
2.17
, we see that with probability
1-2\cdot 2^{n}\cdot 2^{-\varepsilon/3C}=1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
by our choice of
C
, that

(1-\varepsilon)Z\preccurlyeq\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)Z.

Finally, we can see that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}
has the same distribution as
\sum_{i\in L}\frac{1}{p\cdot C}\cdot\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle
, by construction.

Thus, we obtain that with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(m)}
over the sample
L
that

\sum_{i\in L}\frac{1}{p\cdot C}\cdot\langle\psi{|}H_{i}{|}\psi\rangle\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}{|}\psi\rangle,

as we desire.

The bound on the number of sampled terms follows from a simple Chernoff bound.

4.3
Separations Between Sparsifiability of Classical and Quantum Systems

So far, we have showed that random arity-
r
Hamiltonians of rank at least

have near-quadratic size sparsifiers. Now we show that this result is in sharp contrast to the classical setting–the case in which our

\{0,1\}

Proposition

Theorem 4.19
.

Let
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{r}
be a uniformly random predicate with cardinality

\alpha\in(0,1/2)
are constants independent of

(where

r

\operatorname{SPR}(R,n,\varepsilon)\geqslant\Omega_{r}(n^{\lceil\log_{2}r-\log_{2}\log_{2}(1/\alpha)-2\rceil}).

.

If

like in

can be taken to be

, so the lower bound is at least
\Omega_{r}(n^{\lceil\log_{2}r-3\rceil})

To build up to

We give a summary of the projections framework for Boolean CSPs. Given a pool of Boolean variables

A constant

Proof.

By
5.10
, we see that the number of recursive calls made in
Algorithm

3
is bounded by
O_{r}(\log(n))
.

In each such recursive call, the support size of the sparsifier increases by exactly
\bigcup_{F\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}F
. We know that any dominating cover has size
\leqslant O(n)
, and we store
\leqslant 100\varepsilon^{-2}C^{2}\cdot n
many such dominating covers. Thus, in each recursive call, the size of the sparsifier increases by
O(\varepsilon^{-2}C^{2}n^{2})
, and taking the union over all of the recursive calls, the total size of the sparsifier is bounded by
O_{r}(\varepsilon^{-2}C^{2}n^{2}\log(n))
.
∎

5.3.1
Sparsifier Correctness

In this section, we show that
Algorithm

3
actually produces
good sparsifiers
. Towards this end, we first prove the following:

Claim 5.12
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\{(T_{i},M_{i}):{i\in[m]}\}
be a nullity
1
Hamiltonian over
n
qubits. Suppose
\mathcal{M}:=\{M_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
is
(C,2r)
-bounded. Then, letting
\mathrm{DominatingCovers}
denote a disjoint collection of
100\varepsilon^{-2}C^{2}\cdot n
many spanning forests, letting
S
be a uniformly random sample (at rate
1/2
) of
[m]-\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}F
, letting
w:\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D\rightarrow\{1\}
and
w^{\prime}:S\rightarrow\{2\}
,
then with probability
1-2^{-n}
over the sample
S
, it is the case that
w\sqcup w^{\prime}
is an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

First, for each term
(T_{i},M_{i}):i\in\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D
, we create multiple random variables, which we denote by
Y_{i,1},\dots Y_{i,100\varepsilon^{-2}C^{2}\cdot n}
, where each
Y_{i,j}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(M_{i})_{T_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{i}}}}{100C^{2}\cdot n}
with probability
1
.

Otherwise, for the terms
(T_{i},M_{i}):i\in[m]-\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D
, we define the random variables
Y_{i}=2\cdot(M_{i})_{T_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{i}}}
with probability
1/2
, and otherwise is
0
.

We can observe that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i\in\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D}\sum_{j\in[100\varepsilon^{-2}C^{2}\cdot n]}Y_{i,j}+\sum_{i\in[m]-\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D}Y_{i}\right]=\sum_{i\in[m]}(M_{i})_{T_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{i}}}=Z.

Next, we now bound the
importance
of each of our random variables:

(1)

For each
Y_{i,j}
for
i\in\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D
and
j\in[100\varepsilon^{-2}C^{2}\cdot n]
, we see that

Y_{i,j}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(M_{i})_{T_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{i}}}}{100C^{2}\cdot n}\preccurlyeq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{100C^{2}\cdot n}\cdot Z,

as
Z
contains the term
(M_{i})_{T_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{i}}}
.

(2)

Next, we consider the terms
Y_{i}
for
i\in[m]-\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D
. Here, we see that

Z\succcurlyeq\sum_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}\sum_{i\in D}(M_{i})_{T_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{i}}}.

Importantly, because each
D
is a dominating cover, this means that for each term
T_{i}:i\in[m]-\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D
, by
Lemma

5.9
,

\displaystyle(M_{i})_{T_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T}_{i}}\preccurlyeq O\left(C^{2}\cdot\sum_{(M_{j},T_{j})\in D}(M_{j})_{T_{j}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{j}}}\right).

(12)

Thus, we see that

Z\succcurlyeq\sum_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}\sum_{(M_{j},T_{j})\in D}(M_{j})_{T_{j}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{j}}}\succcurlyeq{|}\mathrm{DominatingCovers}{|}\cdot\frac{1}{C^{2}}\cdot(M_{i})_{T_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{i}}}

=\frac{100n}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot(2(M_{i})_{T_{i}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T_{i}}})=\frac{100n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot Y_{i}.

In particular, in the language of
2.17
, we can set the constant
R=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{100n}
. Thus,
2.17
guarantees that with probability
1-2\cdot(2^{n}\cdot\exp(-100n\varepsilon^{2}/3\varepsilon^{2}))\geqslant 1-2^{-n}
that

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)Z\preccurlyeq\sum_{i\in\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D}\sum_{j\in[100\varepsilon^{-2}C^{2}\cdot n]}Y_{i,j}+\sum_{i\in[m]-\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D}Y_{i}\preccurlyeq(1+\varepsilon)Z.

(13)

In particular, for
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
, we can see that
\langle\psi,Z\psi\rangle={\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\psi)
.

Next, we can see that our sampling procedure over the terms
Y_{i}
is exactly equivalent to giving weight
1
to all terms in
\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D
, and sampling the remaining terms at rate
1/2
and giving weight
2
to those that are sampled.

Thus, letting
S
be a uniformly random sample (at rate
1/2
) of
[m]-\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D
, letting
w:\bigcup_{D\in\mathrm{DominatingCovers}}D\rightarrow\{1\}
and
w^{\prime}:S\rightarrow\{2\}
,
then with probability
\geqslant 1-2^{-n}
over the sample
S
, it is the case that
w\sqcup w^{\prime}
is an
\varepsilon
-sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}
, as
Eq.

13
implies that

{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},w}(\psi)\in[1\pm\varepsilon]{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\psi)\,,

as desired.
∎

The preceding claim shows that
in one single round of sampling
, the accuracy of the sparsifier is within the tolerable
(1\pm\varepsilon)
regime. However, we then repeat this procedure, sparsifying part of the sparsifier.

Thus, we get the following claim:

Claim 5.13
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\{(T_{i},M_{i}):{i\in[m]}\}
be a nullity
1
Hamiltonian over
n
qubits. Then, letting
w
denote the returned sparsifier from
Algorithm

3
, it is the case that, for every
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}

{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},w}(\psi)\in(1\pm\varepsilon)^{100r\log(n)}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\psi).

Proof.

We consider the execution of
Algorithm

3
. We let
D^{(1)}
denote the collection of terms in the dominating covers in round
1
, and let
S^{(1)}
denote the resulting sample of the remaining terms in round
1
. In general, we will consider this across all iterations of the above procedure, using
D^{(\ell)}
and
S^{(\ell)}
to denote the recovered forests and sampled terms from round
\ell
.

Likewise, we let
w^{(\ell)}
denote the weight function which gives weight
2^{j}
to terms in
D^{(j)}
for
j\in[\ell]
, and weight
2^{\ell+1}
to terms in
S^{(\ell)}
(and weight
0
to all other terms). We also let
\widehat{w}^{(\ell)}
denote the weight function which gives weight
1
to every term in
F^{(\ell)}
and weight
2
to every term in
S^{(\ell)}
(and weight
0
to every other term).

If we let
\mathbf{1}[A]
for
A\subseteq[m]
denote the weight function which gives weight
1
to terms with index in
A
, then we can interpret
5.12
as saying that (with probability
1-2^{-n}
), for every
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
,

{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\widehat{w}^{(\ell)}}(\psi)\in(1\pm\varepsilon){\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\mathbf{1}[S^{(\ell-1)}]}(\psi).

Now, we claim that, by induction,

{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},w^{(\ell)}}(\psi)\in(1\pm\varepsilon)^{\ell}{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\psi).

Indeed, the base case follows trivially via
5.12
. For the inductive step, let us suppose the hypothesis holds for
\ell-1
. Then, we know that

{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},w^{(\ell-1)}}(\psi)\in(1\pm\varepsilon)^{\ell-1}{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\psi).

We can now write

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},w^{(\ell)}}(\psi)=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}2^{j}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\mathbf{1}[F^{(j)}]}(\psi)+\left(2^{\ell}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\mathbf{1}[F^{(\ell)}]}(\psi)+2^{\ell+1}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\mathbf{1}[S^{(\ell)}]}(\psi)\right).

(14)

By
5.12
, we know that

\displaystyle\left(2^{\ell}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\mathbf{1}[F^{(\ell)}]}(\psi)+2^{\ell+1}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\mathbf{1}[S^{(\ell)}]}(\psi)\right)=2^{\ell}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\widehat{w}^{(\ell}}(\psi)\in 2^{\ell}\cdot(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\mathbf{1}[S^{(\ell-1)}]}(\psi).

(15)

Plugging
Eq.

15
into
Eq.

14
, we see that

{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},w^{(\ell)}}(\psi)\in\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}2^{j}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\mathbf{1}[F^{(j)}]}(\psi)+2^{\ell}\cdot(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\mathbf{1}[S^{(\ell-1)}]}(\psi)

\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}2^{j}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\mathbf{1}[F^{(j)}]}(\psi)+2^{\ell}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},\mathbf{1}[S^{(\ell-1)}]}(\psi)\right)\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},w^{(\ell-1)}}(\psi)\in(1\pm\varepsilon)^{\ell}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\psi),

as we desire.

Plugging in
5.10
(and taking a union bound over all of the failure probabilities of the sparsification rounds), we see that with probability
1-\frac{1}{n^{10}}
, that for every
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
,

{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H},w}(\psi)\in(1\pm\varepsilon)^{100r\log(n)}\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\psi),

as we desire.
∎

With these claims in place, we can now prove our main theorem.

Proof of
Theorem

5.1
.

The algorithm for computing the sparsifier is exactly
Algorithm

3
. By
Algorithm

2
and
5.10
, we can see that
Algorithm

3
runs in time
\mathrm{poly}(m,n)
.

Now, to get a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
sparsifier, we simply invoke
Algorithm

3
with accuracy parameter
\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{200r\log(n)}
. From
5.13
, this then ensures that with high probability, the resulting weight function does constitute a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}
. Lastly, the size bound follows from
5.11
invoked with
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/200r\log(n)
. This concludes the theorem.
∎

6
Sparsifying Quantum MAX-CSPs

6.1
Sparsifying Quantum MAX-CUT

Recall that an instance of quantum MAX-CUT is specified by a
graph

G=(V,E,w)
on
n
vertices (qubits), where each edge
e=(u,v)\in E
corresponds with a Hamiltonian term operating on qubits
u,v
. In quantum MAX-CUT, each Hamiltonian term is of the form
M{|}_{u,v}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-\{u,v\}}
, where

\displaystyle M=\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname{Id}{|}_{1}\otimes\operatorname{Id}{|}_{2}-X{|}_{1}\otimes X{|}_{2}-Y{|}_{1}\otimes Y{|}_{2}-Z{|}_{1}\otimes Z{|}_{2}\right)=vv^{\dagger},

(16)

where
v=\lvert 01\rangle-\lvert 10\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{2}}
.

Going forward, we use our hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
to denote

\mathcal{H}(G)=\sum_{e=(u,v)\in E}w(e)\cdot M{|}_{\{u,v\}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}{|}_{[n]-\{u,v\}}.

We use
\mathrm{OPT}(G)
to denote the
maximum energy
of the above expression, across all states. I.e.,

\mathrm{OPT}(G)=\max_{\lvert\psi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}:\|\psi\|_{2}=1}\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}{|}\psi\rangle.

We have the following proposition:

Proposition 6.1

(See, for instance,
[
33
]
)
.

\mathrm{OPT}(G)\geqslant\frac{m}{16}
, where
m
is the total sum of edge weights in
G
.

Importantly, we can make the following observation:

\displaystyle\widehat{M}:=M+\operatorname{Id}\succcurlyeq\operatorname{Id},

(17)

where now each eigenvalue in
\widehat{M}
is at least
1
.

Now, we can define

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}(G)=\sum_{e=(u,v)\in E}(\widehat{M}){|}_{\{u,v\}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}{|}_{[n]-\{u,v\}}.

We refer to each term in the above expression as
\widehat{H}_{e}
.
We now claim the following:

Claim 6.2
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{e=(u,v)\in E}w(e)\cdot(\widehat{H}_{e}\otimes\operatorname{Id}{|}_{[n]-\{u,v\}})
over
n
qubits. Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a subset
L\subseteq E
,
{|}L{|}\leqslant O(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
, along with new weights
\widetilde{w}:L\rightarrow R_{\geqslant 0}
such that for every state
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
,

\sum_{e=(u,v)\in L}\widetilde{w}(e)\cdot\langle\psi{|}\widehat{H}_{e}{|}\psi\rangle\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\langle\psi{|}\widehat{\mathcal{H}}{|}\psi\rangle.

Moreover, this set
L
can be found in time polynomial in
m,n
, and
\sum_{e\in L}\widetilde{w}(e)=\sum_{e\in E}w(e)
.

Proof.

Again, we use the notion of the
importance
of each term. We see that

\mathrm{Importance}(\widehat{H}_{e})=\max_{\lvert\psi\rangle}\frac{\langle\psi{|}\widehat{H}_{e}{|}\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi{|}\widehat{\mathcal{H}}{|}\psi\rangle}\leqslant\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\widehat{H}_{e})}{\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{\mathcal{H}})}=p_{e}\leqslant O(w_{e}/m),

where we have used that, by
Eq.

17
,
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}\succcurlyeq m\cdot\operatorname{Id}
.

By
4.14
, we then see that if we sample each term with probability
\min(1,p_{e}\cdot\frac{100n}{\varepsilon^{2}})
, and give weight to each sampled term which is
\frac{w_{e}}{\min(1,p_{e}\cdot\frac{100n}{\varepsilon^{2}})}
, then with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
, the resulting set of terms
L
, with weights
\widetilde{w}
, is a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}
. Likewise, by a simple Chernoff bound, the total weight of sampled terms
{|}L{|}
is
\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot m
.

So, to derive the final condition in the claim statement, we simply run the above procedure with
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/10
. This finds
L,\widetilde{w}
which is a
(1\pm\varepsilon^{\prime})
sparsifier such that
\sum_{e\in L}\widetilde{w}(e)\in(1\pm\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot m
. We can now re-scale the weights by a constant factor
\gamma
such that
\sum_{e\in L}\widetilde{w}(e)=m
, while only shifting the energy by a factor of
(1\pm\varepsilon^{\prime})
. Thus, our resulting sparsifier has accuracy
(1\pm\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}\in(1\pm\varepsilon)
, as we desire.∎

Finally, we are now able to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be an instance of quantum MAX-CUT with corresponding hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
. Then, for any parameter
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a set
L\subseteq E
, along with weights
\widetilde{w}:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
such that, letting
\widetilde{G}=(V,L,\widetilde{w})
, for any state
\lvert\psi\rangle
which satisfies

\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}(\widetilde{G}),

then it must also be the case that

\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}(G){|}\psi\rangle\geqslant(1-2\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}(G).

Moreover, this sparsifier
L,\widetilde{w}
can be found efficiently, in time polynomial in
m,n
.

Proof.

We let the set
L
and weights
\widetilde{w}
be exactly those weights returned by
6.2
when invoked with parameter
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/100
. The size of this set
L
already satisfies the conditions set forth above, and the algorithmic efficiency likewise follows.

All that remains is to establish the correctness.
For this, denoting
\widetilde{G}=(V,L,\widetilde{w})
, we see that for every state
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
that

\langle\psi{|}\widehat{H}(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle\in(1\pm\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}\widehat{H}(G){|}\psi\rangle.

Importantly, we know that

\displaystyle\widehat{H}(G)=H(G)+2m\cdot\operatorname{Id},\quad\quad\widehat{H}(\widetilde{G})\in H(\widetilde{G})+2m\cdot\operatorname{Id},

(18)

where we have used the fact that
\sum_{e\in L}\widetilde{w}(e)=\sum_{e\in E}w(e)
in
6.2
.
Plugging this into the above expression, we see that

\langle\psi{|}\widehat{H}(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle=\langle\psi{|}H(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle+2m.

Thus, we obtain that

(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}H(G){|}\psi\rangle+2m-2\varepsilon^{\prime}m\leqslant\langle\psi{|}H(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle+2m\leqslant(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}H(G){|}\psi\rangle+2m+2\varepsilon^{\prime}m.

Simplifying, this means that

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}H(G){|}\psi\rangle-2\varepsilon^{\prime}m\leqslant\langle\psi{|}H(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle\leqslant(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}H(G){|}\psi\rangle+2\varepsilon^{\prime}m.

(19)

Now, we have two cases:

1.

Consider any state
\lvert\psi\rangle
such that
\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle\geqslant\frac{m}{32}
. Then,
2\varepsilon^{\prime}m\leqslant 64\varepsilon^{\prime}\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle
.

Thus, by re-writing
Eq.

19
, we see that

(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}H(G){|}\psi\rangle\leqslant\langle\psi{|}H(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle(1+64\varepsilon^{\prime}),

and

\langle\psi{|}H(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle(1-64\varepsilon^{\prime})\leqslant(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}H(G){|}\psi\rangle.

Re-writing the above expressions, we obtain that

\langle\psi{|}H(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle\in(1\pm 66\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}H(G){|}\psi\rangle.

2.

Consider any state
\lvert\psi\rangle
such that
\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}(G){|}\psi\rangle\geqslant\frac{m}{32}
. Then,
2\varepsilon^{\prime}m\leqslant 64\varepsilon^{\prime}\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}(G){|}\psi\rangle
.

Thus, by re-writing
Eq.

19
, we see that

(1-65\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}H(G){|}\psi\rangle\leqslant\langle\psi{|}H(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle,

and

\langle\psi{|}H(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle\leqslant(1+65\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}H(G){|}\psi\rangle.

Together, the above expressions imply that

\langle\psi{|}H(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle\in(1\pm 66\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}H(G){|}\psi\rangle.

Now, we prove our desired theorem statement: indeed, by
Proposition

6.1
, we know that
\mathrm{OPT}(G)\geqslant\frac{m}{16}
. Thus, by the above expressions, it must also be the case that
\mathrm{OPT}(\widetilde{G})\geqslant\mathrm{OPT}(G)\cdot(1-66\varepsilon^{\prime})
. Now, let us consider any state
\lvert\psi\rangle
which satisfies

\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}(\widetilde{G}).

For this state
\lvert\psi\rangle
, we then know that

\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}(G)\cdot(1-66\varepsilon^{\prime})\geqslant\frac{m}{32}.

Again, using our multiplicative error bound in this regime, we then know that

\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}(G){|}\psi\rangle\geqslant\frac{1}{1+66\varepsilon^{\prime}}\cdot(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}(G)\cdot(1-66\varepsilon^{\prime}).

By choosing
\varepsilon^{\prime}
to be sufficiently small (say
\varepsilon/200
), we then obtain our desired bound that

\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}(G){|}\psi\rangle\geqslant(1-2\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}(G).

This concludes the theorem.
∎

In particular, observe that the sparsification procedure in
Theorem

6.3
is simply a uniformly random sample of the terms. This, in turn, is immediately implementable in insertion-only streams via reservoir sampling (see, for instance
[
40
]
).

In this insertion-only streaming setting, the algorithms operate on a stream of edges, where in each time step, a single new edge is inserted. The resulting graph (the accumulation of all the edges) is then denoted by
G
.

In this setting, we get the following corollary:

Corollary 6.4
.

There is an algorithm, which, for any
\varepsilon>0
, and for any stream of edges defined over a graph of
n
vertices, produces a set
L\subseteq E
,
{|}L{|}\leqslant O(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
, along with weights
\widetilde{w}:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
such that, for any state
\lvert\psi\rangle
which satisfies

\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}(\widetilde{G}){|}\psi\rangle\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}(\widetilde{G}),

then it must also be the case that

\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}(G){|}\psi\rangle\geqslant(1-2\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}(G).

Moreover, this sparsifier
L,\widetilde{w}
can be found efficiently, in time
\widetilde{O}(\max(m,n))
, and space
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
.

Remark 6.5
.

This settles the “information-theoretic” complexity of QMC in the insertion-only streaming setting, answering an open question of
[
33
]
. Indeed, one must only construct the aforementioned sparsifier of the underlying graph, and then solve QMC on this sparsifier. The total space complexity of the streaming algorithm for building this sparsifier is bounded by
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
(classical) bits of space.

6.2
Sparsifying Quantum MAX-CSP

The previous section can be generalized to
any
quantum MAX-CSP problem, provided it satisfies two conditions. In particular, we assume we are working with an
r
-local Hamiltonian with interaction hypergraph
E
:

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{e\in E}H_{e},

where each
H_{e}=M_{e}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{[n]-e}
, where
M_{e}
is a PSD matrix with maximum eigenvalue
O(1)
.

We use
\mathrm{OPT}(G)
to denote the
maximum energy
of the above expression, across all states. I.e.,

\mathrm{OPT}(G)=\max_{\lvert\psi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}:\|\psi\|_{2}=1}\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}{|}\psi\rangle.

Our first assumption is the following:

Proposition 6.6
.

\mathrm{OPT}(\mathcal{H})\geqslant\Omega(m)
, where
m
is the total number of terms in
\mathcal{H}
.

Importantly, we can make the following observation:

\displaystyle\widehat{M}_{e}:=M_{e}+\operatorname{Id}\succcurlyeq\operatorname{Id},

(20)

where now each eigenvalue in
\widehat{M}_{e}
is at least
1
.

Now, we can define

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}(G)=\sum_{e=(u,v)\in E}(\widehat{M}_{e})\otimes\operatorname{Id}{|}_{[n]-\{u,v\}}.

We refer to each term in the above expression as
\widehat{H}_{e}
.
We now claim the following:

Claim 6.7
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{e=(u,v)\in E}w(e)\cdot(\widehat{H}_{e}\otimes\operatorname{Id}{|}_{[n]-\{u,v\}})
over
n
qubits. Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a subset
L\subseteq E
,
{|}L{|}\leqslant O(\varepsilon^{-2}n)
, along with new weights
\widetilde{w}:L\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
such that for every state
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
,

\sum_{e=(u,v)\in L}\widetilde{w}(e)\cdot\langle\psi{|}\widehat{H}_{e}{|}\psi\rangle\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\langle\psi{|}\widehat{\mathcal{H}}{|}\psi\rangle.

Moreover, this set

can be found in time polynomial in
m,n
, and
\sum_{e\in L}\widetilde{w}(e)=m

Proof.

Again, we use the notion of the
importance
of each term. We see that

\mathrm{Importance}(\widehat{H}_{e})=\max_{\lvert\psi\rangle}\frac{\langle\psi{|}\widehat{H}_{e}{|}\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi{|}\widehat{\mathcal{H}}{|}\psi\rangle}\leqslant\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\widehat{H}_{e})}{\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{\mathcal{H}})}=p_{e}\leqslant O(w_{e}/m),

where we have used that, by
Eq.

20
,
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}\succcurlyeq m\cdot\operatorname{Id}
.

By
4.14
, we then see that if we sample each term with probability
\min(1,p_{e}\cdot\frac{100n}{\varepsilon^{2}})
, and give weight to each sampled term which is
\frac{w_{e}}{\min(1,p_{e}\cdot\frac{100n}{\varepsilon^{2}})}
, then with probability
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
, the resulting set of terms
L
, with weights
\widetilde{w}
, is a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
sparsifier of
\mathcal{H}
. Likewise, by a simple Chernoff bound, the total weight of sampled terms
{|}L{|}
is
\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot m
.

So, to derive the final condition in the claim statement, we simply run the above procedure with
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/10
. This finds
L,\widetilde{w}
which is a
(1\pm\varepsilon^{\prime})
sparsifier such that
\sum_{e\in L}\widetilde{w}(e)\in(1\pm\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot m
. We can now re-scale the weights by a constant factor
\gamma
such that
\sum_{e\in L}\widetilde{w}(e)=m
, while only shifting the energy by a factor of
(1\pm\varepsilon^{\prime})
. Thus, our resulting sparsifier has accuracy
(1\pm\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}\in(1\pm\varepsilon)
, as we desire.
∎

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an instance of quantum MAX-CSP with

\mathrm{OPT}(\mathcal{H})\geqslant\Omega(m)
. Then, for any parameter
\varepsilon>0

such that, letting

denote the instance

which satisfies

\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}{|}\psi\rangle\geqslant(1-2\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}(\mathcal{H}).

Moreover, this sparsifier

can be found efficiently, in time polynomial in

Proof.

We let the set

and weights

be exactly those weights returned by

already satisfies the conditions set forth above, and the algorithmic efficiency likewise follows.

All that remains is to establish the correctness.
For this, we see that for every state
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
that

\langle\psi{|}\widetilde{\widehat{\mathcal{H}}}{|}\psi\rangle\in(1\pm\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}\widehat{\mathcal{H}}{|}\psi\rangle.

Importantly, we know that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{H}+m\cdot\operatorname{Id},\quad\quad\widetilde{\widehat{\mathcal{H}}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}+2\cdot m\cdot\operatorname{Id},

(21)

where we have used the fact that

in

Plugging this into the above expression, we see that

Thus, we obtain that

(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}{|}\psi\rangle+2m-2\varepsilon^{\prime}m\leqslant\langle\psi{|}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}{|}\psi\rangle+2m\leqslant(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}{|}\psi\rangle+2m+2\varepsilon^{\prime}m.

Simplifying, this means that

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}{|}\psi\rangle-2\varepsilon^{\prime}m\leqslant\langle\psi{|}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}{|}\psi\rangle\leqslant(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot\langle\psi{|}\mathcal{H}{|}\psi\rangle+2\varepsilon^{\prime}m.

1.

\lvert\psi\rangle

Re-writing the above expressions, we obtain that

Now, we prove our desired theorem statement: indeed, by

Tensor Product Lower Bounds

Consider predicate matrices
M=M_{1}\otimes M_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes M_{r}

M_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
are non-zero PSD matrices for all

\mathcal{H}
is a
r
-partite
r
-uniform hypergraph with the parts being

Claim 7.1
.

Let
M=M_{1}\otimes M_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes M_{r}
be a predicate matrix where
M_{1},\ldots,M_{r}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
are non-zero PSD matrices that are
not

\mathcal{H}
be a
r
-partite
r
-uniform hypergraph. Then for every hyperedge

, there exists a state
\psi_{T}
such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{T,M}(\psi_{T})>0

{\mathcal{E}}_{T^{\prime},M}(\psi_{T})=0
for all
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\{T\}
.

Proof.

For all
i\in[r]
, since
M_{i}
is a non-zero PSD matrix which is not full-rank, there exist unit vectors
\tau_{i},\pi_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that
\langle\tau_{i},M_{i}\tau_{i}\rangle={\|}M_{i}{\|}_{\operatorname{op}}>0
, while
\langle\pi_{i},M_{i}\pi_{i}\rangle=0

\phi_{t}:=\begin{cases}\tau_{p}&\text{if }t=t_{p},\\

{\mathcal{E}}_{T^{\prime},M}(\psi_{T})=\langle\psi_{T},(M|_{T^{\prime}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}_{\overline{T^{\prime}}})\psi_{T}\rangle=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\langle\phi_{t^{\prime}_{i}},M_{i}\phi_{t^{\prime}_{i}}\rangle=\prod_{i=1}^{r}{\|}M_{i}{\|}_{\operatorname{op}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{1}(t^{\prime}_{i}=t_{i})

=\prod_{i=1}^{r}{\|}M_{i}{\|}_{\operatorname{op}}\cdot\mathbf{1}(T^{\prime}=T)\,,

as desired.
∎

Theorem 7.2
.

Let

8
Characterizing the Non-Redundancy of All
2
-Qubit Hamiltonians

In this section, we characterize the non-redundancy of all nonzero 2-qubit predicate matrices
M\in\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}
. The characterization is surprisingly simple.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}
be a nonzero predicate matrix. Then
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)=\Theta(n)
unless
M=M_{1}\otimes M_{2}
where unary predicate matrices
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
have rank one, in which case
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)=\Theta(n^{2})
.

Toward proving
Theorem

8.1
, we first handle a few easy cases.

(1)

We always have that
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)\leqslant 2\binom{n}{2}\leqslant O(n^{2})
, as a (simple) directed graph on
n
vertices has at most
2\binom{n}{2}
edges.

(2)

We always have that
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)\geqslant\Omega(n)
by
Lemma

2.8
, as a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
consisting of
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
disjoint edges is always non-redundant (as our assignment
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
uses separate qubits to satisfy / not-satisfy each constraint).

(3)

If
M=M_{1}\otimes M_{2}
, where
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
have rank 1, by applying
Theorem

7.2
in the case that
r=2
, we get that
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)\geqslant\Omega(n^{2})
.

Thus, to prove
Theorem

8.1
, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)\leqslant O(n)
whenever
M
cannot be expressed as the tensor product of two rank 1 predicate matrices. To do this, we study the
automorphisms
of states
\psi
in the ground state
\Psi_{\mathcal{H}}
of any sufficiently connected Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
.

Automorphisms.

Given a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
(the symmetric group acting on
[n]
), we can define a unitary matrix
U_{\pi}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
which maps any qubit
{|}x\rangle
with
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
to
{|}\pi(x)\rangle
, where
\pi(x)_{\pi(i)}:=x_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. For example, if
x
is the indicator vector of the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
bit, then
\pi(x)
is the indicator vector of the
\pi(i)^{\mathrm{th}}
bit. One can verify for any
\pi,\tau\in S_{n}
, we have that
U_{\pi}\circ U_{\tau}=U_{\pi\circ\tau}
. Given a state
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
, we define its automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(\psi)
as follows:

\operatorname{Aut}(\psi)=\{\pi\in S_{n}:U_{\pi}\psi=\psi\}.

We note that
\operatorname{Aut}(\psi)
is always a subgroup of
S_{n}
. Given a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
, we define
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{H})
to be the greatest common automorphism group of all states
\psi\in\Psi_{\mathcal{H}}
. In other words,

\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{H}):=\bigcap_{\psi\in\Psi_{\mathcal{H}}}\operatorname{Aut}(\psi).

The key technical lemma we need toward proving
Theorem

8.1
is understanding how the structure of
\operatorname{span}(M)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{4}
impacts
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{H})
.

8.1
The Nonsingular Case

In this section, we handle the (general) case in which
\operatorname{span}(M)
contains what we call a
nonsingular
vector.

Definition 8.2
.

We say that
u\in\operatorname{span}(M)
are
nonsingular
if we have that

\det\begin{pmatrix}u(00)&u(01)\\
u(10)&u(11)\end{pmatrix}\neq 0.

Otherwise, we say that
u
is singular.

We now prove our key technical lemma.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}
be a predicate matrix with
u\in\operatorname{span}(M)
non-singular, and let
\mathcal{H}
be an
M
-Hamiltonian. Let

be distinct indices. If

, then the permutation
(jk)
is an element of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
T_{12}:=(1,2),T_{13}:=(1,3)\in\mathcal{H}
, and we seek to prove that
(23)\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{H})
. Fix an arbitrary state

. To show that

\psi_{x}(001)=\psi_{x}(010)
and
\psi_{x}(101)=\psi_{x}(110)

Since

, we have that the following equations are true:

\displaystyle(M{|}_{T_{12}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}{|}_{3})\psi_{x}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(M{|}_{T_{13}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}{|}_{2})\psi_{x}

\displaystyle=0.

Since

\displaystyle u(00)\psi_{x}(000)+u(01)\psi_{x}(010)+u(10)\psi_{x}(100)+u(11)\psi_{x}(110)

\displaystyle=0

(23)

\displaystyle u(00)\psi_{x}(001)+u(01)\psi_{x}(011)+u(10)\psi_{x}(101)+u(11)\psi_{x}(111)

\displaystyle=0

(24)

\displaystyle u(00)\psi_{x}(000)+u(01)\psi_{x}(001)+u(10)\psi_{x}(100)+u(11)\psi_{x}(101)

\displaystyle=0

(25)

\displaystyle u(00)\psi_{x}(010)+u(01)\psi_{x}(011)+u(10)\psi_{x}(110)+u(11)\psi_{x}(111)

\displaystyle=0

(26)

Now, if we consider the expression

Likewise, if we consider

Thus,

.

Given a graph
\mathcal{G}
on vertex set
[n]

if there exist (distinct) indices

\displaystyle e_{1}=(i_{1},j_{1}),e_{2}=(i_{1},j_{2}),e_{3}=(i_{2},j_{2}),\ldots,e_{2k-2}=(i_{k-1},j_{k}),e_{2k-1}=(i_{k},j_{k}),e_{2k}=(i_{k},j_{1}).

(27)

Lemma 8.4
.

Assume that
u\in\operatorname{span}(M)
is nonsingular. If an
M
-Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
has a bipartite cycle, then
\mathcal{H}
is redundant.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
Eq.

27
are edges of
\mathcal{H}
. We claim that
\Psi_{\mathcal{H}}=\Psi_{\mathcal{H}\setminus e_{2k}}
. Clearly
\Psi_{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\Psi_{\mathcal{H}\setminus e_{2k}}
, so it suffices to prove that
\Psi_{\mathcal{H}\setminus e_{2k}}\subseteq\Psi_{\mathcal{H}}
. Consider any
\psi\in\Psi_{\mathcal{H}\setminus e_{2k}}
. By invoking
Lemma

8.3
on the pairs of edges
(e_{1},e_{2}),\ldots,(e_{2k-3},e_{2k-2})
, we have that
(j_{1}j_{2}),\ldots,(j_{k-1}j_{k})\in\operatorname{Aut}(\psi)
. By taking a suitable product of these automorphisms, we may deduce that
(j_{1}j_{k})\in\operatorname{Aut}(\psi)
. Since
\psi\in\Psi_{e_{2k-1}}
, we have that

\psi=U_{(j_{1}j_{k})}\psi\in U_{(j_{1}j_{k})}\Psi_{e_{2k-1}}=\Psi_{e_{2k}}.

Thus,
\psi\in\Psi_{\mathcal{H}\setminus e_{2k}}\cap\Psi_{e_{2k}}=\Psi_{\mathcal{H}}
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 8.5
.

If
u\in\operatorname{span}(M)
is nonsingular, then
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)\leqslant O(n)
.

Proof.

Randomly color every vertex of
\mathcal{H}
uniformly at random either red or blue. Let
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
be the subset of edges that direct from a red vertex to a blue vertex. By

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
has at most

\mathcal{H}
is included in
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
with probability

with at least

edges. Thus,

, as desired.
∎

singular. Since
u
is singular, there exists
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that
u=u_{1}\otimes u_{2}
. Thus, if we let
M_{1}=u_{1}u_{1}^{*}
and
M_{2}=u_{2}u_{2}^{*}
, we have that
M=M_{1}\otimes M_{2}
, with
\operatorname{rank}M_{1},\operatorname{rank}M_{2}=1
.

Now assume
\operatorname{rank}M\geqslant 2
. Pick linearly independent
u,v\in\operatorname{span}(M)
. For any scalar
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, by assumption we have that

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}u(00)+\lambda v(00)&u(01)+\lambda v(01)\\
u(10)+\lambda v(10)&u(11)+\lambda v(11)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=0.\implies

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}(v(00)v(11)-v(01)v(10))+\lambda(v(00)u(11)+u(00)v(11)

\displaystyle-u(01)v(10)-u(10)v(01))

\displaystyle+(u(00)u(11)-u(01)u(10))

\displaystyle=0.

In particular, the coefficient of
\lambda
must be zero:

\displaystyle v(00)u(11)+u(00)v(11)-u(01)v(10)-u(10)v(01)=0

(28)

Since
u
and
v
are singular, we can write
u=u_{1}\otimes u_{2}
and
v=v_{1}\otimes v_{2}
with
u_{1},u_{2},v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Then
Eq.

28
factors as

(v_{1}(0)u_{1}(1)-v_{1}(1)u_{1}(0))(v_{2}(0)u_{2}(1)-v_{2}(1)u_{2}(0))=0.

Thus, either
u_{1}
and
v_{1}
are related by a scalar multiple or
u_{2}
and
v_{2}
are related by a scalar multiple. If
\operatorname{rank}M=2
, then we have that either
\operatorname{span}(M)=\langle u_{1}\rangle\otimes\langle u_{2},v_{2}\rangle
or
\operatorname{span}(M)=\langle u_{1},v_{1}\rangle\otimes\langle u_{2}\rangle.
In either case, we have that
M=M_{1}\otimes M_{2}
with one of
M_{1}
or
M_{2}
lacking full rank.

Now assume
\operatorname{rank}M\geqslant 3
. Consider linearly independent
u,v,w\in\operatorname{span}(M)
and assume they are all singular, so
u=u_{1}\otimes u_{2}
,
v=v_{1}\otimes v_{2}
,
w=w_{1}\otimes w_{2}
with
u_{1},u_{2},v_{1},v_{2},w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
. By the previous discussion, we have each of the following three logical facts:

\displaystyle\langle u_{1}\rangle=\langle v_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle\vee\langle u_{2}\rangle=\langle v_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\langle u_{1}\rangle=\langle w_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle\vee\langle u_{2}\rangle=\langle w_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\langle v_{1}\rangle=\langle w_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle\vee\langle v_{2}\rangle=\langle w_{2}\rangle.

By pigeonhole, either two equalities are true on the left, or two equalities are true on the right, so by transitivity either
\langle u_{1}\rangle=\langle v_{1}\rangle=\langle w_{1}\rangle
or
\langle u_{2}\rangle=\langle v_{2}\rangle=\langle w_{2}\rangle
. Since
u_{1},u_{2},v_{1},v_{2},w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, in either case we cannot have
u,v,w
be linearly independent. Thus, we have completed our classification.
∎

Thus, the cases we have not classified yet are when the predicate matrix
M
has the form
M_{1}\otimes M_{2}
where
(\operatorname{rank}M_{1},\operatorname{rank}M_{2})\in\{(1,2),(2,1)\}.
Since swapping the order of the qubits of
M
leaves
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)
unchanged, we may WLOG assume that
(\operatorname{rank}M_{1},\operatorname{rank}M_{2})=(2,1)
. We now prove an analogue of
Lemma

8.3
.

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}
be a predicate matrix such that
M=M_{1}\otimes M_{2}
with
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
and
(\operatorname{rank}M_{1},\operatorname{rank}M_{2})=(2,1)
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be an
M
-Hamiltonian and
i,j,k\in[n]
be distinct indices. If
(i,j),(i,k)\in\mathcal{H}
, then the permutation
(jk)
is an element of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\operatorname{span}(M)=\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\langle v\rangle
for some nonzero
v\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
T_{12}:=(1,2),T_{13}:=(1,3)\in\mathcal{H}
, and we seek to prove that
(23)\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{H})
. Fix an arbitrary state
\psi\in\Psi_{\mathcal{H}}
. To show that
(23)\in\operatorname{Aut}(\psi)
, it suffices to show
(23)\in\operatorname{Aut}(\psi_{x})
for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{T}
where
T=\{4,\ldots,n\}
. This is equivalent to showing that
\psi_{x}(001)=\psi_{x}(010)
and
\psi_{x}(101)=\psi_{x}(110)
.

Since
\psi\in\Psi_{\mathcal{H}}
, we have that the following equations are true:

\displaystyle(M{|}_{T_{12}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}{|}_{3})\psi_{x}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(M{|}_{T_{13}}\otimes\operatorname{Id}{|}_{2})\psi_{x}

\displaystyle=0.

Thus, for all bits
y,z\in\{0,1\}
we have that

\displaystyle v(0)\psi_{x}(y0z)+v(1)\psi_{x}(y1z)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle v(0)\psi_{x}(yz0)+v(1)\psi_{x}(yz1)

\displaystyle=0.

If we set
z=0
and subtract the above pair of equations, we get that

v(1)(\psi_{x}(y10)-\psi_{x}(y01))=0.

Otherwise, if we set
z=1
and subtract the pair of equations,we get that

v(0)(\psi_{x}(y01)-\psi_{x}(y10))=0.

Since
v
is nonzero, at least one of
v(0)=v(1)
, so we can deduce that
\psi_{x}(y01)=\psi_{x}(y10)
. That is,
(23)\in\operatorname{Aut}(\psi_{x})
, as desired.
∎

By suitably adapting the proofs of
Lemma

8.4
and
Lemma

8.5
, we may deduce the following

Lemma 8.8
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}
be a predicate matrix such that
M=M_{1}\otimes M_{2}
with
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
and
(\operatorname{rank}M_{1},\operatorname{rank}M_{2})\in\{(1,2),(2,1)\}
. Then,
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)\leqslant O(n)
.

This completes the proof of
Theorem

8.1
.

9
A Theory of Non-Redundancy for Generic Hamiltonians

In this section, we provide more general upper bounds on the non-redundancy of random Hamiltonians. In particular, in
Theorem

4.2
, we saw that when the predicate matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
is a random PSD matrix of rank
2^{r-1}+1
, then Hamiltonians using
M
admit sparsifiers of size
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}n^{2})
. As we shall see, we can even show that when the rank falls
below

2^{r-1}
, that the non-redundancy of these Hamiltonians is still bounded.

We show the following theorem:

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
be a random predicate matrix of rank
R=2^{r-1}-1
. Then, with probability
1
over
M
,
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)\leqslant\widetilde{O}(n)
.

To prove
Theorem

9.1
, we will suppose that
\mathcal{H}=(T_{i},M)_{i=1,\dots m}
is a non-redundant Hamiltonian. By
Proposition

2.3
, we can also assume that
\mathcal{H}
is
r
-partite.

9.1
Analyzing Intersecting Terms

Towards proving
Theorem

9.1
, we first prove the following key lemma:

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
be a random predicate matrix of rank
R=2^{r-1}-1
, and let
T,T^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
,
{|}T{|}=r
denote two local constraints. Suppose that
T\cap T^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
, let
a\in T\cap T^{\prime}
, and let
S=T-\{a\}\subseteq[n]
and
S^{\prime}=T^{\prime}-\{a\}\subseteq[n]
.
Then, with probability
1
over
M
, for any state
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{T,M}(\psi)={\mathcal{E}}_{T^{\prime},M}(\psi)=0
, it must be the case that the automorphism
(S,S^{\prime})\in\mathrm{Aut}(\psi)
.

When a predicate matrix
M
satisfies the above requirement, we call it
generic
.

Proof of
Lemma

9.2
.

Without loss of generality, we will assume that
T=\{1,2,3,4,\dots r\}
and
T^{\prime}=\{1,r+1,r+2,\dots 2r-1\}
. As in the proof of
Theorem

4.2
, we let
M
be the span of (random) vectors
v_{1},\dots v_{R}
, and we let
U=T\cup T^{\prime}
. In this setting, our goal is to show that
\psi
satisfies the automorphism
(\{2,3,4,\dots r\},\{r+1,\dots 2r-1\}\})
.

Now, for simplicity, we restrict our attention to the sub-state
\phi=\psi_{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{U}}
for any
y\in\{0,1\}^{[n]-U}
. We construct two matrices
M_{0},M_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r-1}\times 2^{r-1}}
, where the entries of
M_{0},M_{1}
are indexed by bit strings of length
r-1
, where
M_{0}(a,b)=\phi_{0\circ a\circ b}
and
M_{1}({a,b})=\phi_{1\circ a\circ b}
. To show that
\phi
satisfies our desired automorphism, we equivalently must only show that
M_{0}=M_{0}^{\top}
and
M_{1}=M_{1}^{\top}
, as this immediately implies that

\phi_{0\circ a\circ b}=\phi_{0\circ b\circ a},\quad\quad\quad\phi_{1\circ a\circ b}=\phi_{1\circ b\circ a},

and thus
(\{2,3,4,\dots r\},\{r+1,\dots 2r-1\}\})\in\operatorname{Aut}(\phi)
(and correspondingly, of
\psi
). Thus, for the remainder of this proof, we focus on showing that
M_{0}
and
M_{1}
are symmetric.

Now, because
{\mathcal{E}}_{T,M}(\psi)={\mathcal{E}}_{T^{\prime},M}(\psi)=0
, it must be the case that for every
\widehat{y}\in\{0,1\}^{U-T}
and for every
j\in[R]
,
\langle\phi_{\widehat{y}},v_{j}\rangle=0
, and likewise for every
\widehat{y}\in\{0,1\}^{U-T^{\prime}}
,
\langle\phi_{\widehat{y}},v_{j}\rangle=0
. We can now re-write these constraints: for
j\in[R]
, we define
\alpha_{j}
to be a length
2^{r-1}
vector (indexed by strings
a\in\{0,1\}^{r-1}
) such that
\alpha_{j}(a)=(v_{j})_{0\circ a}
. Likewise, we define
\beta_{j}
such that
\beta_{j}(a)=(v_{j})_{1\circ a}
. From these, we construct matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{R\times 2^{r-1}}
, where
A_{j,a}=\alpha_{j}(a)
.

Importantly, the constraints that
\langle\phi_{\widehat{y}},v_{j}\rangle=0
for
\widehat{y}\in\{0,1\}^{U-T}
are in exact correspondence with the property that

\displaystyle AM_{0}+BM_{1}=0.

(29)

Similarly, the fact that
\langle\phi_{\widehat{y}},v_{j}\rangle=0
for
\widehat{y}\in\{0,1\}^{U-T^{\prime}}
implies that

\displaystyle A(M_{0})^{\top}+B(M_{1})^{\top}=0.

(30)

Now, for
M_{0},M_{1}
, we decompose these matrices into their symmetric and anti-symmetric parts. I.e., we write

M_{0}=Q_{0}+K_{0},\quad\quad M_{1}=Q_{1}+K_{1},

where
Q_{0}=Q_{0}^{\top},Q_{1}=Q_{1}^{\top}
, while
K_{0}=-K_{0}^{\top}
and
K_{1}=K_{1}^{\top}
. Plugging in this decomposition into
Eq.

29
and
Eq.

30
(and subtracting one from the other), we derive that

AQ_{0}+BQ_{1}=0

and that

AK_{0}+BK_{1}=0.

Because our goal was to show that, in fact,
M_{0}=M_{0}^{\top}
and
M_{1}=M_{1}^{\top}
, it remains only for us to show that
K_{0}=K_{1}=0
, as this then directly implies that our matrices
M_{0},M_{1}
are symmetric, and we derive our desired automorphism.

Indeed, let us define the function
F_{A,B}(K_{0},K_{1})=AK_{0}+AK_{1}
, where
K_{0},K_{1}
are anti-symmetric matrices in
\mathbb{C}^{2^{r-1}\times 2^{r-1}}
, and
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{R\times 2^{r-1}}
. Because the space of anti-symmetric matrices of size
2^{r-1}\times 2^{r-1}
is of dimension
2^{r-2}\cdot(2^{r-1}-1)
, we see that
F_{A,B}
is a linear map with domain of dimension
2\cdot 2^{r-2}\cdot(2^{r-1}-1)=2^{r-1}\cdot(2^{r-1}-1)
. Likewise, the co-domain of
F
is simply
\mathbb{C}^{R\times 2^{r-1}}
, which is of dimension
R\cdot 2^{r-1}=2^{r-1}\cdot(2^{r-1}-1)
.

Thus,
F_{A,B}
is a
2^{r-1}\cdot(2^{r-1}-1)\times 2^{r-1}\cdot(2^{r-1}-1)
dimensional linear map. We let its determinant be
p(A,B)=\det(F_{A,B})
. Observe that this is a polynomial in
A,B
, and because the entries of the matrices
A,B
are exactly the entries of the vectors
v_{1},\dots v_{R}
, we see
p(A,B)
is simply a polynomial in the entries of the vectors
v_{1},\dots v_{R}
.

Now, we have the following claim:

Claim 9.3
.

The polynomial
p(A,B)
as defined above is not the zero-polynomial.

Proof of
9.3
.

It suffices to find a single choice of
A,B
such that
F_{A,B}
is invertible. Indeed, consider

A=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Id}_{R}&0\end{bmatrix},\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}0&\operatorname{Id}_{R}\end{bmatrix}.

Now, consider any anti-symmetric matrices

K_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0&x_{1,2}&x_{1,3}&\dots&x_{1,2^{r-1}}\\
-x_{1,2}&0&x_{2,3}&\dots&x_{2,2^{r-1}}\\
-x_{1,3}&-x_{2,3}&0&\dots&x_{3,2^{r-1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-x_{1,2^{r-1}}&-x_{2,2^{r-1}}&-x_{3,2^{r-1}}&\dots&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

K_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&y_{1,2}&y_{1,3}&\dots&y_{1,2^{r-1}}\\
-y_{1,2}&0&y_{2,3}&\dots&y_{2,2^{r-1}}\\
-y_{1,3}&-y_{2,3}&0&\dots&y_{3,2^{r-1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-y_{1,2^{r-1}}&-y_{2,2^{r-1}}&-y_{3,2^{r-1}}&\dots&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Note that
AK_{0}
is just
K_{0}
with the last row deleted and
BK_{1}
is just
K_{1}
with the first row deleted. Therefore, we can then see that
F_{A,B}(K_{0},K_{1})=

\begin{bmatrix}-y_{1,2}&x_{1,2}&x_{1,3}+y_{2,3}&x_{1,4}+y_{2,4}&\dots&x_{1,2^{r-1}}+y_{2,2^{r-1}}\\
-x_{1,2}-y_{1,3}&-y_{2,3}&x_{2,3}&x_{2,4}+y_{3,4}&\dots&x_{2,2^{r-1}}+y_{3,2^{r-1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-x_{1,2^{r-1}-2}-y_{1,2^{r-1}-1}&-x_{2,2^{r-1}-2}-y_{2,2^{r-1}-1}&\dots&\dots&\dots&x_{2^{r-1}-2,2^{r-1}}+y_{2^{r-1}-1,2^{r-1}}\\
-x_{1,2^{r-1}-1}-y_{1,2^{r-1}}&-x_{2,2^{r-1}-1}-y_{2,2^{r-1}}&\dots&\dots&\dots&x_{2^{r-1}-1,2^{r-1}}\end{bmatrix}.

To see that this map is invertible, we suppose that we are given a matrix of the form
F_{A,B}(K_{0},K_{1})
, as prescribed above. It is trivial to see then that one can read off the values of
x_{i,i+1}
and
y_{i,i+1}
for
i\in[R]
, as these are “isolated” along the diagonal of
F_{A,B}(K_{0},K_{1})
. Once these values of
x_{i,i+1},y_{i,i+1}
are known, we can then calculate
F_{A,B}((K_{0}){|}_{x_{i,i+1}=0},(K_{1}){|}_{y_{i,i+1}=0})
, where
(K_{0}){|}_{x_{i,i+1}=0}
refers to the matrix where the variables
x_{i,i+1}:i\in[R]
are set to
0
.

Again by calculation, we can see that

\displaystyle F_{A,B}((K_{0}){|}_{x_{i,i+1}=0},(K_{1}){|}_{y_{i,i+1}=0})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&x_{1,3}&x_{1,4}+y_{2,4}&\dots&x_{1,2^{r-1}}+y_{2,2^{r-1}}\\
y_{1,3}&0&0&x_{2,4}&\dots&x_{2,2^{r-1}}+y_{3,2^{r-1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-x_{1,2^{r-1}-2}-y_{1,2^{r-1}-1}&-x_{2,2^{r-1}-2}-y_{2,2^{r-1}-1}&\dots&\dots&\dots&x_{2^{r-1}-2,2^{r-1}}\\
-x_{1,2^{r-1}-1}-y_{1,2^{r-1}}&-x_{2,2^{r-1}-1}-y_{2,2^{r-1}}&\dots&\dots&\dots&0\end{bmatrix}.

From here, we can then compute the values of
x_{i,i+2},y_{i,i+2}:i\in[R-1]
, as these are now “isolated” entries along the off-diagonal.

We then repeat this argument iteratively, where in the
j
th round, where we use
K_{0}^{(j)}=(K_{0}){|}_{x_{i,i+1}=0,\dots x_{i,i+j-1}=0}
, and
K_{1}^{(j)}=(K_{1}){|}_{y_{i,i+1}=0,\dots y_{i,i+j-1}=0}
. For these matrices, we will see that in
F_{A,B}(K_{0}^{(j)},K_{1}^{(j)})
, the entries
x_{i,i+j}
and
y_{i,i+j}
will be isolated on the off-diagonal, and can therefore be explicitly computed.

Thus, by induction, after
R
rounds of this procedure, we will have computed
x_{i,p}
for
i,p\in[2^{r-1}]
, given only
F_{A,B}(K_{0},K_{1})
. Thus, given
F_{A,B}(K_{0},K_{1})
, we are able to compute
K_{0},K_{1}
exactly, and thus
F_{A,B}(K_{0},K_{1})
is invertible.

Thus, for this choice of
A,B
it must be the case that
p(A,B)=\det(F_{A,B})\neq 0
(as a polynomial), as there is a choice of
A,B
which makes
F_{A,B}
invertible.
∎

To conclude,
9.3
establishes that the polynomial
p(A,B)\neq 0
. But, at the same time, we know that the variables in
p(A,B)
are exactly the entries of the vectors
v_{1},\dots v_{R}
which have continuous joint density. Thus, by
2.18
, we know that with probability
1
over
v_{1},\dots v_{R}
,
p(A,B)\neq 0
. This in turn implies that
K_{0}=K_{1}=0
, and so
M_{0}=M_{0}^{\top}
and
M_{1}=M_{1}^{\top}
, thereby deriving our desired automorphism.
∎

9.2
Bounding the Non-Redundancy

Now, we use
Lemma

9.2
to prove
Theorem

9.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

9.1
.

Indeed, suppose that
M
is generic (as in
Lemma

9.2
). Let
\mathcal{H}=(T_{i},M)_{i=1,\dots m}
denote a non-redundant Hamiltonian with predicate matrix
M
. Note that, as per
Proposition

2.3
, we can assume that
\mathcal{H}
is
r
-partite, while only paying a
\widetilde{O}(1)
factor in the resulting bound on
\operatorname{NRD}
.

We now make the following claim:

Claim 9.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(T_{i},M)_{i=1,\dots m}
denote a Hamiltonian with generic predicate matrix
M
. Fix a term
T_{j}
from
\mathcal{H}
, and let
\psi
be a state such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{(T_{i},M)_{i=1,\dots m},i\neq j}(\psi)=0
. Suppose further that there is some term
T_{\ell}:\ell\in[m]-i
such that:

(1)

T_{\ell}\cap T_{j}\neq\varnothing
.

(2)

For
S_{j}=T_{j}-T_{j}\cap T_{\ell}
and
S_{\ell}=T_{\ell}-T_{j}\cap T_{\ell}
, it is the case that
(S_{j},S_{\ell})\in\operatorname{Aut}(\psi)
.

Then, it must be the case that
{\mathcal{E}}_{(T_{j},M)}(\psi)=0
.

Proof.

Indeed, because
{\mathcal{E}}_{(T_{i},M)_{i=1,\dots m},i\neq j}(\psi)=0
, it must be the case that for the term
T_{\ell}
, that for every
y\in\{0,1\}^{[n]-T_{\ell}}
that

\langle\psi_{y},M\psi_{y}\rangle=0.

Now, let us construct the bit string
y^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{[n]-T_{j}}
such that:

(1)

For indices
g\in[n]-T_{j}
,
y^{\prime}_{g}=y_{g}
.

(2)

For indices
g\in T_{j}\cap T_{\ell}
,
y^{\prime}_{g}=y_{g}
.

(3)

If we let
S_{j}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots\}
and
S_{\ell}=\{a^{\prime}_{1},a^{\prime}_{2},\dots\}
, then
y^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}_{1}}=y_{a_{1}}
.

The key observation is exactly that, because of the automorphism
(S_{j},S_{\ell})\in\operatorname{Aut}(\psi)
, it must be the case that
\psi_{y}=\psi_{y^{\prime}}
, and thus,

\langle\psi_{y^{\prime}},M\psi_{y^{\prime}}\rangle=0.

Because the strings
y
and
y^{\prime}
as constructed above are in bijection with one another, this means that for every
y^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{[n]-T_{j}}
,
\langle\psi_{y^{\prime}},M\psi_{y^{\prime}}\rangle=0
, which in turn means that
{\mathcal{E}}_{(T_{j},M)}(\psi)=0
, as we desire.
∎

Next, we let
F\subseteq[m]
denote a subset of
[m]
of size
\leqslant n
which is a spanning forest of
\mathcal{H}
(see
Definition

5.5
and
Proposition

5.6
).

Now, we consider adding each other term
T_{i}:i\in[m]-F
one at a time. We let
\mathcal{H}^{(p)}
denote the result of adding the first
p
terms from
T_{i}:i\in[m]-F
(in addition to
F
). Likewise, we let
\operatorname{Aut}^{(p)}(\psi)
denote the automorphisms that any state
\psi
must satisfy in order for
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}^{(p)}}(\psi)=0
. Now, when we add the
p+1
st term (call this term
T
) to get
\mathcal{H}^{(p+1)}
, we know that there must be some term
T_{j}:j\in F
such that
T_{i}\cap T_{j}\neq\varnothing
. We let
S=T-T\cap T_{j}
and we let
S_{j}=T_{j}-T\cap T_{j}
. We have have two cases:

(1)

If
(S,S_{j})\in\operatorname{Aut}^{(p)}(\psi)
, then we immediately reach a contradiction. Indeed, by
9.4
, this means that for any state
\psi
such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}^{(p)}}(\psi)=0
, it must also be the case that
{\mathcal{E}}_{(T,M)}(\psi)=0
. However, this violates the definition of non-redundancy; indeed, in order for
\mathcal{H}
to be a non-redundant hypergraph, there must be some choice of
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}-(T,M)}(\psi)=0
(and thus
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}^{(p)}}(\psi)=0
), while at the same time
{\mathcal{E}}_{(T,M)}(\psi)>0
.

(2)

So, it must be the case that
(S,S_{j})\notin\operatorname{Aut}^{(p)}(\psi)
. But, by
Lemma

9.2
, we know that any state
\psi
which receives
0
energy on
T,T_{j}
must introduce an automorphism
(S,S_{j})
on
\psi
. This means that
\operatorname{Aut}^{(p+1)}(\psi)=\langle\operatorname{Aut}^{(p)}(\psi)\cup(S,S_{j})\rangle
is a strictly larger subgroup than
\operatorname{Aut}^{(p)}(\psi)
. Thus, it must be the case that

2\leqslant{|}\operatorname{Aut}^{(p+1)}(\psi):\operatorname{Aut}^{(p)}(\psi){|}=\frac{{|}\operatorname{Aut}^{(p+1)}(\psi){|}}{{|}\operatorname{Aut}^{(p)}(\psi){|}}.

Thus, after
p
terms are added (beyond
F
), it must be the case that
{|}\operatorname{Aut}^{(p)}(\psi){|}\geqslant 2^{p}
. Once
p=n\log(n)
, this means that
{|}\operatorname{Aut}^{(p)}(\psi){|}\geqslant n!={|}S_{n}{|}
, and so the automorphism group is the entire symmetric group on
n
elements. At this point,
{|}\operatorname{Aut}^{(p)}(\psi){|}
cannot grow any larger (i.e., case 2 above cannot occur), and so for any additional term it must be that case
1
occurs, which in turn violates the non-redundancy of
\mathcal{H}
. Thus, it must be that the number of terms in any non-redundant hypergraph with generic
M
is
m\leqslant{|}F{|}+n\log(n)\leqslant O(n\log(n))
, as we desire.
∎

Remark 9.5
.

We remark that one
\log n
term in the bound of
Theorem

9.1
can be removed by more carefully analyzing the structure of the symmetric group. More precisely, let
t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell}
be the automorphisms identified in the non-redundant instance considered in the proof of
Theorem

9.1
. These automorphisms have the property that
t_{i}
does not reside in the subgroup of
S_{n}
generated by
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell}\}\setminus\{t_{i}\}
. By a result of Whiston
[
51
]
any such list of group elements must satisfy
\ell\leqslant n-1
(rather than
O(n\log n)
). We omit further details.

10
Conclusion

In this paper, we systematically studied the sparsifiability of Hamiltonians composed of PSD local terms. In comparison to Hamiltonians with negative eigenvalues
[
3
]
, a very rich family of Hamiltonians admit near-linear sparsifiers. A crucial tool we used for constructing these sparsifiers was a quantum analogue of the concept of non-redundancy which captures the interactions between the ground states of various terms in the Hamiltonian. In addition to constructing a few families of sparsifiers, we also systematically studied the non-redundancy of a few types of Hamiltonians, showing the potential for the further construction of Hamiltonian sparsifiers.

Overall, we find that the study of hamiltonian sparsification has been largely unexplored, despite the promise it holds. We hope that others will continue to study questions in this direction, and so we conclude the paper with a few crucial open questions:

Question 10.1
.

For an arbitrary predicate matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{r}\times 2^{r}}
, what is the relationship between
\operatorname{SPR}(M,n,\varepsilon)
and
\operatorname{NRD}(M,n)
? In particular, when is
\operatorname{SPR}(M,n,\varepsilon)=\widetilde{O}_{M}(\operatorname{NRD}(M,n))
?

By a result of Brakensiek and Guruswami
[
16
]
in combination with
Proposition

2.9
, we know that the answer to
10.1
is yes whenever
M
is a classical predicate matrix. A natural quantum extension of
[
16
]
would be to first study
10.1
for
projection matrices

M
, i.e. matrices
M
all of whose eigenvalues are
0
or
1
.

We also highlight a particularly well-studied special case of
10.1
: Quantum Cut.

Question 10.2
.

What is the sparsifiability of Quantum Cut:
M_{\mathrm{QC}}=({|}01\rangle-{|}10\rangle)(\langle 01{|}-\langle 10{|})
?

Although
M_{\mathrm{QC}}
has rank
1
,
M_{\mathrm{QC}}
cannot be decomposed as the product of two rank-
1
tensors. Thus, by
Theorem

8.1
, we know that the non-redundancy of
M
is linear, suggesting the the sparsifiability of
M
could also be near-linear.
