A generalized two-step floating building catchment area model for measuring EMS accessibility with in-building patient access
Authors: Jinpeng Li, Xiaoqing Jin, Weicong Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid access to Emergency Medical Services (EMS) is critical for improving patient survival. Existing studies primarily assess EMS accessibility based on road travel, overlooking the in-building time required to reach patients, which may introduce bias in high-density urban environments. This study aims to improve the measurement of EMS accessibility by explicitly incorporating in-building patient access.MethodsWe developed a generalized two-step floating building catchment area (G-2SFBCA) model that integrates both the Departure Road Trip (DRT) and the Patient Access Trip (PAT), while accounting for spatial variations in building height and occupancy density. Wuhan, China, was used as a case study. Building-level data, EMS station locations, and road network data were combined to estimate total EMS travel time. Accessibility was evaluated under different distance-decay functions and compared with results from the conventional G-2SFCA model.ResultsPAT travel time exhibited a distinct “low–high–low” spatial pattern from the city center to suburban areas, closely associated with variations in building height and density. Incorporating PAT reduced the population covered within a 5-minute EMS response time by 12.9% and increased the population outside the 12-minute threshold by 3.2%. Compared with the conventional model, the proposed approach revealed lower accessibility in transitional high-rise areas and highlighted the overestimation of EMS accessibility when in-building travel time is ignored.ConclusionsThe proposed G-2SFBCA model provides a more realistic and policy-relevant assessment of EMS accessibility, revealing that conventional approaches may substantially overestimate service coverage in high-density urban environments.

Introduction

Emergency Medical Services (EMS) are a foundational component of public healthcare systems, designed to provide rapid medical response to accidents and acute medical events. It begins with essential first aid and seamlessly transitions to expedited medical transport, ensuring swift care during emergencies [31, 44]. An EMS system with robust accessibility to the accident scene is strongly linked to potential survival outcomes, as it dictates the moment when a patient receives on-scene care, the point at which an EMS team can begin treatment at the incident site [27]. Understanding and improving EMS accessibility are vital for strategic interventions that address gaps in emergency care and fortify the healthcare system’s readiness for critical incidents.

When providing emergency care services, an EMS team typically undertakes two different trips (Fig. 1). The first trip, referred to as DRT (that is, Departure Road Trip), involves the trip to a patient’s location, stopping just outside the building where the patient is located. The second trip, known as the Patient Access Trip (PAT), entails accessing the patient’s residence or incident site within the building, commonly on foot or via elevator. Both trips play critical roles in affecting potential survival outcomes [11, 13]. The ease of DRT accessibility is largely determined by the spatial interaction between EMS resource and patients. The PAT interval is often influenced by building height and occupancy density. The building height is commonly represented by the number of floors. When floor count increases, EMS team faces greater travelling costs (e.g., travel time) to reach the patient’s residence. The occupancy density of the building can be represented by the ratio of household-elevator. The higher the occupancy density, the more likely the elevators will be occupied, resulting in longer waiting times for providing emergency medical care.

In the field of healthcare services, accessibility is complex that has been widely explored by studies from various fields, including public policy, geography, and sociology [7, 16]. In general, healthcare accessibility can be categorized into spatial and utilization dimensions. When considering the spatial dimension, healthcare accessibility can be further classified into spatial and aspatial types. Spatial accessibility concerns impedance from geographical dimension (i.e., travel time or distance) between healthcare facilities and patients [12], whereas aspatial accessibility emphasizes non-spatial characteristics—such as demographic factors, health insurance, or socio-economic status—that influence the ease of obtaining healthcare services [43, 51]. From a utilization dimension, accessibility can be divided into revealed and potential dimensions. Revealed accessibility, on the one hand, concerns the actual use of healthcare services and reflects the realization of potential accessibility [17]. On the other hand, potential accessibility refers to the possibility of reaching the healthcare system but does not guarantee that the patient can immediately use it.

This study focuses on potential spatial accessibility to EMS and its spatial variation. A limitation of existing related accessibility methods is that PAT accessibility—and its sensitivity to spatial variations in building height and occupancy density—are often neglected. Most relevant studies consider only a one-way trip when measuring EMS accessibility, either considering the DRT [14, 18], or from the patient’s location to an emergency hospital (Transport Trip, TT) [48, 53]. Although some studies have incorporated two-stage ambulance trips into accessibility measurements [55], none have integrated PAT accessibility into their models. While existing methods for measuring accessibility are well-suited to general healthcare services, they are not fully applicable to EMS systems, in which emergency care teams must complete two consecutive trips (DRT + PAT) to provide immediate medical care to patients in their homes. Moreover, the difficulty of PAT accessibility is largely influenced by building height and occupancy density, which vary significantly across urban areas [36, 39], especially in metropolises. For example, some patients who live near EMS stations may reside in high-rise buildings with high an occupancy density—leading to extended PAT times that can significantly affect the EMS response time.

To address this problem, this study develops a spatial model to measure EMS accessibility, which not only considers DRT accessibility by accounting for the spatial interactions between EMS providers and patients, but also integrates PAT accessibility by incorporating the impact of the building height and occupancy density across space. This approach better aligns the measurement with the unique service characteristics of EMS, providing a more accurate assessment of when EMS personnel can reach a patient to deliver first-aid care and thereby improving EMS service quality. Wuhan, China, is selected as the case study area, and the developed model would be applied. This city has a long history and has experienced rapid growth in recent decades, leading to the construction of numerous residential high-rise buildings. Additionally, Wuhan’s large population and substantial aging population point to a substantial future demand for emergency services, making it a highly representative context for this research.

This research is organized as follows. The next section reviews the relevant studies measuring EMS accessibility, focusing on the different trips. The model specification section introduces the proposed model, which aligns with EMS characteristics by integrating both DRT and PAT accessibility, ensuring that EMS teams can promptly reach patients to deliver emergency medical care. An empirical study is then conducted to measure EMS accessibility in Wuhan, China. Finally, the paper summarizes its key results, policy implications, and contributions.

Related work

Over the past few decades, numerous approaches have been proposed to evaluate potential spatial accessibility to healthcare services. Among them, distance-based measures are commonly applied to assess EMS accessibility using either distance or travel-time indicators. The distance measure typically considers the straight-line or network distance between the nearest EMS station and a patient’s location, while the travel-time measure incorporates ambulance speed variations across different road types and traffic conditions.

Another widely used approach is the provider-to-population ratio (PPR), which is typically derived from data aggregated at defined spatial units, such as administrative regions or healthcare catchment areas. PPRs are particularly useful for EMS policy analysis, as many countries establish minimum service standards based on supply-to-demand ratios—for instance, France mandates one ambulance per 57,000 residents [10]. In contrast, gravity-based models integrate aspects of both distance and population measures by capturing spatial interactions between service providers and potential users, moderated by a distance-decay function. Building on Joseph and Bantock [16], Luo and Wang [23] developed the two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) method, which has since become one of the most widely applied gravity-based techniques.

Numerous scholars have enhanced accessibility models to better suit the healthcare sector. For example, many studies have enhanced gravity-based models by modifying their distance-decay functions using different analytical approaches, such as a discrete function [22], Gaussian function [8, 15], kernel density [12], and hybrid function [5]. Wang, [42] consolidated these advancements into the generalized 2SFCA (G-2SFCA) model, incorporating diverse distance-decay functions. Other modifications to accessibility models include integrating various transportation modes [28, 37], demographic factors [30], and potential interactions between healthcare supply and demand [9, 41]. Additionally, temporal factors [48, 49], online healthcare services [1, 21], and the integration of spatial and non-spatial factors or travel-time competition [41, 51] have also been examined. Recent studies have further addressed EMS accessibility during extreme weather conditions, such as urban flooding [18, 38].

In the field of EMS planning, scholars typically measure EMS accessibility by examining different trips; however, most researchers focus exclusively on single-trip scenarios. First, many studies address EMS accessibility by considering DRT, which evaluates how quickly an EMS team can reach patient communities to provide emergency care [19, 25]. For instance, Xiong et al. [49] developed three spatiotemporal EMS accessibility methods to measure DRT accessibility, taking into account real-traffic conditions and historical EMS demand. Second, some research focuses on a single trip from a patient’s community to a hospital or emergency center. For instance, Xia et al. [48] introduced a GPS-based model to measure EMS accessibility from patient locations to hospitals at different times. More recently, few studies have examined the multi-trip nature of EMS, measuring overall accessibility by considering the ambulance’s entire journey—beginning at EMS stations, traveling to patient communities, and then continuing on to hospitals (DRT + PAT). This more holistic perspective provides deeper insights into EMS accessibility [24]. Along these lines, Luo et al. [26] investigated the optimal placement of EMS stations and emergency hospitals, ensuring that both DRT and Transport Trip (TT) intervals can be completed promptly. Despite these developments, current research on EMS accessibility remains almost entirely centered on the ambulance’s road trip. The impact of PAT accessibility—particularly the effect of building height and density—has yet to be incorporated into existing models.

At present, numerous studies calculate the time individuals spend moving between floors in a building, primarily focusing on the round-trip time (RTT) of its elevators. These studies can be divided into two main categories: (1) general group control (GGC) models and (2) up-peak group control (UPGC) models [35]. The former addresses a mix of traffic patterns inside a building, such as outgoing, incoming, and inter-floor traffic [29], whereas the latter focuses on scenarios in which the majority of people are entering the building and heading to upper floors [34]. Currently, UPGC is widely adopted in practice. The classic UPGC model was developed by Barney and Dis Santos [3] to calculate RTT, relying heavily on two variables: the probable number of stops and the highest reversal floor. With reasonable estimates of the two variables, the remaining parameters can be determined, enabling the simulation of RTT. The traditional equations to compute H and S were developed by Schroeder [32] and subsequently improved by Al-Sharif et al. [2].

Although extensive research addresses EMS accessibility and examines the time individuals spend moving between floors in buildings, there is a notable gap in the literature regarding how variations in urban building height affect the accessibility of emergency personnel entering patients’ homes—particularly when stairs or elevators are involved. In other words, no comprehensive study currently examines EMS accessibility in terms of both ambulance road trips and the in-building trips that follow. To address this gap, this paper proposes a new accessibility model that aims to comprehensively measure spatial accessibility to EMS by integrating the road trip (DRT) and in-building movements (PAT). This approach leverages spatial data on EMS station locations as well as variations in urban building height and density to provide a more holistic assessment of EMS accessibility.

Model specification

This section includes three parts. First, the form of the G-2SFCA model [42] is introduced, which focuses primarily on accessibility from a patient’s location to general healthcare. Second, based on the G-2SFCA model, this study modifies a generalized two-step floating building catchment area (G-2SFBCA) model to measure EMS accessibility in buildings by incorporating the spatial differences in building height and occupancy density across space. The proposed model more accurately reflects the realities of EMS services, where emergency personnel must consider multi-segment trips (DRT + PAT) to reach patients. The third section presents how to estimate the travel time spent by the EMS team ascending within the building (PAT), mainly based on UPGC models developed by Barney and Dis Santos [3] and Al-Sharif et al. [2].

Empirical study

Study area and EMS planning

Wuhan, the capital of Hubei Province in central China, lies at the confluence of the Yangtze and Han Rivers on the Jianghan Plain. Its strategic location, extensive transport network, and strong scientific and educational foundations have made it a major hub for economic development in central China. The city covers 8,569.2 km² and consists of 13 districts, including seven urban and six suburban or rural ones. As most residents are concentrated in the urban districts, this study focuses on these seven districts as the primary research area.

Demographically, Wuhan has nearly13 million population [46]. With continuous urban growth, the city is experiencing a pronounced aging trend. By 2023, more than 2 million residents were aged 60 and above, accounting for 23.05% of the total population and signifying Wuhan’s transition into a severely aging society. This demographic shift poses a major challenge for future urban development and highlights the increasing demand for emergency medical services (EMS). In 2020, Wuhan government [45] launched the ‘12-minute EMS response zone’ initiative, ensuring that EMS teams can reach patients and provide medical care within 12 min of receiving an emergency call.

The height of residential buildings has increased significantly as Wuhan has grown and its population expanded. In the early 21st century, most residential buildings were relatively low, typically ranging from 6 to 12 floors. However, as urbanization accelerated between 2008 and 2015, land resources in Wuhan became more limited, and population density in urban areas rose. To accommodate the increasing demand for housing, more high-rise residential buildings (15–30 floors) began to appear. After 2015, the residential building height continued to rise, with numerous super high-rise buildings (30 floors and above) emerging, particularly in newly developed districts. Currently, high-rise buildings have gradually become the main form of residential housing in Wuhan. Figure 2 illustrates that buildings without elevators are primarily concentrated in the central and surrounding parts of Wuhan, whereas the transitional zones between the city core and the outskirts are largely dominated by residential buildings equipped with elevators.

Data and model setting

Data used in this study fall into three categories: demand, service providers, and the road network. On the demand side, patient locations are represented by the geographic centroids of local communities (known as shequ in China). Shequ is the smallest administrative units characterized by defined geographic boundaries and close social interactions among residents. Data on 1,228 shequs within Wuhan’s main urban area were sourced from the Geographical Information Monitoring Cloud Platform (http://www.dsac.cn/). Residential building data in each shequ were obtained from the HomeLink (https://wh.lianjia.com/) that is one of the largest real-estate agencies in China and free to offer basic information about various types of properties. The building data include details such as spatial locations, number of floors, whether there is an elevator, the ratio of elevators to households and others. There are 82,005 residential buildings with elevators and 9,871 buildings without elevators were adopted here.

In terms of EMS provider, urban area of Wuhan has 54 EMS stations, and their locations sourced from Baidu Map (https://map.baidu.com/) – the biggest online map service in China. Service capacity, measured by the number of ambulances at each station, was obtained from Wuhan Renmin Hospital (https://www.rmhospital.com/). Data on Wuhan’s road network and the corresponding average speeds for different road types were sourced from the Hubei Institute of Land Surveying and Mapping.

As for the model setting, this study selected 12 min as the threshold of the G-2SFBCA model, aligning with the “12-minute emergency response zone” standard set [45], ensuring that ambulances can reach patients and provide medical assistance within the specified time after a patient places an emergency call. According to Wuhan’s Seventh National Population Census [47], the average number of members in each household ( P) is 2.47 people in Wuhan. According to the survey of 500 residents in this study, their average frequency of going out each day was 1.63 times (Q = 1.63).

As shown in Fig. 3(a), the spatial pattern of building height across shequs exhibits clear spatial variability. The number of floors exhibits a pattern of being lower in the central and outer areas, while higher in the transition areas between these two zones. Specifically, high-rise buildings (over 24 floors) are primarily located in the north of Qingshan and Jiangan, as well as the south of Hongshan. In comparison, low-rise buildings (lower than 7 floors) are mainly sited in the east and central areas of city center. Figure 3(b) quantifies the variation in average building height across the traffic ring-road zones. Within the 1st Ring Road, buildings show a median height of approximately 15 floors with a narrow interquartile range, indicating relatively homogeneous mid-rise development in the historic core. Between the 1st and 2nd Rings, the median rises to around 20 floors with a wider spread and higher upper quartile, reflecting newer, higher-rise developments in this transitional zone. The 2nd–3rd Ring zone exhibits the highest median (around25 floors) and largest interquartile range, with upper values exceeding 30 floors, pointing to significant high-rise development in the expanding urban periphery. Outside the 3rd Ring, the median drops sharply to 15 floors with the broadest value range, reflecting a mix of low-rise suburban housing and scattered high-rises in emerging sub-centers.

Figure 6 illustrates EMS accessibility computed using the G-2SFBCA and G-2SFCA methods, incorporating different distance decay functions. Accessibility scores are grouped into five different levels based on the natural breakpoint method. While the patterns of accessibility scores generated by the proposed G-2SFBCA model are similar to those of the G-2SFCA method, several notable differences are evident. For similarities, regardless of the distance decay function applied, the spatial distributions of EMS accessibility calculated by both models consistently demonstrate a general trend of high accessibility level in the central area and low accessibility level in the periphery. Both models also highlight accessibility gaps in the east and south parts of the city, where EMS teams are unable to reach patients within 12 min. For differences, the G-2SFBCA model indicates higher EMS accessibility in the central urban area, with most regions classified as having a “Very High” level of accessibility (Fig. 6(a), (c), (e), and (g)). In contrast, the G-2SFCA method assigns the majority of the central urban area a “High” level of accessibility, which is comparatively lower than the results from the G-2SFBCA model (Fig. 6(b), (d), (f), and (h)). In the transitional zone between the city center and the suburbs—such as the central-eastern part of Hongshan—the EMS accessibility calculated by the G-2SFBCA model is relatively lower than that calculated by the G-2SFCA model. This difference can be attributed to the inclusion of PAT trips in the G-2SFBCA model, which amplifies the disparity in EMS travel times between the city center and the transitional zones. As a result, accessibility is further enhanced in the central urban area while declining even more in the transitional zones.

Discussion

This study refines the traditional G-2SFCA model into the G-2SFBCA model to assess EMS accessibility by incorporating both DRT and PAT trips, while also accounting for factors such as building height and occupancy density. Based on an empirical study in Wuhan, China, significant spatial variation in average residential building heights and occupancy density across shequs was observed. This variation results in notable differences in PAT accessibility, forming a “sandwich-like” spatial pattern, with lower travel times in the central and peripheral areas and higher travel times in the transitional zones. Compared to results that do not consider PAT trips, the inclusion of PAT trips leads to a 12.9% reduction in the population able to reach an EMS team within 5 min and a 3.2% increase in the population located beyond the 12-minute response threshold. Additionally, regardless of the distance decay function applied, the spatial patterns of EMS accessibility computed by the proposed model show comparatively lower accessibility in transitional zones than those generated by the G-2SFCA model. This difference occurs because DRT trips amplify spatial variations in EMS travel time, enhancing accessibility in central areas while reducing it in transitional zones.

With the acceleration of economic development and urbanization, noticeable spatial differences in building height and occupancy density have emerged in many cities around the world, highlighting the importance of considering PAT and building characteristics in the G-2SFBCA model. For example, megacities in China have experienced dramatic vertical expansions of building height, especially in the sub-centers [50]. The variations in building height and occupancy density are also found in major cities in other countries such as the United States [20], Japan [40]. Zambon et al. [54] indicates that vertical expansion and upward development have become important ways to address urban housing needs in the context of growing populations and increasingly constrained urban space. The increase in building height will have a significant impact on PAT travel time. Unlike general healthcare, EMS services are highly time-sensitive, where even a short delay in each trip can lead to a rapid decrease in the EMS survival rate [11, 13]. Therefore, the proposed G-2SFBCA models in this study demonstrates broad applicability, as it comprehensively accounts for various types of trips and building height in measuring EMS accessibility.

The height and occupancy density of residential buildings exhibit a “low-high-low” sandwich structure from the city center to the suburbs, which significantly impacts EMS accessibility, particularly for PAT. First, most residential buildings in metropolitan city centers, are old and outdated with a long history, and urban renewal efforts in those places often focus on renovating these older communities rather than tear-down and rebuilding housing. Consequently, city centers generally have lower residential building heights and occupancy densities [33]. These characteristics and sufficient EMS resources lead to shorter PAT travel times and excellent EMS accessibility in city centers when the G-2SFBCA model is applied. Second, in the suburban areas of large cities, abundant land and lower population density lessen the need for high-intensity development. Consequently, low-rise housing is sufficient to accommodate residents, leading to shorter PAT travel times. Despite relatively favorable PAT accessibility, overall EMS accessibility remains limited due to the sparse distribution of emergency facilities in these peripheral zones. In addition, the transitional areas act as crucial zones linking the urban core with surrounding regions. Urbanization-driven housing demand has intensified building density and height [4], resulting in longer PAT travel times. Despite relatively sufficient EMS resources in these transitional zones, the concentration of high-rise, high-density buildings markedly constrains EMS accessibility when evaluated using the G-2SFBCA model.

Although the UPGC model was originally developed for elevator operations in commercial buildings under up-peak conditions, it is employed here as a practical approximation to estimate in-building travel time rather than as a precise behavioral model of elevator usage. The UPGC model is selected because it provides a tractable and theoretically grounded representation of congestion effects, allowing PAT to be operationalized using widely available building attributes. Moreover, adopting a peak-condition formulation offers a conservative estimate of travel time, which is appropriate in EMS contexts where underestimation of delays may lead to biased accessibility assessments. However, it should be noted that actual in-building travel time may vary across residential communities and time periods, influenced by factors such as occupancy patterns, elevator availability, and daily activity rhythms. It should be emphasized that the core contribution of this study is not the specific modeling of elevator dynamics, but the integration of the Patient Access Trip (PAT) into EMS accessibility measurement. Any reasonable method capable of capturing the relationship between building height, occupancy density, and vertical travel delay could be incorporated into this framework. In addition, the behavior of EMS personnel may differ from that of ordinary residents; for example through priority access to elevators or the use of alternative strategies such as stairs. As a result, the estimated PAT should be interpreted as a generalized approximation rather than an exact prediction of travel time. Nevertheless, the resulting PAT estimates exhibit realistic magnitudes and spatial variation, indicating that the model is sufficient for capturing first-order differences in vertical accessibility.

The study provides several policy implications. First, the empirical study shows that spatial variations in EMS accessibility can reflect the effectiveness of public policies on EMS management and planning. EMS accessibility in the eastern part of Hongshan is extremely poor. EMS stations are unable to provide emergency medical care within 12 min, and further EMS stations can be built in this area to meet the needs of EMS demand. Specifically, spatial optimization models can be used to determine the optimal locations for EMS stations when healthcare resources are limited, ensuring that EMS teams can reach the maximum number of households within a specific time frame (e.g., 12 min in DRT) [25]. Similarly, spatial optimization research can be used to determine the minimum number and optimal locations for new EMS stations required in a region, ensuring that any location can be reached within a specified travel time [52]. Second, after considering PAT accessibility in the G-2SFBCA model, the empirical study indicates that EMS accessibility of some areas (e.g., the north of Hongshan, and the south of Qingshan) has relatively declined. Therefore, more EMS resources need to be allocated in these areas in the future to offset the impact of high-density and high-rise residential buildings on EMS accessibility. Third, since the height and occupancy density of residential buildings can impact EMS accessibility, especially in transitional areas, EMS teams should develop collaborative communication strategies with patients and local communities to facilitate timely preparations before an ambulance arrives. Additionally, local communities should routinely organize training and educational sessions on emergency response protocols to enhance the accessibility and effectiveness of PAT.

This study identifies several areas that warrant further exploration. First, EMS services are influenced by various real-time factors, such as traffic conditions and dynamic population movements. Exploring how to integrate the G-2SFBCA model into spatiotemporal dimensions is an important direction for future research. Second, the empirical study discussed here only considered the characteristics of residential buildings, without accounting for features of office buildings or commercial spaces used by potential users. Future research could address this gap by incorporating different time periods (e.g., working hours and off-work hours) to evaluate the accessibility characteristics of various building types more comprehensively. Additionally, it should be noted that elevator usage conditions vary throughout the day. A potential limitation is that this study did not fully capture differences in elevator waiting time between different time period. Due to the lack of time-specific residential flow data, a uniform waiting-time assumption was applied, which may overestimate patient access time during nighttime or other low-demand periods. While this approach captures congestion effects in a conservative manner, future research could incorporate stochastic and time-dependent elevator usage patterns to better reflect real-world conditions. In addition, future research could account for the regional heterogeneity in EMS planning policies and establish differentiated response time thresholds tailored to different areas.

Conclusion

Given that DRT and PAT significantly impact survival rates, this study develops the G-2SFBCA models, to measure EMS accessibility to explicitly consider both trips. The results of empirical study in Wuhan, China, suggest PAT travel times from the city center to the suburbs exhibit a “low-high-low” pattern, primarily driven by the spatial distribution differences in urban building heights. Furthermore, irrespective of the distance-decay function applied, the spatial patterns of EMS accessibility derived from the G-2SFBCA models consistently exhibit relatively lower accessibility in transitional areas (e.g., the north of Hongshan and south of Qingshan) and higher accessibility in the city center compared to the results of previous models. The findings suggest that traditional 2SFCA-based models, which neglect in-building travel time, may substantially overestimate effective EMS response and survival windows in high-density urban areas. By incorporating PAT, this study provides a more realistic assessment of EMS accessibility, particularly in vertically complex urban environments. Given the global variation in building height and occupancy density, the proposed model offers broad applicability for EMS planning.
