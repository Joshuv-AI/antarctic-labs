Clinical research progress of menin inhibitors for acute myeloid leukemia: latest updates from the 2025 ASH Annual Meeting
Authors: Chenchen Ma, Siyuan Cui, Jingyi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMenin inhibition has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for acute myeloid leukemia (AML) driven by KMT2A rearrangements or NPM1 mutations. Novel menin inhibitors, including revumenib, bleximenib, ziftomenib, and enzomenib, are currently under clinical evaluation, and selected updated clinical results were presented at the 2025 American Society of Hematology (ASH) Annual Meeting. This brief review summarizes the key findings and discusses the emerging clinical questions regarding combination strategies, treatment sequencing, and molecularly defined use of menin inhibitors.Keywords: Menin inhibitors, Clinical trials, Acute myeloid leukemia (AML)

To the editor

Menin inhibition has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) with KMT2A rearrangements (KMT2Ar) or NPM1 mutations (NPM1m). Pivotal clinical studies have supported recent regulatory approvals in defined relapsed/refractory (R/R) molecular subgroups, including revumenib for KMT2Ar acute leukemia and NPM1m AML, and ziftomenib for adult R/R NPM1m AML [1–3]. At the 2025 ASH Annual Meeting, selected updated results were reported for several menin inhibitors, including revumenib, bleximenib, ziftomenib, and enzomenib, across R/R, frontline, and maintenance settings. Collectively, these reports suggest that menin inhibitors are being explored not only as single agents in relapsed disease, but also in combination regimens and in earlier treatment settings.

Revumenib

Revumenib, a first-in-class menin inhibitor with regulatory approval for R/R KMT2Ar acute leukemia, has also shown clinical activity in leukemias with NPM1 mutations and NUP98 rearrangements. Among the agents discussed here, revumenib has one of the most mature clinical datasets. In the phase II AUGMENT-101 study, revumenib monotherapy achieved a CR/CRh rate of 26% in R/R NPM1m AML, with responses observed across patients previously treated with venetoclax, FLT3 inhibitors, IDH inhibitors, or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) [4]. In a small cohort of newly diagnosed patients, the all-oral combination of revumenib with decitabine/cedazuridine and venetoclax produced a 94% overall response rate [5], whereas revumenib combined with intensive chemotherapy showed preliminary high remission rates in a small number of fit patients [6]. Early combination with gilteritinib also appeared feasible in FLT3-mutated disease, although QTc prolongation remained an important safety consideration, particularly in the presence of CYP3A4 interactions [7]. These data support further evaluation of revumenib across multiple settings, although its optimal use outside approved indications or clinical trials remains undefined. Bleximenib

Bleximenib is also being developed across multiple treatment settings. In newly diagnosed fit patients, the combination with intensive chemotherapy showed high remission rates and manageable toxicity [8]. In R/R AML, an all-oral bleximenib-venetoclax doublet demonstrated preliminary activity even in heavily pretreated patients, including those with prior venetoclax exposure [9]. In addition, the phase III Camelot-2 trial may help clarify the role of bleximenib in older or unfit newly diagnosed patients receiving venetoclax and azacitidine [10].

Ziftomenib

In the KOMET-007 study, ziftomenib combined with venetoclax and azacitidine showed encouraging activity in R/R AML, particularly in NPM1-mutated disease, with a low reported incidence of differentiation syndrome and no apparent QTc signal in this update [11]. A separate ongoing study is exploring ziftomenib with quizartinib, reflecting growing interest in combining menin inhibition with FLT3-directed therapy in molecularly selected patients [12].

Enzomenib

Enzomenib monotherapy produced responses in both KMT2Ar and NPM1m R/R AML, with median overall survival exceeding 8 months in both groups [13]. Its triplet combination with venetoclax and azacitidine also appeared active, particularly in patients without prior venetoclax or menin inhibitor exposure, while maintaining a manageable toxicity profile and no grade ≥ 3 QT prolongation [14]. Interpretation

The ASH 2025 updates further support the activity of menin inhibitors in KMT2A-rearranged and NPM1-mutated AML, although their place in routine practice is still being defined. Testing for KMT2A rearrangement and NPM1 mutation is increasingly clinically relevant at diagnosis and, when feasible, at relapse, because these results may affect access to approved therapy and clinical trials. In R/R KMT2Ar or NPM1m AML, an approved menin inhibitor is a reasonable option when available, particularly for patients in whom response could permit transplantation or another consolidation approach. Monotherapy may be suitable for selected patients, whereas those with proliferative relapse, FLT3 co-mutation, prior venetoclax failure, or adverse co-mutations may be considered for clinical trials evaluating rational combinations, including menin inhibitors with venetoclax-based therapy or FLT3 inhibitors. In newly diagnosed older or unfit patients, azacitidine/venetoclax remains the lower-intensity standard, and menin inhibitor triplets are best pursued within clinical trials until randomized data show added benefit. For younger fit patients, intensive chemotherapy is still appropriate for many, although lower-intensity venetoclax- or menin inhibitor-based approaches may become attractive for selected molecular subgroups if durable MRD-negative remissions are achieved. Co-mutations, particularly FLT3 and RAS-pathway lesions, should influence trial selection because they may contribute to resistance and may require rational combinations.

Conclusion

Menin inhibitors have shown clinically meaningful activity in several AML settings, particularly in KMT2A-rearranged and NPM1-mutated disease. However, most ASH 2025 data come from conference abstracts and early-phase studies with small sample sizes, short follow-up, and heterogeneous patient populations and treatment backbones. Direct comparison between regimens is therefore premature. The optimal combination strategy, timing of use, and sequencing after prior venetoclax or menin inhibitor exposure remain unresolved. Recent reports suggest that outcomes after menin inhibitor failure are variable, and switching to another menin inhibitor should not be assumed to provide benefit without reassessing disease biology and resistance mechanisms [15–17]. Prospective studies with longer follow-up are needed to guide biomarker-based use in routine AML practice.
