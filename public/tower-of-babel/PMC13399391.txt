Associations of finerenone with reduced insulin resistance in patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease: A real‐world observational study
Authors: Xin Zhao, Bohan Liu, Ran Sun, Yaxuan Shi, Ya Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThere is evidence linking activation of aldosterone activity with insulin resistance and metabolic risk factors. We hypothesized that finerenone, a nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, might improve insulin resistance in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) and chronic kidney disease (CKD).Materials and MethodsIn a single‐center retrospective study including 45 patients with T2D and CKD treated with finerenone for 12 months between June 2023 and June 2025, we assessed insulin resistance using the Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA‐IR) and triglyceride‐glucose (TYG) index along with metabolic and renal parameters measured at baseline, month‐6, and month‐12.ResultsIn these patients (n = 45; mean age 52.2 ± 13.9 years; 64.4% male; disease duration >10 years in 48.9%; mean HbA1c 9.2 ± 2.5%; 60.0% treated with SGLT2i; 33.3% on GLP1RA), 12‐month treatment with finerenone was associated with improvement in HOMA‐IR (median [interquartile range]: baseline: 34.40 [12.84, 71.50], month‐6: 25.22 [13.54, 78.19], month‐12: 19.44 [9.51, 45.75] [P = 0.038, Friedman test]). TYG index (baseline: 9.49 [8.86, 10.29] month‐6: 9.04 [8.54, 9.71], and month‐12: 8.93 [8.44, 9.62] mg/dL [P < 0.007 Friedman tset]); urinary albumin: creatinine ratio (baseline: 377.39 [127.80, 1327.29], month‐6: 342.96 [87.58, 1442.12], month‐12: 249.52 [68.04, 1198.03] mg/g [p = 0.05, Friedman test]) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (baseline: 112.01 [85.87, 120.00], month‐6: 103.92 [75.25, 120.00], and month‐12120.00 [80.46, 120.00] mL/min/1.73 m2 [P = 0.002 Feirdman test]). There was no treatment discontinuations due to hyperkalemia.ConclusionIn real‐world practice, 12‐month treatment with finerenone was associated with reduced insulin resistance and improved kidney function in patients with T2D and CKD and a manageable safety profile.Keywords: Finerenone, Insulin resistance, T2D and CKD

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes refers to a category of metabolic diseases characterized by elevated blood glucose levels 1 . It is one of the primary etiologies of chronic kidney disease (CKD) 2 and a significant risk factor for the progression of CKD to end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) 3 . The 2023 International Diabetes Federation (IDF) “Diabetes and Kidney Disease Report” indicates that 30%–40% of patients with diabetes develop CKD 4 . The high incidence of CKD in type 2 diabetes (T2D) is attributable not only to hyperglycemia alone but also insulin resistance (IR) which can drive progression of T2D and CKD 5 , 6 . Insulin resistance may impair kidney function in T2D through dysregulation of glucose metabolism, oxidative stress, increased apoptosis, altered renal hemodynamics, and mitochondrial dysfunction 7 , 8 . Besides, IR is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and all‐cause mortality 9 . Several lines of evidence indicate synergistic interactions between IR and aldosterone in causing progressive injury to glomerular and tubulointerstitial compartments, mediated by increased endothelin‐1 production 10 , 11 , activation of inflammatory signaling pathways 12 , 13 , and mitochondrial dysfunction 14 . Insulin resistance promotes secretion of pro‐inflammatory factors (such as TNF‐α, IL‐6, and MCP‐1) from adipose tissues resulting in activation of innate immune cells (e.g., macrophages) in the kidney to exacerbate widespread inflammation 15 . Collectively, these changes can alter the microcirculatory environment with abnormal release of extracellular vesicles, gut microbiota dysbiosis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and renal fibrosis 16 .

Activation of the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) by hyperinsulinemia and aldosterone sets up a “metabolic‐hormonal vicious cycle” to drive decline in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and progression of proteinuria 17 . Other researchers have shown that products secreted by adipocytes can directly stimulate aldosterone secretion from human adrenocortical cells 18 . Others have reported that plasma aldosterone was negatively associated with high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) and positively associated with triglycerides (TG) 19 . To this end, visceral fat associated IR was closely related to elevated plasma aldosterone and other adrenal steroids 20 , 21 .

In support of this notion, patients with primary aldosteronism have increased risk of diabetes with excess aldosterone enhancing insulin clearance and reducing insulin synthesis in pancreatic β‐cells 22 . Activation of MR by aldosterone can induce white adipocyte differentiation in 3T3‐L1 and 3T3‐F442A cells contributing to obesity and metabolic complications 22 . In patients with primary aldosteronism, postoperative immunoreactive insulin levels significantly increased compared to pre‐operative period, albeit plasma glucose levels remained unchanged 23 . Taken together, aside from causing IR, aldosterone excess may perturb glucose metabolism by inhibiting insulin secretion.

These mechanisms suggest that amelioration of IR may be a therapeutic target for T2D and CKD. Several medications may improve IR in T2D and CKD. These include sodium glucose transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, ARNI (angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor), glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RA), and thiazolidinediones (TZD) 24 . Metrics used to evaluate IR include homeostasis assessment model (HOMA)‐IR, Insulin Sensitivity Index (ISI), Triglyceride‐Glucose (TyG) index, Lipid ratios, and Matsuda Index. Although HOMA‐IR derived from concurrent measurement of plasma glucose and insulin or C‐peptide is widely used, TyG index has also been shown to be a sensitive and specific marker for IR. The calculation of TyG index relies only on fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and TG, making it a cost‐effective and accessible metric 25 . The high sensitivity of TyG with low false‐negative rate makes this a popular index of IR in large‐scale epidemiological investigations for identifying individuals at risk for diabetes 26 and CVD 27 .

The novel nonsteroidal MR antagonist (nsMRA), finerenone, has been shown to confer cardiorenal protection. There are also reports suggesting its possible benefits on IR 28 by blocking MR activation by hyperinsulinemia, reducing expression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐6) and pro‐fibrotic factors (e.g., TGF‐β) 29 . In an obese mouse model, finerenone improved IR by promoting recruitment of interscapular brown adipose tissue with enhanced mitochondrial function and improved lipid metabolism 30 . These findings suggest that finerenone may have improve IR albeit with scarcity of clinical data. In this study, using real‐world data, we examined changes in IR‐related indicators (TyG index, HOMA‐IR) along with glucose, lipid and renal parameters in patients with T2D and CKD treated with finerenone.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

This was a single‐center, retrospective cohort including patients with T2D and CKD started on finerenone between June 2023 and June 2025 at the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University. The study protocol was approved by the hospital's ethics committee with waiving of informed consent due to its retrospective and observational nature.

Participant selection

During the study period, 256 patients were started on finerenone, with 45 patients fulfilling all inclusion and exclusion criteria for final analysis. Patients excluded from the analysis (256 – 45 = 221) had similar profiles as the 45 patients included in the analysis (Table S1). Figure 1 shows the selection process of the cohort.

Flowchart for patient selection. A total of 256 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease (T2DM‐CKD) who initially received finerenone treatment were screened. After step‐by‐step exclusion of patients with type 1 diabetes, other kidney diseases, medication changes during follow‐up, and incomplete clinical data, 45 patients were finally included for data analysis.

Inclusion criteria

Only patients with documentation of all data for analysis were included. Diagnosis of T2D and CKD was based on 2024 KDIGO Guidelines update for management of T2D and CKD 31 , 32 . All patients were 18 years old or older and had T2D‐related CKD with evidence of retinopathy. None of the patients had used MRA within 1 year before initiation of nsMRA. All enrolled patients were on stable regimens of blood pressure (BP), lipid, and glucose lowering drugs for at least 1 month prior to starting finerenone and remained unchanged throughout the follow‐up period.

Exclusion criteria excluded type 1 diabetes (T1D) defined by clinical criteria (acute ketotic onset, young age, and/or low C‐peptide levels), other diabetes subtypes and non‐T2D related CKD. The latter was suggested by presence of active urinary sediment abnormalities (microscopic hematuria, dysmorphic red blood cells >5 per high power field [HPF], or red blood cell casts); rapid decline of estimated GFR (defined as yearly decline >5 mL/min/1.73 m2 in the absence of nephrotoxic drugs or acute events); new‐onset or rapidly worsening nephrotic‐range proteinuria within a short period (urine protein >3.5 g/day or a sharp rise in urinary albumin creatinine ratio [UACR]). Other secondary causes of CKD were excluded based on medical history, physical examination, and laboratory tests. These included systemic lupus erythematosus, vasculitis, monoclonal immunoglobulin‐related disease, chronic urinary tract obstruction, or recurrent kidney stones. In patients with nephrotic‐range proteinuria (UACR>3,000 mg/g), serological screening and urinary sediment analysis were performed to exclude other glomerular diseases. Patients with long‐standing severe hypertension (especially predating the onset of T2D) with features of benign hypertensive nephrosclerosis (e.g., mild increase in UACR <300 mg/g), prominent nocturia and slow decline in eGFR and fundoscopic features of predominantly hypertensive arteriolosclerosis. Patients with body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m2 and those who developed allergy to finerenone were also excluded (Figure 1).

Clinical parameters

Baseline demographic included age, gender, duration of diabetes, concomitant medication use, and medical history. Venous blood samples were collected in the morning after at least 8 h of fasting at baseline, month‐6, and month‐12. Laboratory parameters included FPG, HbA1c, total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), HDL‐C, LDL‐C, UACR, eGFR, serum creatinine (Scr), BUN, uric acid (UA), and were measured in the central laboratory of the hospital.

Fasting plasma glucose was measured using the hexokinase method. Fasting insulin (FINS) was measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Roche Cobas e801) for calculating IR indices. Triglycerides and HDL‐C were measured using enzymatic methods. HOMA‐IR was calculated using the formula: HOMA‐IR = [FPG (mmol/L) × FINS (μIU /mL)]/22.5. TyG index was calculated using the formula: TyG index = Ln [Fasting TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL)/2].

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint was change in the mean TYG index from baseline to month‐6 and month‐12 of treatment. The secondary endpoint was change in TC, TG, HDL‐C, LDL‐C, HbA1c, Scr, BUN, UA, and other related indicators from baseline to month‐6 and month‐12 after treatment.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package of Social Sciences (SPSS 26.0). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) and frequency (n, %) as appropriate. Within‐group comparison was employed by paired t‐tests for normally distributed data and Wilcoxon rank sum test for skewed data. For comparisons among three or more groups, repeated measures anova was applied for data with normal distribution, and Friedman's test for skewed data. A P <0.05 (two‐sided) was considered significant.

RESULTS

A total of 256 patients with T2D and CKD were enrolled in this study from June 2023 to June 2025. The observation period was 12 months when 45 patients eligible for analysis (18%) completed the 12‐month follow‐up while on finerenone. A total of 211 patients were excluded due to loss to follow‐up (n = 170), other causes of kidney disease (n = 29), or T1D (n = 12).

Among the 45 patients (64% men) analyzed, the mean age was 52.24 ± 13.93 years, 4.4% had coronary heart disease, 48.9% had hypertension, 48.9% had diabetes for over 10 years, and 17.8% had retinopathy. As shown in Table 1, 6.7% (n = 3) of these patients had nephrotic‐range proteinuria; the diagnostic confirmation of diabetic nephropathy as the cause of CKD in these three patients is detailed in Table S2. Concurrent treatment included ACEI/ARB (55.6%), SGLT2 inhibitors (60%), metformin (44.4%), and lipid‐lowering medications (71.1%) (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

In this real‐world study, we first reported improved TyG index and HOMA‐IR following 12‐month treatment with finerenone in patients with T2D and CKD. These findings suggested that aside from its cardiorenal protective effects, finerenone may also ameliorate IR. TyG index is a sensitive and specific marker of IR 25 and its improvement with finerenone is consistent with the mechanistic effects of MR blockade. In preclinical studies, finerenone reduced expression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines 29 and promoted brown adipose tissue recruitment to improve energy metabolism 30 . However, there was considerable overlap in the confidence intervals of the mean TyG index at different time points suggesting variable responses and that larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these findings. Besides, the clinical meaning of increased recruitment of brown adipose tissue 30 by finerenone remained uncertain in this heterogeneous cohort with T2D.

In this study, the improvement in HOMA‐IR was greater than that in TyG index. Whilst HOMA‐IR is a primary indicator of hepatic insulin resistance with increased hepatic glucose output 33 , the TyG index is more closely associated with systemic dysregulation of lipid metabolism 34 . The discrepant results in these two outcomes suggest finerenone might have greater effects on restoring hepatic insulin and inhibiting hepatic gluconeogenesis than modulating lipid metabolism. Gold‐standard methods such as hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp technique will be needed to confirm these observations.

Although these metabolic improvements with finerenone were modest, they might have implications on clinical outcomes. The activation of MR by hyperinsulinemia with impaired insulin signaling and reduced peripheral glucose uptake can be ameliorated by MR inhibition 5 . Other experimental studies suggested MR blockade could enhance insulin sensitivity by improving mitochondrial function and lipid metabolism 30 . The improved IR with finerenone in our patients with CKD and T2D provided indirect support to these experimental observations. The benefits of finerenone on insulin resistance, inflammation, and fibrosis add to the disease‐modifying nature of this drug. These pleiotropic effects are particularly relevant to patients with T2D in whom excess adiposity can worsen IR and chronic inflammation. To this end, MR overactivation has been implicated in cardiorenal fibrosis as well as IR associated with metabolic syndrome 22 , 23 . By reducing MR overactivation, finerenone may improve IR to confer additional metabolic benefits beyond organ protection.

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not perform renal biopsy to confirm the nature of CKD in these patients with T2D. We applied clinical criteria to include patients with CKD primarily due to diabetes, although the multicausality of CKD is now well recognized. Larger prospective studies incorporating pathological validation will help refine the cohort. Second, to satisfy the inclusion criteria and due to real‐world nature of the study, a considerable number of patients were excluded resulting in selection bias. However, comparison of baseline data between the included and excluded patients showed similar clinical features. Third, during the study period, there was no change in glucose‐lowering regimens with a relatively high mean HbA1c of 9%. This clinical inertia was not uncommon in real‐world practice. In keeping with the effects of finerenone on IR, we observed a drop in HbA1c during the study period. Future randomized studies are needed to clarify the glucose‐lowering effects of finerenone in patients with fair glycemic control. Fourth, this was a single‐center retrospective study with short‐term follow‐up and the generalizability of our findings remains uncertain. The lack of a control group restricted our analysis to pre‐post treatment comparisons which did not allow for adjustment of confounders. A multicenter randomized controlled trial with long term follow‐up (e.g., ≥36 months) in patients with T2D and broad range of kidney function will be needed to confirm the effects of finerenone, preferably using gold‐standard measures such as insulin clamps.

CONCLUSION

In this real‐world study, 12‐month treatment with finerenone was associated with reduced IR in patients with T2D and CKD in addition to improved renal function. These findings provide human evidence in support of the metabolic effects of inhibition of MR beyond inflammation and fibrosis and call for further studies to verify these observations.
