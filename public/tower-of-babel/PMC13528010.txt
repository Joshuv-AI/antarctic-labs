Assessing nursing students’ attitudes toward diabetic foot care across cognitive, affective, and behavioral domains: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Alime Aksel, Yasemin Çelik, Pakize Özyürek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic foot is a serious complication of diabetes that significantly impairs patients’ quality of life. Nursing education plays an important role in shaping students’ knowledge, skills, and attitudes related to diabetic foot care. However, there is limited research assessing their cognitive, affective, and behavioral attitudes toward diabetic foot care. Therefore, this study aimed to assess nursing students’ attitudes toward diabetic foot care across cognitive, affective, and behavioral domains and identify factors associated with these attitudes.MethodsThis descriptive, cross-sectional study was performed between May and June 2024 with third- and fourth-year nursing students enrolled at a public university in western Türkiye. The sample comprised 230 nursing students who were actively involved in clinical practice and volunteered to participate. Data were collected using the student information form and attitude toward diabetic foot care form, which includes cognitive, affective, and behavioral subscales. Descriptive statistics, independent samples t-test, one-way analysis of variance, and Pearson correlation analysis were applied.ResultsThe overall attitude level toward diabetic foot care was moderate. Cognitive and behavioral attitude scores were higher than affective attitude scores. In particular, female students had significantly higher cognitive, behavioral, and total attitude scores than male students (p < 0.05). Students with a first-degree relative with diabetes and those who had encountered diabetic amputation during clinical practice demonstrated significantly higher attitude scores compared with others (p < 0.05). Positive and significant correlations were identified among all attitude subscales (p < 0.001).ConclusionsNursing students demonstrated a moderate attitude level toward diabetic foot care, with relatively stronger cognitive and behavioral components and a weaker affective component. These findings indicate the need to strengthen structured, practice-based educational strategies and interventions that enhance affective awareness related to diabetic foot care in nursing education.Trial registrationClinical trial number: not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04909-0.

Background

Diabetic foot is defined as infection, ulceration, or destruction of deep tissues of the foot associated with neuropathy and/or peripheral arterial disease in individuals with diabetes [1]. It is considered one of the most serious complications of diabetes and significantly impairs patients’ quality of life through prolonged hospitalization, disability, and risk of lower-extremity amputation [1–3]. Chronic hyperglycemia leads to neuropathy and peripheral arterial disease, which in turn cause ulcers, infections, and tissue loss in the lower extremities. These conditions increase the risk of amputation and adversely affect individuals’ physical, psychological, and social well-being [1–3]. According to studies in Türkiye, approximately 20%–25% of individuals with diabetes present with diabetic foot manifestations [4, 5], underscoring the public health significance of diabetic foot care [2].

Neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease, foot deformities, trauma, and infections are well-established etiological factors in diabetic foot development [3, 6]. Therefore, early identification of individuals at risk, timely implementation of preventive strategies before ulcer formation, and prompt treatment of emerging lesions are critical to reducing amputation rates [7, 8]. Preventing of ulcer development is the cornerstone of diabetic foot management, and nurses are expected to play an essential role in this process by applying evidence-based interventions. Accordingly, nursing students must develop adequate knowledge and attitudes toward diabetic foot care during clinical training [9–11].

According to literature, nurses’ and nursing students’ knowledge and attitudes regarding diabetic foot care directly influence the quality of care and clinical outcomes [2, 11, 12]. In Malaysia, a previous study reported that nurses had moderate knowledge but generally positive attitudes, and their knowledge increased with education and clinical experience [12]. Similarly, a study conducted in Indonesia found that nursing students’ knowledge and attitudes regarding diabetic foot care were affected by their education level, clinical exposure, and age [13]. According to conceptual analyses conducted in Asia, diabetic foot care is a multidimensional process that involves physical interventions and patient awareness, self-efficacy, and cultural sensitivity [14]. Collectively, these findings emphasize the critical role of nurses’ and nursing students’ attitudes in ensuring optimal diabetic foot care.

Attitude is a psychosocial construct that encompasses individuals’ emotions, beliefs, and behavioral tendencies toward a specific object, situation, or event [15, 16]. The ABC model, which conceptualizes attitudes through cognitive, affective, and behavioral components, is widely used in analyzing health behaviors and evaluating students’ attitudes [15, 17].

Nursing education plays a vital role in shaping students’ knowledge, skills, and attitudes toward diabetic foot care. However, despite the presence of studies focusing on nurses’ attitudes, there is limited research assessing nursing students’ cognitive, affective, and behavioral attitudes toward diabetic foot care [13]. This gap highlights the need to evaluate attitude levels, particularly among students who have gained clinical experience.

Therefore, the present study aimed to assess nursing students’ cognitive, affective, and behavioral attitudes toward diabetic foot care and identify the factors associated with these attitudes. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on knowledge levels or general attitudes, this study evaluated nursing students’ attitudes across cognitive, affective, and behavioral domains within the framework of the ABC attitude model to ensure a comprehensive assessment of attitudes related to diabetic foot care. In addition, the study provides context-specific evidence regarding nursing students’ preparedness for diabetic foot care within the Turkish nursing education system. The findings are expected to contribute to the development of nursing curricula and strengthen students’ awareness and preparedness for delivering effective diabetic foot care.

Methods

Study design

This descriptive, cross-sectional study was performed between May and June 2024 with third- and fourth-year students enrolled in the nursing department of a public university located in western Türkiye.

Study setting and sample

The study was conducted with third- and fourth-year nursing students enrolled in the Department of Nursing, Faculty of Health Sciences, Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University, located in western Türkiye. The study population comprised all eligible third- and fourth-year nursing students at the relevant institution. The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software based on the expected p-value, power, and effect size. An a priori power analysis was performed using the F-test family in GPower. A minimum of 150 participants was required with parameters set at p = 0.05, power (1–β) = 0.95, and effect size (f) = 0.42. The calculation was further verified using the known population formula (N = 250, p = 0.50, q = 0.50, d = 0.05, and t = 1.96).

Initially, a total of 250 students participated. However, 20 questionnaires with incomplete data were excluded, and the analyses were completed with 230 students. Convenience sampling was used. Although probability-based sampling methods may reduce selection bias, convenience sampling was considered appropriate because the study was conducted with students who were accessible during the data collection period and voluntarily agreed to participate. The participants included third- and fourth-year students who were actively involved in clinical practice and voluntarily agreed to participate. First- and second-year students who had not yet begun clinical training were excluded from the study.

In Türkiye, nursing education consists of theoretical coursework and clinical practice. In the fourth year, students engage in an average of 28 h of clinical practice per week and provide direct patient care. Therefore, it was anticipated that students’ clinical experience could influence their attitudes toward diabetic foot care.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using two forms developed based on relevant literature: Student Information Form and Attitude Toward Diabetic Foot Care Form (ADFCF).

Student information form

The student information form was developed to assess students’ sociodemographic characteristics and their knowledge and perceptions regarding diabetic foot care. It includes 11 items derived from literature [18–22]. The items covered participants’ age, sex, academic year, previous education related to diabetic foot care, clinical experience, encounters with patients with diabetic foot problems, and knowledge and perceptions regarding diabetic foot care. Students were also asked whether they had previously received education related to diabetic foot care and whether this education had been obtained through formal courses or scientific activities such as seminars, congresses, or courses. The items also included questions regarding students’ previous encounters with patients with diabetic foot complications during clinical practice.

Attitude Toward Diabetic Foot Care Form (ADFCF)

The Attitude Toward Diabetic Foot Care Form (ADFCF) was developed by the researchers based on the relevant literature to assess nursing students’ cognitive, affective, and behavioral attitudes toward diabetic foot care. The Affective, Behavioral, and Cognitive (ABC) model of attitude served as the theoretical framework [17].

An item pool of 64 statements was created based on literature. Content validity was assessed by eight faculty members, one diabetes nurse, and one wound care nurse. Each item was evaluated using a four-point rating scale (1 = not appropriate, 4 = appropriate). The content validity index was calculated as 0.975. Based on expert feedback, 17 items were removed, resulting in a 46-item questionnaire.

A pilot study with 45 students confirmed that all items were understandable. Students who participated in the pilot study were not included in the main study sample. The final form comprised three subscales: cognitive attitudes (16 items), affective attitudes (10 items), and behavioral attitudes (20 items).

Items were scored on a five-point Likert scale (1 = never, 5 = always). Reverse-scored items were as follows: cognitive (items 8 and 13), affective (items 3, 4, 5, and 8), and behavioral (items 11, 17, 19, and 20). The total score ranged from 47 to 235, with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes. The wide scoring range allows for a detailed assessment of students’ attitude levels.

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients, which were 0.849 for the cognitive dimension, 0.839 for the affective dimension, 0.788 for the behavioral dimension, and 0.851 for the overall questionnaire. The full version of the Attitude Toward Diabetic Foot Care Form (ADFCF) is provided as a supplementary file (Additional File 1).

Data collection procedure

Data were collected through face-to-face administration of questionnaires in classroom settings by the researchers. The study’s purpose was explained to the participants, and questionnaires were distributed during class breaks or when students were available. Completion of the forms took approximately 15–20 min. The questionnaires were administered anonymously, and no identifying information was collected to minimize response bias.

The data collection process was conducted by the researcher who did not have a direct role in the students’ academic evaluation process. Researchers who were course instructors were not involved in data collection. Students were informed that participation was entirely voluntary.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0. Descriptive statistics included frequency (n), percentage (%), mean (M), standard deviation (SD), minimum (min), and maximum (max) values. Distribution normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Parametric tests were applied to variables with a normal distribution. Independent samples t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. One-way analysis of variance was performed for comparisons involving three or more groups. Welch’s test was applied when homogeneity of variance was not met. Tukey or Tamhane post hoc tests were used for multiple comparisons when significant differences were detected. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships between continuous variables. Statistical significance was considered at p ≤ 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval [23, 24].

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University (Date: 7 March 2024; Approval No: 2024/15). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Students were informed that their data would be used solely for scientific purposes. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The study addressed the following research questions: What are the general attitude levels of nursing students toward diabetic foot care, what are the cognitive, affective, and behavioral attitude levels of students toward diabetic foot care, what factors influence students’ attitudes toward diabetic foot care?

Results

Among students who participated in the study, 62.6% were third-year students, and 37.4% were fourth-year students. Approximately 77.4% of students were female, whereas 22.6% were male. A majority of students (71.3%) reported that they did not follow current developments related to the prevention of diabetic foot ulcers, and 57.8% indicated that they had not received education on this topic. However, 70.0% expressed a desire to receive training on diabetic foot care. Only 4.3% of the students perceived themselves as competent in diabetic foot care, whereas 51.7% considered themselves insufficient and 43.9% were undecided. In addition, more than half of the participants reported that they had not received any formal education regarding diabetic foot care and did not regularly follow developments related to diabetic foot prevention. In addition, 25.7% had a first-degree relative with diabetes, and 45.2% reported having encountered a patient with diabetic amputation during their clinical practice. The mean grade point average (GPA) of students was 2.73 ± 0.30, ranging from 2.00 to 3.65 (Table 1).

Discussion

This study evaluated the attitudes of third- and fourth-year nursing students toward diabetic foot care, including their cognitive, affective, and behavioral components. The results showed that students had moderate overall attitude levels. Similar results have been reported in studies examining nurses’ knowledge and attitudes toward diabetic foot care [25, 26]. Previous international studies have also shown that nursing students and healthcare professionals generally demonstrate moderate knowledge and positive attitudes toward diabetic foot prevention; however, important deficiencies remain in translating knowledge into consistent preventive practices [12, 13, 22]. Studies conducted among nurses in Malaysia and Sri Lanka similarly emphasized the importance of clinical experience and structured education in improving diabetic foot care awareness and practice behaviors [12]. The similarity between these findings and the present study may suggest that theoretical knowledge alone is insufficient for developing strong professional attitudes and consistent preventive care behaviors. Limited clinical exposure and insufficient practical training opportunities may partly explain why students demonstrated only moderate attitude levels despite generally positive cognitive perceptions. Educational approaches such as scenario-based simulations, case discussions, and supervised clinical experiences may support the development of more positive attitudes and professional confidence in diabetic foot care.

Cognitive domain

The cognitive domain reflects students’ knowledge and understanding of diabetic foot care. In the present study, the cognitive scores were relatively high. Comparable findings have been reported in studies indicating that clinical exposure and educational experiences may improve nursing students’ awareness of diabetic foot care practices [22, 27]. However, Sari et al. [13] reported inadequate knowledge levels among nursing students, suggesting that cognitive preparedness may vary according to educational opportunities and clinical experience. The relatively higher cognitive scores observed in the present study may be associated with students’ encounters with individuals with diabetes during clinical placements and their interactions with diabetes nurses. These experiences may contribute to strengthening students’ understanding and awareness of preventive diabetic foot care practices.

Affective domain

The affective domain represents students’ emotions, beliefs, and awareness related to diabetic foot care. In the present study, affective scores were lower than cognitive and behavioral scores. This may stem from differences in students’ motivation, patient sensitivity, and perceptions of professional responsibility. Previous studies have reported that nursing students’ and nurses’ attitudes toward diabetic foot care may be influenced by educational experiences, professional awareness, and perceptions of preventive care responsibilities [11, 13, 16]. Studies based on the ABC attitude model have also emphasized that affective attitudes are closely associated with emotional awareness and professional sensitivity in preventive nursing care practices [16]. In line with these findings, the relatively lower affective scores observed in the present study may reflect differences in students’ motivation, patient sensitivity, and perceptions of professional responsibility related to diabetic foot prevention. Some students may perceive the prevention of diabetic foot complications primarily as the patient’s responsibility, which may reduce emotional engagement with preventive care practices. A considerable proportion of the students also perceived themselves as insufficient in diabetic foot care despite generally positive cognitive and behavioral attitude scores. In addition, more than half of the participants reported that they had not received education specifically related to diabetic foot care. Comparable findings have been reported in previous studies indicating that nursing students and nurses may experience uncertainty and limited confidence in diabetic foot management despite moderate knowledge levels [12]. Limited practical exposure and insufficient structured training opportunities may further affect students’ confidence and preparedness regarding diabetic foot care [22]. These findings may indicate that theoretical knowledge alone is insufficient for developing strong affective awareness and professional confidence in preventive diabetic foot management.

Behavioral domain

The behavioral domain assesses students’ tendencies to translate knowledge and attitudes into practice. In the present study, behavioral attitudes were at a moderate level. According to a study in Indonesia, students had low knowledge levels but positive attitudes toward diabetic ulcer care [13], suggesting difficulties in transferring knowledge into practice. Similarly, previous studies conducted among nurses and nursing students have emphasized that although theoretical knowledge regarding diabetic foot care may be adequate, preventive care behaviors and practical application skills are not always performed consistently in clinical settings [12, 22, 27]. In line with these findings, the moderate behavioral attitude scores observed in the present study may reflect limitations in opportunities for supervised clinical practice and experiential learning related to diabetic foot management. Clinical exposure and hands-on learning experiences appear to play an important role in strengthening preventive care behaviors and improving students’ confidence in diabetic foot care. Integrating case-based learning activities and increasing opportunities for direct patient care experiences may contribute to the development of stronger behavioral competencies among nursing students.

Interpretation of subscale items

The high agreement with the reverse-coded affective item “There is no need to take precautions to prevent the development of diabetic foot ulcers” reflects strong awareness of students regarding the importance of preventive care. Conversely, the low mean score for the item “Diabetic foot ulcers do not cause discomfort to the patient” shows that students recognize the negative impact of diabetic foot ulcers on patients’ quality of life. Similar findings in literature highlight the importance that nurses place on regular foot examinations and patient education [13, 26, 28]. The consistency between these findings and the present study may reflect increasing awareness among nursing students regarding the importance of preventive approaches in diabetic foot management. Overall, the results suggest that students generally recognize the importance of preventive foot care practices for individuals with diabetes.

Associations between gender and demographic characteristics

Female students demonstrated higher cognitive, behavioral, and total attitude scores than male students. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies [25, 29, 30]. However, some studies report higher knowledge levels among male nurses [31]. These inconsistent findings may indicate that gender-related differences in diabetic foot care attitudes and knowledge can vary according to educational and sociocultural contexts. One possible explanation for the higher scores observed among female students in the present study may be related to differences in caregiving perceptions and professional role orientation. Thus, educational programs should incorporate gender-sensitive approaches to enhance motivation among all students regarding diabetic foot care.

Influence of family history of diabetes and clinical experience

Students with a first-degree relative diagnosed with diabetes had higher total, cognitive, and behavioral attitude scores, consistent with previous studies [32–34]. The similarity between these findings and previous reports may suggest that observational learning and personal experiences related to diabetes within the family can contribute to the development of more positive attitudes toward diabetic foot care. In addition, students who had encountered a patient with diabetic amputation during clinical practice had higher cognitive scores, implying that exposure to severe complications may increase awareness. Clinical encounters involving diabetic foot complications may help students better recognize the importance of preventive care and early intervention in diabetic foot management.

Relationships among attitude subscales

Significant positive correlations were found among all attitude subscales. The strongest associations were observed between the cognitive and behavioral subscales, which also demonstrated strong correlations with overall attitude levels. Similar findings have also been reported in studies examining the relationship between knowledge and practice among nurses [35, 36]. However, other studies found no association between knowledge and attitudes [37]. These inconsistent findings may indicate that knowledge alone does not always directly translate into positive attitudes or preventive care behaviors. Educational strategies that combine theoretical knowledge with clinical application opportunities may therefore play an important role in supporting the development of positive behavioral attitudes toward diabetic foot care.

Implications for nursing education and practice

The findings show that although nursing students possess knowledge-based positive attitudes, their affective awareness requires further development. In particular, a considerable proportion of the students reported feeling insufficient in diabetic foot care and lacking specific education related to diabetic foot management. Previous studies have emphasized that nursing education programs focusing on evidence-based practice, professional responsibility, and preventive care may improve students’ attitudes and preparedness regarding diabetic foot care [11, 13, 35]. In line with these findings, the results of the present study highlight the potential importance of strengthening educational content related to preventive diabetic foot care within undergraduate nursing education. Therefore, evidence-based practice, patient simulation, and multidisciplinary educational content should be integrated into the nursing curriculum. Structured educational activities focusing on preventive diabetic foot care and supervised clinical practice opportunities may help improve students’ professional confidence and preparedness. Expanding opportunities for students to interact directly with individuals with diabetes during clinical placements may support the conversion of theoretical knowledge into practice. Clinical exposure and experiential learning opportunities have been reported to strengthen students’ professional awareness and attitudes toward patient-centered preventive care [13, 16]. This approach may also strengthen nursing students’ sense of professional responsibility in diabetic foot care.

Conclusion

This study examined nursing students’ attitudes toward diabetic foot care across cognitive, affective, and behavioral domains. The findings showed that most students did not follow current developments related to the prevention of diabetic foot ulcers. However, a considerable proportion of students expressed willingness to receive education on this topic despite many participants reporting limited formal education and insufficient confidence regarding diabetic foot care. Students who had encountered diabetic foot ulcers, particularly amputation cases, demonstrated higher attitude scores than those who had not encountered such cases during clinical practice. The overall ADFCF scores indicated a moderate attitude level, with higher scores being observed in the cognitive and behavioral domains and lower scores in the affective domain. Female students exhibited significantly higher cognitive, behavioral, and total attitude scores compared with male students.

Significant positive correlations were observed among all subscales, particularly between cognitive and behavioral attitudes. These findings may indicate that although nursing students generally demonstrate positive cognitive and behavioral attitudes toward diabetic foot care, affective awareness and professional confidence related to preventive diabetic foot management may require further support within nursing education.

Strengthening evidence-based educational content, supervised clinical experiences, and practice-oriented learning activities related to diabetic foot care may help improve nursing students’ preparedness and professional awareness regarding preventive diabetic foot management.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Because of its cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be inferred. This study was conducted with third- and fourth-year nursing students from a single public university, limiting the findings’ generalizability. The use of self-report measures increases the potential for social desirability bias. Although questionnaires were completed anonymously, participants may have provided responses that reflected socially desirable attitudes toward diabetic foot care. Furthermore, the single-center design prevented the examination of differences in educational programs and clinical practice structures across universities. Future multicenter studies involving different nursing schools and educational settings are recommended to improve the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias and limited the representativeness of the sample.
