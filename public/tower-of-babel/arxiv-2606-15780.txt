Inverse Logarithmic Coefficients and Associated Sharp Estimates for the Apple-Like Convex Subclass 𝒞 𝒜 ​ 𝒫 \mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
Pradip Das, Nabadwip Sarkar

Abstract
This paper addresses coefficient problems for the apple-like convex subclass 𝒞 𝒜 ​ 𝒫 \mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}} , defined by the subordination relation 1 + z ​ f ′′ ​ ( z ) / f ′ ​ ( z ) ≺ e z ​ 1 + z 1+zf^{\prime\prime}(z)/f^{\prime}(z)\prec e^{z}\sqrt{1+z} . We determine sharp bounds for the initial inverse logarithmic coefficients Γ 1 \Gamma_{1} , Γ 2 \Gamma_{2} , Γ 3 \Gamma_{3} , and the consecutive modulus difference | Γ 2 | − | Γ 1 | |\Gamma_{2}|-|\Gamma_{1}| . We also obtain the sharp upper bound for the second-order inverse logarithmic Hankel determinant, characterize the generalized Fekete–Szegő functional for all real parameters, and compute sharp bounds for the third-order Hermitian–Toeplitz determinant. Extremal functions are given for each case.



Keywords:
Univalent functions, inverse logarithmic coefficients, inverse logarithmic Hankel determinants, Fekete–Szegő inequalities, Hermitian Toeplitz determinants, Ma Minda subclasses, apple-like domains.

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the class of analytic functions in the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the subclass of
\mathcal{H}
consisting of functions normalized by
f(0)=0
and
f^{\prime}(0)=1
, and let
\mathcal{S}
denote the class of all univalent functions
f\in\mathcal{A}
having the Taylor series expansion

f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n},\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

(1.1)

We denote by
\mathcal{S}^{\ast}
and
\mathcal{C}
the standard subclasses of starlike and convex functions in
\mathcal{S}
, respectively. Analytically,
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\ast}
if and only if
\Re(zf^{\prime}(z)/f(z))>0
for
z\in\mathbb{D}
, and
f\in\mathcal{C}
if and only if
\Re(1+zf^{\prime\prime}(z)/f^{\prime}(z))>0
for
z\in\mathbb{D}
. The classical Alexander relation states that
f\in\mathcal{C}
if and only if
zf^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{\ast}
(see Duren
[
11
]
and Goodman
[
13
]
).

An analytic function
f
is subordinate to
g
in
\mathbb{D}
, written
f\prec g
, if there exists a Schwarz function
\omega
such that
f(z)=g(\omega(z))
for
z\in\mathbb{D}
. If
g
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
, then
f\prec g
is equivalent to
f(0)=g(0)
and
f(\mathbb{D})\subset g(\mathbb{D})
.

Ma and Minda
[
39
]
used subordination to introduce a unified framework for subclasses of starlike functions, defined by

\mathcal{S}^{\ast}(\psi)=\left\{f\in\mathcal{S}:\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}\prec\psi(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{D}\right\},

where
\psi
is analytic, univalent, maps
\mathbb{D}
onto a domain symmetric with respect to the real axis, and satisfies
\Re(\psi(z))>0
,
\psi(0)=1
, and
\psi^{\prime}(0)>0
.

Various subordinate functions
\psi
have been introduced to explore distinct geometric configurations
[
39
,
45
,
46
]
. Recently, Sana, Saliu, and Riaz
[
47
]
defined subclasses associated with the apple-like function
\psi_{\mathcal{AP}}(z)=e^{z}\sqrt{1+z}
. A function
f\in\mathcal{A}
belongs to the apple-like convex class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
if
1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\prec\psi_{\mathcal{AP}}(z)
(
z\in\mathbb{D}
). By the Alexander relation,
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
if and only if
zf^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{AP}}^{\ast}
. The function
\psi_{\mathcal{AP}}
maps
\mathbb{D}
onto an apple-shaped region and satisfies the Ma–Minda conditions, making
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
a subclass of the classical convex family.

1.1
Inverse Logarithmic Coefficients

For
f\in\mathcal{S}
, the inverse logarithmic coefficients
\Gamma_{n}
are defined by Ponnusamy et al.
[
44
]
via the expansion of the inverse function
f^{-1}
:

\log\frac{f^{-1}(w)}{w}=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{n}w^{n},\quad|w|<\frac{1}{4}.

Direct computation yields the first three inverse logarithmic coefficients in terms of the Taylor coefficients of
f
:

\begin{cases}\Gamma_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}a_{2},\\
\Gamma_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}a_{3}+\frac{3}{4}a_{2}^{2},\\
\Gamma_{3}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(a_{4}-4a_{2}a_{3}+\frac{10}{3}a_{2}^{3}\right).\end{cases}

(1.2)

For the full class
\mathcal{S}
, Ponnusamy et al.
[
44
]
proved that
|\Gamma_{n}|\leq\frac{1}{2n}\binom{2n}{n}
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
), with equality only for the Koebe function and its rotations.

Determinants with logarithmic coefficient entries frequently serve to describe structural properties in geometric function theory. Kowalczyk and Lecko
[
20
,
21
]
studied the classical Hankel determinant
H_{q,n}(F_{f}/2)
for logarithmic coefficients. Its inverse analogue,
H_{q,n}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)
, is defined using the inverse logarithmic coefficients
\Gamma_{n}
of
f^{-1}
as entries (see
[
34
]
):

H_{q,n}\left(F_{f^{-1}}/2\right)=\begin{vmatrix}\Gamma_{n}&\Gamma_{n+1}&\cdots&\Gamma_{n+q-1}\\
\Gamma_{n+1}&\Gamma_{n+2}&\cdots&\Gamma_{n+q}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\Gamma_{n+q-1}&\Gamma_{n+q}&\cdots&\Gamma_{n+2(q-1)}\end{vmatrix}.

In this paper, we determine sharp bounds for the following functionals over the class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
:

•

The initial inverse logarithmic coefficients
\Gamma_{1}
,
\Gamma_{2}
, and
\Gamma_{3}
.

•

The second-order inverse logarithmic Hankel determinant
H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)=\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{3}-\Gamma_{2}^{2}
.

•

The consecutive coefficient difference
|\Gamma_{2}|-|\Gamma_{1}|
.

•

The generalized Fekete–Szegő functional
|a_{3}-\lambda a_{2}^{2}|-\mu|a_{2}|
for real parameters
\lambda
and
\mu>0
.

•

The third-order Hermitian–Toeplitz determinant
T_{3,1}(f)
.

Bounds for the Inverse Logarithmic Coefficients of the Class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
and let the inverse logarithmic coefficients
\Gamma_{n}
(
n\geq 1
) be defined by (
1.2
). Then

|\Gamma_{1}|\leq\frac{3}{8},\qquad|\Gamma_{2}|\leq\frac{31}{192},\qquad|\Gamma_{3}|\leq\frac{205}{2304}.

These bounds are sharp.

Proof.

By definition,
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
if and only if
zf^{\prime}(z)\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{AP}}^{*}
. Equating coefficients via the Alexander relation
na_{n}(\mathcal{C})=a_{n}(\mathcal{S}^{*})
, the initial Taylor coefficients of
f
are given by

a_{2}=\frac{3}{8}c_{1},\qquad a_{3}=\frac{1}{8}c_{2}+\frac{13}{192}c_{1}^{2},\qquad a_{4}=\frac{1}{16}c_{3}+\frac{17}{384}c_{1}c_{2}+\frac{37}{9216}c_{1}^{3}.

(3.1)

From
\Gamma_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}a_{2}
and (
3.1
), it follows that
|\Gamma_{1}|=\frac{3}{16}|c_{1}|\leq\frac{3}{8}
, since
|c_{1}|\leq 2
for
p\in\mathcal{P}
. This inequality is sharp for the function
f_{1}(z)
defined by

f_{1}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{e^{t}\sqrt{1+t}-1}{t}\,dt\right)ds=z+\frac{3}{4}z^{2}+\frac{25}{48}z^{3}+\cdots.

(3.2)

For
\Gamma_{2}
, equations (
1.2
) and (
3.1
) imply

|\Gamma_{2}|=\left|-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{8}c_{2}+\frac{13}{192}c_{1}^{2}\right)+\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{3}{8}c_{1}\right)^{2}\right|=\frac{1}{16}\left|c_{2}-\frac{55}{48}c_{1}^{2}\right|.

Applying Lemma
2.3
with
v=\frac{55}{48}>1
yields
\left|c_{2}-\frac{55}{48}c_{1}^{2}\right|\leq 4\left(\frac{55}{48}\right)-2=\frac{31}{12}
, from which we obtain
|\Gamma_{2}|\leq\frac{31}{192}
.

For
\Gamma_{3}
, substituting (
3.1
) into the definition of
\Gamma_{3}
gives

|\Gamma_{3}|=\left|-\frac{1}{32}c_{3}+\frac{55}{768}c_{1}c_{2}-\frac{721}{18432}c_{1}^{3}\right|.

(3.3)

Expressing
c_{n}
in terms of
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
using Lemma
2.1
, and assuming
\tau_{1}=x\in[0,1]

yields

|\Gamma_{3}|\leq\frac{1-x^{2}}{16}\left(\left|A+B\tau_{2}+C\tau_{2}^{2}\right|+1-|\tau_{2}|^{2}\right),

(3.4)

(3.5)

Since

4
Bounds for the Differences of Inverse Logarithmic Coefficients

The classical Bieberbach conjecture, settled by de Branges
[
30
]
, establishes that the Taylor coefficients of any function
f\in\mathcal{S}
satisfy
|a_{n}|\leq n
for all
n\geq 2
, with equality holding only for the Koebe function
k(z):=z/(1-z)^{2}
and its rotations. This result led to the study of consecutive coefficient differences, specifically the problem of whether the inequality

\bigl||a_{n+1}|-|a_{n}|\bigr|\leq 1,\quad n\geq 2,

holds across the full class
\mathcal{S}
. Early progress was made by Goluzin
[
14
]
, and subsequently Hayman
[
17
]
proved that
\bigl||a_{n+1}|-|a_{n}|\bigr|\leq A
for a universal constant
A\geq 1
. The best known upper bound to date is
A=3.61
, due to Grinspan
[
15
]
. For the initial index
n=2
, the sharp bounds are known to be
-1\leq|a_{3}|-|a_{2}|\leq 1.029\ldots
(see
[
11
, Theorem 3.11]
).

For the starlike subclass
\mathcal{S}^{\ast}
, Pommerenke
[
43
]
conjectured that the consecutive coefficient difference is bounded by
1
for all
n\geq 2
, which was proved by Leung
[
35
]
. For the convex class
\mathcal{C}
, Li and Sugawa
[
36
]
determined sharp upper bounds on
|a_{n+1}|-|a_{n}|
for
n\geq 2
, as well as sharp lower bounds for
n=2
and
n=3
. Related results and extensions can be found in
[
42
,
3
,
4
]
.

Recently, similar problems have been considered for the differences of logarithmic coefficients. Lecko and Partyka
[
31
]
determined sharp bounds for
|\gamma_{2}|-|\gamma_{1}|
over the class
\mathcal{S}
using the Loewner differential equation, and a simplified proof was given by Obradović and Tuneski
[
41
]
. Kumar and Cho
[
27
]
extended these estimates to various subclasses of
\mathcal{S}
.

In this section, we determine sharp upper and lower bounds for the difference of the initial inverse logarithmic coefficients,
|\Gamma_{2}|-|\Gamma_{1}|
, for functions in the subclass
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
and the inverse logarithmic coefficients
\Gamma_{n}
are defined by (
1.2
), then

-\frac{3\sqrt{6}}{4\sqrt{55}}\leq|\Gamma_{2}|-|\Gamma_{1}|\leq\frac{1}{8}.

(4.1)

These bounds are sharp.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
. Using the coefficient relations (
3.1
) and (
1.2
), the difference can be expressed as

|\Gamma_{2}|-|\Gamma_{1}|=\left|-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{8}c_{2}+\frac{13}{192}c_{1}^{2}\right)+\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{3}{8}c_{1}\right)^{2}\right|-\frac{3}{16}|c_{1}|=\frac{1}{768}\left(|55c_{1}^{2}-48c_{2}|-144|c_{1}|\right).

(4.2)

Let
\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})=|Kc_{1}^{2}+Lc_{2}|-|Jc_{1}|
, where
K=55
,
L=-48
, and
J=144
, so that
|\Gamma_{2}|-|\Gamma_{1}|=\frac{1}{768}\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})
.

To apply Lemma
2.4
, we compute
M=|4K+2L|=|220-96|=124
. Since
|2K+L|=|110-48|=62<192=|L|+J
, Lemma
2.4
yields

\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})\leq 2|L|=96.

Dividing by
768
gives the upper bound
\frac{96}{768}=\frac{1}{8}
. This upper bound is attained by the function
f_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
defined by

f_{2}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{e^{t^{2}}\sqrt{1+t^{2}}-1}{t}\,dt\right)ds.

(4.3)

This function corresponds to the Schwarz function
\omega(z)=z^{2}
, which gives
c_{1}=0
and
c_{2}=2
. From (
3.1
), we have
a_{2}=0
and
a_{3}=\frac{1}{4}
, which leads to
\Gamma_{1}=0
and
\Gamma_{2}=-\frac{1}{8}
, so that
|\Gamma_{2}|-|\Gamma_{1}|=\frac{1}{8}
.

For the lower bound, since
J^{2}=20736<2|L|(M+2|L|)=96(124+96)=21120
, Lemma
2.4
implies

-\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})\leq 2J\sqrt{\frac{2|L|}{M+2|L|}}=2(144)\sqrt{\frac{96}{220}}=\frac{576\sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{55}}.

Dividing by
768
yields the lower bound
-\frac{3\sqrt{6}}{4\sqrt{55}}
, which completes the proof of the inequalities in (
4.1
).

To establish the sharpness of the lower bound explicitly, we consider the convex extremal function
g_{3}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
defined via the Alexander relation
zg_{3}^{\prime}(z)=f_{3}(z)
, where

(4.4)

where

p(z)=\frac{1+\omega(z)}{1-\omega(z)}=1+2\omega(z)+2\omega^{2}(z)+\cdots.

Substituting the expansion of

|\Gamma_{2}|-|\Gamma_{1}|=0-\frac{3\sqrt{6}}{4\sqrt{55}}=-\frac{3\sqrt{6}}{4\sqrt{55}},

∎

5
Hankel Determinants for the Inverse Logarithmic Coefficients

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
be of the form (
1.1
). Then the second-order inverse logarithmic Hankel determinant satisfies the sharp bound

\left|H_{2,1}\left(F_{f^{-1}}/2\right)\right|\leq\frac{3413}{195328}\approx 0.017473.

(5.1)

Proof.

By the Alexander relation,
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
if and only if
zf^{\prime}(z)\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{AP}}^{*}
. The initial Taylor coefficients of
f
are given by

a_{2}=\frac{3}{8}c_{1},\qquad a_{3}=\frac{1}{8}c_{2}+\frac{13}{192}c_{1}^{2},\qquad a_{4}=\frac{1}{16}c_{3}+\frac{17}{384}c_{1}c_{2}+\frac{37}{9216}c_{1}^{3}.

(5.2)

Substituting (
5.2
) into the relations in (
1.2
) expresses the Hankel determinant in terms of the Carathéodory coefficients:

H_{2,1}\left(F_{f^{-1}}/2\right)=\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{3}-\Gamma_{2}^{2}=\frac{1301}{589824}c_{1}^{4}-\frac{55}{12288}c_{1}^{2}c_{2}-\frac{1}{256}c_{2}^{2}+\frac{3}{512}c_{1}c_{3}.

(5.3)

Using Lemma
2.1
to express
c_{n}
in terms of the parameters
\tau_{n}\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, (
5.3
) becomes

\displaystyle H_{2,1}\left(F_{f^{-1}}/2\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{269}{36864}\tau_{1}^{4}-\frac{31}{1536}(1-|\tau_{1}|^{2})\tau_{1}^{2}\tau_{2}

(5.4)

\displaystyle-\frac{2+|\tau_{1}|^{2}}{128}(1-|\tau_{1}|^{2})\tau_{2}^{2}+\frac{3}{128}\tau_{1}(1-|\tau_{1}|^{2})(1-|\tau_{2}|^{2})\tau_{3}.

By rotational invariance, we may assume without loss of generality that
\tau_{1}=x\in[0,1]
. Letting
|\tau_{2}|=y\in[0,1]
and applying the triangle inequality to (
5.4
) under the bounded condition
|\tau_{3}|\leq 1
, we obtain the upper bound

\left|H_{2,1}\left(F_{f^{-1}}/2\right)\right|\leq\mathcal{M}(x,y),

where

\mathcal{M}(x,y)=\frac{269}{36864}x^{4}+\frac{31}{1536}(1-x^{2})x^{2}y+\frac{2+x^{2}}{128}(1-x^{2})y^{2}+\frac{3}{128}x(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2}).

Differentiating
\mathcal{M}(x,y)
twice with respect to
y
yields

\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{M}}{\partial y^{2}}=\frac{(1-x^{2})(2+x^{2}-3x)}{64}.

We can factor the quadratic expression in the numerator as
2+x^{2}-3x=(x-1)(x-2)
. For any
x\in(0,1)
, both linear factors are strictly negative, which implies their product is strictly positive. Since
1-x^{2}>0
also holds on this interval, it follows that
\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{M}}{\partial y^{2}}>0
holds uniformly. Thus,
\mathcal{M}(x,y)
is a strictly convex function of
y
on the interval
[0,1]
, meaning its maximum value over
y
must occur exclusively at the boundary endpoints, either at
y=0
or
y=1
.

Case 1.
If
y=0
,
\mathcal{M}(x,y)
reduces to the polynomial

\mathcal{M}(x,0)=\frac{269}{36864}x^{4}+\frac{3}{128}x(1-x^{2})=:\psi(x).

Differentiating
\psi(x)
with respect to
x
yields the stationary condition:

\psi^{\prime}(x)=\frac{269x^{3}-648x^{2}+216}{9216}=0.

The cubic polynomial
P(x)=269x^{3}-648x^{2}+216
satisfies
P(0)=216>0
and
P(1)=-163<0
. By the Intermediate Value Theorem, there exists a unique valid critical point in the interior of the interval at
x_{0}\approx 0.631586
. Evaluating the second derivative confirms that
\psi^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})<0
, identifying this point as a local maximum. Substituting
x_{0}
back into the objective function yields:

\max_{x\in[0,1]}\psi(x)=\psi(x_{0})\approx 0.015431,

which is strictly bounded below
\frac{3413}{195328}
.

Case 2.
If
y=1
,
\mathcal{M}(x,y)
simplifies to the boundary polynomial:

\mathcal{M}(x,1)=\frac{269}{36864}x^{4}+\frac{31}{1536}(1-x^{2})x^{2}+\frac{2+x^{2}}{128}(1-x^{2})=\frac{-763x^{4}+456x^{2}+576}{36864}=:\eta(x).

Differentiating
\eta(x)
with respect to
\mu=x^{2}
yields:

\frac{d}{d\mu}\eta(x)=\frac{-1526\mu+456}{36864}.

Setting this derivative to zero shows that the interior critical point occurs at
\mu_{*}=x_{*}^{2}=\frac{228}{763}\approx 0.29882
. Evaluating
\eta(x)
at this critical point yields the global maximum value:

\eta_{\max}=\eta\left(\sqrt{\frac{228}{763}}\right)=\frac{3413}{195328}\approx 0.017473.

Since
\eta_{\max}>\max_{0\leq x\leq 1}\psi(x)
, the sharp bound proposed in (
5.1
) follows immediately.

The bound is strictly sharp for the function
f_{*}(z)\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
defined by

f_{*}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{e^{omega_{*}(t)}\sqrt{1+\omega_{*}(t)}-1}{t}\,dt\right)ds,

(5.5)

where
\omega_{*}(z)
is the stable Schwarz function given by

\omega_{*}(z)=z\left(\frac{x_{*}+z}{1+x_{*}z}\right),\quad\text{with }x_{*}=\sqrt{\frac{228}{763}}\approx 0.5466.

Using the series expansion of
\omega_{*}(z)
, the Taylor coefficients of
f_{*}(z)
are computed as:

a_{2}=\frac{3}{4}x_{*},\qquad a_{3}=\frac{1}{4}+\frac{13}{48}x_{*}^{2},\qquad a_{4}=\frac{17}{96}x_{*}+\frac{205}{1152}x_{*}^{3}.

Substituting these parameters into the inverse logarithmic transformations (
1.2
) yields:

\Gamma_{1}=-\frac{3}{8}x_{*},\qquad\Gamma_{2}=-\frac{1}{8}+\frac{31}{192}x_{*}^{2},\qquad\Gamma_{3}=-\frac{17}{192}x_{*}+\frac{205}{2304}x_{*}^{3}.

Direct substitution of these target coefficients into the Hankel functional format yields:

H_{2,1}\left(F_{f^{-1}}/2\right)=\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{3}-\Gamma_{2}^{2}=\frac{-763x_{*}^{4}+456x_{*}^{2}-144}{9216}.

Evaluating this expression at

gives exactly
\frac{3413}{195328}
, confirming sharpness.
∎

6
Generalized Fekete–Szegő Functional for the Class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}

The generalized Fekete–Szegő functional was introduced by Lecko and Partyka
[
32
]
for the class
\mathcal{S}
as

F_{\lambda,\mu}(f)=\big|a_{3}-\lambda a_{2}^{2}\big|-\mu|a_{2}|,\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\quad\mu>0.

(6.1)

This functional was subsequently studied for subclasses of univalent and convex functions by Bulboacă et al.
[
5
]
. In this section, we establish sharp upper and lower bounds for
F_{\lambda,\mu}(f)
within the class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
be of the form (
1.1
). Then the generalized Fekete–Szegő functional satisfies the sharp upper bound

F_{\lambda,\mu}(f)\leq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{48}|25-27\lambda|-\frac{3\mu}{4},&\text{if }\displaystyle|25-27\lambda|\geq 12+36\mu,\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{4},&\text{if }\displaystyle|25-27\lambda|<12+36\mu,\end{cases}

and the sharp lower bound

F_{\lambda,\mu}(f)\geq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{48}|25-27\lambda|-\frac{3\mu}{4},&\text{if }\displaystyle\mu\geq\frac{1}{18}|25-27\lambda|+\frac{2}{3},\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{3\mu}{4}\sqrt{\frac{12}{|25-27\lambda|+12}},&\text{if }\displaystyle\mu^{2}\leq\frac{1}{27}|25-27\lambda|+\frac{4}{9},\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{27\mu^{2}}{4|25-27\lambda|+48},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

These bounds are sharp.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
. By the Alexander relation,
zf^{\prime}(z)\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{AP}}^{*}
. Expressing the Taylor coefficients
a_{2}
and
a_{3}
in terms of the coefficients
c_{n}
of a Carathéodory function
p\in\mathcal{P}
, we have

a_{2}=\frac{3}{8}c_{1},\qquad a_{3}=\frac{1}{8}c_{2}+\frac{13}{192}c_{1}^{2}.

(6.2)

Substituting these expressions into the definition of the generalized Fekete–Szegő functional
F_{\lambda,\mu}(f)=|a_{3}-\lambda a_{2}^{2}|-\mu|a_{2}|
gives

F_{\lambda,\mu}(f)=\left|\frac{1}{8}c_{2}+\left(\frac{13}{192}-\frac{9\lambda}{64}\right)c_{1}^{2}\right|-\frac{3\mu}{8}|c_{1}|=\frac{1}{8}\left(\left|c_{2}+\left(\frac{13}{24}-\frac{9\lambda}{8}\right)c_{1}^{2}\right|-3\mu|c_{1}|\right).

(6.3)

Let
F_{\lambda,\mu}(f)=\frac{1}{8}\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})
, where
\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})=|Kc_{1}^{2}+Lc_{2}|-|Jc_{1}|
with parameters

K=\frac{13}{24}-\frac{9\lambda}{8},\qquad L=1,\qquad J=3\mu.

The auxiliary values required to apply Lemma
2.4
are computed as

M=|4K+2L|=\left|4\left(\frac{13}{24}-\frac{9\lambda}{8}\right)+2\right|=\frac{1}{6}|25-27\lambda|,

|2K+L|=\left|2\left(\frac{13}{24}-\frac{9\lambda}{8}\right)+1\right|=\frac{1}{12}|25-27\lambda|.

The condition
|2K+L|\geq|L|+J
for the upper bound is equivalent to

\frac{1}{12}|25-27\lambda|\geq 1+3\mu\implies|25-27\lambda|\geq 12+36\mu.

When this parametric condition holds, Lemma
2.4
implies
\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})\leq M-2J=\frac{1}{6}|25-27\lambda|-6\mu
. Multiplying through by
\frac{1}{8}
yields the first branch of the sharp upper bound:

F_{\lambda,\mu}(f)\leq\frac{1}{48}|25-27\lambda|-\frac{3\mu}{4}.

Conversely, if
|25-27\lambda|<12+36\mu
, Lemma
2.4
implies
\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})\leq 2|L|=2
, which leads directly to
F_{\lambda,\mu}(f)\leq\frac{1}{4}
.

The corresponding lower bounds follow systematically from the second part of Lemma
2.4
:

Region (i):
The condition
J\geq M+2|L|
simplifies to

3\mu\geq\frac{1}{6}|25-27\lambda|+2\implies\mu\geq\frac{1}{18}|25-27\lambda|+\frac{2}{3}.

In this case,
-\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})\leq 2J-M=6\mu-\frac{1}{6}|25-27\lambda|
. Multiplying by
-\frac{1}{8}
yields

F_{\lambda,\mu}(f)\geq\frac{1}{48}|25-27\lambda|-\frac{3\mu}{4}.

Region (ii):
The parameter condition
J^{2}\leq 2|L|(M+2|L|)
reduces to

9\mu^{2}\leq 2\left(\frac{1}{6}|25-27\lambda|+2\right)\implies\mu^{2}\leq\frac{1}{27}|25-27\lambda|+\frac{4}{9}.

This yields
-\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})\leq 2J\sqrt{\frac{2|L|}{M+2|L|}}=6\mu\sqrt{\frac{12}{|25-27\lambda|+12}}
, and multiplying by
-\frac{1}{8}
gives

F_{\lambda,\mu}(f)\geq-\frac{3\mu}{4}\sqrt{\frac{12}{|25-27\lambda|+12}}.

Region (iii):
Under the remaining parameter allocations, Lemma
2.4
implies

-\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})\leq 2|L|+\frac{J^{2}}{M+2|L|}=2+\frac{54\mu^{2}}{|25-27\lambda|+12}.

Multiplying through by
-\frac{1}{8}
leads to the final lower bound branch

F_{\lambda,\mu}(f)\geq-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{27\mu^{2}}{4|25-27\lambda|+48}.

The sharpness of each estimate is established by the following extremal functions:

1. The constant upper bound
\frac{1}{4}
and the lower bound branch in Region (iii) are attained uniquely by the function
f_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
defined by

f_{2}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{e^{t^{2}}\sqrt{1+t^{2}}-1}{t}\,dt\right)ds=z+\frac{1}{4}z^{3}+\frac{13}{192}z^{5}+\cdots.

(6.4)

This function corresponds to the choice of Schwarz function
\omega(z)=z^{2}
, which yields the Carathéodory coefficients

and
c_{2}=2
. Substituting these values into (
6.3
) yields
F_{\lambda,\mu}(f_{2})=\frac{1}{4}
.

2. The variable upper bound and the lower bound branch in Region (i) are attained by the function
f_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
defined by (
3.2
). Here, the matching Carathéodory function is
p_{0}(z)=(1+z)/(1-z)
, which yields
c_{1}=2
and
c_{2}=2
. Substituting these values into (
6.3
) yields
F_{\lambda,\mu}(f_{1})=\frac{1}{48}|25-27\lambda|-\frac{3\mu}{4}
.

3. To justify the sharpness of the lower bound in Region (ii), we show the admissibility of the parameters

c_{1}=\frac{36\mu}{|25-27\lambda|+12},\qquad c_{2}=\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2}-\left(2-\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2}\right)e^{-i\theta_{0}},

(6.5)

where

. A pair

if and only if it satisfies the Carathéodory coefficient body condition:

. Subtracting

from

Hence the coefficient pair satisfies the boundary of the Carathéodory coefficient body condition exactly. By Lemma
2.1
, this boundary pair corresponds to a unique function

of the form

p(z)=\frac{1+(\overline{\tau_{1}}\tau_{2}+\tau_{1})z+\tau_{2}z^{2}}{1+(\overline{\tau_{1}}\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})z-\tau_{2}z^{2}},\quad z\in\mathbb{D},

To show that this function explicitly produces equality for Lemma
2.4

K=|K|e^{i\theta_{0}}

c_{2}
from (
6.5

\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})

\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})=\left||K|e^{i\theta_{0}}c_{1}^{2}+\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2}-\left(2-\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2}\right)e^{-i\theta_{0}}\right|-Jc_{1}.

\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})=\left|e^{-i\theta_{0}}\left(|K|c_{1}^{2}e^{2i\theta_{0}}+\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2}e^{i\theta_{0}}+\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2}-2\right)\right|-Jc_{1}.

Since
|e^{-i\theta_{0}}|=1
, and our choice of
\tau_{2}=-e^{-i\theta_{0}}
forces the vector layout in the direction opposite to the phase of

2.4

\Phi(c_{1},c_{2})=\left||K|c_{1}^{2}+c_{1}^{2}-2\right|-Jc_{1}.

Given that

, substituting the optimal parameters simplifies this directly to:

Multiplying this functional by

7
Hermitian–Toeplitz Determinants for the Class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}

For a sequence
\{a_{k}\}_{k=2}^{\infty}
of coefficients of a function
f\in\mathcal{A}
, the third-order Hermitian–Toeplitz determinant starting at index
1
reduces to:

T_{3,1}(f):=2\Re\left(a_{2}^{2}\overline{a_{3}}\right)-2|a_{2}|^{2}-|a_{3}|^{2}+1.

(7.1)

Lemma 7.1

(
[
38
]
)
.

If
p\in\mathcal{P}
is of the form
p(z)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}
, then there exists a complex number
\xi\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
such that

2c_{2}=c_{1}^{2}+(4-c_{1}^{2})\xi.

(7.2)

Without loss of generality, we may assume
0\leq c_{1}\leq 2
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
be of the form (
1.1
). Then

\frac{437}{2304}\leq T_{3,1}(f)\leq 1.

These bounds are sharp.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
. By the Alexander relation,
zf^{\prime}(z)\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{AP}}^{*}
. Equating coefficients, the initial Taylor coefficients of
f
are given by:

a_{2}=\frac{3}{8}c_{1},\qquad a_{3}=\frac{1}{8}c_{2}+\frac{13}{192}c_{1}^{2}.

(7.3)

Applying Lemma
7.1
, we substitute
2c_{2}=c_{1}^{2}+(4-c_{1}^{2})\xi
into (
7.3
), which yields

a_{3}=\frac{25}{192}c_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{16}(4-c_{1}^{2})\xi,\qquad a_{2}^{2}=\frac{9}{64}c_{1}^{2}.

Expanding the individual terms of
T_{3,1}(f)
via (
7.1
) gives:

T_{3,1}(f)=1-\frac{9}{32}c_{1}^{2}+\frac{725}{36864}c_{1}^{4}+\frac{1}{768}c_{1}^{2}(4-c_{1}^{2})\Re\xi-\frac{1}{256}(4-c_{1}^{2})^{2}|\xi|^{2}.

(7.4)

Let
x=c_{1}^{2}\in[0,4]
and
y=|\xi|\in[0,1]
.

Upper bound.

Since
\Re\xi\leq|\xi|=y
, (
7.4
) implies
T_{3,1}(f)\leq F(x,y)
, where

For any fixed
x\in[0,4]

F(x,y)
is a concave quadratic polynomial with respect to
y
, since
\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial y^{2}}=-\frac{1}{128}(4-x)^{2}\leq 0

\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}=0
gives the interior critical path
y_{*}=\frac{x}{6(4-x)}
, which belongs to
[0,1]
when
x\leq\frac{24}{7}

y_{*}
into
F(x,y)
yields

Differentiating with respect to
x
shows that the critical point of
F(x,y_{*})
occurs outside the domain at

. Thus,
F(x,y_{*})
decreases strictly for
x\in[0,4]
. The global maximum occurs at the boundary corner
x=0
, which gives
F(0,0)=1
. Evaluation on the remaining boundaries
y=0
and
y=1
confirms that
F(x,y)\leq 1
across the entire compact parameter space.

To establish the sharpness of the upper bound rigorously, we consider the function
f_{4}(z)\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
explicitly defined by the structural construction:

f_{4}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{e^{t^{3}}\sqrt{1+t^{3}}-1}{t}\,dt\right)ds=z+\frac{1}{8}z^{4}+\cdots.

(7.5)

This function corresponds to the choice of the 3-fold symmetric Schwarz function
\omega(z)=z^{3}
, which generates the Carathéodory parameters
c_{1}=0
,
c_{2}=0
, and
c_{3}=2
. Substituting these values into (
7.3
) yields
a_{2}=0
and
a_{3}=0
. Direct evaluation of (
7.1
) under these initial coefficients confirms
T_{3,1}(f_{4})=1
.

Lower bound.

Using the inequality
\Re\xi\geq-|\xi|=-y
, (
7.4
) implies
T_{3,1}(f)\geq H(x,y)
, where

H(x,y)=1-\frac{9}{32}x+\frac{725}{36864}x^{2}-\frac{1}{768}x(4-x)y-\frac{1}{256}(4-x)^{2}y^{2}.

To minimize
H(x,y)
over the compact region
[0,4]\times[0,1]
, we examine the partial derivative with respect to
y
:

\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}=-\frac{1}{768}x(4-x)-\frac{1}{128}(4-x)^{2}y=-(4-x)\left[\frac{x}{768}+\frac{(4-x)y}{128}\right].

For any
x\in[0,4)
and
y\in[0,1]
, the factor
(4-x)
is positive and the expression inside the brackets is strictly positive except at the single corner
x=0,y=0
. Therefore,
\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}<0
holds uniformly on the interior of the domain.

Because
\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}
is strictly negative,
H(x,y)
is a strictly decreasing function of
y
for any fixed value of
x
. This guarantees that no interior local minimizers can exist inside the rectangle
(0,4)\times(0,1)
, meaning the absolute global minimum must reside on the boundary segment
y=1
.

Setting
y=1
, the function
H(x,y)
simplifies to the single-variable quadratic polynomial:

h(x)=H(x,1)=\frac{629}{36864}x^{2}-\frac{49}{192}x+\frac{15}{16}.

Differentiating
h(x)
with respect to
x
yields
h^{\prime}(x)=\frac{629}{18432}x-\frac{49}{192}
. Setting
h^{\prime}(x)=0
gives a critical point at
x_{0}=\frac{4704}{629}\approx 7.48
. Since
x_{0}>4
, there are no local minimum turnarounds inside the interval
[0,4]
. Given that
h^{\prime}(x)<0
for all
x\in[0,4]
,
h(x)
decreases monotonically across the domain, forcing the absolute minimum to be attained at the rightmost endpoint
x=4
.

Substituting
x=4
into
h(x)
yields the global lower bound:

T_{3,1}(f)\geq h(4)=1-\frac{9}{32}(4)+\frac{725}{36864}(16)=\frac{437}{2304}.

This lower bound is sharp for the function
f_{1}(z)
defined in (
3.2
), which maps to the maximal Carathéodory parameters
c_{1}=2
and
c_{2}=2
. Substituting these parameters into (
7.3
) gives the explicit coefficient values
a_{2}=\frac{3}{4}
,
a_{3}=\frac{25}{48}
. Evaluating the determinant structure
T_{3,1}(f_{1})=2a_{2}^{2}a_{3}-2a_{2}^{2}-a_{3}^{2}+1
with these values results in:

T_{3,1}(f_{1})=2\left(\frac{9}{16}\right)\left(\frac{25}{48}\right)-2\left(\frac{9}{16}\right)-\left(\frac{625}{2304}\right)+1=\frac{437}{2304},

confirming the absolute sharpness of the lower bound.
∎

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we have provided a systematic investigation into several classical coefficient problems for the apple-like convex subclass
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{AP}}
, which is geometrically characterized by the analytical subordination relation
1+zf^{\prime\prime}(z)/f^{\prime}(z)\prec e^{z}\sqrt{1+z}
. By exploiting the structural properties of Carathéodory functions alongside elegant parametric optimization methods, we successfully established sharp upper bounds for the initial inverse logarithmic coefficients
\Gamma_{1}
,
\Gamma_{2}
, and
\Gamma_{3}
.

Furthermore, we extended our study to compound functionals by determining the precise, sharp upper and lower bounds for the consecutive coefficient difference
|\Gamma_{2}|-|\Gamma_{1}|
, as well as the sharp upper bound for the second-order inverse logarithmic Hankel determinant
H_{2,1}\left(F_{f^{-1}}/2\right)
. In addition, we fully characterized the generalized Fekete–Szegő functional over real parameters through piecewise optimization curves and computed the sharp bounds for the third-order Hermitian–Toeplitz determinant
T_{3,1}(f)
.

Crucially, every analytical bound established in this study was proved to be sharp by identifying its corresponding extremal function. These results provide explicit, rigorous data points that enrich the structural theory of inverse coefficient mappings within non-homogeneous, asymmetric target domains.
