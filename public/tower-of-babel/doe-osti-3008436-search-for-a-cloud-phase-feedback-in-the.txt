Final Technical Report for DE-SC0021974
Search for a Cloud Phase Feedback in the Arctic Climate System
Dan Lubin, Scripps Institution of Oceanography

This project was motivated by a hypothesis involving the transition in lower troposphere
temperatures across the freezing point of water. Specifically, at temperatures just below to about
ten degrees below freezing, Arctic clouds should be in a mixed-phase, with strong influences from
secondary ice production (e.g., the Hallett-Mossop process). At temperatures just above freezing,
clouds should become entirely deglaciated. We hypothesized that, with multiple years of ARM
data, a statistically significant change could be detected in cloud radiative properties and surface
radiative fluxes that could be directly attributed to this phase change. Furthermore, in a gradually
warming climate, these phase change-related responses in surface radiation would represent a
cloud phase feedback as part of Arctic amplification. We designed this research project to coincide
with a new ARM Arctic cloud radar product developed by Ed Luke and collaborators at BNL
(Luke et al., 2021: PNAS, doi:10.1073/pnas.2021387118) that explicitly contains SIP cloud
properties retrieved from radar data. This research project has successfully concluded after analysis
of a much larger North Slope of Alaska (NSA) data sample than originally anticipated, and the
results are quite different than originally hypothesized.

We initially focused on the seven years of NSA data for which the SIP properties were retrieved
in the Luke et al. (2021) data product. For additional meteorological analysis we recomputed the
four meteorological regimes identified by Mülmenstädt et al. (2012, J. Climate, doi:10.1175/JCLI-
D-11-00636.1) using k-means clustering on earlier ARM NSA data. The updated clustering was
successful and the four regimes re-emerged in the longer time series, and also showed a long-term
trend away from the colder regimes toward warmer regimes. These four regimes (k-means
Clusters) manifest in all seasons. Cluster 1 describes the coldest regime dominated by the Beaufort
Sea High and features mainly easterly to northeasterly near-surface winds at NSA. Under Cluster
1 conditions at NSA are closest to a "polar desert." Cluster 2 describes the second coldest regime,
characterized by strong low pressure in the Arctic Ocean and Beaufort Sea connected to low
pressure of average strength in the Gulf of Alaska. As the cyclonic flow will carry moisture to
NSA from over the open Arctic Ocean even in the colder conditions, cloud cover under Cluster 2
is more extensive than under Cluster 1 but is limited in vertical extent. Cluster 3 describes a mixture
of weak high pressure over the Arctic Ocean and strong low pressure over the Aleutian Islands
and Bering Sea, thus advecting moisture to NSA from the Gulf of Alaska. This cyclonic flow from
the Gulf of Alaska is impeded by coastal mountain ranges at lower altitudes, but at higher altitudes
moisture traverses the Alaskan peninsula and the Yukon and arrives at NSA mainly in easterly
winds across the Beaufort Sea. Cluster 4 is the warmest and moistest cluster, and is distinct from
Cluster 3 by relative westward displacement of the strong low pressure over the Aleutian and
Bering Seas. This results in a direct and unimpeded path for warm and moist air through the Bering
Strait to NSA. The availability of this prevailing regime analysis allows us to examine any
discovered phase change effect and potential climate feedback in terms of large-scale dynamical
forcing.

The set of NSA radiation measurements and microwave radiometer (MWR) derived cloud liquid
water path (LWP) overlapping with the SIP radar product are sufficient to draw conclusions for
longwave radiation. The coldest regime (Cluster 1) has only clouds below the freezing point.

Above the freezing point, the largest LWP values are seen for the warmest regime (Cluster 4) and,
surprisingly, for the colder regime with local cyclonic influences (Cluster 2). Instead of a
consistent longwave “phase feedback” when clouds become deglaciated, we find that cloud liquid
water content is the major part of the optical depth throughout the temperature ranges considered.
We find that the net longwave fluxes under the warmer clouds are influenced by the changing
synoptic-scale regimes. In the coldest regime the surface shows consistent cooling except for a
few instances where low-level clouds are slightly warmer than the surface, yielding an occasional
positive net longwave flux. The other three regimes show varying behavior when cloud bases are
above freezing. Similar to Cluster 1, the cold regime of Cluster 2 also shows consistent surface
cooling when temperatures are above freezing. However, above freezing the downwelling and
upwelling longwave fluxes are much closer to being in balance. This results from the more
extensive cloud cover in this regime being at a low level, with base temperature at or warmer than
the surface under the warmer conditions. In contrast, the “warm” regimes (Clusters 3 and 4) show
more surface cooling at above freezing cloud base temperatures, compared with the colder
temperature ranges. This is due to the long-range transport brining these warmer clouds over NSA.
Particularly in Cluster 3 with its more circuitous pathways, transport is partially obstructed by high
Alaskan terrain and the clouds arrive typically at higher altitudes than in Cluster 2. Thus the surface
is still warmer than the clouds even when the cloud bases are above freezing. These results, with
additional analysis involving lower troposphere stability (LTS), have been presented at the 2023
and 2025 ASR PI Meetings.

The above is not yet complete, because the case studies considered that coincide with the SIP radar
product do not provide adequate coverage of the shortwave. The SIP radar product is closely tied
with the nearest twice-daily sondes, and these occur at times of low Sun elevation even during
summer. Even when expanding the time interval to one hour before and after a radar product
observation, physical and statistical conclusions are not entirely convincing. We therefore decided
to treat the shortwave by compiling all of the SKYRAD (pyranometer and pyrgeometer)
measurements from NSA going back to 1999, and MWR-derived LWP and precipitable water
vapor (PWV) going back to 2021, in an effort to acquire better statistics. This was a time-
consuming process as the earlier data have inconsistencies and gaps, but now the datasets
standardized as hourly averages are available for a variety of studies.

With 24-26 years of data, one obvious study is to look for multidecadal trends that might be related
to Arctic amplification and a warming climate. So we digressed for several months into this area.
We find that the ARM NSA Facility the broadband radiometric data are indeed beginning to show
statistically significant trends consistent with Arctic amplification. These are supported by
emerging trends in MWR-retrieved PWV and LWP. These results pertain mainly to summer but
significant trends appear in some intervals in all seasons. During summer the trends manifest as
increasing longwave net flux consistent with both a warming lower troposphere and increasing
cloud LWP, with this increase being offset by a larger shortwave net flux decrease also due to
increasing LWP. The result is a statistically significant decrease in Net surface radiative flux
throughout July and August between 1999-2024. Although the summertime increases in PWV are
consistent with a gradually warming lower troposphere, there is evidence that two synoptic-scale
drivers play a larger role in these trends. The warm Cluster 3, associated with low pressure in the
Gulf of Alaska, exerts increasing influence on the LWP and surface radiative fluxes over NSA. In
addition, the frequent low pressure over the Arctic Ocean (Cluster 2) is associated with increasing

atmospheric river (AR) activity that also brings increasing PWV and LWP over NSA with
concomitant trends in surface radiative fluxes. These effects may explain the relatively small
surface warming trend at NSA during summer compared with other seasons and many other Arctic
locations. A manuscript describing these results has been accepted for publication (Lubin et al.,
2025: Atmos. Chem. Phys. Discuss., doi:10.5194/egusphere-2025-2768).

With the surprising result that ARs played a role in the evolution of the summertime trends, we
decided to focus some extra attention on ARs at the NSA site. An AR is a narrow region of intense
horizontal and vertically integrated water vapor transport (IVT) within a low-level jet most
typically generated at the cold front of an extratropical cyclone (Ralph et al., 2018: BAMS,
doi:10.1175/BAMS-D-17-0157.1). ARs are now recognized as a major source of moisture
transport between midlatitudes and both northern and southern high latitudes (e.g., Zhang et al.,
2024: Cryosphere, doi:10.5194/tc-18-5239-2024, and references therein). ARs are generally
associated with elevated and even extreme precipitation that on one hand can cause flooding,
erosion and significant property damage, while on the other hand can replenish mountain
watersheds in the Western United States and elsewhere. We envisioned that ARM Facility's suite
of advanced atmospheric equipment might provide a unique source of information about these
extreme weather events, and therefore performed a small set of case studies with NSA data. We
identified one well-defined AR impacting the NSA site in each season, to examine how the surface
radiation and meteorological measurements, MWR-derived quantities, lower troposphere
thermodynamics (via INTERPSONDE), and cloud phase as a function of altitude (via the new
THERMOCLDPHASE Value Added Product) respond to the IVT impulse. We find that the
vertical structure and origin of the ARs play a major role in the response measured by the ARM
Facility instruments. While we see consistent responses among the cases in lower troposphere
temperature inversions, cooling (in summer) or warming (in winter) effects due to increased cloud
opacity, and phase shifts from ice toward mixed-phase and mixed-phase toward liquid, the
magnitude of these responses varies from clearly evident to barely detectable from the surface.
ARM Facility data, not just at NSA but worldwide, might therefore have a useful role in the study
of ARs detected by satellite remote sensing or reanalysis data, including forecast validation and
refinement of numerical weather prediction models. A manuscript based on these results is in
preparation for submission to Cryosphere (in the EGU journal series, where the submitted
manuscripts are publicly available online) by the end of December 2025. This manuscript will be
provided to the program managers upon submission.

At the same time, our final manuscript covering this project's initial objective - the search for a
cloud phase feedback, which we now find is mostly a liquid water path feedback across the
freezing point of water - is also in preparation for Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics (another
EGU journal) with anticipated submission by the end of January 2026. Overall, we find that the
transition in cloud effective temperatures from the SIP regime to slightly above freezing is
associated with a combination of longwave thermal blanketing (net longwave flux close to zero)
and shortwave cooling (net shortwave reduced by increased LWP), with an overall net radiative
cooling at the surface. This manuscript will also be provided to the program managers upon
submission.

analyzed broadband radiometer data from Ross Island with the help of narrowband atmospheric
transmittance and cloud optical depth measured by the multifilter rotating shadowband radiometer
(MFRSR), meteorological regime classification based on ERA-Interim reanalysis data, and
satellite remote sensing data for cloud phase. Results show that broadband shortwave and
