Age modifies the association between oral behaviors and orofacial pain in painful TMD patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jiaqi Liu, Yunhao Zheng, Weijie Tao, Junqi Liu, Yating Yi, Zhiyi Shan, Jun Wang, Xin Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the association between oral behaviors and orofacial pain among painful TMD patients.Methods343 adults with painful TMD completed the Oral Behaviors Checklist (OBC) and Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) for pain intensity; Characteristic Pain Intensity (CPI) was calculated. Patients were grouped as moderate-to-high CPI (≥ 30) or low CPI (< 30), and group differences were compared. Regression analyses were performed, adjusting for age, sex, education, and TMD diagnosis.ResultsGroup comparisons between moderate-to-high CPI (n = 191) and low CPI (n = 152) groups showed no association between the total OBC score and CPI (p = 0.217). Correlation analysis showed the total OBC score correlated with age (ρ=−0.376), sex (ρ = 0.152), and education (ρ = 0.240). CPI correlated with age (ρ = 0.139) and education (ρ=−0.125). After adjusting for age, sex, education, and TMD diagnosis, the total OBC score was positively associated with CPI (per 10 points: mean ratio [MR] = 1.103; 95% CI 1.025–1.187). A significant interaction between age and total OBC score was also observed (MR = 0.919; 95% CI 0.855–0.987), indicating that the association between oral behaviors and CPI weakened with increasing age.ConclusionsAge correlated positively with CPI and negatively with the total OBC score. Although no significant crude association was observed, the total OBC score became positively associated with CPI after adjustment for age and other covariates. These findings suggest that age-related modification may influence the observed association between oral behaviors and orofacial pain in painful TMD patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08937-9.Keywords: Temporomandibular disorders, Oral behaviors, Orofacial pain, Age

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) comprise a spectrum of musculoskeletal conditions affecting the masticatory system and temporomandibular joints (TMJ). Nearly one-third of the global population is affected, with myalgia, joint sounds, and arthralgia most frequently reported, underscoring the public-health relevance of TMD [1]. Beyond prevalence, TMD contribute substantially to the broader societal burden of chronic pain through productivity losses and health-care costs [2–4]. Etiology is multifactorial; biological, behavioral, and psychological domains interact to shape risk and clinical expression, and no single factor explains onset or course [5].

Within the Diagnostic Criteria for TMD (DC/TMD) framework, patients are categorized as pain-related TMD (PT), intra-articular TMD (IT), or combined TMD (CT) based on the presence of pain-related and/or intra-articular disorders [6]. Oral behaviors are commonly quantified using the 21-item Oral Behaviors Checklist (OBC), which yields a total score indexing behavioral load [7]. In parallel with self-report instruments, objective approaches, such as ecological momentary assessment (EMA) combined with ambulatory masseter surface electromyography (sEMG) for awake bruxism [8], and portable single-channel masseter sEMG validated against polysomnography with audio–video (PSG-AV) for sleep bruxism [9, 10] have improved behavioral phenotyping, although the mapping from EMG activity to pain intensity is not always straightforward compared with OBC [11, 12]. Although many oral behaviors are commonplace and generally innocuous, excessive frequency or intensity may precipitate or exacerbate TMD signs and symptoms [13–15].

Evidence linking oral behaviors to orofacial pain is mixed. Prospective and observational data have related higher behavioral load to painful TMD and greater pain impact [12, 16–18], whereas other clinical studies reported weak or null associations across diagnostic subtypes or item-level measures [19, 20]. These discrepancies suggest that study context (e.g., PT/IT/CT composition, awake or sleep behaviors) and analytic control for covariates critically influence observed associations.

Therefore, the present study aims to investigate whether oral-behavior load relates to orofacial pain in painful TMD and whether age modifies the association between oral behaviors and orofacial pain.

Materials and methods

Study population

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted between January 2023 and June 2025 as part of an ongoing clinical investigation of biopsychosocial characteristics in TMD patients at the West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital of Stomatology (WCHSIRB-D-2022-137) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Fully informed consents were acquired from all participants prior to enrollment.

Participants were consecutively recruited from first-visit patients seeking treatment for TMD. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) proficiency in Chinese; (3) diagnosis of painful TMD, including PT and CT, according to DC/TMD Axis I; and (4) availability of complete sociodemographic information and valid responses to the OBC and Numeric Rating Scale (NRS). Exclusion criteria included: (1) history of head or neck trauma or surgery; (2) presence of other orofacial pain conditions or systemic diseases affecting the TMJ (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis); and (3) severe psychiatric disorders or cognitive impairment that could compromise questionnaire reliability.

Sample size calculation

The minimum required sample size was estimated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9, Germany). Assuming a medium effect size (f²=0.25), α = 0.05, power = 80%, and five covariates in the regression model, a total of 269 participants were required. To improve the precision and generalizability of the estimates, recruitment continued beyond this minimum threshold.

Data collection

All participants completed a self-administered questionnaire published previously [21], comprising demographics, medical history, OBC and NRS at the initial visit with the assistance of investigators. All participants underwent a standardized clinical examination performed by three trained specialists following the DC/TMD protocol as described previously [21].

Questionnaire

Demographic information

Participants provided sociodemographic information including age, sex, educational level, and family per capita monthly income. Medical histories, including systemic diseases, trauma, and surgery, were verified through clinical records.

OBC

Oral behaviors were assessed using the validated Chinese version of the 21-item OBC. Participants rated the frequency of each behavior over the past month using a 5-point scale (0 = none of the time to 4 = all of the time). The total OBC score was calculated as the sum of all item scores, with higher scores indicating greater behavioral load. The total OBC score was computed as the sum of item scores. For descriptive item-level frequency comparisons, responses were dichotomized into present (“Yes”) for scores 3–4 and absent (“No”) for scores 0–2, following previous research [22].

NRS

Orofacial pain intensity was evaluated using NRS. Participants rated their current, worst, and average pain intensity on an 11-point scale (0 = no pain, 10 = worst imaginable pain). The Characteristic Pain Intensity (CPI) score was calculated as the mean of these three ratings multiplied by 10 (range: 0–100). Participants were further categorized into two groups: low CPI (< 30) and moderate-to-high CPI (≥ 30).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with R (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with a significance level of α = 0.05. Data normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test, and p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Spearman’s correlation coefficients (ρ) were calculated to assess associations among total OBC score, CPI, age, sex, education, and income. Because no significant correlation was observed between income and either CPI or total OBC score, income was not included in the primary regression models. To evaluate independent associations, regression analyses were conducted. Gamma regression with a log link function was used for CPI due to its skewed distribution, and results were presented as mean ratios (MR = e^β) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Linear regression analysis was used for total OBC score, and regression coefficients (β) with 95% CI were reported. Covariates including age, sex, education, and TMD diagnosis were entered sequentially to examine their potential influence on the association between oral behaviors and orofacial pain. To further assess whether age acted as an effect modifier, an interaction term between age and total OBC score was additionally included in the gamma regression model. Conditional effects analyses were additionally performed based on the interaction model. Representative age values were defined using the median age within each age tertile group. Predicted marginal effects and age-stratified visualizations were generated to illustrate the interaction pattern between age, oral behaviors, and CPI. For age-stratified visualization analyses, participants were categorized into age tertiles based on the distribution of age in the study population.

Spearman’s correlation coefficients among the total OBC score, CPI, age, sex, education and income were displayed in Fig. 2. With regard to the total OBC score, significant correlations between it and age, sex and education were found, with coefficients of − 0.376, 0.152 and 0.240 respectively. As for CPI, significant correlations were also found between it and age and education, with coefficients of 0.139 and − 0.125 respectively. Notably, age was negatively correlated to the total OBC score but positively correlated to CPI, while education displayed opposite characteristics. Collectively, these results suggested that factors such as age and education may influence oral behaviors and orofacial pain in different directions.

As shown in Table 3, regression analyses were performed with potential covariates including age, sex, education, and TMD diagnosis adjusted consecutively. Without adjusting for age, the total OBC score (per 10 points) was not associated with CPI (MR = 1.038; 95% CI 0.969–1.113), whereas participants with postgraduate education or above had significantly lower CPI (MR = 0.729; 95% CI 0.583–0.912). Gamma regression with log link and linear regression revealed opposite associations of age with CPI and total OBC score: per 10 years, CPI increased significantly (MR = 1.144; 95% CI 1.072–1.221), whereas the total OBC score decreased significantly (β=−3.096; 95% CI − 4.031 to − 2.161). Besides, females reported significantly higher total OBC score (β = 4.555; 95% CI 2.191–6.919), and higher education levels were associated with higher total OBC score (Undergraduate or junior college: β = 3.672; 95% CI 0.913–6.431; Postgraduate and above: β = 3.395; 95% CI 0.030–6.760). After adjustment for age, sex, education, and TMD diagnosis, higher total OBC score (per 10 points) was significantly associated with higher CPI (MR = 1.103; 95% CI 1.025–1.187) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of painful TMD patients, the association between the total OBC score and CPI differed across age levels. Although no significant crude association was observed, higher total OBC score became positively associated with CPI after adjustment for age and other covariates, and a significant interaction between age and total OBC score was identified.

Since the purpose of this study is to investigate the association between oral behaviors and orofacial pain, only painful TMD patients (PT, n = 108; CT, n = 235) were included. Within the scope of the present investigation, TMD patients with moderate-to-high and low CPI showed no significant difference in the total OBC score or frequencies of individual OBC items. Likewise, regression analyses exhibited no significant association between CPI and the total OBC score, which could initially suggest that oral behaviors were not significantly associated with orofacial pain, an observation consistent with several previous studies [19, 20, 23]. However, correlation analyses showed that age was negatively associated with the total OBC score but positively associated with CPI, whereas education showed the opposite pattern, indicating that demographic factors may influence oral behaviors and pain intensity in different directions.

Notably, further regression analyses elucidated that increasing age was associated with higher CPI yet lower OBC-total. After adjustment for age, sex, education, and TMD diagnosis, the total OBC score became positively associated with CPI, suggesting a positive association between behavioral load and orofacial pain. More importantly, interaction analysis further demonstrated that age significantly modified the association between total OBC score and CPI. Specifically, the positive association between oral behaviors and pain intensity appeared stronger in younger individuals and gradually attenuated with increasing age. This interaction pattern was further illustrated by the predicted marginal effect plots and age-stratified visualizations. These findings may help explain why previous studies have reported inconsistent associations between oral behaviors and TMD pain when different age compositions or covariate structures were present [19, 20, 24].

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the association between oral behaviors and TMD pain, including muscle overuse, increased jaw loading, and stress-related parafunctional activities [25–29]. However, these mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study and therefore should be interpreted cautiously. Given the cross-sectional and questionnaire-based design, the current findings primarily support an age-dependent association rather than a definitive mechanistic relationship.

A key contribution of the present study is to unveil the opposite association of age with oral behaviors and orofacial pain, as well as the modifying effect of age on the OBC-CPI association. In the present study, increasing age was associated with increased CPI but decreased total OBC score. Moreover, interaction analysis demonstrated that the positive association between oral behaviors and pain intensity weakened with increasing age. These findings suggest that the relationship between behavioral load and pain intensity may differ across age groups rather than remaining constant throughout adulthood.

One possible explanation is that oral behaviors may contribute relatively more to pain amplification among younger individuals, whereas structural degeneration, chronicity, and other biological or psychosocial factors may play a larger role in older adults [7, 19, 30, 31]. Consequently, the crude overall association between oral behaviors and pain intensity may appear weak when age-related heterogeneity is not considered.

The age-OBC pattern found in this study agrees with an Italian population study showing that mean scores of OBC declined with increasing age and that high-risk OBC grades are less prevalent in older adults [7]. Physiologically, aging is accompanied by sarcopenia and deterioration of masticatory muscles (e.g., masseter), with documented age-related reductions in muscle mass and activity and occlusal force that can lower the capacity for sustained jaw-loading behaviors [32]. Psychosocially, awake bruxism is more tightly linked to stress-related factors than to purely dental determinants; age-related changes in daily stress exposures and coping may therefore contribute to fewer waking-state behaviors in older adults [33]. On the other hand, the relationship between age and orofacial pain in TMD is complex and context-dependent. In a sample of 957 adolescents, facial and TMJ pain increased with age, consistent with our results and indicating a greater pain burden with increasing age [30]. Degenerative joint changes of the TMJ rise with age, increasing nociceptive input from cartilage and subchondral bone [31, 34]. In parallel, aging is associated with diminished efficiency of endogenous pain-inhibitory systems (reduced conditioned pain modulation and augmented temporal summation), which is linked to higher pain severity in older adults, including those with chronic pain [35, 36]. These results offer plausible explanations for the age-related patterns observed here.

Sex and education effects in our models also merit discussion. Higher total OBC score was observed among females, consistent with reports of females had significantly higher total OBC score than males in TMD patients and a student population [7, 37, 38]. Education showed a split pattern: higher educational attainment was associated with lower CPI but higher total OBC score. The former aligns with evidence that lower education is correlated with greater pain intensity in TMD patients [39]. Meanwhile, several student-based surveys show high oral-behavior prevalence in well-educated young adults, likely a function of cognitive/desk work demands and stress, offering a plausible account for the education–OBC association in our models [38, 40, 41].

Clinically, these findings highlight the importance of considering age when interpreting the association between oral behaviors and pain intensity in painful TMD patients. Given the complexity and heterogeneity of TMD, individualized assessment remains important in clinical management.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationships among age, oral behaviors and orofacial pain. Hence, prospective, longitudinal studies are needed to establish causality. Besides, reliance on self-reported oral behaviors and pain intensity may introduce recall and reporting biases. However, a recent study suggested that EMG might not be effective in evaluating correlations between oral behavior and orofacial pain, indicating that comparisons of objective behavioral measures and validated self-report instruments are warranted. In addition, psychosocial variables such as anxiety, depression, stress, sleep quality, and pain catastrophizing were not included in the present analyses. These factors may influence both oral behaviors and pain perception and could partly account for the observed associations. Future studies incorporating psychosocial assessments are warranted. Lastly, the single-center sample may limit external validity and generalizability to broader TMD populations, suggesting future research should incorporate multi-center sampling.

Conclusion

Despite the limitations, this study identified opposite associations of age with oral behaviors and orofacial pain among painful TMD patients: age was positively associated with CPI and negatively associated with the total OBC score. After adjusting for age (and other covariates), the total OBC score was positively associated with CPI in painful TMD. Additionally, females showed higher total OBC score, while higher educational attainment was associated with lower CPI but higher total OBC score. These findings highlight the potential influence of age-related heterogeneity and effect modification when interpreting the association between oral behaviors and pain intensity in painful TMD patients. Prospective and longitudinal studies are warranted to further validate these observations.
