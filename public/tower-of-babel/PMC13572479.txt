Use of mesenchymal stromal cells derived from the Wharton’s jelly of human umbilical cord for treating patients with steroid-resistant graft versus host disease: predicting clinical response by a validated immunopotency assay
Authors: Silvia Torrents, Ana García-Buendía, Margarita Codinach, Luciano Rodríguez, Júlia Marsal Ricomà, Maria del Trabazo del Castillo, Cristina Díaz de Heredia, Maria Rosario Pérez-Torres Lobato, Iván López Torija, Alberto Mussetti, Félix López Cadenas, Marta Peña Domingo, Nerea Castillo-Flores, Sergi Querol, Joaquim Vives
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSteroid-resistant (SR) graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) remains a severe complication following allogeneic hematopoietic progenitor cell transplantation (HPCT). Mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC)-based therapies hold promise but face challenges in demonstrating clinical efficacy. This study investigates how in vitro immunopotency, a critical quality attribute (CQA) of MSC, correlates with patient outcomes to inform MSC-based therapy development for SR-GvHD. In this retrospective descriptive case series study, we evaluated 49 patients (median age: 10 years, interquartile range, IQR: 5–17; 19 females, 30 males) treated for post-allogeneic HPCT acute (N = 40) and chronic (N = 9) SR-GvHD across 4 tertiary hospitals (2018–2022). Clinical-grade MSCs derived from the Wharton’s jelly (MSC, WJ) of the umbilical cord of unrelated donors were administered compassionately at a target dose of 1 × 10⁶ MSC, WJ/kg for a median of 4 infusions at days 1, 4 and once weekly thereafter (197 total infusions). MSC were stratified by immunopotency (in vitro suppression of mixed-lymphocyte reactions: >70% vs. 30%–70%) using a validated assay. An overall response was achieved in 71.4% of patients at day 28 post-treatment (complete response in 11 patients), with 1-year survival of 55.1%. Overall survival in acute GvHD responders was significantly different (P = 0.012) than in the non-responding population. This was especially relevant in acute GvHD patients with improved overall survival in the population under 12 years (P = 0.039). Remarkably, higher in vitro immunopotency strength of MSC, WJ batches correlated with improved clinical responses in patients under 12 years (N = 28, P = 0.039). MSC, WJ therapy proved safe and effective for patients with SR-GvHD, with clinical response rates correlating significantly with measured immunopotency levels. Introduction

Acute (a) and chronic (c) Graft versus Host Disease (GvHD) are serious complications following allogeneic Hematopoietic Progenitor Cell (HPC) Transplantation (HPCT, also commonly referred to in the literature as allo-HCT) that occurs in approximately 50% of the patients1. Only 30%–50% of these patients respond to steroids as first-line of treatment, and a second-line of treatment is not universally agreed, with ruxolitinib being an emerging new option2,3. To address this unmet clinical need, Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMP) are currently being developed leveraging the immunomodulatory properties of multipotent Mesenchymal Stromal Cells (MSC). MSC are a type of mesodermal progenitor cells sourced from vascularised tissues4,5 with a remarkable capacity to exert diverse therapeutic effects via paracrine signalling6,7. Although MSC were first described in the 1970’s8, the understanding of their Mechanism of Action (MoA) in the treatment of different conditions has become one of the main challenges for advancing their development towards marketing authorisation9,10. In this respect, a growing body of evidence has shown the safety and signs of efficacy of bone marrow (BM)-derived MSC infusions for the treatment of acute and chronic GvHD, after the pioneering work of Le Blanc and collaborators, realising the potent immunomodulatory properties of this cell type11,12. Our group has investigated Wharton’s jelly-derived MSC (MSC, WJ) as a superior alternative to traditional sources, such as bone marrow or adipose tissue, to overcome the inherent limitations in cell yield and proliferative capacity associated with the latter13–19. Indeed, MSC, WJ offer several advantages, such as : their accessibility, ease of expansion in vitro, excellent safety profile, multipotentiality, immunopotency, minimal ethical concerns, as well as being harvested from healthy, young and same-aged donors, thereby reducing batch-to-batch variability while offering large scale-up manufacturing potential20–23. Importantly, MSC are hypoimmunogenic and can be used in Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA)-mismatched recipients, as we have already reported elsewhere13,17,18.

Although MSC have shown promise as immunomodulatory agents in the treatment of steroid-resistant (SR) GvHD, yet their persistence, immunotolerance and immunogenicity in the allogeneic setting remain subjects of considerable debate in the literature24,25. MSCs exhibit low immunogenicity due to their lack of expression of HLA class II and co-stimulatory molecules (e.g., CD80 and CD86), allowing them to evade host immune detection. Furthermore, they secrete immunosuppressive cytokines such as Transforming Growth Factor beta (TGF-β) and Interleukin 10 (IL-10), which contribute to the induction of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and modulation of pro-inflammatory immune responses26,27. Clinical studies have documented transient but meaningful improvements in SR-GvHD symptoms following allogeneic MSC administration, suggesting that the therapeutic benefits may not necessitate prolonged engraftment of MSCs28–32. This is further supported by the work of Bader and collaborators33, who demonstrated that standardising MSC products (for example, through the use of “Pool-MSCs”) can significantly enhance immunomodulatory potency and yield high clinical response rates in steroid-refractory patients. Such findings reinforce the concept that the efficacy of MSC therapy is primarily driven by the immediate delivery of a robust secretome rather than long-term cellular persistence.

A significant limitation in comparing clinical outcomes across different studies is the inadequate characterisation of cellular products and the lack of correlation between their critical quality attributes (CQAs) and clinical responses. Specifically, the potency assays commonly employed for the release of MSC used in indications related to the immune system are often based on co-culture systems involving activated peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) and MSCs. However, the in vitro immunopotency metrics derived from these assays have not been consistently correlated with clinical efficacy in the published literature. Consequently, the currently available data are highly heterogeneous, reflecting variability in the tissue sources of MSC, dosing regimens and the absence of standardised quality control (QC) criteria for MSC batch release. Additional variability arises from differences in patient characteristics. Despite the efforts of several authors to systematically review the literature and conduct meta-analyses on the use of MSC as treatment for SR-GvHD, the robustness and reliability of their conclusions remain questionable29–31,34–36.

To help to clarify this situation, herein we investigated the impact of in vitro immunopotency of MSC, WJ on the clinical response of SR-GvHD patients, further to standardisation of methods for immunopotency testing37. To this end, we compiled the information on therapies with MSC, WJ approved under compassionate use conducted in four tertiary hospitals in the Catalan Health System between 2018 and 2021 and analysed patient outcomes up to one year of follow-up post-treatment.

Materials & methods

Study aims

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of comprehensively characterised allogeneic MSC, WJ for the treatment of SR-GvHD. Specifically, the study sought to correlate in vitro immunopotency with clinical outcomes in a cohort of paediatric and adult patients treated under compassionate use authorisation.

Study design and inclusion criteria

This retrospective, multicentre case series analysed clinical data (from July 2018 to December 2022) from 49 patients diagnosed with acute or chronic SR-GvHD who had failed conventional lines of therapy. MSC, WJ were administered as an investigational therapy under off-label, compassionate use authorisation. All patients were followed for up to one year post-treatment. As this study utilizes real-world data gathered via clinical and regulatory reporting under compassionate use, it does not follow a blinded, controlled trial framework. However, data collection was standardised across participating hospitals to ensure consistency.

Ethics

Clinical data were collected from the clinical records of patients at four participating hospitals in Barcelona (Spain), namely: Hospital Universitari de la Vall d’Hebron (VHH) and its paediatric unit VHH-Materno-Infantil (VHH-MI), Hospital Sant Joan de Déu (HSJD), Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau (HSP), and Institut Català d’Oncologia de l’Hospital Duran i Reynals (ICO-DiR). Either the legal guardians or the patients themselves signed the informed consent to receive compassionate use MSC, WJ-based therapy. Good clinical practice guidelines were applied according to the Helsinki Declaration. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of VHH with reference No. PR(BST)449/2020.

Compassionate use of MSC, WJ

All applications for compassionate use were first assessed by a member of the Cell Therapy Service (either co-authors NC or SQ) at Banc de Sang i Teixits (BST, Blood and Tissue Bank), after which a manufacturing slot was offered by BST. Written approval was requested from the Spanish Agency of Medicines and Medical Devices (Agencia Española del Medicamento y Productos Sanitarios, AEMPS) was requested by the clinical team, as well as authorisation from the pharmacy department of each hospital. Patients received standard myeloablative or reduced-intensity conditioning, with primary GvHD prophylaxis consisting of methotrexate (MTX) combined with Cyclosporine or Tacrolimus. Subsequent lines of salvage therapy for steroid-resistant GVHD followed standard institutional algorithms prior to WJ-MSC administration.

Diagnostic criteria and clinical assessment of GvHD

The diagnosis of aGvHD was based on clinical evaluation, supported by histological confirmation when possible (in 69.8% of patients). The severity scoring of aGvHD was graded according to MAGIC criteria38. cGvHD was diagnosed according with the National Institutes of Health Consensus Criteria39. The Karnofsky performance status/Lansky status was measured according to scales reported elsewhere40–42.

Cell manufacturing and treatment

MSC, WJ were derived, expanded and characterised following Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant procedures and appropriate donor informed consent, as reported extensively elsewhere23,43,44. Briefly, a fragment of umbilical cord tissue was cut longitudinally and the WJ was scraped with a surgical scalpel, spread uniformly over the plastic surface of a T-flask with re-closable lid (TPP, Trasadingen, Switzerland), and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Next, 10 mL of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) containing 2 mM glutamine, 2 × 104 UI/mL penicillin (Invitrogen, New York, NY, USA), 20 mg/mL streptomycin (Invitrogen), 120 µg/mL amphotericin B (Invitrogen), and 10%-20% human serum B (hSerB, Banc de Sang i Teixits, Barcelona, Spain) were added. After 2–5 days, a washing step with saline solution was performed, and 10 mL of fresh medium was subsequently added. From this point, the culture medium was replaced every 3–4 days. Cells were further expanded in vitro by seeding cell culture flasks at1–3× 103 viable cells/cm2 using expansion medium without antibiotics until at least 5 × 106 total viable cells were reached. Then MSC, WJ were then cryopreserved, generating the master cell bank (MCB). Further expansion was performed after MCB thawing to generate drug product (DP) cryovials. The final product (FP) was defined as the cell suspension resulting from thawing the DP, washed and conditioned for administration in patients at a target dose of 1 × 106 MSC, WJ/kg with an acceptable dose range of 0.7–1.3 × 106 MSC, WJ/kg. MSC identity was confirmed as CD45–/CD105+, CD31–/CD73+, CD90+, HLA-DR− using validated methods reported elsewhere23,44. MSC immunopotency of DP was confirmed by their capacity to inhibit proliferation of stimulated lymphocytes in co-culture (DP batches showed ≥ 30% inhibition of proliferation) using a validated method reported elsewhere37,45. During the process, intermediate and final MSC, WJ-based products were analysed to verify compliance with the established acceptance criteria in conformity with GMP. All MSC products (DP and FP) tested negative for sterility (bacterial and fungal contamination), absence of Mycoplasma and adventitious viruses, and endotoxin levels were always below 1.00 EU/mL. Catalogue references and suppliers for all critical reagents are listed in SUPPLEMENTARY Table 1.

The treatment regime was set as previously described and administered at days + 1, +4, + 11, and + 18, if possible46,47. MSCs were intravenously infused as reported previously47.

Immunopotency assay

The immunomodulation potential of MSC, WJ was determined by their capacity to inhibit the proliferation of polyclonally stimulated lymphocytes in vitro, following an improved method described first by Oliver-Vila and collaborators48, and further optimised by Torrents and collaborators for clinical application37, as described next. Briefly, PBMCS were isolated from buffy coats of healthy donors via density gradient centrifugation (Histopaque-1077), pooled from five donors and cryopreserved until use. For the assay, 2.5 × 106 PBMC/mL were thawed and then labelled with 0.625 µM carboxy–fluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) for 10 min using the CellTrace™ CFSE Cell Proliferation Kit (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA). After washing,1,2× 107 cells/mL were incubated for 12 min at 37 °C, washed again and seeded onto flat-bottomed 24-well plates (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) at 5:1 PBMC: MSC, WJ ratio. MSC were previously seeded and adhered to the plate surface. Lymphocytes were activated with 25 ng/mL Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.5 µM Ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) in a final volume of 0.5 mL/well of DMEM containing 2 mM glutamine and supplemented with 10% hSerB. Proliferation of PBMC was determined by measuring the reduction of fluorescence intensity at day 5 by flow cytometry using a Navios EX, and raw data were analysed with Kaluza Analysis Software (version 2.1, Beckman Coulter). Inhibition of PBMC proliferation was calculated using Eqs. 1, 2 and 3.

Clinical outcome

While acute and chronic GVHD represent pathophysiologically distinct disorders with different clinical trajectories, they share common immune-mediated pathways. In this study, we initially evaluated the combined cohort to maximize statistical power for overall survival trends. However, given their distinct clinical natures, we also analysed response data for acute and chronic GVHD independently. The response to MSC, WJ-based therapy was categorised as complete (CR, for total resolution of all signs and symptoms in all affected organs), partial (PR, in patients who showed a GvHD reduction of at least one full grade according to the aforementioned criteria), or none (NR). Cases of relapse/recurrence (RR) were also considered, defined as situations in which, after having presented a response, there was disease progression. The sum of CR and PR was referred to as the overall response rate (ORR). The primary endpoint of our study was the ORR of GvHD on day 28 after the start of MSC, WJ therapy, which was defined as the proportion of patients who had a CR or PR as compared with baseline organ staging of the disease. This endpoint was chosen given that response to therapy at day 28 has been found to be predictive of clinical outcomes, including response to MSC therapy, overall survival (OS), and treatment-related mortality49. Moreover, the secondary endpoints were the durable ORR plus OS at 1 year following MSC, WJ therapy, alongside verification of the safety and lack of toxicity of the manufactured MSC, WJ products.

Safety assessment

Patients were monitored throughout the MSC infusion and 4 h after the procedure. During the infusion, non-invasive continuous cardiac and respiratory monitoring was performed, and vital signs were recorded hourly. Premedication was not administered to any patient. Since this therapy was not a clinical trial, but an observational retrospective study, only adverse events (AE) according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, v5) occurring within 4 h after infusion were considered, as this information was specifically documented in the patient clinical charts. Nevertheless, patients’ medical records were carefully reviewed in search of other possible AEs during the follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

Response was defined a priori according to objective criteria of clinical improvement after MSC administration and patients were categorised as responders or non-responders for outcome analysis. This terminology reflects outcome classification rather than proof of causality. Categorical variables were compared between responders and non-responders and patient populations under and above 12 years old (years) using Pearson χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. We predefined a binary age stratification (< 12 vs. ≥12) to reflect real-world eligibility for ruxolitinib licensed as second-line therapy by the American Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA). Quantitative variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney nonparametric test. The Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test were used to estimate and compare OS rates in responders and non-responders. Precision of these estimates was assessed using 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) calculated via the Greenwood formula with a log-log transformation to ensure interval boundaries remained within the [0,1] range. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to obtain adjusted estimates for predictors of OS. Patient response was investigated according to in vitro values of immunopotency of MSC, WJ batches stratified as between 30% and 70%, and 70% to 100%. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant for all the statistical tests, performed using the software RStudio (v4.1.2). Unless otherwise stated, data is presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Due to the cohort size (n = 49), multivariable regression analysis to adjust for potential confounders was not performed to avoid model overfitting.

Results

Patients

The population included in the present analysis comprises 49 patients (adults and children) with SR-GvHD plus ≥ 1 additional lines of immunosuppressive treatment both in aGvHD and in cGvHD. MSC treatments were performed between 2018 and 2021 and were followed up to 1 year in: VHH and VHH-MI (N = 19), HSJD (N = 17), HSP (N = 9), and ICO-DiR (n = 4). The baseline characteristics of the 49 patients (28 younger than 12 years) are shown in Table 1. The median age of patients at the time of the treatment was 10 years; sex and age distribution were 30 males (19 of them under 12 years) and 19 females (9 of them under 12 years). The most common indication for HPCT was a malignant condition (63.3%). HPCT was mostly performed using HPC from unrelated donors using peripheral blood (PB; 54.2%), bone marrow (BM; 35.4%) and cord blood units (CBU; 10.4%). Before allogeneic HPCT, patients received either myeloablative (72.7%) or reduced-intensity (27.3%) conditioning. As GvHD prophylaxis, most patients were treated with cyclosporine A-based protocol (71.1%) and 57.1% of them had received more than two previous lines of therapy to treat GvHD. The majority of patients had severe aGVHD (overall grade III + IV = 61%) (Table 2).

MSC, WJ-based treatment

MSC, WJ were successfully manufactured from three different donors with normal karyotype (46 XY, originating from male newborn donors). From the 3 MCBs of MSC, WJ, twenty-six DPs were manufactured in compliance with current GMP in conformity in terms of identity, purity, potency and safety (Supplementary Figure 1 and supplementary Table 2). A total of 197 infusions were administered to 49 patients. Each patient received a median number of four infusions (range: 2–8) (Figure 1). MSC, WJ were infused at a median dose of 1 × 106 viable cells/kg (range: 0.7 × 106 to 1.2 × 106). MSC, WJ-based therapy was administered intravenously and was well tolerated overall, with no AEs reported. Time elapsed from GvHD onset to MSC, WJ treatment was 54 days in responders (N = 35) and 73 days in non-responders (N = 14) (P = 0.6) (Tables 3 and 4).

Clinical outcome

Clinical response was not predicted by the sex of the patient, hospital of treatment, underlying disease, source of allogeneic HPCT, donor-patient match, prior lines of treatment, GvHD prophylaxis, infections prior to MSC, WJ treatment, Karnofsky performance status/Lansky status, or the number of doses of cells infused were predictors of the clinical response. Time elapsed between GvHD diagnosis and MSC, WJ-based treatment was not associated with clinical response either. Only conditioning was found to correlate with clinical response (P = 0.045), as shown in Table 5, which comprehensively details the baseline HPCT characteristics, including donor relationship types (with unrelated donors comprising 65% of the overall cohort) and graft sources. The landmark survival analysis demonstrated distinct outcomes based on response status. At day 28, the estimated survival probability was 97.1% (95% CI: 91.8%–100%) for responders compared to 85.7% (95% CI: 69.2%–100%) for non-responders. This divergence became more pronounced at the 1-year landmark, where the survival probability was 65.7% (95% CI: 51.7%–83.5%) in the responder group versus 28.6% (95% CI: 12.5%–65.4%) in the non-responder group (Figure 2). At the end of the follow-up, 27 patients (55.1%) were alive and 22 (44.9%) had died due to GvHD (N = 7), disease relapse (N = 2), or other causes (N = 13) (Figure 3).

Impact of MSC, WJ’s potency on the clinical response

Taken all the data together (from both aGvHD and cGvHD), the potency of MSC, WJ influenced the kinetics of early clinical response (P = 0.044) (Figure 4). Patients receiving treatment with > 70% immunopotency demonstrated higher cumulative response rates at all early time points compared to those receiving < 70% potency. Specifically, by day 14, the response rate in the high-potency group was 65% (95% CI: 43%–81%) compared to 30% (95% CI: 13%–50%) in the lower-potency group. By day 28, the majority of patients in the high-potency cohort had achieved a response (81%; 95% CI: 59%–92%), whereas the response rate for the lower-potency cohort was 61% (95% CI: 38%–78%). In a subgroup analysis of paediatric patients aged < 12 years (n = 28), treatment potency was a significant predictor of clinical response (P = 0.039). Children receiving high-potency treatment (> 70%) exhibited rapid clinical improvement, with a day 14 cumulative response rate of 80% (95% CI: 47%–94%), compared to 46% (95% CI: 18%–71%) in the lower-potency group. By day 28, the response rate in the high-potency < 12 year paediatric cohort reached 93% (95% CI: 50%–99%), whereas the lower-potency group achieved a plateau of 62% (95% CI: 29%–83%).

Discussion

The therapeutic efficacy of medicinal products is inherently tied to their potency, a CQA that serves as a quantitative measure of biological activity linked to clinical response50. For ATMPs, however, the complex nature of the active cellular ingredient poses unprecedented challenges in maintaining batch-to-batch consistency51. While an ideal potency assay should reflect the intended MoA, capturing the multifaceted nature of MSC-mediated immunomodulation often necessitates an array of in vitro and in vivo assays52. Replicating the clinical environment remains a formidable task, as simplified experimental models risk generating artefactual data that may not faithfully predict in vivo therapeutic performance53.

Although MSC have shown promise as immunomodulatory agents in the treatment of SR-GvHD28–32, the correlation between potency and clinical response remains poorly understood54. In this context, mechanistic studies have provided important insights, including evidence that infused MSCs may undergo apoptosis in vivo and subsequently be engulfed by recipient phagocytes, leading to indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) production that is critical for mediating immunosuppression, particularly in SR-GvHD55. In parallel, emerging clinical evidence suggests that alternative strategies, such as the use of pooled MSCs from multiple donors and placenta-derived decidua stromal cells, may enhance therapeutic consistency and efficacy in aGvHD, highlighting the relevance of cell source and manufacturing approach as additional determinants of clinical outcome56.

To address this gap in the field, herein we present new findings that help to establish a correlation between validated in vitro potency metrics of MSC, WJ-based treatments23,37,44 and clinical outcomes in SR-GvHD patients, encompassing both adult and paediatric populations in four hospitals within the National Health System of Catalonia, Spain. Despite the inherent limitations of being a case series without control group, our study can be regarded as a real-world setting to assess the therapeutic effect of MSC, WJ. In the present study, we confirmed the safety profile of allogeneic MSC, WJ previously observed in other clinical indications and different routes of administration13,17,18. Indeed, MSC, WJ-based therapy demonstrated excellent tolerability in this compassionate use setting, with no AEs reported. As anticipated, OS was substantially higher among responders compared to non-responders, underscoring the clinical importance of achieving a therapeutic response. Notably, the robust response rate at day 28 was achieved despite the heterogeneity of our patient population and the challenges inherent in compassionate use protocols, which often include individuals with severe or life-threatening conditions. These findings suggest that MSC, WJ therapy can provide meaningful benefits across a diverse clinical spectrum.

Remarkably, our data also highlight the critical role of MSC immunopotency in driving therapeutic outcomes. Batches with immunopotency exceeding 70%, as assessed using a GMP validated in vitro potency assay, were associated with improved clinical responses, particularly in patients under 12 years. This finding reached statistical significance and emphasises the potential of stringent potency criteria in optimising MSC efficacy for paediatric SR-GvHD, a group with limited treatment options. This is of special relevance given that ruxolitinib (a Janus Kinase [JAK]-1 and − 2 inhibitor that blocks pro-inflammatory signalling and modulates T-cell subsets to an anti-inflammatory phenotype) is becoming the gold standard for the treatment of adults and paediatric SR-GvHD patients older than 12 years, so stratifying at 12 years ensures that analyses of treatment patterns, outcomes, and intent-to-treat decisions align with the authorised indication and prescribing practice. While ruxolitinib has meaningfully improved outcomes for patients with SR-aGvHD, those patients who fail to respond to or are intolerant of ruxolitinib typically have a dismal prognosis. New treatment options to treat GvHD safely and effectively in this patient population are urgently needed. In this setting, alternative therapies (i.e. extracorporeal photopheresis, etanercept, mycophenolate mofetil or budesonide) have demonstrated low survival (lower than 10%), as reported by Abedin and collaborators57. These outcome data provide an approximate benchmark for initial assessment of the efficacy of novel therapeutic approaches in which allogeneic MSC, WJ could play a beneficial role58. Moreover, using a small number of well-characterized donors allows greater consistency in cell manufacturing and immunopotency strength compared with settings in which each batch originates from a different donor. Importantly, limited-donor approach enhances internal consistency and interpretability of the observed clinical effects, as it substantially reduces inter-donor biological variability, which is a well-recognised source of heterogeneity in MSC-based therapies.

We have invested many efforts in establishing reproducible, robust and scalable bioprocess designs that ensure batch-to-batch consistency in the production of MSC, WJ and comprehensively characterised them in accordance with internationally agreed recommendations in terms of identity, viability, purity and safety59,60. While the cells maintain standard surface marker identity, application-specific immunopotency attributes, rather than structural differentiation potential, serve as the clinically relevant markers of identity and potency for this GvHD indication. We did this by carefully assessing their immunomodulatory properties using an immune functional assay as a potency release criterion developed and validated under GMP, as reported previously37,44,45. This is in line with recommendations by regulatory bodies and scientific societies61,62, since the minimal criteria for characterising MSC were considered to be an insufficient indicator of therapeutic success. Based on the available information regarding the product development programme for remestemcel-L (the only currently marketed ATMP indicated for SR-GvHD), our approach for measuring MSC, WJ potency is similar but yet much simpler. Remestemcel-L’s immunopotency is linked to three CQA aligned with established specifications including (i) cell viability, (ii) the ability of MSC to inhibit IL-2 Receptor alpha (IL-2Rα) expression on activated T lymphocytes, and (iii) the expression levels of Tumour Necrosis Factor Receptor 1 (TNFR1), which are associated with significant suppression of PBMC proliferation59,63. Our strategy is based in (i) cell viability and (ii) quantitative measure of the potential of MSC, WJ to inhibit activated PBMC from validated cryopreserved stocks in co-culture37,44,45. In both cases, MSC are used in the HLA mismatch allogeneic transplantation setting, thus leading to cell rejection. However, as mentioned previously, clinical evidence supports transient but meaningful improvements in SR-GvHD symptoms following allogeneic MSC administration, suggesting that the therapeutic benefits may not necessitate prolonged engraftment of MSCs. Instead, a paracrine mechanism appears to dominate the MoA of MSCs, with either the secretion of extracellular vesicles and bioactive factors or by efferocytosis, which is based on the short-living time of MSC in vivo and it is independent of the expression of HLA markers64. These discrepancies underscore the complex interplay between MSC biology, the host immune system, and the inflammatory milieu in SR-GvHD. We call for comprehensive mechanistic studies and standardised clinical protocols, which are essential to elucidate these dynamics and optimise therapeutic strategies. Meanwhile, standardisation of potency assays is recognised as a key objective despite little agreement regarding their design and lack of inter-laboratory validated reagents and protocols65. In this respect, we have addressed the critical need for standardisation in the analytical methods, e.g. the biological variability of reagents (such as PBMCs) that hinders reproducibility and cross-comparison of results both within and between laboratories. Our findings in the present study underscore the critical role of immunopotency metrics as predictive tools for patient response and highlight the pressing need for the standardisation and validation of QC methods. Beyond the harmonisation of standardised reagents and acceptance criteria, this endeavour will require increased transparency through public databases and scientific publications, and the fostering of strengthened collaborations among ATMP developers. These efforts are essential to enhance our understanding of clinical outcomes, ensure the robustness of clinical data, and advance the field of cell-based therapies.

Besides potency, conditioning intensity emerged as the only baseline variable significantly associated with clinical response to MSC, WJ therapy in this predominantly paediatric and young cohort. This finding is unlikely to be driven by age or frailty, but rather by differences in immune context at the time of MSC administration. Myeloablative conditioning induces profound lymphodepletion and reduces host antigen-presenting cell activity, which may decrease ongoing alloreactivity and create a more permissive environment for MSC-mediated immunosuppression. Experimental and clinical data indicate that MSC function is highly context-dependent and can be impaired in settings of persistent immune activation or excessive inflammatory signalling55. In contrast, reduced-intensity conditioning may preserve residual host immune responses, including activated T cells and antigen-presenting cells, thereby sustaining inflammatory pathways that limit the immunomodulatory effects of MSCs66. In addition, patients receiving reduced-intensity conditioning may have experienced earlier GvHD onset or required earlier intervention, factors that could further influence response. While these observations should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective design, they support the concept that the immune landscape at the time of MSC infusion is a critical determinant of therapeutic efficacy and warrants prospective evaluation67.

Finally, we acknowledge that the patient population analysed in this study was heterogeneous, reflecting the real-world, compassionate-use setting in which MSC, WJ were administered. The cohort included both adult and pediatric patients with steroid-refractory GvHD, spanning acute and chronic forms of the disease and encompassing variability in disease severity, prior lines of immunosuppressive therapy, and timing of treatment. This heterogeneity, coupled with the retrospective design and absence of a control group, introduces potential confounding factors that may have influenced treatment outcomes. Furthermore, our findings are based on unadjusted associations. The relatively small sample size precluded the use of multivariable models to account for all potential confounding factors, such as baseline GvHD grade or concurrent immunosuppressive regimens. However, these characteristics are intrinsic to real-world clinical practice and provide clinically relevant insights into the use of MSC, WJ in a heavily pre-treated and complex GvHD population. Ongoing clinical use of treatment approaches similar to those presented here may provide additional insights on the differential efficacy of MSC by GvHD subtype, but this was beyond the scope of the present predefined analysis focused on the functional activity with GvHD outcomes. While our results provide strong exploratory evidence for the impact of MSC, WJ potency, they should be validated in larger, multi-centre prospective trials.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our data demonstrates that the use of MSC is an effective and safe therapeutic option for treating acute and chronic SR-GvHD and there is a correlation between clinical response and the measured immunopotency assay. Patients receiving WJ-MSCs characterised by high immunopotency (> 70%) experienced significantly enhanced and accelerated therapeutic benefits compared to those receiving lower-potency batches, establishing this assay as a viable predictor of clinical success. These observations illustrate the impact of in vitro potency metrics on the response to treatments in patients affected by this severe complication of allogenic HPCT.
