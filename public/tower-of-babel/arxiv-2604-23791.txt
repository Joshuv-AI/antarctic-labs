Finite-sample Borel–Cantelli inequalities under mixing conditions
Chatchawan Panraksa

Abstract
We prove explicit finite- N N lower bounds for ℙ ⁡ ( ⋃ k = 1 N A k ) \mathbb{P}(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}) when the σ \sigma -algebras generated by an event sequence satisfy quantitative φ \varphi - or α \alpha -mixing bounds. The main φ \varphi -mixing estimate is obtained by a residue-class blocking argument and a one-sided approximate-independence inequality; it has a free spacing parameter L ≥ 0 L\geq 0 , spacing coefficient 1 / ( L + 1 ) 1/(L+1) , and residual terms governed by φ ⁡ ( L + 1 ) \varphi(L+1) . For α \alpha -mixing families, we derive an additive-correction analogue using strong-mixing covariance control. A windowed rate corollary and a second-order Bonferroni refinement parallel the corresponding m m -dependent finite-sample results. The coefficient 1 / ( L + 1 ) 1/(L+1) is sharp as a universal spacing constant only in the zero-residual sense: the full mixing classes contain L L -dependent block constructions with φ ⁡ ( L + 1 ) = 0 \varphi(L+1)=0 and α ⁡ ( L + 1 ) = 0 \alpha(L+1)=0 that asymptotically attain the corresponding bound. This sharpness statement does not assert optimality of the residual penalties.



Keywords:
Borel–Cantelli lemma; mixing coefficients;

\varphi
-mixing;
\alpha
-mixing; finite-sample inequalities; weak dependence.

Introduction

The Borel–Cantelli lemma is a cornerstone of probability theory.
For a sequence of events
(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}
on a probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, its classical first half states that

\sum_{k}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})<\infty
implies
\mathbb{P}(A_{k}\ \mathrm{i.o.})=0
,
while the second half shows that, under mutual independence,

\sum_{k}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})=\infty
implies
\mathbb{P}(A_{k}\ \mathrm{i.o.})=1
;
see, for instance,
[
8
]
.
Because independence is a restrictive assumption, a substantial literature
has developed around relaxations in which the conclusion of the second
lemma continues to hold. Classical extensions include the
Kochen–Stone inequality and the Erdős–Rényi criterion, formulated
for example in
[
1
, Theorems 1.3 and 1.4]
; the
partial-maximum crossing criteria of Ortega and Wschebor

[
13
, pp. 85–98]
; Petrov’s generalization of the
Erdős–Rényi formulation
[
15
, p. 283]
; Chandra’s
monograph treatment of Borel–Cantelli extensions
[
4
]
;
and Freedman’s conditional Borel–Cantelli inequality

[
10
]
. These works provide qualitative, asymptotic, or
conditional background for the second Borel–Cantelli lemma, but they
do not by themselves give the kind of finite-
N
estimate pursued here,
namely one expressed only through the marginal probabilities and a
finite-lag dependence coefficient.

For finite
N
, however, there is also a direct second-moment lower
bound. If
Z_{N}:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}
and

S_{N}:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})
, then the
Paley–Zygmund/Cauchy–Schwarz argument gives the Chung–Erdős
finite union bound
[
5
]

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\Bigr)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{N}>0)\geq\frac{S_{N}^{2}}{\sum_{i,j\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{j})}.

This bound is a natural comparator for the present paper. It can be very
effective when the global pairwise intersections are known or sharply
estimated. The aim here is complementary: we seek explicit finite-sample
lower bounds that use only the one-dimensional marginals and a mixing
coefficient evaluated at a chosen spacing. Thus the intended use case is
one in which detailed pairwise intersection information is unavailable
or inconvenient, but quantitative weak-dependence information is known.
When local intersection information is available, the second-order bound
below connects the same blocking method with Bonferroni-type refinements.

A natural and frequently studied structured dependence is

m
-dependence
: the sequence
(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is called

m
-dependent if any two subcollections whose index sets are
separated by more than
m
positions are independent.
Lu, Shi and Zhao
[
12
]
recently revisited the second
Borel–Cantelli lemma in this regime, establishing that

\sum_{k}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})=\infty
implies
\mathbb{P}(A_{k}\ \mathrm{i.o.})=1
.
Their quantitative rate statement
[
12
, Theorem 7]

carries the coefficient
1/(2m)
. Complementing this asymptotic analysis, the author

[
14
]
obtained an explicit finite-sample lower bound,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{m+1}\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\Bigr),

(1)

whose proof splits
\{1,\dots,N\}
into
m+1
residue classes modulo

m+1
, applies independence within each class, and selects a class with
maximal total mass. The same reference contains a windowed rate corollary
and a second-order, Bonferroni-type refinement involving local pairwise
intersection probabilities obtained via shifted block partitions.

The hypothesis of
m
-dependence, although convenient, is qualitatively
restrictive: it demands that dependence vanish abruptly past a fixed
range. In most time-series, stationary-process, and dynamical-systems
applications the dependence instead
decays
with the gap.
The quantitative theory of such decay is captured by
mixing
coefficients
—the strong-mixing coefficient
\alpha(n)
of Rosenblatt,
the uniform-mixing coefficient
\varphi(n)
of Ibragimov, and related
quantities
\rho(n)
,
\beta(n)
and
\psi(n)
; comprehensive treatments
are given by Bradley
[
2
,
3
]
, Doukhan

[
7
]
, and Rio
[
16
]
. Under the convention made
precise in Section
2.1
,
m
-dependence is
the extreme case in which the relevant past–future coefficients vanish
for all lags larger than
m
. The finite-sample bound
(
1
) therefore raises the natural question addressed
in the present paper:

To what extent does the residue-class estimate
(
1
) persist when
m
-dependence is replaced by a
quantitative mixing condition?

The aim of this note is to provide such an extension. Our main result
(Theorem
3.1
, below) asserts that if
(A_{k})
is

\varphi
-mixing with coefficient
\varphi(\cdot)
, then for every
integer
L\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Biggl(-\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}\Biggr).

(2)

In particular, when
\varphi(L+1)\leq\min_{1\leq k\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})
, this
becomes the clean residual form with exponent

(S_{N}-N\varphi(L+1))/(L+1)
. The proof retains
the residue-class strategy of
[
14
]
, but replaces exact
independence within a class by an approximate-independence estimate
controlled by
\varphi(L+1)
; choosing
L
large enough that
\varphi(L+1)

is small then yields a clean non-asymptotic bound. In the limit

\varphi(L+1)=0
the exponent reduces to
S_{N}/(L+1)
, so
taking
L=m
recovers (
1
) exactly.

The sharpness result proved below should be read in this precise sense:
it concerns the universal spacing coefficient
1/(L+1)
over the full
mixing classes, which include finite-range dependent examples. It does
not claim optimality of the residual terms involving
\varphi(L+1)
or

\alpha(L+1)
, nor of subclasses that exclude such zero-residual
examples. Throughout the paper, sharpness of this coefficient is
therefore a zero-residual statement.

Two complementary extensions are developed. First, we give an

\alpha
-mixing analogue (Theorem
3.5
), which trades
the sharper coefficient of the
\varphi
-mixing inequality for
applicability to a wider class of processes; the proof uses the
elementary event-level covariance control supplied by the strong-mixing
coefficient. Second, we
record a windowed rate corollary (Corollary
3.9
)
paralleling
[
14
, Corollary 3]
and a second-order
refinement (Theorem
3.10
) paralleling

[
14
, Theorem 4]
, in which local pairwise intersection
probabilities appear explicitly in the exponent. Worked examples
illustrate the resulting constants for geometrically
\varphi
-mixing
sequences and polynomially
\alpha
-mixing sequences.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section
2
fixes notation and recalls the standard mixing
coefficients together with the technical inequalities used in the
proofs. Section
3
contains the main results: the

\varphi
-mixing finite-sample inequality
(Section
3.1
), its
\alpha
-mixing analogue
(Section
3.2
), the windowed corollary
(Section
3.3
), the second-order refinement
(Section
3.4
), and a universal sharpness
discussion for the spacing constant (Section
3.5
).
Section
4
works out illustrative examples and compares
the new bounds with the
m
-dependent baseline (
1
)
and with the finite-sample second-moment bound. Section
5

collects concluding remarks, literature comparisons, and open problems.

2
Preliminaries

This section fixes notation, recalls the mixing coefficients used
throughout the paper, and records the three elementary inequalities that
underlie all subsequent arguments: a product-to-exponential bound, a

\varphi
-mixing approximate-independence estimate, and strong-mixing
covariance bounds for
\alpha
-mixing.

Throughout,
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a fixed probability space and

(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a sequence of events, which we often treat through
the
\sigma
-algebras they generate. We use

\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\dots\}
. For any finite collection

\{A_{k}:k\in J\}
we write
\sigma(A_{k}:k\in J)
for the generated

\sigma
-algebra; for integers
1\leq a\leq b
we write

\mathcal{F}_{a}^{b}:=\sigma(A_{j}:a\leq j\leq b),\qquad\mathcal{F}_{a}^{\infty}:=\sigma(A_{j}:j\geq a).

The distance between two nonempty index sets
I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is

\dist(I,J)=\inf\{|i-j|:i\in I,\,j\in J\}
, and we use the convention
that an empty product equals one.

2.1
Mixing coefficients

We recall the standard definitions of the mixing coefficients associated
with two sub-
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
; comprehensive treatments are given in
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
16
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Mixing coefficients between
\sigma
-algebras)
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be sub-
\sigma
-algebras
of
\mathcal{F}
. Define

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle:=\sup\Bigl\{\,|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)|:\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{A},\,B\in\mathcal{B}\end{subarray}\,\Bigr\},

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle:=\sup\Bigl\{\,|\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)-\mathbb{P}(B)|:\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{A},\,\mathbb{P}(A)>0,\\
B\in\mathcal{B}\end{subarray}\,\Bigr\},

\displaystyle\beta(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle:=\tfrac{1}{2}\sup\sum_{i,j}|\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap B_{j})-\mathbb{P}(A_{i})\mathbb{P}(B_{j})|,

\displaystyle\rho(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle:=\sup\Biggl\{\,\frac{|\Cov(X,Y)|}{\|X\|_{2}\|Y\|_{2}}:\begin{subarray}{c}X\in L^{2}(\mathcal{A}),\,Y\in L^{2}(\mathcal{B}),\\
\|X\|_{2}\|Y\|_{2}>0\end{subarray}\,\Biggr\},

\displaystyle\psi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle:=\sup\Biggl\{\,\left|\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)}-1\right|:\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{A},\,B\in\mathcal{B},\\
\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)>0\end{subarray}\,\Biggr\},

where the supremum defining
\beta
is taken over all finite partitions

(A_{i})
of
\Omega
in
\mathcal{A}
and
(B_{j})
of
\Omega
in

\mathcal{B}
.

Given a sequence of events
(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, we set

\alpha(n):=\sup_{k\geq 1}\alpha\!\bigl(\mathcal{F}_{1}^{k},\mathcal{F}_{k+n}^{\infty}\bigr),\qquad\varphi(n):=\sup_{k\geq 1}\varphi\!\bigl(\mathcal{F}_{1}^{k},\mathcal{F}_{k+n}^{\infty}\bigr),

and similarly for
\beta(n)
,
\rho(n)
,
\psi(n)
. We call

(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}

\alpha
-mixing
(respectively,

\varphi
-mixing,
\beta
-mixing,
\rho
-mixing,
\psi
-mixing) if

\alpha(n)\to 0
(respectively,
\varphi(n)\to 0
, …) as

n\to\infty
. Only the coefficients
\alpha
and
\varphi
are used in
the main estimates below;
\beta,\rho
and
\psi
are recalled for
orientation and for the discussion of possible extensions.

When a theorem is applied to a finite family
(A_{k})_{1\leq k\leq N}
, the
family is understood either as the first
N
terms of an infinite
sequence with the stated coefficients, or with the corresponding
finite restricted coefficients. In the finite interpretation, one
equivalently replaces
\mathcal{F}_{k+n}^{\infty}
by

\mathcal{F}_{k+n}^{N}
and takes the supremum only over indices with

k+n\leq N
; the same convention applies to
\alpha,\varphi,\beta,\rho

and
\psi
. If the set of admissible indices is empty, the corresponding
finite restricted coefficient is understood to be
0
. When finite
restricted coefficients are used in the proofs below, every future

\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{a}^{\infty}
is read as

\mathcal{F}_{a}^{N}
, with empty future index sets interpreted according
to this convention. This convention is used only to control pairs or
blocks appearing in the finite proof. In particular, when a finite family
has length
N
and
L+1\geq N
, the restricted coefficient at lag
L+1
is
zero by this convention. This does not create an artificial
strengthening: then each residue class used below contains at most one
index, and, with

S_{N}:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})
,

1-e^{-S_{N}/(L+1)}\leq\frac{S_{N}}{L+1}\leq\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\Bigr).

Remark 2.2

(Monotonicity of coefficients)
.

If
\mathcal{A}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
and

\mathcal{B}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
, then

\alpha(\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{B}_{0})\leq\alpha(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}),\qquad\varphi(\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{B}_{0})\leq\varphi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}).

We shall use this monotonicity when passing from selected events or
blocks to the full past and future
\sigma
-algebras in the definitions
of
\alpha(n)
and
\varphi(n)
.

Remark 2.3

(Inheritance from an underlying process)
.

Suppose
A_{k}\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
for sub-
\sigma
-algebras

(\mathcal{G}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, and define

\mathcal{G}_{a}^{b}:=\sigma(\mathcal{G}_{j}:a\leq j\leq b),\qquad\mathcal{G}_{a}^{\infty}:=\sigma(\mathcal{G}_{j}:j\geq a).

If the underlying process has coefficients

\alpha_{X}(n):=\sup_{k\geq 1}\alpha(\mathcal{G}_{1}^{k},\mathcal{G}_{k+n}^{\infty}),\qquad\varphi_{X}(n):=\sup_{k\geq 1}\varphi(\mathcal{G}_{1}^{k},\mathcal{G}_{k+n}^{\infty}),

then the event sequence
(A_{k})
has coefficients

\alpha_{A}(n)\leq\alpha_{X}(n)
and

\varphi_{A}(n)\leq\varphi_{X}(n)
. Indeed,

\mathcal{F}_{1}^{k}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{1}^{k}
and

\mathcal{F}_{k+n}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{k+n}^{\infty}
, so the claim
follows from Remark
2.2
. The same argument applies
to the finite restricted coefficients formed from

(A_{k})_{1\leq k\leq N}
and
(\mathcal{G}_{k})_{1\leq k\leq N}
.

Remark 2.4

(Hierarchy of mixing conditions)
.

The only comparison between mixing coefficients used below is the
standard implication from
\varphi
-mixing to
\alpha
-mixing. Indeed,
for every pair
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
,

\alpha(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\leq\varphi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}),

because

|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)|=\mathbb{P}(A)\,|\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)-\mathbb{P}(B)|\leq\varphi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

whenever
A\in\mathcal{A}
,
B\in\mathcal{B}
, and
\mathbb{P}(A)>0
, while the
case
\mathbb{P}(A)=0
is trivial. Broader comparison inequalities among

\alpha,\beta,\varphi,\rho
and
\psi
depend on the adopted
normalizations and are not needed here; see
[
2
, §1.1]

for standard conventions. Consequently every
\varphi
-mixing sequence
is
\alpha
-mixing, but the converse may fail.

Remark 2.5

(
m
-dependence as a limiting case)
.

In this paper,
m
-dependence means finite-range independence in the
following separated-subcollection sense: whenever two index sets

I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}
satisfy
\dist(I,J)>m
, the
\sigma
-algebras

\sigma(A_{i}:i\in I)
and
\sigma(A_{j}:j\in J)
are independent. Taking

I=\{1,\dots,k\}
and
J=\{k+n,k+n+1,\dots\}
shows that

\mathcal{F}_{1}^{k}
and
\mathcal{F}_{k+n}^{\infty}
are independent
whenever
n>m
. Hence
\varphi(n)=\alpha(n)=0
for all
n>m
.

Conversely, suppose the past–future independence

\mathcal{F}_{1}^{k}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathcal{F}_{k+n}^{\infty}
holds
for every
k\geq 1
and every
n>m
. For finite separated sets

I,J\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, decompose
I\cup J
into maximal ordered blocks

B_{1}<\cdots<B_{s}
such that consecutive blocks are separated by more
than
m
. No block contains indices from both
I
and
J
, because

\dist(I,J)>m
. The past–future assumption, applied successively at the
gaps between these blocks, implies by induction that the
\sigma
-algebras

\sigma(A_{i}:i\in B_{\ell})
,
1\leq\ell\leq s
, are mutually independent:
after the first
r
blocks have been separated from the remaining
future blocks, one repeats the same past–future split at the next gap.
Therefore the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the blocks lying in
I
is
independent of the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the blocks lying in
J
.
The general countable case follows by the usual monotone-class
extension from finite subcollections. Thus the separated-subcollection
and past–future formulations agree on the present one-dimensional
index set.

In this sense, the mixing setting is a quantitative relaxation of

m
-dependence; all results of the present paper reduce to the
corresponding statements of
[
14
]
under the substitution

\varphi(n)=0
for
n>m
.

2.2
Elementary inequalities

We record three elementary tools used repeatedly below.

Lemma 2.6

(Product-to-exponential bound)
.

For any integer
M\geq 1
and any
x_{1},\dots,x_{M}\in[0,1]
,

\prod_{j=1}^{M}(1-x_{j})\leq\exp\!\Bigl(-\sum_{j=1}^{M}x_{j}\Bigr).

Proof.

The scalar inequality
\log(1-x)\leq-x
for
x\in[0,1]
, applied
term-by-term and then exponentiated, yields the statement.
∎

Lemma 2.7

(Approximate independence under
\varphi
-mixing)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{M}
be sub-
\sigma
-algebras of

\mathcal{F}
such that, for each
1\leq r\leq M-1
,

\varphi\!\Bigl(\,\sigma\!\bigl(\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{A}_{r}\bigr),\;\mathcal{A}_{r+1}\Bigr)\leq\varphi_{\star}.

Let
C_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,M
, and set

u_{j}:=\mathbb{P}(C_{j})
. Then

\mathbb{P}\!\Bigl(\bigcap_{j=1}^{M}C_{j}^{c}\Bigr)\;\leq\;\prod_{j=1}^{M}\min\!\bigl(1,\,1-u_{j}+\varphi_{\star}\bigr)\;\leq\;\exp\!\Bigl(-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\bigl(u_{j}-\varphi_{\star}\bigr)_{+}\Bigr),

(3)

where
(x)_{+}:=\max(x,0)
.

Proof.

We first prove the left inequality by induction on
M
.
The case
M=1
is trivial since

\mathbb{P}(C_{1}^{c})=1-u_{1}\leq\min(1,1-u_{1}+\varphi_{\star})
.
Assume the inequality holds for
M-1
and write

D_{r}:=\bigcap_{j=1}^{r}C_{j}^{c}\in\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{A}_{r})
.
If
\mathbb{P}(D_{M-1})=0
the inequality is immediate. Otherwise, by
definition of the coefficient
\varphi
,

\bigl|\mathbb{P}(C_{M}^{c}\mid D_{M-1})-\mathbb{P}(C_{M}^{c})\bigr|\leq\varphi\!\Bigl(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{A}_{M-1}),\,\mathcal{A}_{M}\Bigr)\leq\varphi_{\star},

so
\mathbb{P}(C_{M}^{c}\mid D_{M-1})\leq 1-u_{M}+\varphi_{\star}
and, since
conditional probabilities take values in
[0,1]
,

\mathbb{P}(C_{M}^{c}\mid D_{M-1})\leq\min(1,1-u_{M}+\varphi_{\star}).

Multiplying by
\mathbb{P}(D_{M-1})
and applying the induction hypothesis gives
the product bound.

For the exponential bound, use

\min(1,1-u+\varphi_{\star})\leq\exp\bigl(-(u-\varphi_{\star})_{+}\bigr),\qquad 0\leq u\leq 1.

Indeed, if
u\leq\varphi_{\star}
the right-hand side is
1
; if

u>\varphi_{\star}
, then

1-u+\varphi_{\star}\leq\exp(-(u-\varphi_{\star}))
by

1-x\leq e^{-x}
. Multiplying over
j
completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

Lemma
2.7
gives a one-sided
approximate-independence bound for the joint
non-occurrence

\bigcap_{j}C_{j}^{c}
, which is precisely the form needed for
Borel–Cantelli-type estimates. The first factor may be kept
unpenalized, replacing

\min(1,1-u_{1}+\varphi_{\star})
by
1-u_{1}
, since no mixing estimate is
used at the first step. We use the uniform penalized form in
Lemma
2.7
to keep the residue-class estimates
notationally simple. A two-sided version, which is not required here,
appears in
[
16
, Ch. 1]
.

Lemma 2.9

(Strong-mixing bounds)
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be sub-
\sigma
-algebras
with
\alpha(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\leq\alpha_{\star}
. Then, for any

A\in\mathcal{A}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}
,

\bigl|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)\bigr|\leq\alpha_{\star}.

More generally, for any random variables
X\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{A})

and
Y\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{B})
,

|\Cov(X,Y)|\leq 4\,\alpha_{\star}\,\|X\|_{\infty}\|Y\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

The first statement follows directly from the definition of

\alpha(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
applied to the events
A
and
B
. The
second is the classical bound of Davydov
[
6
]
; see also
the monograph treatment in
[
3
]
and the covariance
inequality of
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
.
∎

Remark 2.10

(Indicator version)
.

For indicator random variables
X=\mathbf{1}_{A}
and
Y=\mathbf{1}_{B}

we shall only use the first part of Lemma
2.9
, since the
universal bound on indicator covariances by
\alpha_{\star}
is already
sharp and does not involve the extra factor
4
.

2.3
A blocking construction

The proofs of Section
3
rely on a
spacing
partition
of the index set
\{1,\dots,N\}
, which generalises the
residue-class split used in
[
14
]
. Given integers

N\geq 1
,
L\geq 0
(the
spacing parameter
), and

r\in\{1,\dots,L+1\}
, define the
r
-th spaced class

J_{r}(L):=\bigl\{\,k\in\{1,\dots,N\}:k\equiv r\pmod{L+1}\,\bigr\},

(4)

so that
\{J_{r}(L):1\leq r\leq L+1\}
is a partition of
\{1,\dots,N\}

into
L+1
classes, each of which consists of indices at least
L+1

apart. Writing
J_{r}(L)=\{k_{r,1}<k_{r,2}<\cdots\}
, the gaps

k_{r,j+1}-k_{r,j}
are all
\geq L+1
; consequently, for a

\varphi
-mixing sequence, the approximate-independence bound
(
3
) applies within each
J_{r}(L)
with

\varphi_{\star}=\varphi(L+1)
. When
(A_{k})
is
m
-dependent, the
choice
L=m
makes
\varphi(L+1)=0
and recovers the residue-class
partition of
[

3.1

Theorem 3.1

(
\varphi
-mixing inequality)
.

Let
N\geq 1
and let
(A_{k})_{1\leq k\leq N}
be events on

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Let
\varphi(\cdot)
denote either the
ambient event-sequence coefficient or the finite restricted coefficient
defined in Section
2.1
. Then for every
integer
L\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Biggl(-\,\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}\Biggr).

(5)

In particular, if
\varphi(L+1)\leq\min_{1\leq k\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{L+1}\bigl(S_{N}-N\varphi(L+1)\bigr)\Bigr).

(6)

Proof.

Fix an integer
L\geq 0
and recall the spaced classes
J_{r}(L)
of
(
4
). Empty classes are ignored in applications of
Lemma
2.7
; their contribution to the displayed
sums is zero and the corresponding empty intersection is
\Omega
.
For each non-empty
J_{r}(L)
, enumerate its elements in increasing order
as
k_{r,1}<k_{r,2}<\cdots<k_{r,n_{r}}
, and set

\mathcal{A}_{j}:=\sigma(A_{k_{r,j}})
. For
j=1
no mixing estimate
is needed. For
j\geq 2
, since
k_{r,j}-k_{r,j-1}\geq L+1
, the

\sigma
-algebra

\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{A}_{j-1})
is contained in

\mathcal{F}_{1}^{k_{r,j-1}}
, while
\mathcal{A}_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{k_{r,j}}^{k_{r,j}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{k_{r,j-1}+L+1}^{\infty}
. Hence, by the monotonicity in
Remark
2.2
,

\varphi\!\bigl(\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{A}_{j-1}),\,\mathcal{A}_{j}\bigr)\leq\varphi(L+1)=:\varphi_{\star}.

Applying Lemma
2.7
with
C_{j}:=A_{k_{r,j}}

first gives the sharper product estimate

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcap_{k\in J_{r}(L)}A_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\Bigr)\;\leq\;\prod_{k\in J_{r}(L)}\min\bigl(1,1-\mathbb{P}(A_{k})+\varphi_{\star}\bigr).

(7)

Using the exponential part of Lemma
2.7
then gives

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcap_{k\in J_{r}(L)}A_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\Bigr)\;\leq\;\exp\!\Bigl(-\sum_{k\in J_{r}(L)}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi_{\star}\bigr)_{+}\Bigr).

(8)

Since
\bigcap_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}\subseteq\bigcap_{k\in J_{r}(L)}A_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}
for every
r
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcap_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\Bigr)\;\leq\;\min_{1\leq r\leq L+1}\exp\!\Bigl(-\sum_{k\in J_{r}(L)}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi_{\star}\bigr)_{+}\Bigr).

(9)

Because the classes
J_{1}(L),\dots,J_{L+1}(L)
partition

\{1,\dots,N\}
,

\sum_{r=1}^{L+1}\sum_{k\in J_{r}(L)}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi_{\star}\bigr)_{+}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi_{\star}\bigr)_{+},

so at least one class satisfies

\sum_{k\in J_{r^{*}}(L)}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi_{\star}\bigr)_{+}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi_{\star}\bigr)_{+}.

Substituting this class into (
9
) yields

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcap_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\Bigr)\;\leq\;\exp\!\Biggl(-\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi_{\star}\bigr)_{+}\Biggr),

which is (
5
) after taking complements. The simplified
form (
6
) follows on noting that

(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi_{\star})_{+}=\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi_{\star}
for every

k
whenever
\varphi_{\star}\leq\min_{k}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})
.
∎

Remark 3.2

(Edge cases and recovery of the
m
-dependent bound)
.

The theorem includes several boundary cases. When
L=0
, the spaced
classes are single congruence classes modulo one, so the estimate uses
the coefficient at lag one and applies directly to the original order of
the sequence. If
(A_{k})
is
m
-dependent, then
\varphi(n)=0
for all

n>m
; taking
L=m
in (
5
) gives

\varphi_{\star}=0
and the right-hand side reduces to

1-\exp(-S_{N}/(m+1))
, which is exactly

[
14
, Theorem 2]
. Under the finite restricted coefficient
convention of Section
2.1
, if
L\geq N-1

then the coefficient at lag
L+1
is zero and each spaced class contains
at most one event; the resulting bound is consistent with the elementary
inequality displayed in Section
2.1
.

The resulting constant
1/(m+1)
is optimal in the
m
-dependent
zero-residual setting, as shown in Section
3.5
below.
More generally, the sharpness result in Section
3.5

concerns the universal spacing coefficient in the zero-residual case

\varphi(L+1)=0
or
\alpha(L+1)=0
. It should not be read as a claim
that the residual penalties in Theorems
3.1
and

3.5
are optimal.

Corollary 3.3

(Optimised exponent)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
3.1
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\bigl(-\Psi(N,\varphi)\bigr),

(10)

where

\Psi(N,\varphi)\;:=\;\sup_{L\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}.

Remark 3.4

(Choosing the spacing parameter)
.

The choice of
L
balances two effects. Increasing
L
reduces the
mixing residual
\varphi(L+1)
but also decreases the prefactor

1/(L+1)
in the exponent. In applications with a positive lower mass,
one often chooses the smallest
L
for which

\varphi(L+1)\leq\min_{1\leq k\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})
or a fixed fraction of this
minimum; without such a lower mass, the optimised exponent

\Psi(N,\varphi)
records the best finite-
N
choice available from this
argument.

Under the finite restricted coefficient convention of
Section
2.1
, the supremum in
Corollary
3.3
may be computed over the finite range

0\leq L\leq N-1
. At the endpoint
L=N-1
, the restricted coefficient at
lag
N
is already zero and the resulting exponent is
S_{N}/N
. For any

L\geq N
, the restricted coefficient at lag
L+1
is also zero, but the
exponent
S_{N}/(L+1)
is no larger than this endpoint value.

3.2
An
\alpha
-mixing analogue

For
\alpha
-mixing sequences, Lemma
2.7
is no
longer available and must be replaced by the strong-mixing covariance
bounds of Lemma
2.9
. In the argument below this substitution
produces an additive accumulation of the mixing coefficient, rather than
a multiplicative conditional-probability correction, and hence an
additive correction term in the final inequality.

Theorem 3.5

(
\alpha
-mixing inequality)
.

Let
N\geq 1
and let
(A_{k})_{1\leq k\leq N}
be events on

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Let
\alpha(\cdot)
denote either the
ambient event-sequence coefficient or the finite restricted coefficient
defined in Section
2.1
. Then for every
integer
L\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{S_{N}}{L+1}\Bigr)\,-\,\Bigl\lceil\tfrac{N}{L+1}\Bigr\rceil\alpha(L+1).

(11)

Proof.

Fix
L\geq 0
and the classes
J_{r}(L)
of (
4
). Empty
classes are ignored; for such classes the intersection is
\Omega
and
their mass contribution is
0
. For a non-empty class, write its elements
as
k_{r,1}<\cdots<k_{r,n_{r}}
. Set

q_{0}:=1
,
q_{j}:=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\bigcap_{i=1}^{j}A_{k_{r,i}}^{\mathrm{c}}\bigr)

for
1\leq j\leq n_{r}
, and abbreviate
p_{r,j}:=\mathbb{P}(A_{k_{r,j}})
.
For
j=1
no mixing estimate is needed. For
j\geq 2
, write

D_{j-1}:=\bigcap_{i<j}A_{k_{r,i}}^{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{k_{r,j-1}}
. Since

A_{k_{r,j}}^{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{F}_{k_{r,j}}^{k_{r,j}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{k_{r,j-1}+L+1}^{\infty}
, Lemma
2.9
and
Remark
2.2
give

\bigl|\mathbb{P}(D_{j-1}\cap A_{k_{r,j}}^{\mathrm{c}})-\mathbb{P}(D_{j-1})\,\mathbb{P}(A_{k_{r,j}}^{\mathrm{c}})\bigr|\leq\alpha(L+1)=:\alpha_{\star}.

Hence

q_{j}\;\leq\;(1-p_{r,j})\,q_{j-1}+\alpha_{\star}.

(12)

Iterating (
12
) and using
1-p_{r,j}\leq e^{-p_{r,j}}

yields the slightly coarser but convenient estimate

q_{n_{r}}\;\leq\;\prod_{j=1}^{n_{r}}(1-p_{r,j})\,+\,\alpha_{\star}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{r}}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n_{r}}(1-p_{r,i})\;\leq\;\exp\!\Bigl(-\sum_{j=1}^{n_{r}}p_{r,j}\Bigr)+n_{r}\alpha_{\star}.

(13)

Writing
T_{r}:=\sum_{k\in J_{r}(L)}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})
and noting that

\sum_{r=1}^{L+1}T_{r}=S_{N}
, at least one
r^{*}
satisfies

T_{r^{*}}\geq S_{N}/(L+1)
. Also
n_{r}\leq\lceil N/(L+1)\rceil
.
Substituting into (
13
),

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcap_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\Bigr)\;\leq\;\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcap_{k\in J_{r^{*}}(L)}A_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\Bigr)\;\leq\;\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{S_{N}}{L+1}\Bigr)+\Bigl\lceil\tfrac{N}{L+1}\Bigr\rceil\alpha_{\star}.

Taking complements gives (
11
).
∎

Remark 3.6

(Non-triviality of the additive bound)
.

The lower bound in (
11
) is informative only when the
additive correction

\lceil N/(L+1)\rceil\alpha(L+1)
is small compared with

1-\exp(-S_{N}/(L+1))
. If this correction is large, the right-hand side
may be non-positive; this does not affect the validity of the inequality
but indicates that the chosen spacing
L
does not give useful
finite-sample information. Equivalently, since probabilities are
nonnegative, (
11
) may be stated in the clipped form

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\Bigr)\geq\left[1-\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{S_{N}}{L+1}\Bigr)-\Bigl\lceil\frac{N}{L+1}\Bigr\rceil\alpha(L+1)\right]_{+}.

Corollary 3.7

(Sharpened
\alpha
-mixing bound under a lower mass)
.

If, in addition to the hypotheses of Theorem
3.5
,

p_{\min}:=\min_{1\leq k\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})>0
, then for every
L\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{S_{N}}{L+1}\Bigr)\,-\,\frac{\alpha(L+1)}{1-e^{-p_{\min}}}.

(14)

Proof.

Returning to (
13
) and using

\prod_{i=j+1}^{n_{r}}(1-p_{r,i})\leq e^{-(n_{r}-j)p_{\min}}
,

\alpha_{\star}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{r}}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n_{r}}(1-p_{r,i})\;\leq\;\alpha_{\star}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{r}}e^{-(n_{r}-j)p_{\min}}\;\leq\;\frac{\alpha_{\star}}{1-e^{-p_{\min}}}.

The rest of the proof of Theorem
3.5
is unchanged.
∎

Remark 3.8

(Regime of informativeness)
.

Corollary
3.7
removes the
\lceil N/(L+1)\rceil

factor from the additive correction at the price of a uniform
lower-mass assumption, yielding a bound that is non-trivial as soon as

\alpha(L+1)
is small relative to
p_{\min}
. Under a polynomial decay

\alpha(n)=O(n^{-\gamma})
with
\gamma>0
, the bound
(
14
) is of order

\exp(-S_{N}/(L+1))+O((L+1)^{-\gamma}/p_{\min})
. For example, choosing

L+1\asymp N^{1/(1+\gamma)}
gives an exponent of order

S_{N}N^{-1/(1+\gamma)}
with correction

O(N^{-\gamma/(1+\gamma)}/p_{\min})
. Other choices of
L
are possible:
slower growth of
L
gives a larger exponent but a more slowly vanishing
additive correction. This flexibility can make
(
14
) considerably sharper than
(
11
) when
p_{\min}
is bounded away from zero.

3.3
A finite-window rate corollary

The following result is the mixing analogue of

[
14
, Corollary 3]
and mirrors the rate-type statement

[
12
, Theorem 7]
while keeping explicit finite-
N

constants. The assumption below requires that the cumulative mass reach
each integer threshold; in particular, it is available whenever

\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})=\infty
by taking, for example,

\Phi(n):=\min\{M\geq 1:\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\geq n\}
.

Corollary 3.9

(Finite-window rate form)
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be non-decreasing with

\sum_{k=1}^{\Phi(n)}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\;\geq\;n\qquad\text{for every }n\geq 1.

(15)

Then, for all integers
L\geq 0
,
n\geq 1
, and
i\geq 0
, the following
hold. Set

M_{i,n}:=\Phi(i+n)-i.

(i)

If
(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is
\varphi
-mixing with coefficient

\varphi(\cdot)
, then

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=i+1}^{\Phi(i+n)}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{L+1}\bigl(n-M_{i,n}\,\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}\Bigr).

(ii)

If
(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is
\alpha
-mixing with coefficient

\alpha(\cdot)
, then

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=i+1}^{\Phi(i+n)}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{n}{L+1}\Bigr)\,-\,\Bigl\lceil\tfrac{M_{i,n}}{L+1}\Bigr\rceil\,\alpha(L+1).

Proof.

Fix
i\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
and set

I_{i,n}:=\{i+1,i+2,\dots,\Phi(i+n)\}
. Since
\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\leq 1
for every

k
, condition (
15
) implies
\Phi(i+n)\geq i+n
; hence

I_{i,n}
is non-empty. Because

\sum_{k=1}^{i}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\leq i
and

\sum_{k=1}^{\Phi(i+n)}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\geq i+n
by (
15
),

\sum_{k\in I_{i,n}}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{\Phi(i+n)}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\sum_{k=1}^{i}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\;\geq\;n.

Moreover
|I_{i,n}|=M_{i,n}
. Set

B_{\ell}:=A_{i+\ell},\quad 1\leq\ell\leq M_{i,n}.

For the relabelled window sequence
(B_{\ell})_{1\leq\ell\leq M_{i,n}}
,
the corresponding
\sigma
-algebras satisfy, for
1\leq\ell\leq M_{i,n}
,

\sigma(B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell})\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}^{i+\ell},\qquad\sigma(B_{\ell+L+1},B_{\ell+L+2},\dots,B_{M_{i,n}})\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{i+\ell+L+1}^{\infty},

with the second
\sigma
-algebra interpreted as trivial if

\ell+L+1>M_{i,n}
. Hence, by Remark
2.2
, the
finite window sequence has
\varphi
- and
\alpha
-coefficients at lag

L+1
bounded by those of the parent sequence.

For any
c\geq 0
and finite
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
,

\sum_{k\in I}(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-c)_{+}\;\geq\;\left(\sum_{k\in I}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-|I|c\right)_{+}.

Applying this with
c=\varphi(L+1)
and
I=I_{i,n}
, and then applying
Theorem
3.1
to
(B_{\ell})_{1\leq\ell\leq M_{i,n}}
, gives
the displayed
\varphi
-mixing window bound. The
\alpha
-mixing bound
follows in the same way from Theorem
3.5
.
∎

3.4
A second-order Bonferroni refinement

The residue-class bound of Theorem
3.1
uses only
first-order information on the events, namely their individual
probabilities
\mathbb{P}(A_{k})
. As in
[
14
, Theorem 4]
, a
shifted-block Bonferroni argument yields a complementary inequality
involving pairwise intersection probabilities, which is sharper when
the local overlaps are small. We record this refinement under

\varphi
-mixing.

Theorem 3.10

(Second-order
\varphi
-mixing inequality)
.

Let
N\geq 1
and let
(A_{k})_{1\leq k\leq N}
be events on

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Let
\varphi(\cdot)
denote either the
ambient event-sequence coefficient or the finite restricted coefficient
defined in Section
2.1
, and let
L\geq 2
.
Set

T_{L-1}\;:=\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i<j\leq N\\
|i-j|\leq L-1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{j}).

Then

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(S_{N}-T_{L-1}-\kappa_{N,L}\,\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}\Bigr),

(16)

where
\kappa_{N,L}:=\lceil N/L\rceil+1
.

Proof.

For each shift
r\in\{0,1,\dots,L-1\}
, decompose
\{1,\dots,N\}
into
disjoint length-
L
blocks

I_{j}^{(r)}\;:=\;\bigl\{r+(j-1)L+1,\,\dots,\,r+jL\bigr\}\cap\{1,\dots,N\},\qquad j=0,1,2,\dots,

(the first block
I_{0}^{(r)}
being possibly shorter if
r>0
). Empty
blocks are ignored below; their contribution to all sums is zero and
the corresponding empty intersection is
\Omega
. For each non-empty
block set
B_{j}^{(r)}:=\bigcup_{k\in I_{j}^{(r)}}A_{k}
. Whenever

|j-j^{\prime}|\geq 2
, the index sets
I_{j}^{(r)}
and
I_{j^{\prime}}^{(r)}
are
separated by at least
L+1
positions. More precisely, after ordering
the blocks in either parity class, for consecutive same-parity blocks,

\max I_{j}^{(r)}=r+jL,\qquad\min I_{j+2}^{(r)}=r+(j+1)L+1,

before truncation by
\{1,\dots,N\}
; truncation of non-empty endpoint
blocks cannot decrease this separation. Hence the
\sigma
-algebra
generated by all earlier blocks is contained in some

\mathcal{F}_{1}^{t}
, while the next same-parity block is contained in

\mathcal{F}_{t+L+1}^{\infty}
.
Thus the one-sided coefficient needed below is bounded by

\varphi(L+1)
.

Step 1 (parity split).
Write
\mathcal{J}^{(r)}_{\mathrm{even}}

and
\mathcal{J}^{(r)}_{\mathrm{odd}}
for the even- and odd-indexed
blocks. Applying Lemma
2.7
to the block

\sigma
-algebras within each parity (whose pairwise spacings are

\geq L+1
) gives, for each
r
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcap_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{(r)}_{\mathrm{even}}}(B_{j}^{(r)})^{\mathrm{c}}\,\Bigr)\;\leq\;\exp\!\Bigl(-\!\!\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{(r)}_{\mathrm{even}}}\!\!\bigl(\mathbb{P}(B_{j}^{(r)})-\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}\Bigr),

and analogously for the odd indices. Using

\min\{e^{-x},e^{-y}\}\leq e^{-(x+y)/2}
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcap_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\Bigr)\;\leq\;\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j\geq 0}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(B_{j}^{(r)})-\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}\Bigr)\qquad\text{for each }r.

(17)

Step 2 (Bonferroni inside blocks).
The second-order Bonferroni
inequality gives

\mathbb{P}(B_{j}^{(r)})\;\geq\;\sum_{k\in I_{j}^{(r)}}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\,-\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<k\\
i,k\in I_{j}^{(r)}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{k}).

Summing over
j\geq 0
and averaging over
r\in\{0,1,\dots,L-1\}
,

\frac{1}{L}\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}\sum_{j\geq 0}\mathbb{P}(B_{j}^{(r)})\;\geq\;S_{N}\,-\,\frac{1}{L}\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}\sum_{j\geq 0}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<k\\
i,k\in I_{j}^{(r)}\end{subarray}}\!\!\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{k}).

(18)

For a pair
1\leq i<k\leq N
, set
d:=k-i
. If
d<L
, the pair is split
by the
r
-shifted partition precisely when a right endpoint of a block,
that is, a point of the form
r+\ell L
, lies in

\{i,i+1,\ldots,k-1\}
. These are exactly the internal block boundaries
between
i
and
k
. Truncation at the endpoints of
\{1,\dots,N\}
does
not add or remove such an internal boundary, since the pair itself
already lies inside the truncated index set. Since
d<L
, these
d

integers have distinct residues modulo
L
. Hence the pair is split for
exactly
d
of the
L
shifts and lies in a common block for exactly

L-d
shifts. If
d\geq L
, every residue modulo
L
occurs among

i,\ldots,k-1
, so the pair is split for every shift and never lies in a
common length-
L
block. Therefore

\frac{1}{L}\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}\sum_{j\geq 0}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<k\\
i,k\in I_{j}^{(r)}\end{subarray}}\!\!\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{k})\;\leq\;T_{L-1}.

(19)

This intentionally uses the coarse bound
T_{L-1}
; the sharper weighted
count is recorded in Remark
3.11
below.
Combining (
18
) with (
19
),

\frac{1}{L}\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}\sum_{j\geq 0}\mathbb{P}(B_{j}^{(r)})\;\geq\;S_{N}-T_{L-1}.

(20)

Step 3 (positive-part adjustment).
Let
\mathcal{N}_{r}
denote
the number of non-empty blocks in the
r
-shift, so that

\mathcal{N}_{r}\leq\lceil N/L\rceil+1
and

\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}\mathcal{N}_{r}\leq L(\lceil N/L\rceil+1)\leq L\,\kappa_{N,L}
. For every real
a\geq 0
and
c\geq 0
,

(a-c)_{+}\geq a-c
, so

\sum_{j\geq 0}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(B_{j}^{(r)})-\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}\;\geq\;\sum_{j\geq 0}\mathbb{P}(B_{j}^{(r)})\,-\,\mathcal{N}_{r}\,\varphi(L+1).

(21)

Summing (
21
) over
r
and dividing by
L
,

\frac{1}{L}\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}\sum_{j\geq 0}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(B_{j}^{(r)})-\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}\;\geq\;(S_{N}-T_{L-1})-\kappa_{N,L}\,\varphi(L+1),

by (
20
).

Step 4 (selection).
Combining (
17
) with the
inequality
\min_{r}e^{-x_{r}}\leq\exp\!\bigl(-L^{-1}\sum_{r}x_{r}\bigr)
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcap_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\Bigr)\;\leq\;\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2L}\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}\sum_{j\geq 0}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(B_{j}^{(r)})-\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}\Bigr).

The averaged positive-part sum on the left is non-negative; hence from

X\geq Y
and
X\geq 0
we may infer
X\geq Y_{+}
. Applying this with

Y=S_{N}-T_{L-1}-\kappa_{N,L}\varphi(L+1)
and using monotonicity of the
exponential yields (
16
).
∎

Remark 3.11

(Weighted local-overlap variant)
.

The pair-counting step in the proof actually yields a weighted local
overlap. Define

T_{L-1}^{\mathrm{w}}\;:=\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i<k\leq N\\
k-i\leq L-1\end{subarray}}\frac{L-(k-i)}{L}\,\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{k}).

Then the same argument gives the sharper bound

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(S_{N}-T_{L-1}^{\mathrm{w}}-\kappa_{N,L}\,\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}\Bigr).

Since
T_{L-1}^{\mathrm{w}}\leq T_{L-1}
, this variant can be useful when
nearest-neighbour overlaps dominate the local intersection sum.

Remark 3.12

(Comparison with Theorem
3.1
)
.

In the
m
-dependent limit
\varphi(L+1)=0
with
L=m\geq 2
,
inequality (
16
) reduces to

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1-\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}(S_{N}-T_{m-1})_{+}\Bigr),

which is the positive-part form of
[
14
, Theorem 4]
. Under
\varphi
-mixing
the exact comparison with Theorem
3.1
at the same
spacing
L
is obtained by comparing

\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(S_{N}-T_{L-1}-\kappa_{N,L}\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}

with the full first-order exponent

\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(A_{k})-\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}.

Thus the second-order bound improves on Theorem
3.1

whenever the first displayed quantity is larger than the second. Under
the additional lower-mass condition

\varphi(L+1)\leq\min_{1\leq k\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})
, this full residual-retaining
comparison reduces to

\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(S_{N}-T_{L-1}-\kappa_{N,L}\varphi(L+1)\bigr)_{+}\;>\;\tfrac{1}{L+1}\bigl(S_{N}-N\varphi(L+1)\bigr).

If the mixing residuals are neglected, the sufficient comparison becomes

T_{L-1}<\tfrac{L-1}{L+1}S_{N},

matching
[
14
, §3.1]
. Thus any criterion expressed only
in terms of local overlaps, such as the heuristic

\eta<1/(L+1)
in Section
4.4
, should be read
as residual-free unless the preceding residual terms are also checked.

3.5
Optimality of the coefficients

This subsection concerns only the spacing coefficient in the
zero-residual regime. It shows that the coefficient inherited from the

m
-dependent case cannot be improved uniformly over the full mixing
classes, because those classes contain finite-range dependent examples
with vanishing coefficient at the relevant lag. The argument does not
address optimality of the residual penalties in
Theorems
3.1
and
3.5
.

Theorem
3.1
reduces at
L=m
to the bound

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{m+1}S_{N}\Bigr)

of
[
14
, Theorem 2]
. The following proposition shows that
the exponential coefficient
1/(m+1)
is best possible in this universal
zero-residual sense: no strictly larger constant admits a non-asymptotic
bound of the same form valid for every finite
m
-dependent family.

Proposition 3.13

(Sharpness of the
m
-dependent constant)
.

For every integer
m\geq 0
, define

\displaystyle c_{m}^{*}:=\sup\Bigl\{\,c>0\,:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\Bigr)\geq 1-\exp\!\bigl(-c\,S_{N}\bigr)

(22)

\displaystyle\text{for every finite $m$-dependent family}\Bigr\}.

Then
c_{m}^{*}=1/(m+1)
.

Proof.

The inequality
c_{m}^{*}\geq 1/(m+1)
follows from
Theorem
3.1
taken at
L=m
, since
\varphi(m+1)=0
for
every
m
-dependent family (Remark
2.5
).

For the matching upper bound we exhibit a family that saturates the
inequality in the limit. Fix
p\in(0,1)
and a positive integer
q
,
and let
B_{1},\dots,B_{q}
be mutually independent events with

\mathbb{P}(B_{j})=p
. Define

A_{(j-1)(m+1)+r}\;:=\;B_{j},\qquad r=1,\dots,m+1,\quad j=1,\dots,q,

(23)

and let
N:=(m+1)q
. If
|k-k^{\prime}|>m
then
k
and
k^{\prime}
lie in distinct
blocks of length
m+1
, so
A_{k}
and
A_{k^{\prime}}
depend on different
independent
B_{j}
’s. More generally, if two index sets are separated by
more than
m
, then they intersect disjoint collections of the blocks in
(
23
). The corresponding generated

\sigma
-algebras are therefore functions of disjoint subfamilies of the
independent variables
B_{1},\dots,B_{q}
, and hence are independent. Thus

(A_{k})_{1\leq k\leq N}
is
m
-dependent.
For this family,

S_{N}\;=\;(m+1)qp,\qquad\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;=\;\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{j=1}^{q}B_{j}\,\Bigr)\;=\;1-(1-p)^{q}.

If some
c>1/(m+1)
were admissible in the definition (
22
), then
applying (
22
) to this family would give

1-(1-p)^{q}\geq 1-\exp(-c(m+1)qp)
, equivalently

-\log(1-p)\;\geq\;c(m+1)\,p\qquad\text{for every }p\in(0,1).

(24)

But

\inf_{0<p<1}\frac{-\log(1-p)}{p}\;=\;\lim_{p\downarrow 0}\frac{-\log(1-p)}{p}\;=\;1,

so (
24
) forces
c\leq 1/(m+1)
, a contradiction.
Therefore
c_{m}^{*}\leq 1/(m+1)
and the claim follows.
∎

Remark 3.14

(Asymptotic attainment)
.

The family (
23
) attains asymptotic equality in
the low-probability, many-event regime: letting
p\downarrow 0
and

q\to\infty
with
qp\to\lambda\in(0,\infty)
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;=\;1-(1-p)^{q}\;\longrightarrow\;1-e^{-\lambda}.

Since
S_{N}=(m+1)qp\to(m+1)\lambda
, this limit also satisfies

1-e^{-\lambda}=\lim_{p\downarrow 0,\;qp\to\lambda}\left[1-\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{m+1}S_{N}\Bigr)\right].

Thus Theorem
3.1
at
L=m
is
asymptotically
sharp
, even though the strict inequality holds for every finite

(p,q)
.

The sharpness of Proposition
3.13
transfers to the
mixing setting at every spacing parameter
L
in the following restricted
sense: an
L
-dependent family, in the separated-subcollection convention
of Remark
2.5
, is a special case of a
\varphi
-mixing
(respectively,
\alpha
-mixing) family with
\varphi(L+1)=0
(resp.

\alpha(L+1)=0
). Thus the transfer proves optimality of the universal
spacing coefficient only on the zero-residual subfamily; it does not
prove optimality of the residual penalties.

Corollary 3.15

(Sharpness of the spacing constant under mixing)
.

For every integer
L\geq 0
:

(i)

the constant
1/(L+1)
in inequality (
5
) of
Theorem
3.1
cannot be replaced by any strictly larger
universal spacing constant in a bound of the same zero-residual
exponential form, even when restricted to the subclass of

\varphi
-mixing sequences with
\varphi(L+1)=0
;

(ii)

the constant
1/(L+1)
in the exponent of (
11
)
of Theorem
3.5
cannot be replaced by any strictly
larger universal spacing constant in the corresponding zero-residual
form, even when restricted to the subclass of
\alpha
-mixing sequences
with
\alpha(L+1)=0
.

Proof.

Specialising both Theorems
3.1
and

3.5
to sequences for which the relevant mixing
coefficient vanishes at lag
L+1
recovers the
L
-dependent bound

\mathbb{P}(\bigcup A_{k})\geq 1-\exp(-S_{N}/(L+1))
. Under the finite-restricted
coefficient convention, the finite block examples of
Proposition
3.13
apply directly; equivalently, they
may be embedded in an infinite
L
-dependent block sequence, so the same
obstruction applies to ambient coefficients. Applying
Proposition
3.13
with
m=L
yields the claim.
∎

Remark 3.16

(Interpretation of the trade-off)
.

Corollary
3.15
is a universal sharpness statement
over the full mixing classes because those classes contain

L
-dependent examples with
\varphi(L+1)=0
or
\alpha(L+1)=0
. It
therefore identifies the best possible coefficient for the zero-residual
spacing term only. It does not preclude improved residual penalties, or
improved constants under additional assumptions excluding such
vanishing-lag examples. For genuinely mixing sequences with strictly
positive residuals at every lag, Theorem
3.1
presents a
trade-off rather than a proved subclass optimality statement: decreasing

L
improves the prefactor
1/(L+1)
but increases the residual

\varphi(L+1)
. This trade-off is captured by the quantity

\Psi(N,\varphi)
of Corollary
3.3
.

4
Examples and comparisons

This section illustrates the content of
Theorems
3.1
,
3.5
, and

3.10
, together with
Corollary
3.9
, on three concrete classes of mixing
sequences. The purposes are (i) to verify
that the
m
-dependent baseline (
1
) of

[
14
]
is recovered in the limit
\varphi(n)=0
for

n>m
, (ii) to exhibit the explicit form of the constants obtained
under geometric and polynomial decay of the mixing coefficients, and
(iii) to compare the resulting exponents with those available under
classical assumptions. Throughout this section we write
S_{N}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})
and, where relevant, assume a uniform lower mass

p:=\inf_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})>0
.

4.1
Recovery of the
m
-dependent case

Suppose
(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is
m
-dependent, so that

\varphi(n)=0
for every
n>m
. Setting
L=m
in
Theorem
3.1
gives
\varphi(L+1)=0
and

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{S_{N}}{m+1}\Bigr),

which coincides with
[
14
, Theorem 2]
. Similarly, when

m\geq 2
the second-order refinement (
16
)
collapses at
L=m
onto the exponent

\tfrac{1}{2}(S_{N}-T_{m-1})_{+}
, the positive-part form of

[
14
, Theorem 4]
. In particular, the windowed
Corollary
3.9
reduces to

[
14
, Corollary 3]
.

The matching of constants in both the first- and second-order bounds
provides a consistency check on the approximate-independence machinery
of Lemma
2.7
: in the limit

\varphi_{\star}\downarrow 0
no information is lost.

4.2
Geometrically
\varphi
-mixing sequences

Let
(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be
\varphi
-mixing with a geometric decay

\varphi(n)\;\leq\;C\rho^{n},\qquad n\geq 1,

(25)

for constants
C\geq 1
and
\rho\in(0,1)
. This class includes, for
example, stationary Markov chains satisfying a Doeblin minorisation
condition
[
7
, Ch. 3]
. Standard finite-state
Markov-chain mixing bounds also include stationary irreducible
aperiodic chains; see, for example,
[
2
, §3]
.

Proposition 4.1

(Geometric
\varphi
-mixing with positive lower mass)
.

Suppose
(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}
satisfies (
25
) and

p:=\inf_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})>0
. Choose an integer
L_{0}\geq 0
such that

C\rho^{L_{0}+1}\leq p/2
. Then, for every
N\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{S_{N}}{2(L_{0}+1)}\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{pN}{2(L_{0}+1)}\Bigr).

(26)

Proof.

By the choice of
L_{0}
,
\varphi(L_{0}+1)\leq p/2\leq\min_{1\leq k\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})
.
Applying (
6
) with
L=L_{0}
gives

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1-\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{S_{N}-N\varphi(L_{0}+1)}{L_{0}+1}\Bigr).

Since
N\varphi(L_{0}+1)\leq Np/2\leq S_{N}/2
, the first bound in
(
26
) follows; the second follows from
S_{N}\geq pN
.
∎

Remark 4.2

(Choice of the spacing parameter)
.

Under a positive lower mass, a fixed spacing parameter already gives an exponent
linear in
S_{N}
and hence in
N
. An
N
-dependent choice

L=L_{N}\asymp\log N
is still useful in settings without a usable lower
mass, where one wants the cruder residual
N\varphi(L+1)
to vanish;
such a choice is a feature of that proof strategy, not an intrinsic
loss for all geometrically mixing sequences.

Example 4.3

(Two-state Markov chain)
.

Let
(X_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be the stationary irreducible aperiodic Markov
chain on

\{0,1\}
with transition matrix

P=\begin{pmatrix}1-a&a\\
b&1-b\end{pmatrix}
where

a,b\in(0,1)
, and let
A_{k}=\{X_{k}=1\}
. Then

\mathbb{P}(A_{k})=a/(a+b)
for all
k
. With
\lambda=1-a-b
and stationary
law
\pi
, the elementary two-state transition calculation gives

\sup_{x\in\{0,1\}}\|P^{n}(x,\cdot)-\pi\|_{\rm TV}=\max\{\pi_{0},\pi_{1}\}|\lambda|^{n}\leq|\lambda|^{n},

where total variation is normalized as

\|\mu-\nu\|_{\rm TV}:=\sup_{B}|\mu(B)-\nu(B)|
.
Indeed, for any event
H\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{k}
with
\mathbb{P}(H)>0
, let

\mu_{H}
be the conditional law of
X_{k}
given
H
. If
K
denotes the
Markov kernel from the state at time
k+n
to the future path

(X_{k+n},X_{k+n+1},\ldots)
, then for every

B\in\mathcal{F}_{k+n}^{\infty}
,

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathbb{P}(B\mid H)-\mathbb{P}(B)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu_{H}P^{n}K-\pi K\|_{\rm TV}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu_{H}P^{n}-\pi\|_{\rm TV}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x}\|P^{n}(x,\cdot)-\pi\|_{\rm TV}.

Thus the full-chain coefficient satisfies

\varphi_{X}(n)\leq\sup_{x}\|P^{n}(x,\cdot)-\pi\|_{\rm TV}\leq|1-a-b|^{n};

see also the finite-state Markov-chain discussion in

[
2
, §3]
. By
Remark
2.3
, if
\varphi_{A}
denotes the
coefficient of the event sequence
A_{k}=\{X_{k}=1\}
, then

\varphi_{A}(n)\leq\varphi_{X}(n)\leq|1-a-b|^{n},\qquad n\geq 1.

If
a+b=1
, then
\lambda=0
and the chain is independent at positive
lags, so the same conclusion follows with any fixed
\rho\in(0,1)
.
If
a+b\neq 1
, take
\rho=|1-a-b|
and
C=1
in
Proposition
4.1
. For any fixed
L_{0}
satisfying

|1-a-b|^{L_{0}+1}\leq a/(2(a+b))
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}\{X_{k}=1\}\,\Bigr)\;\geq\;1\,-\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{aN}{2(a+b)(L_{0}+1)}\Bigr).

The exact stationary union probability is also explicit:

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}\{X_{k}=1\}\,\Bigr)=1-\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=\cdots=X_{N}=0)=1-\frac{b}{a+b}(1-a)^{N-1}.

For comparison, applying Theorem
3.1
with

\varphi_{A}(n)\leq\rho^{n}
,
\rho=|1-a-b|
, and optimizing over
L
gives

B_{\rm opt}:=\max_{L\geq 0}\left\{1-\exp\!\left(-\frac{N(p-\rho^{L+1})_{+}}{L+1}\right)\right\},\qquad p=\frac{a}{a+b}.

4.3
Polynomially
\alpha
-mixing sequences

Let
(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be
\alpha
-mixing with polynomial decay

\alpha(n)\;\leq\;Cn^{-\gamma},\qquad n\geq 1,

(27)

for constants
C\geq 1
and
\gamma>0
. Polynomial strong-mixing rates
occur in several classes of weakly dependent time-series models; the
following proposition records the finite-sample consequence of such a
rate assumption, independently of the particular source of the decay.

Proposition 4.4

(Polynomial
\alpha
-mixing spacings)
.

Suppose the ambient or finite restricted
\alpha
-coefficients associated
with
(A_{k})_{1\leq k\leq N}
satisfy

\alpha(n)\leq Cn^{-\gamma}
for every lag
n
used below. Then, for
every
\theta\in(0,1]
and every
N\geq 2
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\Bigr)\geq 1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}S_{N}N^{-\theta}\right)-2C\,N^{1-\theta(\gamma+1)}.

(28)

Consequently, the additive correction vanishes whenever

\theta>1/(\gamma+1)
. In particular, the convenient choice

\theta=2/(\gamma+2)
gives

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\Bigr)\geq 1-\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}S_{N}N^{-2/(\gamma+2)}\right)-2C\,N^{-\gamma/(\gamma+2)}.

(29)

As in Theorem
3.5
, the bound is informative only when
the additive correction is smaller than the leading gain.

Proof.

Set
L:=\lceil N^{\theta}\rceil-1
, so that
L\geq 0
and

L+1=\lceil N^{\theta}\rceil
. Since
N\geq 2
and
0<\theta\leq 1
,

N^{\theta}\leq L+1\leq 2N^{\theta},\qquad L+1\leq N,\qquad\left\lceil\frac{N}{L+1}\right\rceil\leq\frac{2N}{L+1}.

Hence

\frac{S_{N}}{L+1}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}S_{N}N^{-\theta},\qquad\left\lceil\frac{N}{L+1}\right\rceil\alpha(L+1)\leq\frac{2N}{L+1}C(L+1)^{-\gamma}\leq 2CN^{1-\theta(\gamma+1)}.

Theorem
3.5
gives (
28
).
The displayed special case follows by substituting

\theta=2/(\gamma+2)
.
∎

Remark 4.5

(Regimes of
\gamma
)
.

Inequality (
28
) is informative when the chosen

\theta
makes the additive correction small and the exponential term
non-negligible. In particular, the correction tends to zero whenever

\theta>1/(\gamma+1)
, while the exponential term decays whenever

S_{N}\to\infty
faster than
N^{\theta}
. Under a uniform lower mass
p>0
,
we have
S_{N}\geq pN
, so every fixed
\theta<1
gives a diverging exponent.
Under the lower-mass hypothesis of
Corollary
3.7
, however, the balancing problem is
different: slower growth of
L
may give a nearly linear exponent with
a more slowly vanishing additive correction. Thus the special rate

\gamma/(\gamma+2)
in (
29
) describes one
convenient no-lower-mass estimate, rather than an intrinsic limitation
of the lower-mass regime.

4.4
Second-order gains in the mixing regime

Theorem
3.10
improves on
Theorem
3.1
whenever the local pairwise overlaps are
sufficiently small after the residual terms are taken into account; see
Remark
3.12
for the full comparison. To
illustrate the residual-free heuristic, suppose the events

(A_{k})_{k\geq 1}
satisfy, in addition to (
25
), a
uniform local-overlap bound

\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{j})\;\leq\;\eta\mathbb{P}(A_{i})\qquad\text{for all }1\leq i<j\leq N,\ |i-j|\leq L-1,

(30)

for some
\eta\in[0,1]
. Summing over pairs with
|i-j|\leq L-1
gives

T_{L-1}\leq(L-1)\eta S_{N}
. If the mixing residuals are neglected, the
second-order exponent exceeds the first-order exponent whenever

\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1-(L-1)\eta\bigr)S_{N}>\frac{1}{L+1}S_{N},

equivalently when
\eta<1/(L+1)
. In this residual-free regime, the exponent of
Theorem
3.10
exceeds that of
Theorem
3.1
; with residuals retained, the comparison is
the one stated in Remark
3.12
.

For example, in the two-state Markov chain of
Example
4.3
, the local-overlap terms can be computed
from the powers of the transition matrix:

\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{i+d})=\frac{a}{a+b}\left(\frac{a}{a+b}+\frac{b}{a+b}(1-a-b)^{d}\right),\qquad d\geq 1,

at stationarity. Substituting these values into
(
16
) gives an explicit comparison with
Proposition
4.1
; the second-order bound is sharper
precisely in the parameter regimes where the resulting
T_{L-1}
is
small enough to satisfy the criterion above.

4.5
Comparison with classical bounds

We conclude this section by placing
Theorems
3.1
and
3.5
in the context
of existing finite-sample and asymptotic Borel–Cantelli refinements.

Chung–Erdős/Paley–Zygmund.

For finite
N
, the most direct comparator is the second-moment union
bound. With
Z_{N}:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}\Bigr)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{N}>0)\geq\frac{(\mathbb{E}Z_{N})^{2}}{\mathbb{E}Z_{N}^{2}}=\frac{S_{N}^{2}}{\sum_{i,j\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{j})}.

This estimate is often called the Chung–Erdős finite union bound
and follows equivalently from Paley–Zygmund or Cauchy–Schwarz. It is
sharper than the first-order mixing bounds when the full matrix of
pairwise intersections is available and small enough. The residue-class
bounds in Theorems
3.1
and

3.5
are complementary: they require only the
marginals and a mixing coefficient at a chosen spacing. The second-order
bound, Theorem
3.10
, lies between these two
forms of information, since it uses only local pairwise intersections
together with a mixing residual.

Erdős–Rényi, Chandra, and Petrov.
The Erdős–Rényi
criterion
[
9
]
is recalled, for example, in

[
1
, Theorem 1.4]
; Petrov obtains a generalization of
that formulation
[
15
, p. 283]
; and Chandra gives a
book-length treatment of Borel–Cantelli extensions
[
4
]
.
These sources are examples of related Borel–Cantelli criteria and
refinements, especially in asymptotic settings. They are not, in
themselves, finite-sample lower bounds with explicit dependence on

N
,
S_{N}
, and a mixing coefficient evaluated at a chosen spacing. The
inequalities of the present paper are therefore
complementary: they trade stronger structured-dependence assumptions for
explicit non-asymptotic rates.

Kochen–Stone and Arthan–Oliva
[
11
,
1
]
.
The
Kochen–Stone inequality

\mathbb{P}(A_{k}\ \mathrm{i.o.})\;\geq\;\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\bigl(\sum_{k\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\bigr)^{2}}{\sum_{i,j\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{j})}

yields a lower bound on the i.o. probability in terms of second moments
of the indicator sums. This is asymptotic in form, whereas the
Chung–Erdős bound above is the finite-
N
analogue for the union
probability. For
\varphi
-mixing sequences satisfying
(
25
) and a uniform lower mass, the Kochen–Stone lower
bound gives an eventual lower bound for the limsup event; Proposition

4.1
gives the corresponding explicit finite-
N

estimate.

m
-dependent results
[
12
,
14
]
.
The
finite-sample bounds of
[
14
]
are recovered exactly under
the substitution
\varphi(n)=0
for
n>m
. The asymptotic

m
-dependent Borel–Cantelli conclusions of
[
12
]
are
consistent with, and can be approached through, the finite-window form
of Corollary
3.9
, but are not identical
finite-sample statements. For genuinely geometrically mixing sequences,
Proposition
4.1
shows that a positive lower mass permits
a fixed spacing parameter and a linear exponent; without such a
lower-mass input, the residual trade-off in Remark
4.2

remains relevant.

5
Discussion

This paper has extended the finite-sample Borel–Cantelli inequality of

[
14
]
from
m
-dependence to quantitative mixing. The
main results (Theorems
3.1
,
3.5
,

3.10
and Corollary

3.9
) provide non-asymptotic lower bounds for

\mathbb{P}(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k})
in terms of the first moments
\mathbb{P}(A_{k})
,
a free spacing parameter
L
, and the mixing coefficient evaluated at
lag
L+1
. Under
m
-dependence they reduce exactly to the
residue-class bounds of

[
14
, Theorems 2 and 4, Corollary 3]
. Moreover,
Proposition
3.13
shows that the coefficient

1/(m+1)
inherited from that setting is optimal in the universal

m
-dependent sense, and Corollary
3.15
transfers
this optimality to the spacing coefficient over the full mixing classes.
The windowed corollary gives a finite-window analogue related to the
rate-type statement of
[
12
, Theorem 7]
, while retaining
explicit finite-
N
constants throughout.

5.1
Relation to existing literature

Many classical relaxations of independence in Borel–Cantelli-type
results use pairwise or global control of intersections. In finite
samples this viewpoint appears already in the Chung–Erdős bound

[
5
]
: for

Z_{N}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}
,

\mathbb{P}(Z_{N}>0)\geq\frac{(\mathbb{E}Z_{N})^{2}}{\mathbb{E}Z_{N}^{2}}=\frac{\bigl(\sum_{k\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\bigr)^{2}}{\sum_{i,j\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{j})}.

Asymptotic descendants and refinements include the criteria of
Erdős–Rényi
[
9
]
, recalled for example in

[
1
, Theorem 1.4]
, the partial-maximum crossing
criteria of Ortega–Wschebor
[
13
, pp. 85–98]
,
Chandra’s monograph treatment
[
4
]
, Petrov’s
generalization of the Erdős–Rényi formulation

[
15
, p. 283]
, and the Kochen–Stone inequality

[
11
]
; see also the quantitative treatment of
Arthan–Oliva
[
1
, Theorem 1.3]
.

The present paper is complementary to this second-moment line. The
Chung–Erdős bound is often preferable when the denominator

\sum_{i,j\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{j})
is explicitly known or can be sharply
estimated. The residue-class inequalities here instead use the
one-dimensional marginals, a spacing parameter, and a mixing coefficient
at the corresponding lag. The second-order theorem bridges the two
viewpoints by adding local intersection information to the same
blocking scheme. Thus the novelty is not that finite-sample lower
bounds are unavailable by other methods, but that the present bounds
give explicit finite-
N
estimates under quantitative mixing assumptions
without requiring the full pairwise-intersection matrix.

Another relevant family consists of
structured-dependence
conditions,
the most classical of which are
m
-dependence

[
12
,
14
]
and Freedman’s conditional framework

[
10
]
. The present paper situates itself in this family
and replaces the hard cut-off of
m
-dependence by quantitative decay of
mixing coefficients, while preserving the elementary residue-class
proof strategy of
[
14
]
.

The residue-class argument underlying Theorem
3.1

preserves the elementary character of
[
14
]
: the proof
uses only De Morgan’s law,
\varphi
-mixing approximate independence,
the product-to-exponential bound, and an averaging argument. In
particular it does
not
invoke any coupling, approximation, or
truncation argument. This differs from much of the standard toolkit for
CLTs and deviation inequalities under mixing
(see
[
2
,
16
]
), where such methods are often central.
The price of this elementary proof is the requirement that

\varphi(L+1)
be small enough to make the penalty term controlled by
the mixing residual negligible; the trade-off is captured by the
quantity
\Psi(N,\varphi)
of Corollary
3.3
.

5.2
On the
\alpha
-mixing inequality

Theorem
3.5
differs structurally from
Theorem
3.1
: the mixing correction appears as an

additive
term
\lceil N/(L+1)\rceil\,\alpha(L+1)
outside the
exponential rather than as an offset inside the exponent. This additive
form is a feature of the covariance-telescoping proof based on
Lemma
2.9
: unlike the
\varphi
-coefficient, the

\alpha
-coefficient does not directly control conditional
probabilities, so the present iteration accumulates
\alpha(L+1)

additively.

Under polynomial decay
\alpha(n)\asymp n^{-\gamma}
with
\gamma>0
,
Proposition
4.4
gives a family of choices

L+1\asymp N^{\theta}
, leading to an exponential term of order

\exp(-cS_{N}N^{-\theta})
and an additive correction of order

N^{1-\theta(\gamma+1)}
. Thus the correction vanishes whenever

\theta>1/(\gamma+1)
, while the best choice of
\theta
depends on the
growth of
S_{N}
and on the desired additive-error tolerance. The special
choice
\theta=2/(\gamma+2)
used in (
29
) is
therefore a
convenient no-lower-mass corollary, not an intrinsic polynomial

\alpha
-mixing rate.

Under the lower-mass hypothesis of
Corollary
3.7
, the balancing problem is different:
slower growth of
L
can give a nearly linear exponent with a more
slowly vanishing additive correction. Whether a comparable improvement
is possible without such a lower-mass assumption is an open problem.

5.3
Sharpness and what remains unknown

Proposition
3.13
together with
Corollary
3.15
identifies the optimal universal
spacing constant in the
m
-dependent setting and in the full mixing
classes containing
L
-dependent examples. This sharpness result concerns
only the coefficient attached to the spacing parameter in the
zero-residual regime. It does not rule out improved residual penalties
or improved constants under additional assumptions that exclude examples
with vanishing mixing coefficients at lag
L+1
.
Two related sharpness questions remain:

(i)

Sharpness of the
\varphi
-mixing penalty.
In the zero-residual
subfamily
\varphi(L+1)=0
, the coefficient
1/(L+1)
in front of
S_{N}

is optimal by Corollary
3.15
; this does not
determine the best possible proportionality constant in the residual
term
N\varphi(L+1)
. Whether that residual constant can be replaced by
a smaller universal value remains open.

(ii)

Sharpness under
\alpha
-mixing.
The additive correction

\lceil N/(L+1)\rceil\,\alpha(L+1)
of Theorem
3.5

is derived from the universal constant
1
in the first part of
Lemma
2.9
. It is plausible that a sharper argument
using the quantitative coupling inequalities of
[
16
]
could
replace the linear factor
\lceil N/(L+1)\rceil
by something
smaller; we have not pursued this here in order to keep the proofs
elementary.

A satisfactory resolution of either question would likely require
tools genuinely beyond the residue-class and shifted-block arguments
of this paper.

5.4
Open problems and directions

We conclude by listing four directions that naturally extend the
results of this paper.

(1) Other mixing coefficients.

One possible direction is to develop analogues for other classical
mixing coefficients. For
\rho
-mixing sequences, covariance control
through maximal correlation suggests an
\alpha
-type inequality with
an additive correction controlled by
\rho(L+1)
, up to constants
depending on the chosen normalization; see, for example,

[
2
, §1.1]
. For
\psi
-mixing or
\beta
-mixing, one
might hope to replace the additive correction by a stronger
multiplicative or coupling-based term, again only up to constants and
normalization choices. We do not formulate these as theorems here,
because the correct constants and the most useful residual form depend
substantially on the coefficient used.

(2) Non-stationary mixing.
The coefficients

\varphi(n)
and
\alpha(n)
used throughout this paper are defined as
suprema over all starting indices. For non-stationary sequences one
may instead work with position-dependent coefficients

\varphi(k,k+n)=\varphi(\mathcal{F}_{1}^{k},\mathcal{F}_{k+n}^{\infty})
.
The residue-class proof of Theorem
3.1
adapts to
this setting at the cost of replacing the uniform bound

\varphi(L+1)
by a maximum over the relevant indices; the resulting
inequality is a finite-
N
bound tailored to the local dependence
structure. Whether such refinement yields qualitatively new examples
is worth investigating.

(3) Conditional analogues.
Freedman’s conditional
Borel–Cantelli inequality
[
10
]
provides a martingale
framework in which the sum
S_{N}
is replaced by the conditional
predictable sum
M_{N}=\sum_{k\leq N}\mathbb{P}(A_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1})
.
A mixing-aware conditional version of
Theorem
3.1
, in which the conditioning

\sigma
-algebra at time
k-1
is separated from the future

\mathcal{F}_{k+L+1}^{\infty}
with the same lag convention as in
Theorem
3.1
, would unify the present paper with the
Freedman framework; we conjecture such a version holds with the same
coefficient
1/(L+1)
.

(4) Functional extensions.
The events
A_{k}
in the present
paper are arbitrary, but many applications replace them by events

\{X_{k}\geq t\}
for a stationary sequence of random variables.
Theorem
3.1
then produces a tail lower bound for

\max_{k\leq N}X_{k}
of exponential type, with a coefficient
depending on the mixing range of the underlying
X
-sequence. The
precise form of the resulting tail inequality, and its relationship
to existing extreme-value results for mixing sequences

[
16
,
3
]
, warrants independent development.

5.5
Summary

The central message of this paper is methodological: the elementary
residue-class and shifted-block arguments of
[
14
]

transfer to the mixing setting with only minor modifications, and the
price of removing the hard
m
-dependent cut-off is a penalty term

N\varphi(L+1)
or
\lceil N/(L+1)\rceil\alpha(L+1)
. Within the class
of sequences whose mixing coefficient vanishes at lag
L+1
, the
resulting spacing coefficient
1/(L+1)
is sharp in the zero-residual
sense. Together, Theorems
3.1
,

3.5
, and
3.10
provide a
non-asymptotic toolkit that complements both the asymptotic

m
-dependent Borel–Cantelli results of
[
12
]
and the
finite-sample
m
-dependent bounds of
[
14
]
.
