Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

CubicPV seeks to improve perovskite solar cell durability and performance through modification
of the NiO surface.

Summary of Research Results:

We have studied different forms/processing routes of NiOx (produced via sputtering, sol-gel,
nanoparticle slurries) and performed cyclic voltammetry (CV) for the films with and without
additional layers processed on the top for passivation1. We found that colloidally grown NiOx
nanoparticles, compared with other forms of NiOx, have the lowest redox activity, which means
they are less reactive towards perovskite layers deposited on top for photovoltaic devices.
Passivation layers, including molecules bound to form self-assembled monolayers2 (SAMs), and
oxides such as aluminum oxide3 on different types of NiOx, have also been studied via CV for
indication of redox activity for NiOx after passivation. The nanoparticle NiOx passivated using
the phosphonic acid carbazole Me-4PACz (SAM) shows the lowest redox activities and also
leads to better photovoltaic performance, including a device efficiency of 21.5%.

Task 2: Fabrication of perovskite solar cells with varying NiO/Perovskite interfacial layers

This task will test NiO/Perovskite interfacial layers using NREL-fabricated devices. The decision
for interfacial layer application methodology and interfacial material identity will be decided
jointly through biweekly meetings but guided by data collected during this project and by the
participant outside of this agreement. The envisioned generalized device architecture will consist
of Indium Tin Oxide (ITO) or Fluorine-doped Tin Oxide (FTO) coated glass/NiO/passivating
interfacial layer/CsFA perovskite (~1.55eV BG) / C60 / SnO2 / Cu. The NiO substrates used will
be sputtered by the contractor. The participant will define the exact composition of the
perovskite and perovskite deposition procedure with input from the contractor with the
generalized goal of making perovskite active layer chemistry like the participants standard
material. Device fabrication will be done by the contractor along with the initial current-voltage
(IV) testing before devices are sent to the participant for accelerated degradation testing.

 2

The initial portion of the project will screen two surface passivation approaches before
optimizing the most promising approach. The two target passivation approaches will be ultra-
thin atomic layer deposition (ALD) metal oxide interlayers and octadecyl phosphonic acid
(OPAs). The more promising approach will then be optimized. In the case the OPAs are selected,
potential targeted variables for optimization will be OPA application method, post-application
removal of excess material, NiO substrate deposition method, NiO treatment method, and
material identity of the OPA layer. In the case of ultra-thin metal oxides, potential targeted
variables will include metal oxide identity and metal oxide thickness.

1. SAMs (Carbazole based Phosphonic acid, Alkyl based phosphonic acid) passivation
for NiOx devices with structure of Glass/ITO/NiO x/SAMs/CsFA Perovskite/ PC60BM
/SnO2/Ag:

We first investigated the photovoltaic performance of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with a p-i-n
structure of Glass/ITO/NiOx/Alkyl-SAMs/Perovskite/PEACl/PC60BM/SnOx/Ag. As shown in
Figure 1, the efficiency increased with the length of the alkyl-SAMs until butyl-phosphonic acid
(BuPA). All alkyl SAMs successfully passivate the NiOx surface, leading to improvement in
device efficiency compared to bare NiOx without passivation. Figure 1 showed a similar trend for
Voc, except for HxPA, which has an obvious increase compared to the rest of the alkyl SAMs’
devices. However, the fill factor (FF) dropped for HxPA at the same time. We speculate that
longer-chain phosphonic acid could lead to higher resistance within the device, which will not
alter the passivation effects on NiOx but impede transport through the layer. The performance
results can be implied through electrochemistry in Figure 1 by an N value, which represents the
number of active sites from the redox activity of different alkyl-SAMs on NiOx. Without alkyl-
SAMs on NiOx, it showed an N number of 6.64 × 1014 cm-2 leading to corresponding PCE of
solar cells in a wide range from 9-12% and Voc from 0.9-0.95 V. When putting alkyl-SAMs on
NiOx, N decreased to 5.11 × 1014 cm-2 for BuPA, which indicates the redox activity was
suppressed. This resulted in power conversion efficiency (PCE) increase for BuPA-passivated
devices with a narrow range from 14.5 to 16.5%.

We further evaluate photovoltaic performance of PSCs with p-i-n structure of
Glass/ITO/NiOx/Carbazole-SAMs/Perovskite/PEACl/PC60BM/SnOx/Ag. SAMs with longer
alkyl chains achieve better performance than their corresponding ones (Me-4PACz v.s. Me-
2PACz, 4PACz v.s. 2PACz, for instance) (Fig.3, Fig.4). This aligns with alkyl-phosphonic acid
passivation only for NiOx. To better understand the underlying mechanism, we have performed
cyclic voltammetry (CV) for different SAMs on ITO/NiOx, which further serve as indicators for
their passivation effects on NiOx. It can be seen from Fig.1 and Fig.2 that Me-4PACz with the
lowest N=4.34×1014 cm-2 possesses the highest performance on average for perovskite solar
cells.

2. Aluminum Oxide Passivation on NiOx devices with structure of
Glass/ITO/NiOx/AlOx/CsFAPerovskite/ PC60BM /SnO2/Ag:

We have performed work to study the passivation via ALD films of aluminum oxide (AlOx) to
passivate NiOx, and it can be seen from figure 6 (a) and figure 6 (b) that the PCE improved after
AlOx passivation. The efficiency is thickness dependent. When the cycle number of AlOx was
varied from 0.5 cycles to 4 cycles, the efficiency dropped from 18% to 4%, as shown in figures
(b), (c), (d), and (e). The devices show ineligible hysteresis for the AlOx-passivated NiOx as well.

We speculate that it could be the work function of the AlOx does not align with NiOx and
perovskite, so we further passivate the AlOx layer with Me-4PACz with a device structure of
ITO/NiOx/AlOx/Me-4PACz/PEACl/PVK/PEACl/PCBM/SnO2(ALD)/Ag. The PCE results are
similar regardless of the cycle number of the AlOx layer, with the highest efficiency of 19.7%.
This indicates that SAMs help with the hole transport between AlO x and perovskite.

Task 3: Light-heat durability testing of perovskite solar cells with interfacial layers

The participant will perform light-heat durability testing of devices fabricated by the contractor
in task 2 using degradation test equipment owned by the Participant. The participant will
fabricate the required equipment for the contractor’s devices in the degradation test equipment
and also provide encapsulation for the contractor’ devices. The primary test methodology will
focus on 1-sun illumination and 75oC heating with periodic IV sweeps, but other temperatures,
light intensities, and spectrums may be explored to understand the degradation mechanisms.

The contractor and participant will jointly analyze the durability data to draw conclusions about
the effectiveness and degradation mechanisms associated with the interfacial layer under test.
Data will then be used to inform the next device sets in task 2.

We have selected different types of NiOx and performed cyclic voltammetry (CV) for the films

Task 5: Other Work - Post-mortem characterization of devices with varying interfacial
layers

If required to elucidate degradation mechanisms, the contractor will provide additional analysis
after degradation testing. The contractor will provide available characterization techniques such
as, but not limited to, x-ray crystallography (XRD), x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS),
time of flight – secondary ion mass spectrometry (Tof-SIIMs), scanning electron microscopy
(SEM), ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorbance, and scanning Kelvin probe microscopy (SKPM)
at the request of the Participant and the availability of funding.

The SEM for NiOx nanoparticles deposited on ITO is shown in figure 10(b) compared to bare
ITO in figure 10 (a). There are pinholes that could be identified as the dark spot in figure 10 (b),
which means the performance of devices fabricated without passivation on NiOx could be
affected by partially insufficient coverage of nanoparticles on top of ITO as well. This could
serve as another reason for the poor PCE for NiOx/Perovskite devices without any passivation.
Cyclic voltammetry comparisons between bare NiOx and passivated layers are shown in figure
10(c) and 10 (d). Both AlOx and Me-4PACz inhibit the redox pair of NiOx within the 0.15 – 0.4
V region. This reduces the chances of reaction between NiOx and the perovskite layer, leading to
better device performance, as shown in figure 5(b) and figure 6(b).
