Antibiotic-induced Malassezia expansion in the infant gut promotes early-life immune dysregulation and airway inflammation in mice
Authors: Erik van Tilburg Bernardes, Thaís Glatthardt, Mackenzie W. Gutierrez, William N. T. Nguyen, Emily M. Mercer, Hena R. Ramay, Carolyn A. Thomson, Tisha Belle A. Halim, Nithya Gopalakrishnan, Katarina MacConnell, Kristen Kalbfleisch, Kamala D. Patel, Eugenia Corrales-Aguilar, Kathy D. McCoy, Stephen B. Freedman, Marie-Claire Arrieta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antibiotics have deleterious consequences for the gut microbiome and can increase the risk of childhood asthma. While the effects of antibiotics on the bacterial microbiome and asthma risk are well characterized, their impact on the fungal microbiome (mycobiome) remains vastly unexplored. We investigated the effect of antibiotic use on the gut mycobiome in an observational, prospective clinical study of young infants. Antibiotic treatment resulted in increased fungal abundance and expansion of the yeast Malassezia spp. in the infant mycobiome. Based on these findings, we colonized germ-free mouse pups with a defined consortium of mouse-derived bacteria (Oligo-MM12) with or without Malassezia restricta. Colonization with this yeast increased myeloid and lymphoid intestinal immune responses deemed critical in atopy development, and elevated airway inflammation in house-dust mite (HDM)-challenged mice and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)-infected mice. Further evaluation in eosinophil-deficient mice revealed that the observed immune response is partially dependent on this cell type. This translational work demonstrates that expansion of Malassezia spp. is a previously overlooked collateral effect of infant antibiotic use, which may offer a potential strategy to prevent or mitigate pediatric asthma and related conditions.

Introduction

Perturbances to the microbiome early in life, known as dysbiosis, precede and are causally associated with immune dysregulation that can result in immune-mediated diseases, such as asthma1–5. Fungi constitute an integral part of the microbiome, and only recently have studies begun to investigate their role in influencing health outcomes in the host6,7. Population-based studies have identified altered mycobiome signatures associated with asthma risk8–12, with some of these signatures causally driving airway inflammation exacerbation in mouse models13,14. For instance, neonatal mice exposed to Pichia kudriavzevii, a fungus increased in Ecuadorian infants at risk of asthma development9, displayed increased airway inflammation following house-dust mite (HDM) challenge, with higher T helper type 2 (Th2) and Th17 cells infiltration in the lungs14.

Fungal symbionts are critical for the induction of early tolerogenic immune responses in the host. This is not surprising considering that fungi are bona fide members of the microbiome, and evolutionary forces likely shaped the mammalian immune system to tolerate commensal fungal presence15. For instance, commensal fungi induced migration of regulatory retinol dehydrogenase positive (RALDH+) dendritic cells (DC) to mesenteric and peripheral lymph nodes in germ-free mice16, supporting gut-associated lymphoid tissue and mucosal tolerance development early in life. Fungal colonizers also promote the development of homeostatic effector T subsets in the intestines and systemically, including Th17 cells, which promote barrier function and tune host immune responses17–19. Yet, it remains unclear how perturbances to the gut mycobiome early in life can impact host immune development. Recent work by Hoskinson et al.20 addressed this gap by characterizing fungal maturation trajectories in the infant gut and showing that delayed mycobiome maturation—marked by persistent Malassezia colonization—was associated with increased risk of atopic dermatitis. These findings suggest that early-life fungal ecology plays a critical role in immune imprinting.

Antibiotic administration during early life is one of the most important dysbiosis-inducing factors, as it can profoundly disrupt the commensal microbiome. Antibiotics induce strong compositional and functional changes in both bacterial and fungal communities21,22, and are one of the strongest risk factors for allergic asthma development1,23–26. In a case-control study in Ecuadorian infants, antibiotic use during the first year of life was associated with fungal overgrowth and increased susceptibility to allergic asthma9. Antibiotic-induced mycobiome changes have also been linked to exacerbation of airway inflammation in animal models13,27–31. Bacterial-fungal interkingdom interactions are reported in many ecosystems, including the neonatal and infant microbiome11,13,32, yet how antibiotics influence these interactions is less known. While this may suggest that fungal overgrowth and/or dysbiosis may contribute to the antibiotic-induced microbiome alterations that cause immune dysregulation during infancy, the impact of early-life antibiotics on the mycobiome and its effects on host immune development remains unknown.

Here, we analyzed the compositional changes to the bacterial and fungal gut microbiome in a prospective clinical study of infants <6 months of age receiving urgent antibiotic therapy. Upon finding that antibiotic use led to the expansion of Malassezia spp., we developed a gnotobiotic mouse model to study the causal contribution of a representative species from this genus in intestinal immune development and susceptibility to allergic and post-viral airway inflammation. This comprehensive study provides new insights into how antibiotics alter infant microbiome composition and further demonstrates how their effects on the mycobiome contribute to host immune dysregulation and allergic airway responses.

Results

The ANTIBIO study

The ANTIBIO study recruited 47 infants aged <6 months evaluated in the emergency department of the Alberta Children’s Hospital (ACH-ED) who were prescribed parenteral (i.e., intravenous or intramuscular) antibiotics (see “Methods” and “Supplemental Methods”). Study participants were separated into two groups based on treatment duration, short-term (receiving antibiotics for 2–3 days; N = 22) and long-term (4–14 days; N = 25). Antibiotic treatment duration included both parenteral antibiotics administered in the hospital and oral antibiotics prescribed at discharge. Study subject characteristics are presented in Table S1. Most variables were distributed similarly between the two treatment groups (P > 0.05), except for antibiotic treatment received, birth weight (median weight at birth: 3.4 kg for short-term vs. 3.0 kg for long-term; P = 0.02), presence of pets in the household (no pets at home: 36.4 % for short-term vs. 68.0 % for long-term; P = 0.03), and heart rate at triage (mean heart rate: 153 bpm for short-term vs. 175 bpm for long term group; P = 0.007) (Table S1). While the ANTIBIO study was not designed to examine the associations between antibiotic use and these participant characteristics, they likely reflect known associations between increased infection risk or severity and the need for prolonged antibiotic administration, as previously described33–37. Nevertheless, as potential confounders of the effects of antibiotics on microbiome composition, these variables were included in our subsequent analyses.

Antibiotic use induced strong fungal and bacterial microbiome changes and expansion of Malassezia spp. in infants

To explore the effect of antibiotics on the intestinal fungal and bacterial biomass, we determined microbial DNA concentrations in feces by qPCR of the internal transcriber spacer (ITS) region38 and the prokaryotic small subunit ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) gene39. Antibiotics reduced bacterial and increased fungal DNA concentrations in the complete cohort of samples (Fig. 1a). However, antibiotic treatment decreased bacterial concentration only in the short-term but not the long-term group (Fig. S1a), suggesting a possible expansion of antibiotic-resistant strains that overtake the newly available ecological niche. We then characterized the impact of short- and long-term antibiotic treatment on the intestinal bacterial and fungal microbiomes via shallow shotgun sequencing and analysis using MetaPhlAn4.040 and ITS2 amplicon sequencing, respectively. Antibiotic treatment had an opposite effect on bacterial and fungal microbiome alpha-diversity. While antibiotics significantly decreased bacterial community species richness (Chao1 index; Fig. 1b) and alpha-diversity (Shannon index; Fig. S1c) in the short- and long-term group, they significantly increased fungal richness (Chao1; Fig. 1c) and alpha-diversity in the short-term treatment group (Shannon index; Fig. S1d). To further scrutinize whether the opposing effects of antibiotic use had occurred in the same samples, we examined these effects at the individual level. For the majority of the study participants, antibiotic treatment concomitantly increased fungal alpha-diversity while reducing bacterial alpha-diversity. Overall, antibiotic use increased fungal diversity in 78.9% (15/19) of short-term (Figs. 1d), and 56.2% (9/16) of long-term samples (Fig. S1e), while reducing bacterial alpha-diversity in 86.7% (13/15) of the short-term (Fig. 1d), and 90.9% (20/22) of the long-term samples (Fig. S1e).

Permutation multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) of bacterial and fungal community beta-diversity based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities revealed that antibiotic use impacted bacterial beta-diversity (Fig. 1e and Table S2). Antibiotic treatment duration significantly explained bacterial community variation in the short-term group (days on antibiotic, R2 = 5.61, P = 0.02), while time point (Pre vs. Post samples) significantly explained community variation in the entire dataset (R2 = 4.41, P < 0.001) as well as in the long-term group (R2 = 2.93, P < 0.001). Other variables significantly associated with bacterial microbiome composition include patient sex, age (days), and birth weight (Table S2). In contrast, antibiotic treatment had little impact on the overall mycobiome community structure of both short- and long-term groups, as well as the entire dataset (Fig. 1f and Table S2). PERMANOVA results showed no significant differences between treatment groups, time points, or duration of antibiotic treatment for fungal communities. Interestingly, in the long-term group, birth weight (R2 = 3.13%; P = 0.004) and patient sex (R2 = 2.86%; P = 0.045) significantly explained variance in mycobiome beta-diversity.

We then evaluated the differential abundance of fungal and bacterial taxa after antibiotic treatment (delta variance). Despite the high inter-individual variability in mycobiome composition (Fig. S2a, c) median relative abundance values for the top 100 fungal amplicon sequencing variants (ASV) merged at the genus level revealed significant reductions of Candida, Mycosphaerella, Saccharomyces, and Cladosporium, as well as a notable increase in Malassezia in both short- and long-term groups (Figs. 1g and S2b, d). We further corroborated these results using DESeq2, which revealed that both short- and long-term antibiotic treatment increased the relative abundance of 14 fungal ASVs, with the largest fold changes (8.62–26.04 log2 fold change) attributed to three Malassezia species: M. restricta, M. globosa, and M. sympodialis in both treatment groups (Fig. S2e and Table S3). Other fungal ASVs increased with antibiotic use included Hyphodontia pallidula, Rigidoporus sanguinolentus, Resinicium bicolor, and Fomitopsis pinicola (Fig. S2e and Table S3), all of which are common environmental fungi41–45.

To determine whether the increase in Malassezia relative abundance reflected true expansion, we normalized genus-level relative abundance by total fungal load measured by ITS qPCR to estimate absolute abundance. This analysis confirmed that antibiotic treatment induced Malassezia expansion, with a significant increase observed in the long-term treatment group, and a similar but non-significant trend in the short-term group (Wilcoxon test; Fig. S2f).

To further examine if the Malassezia spp. expansion could be linked to other study variables beyond antibiotic treatment, we used multivariable linear logistic regression analyses on study participant characteristics and antibiotics used in the clinic and/or at home. The associations between study variables and the observed Malassezia expansion were adjusted for the confounding effects of age, birth weight, total duration of antibiotic treatment, pregnancy duration, and total number of antibiotics prescribed. The total number of antibiotics received was the only variable positively and significantly associated with the increase in Malassezia spp. abundance (OR: 2.1954, CI: 0.3604–4.9453, P = 0.045; Fig. 1h, Table S4). However, the ANTIBIO study was not powered to determine whether specific antibiotic classes differentially contributed to these mycobiome changes, particularly given the heterogeneous and clinically driven prescribing patterns in this infant population.

As expected, infant antibiotic use resulted in very strong compositional changes to the bacterial microbiome. Despite high inter-individual variability (Fig. S3a–d), delta variance analysis identified significant changes in all the ten most abundant bacterial genera (Fig. S3e). Antibiotic treatment increased the relative abundance of Staphylococcus and Enterococcus, while reducing Bifidobacterium, Escherichia, Clostridium, and Phocaeicola genera in both short- and long-term groups (Fig. S3e). DESeq2 further revealed that while most of the differentially abundant bacterial ASVs were reduced, there were a few taxa that increased instead (Fig. S4). These include species of known infectious potential, such as Klebsiella spp., Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus avium, Clostridioides difficile, and Peptoniphilus harei46–51. Overall, antibiotics had a striking acute effect on the diversity and composition of the infant gut bacterial and fungal microbiome.

Antibiotic use impacts microbiome interkingdom interactions

To investigate how antibiotic administration impacts bacterial-fungal interkingdom interactions, we compared co-occurrence microbial networks at the species level using the NetCoMi (Network Construction and comparison for Microbiome data) before and after antibiotic treatment52. To increase statistical robustness for network construction, data from both the short- and long-term antibiotic exposure groups were combined. This approach was necessary because the model requires paired bacterial and fungal datasets for each sample to reliably perform the iterative procedures used to infer co-occurrence networks. Network analysis highlighted interdomain interactions in the infant gut microbiome that included fungi and bacteria (Fig. S5). Before antibiotics, predicted taxa with increased connectivity and positional importance within the network (hub taxa) included the bacteria Bilophila wadsworthia, Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum, Bacteroides ovatus, Clostridium symbiosum, and Sutterella wadsworthensis and the fungi Mycena arcangelia and Hypochnicium geogenium (Fig. S5). Antibiotic treatment changed the associations and hub taxa within post-antibiotic networks, including the loss of core infant microbiome bacterial taxa Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides, replaced by opportunistic pathogens Cutibacterium spp., Prevotella buccalis, Anaerococcus spp., and Peptoniphillus lacydonesis (Fig. S5). Network association analysis revealed differences in the structure of networks before and after antibiotics, with significant differences in measurements for closeness (proximity of nodes; j = 0.208, P = 0.03) and eigenvector centrality (central connectedness of nodes within the network; j = 0.208, P = 0.03) (Table S5), suggesting potentially different connectivity and interactions within the microbiome. Stark differences in hub taxa between both networks were evidenced in a Jaccard’s index of 0.0 (P = 0.003), indicating differences in the positional importance of microbial taxa within the networks (Table S5). While network analyses may or may not reflect real biological interactions due to their correlative, hypothesis-generating nature, these data suggest that infant antibiotic treatment impacts interkingdom co-occurrence dynamics in the microbiome. Interestingly, members of the Malassezia genus (noted by a star in the networks) displayed increased node sizes, closer proximity, and increased associations with hub taxa after antibiotic treatment (Fig. S5), potentially suggesting increased capacity and importance of microbial interactions. Together, these findings reflect that antibiotic use alters bacterial and fungal community composition and co-occurrence dynamics, further supporting their strong impact on the infant microbiome.

M. restricta colonization shifts the bacterial microbiome in gnotobiotic mice

Species from the Malassezia genus have been shown to induce proinflammatory Th1 and Th17 responses in the skin and the gut53,54. Given this and that M. restricta was one of the species with the strongest expansion following antibiotic treatment in infants in the ANTIBIO study, we evaluated the effect of colonization with M. restricta in gnotobiotic mice. Adult germ-free C57Bl/6J mice were colonized with defined microbial mixtures containing Oligo-MM12 bacteria (B), or bacteria plus M. restricta (B + M) (Fig. 2a). The Oligo-MM12 is a synthetic, stable, inheritable, moderate-complexity bacterial consortium55,56 that served as a basis for microbiome-elicited homeostatic immune responses. To ensure the pups were exposed to M. restricta, fresh fungal cultures were also spread on the dam’s abdominal and nipple area during the pups’ first week of life (see “Methods”). To explore the impact of M. restricta colonization on the coexisting bacterial microbiome, we performed 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing from DNA extracted from fecal pellets of F1 mice 21 days after birth. Permutational analysis on bacterial community beta-diversity (Bray–Curtis) showed that M. restricta induced strong community shifts (colonization, R2 = 33.35, P = 0.001; Fig. 2d). M. restricta impacted the relative abundance of key members of the Oligo-MM12 consortium, including an increase in E. faecalis (KB1 strain), and a reduction in Turicimonas muris (YL45), Blautia pseudococcoides (YL58), and Limosilactobacillus reuteri (I49) relative abundance (Fig. 2e, f). We then used untargeted metabolomics in fecal samples from 21-day-old F1 mice to evaluate the effect of M. restricta on microbiome function. Principal component analysis (PCA) plots of the normalized values of 122 characterized metabolites showed substantial shifts in the community metabolic landscape induced by the yeast (Fig. S6a, b). Volcano plot analysis yielded seven significantly differentially detected metabolites between B and B + M mice (FC > 2, P < 0.05; Fig. S6c and Table S6). The most striking change observed in these mice was a 6.56 FC reduction in oleate (Table S6). M. restricta-colonized mice also showed a drastic reduction in cortisol (5.29 FC) and corticosterone (2.23 FC) levels (Table S6). Overall, these data show that even if detectable at very low amounts, M. restricta significantly shifted microbiome composition and functionality.

M. restricta exposure induces early-life immune dysregulation

Considering the known role of the microbiome in regulating host immunity, we determined the impact of M. restricta colonization on early-life immune functions in F1 C57Bl/6J mice at 21 days of age. Flow cytometry analysis on immune cells isolated from the colonic lamina propria (cLP) and mesenteric lymph nodes (mLN) showed that M. restricta induced strong changes to the intestinal immune landscape. Mice in the B + M group had increased counts of eosinophils, CD64+ macrophages, and CD64+ cells producing TNF-α (Fig. 3a–c), supporting increased macrophage activation. We also detected increased monocyte turnover in the gut, with M. restricta colonized mice displaying increased numbers of Ly6C+CD64+ cells, accounting for both recruited (P1, Ly6C+MHC-II-) and transitioning (P2, Ly6C+MHC-II+) monocytes (Fig. 3d). Increased monocytic infiltration is a hallmark of cell recruitment to inflammatory sites57, which was also accompanied by increased neutrophil counts in B + M compared to B-only mice (Fig. 3e). We also determined the impact of M. restricta colonization on immune responses in the draining mLN, as this is an important immune priming site for both intestinal and systemic immune responses62,63. B + M mice displayed increased numbers of migratory dendritic cells (MigDC; CD11c+MHC-IIhi) and MigDCs producing TNF-α in the mLN (Fig. 3h, i), potentially indicating higher fungal antigen trafficking for induction of adaptive immunity at this site. Eosinophils producing IL-6, known to promote Th2 differentiation64, were also elevated in B + M mice (Fig. 3j). By labeling and quantifying eosinophils with immunofluorescence (IF) microscopy, we confirmed that M. restricta increased eosinophil counts in mLN, and revealed eosinophils accumulated in the paracortical region on this lymphoid organ (Fig. 3k, l), where T and B cell regions are localized65,66. Like in the cLP, we also observed increased Th2 and Th17 cells in the mLN of mice colonized with M. restricta (Fig. 3m, n). Several other immune cell types were not impacted by M. restricta colonization in cLP and mLN, including Th1 cells, Tregs, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig S7a–c). Finally, to also assess the impact of M. restricta on systemic humoral immune responses, we determined antibody titers in the plasma of F1 mice. Mice exposed to M. restricta showed reduced concentrations of IgG2b, IgA, and IgM plasma antibodies (Fig. S7d), and a non-significant reduction in IgE (Fig. S7e), supporting a generalized effect on B cell response in the B + M group. Altogether, these data support that Malassezia-derived signals alter early-life immune responses in gnotobiotic mice, including cell types deemed critical in atopy development.

To evaluate the potential associations between the described immune changes and the microbiome, we correlated the relative abundance of the bacterial species (Fig. 2f) with immune features in plasma, cLP, and mLN (Figs. 3 and S7) using the Biconjugate A-Orthogonal Residual (BiCOR) method67. This analysis yielded significant associations between immune changes and bacterial taxa differentially impacted by M. restricta colonization (Fig. 3o). Notably, E. faecalis (KB1), the only bacterial taxon increased in B + M mice, had the largest number of significant and mostly positive associations with several immune readouts, including a positive correlation with CD64+ macrophages, P2 and P3/P4 (CD64+Ly6C−) macrophage subtypes, type-2 conventional DC (cDC2; CD11b+), MigDC, and IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and TNF-α cytokine production by different cell types (Fig. 3o). In contrast, B. pseudococcoides (YL58), T. muris (YL45) and L. reuteri (I49), all of which decreased in abundance upon M. restricta exposure, were inversely correlated with some of the myeloid immune cell types found elevated in B + M mice (Fig. 3o).

While M. restricta exerted strong immunomodulatory effects in gnotobiotic mice, we sought to determine if this was due to working with mice colonized with a low-diversity bacterial community composed mostly of low and medium-immunogenicity species. Thus, to differentiate M. restricta-induced microbiome and immune effects from other immunogenic gut commensals, we upgraded the Oligo-MM12 consortium to also include C. albicans (isolate K3) and Pseudomonas fluorescens (isolate J4) (B + CP), isolated from ANTIBIO infant stool samples, and compared it to mice colonized with all species and M. restricta (B + CP + M). C. albicans is a common intestinal colonizer, identified in fecal samples from a very early age68,69, and induces strong mucosal immune responses17–19,58. P. fluorescens is a recently appreciated member of the intestinal microbiome, with strong immunogenic effects in mammals70,71. Microbiome profiling using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing revealed that M. restricta colonization significantly altered bacterial community structure (colonization effect on beta-diversity, R² = 22.58, P = 0.009; Fig. S8a) and changed the relative abundance of several members of the Oligo-MM12 consortium. These shifts included a significant increase in T. muris (YL45) and decreases in Blautia pseudococcoides (YL58) and Clostridium innocuum (I46; Fig. S8b).

Despite the presence of these additional immunostimulatory microbes, we observed very similar immune effects when M. restricta exposure occurred in combination with C. albicans and P. fluorescens (B + CP + M), such as increased eosinophils, macrophages, Th2, and Th17 cell counts in cLP, compared to B + CP mice (Fig. S8c). We also detected a trend in increased TNF-α+ MigDC, and a significant increase in Th2 and Th17 counts in mLN of B + CP + M mice compared to B + CP (Fig. S8d). Altogether, these results indicate that M. restricta colonization induces local and systemic early-life immune alterations even in the presence of additional immunogenic bacterial and fungal species, supporting a distinct immunomodulatory role for this fungus.

Early-life M. restricta colonization enhances allergic airway inflammation in gnotobiotic mice

To determine the causal role of early-life M. restricta colonization and induced immune dysregulation on allergic airway disease, F1 C57BL/6J gnotobiotic mice were subjected to three weekly intranasal HDM challenges at 7–10 weeks of age (Fig. 4a). B + M mice showed overt airway inflammation, marked by increased inflammatory infiltration in their bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and BALF eosinophilia, compared to unchallenged, naive controls (Fig. 4b–d). Histopathological scoring showed that M. restricta colonization significantly increased lung inflammation, accounting for perivascular, peribronchial, and parenchymal infiltration, as well as epithelial damage, compared to naive and B-only mice (Fig. 4e, f). Following the HDM challenge experiments, we next tested whether other immunogenic microbial species could recapitulate the airway inflammatory phenotype observed with early-life M. restricta colonization. To this end, gnotobiotic mice colonized with the Oligo-MM12 consortium were additionally colonized with C. albicans and P. fluorescens (B + CP) and compared to mice colonized with the same community plus M. restricta (B + CP + M). Consistent with our previous observations, M. restricta exposure significantly enhanced HDM-induced airway inflammation, as reflected by increased total inflammatory cell counts and elevated eosinophil numbers in BALF compared with B + CP mice (Fig. S8e). In contrast, colonization with C. albicans and P. fluorescens in the absence of M. restricta did not reproduce the same magnitude of inflammatory response. These findings indicate that the airway inflammatory phenotype observed following HDM challenge is not simply attributable to colonization with additional immunogenic microbes, but remains associated with early-life M. restricta exposure.

M. restricta-induced immune dysregulation and airway inflammation are partially dependent on eosinophils

A consistent observation across our M. restricta colonization model was the significant eosinophilia in the colon, mLN, and lungs. Eosinophils are tissue-resident immune cells that support intestinal homeostasis in response to microbiota signals72. In addition to their ability to exert antifungal activity, eosinophils mediate and support immune responses in the host via the recruitment and activation of other immune cells72–75. To determine if Malassezia-induced eosinophilia orchestrates the observed immune dysregulation and airway inflammation, we repeated the colonization and HDM inflammation models in eosinophil-deficient ΔdblGATA1 mice76. M. restricta colonization in ΔdblGATA1 mice ablated the M. restricta effect on macrophages, dendritic cells, and myeloid cytokine responses in cLP and mLN seen in wild-type mice colonized with this fungus, yet still induced increased neutrophil counts in their cLP and Th2 and Th17 cells in both cLP and mLN (Fig. S9a, b). This experiment revealed that M. restricta-induced myeloid immune responses are eosinophil dependent, while the Th-skewed immune phenotype is likely mediated by other intestinal immune cells.

To determine if eosinophils were required for M. restricta immune responses in the airways, we subjected ΔdblGATA1 mice to the same HDM challenge scheme as wild-type mice (Fig. 4a). In agreement with previous reports showing eosinophils are dispensable for certain aspects of inflammatory responses to allergens77–79, ΔdblGATA1 mice developed robust airway inflammation, with increased BALF cellularity (Fig. 4g), histopathological score measurement (Fig. 4h), and marked infiltration of neutrophils and macrophage/monocytes (Fig. 4i), which were independent of colonization. Additional characterization of the immune response in the lungs using flow cytometry showed that most of the cell types assessed were present in similar counts between B and B + M ΔdblGATA1 mice (e.g., neutrophils, macrophages, and Th2 cells), although there was an increase in IFN regulatory factor 5 (IRF5)-expressing interstitial macrophages (IM, CD64+SigF-) and Th17 cells in ΔdblGATA1 B + M mice (Fig. 4j–o), further implicating eosinophils in the M. restricta-induced immune responses to aeroallergens in the lungs.

M. restricta enhances airway inflammation in gnotobiotic mice infected with RSV

We also investigated the impact of M. restricta colonization on immune responses in mice infected with respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) at 7 days of age. In addition, we assessed the combined effects of RSV infection and M. restricta colonization on allergic airway inflammation. RSV is a prevalent respiratory pathogen, particularly in infants, frequently leading to hospitalization and significantly increasing the risk of asthma development80. Although the mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear, recent studies implicate the gut-lung axis, linking early-life dysbiosis to worsened RSV outcomes and heightened susceptibility to allergic inflammation81–83. Thus, we hypothesized that early-life colonization with M. restricta would exacerbate antiviral responses to RSV infection and enhance susceptibility to allergic airway inflammation later in life.

To test this hypothesis, we utilized a neonatal RSV infection model in gnotobiotic BALB/c mice, a well-established strain for RSV research84. This model was also selected to evaluate the impact of M. restricta on the broader immune cell landscape involved in airway inflammation after HDM challenges. Compared to C57BL/6J mice, which exhibit predominantly eosinophilic responses, BALB/c mice develop a more mixed inflammatory profile, including mast cells, neutrophils, and lymphocytes85. Colonization was performed as previously, using defined microbial mixtures of Oligo-MM12 bacteria (B) or bacteria plus M. restricta (B + M). F1 offspring were infected intranasally with RSV (105 PFU, Line 19 strain) at 7 days of age, and lung inflammation was assessed 1 week post-infection (Fig. S10a). Immune responses were confirmed in lung tissue 7 days post-infection. While RSV infection reduced CD4+ T cells and increased CD8+ T cells and Th17 cells, colonization with M. restricta did not affect these or other lung immune cells (Fig. S10b–h), indicating no significant effect on the severity or immunophenotype of neonatal RSV infection.

Allergic airway inflammation was also evaluated in 10-week-old BALB/c mice following three weekly intranasal HDM challenges (Fig. 5a). Consistent with our findings in C57BL/6J mice (Fig. 4), M. restricta colonization increased airway inflammation in both previously RSV-infected (B + M + RSV) and non-infected (B + M) mice (Fig. 5b, c), inducing higher levels of neutrophils and lymphocytes in BALF (Fig. 5d, e) and increasing monocytes in uninfected mice (Fig. 5f), compared to naive controls. Notably, RSV infection and M. restricta colonization (B + M + RSV) resulted in enhanced eosinophils and Th2 cells, compared to naive controls (Fig. 5g, h), suggesting that fungal colonization in combination with neonatal RSV infection exacerbates allergic airway inflammatory responses typically less pronounced in this mouse strain. Discussion

Antibiotic-induced intestinal microbiome dysbiosis is a well-known risk factor for childhood asthma1,23–26, and it exacerbates allergic airway inflammation in murine models27–31. Previously, we demonstrated that antibiotic administration and intestinal fungal colonization worsen airway inflammation to ovalbumin allergen in gnotobiotic mice13. This study expands on this by distilling the complex interactions between the largely unexplored fungal dysbiosis, immune dysregulation, and susceptibility to allergic airway inflammation. Our findings highlight how fungal expansion during antibiotic-induced dysbiosis can profoundly modulate both local and systemic immune responses.

To our knowledge, the ANTIBIO Study is the first characterization of the acute effect of antibiotic use on the infant mycobiome. Although it lacked an age-matched untreated control group, the study was powered to detect acute antibiotic-induced mycobiome alterations, based on prior research9. Further, this study allowed us to capture microbiome alterations at the individual level, revealing that antibiotics had opposing effects on bacterial and fungal alpha-diversities in most infants, reshaping cross-kingdom interactions and network dynamics. Importantly, antibiotic therapy markedly increased the abundance of Malassezia species, a fungal genus identified as a hallmark of an immature microbiome. Persistent Malassezia colonization was recently linked to delayed microbiome maturation and elevated risk of atopic dermatitis, an early indicator of asthma progression (Hoskinson et al.20). Our findings, therefore, provide mechanistic support for a potential link between antibiotic-induced fungal expansion and immune dysregulation relevant to atopic disease.

This study showed that combination antibiotic therapy was significantly associated with Malassezia spp. expansion, even after adjusting for confounding factors such as gestational age at birth, antibiotic therapy duration, and birth weight. Reduced birth weight and prolonged antibiotic use have been associated with Malassezia skin colonization, and lung and systemic infections in term and preterm infants86–89. While species of this genus, such as M. globosa, M. restricta, M. sympodialis, and M. furfur predominantly colonize the skin90,91, some are also commonly found in the gut, particularly during infancy8,69,92,93 (Hoskinson et al.20). Patterns of mycobiome development during the first year of life reveal a higher predominance of Malassezia spp. that is later replaced by Saccharomycetales, possibly due to the introduction of complementary feeding at around 6 months of age. As lipid-dependent fungi94, Malassezia spp. may thrive during exclusive lactation due to the high lipid content of breast milk and formula. Supporting this, M. restricta-colonized mice showed reduced oleic acid levels (Fig. S6c), a major fatty acid in breast milk95.

We focused on M. restricta given its pronounced expansion following antibiotic use and its previously reported role in exacerbating chemically-induced colitis in mice53. Although we observed differences in colonization between infants and mice (as often seen in human microbiota-associated mice)96, M. restricta was detected for up to 16 days after neonatal exposure (Fig. 2b), suggesting its ability to colonize the mouse colon. Even in low amounts, M. restricta induced significant taxonomic and functional changes in the microbiome, indicating interactions with bacterial species, as previously described97,98. Recent findings in Canadian infants further support the importance of this genus in early-life microbial ecology, identifying Malassezia as a dominant fungal taxon and a key marker of delayed mycobiome maturation associated with increased risk of atopic dermatitis (Hoskinson et al.20). Our parallel results evidence the important ecological role of Malassezia species, exerting strong interkingdom interactions during microbiome succession, and contributing to immune dysregulation and heightened susceptibility to atopy, particularly in the context of early-life ecosystem disturbances such as antibiotic exposure.

The addition of neonatal RSV infection to this model offers further insight into the interactions between fungal colonization, viral infection, and immune modulation. While M. restricta colonization did not alter the severity of neonatal RSV infection in BALB/c mice, when combined with RSV infection, M. restricta exacerbated allergic airway inflammation following HDM challenges, promoting a mixed inflammatory profile (Fig. 5). This indicates that early-life fungal colonization can amplify viral infection-induced immune priming, enhancing susceptibility to allergic inflammation. These findings underscore the context-dependent nature of fungal-immune interactions and highlight the potential role of M. restricta in shaping the host’s immune response during critical developmental windows.

Our experimental design focused on ecological and immune mechanisms impacted by M. restricta, leaving microbial-derived mechanisms unexplored. This yeast may act directly by interacting with intestinal immune cells, as Malassezia can be found near the intestinal mucosa53, where it can be sensed and phagocytosed19,99. Malassezia spp. may influence immune responses via a diverse arsenal of virulence factors, such as extracellular vesicles (EV)100, which lead to direct immune activation and inflammation101. For example, Zhang et al. showed that M. furfur-derived EV can be absorbed by epidermal keratinocytes and induce IL-6 secretion in vivo, via activation of the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway102. However, the role of Malassezia-derived EV in intestinal immunity remains unexplored. Simultaneously or alternatively, its effect may be indirect, mediated through changes to the gut microbiome. Metabolomics revealed marked reductions in immunoregulatory metabolites with known links to microbial metabolism, such as oleate, cortisol, and corticosterone103–106. Oleate, a known anti-inflammatory fatty acid103,104, may be consumed by lipid-dependent M. restricta, reducing its protective roles in immune modulation107,108. Similarly, glucocorticoid hormones cortisol and corticosterone, influenced by microbiota and fungal exposure109–112, regulate Th cell development105 and myeloid cell functions106. While not directly explored in our work, it is possible that these metabolic shifts could underlie the immune responses observed with M. restricta colonization. However, it is also plausible that the immune effects observed arise indirectly from M. restricta-induced alterations in bacterial community composition or function, suggesting that bacterial-mediated mechanisms may contribute to the immune phenotypes observed.

A key observation of our study is that M. restricta induced eosinophilia across several mouse tissues. Eosinophils recognize fungal-associated patterns, exert antifungal activity, and mediate immune responses by secreting immunoregulatory molecules like granule proteins, enzymes, and cytokines/chemokines73–75. Eosinophil deficiency (i.e., PHIL, α-IL-5) increases colitis severity following DSS treatment113,114 and impairs immune cell migration and intestinal homeostasis72. In ΔdblGATA1 gnotobiotic mice, eosinophil deficiency partially abrogated myeloid immune responses to M. restricta (Fig. S9), consistent with reduced myeloid functions in these mice115. Nevertheless, Th2 and Th17 cells in the cLP, mLN, and HDM-challenged lungs were unaffected by eosinophil depletion (Figs. 4 and S9), aligning with a previous study showing eosinophils are dispensable for intestinal adaptive responses in a Giardia muris infection model116.

Despite the relevance of our findings, several limitations should be considered. While recent work by Hoskinson et al.20 in 3- and 12-month-old Canadian infants highlights the prominence of this genus in early-life mycobiome ecology and its association with delayed fungal maturation and increased atopy risk, further human studies are needed to determine whether antibiotic-induced Malassezia spp. expansion in infancy is transient, whether it contributes to immune dysregulation in infants, and whether it mediates the established association between early-life antibiotic exposure and increased risk of atopy. Our study design also included infants receiving short (2–3 days) or longer (4–14 days) antibiotic courses that likely reflected clinically driven treatment decisions for suspected or confirmed infections. Because detailed clinical diagnoses were not available, we were unable to determine whether the underlying medical indications for antibiotic use contributed to the observed mycobiome shifts. In addition, the ANTIBIO cohort was not powered to resolve the effects of specific antibiotic classes or regimens on fungal expansion. Future studies should therefore examine how different antibiotic strategies influence fungal dynamics and health outcomes in infants.

In addition, while we used a well-established bacterial consortium, it cannot replicate the immune complexity of more diverse microbial communities117,118. Future studies should examine the ecological, functional, and immune consequences of Malassezia spp. colonization in mice harboring more complex microbiomes. Our work also did not investigate if M. restricta colonized other tissues, such as the airway or the skin, where it may drive local immune responses. For example, Malassezia has been associated with acute pulmonary exacerbations in cystic fibrosis patients119 and was elevated in sputum samples from asthmatics120. Finally, our study was not designed to determine the antigenic specificity of the immune responses observed. We therefore cannot distinguish whether the increased Th2 and Th17 populations or circulating antibodies are directed against M. restricta itself, against bacterial members of the consortium, or arise indirectly from microbiome shifts induced by fungal colonization. Future studies will be required to resolve these possibilities.

In conclusion, our reverse-translation approach identified M. restricta as a key driver of intestinal dysbiosis, immune dysregulation, and airway inflammation, unveiling novel mechanisms of mycobiome-induced immune modulation linked to common practice infant antibiotic therapy. The amplification of airway inflammation in RSV-infected mice by fungal colonization underscores the importance of considering fungal dysbiosis not only in allergic airway inflammation but also in the context of viral infections and atopic disease risk. Notably, although conducted independently and using different methodologies, our findings align with recent work in Canadian infants showing that persistent Malassezia colonization is associated with delayed fungal maturation and increased atopy risk. Together, these parallel studies reinforce the ecological and immunological importance of Malassezia in early life and highlight the infant mycobiome as a promising target for therapeutic strategies aimed at preventing immune dysregulation and pediatric allergic disease.

Methods

Study design

This study used a reverse translational approach by combining a population-based, observational, prospective clinical study in young infants receiving antibiotics in the Calgary metropolitan area, Alberta, Canada, followed by experiments in gnotobiotic mice to determine the impact of fungi expanded after antibiotic treatment on microbiome ecology and host immune development. The ANTIBIO clinical study protocols were approved by the Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB17-1951). The caregivers of all participants provided written informed consent. The gnotobiotic mouse experiments were conducted under protocols approved by the University of Calgary Animal Care Committee (AC20-0176), following the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care.

ANTIBIO study recruitment and sample collection

To study the impact of antibiotic use on the infant bacterial and fungal microbiomes, the ANTIBIO study recruited 47 infants aged <6 months evaluated in the ACH-ED, in Calgary, Alberta, from July 2018 to February 2020. To differentiate the microbiome changes attributed to short- and long-term antibiotic treatment, study participants were categorized into two groups: (i) infants receiving antibiotics for 2–3 days (short-term), or (ii) infants receiving antibiotics for 4–14 days (long-term). Two fecal samples were obtained per study participant. An initial stool sample or rectal swab was collected in the hospital by a research nurse from the Pediatric Emergency Research Team (PERT) within six hours of the first dose of antibiotics (pre-antibiotics). A second stool sample or rectal swab was collected within 24 hours of the last dose of antibiotic (post-antibiotics). The follow-up sample was collected either by a PERT nurse in the hospital or by the patient’s caregiver if discharged home with a prescription of oral antibiotics. Antibiotic treatment consisted of parenteral antibiotics received in the hospital or the combination of parenteral plus oral antibiotics prescribed at discharge from the ACH-ED. Enrolment questionnaires were completed in person by the caregivers of all participants. Additionally, PERT Research Nurses collected information from charts and medical records regarding admission and treatment outcomes. Follow-up questionnaires were conducted over the phone on the last day of antibiotic treatment by a member of the Arrieta lab (Dr. Marie-Claire Arrieta or Dr. Erik van Tilburg Bernardes). The study was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and all applicable regulations. Additional ANTIBIO study details are provided in Supplemental Methods.

Microbial DNA isolation and quantification

Microbial DNA was obtained from fecal samples (humans or mice) using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with an added cell lysis step at the TissueLyser II bead beater (Qiagen), eluted in nuclease-free water, and stored at −80 °C. Fungal and bacterial DNA concentrations in fecal samples were determined by qPCR, as previously described13,38,39. The ITS marker and 16S rRNA gene were selected for quantitative amplification of fungal and bacterial DNA, respectively, using iQ SYBR Green Supermix (BioRad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The qPCR runs were performed on the StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) in the Snyder Resource Laboratories, University of Calgary. Detailed qPCR protocols are described in Supplemental Methods.

Shotgun metagenomics sequencing, processing, and bacterial taxonomic assignment

In-house extracted DNA samples were sent to Microbiome Insights (Vancouver, Canada) for shallow shotgun sequencing. Shotgun sequencing was performed as previously described4, with modifications. Libraries were prepared using an Illumina Nextera library preparation kit (Illumina) and sequenced in a NextSeq 500 System (Illumina Inc., San Diego, USA) using paired-end (150 bp × 2) reads. Initial quality evaluation was done using FastQC v0.11.5. Shotgun metagenomic sequence reads were processed in four steps: adapter removal, read trimming, low-complexity-reads removal, and host-sequence removal. Adapter removal was done using cutadapt v2.6. Trimming was done with Trimmomatic v0.36 using custom parameters (LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:36). Low-complexity sequences were detected with Komplexity v0.3.6. High-quality reads were mapped to the human genome (Genome Reference Consortium Human Reference37), and those that mapped to it were removed from the analysis. The remaining reads were taxonomically classified using MetaPhlAn4.040.

ITS2 and 16S sequencing, processing, and bacterial taxonomic assignment

In-house extracted DNA samples were sent to Microbiome Insights (Vancouver, Canada) for ITS2 and 16S amplicon sequencing. The ITS2 and 16S V4 regions were amplified and sequenced as previously described13,69,121. Demultiplexed forward and reverse reads were denoised, filtered, inferenced, trimmed, and merged using the DADA2 v.1.26.0122 package for R (R Development Core Team; http://www.R-project.org). Samples with less than 1000 sequencing reads were excluded, and ASVs appearing in only one sample (singletons) were removed. This sample cut-off was chosen following inspection of the sequencing depth and rarefication curves (Fig. S11), which confirmed sufficient depiction of sample diversity and prevented unbalanced exclusion of pre-antibiotic samples in the ITS2 dataset. Taxonomic assignment of the resulting ASVs was performed using the UNITE v8.2 database123, or an in-house database containing the 16S gene sequences of the Oligo-MM12 consortia strains124. The remaining dataset was filtered for ASV appearing at least three times in 10% of samples. Complete workflow tutorials can be found at https://benjjneb.github.io/dada2/tutorial.html and https://benjjneb.github.io/dada2/ITS_workflow.html.

Gnotobiotic mouse model of M. restricta colonization

Germ-free wild-type C57BL/6J or BALB/c mice were obtained from in-house breeding colonies in the International Microbiome Centre (IMC). Specific pathogen-free ΔdblGATA1 (B6.129S1(C)-Gata1tm6Sho/LvtzJ; JAX Stock No. 033551) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory and rederived germ-free through the axenic embryo transfer program in the IMC. Eight- to 15-week-old germ-free wild-type or ΔdblGATA1 female mice were orally gavaged with a consortium of microorganisms prepared under anaerobic conditions by mixing 100 μL of microbial cultures, as previously described13. The consortia consisted of twelve mouse-derived bacteria species (Oligo-MM12)56 with or without: M. restricta (ATCC MYA-4611), C. albicans (isolate K3), and/or P. fluorescens (isolate J4). Oligo-MM12 bacteria were grown for 2 days at 37 °C in fastidious anaerobe broth (LabM, Heywood, Lancashire, UK) under anaerobic conditions. M. restricta was grown for 7–10 days at 30 °C in mDixon broth. C. albicans K3 and P. fluorescens J4 clinical isolates were grown for 1 day at 30 °C in YM broth (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA). After gavages, mice were paired for mating in a 2:1 female:male ratio per cage. To ensure fungal exposure to F1 mice, corresponding fungal cultures were spread twice on the dam’s abdominal and nipple regions during the pups’ first week of life, as previously described3,13,125. To gain further insight into the implications of antibiotic use in M. restricta colonization, a group of B + M mice was treated with antibiotic amoxicillin-clavulanate (0.2 mg/ml; Sigma, Oakville, ON, Canada; B + M+Abx) from the 2nd to 21st day post birth. Mice were housed inside gnotobiotic flexible-film isolators or isocages in the IMC, under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, 40% relative humidity, 22–25 °C, and ad libitum access to sterile food and water.

Metabolomics sample preparation, run, and analysis

Fecal samples were obtained from F1 mice at 21 days of age and processed for metabolomics, as previously described13,121. Samples were subjected to untargeted metabolomics using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) at the Calgary Metabolomics Research Facility (CMRF), at the University of Calgary, which is supported by the IMC and the Canada Foundation for Innovation (CFI-JELF 34986). Files containing metabolite concentration were received from CMRF, and concentration data were normalized through median, square root transformation, and Pareto scaling on MetaboAnalyst v.5.0126.

Early-life immune assessment

Early-life immune functions were determined in mouse plasma, cLP, and mLN at 21 days post-birth. Three-week-old F1 mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. Blood was drawn by cardiac puncture in syringes primed with heparin (100 U/mL; Sandoz Canada Inc., Boucherville, QC, Canada), and mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 3000xg for 10 min, at 4 °C. IgA, IgG1, IgG2a, IgG2b, IgG3, and IgM titers were determined in plasma via electrochemiluminescence, in Mouse Isotyping Panel 1 Assay Kit (Meso Scale Diagnostics, Rockville, MD, USA), and IgE titers were determined in IgE BD OptEIA ELISA (BD Biosciences), as previously described13. The whole colon and mLN were surgically excised for immune cell isolation. The intestinal immune landscape was characterized in cLP and mLN using flow cytometry, as described below.

Isolation of intestinal immune cells

cLP immune cell isolation was performed as previously described72, with small modifications. Whole colons were excised, cleaned of any fat and connective tissue, cut longitudinally, and washed in ice-cold Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) supplemented with 2% inactivated horse serum (iHS; Sigma). Epithelial layers were removed with two 20 min incubation, at 37 °C in pre-warmed epithelial cell removal solution (HBSS supplemented with 4% iHS [Sigma] and 5 mM EDTA [Sigma]). The remaining tissues containing lamina propria and muscularis layer were digested for 20 min digestion, at 37 °C in pre-warmed digestion medium (RPMI supplemented with 10 mM HEPES [Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA], 10% iHS [Sigma], 10 U/mL DNase I [Sigma], and 1 mg/mL Collagenase Type VIII [Sigma]). Following digestion, the cells were filtered through a 100-µm cell strainer (Thermo-Fisher), pelleted down, resuspended in RPMI supplemented with 10% iHS, and immediately stained for flow cytometry.

Isolation of mLN immune cells

mLN immune cell isolation was performed as previously described127, with small modifications. mLN were cleaned of any fat and connective tissue and digested for 25 min, at 37 °C in digestion medium (RPMI supplemented with 1 mg/ml Collagenase IA [Sigma] and 30 U/ml DNase I [Sigma]). Following digestion, the cells were filtered through a 100-µm cell strainer (Thermo-Fisher), pelleted down, resuspended in RPMI supplemented with 10% iHS, and immediately stained for flow cytometry.

HDM airway inflammation model

An experimental murine HDM (Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus) model was performed as previously described128. Briefly, 7- to 10-week-old F1 mice were immunized intranasally (IN) with 100 µg HDM (Greer Laboratories, Lenoir, NC, USA) in 50 µL sterile PBS (2 mg/mL) three times with 7-day intervals, under light isoflurane anesthesia. In addition, a pooled group of mice from different colonization groups received vehicle solution (50 µL sterile PBS) and were denominated naive. Airway inflammation was assessed 1 day after the last IN challenge. BALF retrieval was performed by 3x 1 mL washes with ice-cold PBS + 10% iHS, in mice anesthetized with ketamine (200 mg/kg; Vetoquinol, Lavaltrie, QC, Canada) and xylazine (10 mg/kg; Bayer Inc., Mississauga, ON, Canada). The recovered BALF was combined in a 15-mL Falcon and kept on ice for total and differential cell counts. Total cell counts in BALF were performed in a hemocytometer. Differentials (eosinophils, neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes) were performed from 200 cells in hematoxylin and eosin-stained CytoSpins (Shandon Cytospin II, Thermo-Shandon, Runcorn, Cheshire, UK) based on standard morphological criteria.

Lung histology

Following BALF retrieval, the left lung was inflated, excised, and fixed in 10% formalin. Lungs were sent to the Alberta Province Laboratories (APL) for paraffin embedding, cut into 4 μm sections, and hematoxylin and eosin staining. Sections were blindly scored, as previously described23, with small modifications. Sections were scored on a 0-to-4 scale (0 = none, 4 = severe disease) for the following parameters: (i) peribronchial infiltration, (ii) perivascular infiltration, (iii) parenchymal infiltration, and (iv) epithelium damage, for a maximum score of 16. Following scoring, representative slides were selected for imaging, using an Aperio AT2 slide scanning microscope (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany), at ×40 magnification. Images had dimensions set in QuPath v.0.4.3 bioimage analyzer129, exported in TIFF format, and opened in ImageJ 1.53t (http://imagej.net) to overlay scale bars (200 μm).

Isolation of lung immune cells

Following BALF retrieval, the right (inferior and post-caval) lobules were excised and collected in RPMI supplemented with 2% iHS. Lung immune cell isolation was performed as previously described130, with modifications. Lobules were minced and digested for 30 min, at 37 °C in digestion medium (RPMI supplemented with 2% iHS [Sigma], 1 mg/ml Collagenase XI [Sigma], and 10 U/ml DNase I [Sigma]). Following digestion, the cells were filtered through a 100-µm cell strainer (Thermo-Fisher), and residual red blood cells were lysed in ACK lysis buffer (Quality Biological, Gaithersburg, USA) for 7 min. Cells were pelleted down, resuspended in RPMI supplemented with 10% iHS, and immediately stained for flow cytometry.

Cells were stained for surface markers for 20 min, fixed, and permeabilized with eBioscience Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer’s protocols, and stained for intracellular markers overnight at 4 °C. The following antibodies were used: BV421 anti-T-bet (clone 4B10; BioLegend, Sand Diego, CA, USA), eF450 anti-IL-5 (TRFK5; Invitrogen), BV570 anti-CD8 (53-6.7; BioLegend), BV605 anti-IL-10 (JES5-16E3; BD Biosciences), BV650 anti-IFN-γ (XMG1.2; BD Biosciences), BV711 anti-GATA3 (L50-823; BD Biosciences), BV750 anti-TCRβ (H57-597; BD Biosciences), BV786 anti-CD4 (RM4-5; BD Biosciences), AlexaFluor488 anti-FoxP3 (FJK-16s; Invitrogen), SparkBlue550 anti-CD45 (30-F11; BioLegend), PE anti-RORγt (B2D; Invitrogen), PE/Dazzle594 anti-CD90.2 (30-H12; BioLegend); PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-CD19 (1D3; BD Biosciences), PE-Cy7 anti-IL-17A (eBIO17B7; Invitrogen), BV421 anti-CX3CR1 (SA011F11; BioLegend), eF450 anti-I-A/I-E (M5/114.15.2; Invitrogen), BV570 anti-Ly6C (HK1.4; BioLegend), BV650 andi-CD64 (X54-5/7.1; BD Biosciences), BV711 anti-TNF (XP6-XT22; BD Biosciences), BV750 anti-Ly6G (1A8; BD Biosciences), BV786 anti-SigF (E50-2440; BD Biosciences), BV786 anti-CD3 (17A2; BD Biosciences), BV786 anti-CD19 (1D3; BD Biosciences), FITC anti-CD11b (M1/70; BD Biosciences), PE anti-IL-6 (MP5-20F3; BD Biosciences), PE/Dazzle594 anti-CD103 (2E7; BioLegend), PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-IL-12 (C15.6; BioLegend), PE-Cy7 anti-CD11c (N4/18; BioLegend), BV421 anti-CD8a (53-6.7; BioLegend), BV421 anti-TNF (XP6-XT22; BD Biosciences), AlexaFluor532 anti-CD45 (30-F11; Invitrogen), PE anti-IRF5 (W16007B; BioLegend), PE-Dazzle549 anti-CD64 (X54-5/7.1; BioLegend), PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-SigF (E50-2440; BD Biosciences), APC anti-IL-6 (MP5-20F3; BD Biosciences), AlexaFluor700 anti-Arg1 (A1exF5; Invitrogen), and APC-Fire750 anti-CX3CR1 (SA011F11; BioLegend). Immunofluorescence staining and analysis

For IF detection and quantification of eosinophils, mLN were excised and fixed overnight in 10% formalin. Paraffin-embedded organs were sectioned by the University of Calgary Diagnostic Services Unit (DSU). IF staining was performed as previously described131, with modifications. Slides were deparaffinized and pre-treated at room temperature with sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 1 h, Digest-All™ 3 pepsin solution (Thermo-Fisher) for 10 min, and Rodent M Block (BioCare Medical; Pacheco, CA, USA) for 30 min. After three washes, slides were incubated with mouse anti-mouse monoclonal EPX (Clone MM25-8.2.2; Mayo Clinic, AZ, USA) and Rat anti-mouse MBP (Clone MT2.-14.7.3; Mayo Clinic) primary antibody mix at 1:250 overnight at 4 °C. After three washes, slides were incubated with goat anti-mouse AlexaFluor488 (polyclonal; Thermo-Fisher) and goat anti-rat AlexaFluor647 (polyclonal; Thermo-Fisher) secondary antibody mix at 1:250 at room temperature for 2 h. After three washes, slides were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma) at 1:1000 at room temperature for 10 min. The stained slides were imaged in a Nikon A1R laser scanning confocal (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), in tiles using a 20 × 0.75 NA objective. Images were stitched together to visualize the entire section. Eosinophil numbers were quantified using FIJI (ImageJ v.1.54 f). Images underwent background subtraction and contrast adjustments, followed by thresholding and subsequent particle analysis using the FIJI BioVoxxel Toolbox plugin. The total tissue area was determined, and the data were expressed as eosinophils per mm2. Representative data for presentation were imported into Adobe Photoshop v.22.4.2 where brightness and contrast changes were applied to the entire image. All final images were compared to the original raw image to ensure data integrity.

RSV neonatal infection in a gnotobiotic mouse model

Germ-free BALB/c mice were colonized by gavage and bred as described above. Once the offspring reached 7 days of age, they were transferred with their dams to isonegative cages in the IMC to undergo RSV infection. Mice were transferred under sterile conditions, maintaining the same light, temperature, and gnotobiotic environment. RSV infection was performed with the Line 19 RSV strain. This strain was originally isolated from an infant with respiratory illness and has been shown in mouse models to elicit airway mucus production, mimicking the human infection132. RSV stock was propagated in mycoplasma-free cell cultures. Before infection, the virus stock was concentrated by centrifugation at 170,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C and resuspended in PBS at a concentration of 2 × 10⁷ PFU/mL. Then, 5 μL (10⁵ PFU) was pipetted onto the neonate’s nose, holding the mice in an upright position until the entire volume was inhaled83. Non-infected controls received PBS only. When the mice were 8–10 weeks old, they underwent the HDM airway inflammation assay as described above. After the endpoint, the recovered BALF samples were used for total and differential cell counts, and cell isolates from the lungs were stained for flow cytometry.

Data visualization and statistical analysis

Bacterial (shotgun and 16S) and fungal (ITS2) community matrices were merged, and all additional filtering and analysis steps were done using the Phyloseq v.1.36.023133 package. Changes in community structure (beta-diversity) were assessed statistically using PERMANOVA134 and visualized in PCoA plots based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities. Changes in community alpha-diversity were measured using the Shannon diversity and Chao1 species richness indices. To further visualize the co-occurring changes in community diversity in ANTIBIO participants, normalized scaled observed fungal and bacterial alpha-diversity were plotted per individual participant. To explore the changes in taxonomic community structure, boxplots were made to identify outliers, and normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. If data were normally distributed (P > 0.05), significant changes in relative abundance were determined with unpaired t-test, and if data were not normally distributed (P ≤ 0.05), significant changes were determined with the Mann–Whitney (Wilcoxon rank-sum) test. The delta relative abundance for the most abundant ASVs was determined as the change between relative abundance values per post- and pre-antibiotic samples. DESeq2 v.1.38.3135 was used to determine differentially abundant ASVs in the shotgun and ITS2 dataset between time points. We used the NetCoMi R package v.1.1.052 to build interkingdom microbial association networks using the ITS2 and shotgun datasets. Bacterial and fungal co-occurrence networks were constructed using relative abundance with variance stabilizing transformation at the species level, a Pearson correlation threshold of 0.5, and a fast greedy clustering algorithm. Hub taxa were identified as those having the highest betweenness centrality, associated with high connectivity with different nodes and sub-networks52. Pair-wise network comparisons were made between pre- and post-antibiotic time points for measures of centrality and hub taxa, calculated based on 5000 permutations. Random forest was performed to determine the most relevant factors (infant, pregnancy and delivery, and antibiotic treatment) predictive of Malassezia spp. expansion. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed using the package lme4 v.1.1.33136 to identify factors associated with an increase in Malassezia abundance. Only the top five predictors identified by random forest were included in the logistic regression analysis to prevent model overfitting. Results are reported as log-transformed OR and 95% CI for each factor. The relative abundance values for the Malassezia genus on pre- and post-antibiotics samples were used to define a Malassezia trend variable (“expansion”: N = 23, “no-expansion”: N = 13) for each participant. The “no-expansion” variable was set as a reference level for both random forest and logistic regression analyses. Fecal metabolite changes between colonization groups were visualized in a PCA plot and a dendrogram of the top 50 most abundant metabolites. To determine the significant differences in metabolite detection, we used volcano plots, combining results from FC analysis and t-test, with an FC and P-value threshold set to 2 and 0.05, respectively. PCA, dendrogram, t-tests, and volcano plot analysis of fecal metabolites from gnotobiotic mice were performed using MetaboAnalyst v.5.0126. Flow cytometry data obtained in the SP6800 Spectral Cell Analyser (Sony) and the Cytek Aurora (Cytek Biosciences) were exported as .fcs files and analyzed using FlowJo v.10.8.1 (https://flowjo.com). Graphs were made and analyzed using Prism v.10.0.2 (GraphPad Software Inc.). Bar graphs display the mean with standard error of the mean (S.E.M.), and all data points represent biological replicates. Outliers were identified using the ROUT test (Q = 1%) and removed. Statistically significant differences between colonization groups were determined with t-test or ANOVA for parametric or Kruskal–Wallis for nonparametric. For all tests, significance was set at P < 0.05.
