Mentees’ Perspectives on Mentorship and Sponsorship in Academic Anesthesiology: Findings from a Single-institution Focus Group

Anne L Donovan, Seyedeh Ala Mokhtabad Amrei, Ting Guo, Joyce Chang, Jina L Sinskey, Oana Maties, Rachel Schwartz

Abstract

Mentorship and sponsorship are important to career advancement and sustainability within academic anesthesiology, but unique challenges contribute to the gap that exists between optimal and actual mentorship and sponsorship. In this article, focus group data from a study conducted among 38 academic anesthesiology faculty members in a single department, stratified by self-identified gender and career stage as well as identity-specific groups, formulate three themes: (1) elevating high-impact mentorship, (2) formalizing sponsorship infrastructure, and (3) implementing a structured program. These themes and their subthemes are examined within a framework of questions that aid in constructing meaning and moving toward practical application. The results of the thematic analysis are well-supported by the literature presented in the final section of the article and also build on current literature by suggesting potential design principles for mentorship and sponsorship structures synthesized directly from academic anesthesiology faculty across diverse career and life stages and identity-specific groups.

Mentorship and sponsorship are critical constructs for career advancement and sustainability in academic medicine. Mentorship is known to benefit mentees, mentors, institutions, and academic fields at large, and to influence individual career development, personal growth, and academic productivity.– Further, mentorship and mentorship programs have been tied to retention in academic medicine., Sponsorship is a distinct yet related concept that is also crucial to establishing momentum in an academic career path.,

A mentor is traditionally defined as an experienced individual who provides guidance, support, and advice to a less experienced individual (i.e., the mentee) to further their development in one or more areas. Mentorship is the process in which mentors and mentees engage primarily for the benefit of the mentee; however, mentor–mentee relationships are increasingly recognized as bidirectional, meaning that both parties contribute to and receive some benefit from the relationship. A mentor may serve other simultaneous roles in addition, including role model (i.e., a source of inspiration often exemplifying qualities one wishes to emulate), advisor (i.e., an authority figure who assists in making informed decisions), sponsor (i.e., an experienced person who provides access to resources or opportunities and/or advocates for more junior individuals using their own influence or capital), and/or coach (i.e., one who provides support or guidance to a learner toward specific goals, often on a shorter term basis).

Unique challenges in academic anesthesiology systematically disadvantage the process of mentorship and sponsorship in the field. There is a growing body of literature documenting the isolated nature of anesthesia practice; limited access to mentors and sponsors, particularly for women and underrepresented individuals;, lack of institutional support structures; and time constraints, which disproportionately affect women. However, many of the existing studies on this subject fail to account for individual variations in mentorship needs and do not provide specific guidance for underrepresented faculty. While studies have described a gap between desired or optimal mentorship and real-life mentorship in academic anesthesiology,,– more work remains to develop practical solutions to these problems. Previously proposed solutions include integrating mentorship, sponsorship, and allyship into the culture of anesthesiology at the departmental and organizational levels to improve belonging and encourage retention and to advance gender equity within anesthesiology leadership structures. However, prior studies lack the detail needed to serve as a practical guide for implementing mentorship and sponsorship structures that are accessible, adaptable, and scalable within academic anesthesiology.

In this article, faculty participating in a series of focus groups conducted within a large academic anesthesiology department describe features of high-impact mentorship and sponsorship and mentorship programs in academic anesthesiology. Faculty perspectives are then related to the broader literature on this topic. This work builds on current literature by suggesting design principles for mentorship and sponsorship structures that are synthesized directly from academic anesthesiology faculty across diverse career and life stages and identity-specific groups.

Approach to Data Collection

The establishment of the focus group, participant selection, and data collection methods used for this Special Article were described previously in our recent publication.

Participant Selection

Focus group participants were faculty members in the department of anesthesia and perioperative care at a large urban academic medical center, which includes an academic tertiary care center and a county hospital with a separate direct leadership structure. Faculty work at six distinct clinical locations, which include a tertiary care academic referral hospital; a hospital dedicated to the care of women, children, and cancer patients; an ambulatory surgery center; an ambulatory orthopedic surgery center; an orthopedic inpatient hospital; and a public county hospital and Level 1 trauma center serving a socioeconomically underserved population. A high degree of inter-group variability in clinical practice settings, subspecialties, and academic interests exists among participants. Faculty volunteered to participate in a focus group by completing an online form presented at the end of an optional department-wide faculty survey. The form allowed volunteers to self-select appropriate focus group(s), indicate a preferred order for group assignment, and provide consent to participate. Based on form responses, participants were assigned to one of nine focus groups (table ): Early career women and men, mid-career women and men, established-career women and men, parents, researchers, and underrepresented in medicine. Grouping by career stage allowed for targeted mentorship and career development questions and for candid conversation between near-peers, while grouping according to gender allowed for discussion of perspectives on the impact of gender on career trajectory. Specific stressors facing vulnerable populations in medicine were discussed within groups for parents, researchers, and underrepresented in medicine individuals; no specific career stage guidance was provided for self-selection into these groups. Because of the inductive aim of the study, saturation was not the criterion on which focus group size was based; however, the number of focus groups conducted was sufficient to achieve code saturation. Early career was defined as assistant professor or instructor rank, mid-career was defined as associate professor rank, and established career was defined as professor rank based on the specific promotion process at the study institution. This study was granted exempt status by the University of California, San Francisco Institutional Review Board (IRB # 23-39253). The manuscript was prepared according to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) checklist.

Focus Group Process

For each focus group, Zoom scheduling software (version 6.3.4, Zoom Video Communications, Inc., USA) was used to select a 1-h meeting time that maximized the number of attendees per group. Each focus group was conducted over Zoom using semi-structured interview guides developed by an established-career female anesthesiology and critical care faculty member (A.L.D.), a mid-career female anesthesiology and critical care faculty member (J.C.), an established-career female anesthesiology faculty member (O.M.), and a female qualitative health services researcher with expertise in physician well-being research (R.S.). Questions were informed by a prior department-wide survey that had been conducted to understand the experiences of faculty members within the department and were pilot tested and refined with clinical colleagues not involved in the focus groups. General categories covered by the interview guides include: demographics; faculty experience; allyship, mentorship, and sponsorship; and perceived needs for a structured departmental mentorship program. Each interview guide was tailored slightly to the focus group of interest (Supplemental Digital Content 1: https://links.lww.com/ALNO/A28). Focus groups were conducted by one of two members of the research team (A.L.D. and R.S.) with support from a third team member (O.M.). The interviews were transcribed, deidentified, and imported into Dedoose (version 10.0.35, SocioCultural Research Consultants, LLC, USA). Only the coding team had access to the transcripts.

Of the interviewers, A.L.D. was known to most focus group participants as a department faculty member; however, because she did not hold any leadership positions related to participants’ clinical or academic work, the relationship was collegial and nonhierarchical. R.S. was known to most participants in the research focus group and similarly had a collegial, nonhierarchical relationship with participants. There were four members of the coding team: two female premedical students (T.G. and S.A.M.A), A.L.D., and R.S. Measures taken to reduce bias during the coding process include involvement of both clinical (A.L.D.) and nonclinical (R.S.) faculty in addition to researchers external to both the department and academic medicine (T.G., S.A.M.A.), which allowed for discussion of the transferability of ideas raised. Reflexivity was employed in the discussion of potential biases arising from lived experiences within the team, thereby challenging assumptions made throughout the coding process. The team used an inductive thematic analysis approach and followed a constructionist paradigm. The entire coding team met weekly for 3 months to establish and refine a codebook. To ensure intercoder reliability, each transcript was reviewed by at least two coders, with any discrepancies resolved through full-team discussion and consensus.

Participant Demographics

Thirty-eight out of 51 faculty members who volunteered for the study ultimately participated in a focus group. Constraints in scheduling a group meeting in this population prevented all volunteers from participating. Each group had three to six participants, with a median of four (table ). A single focus group was conducted for each group except for the established-career men group, which required two separate focus groups because of scheduling constraints (two and four participants per group, total six participants). Data were pooled for this group for the purpose of reporting. Nineteen participants were women and 19 were men. Thirteen participants were early career (average 2.4 yr on faculty, range: 0.5 to 4 yr), 12 were mid-career (average 8 yr on faculty, range: 6 to 10 yr), and 12 were established-career (average 18 yr on faculty, range: 14 to 21 yr) (table ). Additional demographic details were previously reported.

Thematic Analysis

Across focus groups, participants identified mentorship and sponsorship as vital for career fulfillment and retention, particularly during vulnerable transitions. Despite their acknowledged importance, participants noted the “freeform” nature of current practices, and some also shared negative or suboptimal experiences with mentorship and sponsorship. Both positive and negative experiences were integrated to propose structures that participants would find helpful for formalizing mentorship and sponsorship. Consequently, three themes emerged: (1) elevating high-impact mentorship, (2) formalizing sponsorship infrastructure, and (3) implementing a structured program (fig. ). Theme 1 had two subthemes that characterize high-impact mentorship (tailored mentorship and mentorship relationship), theme 2 had three subthemes that advance sponsorship on an individual and departmental or institutional level (network-based sponsorship seeking, intentional sponsorship practices, and departmental structures and culture as sponsorship enablers), and theme 3 had five subthemes that offer participant-proposed considerations for implementing a structured program (accommodate barriers, allow voluntary participation, facilitate intentional mentor–mentee matching, provide meeting structure, and align with professional development initiatives). These themes and subthemes are explored further below using questions as descriptive prompts. Illustrative quotes corresponding to each theme, subtheme, and question are listed in table .

Why Is Mentorship Important in Academic Anesthesiology?

Academic anesthesiology faculty are working in a changing landscape with numerous professional and personal stressors that contribute to difficulty achieving traditional academic success and likely influence the high intent to leave academic practice.,, One research faculty explained their perspective on the importance of mentorship and departmental support in their decision to stay in academic medicine:

“I know a lot of [relatively junior] people who have left [the department] who had a lot of potential...and should have probably stayed in academics, but for whatever reason, like they had childcare demands or they just weren’t getting the data [and] mentorship [they needed], opportunities were missed to help those people like bridge that gap. And I feel like I kind of had moments in my career where, you know, I had my kids and then...I had a sick family member and [there were] a lot of moments where I probably could have left research. And it took a lot of mentorship from my primary mentor, but also just like the department recognizing those stressors on my life and giving me a little bit of a break in terms of the productivity while I was taking care of those things so that I could come back.” (FG8)

To maximize the impact of mentorship, participants discussed the need to tailor mentorship to both career stage and the professional or personal domain for which mentorship is needed.

Participants noted that the type and character of the guidance needed from a mentor change with career stage and professional goals. For example, new faculty benefit from having designated individual(s) available for practical guidance as they adjust to a new system. Early career faculty require concrete direction on tailoring their professional activities to their individual goals and promotion criteria. Mid-career faculty can use mentorship to develop a broader view of their career trajectory and professional identity. An established-career woman described how mentorship needs change in the later stages of one’s career:

“I don’t think you ever make the transition away from being a mentee, [but] I’m more deliberate about [the way I seek advice] and who I go to...[I] always think I should [continue to seek mentorship] if I want to continue to grow, if that makes sense.” (FG5)

In addition, participants emphasized the need for multiple mentors with distinct areas of expertise to target different professional and personal domains. Some areas that may necessitate mentorship include: clinical work, career or promotion, research content or methods, and navigating personal stressors. An early career woman said:

“We don’t have to just stick with one mentor,...we can have [many mentors] for different reasons – like one for research, one for clinical [work], one who is your person to reach out to [for advice], one person who knows [the institution and] how to navigate the system, [and a sponsor who can pass on] a leadership role when they’re done with their [term].” (FG1)

Different mentorship needs were also highlighted for different individuals. Researchers noted the difference between a “career” mentor, who can provide guidance on professional activities and promotion, and a “content” mentor, who has specific expertise in one’s research methods or content area. For parents or caregivers, participants expressed a desire for guidance on navigating caregiving stress and their careers simultaneously. Individuals from underrepresented groups highlighted the impact of being able to seek mentorship from someone with a shared background or experience or from someone willing to understand the impact of the individual’s unique experience on their career.

How Should a Mentee Identify and Select an Appropriate Mentor?

The process of establishing a successful mentorship relationship was frequently described as organic, spontaneous, and self-determined. Participants expressed the need for a genuine connection to form between mentor and mentee, which often starts from a place of curiosity about shared experiences or interests. Often, the mentor is a figure the mentee wishes to emulate in some aspect of their career or life and matches well with their personality; thus, such pairings happen best on a mutually voluntary basis. These relationships were frequently described as organic. In addition, some participants stressed the need for a mentor to be “safe,” with no direct consequences for their career, to reduce pressure perceived from the relationship. An established-career man said:

“I feel like mentorship [grows] organically out of some sort of group identity. Because then...we have a common language, we can start to talk about, like, you know, ‘What do you do?’ ‘What are you interested in?’ ‘Where do you want to go?’ If it’s somebody...who I just really have no connection with, it’s a lot harder to establish that relationship – you can do it, but it’s harder.” (FG6)

What Essential Characteristics Should a Good Mentor Possess?

Based on participant experiences, characteristics of an effective mentor include honesty, safety, approachability, availability and/or generosity with time, attention to detail, use of a systematic approach, genuine interest in the mentee’s goals and success, accountability, growth mindset, and investment in mentorship (fig. ). The mentor’s ability to establish a connection, demonstrate their commitment, and show investment in the mentee’s career was seen to be of utmost importance. Further, formal training or education in effective mentorship was deemed necessary so mentors can provide direct, actionable, effective, and context-specific guidance.

What Should a Mentor Expect from a Mentee?

Mentees and mentors alike underscored that the mentee role is active rather than passive, although younger mentees may require education to this effect. Mentees benefit from being proactive, strategic, organized, hard-working, self-motivated, and goal-oriented (fig. ). They should view the mentor–mentee relationship as a bidirectional partnership, primarily driven by the mentee but with benefits for both the mentor and the mentee. Further, mentees should look for opportunities to join groups where community and mentorship or sponsorship opportunities exist.

Mentors emphasized the importance of the mentee taking initiative to initiate regular contact to ensure the mentor’s guidance is truly desired. Mentors viewed a productive relationship as a collaborative partnership, rather than operating in a relational hierarchy. They expressed appreciation for self-motivation and persistence from the mentee to drive the ongoing relationship and also acknowledged the time commitment required to be an effective mentor. One established researcher explained their approach to being a mentor:

“I recognize the things that got me to where I am [required standing on the shoulders of those who came before me]. So I’m mindful of that and I understand the need to help more junior people. I don’t tend to go run to people and say, well, do you need help? I expect people to come to me and ask me if I would provide help. And invariably I do...If you’re imposing on somebody and saying, look, I can help you, then they feel obliged to use you. That’s probably not the way to go.” (FG8)

How Can an Effective Mentorship Relationship Be Developed, Nurtured, and Sustained?

Establishing and cultivating a meaningful relationship was viewed as essential to high-impact mentorship. Investment from both the mentor and mentee, described as “leaning in,” was seen as a critical part of building and sustaining a mutually productive relationship, as was a growth mindset among both parties through continuing a bidirectional conversation and seeking feedback from each other. Relationships with a genuine connection may ultimately evolve or even dissolve as mentorship needs change over time.

How Does Sponsorship Complement Mentorship?

Sponsorship was viewed as a key counterpart to mentorship in ensuring career advancement and fulfillment, particularly for those in early- and mid-career. Less established faculty discussed the need to obtain sponsorship to achieve academic success. For example, an underrepresented individual said:

“[My mentor] has that individual push to want to seek people out and offer not just mentorship, but sponsorship, which at my stage is what’s so important because I’m not connected. So I can’t be putting out these papers and getting on these grants and things like that unless people are helping me out.” (FG9)

What Intentional Practices Are Recommended for Seeking and Offering Sponsorship?

Participants discussed individual strategies for both attaining and providing sponsorship as well as organizational strategies for promoting sponsorship, discussed further below.

Network-based Sponsorship Seeking

Participants described obtaining sponsorship as an active process that requires leveraging personal and professional networks, engaging in hard work to demonstrate value, and looking beyond immediate departmental boundaries for sponsorship. Casting a wide net to identify sponsors both within and outside the organization was recommended.

Intentional Sponsorship Practices

Participants described effective sponsorship as a deliberate process involving actively creating opportunities for others while “clearing the lane” for other faculty to take on new professional opportunities. They emphasized the need to ensure that sponsorship opportunities are distributed equitably across individuals. An established-career man said the following about his approach to intentional sponsorship:

“[Early in my career], there were probably opportunities that passed by me that I could have been, you know, pushed towards. And so I try very hard [to do this] for junior faculty and residents and fellows – [to give them] unsolicited advice or...encourage them, and then...on the back end, drop a name in someone’s ear about a potential person that might be a good fit for a position or opportunity within the department.” (FG6)

Departmental Structures and Culture as Sponsorship Enablers

Participants highlighted the critical role of departments, institutions, and organizations in promoting sponsorship by developing supportive infrastructure, encouraging innovative sponsorship practices, and promoting culture change. These efforts were seen as essential for embedding sponsorship into departmental norms and creating an environment where sponsorship can thrive.

How Should an Institution or Department Establish, Govern, Evaluate, and Continuously Improve a Formal Mentorship Program?

Participants highlighted the need to formally prioritize mentorship and sponsorship by offering a structured program. The following quote from a mid-career woman explains the rationale:

“...At least the first couple of years, when I was full or near full clinical commitment, you really just have to put your head down and get the work done and there’s nothing left...When there wasn’t a system that could...lower the activation energy at the end of the day when I had no energy and quite frankly, almost left quite a few times because I felt like, ‘what am I doing here?’ [I felt even more lost. Once I] started to like carve out some time where I could work on some of my own projects...that’s kind of what kept me here.” (FG3)

Participants offered the following guidance on potential program organization: (1) accommodate barriers, (2) allow voluntary participation, (3) facilitate intentional mentor–mentee matching, (4) provide meeting structure, and (5) align with professional development initiatives. Further description of these suggestions is provided in figure . Discussion and Literature Review

In this article, academic anesthesiology faculty spanning diverse career stages reflect on the critical role of mentors, mentorship, and sponsorship in career development and professional fulfillment. While many experiences with mentorship and sponsorship were expressed in a positive light, negative experiences reported by faculty were also a formative component of the themes identified. Participants characterized “high-impact” mentorship as targeting guidance to appropriate career milestones (i.e., the right kind of mentorship at the right time) and domains of personal and professional life (i.e., the right mentor for the right problem). Key features intrinsic to a productive mentoring relationship were organic relationship formation, helping mentors be effective in their role, and encouraging mentees to be active in their own professional advancement.

Focus group participants suggested principles important for: (1) elevating high-impact mentorship, (2) formalizing sponsorship infrastructure, and (3) implementing a structured program incorporating modern organizational constructs. While proposed program elements deserve further study before implementation, participants desired tailored mentorship to career-stage and content-area needs, mentor–mentee pairings that foster organic relationship formation, and education for mentors and mentees on effective mentorship strategies and proactive mentee behaviors. Participants suggested elevating sponsorship by using one’s network to actively seek sponsorship opportunities, providing sponsorship intentionally, and focusing on departmental or institutional infrastructure and culture change to enable sponsorship. According to faculty perspectives, structured mentorship programs should focus on eliminating barriers, allowing voluntary participation, facilitating intentional mentor–mentee matching, providing a structured meeting format, and aligning with professional development initiatives. The novelty of this work lies in the synthesis of structures proposed across career stages and identity-specific groups, which captures the priorities of individuals with diverse lived experiences within a single department. The potential design principles for mentorship and sponsorship infrastructure arising from this study generate hypotheses requiring further testing.

The mentor–mentee relationship emerged thematically in various ways, suggesting that mentor–mentee compatibility is critical to constructive relationships. Prior authors have reported that mentees characterize mentoring relationships that form organically within facilitating organizational support systems as higher in satisfaction and perceived success.,, Furthermore, successful mentorship relationships revolve around mutual respect, clear expectations, reciprocity, personal connection, and shared values, whereas failed mentorship relationships are more likely to involve communication challenges, personality differences, a lack of sincere interest in the mentee’s success, and inexperienced mentors. During the initiation and cultivation phases of the mentorship relationship, psychological safety is also key. Given evidence in the literature that women and members of underrepresented groups may face additional challenges in the mentoring relationship, members of vulnerable populations may prefer mentors who share similar backgrounds or characteristics; however, both in our data (n = 20 women, parents, and underrepresented in medicine faculty) and in the literature, the investment of the mentor is prioritized over their actual gender or cultural background.

The need for knowledge and training in mentorship best practices was frequently discussed in focus groups. The importance of mentorship expertise has also been acknowledged by the National Institutes of Health, which now requires that mentors of T32 award recipients receive formal mentor training and periodic knowledge updates. Numerous theories and conceptual models exist that can help mentors guide their mentees toward achieving their goals. One example is the Daloz model of balancing support and challenge, which argues for mentorship practices that couple high support with high challenge to empower the mentee, foster a growth mindset, and promote progress. Similarly, mentorship rooted in the tenets of Ecological Psychology, a framework that considers the interplay between an individual’s career aspirations and environmental influences on their ability to actually achieve their goals, can allow both mentee and mentor to focus an individual’s efforts on the most achievable goals in the context of their current personal and professional life. Thus, mentors with a theoretical understanding of mentorship best practices may be better equipped to guide their mentees toward their goals. Further, characteristics such as honesty, sincerity, active listening, and empathy have been described as desirable in a mentor, so providing formal training to mentors in the social-emotional aspects of mentorship may also be beneficial. In our data, instances of mentorship for primary gain emerged; several suggested design components of a mentorship program, including mentor training, may have prevented or alleviated such experiences.

Mentorship relationships have been described as bidirectional, reinforcing the active role of the mentee. Mentees should intentionally seek out mentors with desired personal or professional characteristics, develop specific goals with a clear timeframe and checkpoints, understand their own strengths and limitations, and be willing to step outside of their comfort zone—elsewhere called “taking the driver’s seat.” Some established-career participants in this study acknowledged that they had not been ideal mentees in their earlier career stages because they lacked understanding of the skills required to engage successfully in the mentoring process. Where authors encountered such dissenting remarks or other negative experiences with mentorship, programmatic design principles to address the participant’s experience were suggested. For example, inspired by the “terrible mentee” in FG5 and other participants’ observations that mentors may feel exploited by a one-sided relationship, providing formal education to mentees so they may effectively execute their roles and responsibilities in their own development was a suggested element of a mentorship program. Similarly, the concerns from participants that described “predatory” mentorship were addressed using the suggestions of voluntary participation, intentional mentor–mentee matching, mentor training, and fostering positive attributes in mentors.

Sponsorship is a vital complement to mentorship—in the words of Ayyala et al., “Mentorship Is Not Enough.” The need for formal sponsorship infrastructure highlighted in this study was characterized by strategic sponsorship-seeking, intentional practices for providing sponsorship, and structures embedded within a departmental culture aimed at enabling sponsorship. These priorities are reflected in the literature as well. Compared with mentorship, sponsorship often occurs more intermittently but with a greater focus on a particular professional development or advancement opportunity, with the overall goal of enhancing an individual’s visibility and professional networks., These relationships differ significantly: sponsorship requires the recipient to earn the sponsor’s respect and trust, as the sponsor leverages their own social capital to create a professional opportunity. Sponsorship recipients, therefore, must demonstrate behaviors or traits that would be considered worthy of sponsorship, such as aptitude for the task, promise of success, follow-through, and dependability, among others. Prior authors have proposed that deliberately using sponsorship for career advancement is an important strategy for early and mid-career faculty., Furthermore, sponsorship can be integrated into departmental culture or formal career development structures. It is widely reported that women tend to be “under-sponsored” and may be excluded from informal networking opportunities, which contributes to ongoing gender disparities; thus, intentionally creating sponsorship and networking opportunities for women may be a key to balancing the ongoing gender inequity reported in academic medicine.,,,

Focus group participants consistently desired structure to increase the impact and utilization of both mentorship and sponsorship. Many such successful structures have been previously described, including a facilitated peer mentoring program, a career-building framework intended for use in national working groups, a research scholars’ program,, and an academic promotion support project including embedded coaching. One such example in anesthesiology is the Women’s Empowerment and Leadership (WELI) program for pediatric anesthesiologists, which has reported positive outcomes including making personal connections, implementing new projects, achieving career advancement, improving work-life integration, and building confidence.– However, other authors report that the introduction of a junior faculty professional development program led to increased burnout and decreased quality of life by the end of the program, likely due to increased workload and challenges with work-life integration,; thus, the impact of such programs on participant well-being must be carefully considered in their design. In this study, participants suggested that a mentorship program should be comprehensive, facilitating matching between compatible mentors and mentees, integrating novel structures beyond the traditional dyadic mentorship relationship, providing structured content to guide mentorship discussions, and aligning mentorship with complementary professional development efforts to encourage open discourse and community building. Many of these suggestions are well-aligned with previously published studies. Newer models of mentorship involving team, peer, and network structures have gained traction in the literature. Novel constructs such as “speed mentoring” and peer- and group mentoring have emerged in response to the notion that no single mentor is likely to meet all of a mentee’s needs. Peer group mentoring programs have demonstrated a positive impact on self-efficacy in career advancement, research, and mentoring, and higher cognitive empathy, among other career-related skills., Ideally, such a program would allow an individual to develop a mentorship network comprised of many mentors with diverse areas of expertise and departmental or institutional affiliations.

While mentors may act as coaches within the purview of their mentorship responsibilities, dedicated coaching has also been incorporated into successful mentorship programs. Coaching is often a shorter-term, performance-driven relationship that can be used within any career pathway. The coaching role is gaining popularity among clinician-educators, as it encourages learners to set goals, engage in self-reflection, and make changes that maximize their performance. Many positive impacts of coaching have been previously described in medical education– and at the faculty level–; therefore, great potential exists to formally integrate coaching practices into a structured program for faculty.

Acknowledgement of Limitations

The authors acknowledge that mentorship and sponsorship principles derived from this study come from a small number of participants at a single institution, which may limit the transferability of the study’s findings. However, the diverse clinical practice of study participants across six separate clinical sites with distinct patient populations, work environments, and leadership structures, along with the inclusion of faculty across career stages and those belonging to underrepresented groups, increases the likelihood that their perspectives reflect broader lived experiences shared by faculty outside the study institution. Focus group studies risk inherent biases stemming from peer influence; attempts were made to limit bias related to the focus group study design by assigning focus groups by career stage and gender, which increased participants’ comfort levels in engaging in candid discussion. Selection bias may have been introduced by selecting participants from those who had completed a previously described department-wide survey, potentially shifting the sample toward a more engaged population. As a result, more disengaged or peripheral faculty’s voices may not have been fully represented.

It is also important to note that an all-women coding team completed the analysis, including that of the all-men focus groups. The team’s analytic perspectives were shaped by lived gendered experiences and social positions, which may have influenced the salience of the identified themes and attributed meanings. Throughout the analytic process, the team engaged in discussions to examine how individual positionality may have affected interpretation and remained attentive to the risks of misinterpreting men’s accounts through the lens of women’s experiences. However, the outsider perspective was valuable for allowing identification of implicit norms and gendered expectations that may have been less visible to those whose positionality was more concordant with the participants’. Instead of viewing gender concordance as a prerequisite for valid interpretation, authors viewed the analytic process as one in which meaning was necessarily co-constructed between the participants and the researchers’ interpretations. As such, findings are presented with the acknowledgement that alternative results may have emerged from a coding team consisting of different gender and professional identities.

Conclusions

Academic anesthesiology faculty spanning all career stages identified their need for high-impact mentorship from trained mentors tailored to individual career development; accessible sponsorship opportunities; and a formal mentorship program incorporating novel structures, discussion guides, education for mentors and mentees, and attention to growing inclusion and belonging. Mentors, sponsors, and coaches all play important roles in facilitating development of fulfilling and sustainable careers in academic anesthesiology, although mentees should be proactive and systematic to intentionally drive their own careers toward their goals.
