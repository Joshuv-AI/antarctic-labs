Factors Affecting Pharmacokinetics and Dosing Strategies of Polymyxin B in Multidrug-resistant Gram-negative Infected Patients with Renal Impairment: A Systematic Review with Expert Opinion
Authors: Asha K. Rajan, Vishal Shanbhag, Muhammed Rashid, Maria Ioana Onofrei, Indu Ramachandra Rao, Sohil A. Khan, Souvik Chaudhuri, Ravindra Prabhu Attur, Hassan Mitwally, Bhuvana Krishna, Vijayanarayana Kunhikatta, Surulivel Rajan, Girish Thunga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveMultidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative infections pose significant therapeutic challenges, with limited evidence guiding optimal polymyxin B dosing in patients with renal impairment. This systematic review aimed to identify key covariates influencing polymyxin B pharmacokinetic and inform dosing strategies in this population.MethodsA comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Cochrane library was conducted from inception to May 2025. Population–pharmacokinetic (pop-PK) studies evaluating polymyxin B in adult patients in adult patients with renal impairment, with or without renal replacement therapy (RRT) were included. Study quality was assessed using a validated critical appraisal of clinical pharmacokinetic studies tool (CACPK).ResultsEleven pop-PK studies (603 patients) were included. Considerable interindividual variability in clearance and volume of distribution was noted. Creatinine clearance (CrCL), RRT modality, and disease severity were key covariates influencing drug exposure. Convective RRT modalities (CVVH, CVVHDF) were associated with increased clearance and lower exposure, whereas diffusive modalities (CVVHD) showed variable effects. However, the clinical relevance of CrCL-based dose adjustment alone remains uncertain and should be interpreted alongside other clinical factors.ConclusionPolymyxin B dosing in renal impairment requires an individualized approach integrating renal function, RRT modality, and disease severity. While a loading dose of 150 mg is appropriate, maintenance dosing should be guided by clinical context, with higher doses in convective RRT and cautious dosing in diffusive modalities. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM), where available, is recommended to optimize efficacy and minimize toxicity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13318-026-01021-1.

Key Points

Polymyxin B pharmacokinetics in renal impairment show high interindividual variability, influenced by creatinine clearance (CrCL), disease severity and renal replacement therapy (RRT) modality.Convective RRT modalities are associated with increased drug clearance requiring higher doses, whereas diffusive modalities show variable exposure requiring cautious dosing.Individualized dosing supported by therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is essential to balance efficacy and toxicity in this population.

Introduction

Despite renewed interest in polymyxins for treating multi-drug resistant (MDR) Gram-negative infections, optimal dosing in patients with renal impairment remains unclear [1]. The United States Food and Drug Administration recommends 15,000–25,000 IU/kg/day (1.5–2.5 mg/kg/day) for patients with normal kidney function, with dose adjustments suggested for renal impairment. However, these guidelines lack robust evidence [2]. In contrast, international consensus guidelines do not recommend dose adjustments in renal impaired patients [3], as no consistent correlation between polymyxin B clearance and renal function has been demonstrated [4, 5]. Most supporting studies investigated doses below 2.5 mg/kg/day, limiting applicability to higher conventional dosing (2.5–3 mg/kg/day) [5–9]. Consequently, existing evidence does not fully support current recommendations of polymyxin B dosing [1].

The incidence of polymyxin-associated acute kidney injury (AKI) varies widely, ranging from 17% to 51%, depending on clinical definitions and the formulations used [10]. This high rate of nephrotoxicity impacts patient outcomes and carries economic implications [10]. Studies suggest a strong association between inappropriate polymyxin B dosing and renal impairment [11]. The absence of uniform dosing strategies results in unpredictable pharmacokinetics, complicating therapeutic management in patients with impaired renal function [12]. In extracorporeal therapies, factors such as continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) techniques, protein binding, dialysis dose, and polymyxin B’s physicochemical properties (e.g., molecular size, lipophilicity) significantly impact drug clearance [13]. Without a standardized dosing protocol, overexposure in renal-impaired patients increases nephrotoxicity risk, while underdosing compromises efficacy and may lead to treatment failure [14].

Few population–pharmacokinetic (pop-PK) studies have focused on optimizing polymyxin B dosing in renal impairment or those undergoing renal replacement therapy (RRT) [4, 5, 15–22]. Consensus guidelines target an area under the concentration–time curve across 24 h at steady state (AUCSS,24h) of 50–100 mg h/L, and an average steady-state concentration (Css.avg) of ~ 2 mg/L to balance efficacy and risk of AKI due to polymyxins [3, 23, 24]. However, significant interindividual variability in polymyxin B pharmacokinetics, especially among patients receiving CRRT, like continuous veno-venous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF), poses challenges in achieving these therapeutic targets by impacting drug clearance [25].

With the growing reliance on polymyxin B amid rising antimicrobial resistance, and the complex interplay between its pharmacokinetics, drug exposure, and clinical outcome, it becomes crucial to identify significant covariates that impact the optimal dosing strategies of polymyxin B. Considering current evidence, establishing a better-informed uniform dosing strategies for polymyxin B in renal-impaired patients and those on RRT is essential. This systematic review presents an overview of significant covariates affecting the pharmacokinetics of polymyxin B in this patient population. The expert opinion section explores the impact of different covariates on the pharmacokinetics of polymyxin B and its dosing recommendations, offering practical guidance for clinicians and pharmacists in managing polymyxin B therapy for patients with renal impairment or on RRT techniques.

Methods

Protocol Registration

The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO with registration number CRD42021294141 [26]. We used a participants, intervention, comparator, outcomes, and study design (PICOS) framework to establish inclusion and exclusion criteria. The review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [27].

Eligibility Criteria and Selection of Studies

We considered all published retrospective and prospective pop-PK studies reporting the factors affecting pharmacokinetics of polymyxin B, which included patients with renal impairment or on RRT modalities. Studies including adult renal-impaired MDR Gram-negative infected patients, with or without RRT techniques, receiving intravenous polymyxin B for a duration ≥ 48 h (once the drug achieves steady-state) were considered. Outcome of interest was to assess the impact of covariates on pharmacokinetic parameters including clearance (CL), volume of distribution (Vd) and half-life (t½). Studies that did not include patients with renal impairment, without sufficient data, narrative reviews, and protocols were excluded. Renal impairment was defined according to the criteria used in individual studies, most commonly based on creatinine clearance (CrCL). However, standardized classifications such as kidney disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO)-defined AKI or chronic kidney disease (CKD) stages were not uniformly reported across studies, limiting the ability to perform stratified analyses. A minimum duration of ≥ 48 h of polymyxin B was required to ensure that pharmacokinetic sampling reflected near steady-state conditions. However, we acknowledge that, in patients receiving CRRT, steady-state assumptions may not always be valid due to dynamic extracorporeal clearance.

Sources of Information

We performed a thorough literature search from inception to May 2025, using relevant MeSH terms and free text keywords of polymyxins and pharmacokinetics across databases such as PubMed/Medline, Scopus, Embase and the Cochrane Library. We also performed a bibliographic search of included studies to identify additional relevant studies. Only studies published in the English language were included. Detailed search strategies for each database can be found in Supplementary table S1.

Study Selection and Data Collection Process

We conducted a title and abstract screening, followed by a full-text assessment based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Collected data included the following details. Study characteristics: study ID, country, study design, study period, population, sample size; patient characteristics: age, male gender, weight, severity score, number of patients with renal impairment, patients on RRT, dialysis parameters, mean/median CrCL; polymyxin B dosing characteristics: duration of therapy, dose, formulation of drug and infusion time, and adverse effects; analysis and sampling strategy: analytical method, sampling strategy, plasma concentration and half-life of the drug, plasma protein binding; modeling parameters: pharmacokinetic model, software and structure, candidate and significant covariates, AUC in a steady state, and at 0–24 h, Vd, clearance, interindividual and residual variability, internal and external validation. Two reviewers (A.K.R., V.S.) independently performed the study selection and data extraction processes, with any discrepancies resolved through consensus or discussion with another member of the research team (G.T.). In cases of missing or incompletely reported pharmacokinetic data, no imputation was performed and analyses were based on available reported data. Study authors were not contacted for additional information, as the review focused on published aggregate data. No restrictions were applied on publication date, and all eligible studies meeting the predefined criteria were considered.

Critical Appraisal

We utilized the validated critical appraisal of clinical pharmacokinetic studies tool (CACPK) [28] to appraise the included studies. This tool is specifically designed to evaluate pharmacokinetic and pop-PK studies, addressing key methodological domains such as sampling design, analytical methods, model development and validation. Conventional risk of bias assessment tools developed for clinical or observational studies may not adequately capture these pharmacometrics specific aspects and therefore were not applied in this review. The CACPK tool comprises of 21 items divided into five sections, each evaluated based on the methodological rigor of the study with responses of “Yes”, “No”, “I do not know”, or “Not Applicable”. A score of 13 or above was deemed high quality, scores between 12 and 13 were considered fair to moderate, and scores below 12 were classified as poor[29, 30]. Two reviewers (A.K.R., V.S.) conducted the quality assessment independently, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with third reviewer (G.T.). The use of a domain-specific appraisal tool enhances the methodological relevance and interpretability of the quality assessment in pharmacokinetic research.

Results

Study Selection

A total of 12,703 records were initially identified through database search. The titles and abstracts screening yielded, 421 studies for full-text assessment, from which eleven articles were included in the systematic review. Critical Appraisal of the Included Studies

The quality of all 11 pop-PK studies included in this review was deemed high (> 14). The average score of the included studies was 16. However, several studies lacked detailed reporting on sampling sites, pharmacokinetic model validation methods, [4, 5, 15, 17, 20, 21], and explicit presentation of pharmacokinetic equations[4, 5, 15, 19–21], which should be considered when interpreting the robustness and reproducibility of these models. While these reporting limitations primarily affect transparency, they may introduce some uncertainty in the interpretation and generalizability of the findings. The critical appraisal of the included studies is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Characteristics of Included Studies

All 11 studies [4, 5, 15–22, 31] were published between 2013 and 2024, with the majority from China (n = 7) [16, 18–22, 31], followed by Brazil (n = 2) [4, 15] and the United states of America (n = 2) [5, 15]. Of these, nine studies were prospective [4, 5, 15–19, 21, 31] and two were retrospective [20, 22] in nature. All the included studies used polymyxin B for infections due to Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Enterobacterales, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Characteristics of Polymyxin B Dosing

Five studies [5, 18, 20, 21, 31] reported the duration of polymyxin B therapy, with median duration ranging from 9 to 14 days. Patients were included after 48 h of initiating polymyxin B therapy. Analysis and Sampling Strategy of Polymyxin B

Among the seven studies [4, 15–17, 19–21] that reported polymyxin B plasma concentrations and AUC levels, substantial variability was observed in the drug’s half-life across different patient populations and RRT modalities. In patients with normal renal function, polymyxin B exhibited a half-life of approximately 26.2 h, consistent with prior pharmacokinetic data [17]. In contrast, patients with renal impairment not undergoing dialysis had a shorter reported half-life of ~ 13.6 h [15]. Although seemingly counterintuitive, this finding may reflect differences in study design, sampling windows, or altered pharmacokinetic behavior due to impaired renal function without dialysis-mediated clearance. Potential factors include tissue distribution, altered protein binding or compensatory tubular reabsorption in non-dialyzed renally impaired patients.

In patients receiving CVVHDF, the half-life was reduced to approximately 6.9 h, suggesting enhanced drug clearance via combined convective and diffusive mechanisms [15]. Conversely, in those receiving CVVHD, the half-life was around 24.6 h, comparable to patients with preserved renal function [17] indicating a lesser effect of diffusive clearance alone. However, in CVVHD patients with underlying renal impairment, the half-life was prolonged to 36.4 h, underscoring the complex interplay between baseline renal function, dialysis modality and extracorporeal drug clearance [17]. Covariates Affecting Pharmacokinetics of Polymyxin B

Significant covariates affecting polymyxin B clearance included CrCL [15, 17, 18, 21, 22, 31], CRRT technique [16, 18] such as CVVHDF [15], body weight [16, 18], fluid overload [31], logarithmic transformed albumin level and sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score [18]. Recent studies with larger patient sample size and severe renal impairment confirmed CrCL significant impact on polymyxin B clearance [21], unlike earlier studies showing a non-significant effect [4]. CVVHDF [15] and CVVH [19] also significantly influenced polymyxin B clearance.

Body weight [15, 16] was a key covariate for Vd in the central compartment, but was not significant in renal transplant patients [21]. While consensus guidelines recommend weight-adjusted dosing, a recent study found no correlation between body weight and polymyxin B clearance [3]. A pop-PK study in sepsis patients showed an inverse relationship between polymyxin B concentration and daily fluid intake or fluid overload [31].

Log-transformed albumin level and GFR [17] affected the Vd in the peripheral compartment, CVVH significantly impacted the peripheral Vd, total and intercompartmental clearance [19], and SOFA score also influenced polymyxin B clearance, indicating the need for maintenance dose adjustments based on severity of sepsis/disease [18]. Table 5 elaborates on the candidate covariates tested in each study.

Effect of RRT Techniques on AUCss24h Levels of Polymyxin B

Among the included studies, AUC at steady state (AUCss) was plotted against dose and various RRT modalities or renal impairment. A comparison of mean AUCss24h of polymyxin B showed that patients on CVVH[19] and CVVHDF[15] had mean AUCss24h levels below the standard 50–100 mg h/L range, while those on CVVHD[17] and without RRT[15] had AUCss24h levels above the standard range. This highlights variability in drug exposure across RRT techniques and dosing differences among studies. Dosing Strategies Based on Significant Covariates Affecting CL and Vd of Polymyxin B

i. Requirement of Loading Dose: Most studies emphasized the need for a loading dose to achieve therapeutic concentrations, particularly for bacteria with minimum inhibitory concentration (MICs)≥ 2 mg/L. Li et al.[21] recommended a 75-mg loading dose followed by a 50-mg twice daily (BD) maintenance dose, effective in approximately 90% of patients but inadequate for MICs ≥ 2 mg/L. Sandri et al[4]. observed reduced drug exposure on the first day without a loading dose compared to steady-state.ii. Discussion

This systematic review has examined covariates influencing polymyxin B pharmacokinetics in patients with renal impairment, including those receiving RRT. The findings underscore the complexity of dosing polymyxin B in this population due to high interindividual variability in drug clearance and exposure. Key determinants include creatinine clearance, body weight, severity of illness, and the type of RRT modality received. These factors necessitate an individualized dosing approach supported by therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) where available.

Impact of Renal Function (CrCL)

Recent pop-PK studies present conflicting evidence regarding the influence of CrCL on polymyxin B clearance. While earlier investigations reported minimal or no effect [4, 20], more recent studies have identified CrCL as a significant covariate, especially in patients with severe renal impairment (CrCL < 40 mL/min) [17, 21, 22, 31]. For instance, Li et al. demonstrated a strong CrCL clearance relationship in renal transplant recipients [21]; however, the extrapolation of these results to the broader population must be approached with caution due to altered physiology in transplant patients.

Moreover, Wang et al. noted differences in clearance and AUC between patients with and without renal impairment but concluded that the two-fold variation did not justify routine dose adjustments [20]. These discrepancies between statistical significance and clinical impact highlight the need for outcome-driven studies to establish dosing thresholds for renal function-based dose adjustment.

As a result, while CrCL is an important determinant of polymyxin B clearance, it should not be used in isolation for dose adjustment and must be interpreted in conjunction with factors such as RRT modality, disease severity, and patient-specific characteristics. An important limitation in interpreting the impact of renal function on polymyxin B pharmacokinetics is the lack of a standardized classification of renal impairment across studies. Most investigations relied on CrCL thresholds rather than internationally accepted criteria such as KDIGO staging for AKI or CKD. This heterogeneity limits comparability between studies and restricts the ability to derive stage-specific dosing recommendations. Future studies should adopt uniform renal function classifications to enable more clinically meaningful stratification and to improve dosing precision. They should also explore integrating CrCL with additional clinical parameters, such as fluid status, concurrent medications, and organ support modalities, to optimize dosing

Importantly, the clinical significance of pharmacokinetic variability associated with covariates such as CrCL should be interpreted in the context of pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic targets, particularly the AUC/MIC ratio. While several studies demonstrated a statistically significant relationship between CrCL and polymyxin B clearance, the magnitude of this effect may not consistently translate into clinically meaningful differences in target attainment, especially for pathogens with low MIC values. Conversely, in infections caused by organisms with higher MICs (≥ 2 mg/L), even modest reductions in drug exposure due to increased clearance may result in suboptimal AUC/MIC ratios and therapeutic failure. Therefore, assessment of covariate effects should extend beyond statistical significance to include their impact on pharmacodynamic target attainment. This highlights the importance of integrating MIC data and, where feasible, TDM to guide individualized dosing strategies in patients with varying renal function.

Fixed Versus Weight-Based Dosing

The appropriate dosing strategy for polymyxin B—whether weight-based or fixed—remains a matter of ongoing debate. Sandri et al. initially recommended weight-based dosing (2.5–3 mg/kg/day based on total body weight) to achieve pharmacodynamic targets, [4] a strategy adopted by consensus guidelines [3]. However, Hanafin et al., in a more recent prospective analysis incorporating data from Sandri et al., proposed fixed loading and maintenance doses (e.g., 200 mg loading, 100 mg q12h maintenance), citing reduced variability in exposure across diverse patient populations [15]. Their findings highlight that, while total body weight may influence Vd, it does not consistently predict clearance in critically ill or renally impaired patients. Thus, fixed dosing with clinical refinements based on renal function, disease severity, and TDM may offer a more pragmatic and consistent approach in real-world settings.

Considerations for Loading Dose and MIC

Loading doses are crucial for achieving therapeutic concentrations, especially in infections caused by organisms with MIC ≥ 2 mg/L. However, this must be balanced against the risk of nephrotoxicity. Luo et al. showed that, while a loading dose of 2.5 mg/kg followed by 1.5–2 mg/kg every 12 h improved target attainment, it also raised the probability of toxicity in patients with renal dysfunction or on CRRT [18]. Similarly, Hanafin et al. found it challenging to achieve target AUCs in high-MIC scenarios without exceeding nephrotoxic thresholds [15]. These findings reinforce the importance of individualized loading doses guided by pathogen susceptibility, renal reserve, and RRT status. In patients with high MICs, combining intravenous polymyxin B with other agents or using adjunctive inhaled formulations may be safer than aggressive monotherapy escalation.

Effect of RRT Modalities on Clearance

RRT type significantly influences polymyxin B pharmacokinetics. Convective therapies such as CVVH and CVVHDF enhance drug clearance due to their ability to remove larger molecules via filtration and adsorption [15, 16, 32]. In contrast, diffusive modalities like CVVHD or SLEDD typically have a less pronounced effect due to the drugs’s large molecular size and high protein binding [17, 25]. Notably, even within similar RRT modalities, drug exposure can vary based on filter type, flow rates, and patient-specific factors. For example, patients receiving CVVHDF demonstrated reduced dose-normalized AUCs and shorter half-lives compared to non-dialysis patients [15]. Meanwhile, some patients on CVVHD with underlying renal impairment exhibited prolonged half-lives up to 36 h, [17] underscoring the complex interplay between baseline renal function and dialysis characteristics. Moreover, although polymyxin B undergoes minimal renal excretion, tubular reabsorption in non-dialyzed patients may prolong drug exposure and contribute to toxicity. In CRRT patients, however, extracorporeal removal bypasses this reabsorption, leading to increased clearance. There findings support the need for dose adjustments in CRRT to avoid therapeutic failure or toxicity [18, 33, 34].

In addition to RRT modality, variability in dialysis parameters such as blood flow rate, dialysate and ultrafiltration rates, and membrane characteristics may significantly influence polymyxin B clearance. However, these parameters were inconsistently reported across studies, limiting the ability to quantitatively assess their impact. As a result, the observed variability in drug exposure across RRT modalities may partly reflect differences in dialysis settings rather than modality alone. Future studies incorporating standardized reporting and sensitivity analyses of dialysis parameters are needed to better define their contribution to pharmacokinetic variability.

Role of Disease Severity

SOFA score was identified as a significant covariate affecting polymyxin B clearance in one pop-PK study [18]. The association likely reflects augmented renal clearance and capillary leak in severely ill patients. However, this finding has not been validated in other studies. As a composite score, SOFA was not developed to guide antimicrobial dosing, and its use in this context should be approached cautiously. Although higher SOFA scores may suggest a need for more aggressive dosing, they should be considered alongside other clinical indicators. Until more evidence emerges, TDM-guided adjustments remain preferable over SOFA-based strategies.

This systematic review highlights the multifactorial determinants of polymyxin B pharmacokinetics in renally impaired and critically ill patients. CrCL and RRT modality are key contributors to variability in drug exposure. While weight-based dosing may offer initial guidance, fixed dosing adjusted for clinical status and supported by TDM may provide more consistent therapeutic outcomes. Importantly, aggressive loading in high-MIC infections must be balanced with toxicity risk, and RRT-specific dose modifications remain essential for optimizing treatment efficacy and safety.

Expert Opinion

Polymyxin B remains a critical last-resort agent for MDR Gram-negative infections, yet dosing in patients with renal impairment—particularly those receiving RRT—poses considerable challenges.[4] Although existing guidelines do not mandate routine dose adjustments based on renal function,[3] accumulating pharmacokinetic data and clinical variability support a shift toward individualized dosing strategies informed by patient condition, pharmacodynamic targets, and, when available, TDM. These recommendations are intended to complement existing guideline-based approaches and should not be considered a substitute for established clinical guidelines.

Pharmacodynamic Targets and Variability

The pharmacodynamic efficacy of polymyxin B is best described by free-drug AUC/MIC, with a proposed target of 50–100 mg h/L and steady-state concentration near 2 mg/L [3, 4, 24]. However, these targets are primarily derived from preclinical models and may not fully capture real-world variability, especially in critically ill or renally impaired populations.

Polymyxin B is moderately protein-bound (~ 50–60%), and alterations in protein binding can increase the unbound drug fraction. While this may enhance bacterial killing, it may also increase the nephrotoxicity risk. Unfortunately, most pop-PK studies report only total concentrations, potentially overestimating target attainment in patients with altered protein binding states [13, 14]. An additional consideration is the distinction between total and unbound polymyxin B concentrations. As only the unbound fraction is Vd active, reliance on total plasma concentrations may not accurately reflect pharmacodynamic target attainment. This is particularly relevant in critically ill patients, where hypoalbuminemia, systemic inflammation, and altered protein binding can increase the unbound fraction, potentially enhancing both efficacy and toxicity. Despite this, most pop-PK studies report total drug concentrations and data on unbound polymyxin B exposure remain limited. Consequently, current PK-PD targets based on total concentrations may either overestimate or underestimate true pharmacologically active exposure in certain patient populations. Future studies evaluating unbound drug concentrations and their relationship with clinical outcomes are essential to refine dosing strategies.

Other contributors to interindividual variability include fluid overload, capillary leak, altered perfusion, and extracorporeal support. For instance, Wang et al. found that fluid status significantly influenced polymyxin B exposure in septic patients [31]. Variability in RRT modalities such as membrane type, flow rates, and timing of drug administration further complicates dosing.

Timing Around Dialysis

In patients receiving intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) or SLEDD, the timing of polymyxin B administration is critical. Administering the drug after dialysis minimizes extracorporeal clearance and ensures adequate post-dialysis plasma concentrations [19, 35]. Although polymyxin B is primarily non-renally eliminated, high-flux dialysis membranes and convective modalities may still remove appreciable drug amounts via filtration or adsorption [13, 25].

The Role of Combination Therapy

Combination therapy is preferred over monotherapy in critically ill patients, particularly when dealing with high-MIC pathogens. Evidence from in vitro synergy studies and clinical observations supports combining polymyxin B with agents such as tigecycline, aminoglycosides, or carbapenems. This approach may improve bacterial killing, reduce resistance development, and allow safer polymyxin exposure levels when monotherapy cannot achieve pharmacodynamic targets [10, 18].

RRT practices vary globally, with CVVH and CVVHD more commonly used in Australia, and IHD or SLEDD more frequently in Asian settings.[36] Despite the growing need for individualized polymyxin B dosing, TDM is not widely accessible, and validated assays for unbound drug measurement remain limited. In such context, clinical monitoring using surrogate markers like creatinine, albumin, and SOFA score becomes essential.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. Firstly, the heterogeneity in patient characteristics and varying degrees of renal dysfunction makes it challenging to compare the impact of specific renal function levels on polymyxin B pharmacokinetics. Secondly, there was inconsistency in detailed reporting of dosing regimens and modification based on the renal function. The variability in reporting makes it difficult to establish clear relationships between dosing modifications by the studies and its influence in pharmacokinetic outcomes. Thirdly, non-uniform methods used to calculate and report AUC values across studies may introduce bias in the comparison of drug exposure. Variability in AUC estimation approaches, including differences in pharmacokinetic modeling techniques, sampling strategies and calculation methods (e.g., model-based vs. non-compartmental analysis), can lead to systematic differences in reported exposure metrics. This heterogeneity limits the reliability of cross-study comparisons and may affect the interpretation of pooled pharmacokinetic findings. Moreover, the lack of detailed and standardized reporting of dialysis parameters across studies precluded sensitivity analyses to evaluate their impact on polymyxin B clearance. Furthermore, the absence of standardized renal impairment classification (e.g., KDIGO-defined AKI/CKD stages) across studies precluded subgroup analyses and limits the applicability of findings to specific stages of renal dysfunction. An additional limitation is the limited correlation between pharmacokinetic findings and clinically relevant outcomes such as mortality, microbiological clearance or treatment success across the included studies. Most pop-PK analyses have focused on identifying covariates influencing drug exposure, without linking these parameters to pharmacodynamic target attainment or patient-centered outcomes. This gap restricts the ability to translate pharmacokinetic variability into definitive dosing recommendations. Future research should prioritize integrated PK-PD studies that correlate drug exposure with clinical and microbiological outcomes to better inform evidence-based dosing strategies.

Future Perspectives

There is a critical need to investigate the impact of different RRT techniques, including HD, SLEDD, and peritoneal dialysis on polymyxin B dosing. Comparative studies of CRRT modalities such as CVVHDF, CVVHD, and SLEDD could clarify their effects on drug clearance. Additionally, assessing RRT parameters like dialysis dose, membrane type, and blood flow rate would refine dosing recommendations.

Incorporating biomarkers such as serum albumin into dosing algorithms may enable more personalized treatment. The impact of fluid overload on polymyxin B pharmacokinetics, especially in CKD and critically ill patients, warrants further study to improve dosing accuracy. Advanced modeling techniques offer promising solutions. Physiologically-based pharmacokinetic modeling can enhance understanding of drug distribution, while Bayesian forecasting can optimize dosing with limited sampling. In-silico modeling could predict drug–protein interactions and genetic influences. These approaches may lead to more precise dosing strategies, ultimately improving outcomes and minimizing toxicity.

Conclusion

This systematic review highlights the complex and multifactorial nature of polymyxin B pharmacokinetics in patients with renal impairment, including those receiving various RRT. While CrCL emerged as a significant covariate, dose adjustment based solely on CrCL may be insufficient and should be integrated with other clinical factors to guide individualized dosing. RRT modality plays a more decisive role, with convective therapies (e.g., CVVH, CVVHDF) associated with enhanced clearance requiring higher maintenance doses, whereas diffusive techniques (e.g., CVVHD, SLEDD) may permit lower doses due to variable drug removal. Body weight affects Vd but not clearance, limiting the utility of weight-based dosing alone. Given the narrow therapeutic index of polymyxin B, individualized dosing based on renal function and dialysis parameters is crucial. TDM, when available, should guide optimization. Until more robust outcome data emerge, cautious, patient-tailored dosing remains the best strategy to maximize efficacy while minimizing nephrotoxicity in this vulnerable population.
