Oral malignant melanoma of the tongue with suspected intralingual in-transit metastasis
Authors: Reon Morioka, Masako Fujioka-Kobayashi, Hiroto Tatsumi, Yuhei Matsuda, Rie Osako-Sonoyama, Michitaka Somoto, Takahiro Kanno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOral malignant melanoma (OMM) is a rare and aggressive malignancy, and involvement of the tongue is particularly uncommon. We report a case presenting with two isolated pigmented tongue lesions clinically resembling benign conditions. Excisional biopsy of the anterior lesion confirmed melanoma, and the tongue base lesion was clinically suspected to represent possible in-transit metastasis. To our knowledge, in-transit metastasis in OMM is not well documented. The patient underwent wide local excision followed by adjuvant pembrolizumab based on cutaneous melanoma guidelines. Final pathological staging was pT3b pN1c cM0 (stage IIIC). At 1-year follow-up, no recurrence or metastasis was observed and oral function was preserved with a palatal augmentation prosthesis. This case highlights diagnostic challenges and the importance of early biopsy and multidisciplinary management. Additional cases and further multicentre studies are needed to validate these observations and guide the development of standardised treatment protocols in the diagnosis and management of OMM.

Background

Oral malignant melanoma (OMM) is a rare malignancy arising from melanocytes of the oral mucosa and accounts for a small proportion of all melanomas.1 Unlike cutaneous melanoma, OMM is not a frequent clinical entity; however, it is clinically significant because of its aggressive biological behaviour and poor prognosis.2 Early diagnosis is often difficult, as oral pigmented lesions may be asymptomatic and frequently resemble benign or reactive conditions encountered in routine dental and oral examinations.3

The tongue is an especially uncommon site for OMM, and limited clinical experience has hindered the characterisation of its patterns of regional spread.4 While in-transit metastasis is a well-recognised feature of cutaneous melanoma, its occurrence and clinical implications in oral mucosal melanoma remain unclear. In addition to date, there have been no well-documented reports describing in-transit metastasis arising from OMM in the literature.5 Consequently, clinicians may underestimate malignant potential when encountering atypical pigmented lesions of the tongue.

This report aims to highlight the diagnostic challenges associated with pigmented oral lesions and to emphasise the importance of careful evaluation, timely biopsy and appropriate multidisciplinary management in patients with suspected OMM. Furthermore, this case may provide novel insights into the metastatic behaviour of OMM by illustrating a rare presentation suggestive of intralingual in-transit metastasis.

Case presentation

A man in his late 60s was referred to a tertiary specialist unit after asymptomatic pigmented lesions of the tongue were incidentally noted during a follow-up at a local hepatology clinic for chronic hepatitis B. The patient had a history of chronic hepatitis B and was receiving antiviral therapy. There was no relevant family history of melanoma or other malignancies, and no history of smoking or alcohol abuse.

On intraoral examination, two isolated melanotic lesions were identified (figure 1). One was a pedunculated, black-pigmented nodular lesion with a smooth surface on the anterior dorsal surface of the tongue. The second lesion was a flat black-pigmented lesion with an apparently normal surface located at the base of the tongue. Both lesions closely resembled benign pigmented conditions, including black hairy tongue, and the patient reported no pain, bleeding or dysphagia.

Intraoral clinical findings at initial presentation. A pedunculated black-pigmented nodular lesion with a smooth surface on the anterior dorsal surface of the tongue and a flat black-pigmented lesion with an apparently normal surface on the base of the tongue (arrows).

Given the atypical appearance and difficulty in excluding malignancy based only on clinical findings, an excisional biopsy of the anterior dorsal tongue lesion was performed for diagnostic purposes. Histopathological examination revealed a nodular, ulcerated malignant melanoma with a Breslow thickness of 2.2 mm (figure 2A,B).

Histopathological findings of the excisional biopsy specimen from the anterior dorsal tongue lesion. (A) Haematoxylin and eosin–stained section showing a pagetoid spread of tumour cells within the basal and parabasal layers of the oral mucosal epithelium. The tumour cells exhibit cytoplasmic vacuolisation and contain melanin pigment granules (original magnification ×10; arrows). (B) Immunohistochemical staining for tyrosinase demonstrating brown cytoplasmic positivity in tumour cells within the invasive nests at intermediate magnification, confirming melanocytic differentiation (original magnification ×20).

Subsequent imaging investigations were undertaken to evaluate the extent of disease. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a slight enhancement in the superficial portion of the lesion at the base of the tongue (figure 3A). MRI showed hyperintensity on T1-weighted images with contrast enhancement involving the base of the tongue and revealed no evidence of intracranial metastasis (figure 3B). Positron emission tomography showed no fluorodeoxyglucose uptake in the lesion at the base of the tongue (figure 3C); however, uptake was observed in the subcutaneous tissue of the left upper arm. Dermatological biopsy of this lesion confirmed an angioleiomyoma, and no distant metastasis was identified. In addition, gastrointestinal endoscopic examination was performed and revealed no evidence of synchronous malignancy. Based on these findings, the diagnosis was OMM of the tongue, clinically staged as cT3b cN1c cM0 (stage IIIC) according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system.6

Imaging findings of the tongue base lesion. (A) Contrast-enhanced CT (axial image) demonstrating slight contrast enhancement in the superficial portion of the tongue base lesion (arrow). (B) Magnetic resonance imaging (axial T1-weighted image with contrast enhancement) showing hyperintensity involving the tongue base lesion (arrow). (C) Axial PET-CT image showing no fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake in the lesion at the base of the tongue (arrow).

The patient subsequently underwent wide local excision of both tongue lesions with adequate surgical margins, in accordance with the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology for cutaneous melanoma,7 given the absence of established treatment guidelines specific to OMM. For postoperative airway management, a tracheostomy was performed, and surgery was conducted with endoscopic assistance (figure 4).

Intraoperative findings. (A) Surgical exposure of the anterior dorsal tongue lesion under endoscopic assistance. (B) Wide local excision of the anterior dorsal tongue with a 2 cm safety margin from the previous biopsy site. (C) Wide local excision of the tongue base lesion with a 2 cm safety margin around the pigmented lesion. (D) Reconstruction of the tongue base defect using a polyglycolic acid (PGA) sheet and fibrin glue after confirmation of negative surgical margins by intraoperative frozen-section examination.

Histopathological examination of the surgical specimens revealed no residual tumour in the anterior dorsal tongue lesion. In contrast, the tongue base lesion showed histological features identical to those observed in the biopsy specimen and was diagnosed as OMM. Based on the final pathological findings, the tumour was staged as pT3b pN1c cM0 (stage IIIC) according to the eighth edition of the AJCC staging system.

Molecular analysis demonstrated no BRAF gene mutation, while immunohistochemical analysis showed a positive expression of programmed death-ligand 1. In the absence of established treatment guidelines for OMM, adjuvant therapy was selected in accordance with the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology for cutaneous melanoma.7 Pembrolizumab was administered at a dose of 200 mg per dose every 3 weeks. The duration of adjuvant therapy was determined based on clinical judgement, and the treatment was continued for 6 months.

Differential diagnosis

At presentation, both tongue lesions clinically resembled benign pigmented conditions. The pedunculated, smooth-surfaced anterior dorsal tongue lesion, along with the overall black discolouration, raised an initial working diagnosis of a benign lesion associated with black hairy tongue. However, the presence of a discrete pigmented nodule and a separate flat pigmented lesion at the base of the tongue made it difficult to exclude a malignant process based on clinical examination alone. Because intraoral assessment is limited and non-invasive diagnostic tools such as dermoscopy are not routinely feasible in the oral cavity, a histopathological confirmation was required.3 8 Excisional biopsy of the anterior lesion established the diagnosis of malignant melanoma and prompted staging investigations and definitive surgical management of both lesions.

Outcome and follow-up

The postoperative course was uneventful. At the 1 year follow-up after treatment, there was no evidence of local recurrence (figure 5A), regional lymph node metastasis, or distant metastasis clinically and radiologically. The functional outcomes were satisfactory, and the patient has been using a palatal augmentation prosthesis since the postoperative period to improve tongue–palate contact (figure 5B,C). Oral intake has remained good, and no treatment-related adverse events requiring discontinuation of therapy were observed.

(B) Palatal augmentation prosthesis fabricated to improve tongue–palate contact after partial glossectomy. (C) Intraoral photograph with the PAP in place, demonstrating adequate tongue–palate contact during oral function.

Discussion

OMM is an uncommon and aggressive malignancy arising from melanocytes of the oral mucosa and is associated with a poorer prognosis compared with that of cutaneous melanoma.2 9 As early lesions are often asymptomatic and may resemble benign pigmented conditions, the diagnosis of this malignancy is frequently delayed until advanced stages. This diagnostic challenge is particularly evident in lesions of the tongue, where clinical experience is limited and access to non-invasive diagnostic tools is restricted.

The tongue is a rare primary site for OMM. A recent scoping review identified only 47 reported cases of tongue-origin malignant melanoma in the English-language literature,4 underscoring the rarity of this condition. In contrast to cutaneous melanoma, the patterns of regional spread in oral mucosal melanoma have not been fully characterised. In-transit metastasis, defined as discontinuous metastatic deposits between the primary tumour and regional lymph node basin, is a well-recognised feature of cutaneous melanoma but has not been clearly documented in OMM.5

From a pathological perspective, the rich lymphatic drainage of the tongue may facilitate locoregional spread via lymphatic routes, as demonstrated by reports of lingual lymph node involvement in carcinoma of the tongue, which reflect the potential for tumour cells to disseminate through this network.10 In tongue squamous cell carcinoma, metastasis to the lingual lymph nodes and intramuscular lymphatic spread have been reported, supporting the plausibility of a similar mechanism in OMM. Although direct evidence remains limited, the spatial distribution of the lesions in this case suggests a lymphatic spread rather than multifocal primary disease. Therefore, the present case is notable for demonstrating a second intralingual lesion clinically suggestive of possible intralingual in-transit metastasis, representing a potential pattern of lymphatic spread. However, the proposed metastatic mechanism remains speculative and cannot be definitively established based on a single case. Additional cases and multicentre studies are required to validate this observation and further elucidate the biological behaviour and metastatic pathways of oral malignant melanoma.

Clinically, both lesions in this patient closely resembled benign conditions, including black hairy tongue. The anterior lesion was pedunculated and smooth-surfaced, features that typically suggest a benign lesion, while the lesion at the base of the tongue showed a flat pigmented appearance with an intact mucosal surface. These findings highlight the limitations of visual inspection alone in the oral cavity. Unlike cutaneous melanoma, dermoscopic evaluation of oral mucosal lesions is less well characterised and not widely adopted in routine practice, and imaging modalities such as positron emission tomography may fail to detect superficial mucosal disease or flat lesions.11 12 Consequently, histopathological examination remains essential for a definitive diagnosis.

There are no established treatment guidelines specific to OMM. In the present case, treatment planning was guided by multidisciplinary tumour board assessment and available evidence from cutaneous melanoma management. No clinical or radiological evidence of cervical lymph node involvement was identified; therefore, elective neck dissection and sentinel lymph node biopsy were not performed. Nevertheless, because of the known risk of occult nodal metastasis, meticulous long-term follow-up with regular imaging remains essential. Adjuvant pembrolizumab was selected based on its demonstrated benefit in high-risk cutaneous melanoma and its use in ongoing clinical studies, including those conducted by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology for cutaneous melanoma,7 which guided our treatment approach. The patient remained disease-free during follow-up following surgical resection and adjuvant pembrolizumab. However, this favourable outcome cannot be attributed to surgery or immunotherapy alone and may instead reflect the benefit of a multimodal treatment approach. Given the single-case nature of this report and the limited follow-up period, the optimal role of adjuvant immunotherapy and its contribution to outcomes in oral mucosal melanoma remain uncertain and warrant further investigation.

This case emphasises several important clinical lessons. Pigmented lesions of the tongue, even when clinically suggestive of benign conditions, warrant careful evaluation, and early biopsy should be considered. An awareness of atypical metastatic patterns, including possible in-transit metastasis, is essential for accurate staging and management. Further accumulation and analysis of similar cases are required to better define the biological behaviour, metastatic pathways, and optimal treatment strategies for OMM of the tongue. In addition, functional rehabilitation following glossectomy is an important component of patient management. Palatal augmentation prostheses (PAP) have been reported to improve speech intelligibility and swallowing function in patients with reduced tongue mobility after glossectomy. Previous studies and reviews have demonstrated that PAP facilitates tongue–palate contact and improves bolus propulsion during swallowing, contributing to functional recovery and improved quality of life.13 Furthermore, multidisciplinary rehabilitation strategies are recommended for patients treated for head and neck cancer to optimise postoperative speech and swallowing outcomes.14

Patient’s perspective

I was not aware of any problem with my tongue and experienced no pain or discomfort. The lesions were discovered incidentally during a routine follow-up for my liver condition, which came as a surprise to me. Being told that I might have a malignant disease in my mouth caused significant anxiety, as I had always thought the discoloration was harmless.

After undergoing biopsy and surgery, I was relieved to learn that the tumors were completely removed. Although the treatment process, including surgery and additional therapy, was physically and mentally challenging, I felt well supported throughout. Using the palatal augmentation prosthesis after surgery helped me adapt to the changes in tongue movement, and I was able to resume eating without major difficulty.
