Removal of aging microenvironment of bone defects can effectively promote bone defect repair
Authors: Junjie Huang, Hong Long, Muhammad Adil Malik, Song Wu, Xu Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBone defects remain a significant challenge in orthopedics, and despite the widespread use of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in regenerative medicine, their therapeutic performance in vivo often falls short of expectations. Emerging evidence suggests that the local microenvironment particularly age-related or injury-induced cellular senescence may compromise MSC function. In this study, we investigated how a senescent vascular niche arising after bone injury influences MSC proliferation, differentiation, and its possible mechanisms involved in regeneration and clearing of the aging microenvironment.MethodsA femoral trochlear defect was created in 3-month-old SD rats (n = 6/group) to characterize temporal senescence changes using SA-β-Gal staining, p16/CD31 immunofluorescence, and expression of Cdkn1a/Cdkn2a. Primary endothelial cells (ECs) were isolated and senescence-induced with 400 µM H₂O₂ for 45 min. MSCs were co-cultured with senescent ECs (SnECs) in 3D collagen I hydrogels (2 mg/mL, ~ 5 kPa). Quercetin, selected from DrugAge screening (20 µM), was incorporated into a 4 wt% thermosensitive hydrogel (TSH-Q) to enable 7-day sustained release. Bone regeneration was assessed by µCT, histology, and immunofluorescence at 1 and 4 weeks post-injury.ResultsBone defects triggered a biphasic senescence response: early senescence occurred predominantly in peri-defect osteocytes at 1 week, while robust senescence was later observed in neovascular endothelial cells by week 4. SnECs significantly impaired MSC biological functions, reducing migration, chondrogenic differentiation (Safranin O intensity), and mineralization (Alizarin Red) (all p < 0.01). Local delivery of quercetin via TSH-Q cleared approximately 81% of p16⁺ endothelial cells in vivo and enhanced bone repair, increasing BV/TV compared with unloaded TSH (p < 0.001). In the early stage of bone defects, aging cells mainly represent bone cells in the tissues surrounding the bone defect. At later staged, with no changes in the microenvironment, the aging of vascular endothelial cells in the new tissues and blood vessels was most significant. We successfully induced endothelial cell senescence and further explored the functional impact of SnECs on MSCs and found that aging ECs led to a decline in MSCs effects in aging, including reduced proliferation, chondrogenic differentiation, osteogenic differentiation, tissue repair, and mineralization. The therapeutic effects of MSCs and the repair of bone defects were effectively promoted by constructing a quercetin thermosensitive hydrogel sustained-release system to improve the aging microenvironment of bone defects.ConclusionsBone injury generates a senescent vascular niche that markedly disrupts MSC-mediated regeneration. Targeted rejuvenation of this niche using sustained-release quercetin effectively restores MSC function and significantly accelerates bone reconstruction. These findings highlight the aging microenvironment as a key therapeutic target for improving MSC-based treatments for bone defects.

Introduction

Bone defects often occur in orthopedics. At present, the method of treating such defects is to transplant autologous bone to local defects to promote bone healing. However, bone transplantation is limited in quality and quantity. Bone harvesting can increase the risk of infection and cause persistent local pain and disability [1]. However, osteochondral injuries and defects are also common pathological changes observed in osteoarthritis (OA).

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are mesoderm-derived progenitor cells that can differentiate into osteocytes, chondrocytes, and adipocytes when stimulated under appropriate conditions [2]. Due to this property of MSC, it has been widely used in the field of tissue regeneration, such as bone regeneration [3]. MSC as a type of cell with multidirectional differentiation ability and is considered a potential treatment for tissue damage and repair. In healthy conditions, adult MSCs are initially considered quiescent until activation by tissue damage, such as following a pathological trigger, which induces proliferation and/or migration out of their niche, and eventually fosters tissue regeneration in a paracrine fashion or by differentiation to replace damaged cells [4–8].

Although MSC treatment has achieved exciting results, it still faces many problems. Therefore, paying attention to the microenvironment when a bone defect occurs and its influence on the differentiation of MSCs for the further development of MSC therapy is important. The function of stem cells is influenced by the tissue microenvironment. Specifically, the local tissue microenvironment can dictate how stem cells integrate into the existing tissue matrix and how successfully they can restore function to the damaged area in question [9]. Endothelial cells play a very important role in tissue repair, especially bone tissue repair [10–11]. In the bone marrow, endothelial cells (ECs) are a principal mediator of regulatory signals that govern hematopoietic and mesenchymal stem cells. EC and osteoblast interactions and niche functions of ECs are fundamental in maintaining bone health and coordinating repair and regeneration following injury [12]. In previous studies we evaluated the effects of IR-induced intra-articular senescent microenvironment (IASM) on the viability, proliferation, and differentiation of transplanted MSCs. IASM could reduce the viability, proliferation, and chondrogenic differentiation of transplanted MSCs [13].

In this study, we report for the first time that a simple defect can induce an aging microenvironment. We evaluated the influence of the aging microenvironment on MSC differentiation, especially osteogenic differentiation and mineralization, and its possible mechanism. At the same time, we explored whether the therapeutic effect of MSC in vivo can be effectively improved after the aging microenvironment is cleared.

Materials and methods

Animal studies

SD rats, aged 3 months old, were purchased from the Department of Laboratory Animals of Central South University (Changsha, China). All rats were maintained in a specific pathogen-free environment (SPF) with free access to water and food under the standard 12 h-light/dark cycles at 22–24.5 °C. A bone defect of 2 mm in diameter and 3.5 mm in depth was made in the middle of the right femoral trochlea using a punch.

Isolation and culture of BMSC

BMSCs were first isolated from the bone marrow (BM) of the tibia and femur of SD rats (aged 4 weeks old, male) as previously described previously [14]. The MSCs were then pooled and cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C. The medium was replaced every 3 days and cells were cultured until 80% confluence was achieved. The MSCs of passage 3 were used for the flow experiments.

Isolation and culture of vascular endothelial cells

SD rats (4 weeks old, male) were used for the extraction of primary vascular endothelial cells (EC). Rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium and then the thoracic aorta was removed. After cleaning with PBS twice, the artery was excised and cut to a length of about 1 cm and the endothelial surface was placed on a culture plate. After about 7 days of culture, the vascular endothelium was removed, and primary endothelial cells were obtained. The anti-CD31 antibody (AiFang Biological, China) was used to identify ECs, and ECs from passage 3 were used in subsequent experiments.

Induction of senescent energetic couloirs

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was used for senescence induction of Senescent Energetic Couloirs (SnECs), ECs were suspended in PBS at 1 × 106 cells/mL density of 1 × 106 cells/mL and exposed to 400 µM H2O2 at 37 °C for 45 min. After treatment, the media of both control and H2O2 treated cells were changed every 3 days.

Senescence assessment

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), western blotting, the EdU assay for cell proliferation detection, SA-β-Gal staining and immunohistochemistry were used for the assessment of tissue and cell senescence.

RT-qPCR was used to detect the expression of senescence markers (Cdkn1a and Cdkn2a) in the defected knee joint and to evaluate the efficacy of five anti-aging drugs. For the animal experiment, total RNA was extracted from the distal femur of rats at 1 week and 1 month after modeling for detection, normal rats acted as control. For the cell experiment, total cell mRNA was collected after evaluation of five anti-aging drugs. RT-qPCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. An initial denaturation step was carried out 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 56 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. The primer sequences are shown in Table 1. Gene transcription levels were normalized to those of β-actin and calculated using the log2 -Ct method (Table 1).

Western blotting was used to evaluate the efficacy of five anti-aging drugs. Total cell protein was collected after treatments. The protein extracts were subjected to SDS-PAGE and the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes and blocked using a blocking buffer (5% skim milk) for 1 h. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-p16 primary antibodies anti-p16 (Abcam, USA) and GAPDH. The following day, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. After being treated with Pierce ECL reagent, protein bands were imaged and protein levels normalized to β-actin levels.

EdU staining

EdU staining was used to measure the proliferation of ECs, SnECs, and MSCs. The proliferating cells were evaluated using the Click-iT Plus EdU Alexa Fluor 488 imaging kit (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342 (Invitrogen, USA). Following the removal of the working solution, images of three random fields from each group were acquired using an optical microscope. Semi-quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software (v1.54, NIH, USA): the number of EdU-positive (green fluorescent) nuclei and total Hoechst-stained nuclei were counted in each field, and the EdU-positive rate was expressed as the percentage of EdU-positive cells relative to total cells counted.

SA-β-Gal staining

ECs, SnECs and MSCs were stained with a senescence detection kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol as described previously [13]. Briefly, the cells were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed using SA-β-gal fixative for 15 minutes at room temperature. Subsequently, they were incubated overnight with the SA-β-gal working solution at 37℃ under a CO₂-free atmosphere. Following the removal of the working solution, images of three random fields from each group were acquired using an optical microscope. The proportion of β-gal-positive cells was then determined using ImageJ software (v1.54, NIH, USA). For each group, blue β-gal-positive cells and total cells were counted across three randomly selected fields, and the senescent cell rate was expressed as the percentage of positive cells relative to the total cell count.”

Cellular immunohistochemistry

ECs, SnECs, and MSCs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, then cells were incubated with rabbit polyclonal anti-p16 antibodies (Abcam, USA) antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The cells were then incubated with a secondary antibody for 1 h. Secondary antibodies were detected using an SABC-POD kit (Boster, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following the removal of the working solution, images of three random fields from each group were acquired using an optical microscope. The proportion of p16-positive cells was quantified using ImageJ software (v1.54, NIH, USA) by counting DAB-stained (brown) positive cells against the total number of cells across three randomly selected fields per group, and expressed as a percentage.

Cell migration assay

The migration capacity of MSCs was detected by cell migration assays as previously described [13], 6-well Transwell plates with polycarbonate membranes with an 8-µm pore size (Corning, USA) were used. Following the migration period, the cells that had successfully migrated to the lower surface of the membrane were fixed and stained with crystal violet. The stained cells were then imaged under a light microscope. The number of migrated cells was quantified by counting the crystal violet-positive cells from several randomly selected fields using ImageJ software.

Chondrogenic differentiation assay of MSCs

MSCs were cultured in chondrocyte differentiation medium for 14 days. Safranine O and Alcian Blue staining were used to evaluate the chondrogenesis of MSC according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For immunohistochemical evaluation, MSCs were fixed incubated with rabbit polyclonal anti-COL II (Abcam, USA) antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Cells were then incubated with a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody and DAPI for nuclear staining. For semi-quantitative analysis of Safranin O and Alcian Blue staining, images were acquired from three randomly selected fields per group. The stained area fraction and mean optical density were measured using ImageJ software (v1.54, NIH, USA) with color thresholding applied to isolate the stained region. COL II immunofluorescence intensity was quantified as mean fluorescence intensity per cell from ≥ 50 cells per group.

Osteogenic differentiation assay of MSC

The MSCs were cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium for 14 days. Alizarin red and ALP staining was used to assess the osteogenicity of MSC according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For immunohistochemical evaluation, MSCs were fixed and incubated with rabbit polyclonal anti-COL I antibodies (Abcam, USA) overnight at 4 °C. Cells were then incubated with a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody and DAPI for nuclear staining. For semi-quantitative analysis of Alizarin Red and ALP staining, the stained area fraction was measured from three randomly selected fields using ImageJ software (v1.54, NIH, USA) with color-based thresholding. COL I immunofluorescence intensity was expressed as mean fluorescence intensity per cell.

Cell scratch assay

The cell scratch assay was performed after 24 h and 48 h to determine the migration ability of ECs and SnECs. Briefly, a confluent cell layer in a 6-well plate was scratched with a 200 µL pipette tip and the cell layer was washed with PBS to remove cell debris. Cells were cultured in serum-free medium and imaged at 0, 24, and 48 h after scratching. The wound closure area was measured at 0, 24, and 48 h using ImageJ software (v1.54, NIH, USA) by calculating the cell-free scratch area in each image. The migration rate was expressed as: Migration (%) = (Area at 0 h − Area at T h) / Area at 0 h × 100.

Tube formation assay

The tube formation assay was used to determine the tube formation abilities of ECs and SnECs. Matrigel was diluted with complete medium (1: 1) and 50 µL diluted Matrigel was added to 96-well plates and incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes. Then 1 × 104 ECs were seeded in each well and imaged by microscopy (Leica) after 24 hours of culture. Image J software (v1.54, NIH, USA) with the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin (Gilles Carpentier, 2012) was used for quantitative analysis. The following parameters were measured per field: total tube length (µm), number of junctions, and number of branching points. Values represent the mean of three independent fields per well.”

Anti-aging drug screening

Drug Age (http://genomics.senescence.info/drugs/), a curated database of life-extending drugs and compounds, was used to screen anti-aging drugs. We queried 3270 items of anti-aging drug information in total, of which 2064 results (hits) were produced that defined drugs with anti-aging effects. Finally, we selected the top 5 most used and effective drugs for the follow-up validation of this study: resveratrol (57 hits), quercetin (34 hits), rapamycin (32 hits), metformin (29 hits), and caffeine (28 hits).

In vitro release of quercetin-loaded Temperature-Sensitive Hydrogel (TSH-Q)

Aqueous solutions of Temperature-Sensitive Hydrogel (TSH) (1.5-5 wt%) were prepared at 4 °C. The sol-gel transition of TSH was determined using the test tube inverting method. In this study, solutions of TSH at 4 wt%, which has a transition temperature at 25 °C were used.

The quercetin TSH (TSH-Q) suspension was prepared in PBS at 4 °C and mixed with TSH. Then TSH-Q was gelated at 37 °C in a 96-well plate. Prewarmed PBS was added to each well and replaced at predetermined time intervals (baseline, 6 h, 12 h, 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, and 14 days). At each sampling time point, an aliquot of the supernatant (50 µL) was mixed with ethanol (100%, 50 µL) and the absorbance was measured at 380 nm.

AM/PI staining

AM/PI staining was used to evaluate the toxicity of five anti-aging drugs. After incubation with 5 drugs, cells were gently washed with PBS 3 times, 100 µL of dye solution was added to each well (both 5 µL of PI solution and 5 µL of calcein AM solution were added to 10 mL of PBS as the working solution) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After incubation at 37 °C for 15–30 min, cells were observed and photographed using a fluorescent microscope. The living cell nucleus emits green fluorescence, whereas the dead cell nucleus emits red fluorescence.

Histological evaluation

Joint samples were collected and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, decalcified in 10% EDTA for 4 weeks, embedded in paraffin, and cut into Sect. (5 μm). For immunohistochemistry, the slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen repair, and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin. The slides were then incubated with rabbit polyclonal anti-COLI and anti-COLII (Affinity, China) antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The slides were then incubated with a secondary antibody for 1 h. Secondary antibodies were detected using an SABC-POD kit (Boster, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Furthermore, the rat knee joint slices were also stained with HE stains, safranin O/fast green stain (G-Clone, China) stain, and TRAP stain according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Fluorescent co-localization staining

Rat samples were fixed with paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and cut into paraffinized sections for immunofluorescence to detect the p16 (senescence marker) and CD31(ECs marker) (BioGems, USA), and DAPI was used for nuclear staining.

Micro- computed tomography measurements

After 1 and 4 weeks of healing, the rats were euthanized, and tissue blocks were harvested. The samples were processed and scanned with a PE Spectrum CT. After scanning, three-dimensional (3D) image models were reconstructed.

Statistical analysis

Values in text and figures are expressed as mean ± SD unless otherwise noted. Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS 17.0 (SPSS, Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Differences with a p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

This study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of our institution (No: 2021-S170), and a signed written consent form was obtained from all study subjects. All experiments involving animals were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the IRB of the Third Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (No: 2021-S170). Each sample was processed after receiving a signed informed consent form.

Results

Appearance of bone defects induced an aging microenvironment

To confirm the senescence of the bone defect-rat model, we collected knee joints from rats at 1 week and 1 month after bone defect modeling and detected the expression of the aging markers Cdkn1a and Cdkn2a by RT-qPCR. At 1 week, the expression of aging markers in the bone defect model increased with statistical significance, and the difference was more evident at 1 month (Fig. 1A). Further SA-β-Gal staining showed that at one week, there was no obvious tissue filling in the bone defect, and the senescent cells were distributed in the bone tissue surrounding the defect (Fig. 1B). At 1 month after the bone defect, the degree of aging of the bone defect was further aggravated. During this period, there were many new tissues and blood vessels in the bone defect. SA-β-Gal staining indicated that the new tissues did not have any obvious signs of senescence meanwhile. Further p16 (senescence marker) and CD31 (vascular endothelial cell marker) immunofluorescence co localization staining results showed that the vascular endothelial cells in the new tissue showed obvious senescence (Fig. 1C).

Evaluation of induction and function of senescent endothelial cells

To evaluate the effects of endothelial cell aging on neovascularization, we first isolated and cultured primary vascular endothelial cells (CD31 positive, Fig. 2A), and then used H2O2 to induce vascular endothelial cells to produce the aging phenotype. The phenotypic identification of ECs senescence was determined by SA-β-Gal staining (Fig. 2B) and immunohistochemistry of the senescence marker p16 (Fig. 2C). Then we further evaluated the proliferative capacity of aging endothelial cells using the EdU proliferation assay (Fig. 2D) and cell migration capacity using the scratch test (Fig. 2E). The results showed that the proliferative capacity of aging endothelial cells was significantly reduced, and the vascular endothelial cell tube formation test (Fig. 2F) showed that the angiogenic capacity of aging endothelial cells was significantly reduced.

At the same time, senescent vascular endothelial cells showed significantly reduced proliferative capacity and angiogenic capacity, indicating that vascular endothelial cell senescence had contributed to significant dysfunction.

Effect of aging vascular endothelial cells on MSC function

The repair of a bone defect is closely related to the differentiation of MSC. Therefore, we further comprehensively evaluated the effects of aging vascular endothelial cells on bone marrow MSC function. First, we evaluated the effects of aging vascular endothelial cells on MSC senescence. Senescent vascular endothelial cells can significantly induce the senescence of bone marrow MSCs. The proportion of SA-β-Gal positive cells was significantly increased in the SnECs-MSCs group (Fig. 3A). MSCs co-cultured with SnECs also showed a higher expression of p16 (Fig. 3B). SnECs inhibited the cell migration ability of MSC (Fig. 3C) and significantly influenced the differentiation ability of MSC (Fig. 3D, E). SnECs inhibited the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs, specifically the expression of COL I in MSCs (Fig. 3D). At the same time, alizarin red staining showed that SnECs could also affect the mineralization capacity of MSCs, leading to a reduction in calcium nodule formation (Fig. 3E).

Efficacy of five anti-aging drugs and construction of their sustained-release system

Through query and analysis of the aging database (http://genomics.senescence.info/drugs/), we identified five different anti-aging drugs that are widely used and have demonstrated obvious effects: resveratrol, quercetin, rapamycin, metformin, and caffeine (Fig. 4A). We first verified the anti-aging effects of these five drugs, and the results showed that these five drugs all showed anti-aging effects at the recommended concentration, of which resveratrol and quercetin had the most significant anti-aging effects (Fig. 4B), which were also supported by evidence from western blotting experiments (Fig. 4C). Then we evaluated the cytotoxicity of these five drugs and their effects on cell proliferation using cell death staining (Fig. 4D) and EdU staining (Fig. 4E). The results showed that quercetin had relatively low cytotoxicity and obvious effects on cell proliferation compared to the other 4 drugs. We used the vial inversion test to investigate the sol-gel transition of TSH (Fig. 4F) at 25 °C. The release profile of the TSH-Q is shown in Fig. 4F. Approximately 37.67% of quercetin was rapidly released on the first day and about 83.48% was cumulatively released over 14 days, with a relatively smooth sustained release trend.

Removal of the aging microenvironment effectively improved the therapeutic effects of MSC

Previous results showed that SnECs had a significant impact on the osteogenic differentiation and mineralization of MSCs. Thus, we used the TSH-Q sustained-release system to systematically evaluate the effects of aging on MSC chondrogenesis and osteogenic differentiation. We first evaluated the effect of senescence on MSC differentiation through a cell experiment in vitro. Safranine O staining (Fig. 5A), alcian blue staining (Fig. 5B), and cellular immunofluorescence of COL II (Fig. 5C) were used to evaluate chondrogenic differentiation of MSC. The culture medium from SnECs significantly inhibited the chondrogenic differentiation of MSC, and quercetin significantly reversed this phenomenon and restored the chondrogenic differentiation ability of MSC. Cellular immunofluorescence of COL I (Fig. 5D), alizarin red staining (Fig. 5E), and ALP staining (Fig. 5F) were used to evaluate chondrogenic differentiation of MSC. The culture medium from SnECs can significantly inhibit the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs, and quercetin can significantly reverse this phenomenon and restore the osteogenic differentiation and mineralization ability of MSC.

To further evaluate the effects of aging on bone defect repair and the effectiveness of the TSH-Q sustained-release system, we induced a bone defect model in rats. SA-β-Gal staining results showed that the slow-release TSH-Q system could significantly remove the aging microenvironment surrounding the bone defect. At the same time, further HE, safranine O staining, and Masson staining showed that after the removal of the aging microenvironment, there was relatively more tissue filling at the defect within 1 week, but most consisted of fiber filling. Discussion

Cellular senescence induces highly stable cell cycle arrest triggered by various stresses. Beyond halting proliferation, senescent cells undergo extensive metabolic, structural, and epigenetic alterations that reshape their behavior and their influence on surrounding tissues. A central feature of senescence is the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), a complex mixture of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, proteases, and extracellular vesicles that mediate most of the non–cell-autonomous effects of senescent cells [15]. These changes are largely driven by persistent macromolecular damage, activation of p53/p21CIP1 and p16INK4a/RB tumor-suppressor pathways, and increased lysosomal content reflected in strengthened SA-β-Gal activity, one of the most widely used senescence markers [16]. In our study, SA-β-Gal staining and p16 immunofluorescence were used to assess the extent of endothelial senescence in bone defect tissues(Fig. 1B, C) [17].

Selecting an appropriate aging model is essential for mechanistic research. Multiple approaches exist to induce senescence, including replicative exhaustion through serial passaging, ionizing radiation, and oxidative stress. Replicative senescence in vitro is induced quite easily by simply passaging cells in culture. Due to constant telomere shortening, senescent cells can be obtained in a few days or weeks, depending on the cell type and passage [18]. The inducing factor most used is radiation, which can induce accelerated senescence in both cancer and normal non-malignant cells [19–20]. Radiation can induce accelerated senescence at lower doses and apoptosis at higher doses, demonstrating the importance of dose selection. Lower doses (0.5–10 Gy) cause senescence, while higher doses (> 10 Gy) can induce cell apoptosis. These responses depend on the severity of the radiation-induced DNA damage and DNA damage response pathway activity [21]. In addition, the effects of fractionated radiation can differ from those of the same quantity of radiation delivered in a single dose. For example, low-dose fractionated radiation-induced the p53/p21 pathway, resulting in a more significant senescence phenotype than a single higher dose even though the same total dose administered was identical [22]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), especially hydrogen peroxide H2O2, represent another widely used and controllable method of generating senescence through oxidative damage [23] that can cause senescence in human and rodent cells [24–25]. In this study, we successfully induced endothelial senescence using H₂O₂, establishing a reliable in vitro SnEC model for subsequent co-culture experiments. (Fig. 2B). In addition, other factors that can cause changes in chromium architecture, mitochondrial dysfunction, mitotic stress, and proteotoxicity can cause cell senescence [26–28].

Age-associated endothelial dysfunction is a key contributor to many chronic diseases including impaired wound healing, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, neurodegenerative disorders, and renal disease. [29–30]. Senescent endothelial cells show altered gene expression, reduced proliferative potential, impaired angiogenic capacity, and structural changes that destabilize vessel integrity [31].

Senescence has traditionally been considered as a defined static cell fate. However, it is now recognized that senescence is a multi-step dynamic process [32]. Senescent cells can signal and influence their surrounding environment. Senescent cells produce a complex mixture of soluble and insoluble factors that are collectively termed the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [33–34]. Senescent cells can affect the function of other cells through SASP, including cytokines and inflammatory factors, or exosomes [13, 35]. In line with this, we observed that SnECs significantly impaired MSC function. Through SASP-related signals, SnECs diminished MSC proliferation, migration, chondrogenic differentiation, and mineralization (Figs. 3 and 5), suggesting that endothelial aging disrupts the regenerative network required for effective bone repair.

MSCs, originally characterized in the 1970s and later standardized by the ISCT, remain a cornerstone of regenerative medicine [36] due to their ability to differentiate into osteogenic, chondrogenic, and adipogenic lineages [37]. Their therapeutic benefits stem from three major mechanisms: (1) direct differentiation into repair-related lineages [38–40], (2) paracrine secretion of trophic and angiogenic factors [41–42],and (3) immunomodulation that reshapes inflammatory responses at injury sites [43]. In addition, MSCs can modulate the inflammatory microenvironment in the defect area and decrease the levels of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [44]. The MSC pathway is a potential strategy to overcome the dilemma of organization repair However, the normal function and phenotype of MSCs are easily affected by their microenvironment (Fig. 3). However, MSC behavior is highly sensitive to their microenvironment. Dysfunctional or stress-primed MSCs not only lose their regenerative capacity but may further exacerbate pathological conditions. Our data demonstrate that the senescent vascular microenvironment that emerges after bone injury profoundly disrupts MSC function, revealing a previously underappreciated barrier to MSC-based therapies [45]. Senolytics, such as dasatinib, quercetin, and the BCL-2 inhibitors ABT-263 and ABT-737, have emerged as a promising strategy for clearing senescent cells and rejuvenating tissue function. In this study, quercetin was selected based on database screening and experimental validation, and subsequently incorporated into a thermosensitive hydrogel for sustained release [46].

In this study, quercetin was selected as an anti-aging drug through database screening and validation, and a temperature sensitive hydrogel was used to construct a sustained-release preparation (Fig. 4), characterized by a good anti-aging effect in vivo and in vitro. Xing et al. designed a microenvironment-responsive quercetin-loaded hydrogel based on the MMPs secreted by senescent cells. This quercetin-encapsulated hydrogel can clear local senescent cells and reduce the secretion of MMPs in the bone and thus promote the repair of bone defects [47]. The research by Xing et al. mainly focuses effects on aging cells brought about by aging, while we observed a biphasic pattern, early senescence was dominated by peri-defect osteocytes, while later-stage senescence was most prominent in endothelial cells within newly formed vessels. Dandan Xing et al. [48] developed a straightforward preparation method of Que-loaded CS-OAlg injectable hydrogels for the scarless healing of burn wounds. CS was not required to be pre-dissolved with acetic acid but was directly mixed homogeneously with the OAlg/Que solution in powder form, and the formation of the hydrogel was triggered by the dropwise addition of acetic acid under rapid stirring. In the present study, we selected a thermosensitive hydrogel primarily as a common delivery vehicle to provide sustained release of quercetin, aiming to prolong its duration of action and better clear the local senescent microenvironment. While numerous studies have indeed indicated that the combination of dasatinib and quercetin (D + Q) may exhibit more potent senolytic effects compared to quercetin alone [49–51], the primary focus of our current work was to validate the presence and role of a specific senescent microenvironment surrounding the bone defect, and to investigate whether its clearance could facilitate bone repair. Subsequent research in our group will delve deeper into the mechanisms underlying the formation of this local senescent microenvironment in bone defects and explore more effective anti-senescence strategies, which may include combination therapies like D + Q.

In this study, we focused on the emergence of an aging microenvironment caused by bone defects. In the early stage of bone defect, the aging cells are mainly bone cells in the tissues surrounding the bone defect. In the later stage, without changing the microenvironment, aging of vascular endothelial cells in the new tissues and blood vessels is most significant (Fig. 1). We then successfully induced endothelial cell senescence (Fig. 2) and further explored the functional impact of SnECs on MSCs (Fig. 3). By targeting this injury-induced senescent niche, the TSH-Q system effectively cleared p16⁺ endothelial cells and improved MSC function (Figs. 4, 5 and 6), ultimately enhancing bone regeneration. These findings emphasize that the microenvironment created by bone injury can independently generate an aging phenotype one that must be addressed for MSC therapies to reach their full therapeutic potential.

Limitation and conclusion

This study has several limitations. Although we identified the emergence of a senescent microenvironment after bone injury, we did not fully explore the upstream triggers or the precise molecular mechanisms driving endothelial senescence in newly formed tissues. Furthermore, while H₂O₂ induction provides a well-established and controllable model of oxidative stress induced senescence, it cannot fully replicate the complexity of the in vivo aging microenvironment created by bone defects, which likely involves mechanical stress, hypoxia, inflammatory signals, and metabolic shifts. Our primary objective was to characterize the presence and impact of senescence in bone defects and to evaluate whether clearing this microenvironment could enhance MSC-mediated repair. However, deeper mechanistic studies on downstream signaling pathways influenced by senescence remain necessary.

Despite these limitations, our findings highlight a key concept: bone defects themselves can generate a localized senescent niche that restricts MSC function and impairs regeneration. Targeted removal of senescent endothelial cells using a sustained-release quercetin hydrogel significantly improved tissue repair. These results suggest that modulating the aging microenvironment is a promising strategy to enhance MSC-based therapies and accelerate bone healing.
