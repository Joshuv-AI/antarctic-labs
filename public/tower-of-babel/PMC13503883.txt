Abdominal massage improves intestinal barrier function via the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway in obesity-associated T2DM rats
Authors: Yiran Han, Chongwen Zhong, Zeyuan Lu, Mingjun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abdominal massage (AM), a traditional Chinese medicine treatment, is gradually being recognized in clinical practice for its ability to improve the symptoms of obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in clinical practice. This study aimed to investigate the effect of AM on an obesity-associated T2DM model induced by a high-fat diet (HFD) combined with a small dose of streptozotocin (STZ). Thirty male Sprague‒Dawley rats were randomly divided into three groups: control group (CON), the obesity-associated T2DM group (MOD), and AM group. After 4 weeks of HFD feeding and STZ injection, the rats received AM for 4 weeks. AM significantly improved glucose intolerance, obesity, and colonic inflammation and reduced fasting blood glucose and glycated serum protein levels. In addition, AM significantly ameliorated glucolipid metabolism abnormalities in obesity-associated T2DM rats, as evidenced by improved glucose tolerance and favorable alterations in lipid profiles. AM also attenuated both systemic and intestinal inflammation, accompanied by a marked reduction in circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines. In summary, our results suggest that AM exerts protective effects against obesity-associated T2DM by improving glucolipid metabolism, attenuating systemic and intestinal inflammation, and restoring intestinal barrier integrity via suppression of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway. Although long-term evaluations and multi-omics analyses are warranted to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms, these results provide a solid theoretical basis for exploring AM as a potential barrier-protecting therapeutic strategy for obesity-related metabolic diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57589-w.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia resulting from insulin resistance and progressive pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, representing one of the most prevalent and burdensome non-communicable diseases worldwide1. Epidemiological evidence indicates that obesity, particularly abdominal adiposity, is among the most critical modifiable risk factors for T2DM development, with obese individuals carrying substantially elevated risk compared to their lean counterparts2,3. The global burden of T2DM continues to escalate at an alarming rate; the International Diabetes Federation estimated that approximately 537 million adults were living with diabetes in 2021, with projections suggesting this figure will rise to 783 million by 20453. T Beyond its direct metabolic consequences, T2DM is associated with heightened susceptibility to infectious diseases4 and imposes substantial clinical and socioeconomic burdens through its multisystemic complications, including cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, neuropathy, and retinopathy.

Current management strategies for T2DM encompass pharmacological interventions, dietary modification, and lifestyle adjustment5–7. However, long-term adherence to conventional pharmacotherapy is frequently compromised by adverse effects, high costs, and patient non-compliance5,6. Dietary self-management, while effective, remains challenging to sustain, particularly as disease duration increases7. These limitations underscore the urgent need for complementary and integrative therapeutic approaches that are safe, effective, and accessible.

Emerging evidence implicates intestinal barrier dysfunction as a pivotal pathophysiological mechanism linking metabolic disturbance with systemic inflammation in T2DM8–11. The intestinal epithelial barrier, maintained principally by tight junction (TJ) proteins including Occludin, Claudin-1, and Zonula Occludens-1 (ZO-1), serves as a critical interface regulating paracellular permeability and preventing the translocation of luminal antigens into the systemic circulation12,13. Disruption of TJ integrity under high-fat diet (HFD) conditions facilitates the translocation of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and other microbial products, thereby activating Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated pro-inflammatory signaling cascades that exacerbate insulin resistance and β-cell impairment8,10,11,13. Hyperglycemia itself further compromises intestinal barrier integrity through direct epithelial toxicity, establishing a self-perpetuating cycle of metabolic and inflammatory dysregulation13.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) therapies, including acupuncture and Tuina (Chinese therapeutic massage), have demonstrated promising efficacy in ameliorating glucolipid metabolism disorders associated with T2DM14–17. Our group preliminarily demonstrated that abdominal massage (AM), a core component of Tuina practice targeting the abdominal region, We have been reported to attenuate insulin resistance through modulation of the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling pathway14–16. Acupuncture at specific acupoints has similarly shown capacity to regulate glycemic control and metabolic biomarkers in both clinical and experimental settings17–19. Data mining analyses of acupuncture prescription patterns for gastrointestinal disorders have identified ST25 (Tianshu) and ST36 (Zusanli) as among the most frequently employed acupoints, reflecting their recognized roles in regulating gastrointestinal function and systemic metabolism20. Notably, acupuncture-based interventions have been shown to modulate intestinal barrier integrity and suppress mucosal inflammatory responses in animal models of gastrointestinal dysfunction21. Nevertheless, whether the combination of AM with acupoint-specific intervention can synergistically target the intestinal mucosal barrier to ameliorate T2DM-associated metabolic dysregulation remains insufficiently characterized22.

Therefore, the present study employed a HFD combined with low-dose streptozotocin (STZ) rat model of T2DM23 to investigate the therapeutic effects of AM at ST25, ST36, and SP6 on glycolipid metabolism and intestinal barrier function, with the aim of elucidating the underlying mechanisms through which TCM manual therapies may confer metabolic benefits in T2DM.

Animals and models

Eight-week-old male Sprague‒Dawley rats weighing 220 g±10 g were used for the study. The rats were housed at Changchun University of Chinese Medicine, Changchun, China, with free access to prescribed food and water under standard conditions (24 °C, 50% relative humidity, and a 12-light‒dark cycle). After a one-week adaptation period, the rats were randomly divided into a normal-fat diet group (NF, n = 10) and a high-fat diet group (HFD, n = 20). The NF group rats received a normal-fat diet, while the HFD group rats were fed a HFD consisting of 70% carbohydrate, 20% protein, and 10% fat, with additional components (ordinary feed: egg yolk powder: lard: sucrose: cholesterol: sodium taurocholate at a 10:1:1:1:0.1:0.05 ratio). Both types of feed were purchased from Changchun Yisi Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the laboratory animal care and use guidelines of the National Institutes of Health and approved by the Ethics Committee of Changchun University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 2024174). All experiments were performed in accordance ARRIVE guidelines.

After 4 weeks of high-fat feeding, rats exhibiting a body weight ≥ 20% higher than the average body weight of the NF group were initially confirmed as successful diet-induced obesity models24. Subsequently, rats were fasted for 12 h. The following day, 10 NF rats received an intraperitoneal injection of citric acid-sodium citrate buffer as blank control, while HFD rats received an intraperitoneal injection of STZ solution at 32 mg/kg STZ induce a T2DM model. The specific STZ dose of 32 mg/kg was selected based on our team’s established modeling protocol22 and further confirmed by a preliminary dose-finding pilot experiment (30, 32, and 35 mg/kg; see Supplementary Table S1 for details), which identified 32 mg/kg as the optimal dose providing the highest modeling success rate with satisfactory animal survival under our experimental conditions.

Blood glucose levels were measured 72 h and 7 d after injection. Rats with random blood glucose levels > 16.7 mmol/L after 7 d were initially considered to be successfully established23. Following a one-week observation period to monitor blood glucose stability, levels were re-measured, and rats exhibiting spontaneous blood glucose recovery (regression) were excluded from the study. The remaining successfully modeled rats then underwent an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) to definitively confirm impaired glucose tolerance relative to the blank control group.

Eventually, 18 HFD rats demonstrated stable hyperglycemia and were randomly assigned to either the T2DM model (MOD)group or the acupoint massage (AM) group (n = 9 per group). The 10 NF rats served as the control (CON) group. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines for laboratory animal care and use and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Changchun University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 2024174). All experiments were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Treatment

Rats in the AM group received a structured manual therapy consisting of two sequential components: general abdominal kneading and rubbing (to relax superficial musculature and promote local circulation), followed by targeted thumb acupressure (point-pressing) applied to specific acupoints. The targeted acupoints included SP6 (Sanyinjiao), RN12 (Zhongwan), RN4 (Guanyuan), ST36 (Zusanli), and ST25 (Tianshu).

To ensure the rigor and reproducibility of the intervention, all human acupoints were systematically mapped to their corresponding anatomical locations in the rat model as illustrated in Supplementary Fig S1. Specifically, the precise anatomical locations of SP6, RN12, RN4 and ST36 were determined in accordance with the national standard Name and Location of Common Acupoints in Laboratory Animals—Part 2: Rat (Standard No. T/CAAM 002-2020), published by the China Association of Acupuncture-Moxibustion25. The location of ST25 was identified based on established rat acupoint mapping literature26. The thumb acupressure was applied to these acupoints sequentially rather than simultaneously; for bilateral acupoints (ST25, ST36, and SP6), the left and right sides were pressed simultaneously. Each acupoint was stimulated for 3 min, yielding a total acupressure duration of 15 min per session. The point-pressing was performed rhythmically at a frequency of 10 compressions per minute; each compression cycle lasted 6 s, comprising approximately 3 s of downward pressure followed by 3 s of gradual release.

Prior to treatment, the operator’s massage technique was standardized using a TPA-II massage measurement instrument (Yilian Medical Development Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) to ensure consistent and reproducible applied force throughout the intervention period. A standardized force of 0.5 kg (≈ 4.9 N) was applied to all acupoints. This parameter was selected based on rat physiology and established protocols from recent experimental Tuina and acupressure studies, which demonstrate that a force of 3–5 N is optimal for rat models27–29. Physiologically, ~ 4.9 N is sufficient to penetrate superficial tissue layers and activate deep subcutaneous and muscular structures required for effective acupoint stimulation. Rats were gently restrained in the supine position by a trained operator during the massage procedure. No anesthesia was used to avoid interference with the autonomic nervous system responses.

Importantly, this moderate force is well-tolerated by adult rats, remains below the nociceptive threshold, and thereby avoids nociceptive stress responses that could otherwise confound systemic inflammatory and metabolic evaluations.

The intervention lasted for 4 weeks, during which the body weight and food intake of were monitored weekly. At the end of the intervention period treatment, all rats were anesthetized via inhalation to minimize suffering, in strict accordance with animal ethics guidelines. Specifically, anesthesia was induced with 3 ~ 4% isoflurane mixed with 100% oxygen in an induction chamber and maintained at 1.5 ~ 2% isoflurane via a nose cone. Under deep anesthesia, blood samples were collected from the abdominal aorta. The rats were then immediately euthanized, and approximately 50 mg of colonic tissue was rapidly excised, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. Animal carcasses were stored at − 20 °C and uniformly disposed of in compliance with institutional biosafety regulations.

OGTT experiment

At the end of the final intervention, the rats were fasted for 12 h without water. The following day, after weighing, a 50% glucose solution was prepared and administered orally at a dose of 2 g/kg. Blood glucose levels from the rats’ tail veins were measured at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min (G₀, G₃₀, G₆₀ and G₁₂₀) post gavage to assess glucose sensitivity. Analyses of biochemical indicators

Fasting blood glucose levels were measured using an automatic blood glucose meter (AICARE, China). Serum levels of Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), Glycated Serum Protein (GSP), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were measured using a fully automated biochemical instrument (Wuhan Xavier Biotechnology Co.).

Pathological and morphological observation

Colon tissue samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated with gradient alcohol, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). The sections were then rendered transparent with xylene and sealed with neutral gum. Morphological changes in the colon tissue were observed under a light microscope, and photomicrographs were taken for analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

To further evaluate intestinal barrier integrity, immunohistochemical staining was performed on paraffin-embedded colon tissue sections. Briefly, Sect. (4 μm thickness) were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded alcohol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) for 15 min at room temperature, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody (dilution 1:200). After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), sections were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Negative controls were processed identically, with the primary antibody replaced by PBS. Stained sections were imaged under a light microscope, occludin, p65 and p-p65 expression was quantified using Aipathwell® software (Servicebio®, Wuhan, China), an artificial intelligence-based digital pathology image analysis platform. The percentage of positively stained area (%Area) and the average optical density (AOD) were measured from five randomly selected fields per section to assess the relative protein expression level.

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol® Reagent according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. RNA was reverse-transcribed using a reverse transcription kit for first-strand cDNA synthesis. Gene expression was detected using qPCR Master Mix (Servicebio, China) in a Stratagene MX3000P RT‒PCR System (Genetimes Technology, Inc., China). Western blot

Total protein was extracted using a mammalian protein extraction kit, and the protein concentration was determined using the BCA method. Proteins (30 µg) were electroblotted onto a PVDF membrane, followed by separation on an 8% SDS-polyacrylamide gel. The membrane was incubated with blocking solution (5% skim milk) for 2 h and then incubated overnight with a 1:500 dilution of antibodies (against TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB p65) at 4 °C. After washing, the membrane was incubated with a 1:2,000 dilution of horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. Following additional washes with Tris-buffered saline, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit and imaged on a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad, 1708370). Image Lab software (Bio-Rad) version 6.0 was used to analyze and semi quantify the blots.

Statistics

The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 18.0. Multiple group comparisons were conducted using ANOVA, and two-group comparisons were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Status of rat model preparation and OGTT experiments

Rats fed a normal diet exhibited a good mental state, smooth fur color, normal food and water intake, normal urine output, rapid body weight gain over time, and dry bedding. After 4 weeks of high-fat feeding and STZ injection, the rats displayed symptoms of depression, polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, dull expression, slow reaction, dull fur, slow body weight gain over time, and wet bedding that required daily changes.

Blood glucose levels were measured in tail vein samples, and Fig. 1 shows that the blood glucose levels of the model rats exceeded 16.7mmol/L. The OGTT indicated significantly elevated blood glucose levels in model rats at all time points compared to those in CON group, exceeding the baseline by more than 20%, confirming successful model induction. No rats died during the modeling period, resulting in a 90%-success rate, with 18 of 20 rats successfully modeling. Two rats were excluded from the study because they were assessed to have failed modeling.

AM improves glucolipid metabolism disorders in obesity-associated T2DM rats

Table 2 summarizes the weekly body weight measurements recorded throughout the intervention period. As shown in Table 2, MOD group exhibited significantly higher body weight compared with CON group (P < 0.05), whereas AM intervention significantly attenuated this increase (P < 0.05). Body weights in the AM group gradually decreased over time and were significantly lower than those in MOD group on day 28 (P < 0.01). The results of weekly random blood glucose observations are summarized in Table 2 and illustrated in Fig. 2a. By day 7 of the intervention, AM group showed a decreasing trend in blood glucose levels, and by day 28, these levels were significantly lower than those in MOD group (P < 0.05). At the conclusion of the intervention period, an OGTT was conducted as shown in Fig. 2b and c, MOD group exhibited significantly impaired glucose tolerance (P < 0.05), whereas AM group demonstrated significantly reduced blood glucose levels across all time points, accompanied by a notable decrease in the area under the curve (AUC). As shown in Fig. 2d and e, HbA1c and GSP levels were significantly elevated in MOD group compared with those in CON group (P < 0.05). Following AM intervention, serum GSP levels were significantly reduced in obesity-associated T2DM rats (P < 0.05), and HbA1c levels showed an improving trend, although the difference did not reach statistical significance compared with MOD group.

Furthermore, to evaluate the effect of AM on lipid metabolism, serum lipid profiles were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 2f–i, TC, TG, and LDL-C levels were significantly elevated, whereas HDL-C was markedly decreased in MOD group compared with CON group (P < 0.01). AM corrects peripheral blood immune inflammation in rats

Figure 3 shows that the serum levels of the proinflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-1β were significantly elevated (P < 0.05), suggesting a heightened state of inflammation in the model rats. Conversely, the level of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-4 was significantly decreased (P < 0.05), which indicated an evident state of elevated inflammation in the serum of model rats. This was evidenced by the detection of relevant inflammatory factors in the serum of the rats in each group. Furthermore, the plasma levels of LPS in obesity-associated T2DM rats were significantly elevated in MOD group (P < 0.05), indicating severe systemic LPS translocation. However, the AM intervention effectively alleviated this increase (P < 0.05).

AM alleviates intestinal mucosal injury and restores tight junction protein expression in obesity-associated T2DM rats.

HE staining (Fig. 4a) revealed that CON group exhibited intact colonic mucosal structure with well-arranged epithelial cells, abundant goblet cells, and no inflammatory cell infiltration. In contrast, MOD group displayed significant pathological alterations, including disrupted mucosal architecture, reduced goblet cells, prominent inflammatory cell infiltration, and submucosal edema. Compared with MOD group, CON and AM groups demonstrated varying degrees of mucosal structural restoration, with improved epithelial arrangement, increased goblet cell numbers, and attenuated inflammatory infiltration, with the AM group showing the most pronounced improvement.

Figure 5 shows the results of the western blot analysis of rat colon tissues, which demonstrated that AM effectively inhibited the activation of TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB p65 (Fig. 5a–d). These proteins are key players in inflammatory signaling pathways. Additionally, RT‒PCR analysis of colonic tissues (Fig. 5e, f) revealed significant decreases in the expression levels of downstream inflammatory factors, specifically lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) and CD14 mRNA, following AM intervention in obese T2DM rats. Besides, p65 and p-p65 expressions by immunohistochemistry were shown in Fig. 5g and h. Compared with MOD group, the %area and AOD of p65 and p-p65 were significantly reduced in AM group, especially in the cell nucleus (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01).

Discussion

In this study, AM significantly ameliorated obesity-associated T2DM in a rat model, as evidenced by improvements in glucolipid metabolism, systemic inflammation, intestinal barrier integrity, and activation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway. These findings provide mechanistic insights into the therapeutic potential of AM as a non-pharmacological intervention for metabolic disorders.

The clinical efficacy of AM, an important therapeutic approach within traditional Chinese medicine, has been supported by evidence showing that it regulates lipid metabolism abnormalities in individuals with obesity-associated T2DM, IR, and glucose metabolism abnormalities in individuals with T2DM17–19. Given the size of the rats, the limited operational area, and other practical constraints, only a subset of clinical prescriptions was selected for the animal experiments. Specifically, the ST25 (Tianshu), RN12 (Zhongwan), RN4 (Guanyuan), ST36 (Zusanli, anatomically referred to as Housanli in rats), and SP6 (Sanyinjiao) acupoints were targeted.

The rationale for this selection is deeply rooted in both traditional theory and modern mechanistic evidence. According to TCM theory, these acupoints are renowned for their ability to regulate Spleen and Stomach function, nourish Kidney Yin, and resolve the accumulation of metabolic ‘Dampness,’ thereby restoring Yin-Yang balance and promoting homeostasis. Furthermore, modern experimental evidence strongly links these specific points to metabolic and immune regulation.

Local abdominal acupoints (RN12, RN4, and ST25) directly overlie the gastrointestinal tract, while distal acupoints (ST36 and SP6) exert systemic neuromodulation. Recent data mining and systematic reviews have identified the specific combination of ST25 and ST36 as a core combinatorial prescription for gastrointestinal disorders20. Experimental models further confirm that stimulating ST25 and ST36 synergistically improves colonic intestinal barrier integrity and effectively suppresses the expression of inflammatory cytokines21. Additionally, evidence shows that stimulating SP6 directly targets metabolic dysfunction by significantly ameliorating glycolipid metabolism disorders in type 2 diabetic models via crucial metabolic signaling pathways30.

Therefore, this strategic combination of local enteric stimulation and systemic neuromodulation provides a robust physiological basis for the improvements in intestinal barrier integrity, LPS-driven endotoxemia and glucose metabolism observed in our study. It is worth noting that the current protocol employed a combined multi-acupoint stimulation strategy rather than isolating individual acupoints. While the synergistic combination of ST25, RN12, RN4, ST36, and SP6 was selected based on established TCM combinatorial theory and prior experimental evidence, the relative contribution and therapeutic superiority of each individual acupoint have yet to be determined. Future studies employing factorial experimental designs, in which each acupoint is stimulated independently and in various combinations, will be necessary to delineate the specific therapeutic role of each point and to identify the optimal acupoint prescription for obesity-associated T2DM.

The coexistence of obesity and T2DM perpetuates a pathological state characterized by chronically elevated circulating LPS levels. This, in turn, increases the expression of inflammatory factors in target organs, thereby driving long-term chronic inflammatory responses. In the present study, AM intervention improved T2DM-related dysglycemia, impaired glucose tolerance, dyslipidemia, and chronic peripheral blood inflammation. The underlying mechanism by which AM improves glucose metabolism is closely related to the alleviation of systemic low-grade inflammation and the subsequent restoration of insulin sensitivity30–32. In the pathogenesis of T2DM, chronic inflammation driven by gut-derived endotoxemia strongly impairs insulin receptor signaling, leading to decreased cellular glucose uptake13,33. By mechanically stimulating the abdomen, AM helps restore the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier (as evidenced by the upregulation of Occludin), which effectively prevents LPS from translocating into the systemic circulation. By blocking this ‘leaky gut’ pathway, AM significantly dampens colon-associated immune-inflammatory signaling and reduces systemic pro-inflammatory cytokines. This reduction in systemic inflammation restores insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues, which fundamentally explains the observed improvements in fasting blood glucose levels and oral glucose tolerance9,13. The robust improvement in lipid metabolism further supports the systemic benefits of AM, as correcting dyslipidemia is essential for breaking the vicious cycle of systemic inflammation and IR.

In the present study, an obesity-associated T2DM animal model was established using a HFD combined with intraperitoneal injection of low-dose STZ, which partially recapitulates the pathogenesis of obesity-associated T2DM in humans. After being fed a HFD for 4 weeks, the rats gained significant body weight, and within 7 days after the intraperitoneal injection of STZ (32 mg/kg), most of the rats exhibited polydipsia, polyphagia, polyuria, and elevated blood glucose. Following AM intervention, the rats demonstrated significant improvements in body weight, blood glucose levels, and serum lipid profiles (Tables 2 and 3; Fig. 2).

Obesity and T2DM can compromise the intestinal barrier and increase intestinal permeability. This may lead to changes in glycolipid metabolic pathways, allowing pathogens and metabolites to migrate from the gut into other tissues of the body and eventually into the circulation30. Alterations in intestinal morphology, increased intestinal permeability, and intestinal barrier damage have been reported to precede the onset of diabetes mellitus31. Furthermore, dysfunction in intestinal immune responses exacerbates intestinal inflammation and metabolic dysfunction (Fig. 4).

Chronic consumption of a high-fat, high-calorie diet has been associated with expansion of gram-negative bacteria in the intestinal tract; this shift in the microbial environment increases plasma LPS levels, a condition known as metabolic endotoxemia31. Although microbiota composition was not directly assessed in the present study, the observed elevation of plasma LPS in the model group is consistent with this well-established pathophysiological mechanism.

In this context, LPS serves as the primary ligand for TLR4. LPS binds to LBP and CD14, forming a trimeric complex that activates TLR4, thereby initiating downstream inflammatory signaling.

The chronic inflammatory response is further exacerbated by LPS-promoted TLR4 polymerization, which disrupts normal immune homeostasis and enabled intracellular signal transduction. TLR4 activation recruits MyD88 via its Toll/IL-1 receptor (TIR) domain, triggering the degradation of IκB, which releases NF-κB from its inhibitory state32. Once activated, NF-κB translocates into the nucleus, where it initiates the transcription of genes encoding cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α32. Once the mucosal barrier is compromised, LPS and other bacterial products translocate from the intestinal lumen into the circulation, where they synergize with CD14 and other co-receptors of TLR4, leading to aberrant secretion of immune mediators33. The findings of the present study indicate that AM modulates the LPS-mediated TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting downstream immune-inflammatory responses. We detected that AM reduced the expression of p65 and its phosphorylation and significantly suppressed the p-p65 expression in the cell nucleus. The concurrent downregulation of the upstream LPS/LBP/CD14 cascade and the reduction in NF-κB-dependent cytokines (TNF-α and IL-1β) following AM intervention functionally support NF-κB pathway suppression. Collectively, these results demonstrate that AM effectively reduces systemic LPS translocation. By mitigating this LPS-driven endotoxemia and suppressing the downstream TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB cascade, AM exerts a protective effect against barrier-related immune-inflammatory dysfunction (Figs. 4 and 5).

Although the current study provides a foundation for utilizing AM to alleviate obesity-associated T2DM, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the absence of 16 S rRNA sequencing or metagenomic analysis precludes direct characterization of AM-induced alterations in gut microbiota composition. Consequently, our conclusions regarding the gut dysbiosis–LPS axis remain inferential, as they are derived from functional indices—including plasma LPS levels, tight junction protein expression, and downstream inflammatory markers-rather than from direct microbiological evidence. Second, the lack of standardized sham control (e.g., non-acupoint light-touch intervention) limits our ability to definitively distinguish specific mechanical transduction effects from non-specific tactile stimulation. Third, intestinal permeability was not directly assessed using established methods such as in vivo FITC-dextran tracking or ex vivo Ussing chamber assays. Future investigations integrating microbiota profiling, multi-omics approaches, rigorous sham-controlled designs, and direct permeability assessments are warranted to delineate the precise biological targets of AM and to validate its mechanistic and therapeutic potential in obesity-associated metabolic disorders.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current short-term study demonstrates that AM effectively alleviates glucolipid metabolism abnormalities by improving glucose tolerance and lipid profiles, while also attenuating both systemic and intestinal inflammation in obesity-associated T2DM. Our functional evidence suggests that these protective effects are closely linked to the improvement of intestinal barrier integrity, as evidenced by the restoration of the tight junction protein Occludin. This enhanced barrier restricts LPS leakage and subsequently dampens the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB-mediated pro-inflammatory cytokine cascade. Although further long-term evaluations and deeper multi-omics analyses are required to fully elucidate its direct impact on gut microbiota and exact molecular targets, these findings provide a solid theoretical basis for exploring AM as a potential barrier-protecting therapeutic strategy for obesity-related metabolic diseases.
