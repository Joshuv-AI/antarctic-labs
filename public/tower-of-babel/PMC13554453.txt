Reglid: novel glycolipids from Korean red ginseng marc
Authors: Ji-Hun Kim, Rami Lee, Sung-Hee Hwang, Yebeen Kang, Hyun-Mee Park, Sun-Hye Choi, Ik-Hyun Cho, Seung-Yeol Nah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGinseng contains ginseng saponins as well as non-saponin components. In a previous study, we introduced a bioactive glycolipoprotein complex called gintonin, which play as a lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) receptor ligand. However, until now new ingredient(s) have not been discovered, except for gintonin. The purpose of this study is to further identify and isolate novel bioactive component(s).MethodsIn the present study, we identified the existence of a group of glycolipids using SDS-PAGE assay from Korean red ginseng marc (KRGM) extract. We isolated a group of glycolipids, named Reglid, through cation exchange chromatography and gel filtration. We analyzed Reglid and further examined in vitro Reglid biological effects on skin cell proliferation, autophagy activity, wound healing and melanin formations.ResultsWe found that Reglid is abundant in KRGM but not in white ginseng or American ginseng. Reglid is H2O-soluble and its molecular weight (MW) is approximately 48 kDa. Biochemical analysis shows that Reglid mainly contains lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) and phosphatidylcholine (PC) as active ingredients, and carbohydrates. In vitro studies showed that Reglid treatment increases skin cell proliferation in human dermal fibroblasts, promotes wound healing in HaCaT cells, and enhances autophagy activity in human skin fibroblasts. Reglid treatment also inhibits melanin production by inhibiting tyrosinase activity in melanoma cells.ConclusionThe present study provides a novel discovery of Reglid, a group of bioactive glycolipids containing LPC, PC, and carbohydrates derived from KRGM, and shows its potential application in cosmeceutical products for functional skin health. Finally, KRGM is a resource for KRGM-derived Reglid preparations.

Introduction

Ginseng has been used for centuries to enhance body vitality, support a healthy life, and alleviate various ailments. Historically regarded as a precious herbal medicine comparable to silver or gold in value, ginseng is renowned for its exceptional restorative properties against mental and physical weaknesses [1]. Thus, it plays a vital role in boosting biological functions, particularly in counteracting various health challenges such as cognitive decline, stress, fatigue, disease, cancer, and diabetes [2]. Currently, it is one of the most widely recognized and consumed medicinal plants in the Far East.

Ginseng contains multiple compounds, among which ginseng saponins (also called ginsenosides) were the first to be identified since the inception of ginseng research [3], establishing themselves as the representative components of ginseng. At the time of their discovery, no other active constituents were identified, making ginsenosides the most frequently cited compounds in physiological and pharmacological studies of ginseng. Ginseng polysaccharides with immunomodulatory effects have also been isolated [4]. Although no other active components have been identified for a long period, recent studies have revealed that ginseng contains gintonin, a novel and specific ligand for GTP-binding protein-coupled lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) receptors, which mainly contains LPAs as functional ingredients [5,6]. The discovery of gintonin revealed the possibility that ginseng contains additional and previously unidentified components, and highlights the need for the isolation and functional characterization of non-saponin compounds from ginseng [7].

On the other hand, Korean red ginseng marc (KRGM) refers to the by-product remaining after the aqueous extraction process used in the production of Korean red ginseng (KRG) products. KRGM is usually either discarded, used as compost in cultivation, or incorporated as feed for livestock, such as cattle and pigs. Recently, it was introduced as an ingredient in dietary supplements for chicken [8]. However, its utilization for recycling remains limited, and a significant amount is discarded, resulting in economic losses. In previous reports, we demonstrated that KRGM also contains gintonin LPAs [[9], [10], [11]].

In the present study, we explored the possibility of existence of another novel bioactive ingredient(s) besides KRGM gintonin [12]. Finally, we isolated a novel glycolipid complexes from KRGM. We named this Reglid, which exists only in the KRGM and KRG. Reglid differs from gintonin, which are glycolipoprotein complexes. Reglid contains lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), phosphatidylcholine (PC), and carbohydrates as main ingredients. Since it is known that LPC plays beneficial roles in cosmetics for skin health, we investigated Reglid's effects on in vitro skin cells and found that Reglid exhibits in vitro whitening effects. We further discussed its characteristics and its potential applications in biological systems as a glycolipid complexes. Thus, KRGM can be a novel resource for the upcycling of KRGM-derived Reglid preparation.

Materials and Methods

Materials

KRGM, which was obtained from a local KRG company as a by-product generated after hot aqueous extraction of KRG, was stored in a dried state. We purchased fermented ethanol from Daehan ethanol life, Korea. We purchased Sudan black B from ACROS Organics, USA. We purchased Tricorn column, XK column, Superdex 200 (10/300) column, Superdex 75 (10/300), Sepharose™ High Performance Resin, and Increase column (29-1487-21) from Cytiva, USA. We purchased Coomassie brilliant blue R-250, Coomassie brilliant blue G-250, Periodic acid, Schiff's reagent, MES, PD-10 resin (Sephadex G-25, G-25-50), and other chemical agents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, USA.

Methods

Preparation of 70% KRGM extract and its fractionations

Prior to applying the ginpolin protocol of KRGM [12], the dried KRGM was pulverized into particles approximately 3 mm in diameter. A 70% ethanol total extract of KRGM was prepared as described in a previous report [9]. KRGM total extract was further divided into three fractions such as gintonin-enriched fraction (GEF), ginseng polysaccharide fraction (GPF), and ginseng residual fraction (GRF) (Fig. S1). GRF is also called crude ginseng saponin fraction (cGSF) [12].

Preparation of SDS-PAGE and gel staining

A 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel was prepared using a hand-cast system (Bio-Rad, USA) for SDS-PAGE as described previously [5]. We performed SDS-PAGE on four preparations like total extract, GEF, GPF, and GRF [12]. Carbohydrate, lipid, or protein staining of gels was also performed as described in previous reports [5]. Thus, the resulting gels were subjected to periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining for polysaccharides, Sudan Black B staining for lipids, and Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining for proteins.

Cation exchange chromatography for isolation of an unidentified component

Cation-exchange chromatography was performed using a FPLC system (Bio-Rad, Biologic FPLC system: Maximizer, Duoflow, Quadtec, Biofrac). The FPLC parameters for cation exchange chromatography are listed in Table S1. An XK column packed with 120 mL SP Sepharose™ High Performance Resin was used as the stationary phase. For the mobile phase, Line A contained 50 mM MES buffer (pH 3.8), which served as both the pre-wash solution and the solvent for dissolving the GRF. Line B contained an elution buffer consisting of 30 mM NaCl in 50 mM MES buffer (pH 3.8). The NaCl concentration in the elution mobile phase was determined using gradient chromatography. The Quadtec UV-vis detector was set at 203 and 210 nm for the detection of phospholipids such as LPC and PC [13], 280 nm for the detection of aromatic amino acids in proteins, and 230 nm for the detection of peptide bonds in proteins. The column was stabilized with ten column volumes (CVs) of Line A mobile phase prior to use. Sample injection (10 mL) was conducted using a lure connector, with a gradient flow rate ranging from 0.65 mL/min to 0.75 mL/min. The column loaded with sample was installed on the FPLC, and a pre-wash was performed with five CVs of Line A mobile phase, using a gradient flow rate from 0.75 mL/min to 0.8 mL/min. A zero-baseline for all four detector wavelengths was established before and after pre-washing. The elution of target molecules bound to the stationary phase via electrostatic interactions was performed with Line B mobile phase (30 mM NaCl in 50 mM MES buffer, pH 3.8) at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min for five CVs. Fractions were collected in 5 mL volumes using a fraction collector, and 120 fractions were obtained. The collected fractions were freeze-dried in powdered form for stabilization.

Desalting of candidate fractions by gel filtration following cation exchange chromatography

Candidate fractions obtained by cation exchange chromatography, as identified by SDS-PAGE, were pooled, freeze-dried, and subjected to gel filtration for further desalting. The specific conditions for gel filtration using FPLC are listed in Table S2. Exclusion was performed using deionized water (line A) at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min. The exclusion volume was set at 60 mL, corresponding to two column volumes (CVs), and fractions were collected in 2.5 mL volumes using a fraction collector. Candidate fractions were predicted based on conductivity among 24 fractions. The fractions corresponding to the peaks observed in the chromatogram were pooled and freeze-dried to obtain a powder for subsequent analysis.

Determination of the molecular weight of Reglid through gel filtration

Gel filtration for molecular weight determination of Reglid was performed using FPLC as described in a previous report using a Superdex 75 10/300 Increase column (Cytiva©, USA). The standard protein marker (Dalton) consists of Aprotinin (6,500), α-Lactalbumin, (14,200), Trypsin inhibitor (20,000), Trypsinogen (24,000), Carbonic anhydrase (29,000), Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (36,000), Ovalbumin (45,000) and BSA (66,000).

Carbohydrate quantification in Reglid

Total carbohydrates were quantified using the Total Carbohydrate Quantification Assay Kit (Abcam, England). The assay was performed in the sequence of standard curve preparation, sample preparation, reaction, measurement, and calculation, as described in the manufacturer's instructions.

Amino acid analysis and its quantitation in Reglid

Reglid (100 mg) was first hydrolyzed in vacuo in 6 N HCl for 24 h at 110°C for general amino acid analysis. Further amino acid composition and its quantitation were performed at Ajoo Scientifics by three times (Gunpo, Korea). Detailed procedures quantitation methods of each amino acid in Reglid were carried out according to company guidelines.

Free choline quantification in Reglid

The total free choline in Reglid obtained after gel filtration was quantified using A Choline Detection Kit (Abcam, UK). Choline assay in Reglid was assayed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

LPC and PC quantitation in Reglid

LPC and PC were quantified using LC-MS/MS, as described previously [13]. Briefly, an Agilent series 1100 HPLC (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) instrument consisting of a G1311A quartz pump, G1313A autosampler, G1322A degasser, G1316A column oven, and API 2000 LC-MS/MS system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) was used [14]. Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) was performed using an electrospray ion source in positive mode (LPC, PC). Data acquisition was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, and SCIEX Analyst 1.4.2 software (ABI Inc., Foster City, CA, USA) was used for data management and control. Chromatographic separation of LPC was performed on an XBridge C18 analytical column (2.0100 mm, 3.5 mm particle size; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA; Fig. 2). Chromatographic separations of PC were performed on an XBridge Hydrophilic Interaction analytical column (2.1 × 100 mm, 3.5 mm particle size; Waters Corporation).

Cell culture

Human dermal fibroblasts (HDF), human keratinocytes (HaCaT), and B16F10 murine melanoma cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagles medium (DMEM; WELGENE, Korea) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) (growth medium (GM) at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. High-glucose DMEM was used for HDF and B16F10 cells, and low-glucose DMEM was used for HaCaT cells. For serum starvation, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated in serum-free medium (SFM) for 4 h prior to treatment.

Cell viability assay

To evaluate Reglid effect on cell viability/proliferation, HDF cells were seeded in 96-well plates (Corning®, USA) at 2 × 104 cells/well in triplicates. At ∼80% confluence, cells were washed twice with PBS and serum-starved in SFM for 4 h. Untreatment wells received GM, whereas Reglid was prepared in SFM and applied at final concentrations of 0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30, and 100 μg/mL (100 μL/well), respectively. Cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 and cell viability was quantified using the WST-1 assay, according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Autophagy assay

HaCaT cells were seeded in triplicate into 6-well plates (Corning® Costar®, USA) at 1 × 105 cells/well (triplicate). At ∼75% confluence, cells were washed twice with PBS and serum-starved in serum free media (SFM) for 4 h. Untreatment wells received GM, whereas other groups were treated in SFM for 24 h with Reglid (1, 3, 10, or 30 μg/mL), LPA (10 μM), EGF (4 ng/mL), GEF (30 μg/mL) or Ki16425 (10 μg/mL) plus GEF (30 μg/mL). Cells were washed twice with PBS and lysed in 100 μL RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors on ice for 1 h. Lysates were centrifuged (13,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C) and supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein samples (20 μg) were used for 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature and washed thrice with Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) (0.1% Tween 20; 10 min each). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against LC3B (Cell Signaling Technology, Boston, MA, USA) or p62/SQSTM1 (Cell Signaling Technology, Boston, MA, USA) diluted 1:1000 in TBST containing 2.5% skim milk. After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (GTX213111-01, anti-mouse; GTX213110-01, anti-rabbit; GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA) diluted 1:2000 were incubated for 2 h at room temperature. The bands were visualized using an enhanced chemilumiescence (ECL) solution (Bio-Rad, USA) and imaged using an iBright CL1000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

In vitro wound healing assay

HaCaT cells were maintained in 100 mm dishes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and subcultured at ∼80% confluence until passage 25. For the scratch assays, cells were seeded 24-well cell culture plate (Corning® Costar®, USA) at 2.5 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h to form a confluent monolayer. After 4 h serum starvation in SFM, a linear scratch was generated using a 200 μL pipette tip. Wells were washed twice with 1x PBS, and treated with untreatment (GM alone), Reglid (1, 3, 10, and 30 μg/mL), LPA (10 μM), or EGF (4 ng/mL) (200 μL/well). Images were acquired at 0 and 24 h using an inverted microscope (AxioVert200; Carl Zeiss, Germany), and wound closure was evaluated by comparing the wound areas over time.

Cell preparation and treatment for melanin formation assays

B16F10 melanoma cells were seeded in a 100 mm dish (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 3 × 106 cells per dish passaged at ∼70% confluence. Experiments were conducted at passage 3. For melanin/tyrosinase-related assays, cells were seeded in 6-well plates (Corning® Costar®, USA) in triplicate and serum-starved for 4 h in SFM at ∼65% confluence. UT wells received GM, whereas treatment groups received Reglid (1, 3, 10, 30, 50, 100, or 200 μg/mL), GEF (30 μg/mL), arbutin (1 mM), ginsenoside Rb1 (30 μM), ginsenoside Rg1 (30 μM) as indicated. For melanin induction, α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH) was added to all wells, and cells were incubated for 48 h.

Melanin content assay

The inhibition of melanin synthesis by Reglid and other agents was evaluated in α-MSH-stimulated B16F10 cells. After 48 h of incubation following α-MSH treatment (100 nM), culture media were removed and cells were washed twice with 1xX PBS. Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor (4°C, 1 h) and centrifuged (13,000 x g, 20 min, 4°C). The protein concentrations in the supernatants were measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After centrifugation, pellets were dissolved in 0.1 N NaOH containing 10% SDS at 80°C for 1 h. They were transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a SpectraMax® ABS Plus Absorbance Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices©, USA). Melanin content was normalized as previously described.

Tyrosinase activity assay

B16F10 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (Corning® Costar®, USA) at a density of 4 × 104 cells per well (triplicate). At ∼65% confluence, cells were washed twice with PBS and serum-starved in SFM for 4 h. UT wells received GM, whereas Reglid treatment groups received Reglid (1, 3, 10, 30, 50, 100, or 200 μg/mL), GEF (30 μg/mL), arbutin (1 mM), ginsenoside Rb1 (30 μM) or ginsenoside Rg1 (30 μΜ). Cells were co-treated with α-MSH (200 nM) and incubated for 72 h. Cells were washed twice with PBS and lysed in 100 μL RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors on ice for 2 h. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4°C for 30 min and protein concentrations were determined by the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For the tyrosinase activity, 10 μg of protein (40 μL) was mixed with 40 μL of 10 mM L-DOPA prepared in 67 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.8), incubated at 37°C for 10 min, and the amount of generated DOPA chrome was measured at 492 nm using a SpectraMax® ABS Plus Absorbance Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices®, USA). The relative tyrosinase activity was calculated from the optical density.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student's t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's multiple comparison test versus the control group.

Isolation of novel glycolipid complexes, Reglid, from KRGM GRF

We first designated a novel component as "Reglid,” which is derived from Korean red ginseng glycolipids of KRGM GRF. In a previous report, we showed that GEF contains very small amounts of LPC and PC, which are precursors of LPA [13]. We assumed that KRGM GRF may also contain more LPC and PC in the form of complexes with carbohydrates (Fig. 1). Because LPC and PC have a positive charge on the head choline [13], we first carried out cation exchange chromatography to isolate Reglid according to the protocol described in the Materials and Methods section. To identify the glycolipids fractions among the 120 fractions obtained via cation-exchange chromatography and to verify the corresponding peaks in the chromatogram, SDS-PAGE and the following gel staining were performed on the candidate fractions (data not shown).

The glycolipids fractions was predicted based on absorbance (203 nm) and conductivity. Finally, the corresponding Reglid peak fractions were collected (Fig. 2A, arrow) and freeze-dried. Because the Reglid fractions obtained after cation exchange chromatography included salt, we removed the salt through gel filtration (also called size exclusion chromatography) and freeze-dried Reglid fractions (Fig. 2B, arrow). The MW of Reglid was determined to be approximately 48.3 kDa, using standard proteins by gel filtration (Fig. 2C, red triangle). The yield of Reglid is approximately 2.10 ± 0.45% from GRF. We applied the same procedure to white ginseng (WG)/white ginseng marc (WGM) and American ginseng (AG)/American ginseng marc (AGM) as we did to KRGM. As shown in Fig. S2(A–D), they almost did not contain Reglid in WG/WGM and AG/AGM in cation exchange chromatography, indicating that Reglid might be unique to KRGM, and other ginseng preparations, except KRG and KRGM, contain little amounts of Reglid.

As shown in Fig. 3A, Reglid exhibited a beige, cotton-candy-like powdered texture, distinct from the original KRGM GRF (Fig. 3A). To confirm whether the fractions collected after gel filtration corresponded to Reglid, we again performed a qualitative analysis using SDS-PAGE and staining. As shown in Fig. 3B, gel staining corresponding to the Reglid, but not the non-Reglid fractions (NR) (Fig. 2A), showed the presence of lipids and polysaccharides, which makes Reglid H2O-soluble. In addition, protein staining was not clearly observed and further amino acid analysis and its total quantitation showed that Reglid contains very small amounts of amino acids by 6.9 μg/mg (Table S3), but GEF contains amino acids by 109 μg/mg [14], confirming that the Reglid were different from those of GEF. Thus, Reglid is clearly distinguished from GEF in that GEF has a slightly higher MW than that of Reglid, and in that GEF contains proteins but in that Reglid does negligible amounts (Fig. 3B). Considering its isolation via cation exchange chromatography, the staining patterns of the three components and its MW, we confirmed that Reglid is a novel component of KRGM that is different from gintonin.Fig. 3Morphological characteristics of Reglid powder isolated from KRGM GRF and SDS-PAGE. Quantitative determination of Reglid LPC and PC by LC-MS/MS

Based on a previous report [13] and its isolation through cation exchange chromatography, we assumed that Reglid might contain choline, LPC, and PC, all of which are positively charged [13]. In addition, based on the qualitative analysis results shown in Fig. 3, Reglid was identified as a glycolipid, which is a complex of positively charged phospholipids with carbohydrates. To perform a quantitative analysis of these components, we performed an ELISA for carbohydrates and LC-MS/MS for LPC C18:2, PC C18:2-18:2, and PC C18:2-16:0, respectively. We found that Reglid contained approximately 1.3% carbohydrates. As shown in Table 1, Reglid contained approximately 1% LPC C18:2 and the amount of LPC was in the order: Reglid > total extract = GRF > GEF > GPF. Reglid also contains approximately 0.7% PC C18:2-18:2 and PC C18:2-16:0, and the amounts of PC C18:2-18:2 were in the order: Reglid > total extract = GRF > GEF > GPF. We found that Reglid contained a negligible amount of choline (data not shown). Effects of Reglid on human skin fibroblast proliferations and in vitro wound healing

Since one of the biological effects of LPC is skin health like anti-skin aging and skin whitening effects [15], we first examined the effects of Reglid and non-Reglid fractions as shown in Fig. 2A, obtained by cation exchange and gel filtration chromatography, on the viability and proliferation of human dermal fibroblasts. Reglid stimulated cell proliferation in a concentration-dependent manner, and we could observe statistically significant increases at a concentration of 30 μg/mL at 24 h. At 48 h, the extent of proliferation slightly decreased compared with that at 24 h. However, Reglid (100 μg/mL) suppressed cell proliferation at both 24 and 48 h (Fig. 4A and B). In contrast, non-Reglid fractions did not affect cell proliferation (Fig. 4A and B), indicating that Reglid but not non-Reglid fractions has cell proliferating effects. In the experiments in vitro wound healing effects of Reglid on HaCaT cells, a human keratinocyte cell line, Reglid treatment also significantly increased cell proliferation and facilitated wound closure at more than 3-10 μg/mL as well as LPA and EGF, which are previously known to promote wound healing [16] (Fig. 5A and B). Discussion

In the present study, we isolated Reglid from KRGM GRF using cation-exchange chromatography, followed by gel filtration chromatography. Reglid is a type of glycolipid complexes in SDS-PAGE that is characteristically found in KRGM but not in white or American ginseng (Fig. S2). Since the Reglid was isolated through cation-exchange chromatography (Fig. 2), we assumed that Reglid might contain phospholipids with positively charged choline, such as LPC C18:2 and PC C18:2-18:2 in their head groups, as shown in a previous report [13]. Thus, LC-MS/MS analysis of Reglid LPC and PC revealed that Reglid contained approximately 1% LPC and 0.7% PCs (Table 1), constituting a complex with carbohydrates that could make Reglid H2O-soluble. The MW of Reglid is approximately 48.3 kDa. In addition, since the protein components of Reglid are not detected by SDS-PAGE, we performed amino acid analysis and its quantitation and found that Reglid contains trace amounts of protein components (Table S3). Thus, Reglid is different from gintonin in that the MW of gintonin is approximately 67 kDa and in that gintonin is a type of glycolipoprotein isolated via anion exchange chromatography [5].

The main component of Reglid LPC C18:2 is the precursor of gintonin LPA C18:2, the major LPA in ginseng gintonin [13]. For example, LPC has a choline group in its head, and the removal of head choline by animal serum autotaxin (lysophospholipase D) makes it LPA C18:2. Thus, KRGM contains LPA C18:2 as well as its precursor LPC C18:2, which is abundant in animal serum (200-300 μM) including human beings. In the present study, we demonstrated that Reglid LPC C18:2 is also present in large amounts in KRGM and can only be isolated by cation exchange chromatography and gel filtration.

Interestingly, GRF contained large amounts of LPC C18:2 and PC C18:2-18:2 compared to GEF and GPF, but the total extract of KGRM also contained LPC as much as GRF, as shown in Table 1. These results indicate that the ginpolin protocol for GRF preparation is also suitable for collecting or concentrating LPC and PC separately from other fractions in the KRGM total extract. However, the amounts of Reglid LPC and PC content were not significantly different from those in the KRGM total extract (Table 1). These results indicate that LPC and PC might not bind tightly to the cation exchange resin used in this study and are easily eluted with low concentrations of 30 mM NaCl compared to high concentrations (>500 mM) of NaCl used in previous gintonin elution [5]. Further studies will be required to improve the method for Reglid LPC and PC concentrations from the KRGM total extract.

In vitro experiments, Reglid but not non-Reglid promoted the proliferation of human-derived dermal fibroblast (HDF) cells, suggesting a proliferative effect on skin cells. Reglid also showed wound healing effects (Fig. 5). In addition, Reglid increased the expression levels of the autophagy markers LC3B-II and P-62, suggesting that Reglid stimulates autophagic activities in skin cells (Fig. 6). In fact, LC3B-II is produced by the conjugation of cytosolic LC3B-I to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) via the ATG3, ATG7, and ATG16L1 complex and is localized to both the inner and outer membranes of autophagosomes. Therefore, increased LC3B-II levels are closely associated with an increase in normal cell number [[27], [28], [29]]. In addition, p62 preferentially binds to LC3B-II and is transported into autophagosomes; due to its higher affinity for LC3B-II than LC3B-I, it is regarded as being closely linked to autophagy activity [30]. Furthermore, Reglid may have skin-whitening effects, as it suppressed melanin pigment formation via tyrosinase inhibition (Fig. 7).

LPC exerts diverse biological effects, including skin [27,[31], [32], [33], [34]]. Currently, it has been known that LPC is a bioactive lipid mediator that acts as a ligand for several G protein-coupled receptors such as GPR119, G2A (GPR132), and GPR4. These GPCRs are involved in various biological functions [28]. For example, LPC acts as a high-affinity endogenous ligand for GPR119, which is predominantly expressed in the pancreas. GPR119 activation by LPC mediates glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, making it a target for anti-diabetic drug development [29]. LPC is a high-affinity ligand for the G protein-coupled receptor G2A, acting as a key signaling molecule in immune regulation such as phagocytic clearance of activated neutrophils and bacterial pathogens [30]. LPC also acts as a modulator and ligand for the G protein-coupled receptor 4 (GPR4), regulating endothelial function, inflammation, and cellular stress in blood vessels [35].

If we hypothesize that Reglid LPC is a main functional component for biological activities, the role of Reglid LPC on skin health is still in its early stages and it is unknown which type(s) of GPCRs mentioned above are involved for Reglid LPC-mediated skin health. Interestingly, in previous report, egg lecithin-derived LPC and its commercial product (Lysofix Dry™) effectively suppressed melanogenesis provoked by α-MSH in B16 cells [15]. In molecular mechanism study, both inhibited the mRNA transcription of microphthalmia-associated transcription factor and tyrosinase under blue light irradiation. Both also increased the viability of B16 and HaCaT cells after UVB and blue light irradiation in vitro. In addition, low-concentration LPC acts as a good emulsifier, moisturizer, and increases the penetration of other active ingredients into the epidermis [15]. However, this report did not clearly demonstrate which type of GPCR receptor(s) are involved in LPC- and its commercial product-mediated inhibition of melanogenesis and skin cell protection against UVB [15]. Thus, previous and present studies showed that LPC might be diversely involved in endocrine, immune, endothelial cells including skin cell functions. However, the upstream and signaling transduction pathways how LPC including Reglid LPC exert its effects for skin health is currently not known. Further study in future will be required to elucidate the detailed molecular mechanism for skin health as well as its corresponding receptor(s).

Glycolipids are formed by the covalent attachment of multiple sugar residues to lipid molecules [36]. These glycolipids are found in various tissues, including cell membranes, brain, nervous system, and immune system [37,38]. They play crucial roles in maintaining the structural integrity of cell membranes, mediating intracellular signal transduction, and activating immune responses by interacting with the immune cells on the cell surface. Representative glycolipid components include gangliosides and cerebrosides, which differ from those in the KRGM Reglid. In this study, we isolated Reglid, a glycolipid complex, from KRGM for the first time. Based on the effects of Reglid on skin cells, Reglid could be used as a functional cosmetic ingredient to improve skin health.

In summary, Reglid was identified as a novel glycolipid complex with functional LPC and PC. Notably, Reglid was not present in other ginseng types, including white ginseng and American ginseng, suggesting its novelty of KRGM. Thus, KRGM is a rich resource for KRGM-derived Reglid preparations.
