Executive Summary/Abstract

 The main objective of this project was to conduct exploratory work to quantify how snow
and rain water inputs influence the CO2 coming from the soil surface (soil CO2 flux), and its
plant and microbial sources, in the East River watershed, near Crested Butte, Colorado.
Knowledge gained from this effort laid the groundwork for a more comprehensive (ongoing)
follow-on grant that is using a combination of experiments, field observations, machine learning
and simulation modeling to fully disentangle these relationships. New field measurements were
made at four locations along an elevational transect on Snodgrass Mountain at Rocky Mountain
Biological Laboratory. These sites were chosen to differ in total snowpack and water table depth,
and included both deciduous (aspen) and evergreen (spruce/fir) forest types. We used automated
measurements of soil CO2 concentrations to quantify the total soil CO2 flux, and the vertical CO2
production within the soil profile at each site. Isotope (radiocarbon, 14C) measurements
determined how much of the CO2 emitted from the soil surface came from plant respiration (root
metabolism) versus microbial respiration (decomposition of soil organic matter) sources.
Supporting data on plant phenology and microbial dynamics provided context for the observed
variation in respiration sources. This work was motivated by our overarching hypothesis that
quantifying belowground plant and microbial processes separately, and how they are influenced
by snow and rain inputs, is necessary for understanding and predicting how the East River
watershed ecosystems will respond to future environmental change.

Original Project Objectives
 We propose to quantify the direct and indirect moisture effects on the soil CO2 flux, and
its plant and microbial sources, across an elevational gradient in the East River watershed, near
Crested Butte, Colorado. Specifically, in the exploratory work described here, we will conduct
initial measurements of soil CO2 flux, and how plant and microbial CO2 fluxes respond to soil
water and temperature, along an elevational transect, and in relation to different forest cover
types. This work will provide a foundation for a full proposal which will explicitly adopt a
“ModEx” (or “Model-Experiment Coupling”) approach to improve model representation of
belowground processes. Our follow-on work will fully disentangle direct and indirect moisture
effects through manipulative experiments, field observations, and model-data integration.
 New field measurements will be made at three sites along an elevational transect on
Snodgrass Mountain at the Rocky Mountain Biological Laboratory (RMBL). These sites will
differ in total snowpack and water table depth, and will include both deciduous- and evergreen-
dominant forest types. Using automated measurements of soil CO2 concentration gradients, we
will quantify total soil CO2 flux and the vertical profile of CO2 production within the soil at each
site. Radiocarbon (14C) partitioning methods will be applied to determine how much of the soil
CO2 flux is coming from plant respiration (root metabolism) and microbial respiration
(decomposition of soil organic matter) sources over annual, seasonal, and episodic timescales.
To better understand the mechanisms driving the observed patterns, the respiration
measurements will be supported with observations of plant phenology and microbial dynamics,
quantified by repeat digital camera imagery and soil/microbial laboratory analyses, respectively.
These data will be analyzed from the perspective of soil ecohydrology, specifically soil water
inputs from snowmelt, monsoon precipitation, and subsurface flow.
 Our overarching research objectives are as follows: Research Objective 1 – Determine
how the type, timing, and amount of moisture inputs influence soil CO2 production and fluxes
over annual, seasonal, and episodic timescales, and how this varies according to forest
(deciduous- vs. evergreen-dominant) type. Research Objective 2 – Determine how different
moisture inputs influence plant root or microbial respiration sources separately, and quantify
how these respiration sources vary over space (elevation transect and vertical soil profile), forest
type, and time. Research Objective 3 – Investigate links between moisture inputs, vegetation
phenology, and soil microbial dynamics, and how they vary over space and time.
 Our original project hypotheses are as follows: Hypothesis 1 – Decreases in snowpack
amount and earlier snowmelt timing will result in more similar seasonal patterns of soil CO2 flux
between the evergreen and deciduous forest types. Hypothesis 2a – Lateral subsurface flow
plays an important role in driving the soil CO2 flux, primarily by stimulating plant root
respiration. Hypothesis 2b – Summer rains play an important role in driving the soil CO2 flux,
primarily by stimulating microbial decomposition. Hypothesis 3 – Differences in phenology
between evergreen and deciduous forests produce distinct seasonal patterns in the soil CO2 flux,
and these seasonal patterns are driven by root respiration, which scales with overall metabolic
activity (longer evergreen growing season, with lower peak metabolic activity; shorter deciduous
growing season, with higher peak metabolic activity).

Summary of project activities

 The project was funded during the COVID19 pandemic (August 2020) which delayed
installation of field equipment. In June 2021, we instrumented four (instead of the proposed
three) sites on Snodgrass Mountain for continuous measurements, entailing two aspen stands and
two spruce-fir stands. This is in addition to our existing two sites lower in elevation.

Field sites and instrumentation for Snodgrass Mountain elevation gradient installed in
June, 2021.

In summer 2022, we measured the soil CO2 flux continuously in three aspen (P.tremuloides) and
three spruce-fir (P.Englemanii, A.lasiocarpa) forests along an elevation gradient in the Upper
Colorado River Basin, near Crested Butte, CO, USA. We applied a radiocarbon source
partitioning approach to separate the microbial and root respiration contributions at three time
points during the summer growing season. At all sites, we found temperature explained 49 to
80% of the soil CO2 flux variability. Soil moisture increased model performance only in the
aspen forests, during late summer. The strong temperature relationship, however, masked the
very different microbial and root respiration source patterns. During August and September,
microbial respiration decreased by 40 to 70%, and root respiration contributions remained the
same or doubled. We attribute these diverging responses of root and microbial respiration to the
influence of drying soils and belowground tree allocation in both forest types. This research
demonstrates that understanding root and microbial respiration separate responses to
environmental drivers are critical in predicting how the soil CO2 flux will change in the future.

All aspects of the project and methodology went as planned, no methodologies were changed.
However, while we saw elevational differences in rain, water table, and snowpack, these
differences were hard to attribute to differences in soil CO2 fluxes or source contributions. We
ended up analyzing the different sites replicates versus a transect. Results from this work are in
the first chapter of PhD student, Austin Simonpietri’s dissertation, and within a manuscript that
is in final preparation to be submitted to JGR Biogeosciences.
