Robot‐Assisted Laparoscopic Partial Adrenalectomy Utilizing Intraoperative Indocyanine Green Near‐Infrared Fluorescence Imaging for Pheochromocytoma: A Case Report
Authors: Nae Takizawa, Haruyuki Ohsugi, Monta Inoue, Jun Matsushita, Hisanori Taniguchi, Hidefumi Kinoshita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionIndocyanine green (ICG) near‐infrared fluorescence (NIRF) imaging facilitates pheochromocytoma identification during adrenal surgery.Case PresentationWe report a 21‐year‐old woman with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2A and bilateral adrenal tumors who underwent ICG‐NIRF–guided, robot‐assisted partial adrenalectomy for pheochromocytomas. Following left adrenalectomy, partial right adrenalectomy was performed using intraoperative ICG‐NIRF imaging. After intravenous administration of 2.5 mg ICG, the normal adrenal cortex exhibited strong fluorescence, whereas the pheochromocytoma showed no fluorescence. However, fluorescence from the overlying normal adrenal cortex partially obscured the tumor, which was identified by intraoperative ultrasonography. Combined ICG‐NIRF imaging and ultrasonography enabled maximal preservation of functional adrenal cortex. Postoperatively, metanephrine and normetanephrine levels normalized, and hydrocortisone was tapered and discontinued.ConclusionICG‐NIRF is a useful adjunct for cortical visualization; however, multimodal guidance is essential for precise tumor delineation during cortical‐sparing adrenalectomy.

Introduction

Indocyanine green (ICG) near‐infrared fluorescence (NIRF) imaging is used for various diseases because of its useful properties [1, 2]. During ICG‐NIRF imaging, normal adrenal cortex fluoresces, whereas pheochromocytomas often hypofluoresces [3, 4]. Consequently, ICG‐NIRF imaging may facilitate tumor delineation and preservation of residual adrenal function [3]. We describe the feasibility and utility of ICG‐NIRF–guided robot‐assisted laparoscopic partial adrenalectomy with intraoperative ultrasonography for pheochromocytomas in a woman with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2A (MEN2A).

Case Presentation

A 21‐year‐old woman with MEN2A and medullary thyroid carcinoma was referred to our hospital. Endocrine evaluations and 123iodine‐labeled metaiodobenzylguanidine (123I‐MIBG) scintigraphy confirmed bilateral adrenal pheochromocytomas. Preoperative imaging showed more pheochromocytomas in the left adrenal gland than in the right (Figure 1A–D). To preserve adrenal cortical function, robot‐assisted laparoscopic left total adrenalectomy and right partial adrenalectomy were planned.

Computed tomography findings. (A) Coronal and (B–D) axial computed tomography images of the abdomen. Yellow and red arrowheads indicate the right and left adrenal tumors, respectively. At least two tumors are identified in the right adrenal gland. In the left adrenal gland, multiple tumors including one lesion larger than that in the right adrenal gland are observed.

Histopathology confirmed multiple pheochromocytomas in the left adrenal gland. One month later, robot‐assisted laparoscopic partial right adrenalectomy was performed using the da Vinci Xi system (Figure 2A shows the port configuration). An AirSeal System port (CONMED, Utica, NY, USA) was used as an assistant port. Monopolar Curved Scissors, Fenestrated Bipolar Forceps, and ProGrasp Forceps were utilized. Vascular control was achieved using Hem‐o‐lok clips (Teleflex, Wayne, PA, USA). No energy‐based sealing devices were used. During surgery, 2.5 mg ICG was administered intravenously, and ICG‐NIRF imaging was performed using the da Vinci Firefly mode. Within 30 s after intravenous ICG administration, the normal adrenal cortex exhibited strong fluorescence, while the tumors did not (Figure 2B). Intraoperative ultrasonography and NIRF imaging findings were consistent (Figure 2C,D). Although a cranial region adjacent to the pheochromocytoma also fluoresced on NIRF imaging, its intensity was lower (Figure 2E). Intraoperative ultrasonography and macroscopic findings identified the region as pheochromocytoma (Figure 2F). Combined ICG‐NIRF imaging and ultrasonography enabled identification of preservable adrenal cortex and delineated the boundary; the tumor was excised under white‐light imaging.

The central adrenal vein was ligated with Hem‐o‐lok clips and divided. The tumor was dissected using cold endoscissors, while confirming the absence of adrenal medullary hyperplasia (Figure 2G). Hemostasis of the resected surface was achieved using a fibrin sealant patch under gentle pressure for approximately 5 min to achieve hemostasis and reduce thermal damage (Figure 2H).

Two portions of normal adrenal tissue adjacent to the tumor, the normal adrenal cortex (Figure 2B) and an additional cranial region located further superior to the red dashed area (Figure 2F), were preserved. Console time was 111 min (including 9 min for ICG‐NIRF imaging and ultrasonography and 24 min for hemostasis); intra‐pneumoperitoneum pressure was maintained at 10 mmHg, estimated blood loss was 35 mL, and specimen weight was 15 g.

Hydrocortisone was initiated intraoperatively and gradually tapered. Plasma fractionated metanephrine and normetanephrine levels normalized (Table 1), and the patient was discharged uneventfully on postoperative day 8.

Discussion

ICG, a nontoxic, water‐soluble fluorescent dye, emits fluorescence under near‐infrared excitation. After intravenous ICG administration, approximately 80% binds to plasma proteins, and free ICG is internalized via organic anion transporters. It is rapidly metabolized in the liver [5]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, ICG retention is primarily driven by the preserved expression of uptake transporters, including bilitranslocase (BTL), and impaired biliary excretion mechanisms, such as reduced expression of efflux transporter MDR3 [6]. Variations in transporter expression may be associated with fluorescence intensity.

ICG‐NIRF imaging has proven useful for intraoperative visualization of the adrenal vasculature. Manny et al. first demonstrated its utility for differentiating normal adrenal cortex from tumor tissue during partial adrenalectomy [3], reporting lower fluorescence intensity in pheochromocytoma than in surrounding normal adrenal cortex [3]. Adrenal cortical fluorescence becomes detectable 15–75 s after ICG administration [7, 8].

The mechanism underlying ICG uptake in the adrenal cortex remains incompletely elucidated. Previous studies have suggested that, similar to renal tumors, decreased BTL expression in pheochromocytoma is associated with reduced fluorescence intensity [3, 9]. Consistent with these reports, the tumor in the present case exhibited low fluorescence intensity. Using publicly available transcriptomic datasets, BTL has not been identified at the gene level, precluding direct evaluation. Instead, analysis of in silico data showed that SLCO (OATP) family members, transporters known to mediate ICG uptake, were more enriched in adrenal cortical cells than in adrenal medullary cells (Supplementary Table 1) [10, 11]. Although these data have inherent limitations, pheochromocytoma, a tumor derived from the adrenal medulla, may similarly exhibit reduced expression of uptake‐related transport systems, potentially contributing to the observed low fluorescence intensity.

Fluorescence intensity findings in pheochromocytoma have been heterogeneous. Berber et al. reported that 50% of pheochromocytoma were nonfluorescent, particularly those larger than 3.2 cm [12]. In the present case, a faintly fluorescent area adjacent to the tumor on ICG‐NIRF imaging was identified as pheochromocytoma by intraoperative macroscopic and ultrasonographic findings. Examination of the resected specimen confirmed that overlying normal adrenal cortex created a false impression of tumor fluorescence (Figure 3). Smaller pheochromocytomas may remain covered by normal adrenal cortex, whereas larger tumors may more clearly exhibit the intrinsic low‐fluorescence characteristics of pheochromocytoma.

Intraoperative ultrasonography is commonly used in partial adrenalectomy; however, it requires direct probe contact with the tissue and provides limited intuitive visualization of the spatial extent of the normal adrenal cortex. In contrast, ICG‐NIRF imaging in partial adrenalectomy enables intuitive visualization of the two‐dimensional distribution of the adrenal cortex. Colvin et al., in comparing the conventional robotic view with ICG fluorescence imaging in adrenalectomy, reported that ICG‐NIRF imaging was superior in delineating adrenal boundaries [13]. ICG‐NIRF imaging may facilitate identification of the boundary between low‐fluorescence tumors such as pheochromocytoma and the fluorescent adrenal cortex, contributing to improved visualization of the preservable normal adrenal cortex.

However, the limitations of ICG‐NIRF imaging should be considered. During right‐sided adrenal surgery, hepatic ICG uptake may cause halation and impair visualization [12]. Furthermore, fluorescence intensity may be influenced by pathological conditions and ICG administration protocols [14]. Fluorescence assessment is also subjective and requires specialized equipment [14]. ICG‐NIRF imaging may be less suitable for real‐time evaluation of bleeding [15].

In patients with MEN2A, cortical‐sparing adrenalectomy may be considered for bilateral pheochromocytomas [16]. However, adrenal medullary hyperplasia has been observed in approximately 58% of patients with hereditary pheochromocytoma and should be excluded during partial adrenalectomy because it can precipitate hypertensive crises [17]. Furthermore, minimizing dissection from the surrounding structures is crucial for preserving the blood supply to the remaining adrenal cortex [16].

In the present case, ICG‐NIRF imaging was useful for visualizing the two‐dimensional distribution of the adrenal cortex. The combined use of ICG‐NIRF imaging and intraoperative ultrasonography facilitated accurate identification of the pheochromocytoma while maximizing preservation of the adrenal cortex.

Conclusion

ICG‐NIRF imaging with ultrasonography may enhance surgical precision and adrenal tissue preservation in adrenal sparing surgery.
