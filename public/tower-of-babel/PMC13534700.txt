Object detection algorithm based on infrared-visible dual-modality feature fusion
Authors: Zedong Huang, Kangkang Du, Xiaohuang Hu, Jianfeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCFM-YOLO, an infrared–visible dual-modality detection algorithm based on YOLOv11, is proposed to improve UAV object detection under adverse illumination and complex aerial backgrounds. The network is redesigned from three aspects: cross-modal feature extraction, lightweight feature fusion, and small-object-oriented detection. First, a Cross-Modality Fusion Mamba (CFM) module is introduced to promote channel-level interaction between visible and infrared features and to model long-range spatial dependencies with selective state-space modeling. Second, a lightweight feature fusion network is used to improve multi-scale information transmission while limiting redundant computation. Third, a P2 detection layer, Ghost convolution, and Focal-WIoU loss are incorporated to enhance small-object localization and alleviate the effect of imbalanced bounding-box samples. Quantitative experiments on the DroneVehicle dataset show that CFM-YOLO achieves 81.6% mAP@0.5 and 60.3% mAP@0.5:0.95, improving over the YOLOv11n-dual/base baseline by 8.4 and 5.5 percentage points, respectively. Qualitative results on the LLVIP dataset further indicate that the proposed method can reduce several missed detections in low-light pedestrian scenes. These results suggest that CFM-YOLO provides a competitive trade-off between detection accuracy and computational cost for UAV-based infrared–visible object detection.Keywords: UAV object detection, Infrared–visible dual-modality, CFM, YOLOv11Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

UAV-based object detection has become an important task in remote sensing and intelligent perception. Visible-light images provide rich texture and color information under normal illumination, but their quality can deteriorate under low illumination, fog, rain, occlusion, and nighttime conditions. Such degradation often weakens object boundaries and reduces the reliability of RGB-based detectors. Infrared sensors capture thermal radiation and are less dependent on ambient light. As shown in Fig. 1, visible imaging is limited to a narrow spectral range of approximately 0.4–0.7 μm, while infrared imaging covers longer wavelength bands related to thermal radiation. Therefore, infrared sensors can provide target information that is less dependent on visible illumination, offering a useful complement to visible images for UAV object detection under low-light or visually degraded conditions.

Spectral ranges of visible and infrared imaging.

Figure 2 illustrates the complementary characteristics of visible and infrared images. In low-light scenes, pedestrians may be difficult to distinguish in visible images but remain clear in infrared images. Infrared images, however, are usually single-channel and contain limited color and texture information. They may also be affected by non-target heat sources, while targets with weak thermal responses may be less distinguishable. Therefore, a single modality is insufficient for robust object detection in complex scenes. Combining visible texture information with infrared thermal saliency can provide a more reliable representation for small-object detection.

Examples of visible and infrared image pairs.

Infrared–visible fusion has been widely investigated for robust perception in remote sensing, surveillance, autonomous driving, and object detection1–3. Existing studies mainly improve cross-modal representation through feature enhancement, attention-guided interaction, and modality-adaptive compensation. Yang et al.4 proposed a cascaded information enhancement and cross-modal attention network for multispectral pedestrian detection. Fu et al.5 introduced long-range attention fusion and asymmetric complementary mask augmentation to strengthen cross-modal interaction. Tian et al.6 designed a cross-modality proposal-guided feature mining head to reduce information loss caused by image misalignment. Recent UAV-oriented methods further adapt fusion strategies to small objects and complex imaging conditions. TAF-YOLO performs visible-infrared adaptive fusion for UAV small-object detection7, while CCSFuse uses collaborative compensation and selective fusion to reduce modality imbalance in RGB-infrared UAV detection8. These studies show that adaptive fusion is useful for infrared–visible UAV perception, but lightweight and robust detection under adverse illumination remains challenging.

Transformer-based models have also been used to improve long-range interaction in multimodal detection. Dosovitskiy et al.9 introduced the Vision Transformer, and Fang et al.10 proposed the Cross-Modal Fusion Transformer for multispectral object detection. Shen et al.11 further developed ICAFusion with iterative cross-attention. These methods improve global feature interaction, but they often increase computational cost. In parallel, recent YOLO- and DETR-based detectors have emphasized small-object representation and multi-scale feature aggregation. MIR-YOLO addresses visible-infrared small-target detection in remote sensing12, SMA-YOLO improves YOLOv8 with parameter-free attention and multi-scale fusion13, and DFAS-YOLO reduces feature shift through feature-aware sampling14. CF-Deformable DETR considers weakly aligned visible-infrared detection using an end-to-end alignment-free design15. Although these methods provide useful references, their attention or sampling structures may still be computationally demanding for lightweight UAV platforms.

Mamba, proposed by Gu and Dao16, is a selective state-space model that can model long sequences with linear computational complexity. VMamba extends this idea to visual tasks by scanning two-dimensional feature maps along multiple directions and feeding the resulting sequences into a selective state-space model17. This mechanism is relevant to infrared–visible UAV detection because small vehicles and pedestrians usually occupy only a few pixels, and their discriminative cues may be spatially separated by occlusion, fog, glare, or background clutter. Visible and infrared features also differ in texture, intensity distribution, and boundary response, so effective fusion requires more than local neighborhood aggregation. Wang et al.18 introduced mask-guided Mamba fusion for drone-based visible-infrared vehicle detection, but the model still has a relatively high computational burden. This motivates the development of a more compact Mamba-based fusion structure.

Overall, existing infrared–visible fusion detectors face two main limitations. Some methods use complex fusion or attention structures, which are difficult to deploy on UAV platforms with strict real-time requirements. Other methods rely mainly on local convolutional aggregation and may not sufficiently model long-range cross-modal dependencies. In UAV scenes, small targets are often affected by scale variation, illumination changes, occlusion, fog, glare, and background clutter. The complementary cues of visible and infrared modalities may appear in different channels or spatial regions, especially under slight misalignment or environmental interference. These factors make lightweight yet effective cross-modal modeling necessary.

To address these issues, a lightweight dual-modality small-object detection framework based on YOLOv11n is developed. The proposed CFM module uses channel exchange to promote early interaction between visible and infrared features, and then applies Mamba-based sequence modeling to capture long-range spatial dependencies. A lightweight feature fusion network is adopted to strengthen multi-scale information flow. In the detection head, the P2 layer is introduced for small objects, the large-object P5 head is removed, Ghost convolution is used to reduce redundant computation, and Focal-WIoU is employed to improve bounding-box regression for small and dense targets.

Materials and methods

YOLOv11

The YOLOv11 architecture is shown in Fig. 3. It inherits the real-time detection framework of the YOLO family and incorporates modules such as C3k2, C2PSA, and SPPF to improve feature extraction and multi-scale representation. YOLOv11 provides several scale variants and can be adapted to different deployment conditions. These characteristics make it a suitable baseline for the infrared–visible UAV detection task considered in this study.

YOLOv11 network architecture.

Figure 4 illustrates the overall architecture of CFM-YOLO for infrared–visible dual-modality object detection in UAV aerial imagery. The network adopts a dual-branch backbone, in which RGB and infrared images are processed by two structurally identical but independently parameterized branches to extract modality-specific features. At the feature fusion stage, the proposed CFM module replaces simple feature concatenation and performs explicit cross-modal interaction through channel exchange and four-branch Mamba-based spatial modeling. This design enables the network to capture long-range dependencies between visible structural cues and infrared thermal responses while maintaining linear computational complexity. To improve multi-scale feature transmission, the neck is redesigned as LFN-DH, namely the Lightweight Fusion Network and Detection Head, which enhances the interaction between shallow spatial details and deep semantic features while avoiding excessive redundant computation. For small-object detection, the original P3/P4/P5 detection head is modified into a P2/P3/P4 detection head by adding a high-resolution P2 layer and removing the large-object-oriented P5 layer. In addition, GhostConv is introduced to reduce computational cost while preserving feature extraction capability. The whole network is trained end to end with the Focal-WIoU detection loss, so that cross-modal feature representation and small-object localization can be optimized in a unified detection framework.

CFM-YOLO network architecture.

CFM module

The Cross-Modality Fusion Mamba (CFM) module is designed for feature-level fusion between visible and infrared branches. Instead of directly concatenating two modalities, CFM first establishes explicit channel-level interaction and then applies Mamba-based sequence modeling to enhance spatial dependency representation. In CFM-YOLO, this module is deployed between the dual-modality backbone and the lightweight feature fusion network at three feature levels. For an input resolution of 640 × 640, the corresponding fused feature maps have spatial sizes of 160 × 160, 80 × 80, and 40 × 40, respectively.

Lightweight fusion network and detection head

YOLOv11 uses a bidirectional feature fusion structure derived from Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) and Path Aggregation Network (PANet), as shown in Fig. 6(a). Although this structure improves multi-scale detection, repeated downsampling reduces the resolution of deep feature maps and may weaken small-object representation. To address this issue, this study modifies the fusion part of YOLOv11. Figure 6(b) illustrates the proposed lightweight fusion network, which adds selected cross-layer connections and improves information transmission between shallow details and deep semantics. This design aims to enhance multi-scale representation while avoiding excessive redundant computation.

Lightweight feature fusion network and detection head.

The detection head is further adapted to UAV small-object detection. Based on the original P3, P4, and P5 detection layers, a P2 head is introduced to preserve higher-resolution features, while the P5 head is removed because large objects are less dominant in the examined aerial scenes. The P2 layer is connected to shallow backbone features and retains one-quarter of the input resolution, which provides more spatial detail for small targets. In addition, the depthwise branch in the detection layer is replaced with a GhostConv19 branch to reduce the number of parameters and computational cost while maintaining feature extraction capability.

Experiment

Datasets

Two public infrared–visible datasets, LLVIP20 and DroneVehicle21, are used in this study. LLVIP, released by Jia et al., is a paired visible-infrared dataset for low-light pedestrian detection. It contains 30,976 images, corresponding to 15,488 aligned visible-infrared image pairs. The official partition is adopted, with 12,025 pairs for training and 3,463 pairs for testing. Since LLVIP provides pedestrian annotations, only the person category is retained. In this work, LLVIP is mainly used for qualitative visualization and supplementary verification of pedestrian detection under low illumination. The main quantitative comparison is conducted on DroneVehicle, where the model is evaluated using mAP@0.5, mAP@0.5:0.95, number of parameters, and GFLOPs.

DroneVehicle is a UAV-based infrared–visible vehicle detection dataset containing 28,439 paired visible and infrared images. The official split includes 17,990 pairs for training, 1,469 pairs for validation, and 8,980 pairs for testing. Five categories are annotated: car, truck, bus, van, and freight car. The visible-light annotations are used as the unified ground truth for both modalities because visible images usually provide clearer object boundaries, while the paired infrared images are spatially aligned with the visible images. This setting also reduces inconsistent supervision caused by modality-dependent boundary ambiguity. Representative visible-infrared image pairs from the DroneVehicle dataset, together with examples from LLVIP, are shown in Fig. 7. Before training, the white borders in the original images are removed to reduce irrelevant background interference.

Comparative experiments

Comparative experiments are conducted on the DroneVehicle dataset to evaluate the proposed method against representative infrared–visible detection models. The comparison includes two single-modality YOLO baselines, a dual-modality YOLOv11n baseline, and recent infrared–visible detection methods, including TAF-YOLO, LF-MDet, C2Former, Super-YOLO, DE-YOLO, MIR-YOLO, LMDENet, and DMM. Specifically, YOLOv8n, YOLOv11n, YOLOv11n-dual/base, and the proposed CFM-YOLO are trained and evaluated under our experimental setting. The results of the other methods are taken from their original papers or reported benchmark results on the DroneVehicle dataset. As shown in Table 1, CFM-YOLO achieves 81.6% mAP@0.5 and 60.3% mAP@0.5:0.95, giving the highest reported accuracy among the compared methods in this table. Relative to YOLOv11n-dual/base, it improves mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95 by 8.4 and 5.5 percentage points, respectively. Although some lightweight methods require fewer parameters, their detection accuracy is lower in the reported results. In contrast, heavy dual-modality detectors such as C2Former and DMM require substantially more parameters or higher GFLOPs. These results suggest that CFM-YOLO offers a practical balance between detection accuracy and computational cost for UAV-based infrared–visible object detection.

Ablation experiments

The ablation study is organized at two levels. The first part evaluates the three main components of CFM-YOLO: the CFM module, the LFN-DH structure, and the Focal-WIoU loss. The second part further analyzes the internal CFM design, fusion position, and detection-head configuration to determine whether each design contributes independently rather than only increasing model complexity.

As shown in Table 2, each proposed component improves the baseline to different degrees. Introducing only CFM raises mAP@0.5 from 73.2 to 77.4%, suggesting that feature-level interaction between visible and infrared modalities is the main source of accuracy improvement. LFN-DH provides a moderate gain by refining multi-scale feature transmission and adapting the head to small targets, while Focal-WIoU improves localization without increasing the number of parameters or FLOPs. When the three components are used together, CFM-YOLO achieves 81.6% mAP@0.5 and 60.3% mAP@0.5:0.95, indicating that the fusion module, the lightweight detection head, and the regression loss are complementary.

Since the above comparison only verifies the effectiveness of the three top-level modules, a more detailed structural ablation was further conducted. The additional experiments focus on four aspects that are directly related to the proposed design: channel exchange, Mamba-based spatial modeling, the position of cross-modal fusion, and the configuration of the detection head.

The results in Table 3 show that the internal components of CFM are not redundant. Removing channel exchange decreases mAP@0.5 to 79.7%, which indicates that the explicit reorganization of modality-specific channels helps reduce the separation between visible and infrared features. The performance drops more markedly when the Mamba branch is removed, and replacing Mamba with ordinary convolution also leads to lower accuracy. This comparison suggests that the sequence modeling branch contributes to capturing long-range spatial dependencies that are difficult to represent through local convolution alone. In addition, removing the multi-head split or the residual modulation coefficient causes a smaller but consistent decline, implying that these two designs help stabilize feature enhancement and preserve modality-specific information.

The ablation results also indicate that the fusion position has a clear influence on detection accuracy. Input-level fusion performs the worst among the tested fusion strategies, probably because early concatenation introduces modality noise before sufficient semantic abstraction is formed. Shallow fusion preserves more spatial detail but lacks high-level semantic guidance, whereas deep fusion improves semantic consistency but may lose fine-grained cues for small vehicles. The proposed feature-level fusion strategy provides a better trade-off by introducing cross-modal interaction at suitable pyramid levels.

For the detection head, the original P3/P4/P5 head is less suitable for the DroneVehicle scenes, where targets are usually small and densely distributed. Adding the P2 head improves detection performance, but retaining P5 increases the computational cost. After removing P5 and replacing standard convolution with GhostConv, the proposed head obtains the best overall result while keeping the computational burden within a manageable range. These findings support the necessity of the channel interaction, Mamba branch, fusion location, and detection-head redesign in the proposed CFM-YOLO framework.

Conclusions

This paper proposes CFM-YOLO, a lightweight infrared–visible dual-modality object detector for UAV aerial imagery. The method introduces a CFM module for channel-level cross-modal interaction and Mamba-based spatial dependency modeling, a lightweight feature fusion network for multi-scale information transmission, and a P2/P3/P4 detection head with GhostConv and Focal-WIoU for small-object localization. Quantitative experiments on the DroneVehicle dataset show that CFM-YOLO reaches 81.6% mAP@0.5 and 60.3% mAP@0.5:0.95, improving over YOLOv11n-dual/base by 8.4 and 5.5 percentage points, respectively. The model has 10.8 M parameters and 28.6 GFLOPs, which is lower than several heavy dual-modality detectors while maintaining competitive accuracy. Qualitative results on LLVIP further show improved pedestrian detection in several low-light examples. Ablation experiments confirm the contributions of the CFM module, LFN-DH structure, Focal-WIoU loss, fusion position, and detection-head redesign.

Several limitations remain. The LLVIP results in this manuscript are mainly qualitative, and more complete quantitative evaluation on pedestrian datasets is needed in future work. The model may also be affected by severe cross-modal misalignment, heavy occlusion, and dense heat-source interference. Future work will focus on more efficient lightweight fusion, stronger alignment correction, and deployment-oriented optimization for UAV infrared–visible detection under complex imaging conditions.
