Barriers to Care for Obstructive Sleep Apnea: Primary Care Perspectives at a Safety Net Hospital
Authors: Michelle Florentine, Arushi Gulati, Ruben Hernandez, Jolie L Chang, Megan L Durr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveGiven the prevalence of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and sparsity of sleep medicine specialists, OSA management often depends on primary care providers (PCPs). We investigate PCP experiences managing OSA within a community health network (CHN) serving underserved patients.MethodsMixed‐methods study was performed with surveys and focused interviews. CHN providers in San Francisco were interviewed.ResultsTen providers (7 PCPs and 3 sleep medicine specialists) were interviewed. A group of 29 PCPs that worked for public institutions were surveyed. Identified patient barriers to OSA care included health care navigation difficulties (70%), low health literacy (68%), and housing and income‐related PAP use limitations (60%). Provider barriers included resource constraints (90%), provider knowledge about OSA disease and PAP troubleshooting (68%), and insurance coverage for sleep studies/PAP equipment (68%). The main barrier to PAP alternative therapy was PCP unfamiliarity with options (80%). While sleep specialists preferred managing OSA from time of PAP presentation, 36% of PCPs refer only after PAP noncompliance despite 78% feeling uncomfortable with PAP management and 96% with less than 3 h of formal OSA training. Most PCPs are interested in learning about PAP troubleshooting (79%) and PAP alternatives (56%).ConclusionPCPs report limited comfort in managing OSA, troubleshooting PAP, and assessing for PAP alternatives. Barriers to OSA care include healthcare navigation difficulties, insurance, and resource constraints. Both PCPs and sleep specialists report unfamiliarity with PAP alternatives. Educational materials aimed at OSA management, PAP troubleshooting, and PAP alternatives may improve equitable OSA care delivery.Level of Evidence3‐ Mixed‐method study with qualitative and quantitative data.Keywords: barriers to care, obstructive sleep apnea, positive airway pressure, primary care education, sleep surgery

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) affects around 9%–38% of adults, and the prevalence is rising [1]. The most common presenting symptom is excessive daytime sleepiness. OSA is associated with reduced quality of life, increased incidence of motor vehicle accidents and increased risk of cardiovascular and metabolic disease [1]. The diagnosis is established via in‐lab polysomnography or a home sleep study. Treatment options vary from conservative measures including diet/behavioral changes, weight loss, positive airway pressure (PAP), and oral appliances to surgical treatments such as soft tissue surgery, maxillo‐mandibular advancement or hypoglossal nerve stimulation [2].

PAP is the first line treatment option, however compliance remains low and varies from 30%–60% [3]. In addition to low PAP compliance, there is a shortage of sleep medicine providers, with only ~7500 board‐certified sleep medicine providers in the United States [4]. As a result, the burden of OSA diagnosis and management has shifted to primary care physicians (PCPs) who may have less training and exposure to PAP management and alternatives to PAP.

Here we aim to evaluate PCP comfort with diagnosing and managing OSA, to understand patient and provider barriers to timely OSA treatment, and to propose relevant targeted interventions including PAP alternatives to improve OSA care delivery.

Methods

We performed a mixed‐methods study. This study was classified as exempt from further review by the University of California San Francisco institutional review board. This study comprised two phases: (1) Qualitative interviews of PCPs and sleep medicine providers aimed at identifying common themes of barriers to OSA care which were then used to build phase two. (2) A 50‐question survey completed by PCPs with more granular data on OSA training and barriers to care. PCPs were recruited during a Primary Care Medicine CME conference in October 2023 in San Francisco, California and asked to complete this survey. Data were collected between September 2023 and May 2024.

Qualitative Interviews

Qualitative data were collected through semi‐structured interviews of PCPs and sleep medicine physicians. Interviews were conducted by two members of the study team (MF or MLD) via Zoom (San Jose, CA) and transcribed using Otter.ai (Mountain View, CA). Verbal consent was obtained prior to each interview. Purposeful sampling was used to identify participants for interviews including those practicing in different hospital and/or clinic settings and those with varying levels of academic appointment and leadership roles. We specifically recruited providers who work in primary care clinics or safety net clinics primarily serving patients with limited access to specialty care, many of whom live below the poverty line. All interview transcripts were analyzed using DeDoose software (version 9.0, SocioCultural Research Consultants LLC, Los Angeles, CA). Interviews were conducted until thematic saturation was achieved and no additional unique perspectives were emerging. In line with previous literature on this technique, thematic analysis was performed via a 6‐step approach: familiarizing with data, generating initial codes, theme identification, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and creating a final report. The first three transcripts were reviewed independently and used to create a codebook by two study team members (ML, AG). The codebook was then applied to all 10 transcripts and used to generate representative themes and subthemes. StataMP 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX) was used for descriptive statistical analysis. These themes were then used to structure the survey that comprises phase two of this study.

Survey Design

To evaluate primary care physician perspectives on barriers to OSA care, an online survey instrument was created based on current relevant literature and common themes identified through the interview phase of this study. The survey included demographics, practice type and region, clinical experience and training background, amount and format of OSA‐related training, OSA‐related patient characteristics, approach to diagnosis and management of OSA as well as interest in additional OSA‐related training. Barriers to care questions were asked using a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The survey was constructed through Research Electronic Data Capture (RedCAP) Nashville, TN.

Patient Barriers to OSA Diagnosis and Management

We asked participants to identify patient barriers to OSA diagnosis and management.

Interview Responses

The top reported patient barrier identified by interview participants was health care navigation challenges—including difficulty coming to appointments, difficulty arranging transportation to sleep labs, and difficulty working with durable medical equipment (DME) companies to obtain PAP equipment. The second most reported patient barrier identified by interview participants was poor health literacy, such as lack of awareness regarding the importance of treating OSA. Finally, socioeconomic status was the third most reported patient barrier identified. This included elements related to housing and income, such as lack of electricity for marginally housed patients or inability to pay the electricity bill and, therefore, inability to use their PAP equipment.

One PCP stated in regard to patient barriers that “…a lot of it is health literacy…digital literacy, level of educational attainment…[the patients] have many jobs, more complicated housing situations, language barriers…challenges with connecting with us on a video visit, let alone coming in to see us.”

Survey Responses

Of PCPs surveyed, a similar pattern of patient barriers was seen (Table 4), where 68% of survey participants reported health care navigation challenges as the primary issue, followed by income, including housing status and insurance (66%), and poor health literacy (52%).

Provider Barriers to OSA Diagnosis and Management

We asked participants to identify provider barriers to OSA diagnosis and management.

Interview Responses

The most commonly reported provider barrier to delivering OSA care was provider and resource constraints. Understanding insurance coverage was the next most common provider barrier, not just in terms of which sleep labs, sleep provider referrals or equipment are covered, but also in understanding how to appropriately document PAP compliance to ensure that patients can keep their PAP equipment. Finally, limited knowledge of PAP management and troubleshooting was the third most common provider barrier identified. In fact, one PCP stated that “…knowing how to troubleshoot the [PAP] devices is a big [barrier]. Because I'm just not comfortable with it. And I'm not sure of the different types and the different brands and supplies.”

Survey Responses

Similar provider barrier themes were reflected in the survey responses. Resource constraints were a primary barrier, including long wait times for sleep medicine consultations (45%), sleep studies (59%), and receiving DME equipment (66%). Providers also reported difficulty navigating insurance coverage (59%). Similarly, limited knowledge of PAP management and troubleshooting was reported by 62% of survey participants. Regarding PAP management, 50% of survey participants reported referring patients to sleep medicine at the time of presentation; however, 36% of PCPs reported referring to sleep medicine only after PAP noncompliance despite 78% feeling uncomfortable or very uncomfortable with PAP management (Table 3). Overall, 96% of PCPs reported less than 3 h of formal OSA training (Table 3).

Barriers to PAP Alternatives

We then queried providers on barriers to PAP alternatives in cases where PAP had failed.

Interview Responses

When interview participants were asked about reasons why referral rates to PAP alternative services were low, both PCPs and sleep medicine providers reported limited knowledge of PAP alternatives and candidacy for PAP alternatives as the main barrier.

One PCP stated in regards to surgical alternatives “what are they? What are the characteristics of candidates for those interventions and, and I don't really know anything about airway devices…” Sleep medicine participants also expressed hesitation regarding safety and efficacy of surgical options. One sleep medicine provider stated “…in my mind, as a non‐surgeon, surgery is still like a big deal… I perceive surgery to be a huge intervention, a huge and potentially, you know, low risk, but potentially catastrophic intervention…one in whatever, 100,000 but you still just don't want that…” Additionally, insurance coverage and accessibility to PAP alternative specialists were the next most common barriers to PAP alternatives reported by interview participants.

Survey Responses

Similar to interview participants, surveyed providers reported limited knowledge of PAP alternatives and candidacy for PAP alternatives as the main barrier to PAP alternative referrals (62%). Very few PCPs reported referring for sleep surgery (~14% of survey participants). More specifically, only 34% referred patients for oral appliances, 14% for soft tissue surgery, and 14% for hypoglossal nerve stimulation (Table 5).

Discussion

The prevalence of OSA is on the rise and there is a scarcity of sleep medicine providers, which has shifted the burden of OSA diagnosis and management to PCPs. While ideally patients would be referred to sleep medicine at the time of diagnosis (according to our sleep medicine physician participants), the majority of PCP participants reported long wait times for sleep studies and sleep medicine consultations as a barrier to OSA care. In fact, while half of our PCP participants refer patients to sleep medicine at the time of presentation, over one‐third refer to sleep medicine only after PAP noncompliance.

It is well recognized that PAP compliance is low amongst the adult population and there is ongoing research examining ways to maximize adherence including PAP‐related technologies, mask interfaces, educational, behavioral, and troubleshooting interventions [5]. Our study, however, suggests that issues with the OSA care pathway start at a much earlier time point than PAP compliance. We aimed to identify barriers to OSA care, both from the provider and patient side, and propose actionable solutions to these issues.

A commonly reported barrier faced by patients was health care navigation challenges revolving around appointment transportation and communication with DME companies. Similarly, providers struggled with understanding which insurance was accepted by various sleep labs and DME companies, leading to a further delay in OSA diagnosis and PAP initiation. This closely echoes the findings of Pendharkar et al., which highlight how the unclear dynamics between providers and the steps in the care pathway pose a significant barrier for patients. This barrier ultimately contributes to delays and obstructs timely access to OSA treatment [6]. However, this barrier is especially important to overcome amongst diverse patient populations, who already face distinct challenges of their own, creating even more difficulties in navigating the system. One unique and often expressed barrier to access is that of language. This is best described by the findings of Pecha et al., in which discordance of language causes misunderstanding and uncertainty, directly contributing to delays in care or hesitancy to pursue OSA management altogether [7].

To address this, our team created an informational pamphlet for both patients and providers, with consolidated information describing insurance coverage options for various sleep labs and DME companies in the region, transportation options as well as contact information for equipment delivery/troubleshooting which we aim to translate into various languages and distribute to local PCPs to facilitate logistical challenges to OSA diagnosis and equipment delivery.

Once patients have received their diagnosis and equipment, PAP management was the next reported PCP barrier. Nearly all participants reported receiving less than 3 h of formal OSA training. Additionally, a majority of PCP participants reported discomfort with PAP initiation and troubleshooting. This is reflected in a study by Grandner et al., a qualitative study of adults with OSA that found that barriers to sleep surgery consultation included OSA diagnosis delays due to limited patient and PCP awareness and a lack of urgency in troubleshooting noninvasive options amongst other logistical factors such as scheduling delays and cost [8]. Reassuringly, 80% of PCP participants reported an interest in learning about PAP troubleshooting. Discomfort with PAP troubleshooting can be improved with education on mask and equipment options and understanding when to refer to sleep medicine providers or how to use them as a resource for troubleshooting. The reported preferred format for such education included virtual sleep medicine presentations, online videos and CME courses and in person sleep medicine presentations.

Finally, when PAP troubleshooting fails, providers reported unfamiliarity with PAP alternatives including surgical and nonsurgical options as well as candidacy criteria and insurance coverage. This unfamiliarity can be improved with education on PAP alternative options and candidacy criteria, incorporating safety and efficacy of options and relevant insurance coverage options. Over half of all participants, including both sleep medicine and primary care providers, expressed an interest in learning about PAP alternatives. As for patients, providing educational‐based tools such as video‐based tools or standardized informational resources that delineate common challenges that arise from PAP can be another way to support them. It may lower the threshold and improve the consistent use of DME equipment and, thus, long‐term and proper management of OSA. This has been particularly emphasized by Gulati et al., whose study highlights how video‐based tools improved a patient's decisional readiness—the initiation step of pursuing OSA treatment. These informational resources can lower the threshold for patients to adopt the use of their CPAP or other recommended treatment option [9].

While certain barriers such as housing and electricity insecurity are difficult to address, our study has identified barriers to OSA care delivery that may benefit from targeted interventions.

Limitations

Our sample size of interviewed providers is heterogeneous, including providers from various, unique healthcare systems including the Veterans' Association and safety net hospitals. While this may provide a more varied perspective, due to the small sample size and female predominance of participants, our study may not capture the common experience of each respective group. Additionally, there is likely regional bias given the high proportion of unsafely housed patients in the greater San Francisco area and subsequently, a more robust safety net healthcare system than other regions. As a result, identified barriers may be system specific and therefore, less applicable to general practice. A few of these healthcare systems have limited insurance coverage of important PAP alternatives including mandibular advancement devices and hypoglossal nerve stimulation which may contribute to limited provider knowledge and experience on these topics. There was also a selection bias of providers who voluntarily participated in the study. Additionally, we had limited sleep medicine representation. Looking forward, we plan to broaden the perspectives to include more sleep medicine providers as we develop these targeted interventions with the hopes of providing more equitable OSA care.

Conclusions

PCPs report limited comfort in managing OSA, troubleshooting PAP, and assessing for PAP alternatives. They report barriers to OSA care such as healthcare navigation difficulties, insurance, and resource constraints. Both PCPs and sleep specialists report unfamiliarity with PAP alternatives. Educational materials aimed at PAP troubleshooting, navigation of overall OSA management workflow, and PAP alternatives may improve equitable OSA care delivery.
