Abstract
A fast and accurate beam-based alignment (BBA) method for harmonic sextupoles has been devel-
oped at NSLS-II using Linear Optics from Closed Orbit Modulation (LOCOM). The approach excites
the beam with simultaneous sine-wave signals at two fast correctors, chosen with an appropriate
phase advance to span the full betatron phase space. This strategy suppresses systematic errors from
hysteresis, while additional errors from orbit drift and power-supply calibration are minimized by the
short measurement time (a few minutes) and reliance solely on beam-based current-to-field conver-
sion of the sextupoles, with hysteresis explicitly included. Simulations indicate that Linear Optics
from Closed Orbits (LOCO) combined with 0.5 mm local orbit bumps can resolve relative sextupole
field offsets (∆k₂) with precision better than 10% of k₂, reflecting to beam-based alignment (BBA)
accuracy finer than 50 µm. Moreover, employing machine-learning-optimized local orbit bumps en-
hances sextupole-induced quadrupole signals in a deterministic manner and maintains orbit stability
under large sextupole strength variations (±40%). The proposed method is experimentally validated
through proof-of-principle measurements at NSLS-II, demonstrating its potential as a fast, precise,
and robust tool for harmonic sextupole alignment.

1. INTRODUCTION
  The National Synchrotron Light Source II (NSLS-II) is a third-generation 3 GeV electron storage
ring (SR) designed with sub-nanometer emittance [2]. The stringent magnet tolerances required for
the lattice are dictated by the need to maximize the dynamic aperture, ensuring efficient injection,
reliable beam capture, and adequate beam lifetime. At such ultra-low emittance, even small magnet
misalignments can strongly affect beam quality through orbit distortions and field offsets. To meet
design requirements, alignment tolerances were set to 30–50 μm for transverse offsets and 0.2 mrad
for roll angles. During installation, vibrating-wire measurements with 5–10 μm resolution were used
to determine the magnetic centers of magnets, which were then fixed to girders and installed in the
tunnel. For sextupoles, field offsets must be controlled to better than 10% of the nominal k₂ values
[2].
  Since commissioning, significant efforts have focused on minimizing transverse coupling to
achieve diffraction-limited vertical emittance (10-10/4π m). Sources of coupling include quadrupole
roll errors and sextupole vertical offsets. Conventional ring realignment using a laser tracker often
degrades coupling, underscoring the need for beam-based alignment (BBA) methods to provide in
situ measurements of magnetic centers. A widely used BBA approach aligns beam position monitors
(BPMs) to quadrupole centers [3], by varying quadrupole strength and observing the resulting orbit
deflection. An alternative strategy employs Linear Optics from Closed Orbits (LOCO) to calibrate
BPMs with respect to sextupole centers [4].
  However, conventional LOCO suffers from several limitations: (i) long measurement times (10–
100 minutes, depending on ring size); (ii) the requirement of at least three sets of optical response
 1

matrices (ORMs), which introduces systematic errors from slow drifts and hysteresis of corrector
magnets; and (iii) sub-micrometer precision demands in orbit measurement to meet the 50 μm align-
ment tolerance, which exceed practical capabilities. As a result, LOCO in its traditional form is in-
sufficient for sextupole alignment at NSLS-II.
  To overcome these limitations, the Linear Optics from Closed Orbit Modulation (LOCOM) method
was recently developed and applied at NSLS-II to identify chromatic sextupole errors [1]. LOCOM
combines fast acquisition with high accuracy, mitigating hysteresis and drift effects. In this work, we
present the first application of LOCOM to sextupole alignment, focusing on the calibration of BPMs
to harmonic sextupole (SH1) centers. Proof-of-principle measurements demonstrate that LOCOM,
combined with 0.5 mm local orbit bumps, can resolve relative sextupole misalignments with better
than 50 μm precision in the horizontal dimension.

2.1.2 Sextupole Scans and Beam-Based Calibration
  In this study, a pair of SH1 sextupoles located in the DW8 straight section was scanned over four
current settings—60%, 80%, 100%, and 140% of the operational value (Fig. 1). Both sextupoles share
the same power supply. LOCOM data were acquired at each setting using paired fast correctors to
excite the beam simultaneously in both planes (details provided in Sec. 2.3). The selected scan range,
kept safely below saturation, ensured linear magnet behavior. The entire measurement sequence—
including acquisition of four LOCOM data sets, beam orbits, and dispersion data—was completed in
under eight minutes. Horizontal and vertical beam orbits at each scan point were stabilized using a
machine-learning-based correction algorithm.
  Orbit correction during sextupole scans is essential to maintain consistent trajectories. Ideally, the
corrected orbits would remain identical across all settings; in practice, residual discrepancies arise
from finite correction accuracy, ground motion, and power-supply fluctuations. The machine learning
algorithm effectively suppressed these deviations, ensuring orbit stability throughout the scan. The
procedure was repeated with both x and y local position bumps, yielding two independent LOCOM
data sets.

2.1.3 Numerical Study of Measurement Precision
  A horizontal local orbit bump of 0.5 mm was generated numerically at the DW8 straight section
[Fig. 2(a)]. The SH1 sextupole power supply was then varied from 60% to 140% of its nominal setting
in steps of 10%. At each setpoint, the induced normal quadrupole component was extracted using
both the LOCO method and direct analytical calculation. The results are shown in Fig. 2(b), where
the blue curve corresponds to LOCO analysis and the orange curve to the analytical model.
  The comparison demonstrates that the relative error in determining the normal quadrupole compo-
nent is better than 0.1 · k₂, corresponding to a sextupole offset resolution of approximately 30–50 μm.
This level of precision meets the alignment tolerance requirement for NSLS-II. However, the absolute
sextupole error obtained from the analysis exceeds 20% of k₂, indicating that while the method pro-
vides highly precise relative alignment information, additional calibration steps are required to con-
trol the absolute accuracy.

2.1.4 Applying Local Bumps to Enhance Alignment Sensitivity
  The simulation study indicates that LOCO can measure the relative change in sextupole strength k₂
with a precision better than 10% of its nominal value. However, determination of the absolute value
of k₂ is less reliable, with errors as large as 20%, due to intrinsic LOCO parameter degeneracies [6,7].
To overcome this limitation, local orbit bumps are applied to deliberately amplify the alignment sen-
sitivity of sextupole measurements.
The procedures are as follows:
 1. A horizontal local orbit bump is established simultaneously at SH1(U) and SH1(D) using
 user-defined orbit offsets.
 2. The shared power supply of SH1(U) and SH1(D) is scanned in the range of 60% to 140% of
 the nominal setting. At each point: a) the beam orbit is corrected to the target trajectory with
 residual root-mean-square (RMS) error below 1 μm in both transverse planes; b) a complete
 LOCOM data set (LOCOM, dispersion, and orbit) is acquired [1].
 3. The above procedure is repeated for a vertical local bump of ∆𝑦𝑦𝑈𝑈,𝐷𝐷 = 0.5 𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚.
 4. For comparison, the procedure is also performed on the reference trajectory (no bump).
  With orbit reproducibility at the micrometer level, residual errors are further suppressed using a
machine-learning-based orbit correction tool. This ensures that the measured sextupole-induced
quadrupole components reflect true alignment offsets rather than orbit drifts or correction inaccura-
cies.

2.2 Creating High-Precision Local Position Bumps via Machine Learning
  We have developed and applied a novel orbit correction method based on machine learning (ML)
techniques [8]. Conventional orbit feedback algorithms exhibit certain limitations. (i) Linearity: They rely on linear
models, which lead to residual orbit errors persisting even after multiple correction cycles. (ii) Sensitivity to machine
parameter variations: Fluctuations in parameters such as ring temperature or power-supply stability introduce inconsist-
encies in the residual orbit errors, which then propagate throughout the storage ring. These limitations hinder the genera-
tion of high-quality local orbit bumps.
 To overcome these challenges, we employed machine learning, in particular deep reinforcement
learning (DRL), as a framework for orbit correction. DRL is well-suited to this problem because: (1)
it does not require an explicit target variable; (2) it operates on reward signals, which naturally capture
orbit-optimization objectives; (3) the agent autonomously learns strategies to maximize rewards, en-
abling it to handle nonlinear, unstructured system dynamics. These features make DRL an effective
approach for orbit control in complex accelerator environments.
 DRL Training Methodology: Large training datasets were generated using response-matrix simula-
tions. A deep reinforcement neural network was then trained to replicate and generalize the behavior
of the inverse response matrix traditionally used in orbit correction. To further improve accuracy and
robustness, experimentally measured response matrices were incorporated into the training process,
allowing the model to adapt to real machine conditions.
 The trained DRL model was successfully deployed at NSLS-II, where it generated high-quality
horizontal and vertical local orbit bumps at the DW8 straight section. The DRL-based corrections
achieved near-perfect local bumps while suppressing residual orbit oscillations elsewhere in the stor-
age ring. This enabled orbit control at the micrometer level, which is critical for high-precision sex-
tupole beam-based alignment. Figures 3 and 4 illustrate the resulting horizontal and vertical local
bumps, respectively.

2.3 Experimental Results
2.3.1 Extracting Sextupole Alignment Errors
  Horizontal [0.5 mm, 0.5 mm] and vertical [0.5 mm, 0.5 mm] local orbit bumps were applied at the
DW8 straight section. In both cases, the upstream and downstream BPMs located at SH1(U) and
SH1(D) remained stationary, confirming the accuracy of the local bump generation.
  The induced quadrupole fields were then analyzed. Horizontal offsets of the sextupole centers gen-
erated normal quadrupole components, while vertical offsets produced skew quadrupole components.
These components were quantified using LOCOM-based lattice analysis, enabling characterization
of the SH1(U) and SH1(D) sextupoles and determination of their BBA errors.
  Applying this procedure yielded BBA measurements for both SH1(U) and SH1(D) with a precision
of approximately 50 μm. This represents the first successful extraction of sextupole alignment errors
for the SH1 family in the DW8 straight section with this level of accuracy.

3 Conclusions
  A fast and precise sextupole alignment method based on Linear Optics from Closed Orbit Modula-
tion has been developed and experimentally validated at NSLS-II. The technique employs simultane-
ous sine-wave beam excitation at two fast correctors with an optimized phase advance, providing full
coverage of the betatron phase space along the design ellipse. By repeating the LOCOM measurement
four times for horizontal and vertical local position bumps, as well as for the reference trajectory, a
complete set of 12 datasets can be collected in under 25 minutes. With a measurement precision of
0.1 μm, this procedure achieves the required 50 μm BPM-to-sextupole calibration tolerance.
  The integration of LOCOM with machine-learning–generated local orbit bumps enables high-speed
measurements with sub-micrometer accuracy, while ensuring orbit stability across a wide range of
sextupole strength variations. A proof-of-principle experiment at NSLS-II demonstrated that the
method reliably identified sextupole alignment errors within specification, confirming its feasibility
as a robust tool for beam-based alignment.
  A current limitation is the cross-talk among fitting parameters in Linear Optics from Closed Orbits
Modulation, which prevents direct extraction of absolute sextupole k₂ values. Future work will focus
on mitigating this degeneracy through improved algorithms and targeted studies, such as operating
with SH3 and SH4 power supplies deactivated in the DW8 straight section. These refinements are
expected to further enhance the robustness and applicability of the method for routine sextupole align-
ment in storage rings.
