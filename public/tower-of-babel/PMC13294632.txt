The Landscape of Disulfidptosis in Preeclampsia Reveals a Novel 5-Gene Diagnostic Signature via Machine Learning
Authors: Xiangbei Chen, Xuemei Chen, Boen Zhao, Ping Lv, Dong Pang, Lichuan Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePreeclampsia (PE), a pregnancy-specific pathological condition, has shown a growing incidence over recent decades. Disulfidptosis is a newly discovered mode of programmed cell death that differs from traditional cell death pathways in its molecular mechanisms. Numerous studies have reported the association between disulfidptosis and various diseases; however, the role of disulfidptosis in the pathogenesis of PE remains unknown.Patients and MethodsThis study first analyzed the expression patterns of disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs) using the GSE75010 dataset. Based on these results, unsupervised consensus clustering was conducted specifically on the PE samples included in this dataset. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis and machine learning algorithms were utilized to identify hub genes related to PE and disulfidptosis clusters. Ultimately, the expression profiles of these hub genes were validated using the independent datasets GSE4707, GSE30186, and GSE54618, as well as quantitative PCR (qPCR).Results9 DRGs showed abnormal expressions in the PE samples (P < 0.05). Subsequently, two disulfidptosis clusters were identified, each with its own unique functional pathway. Among the four algorithms examined, the support vector machine delivered the most dependable predictions (sensitivity = 0.889). In addition, the genes SASH1, CST6, CCBL1, FSTL3, and SPAG4 were determined as the central genes. A diagnostic model was established using these five genes, The high-fitting of the calibration curve and the positive net benefit of the broad threshold of the DCA decision curve demonstrate that this model has potential good clinical decision-making value. Meanwhile, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis of the external validation dataset indicates that the predictive performance of this model is excellent (AUC =1), the qPCR validation demonstrated that the differences in expression of the five genes in the PE and non-PE controls (NC) samples were statistically significant (P < 0.05). However, the area under the curve (AUC) values of the external validation all being equal to 1 may indicate a problem of overfitting. The sample size verified by qPCR is relatively small, which has statistical limitations.ConclusionThis study proposes a new diagnostic model for PE, which can serve as a framework for studying disease heterogeneity and provides a basis for understanding the role of disulfidptosis in the occurrence of PE.Keywords: disulfidptosis-related-genes, hub genes, WGCNA, SVM

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is a gestation-linked disorder characterized by the emergence of hypertension plus either proteinuria or systemic organ compromise beyond the 20th week of pregnancy.1 Globally, PE develops in roughly 2–8% of all pregnancies.2 The condition is associated with a significant increase in the risk of life-threatening complications such as cerebral hemorrhage, pulmonary edema, coronary heart disease, acute kidney injury, liver failure or rupture, disseminated intravascular coagulation, eclampsia, and placental abruption.3,4 Meanwhile, PE also imposes a major burden on clinical practice and the economy. The latest research at the genetic level indicates a correlation between PE and decreased total circulating free DNA (cfDNA), as well as fetal fraction.5 However, due to the clinical heterogeneity and complex genomic classification, the diagnostic methods and therapeutic options for PE remain unsatisfactory. At present, sFlt-1 and placental growth factor (PlGF) have been applied in the screening and diagnosis of suspected preeclampsia in clinical practice. Both have a relatively high negative predictive value and can effectively rule out the possibility of disease in healthy pregnant women, showing significant advantages in disease screening. However, this type of marker has obvious shortcomings. Its sensitivity to confirming positive cases of preeclampsia is relatively low, making it difficult to achieve precise diagnosis, and its clinical diagnostic efficacy has significant limitations. Moreover, owing to the small number of cohorts and a lack of bioinformatics resources, the research on biomarkers related to PE has also been limited. This highlights the necessity of identifying new biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Disulfidptosis is a newly discovered form of regulated cell death that occurs in glucose-starved, SLC7A11-overexpressing cells, characterized by NADPH depletion and subsequent cytoskeletal collapse.6,7 Specifically, the high SLC7A11 expression and the depletion of NADPH are the core processes of disulfidptosis. These processes inhibit the conversion between cysteine and cystine, causing the accumulation of disulfide bonds, thereby triggering disulfide stress, and ultimately leading to cytoskeletal collapse.8 Previous studies have revealed that disulfidptosis is associated with a variety of diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease,9 Parkinson’s disease,10 Metabolic cancer,11 and gastrointestinal tumors.12 However, no definite research has elucidated the relationship between disulfidptosis and PE. Based on this, this study conducts an exploratory analysis to preliminarily investigate the potential connection between the two.

This study first profiled disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs) expression and identified differentially expressed DRGs between PE and NC. Subsequently, the PE training-set samples were isolated, and the differentially expressed DRGs were used to drive consensus clustering. Our results demonstrated that these PE samples could be divided into two disulfidptosis clusters, each characterized by unique pathway enrichments. Thereafter, using the Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) algorithm, key genes related to the disulfidptosis clusters were identified. An intersection analysis was conducted with PE-associated key genes to determine those shared between the PE module and disulfidptosis clusters. Four distinct machine learning approaches were compared, leading to the development of a diagnostic model for PE. The performance and stability of this model in the diagnosis of PE were determined using independent validation datasets GSE54618, GSE4707, and GSE30186. Finally, this model was evaluated using quantitative PCR (qPCR). The research flowchart is shown in Figure 1.

Materials and Methods

Data Collection and Sample-Specific Details

Four publicly available datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Among them, GSE75010, including tissue samples from 80 PE patients and 77 NC, was used as the training dataset to establish the diagnostic model for PE. In this dataset, four tissue biopsies were collected from each placenta (one sample/quadrant, excluding the chorionic plate). These samples were rinsed in PBS to remove contaminated maternal blood, pooled together, crushed into powder, and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, thereby obtaining the placental tissue RNA for each sample. Three independent datasets, GSE54618, GSE4707, and GSE30186, were employed as validation datasets to evaluate the predictive ability of the diagnostic model. All three datasets contain placenta tissue information from both PE patients and healthy controls. All gene expression profiles were normalized using log2 transformation to eliminate batch effect and systematic bias.

Identification and Analysis of DRGs

To identify differentially expressed DRGs, a comprehensive review of existing literature on disulfidptosis was conducted. Subsequently, an extensive search of previously reported DRGs was conducted from the literature13 and the GeneCards database (http://www.genecards.org/), yielding a total of 33 DRGs. Next, the “limma” R package was used to identify differentially expressed DRGs between PE and NC samples.14 The results were visualized using the “ggpubr” and “pheatmap” R packages. Moreover, the relationships among the differentially expressed DRGs were illustrated through the “circlize” R package.15

Classification of PE Samples Using Consensus Clustering

The “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package was employed to carry out cluster analysis of PE samples,16 utilizing DRG expression levels within the training dataset as the basis. The optimal number of clusters was determined by consensus matrix plots, consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) plots, and trace plots. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then conducted to visualize the distribution of the relevant patterns, with the clustering results being primarily concentrated in the first two principal components.

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA)

The “GSVA” R package17 was employed to conduct enrichment analysis, aiming to explore the biological functions and pathways associated with various clusters. This analysis utilized 2 gene set: “c5.go.symbols” and “c2.cp.kegg.symbols.gmt”, which were sourced from the Molecular Signature Database (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). Differences in GSVA enrichment scores between clusters were assessed using t-tests, with Benjamini-Hochberg correction applied to adjust p-values for multiple comparisons. Adjusted P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. These gene sets represent a collection of gene ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways that are essential for understanding cellular processes and molecular functions. The most significantly enriched pathways (P < 0.05, Student’s t-test) were visualized using bar plots. In these plots, up-regulated pathways were highlighted in red, while down-regulated pathways were marked in blue, providing a clear visual representation of the differential enrichment between clusters.

Utilizing WGCNA for the Detection of Key Genes and Their Association with PE-Related Traits

To identify key genes relevant to PE, WGCNA analysis was performed using the R package “WGCNA”, aiming to explore the relationship between gene modules and disease-specific traits. First, the top 25% of genes with the highest variance from the GSE75010 dataset were selected for further analysis. Hierarchical clustering was conducted on the PE samples, and outlier samples were removed. A similarity matrix was established by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient, which was then turned into an adjacency matrix and a topological overlap matrix (TOM) using a proper soft threshold power. A dynamic tree-cutting algorithm with a minimum module size of 50 genes was applied to divide the genes into distinct co-expression modules. Hub genes were defined by gene significance (GS) > 0.5 and module membership (MM) > 0.8. Each module was assigned a unique color, and the eigengene profile of each module represented the global gene expression within that module.

Development and Validation of a Machine Learning-Based Diagnostic Model for PE

Utilizing the R-based “WGCNA” package, cross-analysis was performed on genes from the most significant module to identify key genes with diagnostic potential for particular aspects of PE. Subsequently, machine learning algorithms were applied to construct the diagnostic model. Specifically, the “kernlab”, “randomForest”, and “xgboost” packages were employed to establish four distinct machine learning models and determine the importance of these key genes. In these analyses, the disease phenotype was designated as the dependent variable, and the gene set identified by WGCNA served as the primary variable. Thereafter, the “caret” R package was utilized to construct the machine learning models, and model interpretability was explored using the “DALEX” R package. To identify the best-performing diagnostic model, cumulative residual distribution maps and residual box plots were generated using the plot function. Model performance was evaluated using the “pROC” R package. The model was further refined by selecting the five most crucial features. Finally, the diagnostic accuracy of the model was validated using three additional independent datasets. All machine learning models were constructed using the caret package in R (version 4.2.1). The training dataset and the validation datasets were randomly split into a training subset (70%) and an internal test subset (30%) using createDataPartition with a fixed seed (set. seed (123)) to ensure reproducibility. To assess model stability and mitigate overfitting, 5-fold repeated cross-validation (repeatedcv, n=5) was performed on the training subset using trainControl (method=“repeatedcv”, number=5, savePredictions=TRUE). Hyperparameter tuning was conducted via caret’s default grid search strategy, where the optimal combination of parameters was selected based on cross-validation performance to balance model complexity and generalization.

Blood Sample Verification

A total of 10 blood samples were collected from the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region People’s Hospital, including 5 samples from PE patients and 5 control samples. All the PE patients included in the experiment did not have any other pregnancy-related diseases. The control group consisted of pregnant women of similar age without preeclampsia or any other obstetric or internal medical complications. All the subjects excluded factors such as known genetic diseases, pre-pregnancy diabetes, and chronic hypertension that might affect the experimental results. This experiment has been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region People’s Hospital (Approval number: KY-ZC-2023-160). DNA was extracted from blood samples using the Ezup Column Blood Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China; Ref: B518253-0050). Subsequently, qPCR was performed using the 2×Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (YUNBIO, Kunming, China; Ref: QP101A) on a LightCycler® 96 instrument (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Switzerland). The β-Actin gene was amplified as an internal control. The primer sequences used are shown in Additional file 1: Supplementary table 1.

Statistical Analysis

After conducting data analysis, the expression levels of SASH1, CST6, and FSTL3 in both the PE group and the NC group were all normally distributed. Therefore, the unpaired t-tests and Welch t-tests were used. However, the expression levels of SPAG4 and CCBL1 did not fully follow a normal distribution, so the Mann–Whitney test was employed. Statistical analyses were conducted using Graphpad Prism 10.5. In this study, P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Aberrant Expression Pattern of DRGs in PE

Previously reported DRGs were retrieved from literature and public databases, identifying a total of 33 DRGs. To investigate the expression profiles of these DRGs in PE, a total of 80 PE samples and 77 NC samples were analyzed in the GSE75010 dataset after log2 transformation. The results revealed that 9 of the 33 DRGs were differentially expressed in PE samples. The expression levels of FLNB, GYS1, IQGAP1, RPN1, and TM9SF2 were significantly enhanced (P < 0.001) in PE samples, whereas the levels of ABI2, CAPZB, NDUFS1, and SLC7A11 were downregulated (P <0.05) (Figure 2A). The overall expression patterns of these differential DRGs between PE and NC tissues were visualized by a heatmap (Figure 2B). Their chromosomal distributions are presented in Figure 2C. In addition, correlation analysis revealed significant associations among these DRGs (Figure 2D), suggesting potential mutual regulatory relationships.

Construction of Unsupervised Disulfidptosis Clusters in PE Samples Using ConsensusClusterPlus

To investigate the distinct expression patterns of DRGs in PE, the ConsensusClusterPlus package was applied to classify the 80 PE samples from the training dataset.18 Using the consensus matrix visualizations, the optimal cluster count was determined to be k=2 (Figure 3A), supported by minimal fluctuations in the CDF curves across consensus indices (Figure 3B), and further corroborated by the stable trace plot (Figure 3C). Additionally, both clusters demonstrated a consistency score exceeding 0.8 when k=2 (Figure 3D). Consequently, the 80 PE samples were stratified into two distinct disulfidptosis clusters: Cluster 1 (C1, n=45) and Cluster 2 (C2, n=35). PCA provided further evidence to verify the distinct separation observed between the two clusters (Figure 3E).

Consensus clustering analysis of differentially expressed DRGs in PE samples. (A) The samples were divided into two clusters when k = 2. (B) CDF curves illustrating variations across various consensus indices. (C) Trace plot illustrating clustering stability across different k values. (D) Consistency scores for clustering solutions with k values ranging from 2 to 9. (E) PCA showing the distribution of the two disulfidptosis clusters identified by unsupervised consensus clustering.

To characterize the molecular features of these disulfidptosis clusters, a systematic comparative analysis was performed. Among the 9 previously identified DRGs, 5 exhibited significant differential expression between C1 and C2 (P <0.05) (Figure 4A). A heatmap was generated to illustrate the relative expression patterns of all 9 DRGs across the two clusters (Figure 4B). Furthermore, GSVA was conducted to explore pathway differences between the clusters (Figure 4C). After FDR correction, pathways with a p-value < 0.05 are considered to be significantly enriched. The results revealed the upregulated pathways in the C2 cluster (compared to C1) mainly involve carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism, including galactose metabolism, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, and starch and sucrose metabolism. These pathways play critical roles in maintaining cellular redox balance and antioxidant defense, processes closely linked to the regulation of disulfidptosis via reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis. In contrast, the pathways that were downregulated in C2 were mainly related to oxidative phosphorylation and energy metabolism, such as the citric acid cycle and pyruvate metabolism. These changes are consistent with the mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired energy production in the placenta of preeclampsia, which may exacerbate the susceptibility to oxidative stress and disulfidptosis perturbation. Collectively, these findings highlight distinct molecular signatures between the two disulfidptosis clusters and provide insight into the potential mechanisms underlying their association with PE.

Construction of Co-Expression Networks and Identification of Disulfidptosis-Related Hub Genes in PE

To pinpoint key genes linked to PE, the WGCNA algorithm was utilized to construct co-expression networks and corresponding modules. The top 25% most variable genes were selected from the GSE75010 dataset, and outlier samples were excluded. A scale-free network was then established using a soft-thresholding power of β = 10, achieving a scale-free topology fit index (R2) of 0.9 (Figure 5A and B). A total of 15 separate co-expression modules were detected, with the turquoise module showing the most robust correlation with PE (R = 0.66, P = 6e-21; Figure 5C). A total of 62 genes originating from this module were selected for further analysis. The scatter plot revealed a marked positive correlation between module membership and gene significance within the turquoise module (cor = 0.9, P < 1e-200) (Figure 5D).

Construction of the co-expression network and identification of PE-related modules using WGCNA. (A) Scale-free topology model fit index under different soft-thresholding powers. (B) Clustering dendrogram of gene co-expression, with colors representing distinct modules. (C) Correlation heatmap showing associations between co-expression modules and clinical traits. (D) Scatter plot showing the correlation between module membership and gene significance in the turquoise module related to PE.

In a subsequent analysis, the WGCNA algorithm was employed to identify key genes associated with disulfidptosis clusters in PE within the same dataset. A scale-free network was constructed with a soft-thresholding power of β = 14 and an R2 value of 0.9 (Figure 6A and B). The grey module exhibited the highest correlation with the disulfidptosis clusters (R = 0.51, P = 2e-06; Figure 6C), and 39 genes from this module were selected for further analyses (Figure 6D). Next, a cross-comparison was performed between the gene sets derived from both analyses. The results identified 14 overlapping genes shared between PE-associated module genes and disulfidptosis cluster-associated module genes (Figure 7A). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these shared genes were primarily enriched in biological processes such as MAP kinase activity, cadherin binding, fibronectin binding, and myeloid leukocyte differentiation (Figure 7B). Similarly, Kyoto Encyclopedia of KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were predominantly involved in adhesion molecule disorders and activation of the HIF-1 signaling pathway (Figure 7C). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these genes are mainly involved in focal adhesion, cell-substrate junction, peroxisome and HIF-1 signaling pathways. These pathways play a role in the pathogenesis of preeclampsia (by regulating the function of trophoblast cells and placental hypoxia) and disulfidptosis (by regulating the redox balance and stress response of cells).

Development and Validation of a Five-Gene Diagnostic Model for PE via Machine Learning

To further analyze the 14 candidate genes identified by WGCNA and to screen for hub genes with diagnostic potential for PE, four machine learning algorithms were applied, including Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), and Generalized Linear Model (GLM). Diagnostic models were constructed using 70% of the GSE75010 samples as the training dataset. The cumulative residual distribution plots (Figure 8A) and residual boxplots (Figure 8B) demonstrated that the SVM, XGB, and RF models exhibited relatively lower residual values, indicating better model fitting and stability. The top 10 variables ranked by root mean square error (RMSE) for each model are shown in Figure 8C.

To assess diagnostic performance, the remaining 30% of the GSE75010 samples were used as a test set to generate ROC curves (Figure 8D). All four machine learning models demonstrated excellent classification performance, with AUC values exceeding 0.85. Considering both the comprehensive predictive capability and model stability, the SVM model was selected as the optimal diagnostic tool for PE. From this model, five key genes — SASH1, CST6, CCBL1, FSTL3, and SPAG4 — were identified as hub genes, which were used to establish a five-gene diagnostic model for PE.

Furthermore, a nomogram was constructed, incorporating the five genes SASH1, CST6, CCBL1, FSTL3, and SPAG4 (Additional file 2: Figure S1A). The calibration curve demonstrated strong agreement between the model’s predictions and observed outcomes, supporting its high predictive accuracy (Additional file 2: Figure S1B). Additionally, the DCA results confirmed the substantial clinical utility of the model for supporting decision-making (Additional file2: Figure S1C).

To validate the predictive performance of this five-gene model, independent external validation was performed using the GSE54618, GSE4707, and GSE30186 datasets. ROC analyses demonstrated perfect classification performance in all three external datasets, with AUC values of 1 (Figure 8E–G). These results underscored the robust discriminative power, stability, and strong clinical potential of the constructed diagnostic model.

Validation of Diagnostic Markers

qPCR was used to detect the expression levels of the five genes in 10 peripheral blood samples (five PE samples and five control samples). The results revealed that CST6 and CCBL1 expression were significantly increased in the PE group (P < 0.05), whereas lower expressions of SASH1, FSTL3, and SPAG4 were observed compared to those in the NC group (P < 0.01) (Figure 9). These findings indicate that the hub genes play a crucial role in disulfidptosis-related processes in PE. However, the underlying mechanism of their action needs further investigation.

Discussion

PE affects 3–8% of pregnancies and is a major contributor to both immediate and long-term health risks for mothers and their newborns, as well as a significant cause of mortality.19 Biomarkers like sFlt-1 and placental growth factor (PlGF) are increasingly employed to facilitate the diagnosis of PE in cases with clinical suspicion. While their high negative predictive value reliably excludes the disease in women with normal levels, their sensitivity for confirming positive cases is limited.20 Hence, novel diagnostic strategies are required to advance the detection rate. In addition, understanding the mechanism underlying PE and formulating targeted intervention measures is essential to improve patient prognosis. This study aims to explore the relevant factors of PE and its pathological mechanism, and search for new diagnostic markers of PE.

We systematically analyzed the transcriptome profiles of PE and NC samples to determine the abnormal expression patterns of DRGs in PE, thereby discovering the potential connection of disulfidptosis in the pathogenesis of PE. We found that the mechanism of PE formation might be related to the correlation of disulfidptosis in metabolic pathways. In the pathway enrichment differences between the two disulfidptosis clusters, the upregulation of redox-related metabolic pathways in Cluster C2 indicates that the enhancement of the cellular stress response may prevent or promote disulfidptosis through the balance between antioxidant capacity and ROS generation. Conversely, the suppression of oxidative phosphorylation pathways aligns with the known placental bioenergetic defects in PE, which could further sensitize cells to disulfidptosis under hypoxic and oxidative stress conditions. These mechanisms further reveal the potential connection between PE and disulfidptosis, and highlight potential therapeutic targets for modulating disulfidptosis in PE. Furthermore, the functional enrichment of the key genes suggests that these key genes may promote the production of PE by regulating signaling pathways. Specifically, the functional enrichment of the key genes is highly expressed in focal adhesion, cell-substrate junction, peroxisome, and HIF-1 signaling pathways. This is consistent with the pathogenesis of PE, which occurs through regulating the function of trophoblast cells and placental hypoxia, and the regulation of cellular redox balance and stress response by disulfidptosis. Thus, it indicates the potential mechanism of the key genes in PE and disulfidptosis.

Machine learning frameworks have emerged as a typical channel for screening and interpreting large amounts of bioinformatics data.21 Machine learning enables the simultaneous analysis of multiple variables, such as clinical and laboratory data, and automatically selects the feature with the largest amount of information. In recent years, machine learning has been increasingly used in the prediction of PE. For decades, hypertension and proteinuria have served as the primary screening tools for identifying patients at risk of adverse outcomes from PE.22 However, these traditional diagnostic criteria have a low positive predictive value of merely 20–30% for complications.23 Therefore, new biomarkers should be developed for more accurate and early detection of PE, thereby improving the prognosis of PE patients. Genetic diagnostic models offer novel perspectives on predicting a wide range of diseases in clinical practice. The expression matrices of PE patients from the training set were integrated, and feature gene expressions from all PE samples were extracted to construct the initial matrix for consensus clustering. The optimal number of clusters was identified to improve model stability and prevent overfitting, thereby facilitating the exploration of potential DRGs. Next, a comparative analysis of the four machine learning algorithms was performed to build a PE diagnostic model. The results indicated that the SVM approach outperformed its counterparts, achieving the highest levels of stability and predictive accuracy. The five most important variables, including SASH1, CST6, CCBL1, FSTL3, and SPAG4, were used as hub genes for further analysis. The five-gene diagnostic model exhibited robust performance and strong generalizability across independent validation cohorts and qPCR validation, highlighting its potential clinical utility. The SASH1 gene is a member of the SLY family of signal adapter proteins.24 Some studies have employed RNA sequencing, revealing that SASH1 is upregulated in the placenta of patients with PE, indicating its role in PE.25 HTR-8/SVneo is a trophoblast cell line characterized by its progenitor-like phenotype. The model developed by Graham et al26 is extensively utilized to mimic and investigate the invasive behavior of trophoblasts.27 Liu et al discovered that the proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of cells were significantly reduced after overexpressing SASH1 in the human trophoblast cell line HTR-8/SVneo, whereas the apoptosis rate increased.28 These results suggest that SASH1 may inhibit the invasion and migration abilities of trophoblast cells. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that insufficient invasion of trophoblast cells can lead to inadequate vascular remodeling, thereby causing PE.29 However, our qPCR research results indicated a downward trend in the expression of SASH1 in the peripheral blood of PE patients. Still, whether the different expression of SASH1 in the placental tissues and blood of PE patients indicates a potential relationship between them remains to be further investigated. CCBL1 is a key metabolic enzyme in the human body. Aminooxyacetic acid (AOAA) acts as an antagonist of cysteine conjugate β-lyase (CCBL) within the glutathione metabolic pathway.30 Anthony et al discovered that in a rat model exposed to trichloroethylene, inhibition of CCBL activity by AOAA led to a decrease in the activity of γ-glutamyl transferase.31 This effectively reduces the circulating level of cysteine.32 Interestingly, the total plasma cysteine is positively correlated with PE, very low birth weight, and preterm birth.33 Therefore, reducing systemic cysteine by inhibiting the activity of CCBL may reduce the occurrence of PE. However, research found that this effect is more likely to be caused by the inhibition of CCBL in non-placental tissues; yet, whether CCBL-like genes in placental tissue affect PE remains uncertain. FSTL3, located on chromosome 19p13, is a follistatin-module protein family member.34 Sandra et al reported reduced FSTL3 expression in the placental tissue of pregnant women with PE during the early stage of pregnancy.35 In contrast, Xie et al demonstrated enhanced FSTL3 expression in the serum and tissues of patients with PE.36 These controversial results might be attributed to the differences in the gestational stage and fetal age of the patients during the sampling process. Biron-Shental et al reported significantly enhanced FSTL3 expression in trophoblasts under hypoxic culture conditions.37 Whether these conditions are related to the differences of FSTL3 in the early and late stages of PE requires further investigation. Cystatin 6 (CST6), a member of the type 2 cystatin family, is a cysteine protease inhibitor located on the surface of the placenta. Notably, CST6 may possibly be released into the maternal circulation.38 Legumain (LGMN) is a cysteine protease mainly located in the lysosomal system39 that may participate in regulating autophagy. One research indicated that CST6 plays a key role in regulating the proteolytic activity of LGMN.39,40 The protein cystatin E/M, encoded by the CST6 gene, suppresses the activity of two classes of cysteine proteases via separate binding mechanisms. Its targets include papain-like enzymes—such as cathepsins B, L, and V—as well as LGMN.41 Through this dual inhibition, cystatin E/M reduces the proteolytic erosiveness associated with LGMN, affecting cellular autophagy. The research revealed that the cellular autophagy process is dysfunctional in PE.42 These findings indicate that CST6 is highly likely to affect the autophagy process of cells by regulating the proteolytic activity of LGMN, leading to the occurrence of PE. SPAG4, a SUN domain protein first identified in the mammalian sperm tail, also serves as a clinically relevant cancer biomarker.43 Recent studies demonstrate that SPAG4 depletion attenuates the invasive and migratory capacities of cancer cells, whereas its elevated expression promotes these malignant behaviors.44 This suggests that SPAG4 also exerts a regulatory effect on the invasion and migration abilities of placental trophoblast cells. However, the association between this gene and PE remains unknown.

Nevertheless, the limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. At present, the mechanisms between PE and disulfidptosis DNA death are not well understood, and related research is still in its infancy. Further experiments are needed to elaborate on the detailed mechanisms between them in the future. The high AUC values observed in both internal and external validation (approaching 1.0) likely reflect the strong discriminative power of the selected DRGs. However, such near-perfect performance may also be indicative of potential overfitting risk, particularly given the relatively small sample size in the training cohort and the limited number of external validation datasets. Future studies with larger, multi-center cohorts are needed to further validate the robustness and generalizability of the model. Another limitation of this study lies in the relatively small sample size for the qPCR verification. The limited sample quantity may reduce the statistical power of the verification results. Considering that this work is a preliminary exploratory study on the characteristics related to disulfidptosis in preeclampsia, we will recruit more clinical samples in subsequent studies for a larger-scale verification to further confirm our findings. Subsequently, to explore the clinical value of this five - gene model, our qPCR validation utilized peripheral blood samples, which exhibit certain differences from the gene expression in placental tissues within the training set. Therefore, the blood - based results should be regarded as exploratory rather than the final validation of placental characteristics. Future research requires matched placental tissue samples to confirm these findings at the primary disease site. Additionally, the concentrations of genes in the 5-gene diagnostic model for different types of preeclampsia may vary, the research on PE can be further refined in terms of the disease course, and can be analyzed separately by distinguishing between early-stage and late-stage disease, as well as between mild and severe PE cases.

Conclusion

Consensus clustering identified two clusters related to disulfidptosis in the PE samples. Each cluster had its own unique enriched pathways. Using the SVM algorithm, a diagnostic model for PE was established, and five key genes were identified. The model exhibits favorable performance in independent validation and peripheral blood qPCR. As an exploratory bioinformatics study, the central genes of this model may serve as candidate biomarkers. These findings have enhanced our understanding of the heterogeneity of PE and provided new ideas for the diagnosis of PE.
