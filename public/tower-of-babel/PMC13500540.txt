Incremental model predictive control of PMSM based on parameter tuning of multi-layer perceptron neural network and disturbance observer
Authors: Guangyong Yu, Zhiqiang Wu, Yuzhi Wang, Yuliang Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To enhance the control performance of permanent magnet synchronous motors (PMSM) under complex operating conditions such as sudden load changes and parameter perturbations caused by strong external disturbances, this paper proposes an incremental model predictive control (IMPC) strategy based on a disturbance observer (DOB), referred to as DOB-IMPC. Firstly, an incremental predictive model of PMSM is constructed, taking advantage of its inherent integral characteristic to improve the system’s robustness. Secondly, a DOB is introduced to estimate the aggregated disturbances, including load disturbances and parameter uncertainties in real time, and the estimated values are fed forward to compensate for the predictive model. Subsequently, an internal optimization process of IMPC is designed based on this model, and the optimal control input sequence is obtained by solving a constrained quadratic programming problem. For the parameter setting of the IMPC itself and the additional tuning burden introduced by the DOB, conventional approaches relying on empirical expertise and repeated trial-and-error are inefficient and cannot readily guarantee optimal control performance. This paper develops a parameter-tuning mechanism based on a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) neural network, which integrates the real-time state variables of the PMSM with the overall structural information of the DOB-IMPC framework to achieve online tuning of key control parameters. Simulation and experimental results demonstrate that, compared with conventionally manually tuned MPC, the MLP-based tuning strategy reduces the speed root mean square error (RMSE) by 46.2% and the maximum speed fluctuation by 29.0% while maintaining zero overshoot. With the further incorporation of the DOB, the maximum speed fluctuation under sudden load disturbance is further reduced by 31.2% compared with MPC, and the speed RMSE under parameter perturbation conditions is reduced by 75.8%. Collectively, these results confirm that the proposed strategy delivers measurable gains in dynamic response, disturbance rejection, and parametric resilience—without compromising stability or implementation feasibility.

Introduction

Motivation

Permanent magnet synchronous motors (PMSMs) are widely employed in high-performance electric drive applications, owing to their high efficiency, power density, and rapid dynamic response1–5. Field-oriented control (FOC) continues to serve as the prevailing control architecture in PMSM servo systems, offering a mature and widely validated framework for torque and flux regulation6–8. Within FOC frameworks, proportional–integral–derivative (PID) control continues to serve as the most prevalent inner-loop strategy—largely due to its simplicity, robustness under nominal conditions, and straightforward implementation. Yet PID tuning remains sensitive to operating point shifts, and anti-windup handling of current limits is typically implemented via fixed saturation boundaries or output clamping—a passive, non-adaptive approach that offers limited flexibility under transient or heavily loaded conditions9. Moreover, parameter variations (e.g., in stator resistance or inductance) and unmeasured load disturbances degrade the performance of linear PID controllers, often resulting in compromised tracking accuracy and sluggish disturbance rejection10. To address these limitations, nonlinear and adaptive strategies—such as sliding mode control11, model reference adaptive control12, and neural network–based disturbance compensation13—have received growing attention in PMSM servo applications. While such methods demonstrate improvements in transient response and steady-state precision over conventional PID, they frequently introduce new challenges: chattering in sliding mode implementations, convergence guarantees in adaptive laws, or computational overhead and training dependency in learning-based approaches.

Model Predictive Control (MPC), as an advanced control method, has certain advantages, such as the improvement of speed response. The main principle is to predict the future behavior of state variables through the drive system model14–16. With the rapid development of computer hardware and the progress in MPC research, the model predictive control method is increasingly applicable to the application of electric drive systems with high real-time requirements17–19. However, throughout the entire control process, the temperature fluctuations of PMSM and the changes in the saturation of the stator winding can lead to a mismatch of parameters related to inductance and stator resistance, which may result in inaccurate prediction outcomes and ultimately affect the system performance20. In addition, significant variations in load torque are common, requiring robust control systems to maintain optimal response performance. With challenges like model parameter variations and external load disturbances emerging, more researchers are focusing on robust MPC control techniques21. Meanwhile, the performance of MPC largely depends on the tuning of its internal parameters. However, effective parameter tuning methods remain unclear, and engineers need to adjust them through repeated trial-and-error processes22,23. Therefore, how to optimize and adjust the internal parameters of MPC more efficiently has also become one of the important research directions at present.

Related research

Robust control and observer-based compensation have gained traction in high-performance PMSM control. Reference24 combines sliding mode control with an observer to improve robustness against disturbances and parameter variations. This study verified the effectiveness of combining observer compensation with robust control in PMSM control, but its focus was on the sliding mode observer control framework. The problem of embedding compensation for lumped disturbances and the tuning of composite control parameters in the predictive control framework has not yet been further addressed.

Reference25 integrates model predictive control (MPC) with an extended state observer (ESO) for robust speed regulation. However, its performance is highly sensitive to PMSM parameter mismatches and demands high sampling rates—limiting practical implementation. Reference26 deploys a load disturbance observer in the outer speed loop and stator flux/torque observers in the inner current loop to estimate disturbances and parameters online. While this improves robustness against uncertainties, it introduces significant design coupling and structural complexity. Reference27 proposes deadbeat current predictive control to suppress parameter sensitivity; yet it still requires two independent observers, adding computational and tuning overhead. Reference28 enhances IMPC with an integral term to attenuate steady-state disturbances. Nevertheless, exclusive reliance on integral action degrades transient response—especially under abrupt load changes or modeling errors—thereby compromising both dynamic accuracy and robustness.

The disturbance observer (DOB) method offers a comprehensive solution, as it enables linear disturbance estimation and can be directly embedded into the MPC prediction framework29. In30, the DOB was integrated into a robust MPC framework—formulated via linear matrix inequalities (LMIs)—for the speed control of PMSMs under parametric uncertainties31 proposed a high-gain DOB embedded in the predictive current controller to enhance the robustness of PMSMs against parameter perturbations.

In32, a disturbance observer-based model predictive control (DOB-MPC) strategy was proposed for PMSM drive systems. By incorporating lumped disturbance estimation into the predictive model, this method achieves feedforward compensation for multiple disturbances and effectively enhances the anti-disturbance capability of the control system. However, this compound control structure involves a considerable number of tunable parameters, and the coupling between these parameters increases the difficulty of parameter tuning. Reference32, however, does not include a systematic parameter adjustment method to address this issue.

In33, a deep reinforcement learning (DRL)-based method was introduced to adjust weighting parameters over a specified time interval, thereby reducing the workload of engineers to some extent. However, this method still exhibits certain similarities to manual tuning by engineers, as multiple preliminary trials are typically required before suitable parameters can be obtained, which limits its convenience for engineering implementation. The effectiveness of neural networks in modeling complex nonlinear systems, state observation, and control-parameter tuning has been widely validated, making them one of the major research directions in intelligent control. To date, most studies integrating control systems with neural networks have primarily employed neural networks to emulate controller behavior, rather than directly estimating the key internal parameters that define the controller structure34,35. For instance36, introduced an adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system (ANFIS) in place of the PI adaptation loop in a conventional MRAS, leading to improved speed and torque estimation accuracy for sensorless induction motor control. In37, an online-trained radial basis function (RBF) neural network was incorporated into the FOC/MRAS framework for induction motors to enhance estimation performance under parameter variations and load disturbances. In38, an RNN was combined with a nonlinear adaptive observer and passivity-based control to compensate for unmodeled dynamics and parameter uncertainties, thereby enhancing the robustness of induction motors under dynamic operating conditions. Overall, although these methods have achieved favorable performance in state observation, online adaptive compensation, or intelligent predictive control, they have not specifically addressed the rapid selection and tuning of internal parameters in composite IMPC controllers.

Unlike most existing studies that use neural networks to emulate controller behavior or replace observer structures, this work retains the original optimization structure of composite IMPC and the disturbance-rejection mechanism of the DOB, while introducing an MLP to rapidly tune the key internal parameters of the controller. Rolling optimization

To ensure a smooth transition of the system’s actual output to the expected value and avoid sharp fluctuations, it is usually necessary to introduce a smooth transition curve between the output and the expected value, which is called the reference trajectory. In this section, a first-order exponential function is adopted to design the reference trajectory:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {y_{r} (k + i) = \alpha^{i} y(k) + (1 - \alpha^{i} )y^{*} (k)} \\ {\alpha = e^{ - T/\tau } } \\ \end{array} } \right.$$\end{document}

In the equation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{r} (k)$$\end{document} represents the reference trajectory, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y^{*} (k)$$\end{document} is the expected output value, y(k) is the actual output value, the parameter τ is the time constant of the reference trajectory, and α is the softening factor. Equation (20) can be written in vector form as follows:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_{r} = \left[ {\begin{array}{*{20}l} {y_{r} (k + 1)} \hfill & {y_{r} (k + 2)} \hfill & \cdots \hfill & {y_{r} (k + N_{p} )} \hfill \\ \end{array} } \right]$$\end{document}

The objective of MPC is to solve for the optimal control sequence by minimizing the deviation between the given reference output sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_{r}$$\end{document} and the actual output sequence Y. Therefore, it is necessary to first define the quadratic performance index function:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{gathered} J = (Y_{p} + E_{p} - Y_{r} )^{T} Q(Y_{p} + E_{p} - Y_{r} ) \\ + (\Delta U - \Delta U^{s} )^{T} R(\Delta U - \Delta U^{s} ) \\ \end{gathered}$$\end{document}

Among them, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q = diag(q_{1} ,q_{2} , \cdots ,q_{{N_{p} }} )$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R = diag(r_{1} ,r_{2} , \cdots ,r_{{N_{c} }} )$$\end{document} are respectively used to adjust the influence of the output tracking error and the control increment on the performance index function. The larger the elements of Q, the stronger the controller’s penalty on the reference speed tracking error; the larger the elements of R, the more obvious the controller’s suppression effect on the control input variation. In this paper, the values of Q and R are comprehensively considered based on the system’s dynamic response speed, overshoot, and the smoothness of the control input and are determined through simulation comparisons.

Furthermore, the performance index function shown in (23) is in the form of a standard quadratic type. When Q is a semi-positive definite matrix and R is a positive definite matrix, the objective function is convex with respect to the control increment sequence Δ U. Particularly, since R takes a positive definite diagonal form, the optimization problem has a unique optimal solution. If the input constraints and control increment constraints form a non-empty convex set, then this optimization problem is feasible within the standard linear MPC framework.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta U^{s}$$\end{document} is the expected sequence of control increments at the system’s steady state. Based on the disturbance estimate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{d}(k)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta U^{s}$$\end{document} can be calculated as:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta U^{s} = V_{u}^{ + } [Y_{r} - V_{x} x_{r} (k) - V_{d} \Delta d_{L} ]$$\end{document}

In the formula, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{r} (k)$$\end{document} represents the expected state vector, expressed as:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{r} (k) = \left[ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {\Delta i_{qr} (k)} \\ {\Delta \omega_{r} (k)} \\ {\omega_{r} (k)} \\ \end{array} } \right] = \left[ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} { - \frac{{3n_{p} \psi }}{2J}(\hat{d}_{2} (k) - \hat{d}_{2} (k - 1))} \\ 0 \\ {\omega_{r} } \\ \end{array} } \right] \cdot V_{u}^{ + } = (V_{u}^{T} V_{u} )^{ - 1} V_{u}^{T}$$\end{document} is the pseudo-inverse of matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{u}$$\end{document}.

Furthermore, the second term of the performance index function introduces compensation for the expected control increment sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta U^{s}$$\end{document}. When there are parameter perturbations or load disturbances, as long as the disturbance observer can precisely estimate the interference, zero steady-state error tracking of the expected rotational speed can be achieved.

Solving under constraints

After defining the performance index function J, the optimal control increment sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta U^{s}$$\end{document} is solved by minimizing this function. Meanwhile, during the optimization process, current and voltage constraints are introduced in combination with the actual operating conditions to ensure the safe operation of the motor.

Therefore, the above-mentioned optimization solution process can be described as a quadratic programming problem with constraints, which can be solved by using the Lagrange duality principle.

To solve the above-mentioned constrained optimization problem, the Lagrange multiplier \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda \ge 0$$\end{document} is introduced and the Lagrange function is constructed as follows:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L(\Delta U,\lambda ) = \frac{1}{2}\Delta U^{T} H\Delta U + \Delta U^{T} K + \lambda^{T} \left( {Z\Delta U - \mu } \right)$$\end{document}

Design of MLP neural network parameter tuning control strategy based on DOB-IMPC

This paper has conducted a detailed theoretical derivation and design of the DOB-IMPC control strategy. However, the performance of the control strategy largely depends on the settings of several key parameters, such as the weighting matrices Q,R of the internal cost function in IMPC, the softening parameter α of the reference trajectory, and the pole distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{1} ,p_{2}$$\end{document} in the gain matrix L of the state observation matrix in DOB.

Therefore, this section proposes a parameter tuning strategy based on a feedforward fully connected neural network (MLP). In the offline phase, large-scale multi-condition simulations are leveraged to systematically generate a highly comprehensive training dataset, which establishes an accurate mapping between system state variables and optimal control parameters. Subsequently, an MLP model is constructed and trained using this dataset as supervisory signals. During online operation, the motor’s real-time operating states are collected as inputs to the pre-trained neural network, and updated optimal parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q,R,\alpha ,p_{1} ,p_{2}$$\end{document} are dynamically output via inference. This approach thus satisfies the system’s requirements for high real-time performance and strong adaptability. Not only does it ensure the controller’s control performance, but it also significantly reduces the time required for parameter tuning. Input feature signal acquisition and processing

Set the number of training samples N to 8001.In Simulink, conduct a preliminary debugging of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q,R,\alpha ,p_{1} ,p_{2}$$\end{document} to make the actual output speed of the PMSM control system roughly consistent with the target speed.

The required training data is collected and saved to the MATLAB workspace in the form of an array through the Scope module. To ensure the representativeness and coverage of the samples, the simulation scenarios are systematically designed, covering various load disturbances and different speed conditions, effectively covering common operating conditions and expanding the distribution range of training data in the state space.

Parameter search range selection

For the parameters to be tuned,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q,R,\alpha ,p_{1} ,p_{2}$$\end{document}, a random search method is adopted to generate candidate parameters within the preset range. Since the parameters to be tuned span multiple orders of magnitude, a logarithmic transformation is applied to them to make the sampling distribution uniform and thereby accelerate the convergence speed of the neural network. This compresses the differences in orders of magnitude to an approximately linear scale, facilitating subsequent neural network training. The amount of data in this candidate interval is much larger than that of the input feature signals collected in the previous step, to enhance the matching accuracy between the input signals and the parameters to be tuned.

Dataset construction

Match the 7 groups of neural network input feature signals generated in the above steps with the rows of parameters to be tuned, and then input them into the DOB-IMPC algorithm for operation. After each group of candidate parameters to be tuned is matched with the input feature signals, the corresponding output speed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega_{s}$$\end{document} will be calculated.

The mean square error (MSE) between the output speed and the target speed is computed to assess the control performance of each candidate parameter set. This MSE serves as the dataset’s optimization objective, and all results are logged in the MATLAB workspace. A systematic search—comprising multiple iterations—is conducted across the full candidate parameter space, comparing the MSE associated with each parameter combination. The parameter set that achieves the lowest MSE across the entire search space is selected as the optimal solution for the given input conditions. Finally, each input feature vector and its corresponding optimal parameter combination are archived to form the supervised learning dataset for neural network training.

Data set partitioning

The collected data is randomly divided into a training set (70%), a validation set (15%), and a test set (15%) to ensure that the model has good generalization ability during the training process and to avoid overfitting.

Real-world operating conditions exhibit considerable diversity; consequently, the present dataset—generated using the DOB-IMPC algorithm—inevitably inherits certain model-induced biases and limitations. To enhance generalization and robustness, future work will incorporate broader stochastic sampling strategies or empirical measurements.

This study does not aim to exhaustively enumerate all possible scenarios. Instead, it adopts a systematic workload design strategy to construct a representative multi-condition dataset encompassing diverse speed reference trajectories, multi-level load disturbances, and critical boundary cases. Input variable ranges are deliberately expanded, and large-scale candidate parameter sets—generated via randomized parameter sampling—are integrated to ensure sufficient diversity across both state and parameter spaces. While full coverage of the operational envelope remains infeasible, the resulting dataset effectively spans the dominant operating regions of PMSM control, thereby enabling the trained neural network to generalize reliably.

Design of MLP neural network model

Normalization and denormalization of inputs and outputs

The training data for neural networks should be widely covered and evenly distributed. Normalization converts input and output variables of different magnitudes to a similar range, preventing features with large values from dominating the training, accelerating the convergence speed, and enhancing the stability of the activation function.

This study first employs Z-score standardization to scale the original 7-dimensional input features to a similar scale, eliminating the dimensional differences of various physical quantities and avoiding the imbalance of weights in the input layer of the training architecture. This lays a foundation for the stability of the subsequent network training process. The formula is as follows:36\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X^{\prime} = \frac{{X - \mu x}}{{\delta x}},\,\,\,\,\,Y^{\prime} = \frac{{Y - \mu y}}{{\delta y}}$$\end{document}

In the formula, X,Y represent the input and output data respectively, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu x,\delta x$$\end{document} are the mean and standard deviation of the input data \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X,and\,X^{\prime}$$\end{document} is the normalized input used for neural network training. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu y,\delta y$$\end{document} are the mean and standard deviation of the output data Y, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y^{\prime}$$\end{document} is the normalized output, which is the actual learning target of the network. After training is completed, the predicted values output by the network are restored to the scale of the original physical quantity through reverse normalization, and then compared with the real data for subsequent error index evaluation.

Network structure construction

The MLP network structure adopted in this paper is not arbitrarily selected but determined after comprehensive consideration of the complexity of system modeling, computational burden, and real-time control requirements. Firstly, as a universal function approximator, MLP has been proven to be capable of effectively approximating complex nonlinear mapping relationships, making it suitable for modeling the multi-variable coupling characteristics in motor drive systems.

Secondly, in terms of network structure design, the scale of the hidden layers (128–64-32) is reduced layer by layer. This approach not only ensures that the network has sufficient capacity to represent the nonlinear relationship between the input and output but also reduces the dimensionality of the features layer by layer, thereby minimizing redundant information and enhancing computational efficiency. This is essential for meeting the real-time requirements of online control.

Furthermore, through multiple sets of network structure comparison experiments (such as different hidden layer scales and numbers of layers), this paper found that the current structure achieves a better trade-off between prediction accuracy and computational complexity. Therefore, the five-layer MLP structure is ultimately selected for the online adjustment of control parameters.

The input layer I consists of 7 neurons, corresponding to the input feature signals collected and processed from the DOB-IMPC algorithm. The hidden layer H is composed of three layers, with dimensions gradually decreasing from 128 to 64 and then to 32. This ensures the adequate learning of the complex nonlinear relationship between the input and output while compressing and extracting high-order features strongly related to the target, thereby enhancing generalization ability and prediction stability. The optimization process of neural networks uses the Adam optimizer in combination with the backpropagation algorithm to calculate and update the gradients of the loss function with respect to the parameters of each layer of the network by applying the chain rule, making the next round of predictions closer to the true values and improving the performance of the model. Common loss functions include mean squared error (MSE) and cross-entropy, etc. This network selects the MSE type because it has good differentiability and is mathematically convenient to handle, and is often used in regression problems. The specific formula is as follows:40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{MSE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{N} {\left\| {\hat{y}_{i} - y_{i} } \right\|}^{2}$$\end{document}

In the formula, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{MSE}$$\end{document} represents the mean squared error loss of a batch of samples, N is the number of samples, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_{i}$$\end{document} indicates the predicted value of the i-th sample, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{i}$$\end{document} represents the true value of the i-th sample.

At the same time, the root mean square error (RMSE) is introduced as an evaluation metric to directly measure the prediction effect. That is, the square root of the MSE is taken to keep the unit of the error consistent with the original data and intuitively reflect the accuracy of the model training.

In the training process, the Adam optimization algorithm was adopted to adjust the network parameters. The initial learning rate was set to and the learning rate for each parameter was adaptively adjusted according to the gradient to enhance the stability and convergence speed of the training. The entire training process was conducted for 200 rounds, with the parameters updated once per batch. The training data was shuffled after each epoch to improve generalization ability. Additionally, the validation error was calculated every several steps (set by the Validation Frequency) during the training to monitor overfitting or trigger the early stopping mechanism.

The training label is defined as a set of parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y = \left[ {Q^{*} ,R^{*} ,\alpha^{*} } \right]$$\end{document} that can minimize the prediction output error of MPC. Explanation of the stability of closed-loop systems

For the stability analysis, the PMSM tracking error state is denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{k}$$\end{document}, which includes the current and speed tracking errors. After DOB compensation, the load disturbance and model parameter uncertainty cannot be completely removed, and the remaining part is denoted as the residual disturbance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{d}_{k}$$\end{document}. Therefore, the compensated closed-loop system can be represented as the nominal IMPC closed-loop system subject to a bounded residual disturbance:41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{k + 1} = f_{MPC} (x_{k} ,\theta_{k} ) + \tilde{d}_{k}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta_{k} = \left[ {Q_{k} ,R_{k} ,\alpha_{k} ,p_{1k} ,p_{2k} } \right]$$\end{document} denotes the controller parameters generated by the MLP. It should be noted that the MLP does not directly generate the control input and does not modify the structure of the prediction model. It only schedules the controller parameters. To prevent unreasonable parameters from destroying the convexity of the optimization problem and the stability of the observer, the MLP output is restricted to a predefined admissible set through saturation or projection:42\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta_{k} = \Pi_{\theta } (f_{MLP} (z_{k} ))$$\end{document}

Within this set, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_{k}$$\end{document} remains positive semi-definite, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{k}$$\end{document} remains positive definite, and the DOB-related parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{1k}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{2k}$$\end{document} remain within the stable observer parameter region. Therefore, the Hessian matrix of the IMPC optimization problem remains positive definite, and the rolling optimization problem remains convex and uniquely solvable.

The finite-horizon optimal cost function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_{N}^{*} (x_{k} ,\theta_{k} )$$\end{document} is selected as a Lyapunov-like function. Under recursive feasibility and in the absence of residual disturbance, the nominal IMPC closed-loop system satisfies the cost decrease condition:43\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_{N}^{*} (x_{k + 1} ,\theta_{k} ) - J_{N}^{*} (x_{k} ,\theta_{k} ) \le - c_{1} \left\| {x_{k} } \right\|^{2}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c}_{1}>0$$\end{document}. When the bounded residual disturbance caused by DOB estimation errors and external disturbances is considered, the cost decrease relation can be written in the ISS form:44\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_{N}^{*} (x_{k + 1} ,\theta_{k} ) - J_{N}^{*} (x_{k} ,\theta_{k} ) \le - \overline{c}_{1} \left\| {x_{k} } \right\|^{2} + \overline{c}_{2} \left\| {\tilde{d}_{k} } \right\|^{2}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{c}_{1}> 0$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{c}_{2}> 0$$\end{document}. This inequality indicates that the current and speed tracking errors of the PMSM remain bounded when the residual disturbance is bounded. When the residual disturbance decreases, the closed-loop state approaches the stable behavior of the nominal IMPC system. Therefore, under the conditions of recursive feasibility, bounded DOB residual disturbance, and admissible MLP-generated parameters, the proposed MLP-DOB-IMPC closed-loop system is robustly bounded in the sense of input-to-state stability.

Experimental verification in an embedded hardware environment

In Section V A, this paper conducts extensive simulation verification on the steady-state and dynamic performance of the designed composite IMPC control strategy. To further verify the feasibility of this control strategy in practical engineering applications, this section takes the surface-mounted permanent magnet synchronous motor as the research object and builds a PMSM hardware experimental platform. The main parameters of the PMSM used in the experiment are consistent with those in Table 2.

The hardware experimental platform consists of the PMSM core, control board, drive board, position detection unit, current detection unit, DC power supply, and the upper computer debugging system. The control system uses the control board equipped with the TMS320F28335 main control chip as the core control unit. The drive board and position detection board are integrated with the control board to form an integrated control platform. The composite IMPC control algorithm is compiled in the Code Composer Studio development environment and burned onto the TMS320F28335 main control chip through the XDS100V3 simulator to achieve the embedded operation of the control algorithm. The structure of the hardware experimental platform is shown in Fig. 13.Fig. 13Hardware experimental platform equipment.

Hardware experimental platform equipment.

The inverter is composed of NTMFS3D2N10MD MOSFETs forming a three-phase bridge arm and generates three-phase drive voltages using the SVPWM method. Rotor position is measured using the AS5048 magnetic encoder, and phase currents are sensed via the MT9221 current detection IC. Based on these measurements—along with speed estimates derived from position differentiation—the controller performs coordinate transformation, state feedback, disturbance estimation, and model-based predictive control computations. The block diagram of the hardware deployment architecture is shown below Fig. 14.Fig. 14The block diagram of the hardware deployment architecture.

The block diagram of the hardware deployment architecture.

The control system adopts a synchronous control method triggered by PWM interrupts, with a sampling period set at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{s} = 50\mu s$$\end{document}, corresponding to a PWM frequency of 20 kHz. Within each control cycle, the system successively performs current sampling, position reading, speed calculation, coordinate transformation, MLP parameter adjustment, DOB disturbance estimation, IMPC optimization calculation, and SVPWM duty cycle update. Therefore, this hardware platform can achieve a complete closed-loop control process from current and position sampling, MLP parameter adjustment, DOB disturbance observation, and IMPC control calculation to SVPWM output, further verifying the feasibility of the embedded implementation of the proposed MLP-assisted composite IMPC control strategy in PMSM drive systems.

To reduce the impact of external load disturbances and internal parameter perturbations of the motor on the dynamic and steady-state performance of the motor control system, and to address the challenges of complex controller parameter tuning and low precision, this paper proposes a composite MLP-DOB-IMPC control strategy.
