Capsulotomy With Supine Nd:YAG Laser in Children
Authors: Nike Klun, Manca Tekavčič Pompe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo evaluate the visual outcomes of Nd:YAG laser posterior capsulotomy performed in the supine position under general anesthesia in children with visual axis opacification after cataract surgery.Materials and MethodsA retrospective clinical case series of 13 eyes of 9 children with visual axis opacification following congenital, uveitic, or juvenile cataract surgery was conducted. All patients underwent Nd:YAG laser posterior capsulotomy in the supine position under general anesthesia at the Eye Clinic, University Medical Centre Ljubljana, between September 2019 and November 2025.ResultsPosterior capsulotomy was successfully performed in all 13 eyes. The median age at treatment was 9.1 years (range: 0.9–14 years) and the median follow‐up was 23.8 months (range: 2–60 months). Mean best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) improved from 0.85 ± 0.60 logMAR preoperatively to 0.64 ± 0.73 logMAR postoperatively (p = 0.002). Three eyes (23%) required a secondary Nd:YAG capsulotomy due to recurrent VAO. Transient intraocular pressure elevation occurred in two eyes (15%) and resolved with topical treatment. No other complications were observed.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the feasibility of performing Nd:YAG laser posterior capsulotomy under general anesthesia to manage VAO in children who are unable to undergo the procedure in the standard seated position.

Introduction

Pediatric cataracts are a major cause of preventable childhood blindness worldwide [1]. The incidence varies with age, from approximately 2.5 per 10,000 children in the first year of life to 3.5 per 10,000 by adolescence [2].

Early diagnosis and effective management are crucial for preventing the development of irreversible visual deprivation, caused by amblyopia, nystagmus, and strabismus, which can all develop if the visual axis is obscured at an early age [3]. The goal of pediatric cataract surgery is not only to remove the opacified lens but also to preserve long‐term clarity of the visual axis [4, 5]. Posterior capsular opacification (PCO), proliferation of lens material, and secondary membranes are major causes of visual axis opacification (VAO), which is the most frequent reason for reoperations in children [6]. Several surgical techniques have been developed to minimize VAO, including large enough primary posterior capsulotomy with anterior vitrectomy, posterior or anterior optic capture, and bag‐in‐the‐lens implantation [3, 7, 8].

Uveitic cataract surgery in children is often challenging due to limited surgical exposure caused by posterior synechiae, fibrinous membranes, and residual inflammation [9]. Aspiration of the lens material in these cases is frequently performed without posterior capsulotomy to minimize contact with vitreous, leading to an early PCO. Older children (usually above 7 years of age) may later be treated with neodymium‐doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd:YAG) laser capsulotomy, whereas younger children require surgical procedures, such as posterior capsulotomy via a pars plana or an anterior approach, the latter being especially suitable in aphakic eyes [9, 10].

When visually significant, VAO requires secondary intervention [6]. Current treatment options include secondary surgical capsulotomy under general anesthesia or Nd:YAG laser capsulotomy. The latter has the benefit of being a non‐invasive office‐based procedure that can be performed under topical anesthesia; however, it requires steady fixation [11, 12]. Therefore, young children, uncooperative children, or adults, and/or patients with nystagmus, may not be suitable candidates for the standard seated procedure [13].

Several attempts have been made to perform Nd:YAG capsulotomy under general anesthesia. Previous strategies include repositioning patients from supine position to lateral decubitus, upright sitting, prone or supine position with the head turned laterally, to access the visual axis [14, 15]. Each approach, however, introduces potential risks, including orthostatic hypotension, decreased myocardial contractility, musculoskeletal strain during patient repositioning, risk of extubation in the upright position, and the need for constant ocular alignment adjustments in the lateral decubitus position [14, 15, 16]. Performing Nd:YAG laser capsulotomy entirely in the supine position under general anesthesia represents an optimal approach for performing this procedure in children unable to cooperate awake. This position minimizes standard procedural challenges and avoids complications seen with other positions.

The aim of the study is to present a clinical series of nine children after an earlier cataract extraction who underwent Nd:YAG capsulotomy under general anesthesia using a supine YAG laser.

Materials and Methods

A retrospective clinical case series study of 13 eyes of 9 children, who developed VAO following congenital, uveitic, or juvenile cataract surgery, was conducted. All patients underwent posterior capsulotomy using a supine YAG laser (MR Q SUPINE, Meridian Medical) under general anesthesia. All procedures were performed in the operating room of the Eye Clinic, University Medical Centre Ljubljana, between September 2019 and November 2025. A similar technique was previously described by Atkinson and Hiles [17]. The procedure was performed using the Microruptor III device (H. S. Meridian, Bern, Switzerland), which allowed a 90‐degree rotation of the laser delivery system, enabling the procedure to be performed in the supine position.

Inclusion criteria were patients younger than 15 years, with a history of cataract extraction followed by significant VAO and insufficient cooperation to safely perform a standard Nd:YAG capsulotomy under local anesthesia.

Preoperative and follow‐up assessments included a standard ophthalmological examination with an age‐appropriate visual acuity test (using the preferential looking test, Lea symbols charts, or Snellen charts). Posterior capsulotomies were performed in the supine position under general anesthesia, using either a laryngeal mask airway or endotracheal intubation. All procedures followed a standardized technique using a direct capsulotomy lens. During each procedure, the number of pulses, pulse energy, and total energy delivered were recorded. Postoperative ophthalmological examination was performed a few hours after the procedure, and at the first follow‐up visit 1 week later, during which intraocular pressure (IOP) was assessed. All children were regularly followed up by a pediatric ophthalmologist.

The primary outcomes of this study were the best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) measured before surgery and at the last available follow‐up, as well as the clarity of the visual axis following Nd:YAG capsulotomy. Secondary outcomes included IOP elevation during the first postoperative week and the need for any additional surgical intervention. Clinical data were collected retrospectively through chart review.

Data were analyzed with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27). Continuous variables, including age, number of laser pulses, total energy delivered, and BCVA, were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. BCVA values were converted from decimal units to the logarithm of the minimal angle of resolution (logMAR). To compare pre‐ and postoperative BCVA values, the data were analyzed with the paired statistical test (Wilcoxon signed‐rank test). Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

The study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Slovenian National Medical Ethics Committee (0120‐23/2026‐2711‐3).

Results

In total, 13 eyes of 9 children were included in the study. The cohort comprised six boys and three girls. The median age at the time of Nd:YAG capsulotomy was 9.1 years (IQR: 5.7–12.7 years; range: from 11 months to 14.2 years). Four patients underwent bilateral Nd:YAG capsulotomy, while five were treated unilaterally. The underlying causes of VAO included congenital (5 eyes), juvenile (6 eyes), and uveitic cataract (2 eyes). All eyes, except for patient number 1, were pseudophakic. All implanted IOLs were 3‐piece AR40e lenses positioned within the capsular bag. The median follow‐up time was 23.8 months (IQR: 5.0–54.1 months; range: 2.0–60.0 months) (Table 1).

Of the 13 eyes with successful initial capsular opening, three eyes (23%) required secondary Nd:YAG capsulotomy due to recurrent VAO. The affected eyes represented different cataract etiologies, including one uveitic, one congenital, and one juvenile cataract. In two patients, the repeated procedure was performed in the supine position under general anesthesia, whereas in one patient it was carried out using a standard seated laser under local anesthesia (Table 1). The intervals between the initial and the secondary laser treatment ranged from 16.1 to 52.6 months (median: 22.0 months). During secondary capsulotomy, the mean number of laser pulses per eye was 76.0 ± 5.2 (range: 70–79), and the mean total energy delivered per eye was 110.2 ± 44.5 mJ (range: 70.0–158.0 mJ); all values are in the same range as values from the initial capsulotomy.

Elevation of IOP within the first postoperative week was observed in 2 of 13 eyes (15%), specifically in patients 2 (left eye) and 6 (left eye). Elevated IOP was treated with antihypertensive drops. By the next visit, IOP had normalized in both patients. No other intraoperative or postoperative complications related to the supine Nd:YAG laser procedure were recorded.

Discussion

In this study, supine Nd:YAG posterior capsulotomy was performed in 13 eyes of 9 children aged between 11 months and 14 years. A posterior capsular opening was successfully made in all 13 eyes without any major complications. The BCVA improved significantly in all treated eyes. The median follow‐up time was 23.8 months, during which the optical axis remained clear in the majority of eyes. Clinically significant recurrence of PCO was observed in 3 out of 13 eyes (23%) after a median period of 22.0 months, and was successfully managed by repeated Nd:YAG posterior capsulotomy.

If the standard seated Nd:YAG capsulotomy with topical anesthesia cannot be performed in children, it is optimal to conduct the entire procedure under general anesthesia. In this setting, patient positioning, rather than laser application itself, becomes the main technical challenge. The use of a standard seated laser requires alternative patient repositioning strategies, which are associated with additional procedural risks [11, 18]. Nd:YAG capsulotomy in the supine position eliminates this step and allows safe and efficient treatment under general anesthesia.

All children treated with the supine laser in our series demonstrated improvement in BCVA, with no eyes showing postoperative deterioration in visual acuity. These outcomes are consistent with those reported with the standard seated laser approach. Choi et al. [12] likewise reported an improvement in BCVA in all treated children, with a mean logMAR improving from 0.61 ± 0.36 to 0.25 ± 0.24. Similarly, in our study, the mean BCVA improved from 0.85 ± 0.60 logMAR to 0.64 ± 0.73 logMAR.

In our cohort, the mean total energy used for the initial procedure was 115.3 ± 65.6 mJ (range: 56.0–276.0 mJ), which was slightly higher than the energy typically reported in adult patients undergoing standard seated Nd:YAG capsulotomy [19]. However, it is comparable to the energy levels previously reported in children. Atkinson and Hiles [17] reported total laser energies ranging from 37 to 1700 mJ in children treated under general anesthesia in the supine position, while Kinori et al. [11] reported energies ranging from 36 to 703 mJ in the procedures performed in the lateral decubitus position. Choi et al. [12] reported similar mean total energy values (99.5 ± 36.1 mJ; range: 49–190 mJ) using the standard seated approach in awake, cooperative children. Differences in total energy used across studies are expected, since the energy needed to disrupt the opacified posterior capsule depends on capsule density and thickness, which may be influenced by patient age and the underlying morphology of VAO [20].

Of the 13 eyes with successful initial capsular opening, 3 eyes (23%) required secondary Nd:YAG capsulotomy after a median period of 22.0 months, due to clinically significant recurrence of PCO. This is comparable to the findings of Choi et al. [12], where 24.1% of children required additional procedures. In the same study, no significant complications were observed, apart from IOL pitting and transient IOP elevation in 14% of cases. Similarly, in our study, no major complications were recorded, except transient IOP elevation, which occurred in 15% of eyes and resolved after a short period of topical antiglaucoma medication.

The main limitation of this study is its small cohort size, which reduces statistical power and limits the generalizability of the results. In addition, only a few studies have reported on Nd:YAG capsulotomy being performed in the supine position under general anesthesia in children. Consequently, our results can be compared primarily with studies using alternative patient positions, which restricts direct comparison. Future research should include a larger cohort and consider separating outcomes based on the underlying cause of VAO.

Conclusion

Supine Nd:YAG laser posterior capsulotomy performed under general anesthesia may offer a practical treatment option for children with VAO following cataract surgery who cannot undergo the procedure in the standard seated position. The procedure was completed successfully in all patients, with no serious intraoperative or postoperative complications.
