Views and experiences of weight management for people living with mobility‑limiting conditions, intellectual disabilities or severe mental illness: a qualitative evidence synthesis
Authors: Katy Sutcliffe, Dylan Kneale, Velichka Dimitrova, Silvy Mathew, Claire Stansfield, Karen Schucan Bird, Carol Rivas, Rachael Hunter, Alison O'Mara-Eves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (qualitative). The objectives are as follows:This qualitative evidence synthesis (QES) aims to address the question:What is known from qualitative evidence about behavioural weight management for people living with mobility‐limiting conditions, intellectual disabilities, or severe mental illness?The five review objectives are as follows.To explore individuals' experiences with weight and behavioural weight‐management programmes. This includes understanding how individuals living with mobility‐limiting conditions, intellectual disabilities, severe mental illness and their carers experience managing their weight and how they perceive and engage with behavioural weight‐management programmes, including how accessible, supportive, and relevant they find the programme elements.To identify barriers to and facilitators of managing a healthy weight and accessing behavioural weight‐management programmes. Focusing on the unique obstacles encountered by each population group, we will examine issues such as physical accessibility, communication challenges, financial constraints, and gaps in tailored support that may prevent equitable participation. We will also include people’s perspectives on structural and system‐level barriers, as well as the influence of intersectional factors (e.g. gender and ethnicity), on the challenges of maintaining weight and accessing and benefiting from support.To understand the impact of stigma and discrimination on programme engagement and outcomes. This objective seeks to uncover how stigma related to weight, disability, or mental health influences individuals’ willingness to participate, their inclusion and sense of belonging with others within the programmes, and overall programme satisfaction. It also examines the role of stigma as a potential barrier to success.To capture the perspectives of programme providers and healthcare practitioners. Gathering insights from healthcare providers and programme facilitators, this objective assesses which programme elements are considered successful and which present ongoing challenges. This includes evaluating aspects like accessibility, engagement strategies, and support mechanisms that may require improvement.To produce an inclusive piece of research through incorporating the insight of people with lived experience and professional expertise in interpreting and considering the implications of the evidence. By synthesising this qualitative evidence, the review aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of how to design and implement behavioural weight‐management programmes that are more inclusive, effective, and responsive to the needs of these populations.

Background

Description of the topic

Obesity is linked to several chronic diseases, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and certain cancers, and increased risk of anxiety and depression, which all contribute to reduced life expectancy and lower quality of life [1].

Obesity is a complex condition arising from many factors outside an individual's control. Obesity rates and associated health impacts are disproportionately high among some population groups that have metabolic predispositions to weight gain or face health inequalities in access to effective behavioural weight management. Barriers like restricted access to affordable, nutritious food and safe exercise opportunities often challenge some populations, increasing their risk [1, 2].

For this qualitative evidence synthesis (QES), we will focus on three specific populations that are disproportionately affected by these issues.

People living with mobility‐limiting conditions: for example, people with a neurological or musculoskeletal condition that directly affects mobility, such as multiple sclerosis or spinal cord injury, leading to challenges in some daily activities [3].Individuals living with intellectual disabilities: people who have a reduced intellectual ability and experience difficulty with some everyday activities. For example, household tasks, socialising or managing money, which affects them for their whole life [4].Individuals living with severe mental illness: people who experience psychological problems that are often so debilitating that their ability to engage in various everyday and work activities is severely impacted. It includes conditions like schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder and bipolar disorder that require ongoing medical care [5].

Obesity is a complex, chronic condition influenced by numerous factors beyond individual control, including genetics, socioeconomic status, and environmental barriers [19, 20]. Below we outline some of the specific challenges faced by each group:

Individuals living with mobility‐limiting conditions face specific challenges due to health complications. Reduced physical activity, often stemming from pain, muscle atrophy or limited mobility, results in decreased energy expenditure and a higher propensity for weight gain [3, 13]. Environmental barriers, such as inaccessible exercise facilities, inadequate transportation, and a lack of adaptive fitness programmes, further hinder their ability to engage in physical activity [13, 21]. Additionally, individuals living with mobility‐limiting conditions may encounter challenges with maintaining a healthy diet due to dependence on caregivers, limited autonomy in food choices, or impairment‐related socioeconomic barriers [3]. Barriers to healthy eating for those with impaired mobility include challenges in food procurement, preparation, and consumption. Factors such as limited energy, and limited hand function and mobility restrict cooking and shopping, while reliance on personal aids or caregivers can limit autonomy over food choices [22].Individuals living with intellectual disabilities face elevated risks for weight gain due to a combination of biological, lifestyle, environmental, and systemic factors. Individuals living with genetic conditions such as Down syndrome and Prader‐Willi syndrome are predisposed to obesity [23, 24]. High levels of physical inactivity and unhealthy diets, exacerbated by limited access to tailored interventions, further contribute to the problem [15, 23]. Caregivers' lack of knowledge or resources to promote healthy behaviours, can compound these challenges [12, 15]. Medications, such as antipsychotics, also play a role in weight gain, while systemic gaps in healthcare services and insufficient involvement of individuals living with intellectual disabilities in intervention design hinder the effectiveness of health promotion programmes [15, 16, 24].Individuals living with severe mental illness face a combination of biological, behavioural, and environmental challenges for weight management [11, 25, 26]. Antipsychotic medications, especially second‐generation agents like olanzapine and clozapine, are a primary driver of weight gain, as they increase hunger, decrease satiety, and alter metabolic processes [25, 26]. Unhealthy diets, including the consumption of energy‐dense, nutrient‐poor foods, and low intake of fruits and vegetables are common, often exacerbated by mood, food insecurity and reliance on unhealthy institutional meals in inpatient settings [11, 26, 27]. Sedentary behaviour is significantly higher among individuals living with severe mental illness due to reduced physical activity opportunities, both in community and inpatient settings, compounded by anhedonia (being unable to feel pleasure in things) or the sedative effects of some medications [11, 27]. Emotional eating, linked to feelings of worthlessness and low self‐esteem, further worsens weight outcomes [11]. Moreover, physical health issues can be overlooked and left untreated among people with severe mental illness because the need for psychiatric care overshadows recognition of physical health symptoms [28].

Weight‐related stigma is another challenge that these populations face, both in their everyday lives and when seeking healthcare services. This sort of stigma is characterised by prejudiced, stereotyped, and discriminatory behaviour towards people living with obesity, often driven by misconceptions about the causes of obesity [29]. In healthcare settings, negative experiences such as insensitive discussions about weight, can deter individuals from seeking care and exacerbate health issues, including psychological stress [20]. They may also experience depression, anxiety, low self‐esteem and poor body image [30]. These psychological effects can hinder the effective management of weight by reducing motivation, increasing emotional eating, and discouraging individuals from seeking or adhering to weight‐management interventions. These populations are also at risk of stigma relating to their intellectual disability, severe mental illness or mobility‐limiting conditions, meaning that they face ‘double stigma’ [31].

To approach this topic in a respectful, humble, sensitive, and non‐stigmatising manner, we made a conscious effort to use 'people‐first' language throughout our work. People‐first language puts the person before their condition; it aims to avoid defining a person’s identity by their health condition or disability. We referred to various contemporary literature and guidance on people‐first language [20, 32]. We use the term ‘obesity’ only when describing the condition in general, while adopting people‐first language when referring to individuals living with the condition (e.g. ‘individual with obesity’ or ‘people living with obesity’). Whilst we use the term obesity to signal a health risk or inequality, we acknowledge growing consensus that adiposity does not always adequately reflect the health status of an individual, and that measures of obesity based on body mass index (BMI) can both underestimate and overestimate adiposity [33]. For example, ways of measuring adiposity may not be reflective of other physical changes such as higher fluid retention in the body caused by some medical conditions or treatments (e.g. lymphoedema).

Why is it important to do this review?

As the underlying causes of obesity in all populations are a complex mix of biological, psychological, environmental, and social factors, there can be no ‘quick fix’. Experts generally agree that a range of approaches is needed to reduce obesity rates, including interventions at both the individual and population levels [34].

This review focuses on individual‐level interventions, specifically how healthcare practitioners can provide effective support to individuals living with obesity who want to reduce their weight. Population‐level interventions, such as the ‘sugar tax’ introduced in the UK in 2018, seek to change the environment in which people live to make it easier for them to engage in healthy eating and regular physical activity [35].

Weight‐management interventions can address varying levels of need. This includes population‐wide prevention through healthy lifestyle promotion and interventions for people living with overweight and obesity. These include structured, community‐based behavioural weight‐management support. Those with more complex needs may require specialist, multidisciplinary services, including intensive behavioural interventions as well as psychological or pharmacological support. Those with a very high BMI or very complex needs, may be referred for bariatric surgery alongside postoperative care [20].

Multi‐component behavioural weight‐management interventions, which support individuals to increase exercise and improve their diet, have been found to be successful in helping individuals to reduce their weight [36]. These include programmes, courses, clubs, or groups provided by the public, private and voluntary sectors. However, evidence reviews about these kinds of programmes have not focused specifically on the needs and outcomes of our groups of interest. Additionally, although these interventions have shown promise, there is variability in how consistently these services are applied across different settings [8, 15, 20, 37, 38].

How this review might inform or supplement what is already known in this area

This QES will be complementary to a subsequent effectiveness review. The two reviews will be integrated into a mixed‐methods synthesis.

Scoping work identified seven mixed‐method systematic reviews on weight‐management interventions for the populations of interest. Of these, three focused on weight‐management interventions for people living with intellectual disabilities [8, 15, 38]. The findings emphasise the effectiveness of multicomponent interventions that integrate dietary changes, physical activity, and behaviour‐modification strategies, particularly when they involve caregivers, addressing broader socio‐ecological factors and tailoring to individual contexts. Tailored approaches, including simplified communication tools, caregiver support, and individualised strategies, are consistently highlighted as essential to engagement and success. For instance, there has been specific emphasis on the importance of the role of support staff in promoting health and fitness among people living with intellectual disabilities [8, 15, 38]. Across the reviews, qualitative evidence found that fostering autonomy, social connectedness, and enjoyment in interventions emerged as significant factors influencing participation. Additionally, qualitative findings emphasised the broader benefits of these programmes, such as improved self‐esteem and social interaction, alongside the challenges of accessibility and sustained engagement [8, 15, 38].

Two mixed‐method reviews on individuals living with severe mental illness highlight the importance of participatory approaches, regular support, and tailored strategies to improve adherence and address systemic barriers like stigma and healthcare inequalities [17, 25]. The reviews emphasise the importance of knowledgeable facilitators who demonstrate empathy and understanding of severe mental illness, alongside tools and materials adapted to the cognitive and emotional needs of participants. They also point to the effectiveness of tailored interventions that prioritise education on weight gain mechanisms, use supportive tools (e.g. pedometers and cookbooks), and provide interim booster support (e.g. phone check‐ins) [17, 25].

Two mixed‐method reviews on weight‐management interventions for individuals living with mobility‐limiting conditions, such as spinal cord injuries or chronic musculoskeletal pain, shared themes with the other population groups [21, 39]. Tailored interventions that address specific physical and environmental challenges, including accessibility of equipment, specialised facilities, and healthcare professionals who are knowledgeable about disability‐specific needs were highlighted as important [21, 39]. Participants and healthcare professionals often recognised the link between behavioural weight management and overall health, yet identified significant barriers, such as limited mobility, pain, and a lack of customised guidance or resources. For spinal cord injuries, the inclusion of caregivers was noted as pivotal, both as enablers and as individuals who also face burdens [21]. Similarly, for chronic musculoskeletal pain, interventions integrating weight and pain management were perceived as more effective due to the interrelated nature of these issues. Peer support and group‐based activities were also valued for fostering a sense of community and reducing isolation. Practical support, including transport and flexible scheduling, further facilitated engagement [21, 39].

Gaps in existing research

Of the seven mixed‐method reviews we identified, most found very few qualitative studies, or focused predominantly on intervention effectiveness, leaving a significant gap in understanding the nuanced, lived experiences of diverse populations [8, 15, 17, 21, 25, 38, 39]. The mixed‐method systematic review by Doherty and colleagues undertook a more in‐depth analysis of qualitative evidence, but searches for this review were undertaken in 2015, and it focused exclusively on people living with learning disabilities, leaving a gap in understanding of current experiences across the three population groups [8]. Further, where evidence from QES is available, it focuses on interventions rather than broader lifestyle challenges, leaving a need for a more holistic understanding of the issues. Research often lacks comprehensive exploration of patient and caregiver experiences, particularly how systemic barriers like inaccessible facilities and inadequate professional knowledge impact engagement with interventions [21]. Additionally, studies frequently overlook the social determinants of health, such as financial constraints and community‐level barriers, and rarely consider intersectional issues like gender or age, which can significantly shape experiences with behavioural weight‐management programmes [13].

Addressing these gaps requires a stronger focus on qualitative methodologies to uncover the nuanced, lived experiences of these populations and inform more inclusive, effective interventions. Other authors have also highlighted the need for QES, specifically addressing the experiences and complexities of behavioural weight management among people living with mobility‐limiting conditions, intellectual disabilities, and severe mental illness [11, 15, 16, 25, 40].

Why qualitative evidence synthesis is needed

At present, it is not clear how much evidence is available on the views and experiences of behavioural weight management for the three population groups, nor what the common elements and distinctions are in these experiences. This comprehensive QES will consolidate and analyse findings from diverse populations to identify experiences common to all groups; comparison across the different groups will allow for a deeper understanding of the barriers, facilitators, and unique needs specific to each group.

By synthesising qualitative data, this review will capture nuances in how behavioural weight‐management interventions are perceived, understood, and enacted, particularly where interventions may fall short or where they have successfully addressed specific challenges. The knowledge gained from this QES will enable us to undertake an informed approach to the subsequent effectiveness synthesis, for example, by highlighting gaps in the current effectiveness literature. As such, the QES will be fundamental for informing more effective, tailored interventions and will enable the integration of participant, caregiver and professional perspectives into future programme design and implementation [41].

Perspective

This review will focus on adults with increased risk of weight gain, specifically those with a mobility‐limiting condition, an intellectual disability, or a severe mental illness. We will also include studies that report the views and experiences of carers or support staff, or practitioners.

We will exclude the general population, other than the three population groups mentioned above, and individuals under 18 years of age. We will also exclude studies where only a minority of participants (i.e. fewer than 75%) meet the inclusion criteria.

Setting

We will include perspectives of people in community and institutional settings. Although most adults in the UK with mobility‐limiting conditions, intellectual disabilities [45], or severe mental illness [46], live in community settings, we are interested in the range of barriers for these populations, and so will include all settings.

Phenomenon of interest

Multi‐component behavioural weight‐management interventions that aim to reduce a person's energy intake and help them to be more physically active by encouraging behaviour change. They can focus on diet, physical activity, behaviour change or any combination of these elements [20]. These can vary from more manualised or standardised approaches (for example, large‐scale interventions delivered by commercial providers) to more bespoke or tailored approaches (for example, delivered through the voluntary sector). Current National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines advocate for multi‐component behavioural weight‐management interventions; however, there is limited evidence on their effectiveness specifically among these groups [20]. To support this, we will include studies about the views and experiences of people accessing behavioural weight‐management interventions and include studies that report on people’s views on managing weight generally. This will help in gaining a comprehensive understanding of the broader, systemic, and personal challenges and drivers experienced by these groups in managing their weight.

We will exclude studies about pharmacological intervention only and surgical intervention (e.g. bariatric surgery).

Environment

Studies will not be limited to any particular country. Because this review focuses on very specific populations, that is, people living with one of the three condition types as well as obesity, we anticipate that there may be few qualitative studies. Although the review has been commissioned to support policy and practice in England, to increase the likelihood of finding studies representing all the populations of interest, we will include studies from any country.

Timing

From 2000 onwards, in order to understand current views and experiences. We consulted interestholders with lived experience to determine this cutoff point and agreed it to be sufficiently broad to capture a range of experiences, but sufficiently recent to capture relevant experiences.

We will exclude studies published before 2000.

Findings

Studies that collect empirical qualitative data about people's experiences, views and beliefs using any qualitative designs (either as part of a trial study or observational study).

We will exclude studies that do not collect qualitative data; studies that do not contain sufficiently rich data or details about data collection and analysis, such as studies using a survey or questionnaire design but with some open‐ended questions, brief reports, conference abstracts, and master’s theses. Preprints and non‐empirical articles such as commentaries will also be excluded.

Language

We will only include English‐language studies. Although this could limit the range of experiences included, the review will involve synthesising rich textual data from included studies. Synthesis of rich multilingual textual data is challenging as there is nuance in language that does not easily translate.

However, to ensure that our review is relevant to a global audience, if the pool of included studies comprises only studies conducted in English‐speaking countries or in the Global North, we will undertake additional purposive searches to identify studies conducted in settings where English is not an official or widely spoken language. In this case, we will engage an information specialist with experience of working in linguistically diverse contexts to advise on appropriate databases and search strategies for identifying literature from settings where English is not the dominant language, or is used only in specific contexts.

We will carry out forward and backward citation searching of eligible references after full‐text screening using Open Alex and Scopus. Selection of studies

The search records will be exported to EPPI‐Reviewer [47]. After removing duplicate records, at least two review authors will independently double‐screen a sample of titles and abstracts for relevance, to pilot the eligibility criteria described above. We will discuss all disagreements and refine the inclusion and exclusion criteria where necessary. Where relevance is still under question, we will consult other members of the review author team. We will continue to sample and screen batches of records, checking levels of agreement once both review authors have completed their batch. Once agreement is over 90%, a single review author will screen the remaining titles and abstracts, consulting others in cases of uncertainty.

We will apply priority screening, a text‐mining process increasingly used in systematic reviews, to identify relevant titles and abstracts. Text mining is a type of machine learning whereby reviewing software ‘learns’ to recognise citations that are likely to be included and excluded based on how researchers apply screening criteria [48, 49]. Citations are then sorted so that those most likely to be included are screened first, prioritising them to significantly speed up the screening process [50, 51]. The implementation of this in EPPI Reviewer software uses an active learning approach, in which the prioritised list continues to update its ordering based on new information received by the system as the human reviewers mark more records as includes and excludes. That is, it continues to ‘learn’ as the review authors progress through screening, avoiding a bias towards studies that were included in the original set of records used to train the system [50].

If appropriate, we may also adopt screening truncation, where we would stop screening before reaching the end of the prioritised list and automatically exclude those towards the end of the list. We will only adopt screening truncation if we have evidence that it is unlikely that further relevant records will be found. To inform this decision, we will calculate a baseline inclusion rate by screening a random sample of records. The baseline inclusion rate will enable us to estimate how many records we would expect to include from the full list. We will use this estimate to help us determine if and when it is ‘safe’ to stop screening (i.e. the likelihood of missing a record in the remaining documents is low). If it is decided that it is ‘safe’ to stop screening before having examined all the records, then we will use a range of methods to check the unscreened records. These will include techniques such as searching the unscreened records for keywords and using document clustering to identify any relevant groups of studies that we may have missed. These methods have been used in other reviews [52].

We will retrieve the full texts of all records that meet the eligibility criteria at the title and abstract screening stage. Initially, we will examine each full‐text record in duplicate to confirm eligibility. We will discuss cases of uncertainty. Depending on the nature and volume of the evidence located, we may adopt a ‘purposive sampling’ approach following full‐text screening, that is, apply further inclusion criteria to reduce the number of included studies. Possible additional inclusion criteria may be to only include studies with conceptually rich or contextually thick data, or both [53].

Data management, analysis and synthesis

We will employ thematic synthesis as the synthesis method. Thematic synthesis is a well‐established approach to synthesising evidence on perspectives and experiences, as well as on perceptions, appropriateness and factors influencing the implementation of behavioural weight‐management interventions [55]. Prior to data extraction and synthesis, we will develop a logic model through discussion with interestholders involved in this review to sensitise the team to potentially important considerations and inform decisions made in the review. The logic model will depict our initial theorising of how people across our three condition groups may encounter issues with managing their weight, how people access services, and how interventions could help people manage their weight.

This evidence will be generated from the experiences of individuals, carers, and healthcare professionals. The three key stages of thematic synthesis are outlined elsewhere in detail [55], but in brief will involve the following.

Coding: gaining empirical intimacy with studies through in‐depth familiarisation with each study and becoming immersed in the data through line‐by‐line reading and initial coding. Our coding will be inductive, but we will draw on the logic model at a later stage to help identify any additional findings or insights. Coding will capture first‐order data (i.e. original data and quotes) as well as second‐order author interpretations of the data.Descriptive themes: observing similarities and differences between the codes and grouping the codes into a hierarchical structure to develop higher‐level descriptive themesAnalytical themes: developing analytical themes through critical interrogation of the descriptive themes and interpreting them in relation to the review question, cross‐checking the interpretation and explanation of themes with the original studies, and exploring the distinction between themes and subthemes.

As outlined earlier, and following the guidance on equity, the QES will examine patterns and commonalities across all three groups, as well whether themes are specific to, or more strongly associated with, one of the individual groups. Where we observe substantial differences across the groups, we will represent them within the logic model if appropriate, or will construct individual logic models to further articulate different pathways around barriers and facilitators to weight management, and the experience of behavioural weight‐management interventions.

In the event that the search yields a very large number of studies, which preclude developing in‐depth knowledge with each study and which might undermine the synthesis, any approach to sampling the studies will include the different categories of population, as well as potentially different conditions within each population, as criteria to ensure a balanced sample [53].

In this protocol, based on input from interestholders involved in this review, with lived and professional experience (see below), we use person‐first language. We acknowledge that there exists a diversity of views around the appropriate choice of language, and that condition‐first language can help to illuminate shared experiences of stigma, discrimination, and oppression. However, given the range of different conditions explored here, and based on the input of our interestholders, we have elected to use person‐first language in this protocol and will continue to do so throughout the review.

There exist similar debates about the choice of language to describe each of the population groups included in this review. After consulting with our interestholders, we use the term ‘intellectual disability’, which is thought to be widely used internationally, although we acknowledge that ‘learning disability’ may be more common in some settings (e.g. the UK). Finally, we use the term ‘mobility‐limiting conditions’ rather than physical disabilities to better accommodate the span of different conditions and experiences that are within the scope of this review.

Review author reflexivity

Our research team comprises individuals with diverse professional and personal experiences, including expertise in obesity, stigma, behavioural weight‐management interventions, and intersectionality. These experiences inform our understanding of the complex issues surrounding weight and obesity, yet we acknowledge that our perspectives are shaped by our backgrounds. We remain vigilant about how these experiences may influence our interpretations and aim to minimise biases through collaborative and iterative team discussions.

We recognise the profound impact of stigma, systemic inequities, and intersectionality on the experience of the three populations with overweight and obesity. Through this research, we are committed to exploring contextual factors that shape these experiences, such as societal norms, healthcare systems, and cultural attitudes. Additionally, we approach the review with openness, humility and sensitivity, using definitions and frameworks developed by the respective communities themselves, such as the Mencap definition for intellectual and learning disabilities. We will use person‐first language throughout our work to approach the topic in a sensitive manner and reduce any potential stigma [32].

We acknowledge that behavioural weight‐management programmes represent only one facet of addressing obesity‐related issues. A broader systems approach is necessary to address comprehensively the complex interaction of factors contributing to obesity. This perspective will inform our synthesis and interpretation of our findings.

Central to our approach is the engagement of individuals with lived experience. We are committed to ensuring that their voices shape the research process, from the development of the review questions to the interpretation and dissemination of findings. By incorporating their insights, we aim to produce research that is relevant, respectful, and reflective of the needs and perspectives of those most affected.

We will seek ethics approval for this review. While the use of publicly available data minimises direct ethical concerns, we are committed to identifying and addressing any ethical issues raised by the studies included in the review. This includes evaluating the ethical rigour of primary studies and considering their implications for vulnerable populations.

Interestholder involvement and engagement

We will engage with interestholders with lived and professional expertise at key points during the production of the review and adopt the values of co‐production where possible. For example, we will ensure that we challenge the status quo and avoid using stigmatising language and research practices, and aim to ensure a diversity of perspectives are represented within the production of the review (see O'Mara‐Eves 2022 for a full outline of the values of co‐production [58]). We will engage with individuals with lived experience and expertise relevant to the topic (e.g. people living with mobility‐limiting conditions, intellectual disabilities, or an enduring severe mental illness; as well as general practitioners (GPs), carers, health professionals, and researchers). Two contributors with lived experience informed the scope, review questions and inclusion criteria for this protocol, and we have engaged with contributors with relevant practitioner and academic expertise in shaping the work. For example, how to specify the range of conditions to cover for each population group. We will engage with a broader group of interestholders at further planned points during the production of the review, including when developing data extraction tools, during the synthesis, and when summarising our findings.
