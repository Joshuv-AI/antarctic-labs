Immune subtyping of colorectal adenoma identifies a subtype with activated adaptive immunity ahead of progressing to cancer
Authors: Ke Chen, Kaiqin Xu, Yu Ding, Hua Yin, Zhifeng Feng, Lijie Li, Hao Xu, Wangxiong Hu, YuJie Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal adenomas (CRA) represent precursor lesions with varying risks of malignant transformation. However, molecular subtyping, particularly immune-related classification, remains underexplored in adenomas. This study aims to characterize the immune landscape of CRA through immune subtyping and evaluate its association with cancer progression, gene expression signatures, and functional pathways.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of transcriptomic data from multiple cohorts of CRA samples. Immune subtypes were identified using non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) based on immune-related genes. Diverse deconvolution algorithms were used to estimate immune cell infiltration. The immune status alteration in premalignant lesion was further consolidated by single-cell transcriptome data. Differential gene expression analysis was performed between subtypes, followed by functional enrichment analyses (Gene Ontology [GO] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes [KEGG]).ResultsTwo distinct immune subtypes were identified: an immune-enriched subtype characterized by high lymphocyte infiltration and elevated expression of immune-related genes, and an immune-deficient subtype with suppressed immune activity. Differential expression analysis revealed significant upregulation of immune response genes (e.g., CD4, CD86, HLA-DRA) in the immune-enriched subtype. GO and KEGG analyses highlighted enrichments in leukocyte transendothelial migration, chemokine signaling, and antigen processing and presentation pathways. Single-cell result revealed an early occurrence of TIGIT activation and exhausted CD8 T cell features in adenoma when compared to normal tissue.ConclusionThis study delineates distinct immune subtypes within CRAs. The immune-enriched subtype demonstrates activated adaptive immunity and may reflect a higher potential for immune surveillance, while the immune-deficient subtype exhibits stromal features suggestive of progressive transformation. These findings provide insights into early immune microenvironment alterations and may inform strategies for risk stratification and immunoprevention in colorectal carcinogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-026-05492-1.Keywords: Colorectal adenoma, Immune subtyping, Single cell sequencing, Tumor microenvironment, Differentially expressed genes

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1], with its pathogenesis predominantly following the well-established adenoma-carcinoma sequence [2, 3]. While conventional histopathological assessment of colorectal adenomas (CRA) provides critical information on dysplasia grade and villous architecture, it offers limited insight into the complex molecular and immune microenvironment (IME) that dictates the likelihood of malignant progression. A significant proportion of CRC cases originate from these precursor lesions, underscoring the urgent need to identify high-risk adenomas through more sophisticated molecular subtyping [4–6].

The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is increasingly recognized as a pivotal determinant of cancer initiation, progression, and therapeutic response. The well-known consensus molecular subtypes (CMS) system has classified CRC tumors into four distinct subtypes (CMS1-4), each with characteristic immune and stromal features [7]. Notably, the CMS1 (immune-hot, microsatellite unstable) subtype is associated with a pronounced cytotoxic lymphocyte infiltration and a favorable prognosis in early-stage disease. However, the immune heterogeneity remains unclear in precancerous adenoma stage and is urgently necessary to clarify. Understanding immune alteration in premalignant lesions could stratify patients for surveillance intensity and reveal early immunotherapeutic targets [8].

Therefore, the central hypothesis of this study is that CRAs have undergone immune remodeling and can be classified into distinct immune subtypes that correlate with known CRC features and risk factors. By integrating bulk transcriptomic profiling and single cell transcriptomic datasets with advanced bioinformatic deconvolution using xCell, CIBERSORT, ESTIMATE, and Seurat pipeline, we aim to map the landscape of immune infiltration and change in CRA. This work aims to identify a high-risk immune phenotype, characterized by specific DEGs and enriched pathways revealed through GO, KEGG, and GSEA, that may predispose to rapid progression along the adenoma to cancer sequence, ultimately informing targeted surveillance and interception strategies.

Materials and methods

Study population and data acquisition

This study utilized publicly available gene expression datasets and matched clinical information from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. A systematic search was conducted to identify datasets containing transcriptomic profiles (> 20 sample size) of human colorectal adenoma samples. The inclusion criteria were: (1) microarray or RNA-sequencing data from fresh-frozen or formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) adenoma tissue; (2) availability of corresponding normal colonic mucosa samples for comparison; and (3) accessible raw or normalized expression data.

A total of six datasets (GSE8671, GSE20916, GSE37364, GSE41657, GSE117606 and GSE117607) were selected for further analysis, comprising 371 adenoma samples, 172 CRCs and 191 normal mucosa samples. To integrate transcriptomic data derived from heterogeneous microarray platforms, a standardized preprocessing pipeline was applied. Raw expression data from each platform were first independently normalized using platform-specific methods: The Microarray Suite 5 (MAS5) algorithm was used with default parameters to background correction, quantile normalization, and summarization for Affymetrix arrays, while quantile normalization was performed for Agilent platforms. Probe-level data were subsequently mapped to official gene symbols using the corresponding platform annotation files. For probes mapping to multiple genes, the probe was excluded to avoid ambiguity; for genes represented by multiple probes, the probe with the highest mean expression across all samples was retained as the representative value.

To ensure cross-platform gene consistency, only genes present across all platforms were retained for downstream analysis, while platform-exclusive genes were discarded. Following gene filtering, the sva package was used to perform batch correction using the ComBat method, which employs an empirical Bayes framework to adjust for non-biological technical variation while preserving biological heterogeneity. The effectiveness of batch correction was assessed by principal component analysis (PCA) plots before and after correction to confirm the removal of systematic technical variation while preserving biological signals. The finalized integrated matrix, comprising uniformly processed and batch-corrected gene expression profiles, was used for all subsequent analyses.

Additionally, to eliminate the interference of different chip platforms on the results as much as possible, Affymetrix HG-U133 Plus 2.0 (GSE8671, GSE20916, and GSE37364, GPL570) cohorts were combined into a batch-corrected single combined expression matrix for used as discovery datasets. Agilent (GSE41657, GPL6480) and Affymetrix HT HG-U133 + PM Array Plate (GSE117606 and GSE117607, GPL25373) were used as validation datasets, respectively.

Single cell data processing

Single cell transcriptome sequencing data (GSE161277), which harbors four patients with matched normal, para-cancer, adenoma, and CRC samples, was downloaded from GEO database. Raw sequencing data were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using the Cell Ranger pipeline (v7.0.0, 10x Genomics). Dimensionality reduction and clustering were executed utilizing the Seurat R package (v4.3.0) [9]. Low-quality cells (> 20% mitochondrial genes, or < 500 detected genes) were excluded. Pseudotime trajectory inference was conducted using Monocle3 [10] to model the transitional dynamics of cell states. To map intercellular interactions, CellChat (v1.6.0) was applied to the combined single-cell dataset to infer ligand-receptor communications between different cell subsets. To quantify tumor characteristics and normal differentiation status, we constructed two gene signature scoring systems based on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between tumor colonocytes and mature colonocytes. The tumor score was calculated using the top 50 genes significantly upregulated in tumor colonocytes (adj.P < 0.01, log2FC > 1), while the normal score used the top 50 genes upregulated in mature colonocytes (adj.P < 0.01, log2FC < -1). The exhaustion score in single CD8 T cell was computed based on the expression levels of canonical immune checkpoint genes, including PDCD1, LAG3, HAVCR2 (TIM-3), TIGIT, CTLA4, TOX, NR4A1, and ENTPD1. The aforementioned scores were computed using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function, which performs background correction by subtracting the average expression of control gene sets matched for expression levels.

Identification of DEGs

Differential gene expression analysis between adenoma samples and normal mucosa controls, as well as between the identified immune subtypes, was performed using the limma package in R [11]. Genes with an adjusted P-value (Benjamini-Hochberg) < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 1 were considered statistically significant DEGs.

Functional enrichment analysis

The biological functions and pathways associated with the identified DEGs were investigated through three complementary enrichment analyses: (1) Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the KOBAS3.0 [12] to identify significantly dysregulated metabolic and signaling pathways (adjusted P-value < 0.05). (2) Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA): To overcome the arbitrary thresholds of DEG analysis, pre-ranked GSEA was performed using the clusterProfiler R package [13], genes were ranked by their log2 fold change, and enrichment was tested against the Hallmark (H: hallmark gene sets) and C7 (immunologic signature gene sets) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [14]. A false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 was considered significant as per established guidelines.

Immune and stromal cell infiltration estimation

The relative abundance of tumor-infiltrating immune and stromal cell populations was estimated using different deconvolution algorithms, including TIMER [15], quanTIseq [16], MCP-counter [17], EPIC [18], xCell [19] and CIBERSORT [20]. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to compare cell infiltration scores between groups.

Immune subtyping of adenomas

To identify distinct immune subtypes within the CRA cohort, non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) was performed on the batch-corrected gene expression matrix using the NMF package (v0.23.0). The analysis was restricted to a curated list of 1,836 immune-related genes, as defined by our previous study [21]. The factorization rank (k), representing the number of subtypes, was determined by iterating k from 2 to 6 and calculating the cophenetic correlation coefficient, dispersion, and residual sum of squares. The optimal k was selected at the value where the cophenetic coefficient began to decline. The NMF algorithm was run with 100 random initializations to ensure stability and avoid local minima. Samples were assigned to an immune subtype based on the highest membership probability in the resulting coefficient matrix.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.1.1). Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, unless otherwise specified for multiple testing corrections.

Results

Identification of distinct immune subtypes in CRA

To elucidate the heterogeneity of the TIME in pre-malignant CRA, we performed NMF clustering based on the expression profiles of 1,836 immune-related genes. This analysis robustly stratified the batch-corrected discovery cohort of 116 adenoma samples into two distinct immune subtypes (Fig. 1A and B, Figure S1), which we designated as adenoma immune subtype 1 (AIS1, n = 53) and adenoma immune subtype 2 (AIS2, n = 63). The clustering stability was confirmed by consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) and tracking plot analysis (Fig. 1A). To validate the two adenoma immune subtypes identified by the IRGs, we used another independent cohorts comprising 204 adenomas and also found the adenomas samples mainly fell into two clusters (Figure S2 and Table S1). However, there were no significant differences in clinical features such as age, sex, and pathological patterns between the two subtypes (Table S2).

We further investigated the TIME differences between the AIS1 and AIS2 subtypes in view of the unsupervised clustering was based on the IRGs. The AIS2 subtype was characterized by a significantly elevated leukocyte fraction (P < 0.0001, Wilcoxon rank-sum test), as estimated by the ESTIMATE algorithm, which also indicated remarkably lower tumor purity in this group (Fig. 1C). Subsequent deconvolution using different deconvolution algorithms revealed that the immune-hot subtype AIS2 was consistently markedly enriched for adaptive immune cells, including B cells, M2 macrophages, and plasma cells, as well as various antigen-presenting cells across deconvolution algorithms (e.g., different types of dendritic cells, Fig. 2, P < 0.01 for all). Conversely, the immune-cold subtype AIS1 exhibited a relative enrichment in certain immunosuppressive cell populations, such as Th2 cells (P = 6.9e-05, Fig. 2).

Adenoma has undergone immune reshuffling from normal tissue

To explore whether the IME have been reshuffled in precancerous condition, we compared the immune infiltration in normal mucosa, CRA and CRC using xCell because its more abundant cell inference. Notably, many immune cells such as B cells, CD8 T cells, CD8 Tcm, cDC, class switched memory B cells, DC, iDC, macrophages, mast cells, pDC, and plasma cells were significantly lowerly infiltrated in CRA and CRC than in normal mucosa (Figure S3). This trend was validated in another cohort GSE41657 that contained matched normal mucosa, CRA and CRC (Figure S4), suggesting that IME had been transformed in CRA and this change may be closely associated with high-risk progress to CRC.

To further clarify the TIME variation in adenoma, we deciphered the cell component change from matched normal, adenoma, and CRC using single cell sequencing dataset. Notably, colonocyte, fibroblast, CD8 T cells and NK cells have begun to decrease in adenomas. In contrast, stem cells, Tuft cells, proliferating cells, and neutrophil have begun to increase in adenomas (Fig. 3A-F).

We next investigated the tumor score derived from tumor cells in different stages of CRC development and found that the tumor score gradually increased from normal, paracancer, adenoma, and CRC (Fig. 4A). In addition, the exhausted CD8 T cell, coupled with total checkpoint strength and immune suppression score, rose sharply in adenoma and peaked in CRC (Fig. 4B and C). The cell-cell interaction number and strength within TIME also elevated dramatically in adenoma (Fig. 4D and E). These pre-malignant immune alteration showed a strong predisposition to progress to CRC implied an immunosuppressive microenvironment had begun to appear at the adenoma stage. Thus, we further inspected the potential link of the immune checkpoints evolution in different stages of disease progression. Results showed that the most notable change is the enhanced TIGIT pathway in adenoma and CRC compared to the normal tissue (Fig. 4F-K). This suggests that the immune contexture of the pre-malignant lesion may prefigure its molecular evolutionary trajectory.

Differential gene expression and functional enrichment analysis

To identify the molecular drivers underlying the two immune subtypes, we performed differential gene expression analysis, identifying 556 significant DEGs between Immune-Hot and Immune-Cold adenomas. GO enrichment analysis of the 471 upregulated genes in the immune-hot subtype AIS2 revealed a strong overrepresentation of terms related to extracellular space and adaptive immune response, including extracellular region (corrected P-value = 8.69e-54), collagen-containing extracellular matrix (corrected P-value = 1.64e-28), extracellular matrix structural constituent (corrected P-value = 3.80e-12), immune response (corrected P-value = 1.10e-18), inflammatory response (corrected P-value = 8.13e-16), neutrophil chemotaxis (corrected P-value = 3.46e-9), MHC class II protein complex (corrected P-value = 2.34e-7) and cytokine-mediated signaling pathway (corrected P-value = 3.80e-12). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further highlighted significant enrichment for immune-related pathways such as Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (corrected P-value = 1.45e-10), Antigen processing and presentation (corrected P-value = 6.88e-5), Osteoclast differentiation (corrected P-value = 2.43e-7), Leukocyte transendothelial migration (corrected P-value = 1.05e-4), and Chemokine signaling pathway (corrected P-value = 3.76e-7, Fig. 5A). Additionally, we also observed non-immunologically related Metabolic pathways (corrected P-value = 5.74e-6), Cell adhesion molecules (CAMs, corrected P-value = 2.51e-5), Proteoglycans in cancer (corrected P-value = 8.33e-5), Vascular smooth muscle contraction (corrected P-value = 2.85e-4), ECM-receptor interaction (corrected P-value = 6.08e-4) and NF-kappa B signaling pathway (corrected P-value = 1.35e-3) were enriched in the AIS2 subtype.

Functional enrichment analysis of the AIS groups. A Enriched KEGG pathways in the AIS2 subgroup. Larger solid circles correspond to a larger gene set size. B Enriched hallmarks between the AIS1 and AIS2 subgroups determined by GSEA

To gain a more nuanced understanding of coordinated biological processes, we performed GSEA. This confirmed a significant positive enrichment of hallmark gene sets including allograft rejection (normalized enrichment score, NES = 0.62, q-value = 1.38E-09), KRAS signaling UP (NES = 0.48, q-value = 4.88E-06), Inflammatory Response (NES = 0.46, q-value = 7.92E-06), Interferon Gamma Response (NES = 0.46, q-value = 8.84E-06), and IL6-JAK-STAT3 Signaling (NES = 0.56, q-value = 8.84E-06) in the immune-hot subtype AIS2 (Fig. 5B). Conversely, the immune-cold subtype AIS1 was enriched for G2M checkpoint (NES = -0.53, q-value = 1.38E-09), Mitotic spindle (NES = -0.34, q-value = 2.59E-05), and E2F target (NES = -0.58, q-value = 1.38E-09, Fig. 5B).

Discussion

This study utilized a comprehensive approach to de novo define and characterize distinct immune subtypes within CRA, the known precursors to the majority of CRC. As far as we know, very few studies have conducted molecular classification of intestinal adenomas based on its own transcriptome. In contrast to directly align CRA to CRC CMS subtypes that may ignore the immune characteristics of the adenoma itself [8, 22], we have fundamentally identified two robust immune subtypes that correlate with distinct biological pathways, immune microenvironments, and potential clinical trajectories, indicating a high heterogeneity of intestinal adenomas. These findings provide a nuanced view of adenoma immunobiology, suggesting that the immune contexture is established early in the carcinogenic process and may influence subsequent progression. Intriguingly, we found a dynamic immune evolution during colorectal tumorigenesis: from normal tissue to adenoma to CRC, tumor score, exhausted CD8 T cells, immune suppression, and cell-cell interaction intensity all increase stepwise, with a marked jump at the adenoma stage, suggesting that an immunosuppressive microenvironment begins to emerge early in premalignant lesions. Notably, the TIGIT pathway is strongly upregulated in both adenoma and CRC versus normal tissue, indicating that premalignant immune contexture may foreshadow molecular evolutionary trajectory and offer targets for early intervention (Fig. 4).

This stratification is highly consequential, as it mirrors the immune classification often observed in invasive CRC, indicating that these fundamental microenvironmental patterns are not late adaptations but are present in pre-malignant lesions [23, 24]. The immune-enriched adenomas characterized by strong immune activation and lymphocyte infiltration. This suggests that some immunogenic phenotype of CRCs may originate from a subset of adenomas that already exhibit a robust, albeit potentially ineffective, anti-tumoral immune response. Conversely, the immune-desert adenomas showed lack of immune infiltration, coupled with the upregulation of core metabolic and biosynthetic pathways identified in the DEG, GO, and KEGG analyses, points to an intrinsic tumor biology that is immunologically silent or suppressive. This subtype may represent adenomas that evolve strategies to evade immune surveillance from their earliest stages, potentially through metabolic competition or the absence of neoantigen presentation [24].

The immune-enriched subtype AIS2 was replete with different states of DC cells (immature dendritic cells (iDC), activated dendritic cells (aDC), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC), and conventional dendritic cells (cDC) and M1 macrophages, confirming an active anti-tumor milieu. The immune-cold subtype AIS1 showed an abundance of Th2 cells, corroborating the pathway findings of an active G2M checkpoint. These precise cellular definitions strengthen the validity of our transcriptomic-based classification.

This study is limited by its retrospective nature and reliance on publicly available genomic datasets. Validation in a larger, prospectively collected cohort with paired immunohistochemical analysis or even spatialomics is essential to confirm the spatial distribution of immune cells, particularly in the immune-cold subtype. Furthermore, longitudinal data tracking the progression of these specific adenoma subtypes to cancer would be invaluable in definitively establishing their prognostic significance.

In conclusion, we propose that immune subtyping of CRA reveals biologically distinct pathways of tumor-immune co-evolution. The identification of an immune-cold subtype, and its association with specific metabolic pathways offers a novel conceptual framework for adenoma interception. These subtypes may have potential value as biomarkers for risk stratification in patients with advanced adenomas, and could possibly help identify individuals who might be considered candidates for more tailored surveillance protocols or exploratory immunopreventive approaches targeting tumor microenvironment modulation to potentially delay or interrupt progression toward invasive disease.
