Learning pathway determines motor organization and long-term retention of tool use in hornbills
Authors: Elias Garcia-Pelegrin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySocial learning is widely used to explain behavioral traditions, yet whether it determines their long-term stability remains unclear. This study tested this in hornbills learning a novel stone-dropping tool task by dissociating individual reinforcement from action-based demonstration by a human experimenter. Hornbills did not innovate after exposure to the solved outcome alone. Although both routes enabled acquisition, they generated different motor solutions and sharply different retention. Individually trained birds solved rapidly using push-based insertions, whereas socially trained birds learned more slowly and adopted grab-lift actions matching the demonstration. After 12 months, all socially trained birds retained the behavior, whereas none of the individually trained birds did. Observing organized action may therefore promote the consolidation of novel behavior into durable, tradition-like forms.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Natural sciences, Zoology

Introduction

Social learning enables individuals to acquire novel behaviors through exposure to the actions or outcomes of others and plays a central role in the emergence and maintenance of behavioral traditions across taxa.1,2 By reducing the costs and risks of individual exploration, social transmission can accelerate the spread of innovations and generate population-level behavioral variation.3,4 In some systems, socially transmitted foraging techniques persist across generations, forming tradition-like behavioral patterns.5,6 Understanding how socially acquired behaviors are encoded and stabilized is therefore critical for explaining the emergence of animal cultures.7,8,9

Despite broad agreement that social learning facilitates behavioral transmission to some degree, its underlying mechanisms remain contested. Some suggest that social learning reflects domain-general associative processes operating on socially derived stimuli.10,11,12 Observing others may enhance attention to objects or locations (stimulus or local enhancement),13 thereby facilitating individual trial-and-error learning without copying motor structure.14 In contrast, action-copying accounts argue that observing temporally organized motor sequences may lead to encoding of structured motor representations that constrain behavioral form.15,16 Distinguishing between copying outcomes and copying actions remains central to comparative cognition, particularly in tool-use contexts where motor structure defines the identity of the behavior itself.17,18,19,20

Tool use in birds has served as a primary model for investigating the evolution and distribution of cognition. In New Caledonian crows (Corvus moneduloides), tool manufacture varies across populations in ways consistent with socially influenced traditions.21,22 Meanwhile, laboratory studies show flexible tool use and manufacture in other corvids, including rooks (Corvus frugilegus) and Hawaiian crows (Corvus hawaiiensis).23,24 Convergent evidence from parrots further indicates that tool-related problem solving is not confined to a single avian lineage. Experimental work in kea (Nestor notabilis) and Goffin’s cockatoos (Cacatua goffiniana) demonstrates flexible tool manufacture and use in novel laboratory tasks, including spontaneous combination and modification of objects to obtain rewards,25,26 suggesting that tool use has evolved independently across multiple avian clades.

Given that tool use has been central to discussions of animal traditions,7,22 understanding its evolutionary significance requires examining not only how such behaviors are acquired but also how stable they remain over time. Cultural behaviors can only persist within populations if they are consolidated and retained beyond initial acquisition. Although long-term memory capacities are well documented in birds, including episodic-like memory in scrub jays (Aphelocoma coerulescens)27 and extended spatial retention in food-caching species,28,29 most experimental social-learning studies assess performance immediately or within days of acquisition.30,31,32 Consequently, whether different informational routes to learning produce differences in long-term behavioral stability remains largely unresolved.

At the same time, research on avian tool cognition has been strongly biased toward corvids and parrots, taxa that include habitual tool users or species with extensive object manipulation experience. This focus makes it difficult to determine whether durable tool-use learning reflects lineage-specific ecological specialization or more general cognitive capacities associated with the avian pallial expansion seen in corvids and psittacids.33,34,35 Discriminating between these alternatives requires testing large-brained but non-habitual tool users under controlled conditions and evaluating not only acquisition, but retention. Addressing this bias requires expanding experimental investigation beyond traditional corvid and psittacid models to other encephalized avian lineages. Addressing this issue requires extending experimental investigation beyond traditional corvid and psittacid models to other encephalized avian lineages and evaluating not only acquisition but also long-term retention. If action-based demonstration promotes more robust motor encoding than outcome-based shaping alone, it may generate greater behavioral persistence over extended intervals.

Hornbills (Bucerotidae) provide a compelling comparative test case. They possess relatively large brains for their body size35 and several species exhibit complex social dynamics, including long-term pair bonds and fission-fusion foraging aggregations that create repeated opportunities for social information use and transmission.36,37 Despite these traits, hornbills are not habitual tool users and remain largely absent from experimental social learning research. Yet recent evidence demonstrates advanced object permanence in Oriental pied hornbills (Anthracoceros albirostris), with performance comparable to that reported in corvids, parrots, and great apes.38 In addition, hornbills exhibit flexible motor responses to novel problem-solving tasks, adjusting their action strategies in response to task affordances rather than rigidly applying stereotyped movements.39 Related object-dropping capacities have also recently been reported in the Southern ground hornbill (Bucorvus leadbeateri) in an Aesop’s-fable-style stone-dropping task.40 Although Bucorvus is a sister lineage to, and phylogenetically distant, from the remaining hornbills,41 this finding suggests that object-dropping solutions may be present across more than one branch of Bucerotiformes. Together, these findings indicate representational and motor integration capacities relevant to innovation, suggesting that hornbills possess the cognitive prerequisites for acquiring and organizing novel tool-use behaviors. Testing tool-use acquisition and retention in hornbills, therefore, allows the evaluation of whether durable tool learning reflects lineage-specific adaptation or more broadly distributed neurocognitive capacities.

This study experimentally investigated acquisition and long-term retention of a novel stone-dropping tool-use behavior (Figure 1 and Videos S1, S2, and S3) in a sample of 10 hornbills from 5 different species (see Methods). By dissociating outcome exposure, individual reinforcement shaping, and action-based social demonstration (provided by a human experimenter rather than by a conspecific, allowing the demonstrated motor sequence to be standardized across subjects), the study tested whether exposure to the solved outcome alone elicits innovation, whether distinct informational routes enable acquisition, and critically, whether the learning route predicts retention after a 12-month interval. By assessing both immediate probe performance and delayed retention, and by analyzing motor patterns used during solution, we examined whether action-based social input promotes more durable and structured behavioral encoding than individual reinforcement alone. If social demonstration yields more stereotyped motor sequences and greater long-term stability, this would support the hypothesis that observing organized action scaffolds persistent motor representations. Conversely, equivalent acquisition and retention across conditions would favor accounts in which social learning operates primarily through domain-general associative processes.

Results and discussion

Acquisition performance and motor form across learning routes

Figure 2 shows acquisition performance and motor form across the two guided learning routes. End-state exposure alone did not elicit spontaneous innovation: No subject dropped a stone into the apparatus to dislodge the food reward after seeing only the solved outcome (see Video S1). By contrast, both guided routes supported acquisition (see Videos S2 and S3), with 4/5 birds reaching criterion in the individual learning condition and 4/5 birds reaching criterion in the social learning condition. However, the two routes produced different acquisition trajectories. Among solvers, individual learning birds reached first success in the first acquisition session, whereas social learning birds required multiple sessions to acquire the task (individual: median = 1 session, mean = 1.0, SD = 0; social: median = 8.5 sessions, mean = 8.0, SD = 2.16; Wilcoxon rank-sum: W = 0, p = 0.0211; Figure 2A). Latency to solve during the first successful acquisition session did not differ between conditions (individual: median = 129 s; social: median = 228 s; Wilcoxon rank-sum: W = 7, p = 0.885).

Individual learners (orange) reached first success in the first session, whereas social learning birds (purple) required multiple sessions, although latency during the first successful session did not differ between conditions.

(B) Motor form expressed at first success. Each point represents one bird. All individual learners used a Push insertion solution, whereas all social learning birds used a Grab insertion solution.

(C) Manipulation effort at first success (number of object manipulations during the first successful trial). Points represent individual birds; horizontal bars indicate group medians.

Despite equivalent acquisition success, the learning route completely predicted the motor form of the solution. All individual learning solvers used a Push insertion solution, whereas all social learning solvers used a Grab insertion solution (4/4 in each condition; Fisher’s exact test: p = 0.0286; Figure 2B). Social learning solvers also required more manipulations before success than individual learning solvers (individual: median = 1, mean = 1.25; social: median = 3, mean = 3.5; Wilcoxon rank-sum: W = 0.5, p = 0.0360; Figure 2C). Thus, the informational route did not simply determine whether hornbills acquired the task but shaped the motor organization through which the solution was expressed.

One-year retention and motor persistence

Figure 3 shows retention performance after a 12-month interval. The primary retention endpoint included seven birds: four individual learning birds and three social learning birds. Retention differed sharply by learning route. All social learning birds succeeded at 12 months, whereas no individual learning bird solved the task (3/3 versus 0/4; Fisher’s exact test: p = 0.0286; Figure 3A). Among the social learning birds that retained the behavior, motor form also persisted across the 1-year interval. Each retained bird expressed Grab as its dominant Stage 3 solution, matching its dominant motor form during the immediate post-acquisition test sessions (3/3 birds; Figures 3B and 3C). Successful retention did not necessarily reflect effortless performance: at the individual level, two of the three retained social learning birds showed higher median manipulation counts at 12 months than during acquisition, whereas one bird showed a slight reduction. Thus, socially learned performance remained successful and motorically stable, even though solving after a long delay often involved additional attempts to position and insert the stone.

(D) Individual learning birds: per-bird action composition (Grab vs. Push) at the first successful stage 2 acquisition session and at the unsuccessful 12-month post-acquisition session.

By contrast, individual learning birds did not solve the task at 12 months, but manipulation descriptions show that they continued to engage with the apparatus and expressed push-dominant strategies. In both Stage 3 post-acquisition test sessions, trial-level behavior contained repeated “push” actions interleaved with some “grab” actions (Figure 3D). These data indicate that individual learning birds did not fail due to non-engagement; rather, they persisted in push-oriented manipulation patterns during delayed testing but did not achieve successful insertion and reward release.

Learning route dissociates acquisition from long-term retention

The dissociation between acquisition and retention suggests that successful task performance alone is an incomplete measure of behavioral learning. Individual reinforcement shaping produced rapid acquisition, yet this solution did not persist after 12 months. In contrast, action-based social exposure produced slower acquisition but durable retention and persistent motor organization. Together, these findings indicate that action-based social input was associated with structured and persistent motor representations in a non-canonical tool-using lineage (Bucerotiformes).

One possible contributor to this difference is that socially trained birds accumulated more interaction with the apparatus before achieving their first successful insertion. Because they required more sessions to reach acquisition, they experienced more opportunities to engage with the apparatus and stone prior to solving the task. Greater repetition of action attempts could strengthen the association between behavior and reward, potentially facilitating consolidation into long-term memory. However, once the first successful insertion occurred, all individuals were transferred to identical post-acquisition test session schedules irrespective of condition, ensuring that post-acquisition reinforcement history was equivalent. Thus, while greater pre-acquisition interaction may have contributed to consolidation, it likely reflects a natural consequence of the learning pathway rather than differential reinforcement following success.

The route-dependent motor divergence further suggests that social information structured the form of the behavior. Individual learning birds converged on push-based insertions, whereas social learning birds converged on grab-lift insertions. Because both motor patterns were physically possible across conditions, this divergence is difficult to explain through apparatus constraints alone. It is also difficult to reconcile with accounts in which social learning operates purely through outcome emulation or stimulus enhancement.8,12,42 If social input merely increased attention to the apparatus, stone, or reward location, motor patterns should have converged toward individually discovered solutions. Instead, action exposure predicted motor identity, immediate stabilization, and long-term persistence, consistent with the encoding of organized motor sequences.15,16,19

Durability was not accompanied by identical efficiency. Retained social learning birds often required more manipulations at 12 months than during acquisition, suggesting that the behavioral structure remained available even if procedural fluency diminished over time. The persistence of grab-lift actions despite the availability of a less effortful push solution suggests that retained behavior was not simply governed by immediate motor efficiency but may have reflected persistence of the originally acquired action sequence. By contrast, individual learning birds continued to engage with the apparatus at 12 months and frequently produced push-dominant manipulation sequences, indicating that failure was not attributable to non-engagement. Instead, their interactions remained effortful but structurally insufficient to generate successful insertion and reward release. This suggests that individual reinforcement can generate short-term performance solutions that remain behaviorally accessible in some form, but do not necessarily consolidate into durable tool-use routines.

This dissociation has implications for how behavioral traditions stabilize in animal populations. Behaviors acquired rapidly through direct reinforcement may remain fragile, whereas behaviors acquired through structured social input may persist without ongoing reinforcement. Such differences in consolidation could determine whether innovations last long enough to be socially transmitted, suggesting that the architecture of initial encoding may shape the emergence and maintenance of behavioral traditions.

Implications

These findings extend comparative evidence beyond traditional corvid and psittacid models. Hornbills are not habitual tool users and remain largely absent from experimental social learning research. Demonstrating durable tool retention in this lineage suggests that the cognitive prerequisites for structured and persistent tool behavior are not restricted to lineages with ecological tool specialization. If durable motor encoding can be induced through action-based social exposure in a non-tool-specialist lineage, then lineage-specific ecological adaptation may not be a necessary condition for stabilized tool behavior. Instead, broader neurocognitive capacities may support the emergence of tradition-like stability under appropriate informational conditions. In birds, such capacities are often linked to the enlargement of the nidopallium caudolaterale (NCL), a prefrontal-analogous structure associated with executive control, working memory, and flexible action selection.34,43 Hornbills possess relatively large brains for their body size,35 and recent behavioral work demonstrates complex cognitive capacity in this lineage.38,39,44 The present findings, therefore, suggest that the neurocognitive architecture required for structured social learning and durable tool behavior may be more broadly distributed across encephalized avian taxa than previously appreciated. Importantly, this does not imply that hornbills exhibit cumulative culture or high-fidelity imitation, but rather that they possess the representational and motor integration capacities necessary to organize socially acquired action into stable behavioral patterns.

Indeed, the present findings do not require invoking high-fidelity imitation. The data do not demonstrate copying of precise motor kinematics, but rather route-dependent organization of action structure. Socially trained individuals converged on a grab-lift-based solution, whereas individually reinforcement-trained birds converged on a push-based solution. The mechanism underlying this divergence may involve attentional biasing toward demonstrated affordances, enhanced encoding of motor sequencing, or differences in exploratory dynamics shaped by observation. Furthermore, while motor divergence strongly suggests structured action encoding, the present design cannot fully disentangle imitation from other forms of action-based learning such as emulation.13,14 Future work manipulating the visibility of motor trajectories independently of outcome information would help refine mechanistic interpretation. Thus, this study does not claim that hornbills imitate in the strict sense, but the results demonstrate that they use social information in ways that alter behavioral structure and durability.

Overall, the informational route during acquisition shaped not only whether a novel tool-use behavior was learned, but whether it endured. In hornbills, action-based social learning scaffolded route-specific motor organization and 1-year retention, whereas individual reinforcement produced rapid acquisition without durable performance. These findings suggest that behavioral traditions may depend not only on innovation or social exposure, but on whether transmitted information organizes action in ways that can be stabilized over time.

Limitations of the study

The present study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample size was necessarily small (see STAR Methods, “subjects”), reflecting the practical constraints of conducting longitudinal cognitive experiments with large, long-lived, non-model avian species in zoological settings.45,46 Moreover, the sample comprised of five distinct hornbill species, and species could not be evenly balanced across conditions. The study, therefore, does not test species-level differences and should not be interpreted as showing that all hornbill species respond similarly. Instead, hornbills are treated here as an underrepresented comparative lineage for asking whether non-habitual tool-using birds can acquire and retain a novel tool-use routine under different learning conditions. Future work with larger within-species samples will be needed to determine whether these effects generalize across Bucerotidae or vary with ecology, morphology, and social organization.

The demonstrated grab-lift action was selected because it was physically possible but distinct from the push-based insertion that hornbills were expected to discover through individual interaction with the apparatus. This made it possible to ask whether action-based exposure could bias the motor form of the solution. However, the fact that individual learning birds did not spontaneously converge on this action suggests that the demonstrated motor form may have been less natural, less efficient, or more demanding than the push solution. This strengthens the interpretation that social learning birds were influenced by the demonstrated action, but it also means that motor form and learning route were confounded in the present design.

It thus remains possible that the physical demands of the resulting actions contributed to differences in retention. The grab-lift insertion expressed by social learning birds required picking up the stone and positioning it above the aperture, whereas individual learning birds typically pushed the stone toward the opening. Actions requiring greater motor coordination or attentional engagement may generate stronger encoding and therefore more durable representations.47,48 Because learning route, motor form, and pre-acquisition experience covaried in the present design, the retention difference cannot be attributed uniquely to the social source of information. Instead, the findings show that action-based demonstration generated a distinct motor solution, which was associated with stronger long-term retention. Future work that independently manipulates demonstrator identity, motor form, and reinforcement history will be needed to determine which component drives the retention advantage. The use of a human demonstrator also limits the naturalistic interpretation of the social learning condition. Hornbills may attend differently to humans and conspecifics, and conspecific demonstrations could plausibly produce faster acquisition or different patterns of motor matching. The slower acquisition observed in the social learning condition may therefore partly reflect the use of a heterospecific demonstrator. Nevertheless, the convergence of social learning birds on the demonstrated grab-lift solution suggests that they extracted action-relevant information from the demonstration despite the demonstrator being human.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ten adult hornbills representing five species participated: oriental pied hornbills (Anthracoceros albirostris, n = 5), great pied hornbills (Buceros bicornis, n = 2), trumpeter hornbill (Bycanistes bucinator, n = 1), Papuan hornbill (Rhyticeros plicatus, n = 1), and plain pouched hornbill (Rhyticeros subruficollis, n = 1). None had prior experience with stone-dropping tasks or comparable object-insertion problems. Subjects were housed at the Mandai Wildlife Reserve Collection either individually (if unpaired) or in stable pairs within outdoor aviaries measuring 6 × 4 × 3 m (width × length × height). Birds remained on their standard feeding schedule throughout the study, receiving a diet of fresh fruits and formulated nutrient pellets, with continuous access to water. No food deprivation procedures were used.

Following Stage 1 (End-State Exposure) testing, individuals were allocated to acquisition conditions (see Table S1). Four birds were assigned to the Individual learning condition and five to the Social learning condition. One socially trained individual (Harriet (Rhyticeros subruficollis)) died of unrelated causes prior to the 12-month retention test and was therefore unable to perform delayed testing. All testing sessions were video recorded for subsequent behavioral coding. Primary coding was conducted by a single experimenter (E.G.-P.). A subset of sessions (20%) was independently coded by a second observer from video recordings to assess inter-rater reliability.

Method details

Apparatus

The apparatus consisted of an opaque 3D printed red slanted tube mounted on a stable base. A reward platform was positioned within an internal compartment such that insertion of a stone through the top aperture dislodged the platform and released a food reward at a retrieval opening located at the base. The internal configuration prevented direct access to the reward without stone insertion (see Figure 1).

At the beginning of each trial, a single stone was placed adjacent to the tube opening within one body length of the subject. The relative spatial arrangement of stone and apparatus was standardised across trials and conditions.

Experimental design

The experiment comprised of three stages designed to dissociate informational routes to acquisition and to evaluate long-term retention.

Stage 1: End-State Exposure

Subjects were presented with the apparatus in a solved configuration (i.e., with the reward dispenser already been activated and thus all the rewards having fallen on the floor of the testing enclosure). Following consumption of all the reward pellets and a 15-min exposure period, the subject was given access to the baited apparatus and stone (placed on top of the apparatus within 5 cm of the hole) for another 15 min. This phase aimed at assessing spontaneous tool use innovation based solely on outcome information (see Video S1).

Stage 2: Acquisition and test sessions

Subjects were randomly assigned to one of two experience conditions designed to contrast individual reinforcement learning with socially scaffolded action learning. Across both conditions, successful completion was defined as full insertion of the stone into the apparatus tube, resulting in mechanical activation and reward release.

In the Individual learning condition, no demonstrator was present. To enable reinforcement of insertion behavior without prior action demonstration, a reward pellet was positioned between the stone and the outer surface of the apparatus, immediately adjacent to the insertion hole. When a bird reached to retrieve the pellet, contact with the stone displaced it forward and caused it to drop into the insertion tube, thereby activating the mechanism and releasing the reward (see Video S2).

In the Social learning condition, observers witnessed the experimenter perform the complete action sequence required to insert the stone and release the reward. The demonstrator executed a grab-lift-based insertion sequence in full view of the observer (see Video S3).

Immediately after consuming all rewards available during the Acquisition session (individual or social), subjects were given a Test session in which they had access to the baited apparatus and the stone under identical physical conditions across treatments. Test sessions lasted a maximum of 15 min or until the subject solved the task, terminating 1 min after the first successful stone insertion. Sessions were conducted immediately prior to routine feeding to standardise motivational state. Birds that failed to solve the task were tested again on subsequent days for up to 10 acquisition an test sessions.

Stage 2.1: Immediate post-acquisition test sessions

After acquisition, subjects participated in three post-aquisition sessions administered during three consecutive days (1 session per day) to evaluate short-term stabilisation. Each session was conducted without demonstrations or shaping and lasted up to 15 min, or until the task was solved.

Stage 3: Long-term retention sessions

Approximately 12 months after acquisition, subjects were re-tested in two post-acquisition test sessions. The apparatus configuration was identical to acquisition. No additional demonstrations or shaping occurred between acquisition and retention testing.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Coding and reliability

Behavioral coding

All sessions were video recorded and analyzed frame-by-frame. For each trial, the experimenter recorded whether the subject successfully inserted the stone and released the reward, the latency to solve the problem, and the motor actions used to solve it.

Motor actions in successful trials were classified as push-based (directly pecking or pushing the stone into the aperture without using the bill) or grab-based (grabbing with the bill, lifting and positioning before release, including dropping or throwing from a distance). All grab-based variants were grouped together for analysis.

Manipulation count was defined as the number of discrete contacts with the stone resulting in displacement or reorientation from first contact to successful insertion.

Inter-rater reliability

To assess coding reliability, a second independent coder, blind to experimental condition and study hypotheses, re-coded a subset of trials comprising 20% of all video trials, randomly selected across individuals, stages, and conditions. Agreement was assessed for (i) motor form classification (push-based vs. grab-based insertion), (ii) success outcome (Solved vs. not solved), and (iii) manipulation count.

Inter-rater agreement for motor form classification was high (Cohen’s κ = 0.80), indicating substantial agreement. Agreement for success outcome was near perfect (κ > 0.90). For manipulation counts, inter-rater reliability was assessed using an intraclass correlation coefficient (two-way random effects model), which indicated strong agreement (ICC >0.85). Discrepancies were resolved through joint review prior to final analysis.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3). Given the modest sample size (N = 10) and complete separation in several categorical outcomes (e.g., motor form and 12-month retention success by condition), we used conservative non-parametric and exact tests.

Acquisition success and delayed retention success were summarised descriptively by condition. For the primary retention endpoint, condition differences in success were tested using Fisher’s exact test.

Acquisition speed was quantified as the number of acquisition sessions required to reach first success. Differences in sessions-to-first-success were evaluated using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test including only birds that solved during Stage 2 acquisition. Latency to solve on the first successful acquisition session was compared between conditions using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Motor forms were categorized as Push (peck-push insertion) or Grab (grab-lift-drop or grab-lift-throw insertion). Condition differences in motor form at acquisition (in successful trials only) were assessed using Fisher’s exact test. Motor stereotypy across immediate post-aquisition sessions was quantified descriptively as the proportion of successful post-aquisition trials performed using the individual’s dominant motor category.

Manipulation count was defined as the number of discrete manipulation events from first interaction with the stone to session termination or success. Manipulation counts at acquisition (in successful trials only) were compared between conditions using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. To evaluate whether retained performance after a 12-month interval required greater action effort, manipulation counts for Social learning birds were summarised at the individual level (median manipulations per bird at Stage 2 and Stage 3). For Individual learning birds at 12 months (which did not succeed), engagement and strategy were characterised descriptively using manipulation descriptions, including trial-level counts of “push” and “grab” tokens as a conservative index of strategy emphasis.
