Allostatic load and uterine fibroids: role in pathogenesis and racial disparities
Authors: Richard Chern, Rachel Michel, Warren B Nothnick, Julie Christianson, Mary Elizabeth Hughes, Mostafa A Borahay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUterine fibroids are the most common gynecologic tumors. Despite their non-cancerous nature, they pose significant symptoms that can impact women’s quality of life and mental health. Black women have the highest incidence of uterine fibroids, with a lifetime risk of about 80%. Allostatic load reflects the cumulative biological consequences of chronic stress arising from environmental and social exposures, including low socioeconomic status, microaggression, and racial discrimination. Disproportionately prevalent among Black women, allostatic load is associated with adverse health outcomes across multiple organ systems, and emerging epidemiological evidence suggests a plausible association with uterine fibroids. This review examines how allostatic load is measured and its effects on the body’s regulatory systems, including neuroendocrine, immune, and metabolic pathways. Importantly, allostatic load is presented here as a hypothesis-generating integrative framework rather than an established causal mechanism. This review also explores the epidemiological and biological evidence linking chronic stress to uterine fibroids, with particular attention to the potential role this framework plays in contributing to racial disparities in fibroid burden among Black women. A distinction is drawn between the biological embedding of chronic stress (allostatic load), structural determinants of health (socioeconomic status, healthcare access), and interpersonal factors (clinician bias, institutional mistrust)—each representing complementary but conceptually distinct contributors to observed disparities. In addition, this review discusses the potential impact of stress, lifestyle factors, and genetic predisposition on fibroid development, and recommends preventive strategies encompassing both public health and individual-level interventions.Keywords: Uterine fibroids, Leiomyoma, Allostatic load, Chronic stress, Mental health, Racial disparities

Introduction

Uterine fibroids, or uterine leiomyomas, are the most common benign tumors in women, with around 200,000 cases reported annually. Black women are disproportionately affected, with approximately 80% developing fibroids at some point in their lives compared to 70% of Caucasian women. Fibroids are hormone-dependent tumors, primarily thriving on estrogen, which means they often develop after the onset of menstruation and typically shrink after menopause [66]. The primary symptoms of fibroids include heavy, prolonged, and/or irregular menstrual bleeding, pelvic pain, frequent urination, constipation, and pain during intercourse, all of which can significantly impact a woman’s quality of life. The reasons for the higher incidence of fibroids among Black women are not fully understood but several factors have been proposed. Growing evidence suggests that chronic psychosocial stress from environmental exposures, in conjunction with genetic susceptibility, may be associated with the development or worsening of uterine fibroids, though direct causal relationships remain incompletely established. The physical symptoms associated with uterine fibroids are frequently accompanied by psychological distress, including depression and anxiety disorders [29], suggesting a potentially bidirectional relationship in which stress may contribute to fibroid pathology while fibroid burden may, in turn, exacerbate psychological stress.

Overview of allostatic load

a. What is allostatic load?

Overview

Allostatic load refers to the cumulative physiological and psychological burden that accrues when the body’s stress-response systems are repeatedly or chronically activated beyond their adaptive capacity. Under normal conditions, the body maintains internal stability through homeostatic mechanisms, mounting a coordinated stress response—mediated primarily by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and the sympathetic nervous system—that resolves once the stressor has passed. However, when stressors are prolonged, repeated, or perceived as uncontrollable, these same adaptive systems can become dysregulated, shifting from protective to pathological. Individual susceptibility to this transition is shaped by genetic predisposition, early life experiences, and environmental context, which collectively modulate both the magnitude and duration of the stress response [53].

The biological consequences of sustained allostatic load are multisystemic. At the neuroendocrine level, chronic stress activates the pituitary gland to release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), which in turn drives adrenal secretion of cortisol. While cortisol is essential for short-term metabolic and immune regulation, its chronic elevation disrupts the negative feedback mechanisms that normally constrain HPA axis activity. This dysregulation has been associated with a broad range of adverse health outcomes, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes, musculoskeletal disorders, periodontal disease, reproductive dysfunction, and malignancy. Chronically elevated cortisol also promotes oxidative stress—defined as an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidants—with downstream consequences including DNA and RNA damage [30].

At the neuropsychological level, high allostatic load has been associated with structural changes in stress-sensitive brain regions, including the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, contributing to impaired cognitive function and emotional regulation [53]. These changes may manifest clinically as major depressive disorder (MDD), generalized anxiety, panic disorder, and, in the context of sustained or severe exposure, accelerated cognitive decline [55]. Importantly, psychological and physiological dysregulation are bidirectionally linked: mental health sequelae of allostatic load may themselves perpetuate HPA axis activation, further compounding the cumulative biological burden.

b. Stressors, perception of stress, and allostatic load

The relationship between stressors, perception of stress, and allostatic load is shaped by a dynamic interplay of external challenges, individual interpretations, and the body’s physiological reactions. Stressors, which can be physiological or psychological in nature and range from acute to chronic in duration, disrupt an individual’s homeostasis. The way these stressors are perceived is crucial, as individual responses are influenced by factors like past experiences, personality traits, coping skills, and social support systems. This means that the same stressor can evoke different reactions in different people depending on how they perceive its intensity and their capacity to handle it [84]. When stressors repeatedly activate the body’s stress response systems, this can lead to a cumulative strain known as allostatic load. This strain, caused by the overwork of physiological systems can contribute to a variety of health issues over time. The way stressors are perceived and managed significantly affects the development of allostatic load, which in turn impacts long-term health outcomes [61].

c. Sources of psychological and physiological stress

Socioeconomic Status (SES)

Several factors contribute to psychological stress, including low socioeconomic status (SES). Individuals with low SES, generally have low incomes, and may find securing basic necessities such as food, shelter, and healthcare difficult. This insecurity is a major source of stress, particularly for those in caretaker roles who are responsible for providing for their families [46]. Furthermore, low SES often means working low-paying or minimum-wage jobs, which are typically insecure and come with poor working conditions, increasing stress. Access to healthcare is another critical issue for those with low SES. Healthcare in the United States is notoriously expensive, and without health insurance, medical bills can be prohibitively costly. Families in low SES often lack health insurance and may avoid seeking medical care until absolutely necessary. When they do seek treatment, it is often too late, leading to worse health outcomes [9].

Microaggressions and discrimination

Black women among other racial minority groups frequently encounter microaggressions and discrimination, both of which can have profound and lasting effects. Frequent exposure to microaggressions—subtle, often unintentional biases or insults—whether directly experienced or witnessed in various settings, can be a source of chronic stress. This persistent stress can lead to physical health problems, lower self-esteem, and negatively impact mental well-being. Beyond microaggressions, many minority groups face overt discrimination, particularly in the workplace. This discrimination can manifest in hiring practices, where individuals might be denied jobs based on their race, nationality, religion, or sexual orientation. Such practices contribute to job disparities, resulting in lower wages, fewer job opportunities, and the challenge of overcoming the “glass ceiling” that limits career advancement for marginalized groups [80].

Residential segregation

Despite federal laws prohibiting discrimination based on race, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, and disability, housing discrimination remains a significant issue in the United States [32]. Housing discrimination manifested in various forms in the past, including practices like blockbusting, redlining, and the remnants of Jim Crow laws [23]. The legacy of these practices remains, as shown by marked residential segregation by race in US communities. Moreover, areas with high concentrations of Black people are less likely to have resources supporting good health. For example, one study found that non-Hispanic Black Americans were more likely to report living in food swamps than non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Asian, or Hispanic adults. Additionally, residents of perceived food deserts and swamps had poorer diets compared to those not living in such areas [17]. Food deserts are areas where access to affordable and nutritious food is limited, while food swamps are areas where unhealthy food options, such as fast food and junk food, are more prevalent than healthier choices [18]. Research has also shown that predominantly Black neighborhoods have less access to chain supermarkets or large grocery stores and more access to fast-food outlets compared to non-Hispanic White neighborhoods [44]. Consequently, lack of access to healthy food options, combined with lower income levels, can lead residents to choose cheaper, less nutritious fast food, contributing to higher rates of obesity and other health issues among Black women [68].

d. How is allostatic load measured?

Allostatic load is quantified using composite biomarker indices that capture cumulative physiological dysregulation across multiple organ systems, rather than through subjective stress appraisal alone. The most widely used approach aggregates 10–15 biomarkers spanning neuroendocrine, cardiovascular, metabolic, and immune domains, with each biomarker assigned a risk score based on whether it falls within a clinically adverse range [71]. The composite allostatic load index thereby reflects the total burden of biological dysregulation attributable to chronic stress exposure, independent of any individual system.

Neuroendocrine markers reflect HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system activity and include urinary or salivary cortisol, urinary catecholamines (epinephrine and norepinephrine), and dehydroepiandrosterone sulphate (DHEA-S) [71]. Cortisol is the primary index of HPA axis output, and its chronic elevation is associated with immune suppression, metabolic dysregulation, and hippocampal structural change [53]. DHEA-S, which exerts antagonistic effects to cortisol, tends to decline with sustained stress and is included as an index of neuroendocrine imbalance [30].

Cardiovascular markers typically include resting systolic/diastolic blood pressure and resting heart rate. These capture the cumulative vascular toll of sustained sympathetic activation and are among the most robustly replicated components of the allostatic load index [71].

Metabolic markers reflect the downstream consequences of chronic cortisol elevation and autonomic dysregulation on energy metabolism. They commonly include waist-to-hip ratio or waist circumference as indices of visceral adiposity, fasting glucose or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and triglycerides [71]. Visceral adiposity is of particular relevance given evidence linking it to fibroid pathogenesis through adipokine signaling and chronic inflammation [30].

Inflammatory markers, most commonly high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), are increasingly incorporated into allostatic load indices as evidence of the immunological consequences of chronic stress has grown [30]. Elevated inflammatory tone is a recognized feature of high allostatic load and has direct mechanistic relevance to fibroid development, as discussed in subsequent sections.

Allostatic load scores are most commonly derived using one of two approaches: a count-based method, in which each biomarker exceeding a predetermined risk threshold—typically the highest or lowest quartile of a reference population—contributes one point to the composite score; or a continuous summation method, in which standardized values across biomarkers are summed [71]. Both approaches have been validated in large epidemiological cohorts, including the MacArthur Studies of Successful Aging [71]. Higher allostatic load scores have been consistently associated with increased morbidity, accelerated biological aging, and all-cause mortality across diverse populations [30].

It is important to distinguish allostatic load—a biological composite—from perceived or self-reported stress, which reflects subjective appraisal of environmental demands. While the two constructs are conceptually related and empirically correlated, they are not equivalent: individuals may carry high allostatic load in the absence of consciously reported distress, particularly when chronic stressors have been normalized through prolonged exposure [54]. Self-reported instruments such as the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) and the Adverse Childhood Experience International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ) are valuable tools for characterizing the psychosocial exposures that contribute to allostatic load accumulation, but they should not be conflated with direct measurement of allostatic load itself [71].

In the context of racial health disparities, allostatic load indices have been shown to be significantly higher in Black Americans compared to White Americans even after adjustment for socioeconomic status [28]. This is consistent with the hypothesis that chronic exposure to racism and structural disadvantage accelerates biological aging and cumulative physiological dysregulation—a phenomenon described as “weathering” by Geronimus et al. [28]. This racial differential in allostatic load provides a biologically grounded framework for understanding the disproportionate fibroid burden among Black women, as explored in subsequent sections.

Allostatic load and uterine fibroids

a. Epidemiologic evidence of the association

Meta-analysis study

A meta-analysis by Qin et al. examined the link between chronic stress and the risk of developing uterine fibroids by analyzing data from various observational studies. The researchers systematically searched databases such as PubMed and Embase, selected studies that met specific inclusion criteria, and extracted data on stress exposure and fibroid incidence. The analysis found that chronic psychological stress may increase the risk of uterine fibroids by 24%. Subgroup analyses showed that non-Hispanic Black women experiencing severe stress had a higher prevalence of fibroids than those with no or mild stress [64]. In addition, the subgroup meta-analysis further suggested that non-Hispanic Black women might be at a higher risk for fibroids under chronic stress compared to other racial groups.

Prospective cohort study on lifetime abusive events

The study analyzed data from a prospective cohort of Black women to investigate the relationship between lifetime abuse experiences and the presence of uterine fibroids. Researchers collected detailed information through surveys on various forms of abuse—physical, sexual, and emotional. They then used statistical analyses to assess how these abuse histories correlated with fibroid development. The analysis, which controlled for potential confounding factors, revealed a significant association between lifetime abuse victimization and an increased risk of uterine fibroids. Women with a history of abuse were found to have a notably higher likelihood of developing fibroids compared to those without such experiences. This association remained significant even after adjusting for other variables, indicating that a history of abuse, a source of chronic stress, may be an important risk factor for fibroid development [90].

b. Potential mechanisms of the association

Moderate levels of stress, often called “positive stress”, or “eustress”, can enhance performance by motivating individuals and sharpening focus. This concept is illustrated by the stress curve, where moderate stress leads to optimal functioning. However, when stress levels exceed this optimal range, performance declines, and the risk of stress-related health issues increases [47].

HPA axis and cortisol

Cortisol levels are regulated through a negative feedback system in the hypothalamus. However, prolonged stress can contribute to persistently elevated cortisol levels, which can disrupt the normal functioning of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in broader endocrine imbalances [41]. This disruption can affect the regulation of key reproductive hormones such as estrogen and progesterone [12]. Since fibroids are estrogen-sensitive, these hormonal disturbances may promote their growth [70].

Moreover, when stress activates the HPA axis, it suppresses the female reproductive system. Corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and CRH-induced proopiomelanocortin peptides, like Beta endorphin (β-endorphin), inhibit the production of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) and other hormones essential for regulating the menstrual cycle and reproductive health. Additionally, cortisol suppresses the function of the gonadal axis at the hypothalamic, pituitary, and uterine levels. This suppression may lower the levels of hormones that typically prevent fibroid growth, potentially creating conditions that encourage the development or worsening of these benign tumors [42].

Immune system

Chronic psychological stress has been shown to activate the immune system. Single-cell analysis of uterine fibroid tissues has revealed diverse immune cell populations, including T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and B cells. In fibroids, there is an upregulation of genes involved in leukocyte migration, cytokine production, and the inflammatory response compared to the myometrium. Key inflammatory cytokines such as Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), Interleukin 6 (IL-6), Interleukin (IL-10), Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), Activin-A, and Transforming Growth Factor-Beta (TGF-β) are also expressed in fibroids. In addition, flow cytometry of peripheral white blood cells from women of reproductive age shows a distinct immune profile in those with fibroids compared to those without. Moreover, women with fibroids exhibit significantly higher counts of CD4+ naïve and central memory T cells, regulatory T cells, and follicular helper T cells, while counts of terminally differentiated effector memory cells and helper T2 cells (Th2) are lower. Central memory CD8+ cells and cytotoxic T cells are also elevated in women with fibroids, whereas NK and Gamma Delta T (γδT) cell counts are decreased [40].

Furthermore, inflammatory cells are present not only within fibroids but also in the surrounding and normal myometrium. The number of macrophages is significantly higher within fibroids and the adjacent myometrium compared to the more distant normal myometrium. This increased inflammation around and inside fibroids may contribute to fibrosis. For instance, TNF-α, an inflammatory cytokine highly expressed in fibroids, significantly elevates the mRNA levels of Activin-A in fibroid and myometrial cells. Since Activin-A promotes fibrosis, its upregulation through TNF-α could play a key role in UF development. Moreover, fibroid progenitor cells show higher levels of Th2 cytokines and lower levels of Th1/Th17 cytokines compared to myometrial progenitor cells [40]. All these cytokines may influence the Jun N-terminal kinases (JNKs) and p38-mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) pathways. This activation of immune and cellular pathways may play a role in the development of uterine fibroids, suggesting a link between stress and the onset of this condition [69].

Imbalanced microbiota

Stress can induce changes in the microbiota, which can cause hormonal imbalances and increase inflammation. These disruptions then contribute to the formation and growth of uterine fibroids. Distress signals are sent to the gut through the autonomic and circulatory systems [51]. A recently discovered pathway involving bone marrow shows that immune cells can transmit psychological stress to the gut. Stress, combined with the inflammation can lead to the overgrowth of harmful bacteria, causing dysbiosis [93].

Both chronic and acute stressors can disrupt gut bacteria across various regions, including the gut’s lumen and mucosal lining [7]. In vitro studies reveal that catecholamines can increase certain bacterial populations by 10,000-fold and enhance their virulence within just 14 h, allowing pathogenic bacteria to outcompete beneficial ones. [27].

Moreover, gut microbiota influences estrogen metabolism through the activity of the β-glucuronidase enzyme [93]. Research has shown that many gut microbiomes exhibit this enzyme’s activity, which can elevate estrogen levels in the body. β-Glucuronidase interferes with the binding of estrogen to glucuronic acid, thereby reducing the inactivation of estrogen and increasing its levels [38]. Since fibroids grow in response to estrogen, higher estrogen levels can further stimulate fibroid growth.

In addition, the gut microbiota affects immune responses, which can also impact fibroid pathogenesis. Interactions between gut microbiota and the host’s innate and adaptive immune systems are vital for maintaining intestinal balance and preventing inflammation. Gut microbes break down proteins and complex carbohydrates to produce metabolites that influence immune cell activity and the gut lining. These metabolites have anti-inflammatory and immuno-modulatory effects, aid in vitamin production, regulate immune responses, and help maintain the gut wall’s integrity. When these functions are disrupted by dysbiosis, it can lead to inflammation and potentially contribute to fibroid pathogenesis [38].

Beyond the gut microbiota, research has also shown that the microbiota in the vagina and cervix of fibroid patients differs in composition and ecological network from that of controls [49]. Imbalances in these microbiotas can lead to immune-inflammatory responses and alterations in gut metabolites, which may contribute to the development of fibroids [33].

Health behaviors

Research by Vafaei et al. found that a higher Body Mass Index (BMI) is associated with a greater risk of fibroids, particularly in premenopausal women. [82]. Stress can promote adverse health behaviors that then lead to obesity. While short-term stress typically reduces appetite, chronic stress may enhance appetite. Elevated cortisol levels from chronic stress boost appetite and often drive individuals to seek out high-sugar, high-fat foods as a form of comfort [79]. This stress-induced eating is influenced by the mesolimbic dopaminergic circuit, which affects motivation and the brain’s reward center, making stress eating a way to temporarily alleviate stress. Consuming low nutrient foods can also lead to an unbalanced diet, potentially resulting in vitamin D deficiency, which has been identified as a risk factor for uterine fibroids [4]. Additionally, a sedentary lifestyle can contribute to an increased risk of uterine fibroids [82].

Early life stress

Early life stress (ELS), defined as stress during the critical developmental periods in infancy and childhood [36], can include abuse (sexual, emotional), neglect (emotional, physical) and parental loss (death, separation [37]). A high incidence of ELS has been reported by patients with uterine pathologies [58], including uterine leiomyoma [14, 86, 89, 94]. In a study of 103 women undergoing hysterectomy for uterine leiomyoma, a history of substance abuse in the childhood home and early sexual abuse were both associated with greater symptom severity [94]. A separate prospective study of ~ 68,000 women enrolled in the Nurses’ Health Study II reported a dose–response association between the severity and chronicity of child/teen sexual and physical abuse and an increasing risk of clinically detected uterine leiomyoma [14].

Incidentally, many of the established risk factors for developing leiomyoma are also associated with high Adverse Childhood Events (ACE) exposure, including African American descent [81], high body mass index (BMI) [67], and earlier age at menarche [21]. Experiencing physical and sexual abuse early in life doubles the likelihood of developing severe obesity [67], with formerly-abused adults weighing an average of 9 pounds more than their un-abused peers [6, 52, 60, 88]. In addition to obesity in adulthood, an association between ELS and childhood obesity has been established [31, 72–75]. Children who experienced neglect have a faster rate of BMI growth over time compared to children who did not experience maltreatment [72]. Children who are overweight or obese often experience an earlier onset of menarche [3], which also increases the likelihood of developing uterine leiomyoma [85].

Collectively, the epidemiological evidence suggests an association between early life stress and increased risk of uterine leiomyoma, though describing this relationship as a “clear causal link” would overstate the current state of evidence. The proposed mechanistic pathways involve dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axes, which are biologically plausible given the established role of chronic stress in hormonal modulation. However, direct mechanistic studies linking early life stress to leiomyoma initiation or growth remain scarce, and the association is likely confounded by shared downstream mediators such as obesity and earlier menarche. These associations therefore warrant reframing as hypothesis-generating epidemiological observations with plausible endocrine pathways, rather than confirmed causal relationships. Prospective mechanistic studies—ideally incorporating longitudinal biomarker data and biological specimens—are needed to establish the nature and magnitude of any direct relationship.

c. Role of allostatic load in uterine fibroid racial disparities

Racial disparities in uterine fibroid burden among Black women are almost certainly multidetermined, reflecting the convergence of biological, structural, and interpersonal factors. Before reviewing the specific contributors, it is important to establish a conceptual distinction between three complementary but distinct pathways. First, allostatic load—the biological embedding of chronic stress—operates through neuroendocrine, immune, and metabolic mechanisms that may directly influence fibroid pathogenesis (e.g., HPA axis dysregulation, altered cytokine profiles, and epigenetic changes). Second, structural determinants of health—encompassing socioeconomic deprivation, insurance status, residential segregation, and barriers to healthcare access—operate primarily through their effects on care-seeking behavior, diagnostic delay, and treatment utilization, rather than through direct biological pathways. Third, interpersonal factors—including clinician bias, institutional mistrust rooted in historical injustice, and differential patient-provider communication—shape healthcare engagement and treatment decisions. These three domains are deeply interconnected: structural inequities generate chronic psychosocial stressors that accumulate as allostatic load, which in turn may amplify biological vulnerability.

Delayed treatment due to economic obstacles (structural determinant)

Economic deprivation is a structural determinant of health that shapes healthcare access and utilization independently of, though often in concert with, the biological effects of allostatic load. Many Black women disproportionately face lower socioeconomic status, which constrains access to timely and appropriate care. While lower SES is itself a potent source of chronic stress that contributes to allostatic load, its primary effect on fibroid outcomes in this context is through delayed diagnosis and treatment. The absence of insurance or financial resources frequently discourages Black patients from seeking medical care or restricts access within certain healthcare systems. Consequently, economic barriers are associated with delayed presentation at more advanced disease stages [56]. One multivariate analysis found that women with low SES are more likely to be diagnosed at an advanced stage [25]. This delay in treatment often leads to more Black women undergoing hysterectomies, as they may only seek intervention when symptoms are far advanced.

Institutional mistrust and clinician bias (interpersonal and historical factors)

It is important to recognize that the factors discussed here—institutional mistrust and clinician bias—are interpersonal and historical determinants of health. While they generate chronic psychosocial stress that may contribute to allostatic load over time, their primary mechanism of effect on fibroid outcomes operates through the healthcare engagement pathway rather than directly through biological stress pathways. Black women’s reluctance to seek timely care for fibroids frequently extends beyond economic barriers and reflects a well-founded mistrust of medical institutions rooted in documented historical injustice. Historically, unethical experimentation involved performing surgery on enslaved Black women without anaesthesia to advance gynaecological knowledge [77]. In subsequent decades, systemic failures to obtain informed consent meant that Black women routinely underwent procedures without proper knowledge or approval [63]. Structural neglect and discrimination contributed to lower-quality care and fewer resources for Black patients, leaving a legacy of institutional mistrust that continues to shape present-day healthcare-seeking behavior [63].

At the interpersonal level, clinician bias represents a further contributor to disparate outcomes that is distinct from—though may interact with—allostatic load. Evidence indicates that clinicians are more likely to underestimate and inadequately manage pain and symptoms in Black women compared to non-Black women. Implicit biases may manifest as underrecognition of symptom severity, reduced likelihood of referral for specialist evaluation, or differential treatment recommendations [83]. Examples include systematic underestimation of reported pain levels, or reduced probability of referral for uterine-sparing procedures [11]. These compounded burdens can ultimately contribute to why many Black women may delay seeking treatment for symptomatic fibroids, potentially leading to worsened disease pathology [43].

Hypertension

Many studies have shown a connection between stress and hypertension [65], and observational data suggest an association between hypertension and fibroids, though the relationship is likely confounded by shared risk factors including age, obesity, and differential healthcare utilization. The suggestion that untreated hypertension may be more strongly associated with fibroid risk is intriguing but should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than indicative of causality. In a study by Hicken et al., researchers found that the chronic stress associated with racism-related vigilance—given its anticipatory and persistent nature—was associated with racial disparities in hypertension prevalence. Black individuals reported higher levels of vigilance compared to White and Hispanic individuals, and this heightened vigilance was positively associated with hypertension prevalence among Black, but not White adults. As vigilance increased, so did the disparity in hypertension between Black and White individuals, suggesting that the chronic anticipatory stress of discrimination may represent a pathway through which allostatic load contributes to hypertension risk [39]. This positions hypertension as a potential biological mediator through which allostatic load may influence fibroid pathology, rather than as a direct consequence of stress alone. [39].

A prospective cohort study by Mitro et al. in the U.S. involving 2,570 middle-aged women found an association between hypertension and uterine fibroids. The study indicated that women receiving antihypertensive treatment was associated with a decreased risk of developing fibroids, whereas those not on treatment had an increased risk [57]. Similarly, a prospective cohort study found that individuals with hypertension are at a higher risk for developing fibroids compared to those with normal blood pressure. The study suggests that hypertension may affect fibroid development by altering blood flow and hormone levels [13].

Vitamin D deficiency

Chronic stress from environmental factors like low socioeconomic status or discrimination can result in an imbalanced diet and vitamin D deficiency, which in turn is linked to a higher risk of developing fibroids. Research from a blinded randomized clinical trial shows that vitamin D plays a crucial role in regulating cell growth and differentiation, so a deficiency could result in abnormal fibroid cell growth. Furthermore, Vitamin D also impacts hormonal balance, including estrogen levels, so disruptions in estrogen due to vitamin D deficiency can influence fibroid growth [20].

Black women in general have lower levels of 25-hydroxy vitamin D, or 25(OH)D for short, compared to Caucasians. This disparity is partly due to darker skin pigmentation, which reduces the skin’s ability to produce vitamin D from sunlight. From puberty onward, the average vitamin D intake for Black women tends to fall below recommended levels, even when taking supplements. Additionally, Black women often have lower levels of vitamin D-binding protein, possibly due to a prevalent genetic variant (S. S. [35]). In essence, chronic stress can contribute to vitamin D deficiency, and this issue is particularly pronounced among Black women, who are already at a higher risk of deficiency due to factors like skin pigmentation and lower vitamin D intake [1]. This compounded deficiency increases their risk of developing fibroids.

High obesity rates

Chronic stress due to systemic racism, which disproportionately affects Black women, is associated with higher obesity rates through two distinct mechanisms. First, as a structural determinant, housing discrimination and neighbourhood-level food environments restrict access to nutritious food and safe spaces for physical activity. Second, the chronic psychosocial stress generated by racism may drive allostatic load-mediated biological changes, including HPA axis dysregulation and cortisol-driven appetite changes, that independently promote adiposity. Elevated obesity rates may, in turn, be associated with a higher likelihood of fibroid pathology within this population. For example, systemic racism expressed in housing discrimination is a significant factor contributing to the high obesity rates among Black women. It not only increases stress but also restricts access to healthy foods and grocery stores. This heightened stress can lead to poor eating habits, which may potentially contribute to the development of uterine fibroids [19].

In the United States, obesity rates (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) are almost double in Black women compared to White women [91]. Research has shown that the combination of stress and poor diet can have serious health consequences. For instance, a study by Kejuan Sun found that individuals with uterine fibroids had significantly higher levels of visceral fat compared to those without fibroids. This association persisted even after accounting for factors such as age, BMI, and hormonal influences. The results suggest that elevated visceral fat may increase the risk of developing uterine fibroids, possibly through mechanisms involving adipokines and chronic inflammation, both of which are influenced by the increased stress and poor diet linked to systemic racism [76].

Other research indicates that obesity itself plays a direct role in the development of uterine fibroids by altering the environment of myometrial stem cells. Obesity induces oxidative stress and DNA damage in these stem cells, which contributes to their transformation into fibroid cells [2]. Additionally, obesity promotes increased cell proliferation and changes in the extracellular matrix, both of which encourage fibroid growth [34].

Differences in genetic and epigenetic expression

Emerging evidence suggests that chronic psychosocial stress may influence microRNA (miRNA) expression in the myometrium, potentially modulating fibroid-related gene regulatory pathways, though the causal direction of this relationship remains to be established [22]. MicroRNAs are small non-coding RNAs that post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression and protein production, and their dysregulation has been implicated in a range of proliferative and fibrotic conditions. In a study by Dye et al., levels of miR-27a-5p were found to differ between myometrial and fibroid tissue and were significantly associated with psychosocial stress scores, raising the possibility that cumulative stress exposure may dysregulate this miRNA in ways that could contribute to myometrial transformation—though this remains an association requiring mechanistic validation [22].

Independently of stress, racially patterned molecular differences in myometrial tissue may represent an additional, distinct contributor to fibroid disparities. Paul et al. reported that myometrial tissues from Black women exhibited distinct transcriptomic and DNA methylation profiles compared to those from White women [62]. Notably, hypomethylation and elevated expression of the VWF gene were observed in myometrial samples from Black women and in fibroids across both groups, suggesting that molecular susceptibility to fibroid development may originate in the myometrium prior to fibroid formation—a finding with important implications for understanding the biological basis of racial disparities [62].

Further supporting the existence of race-associated molecular heterogeneity in fibroids identified significant differences in the regulation of miR-21, miR-23b, and miR-197 between Black and White women with fibroids, matched for tumor size and age. Notably, while other racial groups showed marked Retinoid X receptor alpha (RXRα) overexpression in fibroid tissue, Black women exhibited minimal changes in RXRα expression, suggesting a distinct molecular mechanism may underlie fibroid pathogenesis in this population. It should be noted, however, that these findings are based on relatively small, cross-sectional comparisons, and replication in larger, prospective cohorts is needed before definitive conclusions can be drawn. Collectively, these observations indicate that stress-associated epigenetic changes and race-associated baseline molecular differences in the myometrium may represent complementary but mechanistically distinct pathways contributing to fibroid development and racial disparities—a distinction that warrants explicit investigation in future studies.

Preventative and therapeutic implications

a. Public health interventions

Education

Stress, whether psychological or physiological, is strongly associated with fibroid development, but certain preventative measures can help reduce this risk. Education plays a crucial role in influencing health outcomes, including the risk of developing uterine fibroids. Research indicates that women with a high school education or less are more likely to undergo a hysterectomy compared to those with higher levels of education [24]. Higher education often provides better access to health information and encourages healthier behaviors. In contrast, lower education levels can negatively impact general health, comprehension of medical issues, and the ability to navigate the healthcare system, potentially leading to poorer treatment outcomes [92].

Furthermore, education can empower women with essential knowledge about their condition and treatment options. Community-based educational initiatives can help women understand symptoms, risk factors, and the importance of regular screenings. Workshops, seminars, and informational materials offer practical insights into effective self-care strategies and lifestyle modifications that may alleviate symptoms. Integrating these educational efforts into healthcare access programs ensures that women are well-informed and able to make timely, proactive decisions regarding their health. This approach not only enhances their ability to seek appropriate medical care but also improves overall health outcomes and quality of life by fostering a more comprehensive and proactive management of their fibroids [45]. While these systematic changes offer significant benefits, they may not be feasible for everyone, especially those facing financial difficulties with tuition or living far from educational institutions.

Improved access to healthcare

Improved access to healthcare can be achieved by increasing the availability of gynecological services through community health programs, mobile clinics, and partnerships with local healthcare providers. Ensuring that women have timely access to screenings, diagnostics, and treatment options helps in early detection and can significantly improve health outcomes. Additionally, addressing barriers such as cost, transportation, and lack of insurance is essential to ensure that all women receive the care they need [87]. However, these systematic changes may not be feasible for everyone, particularly for those without healthcare insurance.

Support services

Support services for women with fibroids are crucial for addressing the emotional and psychological impacts of the condition. These services often include counseling to help manage stress and anxiety and peer support groups where women can connect, share experiences, and exchange coping strategies together. Integrating holistic approaches, such as mindfulness practices, yoga, or dietary counseling, can further support emotional well-being and symptom management. Additional resources like financial assistance programs, workplace accommodations, and health apps further enhance access to comprehensive care and support. Access to such comprehensive support not only improves overall well-being but also enables women to navigate the challenges of living with fibroids more effectively, fostering a greater sense of control and resilience in managing their health [59].

b. Individual health interventions

Access to nutrient rich foods

Research indicates that individuals with higher socioeconomic status (SES) often consume more nutrient-dense foods, such as dietary fiber, vitamin C, folate, beta-carotene, calcium, and iron. In contrast, those with lower SES may have diets high in fat, low-cost, and deficient in fruits and vegetables. These low nutrient dietary patterns and nutrient deficiencies are associated with an increased risk of developing uterine fibroids [78]. Nutrient-rich foods like vegetables, fruits, and omega-3 fatty acids have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which may help reduce the risk of fibroid development [82]. Conversely, high-fat dairy products and excessive caffeine intake can disrupt estrogen levels and promote inflammation, potentially increasing the risk of fibroids [48].

Limit alcohol consumption

Many people turn to alcohol as a way to cope with stress. However, research has shown a connection between excessive alcohol consumption and an increased risk of developing uterine fibroids [16]. Alcohol consumption can impair estrogen metabolism, resulting in increased levels of endogenous estrogen. This elevation in estrogen may contribute to a delayed onset of menopause. Furthermore, alcohol may affect luteinizing hormone (LH) levels, which in turn influences the release of estradiol from the ovaries. Elevated estrogen, combined with growth factors such as insulin-like growth factor (IGF) and epidermal growth factor (EGF), is believed to promote the growth of fibroids [15]. To reduce the risk of fibroids, it is advisable for women to moderate their alcohol intake.

Avoid sedentary lifestyle

Engaging in at least four hours of intense physical activity per week can lower the risk of developing fibroids [8]. Obesity affects fibroid formation by elevating hormone levels, reducing serum hormone-binding globulin, altering estrogen metabolism in premenopausal women, and disrupting signaling processes in myometrial cells, including insulin receptors and insulin-like growth factors [82]. Additionally, Ali et al. demonstrated that adipocytes contribute to increased fibroid cell growth, inflammation, and fibrosis. Their study showed that fibroids in obese patients had higher levels of TNF-α, Monocyte Chemoattractant Protein-1 (MCP-1), phospho-NF-κB p65, Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor A (VEGF-A), and TGFβ3 compared to lean individuals. Moreover, a significant cross-sectional study found a positive link between African ancestry, elevated BMI, and a higher risk of fibroids [5]. To reduce the risk of fibroids, maintaining a healthy weight through regular physical activity and a balanced diet is recommended.

Resilience

Resilience varies widely among individuals, influencing how they handle stress. Some people have a high tolerance for stress and can cope effectively, while others may struggle to manage stress and may become overwhelmed. Resilience is the capacity to endure and adapt to environmental challenges without succumbing to them. For example, after experiencing a traumatic event, a person might face immediate mental and physical effects. However, over time, a resilient individual often recovers and returns to a level of functioning similar to their pre-trauma state [10]. Factors such as age, life experience, positive emotions, and self-belief can enhance one’s ability to handle stress [50].

Building resilience requires a blend of mindset shifts, habits, and practical strategies that enable individuals to adapt and thrive in the face of adversity. Cultivating a positive outlook, through practices like optimism and gratitude, helps to shift focus from challenges to potential solutions. Strengthening emotional regulation, using techniques such as deep breathing, meditation, and journaling, aids in managing stress and processing emotions in a healthy manner. Developing a solution-focused mindset enhances problem-solving skills, making it easier to tackle difficulties effectively. Embracing flexibility and adaptability, by being open to change and willing to learn new skills, further bolsters resilience. Additionally, fostering a sense of purpose provides motivation and direction, helping individuals remain grounded and focused during tough times. These interconnected strategies create a strong foundation for resilience, empowering individuals to navigate life’s challenges with greater confidence and strength [26].

Concluding remarks

This review has examined the existing evidence on the relationship between chronic stress, allostatic load, and the pathogenesis and racial disparities of uterine fibroids. The evidence reviewed supports allostatic load as a biologically plausible and conceptually useful integrative framework for understanding fibroid disparities, rather than as a proven causal pathway. The development of uterine fibroids is clearly shaped by both genetic predisposition and environmental exposures, and chronic stress represents one mechanism through which environmental adversity may be biologically embedded. Several pathways by which allostatic load may plausibly contribute to fibroid risk have been identified, including dysregulation of the HPA axis and reproductive hormones, modulation of immune and inflammatory responses, alteration of gut and reproductive microbiota, and epigenetic changes in myometrial tissue including microRNA dysregulation. However, the evidence base currently consists predominantly of observational epidemiological studies with design limitations, and direct mechanistic evidence linking allostatic load to leiomyoma initiation or growth in humans remains limited. It is equally important to recognize that observed racial disparities in fibroid burden among Black women are not solely attributable to allostatic load. Structural determinants—including socioeconomic deprivation, insurance gaps, and residential segregation—and interpersonal factors—including institutional mistrust and clinician bias—are distinct contributors that interact with biological vulnerability and deserve explicit attention in both research and clinical contexts. Black women in the United States face disproportionately high levels of chronic psychosocial stress driven by structural racism, which may cumulatively increase allostatic load and thereby contribute to biological differences in fibroid risk. However, this relationship should be framed as an association with plausible mechanistic underpinning, not a demonstrated causal chain. Prospective studies incorporating longitudinal biomarker data, mechanistic investigation in human tissue, and rigorous control for confounding are required to establish whether and to what degree allostatic load causally contributes to fibroid development, and to quantify its contribution to Black-White disparities in fibroid incidence and severity.
