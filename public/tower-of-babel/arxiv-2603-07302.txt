Training for Trustworthy Saliency Maps: Adversarial Training Meets Feature-Map Smoothing
Dipkamal Bhusal, Md Tanvirul Alam, Nidhi Rastogi

Abstract
Gradient-based saliency methods such as Vanilla Gradient (VG) and Integrated Gradients (IG) are widely used to explain image classifiers, yet the resulting maps are often noisy and unstable, limiting their usefulness in high-stakes settings. Most prior work improves explanations by modifying the attribution algorithm, leaving open how the training procedure shapes explanation quality. We take a training-centered view and first provide a curvature-based analysis linking attribution stability to how smoothly the input-gradient field varies locally. Guided by this connection, we study adversarial training and identify a consistent trade-off: it yields sparser and more input-stable saliency maps, but can degrade output-side stability, causing explanations to change even when predictions remain unchanged and logits vary only slightly. To mitigate this, we propose augmenting adversarial training with a lightweight feature-map smoothing block that applies a differentiable Gaussian filter in an intermediate layer. Across FMNIST, CIFAR-10, and ImageNette, our method preserves the sparsity benefits of adversarial training while improving both input-side stability and output-side stability. A human study with 65 participants further shows that smoothed adversarial saliency maps are perceived as more sufficient and trustworthy. Overall, our results demonstrate that explanation quality is critically shaped by training, and that simple smoothing with robust training provides a practical path toward saliency maps that are both sparse and stable. Code is available at https://github.com/dipkamal/robustness_plus_smoothing .

Introduction

Saliency maps are among the most widely used tools for interpreting image classifiers, providing pixel-level visualizations of which input regions most influence a model’s prediction. Gradient-based methods such as Vanilla Gradient (VG)
37
and Integrated Gradients (IG)
42
are especially popular because they are simple, model-agnostic, and computationally efficient. However, in practice these explanations often suffer from noise, instability, limited faithfulness, and vulnerability to manipulation
1
;
24
;
30
;
14
;
54
. These shortcomings raise a central question:
when can we trust gradient-based explanations?

For saliency maps to be trustworthy, they should satisfy several desiderata:
(1)
Sparsity
—explanations should be concise and focus on a small set of discriminative pixels
7
;
(2)
Stability
—explanations should remain consistent under small perturbations
4
; and
(3)
Faithfulness
—highlighted features should reflect what the model actually uses to make its decision
34
. Most prior work pursues these goals by proposing new attribution algorithms or post-hoc smoothing schemes
39
;
6
;
36
;
40
;
5
. In contrast, we adopt a training-centered view and ask:

How does the training procedure shape the quality of standard gradient-based explanations?

We start by analyzing the stability of gradient attributions under input perturbations. Under a single-layer model assumption, we show that attribution variation is controlled by the curvature of the underlying score function: for VG and IG, small changes in the input can produce large changes in the explanation when the gradient field varies rapidly, i.e., when the local Jacobian is highly non-Lipschitz. This perspective provides a principled link between
model sensitivity
and
explanation stability
, and motivates why training procedures that reduce local sensitivity should improve stability.

A natural candidate is
adversarial training

15
;
29
, which explicitly enforces prediction consistency in a neighborhood around each training example. Empirically, we confirm prior observations that adversarially trained models tend to produce
sparser
saliency maps
12
;
7
and improve
input-side stability
under Gaussian noise. However, we uncover previously under-quantified trade-off: while adversarial training makes attributions sharper, it can
degrade output-side stability

2
.

To address this failure mode, we propose a simple training-time regularizer:
feature-map smoothing
. We insert a lightweight, differentiable Gaussian filter in intermediate layers during adversarial training to suppress high-frequency activation fluctuations that propagate into the input-gradient field. Intuitively, smoothing reduces abrupt changes in internal representations and dampens oscillations of the Jacobian, improving stability of gradient-based attributions. Across datasets, we show that this intervention preserves the sparsity benefits of adversarial training while improving both input-side stability (SSIM) and output-side stability (ROS), without sacrificing faithfulness as measured by ROAD
34
. A human study further confirms that the resulting explanations are perceived as more sufficient and trustworthy.

We evaluate our approach on FMNIST
46
, CIFAR-10
25
, and ImageNette
20
, comparing naturally trained models, adversarially trained models, and adversarially trained models with Gaussian feature-map smoothing. Our results show a consistent pattern:

(i)
adversarial training increases sparsity but can harm output-side stability, and

(ii)
feature-map smoothing recovers stability while largely preserving sparsity and faithfulness of adversarial training.

Figure
1
illustrates the qualitative effect. Natural models produce noisy, diffuse maps; adversarial training yields sharper but sometimes brittle explanations; and adversarial training with feature-map smoothing produces saliency maps that are sparse and structurally coherent. We provide more examples in
Appendix

I
.

Our main contributions are:

•

We provide a curvature-based analysis connecting the stability of Vanilla Gradient (VG)

•

We empirically identify and quantify a trade-off induced by adversarial training: improved sparsity

Background

Feature attribution methods.
Feature attribution methods assign an importance score to each input feature for a given, visualized as saliency maps in images. Vanilla Gradient (VG)
37
computes the gradient of the predicted-class score with respect to the input. While simple and efficient, VG produces noisy and visually cluttered maps. Integrated Gradients (IG)
42
improves on VG by averaging gradients along a path from a baseline to the input, and satisfies axioms such as sensitivity and completeness
41
. SmoothGrad
39
and NoiseGrad
6
reduce noise by aggregation. Despite their popularity, gradient-based explanations can be sensitive to input perturbations, depend on hyperparameters, and can be manipulated without changing the predicted label
24
;
14
;
17
;
43
. This has motivated methods that add explanation-oriented regularization
21
;
48
;
27
;
13
;
49
.

Training-centered approaches.
A growing body of work argues that explanation quality is shaped not only by the explainer, but also by the
trained model
itself. Training-time interventions include gradient regularization
35
, explanation constraints
11
, and architectural modifications that promote smoother representations
23
;
10
. Adversarial training has received particular attention because robust models often exhibit visually sharper and sparser gradients. Etmann et al.
12
connect robustness to input-gradient alignment and interpretability on MNIST-scale settings. Zhang et al.
53
show that robust models tend to rely more on shape-biased representations, which can yield more structured saliency maps.
Chalasani et al.
7
quantify sparsity improvements from
\ell_{\infty}
adversarial training using the Gini index.

Methodology

3.1
Setup

Let
z(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
denote the logits of a differentiable classifier, and

\hat{y}(\mathbf{x})=\arg\max_{k}z_{k}(\mathbf{x})
its predicted label.
For an input
\mathbf{x}
, we define the scalar score as the predicted-class logit

f(\mathbf{x}):=z_{\hat{y}(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}.

(1)

We consider two standard input-gradient attributions:
Vanilla Gradient (VG)

37
and
Integrated Gradients (IG)

42
. For
VG
, the feature attribution vector
\phi(\mathbf{x})
for an input
\mathbf{x}
is given by:

\phi_{\mathrm{VG}}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}),

(2)

which measures the sensitivity of the score to each input feature.

For
IG
with baseline
\mathbf{u}
:

\phi_{\mathrm{IG}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})=(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\odot\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f\!\left(\mathbf{u}+\alpha(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\right)\,d\alpha,

(3)

where
\odot
is the Hadamard product. In practice, we approximate the integral with
S
steps and use a zero baseline.

To formalize stability, we consider
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{x}}
a perturbed version of input image
\mathbf{x}
where
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{x}}
indicates a neighborhood of inputs
\mathbf{x}
where the model prediction is locally consistent. We use
\|\cdot\|_{2}
unless stated otherwise.

3.2
A curvature view of attribution stability

For simplicity, let us examine a single-layer model with the following form:

{f}(\mathbf{x})=H(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle),

where
H
is a differentiable scalar-valued activation function,
\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle
is the dot product between the weight vector
\mathbf{w}
and input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Then, we obtain the feature attribution for Vanilla Gradients (VG), as:

\phi_{\text{VG}}(\mathbf{x})=H^{\prime}(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle)\,\mathbf{w}.

(4)

For
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{x}}
:

\phi_{\text{VG}}(\mathbf{x^{\prime}})=H^{\prime}(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x^{\prime}}\rangle)\,\mathbf{w}.

(5)

Let
s=\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle
and
s^{\prime}=\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\rangle
. Then,
\phi_{\text{VG}}(\mathbf{x})=H^{\prime}(s)\,\mathbf{w}
and
\phi_{\text{VG}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=H^{\prime}(s^{\prime})\,\mathbf{w}
. Hence, the stability of explanations can be computed by measuring the norm of the difference between the perturbed-input explanation and the original explanation:

\displaystyle\big\|\phi_{\text{VG}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\phi_{\text{VG}}(\mathbf{x})\big\|

\displaystyle=\big\|\big(H^{\prime}(s^{\prime})-H^{\prime}(s)\big)\,\mathbf{w}\big\|

(6)

\displaystyle=\big|H^{\prime}(s^{\prime})-H^{\prime}(s)\big|\,\|\mathbf{w}\|.

(7)

By the mean-value theorem applied to the scalar function
H^{\prime}
on the interval between
s
and
s^{\prime}
, there exists

\xi
between
s
and
s^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(s^{\prime})-H^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=H^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\,(s^{\prime}-s).

(8)

Figure 2
:
Effect of training regimes on intermediate feature maps (channel= {7, 21, 127}, after first residual block) and saliency maps:
(a) naturally trained, (b) adversarially trained, (c) adversarially trained with feature-map smoothing, and (d) corresponding VG saliency maps.

Taking absolute values and using
s^{\prime}-s=\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\rangle
, we get

\displaystyle\big|H^{\prime}(s^{\prime})-H^{\prime}(s)\big|

\displaystyle=\big|H^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\big|\,\big|\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\rangle\big|

(9)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sup_{z}\big|H^{\prime\prime}(z)\big|\Big)\,\big|\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\rangle\big|.

(10)

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz
3
,

\displaystyle\big|\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\rangle\big|\;\leq\;\|\mathbf{w}\|\,\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|.

(11)

Combining (
7
)–(
11
) yields

\displaystyle\big\|\phi_{\text{VG}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\phi_{\text{VG}}(\mathbf{x})\big\|\;\leq\;\Big(\sup_{z}\big|H^{\prime\prime}(z)\big|\Big)\,\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2}\,\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|.

(12)

Thus, the
VG
attribution stability is controlled by the activation curvature
\sup_{z}|H^{\prime\prime}(z)|
and the weight norm
\|\mathbf{w}\|
.

For Integrated Gradients (IG), we use the closed-form expression for a single-layer model
f(\mathbf{x})=H(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle)

7
:

\phi_{\text{IG}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})=\big[H(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle)-H(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u}\rangle)\big]\;\frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\odot\mathbf{w}}{\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}\rangle},

(13)

Proof for Eqn.
13
is provided in
Appendix

C
. The stability of the explanation is defined similar to VG as:

\Delta_{\text{IG}}=\|\phi_{\text{IG}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\mathbf{u})-\phi_{\text{IG}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})\|

for a nearby
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{x}}
with the same predicted label. By mean-value theorem and Cauchy–Schwarz
3
yields

\begin{split}\Delta_{\text{IG}}\;\leq\;\Big(\underbrace{\sup_{z}\!|H^{\prime}(z)|}_{\text{activation slope}}\;\|\mathbf{w}\|\\
\;+\;\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\sup_{z}\!|H^{\prime\prime}(z)|}_{\text{activation curvature}}\;\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2}\,\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}\|\Big)\;\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|\ \end{split}

(14)

Full derivation is provided in Appendix
D
. Thus, like
VG
,
IG
stability is governed by the activation’s curvature and the weight scale;
IG
additionally depends (benignly) on the baseline distance
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}\|
.

Takeaway.

Eq.

12
and
Eq.

14
shows that
gradient explanations are stable when the model’s logit function has low effective curvature and the gradient field varies smoothly
.
This motivates focusing on
training procedures
that shape local sensitivity and curvature of the predictor rather than designing new explainers.

3.3
Adversarial training: invariance helps, but reveals a stability trade-off

A standard way to enforce local invariance is adversarial training
15
, which solves the min–max objective:

\min_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\max_{\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon}\mathcal{L}\!\left(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\delta},y;\theta\right)\right],

(15)

where, the inner maximization finds the perturbation
\delta
(bounded by
\epsilon
) that maximally increases the loss often approximated by projected gradient descent (PGD)
29
, and the outer minimization updates the model to resist such worst-case perturbations.

Because adversarial training explicitly enforces
prediction consistency
over an
\ell_{\infty}
ball, it often reduces input-level sensitivity and improves

input-side stability
(e.g., higher structural similarity under Gaussian input noise). Adversarial training also commonly produces
sparser
saliency maps, concentrating attribution on fewer pixels
7
. However, prediction invariance does not guarantee that the
gradient field
is smooth in a way aligned with output behavior. Empirically, we observe a consistent trade-off:

Adversarial training improves sparsity and input-side stability, but can worsen output-side stability (ROS),

meaning that explanations may fluctuate even when logits change little and the predicted class is unchanged. This failure mode motivates adding a training-time prior that directly suppresses
high-frequency representation changes
that amplify gradient oscillations.

3.4
Feature-map smoothing as a stability prior

We propose augmenting adversarial training with
feature-map smoothing

47
, a lightweight architectural regularization that encourages smoother intermediate representations and, in turn, a smoother input–gradient field.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times H\times W}
be a feature map with
K
channels. We apply a differentiable spatial low-pass filter
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}
(Gaussian with bandwidth
\sigma
) independently per channel:

\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{smooth}}=\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(\mathbf{A}).

(16)

We then use a
1\times 1
convolution to re-mix channels after spatial smoothing, and add a residual connection:

\mathrm{SmoothBlock}(\mathbf{A})\;=\;\mathbf{A}\;+\;\mathrm{Conv}_{1\times 1}\!\left(\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{smooth}}\right).

(17)

Figure
3
illustrates the block. In practice,
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}
is implemented as a fixed depthwise convolution (per-channel Gaussian kernel), while
\mathrm{Conv}_{1\times 1}
is learned and preserves the channel dimension.

Figure 3
:
Feature-map smoothing block. A spatial filter is followed by a
1\times 1
convolution and a residual connection.

Using a spatial smoothing suppresses high-frequency activation components that can induce sharp, localized changes in
f(\mathbf{x})
and its derivatives.
Intuitively, this reduces the effective curvature of the end-to-end mapping and stabilizes
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x})
,
while the residual path preserves representational capacity and model accuracy.

Training with smoothing.

Our final method performs adversarial training
Eq.

15
on a network where SmoothBlocks are inserted at selected intermediate layers. Unless stated otherwise, we insert the SmoothBlock after the first convolutional/residual block (validated in
Section

6.1
). This preserves the sparsity and invariance benefits of adversarial training, while empirically restoring stability without sacrificing sparsity.
Fig.

2
(c–d) illustrates smoothing effect in the intermediate feature maps: it reduces high-frequency noise, producing saliency maps that are sparse yet structurally coherent.

We evaluate saliency-map quality on three image classification benchmarks: FMNIST

(A)

5
Human Perception of Explanation Quality

Our quantitative results show that adversarial training increases saliency sparsity and improves input-side stability, but can degrade output-side stability (ROS), while adding Gaussian feature-map smoothing restores stability without sacrificing sparsity. We next ask whether these quantitative changes translate into improvements in
human-perceived
explanation quality.

5.1
Motivation

Saliency maps are ultimately used by humans to reason about model decisions. Functionally grounded metrics (e.g., sparsity
7
, SSIM
1
, ROS
2
, ROAD
34
) provide useful proxies, but they do not directly measure whether an explanation is perceived as understandable. We therefore ask:

Do the stability improvements from feature-map smoothing make gradient-based saliency maps more sufficient and trustworthy to humans?

5.2
Study design

We conducted a user study with 65 graduate students (Ph.D. and M.S.) with at least one year of experience in computer vision
1
1

1

The study was IRB-approved by our institution.
. Participants were shown saliency maps generated using Vanilla Gradients (VG) for 10 randomly selected test images drawn from FMNIST and CIFAR-10. For each image, we generated VG saliency maps from three models: naturally trained (
N
), adversarially trained (
A
), and adversarially trained with Gaussian smoothing (
G
). Thus, each participant evaluated
20\times 3=60
image–saliency pairs. Trials were presented in randomized order and model identity was not revealed (see
Fig.

10
in
Appendix

F
).

For each trial, participants rated the explanation on a 5-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree, 5=strongly agree) along two axes adapted from the Hoffman satisfaction scale
18
:

•

Sufficiency:
“The explanation provides sufficient information to understand the model’s decision.”

Takeaway.

These ablations suggest that
local smoothing
that reduces high-frequency fluctuations in intermediate feature maps is the key mechanism for improved stability rather than Gaussian filtering specifically although the impact differs based on dataset and the filter type. 7
Limitations and Future Directions

Our study focused on input-gradient explanations. It remains unclear whether the same stability trends and training-time fixes extend to other explanation families such as perturbation-based (e.g., LIME
33
, SHAP
28
) or concept-based methods (e.g., TCAV
22
). Second, our experiments are limited to mid-scale vision datasets and convolutional architectures. Evaluating larger-scale settings and modern models (e.g., Transformers and vision–language models) is an important next step, especially since adversarial training can behave differently at scale
52
. Finally, we primarily use simple local smoothing (Gaussian, with mean/median ablations). More expressive regularizers
50
or learned smoothing modules may yield better trade-offs between accuracy, robustness, and explanation stability.

8
Conclusion

We showed that explanation quality is strongly shaped by how a model is trained. While adversarial training typically yields sparser and more input-stable saliency maps, we find it can worsen output-side stability, with explanations changing noticeably under prediction-preserving perturbations. To address this, we augment adversarial training with a lightweight feature-map smoothing block. Across datasets and architectures, smoothing consistently improves stability while largely preserving the sparsity and robustness benefits of adversarial training, and it aligns with higher human ratings of explanation quality. These results support a training-centered path toward more trustworthy gradient-based saliency maps.

Appendix C
Closed-form expression for Integrated Gradients (IG)

The feature attribution score computed by Integrated Gradients (IG) for feature
i
of input image
\mathbf{x}\in R^{d}
with baseline
\mathbf{u}
, model
f
is given by:

IG_{i}^{f}(\mathbf{x,u})=(x_{i}-u_{i}).\int_{\alpha=0}^{1}{\partial_{i}{f}(\mathbf{u}+\alpha(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}))}\partial\alpha

(18)

For an input image
\mathbf{x}
, IG returns a vector
IG^{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\in R^{d}
with scores that quantify the contribution of
x_{i}
to the model prediction
{f}(\mathbf{x})
. For a single layer network
{f}(\mathbf{x})=H(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle)
where
H
is a differentiable scalar-valued function and
\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle
is the dot product between the weight vector
\mathbf{w}
and input
\mathbf{x}\in R^{d}
, IG attribution has a closed form expression
7
.

For given
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{u}
and
\alpha
, let us consider
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}+\alpha(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})
. If the single-layer network is represented as
{f}(\mathbf{x})=H(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle)
where
H
is a differentiable scalar-valued function,
\partial_{i}{f}(\mathbf{v})
can be computed as:

\displaystyle\partial_{i}f(\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{v})}{v_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial H(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}\rangle)}{\partial v_{i}}

\displaystyle=H^{\prime}(z)\frac{\partial\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}\rangle}{\partial v_{i}}

\displaystyle=w_{i}H^{\prime}(z)

(19)

Here,
H^{\prime}(z)
is the gradient of the activation
H(z)
where
z=\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}\rangle
. To compute
\frac{\partial{f}(\mathbf{v})}{\partial\alpha}
:

\frac{\partial{f}(\mathbf{v})}{\partial\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\frac{\partial{f}(\mathbf{v})}{\partial v_{i}}\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial\alpha})

(20)

We can substitute value of
\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial\alpha}=(x_{i}-u_{i})
and
\partial_{i}{f}(\mathbf{v})
from Eq.
C
to Eq.
20
.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{f}(\mathbf{v})}{\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}[w_{i}H^{\prime}(z)(x_{i}-u_{i})]

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\rangle H^{\prime}(z)

(21)

This gives:

df(\mathbf{v})=\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\rangle H^{\prime}(z)\partial\alpha

(22)

Since
\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\rangle
is scalar,

H^{\prime}(z)\partial\alpha=\frac{d{f}(\mathbf{v})}{\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\rangle}

(23)

Eq.
23
can be used to rewrite the integral in the definition of
IG_{i}^{f}(\mathbf{x})
in Eq.
18
,

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha=0}^{1}\partial_{i}f(\mathbf{v})\partial\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{\alpha=0}^{1}w_{i}H^{\prime}(z)\partial z~~~\textnormal{[From Eqn. \ref{suppeqn:partialfv}]}

\displaystyle=\int_{\alpha=0}^{1}w_{i}\frac{d{f}(\mathbf{v})}{\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{i}}{\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\rangle}\int_{\alpha=0}^{1}{d{f}(\mathbf{v})}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{i}}{\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\rangle}[{f}(\mathbf{x})-{f}(\mathbf{u})]

(24)

Hence, we obtain the closed form for Integrated Gradients from its definition in Eqn.
18
as

\displaystyle IG_{i}^{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=[{f}(\mathbf{x})-{f}(\mathbf{u})]\frac{({x_{i}}-{u_{i}}){w_{i}}}{\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\rangle}

\displaystyle IG^{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=[{f}(\mathbf{x})-{f}(\mathbf{u})]\frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\odot\mathbf{w}}{\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\rangle}

(25)

Here,
\odot
is the entry-wise product of two vectors.

Appendix D
Stability bound for Integrated Gradients

We analyze the single-layer model
{f}(\mathbf{x})=H(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle)
with a differentiable scalar activation
H
.
For a baseline
\mathbf{u}
, Integrated Gradients (IG) is

\begin{split}\phi_{\text{IG}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})=(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\odot\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}{f}\!\big(\mathbf{u}+\alpha(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\big)\,d\alpha\\
=(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\odot\left(\int_{0}^{1}H^{\prime}\!\big(\langle\mathbf{w},\,\mathbf{u}+\alpha(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\rangle\big)\,d\alpha\right)\mathbf{w}.\end{split}

(26)

Equivalently, using the closed form of
7
, when
\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}\rangle\neq 0
,

\phi_{\text{IG}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})=\big[H(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}\rangle)-H(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u}\rangle)\big]\;\frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\odot\mathbf{w}}{\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}\rangle}.)

Let
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}
and define the path-average

A(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})\;:=\;\int_{0}^{1}H^{\prime}\!\big(\langle\mathbf{w},\,\mathbf{u}+\alpha(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\rangle\big)\,d\alpha.

so that,
\phi_{\text{IG}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})\;=\;\big(\mathbf{d}\odot\mathbf{w}\big)\,A(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})
.

Consider a nearby point
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
(same predicted label) and set
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{u}
.
Then, similar to Vanilla Gradient, the stability for Integrated Gradient can be computed as the norm of the difference between IG explanation for noisy image and the original image.

\begin{split}\Delta_{\text{IG}}\;:=\;\big\|\phi_{\text{IG}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\mathbf{u})-\phi_{\text{IG}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})\big\|\\
\;=\;\big\|\;(\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\!\odot\!\mathbf{w})\,A(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\mathbf{u})\;-\;(\mathbf{d}\!\odot\!\mathbf{w})\,A(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})\;\big\|.\end{split}

(27)

Because we want to bound the difference of two products (consisting of composite terms), there is no simple rule that lets us directly split this as a product of differences. Unlike sums (
||a+b||\leq||a||+||b||
, the norm of a difference of products cannot be written as a clean product of differences. So, to separate the two kinds of changes (the change in
d
and the change in
A
), and apply the triangle inequality, we add–and–subtract
(\mathbf{d}\!\odot\!\mathbf{w})A(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\mathbf{u})
):

\begin{split}=||\underbrace{(\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\odot\mathbf{w})A(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{u})-(\mathbf{d}\odot\mathbf{w})A(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{u})}_{\text{difference in }\mathbf{d}}+\\
\underbrace{(\mathbf{d}\odot\mathbf{w})A(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{u})-(\mathbf{d}\odot\mathbf{w})A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})}_{\text{difference in }A}||.\end{split}

(28)

Hence, we get,

\begin{split}\Delta_{\text{IG}}\;\leq\;\underbrace{\big\|\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\!\odot\!\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{d}\!\odot\!\mathbf{w}\big\|}_{\text{(i)}}\;\cdot\;\underbrace{\big|A(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\mathbf{u})\big|}_{\text{(ii)}}\;+\;\\
\underbrace{\big\|\mathbf{d}\!\odot\!\mathbf{w}\big\|}_{\text{(iii)}}\;\cdot\;\underbrace{\big|A(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\mathbf{u})-A(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})\big|}_{\text{(iv)}}.\end{split}

(29)

We bound the four factors:

Linear factor in
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}

Since

\mathbf{d}^{\prime}-\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}
, so

\big\|\mathbf{d}^{\prime}\!\odot\!\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{d}\!\odot\!\mathbf{w}\big\|=\big\|(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})\!\odot\!\mathbf{w}\big\|\;\leq\;\|\mathbf{w}\|\,\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|.

Bounding the path-average
A(\cdot)

For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
|H^{\prime}(\cdot)|\leq\sup_{z}|H^{\prime}(z)|
. Hence

|A(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\mathbf{u})|\;\leq\;\sup_{z}|H^{\prime}(z)|.

Size of
\mathbf{d}\!\odot\!\mathbf{w}

\big\|\mathbf{d}\!\odot\!\mathbf{w}\big\|\leq\|\mathbf{w}\|\,\|\mathbf{d}\|
.

Differentiate inside the integral and apply the chain rule:

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}A(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})=\int_{0}^{1}\alpha\,H^{\prime\prime}\!\big(\langle\mathbf{w},\,\mathbf{u}+\alpha(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u})\rangle\big)\;\mathbf{w}\;d\alpha,

so

\begin{split}\big\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}A(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})\big\|\;\leq\;\left(\int_{0}^{1}\alpha\,d\alpha\right)\;\sup_{z}|H^{\prime\prime}(z)|\;\|\mathbf{w}\|\\
\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\sup_{z}|H^{\prime\prime}(z)|\;\|\mathbf{w}\|.\end{split}

(30)

By the mean-value theorem along the line segment
[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}]
,

\big|A(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\mathbf{u})-A(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{u})\big|\;\leq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\sup_{z}|H^{\prime\prime}(z)|\;\|\mathbf{w}\|\;\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|.

Putting the bounds together:

\begin{split}\Delta_{\text{IG}}\;\leq\;\underbrace{\|\mathbf{w}\|\,\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|}_{(i)}\cdot\underbrace{\sup_{z}|H^{\prime}(z)|}_{(ii)}\;+\;\\
\underbrace{\|\mathbf{w}\|\,\|\mathbf{d}\|}_{(iii)}\cdot\underbrace{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sup_{z}|H^{\prime\prime}(z)|\;\|\mathbf{w}\|\;\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|\right)}_{(iv)}\end{split}

(31)

Equivalently,

\Delta_{\text{IG}}\leq\;\Big(\sup_{z}|H^{\prime}(z)|\,\|\mathbf{w}\|\;+\;\tfrac{1}{2}\sup_{z}|H^{\prime\prime}(z)|\,\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2}\,\|\mathbf{d}\|\Big)\;\cdot\;\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|.

Collect terms into a single constant that depends only on local quantities:

\begin{split}\Delta_{\mathrm{IG}}\;\leq\;C_{\mathrm{IG}}\;\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|,\quad\\
\text{with}\quad C_{\mathrm{IG}}:=\sup_{z}|H^{\prime}(z)|\,\|\mathbf{w}\|\;+\;\\
\tfrac{1}{2}\,\sup_{z}|H^{\prime\prime}(z)|\;\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2}\|\,\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{u}\|.\end{split}

(32)

Thus, for the single-layer model, IG stability—like VG—is primarily controlled by activation curvature
\sup|H^{\prime\prime}|
and the weight scale
\|\mathbf{w}\|
.

Appendix G
Evaluating explanations

Evaluating saliency maps remains challenging, as qualitative visual assessments can be subjective. Recent research emphasizes the need for functionally grounded metrics.

Sparsity.
Chalasani et al.
7
proposed the Gini index to quantify sparsity of attribution vectors, encouraging explanations that focus on a small set of discriminative features. Given a vector of attribution
\phi(\mathbf{x})\in R^{d}
, the absolute of the vector is first sorted in non-decreasing order, and the Gini index is computed using Eqn.
33
.

G(\phi(\mathbf{\mathbf{x}}))=1-2\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\phi(\mathbf{x})_{(k)}}{||\phi(\mathbf{x})||_{1}}\frac{d-k+0.5}{d}

(33)

The Gini Index values lie in between
[0,1]
; A value of 1 indicates perfect sparsity, where only one element in the vector
\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x})>0
. The sparsity is zero if all the vectors are equal to some positive value.

Stability.

Stability
in explanations quantifies the variance of attribution vectors within a small neighborhood of a given input. For similar inputs, it is expected that the explanations remain similar. We measure two type of stability.

For
input-side stability
, we use structural similarity index (SSIM). For each image, we add Gaussian noise and generate its noisy version such that the model prediction is consistent. We then compute the saliency map of the two images and measure the structural similarity between the maps following Adebayo et al.
1
. Higher SSIM indicates structurally consistent attributions.

For
output-side stability
, we following Agarwal et al.
2
, and measure relative output stability (ROS) which, which relates changes in attribution to changes in logits:

\begin{split}ROS=max_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\frac{||\frac{\phi(\mathbf{x})-\phi(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}{\phi(\mathbf{x})}||}{max(||{z(\mathbf{x})-z(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}||_{p},\epsilon_{min})}\\
\forall\mathbf{x}^{\prime}~s.t.~\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{x}};\hat{y}_{\mathbf{x}}=\hat{y}_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\end{split}

(34)

where
z(\cdot)
are logits, and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{x}}
is an
\ell_{\infty}
ball with consistent model predictions. Lower ROS is better (more stable for similar predictions).

Faithfulness.
Faithfulness measures whether explanations reflect the model’s actual decision-making. Early approaches such as insertion/deletion
31
and ROAR
19
either suffer from distribution shift or are computationally expensive. ROAD
34
mitigates these issues by combining feature removal with debiasing, producing a stable, efficient metric.

ROAD measures the accuracy of a model on the provided test set at each step of an iterative process of removing
k
most important pixels. Removal of pixels is done with a noisy linear imputation to avoid out-of-distribution samples. We set
k=5
in our experiments, and adopt the MoRF (Most Relevant First) removal strategy where a faster drop in accuracy with increase in removal of
k
most important features indicate that key discriminative features are being removed. ROAD demonstrates consistent results with both MoRF and LeRF (Least Removal First) removal strategy.

We quantify ROAD plot using ROAD-AOPC score:

\mathrm{AOPC}=\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\Big(f(\mathbf{x}^{(0)})-f(\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\Big),

(35)

where
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
is obtained by removing the
k
most relevant pixels and
f(\cdot)
denotes the model output. Higher AOPC indicates that the explanation better identifies features essential for prediction, i.e., more faithful attributions.

Appendix H
Implementation details: Filters

•

Mean filter:
A mean filter replaces each feature with the average of nearby features within a defined kernel. For an input feature map (
I
) of size
H
x
W
and a
K
-sized kernel, the output feature map
O(u,v)
is calculated using Eqn.
36
:

O(u,v)=\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}I(u+i,v+j)

(36)

Here,
u
and
v
represent spatial coordinates in the output feature map, ranging from 0 to
H-K
and 0 to
W-K
respectively.
I(u+i,v+j)
denotes the feature value at spatial location
(u+i,v+j)
in the input feature map. This operation is applied independently to each channel of the input.

•

Median filter:
A median filter computes the median value within a small sliding window over the feature map, given by Eqn.
37
. Given an input feature map
I
and a median filter window size
K
, the output feature map
O(u,v)
is computed using Eqn.
37
:

O(u,v)=median(I(u-\frac{K}{2}:u+\frac{K}{2},v-\frac{K}{2}:v+\frac{K}{2})

(37)

Here,
I(u-\frac{K}{2}:u+\frac{K}{2},v-\frac{K}{2}:v+\frac{K}{2})
represents the subset of the input feature around
(u,v)
with a size of
K
x
K
. This operation is applied independently to each channel of the input feature map.

•

Gaussian filter:
A Gaussian filter applies a smoothing effect to feature maps by convolving them with a Gaussian kernel, effectively reducing Gaussian noise. The degree of smoothing can be adjusted by modifying the standard deviation (
\sigma
) of the Gaussian kernel. Given an input feature map
I
and a Gaussian filter kernel
K
, the output feature map
O(u,v)
is calculated with Eqn.
38
:

O(u,v)=(I*K)(u,v)

(38)

Here,
*
denotes 2D convolution. The Gaussian kernel
K
is generated using a Gaussian function with a specific standard deviation
\sigma
, defined in Eqn.
39
:

K(u,v)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}e^{(-\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}})}

(39)

This operation is independently applied to each channel of the input feature map.

Implementation:
We utilize the differentiable local filters available in Kornia
32
. We use a 3x3 Kernel for mean, median, and Gaussian filtering. The standard deviation of the kernel for Gaussian filtering was computed as
(0.3*((\mathbf{x}.shape[3]-1)*0.5-1)+0.8,0.3*((\mathbf{x}.shape[2]-1)*0.5-1)+0.8)
where
\mathbf{x}
is the input image.
