Durability analysis of alkali-activated steel slag-GGBS-coal gangue composite mixtures
Authors: Shibo Zhao, Wenlong Tang, Chuqiao Meng, Haipeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study developed a low-carbon pavement base material utilizing steel slag, GGBS, and coal gangue activated by sodium silicate. The mixture proportions were optimized using the uniform design method, and the mechanical properties and durability were systematically evaluated. The results indicate that the system achieves optimal performance when the mass percentages of coal gangue, steel slag, GGBS, and sodium silicate are 52.6%, 36.8%, 6.3%, and 4.3%, respectively. Microscopic analysis (XRD, SEM, and FTIR) reveals that the alkali-activation process dissolves active components to generate substantial C-A-S-H gels and a dense three-dimensional network. Specifically, FTIR spectra confirm the depolymerization and subsequent reorganization of the aluminosilicate framework, providing the fundamental mechanism for the material’s macroscopic performance. The optimal mixture achieved a 7-day unconfined compressive strength of 6.52 MPa, satisfying the requirements for high-grade highway bases. After five freeze-thaw cycles, the specimens maintained a strength retention rate exceeding 85% with a mass loss of less than 2%. Furthermore, due to sustained hydration, the strength increased by up to 37.5% after ten wet-dry cycles, demonstrating exceptional durability. This work provides a feasible and sustainable approach for the large-scale utilization of industrial solid wastes in green road engineering.

Introduction

In recent years, significant progress has been made in research on the durability of alkali-activated materials1–3. Studies have shown that alkali-activated slag materials typically exhibit excellent resistance to sulfate attack; however, the issue of drying shrinkage remains prominent4. Regarding the freeze-thaw resistance of alkali-activated materials, scholars have indicated that it is closely related to pore structure and degree of water saturation. Furthermore, research on heavy metal solidification/stabilization has demonstrated that alkali-activated systems can effectively immobilize heavy metal ions from raw materials such as steel slag, thereby reducing the risk of environmental leaching5. Nevertheless, systematic investigations into the long-term durability—particularly concerning freeze-thaw resistance, water stability, and volume stability—of the multi-solid-waste synergistic activation system comprising coal gangue, steel slag, and GGBS remain relatively scarce, especially for pavement engineering applications6.

Against this background, this study aims to synergistically utilize these three wastes to develop an alkali-activated pavement composite material. The research commenced with a systematic characterization of the raw material properties. Based on relevant specifications, the dosage range for each component was determined, and the mixture proportion was optimized using a uniform design method7,8. Mechanical performance was evaluated through unconfined compressive strength tests9. With a focus on durability issues that have received insufficient attention in current research, a comprehensive assessment was conducted on frost resistance, water stability, and water immersion expansion characteristics. The results demonstrate that the optimized mixture meets the technical requirements for high-grade highway base courses in terms of both strength and durability. This validates the feasibility of applying the sodium silicate-activated coal gangue–steel slag–GGBS system in pavement engineering10, providing technical support for the resource utilization of industrial solid wastes11 and the development of low-carbon pavement materials.

Experimental program

In accordance with the JTG 3341–2024 standard, this study conducted 7-day unconfined compressive strength tests, freeze-thaw cycle tests, and wet-dry cycle tests on Φ150 mm × 150 mm standard specimens with different mix proportions (coefficient of variation < 15%). The loading rate for the strength tests was controlled at 1 mm/min, and the average value of the valid data was taken as the representative value. The testing equipment included a DYE-2000 electro-hydraulic pressure testing machine, an SHBY-40B constant temperature and humidity curing box, and a 101-4 digital display constant temperature forced air drying oven.

In the freeze-thaw cycle tests, the procedure strictly followed the Specifications for Design of Highway Asphalt Pavement (JTG D50-2017). Control and experimental groups were established. After 28 days of curing, the experimental group was subjected to five freeze-thaw cycles, after which the strength was measured to calculate the strength loss rate. Although five cycles are sufficient to preliminary reveal the damage evolution mechanism of the material, we acknowledge that this number of cycles has limitations in evaluating long-term durability for alpine regions. Consequently, future research should focus on extended freeze-thaw cycles under more extreme conditions to further verify the long-term reliability of the material. In the wet-dry cycle tests, conducted in strict accordance with the Testing Methods of Cement and Concrete for Highway Engineering (JTG 3420 − 2020), the specimens were sequentially exposed to 24 h of water immersion, 12 h of drying at 85 °C, and 8 h of re-immersion, for a total of 10 cycles. The test was terminated if the mass loss rate exceeded 5%, after which the final strength was determined and compared with that of the control group.

Microstructural characterization is essential for understanding the structure-property relationships of materials. In this study, a multi-scale characterization of the samples was performed using X-ray diffraction, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy, and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. XRD was employed to analyze the crystal structure and phase composition, FT-IR to detect surface functional groups and chemical bonds, SEM to observe the microscopic morphology and distribution, and EDS to examine the elemental distribution in micro-regions.

Results and discussion

Raw material characterization

Fig. 2Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of coal gangue.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of coal gangue.

As shown in Fig. 2, Based on the comprehensive analysis of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images, the microstructure of the coal gangue samples exhibits morphological characteristics typical of layered silicate minerals. Under low-magnification observation, the specimens exhibit distinct agglomeration phenomena, where numerous irregular particles form aggregates through edge adhesion. High-resolution images clearly reveal that the fundamental units constituting these aggregates are flaky or platy crystals. These units are densely interconnected through random stacking, forming a compact layered composite structure.

Further examination reveals the presence of extensive micron- and submicron-scale interlayer gaps, channels, and exfoliation surfaces between the lamellae. These interconnected pores constitute a complex three-dimensional network structure. Such a porous architecture not only significantly increases the specific surface area of the material but also provides pathways for the transport of fluids or ions.

This microstructural configuration, characterized by a dense layered matrix coupled with an open pore network, is critical in determining the physicochemical properties of coal gangue15. It not only explains the material’s observed looseness and adsorption potential but also provides a structural basis for understanding its behavior during subsequent mechanical activation or chemical treatment processes.

Fig. 3EDS analysis of coal gangue.

EDS analysis of coal gangue.

As shown in Fig. 3, Energy-dispersive spectroscopy analysis reveals that the coal gangue is primarily composed of silicon and aluminum. The silicon peak (0.45 keV) shows the highest intensity, followed by the aluminum peak(1.49 keV), confirming its nature as a typical aluminosilicate mineral rich in active silicon‑aluminum components capable of forming geopolymer networks. The spectrum also exhibits a potassium peak and trace peaks of iron and calcium, corresponding to common impurity minerals in coal gangue. The material demonstrates a high‑silicon, moderate‑aluminum, and low‑calcium profile16. The relatively high Si/Al ratio indicates the need for supplementing sufficient calcium sources during alkali activation to optimize the formation of gel phases.

Fig. 4EDS analysis of GGBS.

EDS analysis of GGBS.

As shown in Fig. 4,Energy-dispersive spectroscopy analysis shows that the sample is mainly composed of silicon and calcium, consistent with the typical characteristics of slag. In particular, the calcium element exhibits an extremely sharp peak at approximately 3.69 keV, directly confirming that this slag powder is a high‑calcium material. This provides a material basis for its function as the primary calcium source in alkali‑activated systems, which is crucial for the development of gel strength17. Simultaneously, the characteristic peak of silicon at approximately 1.74 keV is also prominent, indicating a rich silicate structure that can serve as the skeletal component of the gel network. Furthermore, distinct peaks for magnesium and sodium are detected in the spectrum. These elements typically exist in solid solution within the gel phase, contributing to enhanced structural stability. The carbon peak intensity is very low, consistent with the low carbonate content characteristic of blast furnace slag, confirming that its cementitious activity primarily originates from glassy aluminosilicate rather than carbonate components. The EDS characteristics of this slag powder demonstrate that it is rich in key reactive components such as calcium, silicon, and aluminum, particularly its high calcium‑to‑silicon ratio, making it an ideal precursor for alkali‑activated cementitious materials18.

Fig. 5SEM analysis of GGBS.

SEM analysis of GGBS.

Figure 5 reveals that the GGBS particles exhibit a highly heterogeneous composite structural. The particle skeleton is composed of large, smooth, and dense glassy particles with fine aggregates attached to their surfaces19. Notably, the micromorphology of the particles presents a dichotomous distribution: some particles maintain a complete and smooth glassy state, reflecting brittle fracture during the grinding process; in contrast, other particles exhibit extremely rich surface textures, which may result from the release of internal stresses generated during rapid cooling.

High-resolution images further reveal the presence of numerous open pores on the particle surfaces and along their uneven interfaces. These features not only significantly increase the specific surface area of the particles but, more importantly, act as “capillary channels” that enable the alkali activator to penetrate the interior of the particles through capillary adsorption, thereby facilitating erosion and depolymerization reactions from the surface inward. Furthermore, the abundant surface defects possess high surface energy, serving as preferential nucleation sites in the early stages of the reaction and promoting the rapid formation of sodium aluminosilicate hydrate (N-A-S-H) gel.

In summary, the micromorphology of this GGBS achieves a balance between “structural stability” and “chemical reactivity”: the smooth, dense particles ensure the packing density of the paste, while the rough, porous particles provide high reactivity. These multiscale morphological characteristics suggest that, as a precursor, it exhibits excellent geopolymerization reaction kinetics and holds promise for forming a dense interfacial transition zone in alkali-activated systems20.

Unconfined compressive strength

During the unconfined compressive strength test, the failure process of the specimens exhibited distinct stage-by-stage characteristics, which is consistent with the typical failure mode of alkali-activated materials. Initial cracks initiated at approximately 35% of the failure load at one-quarter of the specimen height—a location typically corresponding to the weak zone of the interfacial transition zone. Liu et al.19 observed a similar phenomenon in their study on slag-based geopolymers and attributed it to the relatively low bond strength between the aggregate and the cementitious matrix in this region.

As the load increased to 65% of the failure load, the cracks propagated stably to two-thirds of the specimen height, accompanied by surface particle spalling. The particle spalling observed in this study is highly consistent with the “surface peeling” characteristics of alkali-activated materials during the mid-to-late loading stages reported by Guo et al.21, who attributed this phenomenon to the progressive failure of the interface between the gel phase and unreacted particles.

Upon further loading to near the ultimate state, the cracks rapidly penetrated the full height of the specimen, with significant bulging deformation occurring in the central region, ultimately leading to aggregate-matrix debonding and loss of bearing capacity. This bulging failure mode indicates that the specimen had entered the plastic flow stage (Fig. 6) According to recent studies22,23, the failure mechanism at this stage in alkali-activated systems primarily involves the disintegration of the C-A-S-H gel network and the collapse of the microstructure. Notably, the failure process observed in this study differs from that of ordinary Portland cement concrete: the latter typically exhibits more brittle splitting failure, whereas the former, due to the viscoplastic characteristics of the gel phase, demonstrates more pronounced ductile failure features24,25. This difference further confirms the potential advantages of alkali-activated materials in terms of energy dissipation.

Analysis of the strength development mechanism

As shown in Fig. 7, the strength development of all mixes exhibits a clear and consistent trend: the higher the early-age strength, the lower the subsequent strength growth rate.For instance, Mix 1 achieved a 28-day strength increase of 68.1%, while Mix 6 showed only a 28.4% growth. This phenomenon is closely associated with the reaction kinetics characteristic of alkali-activated cementitious systems. Mix with higher alkalinity or reactivity facilitate rapid dissolution of aluminosilicate phases from the precursors at an early stage, generating a substantial amount of initial gel. This results in higher 7-day strength but also consumes more reactive components, thereby limiting further strength gain at later ages. Conversely, Mix with lower alkalinity or slower reaction initiation (e.g., Mix 1 and Mix 4) produce less gel initially. However, under sustained alkaline conditions, the active components continue to dissolve gradually and participate in polycondensation, leading to a significant enhancement in later-age strength26.

Fig. 7Unconfined compressive strength of different formulations.

Unconfined compressive strength of different formulations.

At 28 days of curing, Mix 6 demonstrated the best overall mechanical performance, also exhibiting the highest 7-day strength, making it suitable for engineering applications with strict requirements for early-age strength.

For projects prioritizing both high early-age strength and maximum long-term strength, Mix 6 should be selected preferentially.

When long-term strength development and economic efficiency (potentially by reducing water glass dosage) are of greater concern, Mix 1 or Mix 4 offers advantages due to their notable later-age strength gain.

The observed differences in strength development patterns fundamentally stem from variations in the alkali-activation reaction pathways and the evolution of gel microstructures across different Mix. High-strength Mix typically correspond to faster reaction rates and the earlier formation of a dense gel framework, which has been widely confirmed in existing studies. In contrast, Mix with high growth potential (e.g., Mix1) may follow a “delayed acceleration” mode: an open, high-specific-surface-area initial gel network forms at early ages, providing ample sites for the subsequent deposition and infilling of continuously forming reaction products. This leads to progressive microstructural densification over time.

Mechanism analysis

In alkali-activated cementitious systems, strength originates from the three-dimensional network gel (C-A-S-H/N-A-S-H) formed by active components through a “dissolution-recondensation” reaction. In brief, highly reactive silico-aluminocalcic raw materials dissolve under alkali activation → generating precursors rich in Ca²⁺, silicate, and aluminate ions → forming a three-dimensional gel network framework based on silicon-oxygen/aluminum-oxygen tetrahedra through polycondensation. High‑Strength Group (Represented by Mix 6): Efficient Synergy between Reactivity and Activation. Mix 6 is characterized by a composition of “high steel slag content, low coal gangue content, and moderate dosages of water glass and GGBS,” forming an ideal system with “high‑calcium, moderate silico‑aluminate, and appropriate alkalinity.” The steel slag provides abundant Ca²⁺ and reactive silico‑aluminate sources, serving as the skeleton for gel formation. The low coal gangue content maximizes the proportion of reactive components, while the moderate amount of water glass effectively initiates the reaction, and its sodium silicate also participates in polycondensation. Consequently, the reaction proceeds rapidly, resulting in the formation of a dense gel network and achieving the highest 7‑day strength.

Low‑Strength Group (e.g., Mixes 1, 2, and 4): Insufficient Reactivity or Alkali Imbalance. Mix 1 exhibits the lowest strength due to its “low‑activity matrix” and “excessive water glass dosage.” Its low steel slag content and high coal gangue content result in a weak reactive base, while the highest water glass dosage leads to alkali surplus. Excessive Na⁺ remains in the pores, contributing to a loose microstructure27. Mixes 2 and 4 are characterized by excessively high coal gangue content, which significantly dilutes the reactive components. Even with adequate water glass, the insufficient formation of gel limits strength development.

The steel slag content shows a significant positive correlation with strength, serving as the primary calcium source and reactive silico‑aluminate source that directly determines the quality of the gel network. Coal gangue acts as a low‑reactivity filler; its excessive content dilutes the reactive system and reduces the proportion of cementitious products. The dosage of water glass exhibits an optimal window: a moderate amount efficiently activates the system when reactive raw materials are sufficient, whereas increasing its dosage yields limited improvements when raw materials are deficient. GGBS contributes to the pozzolanic effect, promoting later‑age strength development.

Pavement base materials in road engineering must possess both adequate mechanical strength and excellent durability to ensure structural load-bearing capacity and extend service life28. In practice, roads are subjected to long-term traffic loads and cyclic variations in temperature and humidity, which can lead to moisture intrusion, resulting in strength degradation and cumulative deformation of the base layer. In permafrost regions of China, freeze-thaw cycles exacerbate phase changes and migration of pore water, inducing structural damage and compromising the long-term performance of the base. Therefore, systematic evaluation and enhancement of the durability of base materials under freeze-thaw conditions are of critical importance for ensuring the safety and long-term functionality of road engineering in cold regions.

Freeze–thaw resistance

In real-world conditions, pavement structures operate within complex and variable environments, making base materials susceptible to moisture intrusion and migration29. This can lead to issues such as strength degradation and cumulative deformation, ultimately compromising the overall serviceability of the pavement.

Freeze-thaw cycles accelerate the phase transition and migration of pore water within the material, undermining the long-term performance and structural integrity of the base layer. Therefore, this chapter conducts freeze-thaw cycle experiments to analyze the frost resistance of the materials30.

As shown in Fig. 8, a systematic analysis of the 7-day unconfined compressive strength test results reveals that although Mix 1 (2.66 MPa) exhibits the lowest strength among the six groups, it still satisfies the strength evaluation requirements for semi-rigid base and subbase materials of primary highways and expressways, as specified in the JTG 2182 − 2020 standard.The balanced proportions of coal gangue, steel slag, and GGBS in this mix reflect an effective synergistic utilization of solid wastes, offering dual benefits of resource recycling and cost control. Mix 6 exhibited the highest strength (6.52 MPa). To assess the representativeness, engineering applicability, and coverage of extreme conditions of the mix proportions, Mix 1 and Mix 6 were selected as the two representative mixes for freeze-thaw cycle testing and uniformly designated as the freeze-thaw test groups.

Durability

Strength mechanism

The underlying mechanisms of dry-wet cycling and freeze-thaw cycling in coal gangue-based cementitious materials are fundamentally distinct. Dry-wet cycling disrupts the hydration equilibrium through periodic hydrothermal stress, driving a “thermal activation–rehydration” effect. This prompts unreacted active components to participate further in cementitious reactions, guiding the preferential deposition of gel products within pores and at interfaces, and exhibiting a dual mechanism characterized by both damage and enhancement. In contrast, freeze-thaw cycling primarily relies on the crystallization pressure from pore water phase transitions, leading to cumulative damage.

The performance evolution of the two typical mixtures in this study further confirms the dominant role of the cementitious component content. Mix 1, characterized by a high coal gangue content (69.20%) and a low total cementitious component content (25.95%), forms a structure dominated by an aggregate skeleton. Here, the cementitious phase primarily serves as a filler and binder, resulting in a 28-day compressive strength of only 4.52 MPa. This indicates limited early-age hydration and a relatively high porosity13. During dry-wet cycling (Fig. 9), although periodic hydrothermal stress promotes secondary hydration and gel deposition in the interfacial transition zone, the inherent insufficiency of active cementitious materials limits this effect. Consequently, the strength increases by only 21.2% after 10 cycles, reaching 5.48 MPa (Table 15).

In contrast, Mix 6, with a lower coal gangue content (52.63%) and a significantly higher total cementitious component content (43.16%), features a cementitious phase that dominates the system, forming a continuous three-dimensional network. This results in a 28-day compressive strength of 8.47 MPa, reflecting more complete early hydration and a denser microstructure. The “thermal activation–rehydration” effect during dry-wet cycling continuously activates unreacted particles, generating substantial C–S–H gel that fills pores and reinforces interfaces. After cycling, the strength increases significantly by 37.5% (reaching 11.65 MPa), demonstrating the structural optimization achieved through the deep exploitation of the cementitious potential.

It is important to note that the freeze-thaw and wet-dry cycle tests conducted on the alkali-activated coal gangue-steel slag-GGBS mixture in this study rely primarily on short-term data, with the aim of elucidating early-stage durability evolution and micro-degradation mechanisms35. In practice, however, pavement base materials endure complex temperature and moisture fluctuations over years or even decades. Consequently, short-term data are insufficient to fully capture the mixture’s long-term durability over its entire life cycle, thereby somewhat limiting the direct engineering applicability of our current conclusions. Future work should therefore prioritize extended durability cycle testing and long-term field monitoring on physical test sections to further validate and refine the long-term serviceability of the mixture.

In summary, the impact of dry-wet cycling on coal gangue-based materials is essentially a competitive outcome between “thermally activated secondary hydration” and “hygrothermal stress damage.” Systems with a richer cementitious component exhibit a more pronounced response to this process, thereby achieving greater strength improvements. Therefore, increasing the cementitious component content to reduce porosity and refine the pore structure is the core regulatory strategy for maximizing performance gains and minimizing damage in materials exposed to complex environments.

Microstructural characterization

After wet‑dry cycling, the XRD pattern of the sample displays typical characteristics: the coexistence of newly formed calcite diffraction peaks and raw‑material quartz peaks. This indicates that carbonation did not significantly degrade the gel skeleton36. Destructive carbonation would have consumed a substantial portion of the gel‑phase components, leading to a flattening of the amorphous hump. In practice, the amorphous hump remains prominent, demonstrating that the primary gel structure is preserved. The emergence of new calcite peaks suggests that carbonation products mainly act as microcrystalline fillers deposited within gel pores, forming a “local filling–densification” mechanism. This microstructural feature explains the increase in strength observed after wet‑dry cycling (Fig. 10).

A prominent amorphous hump is observed within the range of 20°–40°, indicating a high content of amorphous phases. This suggests a comprehensive alkali-activation reaction that generated a substantial amount of amorphous gel products. Furthermore, the absence of sharp diffraction peaks confirms that the majority of the raw materials have been transformed into gels. According to the Scherrer equation, significant broadening of the main calcite peak in wet‑dry cycled samples indicates that the crystallites are in the nanometer‑to‑micron range37. Such a microcrystalline morphology is conducive to pore penetration and densification. By comparing the peak width with that of standard calcite powder, the “microcrystalline filling–densification” mechanism can be directly validated.

A comparison of the XRD patterns of samples subjected to wet‑dry and freeze‑thaw cycling reveals notable differences. The wet‑dry cycled sample exhibits a relatively smooth background with high signal‑to‑noise ratio, reflecting structural densification after carbonation filling, which reduces incoherent X‑ray scattering. In contrast, the freeze‑thaw cycled sample shows a significantly elevated and broadened background, along with a more pronounced diffraction hump. This is attributed to the enhanced diffuse scattering of X‑rays caused by microcracks and structural defects induced by frost‑heave stress.

As shown in Fig. 13, The mineralogical changes of the mixtures under different environmental actions were characterized by XRD. The results are interpreted as follows:

Control Group: Initial Hydration Baseline at Ambient Temperature.

The XRD pattern of the control group illustrates the crystalline phase distribution after 28 days of standard curing. A sharp, dominant diffraction peak observed near 2θ = 27.3° corresponds to quartz (SiO2), an inert mineral residue from the coal gangue that remained unreacted. Additionally, diffraction peaks around 33.8° and 50.0° represent the primary calcium silicate hydrate (C-(A)-S-H) gels and minor microcrystalline phases generated during initial alkali activation. This pattern establishes the baseline physicochemical skeleton of the system in the absence of extreme hydrothermal fluctuations.

Freeze-Thaw Group: Inhibition of Activation and Network Weakening.

Compared to the control group, the intensities of the characteristic peaks for major hydration products (e.g., near 36.1° and 41.8°) are significantly reduced, and no new crystalline phases are detected. This indicates that the sustained low-temperature environment severely inhibited the alkaline activation of the glassy phases in the steel slag and ground granulated blast furnace slag, causing the secondary hydration process to stagnate. Without sufficient new cementitious products to repair the microstructural damage induced by frost heave pressure, the matrix density decreased, leading to the observed degradation in macroscopic strength.

Dry-Wet Group: Crystalline Strengthening Induced by Deep Thermal Activation.

The XRD pattern for the group subjected to 10 dry-wet cycles exhibits a profound shift in phase composition. The most striking feature is the explosive emergence of an intensely sharp diffraction peak at approximately 2-theta = 22°, accompanied by a series of new sharp peaks at 31.0° and 45.2°. The sudden appearance of these high-intensity peaks demonstrates that the dry-wet process transcends simple physical moisture migration, instead triggering a potent hydrothermal-like synthesis effect. The 85 °C drying stage, coupled with subsequent immersion, provides deep secondary thermal activation for the previously dormant solid waste particles. This process not only accelerates the formation of C-(A)-S-H gels but also induces their transformation into highly crystalline mineral phases with superior mechanical properties40. These newly formed high-strength crystalline phases interpenetrate the pores and initial microcracks of the coal gangue aggregates, achieving microstructural “self-healing” and reconstructing a dense, robust 3D load-bearing framework, which fundamentally drives the leap in material strength.
