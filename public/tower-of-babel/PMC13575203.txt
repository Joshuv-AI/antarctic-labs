An F1-score-weighted ensemble of deep learning models for enhanced cloud detection in remote sensing imagery
Authors: Nan Ma, Lin Sun, Yanhui Guo, Nannan Hu, Shiyuan Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cloud detection algorithms in remote sensing imagery exploit spectral and spatial characteristics, either independently or synergistically, to facilitate the discrimination between cloud and underlying surfaces. Individual cloud detection methods manifest unique operational advantages but suffer from inherent limitations, such as bright surface misclassification and thin cloud omission, leading to unstable performance across heterogeneous landscapes. A critical research gap exists in how to systematically integrate complementary strengths of multiple deep learning models while mitigating their individual weaknesses. To address this gap, this paper proposes a novel ensemble framework based on multiple deep learning models, using an F1-score-weighted fusion strategy. The framework first evaluates three representative deep learning-based detection algorithms (CD-SLCNN, SRMF-CD, CNN-TransNet) on Landsat-8 imagery, then derives algorithm-specific weights from their F1-scores on a validation set to produce a weighted probabilistic fusion. Quantitative comparisons on a hold-out test set show that the proposed method outperforms standard ensemble baselines: it improves F1-score by 4.7% points over majority voting and by 1.9% points over bayesian model averaging, while also reducing misclassification and omission errors across heterogeneous landscapes. The proposed F1-weighted ensemble thus provides a robust and accurate solution for large-scale cloud detection.

Introduction

Cloud detection constitutes a fundamental and critical cornerstone in remote sensing image processing, with profound implications for a wide spectrum of applications, including surface information extraction1, change detection2, and atmospheric correction3. Precise cloud identification significantly mitigate cloud-induced interference in surface feature extraction processes, thereby enhancing the robustness and reliability of subsequent analytical procedures4,5.

The rapid advancement in remote sensing technology coupled with revolutionary developments in computer vision has facilitated the evolution of cloud detection methodologies from conventional algorithmic approaches to sophisticated deep learning paradigms, yielding substantial improvements in both detection accuracy and computational efficiency6–8. Early cloud detection methodologies primarily relied on spectral characteristics, exemplified by the threshold-based approach proposed by Saunders and Kriebel9 and the decision tree method developed by Irish et al.10. While these methods offered computational efficiency, they demonstrated limited adaptability to complex and dynamic surface and atmospheric conditions11. Subsequently, the integration of machine learning techniques into cloud detection domains marked a significant advancement, as illustrated by the Support Vector Machine (SVM) implementation by Joshi et al.12and the Random Forest (RF) approach adopted by Wei et al.13, which enhanced detection accuracy through the learning of manually engineered features. The emergence of deep learning methodologies heralded a breakthrough in cloud detection capabilities. Notably, the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based approach14,15 enabled automatic extraction of high-level cloud features, while the U-Net architecture and its variants, achieved remarkable results in cloud segmentation tasks16–18.

Contemporary cloud detection research continues to advance toward enhanced accuracy, improved generalization capability, and increased computational efficiency. The innovative application of attention mechanisms, Transformers, and multi-modal data fusion techniques has substantially elevated cloud detection performance19–22. Chen et al. propose a multiscale attention feature aggregation network. This framework integrates a multiscale strip pooling attention module for contextual feature extraction, a deep multi-head feedforward transfer attention module for guided feature mining, and a bilateral feature fusion module with boundary refinement to precisely recover boundary details, improving segmentation accuracy across complex scenes23. Xu et al. developed an integrated transformer (TransGA-Net) with gradient aware feature aggregation using Transformer as the encoder, enhancing the modeling ability of global features and long-term dependencies24.Furthermore, multimodal data such as hyperspectral imagery, SAR images, polarimetric images, and atmospheric data have been applied to cloud detection research, enhancing detection accuracy in complex surface environments, particularly excelling in challenging scenarios such as urban areas and mountainous terrain25,26. Beyond these developments, the remote sensing community has recently witnessed the emergence of large-scale foundation models and hybrid paradigms. For instance, SpectralGPT has demonstrated powerful generalization capabilities for various spectral interpretation tasks27, and Fleximo provides a flexible architecture for adapting foundation models to diverse downstream applications28. Moreover, beyond purely data-driven approaches, hybrid strategies that couple model-driven and data-driven priors—such as learning disentangled priors for hyperspectral anomaly detection—have shown promise in addressing ill-posed inverse problems. While these advanced AI paradigms open new avenues for remote sensing, they are not yet widely adopted for operational cloud detection, and our work focuses on a complementary direction: ensemble learning from existing deep models. Nevertheless, the majority of existing cloud detection methods exhibit optimal performance only under specific sensor configurations or scene conditions, with their effectiveness still substantially influenced by surface characteristics and cloud morphology. For instance, while certain methods demonstrate exceptional performance in vegetated regions, they are prone to false positives over high-albedo surfaces such as urban areas and bare soil. The detection of thin clouds and cloud boundaries remains particularly challenging, with different methodologies exhibiting varying degrees of omission or over-identification when handling these complex cases, thereby impacting overall detection performance. Maintaining high accuracy and stability across diverse geographical environments and atmospheric conditions continues to represent a crucial objective in cloud detection research.

To enhance cloud detection across diverse surface environments, we previously proposed three methodologies: CD-SLCNN29, SRMF-CD30, and CNN-TransNet31. The CD-SLCNN approach achieved multi-source satellite data cloud detection by utilizing apparent reflectance information between clouds and typical surface features. However, this method exclusively relied on spectral information without fully exploiting spatial relationships among different pixels, resulting in thin cloud omission and misclassification between clouds and high-reflectance surfaces32. The SRMF-CD methodology integrated prior surface reflectance data with multi-scale feature networks for cloud detection. While this approach improved thin cloud detection and cloud identification over high-reflectance surfaces, its dependence on temporal difference information led to potential misclassification in areas of significant surface change33. Furthermore, due to local information loss and insufficient global information capture, the SRMF-CD method required enhancement in cloud boundary delineation precision and demonstrated limitations in cloud identification over snow/ice-covered regions. The CNN-TransNet approach incorporated spectral, spatial, and temporal information through CNN-Transformer modules and difference feature components, enhancing the discrimination between clouds and surface features. Compared to SRMF-CD, CNN-TransNet partially mitigated the impact of surface changes, while its dual-branch network architecture captured comprehensive spatial detail features and global information, thereby improving detection accuracy. Although this method achieved accurate cloud identification across various scenarios with stable performance and reduced sensitivity to surface types, it still exhibited varying degrees of thin cloud omission34.

A critical analysis of the above three representative methods reveals a persistent and fundamental challenge: no single deep learning-based cloud detection algorithm can maintain optimal and stable performance across the full spectrum of heterogeneous landscapes (e.g., vegetation, urban, bare soil, ice/snow, water bodies) and cloud morphologies (e.g., thick clouds, thin clouds, cloud boundaries). Each method possesses complementary strengths—SRMF-CD excels in bare soil and water bodies but underperforms in urban areas; CNN-TransNet achieves robust detection across most scenarios yet struggles with thin cloud edges; CD-SLCNN is computationally efficient but highly sensitive to high-albedo surfaces. Different cloud detection methods demonstrate distinct strengths and spatio-temporal applicability when confronting complex surface environments and diverse cloud morphologies. In large-scale remote sensing scenes, the heterogeneous nature of surface types coupled with the complexity of cloud formations makes it impractical for any single method to consistently achieve high accuracy and stability. Consequently, the integration of multiple detection methods has emerged as a promising approach, leveraging the complementary advantages of various techniques while mitigating their individual limitations. This fusion strategy enables the synergistic exploitation of different algorithms’ strengths, thereby enhancing both the accuracy and robustness of cloud detection35. However, an effective strategy to systematically integrate these complementary strengths while mitigating their individual weaknesses remains conspicuously absent.

Building upon the aforementioned analyses, this study proposes a novel F1-score-driven fusion methodology for cloud detection in remote sensing imagery. Through systematic evaluation of extant cloud detection paradigms, we identified and incorporated three representative deep-learning models: CD-SLCNN, SRMF-CD, and CNN-TransNet to develop a probabilistic fusion model. The model leverages scenario-specific weighting coefficients derived from F1-score performance metrics, enabling adaptive integration of complementary model strengths. Dataset construction

Data source

To establish an objective evaluation framework for the cloud detection capabilities of CD-SLCNN, SRMF-CD, and CNN-TransNet methods, a standardized dataset was implemented for experimental validation. In this study, used the authoritative cloud detection benchmark datasets L8 Biome36 and SPARCS datasets37 as fundamental data sources to develop the Landsat-8 Cloud Detection Dataset Combined Difference Feature (L8DIFF-CD).

L8 Biome was specifically designed to assess algorithm generalizability across diverse land surface types. It comprises 96 Landsat-8 scenes, stratified equally across eight global biomes: barren, forest, grass/crops, shrubland, snow/ice, urban, water, and wetlands (12 scenes per biome). This balanced biome distribution enables systematic evaluation of performance variations under different surface conditions. The original annotation scheme includes five classes: fill, cloud shadow, clear, thin cloud, and thick cloud. SPARCS provides 80 manually annotated 1000 × 1000 pixel subscenes selected from Landsat-8 acquisitions. It features a finer annotation scheme with seven classes (shadow, shadow over water, water, snow, land, cloud, flooded). More detailed information about the dataset can be found in https://landsat.usgs.gov/landsat-8-cloud-cover-assessment-validation-data available.

L8DIFF-CD dataset

Through systematic inspection and comprehensive analysis of the complete image repository and associated cloud masks, we selected 108 images with corresponding cloud masks to establish L8DIFF-CD dataset, comprising 32 images from L8 Biome (4 scenes per biome) and 76 from the SPARCS dataset, covering barren, forest, grass/crops, shrubland, snow/ice, urban, water, and wetlands. The visible and near-infrared spectral bands (specifically bands 2, 3, 4, and 5) from the Landsat-8 OLI sensor were utilized in this study. Additionally, while the source datasets cloud masks originally encompassed multiple categories (clouds, cloud shadows, and clear sky), these were systematically reclassified into a binary classification scheme, where 0 and 1 denote clear-sky and cloud pixels, respectively.

Given the utilization of difference features as model inputs in both SRMF-CD and CNN-TransNet architectures, clear-sky (cloud-free) imagery are used to generate temporal difference images. In this study, 108 cloud-free images were selected based on the latitude, longitude, and acquisition time of the cloudy images. To minimize the impact of surface phenological variations, cloud-free images were systematically selected from identical or temporally proximate months relative to their cloud-contaminated counterparts. While subtle surface variations (e.g., crop growth, snow melt) may still exist, the reflectance increase caused by clouds—even thin clouds—is typically an order of magnitude larger than natural surface changes over such short intervals. Similarly, multi-band images were generated through band composition of the acquired Landsat-8 cloud-free images.

After preparing cloudy images, cloud-free images, and cloud masks, radiometric calibration was performed on 108 Landsat-8 images to obtain apparent reflectance data. While Landsat-8 images cover extensive geographical areas and typically contain vast clear-sky pixels (including vegetation, bare soil, and water bodies), such continuous clear-sky regions would create an imbalanced training dataset for subsequent thick and thin cloud detection experiments, potentially compromising detection accuracy. Therefore, this study carefully selected regions containing clouds or representative surface features from the full Landsat-8 scenes. The final dataset comprised images ranging from 1000 × 1000 to 5000 × 5000 pixels. These images encompass diverse land cover types, including forests, croplands, oceans, deserts, urban areas, and bare lands, as well as thick clouds, thin clouds, and broken clouds of varying sizes and densities.

The difference images were generated by computing the difference between cloudy and cloud-free images. Specifically, pixel-wise subtraction was performed between cloudy and cloud-free images of the same region to obtain a pixel difference image. Upon completion of the differential processing step, yielding 108 difference feature images (pixel values: 0–1), a final multi-channel composite image was created. This composite merges the spectral, spatial, and time information from both the initial apparent reflectance data and the resultant difference features, with each source contributing four spectral bands. In composite images, channels 1–4 represent the apparent reflectance data, while channels 5–8 encode the difference features. The resultant L8DIFF-CD cloud detection dataset comprises 108 composite images and their corresponding cloud masks.

The dataset was partitioned into training (60%, 65 images), validation (20%, 22 images), and test (20%, 21 images) sets using a stratified random sampling strategy based on three factors that substantially influence cloud detection performance: surface type, cloud cover percentage, and cloud state. Surface types were defined according to the eight global biomes from the L8 Biome dataset (barren, forest, grass/crops, shrubland, snow/ice, urban, water, wetlands). Cloud cover was grouped into deciles (0‑10%, 10‑20%, …, 90‑100%). Cloud state was categorised as thick, thin, or broken cloud. The stratification ensured that the relative frequencies of these factors were approximately preserved across the three subsets. Landsat‑8 scenes have large spatial footprints and originate from globally distributed locations. Because the split was performed on whole scenes and conditioned on surface type and cloud properties, scenes assigned to different subsets are typically far apart. A post‑split check of the WRS‑2 path/row indices confirmed that no two scenes from different subsets share the same or immediately adjacent tiles. Therefore, the three subsets are spatially independent. In the test set (21 images), vegetation (forest, grass/crops and wetland) accounts for 8 images (≈ 35% of pixels), water for 6 images (≈ 28% pixels), bare soil for 3 images (≈ 15% pixels), urban for 2 images (≈ 10% pixels), and ice/snow for 2 images (≈ 5% pixels).Table 1 provides detailed information on the validation and testing data. All reported performance metrics in Sect. 4 are computed exclusively on this held-out test set.

All Landsat-8 OLI imagery in this study was processed from the L8 Biome and SPARCS benchmark datasets provided by the USGS Earth Explorer. False-color composite images (bands 4, 3, and 2)and difference feature visualizations were generated using GDAL (version 3.4.1, https://gdal.org/), matplotlib (version 3.5.2, https://matplotlib.org/) and visio(version 2024, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/visio). The derived L8DIFF-CD dataset is publicly available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20582783.

Methodology

In this study, we propose a novel ensemble framework for cloud detection that integrates the complementary strengths of three deep learning models—CD-SLCNN, SRMF-CD, and CNN-TransNet—to achieve high-precision cloud identification in remote sensing imagery. The framework implements a systematic two-stage methodology: first, conducting a comprehensive evaluation of the individual model detection results, followed by an weighted probabilistic fusion based on F1-score metrics to generate consolidated detection outputs. The key distinction between the proposed ensemble framework and conventional cloud detection methods—including the three individual deep learning models we integrate, lies in its systematic exploitation of complementary strengths through a performance-driven weighting mechanism. Unlike CD-SLCNN (spectral-only), SRMF-CD (temporal-difference-only), or CNN-TransNet (spectral-spatial-temporal combined), our approach does not favor any single feature set or network architecture. Instead, it learns to adaptively weigh their probabilistic outputs based on quantitative performance metrics (F1-score), thereby producing a consensus decision that is more robust and accurate than any individual model, particularly in heterogeneous landscapes. Furthermore, in contrast to fixed-rule fusion strategies (e.g., majority voting or simple averaging), the proposed method employs scenario-specific weighting coefficients derived from empirical F1-score evaluations, enabling an adaptive and intelligent integration. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the process consists of three key modules: (1) Analysis of individual model detection results, (2) F1-score-driven performance evaluation, and (3) Weighted multi-model fusion. This F1-weighted ensemble not only improves detection accuracy and robustness but also offers reliable support for operational remote sensing image processing across diverse and heterogeneous landscapes.

Vegetation : Figure 2a and Figure 2b illustrate land surface coverage predominantly characterized by vegetation, encompassing forests, shrubs, and cultivated lands. Under vegetation surface conditions, the CD-SLCNN, SRMF-CD, and CNN-TransNet methods demonstrate relatively satisfactory performance in identifying thick clouds, with CD-SLCNN and CNN-TransNet methods achieving more refined cloud contours compared to the SRMF-CD method. Notably, the CD-SLCNN method relies exclusively on spectral information from clouds and typical ground objects. The inherent challenge arises from the fact that thin clouds exhibit apparent reflectance values closely resembling the surface reflectance, which results in significant omissions of thin cloud detection (as demonstrated in Figure 2b)). In vegetation regions, the apparent reflectance variations within cloud-covered areas are substantially more pronounced than surface variations, providing a favorable condition for the SRMF-CD and CNN-TransNet methods. These two methodologies effectively capture differential information, enabling a more comprehensive cloud identification process. The SRMF-CD method exhibits the least cloud omission, though it simultaneously demonstrates occasional instances of cloud over-detection. This observation underscores that in complex underlying surface, different cloud detection approaches still exhibit variations in precision and completeness.

Urban : Figure 3a and Figure 3b illustrate cloud detection challenges in urban environments with varying cloud densities. In Figure 3a, characterized by thick clouds, CD-SLCNN, SRMF-CD, and CNN-TransNet methods demonstrate adequate cloud identification capabilities. The CD-SLCNN method, however, reveals limitations by exclusively relying on spectral information, often misinterpreting bright urban structures as clouds and causing over-detection. In Figure 3b, featuring thin clouds, the detection complexity increases significantly. The thin clouds’ reflectance closely resembles that of urban surfaces, resulting in omission across all three methods. The CNN-TransNet method stands out by integrating spectral, spatial, and temporal information, along with emphasizing global features and contextual relationships, thereby minimizing cloud omissions more effectively compared to the other approaches.

Bare Soil : Figure 4a and Figure 4b depict thick and thin cloud distributions over bare land surfaces. The cloud detection results reveal significant methodological variations. The CD-SLCNN method demonstrates notably weak cloud identification capabilities in bare soil areas, where the high-luminance surface’s spectral characteristics closely resemble cloud signatures, leading to substantial cloud omissions, particularly for thin clouds. In contrast, the SRMF-CD method achieves robust cloud detection in bare land regions, effectively identifying both thick and thin clouds while experiencing minimal misclassification. The CNN-TransNet method performs well in detecting thick clouds but exhibits slight omissions along thin cloud edges.

Ice/Snow : Ice/snow share similar color characteristics with clouds and exhibit high reflectance within the visible light spectrum, making accurate cloud-ice/snow differentiation a significant challenge in cloud detection tasks. Figure 5a and Fig. 5b depict large-scale ice and snow-covered scenes, revealing distinct methodological performance. The CD-SLCNN method successfully identifies clouds above ice/snow-covered surfaces but frequently misclassifies mountain edge ice/snow regions as clouds, resulting in extensive misidentification. Although clouds and ice/snow exhibit different textural features, the SRMF-CD method demonstrates improved discrimination between clouds and ice/snow, yet experiences occasional thin cloud omissions in such regions. Notably, the CNN-TransNet method excels in comprehensively detecting clouds of various morphologies above ice/snow surfaces, presenting the least occurrences of cloud omission and misclassification. This approach effectively leverages multi-dimensional information to overcome the inherent spectral and textural similarities between clouds and ice/snow surfaces.

Water : Figure 6a and Figure 6b predominantly feature vegetation and water surface types. Weighted multi-model fusion

Based on both qualitative analysis and F1-score evaluation of cloud detection results from CD-SLCNN, SRMF-CD, and CNN-TransNet methods, the F1-scores effectively measure the consistency between detection results and reference cloud masks, reflecting the performance of each method. Consequently, a fusion approach based on F1-scores is proposed in this study to integrate these three cloud detection methods. The fusion methodology assigns weights to each method according to their mean F1-scores obtained from the validation dataset. These weights are then used to compute a weighted combination, resulting in a final cloud detection probability model. In the probability map, a pixel value of 1 indicates 100% confidence of cloud presence, while a value of 0 represents clear sky conditions. The weight calculation is formulated as follows:

2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${W_i} = \frac{{F1 - score}}{{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^n {F1 - score} }}$$\end{document}

Where Wi denotes the method weight, F1-score represents the mean F1-score, and n equals 3 for the number of methods. The calculated weights for CD-SLCNN, SRMF-CD, and CNN-TransNet are 0.26, 0.36, and 0.38, respectively. The fusion formula is defined as:

3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Fuse = 0.26 \times {P_{CD - SLCNN}} + 0.36 \times {P_{SRMF - CD}} + 0.38 \times {P_{CNN - TransNet}}$$\end{document}

The fusion results are expressed as probability values ranging from 0 to 1, indicating the likelihood of pixels being classified as cloud or non-cloud. To determine the optimal decision threshold for binarizing the fused probability map, we adopted a principled approach based on the validation set. Specifically, we computed the precision-recall curve across threshold values ranging from 0 to 1 (step size 0.01) on the validation set and selected the threshold that maximized the F1-score. As shown in Fig. 7, the maximum F1-score on the validation set was achieved at a threshold of 0.35. This threshold was therefore selected for all subsequent test set evaluations.

Experiments and results analysis

Experimental details

All experiments were conducted on a high-performance computing platform with the following specifications: an Intel Xeon® Platinum 8225 C processor (2.5 GHz), an NVIDIA Tesla T4GPU (11 GB GDDR6 memory), and 32 GB of system RAM. The experimental environment was configured using Python 3.8 and PyTorch 1.8 framework, with all algorithms implemented in Python programming language.

For comprehensive cloud detection experiments comparing CD-SLCNN, SRMF-CD, and CNN-TransNet methodologies, the following data configurations were employed: CD-SLCNN Method: Utilized the Landsat-8 spectral library for training, focusing on apparent reflectance information across red, green, blue, and near-infrared spectral bands. Validation was performed using the L8DIFF-CD dataset’s spectral bands 1–4, representing the apparent reflectance characteristics; SRMF-CD Method: Conducted cloud detection experiments using differential spectral information from bands 5–8 of the L8DIFF-CD dataset, capturing inter-band variations in the near-infrared and visible spectrum; CNN-TransNet Method: Integrated both apparent reflectance and spectral difference information from the complete band range (bands 1–8) of the L8DIFF-CD dataset, enabling a comprehensive multi-spectral analysis approach. Additionally, both SRMF-CD and CNN-TransNet models share the following training configurations: the total number of training images is approximately 20,000, the batch size is set to 4, the initial learning rate is 0.001, and the number of training epochs is 200. The only difference lies in the optimizer: SRMF-CD uses the Adam optimizer, while CNN-TransNet adopts the RMSprop optimizer.

All experimental results reported in this section are computed on the independent test set, which was not used in any capacity for training the individual models, computing fusion weights, or selecting the decision threshold.

Conclusion

Cloud detection methods face inherent limitations in complex scenarios, with their performance exhibiting considerable variability across diverse environmental contexts. To address this challenge, this study developed an F1-score-weighted ensemble framework based on multiple deep learning models to achieve stable and accurate cloud detection. We systematically evaluated the performance of three representative deep-learning-based detection models—CD-SLCNN, SRMF-CD, and CNN-TransNet on Landsat-8 imageries. Based on the performance of the three models across different remote sensing scenarios, an F1-score-derived weighting strategy was employed to integrate their outputs into a unified probabilistic detection model, with decision thresholds rigorously weighted to distinguish between cloudy and clear-sky pixels. Both qualitative and quantitative assessments demonstrated that the proposed F1-weighted ensemble approach significantly reduced misclassification errors and cloud omission, while maintaining high robustness across heterogeneous landscapes. The integration of multiple models enables a synergistic combination of their complementary strengths, offering a reliable solution for large-scale cloud detection applications.

Despite the promising results achieved by the proposed F1-score-weighted ensemble framework, several challenges remain to be addressed in future work:

First, the current fusion strategy employs globally fixed weights derived from average validation F1-scores. However, the relative performance of individual models varies substantially across different surface types (e.g., CD-SLCNN outperforms others in certain scenes despite its lower average F1-score). This observation motivates the development of scene-adaptive or conditional fusion mechanisms that dynamically adjust weights based on input image characteristics (e.g., land cover type, cloud fraction, or scene texture).

Second, the computational overhead of running multiple deep learning models in parallel remains non-negligible. Future research could explore knowledge distillation techniques to compress the ensemble into a single efficient model that approximates the fusion output while reducing inference time.

Third, while our framework integrates three specific deep learning models, the methodology is general and could be extended to incorporate a broader range of cloud detection algorithms, including recent foundation models (e.g., SpectralGPT, Fleximo) or hybrid model-data-driven approaches. Evaluating the scalability and performance gains of larger ensembles represents another promising direction.

Finally, the reliance on paired cloudy-clear reference images for difference feature computation limits applicability to satellites without frequent revisit cycles. Developing self-supervised or single-image fusion strategies that do not require temporal differencing would broaden the practical utility of ensemble-based cloud detection.
