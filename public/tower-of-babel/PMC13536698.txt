Psychometric evaluation and cross-cultural adaptation of the Persian version of the Relocation Stress Syndrome Scale – Short Form (RSSS-SF) in patients transferred from intensive care units to general wards
Authors: Sajjad Soofi, Masoumeh Zakerimoghadam, Melika Zoroufchi, Özkan Görgülü, Ali shafahi, Sahar Khoshkesht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsThe transition from intensive care units to general wards is a critical and stressful phase in patient recovery, often associated with adverse psychological and clinical outcomes. Relocation Stress Syndrome (RSS) is a recognized condition resulting from this transition, highlighting the need for valid and culturally adapted assessment tools to accurately measure patient stress and guide nursing interventions.MethodsThis methodological study was conducted to adapt and validate the Persian version of the Relocation Stress Syndrome Scale–Short Form (RSSS-SF). The final Persian version was administered to 155 patients who had been transferred from intensive care units (ICUs) to general wards in Tehran hospitals. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 29 and AMOS 24.Item–total correlations, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients, and exploratory factor analysis (EFA) were conducted to examine internal consistency and factor structure. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was subsequently performed to test the goodness-of-fit of the model. All participants were fully informed regarding the study objectives and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality and anonymity of the collected data were assured. Introduction

With the expansion of intensive care units (ICUs) in recent years, significant progress has been made in the management and care of patients with severe and complex diseases [1]. ICUs play a vital role in healthcare systems by providing advanced treatment and continuous monitoring for patients with the most critical clinical conditions, using specialized equipment and highly trained healthcare professionals [2, 3]. Estimates indicate that approximately 30 to 45 million adults worldwide develop critical illnesses each year, many of whom require admission to intensive care units [4]. In the United States alone, nearly five million patients are admitted to ICUs annually [5].

After stabilization of their clinical condition, patients are typically transferred from ICUs to general wards. This transfer, commonly referred to as a “transition,” represents a sensitive and critical phase in the patient’s recovery process [6]. According to Meleis’ Transition Theory, transition is defined as the passage from one life phase or health status to another. Given that nurses are extensively involved in various health- and illness-related transitions, this theory is widely applied in nursing and is considered a key conceptual framework within the discipline [7]. Based on this theory, adequate patient preparation, educational and supportive care, and continuity of therapeutic relationships can reduce negative transition outcomes and enhance patient adaptation [7, 8]. Therefore, because transitions significantly affect patients’ health, nursing interventions should be designed to prevent adverse outcomes and improve health-related results [9].

Transition from the ICU to general wards is considered one of the most complex and challenging types of transitions, as patients move from an environment with continuous monitoring and advanced care to settings with lower levels of supervision [6]. Despite the existence of professional care guidelines, this transfer can be a stressful experience and may lead to adverse outcomes such as ICU readmission, as well as negative emotional responses including depression, uncertainty, anxiety, and stress [6, 10]. These stress- and anxiety-related responses during the transfer process are collectively referred to as Relocation Stress Syndrome (RSS). RSS is defined as a condition in which individuals experience physiological or psychosocial disturbances, such as reduced quality of life, increased mortality, and disease recurrence, following relocation from one care environment to another [10]. Accordingly, assessing the level of stress during patient transfer and designing interventions to reduce it are considered essential [11].

Before 2008, relocation stress was primarily assessed using general instruments such as the Faces Anxiety Scale or the Visual Analog Scale, which did not comprehensively capture the conceptual dimensions of relocation stress syndrome [12–14]. In 2008, a 23-item Relocation Stress Syndrome Scale was developed and demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability; however, its length and the physical and psychological condition of patients at the time of transfer limited its clinical applicability [10, 11]. To address these limitations, a shortened 10-item version of the scale was developed and validated, preserving the factor structure of the original instrument while offering greater clinical efficiency and user-friendliness. The multidimensional structure of relocation stress, including emotional, physical, and care-related experiences, is conceptually consistent with the transition-related responses described in Meleis’ Transition Theory. Therefore, the RSSS-SF can be considered a clinically relevant instrument for assessing patients’ transition experiences during ICU-to-ward transfer.

This short form enables rapid and accurate assessment of relocation stress and can serve as a foundation for designing targeted nursing interventions to reduce patient stress during transitions [10]. Nevertheless, effective use of psychometric instruments requires cultural and linguistic adaptation to the target population.

Despite the clinical importance of relocation stress syndrome and its impact on patient outcomes, no valid and reliable Persian version of the short-form Relocation Stress Syndrome Scale has yet been reported for use among Iranian patients. This gap complicates evidence-based intervention planning and accurate assessment of patient stress during ICU-to-ward transitions. Therefore, the present study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the validity and reliability of the short-form Relocation Stress Syndrome Scale in Iranian patients, in order to provide a culturally appropriate and reliable tool to improve the quality of care and patient empowerment.

Method

Study design

This research was conducted as a Cross-Cultural Validation Studies focusing on the translation, cultural adaptation, and psychometric evaluation of the 10-item Relocation Stress Syndrome Scale (RSSS-SF) in adult patients transferred from intensive care units to general wards. The study methods and reporting followed the COSMIN (Consensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement Instruments) guidelines for the translation, cross-cultural adaptation, and psychometric validation of patient-reported outcome measures [15].

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

1. Minimum hospitalization of 48 h in the intensive care unit to ensure that the patient has sufficient exposure and experience in the ICU setting. 2. Age between 18 and 65 years. 3. Ability to read and write. 4. Absence of psychiatric disorders.

Exclusion criteria

1. Readmission to the intensive care unit (ICU). 2. Unwillingness to continue participation. 3. Not completing more than 20% of the questionnaire items.

Participants and setting

This study was conducted at Imam Khomeini Hospital Complex, Tehran, Iran, affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences. The study population included all patients admitted to intensive care units. A convenience sampling method was used to recruit participants from the target population. The study was carried out from June 2025 to November 2025.

Sample size

Following the commonly applied rule of thumb for factor analysis, a participant-to-variable ratio of 5:1 was considered for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and 10:1 for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) [16]. Based on this guideline, a total sample size of 155 participants was considered sufficient for conducting both EFA and CFA. Due to sample availability limitations, both analyses were performed using the same sample.

Instrument: RSSS-SF description

The short-form of the Relocation Stress Syndrome Scale (RSSS-SF) was first developed by Won et al. (2022) in South Korea for patients transitioning from intensive care units (ICUs) to general wards. This scale comprises 10 items and demonstrates excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95. The RSSS-SF encompasses three distinct factors: Physical Factors, Perception of Nursing Care, and Emotional Factors [10]. The first factor, Physical Factors, includes five items reflecting patients’ experiences of physical needs and dependency following the transfer. The second factor, Perception of Nursing Care, consists of three items that assess how patients perceive the care provided by nurses in the general ward, capturing feelings of neglect or insufficient attention. The third factor, Emotional Factors, includes two items focusing on psychological concerns and anxieties related to the transition, such as worry about the future. Collectively, these three factors explain 82.07% of the total variance, with all factor loadings exceeding 0.5, indicating stable construct validity. Respondents rate each item on a five-point Likert scale (0 = Strongly Disagree, 1 = Disagree, 2 = Neither Agree nor Disagree, 3 = Agree, 4 = Strongly Agree). Total scores are calculated by summing the item scores, with higher scores indicating greater stress experienced during the transfer from the ICU to a general ward. The total possible score ranges from 0 to 40 [10].

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation process

Forward translation

The Persian version of the RSSS-SF was initially translated independently by three bilingual translators fluent in both English and Persian, including two with a nursing background and one with a general linguistic background [17].

Expert panel review

After forward translation, the Persian version of the RSSS-SF was reviewed by an expert panel to ensure clarity, cultural relevance, and conceptual equivalence. Experts were selected based on their academic background in nursing or psychology, clinical experience in intensive care setting, and prior experience in instrument development or psychometric evaluation. The panel consisted of three nursing faculty members, including a critical care specialist and a psychologist, along with ten additional faculty members with expertise in critical care and instrument development. The panel evaluated the translated items for linguistic accuracy, comprehensibility, relevance, and the logical sequence of questions. Ambiguous or unclear items were discussed and revised through consensus among the translators, research team, and experts [18, 19].

Backward translation

After completing the forward translation, the Persian version of the scale was back-translated into English by an independent translator fluent in both Persian and English, who was not familiar with the original scale. The purpose of this process was to verify the accuracy and conceptual equivalence of the Persian translation. Any discrepancies between the back-translated version and the original English version were reviewed and resolved through discussion among the translators and research team, ensuring that the Persian version accurately reflected the content and meaning of the original scale. The back-translated version was then submitted to the original developer of the scale for confirmation [17].

Cognitive interviewing (Pre-test)

A pre-test was conducted with 10 patients during their transition from the intensive care unit (ICU) to general wards. The participants were selected to ensure maximum diversity in terms of gender, age, and socio-economic status. During the cognitive interviews, patients were asked a series of questions to assess the clarity, comprehensibility, and relevance of each item, including: Are the items understandable to you? When you read or hear each statement, do you feel that you accurately grasp its meaning? Do you feel the need to rephrase any item in your own words to understand it better? What do you think each item is measuring? How easy or difficult is it for you to answer these questions? The feedback obtained from this pre-test was used to identify ambiguous or unclear items and to make necessary modifications to the Persian version of the questionnaire before proceeding to formal validity testing [20].

Face validity

Face validity of the Persian version of the RSSS-SF was assessed both qualitatively and quantitatively.

Qualitative assessment: Ten patients from the target population were asked to review the questionnaire and provide feedback on item clarity, comprehensibility, and relevance. They were encouraged to comment on any ambiguous or confusing wording and suggest improvements [21].

Quantitative assessment (Impact Score): The same participants rated the importance of each item using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The impact score for each item was calculated using the formula:

Items with an impact score of ≥ 1.5 were considered acceptable for inclusion in the final questionnaire [19].

Content validity

Content validity of the Persian version of the RSSS-SF was assessed using a panel of experts. Experts were selected according to their expertise in critical care nursing, psychometric evaluation, and academic experience in instrument development. Experts evaluated the instrument regarding Persian grammar, the importance of each item, as well as the sequence and clarity of items. Quantitative content validity was examined using the Content Validity Index (CVI). An item-level CVI (I-CVI) threshold of ≥ 0.78 was considered acceptable for retaining each item. Experts rated each item on a 4-point Likert scale: 1 = not relevant, 2 = somewhat relevant, 3 = relevant, and 4 = highly relevant. Additionally, the study objectives and theoretical and operational definitions of the variables were provided to the experts in advance. Construct validity was subsequently assessed using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) [18, 19].

Reliability

Internal consistency of the questionnaire was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha [22, 23].

Data collection

Data were collected through interviewer-administered questionnaires. After selecting the study units and obtaining informed consent, participants completed a demographic information form and the Persian version of the short-form Relocation Stress Syndrome Scale (RSSS-SF) at the bedside in the intensive care units. The completion of each questionnaire took approximately 10 min.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 and AMOS version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics, expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median (interquartile range, IQR), and frequency (percentage).

Item analysis included examination of item means, corrected item–total correlations, and Cronbach’s alpha if item deleted to assess the homogeneity and internal consistency of each item. The internal consistency of the total scale and each subscale was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient.

To examine the underlying factor structure of the Persian version of the RSSS-SF, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted using principal component analysis with varimax rotation. The suitability of data for factor analysis was assessed by the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Factors were retained based on eigenvalues greater than 1.0, scree plot examination, and parallel analysis.

To verify the factor structure identified in the EFA, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed using structural equation modeling in AMOS. Model fit was assessed using multiple fit indices: chi-square/degree of freedom (χ²/df), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI), Adjusted Goodness-of-Fit Index (AGFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Acceptable thresholds were χ²/df < 3, CFI/GFI/IFI ≥ 0.90, AGFI ≥ 0.80, and RMSEA ≤ 0.08.

Construct validity was supported when factor loadings were statistically significant and theoretically consistent with the original model. Internal consistency reliability was further verified by Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for each dimension. Convergent validity was examined by the magnitude of standardized factor loadings and the degree of inter-item correlations.

Result

Participants’ characteristics

A total of 155 participants were included in the study (Table 1). The mean age was 52.54 ± 16.55 years (median = 54, IQR = 23.5), and 56.1% were male. The majority of the participants were married (76.1%), and 47.7% resided in Tehran. Regarding educational level, 14.2% were illiterate, 31.6% had primary, 39.4% had secondary, and 14.8% had university education. In terms of occupation, 39.4% were housewives, 34.8% self-employed, and 14.2% employees. Moreover, 43.2% of the participants had a previous history of ICU admission. The average duration of previous ICU stay was 24.65 ± 47.96 months (median = 6, IQR = 22).

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

The data were suitable for factor analysis (KMO = 0.74; Bartlett’s χ² = 419.33, p < .001). A three-factor solution emerged, explaining 57.97% of the total variance (Table 2 (see Additional file 1), Fig. 1).

Factor loadings ranged from 0.613 to 0.848, with the strongest loadings on the items “Ward nurses ignore my concerns and needs” (0.848) and “Ward nurses do not pay attention to me” (0.792). The factor structure was consistent with the original Korean version of the RSSS-SF developed by Won & Son (2020).

Reliability

The overall internal consistency of the Persian RSSS-SF was Cronbach’s α = 0.83, indicating acceptable internal consistency. Within subscales, internal consistency coefficients were α = 0.73 for Perception of nursing care, α = 0.438 for Physical factors, and α = 0.247 for Emotional factors. Given that emotional subscale consists of two item, the spearman-brown coefficient was calculated and indicated low internal consistency(r = .29), suggesting limited homogeneity between the two items in this subscale. Although the reliability of the emotional factor was modest, the total scale demonstrated acceptable and adequate internal consistency for research use.

The content validity of the instrument was confirmed with an overal CVI of 0.87, indicating good content validity for the persian version of the RSSS-SF.

The three-factor model identified in the EFA was evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis (Table 3). Discussion

The present study aimed to adapt and validate the Persian version of the Relocation Stress Syndrome Scale–Short Form (RSSS-SF-Fa) among patients transferred from intensive care units (ICUs) to general wards. The findings support the construct validity and reliability of the Persian version, suggesting that it is a psychometrically sound tool for assessing relocation stress in Iranian clinical settings.

The exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses supported the three-factor structure of the scale—perception of nursing care, physical factors, and emotional factors—which is consistent with the original Korean version developed by Won and Son (2020) [10]. The total variance explained (57.97%) and the factor loadings (0.61–0.85) indicate that the Persian version adequately captures the conceptual dimensions of relocation stress. These findings are comparable to those reported in the original study, where a similar three-factor solution explained 53.89% of the total variance. The confirmatory factor analysis further demonstrated acceptable model fit indices (χ²/df = 2.13, CFI = 0.91, RMSEA = 0.07), suggesting that the factor structure is stable and generalizable within the Persian context. These outcomes reinforce the cultural equivalence of the scale and its cross-cultural adaptability.

The internal consistency of the total scale was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.83), which aligns with the reliability values reported in the original study (α = 0.88). Although the emotional subscale demonstrated low internal consistency (0.247), additional analysis confirmed limited homogeneity between the two items in this subscale, as also supported by the Spearman–Brown coefficient. This finding suggests that the emotional dimension may not be fully stable in this population and should be interpreted with caution. The low reliability may be partly attributed to the limited number of items as well as potential cultural differences in the expression of emotional responses during hospitalization. Overall, the total scale demonstrates acceptable reliability for research and clinical screening purposes.

The results suggest that relocation stress among Iranian patients may reflect psychological and interpersonal dimensions observed internationally—particularly the perceived quality of nursing care and the loss of ICU security. However, certain items (e.g., the family’s role in personal care) showed different response patterns, reflecting Iran’s strong family-oriented healthcare culture, where familial involvement in post-ICU care is viewed positively rather than as a stressor. This cultural nuance supports the need for careful semantic adaptation and highlights the importance of culturally sensitive nursing interventions aimed at family inclusion during patient transfer processes.

Conclusion

In summary, the Persian version of the RSSS-SF demonstrated satisfactory validity and reliability, supporting the factorial structure of the original instrument. The scale is a practical and culturally appropriate tool for assessing relocation stress among Iranian ICU patients and can facilitate the development of patient-centered, family-integrated nursing strategies to improve post-transfer adaptation and recovery.

Limitations and recommendations

Several limitations should be noted. First, the study used a cross-sectional design, which did not allow for the assessment of test–retest reliability or temporal stability. Second, the sample was recruited from hospitals in Tehran and surrounding regions, which may limit generalizability to other provinces or cultural subgroups. Future research should include longitudinal assessments, test–retest analyses, and diverse hospital settings to establish broader applicability. Additionally, exploring the predictive validity of the RSSS-SF in relation to post-transfer anxiety, depression, or readmission rates would provide further clinical insight. Moreover the emotional subscale showed internal consistency, which may be related to the limited number of items. Therefore, further psychometric evaluation of this subscale in larger and more diverse sample is recommended.
