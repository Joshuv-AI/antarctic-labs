ABSTRACT
This report presents an evaluation of the IEC 62443 standards in the context of electric grid
substations, as part of a collaborative effort among Sandia National Laboratories (SNL), Idaho
National Laboratory (INL), and the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). The primary
objective is to assess the applicability of these standards to enhance cybersecurity measures for
industrial automation and control systems (IACS) within the energy sector. The evaluation identifies
strengths, such as the scalability of security levels and the structured lifecycle guidance provided by
IEC 62443. However, it also highlights significant gaps, including limited integration of physical
security, insufficient guidance for legacy systems, and challenges in addressing emerging threats like
supply chain vulnerabilities. Recommendations for refining the standards are proposed, including
the need for tailored guidance for securing legacy systems, integrating physical security with
cybersecurity frameworks, and enhancing interoperability across multi-vendor environments. By
addressing these gaps, the IEC 62443 standards can be strengthened to ensure comprehensive
cybersecurity for electric grid substations, thereby supporting the resilience and reliability of critical
energy infrastructure.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report outlines the findings of a collaborative project involving Sandia National Laboratories
(SNL), Idaho National Laboratory (INL), and the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL)
aimed at developing application profiles for energy sectors in alignment with ISA99 and IEC 62443
standards. The focus of this effort is to evaluate the applicability of IEC 62443 standards to electric
grid substations, which are vital components of the energy infrastructure.
The IEC 62443 series provides a robust framework for securing industrial automation and control
systems (IACS), emphasizing risk management, security requirements, and lifecycle approaches. This
evaluation utilizes a reference model based on electric grid substation topology to assess the
standards' effectiveness in addressing cybersecurity needs for current and future grid systems.
Key strengths of the IEC 62443 standards include their scalability through defined security levels
(SL1–SL4) and comprehensive coverage of system and component-level requirements. However,
the analysis reveals several challenges and gaps, such as the limited integration of physical security
measures, insufficient guidance for legacy systems, and a lack of specificity regarding power system
requirements. Additionally, the standards often assume detailed system design and vendor-specific
implementations, which are not applicable to abstract models.
To address these identified gaps, several recommendations are proposed, including the integration
of physical security with cybersecurity frameworks, tailored guidance for securing legacy systems,
and methodologies for real-time operational constraints. By refining the IEC 62443 standards to
better address the unique challenges of electric grid substations, the resilience and security of critical
energy infrastructure can be significantly enhanced.

1. PURPOSE
This report is in response to the collaborative effort of Sandia National Laboratories (SNL), Idaho
National Laboratory (INL) and the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) to develop
application profiles for specific energy sectors, aligning with ISA99 and IEC 62443 standards.
Key actions include developing application profiles in accordance with ISA/IEC 62443-1-5, with a
specific emphasis on ISA99 Working Group 14 (WG14) to gather stakeholder feedback, and
mapping profiles to relevant standards while addressing any gaps with standard-setting bodies. The
primary goal is to enhance secure energy infrastructure by expanding these profiles to encompass
95% of installed energy applications, as well as future grid systems, detailing devices, functions,
security levels, features, and involved parties. As part of this effort, SNL will focus upon the
developing a reference model based upon an electric grid substation topology which will be used to
evaluate the IEC 62443 standard for its application within this context. Using the reference model,
both IEC 624433 standard strength and possible challenges will be identified and discussed.

This report is organized to first provide an introduction and background to the IEC 62443/ISA99
standards, followed by an overview of the electric grid topology model used for the standard
evaluation. Next, the process for the evaluation will be outlined in the standards gap analysis,
followed by an examination of the identified standards gaps and the corresponding
recommendations.

2. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND
IEC 62443 is a series of international standards designed to establish a cybersecurity framework for
industrial automation and control systems (IACS) operational technology (OT). The primary goals
of IEC 62443 include providing guidelines for risk management, defining security requirements for
systems and components, and establishing security levels to address varying degrees of risk [1]
[2]. The standard aims to promote a risk-based approach to cybersecurity, enabling organizations to
identify vulnerabilities and implement tailored security measures throughout the lifecycle of
industrial systems [3].

2.1.      ISA/IEC 62443 Overview

The ISA/IEC 62443 series is organized into nine distinct sections that address key areas of
cybersecurity in Industrial Automation and Control Systems (IACS), as summarized in Figure 1.
Each section in ISA/IEC 62443 focuses on specific aspects of cybersecurity, enabling organizations
to implement and manage security measures tailored to their operational needs [3].

 Figure 1. Diagram of the ISA/IEC 62443 Series of standards from [3]

2.2. IEC 624433 Central Features
The ISA/IEC 62443 standards are designed to address cybersecurity for industrial automation and
control systems and encompass several key features. These features include risk assessment and
management, security levels, and a zone and conduit model. Additionally, the standards adopt a
lifecycle approach, clearly define roles and responsibilities, and provide frameworks for compliance
and certification. They also offer guidance for implementing security controls, emphasize
interoperability and integration, promote continuous improvement, and encourage collaboration and
information sharing among stakeholders [3]. For this effort, several features would not be directly

applicable to the model under consideration and would thus be out of scope for evaluation. Key
areas for this application are discussed in the following sections.

2.2.1. Security Levels
The IEC 62443 standards include a key feature known as security levels (SL), which are utilized to
assess the cybersecurity risks associated with operational technology (OT) and industrial control
systems (ICS). This feature enables industrial organizations, as well as asset owners and operators, to
gain insights into their assets, understand their roles within the overall infrastructure, identify
potential security vulnerabilities, and proactively address these weaknesses before they can be
exploited by malicious actors [4].

There are five distinct Security Level grades, numbered from 0 to 4. SL 0 represents the lowest risk
level, while SL 4 signifies the highest or most vulnerable level. The framework necessitates that SL 4
adheres to more stringent compliance requirements compared to SL 0. These varying levels reflect
the system's resilience against different types of attackers as summarized in Table 1 [2].

2.2.2. Zones and Conduits
An essential phase in the risk assessment process involves dividing the System Under Consideration
(SuC) into distinct zones and conduits. This division aims to pinpoint assets that possess similar
security attributes, thereby allowing for the establishment of unified security requirements that help
mitigate cybersecurity risks.

A zone refers to a collection of logical or physical resources categorized based on risk factors or
other criteria, including the importance of the assets, their operational roles, their physical or logical
locations, the access needed, or the organization responsible for them [5]. Similarly, a conduit is
described as a logical assembly of communication pathways that have shared security requirements,
linking two or more zones [6]. By segmenting the SuC into zones and conduits, the overall risk can
also be diminished by narrowing the potential impact of a successful cyber-attack. IEC 62443 Part 3-
2 specifies or suggests that certain assets should be categorized as follows [7]:

 a) Business and control system assets

 b) Safety-related assets
 c) Temporarily connected devices
 d) Wireless devices
 e) Devices linked through external networks

2.2.3. Roles, Control System, Automation Solutions and IACS
The ISA/IEC 62443 series establishes relationships between roles, control system, automation
solution and IACS as shown in Figure 2. The left side of the figure outlines the roles defined in the
ISA/IEC 62443 series. The Asset Owner is the organization responsible for and operating the
Industrial Automation and Control System (IACS). The Maintenance Service Provider offers
support for automation solutions, while the Integration Service Provider manages integration tasks
such as design, installation, and risk assessment for the automation solution. The Product
Supplier manufactures and supports the hardware and software products used in the IACS. On the
right side, the figure identifies various system types within the ISA/IEC 62443 series. IACS
Components include embedded devices designed for monitoring and control, host devices that are
general-purpose and capable of running software applications, network devices that facilitate data
flow, and software applications that interface with control systems. The IACS System consists of an
integrated set of components provided by multiple suppliers. The Automation Solution represents a
specific implementation of a control system at a facility, which encompasses essential safety and
control functions, along with the operational and maintenance policies necessary for effective
support. Overall, the IACS integrates the automation solution with the required procedures to
ensure efficient operation and maintenance [8].

2.3.       ISA99 WG-14
ISA99 is a committee within the International Society of Automation (ISA) that focuses on
developing standards and guidelines for cybersecurity in industrial automation and control systems
(IACS). Established in response to the increasing need for robust cybersecurity measures in
industrial environments, ISA99 aims to address the unique challenges posed by the convergence of
information technology (IT) and operational technology (OT). Key objectives of ISA99 include

 13

developing standards that provide guidelines for implementing cybersecurity measures across
various industries, promoting best practices by offering resources and methodologies, facilitating
collaboration among stakeholders such as manufacturers, system integrators, and end-users, and
addressing emerging threats by continuously monitoring the evolving cybersecurity landscape [9].

ISA99 Working Group 14 (WG14) is a specific subgroup within the ISA99 committee that focuses
on the development of the ISA/IEC 62443 series of standards. Its key responsibilities include
drafting and refining the ISA/IEC 62443 standards, collaborating with international organizations
like the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) to ensure global recognition and
applicability. Additionally, the working group is committed to continuously updating and improving
the ISA/IEC 62443 standards to address emerging risks and vulnerabilities in industrial automation
and control systems. ISA99 WG is currently preparing a draft document that details the scope of
their developed profiles which aims to create a set of security profiles for transmission and
distribution applications, focusing on control centers and substations. The profile document
outlines the importance of risk assessment, defining security levels, and implementing mitigation
strategies to enhance the resilience of electric grid operations, as well as discussing the roles of
various stakeholders, including asset owners, system integrators, and product suppliers, in ensuring
compliance with the established cybersecurity standards [10].

4.1.1. Standards Analysis Process
 The evaluation process is structured to focus on the IEC 62443 parts that are both directly
 applicable and in scope for the analysis of electric grid substation elements. By narrowing the
 investigation to these parts, the process ensures alignment with the abstract nature of the substation
 model, which lacks vendor-specific details and operational data. Parts such as Part 1-1, Part 1-4, Part
 2-5, Part 3-3, and Part 4-2 are prioritized because they provide relevant frameworks, lifecycle
 approaches, and security requirements that can be applied generically to the model. These parts offer
 actionable insights into modeling, risk assessment, and technical security measures, making them
 suitable for the evaluation. Conversely, parts that require detailed system implementations, vendor-
 specific considerations, or operational metrics are excluded, as they fall outside the scope of the
 abstract model. This method was selected so the evaluation could remain practical and relevant to
 the context of electric grid substations while avoiding unnecessary complexity.

4.1.2. IEC 62443-1-1: Terminology, concepts, and models

 As IEC 62443 (Part 1-1) introduces the foundational principles of the IEC 62443 standards and
 provides a framework for modeling industrial communication systems using “Zones,” “Security
 Levels,” and “Conduits,” the process for standard gap analysis involved modifying the base electric
 grid substation model to incorporate these elements. Figure 4 depicts the process for modifying the
 base substation model to incorporate the IEC 62443-1-1 elements.

Using the base architecture, depicted in Figure 3, Process, Control Center and Enterprise Zones
were identified as shown in Figure 5. The process zone includes devices such as process interface
units (sensors, actuators, field devices, etc.), merging units, RTUs, PLC bay control units,
etc. Similarly, the Control Center Zone focuses on the OT control elements, such as the OT HMI
systems, security and data management systems, firewalls, IDS/IPS and RTU Gateways.
Additionally, the Enterprise Zone was identified as those devices that are primarily IT organizational

systems [7].

Level 2 (Station Level) and Level 3 (Communications) align to the Control Center Zone and Security
Level 4 (Network Level) aligns to the Enterprise Zone. As part of the assignment of security levels,
example cybersecurity offerings and responsible parties were identified for each of the security

4.1.3. IEC 62443-1-2: Master glossary of terms and definitions (Out of Scope)
 IEC 62443-1-2 (Part 1-2) is master glossary of terms and definitions and as such would not be
 directly applicable to the evaluation of an electric grid substation model. Additionally, this part
 would be considered out of scope as it does not provide actionable guidance for modeling or
 evaluation the cybersecurity posture of an electric grid substation. [14].

4.1.4. IEC 62443-1-3: System security conformance metrics (Out of Scope)

 IEC 62443-1-3 (Part 1-3) provides system security conformance metrics. As the purpose of this
 effort is to ascertain how the IEC 62443 standards could apply to electric grid substation model
 which by its definition is an abstraction of high-level elements rather specific guidance of metrics
 would not be application due to the lack of detailed processes or technical requirements. Similarly,
 Part 3-1 would be out of scope for this evaluation as this abstract model does not include
 operational or vendor-specific implementations [15].

4.1.5. IEC 62443-1-4: IACS Security lifecycle and use cases
 IEC 62443-1-4 (Part 1-4) provides a detailed framework for managing the security lifecycle including
 risk assessment, operation and maintenance, and secure decommissioning. As this model is an
 abstraction of an actual system, the operation, maintenance and secure decommissioning elements
 of this framework would be considered not directly applicable and similarly, not in scope. However,
 the element of a risk assessment is applicable for inclusion within this evaluation as these concepts
 can be applied generally without vendor-specific details [16].

 As electric grid substations are critical infrastructure that require tailored cybersecurity measures to
 ensure operational reliability and grid stability, identifying the risks and vulnerabilities of the
 elements within the architecture is necessary [4], [17]. Table 3 provides a summary of some
 identified risks, threats and vulnerabilities for key substation elements, including Process Units,
 PLCs, RTUs, HMIs, Bay Controllers, OT DMZ, IT DMZ, and Network Controllers, that could
 compromise the security or functionality of these elements.

 For each element, the assessment outlines the potential impact of these risks, such as operational
 disruptions, data breaches, or cascading failures, and provides security requirements aligned with the
 IEC 62443 Security Levels (SL1–SL4) [18], [19]. These security levels range from basic protection
 against accidental incidents (SL1) to advanced protection against intentional incidents by high-skill
 attackers (SL4). By tailoring security measures to the specific needs of each substation element, this
 approach ensures alignment with operational requirements and IEC 62443 standards, offering a
 scalable framework for addressing cybersecurity challenges in electric grid substations.

4.1.16.   Alignment of IEC 62443-3-3 and IEC 62443-4-2

 IEC 62443-3-3 and IEC 62443-4-2 are complementary standards within the IEC 62443 series, both
 addressing cybersecurity for Industrial Automation and Control Systems (IACS). While they share
 similarities, such as the use of Foundational Requirements (FRs) and Security Levels (SL1–SL4) to
 define security objectives, their focus and scope differ significantly [13], [24].

 IEC 62443-3-3 focuses on system-level security requirements, providing a framework for securing
 the entire IACS environment, including interactions between components, communication
 pathways, and operational processes. In contrast, IEC 62443-4-2 addresses component-level security
 requirements, offering detailed technical measures to secure individual devices, such as PLCs, RTUs,
 HMIs, and network controllers. For further clarification, IEC 62443-3-3 is suited for defining
 system-wide security measures, while IEC 62443-4-2 is tailored for securing specific substation
 components. Table 7 provides a summary the differences between these two standards, particularly
 in the context of electric grid substations [13], [24].

6. CONCLUSION

This report is part of a collaborative effort between SNL INL and NREL to develop application
profiles for specific energy sectors in alignment with ISA99 and IEC 62443 standards. The primary
focus of this effort is to evaluate the applicability of IEC 62443 standards to an electric grid
substation model, identifying both strengths and challenges within the context of secure energy
infrastructure. Using a reference model based on electric grid substation topology, this evaluation
aims to assess the standard’s ability to address cybersecurity needs for current and future grid
systems.
IEC 62443 provides a useful framework for industrial automation and control systems (IACS)
cybersecurity, offering system-level requirements in IEC 62443-3-3, component-level requirements
in IEC 62443-4-2, and lifecycle implementation guidance in IEC 62443-2-5. The standards
leverage Foundational Requirements (FRs) and Requirement Enhancements (REs) to address key
security objectives such as authentication, data confidentiality, system integrity, and resource
availability. These elements are critical for ensuring the security and resilience of electric grid
substations, which are foundational to energy infrastructure.
The evaluation revealed several strengths of IEC 62443 standards, including their scalability
through Security Levels (SL1–SL4), comprehensive coverage of system and component-level
requirements, and structured lifecycle guidance for asset owners. These strengths make IEC 62443
highly applicable for defining cybersecurity measures across substation elements such as Process
Units, PLCs, RTUs, HMIs, Bay Controllers, OT DMZ, IT DMZ, and Network Controllers. The
standards effectively address system-wide security needs and provide actionable technical measures
for securing individual components.
However, the analysis also identified challenges and gaps in applying IEC 62443 to the electric grid
substation model. Key weaknesses include limited integration of physical security measures,
insufficient guidance for legacy systems, lack of specificity for power system requirements, and
challenges in addressing emerging threats such as supply chain vulnerabilities and IoT risks.
Additionally, the standards often assume detailed system design, vendor-specific implementations,
and operational data, which are not present in an abstract substation model. This limits the direct
applicability of certain parts, such as IEC 62443-2-1, IEC 62443-2-4, and IEC 62443-3-2, to high-
level substation evaluations.
To address these gaps, several recommendations have been proposed. These include expanding the
standards to include tailored guidance for securing legacy systems, integrating physical security with
cybersecurity frameworks, providing methodologies for real-time operational constraints and fault
tolerance, and enhancing interoperability across multi-vendor environments. Additionally, the
standards could benefit from more detailed risk prioritization frameworks specific to substations,
such as cascading failure prevention and grid recovery strategies.
While IEC 62443 provides a strong foundation for securing electric grid substations, its application
to abstract models requires refinement to better address the unique challenges of critical
infrastructure. By incorporating the proposed additions and addressing identified gaps, the standards
can be further strengthened to ensure comprehensive cybersecurity for electric grid substations,
both at the system and component levels.
