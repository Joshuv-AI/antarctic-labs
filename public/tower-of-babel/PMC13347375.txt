Metal-Free Analcime Derived from Rice Husk Ash for Visible-Light-Enhanced Heterogeneous Activation of H2O2: Application in Alkaline Indigo Carmine Degradation
Authors: Tam Minh Le, Chuyen Van Hoang, Collin G. Joseph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of sustainable, metal-free advanced oxidation processes is crucial for efficient wastewater treatment. In this study, analcime zeolite was rapidly synthesized from agricultural waste-derived rice husk ash via a facile 5 min microwave-assisted route. The resulting material exhibited a quasi-spherical morphology with a refined particle size (∼5.6 μm), significantly outperforming conventionally synthesized hydrothermal analcime in H2O2 activation. Under visible LED irradiation, the analcime/H2O2 system demonstrated exceptional performance at pH 9, achieving ∼96% degradation of indigo carmine within only 30 min. Systematic investigations revealed that alkaline conditions facilitate superior analcime dispersion and accelerated reactive species generation via surface-mediated activation. Radical formation studies confirmed that hydroxyl radicals (•OH) are the primary oxidative species generated through H2O2 activation, synergistically supported by superoxide radicals (•O2 –). Total organic carbon analysis further evidenced significant partial mineralization, indicating the effective breakdown of the dye’s structure. The analcime activator maintained high stability and reusability over multiple cycles without significant loss of activation efficiency. This work provides a green and cost-effective strategy for valorizing agricultural byproducts into high-performance activators for visible-light-assisted H2O2 activation and environmental remediation under mild alkaline conditions.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of modern society and economic growth has been accompanied by an increasing demand for aesthetic and visual products, leading to the substantial expansion of the dye, pigment, and ink industries. As a result, large volumes of highly colored dye-containing wastewater are continuously discharged into the environment. These effluents, which often contain persistent organic colorants, constitute a major source of environmental pollution and pose serious risks to human health and natural ecosystems. In aquatic environments, organic dyes can significantly deteriorate water quality, disrupt ecological balance, and exert toxic effects on aquatic organisms due to their chemical stability, low biodegradability, and potential for bioaccumulation. Among these pollutants, indigo carmine (IC) is one of the most extensively used dyes in textile and related industrial applications. Even at low residual concentrations, IC can impart intense coloration to wastewater and exhibits notable toxicity, making its efficient removal a critical environmental concern. , In response to the escalating risks associated with dye pollution, the visible-light-assisted advanced oxidation process has emerged as a promising and efficient approach for wastewater treatment. The photocatalytic technology leverages advanced structural designs, such as string-cage or heterojunction frameworks, to efficiently harness light energy for the profound detoxification of organic pollutants. Beyond mere degradation, it facilitates the transformation of complex contaminants into harmless mineralized products, providing a sustainable pathway for high-performance wastewater remediation using renewable solar energy. , However, the practical implementation of visible-light-assisted catalytic systems is still constrained by several challenges, including limited degradation efficiency, slow reaction kinetics, high catalyst synthesis costs, and dependence on specific light sources. −

Zeolites have been extensively employed as heterogeneous catalysts in a wide range of industrial processes and, more recently, have attracted growing interest as visible-light-assisted active platforms for oxidation-promoting remediation of organic dye pollutants from wastewater. , As crystalline aluminosilicates, zeolites can be synthesized through diverse routes, giving rise to a broad spectrum of framework topologies derived from various raw materials. , Their distinctive crystalline architectures, which are characterized by high specific surface areas, well-defined microporous structures, and excellent ion exchange and adsorption capabilities, render zeolites particularly attractive for catalytic and environmental applications. ,

Despite extensive research on zeolite-assisted activation systems for the degradation of organic pollutants, the fundamental interaction mechanisms between zeolite frameworks and dye molecules are still not fully elucidated. Zeolites with high Si/Al ratios are widely recognized for their enhanced hydrophobicity, superior framework stability, and the presence of surface-active sites, features that collectively underpin their promising performance as heterogeneous activators in advanced oxidation processes (AOPs). Within this class of materials, analcime has emerged as a promising zeolite of which the Si/Al ratio and activation properties can be tailored through appropriate synthesis strategies, thereby exhibiting favorable performance in oxidation-promoting applications. In such systems, hydrogen peroxide can be efficiently activated at solid surface sites, leading to the formation of highly reactive oxygen species (ROS) that govern the degradation of complex organic molecules. , The synergistic combination of adsorption on the zeolite surface and subsequent surface-mediated radical formation enables efficient cleavage of aromatic rings and chromophoric structures in dye molecules. Owing to this dual-functional support, transition-metal-free zeolite/H2O2-based systems have attracted increasing attention as environmentally benign alternatives to conventional Fenton processes, which typically require strongly acidic conditions and generate large amounts of iron-containing sludge. , However, despite the significant potential of visible-light-assisted H2O2 activation, several critical bottlenecks persist in its practical implementation. First, the low photonic efficiency remains a primary concern, as H2O2 exhibits negligible absorption in the visible spectrum (λ > 400 nm), necessitating high-performance activators to facilitate surface charge–transfer complexes (SCTC) for effective activation. Second, the scavenging effect of H2O2 at supra-optimal concentrations often leads to the unproductive consumption of hydroxyl radicals, forming less reactive hydroperoxyl species and reducing the overall mineralization rate. Furthermore, many heterogeneous systems suffer from deactivation and metal leaching, which not only compromise long-term stability but also pose risks of secondary environmental contamination. , Lastly, mass transfer limitations at the solid–liquid interface, often exacerbated by particle aggregation, frequently hinder the diffusion of reactants to active sites. Addressing these challenges requires the development of transition-metal-free, highly dispersible activation frameworks capable of effectively harvesting visible light and preconcentrating oxidants at the interface to overcome inherent kinetic barriers. To the best of our knowledge, the visible-light-assisted activation of H2O2 over analcime as a heterogeneous activator under alkaline conditions has not been systematically investigated.

In this study, analcime zeolite was synthesized via hydrothermal and microwave-assisted methods using silica derived from rice husk ash (i.e., an abundant, low-cost, and environmentally benign agricultural byproduct). The valorization of rice husk ash as a sustainable silica source provides a green and economically viable strategy for the activator synthesis. The surface morphology, crystalline structure, and visible-light-assisted activation efficiency of the synthesized analcime were systematically investigated under LED irradiation. These analyses provide key insights into the mechanistic role of zeolite surfaces in the heterogeneous activation of H2O2, contributing to the development of robust, metal-free, visible-light-driven activation systems for environmental remediation and water resource protection.

Experimental Description

Chemicals and Apparatus

In this study, analytical-grade sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 99%, Merck), aluminum chloride hexahydrate (AlCl3·6H2O, 99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich), absolute ethanol (C2H5OH, Merck), IC dye (C16H8N2Na2O8S2, 99.9%, HiMedia), terephthalic acid (TA), benzoquinone (BQ) (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck Group, Germany), locally sourced rice husk collected in Vietnam, and laboratory-prepared distilled water were used as received without further purification. Microwave-assisted synthesis was performed using a commercial microwave oven (Severin MW 7869, Germany) equipped with a self-fabricated Teflon reactor, while hydrothermal synthesis was conducted in a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. The phase composition and chemical characteristics of the synthesized materials were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD; Empyrean, PANalytical, UK), Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR; Jasco 4600 Type A, Japan) spectroscopy, and UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS; Jasco V770, Japan). Particle morphology and elemental composition were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; TM4000plus, Hitachi; JSM-IT200, JEOL, Japan) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS; Quantax, Bruker, USA). Textural properties were determined from N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms measured at −196 °C using a TriStar II Plus system (Shimadzu, Japan), while particle size distribution was analyzed by dynamic light scattering (DLS; SZ-100Z2, Horiba, Japan) after ultrasonic dispersion at 50 °C and 300 W for 30 min. Dye concentrations were quantified by UV–Vis spectroscopy (UH5300, Hitachi, Japan). Additional laboratory equipment, including a magnetic stirrer, an analytical balance, a pH meter, a furnace, a water bath, and a centrifuge, was employed throughout the experiments. A household white LED lamp (176-264 VAC/50 Hz, rated power of 30 W, and luminous flux of 2700 lm; Rang Dong Company, Vietnam) was used as the visible-light irradiation source. The photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded at room temperature on a FluoroMax-4 spectrofluorometer (HORIBA Scientific, USA) equipped with a 150 W xenon arc lamp.

Zeolite Synthesis via Conventional Hydrothermal and Microwave-Assisted Routes

Rice husk was first calcined at 900 °C for 5 h to obtain high-quality rice husk ash. Predetermined amounts of NaOH and AlCl3·6H2O were then accurately weighed, dissolved in distilled water, and magnetically stirred for approximately 15 min until a homogeneous and stable solution was obtained. The rice husk ash was subsequently added in calculated quantities to achieve the desired molar composition of the reaction mixture, corresponding to the zeolite composition reported in the literature. The suspension was further stirred to form a homogeneous gel. The resulting gel was aged under continuous stirring at room temperature for at least 24 h, after which it was subjected to two synthesis routes: hydrothermal crystallization in a Teflon-lined autoclave at 170 °C for 36 h and microwave-assisted synthesis using a microwave oven operated at 2500 W for 5 min, following previously reported procedures. After crystallization, the zeolite products were recovered by filtration, repeatedly washed with deionized water using a vacuum pump and a Büchner funnel until neutral pH was reached, and finally dried at 60 °C for 24 h to remove residual moisture.

Visible-Light-Assisted Catalytic Degradation of IC

The visible-light-assisted catalytic activity of the prepared samples was evaluated by dispersing a defined amount of analcime (100, 200, or 300 mg) into 200 mL of an aqueous IC solution (IC; concentrations of 30, 50, or 70 ppm) containing hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, prepared from a 30 wt % stock solution to a final concentration of 100, 200, or 300 mg L–1). The initial pH of the suspension was adjusted to 5, 7, or 9 prior to irradiation. The resulting mixture was magnetically stirred in the dark for 30 min to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium, followed by exposure to visible-light irradiation from a lamp positioned vertically 10 cm above the solution surface. At predetermined time intervals, aliquots were withdrawn and immediately centrifuged to separate analcime from the liquid phase. The residual dye concentration in the supernatant was determined by UV–Vis spectroscopy. This procedure was repeated throughout the reaction to monitor the degradation kinetics and to construct the visible-light-assisted catalytic efficiency profiles. Each experiment was repeated at least three times to obtain the average value.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Analcime Zeolite

Particle Morphology, Size Distribution, and Compositional Analysis

SEM Analysis

Figure a,d shows the representative SEM images of analcime crystals synthesized via microwave-assisted and hydrothermal routes, respectively. As observed, analcime particles obtained from both synthesis methods exhibit a distinct morphology with well-developed polyhedral facets, resulting in a characteristic quasi-spherical, “soccer ball-like” appearance. This morphology is a characteristic structural feature of analcime zeolite and is consistent with a previous report by Novembre and Gimeno. In both synthesis routes, the crystals are generally well-formed and structurally intact. Only a small number of fractured particles and trace amounts of unidentified impurities are observed, whose presence is negligible and does not significantly affect the overall morphological features.

(a,b,d,e) Particle morphology (SEM images were recorded at suitable magnifications) and (c,f) size distribution of the synthesized zeolites via microwave-assisted and hydrothermal methods.

Despite the similarity in crystal shape, the particle sizes derived from the two synthesis routes differ significantly, as illustrated in Figure a,b,d,e. The microwave-assisted synthesis yields relatively small analcime particles with sizes predominantly distributed in the sub-10 μm range. Quantitative particle size analysis indicates that the microwave-derived analcime exhibits particle diameters ranging from approximately 1 to 11 μm, with the majority of particles concentrated between 3 and 8 μm. The average particle size is approximately 5.6 ± 1.9 μm, and the distribution is moderately narrow. This size distribution (Figure c) suggests that microwave irradiation effectively accelerates nucleation while suppressing excessive crystal growth. In contrast, hydrothermal synthesis produces significantly larger analcime crystals with a much broader size distribution (Figure f). The particle sizes obtained via the hydrothermal route span a wide range from approximately 5 to 59 μm, with most particles distributed between 15 and 35 μm. A considerable fraction of large crystals exceeding 40 μm is also observed, resulting in an average particle size of approximately 23.2 ± 11.2 μm. The broad and right-skewed particle size distribution reflects the prolonged crystal growth and Ostwald ripening processes typically associated with conventional hydrothermal synthesis.

The pronounced differences in particle size distributions between the microwave-assisted and hydrothermal methods are consistent with our previous observations reported for zeolite 4A synthesized via microwave-assisted and hydrothermal routes. The substantially smaller particle size obtained through microwave-assisted synthesis is expected to increase the external surface area and the number of accessible (effective) active sites, which may in turn enhance colorant degradation. These structure–property relationships will be further examined and discussed in the following sections.

EDS Analysis

The elemental composition of the analcime zeolite samples was determined by EDS, with characteristic peaks corresponding to C, O, Na, Al, and Si detected at approximately 0.27, 0.51, 1.05, 1.49, and 1.75 keV, respectively. As shown in Figure a,b, the calculated Si/Al molar ratios were 2.78 ± 0.21 for the microwave-assisted sample and 2.64 ± 0.46 for the hydrothermally synthesized sample. These values confirm that both synthesis routes successfully preserve the characteristic analcime framework composition. The slight difference in Si/Al ratios suggests that the two methods primarily differ in their heat-transfer mechanisms, which influence crystal growth behavior rather than altering the framework chemistry. The Si/Al ratio plays a pivotal role in modulating the density of surface-active sites, which correlate with the enrichment of surface silanols and Lewis acidic centers. These act as the primary coordination centers enhancing the generation of ROS in AOPs without the need for a conventional semiconducting transition. ,

X-ray Diffraction and Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy Analysis

To further verify the crystal structure of both synthesized products, XRD and FTIR analyses were carried out. Figure a presents the XRD patterns of the zeolite materials synthesized via the two different routes. The diffraction results clearly confirm the successful formation of the analcime crystalline phase in both samples, as evidenced by the presence of sharp and well-defined peaks that closely match the reported pattern of analcime zeolite by Novembre et al. The XRD patterns display a series of characteristic reflections at 2θ = 15.841° (211), 18.298° (220), 24.252° (321), 25.991° (400), 30.581° (332), 31.974° (422), and 33.310° (431). Additional diffraction peaks observed at higher angles, including 35.883°, 37.074°, 40.564°, 47.805°, 52.49°, 54.25°, 57.71°, and up to 78.233°, are attributed to high-index lattice planes of analcime. The presence and good agreement of all reflections with the literature further confirm the formation of a highly crystalline and phase-pure analcime structure. The close correspondence between the experimental patterns and the reference demonstrates that both synthesis routes successfully yield analcime zeolite with a well-defined framework structure.

(a) XRD patterns and (b) FTIR spectra of the synthesized analcime zeolites via different methods.

Figure b shows the FTIR spectra of analcime zeolite samples synthesized by hydrothermal and microwave-assisted routes. The two spectra exhibit a nearly complete overlap, indicating that both synthesis methods produce structurally equivalent analcime frameworks. The absorption band positioned at 1635 cm–1 is attributed to the bending vibration of structural water molecules associated with the hydrated zeolite structure. The prominent band at around 964 cm–1 corresponds to the asymmetric stretching vibrations of T–O bonds (T is Si or Al) within the aluminosilicate framework, which are sensitive to Al-substitution and framework hydration. The band observed at approximately 734 cm–1 is assigned to symmetric T–O–T stretching or Si–O–Al bending vibrations, while the peak at about 618 cm–1 is related to T–O bending modes (Si–O–Si/Si–O–Al), further confirming the preservation of the analcime framework. The absence of additional bands associated with amorphous silica, extra-framework aluminum species, or organic impurities indicates a high phase purity of the synthesized materials. The strong similarity between the FTIR spectra is consistent with the XRD results, confirming that both synthesis routes yield an identical analcime framework, with any differences mainly arising from variations in crystal growth and particle size rather than changes in the framework structure.

Investigation of the Visible-Light-Assisted H2O2 Activation over Analcime Zeolite

Synergistic Effect of Analcime, H2O2, and Visible Light on Indigo Carmine Degradation

A series of systematic control experiments were conducted at neutral pH to elucidate the individual and combined roles of analcime zeolite, H2O2, and visible-light irradiation in IC degradation. Detailed reaction conditions and corresponding efficiencies are summarized in Table S1 (Supporting Information). As illustrated in Figure a, the addition of 100 mg of analcime (Ana-MW) in the absence of H2O2 resulted in a negligible degradation efficiency of only 2.2 ± 1.2% after 210 min of irradiation. This is consistent with the diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) results (Figure b), which indicate a wide band gap (E g) of approximately 3.5 eV, confirming that analcime exhibits very limited intrinsic photocatalytic activity under visible light.

(a) Synergistic effects of analcime, H2O2, and visible light on IC degradation (*denotes the synergistic Ana-MW/H2O2/light system); (b) determination of the analcime band gap energy (E g) using Tauc’s method derived from DRS spectra; and (c) kinetics of IC degradation. (Notation Ana-MW and Ana-HT denote analcime synthesized via microwave-assisted and conventional hydrothermal methods, respectively).

Control experiments revealed that both the Ana-MW/H2O2 system in the dark and the H2O2/visible-light system (in the absence of analcime) yielded poor degradation efficiencies of 6.3 ± 1.9% and 7.3 ± 2.9%, respectively. These results underscore the inability of analcime to activate H2O2 without light irradiation, as well as the negligible photolysis of H2O2 under visible light without a suitable photoactivator. The negligible dye degradation observed from the spontaneous dissociation of H2O2 aligns with the existing literature, confirming the high stability of H2O2 in the absence of catalysts or external activators. In contrast, a robust synergistic effect was observed in the combined Ana-MW/H2O2/light system (noted with * in Figure a). This explicitly demonstrates that analcime plays a pivotal role in facilitating H2O2 activation for IC degradation. Given the limited porosity of analcime discussed previously, dye removal cannot be attributed to adsorption or inherent catalytic activity. Therefore, it is hypothesized that the process is driven by a surface-mediated heterogeneous H2O2 activation mechanism; this hypothesis will be validated through subsequent experiments.

All experiments described above were conducted using analcime synthesized via the microwave-assisted (MW) method. The influence of the synthesis pathway was evaluated by comparing the performance of analcime prepared via microwave-assisted and conventional hydrothermal methods under identical reaction conditions. This comparison aims to elucidate how different crystallization kinetics and energy delivery mechanisms affect the resulting physicochemical properties and, consequently, the H2O2 activation efficiency of the zeolitic frameworks. Although both samples possess the same analcime structure, their H2O2 activation performances differ markedly (Figure a). The microwave-synthesized analcime achieved an IC degradation efficiency of 18.3 ± 4.5%, significantly outperforming its hydrothermally synthesized counterpart Ana-HT (12.8 ± 1.9%). This corresponds to a ∼43% increase in IC degradation performance, representing an approximately 1.4-fold enhancement over the traditional method. The observed enhancement is primarily attributed to the significantly reduced particle size of microwave-synthesized analcime. While the total pore volumes of both samples show negligible differences (Table ), their effects on IC degradation are markedly distinct. This suggests that the reaction is predominantly driven by the external surface area, which is strictly dependent on particle size. This originates from the intrinsic advantages of microwave heating, such as rapid, homogeneous volumetric heating and accelerated nucleation, which suppress crystal growth and agglomeration, resulting in finer particles. Due to the low porosity of the analcime framework and the bulky molecular structure of IC, the diffusion of dye molecules into the internal pore system is highly restricted. Consequently, the adsorption and subsequent H2O2 activation occur almost exclusively on the external surface of the particles. This highlights that the degradation process is a surface-mediated reaction, where the efficiency is primarily governed by the availability and reactivity of external active sites rather than the internal surface area of the zeolite. Accordingly, smaller crystals provide a superior surface-to-volume ratio, yielding a higher density of accessible active sites, structural defects, and Lewis acid sites as discussed in the literature. ,, This reduced particle size simultaneously shortens diffusion path lengths for both the reactant dye and the desorbing intermediates while minimizing light scattering to allow for more efficient photon penetration into the suspension. , Due to its higher activity, only the analcime synthesized via the microwave-assisted method was employed in all subsequent experiments.

The linear regression of ln­(C 0/C t ) as a function of irradiation time (Figure c) confirms that the degradation of IC follows pseudo-first-order kinetics across all experimental sets (R 2 > 0.98). Furthermore, a significant synergistic effect was observed among the analcime, the oxidant, and light irradiation. The apparent rate constant of the integrated system, (10.42 ± 0.40) × 10–4 min–1, substantially outperformed the individual or partially combined processes. Specifically, the rate of the complete system was approximately triple that of the dark reaction (Ana-MW/H2O2, (3.81 ± 0.23) × 10–4 min–1) and significantly higher than the rates obtained using Ana-MW alone (1.05 ± 0.24) × 10–4 min–1 or only H2O2 (3.15 ± 0.17) × 10–4 min–1. This synergy underscores the critical role of the analcime surface in the heterogeneous activation of H2O2.

It should be noted that the above results were obtained under the following initial conditions: an IC concentration of 70 ppm, pH 7, an activator dosage of 100 mg, and a H2O2 concentration of 100 mg L–1 in a 200 mL reaction volume. To gain insights into the system’s performance, the effects of critical operational parameters, namely, analcime loading, initial dye concentration, H2O2 dosage, and solution pH, were systematically investigated.

Effect of Key Reaction Parameters on IC Degradation Performance

Effect of Analcime Zeolite Loading

Under neutral pH, the effect of the activator on IC degradation was first evaluated by varying the analcime loading from 100 to 300 mg, while maintaining all other experimental parameters constant, including an initial IC concentration of 70 ppm, a reaction volume of 200 mL, and an H2O2 concentration of 200 mg L–1 (see Table S2, Supporting Information). The corresponding results are presented in Figure a. Increasing the analcime dosage from 100 to 200 mg led to a substantial enhancement in degradation efficiency, rising from 18.3 ± 4.5% to 42.9 ± 3.6%. However, a further increase to 300 mg resulted in a decline in degradation efficiency to 35.6 ± 3.2%. This decrease can be attributed to excessive particle aggregation and self-shading effects at higher loadings, which limit visible-light penetration, reduce effective photon utilization, and hinder the activation of surface-active sites due to optical shielding effects. Consequently, an analcime loading of 200 mg was selected for all subsequent experiments.

Effects of (a) analcime zeolite dosage; (b) initial IC concentration on the visible-light-assisted catalytic degradation performance; and (c) H2O2 concentration.

Effect of Initial Dye Concentration

The influence of initial IC concentration on degradation performance was investigated at 30, 50, and 70 ppm, with the other conditions similar to the previous experiments and summarized in Table S3 (Supporting Information) and the results illustrated in Figure b. The degradation efficiencies decreased from 48.6 ± 4.2% to 32.4 ± 3.5% and 18.3 ± 4.5% as the IC concentration increased from 30 to 50 and 70 ppm, respectively. This trend is attributed to the increased number of dye molecules competing for a limited number of active sites on the analcime surface, as well as for the reactive radical species generated in the system. Furthermore, at higher concentrations, a light screening effect occurs, where the excess dye molecules absorb or scatter the incoming radiation, thereby reducing the photon flux reaching the analcime surface. , In the following experiments, the IC concentration was fixed at 30 ppm.

Influence of H2O2 Concentration

Table S4 (Supporting Information) summarizes the reaction conditions employed to evaluate the influence of H2O2 concentration on IC degradation performance within the analcime/H2O2/light system, with the corresponding results shown in Figure c. As observed, increasing the H2O2 concentration significantly enhanced the degradation of IC, highlighting the crucial role of H2O2 as a precursor for ROS formation. Indeed, raising the H2O2 concentration from 100 to 200 mg L–1 resulted in a pronounced increase in degradation efficiency from 18.3 ± 4.5% to 31.1 ± 4.1%. In contrast, a further increase to 300 mg L–1 led to only a marginal improvement, reaching 34.5 ± 3.4%. The further increase of H2O2 concentration can attribute to the scavenging of hydroxyl radicals by excess H2O2, accompanied by the formation of less reactive species such as hydroperoxyl radicals. ` Thus, the effective oxidative capacity of the system is compromised, resulting in limited additional degradation and unnecessary chemical consumption. Consequently, low H2O2 concentrations were considered for subsequent experiments to minimize radical scavenging effects and ensure cost-effectiveness.

Based on the above results, a set of reaction conditions was selected to ensure observable degradation kinetics while maintaining practical reaction durations. Accordingly, an activator loading of 200 mg per 200 mL of IC solution (30 ppm) and an H2O2 concentration of 100–200 mg L–1 were employed. This setup avoids interferences such as excessive oxidant dosage, particle overcrowding, or severe mass transfer limitations.

Role of pH in IC Degradation Performance

Point of Zero Charge and Surface Charge Behavior of Analcime

To clarify the pH-dependent surface properties of analcime, point-of-zero-charge (pHpzc) measurements were conducted, with results presented in Figure a. The analcime surface exhibits a net positive charge under strongly to moderately acidic conditions and gradually becomes negatively charged as pH increases, yielding a pHpzc value of approximately 5.4. From an electrostatic standpoint, pH values below the pHpzc favor attractive interactions between the positively charged analcime surface and the anionic IC molecules (Figure S1, Supporting Information), thereby enhancing dye adsorption. Conversely, a significant reduction in adsorption capacity is anticipated at higher pH values due to electrostatic repulsion. To validate these effects, control experiments were executed in the dark (Table S5, Supporting Information). As illustrated in Figure b, the adsorption of IC on analcime is strongly governed by pH, highlighting the influential role of electrostatic forces. The adsorption capacity markedly decreases from 14.6 ± 1.5% at pH 3 to only 1.5 ± 0.7% at pH 9, a trend that underscores the shift in the surface charge of analcime. As the pH increases beyond pHpzc, the analcime surface becomes increasingly negatively charged, exerting a strong repulsive force against the anionic IC species. However, this limited adsorption capacity does not necessarily affect low IC removal efficiency. Rather, the overall degradation performance is governed by several other critical factors, including suspension stability, photonic efficiency, and the rapid generation of reactive species at the solid–liquid interface. −

Effect of Initial Solution pH on Visible-Light-Assisted IC Degradation Performance of the Analcime/H2O2 System

To demonstrate that IC removal is not merely a function of electrostatic interaction, experiments using the analcime/H2O2/light system were conducted at pH 9, where electrostatic interactions are minimal. Control tests were performed using 100 mg L–1 H2O2, the other conditions specified in the caption of Figure . As illustrated in Figure a (and summarized in Table S6, Supporting Information), control experiments confirmed that IC remains highly stable in an alkaline environment, with a negligible self-degradation of only 3.5 ± 0.3%. Furthermore, the addition of H2O2 alone resulted in a moderate decolorization of 35.1 ± 3.3% in the dark and 37.8 ± 2.1% under light irradiation after 210 min, suggesting that direct photolysis of the oxidant is limited. The addition of analcime/H2O2 in the dark led to a small increase (41.2 ± 2.7%), confirming that neither analcime’s adsorption nor direct oxidation by H2O2 is the primary driver of the reaction. In contrast, a pronounced synergistic effect was observed under visible-light irradiation. Even at this relatively low H2O2 dosage, the overall degradation efficiency reached 84.6 ± 3.7%. These findings provide evidence of the potent synergy between visible light, the analcime activator, and H2O2 within an alkaline medium, highlighting a light-dependent activation mechanism. Notably, the integration of the analcime activator into the H2O2/light system under the studied conditions resulted in a more than 105% increase in degradation efficiency compared to the analcime/H2O2/dark process alone, clearly demonstrating the potent synergistic effect of the activator under visible light.

To further verify the degradation kinetics as a function of oxidant dosage, experiments with a doubled H2O2 concentration (200 mg L–1) were executed across a wide pH range. As presented in Figure b and Table S7, the degradation at pH 9 with this oxidant level exhibited exceptionally rapid kinetics, achieving 50.3 ± 3.3% removal within only 10 min and reaching 95.8 ± 2.9% after 30 min. The high degradation efficiency within a short reaction time frame is of significant technical importance. By minimizing the nonproductive decomposition of H2O2 into O2 over extended operational periods, the system ensures that the oxidant is preferentially channeled toward the generation of ROS, thereby enhancing overall process efficiency. In contrast, acidic media remained inefficient, reaching only 22.9 ± 2.6% after 210 min, despite the enhanced adsorption confirmed by the intensified blue coloration of the analcime particles (Figure S2, Supporting Information). This discrepancy is attributed to the significant physical transformation of the system. As seen in Figure S2, while acidic conditions induced severe particle aggregation that hindered light penetration and restricted active site accessibility, alkaline conditions promoted a favorable environment for the uniform dispersion of analcime particles. Although neutral pH eventually attained a substantial final efficiency (82.5 ± 3.7%), it was characterized by significantly slower initial kinetics compared to the alkaline medium. Ultimately, the superior performance at pH 9 is governed by a synergistic interplay between improved activator dispersion (enhancing both mass transfer and photonic efficiency) and the activation of a more effective radical-driven oxidation pathway. The dye degradation efficiency of the analcime/H2O2/light system in this study reached a high level (∼95%), which is comparable to the performance (85–95%) of traditional photocatalysts like TiO2 or g-C3N4 reported by Ahmad et al. Even though TiO2 and carbon-based semiconductors are promising, they are often hindered by the rapid recombination of photogenerated charge carriers, a significant drawback. Furthermore, these semiconductor systems require strict pH control or acidification to prevent activity loss or material instability.

Under pH 9, the degradation kinetics of IC yields a normalized rate constant k norm of 0.0881 min–1 g–1 L (detailed in Table S8, Supporting Information). Remarkably, the k norm value of the analcime/H2O2/light system is comparable to that of commercial TiO2 P25 under UV irradiation (k norm = 0.0922 min–1 g–1 L reported by Ortiz et al.). While most AOPs require neutral or acidic conditions, analcime maintains superior efficiency at pH 9.0, which is highly compatible with the alkaline nature of real textile effluents. Furthermore, the use of rice husk ash as a precursor offers a cost-effective and sustainable green alternative to expensive commercial catalysts, providing a metal-free solution that eliminates the risk of secondary pollution.

Role of Reactive Oxidative Species under Alkaline Conditions

To elucidate the dominant oxidative pathways responsible for the enhanced catalytic activity at pH 9, the generation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) was directly verified through a site-specific fluorogenic reaction. Indeed, TA was employed as a sensitive probe molecule to capture •OH species. As depicted in Figure a, the characteristic emission peak of 2-hydroxyterephthalic acid (the specific oxidation product resulting from the reaction between TA and •OH) emerges at 425 nm (under 315 nm excitation). PL analysis reveals rapid generation of •OH radicals initiated by the analcime surface. According to the reaction pathways proposed by Buxton et al., under alkaline conditions, superoxide radical anions (•O2 –) can also play a role alongside hydroxyl radicals •OH. To validate their respective contributions to the degradation process, radical scavenging assays were conducted, as illustrated in Figure b (with comprehensive data provided in Table S9, Supporting Information). TA and benzoquinone were employed as selective scavengers for •OH and •O2 –, respectively, following established protocols. , In the absence of scavengers, the analcime/H2O2 activation system achieved approximately 95% removal of IC within 30 min under visible-light irradiation, demonstrating the efficient generation of ROS under mildly alkaline conditions. Upon the introduction of these scavengers, marked suppressions in degradation efficiency were observed. Notably, the addition of TA resulted in the most profound inhibition, reducing the degradation efficiency to 54.5 ± 5.5%, whereas the addition of benzoquinone resulted in a decrease to 61.5 ± 3.6%. These results confirm that while •O2 – provides a significant synergistic effect, •OH radicals serve as the primary oxidative drivers in this system. This further validates that the analcime surface effectively functions as an effective activator, facilitating the decomposition of H2O2 into a cascade of ROS, with the •OH pathway being the dominant mechanism for the oxidative cleavage of the IC molecule.

When compared with the pristine material (Figure b), the recovered sample exhibits a high degree of spectral overlap, indicating that the reused material retains its structural integrity after the reaction. This close similarity confirms the stability of the crystalline framework. Moreover, the FTIR spectrum of the analcime–H2O2 system provides insights into the possible reaction mechanism under light irradiation.

(a) FTIR spectra of analcime interacted with H2O2 and recovered analcime after five cycles and (b) Py-FTIR spectra of hydrothermal and microwave-assisted analcime recorded after saturated pyridine adsorption at room temperature and subsequent desorption at 150 °C (20 mg self-supporting wafers).

The heterogeneous mechanism of H2O2 activation was elucidated by monitoring the structural evolution of the analcime surface via FTIR spectroscopy. The visible-light-assisted activation of H2O2 over the metal-free analcime framework at pH 9.0 is a surface-mediated process involving the coexistence of molecular H2O2 and hydroperoxide anions (HOO–). Given the weak acidity of hydrogen peroxide, the alkaline environment initiates its deprotonation into HOO–. As noted by Karamoschos and Tasis, these HOO– species exhibit significantly higher molar absorptivity at longer wavelengths than neutral H2O2, thereby enhancing their responsiveness to visible light. Both peroxide species can migrate to the analcime interface, forming an extensive intermolecular hydrogen-bonding network with surface silanol groups (Si–OH). ,, The emergence of a prominent, broad absorption band at 3232 cm–1 signifies a substantial bathochromic shift (redshift) from the typical free silanol vibrations, which are generally observed in the 3740–3750 cm–1 region. This significant redshift confirms the formation of robust intermolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions between the adsorbed peroxide species and the analcime framework. Such interfacial interactions effectively trap and preconcentrate the oxidant at the zeolite–liquid boundary, inducing a “surface enrichment” effect. As previously discussed by Wang and Peng, this phenomenon ensures an elevated local concentration of reactive species at the active sites, thereby overcoming the kinetic constraints typically associated with diffusion-limited, bulk-phase reactions. These adsorbed species subsequently coordinate with framework Lewis acid sites (Al3+) to form a surface complex transfer center (SCTC). , This complexation is evidenced by the specific vibrational signature at 1358 cm–1. Thus, this electronic interaction induces significant tensile strain on the O–O bond, substantially lowering its dissociation energy and facilitating facile homolytic cleavage upon the absorption of white light photons. ,

The preactivated O–O bond undergoes cleavage through two synergistic pathways: (i) photolytic cleavage, where visible-light absorption by the SCTC triggers the homolytic cleavage of the O–O bond to form reactive •OH radicals, ,,, and (ii) heterogeneous Haber–Weiss-like interaction, where the “surface enrichment” of peroxides facilitates a bimolecular interaction in which nucleophilic HOO– ions attack the weakened O–O bonds of adjacent adsorbed H2O2 molecules. This interfacial process results in the intensive generation of •OH and •O2 – radicals. ,,, This dual-species mechanism justifies the high efficiency at pH 9.0, providing an optimal synergy between the oxidant H2O2 and the potent nucleophilic activator HOO–. An analysis was conducted to elucidate the roles of different acid sites via pyridine-adsorbed FTIR (Py-FTIR), with the results illustrated in Figure b. The diagnostic bands at 1545 and 1454 cm–1 confirm the presence of Brønsted and Lewis acid sites, respectively. These active centers are instrumental in forming the hydrogen-bonding network and intermediate complexes discussed above. The data reveals a predominance of Lewis acid sites, consistent with the high Si/Al ratio inherent in the analcime framework. Notably, the microwave-assisted sample exhibited a 51% increase in the peak area of Lewis acid sites compared to its hydrothermal counterpart, providing experimental evidence for the significantly enhanced H2O2 activation activity of the microwave-synthesized material.

Further investigation was conducted to validate the role of structural defects on the material’s performance. As illustrated in the SEM images (Figure ), the synthesized analcime manifests as well-defined, highly crystalline polyhedral featuring sharp edges and smooth facets. The high structural integrity of these crystals, characterized by the absence of mechanical fractures or grain fragmentation, suggests that surface defects arising from crystal breakage are negligible. Stability Evaluation of Analcime Zeolite over Multiple IC Degradation Cycles

To evaluate the reusability and stability of the analcime zeolite, two parallel sets of visible-light-assisted IC degradation cycles were performed. In the first approach, designed to simulate continuous treatment by evaluating accumulation-induced effects in the reaction medium, the suspension was heated at 80 °C for 2 h between cycles to decompose residual H2O2, followed by volume compensation with distilled water and the replenishment of IC in solid form. In contrast, a second series of experiments were conducted using fresh IC solutions for each cycle, where the analcime catalyst was recovered by centrifugation and thermally regenerated at 450 °C for 4 h before reuse. For both protocols, each subsequent cycle was preceded by 60 min of dark stirring to re-establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium, and the comparative performance results are illustrated in Figure .

Reusability of analcime zeolite over five consecutive cycles under two conditions: (a) cumulative reaction matrix (80 °C intermediate heating) and (b) thermal regeneration (450 °C calcination).

In the sequential cycling series, the degradation efficiency exhibited only a minor decline between individual runs, with a cumulative loss of less than 2% by the final cycle. In comparison, the series using analcime regenerated at 450 °C showed an even more negligible decrease, limited to under 1.2%. This sustained performance demonstrates that analcime maintains high H2O2 activation throughout the validated experiments, suggesting that the accumulation of reaction byproducts on the surface is insignificant.

This conclusion is further supported by postreaction XRD analysis of the material recovered after five consecutive cycles (Figure a), which showed no detectable changes in diffraction patterns compared to the fresh sample, confirming that the crystalline framework of the analcime remains entirely intact. Such a minimal activity loss (Figure ) underscores the excellent structural stability of the zeolite lattice and the robust durability of its active sites. ,, While textural analysis revealed limited intrinsic porosity, this dense and compact architecture serves as a decisive factor in enhancing the mechanical and structural integrity of the particles, ensuring effectiveness over multiple cycles.

Furthermore, the highly oxidative environment generated in the analcime/H2O2 system likely inhibits the accumulation of reaction intermediates that could otherwise foul the surface. , Consequently, surface passivation is effectively suppressed, allowing the activator to retain its functionality without the rapid deactivation typical of conventional metal-based Fenton catalysts, which often suffer from iron leaching. Additionally, the ability of this analcime-based system to operate efficiently under alkaline conditions represents a significant advantage for textile wastewater treatment, as these effluents inherently exhibit high pH levels. By eliminating the preliminary acidification required by traditional Fenton processes, this system simplifies operations, reduces chemical consumption, and enhances overall process sustainability. Moreover, the introduction of Cl– and CO3 2– (1000 ppm) to enhance the complexity of the simulated matrices (Table S10, Supporting Information) did not hinder IC degradation performance; the material maintained an efficiency exceeding 95% within 30 min. This robust performance in the presence of competing ions underscores the material’s potential for textile wastewater treatment. Nevertheless, further optimization of industrial processing parameters remains essential to ensure feasibility for full-scale implementation.

Evaluation of Mineralization Efficiency by TOC Analysis

The mineralization of IC was evaluated through the evolution of TOC under visible-light-assisted H2O2 activation over the analcime zeolite, as illustrated in Figure . A continuous decline in IC concentration was observed as the reaction progressed, reflecting efficient decolorization of the dye molecules. Specifically, the IC concentration dropped to 3.2 ± 0.3, 1.9 ± 0.6, and 1.7 ± 0.7 ppm after 20, 50, and 70 min of irradiation, respectively. Concurrently, the TOC content exhibited a steady decrease, confirming the progressive degradation of organic carbon within the system. At these corresponding intervals, TOC values reached 3.6 ± 0.9, 1.8 ± 0.3, and 1.5 ± 0.5 mg L–1. The closely aligned reduction trends between IC concentration and TOC indicate that decolorization is accompanied by a significant degree of mineralization. Notably, during the initial stages, IC decolorization proceeded slightly faster than TOC removal. This suggests that the early degradation phase predominantly involves the cleavage of chromophoric structures, while organic intermediates remain temporarily in the bulk solution. Such behavior is characteristic of AOPs, where the disruption of color-bearing functional groups precedes the complete oxidative breakdown of the molecular backbone. With extended irradiation, the sustained TOC decline demonstrates that these intermediate species are further oxidized into smaller fragments and eventually mineralized into CO2 and H2O. , Overall, these findings highlight that the analcime/H2O2 system not only achieves rapid decolorization but also promotes effective mineralization, positioning analcime as a high-performance and environmentally compatible activator for the treatment of dye-contaminated wastewater.

Conclusions

This study successfully demonstrates that analcime zeolite synthesized from rice husk ash is an efficient and stable activator for visible-light-assisted H2O2 activation. While the material exhibits limited intrinsic photocatalytic activity, a pronounced synergistic effect between the analcime framework, H2O2, and visible-light irradiation significantly enhances dye removal, particularly in alkaline media. The microwave-assisted route proved superior to the conventional hydrothermal method, yielding crystals with refined particle size and a 51% increase in Lewis acid site density, which were crucial for the formation of surface complex transfer centers (SCTC). The degradation efficiency was strongly dependent on operational parameters, with a removal of approximately 95.8% achieved at pH 9 within 30 min. Radical scavenging experiments confirmed that hydroxyl radicals (•OH) are the predominant reactive species, with contributions from superoxide radicals (•O2 –). TOC analysis and five-cycle reusability tests further validated the progressive mineralization and structural robustness of the activator. Crucially, this work serves as a prime example of utilizing interfacial engineering on a waste-derived material platform to achieve optimal performance for specific applications. By precisely controlling the surface-active sites and structural integrity, we have developed a case-specific solution that overcomes the traditional bottlenecks of low surface area and metal-free limitations. These findings highlight the potential of rice-husk-derived analcime as a sustainable, transition metal-free, and environmentally benign H2O2 activator, offering a promising inspiration for the future design of low-cost, high-efficiency AOPs. Future efforts will focus on extending this interfacial engineering strategy to high-silica zeolites (e.g., ZSM-5, Beta) and developing continuous-flow systems integrated with machine leaning-based optimization for practical wastewater treatment.
