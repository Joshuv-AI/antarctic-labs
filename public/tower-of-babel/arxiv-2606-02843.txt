Abstract
The classical no- k k -in-line problem asks for the largest number of points that can be placed on an n × n n\times n grid without having k k of them collinear. A natural extension, motivated by the analogous question by Erde for k ∈ ℤ k\in\mathbb{Z} , is the extensible no- ( k ⁡ ( n ) + 1 ) (k(n)+1) -in-line problem , which seeks a subset of points in ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2} with maximal possible density such that at most k ⁡ ( n ) k(n) points are collinear within the subgrid [ 1 , n ] 2 [1,n]^{2} . We construct optimal sets for linear functions and positive-density sets for power functions. We prove that any configuration achieving lim inf S n n ​ k ​ ( n ) ≥ 0.897 \liminf\frac{S_{n}}{nk(n)}\geq 0.897 must satisfy k ⁡ ( n ) = Ω ⁡ ( n c ) k(n)=\Omega(n^{c}) for some c > 0 c>0 constant; therefore, the extensible no- k k -in-line problem has no configuration with this property when k k is a constant. Finally, we reduce the problem to the extensible no- k k -in-line problem, showing that if a positive-density point-set exists for a constant limiter function, then one also exists for any sufficiently regular function k ⁡ ( n ) k(n) .

Introduction

The
no-three-in-line
problem asks about the largest number of points that can be placed in a square lattice of size
n\times n
without three of them lying on the same line. It is one of the oldest and most studied problems concerning lattice configurations. The question was introduced by Dudeney in 1900 for the case

]

. A trivial upper bound for the number of placeable points is

, by the pigeonhole principle; placing

n=56
and for
n=58
, a construction containing
2n
points is known from the work of Flammenkamp and Prellberg
[
1
]
. The best known lower bound of
1.5n(1-o(1))
was established by Hall, Jackson, Sudbery, and Wild
[
9
]
. However, there is a lack of consensus on the asymptotic number of placeable points,
1.5n,\left(\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}n\approx 1.87n
and
2n
are all among the suggested values
[
7
,

,

]
.

A natural generalization of the previous problem is the
no-(
k+1
)-in-line
problem, which permits at most
k
points per line. The trivial upper bound in this case is
kn
by similar arguments. This problem was solved very recently by Kovács, Nagy and Szabó
[
10

who proved the existence of a construction of size

using probabilistic and combinatorial techniques. Based on this construction, Grebennikov and Kwan
[

subsequently improved the bound on

.

Let

denote the set of lattice points in the square with vertices
(1,1),(1,n),(n,1),(n,n)
.

Erde introduced the
extensible no-three-in-line
problem at the Third Southwestern German Workshop
on Graph Theory. The problem asks whether

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|S\cap[1,n]^{2}\right|}{n}=0

holds for every set
S\subset\mathbb{N}^{2}
of points in general position. Nagy, Nagy and Woodroofe
[

]
proved that there exists a set
S
of points in general position satisfying

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|S\cap[1,n]^{2}\right|}{n/\log^{1+\varepsilon}n}>0.

This was improved recently by Ghosal by a
\sqrt{\log n}
factor to
\Omega(n/\sqrt{\log n})

[
5
]
.
Nagy, Nagy and Woodroofe also provided computational evidence for values up to
n=10000
, suggesting the existence of a point set
S
, satisfying

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|S\cap[1,n]^{2}\right|}{n}>0.8.

In this article, we examine the
extensible no-
(k(n)+1)
-in-line problem
. This problem resembles the extensible-no-
k
-in-line, however, instead of a constant
k

by

, a monotone increasing function of

Definition 1.1

(Density)
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{+}\times\mathbb{N}^{+}
be a set of lattice points
The density of the point set is defined as:

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|S\cap[1,n]^{2}\right|}{k(n)n}.

Problem 1.2

(Main problem)
.

Find the largest achievable density defined in Definition
1.1
given that no line admits more than
k(n)
points in
[1,n]^{2}
.

Remark 1.3
.

We assume that

is monotone without loss of generality, as any non-monotonic

can be replaced by
k^{\prime}(n)=\inf_{m>n}k(m)
without changing the set of valid configurations.

In Section
2
, we investigate the case of linear
k(n)
and prove the following theorem:

.

Let
k(n)=c\cdot n
for some
0<c<1
. Then there exists a set of points
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{+}\times\mathbb{N}^{+}
such that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{|S\cap[1,n]^{2}|}{k(n)\cdot n}=1

and each line contains at most
k(n)
points in
S\cap[1,n]^{2}
.

We further verify that if
k(n)=cn^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\leq 1
and constant

, then a positive lower bound is achievable, meaning the limit inferior is strictly positive.

Theorem 1.5
.

Suppose that
k(n)=cn^{\alpha}
for some
0<\alpha\leq 1
,
c\in\mathbb{R^{+}}
. Then, there exists a set
S
satisfying

\liminf\frac{|S\cap[1,n]^{2}|}{k(n)\cdot n}>0

and no
k(n)+1
points are collinear within

for all

.

In Section
3
, we prove that
\liminf
values close to

Theorem 1.6
.

Any configuration satisfying

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|S\cap[1,n]^{2}\right|}{nk(n)}\geq 0.897

must satisfy
k(n)=\Omega(n^{c})
, where

As
k(n)\equiv k\notin\Omega(n^{c})
for any constant

, this result provides an upper bound for Erde’s problem:
\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|S\cap[1,n]^{2}\right|}{2n}<0.897
. Computational evidence of Nagy, Nagy and Woodroofe suggests that
\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|S\cap[1,n]^{2}\right|}{n}>0.8
, resulting in
\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|S\cap[1,n]^{2}\right|}{2n}>0.4
in our case, leaving a significant gap between the theoretical and experimental results. By generalizing the technique used in the proof, we show that if a construction has density 1, then
k(n)=\Omega(n^{c})
for any constant
c<1
.

In Section 4, we establish a direct connection between the classical extensible no-three-in-line problem and our generalized no-
(k(n)+1)
-in-line variant. We introduce the concept of
sublinear limiter functions
to formalize the notion of a sufficiently regular function, and prove that a positive-density solution to Erde’s original question yields a universal lower bound for these limiters. Specifically, our results show that a positive answer to Erde’s question for
k=2
would imply the existence of a positive-density set for any sublinear limiter
k(n)
. This reduces the generalized problem to the classical case, reinforcing the suggestion that the difficulty of this problem increases as the growth rate of
k(n)
decreases.

4
Extending the solution for the extensible no-3-in-Line problem to sublinear limiters

In this section, we will prove that if there exists a point-set
S
that has a positive density for the no-3-in-line problem, then for every sufficiently regular function
k(n)
including
k(n)=cn^{\alpha}
and

that has a positive density for the no-

-in-line problem.

First, we define a family of functions whose members have suitable properties to be used as extensible limiter functions and have not been discussed in the previous chapters.

Definition 4.1

(Sublinear limiter)
.

We call
k(n)
a sublinear limiter function if it satisfies the following properties:

(1)

k(n)=o(n)

(2)

k
is unbounded

(3)

k
is monotonically increasing

(4)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}2k(i)}{nk(n)}\geq 1

(5)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{k(\lfloor\lambda n\rfloor)}{k(n)}\geq\lambda
holds for all
\lambda\in[0,1]

Remark 4.2
.

These properties arise naturally when defining sublinear limiter functions, this remark explains shortly why each point of the definition is relevant.

(1)

The case
k(n)=cn
was fully discussed in Section
2
and that construction gives a positive density solution for every

. We only consider functions with

in this section because our goal is a positive density set for

.

(3)

Given a function

limiter function. Indeed, if the maximum number of points allowed on a line in

is smaller than in
[1,n]^{2}
for some

is an indirect upper bound on the possible number of points on a line in

as well.

(2)

If
k(n)
is bounded by a constant then the problem is essentially the same as
k(n)=k
. A bounded, monotone natural valued function is constant from some point

and the studied
\liminf\frac{|S\cap[1,n]^{2}|}{nk(n)}
will not be different if we delete the points from

as we decreased the numerator by a constant and the denominator goes to infinity. We will construct a positive density set for
k(n)
from a positive density set for

which is also a positive density set for

(4)

If the left hand side is smaller than 1, we immediately get an upper bound for the largest possible

(5)

We exclude functions with large jumps including those constructed in the proof of Theorem

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{H}{4}\frac{k(\lfloor Hn\rfloor)}{k(n)}\geq\frac{H}{4}
