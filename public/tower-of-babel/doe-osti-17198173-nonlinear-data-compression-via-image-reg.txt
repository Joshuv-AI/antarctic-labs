ABSTRACT
This report details novel registration methods for the alignment of simulation outputs in bounded
domains for the purpose of data compression. The downstream application of interest is model
reduction, wherein data is used to train a cheaper, predictive approximation of a high-fidelity model.
Many of these methods depend on the compression of the simulation data into a low-dimensional
reduced basis. Registration methods learn a smooth, invertible domain deformation that can be
used to align disparate simulation fields. In the context of data compression, solution fields that
exhibits localized, moving features may be more amenable to reduced representations after being
aligned through a registration technique. A key challenge is the development of methods that can
align data in bounded domains without deforming the shape of the domain. The first approach uses
radial basis functions to build the mapping, with a novel hierarchical and composed strategy to
preserve boundaries and prevent against invalid mappings. The second approach builds the map by
integrating along a vector flow, using a constrained kernel to restrict the set of admissible mappings.
The methods are demonstrated on 2D and 3D transient problems and steady aerodynamic simulation
data on curved and non-convex domains.

1. INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

While modeling and simulation capabilities have continued to scale up and mature, many of the
computational engineering tasks of relevance to analysts at the lab require multiple simulations of a
partial differential equation (PDE). Optimizing the design of a high-speed vehicle, quantifying the
uncertainty of constitutive model parameters, assimilating real-world experimental measurements
into simulation data, among other tasks, all require repeated queries of a simulation code. The value
of approximate surrogate models, which sacrifice physical fidelity for more rapid evaluations, has
long been appreciated as a means to accelerate and enable these so-called outer-loop workflows.
Models that consider a reduced or simplified set of physics are an interpretable and verifiable
means of obtaining faster computational models, but these can become challenging to maintain
and extend. Data-driven methods, using well-selected high-fidelity simulation outputs to construct
efficient surrogate models, are an appealing alternative approach due to their relative lack of human
intervention and their synergy with recent developments in data processing and AI. There are a wide
variety of such methods, from projection-based reduced-order models (pROMs) to nonintrusive,
purely data-driven surrogates [2]. Many of them share a common core step: after collecting training
data consisting of simulation snapshots, there is an attempt to extract a low-dimensional reduced
basis (RB) that can represent the solution manifold of the PDE. Classical methods that learn a
linear approximating subspace, such as the proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) [30], have
had remarkable success. They have been used to construct efficient ROMs for a wide swath of
problems, including for challenging and contemporary Sandia exemplars [28].
There is a relevant regime where these classical methods struggle: simulation fields with moving,
locally supported features. A simulation that takes an initial pulse and sends it across the domain
with a fixed velocity can already cause difficulties. This challenge is realized in practice often
by the presence of moving shocks, such as in hypersonic flow over a trajectory that changes bow
shock shape and location, or during detonation simulations over a large time range. This challenge
is formalized by the presence of a large Kolmogorov N-Width (KNW), which reflects the ability
of linear bases to approximate a solution manifold. This has led to great interest in developing
nonlinear methods for ROMs.
As an actively developing field, there are a wide range of techniques. These include the use of
adaptive linear bases, the addition of nonlinear closure terms to linear bases, and fully nonlinear
methods. We focus on a class of methods recently termed transformation-based methods, which
attempt to transform the simulation data so that it is more amenable to linear methods [10]. For
example, a spatial deformation may be constructed that moves a time- or parameter-varying shock
to a stationary position. In particular, we will focus on registration methods. These methods,
taking inspiration from image processing techniques, attempt to learn smooth, invertible spatial
mappings purely from snapshot data [32]. Their physics-agnostic nature diversifies their potential
impact, and they have been used to construct some of the most accurate and data-efficient ROMs

 11

for challenging and relevant exemplars such as 3D high-speed aerodynamics [26, 18] and complex
time-dependent conservation laws [23].
The key question posed in the LDRD proposal was the following: can registration methods can be
made scalable and reliable enough to be applied in an unsupervised fashion to Sandia exemplars?
The implicit mathematical gap in the literature precluding the use of existing methods is their
restrictions on computational domain shape. The original methods developed in [32, 33], were
restricted to rectangular-like domains, due to their use of tensorized polynomials to represent the
mapping. Two of the state-of-the art mapping-based methods previously mentioned, also use
tensorized splines and therefore require (sub-)domains that are isomorphic to a cube [26, 23].
Recent efforts have made strides in this concern. The elasticity-based methods of [16] manually
impose displacements that can align solution fields while holding domain boundaries in place or
deforming them. The ROMs with implicit feature tracking (ROM-IFT) method is amenable to
a general class of mappings, but early demonstrations were built off of mappings extracted from
a specialized shock tracking schemes [20, 21]. More recently, this approach was coupled with
a routine based on radial basis function (RBF) mesh deformation, using a small set of discrete
landmarks to represent shock structures and parametrize the mesh motion [39]. Taking a different
approach, the vector-flow based method of imposed tangential motion constraints in a specialized
finite element discretization of a velocity field whose induced motion achieves alignment [17, 18].
These last two methods represent the state-of-the-art of the two classes of methods recently formal-
ized in [12], which gives the mathematical requirements for constructing registration methods that
preserve general domain boundaries. It presents two different approaches: one where the alignment
is directly parametrized in a basis (displacement-based methods) and the other where alignment
is achieved by integrating along a learned velocity field (vector flows). This project attempted
to build off the state-of-the-art by considering both such approaches, incorporating novelties to
keep the methods scalable and easy to interface with. We develop two novel approaches that are
shown to be capable of robustly aligning simulation data over three-dimensional and unstructured
geometries.

2. RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT METHODOLOGY

We considered two approaches to construct the aligning mappings. The first thrust directly repre-
sented the mappings with radial basis functions (RBFs), requiring novel developments for three-
dimensional geometries and ensuring that the resulting maps remained diffeomorphic. The second
method instead induces mappings with flows along learned vector flows, akin to Large Defor-
mation Diffeomorphic Metric Mapping (LDDMM) methods developed in the image processing
community.

2.2. Hierarchial and composed radial basis functions

We introduce two methods for aligning PDE solution fields with RBFs. The first, based on the
alignment of a sparse set of landmarks, borrows heavily from the method in [39]. The second
method is a reparametrization of the strategy introduced in [23], which used piecewise cubic
Hermite interpolating polynomials (PCHIPs) to align solution fields rather than RBFs. Although
different formulations more well-suited for different classes of problems, they share common
approaches for promoting invertibility and boundary preservation.

RBFs are capable of preserving domain boundaries, but they must have sufficient control point
coverage on the boundary. Furthermore, these boundary control points mut have corresponding
displacements that are only allowed to slide tangentially along the boundary, which is nontrivial for
curved and intersecting domains.
Additionally, there is no guarantee that the RBF mesh mappings are diffeomorophic—we require that
the mapping is invertible and that its inverse is continuous. Without this guarantee, mesh tangling
can occur, which will limit the applicability of these mappings to downstream workflows. This
weakness of RBFs was raised in early works on PDE field registration [32]; although diffeomorphic
mappings can be promoted with moderate deformations, sufficient control points, and the correct
choice of hyperparameters, we will strive in this work to have a method that automatically guards
against loss of map invertibility.

2.2.5. Preserving invertibility with penalties and restaging

Registration methods protect from noninvertible mappings by using parametrizations that remain
provably diffeomorphic, or by using penalty terms that discourage mesh entanglement.
The penalties can easily be incorporated into both our sparse and dense approaches, by regularizing
the optimization statements in (2.6) or (2.9). As the invertibility of the mapping is reflected in its
effect on a computational grid, we use the quality of a deformed computational mesh to inspect map
quality. A folded mesh is a sign of an invalid map and can be measured by inspecting the determinant
of the Jacobian of the deformed mesh. When using RBFs, the Jacobian of the transformation, J is
available analytically. We can then add a barrier penalty term to our optimization statements that
blows up if det J goes below a threshold 𝜀 at any point in the domain

Each method will be tested on a combination of synthetic and numerical simulation data. Simulation

of the implicit goals of this project; these methods aim to be physics-agnostic and easily couplable
with a variety of data sources.

In each case, the following metrics will be considered. To determine alignment effectiveness, we
can consider the error metric

4. ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS

4.1. Distribution and next steps

This work has been presented at internal and external seminars. Overviews of both methods and a
subset of these results were presented at the 2026 World Conference for Computational Mechanics.
These results have also been accepted for presentation at the upcoming SIAM Texas-Louisiana
Sectional Meeting 2026.
The abbreviated timeline has led to no submitted publications at this time, but two are in the works.
The first one, focusing on the RBF work, will discuss techniques needed to make RBFs capable
of robustly aligning simulation data in a parametrized context. We intend to include sparse and
dense RBFs, comparing and contrasting the costs and techniques for preserving invertibility and
generalizability to three-dimensions. The other publication will cover the vector flow techniques.
This will include proofs of the boundary-preserving identities, explorations of low-rank techniques
for the boundary inverse operators, and examples on other hypersonic examples.
We will have the bandwidth to finish this work as it has been included in an accepted Grand
Challenge LDRD. This work will require rapid evaluation of hypersonic flow fields with changing
shock patterns. Existing methods for this capability require specific geometries and grids; we
expect that the methods developed in this LDRD will be able to be used for this purpose. While
the RBF method was originally chosen to interface with 3D geometries, we believe that the ease of
implementing the vector flow method will make it the preferred approach for these problems. After
aligning a training set of simulation data with a reference snapshot, we will use POD methods to
extract reduced bases for the solution field and the mapping. Then we will use basis coefficient
interpolation to get easy-to-implement ROMs that capture the shock structures well. We have
experience implementing these approaches, so the main barrier will be in getting a mapping that
preserves the boundary, avoids folds, and aligns the shocks. This LDRD has made significant
progress towards this goal. Closing this loop on the ROM construction, as well as confronting
any additional difficulties encountered with sharp 3D hypersonic geometries, will comprise the
immediate next steps. A combination of RBF and vector flow methods might also be suitable for
including shock alignment and domain deformation; as the vector flow precomputations assume a
fixed domains, RBFs could be used in their more traditional role of deforming the domain.
The methods in this proposal were also used by summer Graduate Intern Mike Ackermann to
complete an intrusive ROM for time-dependent conservation laws with shocks. Previous work
from this group had developed a mapping method that was restricted to 1D linear advection
problems. The registration approaches in this work were able to plug into his method and enable a
ROM that could align more general structures, creating online efficient ROMs that captures shock
positions and widths with high accuracy. While this work was funded under the ASCR ASCEND

 37

project, it shows the potential future for these LDRD results: with methods that can operate on
relevant data and geometries, we hope to couple this work to other existing efforts at the labs.

4.2. Potential impact

What might these projects look like? While the following is speculative, here are some potential
future R&D applications that might stem from this work:

 • Integrate mappings with pROM ecosystem: By incorporating these mappings into the
 greater Pressio ecosystem, we could enable the use of mapped projection-based ROMs;
 similar techniques, such as the grid-tailored ROMs of [4], have proven to be very effective for
 parametrized hypersonic flows, but their current implementation is limited to certain grids
 and geometries. The methods developed in this LDRD, if coupled with Pressio, would be
 able to extend this to more general and mission-relevant geometries. Furthermore, this would
 allow further development of the mapped L1 ROMs developed under ASCEND.
 • Mapped scientific machine learning: Nonintrusive and purely data-driven methods have
 grown increasingly popular due to their ease of coupling with simulation workflows. Prior
 work has shown that incorporating similar mapped methods inside a physics-informed neural
 network (PINN) framework greatly improved predictive capability [22]. Similarly, aligning
 operations have shown to increase the capability of operator inference (OpInf) [14] and
 symbolic methods [19] to model shock-dominated flows. There is an existing gap in seeing
 whether these early studies continue to show benefits for the more contemporary data-driven
 methods used today. The presence of robust and efficient maps would allow such studies.
 • Novel applications: These methods have been primarily used in the fluid dynamics commu-
 nity, but we have made no assumptions on the physics. A particularly compelling use-case
 would be simulations with level sets of some kind, like wildfire simulations or multiphase
 flows. The jumps in level-set functions can be discontinuous and complicate outer-loop work-
 flows; these mappings may be useful in discovering low rank structures in such simulations.
 • Novel outer-loops: We have primarily been operating in the context of reduced-order mod-
 eling, but aligning deformations may have other uses. There are existing works on the
 benefit of problem-specific solution mappings to enable data assimilation and adjoint-based
 PDE-constrained optimization for problems with shocks [24, 29, 34]. The data-driven maps
 developed in this LDRD may be a means for us to explore similar methods on larger-scale
 problems.

5. CONCLUSIONS

This project developed approaches for the alignment of PDE solution fields in bounded domains.
These registration methods are a first step to incorporating transformation-based ROMs into more
varied and realistic exemplars. Two approaches were considered. These methods shared the
challenges of building invertible mappings that allow boundary-tangential motion without changing
the shape of the domain.
The first method tools from radial basis function (RBF) mesh deformation. This required a
hierarchical approach for building boundary-conforming mappings, while composed maps and
penalized RBF fits were used to promote map invertibility. The vector flow method used a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) with a kernel constructed to only allow tangential motion
at the boundaries. The RBF methods were used to align time-dependent solution fields in 2D and
3D domains, and the vector flow method was applied to steady-state problems over domains with
curved boundaries and sharp corners.
The RBF methods built around sparse landmark registration are extremely fast but are limited in
the solution fields they can align. For fields with intersecting features, specialized feature detection
methods are needed to generate meaningful sparse landmarks. The RBF methods that minimize a
field alignment objective can match more complex patterns, but are currently too inefficient to be
applied to 3D unsteady problems. Either approach requires an automation of the boundary element
extraction to be applied to more complex geometries.
The vector flow method has an integration advantage in that it only requires boundary points and
normal vectors to construct the correct motions. The method is also mesh-free and does not depend
on the underlying discretization. Similar to the sparse landmark methods however, the success
of the method depends heavily on the extraction of meaningful point clouds. The optimization
problem is also not trivial to solve, and specialized algorithms may be required as we extend to 3D
and more challenging problems.
Future work will evaluate the usefulness of these methods in downstream reduced order modeling
applications. The utility of these mappings for data compression were observed in the decay of
the snapshot matrix singular values before and after mapping the data. They proved capable of
mitigating a large Kolmogorov N-width, but this is not the whole picture when it comes to ROM
construction. These mappings introduce theoretical and practical challenges into downstream tasks.
Evaluating and addressing these challenges will be the primary purpose of follow-on work.
