Quotient Admission Algorithms for Witness-Supported Graph Windows
Yushan Li

Abstract
We formulate the quotient admission problem for finite graph-window rows. The input is a finite row set, an admissible evidence map, semantic labels, witness-support hypergraphs, and atom-level admissibility predicates. The output is a quotient decision on evidence atoms, with possible decisions certificate, residual, low-confidence, or blocked. The problem asks for the maximal guard-respecting atom-level decision map that uses no refinement beyond the admissible evidence partition. We prove an atom-union characterization of identifiable classes, give a witness-support hypergraph guard for certificate admission, characterize projected-label conflicts as blocked atoms, and present quotient admission algorithms with correctness, maximality, and complexity guarantees. With explicit evidence vectors and hyperedges, the algorithms run in expected O ⁡ ( B + I + n ) O(B+I+n) time and space by hashing and deterministic O ⁡ ( B + I + n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(B+I+n\log n) time by sorting under a key-linear comparison model, where n n is the number of rows, B B is the total evidence encoding length, and I I is the total hyperedge incidence size. We also prove a magnitude-only indistinguishability lower bound: any evaluator that observes only residual magnitudes fails on instances whose evidence atoms require different residual decisions after the magnitudes collapse them.

Introduction

Many finite decision procedures first construct local rows and then decide what can be exposed from the information carried by each row. The row may come from a graph window, a local consistency check, a finite hypothesis family, or a candidate-and-witness construction. Once the row is fixed, the algorithmic object is a quotient: which decision is identifiable from the admissible evidence, and which row classes must be blocked because the evidence partition cannot separate their projected decisions?

This paper studies that question as a finite combinatorial problem. A finite row set
\mathcal{R}
is mapped by an admissible evidence map
e:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{E}
into an evidence space. The fibers of
e
form an atom partition
\mathcal{P}_{e}
. Any decision rule that uses only admissible evidence must be constant on these atoms and therefore factors through the quotient map
q_{e}:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{P}_{e}
. The remaining task is to define a feasible and maximal atom-level admission map

F:\mathcal{P}_{e}\to\{\mathsf{C},\mathsf{R},\mathsf{L},\mathsf{block}\},

where
\mathsf{C}
denotes certificate admission,
\mathsf{R}
denotes residual admission,
\mathsf{L}
denotes low-confidence admission, and
\mathsf{block}
denotes a blocked atom.

The witness structure is represented by finite hypergraphs. For each row
r
, a hypergraph
H_{r}=(V_{r},\mathcal{W}_{r})
contains the candidate token
q_{r}
and witness-support tokens. The support surface is the union of all witness hyperedges, and candidate participation records whether some witness hyperedge contains
q_{r}
. Certificate admission requires both a certificate-admissibility predicate and this hypergraph guard. Residual admission is governed by a separate residual-admissibility predicate and a support-compatibility predicate
\kappa
. This separation keeps residual magnitude, support compatibility, and admission feasibility as distinct finite predicates.

Several application areas instantiate this row model. In graph optimization and factor-graph reasoning, local windows and loop candidates often carry witness and residual information

. The present paper abstracts the common finite quotient structure: evidence atoms, witness-support set systems, blocking of indistinguishable projected conflicts, and conservative admission.

3
Problem Statement

We first state the finite quotient admission problem in full. All sets in this section are finite.

Definition 1

(Graph-window row)
.

A graph-window row is a tuple

r=(G_{r},q_{r},W_{r},\eta_{r}),

where
G_{r}
is a finite graph window,
q_{r}
is a distinguished candidate token,
W_{r}
is a finite witness pool, and
\eta_{r}
is any remaining admissible metadata.

Definition 2

(Admissible evidence and atoms)
.

Let
e:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{E}
be an admissible evidence map. The evidence sigma-algebra is

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{adm}}=\sigma(e).

Define an equivalence relation
\sim_{e}
on
\mathcal{R}
by

r\sim_{e}r^{\prime}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e(r)=e(r^{\prime}).

For every
v\in\operatorname{im}(e)
, define the atom

A_{v}=e^{-1}(v).

The atom partition
\mathcal{P}_{e}=\{A_{v}:v\in\operatorname{im}(e)\}
is the set of
\sim_{e}
-equivalence classes, and the quotient map
q_{e}:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{P}_{e}
sends a row to its evidence atom.

Definition 3

(Semantic labels and admission decisions)
.

The semantic label set is

\mathcal{O}=\{\mathsf{C},\mathsf{R},\mathsf{D},\mathsf{L}\},

where
\mathsf{C}
denotes certificate-primary,
\mathsf{R}
denotes residual-primary,
\mathsf{D}
denotes dual information, and
\mathsf{L}
denotes low-confidence. The admission output set is

\mathcal{A}=\{\mathsf{C},\mathsf{R},\mathsf{L},\mathsf{block}\}.

The conservative projection
\pi:\mathcal{O}\to\mathcal{A}
is

\pi(\mathsf{C})=\mathsf{C},\qquad\pi(\mathsf{R})=\mathsf{R},\qquad\pi(\mathsf{D})=\mathsf{L},\qquad\pi(\mathsf{L})=\mathsf{L}.

Definition 4

(Quotient admission input)
.

An instance of the quotient admission problem consists of

(\mathcal{R},e,\ell,\{H_{r}\}_{r\in\mathcal{R}},\operatorname{supp},\operatorname{part},\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{cert}},\kappa,\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{res}}),

where
\ell:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{O}
is a semantic labeling,
H_{r}
is a witness-support hypergraph for row
r
, and

\operatorname{supp},\operatorname{part},\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{cert}},\kappa,\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{res}}:\mathcal{R}\to\{0,1\}

are
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{adm}}
-measurable predicates. The predicates respectively encode nonempty support, candidate participation, certificate admissibility, residual support compatibility, and residual admissibility.

Definition 5

(Feasible quotient admission map)
.

A feasible quotient admission map is a function

F:\mathcal{P}_{e}\to\mathcal{A}

satisfying the following constraints for every atom
A\in\mathcal{P}_{e}
.

(F1)

Atom-level output.
The decision depends only on
A
, not on a row inside
A
.

(F2)

Projected-label blocking.

F(A)=\mathsf{block}
if and only if
\pi\circ\ell
is not constant on
A
.

(F3)

Certificate guard.
If
F(A)=\mathsf{C}
, then
(\pi\circ\ell)(A)=\mathsf{C}
,
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{cert}}(A)=1
,
\operatorname{supp}(A)=1
, and
\operatorname{part}(A)=1
.

(F4)

Residual guard.
If
F(A)=\mathsf{R}
, then
(\pi\circ\ell)(A)=\mathsf{R}
,
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{res}}(A)=1
, and
\kappa(A)=1
.

(F5)

Conservative projection.
If
(\pi\circ\ell)(A)=\mathsf{L}
and
\pi\circ\ell
is constant on
A
, then
F(A)=\mathsf{L}
.

(F6)

No hidden refinement.
No branch of
F
may use a predicate that is not
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{adm}}
-measurable.

Here
(\pi\circ\ell)(A)
and the predicate values on
A
are well defined whenever the relevant function is constant on
A
.

Definition 6

(Maximal feasible map)
.

A feasible quotient admission map is maximal if, for every atom
A
on which
\pi\circ\ell
is constant, it outputs
\mathsf{C}
whenever the projected label and certificate guard allow
\mathsf{C}
, and it outputs
\mathsf{R}
whenever the projected label and residual guard allow
\mathsf{R}
. All remaining nonblocked atoms are mapped to
\mathsf{L}
.

4
Evidence Atoms and Boolean Structure

The first structural fact is the standard finite quotient characterization. It is stated here because every later admissibility constraint is imposed on atoms.

Theorem 1

(Atom-union characterization)
.

For
P\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

P
is
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{adm}}
-measurable.

(ii)

P
is a union of atoms in
\mathcal{P}_{e}
.

(iii)

There exists
S\subseteq\operatorname{im}(e)
such that
P=e^{-1}(S)
.

Moreover, every map
f:\mathcal{R}\to X
into a finite set
X
that is constant on atoms factors uniquely as
f=\bar{f}\circ q_{e}
for a map
\bar{f}:\mathcal{P}_{e}\to X
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{R}
is finite,
\sigma(e)
is the finite Boolean algebra generated by the fibers of
e
. Thus every
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{adm}}
-measurable set is a union of fibers, and the fibers are exactly the atoms in
\mathcal{P}_{e}
. Conversely, any union of atoms is the inverse image under
e
of the corresponding subset of
\operatorname{im}(e)
. This proves the equivalence.

For factorization, define
\bar{f}(A)=f(r)
for any
r\in A
. The definition is independent of the representative because
f
is constant on atoms. Since
q_{e}
is surjective onto
\mathcal{P}_{e}
, the factorization is unique.
∎

Corollary 1

(Boolean algebra of identifiable classes)
.

The identifiable subclasses of
\mathcal{R}
form a finite Boolean algebra isomorphic to
2^{\mathcal{P}_{e}}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
1
, every identifiable subclass is uniquely determined by the set of atoms that it contains. The Boolean operations correspond to union, intersection, and complement of these atom index sets.
∎

Corollary 2

(Counting identifiable classes)
.

The number of identifiable classes is
2^{|\mathcal{P}_{e}|}
.

Proof.

Each identifiable class is uniquely specified by choosing an arbitrary subset of the atom set
\mathcal{P}_{e}
.
∎

Corollary 3

(Exact-twin indistinguishability)
.

If
r,r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
satisfy
e(r)=e(r^{\prime})
, then every admissible evaluator assigns them the same quotient decision. Hence rows whose projected semantic labels differ inside one atom cannot be separated without violating the no-hidden-refinement rule.

Proof.

The equality
e(r)=e(r^{\prime})
places
r
and
r^{\prime}
in the same atom. Every admissible evaluator factors through
q_{e}
and is therefore constant on that atom.
∎

5
Witness-Support Hypergraphs

The witness component is a finite set system. It supplies guards for certificate admission but does not refine the evidence partition unless the derived predicates are included in the admissible evidence map.

Definition 7

(Witness-support hypergraph)
.

For row
r
, let
H_{r}=(V_{r},\mathcal{W}_{r})
be a finite hypergraph with
q_{r}\in V_{r}
. The vertices encode the candidate token and witness-support tokens. Each hyperedge
W\in\mathcal{W}_{r}
satisfies
W\subseteq V_{r}
, so membership statements such as
q_{r}\in W
are well defined. Define

\Sigma_{r}^{\mathrm{sup}}=\bigcup_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{r}}W,\qquad\operatorname{St}_{r}(q_{r})=\bigcup\{W\in\mathcal{W}_{r}:q_{r}\in W\}.

The set
\Sigma_{r}^{\mathrm{sup}}
is the support surface and
\operatorname{St}_{r}(q_{r})
is the candidate-support surface. The row predicates are

\operatorname{supp}(r)=\mathbf{1}[\Sigma_{r}^{\mathrm{sup}}\neq\varnothing],\qquad\operatorname{part}(r)=\mathbf{1}[\operatorname{St}_{r}(q_{r})\neq\varnothing].

Definition 8

(Atom-level guards)
.

The predicates
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{cert}}
,
\kappa
, and
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{res}}
are atom-level,
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{adm}}
-measurable predicates. The same is required of
\operatorname{supp}
and
\operatorname{part}
when they are used by a quotient admission map. Thus each predicate has a well-defined value on every atom
A\in\mathcal{P}_{e}
.

Definition 9

(Certificate-valid atom)
.

An atom
A
is certificate-valid if some row
r\in A
satisfies
\Sigma_{r}^{\mathrm{sup}}\neq\varnothing
, there exists
W\in\mathcal{W}_{r}
with
q_{r}\in W
, and
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{cert}}(A)=1
. Because the predicates are
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{adm}}
-measurable, these conditions then hold throughout the atom.

Proposition 1

(Certificate-validity implies the support guard)
.

Let
F:\mathcal{P}_{e}\to\mathcal{A}
be an atom-level decision map that uses no hidden refinement and assigns
F(A)=\mathsf{C}
only when
A
is certificate-valid. Then
F(A)=\mathsf{C}
implies
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{cert}}(A)=1
,
\operatorname{supp}(A)=1
, and
\operatorname{part}(A)=1
.

Proof.

Fix an atom
A
with
F(A)=\mathsf{C}
. By the hypothesis on
F
, the atom
A
is certificate-valid. Hence
A
contains a row
r
with
\Sigma_{r}^{\mathrm{sup}}\neq\varnothing
, so
\operatorname{supp}(r)=1
. The same row has a witness hyperedge
W\in\mathcal{W}_{r}
with
q_{r}\in W
, so
\operatorname{St}_{r}(q_{r})\neq\varnothing
and
\operatorname{part}(r)=1
. Certificate validity also gives
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{cert}}(A)=1
. Since
\operatorname{supp}
and
\operatorname{part}
are
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{adm}}
-measurable, they are constant on
A
. Therefore
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{cert}}(A)=1
,
\operatorname{supp}(A)=1
, and
\operatorname{part}(A)=1
.
∎

Proposition 2

(Residual admissibility is not residual magnitude)
.

Residual magnitude can be used only after residual admissibility and support compatibility have been established at the atom level. In particular, a feasible quotient admission map cannot replace
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{res}}
and
\kappa
by a scalar residual magnitude unless that scalar is sufficient to recover those predicates on atoms.

Proof.

The residual guard in the problem definition requires
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{res}}(A)=1
and
\kappa(A)=1
before residual admission is possible. A scalar magnitude is not one of these predicates. It may be part of the admissible evidence vector, but it determines residual admission only if the corresponding atom-level predicates are functions of that scalar on the instance.
∎

Theorem 2

(Correctness and maximality of quotient admission)
.

Assume
BuildAtoms
returns atom records rather than a measurability report. Then
QuotientAdmission
returns a feasible quotient admission map. Moreover, it is maximal among all evaluators that use no hidden refinement and obey the same projected-label, certificate, residual, and conservative-projection guards.

Proof.

The output is atom-level by construction:
QuotientAdmission
assigns one value to each atom record. If an atom contains more than one projected label, the first branch blocks it, so projected exact-twin conflicts are not admitted. If the algorithm outputs
\mathsf{C}
, then the certificate branch was taken, which requires
\operatorname{PLab}(A)=\{\mathsf{C}\}
,
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{cert}}(A)=1
,
\operatorname{supp}(A)=1
, and
\operatorname{part}(A)=1
. If it outputs
\mathsf{R}
, then the residual branch was taken, which requires
\operatorname{PLab}(A)=\{\mathsf{R}\}
,
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{res}}(A)=1
, and
\kappa(A)=1
. Atoms whose projected label set is
\{\mathsf{L}\}
fail both positive branches and are assigned
\mathsf{L}
; this includes atoms whose semantic labels are contained in
\{\mathsf{D},\mathsf{L}\}
. Because
BuildAtoms
verifies atom-level predicate values, no branch uses information that is unavailable in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{adm}}
.

The algorithm never admits a residual decision from residual magnitude alone. Indeed, residual magnitude is not an input to the residual branch; the branch requires the atom-level predicates
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{res}}
and
\kappa
.

For maximality, let
G:\mathcal{P}_{e}\to\mathcal{A}
be any feasible evaluator obeying the same guards. On any atom with nonconstant projected labels, feasibility forces
G(A)=\mathsf{block}
, and the algorithm also outputs
\mathsf{block}
. On an atom with projected label
\mathsf{C}
and satisfied certificate guard, any feasible map may output
\mathsf{C}
, and the algorithm does so. On an atom with projected label
\mathsf{R}
and satisfied residual guard, any feasible map may output
\mathsf{R}
, and the algorithm does so. On every other nonblocked atom, the corresponding positive guard is absent or the projected label is
\mathsf{L}
, so no guard-respecting evaluator can assign a positive decision that the algorithm omits. Hence the algorithm is maximal with respect to positive admissions under the stated feasibility constraints.
∎

Theorem 3

(Complexity)
.

Let
n=|\mathcal{R}|
, let

B=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}|e(r)|,\qquad I=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{r}}|W|,

where
|e(r)|
is the explicit encoding length of the evidence vector and
I
is the total hyperedge incidence size. If evidence vectors and hyperedges are explicitly encoded, then
BuildAtoms
followed by
QuotientAdmission
runs in expected
O(B+I+n)
time and space using hashing. In the deterministic comparison model where lexicographic sorting of encoded vectors has total key-inspection cost
O(B+n\log n)
, the running time is
O(B+I+n\log n)
and the space usage is
O(B+I+n)
. Without that key-linear sorting assumption, the deterministic fallback bound is
O(I+n\log n\cdot L)
, where
L=\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}|e(r)|
, plus the linear cost of storing the encoded evidence.

Proof.

Computing support and candidate participation scans each hyperedge incidence once, contributing
O(I)
. Reading and hashing the evidence vectors contributes
O(B)
expected time and stores
O(B+n)
encoded data and row references. Bucket aggregation scans each row and each stored predicate value a constant number of times, contributing
O(n)
.
QuotientAdmission
scans the atom records once, which is
O(n)
in the worst case because there are at most
n
atoms. Thus the expected hashing bound is
O(B+I+n)
time and space.

For the deterministic bound, sort the explicitly encoded evidence vectors lexicographically and group equal consecutive vectors. Under a key-linear comparison model, the total number of inspected evidence symbols over the sorting and grouping phase is bounded by
O(B+n\log n)
; this holds, for example, when encoded vectors are interned, radix-sorted over a finite alphabet, or compared using longest-common-prefix accounting. The remaining scans contribute
O(I+n)
, giving
O(B+I+n\log n)
time and
O(B+I+n)
space. If comparisons are charged by worst-case key length without such accounting, sorting contributes
O(n\log n\cdot L)
for
L=\max_{r}|e(r)|
, yielding the stated deterministic fallback bound.
∎

7
Indistinguishability Lower Bound

The next theorem isolates a lower bound for evaluators that observe only residual magnitudes. It is a finite indistinguishability argument: collapsed atoms cannot be separated by an oracle whose observation is constant on their union.

Definition 10

(Magnitude-only evaluator)
.

Let
\mu:\mathcal{R}\to M
be a residual-magnitude map into a finite or countable set
M
. A magnitude-only evaluator is a map
g\circ\mu:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{A}
for some
g:M\to\mathcal{A}
.

Theorem 4

(Magnitude-only oracle lower bound)
.

Suppose there exist atoms
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{e}
and a value
m_{0}\in M
such that
\mu(r)=m_{0}
for every
r\in A\cup B
. Suppose also that
A
has residual label and satisfies the residual guard,

\operatorname{PLab}(A)=\{\mathsf{R}\},\qquad\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{res}}(A)=1,\qquad\kappa(A)=1,

while
B
does not satisfy the residual guard or does not have projected residual label. Then no magnitude-only evaluator can agree with the maximal quotient admission map on
A\cup B
.

Proof.

Every magnitude-only evaluator assigns one value
g(m_{0})
to all rows in
A\cup B
. The maximal quotient admission map assigns
\mathsf{R}
on
A
by Theorem
2
. It does not assign
\mathsf{R}
on
B
because the projected residual label or residual guard is absent there. Therefore no single value
g(m_{0})
can agree with the quotient map on both atoms.
∎

Corollary 4

(Support compatibility cannot be recovered after collapse)
.

If residual magnitudes collapse two atoms that differ in support compatibility and residual admission, then residual admission is not identifiable from residual magnitude alone.

Proof.

This is Theorem
4
with the difference between the two atoms witnessed by
\kappa
and
\operatorname{adm}_{\mathrm{res}}
.
∎

9
Conclusion

Quotient admission is a finite combinatorial problem: an evidence map induces atoms, identifiable classes are unions of atoms, witness-support hypergraphs impose certificate guards, residual admission requires atom-level compatibility, and projected exact-twin conflicts are blocked. The resulting algorithms compute the maximal guard-respecting atom-level decision map under the no-hidden-refinement rule, with linear expected time under hashing and a model-explicit deterministic sorting bound. Magnitude-only evaluators cannot recover residual decisions after they collapse atoms whose support compatibility differs.
