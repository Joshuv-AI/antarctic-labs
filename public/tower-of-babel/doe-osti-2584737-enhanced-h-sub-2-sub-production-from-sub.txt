Public Executive Summary
Geologic hydrogen (H₂) generation through serpentinization of ultramafic rocks presents a promising
alternative to conventional hydrogen production, utilizing Earth's abundant magnesium- and iron-rich
minerals. This study explores the use of short-chain organic acids (specifically malic acid, MA; and citric
acid, CA) to enhance hydrogen yields by stimulating mineral dissolution and iron oxidation from ultramafic
rock samples. Batch experiments were conducted with crushed peridotite samples from the Tamarack
Complex (Minnesota) and Del Puerto Ophiolite (California). Experiments employed 0.05 M and 0.1 M
solutions of MA or CA, under 500 psi (35 bar) nitrogen pressure, at temperatures up to 150 °C. Real-time
gas monitoring, aqueous chemistry and solid-phase analyses were performed to track reaction progress and
hydrogen production.
Experimental results showed that decreasing grain size from 750 μm to 15 μm led to a 3.5-fold increase in
hydrogen yield, attributed to increased surface area. Mineralogical analysis on the reacted products
confirmed the conversion of olivine to brucite and serpentine phases. Tamarack samples demonstrated
higher reactivity (more rock dissolved) than Del Puerto, though an 8-point increase in rock Mg# resulted
in 0.44 times increase in H2 yield, underscoring the greater influence of grain size and surface accessibility
over bulk composition. At concentrations of 0.05 M, MA produced 1.4 times more H₂ than CA. At double
the concentration (0.1 M), MA continued to outperform CA by producing 1.3 times more hydrogen,
suggesting that MA promotes greater mineral dissolution and surface reactivity as well as more effective
iron oxidation, which is critical for sustained H₂ production. Citric acid facilitated higher mobilization of
transition metals (e.g., Fe, Cr, Ni). Elevated temperatures further enhanced yields, with MA remaining
stable and catalytically active up to 175 °C. In contrast, CA decomposed at temperatures above 150 °C.
Malic acid’s yield benefits may offset its cost, making organic acid-assisted serpentinization a promising
pathway for sustainable, large-scale hydrogen production.

Project Activities
This project focused on enhancing hydrogen production through serpentinization reactions by investigating
the catalytic effects of two organic acids (malic and citric acid) under controlled laboratory conditions.
Using batch experiments with different rock types (Tamarack peridotite and Del Puerto ophiolite) and
varying grain sizes, acid concentrations, temperatures, and pressures, the study tested the hypothesis that
organic acids promote mineral dissolution and iron oxidation, thereby increasing hydrogen yield. Results
demonstrated that hydrogen production significantly increases with smaller grain size, and at both lower
concentration and higher temperature, malic acid generally outperforms citric acid. The highest hydrogen
yields were noted at conditions of 150 °C in the presence of 0.1 M malic acid, which remains stable at this
elevated temperature (unlike citric acid). Techno-economic and life cycle assessments further indicated that
despite differences in environmental impact and cost, organic acid stimulation can significantly lower the
levelized cost of geologic hydrogen production, confirming the potential of this approach for sustainable
energy generation.
Experimental system built and validated

Custom high-pressure, high-temperature batch reactors were developed at LLNL and MS&T to simulate
geological conditions and provide in-line sampling capabilities for both liquids and gases (M1.4). A total
of 18 experiments were conducted at both LLNL and Missouri University Institute of Science and
Technology using peridotite rock samples from two locations (Del Puerto (California); Tamarack
(Minnesota) samples T-700 and T-709; M1.1, Fig. 1) with different grain sizes (15, 45 and 750 μm), at
water-to-rock ratios of 2.2 and 2.7, under nitrogen pressure (35 bar) and temperatures ranging from 100 °C
to 150 °C. Organic acids (citric and malic) at different concentrations were used as reactants.

Rock samples characterized

Ultramafic rock samples were collected and subjected to detailed mineralogical and petrographic
characterization, including XRD, EPR, SEM, optical microscopy and TGA, to determine changes in
mineral phases, porosity, and surface area before and after reaction. These characterization methods
allowed quantitative confirmation of reaction pathways, iron oxidation, and hydrogen yield, providing
critical data to support further modeling and reaction optimization. Microscopic and SEM images are
summarized in Figure 2. Rock compositions were thoroughly characterized to evaluate influence on
hydrogen generation via serpentinization under non-stirred gas-phase conditions, corresponding to M1.6.
The two Tamarack samples (T-709 and T-700) consisted primarily of olivine (≥70%), with minor pyroxene
(≤10%), and trace amounts (~5% or less) of plagioclase, grunerite (amphibole), chromite, and
chlorite/biotite. T-709 is classified as a feldspathic peridotite with limited serpentinization, whereas T-700
contains slightly less plagioclase and pyroxene and exhibits an even lower degree of serpentinization. In
contrast, the Del Puerto (DP) sample is a serpentinized ophiolite, composed of olivine, pyroxene, and
serpentinite phases.

Baseline hydrogen production rate quantification

As part of M1.5, a baseline hydrogen yield from the Tamarack rock materials (15 m grain size) was
measured at 500 psi and 100 °C over a 31-day reaction period (gray dashed line; Fig. 3b). In order to
compare baseline rates between different grain sizes, a correction was applied to account for increased grain
size/correlated decreased surface area to estimate an equivalent production rate for the larger grain size
(750 m) used in LLNL experiments (shown as black dashed line in Fig. 3a). The addition of both organic
acid resulted in increased hydrogen yield from ~2.4 to 27.6 times greater than the baseline rate obtained in
acid-free water under the same temperature and grain size conditions (see Table 2 for details) in the
unstirred and stirred gas phase experiments, respectively. These results suggest that gas stirring strongly
influences the accurate quantification of the resulting gas composition, as secondary gas (e.g., CH4, H2S,
CO2 and H2O) accumulation may significantly influence hydrogen yield estimates.

Effect of OA strength on iron oxidation coupled with H2 production

To fulfill M1.6, experiments were conducted at 100 °C using two grain sizes (750 μm and 15 μm; see Table
2) and two organic acid concentrations (0.05 M and 0.1 M), with corresponding water-to-rock (w/r) ratios
of 2.2 and 2.7, respectively. After correcting for surface area differences, results indicated that at the lower
(0.05 M) acid concentration, both malic and citric acid addition stimulated hydrogen production above the
baseline rate (resulting in a 12x and 8.6x increase for MA and CA, respectively; Table 2), with malic slightly
outperforming citric acid stimulation (see Figure 3). At the doubled/higher (0.1 M) acid concentration,
malic acid again outperformed citric acid in terms of stimulated hydrogen production, producing a 2.4x and
1.9x increase for MA and CA, respectively. These results suggest that stronger acid conditions enhance
mineral dissolution and surface reactivity, leading to increased hydrogen generation.

Impact of surface area on H2 production

Experiments were conducted on two size fractions, and we attempted to compare findings by normalizing
the rate per surface area values using a correlation of surface area with grain size (a fair assumption in the
case of euhedral olivine mineral grains). We assumed that a 50-fold decrease in grain size (from 750 µm to
15 µm) would theoretically result in a 4.4-fold increase in surface area (from 18.03 g/m2 to 2.568 g/m2;
M1.6; Table 3), which we then used to normalize rates obtained from the two different grain sizes.
Experimental results showed that decreasing grain size led to a 3.5-fold increase in hydrogen yield,
attributed to increased surface area.

Post-reaction XRD analysis of solid phases from experiments with both rock types revealed reduced olivine

20%) for DP (partially serpentinized) samples.
Using the Del Puerto rock sample, ICP-MS analysis indicated that the addition of citric acid (CA) led to
higher concentrations of total iron and reduced iron (Fe²⁺) in solution (Figure 5), with a corresponding
decrease in solid-phase Fe³⁺. This trend was further supported by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR)
data (Table 3). The elevated Fe²⁺ levels in the citric acid solution suggest that more effective complexation
may inhibit the oxidation of iron, a step typically required for hydrogen reduction and production. These
findings point to an enhancement of reaction kinetics by organic acid addition, promoting hydrogen
generation. Furthermore, despite an 8-point difference in Mg#, hydrogen yield increased by 0.44×,
indicating that mineral texture, surface accessibility, and microstructural features exert a greater influence
on reactivity than bulk composition alone.

Temperature effect on iron oxidation and H2 production

A comparative analysis of two Tamarack rock samples (T-700 and T-709) was conducted to fulfill M1.7 to
evaluate the hydrogen increase by increasing temperature. As shown in Figure 6, pH measurements from
experiments using T-700 with a 45μm grain size at 100 °C and 130 °C comparison indicate that higher
temperatures resulted in elevated pH values and lower concentrations of Fe²⁺ and lower Fe²⁺/total Fe ratios.
These results suggest that increased temperature increases iron oxidation, and that will also increase the
hydrogen reaction rate which consists of findings by Leong et al. (2023) and Miller et al. (2017).
Figure 7 and Table 2 present H₂ evolution from two separate sets of experiments using T-709 at 100,
125 and 150 °C under 500 psi headspace pressure. The highest hydrogen yield was observed at 150 °C with
malic acid, exhibiting a reaction rate nearly 5 orders of magnitude greater than those reported in previous
studies (e.g., Leong et al., 2023). It is notable that hydrogen production in the 150 °C/citric acid system
declined after 12 hours, likely due to liquid accumulation in the gas sampling chamber or a potential
reduction in catalytic efficiency, the latter of which will require further investigation. This behavior can be
explained by the thermal stability of the acids, with citric acid unstable around 150 °C, whereas malic acid
remains stable up to ~175 °C.

Life-Cycle and Technology-to-Market Analyses

GE Aerospace and Shell provided information about the geologic hydrogen production process to Argonne
National Laboratory to facilitate a life cycle assessment (LCA) to evaluate the environmental impacts of
using malic and citric acids to enhance hydrogen production via serpentinization. These organic acids
promote hydrogen generation through chelation and acidification. Both acids are biodegradable, but citric
acid has a higher carbon intensity of 1.5-3 kg CO2 equivalent/kg acid due to its biogenic production
pathway. On the other hand, malic acid is produced by biotechnological routes, resulting in a lower carbon
intensity of 1.5-2.5 kg CO2 equivalent/kg acid.
Literature predicts the carbon intensity of geologic hydrogen to be 0.4 kg CO2 equivalent/kg H2, including
the carbon intensity from embodied emissions (Brandt, 2023). Even with the addition of citric or malic

A preliminary techno-economic analysis conducted by GE Aerospace and Shell suggests that the addition
of organic acid catalysts has the potential to decrease the levelized cost of stimulated geologic hydrogen.
The techno-economic model takes into consideration the capital cost of well drilling and surface equipment,
as well as feedstock costs for water, organic acids, and electricity. Using the results from the experiments
presented in Table 2, the addition of malic acid and citric acid were assumed to increase the hydrogen
production rate by 8.2× and 1.4× respectively. The results from the preliminary model show that for malic
acid, this increase in hydrogen production rate makes up for the added cost of the organic acid feedstock.
On the other hand, citric acid does not substantially reduce the levelized cost of hydrogen. Figure 8 shows
that the malic organic acid catalyst lowers the levelized cost of stimulated geologic hydrogen across a range
of values of ultimate hydrogen yield per rock.
