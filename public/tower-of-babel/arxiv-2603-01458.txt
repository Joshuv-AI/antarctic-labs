Generalized quantum master equation from memory kernel coupling theory
Rui-Hao Bi, Wei Liu, Wenjie Dou

Abstract
The generalized quantum master equation provides a powerful framework for non-Markovian dynamics of open quantum systems. However, the accurate and efficient evaluation of the memory kernel remains a challenge. In this work, we introduce a comprehensive tensorial extension to the Memory Kernel Coupling Theory (MKCT) to overcome this bottleneck. By elevating the original scalar formalism to a tensorial framework, the extended MKCT enables the calculation of general expectation values and cross-correlation functions. We demonstrate the numerical accuracy and efficiency of this method across multiple benchmark systems: capturing transient populations and coherences in the spin-boson model, resolving the excitonic absorption spectrum of the Fenna-Matthews-Olson complex, and simulating charge mobility in one-dimensional lattice models. These successful applications establish the tensorial MKCT as a highly efficient tool for investigating complex dynamics in open quantum systems.

Generalized quantum master equation, absorption spectra, transport

Theory

2.1
Preliminary considerations

Let us consider an open quantum system described by the following Hamiltonian:

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{s}+\hat{H}_{b}+\hat{H}_{sb}.

(1)

Here,
\hat{H}_{s}
is the system Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}_{b}
is the bath Hamiltonian, and
\hat{H}_{sb}
is the system-bath interaction Hamiltonian. In the Heisenberg picture, the system operator
\hat{A}
evolves under the Liouville equation

\derivative{t}\hat{A}(t)=\frac{\textup{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{L}\hat{A}(t)=e^{\textup{i}\mathcal{L}t}\hat{A},

(2)

where the total Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}
is given by

\mathcal{L}(\cdot)=(\mathcal{L}_{s}+\mathcal{L}_{b}+\mathcal{L}_{sb})(\cdot)=\commutator{\hat{H}_s + \hat{H}_b + \hat{H}_{sb}}{(\cdot)}.

(3)

We are interested in the quantum dynamics of an operator
\hat{A}
, characterized by its expectation value and its cross-correlation with another operator
\hat{B}
. Throughout this work, we assume a factorized initial condition
\hat{\rho}_{0}=\hat{\sigma}_{0}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}
, where the system is prepared in an arbitrary state
\hat{\sigma}_{0}
and the bath is initially in thermal equilibrium:
\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}=e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{b}}/\Tr[e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{b}}]
. Under this condition, the expectation value evolves as

\expectationvalue{\hat{A}(t)}=\Tr[\hat{A}(t)\hat{\rho}_{0}],

(4)

and the two-time correlation function is given by

C_{AB}(t)=\expectationvalue{\hat{A}(t)\hat{B}(0)}=\Tr[\hat{A}(t)\hat{B}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{0}].

(5)

In Eq. (
5
), we consider the specific case where the second operator is evaluated at the initial time
t=0
.

We span the system Liouville space using a set of orthonormal basis operators
\{\hat{\phi}_{i}\}
that satisfies
\Tr[\hat{\phi}_{i}\hat{\phi}_{j}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{ij}
. Using this basis, any operator
\hat{A}
can be expanded as

\displaystyle\hat{A}=\sum_{i}\Tr[\hat{A}\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\dagger}]\hat{\phi}_{i}=\sum_{i}A_{i}\hat{\phi}_{i},

(6a)

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\dagger}=A_{i}^{*}\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i}\Tr[\hat{\phi}_{i}\hat{A}^{\dagger}]\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\dagger}.

(6b)

By applying this expansion, we can express Eq.
4
as:

\expectationvalue{\hat{A}(t)}=\sum_{ij}A_{i}\mathcal{C}_{ij}(t)\sigma_{0,j}^{*}.

(7)

Here, we have introduced the matrix
\bm{\mathcal{C}}(t)
, whose
i,j
-th element is defined as

\mathcal{C}_{ij}(t)\equiv\Tr[(e^{\textup{i}\mathcal{L}t}\hat{\phi}_{i})\hat{\phi}_{j}^{\dagger}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}],

(8)

which can be interpreted as the autocorrelation functions between the basis operators
39
;
40
. Furthermore, we note that the definition of
\bm{\mathcal{C}}(t)
is closely related to the concept of dynamical maps introduced in previous studies
34
;
6
. Similarly, the correlation function
C_{AB}(t)
in Eq.
5
can also be expressed in
\bm{\mathcal{C}}(t)
as:

C_{AB}(t)=\sum_{ij}A_{i}C_{ij}(t)\Tr[\hat{\phi}_{j}(\hat{B}\hat{\sigma}_{0})^{\dagger}].

(9)

In the following, we show that
\bm{\mathcal{C}}(t)
ssatisfies the generalized quantum master equation (GQME)
49
;
22
;
39
;
40
. To begin, we define the projection operators
\{\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{i}\}
as:

\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{i}\hat{X}=\hat{\phi}_{i}(\hat{X},\hat{\phi}_{i})=\hat{\phi}_{i}\Tr[\hat{X}\hat{\phi}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}],

(10)

where
\hat{X}
is an arbitrary operator in the composite system-bath space, and
(\hat{X},\hat{\phi}_{i})
denotes the Mori product. By summing the individual projections to form the total projection operator
\bm{\mathcal{P}}\equiv\sum_{i}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
, we define a projection that essentially performs a partial trace over the bath degrees of freedom with respect to the thermal equilibrium state
\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}
. As detailed in Appendix
A
, the operator
\bm{\mathcal{P}}
leads to the following GQME for the basis autocorrelation functions:

\derivative{t}\bm{\mathcal{C}}(t)=\bm{\Omega}\bm{\mathcal{C}}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\differential{\tau}\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\tau)\bm{\mathcal{C}}(t-\tau),

(11)

where the frequency matrix
\bm{\Omega}
and the memory kernel
\bm{\mathcal{K}}
are defined as

\displaystyle\Omega_{ij}

\displaystyle\equiv(\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{\phi}_{i},\hat{\phi}_{j}),

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv(\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{f}_{i}(t),\hat{\phi}_{j}),

(13)

with the fluctuation operator given by:

\hat{f}_{i}(t)=e^{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}t}\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{\phi}_{i}.

(14)

Here,
\bm{\mathcal{Q}}=\bm{1}-\bm{\mathcal{P}}
the projection onto the complementary subspace, i.e., the bath degrees of freedom.

2.2
Memory Kernel Coupling Theory

In the original formulation of MKCT
31
, we considered the scalar autocorrelation
C_{AA}(t)
of a single operator
\hat{A}
. We found that by defining higher-order moments and memory kernels as:

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}=((\textup{i}\mathcal{L})^{n}\hat{A},\hat{A})(\hat{A},\hat{A})^{-1},

(15a)

\displaystyle K_{n}(t)=((\textup{i}\mathcal{L})^{n}\hat{f}(t),\hat{A})(\hat{A},\hat{A})^{-1},

(15b)

the kernels
K_{n}(t)
satisfy a set of initial value problems:

\derivative{t}K_{n}(t)=K_{n+1}(t)-K_{1}(t)\Omega_{n},

(16)

where the initial conditions are determined by

K_{n}(0)=\Omega_{n+1}-\Omega_{n}\Omega_{1}.

(17)

With proper truncation
31
;
29
, the MKCT equation (Eq.
16
) yields the full hierarchy of higher-order memory kernels
K_{n}(t)
using only the information contained in the higher-order moments
\{\Omega_{n}\}
.

In the following, we demonstrate that the core ideas of the scalar formulation can be generalized to an identical MKCT equation for the tensorial kernel
\bm{\mathcal{K}}(t)
. Specifically, we define the
n
-th order frequency matrix (moment)
\bm{\Omega}_{n}
and memory kernel matrix
\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{n}(t)
via their elements:

\displaystyle\Omega_{n,ij}=((\textup{i}\mathcal{L})^{n}\phi_{i},\phi_{j}),

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{n,ij}=((\textup{i}\mathcal{L})^{n}\hat{f}_{i}(t),\phi_{j}).

(19)

Here, the subscript
n,ij
denotes the
(i,j)
-th element of
n
-th order quantity. Taking the time derivative of
\mathcal{K}_{n,ik}(t)
yields:

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\mathcal{K}_{n,ik}(t)

\displaystyle=\left((\textup{i}\mathcal{L})^{n}\left\{\bm{1}-\sum_{j}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{j}\right\}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{f}_{i}(t),\hat{\phi}_{j}\right),

(20)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{n+1,ik}(t)-\sum_{j}K_{1,ij}(t)\Omega_{n,jk},

which simplifies to the matrix equation:

\derivative{t}\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{n}(t)=\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{n+1}(t)-\bm{K}_{1}(t)\bm{\Omega}_{n}.

(21)

At
t=0
, substituting
\hat{f}_{i}(0)=\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{\phi}_{i}
into the matrix MKCT Eq.
21
yields the initial condition:

\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{n}(0)=\bm{\Omega}_{n+1}-\bm{\Omega}_{n}\bm{\Omega}_{1}.

(22)

Eqs.
21
and
22
parallel the equation structures of the original scalar MKCT formulation [Eqs.
16
and
17
], except that the moments and memory kernels are now matrix quantities.

Despite their simplicity, the MKCT equations (Eqs.
16
and
21
) should be solved carefully. Since simple truncation at the
n
-th order is known to cause numerical instabilities
31
;
3
;
29
, previous works have introduced two distinct numerical strategies: a dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) approach
48
;
30
for extrapolating
\mathcal{K}(t)
, and an alternative Padé approximant-based method
3
;
32
for fitting higher-order terms. A very recently projected MKCT strategy shows promise in systematically converging the scalar MKCT
29
. In the following, we will extend the Padé-based methodology, previously established for scalar kernels, to the tensorial kernels treated in this work.

The Padé approach exploits the fact that the memory kernels
\bm{\mathcal{K}}(t)
decay rapidly. Consequently, a Padé-approximant of the series expansion of
\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{n}(t)
is sufficient to reproduce the kernel faithfully
3
. This series expansion is constructed by calculating various orders of the time derivatives of
\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{n}(t)
evaluated at
t=0
, which are obtained iteratively using the following equation:

\displaystyle\derivative[m]{t}\mathcal{K}_{n,ik}(0)

\displaystyle=\left((\textup{i}\mathcal{L})^{n}\left\{\bm{1}-\sum_{j}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{j}\right\}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L})^{m}\hat{\phi}_{i},\hat{\phi}_{k}\right),

(23)

\displaystyle=\derivative[m-1]{t}\mathcal{K}_{n+1,ik}(0)-\sum_{j}\tilde{\Omega}_{m,ij}\Omega_{n,jk}.

Here, we introduce the auxiliary quantity
\tilde{\bm{\Omega}}_{m}
, defined as

\tilde{\Omega}_{m,ij}=(\textup{i}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L})^{m}\hat{\phi}_{i},\hat{\phi}_{j}).

(24)

The auxiliary quantity
\tilde{\bm{\Omega}}_{m}
itself satisfies a recursive relation when expanding:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}_{m,ij}

\displaystyle=\left(\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\left\{\bm{1}-\sum_{j}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{j}\right\}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L})^{m-1}\hat{\phi}_{i},\hat{\phi}_{k}\right),

(25)

\displaystyle=((\textup{i}\mathcal{L})^{2}(\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L})^{m-1}\hat{\phi}_{i},\hat{\phi}_{j})-\sum_{j}\tilde{\Omega}_{m-1,ij}\Omega_{1,jk}.

By iteratively expanding these recursive relations (Eqs.
23
and
25
), we express both
\derivative[m]{t}\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{n}(0)
and
\tilde{\bm{\Omega}}_{n}
so they are exclusively determined by higher-order moments
\{\bm{\Omega}_{n}\}
:

\displaystyle\derivative[m]{t}\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{n}(0)=\bm{\Omega}_{m+n+1}-\bm{\Omega}_{m+n}\tilde{\bm{\Omega}}_{0}-\dots-\tilde{\bm{\Omega}}_{m}\bm{\Omega}_{n},

(26)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\Omega}}_{n}=\bm{\Omega}_{m+1}-\tilde{\bm{\Omega}}_{0}\bm{\Omega}_{m}-\dots-\tilde{\bm{\Omega}}_{m-1}\bm{\Omega}_{1}.

(27)

Eqs.
26
and
27
allow us to calculate the time derivatives of
\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{n}(t)
at
t=0
up to arbitary orders. By applying Padé approximant element-wise, we can obtain an approximation of
\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{n}(t)
. Substituting this result into the hierarchy allows us to terminate the MKCT at the
(n-1)
-th order.

The tensorial extension of the MKCT successfully addresses some limitations of its scalar predecessor. While the original MKCT formalism cannot be used to predict general expectation values and cross-correlation functions, the tensorial framework yields the complete basis correlation matrix
\bm{\mathcal{C}}(t)
. This comprehensive matrix provides direct access to
\expectationvalue{A(t)}
and
C_{AB}(t)
via Eqs.
4
and
9
, respectively (as demonstrated in Secs.
3.1
and
3.3
).

3.1
Spin-boson model

Let us consider the spin-boson model, defined by the Hamiltonian:

\hat{H}=\frac{\omega_{s}}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}+\Omega\hat{\sigma}_{x}+\hat{\sigma}_{z}\sum_{j}c_{j}\hat{q}_{j}+\sum_{j}\frac{\omega_{j}}{2}(\hat{p}_{j}^{2}+\hat{q}_{j}^{2}),

(29)

where a single harmonic bath couples to the system via the
\hat{\sigma}_{z}
operator. For this model, the original MKCT was able to accurately calculate the autocorrelation function
C_{AA}(t)

31
;
3
, but it was incapable of evaluating cross-correlation functions
C_{AB}(t)
or general expectation values, such as state populations.

These limitations are overcome by the tensorial MKCT introduced in this work. The new approach retains the original procedures: mapping moments (
\bm{\Omega}_{n}
) to the memory kernel (
\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{1}(t)
), thereby facilitating the straightforward calculation of the full basis correlation matrix
\bm{C}(t)
via the GQME (Eq.
11
). A comparison of each matrix element against exact benchmarks is presented in FIG.
6
of Appendix
B
to demonstrate the numerical fidelity of this extension.

Combined with Eq.
4
,
\bm{\mathcal{C}}(t)
enables us to obtain exact population and coherence dynamics. For example, starting from the initial state
\sigma(0)=\outerproduct{0}{0}
, FIG.
1
demonstrates that both the populations and coherences obtained from our method agree well with the DEOM results. Furthermore, the same
\bm{\mathcal{C}}(t)
directly yields the cross-correlation function
C_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}(t)
via Eq.
9
. As shown in FIG.

, the dynamics generated using this approach are again identical to those from DEOM.

3.2
Absorption spectrum of Fenna–Matthews–Olson (FMO) complex

The original MKCT was successfully employed to simulate the absorption spectra of two-level systems
31
;
3
. However, it has not yet been applied to multi-level systems. To address this, we consider the 7-site FMO complex of
Chlorobium tepidum
, described by the Hamiltonian, partitioned into electronic, vibrational, and coupling terms:

\hat{H}_{\text{mol}}=\hat{H}_{\text{e}}+\hat{H}_{\text{vib}}+\hat{H}_{\text{e-vib}}.

(30)

The electronic (excitonic) component is given by

\hat{H}_{\text{e}}=\sum_{m=1}^{7}\epsilon_{m}\outerproduct{m}{m}+\sum_{m=0}^{7}\sum_{n<m}J_{mn}(\outerproduct{m}{n}+\outerproduct{n}{m}),

(31)

where the site energies
\epsilon_{m}
and couplings
J_{mn}
are taken from Ref.
9
. The vibrational bath,
\hat{H}_{\text{vib}}
, is modeled as a set of independent harmonic oscillators associated with each site:

\hat{H}_{\text{vib}}=\sum_{m=1}^{7}\sum_{j}\frac{\omega_{j}}{2}(\hat{p}_{mj}^{2}+\hat{q}_{mj}^{2}).

(32)

The interaction between the excitonic and vibrational degrees of freedom is described by the linear coupling term:

\hat{H}_{\text{e-vib}}=\sum_{m=1}^{7}\outerproduct{m}{m}\sum_{j}c_{mj}\hat{q}_{mj},

(33)

where the coupling is exclusively diagonal. We assume the spectral density
J_{m}(\omega)
is is identical for all sites and follows the Ohmic form defined in Eq.
28
.

To simulate the absorption spectra, we calculate the dipole autocorrelation function as follows:

C_{\bm{\mu}\bm{\mu}}(t)=\Tr[\left(e^{\textup{i}\mathcal{L}t}\hat{\bm{\mu}}\right)\cdot\hat{\bm{\mu}}\outerproduct{0}{0}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}],

(34)

which is related to the absorption line shape
I(\omega)
via a Fourier transform:

I(\omega)\propto\real\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{t}C_{\bm{\mu}\bm{\mu}}(t)e^{\textup{i}\omega t}.

(35)

We assume that the magnitudes of the transition dipole moments are identical for all seven sites
7
;
17
:

\hat{\bm{\mu}}=\sum_{m=1}^{7}\bm{e}_{m}(\outerproduct{0}{m}+\outerproduct{m}{0}),

(36)

where the orientation vectors
\bm{e}_{m}
of each chromophore are assumed to point from the B to D nitrogen atoms of each Bchl
a
molecule
58
.

(a), the tensorial MKCT predicts an absorption lineshape identical to the DEOM reference; both results show qualitative agreement with the experimental data. The averaged time-correlation function

decays, MKCT only requires the calculation of a few moments with the DEOM Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DEOM}}
. These moments then yield memory kernels via a fast Padé approximant, allowing the correlation matrix
\bm{\mathcal{C}}(t)
to be efficiently computed in the frequency domain via the QME.

3.3
Transport properties in one-dimensional chain models

Thus far, we have demonstrated in Secs.
3.1
and
3.2
that the tensorial MKCT framework effectively captures general expectation values and the spectral features of multi-level open quantum systems. To further evaluate the versatility of this method, we now apply it to one-dimensional transport problems. Specifically, we investigate the dissipative Holstein model
26
and the global-solvent tight-binding model
24
.

To efficiently simulate the single-mode Holstein model, the original Hamiltonian
18
is transformed by incorporating the vibrational modes of frequency
\Omega
into the effective baths, as detailed in Refs.
25
;
15
;
26
. The resulting effective Hamiltonian is given by

\hat{H}_{\text{hol}}=\hat{H}_{\text{e}}+\hat{H}_{\text{vib}}+\hat{H}_{\text{I}}.

(37)

Here, the electronic component is described by a one-dimensional tight-binding model:

\hat{H}_{\text{e}}=J\sum_{j}(\hat{c}_{n+1}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{n}+\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{n+1}),

(38)

where
\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{c}_{n}
are the electronic creation and annihilation operators at the
n
-th site, respectively, and
J
represents the inter-site hopping integral. Note that the periodic boundary condition is applied. The effective vibrational Hamiltonian is given by

\hat{H}_{\text{vib}}=\sum_{n}\sum_{j}\omega_{nj}\hat{b}_{nj}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{nj},

(39)

where each site is coupled to an independent local bath. Here,

\hat{b}_{nj}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{b}_{nj}
are the bosonic creation and annihilation operators for the
j
-th bath mode at the n-th site. The system-bath interaction Hamiltonian is defined as

\hat{H}_{\text{I}}=\sum_{n}\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{n}\sum_{j}g_{nj}(\hat{b}_{nj}^{\dagger}+\hat{b}_{nj}).

(40)

where
g_{nj}
represents the coupling constant to the individual modes. The effect of these local interactions is characterized by the Brownian spectral density function:

J(\omega)=\frac{\lambda\Omega^{2}\gamma\omega}{(\omega^{2}-\Omega^{2})^{2}+4\gamma^{2}\omega^{2}}.

(41)

In this expression, the dimensionless parameter
\lambda
characterizes the electron-phonon coupling strength,
\Omega
is the characteristic vibrational frequency, and
\gamma
represents the dissipation parameter, or bath friction.

4
Conclusion

In summary, we have introduced a tensorial extension to the MKCT method. This generalized approach maintains an equation structure perfectly parallel to its scalar predecessor, with the crucial distinction that the moments
\bm{\Omega}_{n}
and memory kernels
\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{n}(t)
are now evaluated as tensorial quantities. The new formulation addresses key limitations of its scalar counterpart, enabling the direct and accurate calculation of general expectation values, including transient state populations and coherences. It also allows for the correct evaluation of correlation functions in multi-level systems at a computational cost significantly lower than that of DEOM. The successful application of this method to one-dimensional transport models demonstrates its robustness across varying temperature and coupling regimes. These results suggest that tensorial MKCT holds promise for simulating complex non-Markovian dynamics in larger open quantum systems.

Appendix A
Generalized quantum master equation for
\mathbf{\mathcal{C}}(t)
from Mori-Zwanzig projection techniques.

The derivation of Eq.
11
can be found in previous literature (e.g., Ref.
66
;
39
;
5
), but we include it here for completeness. We begin with the Liouville–von Neumann equation for the basis operator
\hat{\phi}_{i}
:

\derivative{t}\hat{\phi}_{i}(t)=\textup{i}\mathcal{L}e^{\textup{i}\mathcal{L}t}\hat{\phi}_{i}.

(44)

The full propagator
e^{\textup{i}\mathcal{L}t}
satisfies the operator identity

e^{\textup{i}\mathcal{L}t}=e^{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}t}+\int_{0}^{t}\differential{\tau}e^{\textup{i}\mathcal{L}(t-\tau)}\bm{\mathcal{P}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}e^{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\tau}.

(45)

Applying Eq.
45
to
\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{\phi}_{i}
yields:

\displaystyle e^{\textup{i}\mathcal{L}t}(\bm{1}-\sum_{j}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{j})\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{\phi}_{i}=e^{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}t}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{\phi}_{i}+\int_{0}^{t}\differential{\tau}e^{\textup{i}\mathcal{L}(t-\tau)}\sum_{j}\left(\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{j}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}e^{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\tau}\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{\phi}_{i}\right).

(46)

Using Eqs.
44
,
10
, and the definitions of the frequency matrix, memory kernel, and fluctuation operator [Eqs.
12
–
14
], we obtain:

\derivative{t}\hat{\phi}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j}\Omega_{ij}\hat{\phi}_{j}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\differential{\tau}\sum_{j}K_{ij}(\tau)\hat{\phi}_{j}(t-\tau)+\hat{f}_{i}(t).

(47)

Taking the trace
\Tr[(.)\hat{\phi}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}]
on both sides of Eq.
47
, we obtain the GQME for the correlation matrix:

\derivative{t}\mathcal{C}_{ik}(t)=\sum_{j}\Omega_{ij}\mathcal{C}_{jk}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\differential{\tau}\sum_{j}K_{ij}(\tau)\mathcal{C}_{jk}(t-\tau)+\Tr[\hat{f}_{i}(t)\hat{\phi}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}].

(48)

To complete the derivation of Eq.
11
, we show that the term
F_{ik}(t)\equiv\Tr[\hat{f}_{i}(t)\hat{\phi}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}]
vanishes for all
t
. We first verify that
F_{ik}(0)=0
:

\displaystyle F_{ik}(0)

\displaystyle=\Tr[\left\{(\bm{1}-\sum_{j}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{j})\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{\phi}_{i}\right\}\hat{\phi}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}],

(49)

\displaystyle=\Tr[(\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{\phi}_{i})\hat{\phi}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}]-\sum_{j}\Tr[(\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{\phi}_{i})\hat{\phi}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}]\Tr[\hat{\phi}_{j}\hat{\phi}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}]=0.

Here, we used the orthonormality condition

\Tr[\hat{\phi}_{j}\hat{\phi}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}]=\Tr_{s}[\hat{\phi}_{j}\hat{\phi}_{k}^{\dagger}]\Tr_{b}[\hat{\rho_{b}^{\text{eq}}}]=\delta_{jk}.

(50)

Next, we show that
\derivative{t}F_{ik}(t)=0
:

\displaystyle\derivative{t}F_{ik}(t)

\displaystyle=\Tr[\left\{(\bm{1}-\sum_{j}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{j})\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{f}_{i}(t)\right\}\hat{\phi}_{k}^{\dagger}],

(51)

\displaystyle=\Tr[\left\{\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{f}_{i}(t)\right\}\hat{\phi}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}]-\sum_{j}\Tr[\left\{\textup{i}\mathcal{L}\hat{f}_{i}(t)\right\}\hat{\phi}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}]\Tr[\hat{\phi}_{j}\hat{\phi}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{b}^{\text{eq}}]=0.

Since
F_{ik}(0)=0
and its time derivative vanish, we conclude that
F_{ik}(t)=0
. Substituting this into Eq.
48
yields Eq.
11
in the main text.
