Retrospective Cross‐Sectional Study of Transthoracic Echocardiographic Reports in Patients Diagnosed With Aortic Regurgitation in Mashhad, Iran; to Evaluate Echocardiographic Determinants of AR Severity, With Specific Focus on STJ‐Related Parameters
Authors: Ali Haghighi, Hedieh Alimi, Vafa Baradaran Rahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAortic regurgitation (AR) is associated with characteristic changes in aortic root geometry. Data on detailed echocardiographic patterns of AR in Iranian cohorts remain limited. This study aimed to describe the echocardiographic and etiologic characteristics of patients with AR and to assess the associations between standard aortic root measurements—including the aortic annulus (AA), sinuses of Valsalva (SOV), sinotubular junction (STJ), and ascending aorta (ASC)—and AR severity.MethodsThis retrospective cross‐sectional study reviewed transthoracic echocardiography reports of patients diagnosed with AR (n = 445) between 2017 and 2023 at a tertiary center. All examinations were performed and interpreted by a single experienced cardiologist. Aortic root dimensions and left ventricular measurements were extracted. Associations with AR severity were examined using appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests based on distributional assessment. Ordinal regression analysis was used to evaluate the independent association of aortic root dimensions and indexed parameters with AR severity.ResultsGreater AA, SOV, STJ, and ASC diameters were associated with higher AR severity in unadjusted analyzes, with SOV demonstrating the strongest relationship. All variables, including ASC, STJ, SOV, AA, and STJ/BSA, were correlated with the severity of aortic regurgitation. In multivariable ordinal regression analysis, ascending aorta diameter and the STJ/AA ratio were independently associated with AR severity, whereas STJ diameter and the STJ/BSA ratio were not significant after adjustment. STJ diameter showed no correlation with age. Degenerative and rheumatic etiologies were the most common causes of AR.ConclusionAortic root dimensions were associated with AR severity, underscoring the role of aortic root geometry in disease expression. The STJ/AA ratio emerged as an independently associated geometric parameter in adjusted analyzes, while STJ/BSA did not retain significance. As this was a retrospective cross‐sectional analysis, findings reflect associations rather than causal relationships and require confirmation in prospective, standardized studies.Keywords: aortic regurgitation, aortic root geometry, disease severity, sinotubular junction, transthoracic echocardiography

Introduction

Aortic regurgitation (AR) is one of the most common valvular heart diseases, with an estimated prevalence of 0.5% in the general population and a higher prevalence in older adults [1]. AR accounts for over 50% of aortic valve replacements in the United States and is detected in approximately 75% of older patients with calcific aortic stenosis, although in these cases, the severity of the disease is mild [2]. AR may be caused by diseases of the valve or by aortic root abnormalities, and it presents itself in chronic and acute forms [3, 4].

Chronic AR has a range of etiologies, including rheumatic heart disease and congenital valve abnormalities such as a bicuspid aortic valve [5]. In industrialized countries, age‐related degenerative changes and aortic root dilation are more common causes, with senile valvular calcification being particularly common in older adults [6, 7]. Idiopathic aortic root dilation, which is a leading cause of chronic AR, has been independently associated with aging but is not directly associated with blood pressure levels. This dilation brings about left ventricular enlargement, hypertrophy, and dysfunction [8].

Assessment of AR severity relies not only on Doppler‐based parameters but also on structural evaluation of the aortic root [4, 9]. Dimensions of the aortic annulus, sinuses of Valsalva (SOV), sinotubular junction (STJ), and ascending aorta play a major role in determining leaflet coaptation and therefore influence the degree and progression of AR [1, 10]. Previous research has shown that enlargement of specific segments of the aortic root—particularly the SOV and ascending aorta—correlates with AR severity and adverse outcomes, underscoring the prognostic value of precise aortic root measurements [7, 11]. Several studies have also highlighted the specific structural and functional importance of the sinotubular junction (STJ) in the pathophysiology of Furukawa et al. (1999) demonstrated that isolated dilation of the STJ can impair leaflet coaptation and directly contribute to functional aortic regurgitation, emphasizing the central role of STJ geometry in maintaining normal valve competence [8]. Building upon this, Maselli et al. (2007) showed that the ratio between the STJ and aortic annulus significantly affects aortic root configuration and valve dynamics; deviations from optimal STJ/annulus proportions were shown to alter cusp alignment and potentially predispose to regurgitation [10]. Similarly, Marom et al. (2013), using computational modeling of the aortic root, identified specific STJ‐to‐annulus ratios that optimize biomechanical performance and leaflet behavior, supporting the relevance of indexed STJ measurements in understanding AR mechanisms [12].

These findings collectively suggest that STJ‐related indices may provide meaningful structural and functional insight and highlight the need to further characterize these parameters within population‐specific clinical contexts. Given that aortic root diameters may vary across populations and are known to be affected by demographic and anthropometric factors such as age, sex, and body size [1, 7]. Population‐specific data are essential for accurate interpretation. However, evidence from Middle Eastern cohorts, including Iranian populations, is limited. The present study aims to characterize the echocardiographic features of AR in an Iranian cohort and to explore the relationship between AR severity and root dimensions, and to assess which parts of the aortic root are affected in the aortic regurgitation and see if an age and sex‐independent index can be found in the echocardiographic evaluation of aortic regurgitation with specific attention to the sinotubular junction and indexed parameters.

Methods

This study examined the echocardiographic reports of all patients diagnosed with AR who visited Ghaem Hospital in Mashhad between the years 2017 and 2023. All echocardiography reports were conducted by a single experienced operator to ensure consistency and accuracy in evaluations. The chosen time frame was selected in order to cover all the data available.

In this study, echocardiographic reports were gathered, and the individual patient data were entered systematically into SPSS version 25 by a researcher for statistical analysis, and were double‐checked by another researcher. The statistical analyzes were carried out by an experienced statistician in order to obtain meaningful results from the collected data and to assess correlations and various characteristics.

Study Design

This was a retrospective cross‐sectional review of echocardiography reports of patients with aortic regurgitation.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Patients were included if their echocardiography report documented the presence of AR. No age‐based exclusion criteria were applied.

Reports with incomplete or missing essential echocardiographic measurements were excluded from the analysis.

Variables

The variables examined in the echocardiographic reports include the following qualitative and quantitative variables:

Demographic Information: Name, age, gender, body surface area

Echocardiographic Features: LVEDD (Left Ventricular End‐Diastolic Diameter), LVEDV (Left Ventricular End‐Diastolic Volume), PAP (Pulmonary Artery Pressure), AA (Aortic Annulus), AO VTI (Aortic Valve Velocity Time Integral), LVOT VTI (Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Velocity Time Integral), STJ (Sinotubular Junction), SOV (Sinus of Valsalva), ASC (Ascending Aorta), LVEF (Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction), RWMA (Regional Wall Motion Abnormalities), LV Diastolic Dysfunction, MS (Mitral Stenosis), MR (Mitral Regurgitation), AS (Aortic Stenosis), AR severity, STJ/AA (Sinotubular Junction to Aortic Annulus Ratio), STJ/BSA (Sinotubular Junction to Body Surface Area Ratio).

Sample Size and Statistical Methods

The data analysis was carried out using SPSS version 25. The study population consisted of all patients with AR referred to the echocardiography department at Ghaem Hospital. A total of 445 patients were included in the study. First, the normality of the data was checked using the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test.

For descriptive statistics, the mean and standard deviation were used for normally distributed data, and for data that were presumed non‐normal, the median and interquartile range were utilized. The chi‐square test was used to compare qualitative variables between groups. Additionally, Pearson and Spearman correlations were performed to evaluate relationships between parametric and non‐parametric parameters. Ordinal regression was performed to evaluate the predictors of AR severity. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyzes. To handle missing data, deletion was performed for small amounts (< 5%), and multiple imputation was used for larger quantities.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in full compliance with ethical principles, and patient privacy was fully protected. All echocardiographic reports were reviewed retrospectively. During data extraction, no direct patient identifiers (including names, national identification numbers, medical record numbers, or contact information) were recorded in the study database. Each record was entered using a non‐identifiable study code. The dataset was accessible only to the study investigators and was not shared with third parties. All analyzes were conducted on de‐identified data.

The requirement for informed consent was waived by the institutional ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data.

Ethical Approval Code and Date

Results

Descriptive Results

Most patients showed preserved left ventricular wall motion, with no detectable abnormalities in approximately half of the cohort (about 52%). When present, wall motion abnormalities were predominantly regional rather than global, with regional wall motion abnormalities being more frequent (≈29%) compared with septal or global abnormalities (each ≈9.5%).

Diastolic function was largely preserved or only mildly impaired, with nearly half of patients having normal diastolic function (≈48.5%) and most of the remainder demonstrating grade I dysfunction (≈40.7%), while advanced diastolic dysfunction was relatively uncommon (grades II–III combined < 11%).

Mitral valve involvement was common but generally mild to moderate in severity. Most patients exhibited either mild or mild‐to‐moderate mitral regurgitation (together ≈66%), whereas severe regurgitation was observed in a small minority (≈5.6%), and complete absence of mitral regurgitation was infrequent (≈2%).

In contrast, mitral stenosis was absent in the majority of patients (≈78%), and when present, severe forms accounted for a limited proportion of cases (≈14.8%). Similarly, most patients did not have concomitant aortic stenosis (≈77.6%), with the remaining cases distributed across mild‐to‐severe categories, without a clear predominance of advanced disease.

Valve morphology analysis showed that rheumatic and degenerative changes were the most common cusp abnormalities (≈28% and ≈27.3%, respectively), followed by congenital abnormalities (≈10.1%) and isolated aortic root dilation (≈12.6%), while about one‐fifth of patients had no identifiable cusp pathology (≈21.1%).

Evaluation of the aortic root demonstrated that the majority of patients had normal root dimensions (≈60.3%). Among abnormal findings, isolated ascending aorta dilation was the most frequent pattern (≈30.3%), whereas dilation of the sinus of Valsalva alone (≈1.4%) or combined dilation of the sinus and ascending aorta (≈8.1%) was less commonly observed (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, echocardiographic reports from 445 patients with aortic valve regurgitation were analyzed. The mean age of the patients was 58.25 years, with an almost equal number of males and females. The prevalence of coexisting valvular diseases, aortic cusp abnormalities, and aortic root abnormalities across different severity categories of AR was reported. AR severity relationship with various factors, including aortic root diameters, was analyzed. It was observed that rheumatic and degenerative changes were the most common cusp abnormalities among our patients. Among patients with severe aortic regurgitation, isolated aortic root dilation was most common, which could be due to various genetic and non‐genetic factors in the Iranian population, highlighting the need for further research. It was observed that aortic root abnormalities and aortic cusp abnormalities were significantly related to AR severity. When multivariable ordinal regression analysis was performed to adjust for potential confounders, only selected aortic root–related parameters remained independently associated with AR severity. Specifically, ascending aorta diameter showed a significant positive association with increasing AR severity, while age demonstrated an inverse association. Among STJ‐related indices, only the STJ‐to–ascending aorta (STJ/AA) ratio remained independently associated with AR severity, whereas absolute STJ diameter and STJ indexed to body surface area (STJ/BSA) did not show independent associations in the adjusted model.

Hemodynamic changes in the left ventricle (LV) are crucial in evaluating the impact of AR on cardiac function. LV dilation is typically seen in cases of moderate‐to‐severe chronic AR, whereas acute severe or mild chronic AR may not lead to LV dilation [4, 9]. In a study by Siani et al. on chronic aortic regurgitation with moderate to severe intensity, LV dilation was reported with an end‐diastolic diameter over 56 mm, end‐diastolic volume over 82 mL/m², end‐systolic diameter over 40 mm, or end‐systolic volume over 30 mL/m² [13]. In our study, more than half of the patients demonstrated LV diastolic dysfunction. The mean LVEDD was approximately 49 mm, and the mean LVEDV was around 114 mL/m². Among our patients with moderate‐to‐severe AR, 47.8% had an LV end‐diastolic diameter above 56 mm, and 87.5% had an LV end‐diastolic volume above 82 mL/m². Given potential confounding factors, such as ischemic heart disease, ventricular dilation cannot be attributed solely to AR.

A Swedish registry study reported that among 13,853 patients with AR, 17.9% had concurrent aortic stenosis (AS) and 10.7% had mitral regurgitation [5]. In our study, approximately 23% of patients had co‐existing AS. In a retrospective study by Yang et al., moderate‐to‐severe MR was present in 14% of patients with chronic moderate‐to‐severe AR and was associated with higher mortality [6]. In our cross‐sectional cohort, 30% of patients with moderate‐to‐severe AR had concomitant moderate‐to‐severe MR; however, no significant association was observed between MR severity and AR severity. This discrepancy may be explained by limited access to patients' medical histories and by the inclusion of patients with varying underlying conditions and MR severity. A study by Butcher et al. on patients with bicuspid aortic valves found that severe MR accompanying severe AR was associated with higher mortality, whereas MR in the absence of severe AR was not. These findings suggest that the clinical impact of co‐existing MR may depend on disease severity and patient selection [14].

A 2011 study on the epidemiology of valvular heart disease identified degeneration as the most common cause of AR in industrialized countries, accounting for 50.3% of cases, followed by rheumatic and congenital causes, each at 15.2% [15]. In our study, degeneration (27.3%) and rheumatism (28%) were the most common causes of aortic regurgitation, highlighting the high prevalence of rheumatic disease in this region. In a 2022 study on valvular disorders, 30% of patients with bicuspid aortic valves had moderate‐to‐severe AR, which was consistent with our finding of 29% moderate‐to‐severe AR in patients with bicuspid valves in this study [16].

Isolated aortic root dilation was most frequently observed among patients with severe AR. Root dilation may result from older age, aortic wall weakening, and atherosclerotic risk factors such as smoking, diabetes, and dyslipidemia [1, 2]. We suggest investigating the causes of isolated root dilation in Iran in future studies.

Our study also found significant associations between the diameters of the sinuses of Valsalva, ascending aorta, aortic annulus, and sinotubular junction with body surface area (BSA), and between SOV, ASC, and AA with age. However, while STJ diameter was associated with BSA, no significant relationship was observed between STJ diameter and age. Although unadjusted analyzes suggested an association between STJ/BSA and AR severity, this relationship did not persist after multivariable adjustment. In contrast, the STJ/AA ratio demonstrated an independent association with AR severity, suggesting that relative aortic root geometry, rather than absolute STJ size or simple indexation to BSA, may better reflect structural remodeling associated with increasing AR severity. Consistent with our findings, Campens et al. reported that SOV and ASC diameters strongly correlate with age, BSA, and weight [7].

Furukawa et al. examined STJ dilation and its impact on AR in patients with normal aortic valves, finding that STJ diameter can predict AR post‐aortic valve surgery [8]. Maselli et al. observed that increased STJ size could lead to central AR jetting, whereas an STJ diameter below 30 mm could cause eccentric AR jetting. They also recommended an optimal STJ‐to‐annulus ratio close to 1:1, which was supported by findings of a 2013 study suggesting an ideal STJ‐to‐annulus ratio between 0.8 and 1.0 [10].

Lacanna et al. found that an STJ/AA ratio greater than 1.66 was associated with severe functional AR [17]. In contrast to prior studies emphasizing absolute STJ measurements, our findings support the potential relevance of the STJ/AA ratio as an exploratory index associated with AR severity. Differences between studies may reflect heterogeneity in patient populations, underlying etiologies such as rheumatic or degenerative disease, and variations in aortic root geometry.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our study. The lack of access to patients' medical and surgical histories and their medication use may introduce bias or limit the comprehensiveness of our analysis, potentially impacting the reliability of observed relationships. echocardiographic examinations were performed as part of routine clinical care rather than for research purposes, and echocardiographers were not blinded to patients' clinical information, which may have introduced measurement or interpretation bias. Furthermore, intra‐observer and inter‐observer variability could not be assessed because the study was based on retrospective review of archived echocardiographic reports without repeated measurements or re‐evaluation by independent observers. The observational nature of our study could overlook potential confounding variables such as comorbidities. Finally, the cross‐sectional design limits our ability to draw causal inferences, underscoring the need for future research with larger, more diverse samples and longitudinal designs to confirm our findings.

Conclusion

This cross‐sectional study analyzed echocardiographic findings in 445 patients with aortic valve insufficiency and identified significant associations between aortic regurgitation severity and left ventricular functional parameters. After multivariable adjustment, the STJ‐to–ascending aorta (STJ/AA) ratio emerged as the only STJ‐related parameter independently associated with AR severity, while STJ indexed to body surface area did not retain statistical significance.

Isolated aortic root dilation was more frequently observed among patients with severe aortic valve insufficiency; however, its underlying mechanisms, including potential genetic or regional factors, require further investigation. Rheumatic heart disease and degenerative changes were the most commonly observed etiologies of aortic regurgitation in this cohort, consistent with previously reported regional patterns. Given the cross‐sectional design and potential confounding by unmeasured clinical variables, causal inferences cannot be drawn.

Future prospective studies incorporating comprehensive clinical data and longitudinal follow‐up are needed to validate these findings and to further clarify the role of relative aortic root geometry in the assessment of aortic regurgitation severity.
