A new eucrocopodan archosauriform from the Middle Triassic of southern Brazil and the phylogeny of Euparkeriidae
Authors: Maurício S. Garcia, Gabriela M. Cerqueira, Francesco Battista, Marco B. de Andrade, Rodrigo T. Müller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Archosauriformes comprise a diverse range of reptiles, including the crown-group Archosauria, which flourished during the Triassic Period. Early-diverging archosauriforms, such as proterosuchids and erythrosuchids, are becoming progressively well-known due to recent studies and consistently resolve at the base of the clade. More crownward, Eucrocopoda includes archosauriform taxa that increasingly approximate the ancestral archosaur body plan. Early-diverging eucrocopodan archosauriforms have a widespread paleogeographic record but remain poorly understood in terms of ingroup relationships. Within this radiation, Euparkeriidae is particularly challenging, because its ingroup composition and monophyly is debated, with some authors supporting a non-monospecific Euparkeriidae, whereas others fail to recover this hypothesis. The eponymous Euparkeria capensis is known from the Early to Middle Triassic of South Africa, whereas other putative euparkeriids are primarily known from the Early to Middle Triassic of China, Germany, Poland, and Russia. Here, we describe a new early-diverging eucrocopodan (Silescelida acristata gen. et sp. nov.) from the Middle Triassic of southern Brazil. Phylogenetic analyses incorporating this taxon suggest a possible placement within Euparkeriidae, though its position shows instability depending on the operational taxonomic units considered, especially among other putative euparkeriids. This discovery not only informs on the temporal and paleogeographic distribution of euparkeriids but also sheds light on the origin and early evolution of eucrocopodans, representing the first record of this archosauriform grade in the Triassic of Brazil. More broadly, the new taxon underscores the significance of South American Triassic deposits within the evolutionary history of archosauriforms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53740-9.

Introduction

Archosauriformes comprise a diverse range of reptiles, including the crown-group Archosauria, which flourished during the Triassic Period and subsequently gave rise to major vertebrate lineages such as dinosaurs (including birds), pterosaurs, and crocodylomorphs, establishing a long-lasting evolutionary dominance in terrestrial ecosystems1–3. The Early to Middle Triassic marks the onset of the diversification and rise of non-archosaurian archosauriforms to ecological dominance2,4,3,5,6,1. Understanding these taxa is central not only because they were key components of early Mesozoic ecosystems, but also because they provide insight into the geographical and temporal origins, ancestral morphology, and subsequent character evolution of early archosaurs2,4. Within non-archosaurian archosauriforms, recent work has mostly focused on the anatomy and phylogeny proterosuchids and erythrosuchids, sprawling-gaited quadrupedal predators4,7–12. On the other hand, the semi-erect gracile quadrupedal predatorial early-diverging members of Eucrocopoda or stem-archosaurs (forms outside Proterochampsia plus Archosauria), have received relatively less attention since a series of revisions of the clade Euparkeriidae13–15,6.

This clade is mainly known by the emblematic stem archosaur Euparkeria capensis from the Early to Middle Triassic of South Africa16, and is currently the only definite member of Euparkeriidae13,17. Other putative euparkeriids come essentially from China, Germany, Poland, and Russia13–15,6,18. Euparkeria capensis and Euparkeriidae have been placed just outside Archosauria in all the major modern phylogenetic analyses of non-archosaurian archosauriforms2,4. As a result, this species, along with other putative euparkeriids, holds great importance for understanding archosaurian origins, as it has long been regarded as representing an approximation of the ancestral body plan of Archosauria2,13,19,20.

Numerous archosauriform taxa have been recovered from the Middle Triassic of Pinheiros-Chiniquá Sequence of the Santa Maria Supersequence in southern Brazil, ranging from proterochampsians21–23, to archosaurs such as pseudosuchians24–30 and avemetatarsalians24,31–34. However, all of these are included in the Proterochampsia plus Archosauria clade. This unit, nonetheless, preserves an important fauna from the transition from the Middle Triassic to the early Late Triassic. Recently, Philipp et al.35 corroborate a Ladinian age for the Pinheiros-Chiniquá Sequence and with recent fieldwork and reassessment of collection materials, a variety of new tetrapod specimens and taxa have been published in recent years36,27,37–39,33,32,28,21,40,41,29,34,23,22,30,42.

In this contribution we present a new stem-archosaur taxon, with possible affinities to Euparkeriidae, from the Pinheiros-Chiniquá Sequence. This is the first taxon of this unit to fall outside the Proterochampsia plus Archosauria clade. Previously, a portion of the specimen bearing its collection code had been accidentally lost, hindering the verification of its provenance and associated data for over 20 years. However, during a recent visit to the collection at the Pontifícia Universidade Católica do Rio Grande do Sul, MSG and GSM rediscovered the missing piece, allowing for a complete assessment of the specimen. Herein we describe the new taxon and investigate its phylogenetic position, exploring its implications for stem-archosaur radiation and diversity in the Middle Triassic deposits of southern Brazil.

Phylogenetic analysis. MCP 4186-PV was scored in the data matrix of Sengupta et al.43, which is one of the latest iterations of the CoArTreeP (the Complete Archosauromorph Tree Project4, comprising a wide array of Triassic archosauromorphs. We added the following operational taxonomic units (OTUs): “Wangisuchus tzeyii” (holotype only, based on Sookias et al.15), “Turfanosuchus shageduensis” (based on Sookias et al.15), Marcianosuchus angustifrons18, and an OTU combining the scores of “Turfanosuchus shageduensis” and Halazhaisuchus qiaoensis, following the hypothesis that these are possibly conspecific15.

The dataset totaled 287 OTUs and 919 characters. We analyzed the dataset using TNT v1.6 software44, mostly employing the same methodology as in previous iterations of the dataset. We deactivated characters 9 and 119 before the searches and the following characters were considered additive (ordered): 100, 122, 127, 146, 153, 156, 157, 171, 176, 177, 187, 202, 221, 227, 263, 266, 278, 279, 283, 324, 327, 331, 337, 342, 345, 351, 352, 354, 361, 365, 368, 370, 377, 379, 386, 387, 398, 410, 414, 424, 425, 430, 435, 446, 448, 454, 455, 458, 460, 463, 464, 470, 472, 478, 482, 483, 485, 489, 490, 502, 504, 510, 516, 520, 521, 529, 537, 546, 552, 556, 557, 567, 569, 571, 574, 581, 582, 588, 636, 648, 652, 662, 701, 731, 735, 737, 738, 743, 749, 766, 784, 803, 809, 810, 816, 850, 851, 872, 875, 885, and 888.

The following OTUs were deactivated before the tree searches in all analyses because they were scored only for the purpose of morphological disparity analyses, are not diagnostic at a species level, or are combinations between other OTUs: ISIR 1132, Protanystropheus antiquus (= Tanystropheus antiquus), Trachelosaurus fischeri, Tanystropheus haasi, Malerisaurus all NA, Arctosaurus osborni, CRILAR-Pv 461, CRILAR-Pv 462, CRILAR-Pv 497, Chanares rhynchosaur, PVSJ 2728, Eorasaurus olsoni, Archosaurus rossicus holotype, Blomosuchus georgii holotype, Samsarasuchus pamelae expanded, Kalisuchus rewanensis combined, UFSM 11444, UFSM 11394, UNIPAMPA 271, UNIPAMPA 684, Vonhuenia friedrichi holotype, Chasmatosuchus rossicus holotype, Jaikosuchus Ochev hypodigm, Chasmatosuchus vjushkovi, Koilamasuchus gonzalezdiazi, CRILAR-Pv 499, Shansisuchus kuyeheensis, Uralosaurus combined, Triopticus primus, Otter Sandstone archosaur, Dagasuchus santacruzensis, Ctenosauriscus koeneni, Hypselorhachis mirabilis, Waldhaus poposauroid, Jaikosuchus + Vytshegdosuchus, Bystrowisuchus flerovi, Bromsgroveia walkeri, Lutungutali sitwensis, Nyasasaurus parringtoni, and Ignotosaurus fragilis. Petrolacosaurus kansensis was used as the outgroup for rooting the most parsimonious trees (MPTs).

The analyses were conducted under implied weighting with concavity constants (k) of 23, average of the range of values recommended by Ezcurra45. The same (or similar) value was also used by Ezcurra et al.10, Sengupta et al.43, and Dalle-Laste et al.46 in their recent iterations of this dataset. Maximum number of trees in the memory was set to 100.000. Search strategies for the MPTs include the ‘New technology search’ with a combination of the tree-search algorithms: Sectorial searches, Ratchet and Tree Fusing at default settings, until 100 hits of the same minimum tree length were achieved. The most parsimonious trees obtained were subjected to a final round of tree bisection reconnection (TBR) and reconnection branch swapping. Zero-length branches in any of the recovered most parsimonious trees were collapsed.

Holotype. MCP 4186-PV, a left scapula, a right ilium, and a left femur (Fig. 1C, D). The elements were found in association (attached to each other) and are size-compatible; therefore, they are considered to belong to the same individual.

Etymology. The generic name combines the Latin siles (“silence”) and the Greek skelēs (“hind leg”), referencing the loss of the proximal femur bearing the specimen’s collection code, which obscured its provenance until its recent rediscovery. The specific epithet derives from the Latin prefix a- (“without”) and cristāta (“crested”), referring to the absence of a crest or flange attachment site for the m. caudofemoralis (= fourth trochanter), a diagnostic feature of the taxon.

Type locality, age, and horizon. Posto (or Posto de Gasolina; Fogliarini) site (29°37’38.9”S 53°22’06.5”W), municipality of Dona Francisca, Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil (Fig. 1A, B). Dinodontosaurus AZ of the Pinheiros-Chiniquá Sequence of the Santa Maria Supersequence, Paraná Basin51,52. It is considered Ladinian in age30,35,53–58.

Diagnosis. The holotype of Silescelida acristata differs from all other known non-archosaurian archosauriforms in the following combination of traits: scapular blade without an anteroposterior constriction at its base; asymmetrical expansion of the scapular blade at its dorsal portion; straight posterior margin of the scapular blade, forming a right angle with the dorsal margin of the glenoid; elliptical tuber for the m. triceps dorsal to the glenoid; dorsal margin of the dorsal iliac blade straight in dorsal and lateral views; dorsal iliac blade as deep as the acetabulum; notch between the ischiadic peduncle and the ventral apex of the medial acetabular wall; medially expanded femoral head; absence of a longitudinal groove on the proximal surface of the femoral head; posteromedial tuber as the only well-developed tuber on the femoral head; absence of an elevated mound or crest-like structure as the attachment site for the m. caudofemoralis (local autapomorphy); extensor fossa on the anterior surface of the femoral distal end; distal femoral condyles with the same degree of ventral development (not uneven).

Description. Scapula. The left scapula is incomplete, lacking the dorsal portion of the scapular blade and the anteriormost portion of the acromion process, and will be described held vertically (dorsal and ventral will be used instead of distal and proximal) (Fig. 2). The preserved portion of the scapular blade is smooth and mostly featureless, measuring 48.4 mm from the ventralmost portion to the level of the acromion process and glenoid fossa. The scapular blade is lateromedially compressed, and its medial surface is concave, with a gentle curvature. Dorsal to the scapular body, the scapular blade extends without an anteroposterior constriction and expands dorsally. The degree of this expansion cannot be assessed because the element is incomplete, but it is clearly asymmetrical, where the posterior margin expands more dorsally than the anterior one. However, the minimum anteroposterior width of the scapular blade is 23.4 mm, and the anteroposterior maximum width of the scapular blade is 28.4 mm. Both the anterior and posterior margins of the scapular blade are concave, but the posterior margin, as preserved, is straighter because of the asymmetrical dorsal expansion of the blade. The acromion process is only partially preserved.

The angle between the dorsal margin of the acromion process and the anterior margin of the scapular blade is obtuse. The preserved portion of the acromion process forms a straight angle relative to the glenoid fossa. The length of the scapular body from acromion process to glenoid fossa is 42.5 mm. Dorsal to the glenoid fossa, in the posterior portion of the scapular body in lateral view there is an elliptical tuber for the m. triceps. The glenoid fossa corresponds to a rounded cavity in the posterior surface of the scapular body. It projects posteriorly and its dorsal margin forms a right angle in relation to the posterior margin of the scapular blade. The margin of articulation with the coracoid is irregular and extends more ventrally on its posterior half. In medial view, a fragment of bone is attached to this area. It corresponds to a fragment of the femoral shaft, which remained attached to the scapula during preparation.

Ilium. The preserved right ilium is incomplete, lacking the pubic peduncle, the preacetabular process, and the anterior portion of the dorsal iliac blade (Fig. 3). The preserved portion of this blade measures 45.2 mm in length and 21.7 mm of maximum height to the supraacetabular crest. The dorsal iliac blade is straight in dorsal view and does not present a marked concavity above the acetabulum. Its dorsal margin is also straight in lateral view. The postacetabular process is well-developed and tapers posteriorly. Its ventral surface is straight and lacks both a brevis shelf and fossa. The ischiadic peduncle is mostly vertically oriented, with a moderately expanded articular surface. Along the ventral margin of the acetabulum, which is convex and subtriangular in lateral view, there is a notch between the ischiadic peduncle and the ventral apex of the medial acetabular wall. The acetabulum excavates the ventral half of the lateral surface of the ilium. It is subcircular and roofed by a poorly developed supraacetabular crest. The acetabulum is 21.7 mm in height, from the ventral apex of the medial acetabular wall to the supraacetabular crest. Only the posterior portion of the supraacetabular crest is preserved. It extends laterally but does not cover the dorsal portion of the acetabulum in lateral view. In medial view, there is a gently raised shelf extending from above the acetabular medial wall to the postacetabular process. Scars and striations in this area suggest the attachment sites for the two primordial sacral vertebrae.

Femur. The left femur is nearly complete and will be described held vertically (as in parasagittal stance) (Fig. 4). Sections of the femoral shaft around its mid-portion remained attached to the ilium during preparation. The element has a maximum proximodistal length of 172.9 mm. The femur is sigmoid in posteromedial and anterolateral views. The femoral head is moderately expanded and is anteromedially oriented, but does not present a kink or concavity on its ventral surface in the transition from the shaft. Therefore, transition from the femoral head to the shaft is smooth and continuous. The proximal surface of the head is lacks a longitudinal groove. The posteromedial tuber is the largest and more distinct of the femoral proximal tubera. Other tubera, such as the anteromedial and anterolateral are poorly expanded. There is no longitudinal sulcus medial to the posteromedial tuber. The long axis of the femoral head measures 43.3 mm in length. No evidence of an anterior trochanter, trochanteric shelf or anterolateral scar is present.

In the proximal half of the femur, near the mid-portion of the shaft on the posterior surface, faint striations are present, corresponding to the attachment site of the m. caudofemoralis (i.e., fourth trochanter of archosauriforms). This area is barely elevated with respect to the shaft but does not form a distinct mound or crest. In the distal end of the femur, there are three well-defined condyles. All condyles project posteriorly and present the same degree of ventral development. In distal view, the condyles are separated by a popliteal fossa. This fossa extends proximodistally for about 20% of the total femoral length. Condyles are subequal in size, except for the crista tibiofibularis, which is slightly smaller than the lateral and medial condyles. The surface between the lateral condyle and crista tibiofibularis is smooth and does not present a proximodistally oriented sulcus. The anterior surface of the distal end is concave and has a weak extensor fossa. The distal surface of the femur is excavated by a curved intercondylar groove, extending from the medial condyle to the crista tibiofibularis. The long axis of the distal end measures 36.1 mm.

The scapular blade of Silescelida acristata lacks a proximal anteroposterior constriction (Fig. 5A–F), a condition shared with several early-diverging eucrocopodans (e.g., Halazhaisuchus qiaoensis, Marcianosuchus angustifrons) and poposauroids (e.g., Poposaurus gracilis and Shuvosaurus inexpectatus)15,18,59,60, but contrasting with taxa in which the blade narrows proximally before expanding distally, such as some early-diverging eucrocopodans (e.g., “Turfanosuchus shageduensis”), the aphanosaur Teleocrater rhadinus, loricatans (e.g., Prestosuchus chiniquensis), and the erpetosuchid Parringtonia gracilis15,27,61,62.

Distally, the scapular blade of Silescelida acristata is asymmetrically expanded, a feature also observed in some eucrocopodans (e.g., Halazhaisuchus, “Turfanosuchus shageduensis”), the erythrosuchid Garjainia prima and the loricatan Batrachotomus kupferzellensis9,15,63, but absent in most early archosaurs (e.g., Parringtonia, Prestosuchus, Teleocrater)27,61,62.

The posterior margin of the proximal half of the scapular blade in Silescelida acristata is straight and forms a right angle with the dorsal margin of the glenoid (Fig. 5A–F). This configuration is shared with several early-diverging archosauriforms (e.g., Garjainia, Halazhaisuchus) and pseudosuchians (e.g., Prestosuchus, Shuvosaurus)9,15,27,60. Although the scapular blade of Teleocrater also has a straight posterior margin, the posteroventral orientation of its glenoid prevents the formation of a right angle between these margins62.

An elliptical tuber for attachment of the m. triceps dorsal to the glenoid (Fig. 5A–F) is present in Silescelida acristata and several early-diverging eucrocopodans (e.g., Halazhaisuchus, “Turfanosuchus shageduensis”, Marcianosuchus), whereas pseudosuchians typically exhibit a teardrop-shaped tuber and erythrosuchids a rounded morphology15,18. Parringtonia uniquely combines a teardrop-shaped tuber with an additional dorsal tubercle61, while poposauroids and aphanosaurs show either a reduced scar (e.g., Poposaurus) or complete absence of a homologous structure (e.g., Teleocrater, Shuvosaurus, Mandasuchus tanyauchen)59,60,62,64.

The iliac blade of Silescelida acristata has a straight dorsal margin in lateral view (Fig. 5G–J), a condition shared with some early-diverging eucrocopodans (e.g., Dorosuchus neoetus, Euparkeria capensis, Osmolskina czatkowicensis) and Mandasuchus6,14,64,65, but differing from erythrosuchids (e.g., Erythrosuchus africanus, Garjainia), many pseudosuchians (e.g., Gracilisuchus stipanicicorum, Parvosuchus aurelioi, Poposaurus, Shuvosaurus), and dinosauromorphs in which the margin is convex or concave11,29,32,32,59,60,66. Although the iliac blade of Batrachotomus kupferzellensis shows a mostly straight dorsal margin laterally, it differs from Silescelida acristata in having a sigmoid outline in dorsal view63. Moreover, the iliac blade has about the same depth as the acetabulum in Silescelida acristata, whereas in most pseudosuchians (e.g., Prestosuchus, Tainrakuasuchus bellator), the acetabulum is deeper than the iliac blade.

A notch between the ischiadic peduncle and the ventral apex of the convex medial acetabular wall is present in Silescelida acristata (Fig. 5G–J) and some early-diverging eucrocopodans (e.g., Dorosuchus, Euparkeria), and several early archosaurs (e.g., Tainrakuasuchus, Teleocrater, Gondwanax paraisensis)6,14,30,34,62. In derived poposauroids and dinosauromorphs, partial or total acetabular perforation renders this region non-comparable59,60.

The femur of Silescelida acristata exhibits an expanded, anteromedially oriented femoral head (Fig. 5K–V), a widespread condition among eucrocopodans (e.g., Euparkeria capensis, Mandasuchus, Prestosuchus, Gondwanax)13,27,34,64, but absent in non-eucrocopodan archosauriforms2,4. In Stenoscelida aurantiacus the femoral head is expanded and anteriorly oriented, whereas in other proterochampsids it is medially oriented67. Parvosuchus, on the other hand, presents a poorly expanded femoral head29.

Unlike several eucrocopodan taxa that bear a longitudinal proximal groove in the femoral head (e.g., Stenoscelida, Mandasuchus, Teleocrater, Gamatavus antiquus)32,62,64,67, this feature is absent in Silescelida acristata and some early-diverging eucrocopodans (e.g., Dorosuchus, Euparkeria) and some pseudosuchians (e.g., Parvosuchus, Batrachotomus)13,14,29,63.

In Silescelida acristata, the posteromedial tuber is the only well-developed tuber on the femoral head (Fig. 5K–V) as in “Turfanosuchus shageduensis”, whereas in most eucrocopodans present either well-developed proximal femoral tubera or a reduced posteromedial tuber14,29,32,59,62,64,67. A ligament sulcus separating the posteromedial and anteromedial tubera is absent in Silescelida acristata but present in derived poposauroids and some dinosauromorphs59,60,68–70.

The complete absence of a distinct attachment site for the m. caudofemoralis (= fourth trochanter) in Silescelida acristata (Fig. 5K–V) is shared with Gondwanax, lagerpetids, and pterosaurs71–73, whereas most eucrocopodans show a low crest, flange or mound-like structure (2,4,14,15,27,59,62,66), and poposauroids exhibit extreme reduction or modification of this attachment site. In Shuvosaurus this attachment site is developed as a deep fossa60.

An extensor fossa on the anterior surface of the femoral distal end (Fig. 5K–V) is present in Silescelida acristata, some archosauriforms (e.g., Dorosuchus, Garjainia, Stenoscelida, Poposaurus and Shuvosaurus), and to a lesser degree in “Turfanosuchus shageduensis”9,14,15,59,60,67. The anterior surface of the femoral distal end is nearly straight or convex, lacking completely an extensor fossa, in most archosauriforms, (e.g., Retymaijychampsa, Teleocrater, Gondwanax, Mandasuchus, and Prestosuchus)22,27,34,62,64.

Finally, the distal femoral condyles of Silescelida acristata are symmetrically developed ventrally (Fig. 5K–V), a condition shared with most eucrocopodans (e.g., Euparkeria capensis, Marcianosuchus, Gondwanax, Stenoscelida)13,18,34,67, but contrasting with the asymmetric condylar development observed in many pseudosuchians (e.g., Prestosuchus, Batrachotomus) and the proterochampsian Retymaijychampsa22,27,63.

Discussion and final remarks

Phylogenetic analysis. In most analyses Asperoris mnyama, Cuyosuchus huenei, and Dorosuchus are found as the earliest-diverging eucrocopodans. Halazhaisuchus and “Turfanosuchus shageduensis” are consistently recovered as sister taxa in the analyses where they are considered as separate OTU. Similarly, in all topologies where only the holotype of Osmolskina was included instead of the complete hypodigm, it consistently nests with NMQR 3570 outside Archosauriformes. Besides Euparkeria, the only taxon to nest within Euparkeriidae in nearly all topologies is “Wangisuchus tzeyii”. Taken together, these results highlight the complex and unstable relationships among early-diverging eucrocopodans and need for revision of some taxa.

Moreover, we recovered divergent positions for Osmolskina when employing data from either the holotype or the complete hypodigm. The “complete” Osmolskina was consistently recovered as an early-diverging eucrocopodan archosauriform (Fig. 6A, B), whereas the holotype nested as a non-archosauriform archosauromorph sister taxon to NMQR 3570 (Fig. 6C, D), a partial left maxilla from the Lystrosaurus Assemblage Zone of the lower Katberg Formation74. The same results occur in previous versions of this dataset (e.g., Sengupta et al.43). When Osmolskina is completely absent from the analysis, previous iterations recover NMQR 3570 as early-diverging non-proterosuchid archosauriform (e.g., Sengupta et al.43). This could mean that either the complete hypodigm of Osmolskina is possibly chimeric (since other unnamed archosauromorphs are known from the same locality; Borsuk-Bialynicka and Evans75) or that the morphology of the maxilla of non-archosaurian archosauromorphs is conservative (or plesiomorphic). We favor the second hypothesis at the moment because the cranial and postcranial material of Osmolskina is overall consistent with the morphology observed in other early-diverging eucrocopodans (e.g., Euparkeria, Halazhaisuchus), although referral of disassociated elements to a single taxon could be problematic.

Concerning the putative euparkeriids added to the analysis, “Turfanosuchus shageduensis” was consistently recovered as the sister taxon to Halazhaisuchus (Fig. 6A, D), potentially supporting the possibility that they are conspecific15. However, because their OTUs differ in several character states, we argue “Turfanosuchus shageduensis” may be closely related to Halazhaisuchus qiaoensis without necessarily being conspecific with the latter. Additionally, there is little to no support for a close relationship between Turfanosuchus dabanensis and “Turfanosuchus shageduensis”. Their distinct character states are reflected in their disparate phylogenetic positions: while “Turfanosuchus shageduensis” is recovered as an early-diverging eucrocopodan archosauriform (as in Sookias et al.15), Turfanosuchus dabanensis is placed as a gracilisuchid pseudosuchian, as observed in previous analyses29,76. 13 steps are required to force monophyly between Turfanosuchus dabanensis and “Turfanosuchus shageduensis” in our dataset. Regarding “Wangisuchus tzeyii”, its inclusion in the dataset was exploratory given that the holotype consists solely of a single undiagnostic partial maxilla. All analyses nested “Wangisuchus tzeyii” within Eucrocopoda, and most specifically nearly all recovered it as an euparkeriid (Fig. 6). Throughout our analyses, this was the only OTU to repeatedly nest as an euparkeriid.

All of our phylogenetic analyses consistently placed Silescelida acristata as an eucrocopodan archosauriform. In some of the trees of analysis II, Silescelida acristata nests within Euparkeriidae, suggesting possible affinities with this clade, but in most other topologies across all analyses the placement of Silescelida acristata varies within the base of Eucrocopoda. Hence, Silescelida acristata represents the first early-diverging eucrocopodan taxon for the Triassic of southern Brazil and the first putative euparkeriid for the Triassic of South America.

The monophyly of a non-monospecific Euparkeriidae remains ambiguous, as phylogenetic results vary among analyses. While some recover a monophyletic Euparkeriidae, others instead resolve putative euparkeriids as paraphyletic lineages or large polytomies leading toward Proterochampsia and Archosauria. This pattern suggests that Euparkeriidae may represent a more restricted clade than traditionally assumed, or that multiple stem-archosaur lineages retained the euparkeriid-like morphology, plesiomorphic for eucrocopodans. Consequently, early eucrocopodan diversification during the Early–Middle Triassic was likely complex, potentially involving parallel evolutionary trajectories across different paleogeographic regions. A comprehensive reassessment of putative euparkeriids is therefore needed to clarify their relationships, taxonomic validity, and the role of convergence among early-diverging eucrocopodans.

Taxonomic status and implications. Silescelida acristata is considered an early-diverging eucrocopodan archosauriform based on a combination of traits: anteromedially oriented femoral head; absence of a longitudinal groove in the proximal surface of the femoral head; posteromedial tuber as the only well-developed tuber on the femoral head; attachment site for the m. caudofemoralis in the posterior surface of the femur without a trochanteric fossa; attachment site for the m. caudofemoralis developed as a faint bump rather than a mound or crest or an internal trochanter; attachment site for the m. caudofemoralis not convergent with the proximal end of the femur; well-separated condyles in the femoral distal end. These traits support Silescelida acristata not only outside the Proterochampsia plus Archosauria clade, but also outside Proterosuchidae and Erythrosuchidae2,4.

Silescelida acristata is among the youngest records of early-diverging eucrocopodan and putative euparkeriid worldwide occurring in the Ladinian (Figs. 6D and 7A). Asperoris of Tanzania may be of approximate age as Silescelida acristata (94), but Cuyosuchus from Argentina is currently the youngest taxon of this grade (although it does not nest with Euparkeria in any of the analyses) and the only one to unambiguously occur in the Late Triassic77. Other putative euparkeriids (assuming non-monospecific Euparkeriidae) come from China, Germany, Poland, and Russia (Fig. 7A), whereas Euparkeria comes from South Africa13. Apart from the latter, which comes from Early Triassic sediments16, all other early-diverging eucrocopodans from southern Pangaea come from late Middle Triassic or early Late Triassic sediments (Nesbitt et al.78; Ezcurra et al.77; this contribution) (Fig. 7A). On the other hand, all early-diverging eucrocopodans from northern Pangaea occur in the Early Triassic and early Middle Triassic13(Fig. 7A). This could represent either a later dispersal into southwestern Pangea or that the forms found in South America, for instance, are late survivors of dispersals that occurred as early as the Early Triassic, but which cannot yet be corroborated by body fossil records.

Recent efforts in collecting and studying archosauromorphs from the Lower Triassic Sanga do Cabral Supersequence of southern Brazil have revealed a variety of forms, but no eucrocopodan archosauriform is currently known from these beds12,79–82. Through Silescelida acristata (Fig. 7B) we provide further evidence for South America as a critical region in archosauriform evolution. Previous studies have largely focused on the radiation of proterosuchids and erythrosuchids in the aftermath of the end-Permian extinction1,3,4,10, with relatively less emphasis on Middle Triassic eucrocopodans. This is because some of them are now considered nomina dubia or are too fragmentary to further assess without new material14,15. The presence of a previously unrecognized early-diverging eucrocopodan in Brazil suggests that these taxa were more widespread than previously appreciated and supports the notion that South America played a vital role in early archosauriform diversification.
