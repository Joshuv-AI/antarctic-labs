Lived experience of nursing students during their paediatric clinical placement: a qualitative study protocol
Authors: Sahar Alviri, Sara Rahimi, Pooya Pakzad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPaediatric clinical education presents unique challenges for nursing students owing to the clinical complexity and emotional sensitivity of caring for ill children and their families. This high-stakes period profoundly influences students’ professional identity. This study aims to explore the lived experiences of nursing students during their paediatric practicum through a descriptive phenomenological lens.Methods and analysisThis study will be conducted using a qualitative design with a descriptive phenomenological approach. Data will be collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews with undergraduate nursing students who have completed their paediatric clinical rotation. Purposive sampling will be employed, continuing until data saturation is achieved. Interviews will be recorded in a quiet confidential setting and subsequently transcribed verbatim. Data analysis will follow Colaizzi’s seven-step method, involving independent analysis by three researchers followed by group consensus. To ensure rigour and trustworthiness, strategies such as researcher triangulation, member checking and maintaining an audit trail will be utilised.Ethics and disseminationThe Ethics Committee of Qazvin University of Medical Sciences has granted ethical approval for this study. The findings will be published in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at relevant conferences. Furthermore, the results will be shared with nursing faculties and paediatric nursing departments to inform educational practices.

Introduction

Clinical education forms a critical bridge between theoretical knowledge and practice in nursing curricula, fostering essential competencies such as patient care, critical thinking, teamwork and evidence-based practice. Students describe effective clinical learning environments as safe, supportive and motivating, promoting professional attributes including accountability and communication skills. However, clinical placements in paediatric wards present unique challenges, including the care of critically ill children, high parental expectations and complex triadic communication among physicians, children and nurses.1

The undergraduate nursing programme in Iran spans eight semesters over a 4-year period. Throughout this curriculum, students complete comprehensive coursework in both child health and paediatric nursing. This instruction follows a structured sequence, wherein theoretical education in these domains is consistently succeeded by corresponding clinical training. During the paediatric practicum, students encounter children across various developmental stages. This exposure allows them to assess growth and development, identify health needs and deliver comprehensive, family-centred nursing care throughout the continuum of health and illness.2 Despite its importance, clinical placement in Iran particularly in paediatric settings is frequently disrupted by multiple challenges that adversely affect students’ learning and lived experiences. These include predominantly theoretical pre-clinical preparation with limited hands-on practice in essential skills such as suctioning and Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR), absence of insurance coverage, heavy and poorly structured shift schedules, excessive focus on routine tasks at the expense of meaningful learning opportunities, negative interactions with staff, subjective and unclear evaluation methods, inadequate physical facilities and ongoing uncertainty regarding the development of professional identity.3

Studies carried out in Qom and Kashan, among other parts of Iran, have highlighted the lack of efficient clinical facilitators. These results emphasise attention to important barriers like poor communication in clinical settings, heavy workloads, insufficient supervision and a focus on routine tasks.4 In Yazd, the fragmented nature of clinical placement, lack of cooperation from the healthcare team and restricted access to welfare and educational facilities are obstacles to attaining educational goals.5 Unsupportive environments and insufficient teacher competency are learning obstacles in Tehran that lower the standard of care.6

There are four main types of general challenges: instructors, uncooperative patients, task performance and time/space constraints. These challenges can be mild to severe.7 Difficulties in patient interaction, such as communicating with taciturn (74.4%), agitated (58.9%) or non-verbal (54.4%) individuals, combined with theory-practice gaps and lack of experience, exacerbate student anxiety.8 In Shiraz, poor communication with staff and patients, not being ready and emotional responses like anxiety and feeling incompetent make it harder to learn.9 Furthermore, verbal/physical violence, fear, socio-cultural challenges and inefficient training undermine support.10

Graduate nurses require diverse competencies to manage organisational complexities and provide personalised nursing, necessitating a strong focus on clinical reasoning and decision-making.11 In the field of paediatric nursing, where peer support, multi-source feedback and a safe environment are essential, students deal with issues like fear of dealing with sick children or challenging parents.12

Strong communication, professional competence and motivational support can all be fostered by clinical educators. Conversely, their efficacy is compromised by flaws like apathy and a dependence on experience rather than scientific evidence.13 Future career decisions are greatly influenced by paediatric clinical experiences. While bad experiences serve as a deterrent, positive, encouraging learning environments make an organisation appealing for future employment.14 The readiness of Iranian students is often compromised by Agony of Failure, apathy, intellectual inadequacy, alienation from the environment, reality shock and fear of medical errors.15 Themes like relational practice with children and families, knowledge consolidation, identity formation and exposure to new clinical aspects enrich the learning experience in Canadian paediatric nursing, but limitations still impede advancement.16

A qualitative phenomenological study is both necessary and strategic given the variety of issues found and the special characteristics of paediatric wards. By capturing the genuine voices of nursing students, this protocol extracts the deep meanings, feelings and personal narrative of their experiences with sick children and their families. In addition to identifying current obstacles, examining these lived experiences offers a fundamental framework for improving clinical procedures and raising the standard of paediatric nursing education.

Methodology

Study design

This study employs a qualitative design with a descriptive phenomenological approach grounded in Husserlian philosophy. Descriptive phenomenology seeks to explore and describe the essential structure of a phenomenon as experienced by individuals from their first-person perspective, while emphasising bracketing and the suspension of researchers’ preconceptions.17 This approach was considered most appropriate for the present study because the aim is to obtain rich descriptions of nursing students’ lived experiences during paediatric clinical placement and to identify the shared meanings and essential dimensions of these experiences.

Descriptive phenomenology was selected over interpretive (Heideggerian) phenomenology because the current study prioritises detailed description of participants’ experiences rather than interpretive reconstruction of meaning within broader sociohistorical contexts.18 In addition, unlike grounded theory, which aims to generate explanatory theory, or case study methodology, which focuses on an in-depth understanding of a bounded system or case, descriptive phenomenology is specifically concerned with describing the essence and common structure of lived experience across participants.19

Data will be collected through individual, face-to-face, in-depth semi-structured interviews and analysed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method, which is philosophically aligned with the Husserlian descriptive phenomenological tradition and therefore ensures methodological coherence throughout the study.20 In the study, the reporting will be done considering the criteria of COREQ.21

Research team and reflexivity

The research group will include three nursing researchers with experience in clinical student teaching. Interviews will be conducted by one member of the research team who has received training in qualitative interviewing techniques. Given the researchers’ prior clinical and educational experiences, reflexivity and bracketing will be maintained throughout the study process to minimise the influence of preconceptions on data collection and analysis. Prior to and during the interviews and analysis, researchers will identify and document their assumptions, expectations and experiences related to paediatric clinical education through reflexive journaling and regular team discussions. Bracketing will be used to help maintain focus on participants’ descriptions of their lived experiences rather than researchers’ interpretations, consistent with Husserlian descriptive phenomenology.17 22 Data analysis will be conducted collaboratively, and analytical decisions will be reviewed through consensus among the researchers. The interviewer had no prior educational or evaluative relationship with the students.

Participants

The study participants will be undergraduate nursing students enrolled at a university of medical sciences who have completed their first paediatric clinical placement. These students are clinically experienced with children and their families and are in a good position to discuss the lived experience of placement in a paediatric clinical environment. The inclusion criterion of this study will include having a paediatric placement, willing and prepared to take the in-depth individual interview and being able to communicate his/her experience in Persian in a clear and fluent form. The exclusion criteria of this study will be failure to complete the placement, lack of desire to remain involved and inability to express experiences verbally.

Patient and public involvement

As this qualitative protocol focuses on the lived experiences of nursing students during paediatric clinical placement. Key stakeholder perspectives within the nursing education context informed the development of the research questions and interview guide. Participant involvement will occur through member checking, with a subset of students invited to review preliminary interpretations. On study completion, a plain-language summary of the findings will be shared with participants, and results will be disseminated to relevant nursing education stakeholders to inform improvements in clinical training.

Sampling method

Purposive sampling will be adopted in this study since the researcher will consider individuals who have relevant and rich experience of internship in the paediatric sector and who will volunteer to participate in the study. Both sexes will be selected to help represent a diversity of views. This sampling method is used when conducting phenomenological studies to reach meaningful and rich experiences.19 Recruitment will be conducted by the research team, all of whom are faculty members of the nursing school. Eligible students will be identified based on the inclusion criteria and informed about the study through an initial informational message delivered by a faculty member who is not directly involved in their clinical assessment. To minimise potential power imbalance between faculty researchers and student participants, students will be clearly informed that participation is entirely voluntary and that participation or non-participation will not influence their academic evaluation, clinical assessment or relationship with faculty members. Recruitment and interview arrangements will be conducted in a manner intended to reduce perceived coercion and support open expression of experiences. Informed consent will be obtained prior to data collection.

Sample size

The process of information gathering will last until data saturation is achieved. Interviews will continue until data saturation is achieved, defined as the point at which no new themes or insights emerge from the analysis. Saturation is viewed as a subjective and context-specific process in qualitative studies that have to be analysed systematically and thoroughly.22 Creswell recommended a sample of 6–18 participants in phenomenological research and stated that it might be altered according to the saturation.21

Data collection

Once the required permission is received, the researcher will sit with the nursing students in the university or children’s hospital and following the explanation of why the study is being conducted and the informed consent will request them to join the study. To begin with, they will be informed that their involvement in this study is voluntary and that the information will not be shared with any other party. Semi-structured face-to-face and individual interviews with the participants will be used to collect the data. The interviews are expected to take 30–60 min and will be held in a quiet place where no other individuals will be present to provide the interviewee with a friendly atmosphere where he/she is not afraid to express himself/herself freely. All the interviews will be tape-recorded with the consent of the participants. The primary interview questions were to be developed in accordance with the research literature and being focused on the lived experience of the participants. Non-directive and open-ended questions were applied to enable unstructured description of the experiences.

Ethics and dissemination

The objectives of the research will be clarified to the students, and informed consent will be received among the interviewees. The students will be informed that they can be involved in the study on a voluntary basis and they have a chance to withdraw at any time. They will be informed that the information they will provide and the interviews will not be disclosed to the researchers and it will not disrupt the course procedure, exams and their grades. This study will be conducted in full accordance with the ethical principles for medical research involving human participants as outlined in the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.23 The Ethics Committee of Qazvin University of Medical Sciences has also given its consent to this study with ethics code IR.QUMS.REC.1404.315. Students will not receive financial benefits from this study. The interviewer had no prior educational relationship with the students. Students participating will be told ways in which the interviews will be carried out, transcripts of the interviews and where they will be stored. The findings will be published in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at relevant conferences. Furthermore, the results will be shared with nursing faculties and paediatric nursing departments to inform educational practices.

Data analysis

The analysis of data based on the in-depth individual interviews will be carried out through the descriptive phenomenological approach and Colaizzi’s seven-step analysis. The interview guide was developed based on a review of relevant literature and guided by descriptive phenomenological principles, with a focus on exploring nursing students’ lived experiences during paediatric clinical placement. Open-ended and non-directive questions were used to encourage rich, in-depth descriptions. The interview guide is presented in table 1. The interview shall be tape-recorded and transcribed verbatim under the consent of participants. Two researchers will listen to the audio files multiple times and transcribe the interviews verbatim to enhance accuracy. In case there were any deviations in the transcripts, the latter was revised by returning to the audio file and a single final version was obtained.

Study timeline

The study design was finalised in September 2025. To evaluate the clarity, flow and appropriateness of the interview questions, as well as the estimated interview duration, a pilot interview was conducted with an undergraduate nursing student in November 2025. Data from the pilot interview will not be included in the final analysis. The main data collection interviews are planned to commence in February 2026 and will continue until data saturation is achieved. It is anticipated that data analysis will be completed by June 2026, followed by manuscript submission in July 2026, with expected publication in 2027.

Discussion

Paediatric clinical clerkships offer a crucial paradigm of achieving the knowledge, skills and attitudes of nursing students. Students face special challenges because of the complicated character of this sphere such as communicating with different ages,16 adjusting to parental expectations1 and special situations in emotional relations like terminal disease or death.12 The article will seek to give an in-depth interpretation of these lived experiences. Findings are expected to provide useful information on the challenges, needs and opportunities in this important educational stage. In addition, the findings will be used to design education to be applied to faculty to reduce student anxiety in the paediatric environment. Finally, these lessons can be applied to the clinical environment to make it more conducive, encouraging and productive to a learning process of nursing students.
