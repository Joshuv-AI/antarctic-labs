Core-shell particle engineering overturns conventional purity-corrosion dependence in Mg alloys
Authors: Jeong-Ki Kim, Jaeho Kwon, Du-Won Min, Joonghoon Kim, Seongwook Chae, Seung Geol Lee, Byoungkoo Kim, Sung Soo Park, Nack J. Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magnesium (Mg) alloys offer high weight-saving potential for transportation technologies; however, their use remains constrained by poor corrosion resistance, which is strongly dependent on alloy purity. The presence of impurities, such as iron (Fe), severely degrades the corrosion resistance of Mg alloys. Herein, we present an alloy design strategy that substantially improves the corrosion resistance, even when using low-purity Mg. Our method produces core-shell particles, where an Fe-rich core is encapsulated by an Fe-depleted shell embedded in the Mg matrix. This shell acts as a local microscavenger that isolates the core and suppresses impurity-triggered corrosion, resulting in corrosion resistance achievable only when using ultrahigh-purity Mg. This approach provides a practical pathway for the development of high-performance Mg alloys without relying on costly purification.

INTRODUCTION

Magnesium (Mg) alloys, which are the lightest structural alloys, were actively used in military aircraft and missiles during World War II, with global production reaching a maximum of 232 kilotons per year (1–7). Despite the subsequent expansion of the transportation system and the growing need for fuel efficiency improvements in the 21st century, the current demand for Mg as a lightweight material is rather low (8, 9). This is mainly because Mg alloys, although acceptable for short-term use, have not yet demonstrated sufficient long-term reliability, particularly in terms of corrosion resistance, which limits their applicability in numerous weight-sensitive systems (10–13).

The corrosion resistance of Mg alloys is strongly dependent on their purity (i.e., the higher the purity, the greater the corrosion resistance) (14). Therefore, the most direct and effective way to solve corrosion-related issues associated with purity levels is to manufacture alloys using ultrahigh-purity Mg (UHP-Mg) with ≥99.999 weight % (wt %) purity. However, UHP-Mg is substantially more expensive than lower-grade high-purity Mg (HP-Mg, ≤99.99 wt % purity) (15, 16). The most common impurity in commercial Mg alloys is Fe, which has limited solubility in Mg and can form coarse intermetallic particles during solidification with other alloying elements, such as Al, which is one of the most widely used Mg-alloying elements (17–19). Al-Fe intermetallic particles have a very high electrochemical nobility in relation to Mg (20, 21), and in a corrosive environment, these particles act as microcathodes, promoting the decomposition of the relatively active Mg via microgalvanic corrosion, which is the main type of corrosion in Mg alloys. To address this issue, a small amount [∼0.1 atomic % (at %)] of Mn has been traditionally added in Mg-Al–based alloys to make Fe exist within the electrochemically less noble Al-Mn [e.g., Al(Fe,Mn)] particles rather than Al-Fe particles (e.g., Al5Fe2), with a resultant improvement in corrosion resistance (22, 23). In other words, this can be considered as the scavenging of Fe atoms into Al-Mn intermetallics. Recently, it has been shown that depending on the Fe and Mn contents, Fe-rich Al(Fe,Mn) particles can be surrounded by low-Fe Al8Mn5 shells in a high (∼9 wt %) Al-containing Mg alloy (24). Although Fe atoms are clearly entrapped in the core phase of such particles and are therefore not in direct contact with Mg, no clear improvement in corrosion resistance has been confirmed in these alloys, possibly because the electrochemical nobility of the shell phase, Al8Mn5, is still considerably higher than that of the Mg matrix and thus is not immune to microgalvanic corrosion (25, 26). Nevertheless, experimental observations would provide insights into the effectiveness of replicating such a unique phase transformation behavior in other Mg alloys with an outer shell made of a phase that suppresses microgalvanic corrosion.

In this study, we report an alloy design concept that can achieve the excellent corrosion resistance of UHP-Mg, even when using the commercially preferred HP-Mg. The desirable microstructural evolution is such that during solidification, a deleterious Fe-rich core primary phase is completely encapsulated by an Fe-free shell phase, forming a core-shell duplex structure, which is then surrounded by a later-forming solid Mg. In such cases, Mg is separated from the corrosion-inducing Fe-rich phases. We implemented this concept in the most common Mg alloy type (Mg-Al). The corrosion-specific alloy design reported here includes an alloying element that most effectively enables the formation of core-shell duplex particles capable of entrapping impurities.

RESULTS

Alloy concept and element to entrap Fe

In our alloy design, Fe-rich particles are to be encapsulated by an Fe-depleted particle, forming a core-shell duplex structure. For such a phase transformation to occur during solidification, the Fe-depleted phase must form before Mg is formed. However, this must occur after the formation of the Fe-rich phase, which forms as the primary solid phase in Mg-Al alloy systems (24, 27, 28). To the best of our knowledge, no attempt has been made to intentionally create an alloy with the aforementioned phase-transformation behavior. In a conventional Mg–2.7Al–0.12Mn–0.002Fe (at %) alloy, for instance, three phases of Al(Mn,Fe), Mg, and Al8Mn5 are thermodynamically predicted to form sequentially from the liquid phase at an early stage of solidification (Fig. 1A). The formation temperature of the Al8Mn5 phase is lower than that of Mg. In such cases, the Fe-rich Al(Mn,Fe) phase is expected to be surrounded by Mg rather than Al8Mn5. This may also explain why the formation of an Fe-rich core/low-Fe shell duplex structure is rarely observed in Mg-Al alloys, except in a few high-Al–containing alloys (24). This indicates that Mn is not a suitable alloying element for this alloy design.

In addition to the formation of the abovementioned core-shell duplex structure, an additional requirement is that the outer shell phase in contact with Mg should be less noble than the core phase and preferably have an electrochemical potential similar to that of the Mg matrix. The alloying elements that satisfy the requirement of similar electrochemical potentials between the shell phase and Mg are primarily rare-earth (RE) elements (29, 30). Table S1 lists the RE elements showing the difference between their standard reduction potentials (SRPs) and that of Mg, ranging from 0.01 (La and Pr) to 0.27 (Sc). Mn has a much higher SRP (1.18) than RE elements. To determine whether any of these RE elements could form a solid phase before Mg during solidification, thermodynamic calculations of the formation start temperatures of the Al-X (X = RE) and Mg phases (TAl-X and TMg, respectively) were performed for RE-microalloyed Mg–2.7Al–0.03X (in at %) alloys under Scheil cooling mode (table S1). The Fe impurity was not considered in the calculations because the thermodynamic database involving RE elements with Fe is currently incomplete, and the formation sequence between the Al-X and Mg phases in each RE-containing alloy is of primary interest. Of the various RE elements considered, only the addition of Sc was expected to result in the formation of the Al-X (Al2Sc) phase before Mg formed during solidification. In addition, compared with other Al-X phases (except X = Y, Tm, and Lu), the fraction of Al2Sc was relatively low (0.1%), which should be beneficial for reducing the risk of microgalvanic corrosion.

To visually represent the results presented in table S1, the temperature-dependent phase fractions of three representative Mg–2.7Al–0.03X (X = Y, Gd, and Sc) alloys were calculated in the temperature range of 500° to 800°C, where the liquid, Mg, and Al-X phases exist (Fig. 1, B to D). For the Y- or Gd-containing alloys, the first solid phase to form appears to be Mg, and then an Al-X phase (i.e., Al2Y or Al2Gd) begins to form at temperatures 23.5° to 40.9°C lower than the TMg, as shown in Fig. 1 (B and C). Meanwhile, for the Sc-added alloy (Fig. 1D), Al2Sc is formed as the primary solid phase at a temperature ∼44°C higher than TMg. These results suggest that the co-addition of Al and Sc to Mg is a possible approach for isolating any Fe-rich phase from Mg by placing an interphase layer of Al2Sc. Therefore, alloys with Y or Gd are expected to have a microstructure in which the Fe-rich and Al-X (X = Y or Gd) phases exist separately within the Mg matrix (Fig. 1E). However, alloys with Sc can exhibit a microstructure in which the Al-X phase encapsulates the Fe-rich phase (Fig. 1F).

Additional thermodynamic calculations showed that TAl-X increased with increasing X content, and the threshold contents where TAl-X and TMg become the same are listed in table S1. Sc has a much lower threshold content than all the other RE elements considered. For example, more than 0.18 at % (0.65 wt %) of Y is required to form an Al-Y phase before Mg during solidification. In contrast, only 0.014 at % (0.026 wt %) of Sc is needed to make TAl-X and TMg equal, demonstrating that Sc is the element that satisfies the alloy design concept of TAl-X > TMg, even with the smallest addition among the RE elements considered.

Sc-based core-shell particle formation

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed approach, a Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc (at %) alloy was experimentally fabricated by conventional ingot casting. A Mg–2.7Al (at %) alloy without Sc was prepared for comparison. The composition of the analyzed alloys is summarized in table S2. The Fe impurity content of both alloys was 45 parts per million (ppm), similar to the Fe content of commercial Mg alloys such as AZ31B (31). On the basis of the data reported thus far (32), the Fe-rich primary phases possible in Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc (with Fe impurities) include AlxFe2-xSc, Al8Fe4Sc, and Al2Sc. Our first-principles calculations indicate that the formation energy of AlxFe2-xSc is the lowest among the possible phases, suggesting the probability of its formation in the alloy (table S3).

Both alloys showed similar polygranular microstructures with Mg grain sizes of ∼320 μm and fine spotty particles embedded in the Mg matrix (fig. S1) in the solutionized condition. The fine particles had a rather irregular morphology, and the corresponding energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) maps showed that the particles in the binary Mg–2.7Al alloy mainly contained Al and Fe, whereas those in the ternary Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy contained Al, Fe, and Sc (fig. S2). Fe, which exists as an impurity element in the alloys, is detected mostly in the center of the particles in the Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy, suggesting that the particles in the Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy consist of a duplex structure. Unlike Fe, impurities such as Cu and Ni were not clearly detected within these particles, regardless of the addition of Sc.

A more detailed analysis of the particles within the alloys was conducted using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and EDS. The particles observed in the Mg–2.7Al alloy were identified as the orthorhombic Al5Fe2 phase (fig. S3), as reported elsewhere (21). TEM and EDS showed that the particles present in the Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy consisted of two phases, where one phase encapsulated the other. As shown in Fig. 2A, the inner core phase was enriched with Al, Fe, and Sc, whereas the outer shell phase was enriched with Al and Sc, with no trace of Fe. The TEM diffraction analysis of the inner core phase indicated that it had a hexagonal close-packed structure with lattice parameters of a = 0.530 nm and c = 0.866 nm (Fig. 2B). By contrast, the outer shell phase had a face-centered cubic structure (a = 0.758 nm), as shown in Fig. 2C. These two phases have an orientation relationship of [0001]core // [111]shell and (1¯21¯0)core // (2¯20)shell (Fig. 2D). From additional quantitative EDS analysis (fig. S4), we concluded that the inner core phase is AlxFe2-xSc (x = 1.5 to 1.8 in this study), similar to those reported previously (32), and the outer shell phase is Al2Sc.

As schematically shown in Fig. 2E, the formation of such a unique core-shell–type duplex structure proceeds in such a way that the core phase (AlxFe2-xSc) is first formed, and then the shell phase (Al2Sc) grows through heterogeneous nucleation on the surface of the core phase in the melt, completely encapsulating the core phase. As the calculated lattice mismatch between AlxFe2-xSc and Al2Sc is approximately 1.2%, they are expected to have low interfacial energies promoting subsequent nucleation and growth of the Al2Sc phase on the surface of the AlxFe2-xSc particle.

Corrosion effect of Sc microalloying

Figure 3 shows the effect of Sc microalloying on the corrosion properties of the Mg–2.7Al alloy after immersion in a 0.6 M aqueous NaCl solution. Observations after immersion indicate that the Mg–2.7Al alloy was severely damaged under the corrosive condition (Fig. 3A), whereas the Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy appeared to retain its original rectangular shape without appreciable macroscopic degradation (Fig. 3B). The difference in corrosion behavior between the alloys was analyzed through cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) micrographs, which showed that a cracked oxide layer with a thickness of 200 to 600 μm forms on the binary Mg–2.7Al alloy (Fig. 3C), whereas a relatively thin oxide layer with a thickness of <30 μm is observable in the ternary Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy (Fig. 3D). The evolution of the corrosion phenomena in the alloys with immersion times of up to 72 hours is presented in Fig. 3 (E to H). For the binary Mg–2.7Al alloy, filiform corrosion initiated and proceeded immediately after immersion for 0.5 hours and gradually extended with increasing immersion time. However, such filiform corrosion was not detected in the ternary Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy at immersion times of up to 72 hours.

The abovementioned change in the corrosion behavior via the microalloying addition of Sc was measured through weight-loss-based evaluation (Fig. 3I). The binary Mg–2.7Al and ternary Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloys had corrosion rates of 22.2 ± 5.1 and 0.38 ± 0.05 mm year−1, respectively, showing that the ternary Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy was approximately 60 times more corrosion-resistant than the binary Mg–2.7Al alloy under the corrosive condition utilized in the present study (table S4). The generated H2 gas was simultaneously measured during the immersion procedure, and the measured H2 volume values were 25.0 ± 3.45 and 0.29 ± 0.05 ml cm−2 for the Mg–2.7Al and Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloys, respectively, confirming the relatively slow H2 evolution reaction in the latter. The calculated corrosion rates based on the generated H2 volume values were 17.4 ± 2.40 and 0.20 ± 0.03 mm year−1 for the Mg–2.7Al and Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloys, respectively. Figure 3I shows that the present HP-Mg-grade Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy had a lower corrosion rate than the UHP-Mg-grade Mg–2.7Al alloy reported in the literature, although the former had more than six times the Fe content than the latter (45 versus 7 ppm) (21). As shown in Fig. 3I, the Mg–2.7Al alloy (Fe content of 45 ppm) investigated in this study exhibited a corrosion rate 40 times higher than that of the UHP-Mg–grade Mg–2.7Al alloy (Fe content of 7 ppm), confirming the detrimental effect of Fe on corrosion resistance. These results clearly demonstrate that the level of corrosion resistance achievable using UHP-Mg can be realized by the microalloying addition of Sc without further refining for Fe reduction. We believe that this alloying method could be a better option for improving corrosion resistance than costly purification to obtain UHP-Mg.

This study showed that the addition of Sc to an HP-Mg–grade Mg-Al alloy (Fe content of 45 ppm) resulted in the formation of core-shell duplex particles, which might prevent the occurrence of microgalvanic corrosion (33, 34). To evaluate the relative susceptibility of the alloys to microgalvanic corrosion, scanning Kelvin probe force microscopy (SKPFM) was conducted to measure the Volta potentials of the particles present in the alloys with and without Sc microalloying (Fig. 4). The core-shell duplex particle present in the ternary Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy had a much lower Volta potential than the single-phase Al5Fe2 particle in the binary Mg–2.7Al alloy (Fig. 4, A to D), suggesting that the Sc-containing ternary Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy had a relatively lower tendency to undergo microgalvanic corrosion than the binary Mg–2.7Al alloy without Sc addition. Notably, the Volta potential level of the Al2Sc shell, which is in direct contact with the Mg matrix in the Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy, is only ∼30% of that of Al5Fe2 present in the Mg–2.7Al alloy. Corrosion-induced morphological changes occurred, particularly near the second-phase particles that could act as microcathodes, indicating the occurrence of microgalvanic corrosion in the alloys. The Mg–2.7Al alloy showed the distinctive formation of oxide scales and circular pits around the Al5Fe2 particles (Fig. 4E). A distinct corrosion behavior was observed for the Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy, which showed the formation of only dome-like oxide scales without pitting around the core-shell duplex particles (Fig. 4F). This indicates that microgalvanic corrosion between the Mg matrix and second-phase particles occurred more slowly in Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc than in the Mg–2.7Al alloys.

Only dome-like oxide scales without pitting formed around the core-shell–type particles in the Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy, whereas distinctive oxide scales and circular pits appeared around Al5Fe2 particles in the Mg–2.7Al alloy, demonstrating the distinctive difference in corrosion resistance between these alloys.

The possible effect of Sc microalloying on modifying the stability of the surface oxide film, thereby improving the corrosion resistance of the Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy, was ruled out because electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) did not show any appreciable difference in the oxide layer between the two alloys (fig. S5 and table S5). Instead, the improved corrosion resistance is attributed to the suppression of the cathodic reaction arising from Sc-induced modifications to cathodic particles. In the Sc-free alloy, Fe-rich intermetallics act as highly active cathodic sites owing to their relatively noble Volta potential compared to the Mg matrix. This potential difference results in strong galvanic coupling, promoting electron transfer to these particles and accelerating the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), which consumes electrons released from Mg dissolution. In contrast, with the addition of Sc, the Volta potential of the intermetallic particles is reduced because of Sc incorporation into the intermetallic phases. This lowers the potential difference with respect to the Mg matrix, thereby weakening the galvanic driving force for HER. Furthermore, these particles evolve into core-shell structures consisting of an Fe-rich core and an Fe-depleted shell, where the shell exhibits a lower Volta potential than the core. Because the shell is preferentially exposed to the electrolyte, HER predominantly occurs on the shell surface, while the contribution from the Fe-rich core is limited because of restricted exposure. Consequently, electrons from Mg dissolution are preferentially transferred to the shell rather than the core, effectively redirecting the electron transfer pathway. In addition, the reduced potential of the Fe-rich core phase becomes particularly relevant when the core is locally exposed because of surface grinding or polishing. In such cases, electron transfer to the exposed core must proceed through the surrounding shell whose lower electrical conductivity as an intermetallic phase imposes kinetic limitations. These combined effects suppress the cathodic reaction, thereby enhancing corrosion resistance. Consistent with this interpretation, the alloy containing insufficient Sc to induce the formation of the core-shell phase (Mg–2.7Al–0.01Sc) exhibited inferior corrosion resistance, as shown in figs. S6 and S7.

To determine the commercial viability of the proposed approach, Sc was added to one of the most commonly used Mg alloys, Mg–2.7Al–0.38Zn (at %), equivalent to Mg–3Al–1Zn in wt %, that is, AZ31 without Mn, and its corrosion resistance and tensile properties were evaluated (fig. S8). As in the case of the binary Mg–2.7Al alloy, the addition of 0.03Sc to the Mg–2.7Al–0.38Zn alloy resulted in a marked improvement in corrosion resistance (from 58.4 to 0.47 mm year−1 after 72 hours), indicating the commercial viability of Sc microalloying for improving the corrosion resistance of Mg alloys. The corrosion resistance of the Mg–2.7Al–0.38Zn–0.03Sc alloy was also higher than that of the AZ31 alloy (0.47 versus 3.52 mm year−1 after 72 hours); however, their tensile properties were comparable, indicating that the Sc-containing Mg alloy can be directly used in weight-sensitive applications requiring high corrosion resistance.

DISCUSSION

In summary, the proposed alloy design route enables the development of highly corrosion-resistant Mg alloys. This demonstrates that the level of corrosion resistance enhancement that can be achieved by microalloying Sc at compositions similar to those of existing commercial Mg alloys is far greater than previously thought, which might lead to the widespread application of structural Mg alloys in the future. Furthermore, the relatively simple recipe proposed here is compatible with existing commercial processes used in the Mg industry. Therefore, the proposed method for tailoring Fe-containing particles could be useful in many other Mg alloy applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation and microstructural characterization

Pure Mg (purity 99.98 wt %), pure Al (purity 99.99 wt %), and pure Sc (purity 99.9 wt %) were used to prepare Mg alloys with nominal compositions (at %) of Mg–2.7Al and Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc. To facilitate the alloying of Sc, a Mg–10Sc (wt %) master alloy was prepared before the Mg–2.7Al–0.03Sc alloy. The alloys were produced by induction melting in a graphite crucible under purging with inert gases from a CO2 and SF6 mixture. Each alloy sample in the molten state was stabilized at 700°C for 10 min, and then it was poured into a steel mold preheated to 200°C. The cast samples were 12 mm in thickness, 50 mm in width, and 100 mm in height. The cast samples were subjected to heat treatment at 420°C for 1 hour to enhance microstructural homogeneity, followed by water quenching. The samples for microstructural characterization were prepared by mechanical grinding and surface polishing. Mechanical grinding was carried out in a water atmosphere with SiC papers up to 1200 grit, followed by surface polishing in an ethanol atmosphere using 1.0-μm diamond paste and a 0.04-μm colloidal silica solution. Microstructural examinations were conducted using a Leica DM4 M optical microscope, a Quanta 200-FE SEM with an EDAX Apollo X EDS system, and a JEM-2100F Cs-corrected TEM device. All TEM characterizations, including STEM and EDS, were performed at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. A Quanta three-dimensional Field Emission Gun (FEG) focused ion beam (FIB) equipment was used to prepare the TEM samples. Initial milling was performed at an accelerating voltage of 30 kV, and final milling was performed at 5 kV to minimize the FIB-induced damage.

Computational methods

Thermodynamic calculations of the phase evolution were performed using FactSage software version 8.3 with the FTlite database for Mg-based alloys. To approximate the nonequilibrium solidification behavior of the Mg–2.7Al–based alloys (at %) containing various X elements, the Scheil-Gulliver simulation mode was applied under the assumption that no diffusion was allowed in all solid phases while complete mixing occurred in the liquid phase. The simulations were conducted with a cooling step of 1°C, starting from 800°C (the onset of solidification) and continued until the liquid phase completely disappeared. On the basis of the simulation results, data on the formation start temperatures and phase fractions of only the liquid, primary Al-X (including the stoichiometric forms), and Mg matrix phases were extracted. The primary Al-X phase was selected from among the various intermetallic compounds because during the solidification process (assuming that Fe-containing phases form at higher temperatures), this phase plays a critical role in predicting the shell phase that completely encapsulates an Fe-rich core phase. Density functional theory calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package.

Corrosion tests and electrochemical analysis

The corrosion rates of the Mg alloy samples were measured by room-temperature immersion tests in a 0.6 M aqueous NaCl solution for 72 hours. Cuboidal samples with a side length of 10 mm were prepared by mechanical polishing using SiC papers of up to 1200 grit in a water atmosphere and subsequently cleaned with compressed air before the immersion test. After the immersion for 72 hours, the corroded samples were directly immersed in a solution consisting of 200 g liter−1 CrO3, 10 g liter−1 AgNO3, and 20 g liter−1 Ba(NO3)2 at 25°C to remove oxidized corrosion products, and the weight loss was measured. The H2 gas generated during the immersion tests was captured. Three samples were tested under the same conditions in each experiment to ensure reproducibility. To compare the electrochemical potential differences between the Mg matrix and secondary phase particles, the Volta potential distribution was examined through SKPFM with a Bruker Dimension Icon atomic force microscope. SKPFM was performed under an atmospheric condition at a controlled relative humidity of 40% and at 25°C. Electrochemical analysis was carried out by potentiodynamic polarization tests and EIS in 0.6 M NaCl solution at 25°C. Potentiodynamic polarization curves were obtained using a Reference 600 potentiostat in 0.6 M NaCl solution at 25°C after deaeration with N2 gas. The polarization curve of each sample was obtained from −0.3 VSCE to film breakdown and potential scan rate was 1 mV s−1. A conventional three-electrode cell with a saturated calomel reference electrode (SCE), a Pt counter electrode, and a working electrode (sample) was used. EIS was performed over scan frequencies ranging from 100 kHz to 0.03 Hz with a perturbation amplitude of ∼10 mV.
