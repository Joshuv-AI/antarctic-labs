Characterising ‘Watch and Wait’ prescribing patterns in paediatric otitis media using large language models and pharmacy dispense data
Authors: Jessica Pourian, Ben Michaels, Anh Vo, A Jay Holmgren, Augusto Garcia-Agundez, Valerie Flaherman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAcute otitis media (AOM) is a leading cause of antibiotic prescribing in children although many cases resolve without treatment. ‘Watch and wait’ or safety-net antibiotic prescriptions (SNAPs) are recommended to support antibiotic stewardship. However, SNAP use and dispensing patterns are not well captured in structured data. This study evaluated SNAP prescribing, dispensing and associated demographic and clinical factors using a large language model (LLM).MethodsRetrospective cross-sectional study of 4370 AOM encounters (ages 6 months–<18 years) from 1 January 2021 to 1 January 2024 at a single academic centre, including emergency and outpatient visits. An LLM reviewed clinician notes to classify prescriptions as immediate (‘treatment today prescriptions’ (TTPs)) or delayed (SNAP). Dispensing data were linked from Epic Clarity.ResultsOf 4370 encounters, 76.6% received TTP and 23.4% received SNAPs. Patients who spoke a language other than English and those in lower socio-economic quartiles were less likely to receive SNAPs (eg, average marginal effect (AME) = –8.0%, p<0.001). Boys were also less likely to receive SNAPs. SNAPs were less likely to be filled than TTPs (AME = –12.1%, p<0.001). Among patients who received a SNAP, time to dispensing did not differ significantly across demographic or clinical factors.ConclusionSNAP prescribing varied across demographic and clinical groups, while dispensing patterns among SNAP recipients were similar across groups. These findings highlight variability in SNAP use and underscore the need for further research to better understand factors influencing prescribing and to support equitable antibiotic stewardship.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study uses a large language model (LLM) to classify clinician free-text notes in order to identify delayed antibiotic prescriptions for acute otitis media within a large, real-world EHR and links it to a pharmacy dataset. By integrating unstructured clinical documentation with dispense records, this approach overcomes prior data limitations and enables systematic analysis of prescribing practices at scale. The study provides detailed characterisation of prescribing patterns by clinical setting and patient demographic factors, and, for the first time, compares fill behaviour between delayed and immediate antibiotic prescriptions. These findings expand the methodological toolkit available for evaluating prescribing equity and antibiotic stewardship using routinely collected health data.

Introduction

Acute otitis media (AOM) affects millions of children each year and is the most common indication for which antibiotics are prescribed to children.13 Studies estimate that nearly 85% of cases resolve without treatment.4 To promote antibiotic stewardship, the American Academy of Pediatrics and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommend safety-net antibiotic prescriptions (SNAPs).5 6 At our institution, clinicians follow these national guidelines, in which parents are instructed to monitor the child for 1–2 days to see if they improve before using antibiotics.

SNAPs comprise 2–5% of all antibiotics prescribed to children but little is known about their usage patterns—how prescribing varies across social determinants of health (SDoH) and whether disparities exist in their utilisation.7 Research has been limited by the absence of a structured data identifier, making it difficult to systematically track prescription and fill rates. Identifying SNAPs typically requires manual chart review, a significant barrier to epidemiological studies.8 Improving our ability to study these patterns is critical to advancing equitable prescribing practices and strengthening antibiotic stewardship efforts, as prior studies have shown that historically advantaged groups tend to receive antibiotics more frequently than less advantaged groups.911

Studying these disparities is important as they may reflect implicit biases in clinician decision-making or gaps in provider–patient communication. If prescribing rates differ across patient groups but fill rates do not, this could indicate clinician bias. Conversely, if fill rates vary significantly by SDoH, this may signal a need for tailored educational tools, language-concordant materials or follow-up workflows. Understanding whether disparities stem from prescribing behaviour, patient follow-through or both is critical to informing targeted quality improvement efforts that promote equitable and appropriate use of antibiotics.

With the advent of large language models (LLMs) that can perform classification of clinical documentation at scale, this task can now be effectively automated.12 13 Our previous work in reviewing an LLM’s ability to review a physician’s note to distinguish SNAP from an antibiotic prescription meant to start immediately (treatment today prescription, TTP) demonstrated an accuracy rate of 98% when compared with a clinician-reviewed labelled test dataset.14 Once SNAPs are identified, linking them to pharmacy fill data provides insights into patient behaviour, such as if and when prescriptions are picked up. Pharmacy fill data captures the majority of outpatient fills and is effective for assessing medication adherence.15

Our objective was to fill these critical knowledge gaps by combining LLM–derived encounter labels with dispenses from pharmacy databases. With these tools, we were able to evaluate prescribing patterns and real-world fill behaviour at scale. This approach enables us to assess: (1) whether disparities exist in SNAP use across patient SDoH, (2) whether SNAPs fill rates differ from TTP prescriptions and (3) whether SDoH influences if and when SNAPs are picked up. Understanding these patterns can identify barriers to antibiotic stewardship, inform clinical decision-making and institutional policy, and guide targeted interventions to improve equitable paediatric care delivery.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at a single West Coast, tertiary care academic medical centre. The study included outpatient and emergency department (ED) encounters between 1 January 2021 to 1 January 2024.

Participants

Inclusion criteria were (1) patient aged 6 months to <18 years; (2) encounter diagnostic code for AOM (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes H65, H66 and H67); (3) received a prescription of amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate or cefdinir.16 17 We included only encounters with these antibiotics as they are the most common AOM antibiotics used at our institution.18 19 Exclusion criteria were (1) inpatient encounter on day of outpatient encounter, (2) received ceftriaxone, (3) no associated note. An individual patient may have had multiple encounters included if they experienced AOM more than once during the study period.

Variables

Data points were extracted from the electronic health record (EHR) reporting server (Epic Clarity) using Structured Query Language. Records were randomly screened for completeness and viability of inclusion in the final data analysis. Variables extracted included EHR-available demographics such as age, sex, race, ethnicity, insurance type, language, clinical setting and the Vizient Vulnerability Index (VVI). Census tract location was derived in the EHR and matched to the VVI. This index, which combines nine vulnerabilities, was used to assess differences in socio-economic status (SES) among patients and split into even quartiles.20

Patient-reported EHR race was consolidated, with single-race responses grouped accordingly and multiracial responses categorised as ‘Mixed’. ‘Declined’ and ‘Unknown’ were collapsed. ‘Other’ was retained as a distinct category. Insurance type was categorised as public (Medicaid or other government plans) or private (commercial/employer-based), as documented in the EHR. Other data included clinician’s note, antibiotic order details (name, dose, duration) and pharmacy dispense data, which was obtained from SureScripts and DrFirst.21

We limited dispense data to medications picked up within 14 days of the encounter since AOM is expected to resolve within 2 weeks,22 and any symptoms persisting beyond this period are typically indicative of a new infection.23 Restricting the time frame also ensured the treatment was for a single episode of AOM, rather than instances in which a prescription was issued months later.

This study was deemed not human subjects research under our institutional review board under #23-40424.

Identifying SNAPs

Treatment plans were identified by using Versa, a protected health information-safe, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant, deployment of the GPT-4o LLM specific to our institution. The model temperature was set to zero to ensure deterministic outputs and minimise variability. Versa classified encounters as SNAP or TTP by analysing the free text of the clinician’s note. This approach has demonstrated 98% agreement between model and human reviewers.14

Experiments were run on 16 August 2024, with Application Programming Interface V.0.3.3.

Statistical Methods

Categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables, including age and VVI, were binned to facilitate interpretation.

Comparisons between groups were performed using chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the association between prescribed treatment plans (SNAP vs TTP) and medication fill, adjusting for demographic variables. For categorical variables included in the logistic regression, reference groups were selected to reflect the largest or most clinically relevant comparison category. Specifically, reference groups were defined as follows: TTP for treatment plan type, white for race, Not Hispanic or Latino for ethnicity, English for language, private for insurance type, outpatient for encounter type, male for sex and the lowest VVI quartile (representing the highest SES).

Average marginal effects (AMEs) were calculated to estimate the adjusted absolute difference in the probabilities between groups. ORs with 95% CIs are reported for reference. A linear regression model was used to explore the correlation of the same predictor variables with SNAP versus TTP dispensing times. Statistical significance was defined as p values <0.05. All analyses were conducted using R software (2023.09.1 Build 494).

Results

Selecting encounters

The initial dataset included 5751 encounters. Among these, Versa classified 19.4% (n=1110) as receiving a SNAP, 62.6% (n=3571) as receiving TTP and 18.0% (n=1026) as ‘other’.14 The ‘other’ category comprised encounters where the patient was already on antibiotics for unrelated reasons or were not prescribed antibiotics. These were excluded from analysis. Our final dataset included 4370 encounters (online supplemental figure 1).

A total of 4370 encounters were included (table 1). Within the ‘other’ self-reported race category, 76.0% (n=1363) patients indicated their ethnicity as ‘Hispanic or Latino’. Among patients who did not primarily speak English, 76.2% were (n=762) Spanish speakers.

Treatment practice patterns

Most encounters (76.6%, n=3349) had a TTP prescribed, while 23.4% (n=1021) had a SNAP.

Comparing the frequency of SNAP to TTP in χ² testing (online supplemental eTable 1), differences were noted by clinical setting, sex, language, insurance, VVI and race.

In a logistic regression model among all patients, we found patients who spoke a language other than English were significantly less likely to receive a SNAP (AME = –8.0%, p<0.001; OR=0.61, p<0.001) (table 2). Older children had a higher probability of receiving a SNAP compared with those aged 6–12 months. Male patients were also less likely to receive a SNAP (AME = –2.5%, p<0.046; OR=0.86, p=0.046). Higher VVI quartile (indicating lower SES) was associated with a lower likelihood of receiving SNAPs.

A sensitivity analysis using only outpatient encounters found that age, male sex, Asian race and Q4 VVI quartile were significant variables for receiving a TTP over a SNAP, but language was not (online supplemental eTable 2). The same analysis in the ED (eTable 3) yielded fewer SNAPs for patients who spoke a language other than English and for patients in the two lowest SES brackets. Sensitivity tests limiting the sample to only patients with public insurance demonstrated that patients who did not speak English were less likely to receive a SNAP.

Another analysis among English speaking patients showed that patients in lower SES quartiles were less likely to receive a SNAP.

SNAP versus TTP: Fill versus no fill analysis

Of the 4370 encounters that had an antibiotic prescription of any type, 3265 (74.7%) were filled. Among the 1021 SNAPs, 677 (66.3%) were filled. In contrast, among the 3,349 TTP prescriptions, 2588 (77.3%) were filled. The difference in fill rates between SNAP and TTP was statistically significant (χ²=49.26, df=1, p<0.001).

In the logistic regression, patients prescribed SNAP were significantly less likely to fill their prescription than those with TTP (AME=−12.1%, p<0.001; OR=0.55, p<0.001) (table 3). Outpatients were more likely to fill any prescription than ED patients (AME=3.7%, p=0.014; OR=1.23, p=0.01).

SNAP versus TTP: days to dispense

Most prescriptions of any type (72.7%, n=3176) were picked up within 48 hours. Linear regression accounting for demographic variables showed that the SNAP treatment plan only marginally increased dispense time, with patients waiting 2 hours longer on average compared with TTP (β=0.1 days, p<0.011) (figure 1, table 4). Outpatient clinics were associated with a nearly 8 hour shorter dispense time than the ED (β=−0.33 days, p<0.001).

SNAPs were significantly less likely to be picked up than TTP overall, highlighting differences in patient follow-through behaviour between treatment plans. Among the antibiotics that were picked up, the difference in days to dispense by treatment plan was statistically but not clinically significant at 0.1 days (2 hours). SNAP fill rate and time to dispense

Among patients prescribed a SNAP, χ² analyses revealed no significant differences across demographic or clinical variables for fill rates.

However, in logistic regression modelling of fill versus no fill for SNAP patients only (n=1021), patients who self-categorised their race as ‘other’ (n=388) were significantly less likely to pick up their SNAP (AME=−11.2%, p=0.02; OR=0.61, p=0.02), while no other variables showed significant effects in the overall model. Of note, within these 388 patients who reported their race as ‘other’, 296 had identified their ethnic group as Hispanic and 222 were in the two lowest SES brackets. However, neither identifying as Hispanic nor being in the lowest SES brackets were independently associated with SNAP pickup in the model.

Finally, among patients who filled their SNAP, linear regression found no significant associations between time to dispensing and any demographic or clinical variables, including sex, race, ethnicity, clinical setting, language, VVI, age or insurance (p>0.1 for all).

Discussion

This study provides insights into antibiotic prescribing patterns for paediatric AOM, illustrating that significant disparities exist in SNAP prescribing practices based on patient language and SES. Patients who self-identify as ‘other’ are also less likely to pick up their SNAPs. These findings suggest that certain patient populations are less likely to be offered SNAPs, underscoring the need for targeted interventions to ensure equitable healthcare delivery.

Among all variables, the most pronounced disparity was between patients who spoke English and those who did not when presenting to the ED. This might be attributable to communication barriers, difficulty recalling SNAP eligibility criteria during an encounter, worries about cultural norms favouring immediate antibiotic use, need for interpreter or some other factor. Additionally, patients presenting to the ED may have greater illness severity, which could appropriately reduce the use of SNAPs in favour of immediate antibiotic treatment.

The language difference was not present in the outpatient setting, which may reflect reduced illness severity, greater physician familiarity with patients, the tendency for patients to choose a language-concordant primary care provider, more time for communication and scheduling of an interpreter that may have allowed for better explanation of treatment options, and stronger patient–provider trust that may facilitate shared decision-making around SNAPs.

That lower SES patients received fewer SNAPs—even after controlling for insurance and language—may also reflect physician concerns about health literacy, follow-up or a preference for antibiotics as a quick fix, consistent with studies showing greater antibiotic use in children from high-poverty areas.24 Patients from lower SES quartiles were less likely to pick up medication, perhaps due to transportation or cost concerns.25

Older children were more likely to receive a SNAP, aligning with prior research suggesting younger children more often receive immediate antibiotics, possibly due to clinical appropriateness or caution.26 ,27 This pattern may reflect differences in clinical presentation or guideline-based eligibility; however, eligibility for SNAPs was not directly assessed in this study.

Boys were also less likely to receive a SNAP, which reflects previous research that AOM is more common in boys, as are antibiotic prescriptions in general.28 29

Although the time to dispense for SNAP versus TTP was statistically significant, the 2 hour difference is not meaningful. The lower fill rate among SNAPs may indicate that delayed prescribing is sometimes functioning as intended, although family understanding of the strategy was not directly assessed. These findings support the hypothesis that SNAPs may reduce antibiotic prescription fills and, in turn, antibiotic use. However, the 66% SNAP pickup rate suggests that families may still perceive a need for antibiotics, even though recurrence and complications are now uncommon in the postvaccine era.3032

The 77% fill rate for TTP aligns with prior findings of 80% for antibiotics for paediatric pneumonia33 and 84% for antibiotics overall.34 It slightly exceeds the 65% fill rate reported after ED visits.35 However, these rates reflect dispense, not use—adherence for antibiotics is estimated to be closer to 62%.36

Finally, patients seen in ambulatory clinics were more likely to fill prescriptions—and to do so more quickly—than those seen in the ED, which might be due to better alignment of clinical schedules with pharmacy hours, proximity to care and access to clinic support services such as social workers. On-site pharmacies may also facilitate timely dispensing. In contrast, ED patients may face barriers such as transportation, parking or pharmacy closures, and may receive an initial antibiotic dose on-site, which may delay the urgency of filling the prescription.

To address disparities in SNAP use, interventions should focus on supporting clinicians through education and standardised language-concordant instructions embedded in after-visit summaries. Additionally, it may be helpful to prominently post SNAP eligibility criteria in the ED and to integrate clinical decision support tools such as order sets.

This study has several limitations

First, it was conducted at a single academic centre in a large city, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other settings. Reproducing this study at other institutions may be challenging, as it requires access to a HIPAA-compliant LLM; however, our prior work details the model prompt and development, along with a GitHub repository, to facilitate adaptation by other institutions.14 The financial cost of LLM use may also limit feasibility in resource-constrained settings; however, in our institution the analysis remained within a subsidised usage tier, and costs are difficult to generalise given that pricing varies by model, token usage and is rapidly evolving.

Importantly, dispensing medication does not imply use. Families may pick up antibiotics early out of convenience, alongside other medications like antipyretics, with plans to use the antibiotic only if symptoms worsen. Our analysis did not assess medication adherence post-fill due to the challenges of surveying a large population, which could also introduce recall bias. We also only examined the top three antibiotics; it is possible that patients who receive non-first line antibiotics have different prescribing and fill patterns.

Further, treatment classification relied on clinician documentation. Notes can be vague and encounter diagnoses are often misclassified (eg, AOM coded for otitis externa).

We were also restricted to pharmacies that use electronic prescribing, such as CVS or Walgreens, and reflect whether a prescription was filled, regardless of whether payment was made via insurance or cash. Although these pharmacies represent over 98% of prescriptions in our dataset, patients using independent pharmacies may have different fill patterns.

Notably, we did not examine whether a patient was eligible for a SNAP. SNAPs are recommended in the 6–24 months age group only if the AOM is unilateral and symptoms are mild. Since our study did not assess the laterality nor severity of infections, the higher incidence of TTP in younger age groups is likely consistent with established clinical guidelines. It is also possible that children from lower SES face barriers to accessing care, leading them to present with more severe symptoms, which may appropriately increase the likelihood of receiving a TTP.

Finally, our findings are subject to clinician selection bias—physicians may base their prescribing decisions on nuanced clinical judgement not captured in the EHR. A randomised trial or quasi-experimental study may be necessary to understand both prescribing behaviour and patient adherence patterns.

Future research could explore whether SNAPs help prevent the need for follow-up visits. Additionally, examining how patient–provider language concordance influences prescribing could provide insight into communication-driven disparities. Future work could evaluate whether an LLM can determine SNAP eligibility, though sparse documentation in physician notes may limit performance. Expanding the scope of data sources, including claims data, could provide a more comprehensive view of adherence.

Conclusion

This study identifies differences in SNAP prescribing for paediatric AOM across multiple factors, including language, sex, SES and care setting. Families who speak a language other than English were less likely to receive a SNAP, as were boys. Overall, SNAPs were filled less often than TTP. Fill behaviour among those who received a SNAP was consistent across demographic groups. These findings highlight variability in SNAP use and underscore the need for further research to support equitable antibiotic stewardship.
