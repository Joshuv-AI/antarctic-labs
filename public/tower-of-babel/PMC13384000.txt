Genetic diversity and pathogenicity of goose astrovirus genotype 2 in geese, ducks and chickens
Authors: Lili Liu, Hongke Xu, Yujiao Xie, Wenxi Li, Mengdi Guan, Mengjing Wang, Xuan Yang, Mingfeng Liu, Zhanshuai Li, Anran Ren, Yi Tang, Jing Guo, Xuyong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGoose astrovirus genotype 2 (GAstV-2), a member of the Avastrovirus (AAstV) genus, is an important emerging pathogen closely associated with gosling gout that has caused enormous economic losses to the goose industry since it was first reported in 2015 in China. However, public GAstV-2 sequencing data remain scarce (with only 278 records to date), and its genetic evolution, pathogenic features, and cross-species transmission potential have not been fully elucidated. To address this gap, we isolated 12 GAstV-2 strains from clinical samples of gout-affected goslings in Shandong and Guangdong Provinces (2021–2024) and successfully sequenced their complete genomes. Bayesian time-resolved phylogenetic analyses revealed that GAstV-2 has evolved into two distinct lineages, with our 12 isolates distributed in both lineages (nine viruses clustered in Lineage 1 and three viruses clustered in Lineage 2). Animal infection experiments demonstrated that all the strains replicated efficiently in goslings, causing renal hyperemia and swelling and mild urate deposition in the ureter, with two strains causing 20% mortality. Significantly, histopathological lesions and viral antigens were detected in the heart, liver, and kidney of the inoculated geese. Notably, GAstV-2 could infect and replicate in both ducks and chickens without prior adaptation, resulting in histopathological lesions to the heart, liver and kidney of the birds. These findings enrich our understanding of the GAstV-2 prevalence, clarify the evolutionary dynamics of this virus, and highlight its role as cross-species infection potential, providing a critical basis for targeted surveillance and control strategies.Keywords: Astroviruses, AAstV, Goose astrovirus 2, GAstV-2

Introduction

Astroviruses (AstVs) belong to the family Astroviridae and are characterized by their single-stranded, positive-sense RNA genomes and nonenveloped, icosahedral virions (Cortez et al., 2017). Since the first detection of human astrovirus (HAstV) in 1975, AstVs have been identified in a wide range of mammals and birds, highlighting the increasing potential for zoonotic transmission (Appleton and Higgins, 1975; Schoub et al., 1975; Zhang et al., 2018; Qin et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2015). Currently, AstVs are classified into two genera on the basis of their host specificity: Mamastrovirus (MAstV), which infects mammals; and Avastrovirus (AAstV), which infects avian species (Guix et al., 2005; Bosch et al., 2014; De Benedictis et al., 2011). AAstV exhibits remarkable genetic diversity, with strains originating from chickens, turkeys, pigeons, ducks, geese, and wild birds (Bi et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2011; Ng et al., 2024; Zhirov et al., 2025). Notably, AAstV strains have been linked to various clinical diseases in avians, including poult enteritis mortality syndrome in chickens and turkeys, fatal hepatitis in ducklings, and gout in goslings, resulting in substantial economic losses in the poultry industry (Xu et al., 2024; Canelli et al., 2012; Niu et al., 2018).

Goose astroviruses (GAstVs) are newly emerged members of the AAstV genus that mainly infect geese. On the basis of genetic differences, they are classified into goose astrovirus genotype 1 (GAstV-1) and genotype 2 (GAstV-2). GAstV-1 strains are generally associated with gosling enteritis and occasional gout symptoms, whereas GAstV-2 strains are strongly linked to outbreaks of gosling gout and are characterized by high incidence and mortality rates (Wei et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2019). Since its 2015 emergence in commercial geese in Guangdong, Shandong, Jiangsu, and Anhui Provinces, China, GAstV-2 has become epidemic among goslings and has emerged as a major pathogen threatening the goose industry (Zhang et al., 2018; Niu et al., 2018; Yuan et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2017). GAstV-2 primarily infects goslings within three weeks of birth, causing clinical gout and severe multiorgan pathological lesions, including urate deposits on the surfaces of the liver and heart, urate accumulation in the ureters and joint cavities, and renal swelling and pallor in affected geese (An et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2021; Xiang et al., 2024). Over the past decade, GAstV-2 infections and associated gout outbreaks in goslings have been detected in major commercial goose-farming provinces in China, with mortality rates exceeding 50% in affected flocks (Li et al., 2025). Additionally, previous studies have reported that GAstV-2 infections in ducks are associated with clinical visceral gout, renal hemorrhage and swelling (Wei et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021). Recently, GAstV-2 has been isolated from chickens with nephritis in China, underscoring its potential to spill over into chicken populations (Wei et al., 2023). Despite the increasing reports of GAstV-2 infections in recent years, the genetic and evolutionary characteristics of GAstV-2, as well as its replication patterns and pathogenic potential in geese, ducks, and chickens, remain largely unclear.

In this study, we isolated twelve GAstV-2 strains from clinical goose samples in eastern China and characterized their detailed epidemiological and evolutionary dynamics to elucidate their spatiotemporal patterns and genetic profiles. The replication patterns and pathogenicity of these GAstV-2 isolates in geese, ducks, and chickens demonstrate the threat of GAstV-2 to goslings and its potential for spillover to ducks and chickens. These findings support the refinement of GAstV-2 surveillance at the interface of domestic geese, ducks, and chickens, emphasizing the need to prioritize epidemiological monitoring and the development of antiviral strategies to control endemic GAstV-2 in China.

Results

Phylogenetic landscape and genotypes of avian astroviruses (AAstVs) in avian species

To characterize the genetic diversity of avian AAstVs, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using available global AAstV genomic sequences retrieved from the GenBank database. AAstVs have been detected predominantly in turkeys, chickens, domestic ducks, and geese. Since the first identification of turkey astroviruses in the USA in 1980, ten AAstV genotypes have been documented across avian species worldwide (Fig. 1) (McNulty et al., 1980). These include avian nephritis viruses (ANVs) in chickens and pigeons; turkey astroviruses (TAstV-1 and TAstV-2); chicken astrovirus (CAstV); duck astroviruses (DAstV-1, DAstV-2, DAstV-3, and DAstV-4); and the emerged goose astroviruses (GAstV-1 and GAstV-2) identified in China (Fig. 1). Notably, a distinct phylogenetic distance was observed between the GAstV-1 and GAstV-2 clusters, reflecting significant genetic divergence between these two goose astrovirus genotypes (Fig. 1). The continuous emergence of novel AAstV strains and the expanding diversity of genotypes underscore the rapid evolutionary dynamics of AAstVs, as well as their growing potential risks to avian populations.

Reported sequence distribution of GAstV-2 in China

To date, GAstV-2 has been exclusively documented in China, indicating its unique epidemiological relevance in this region. To characterize the spatiotemporal distribution of publicly reported GAstV-2 strains, we collected and analyzed all available GAstV-2 sequences from the GenBank database. Since the first GAstV-2 sequence was deposited in 2017, a total of 278 complete or partial genome sequences had been submitted to the database as of October 31, 2025. The majority (n = 256) were isolated from geese, supporting that geese represent the primary natural host of GAstV-2. Notably, one strain from chickens and eight strains from ducks have been reported in at least 15 provinces of China (Fig. 2A). However, more than 55% of these sequences lacked clear information about isolation locations. Therefore, the geographical distribution presented here was based only on strains with available location records, and should be regarded as partial and preliminary rather than a complete nationwide epidemiological profile. Despite these limitations, analysis of the available location data showed that reported GAstV-2 strains were mainly distributed in eastern China, including Guangdong, Shandong, and Anhui provinces (Fig. 2B). Collectively, these results indicate that the publicly reported GAstV-2 strains are predominantly derived from geese and concentrated in eastern China.

Identification and isolation of GAstV-2 from clinical samples of farmed geese

Farmed goslings are highly susceptible to gout disease, which is characterized by typical clinical manifestations, including urate deposits on the surfaces of the liver and heart, urate accumulation in the ureters, and renal enlargement (Fig. 3A). Liver, heart, and kidney samples were collected from sick or deceased goslings presenting with gout symptoms between 2021 and 2024. These samples were subjected to reverse transcription–polymerase chain reaction (RT–PCR) using specific primers targeting viruses commonly infecting geese, including avian influenza viruses (AIVs), duck reoviruses (DRVs), goose parvovirus (GPV), goose circovirus (GoCV), GAstV-1, and GAstV-2 (Table S1). GAstV-2-positive samples were further inoculated into LMH cells for virus isolation. A typical cytopathic effect (CPE) was observed in the inoculated LMH cells within 96 hours post-inoculation (hpi) (Fig. 3B). The presence of GAstV-2 in the infected LMH cells was confirmed by indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) using a mouse-derived anti-VP27 monoclonal antibody (Fig. 3C). Additionally, astrovirus virions were visualized in LMH cell cultures via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 3D). A total of twelve GAstV-2 strains were isolated from clinical goose samples collected in Shandong and Guangdong Provinces, China, between 2021 and 2024 (Table 1). The complete genomes of these twelve strains were sequenced, and the sequences have been deposited in the GenBank database (accession numbers: PX703590 to PX703601).

Phylogenetic analysis of GAstV-2

To clarify the phylogenetic landscape and evolutionary dynamics of GAstV-2 strains currently circulating in geese, a Bayesian time-resolved phylogenetic tree was constructed on the basis of the complete genome sequence of GAstV-2. This time-scaled tree was inferred using complete GAstV-2 sequences (n = 89), including the twelve strains sequenced in this study and 77 additional sequences retrieved from the GenBank database (Fig. 4). Notably, nearly all the GAstV-2 strains for which complete sequences were available in the database were isolated from geese, except for three duck-derived strains and one chicken isolate. Overall, since its emergence in geese in 2015, GAstV-2 has evolved into two dominant lineages (Lineage 1 and Lineage 2), as illustrated in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 4). The twelve GAstV-2 strains isolated in this study shared 94.3–99.9% nucleotide sequence identity and were clustered into these two lineages as follows: nine strains in Lineage 1 and three in Lineage 2. Within Lineage 1, the nine strains exhibited 95.4–99.9% genetic similarity, whereas the three Lineage 2 isolates shared 96.9–97.8% nucleotide sequence identity (Fig. 4). Notably, Lineage 2 has been reported in at least nine provinces across China, whereas Lineage 1 has been documented in eight provinces, with a predominant concentration in Shandong Province (Figs. 2, 4).

Evolutionary trend of GAstV-2 viruses. A Bayesian time-resolved phylogenetic tree of GAstV viruses was constructed to reveal their evolutionary trends on the basis of the GAstV-2 virus sequences (n = 89) available in the GenBank database. The ORF1a, ORF1b, and ORF2 genes of the twelve GAstV-2 isolates shared nucleotide identity similarities of 96.6–100%, 97.0–100%, and 96.8–100%, respectively. To further characterize the evolutionary divergence of GAstV-2, Bayesian time-resolved phylogenetic trees were constructed for each of these three genes (Figure S1, Figure S2, Fig. 5). The evolutionary trajectories of the ORF1b and ORF2 genes were similar, and the genes clustered into the same two dominant lineages as those in the complete genome tree (Figs. 4, 5, Figure S1, Figure S2). However, the ORF1a genes of all the available GAstV-2 strains formed two distinct lineages, which differed from the phylogenetic patterns of ORF1b and ORF2 (Figure S1, Figure S2). Consistent with the complete genome tree, the twelve isolates in this study clustered into Lineage 1 (nine strains) and Lineage 2 (three strains) in the ORF1a, ORF1b, and ORF2 gene trees. Collectively, the evolutionary dynamics and phylogenetic lineages identified in this study reflect the genetic divergence of circulating GAstV-2 strains in China, providing insights into the evolutionary history and spread of the virus in geese.

Replication and virulence of GAstV-2 strains in goslings

To evaluate the pathogenicity of GAstV-2 isolates belonging to different evolutionary lineages, three representative strains were selected for experimental infection: two strains (D202/2023 and D198/2024) from Lineage 1 and one strain (D204/2023) from Lineage 2, which together adequately represent the two major genetic lineages of the 12 isolates in this study. The TCID50 values of D202/2023, D204/2023 and D198/2024 were determined using LMH cells, which were calculated as 102.75 TCID50/0.1mL,103.5 TCID50/0.1 mL and 101.66 TCID50/0.1 mL, respectively. Notably, all three GAstV-2 strains efficiently replicated in the heart, liver, and kidneys of infected goslings, with viral titers ranging from 5.24 to 7.92 log10 copies/µL (Figure 6A). Virulence variations were observed among the strains: D202/2023 and D204/2023 caused 20% mortality, whereas no mortality was recorded in the D198/2024-infected group throughout the 14-day observation period (Fig. 6B). Compared with the PBS-inoculated control group, goslings infected with the tested GAstV-2 strains displayed typical clinical signs, including mental depression, huddling behavior, lethargy, ruffled and matted feathers, excretion of white watery-pasty feces, and fecal soiling of the perianal plumage. However, significant urate deposition on organ surfaces was not detected at 3 dpi in the experimentally infected goslings, except for renal hyperemia and swelling (Figure S3).

Histopathological analysis by H&E staining revealed varying degrees of tissue damage in the target organs of goslings across all infected groups. Cardiac tissue exhibited mild edema, while hepatocytes were mainly characterized by mild hydropic degeneration, fatty degeneration and severe vacuolar degeneration. Renal tissue showed degeneration and necrosis of renal tubular epithelial cells, accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 6C). To further confirm viral antigen distribution, we performed IHC staining on heart, liver, and kidney samples from the D198/2024-infected group, which confirmed the presence of viral antigens in these organs. Notably, IHC staining revealed a more extensive distribution of viral antigens in the livers of inoculated goslings than in their kidneys and hearts, and this finding was positively correlated with the higher viral titers detected in the liver. (Fig. 6A, D). Viral neutralization assays demonstrated that all the tested GAstV-2 strains induced high titers of neutralizing antibodies in the surviving goslings (Fig. 6E). Collectively, these results indicate that currently circulating GAstV-2 strains can efficiently replicate in goslings and cause certain pathological damage to key target organs, with strain-specific differences in virulence.

GAstV-2 can infect and replicate in ducks

Although GAstV-2 has been isolated from ducks, its replication and virulence in ducks remain largely uncharacterized (Wei et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021). To assess the potential incursion risk of GAstV-2 to duck populations, three GAstV-2 isolates (D202/2023, D198/2024, and D204/2023) were further used to experimentally infect three-day-old ducklings. Ducklings inoculated with GAstV-2 exhibited typical clinical signs, including mental lethargy, excretion of white watery feces, and fecal soiling of the perianal region. Viral replication was confirmed in the heart, liver, and kidneys of infected ducklings at 3 dpi, with a tissue tropism pattern showing significantly higher viral titers in the kidneys than in the heart and liver (Fig. 7A). The D202/2023 and D198/2024 viruses caused 40% of the ducks to die during the observation period, while no deaths were recorded in the D204/2023 group (Fig. 7B). Gross pathological examination at 3 dpi revealed renal enlargement and mild urate deposition in the ureters of the ducklings (Figure S4). Histopathological analysis by H&E staining revealed that the three GAstV-2 strains mainly caused pathological damage in the kidneys of infected ducklings, manifested as degeneration and necrosis of renal tubular epithelial cells accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration; whereas no obvious pathological lesions were observed in cardiac and hepatic tissues (Fig. 7G). To further characterize the distribution of viral antigens, IHC staining was performed on heart, liver and kidney tissue samples from ducklings infected with the D198/2024 strain. The results showed that viral antigens were exclusively distributed in the kidney tissues (Fig. 7G). Interestingly, the host immune response to GAstV-2 infection was limited because viral neutralizing antibodies were detected only in the D204/2023-infected group, with no significant antibody production observed in the D202/2023 and D198/2024 groups (Fig. 7C). These results indicated that the ducklings are susceptible to the current circulating GAstV-2 strains and the kidney of duckling serves as the major target organ for GAstV-2.

Chickens are susceptible to the current GAstV-2 strains

Although GAstV-2 infections in chickens have been previously documented, the replication dynamics and virulence characteristics of this virus in chickens remain poorly understood (Wei et al., 2023). To systematically evaluate the potential infection risk of circulating GAstV-2 strains in chickens, three GAstV-2 isolates (D202/2023, D198/2024, and D204/2023) were further used to experimentally infect three-day-old SPF chickens. Viral replication was confirmed in the heart, liver, and kidneys of inoculated chickens at 3 dpi. Notably, viral titers in the liver and kidneys were significantly greater than those in the heart, indicating that the liver and kidneys serve as the primary target organs for GAstV-2 replication in chickens (Fig. 7D). However, clinical symptoms and mortality was not recorded in any of the three strain-infected groups throughout the 14-day observation period (Fig. 7E, Figure S5). Histopathological analysis by H&E staining of D198/2024 group revealed characteristic lesions in kidneys of inoculated chickens, including edema and degeneration of renal tubular epithelial cells, accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration. IHC staining results indicated that viral antigens were predominantly distributed in the renal tissues of chickens (Fig. 7G). Serological analysis revealed high titers of viral neutralizing antibodies in the serum of all surviving chickens at 21 dpi, suggesting a robust host immune response to GAstV-2 infection (Fig. 7F). These results confirm that chickens are susceptible to currently circulating GAstV-2 strains, highlighting the potential incursion risk of GAstV-2 to chicken populations.

Discussion

In recent decades, the increasing prevalence of emerging infectious diseases in waterfowl has posed a significant threat to the global poultry industry and public health security (Cui et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2025; He et al., 2022). As an emerging pathogen identified in geese in recent years, GAstV-2 has been confirmed to be strongly associated with gosling gout, causing substantial economic losses to the goose industry in China (Xiang et al., 2024; Fei et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). In this study, we conducted an in-depth investigation into the prevalence and evolution of GAstV-2, evaluating twelve GAstV-2 strains isolated from clinical samples and publicly available sequence data. Additionally, we systematically characterized the replication and pathogenicity of these strains in geese, ducks, and chickens. The findings concerning the genetic and biological characteristics of GAstV-2 will facilitate a comprehensive understanding of its evolutionary trends and cross-species transmission potential among domestic avian species.

Since the first report in commercial geese in China in 2015, an increasing number of gosling gout cases caused by GAstV-2 infections have been documented (Zhu and Sun, 2022; Zhang et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024). However, only 278 GAstV-2 sequence records are currently retrievable from public databases (e.g., GenBank), with more than 55% lacking annotated isolation locations. It should be emphasized that the analysis based on GenBank sequences only reflects the spatiotemporal distribution of reported GAstV-2 strains rather than a full national epidemic picture due to the lack of standardized surveillance, sampling denominator and flock-level positivity rate. The scarcity and incompleteness of this viral information have to some extent constrained in-depth analyses on the epidemiology and evolution of GAstV-2. In this study, twelve GAstV-2 strains were isolated from clinical samples of farmed goslings in Shandong and Guangdong Provinces, China, between 2021 and 2024, and their complete genomes were successfully sequenced. This work will significantly enrich the available GAstV-2 sequence resources, thereby contributing to elucidating the spatiotemporal phylogenetic landscape of GAstV-2. As a distinct genotype within AAstV, GAstV-2 has evolved into two discrete lineages. The phylogenetic divergence of GAstV-2 observed herein underscores the importance of implementing active surveillance to monitor the evolution and mutation of circulating GAstV-2 strains in China.

GAstV-2 infection primarily affects farmed goslings within three weeks of birth, with extensive visceral urate deposition as a hallmark clinical manifestation. All twelve GAstV-2 strains in this study were isolated from tissue specimens of goslings that presented with symptoms of gout. In gosling infection experiments, although all three selected strains successfully infected goslings and replicated efficiently—with two strains inducing 20% mortality—only mild urate deposition was detected in the renal ureters. This clinical presentation differs from the extensive visceral urate deposition observed in naturally infected goslings in commercial flocks, suggesting that excessive nutritional and host metabolic factors in large-scale farming systems may exacerbate gout symptoms following GAstV-2 infection. Nevertheless, histopathological lesions were observed in the heart, liver, and kidneys of the inoculated goslings, confirming the inherent virulence of GAstV-2 to geese. Due to the limited number of animals (n = 5 per group) used for survival observation, the differences in mortality should be interpreted cautiously. A previous study mapped the localization and distribution of GAstV-2 antigens in goose tissues, identifying renal tubular epithelial cells as the primary site of viral antigen accumulation (Wei et al., 2023). In the present study, GAstV-2 (D198/2024) antigens were consistently detected in the heart, liver, and kidneys of inoculated goslings and were correlated with the viral titer distribution across these organs. Additionally, high levels of neutralizing antibodies were detected in the serum of surviving goslings at 21 dpi, indicating that neutralizing antibody titers can serve as a reliable indicator for assessing prior GAstV-2 infection in nonvaccinated goose flocks.

Previous studies have reported GAstV-2 infection in farmed ducks and chickens, which was characterized by visceral gout outbreaks or renal hemorrhage and swelling in ducks and nephritis in laying hens(Fig. 2A) (Wei et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2023). In this study, we experimentally infected ducks and chickens with GAstV-2 strains to evaluate the current cross-species transmission potential risks of these viruses. Our results demonstrate that GAstV-2 can replicate in ducks without prior adaptation, resulting in renal enlargement, mild urate deposition in the ureters, and significant pathological lesions in the heart, liver, and kidneys. Notably, strains D202/2023 and D198/2024 caused a 40% mortality rate in inoculated ducklings, highlighting the high pathogenicity of certain GAstV-2 strains to ducks. Intriguingly, no significant immune response to GAstV-2 infection was observed in ducks, which differed from the responses observed in geese and chickens. These findings confirm that ducks are susceptible to currently circulating GAstV-2 strains. In chickens, GAstV-2 efficiently replicated in the liver and kidneys but was nonlethal to inoculated birds. He et al. previously reported that chickens inoculated with GAstV-2 displayed clinical signs, including depression, anorexia, and diarrhea, accompanied by extensive organ damage and histopathological changes in the heart, liver, spleen, kidneys, and thymus (He et al., 2023). In contrast, we did not observe obvious clinical symptoms or severe organ damage in the chickens; however, histopathological lesions were detected in the heart, liver, and kidneys. These results suggest a potential “subclinical infection” pattern of GAstV-2 in chickens, which could facilitate the silent spread of the virus within and between poultry flocks. It should be emphasized that the susceptibility of ducks and chickens to GAstV-2 was determined via intramuscular inoculation in this study, which is an artificial infection route rather than a natural transmission pathway. This inoculation route may potentially influence viral tissue tropism and pathogenicity. Therefore, the cross-species transmission risk should be interpreted with caution, and the present results only confirm the infection potential and host susceptibility of GAstV-2 in different avian species, rather than its natural transmissibility.

In conclusion, our findings enhance the current understanding of the prevalence, genetics, replication characteristics, and virulence of GAstV-2 in China and further emphasize its threat to the goose industry, including significant cross-species transmission potential to duck and chicken flocks. Unfortunately, no effective commercial drugs or vaccines are currently available to address GAstV-2 infection and prevalence in farmed geese. Moving forward, a comprehensive set of measures, including strengthening active surveillance networks, improving biosecurity protocols in goose farms and hatcheries, and accelerating the development of anti-astrovirus drugs or vaccines, will be critical for controlling the prevalence of GAstV-2 in avian populations.

Methods

Experimental animals

Three-day-old SanHua geese without prior vaccination were purchased from a local goose hatchery. The oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs of the geese were tested to rule out infections with viruses, including AIVs, DRVs, GPV, GoCV, GAstV-1 and GAstV-2. The serum of each gosling was collected to detect neutralizing antibodies against GAstV-2. Goslings with serum antibody titers greater than 3 log2 PD50/0.1 mL were excluded from the experimental study. Three-day-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) chickens and one-day-old SPF ducks were purchased from Shandong Healthtech Laboratory Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. (Ji’nan, Shandong, China). The grouped animals were housed in biosafety isolators under controlled temperature and humidity conditions, with free access to water and feed during the experimental period. No gender distinction was made for the experimental animals in this study.

Viruses and cells

The twelve GAstV-2 strains were isolated from clinical samples of farmed geese and stored at our laboratory. Leghorn male hepatoma (LMH) cells were cultured with DMEM-F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Sample collection

Samples were collected from poultry farms in Shandong and Guangdong Provinces between 2021 and 2024 for diagnosis. Clinical anatomical diagnosis was first performed on these sick or deceased geese, with a focus on the typical pathological changes in their visceral organs. The organ samples (liver, spleen, heart, kidney, pancreas, trachea and lung) of the sick or dead geese were stored in a −80°C freezer for virus identification and isolation.

Virus identification and isolation

The organ samples were homogenized in PBS and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm to collect the supernatant, after which total DNA/RNA was extracted using a DNA/RNA isolation kit. Random primers were subsequently used for reverse transcription of RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA). RT–PCR was subsequently performed to identify AIVs, DRVs, GPV, GoCV, GAstV-1, and GAstV-2 via specific viral primers, as listed in Table S1. The samples positive for GAstV-2 were then inoculated into LMH cells for virus isolation. After three passages in LMH cells, the GAstV-2 isolates were identified via RT–PCR and stored at −80°C. The viral titers of the GAstV-2 isolates were determined according to the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) in LMH cells.

Transmission electron microscopy assay

The method used for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) has been described previously. Briefly, the viruses were cultured in LMH cells and purified by sucrose gradient density centrifugation. The obtained virions were stained using standard procedures and observed under an electron microscope at 80 kV (Sun et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2022).

Indirect immunofluorescence assay

LMH cells cultured in six-well plates were infected with GAstV-2. At 48 hpi, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 30 min. The cells were then blocked with 5% skim milk, followed by incubation with a mouse monoclonal antibody against VP27 of GAstV-2 at room temperature for 50 min. A fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-coupled goat anti-mouse antibody was added for VP27 detection. After incubation with secondary antibodies, the cells were incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min. The cells were then observed with a fluorescence microscope.

Genome sequencing of GAstV-2

Viral RNA was extracted from the LMH cell supernatant, and cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription. Specific primers were used to amplify the PCR product of GAstV-2. The full-length sequences of the viruses were sequenced on an Applied Biosystems DNA Analyzer and assembled via the SeqMan program (DNASTAR, Madison, WI, USA).

Phylogenetic analysis

The complete genome sequences of global avian astroviruses, as well as the sequences of ORF1a, ORF1b, and ORF2 and the complete genome of GAstV-2, were retrieved from the GenBank database. Among the 278 GAstV-2 sequences retrieved from GenBank, most were partial genome sequences or lacked clear sampling dates. For reliable Bayesian time-resolved phylogenetic analysis, only full-length genome sequences with definite collection dates were included, resulting in a total of 89 qualified sequences for final analysis. The retrieved GenBank sequences and the genome sequences of the twelve newly obtained strains were first subjected to multiple sequence alignment using MAFFT software (v7.505) with default parameters. After alignment, sequence refinement and selection were performed using MEGA7 to ensure data quality for subsequent analyses.

A phylogenetic tree was constructed with IQ-TREE software (v2.2.0) to delineate the overall phylogenetic landscape of avian astroviruses. For GAstV-2 specifically, maximum clade credibility (MCC) phylogenetic trees were generated using BEAST software (v1.10.4) on the basis of four datasets: complete genome sequences, the ORF1a gene, the ORF1b gene, and the ORF2 gene. The optimal nucleotide substitution model was determined via IQ-TREE software (v2.2.0), with the GTR + F + G4 model identified as the best fit for all the datasets. A log-normal uncorrelated relaxed clock was selected as the molecular clock model. The convergence of the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs was verified using TRACER software (v1.7.1), and only parameters with an effective sample size (ESS) ≥ 200 were considered reliable. Following a 10% burn-in period to discard unstable early iterations, MCC trees for each dataset were generated using the TreeAnnotator tool integrated within the BEAST package.

Goose study

Three-day-old goslings were used to test the replication and pathogenicity of GAstV-2. Oropharyngeal and cloacal swabs were collected to detect the viruses that are commonly found in geese via RT–PCR, while the presence of antibodies against GAstV-2 in the serum was tested via a virus-neutralizing antibody assay. Specific quantitative primers were designed based on the conserved sequences of the ORF1a gene of goose astrovirus strains D202/2023, D204/2023 and D198/2024. Viral loads in various goose tissues were determined by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) using the SYBR Green fluorescent dye method. Ten goslings were used in each group and were inoculated by leg muscle injection with 101.5 TCID50/0.1 mL of the indicated viruses in a volume of 0.2 mL. Ten geese inoculated with PBS were set as the negative control group (Mock). At 3 dpi, five birds in each group were euthanized and the heart, liver and kidney were collected for virus detection by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) (three birds/group) and histological analysis (two birds/group), respectively. The remaining five geese were monitored daily for two weeks to observe their clinical symptoms and survival. Mortality rates were calculated based on these five birds per group. The serum of the remaining geese in each group was collected at 21 dpi to detect the neutralizing antibody levels in LMH cells. To minimize the potential confounders, two operators who were not aware of this experiment were designed to record the daily weight, mortality, and morbidity of the infected geese. The geese lost above 25% of their initial weight were euthanized using CO2 inhalation and recorded as having died on the following day.

Duck study

Three-day-old SPF ducklings were grouped to test the replication of GAstV-2 in ducks. The randomly grouped ducks were inoculated with the indicated viruses by leg muscle injection with 101.5 TCID50/0.1 mL in a volume of 0.2 mL. The methods were the same as those used for the geese study described above.

Chicken study

Three-day-old SPF chickens were divided into four groups randomly to test the replication of GAstV-2 in the chickens. The experimental methods were the same as those used in the duck study described above.

Virus neutralization antibody assay

The serum of the birds was collected to detect the presence of neutralizing antibodies against GAstV-2 in LMH cells. Briefly, LMH cells were cultured in 96-well plates. The serum was diluted twofold and was incubated with an equal volume of virus (200 TCID50) for one hour. LMH cells were subsequently inoculated with mixtures of serum and virus for one hour and observed for 48 hours. The cytopathic effect of the LMH cells was observed to calculate the neutralizing antibody levels.

Histopathology analysis

The liver, spleen, and kidney of the geese, ducks and chickens were collected at 3 dpi and immersed immediately in 10% neutral-buffered formalin solution for fixation. After gradient dehydration using serial concentrations of ethanol and clearing of the dehydrated tissue, the tissue was embedded in molten paraffin. The paraffin block was then sliced into 4-µm sections for deparaffinization and hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining. The tissue slides were examined under a light microscope to analyze cellular morphology, tissue structure, and pathological changes. Histopathological changes were evaluated by microscopic observation and described qualitatively; no standardized lesion scoring system was applied.

Immunohistochemical assay

The paraffin-embedded tissue sections were treated with citrate buffer to expose the masked antigens. The sections were then incubated with blocking solution to prevent nonspecific antibody binding. A mouse monoclonal primary antibody against the target VP27 antigen of GAstV-2 was incubated overnight. Afterward, an enzyme-conjugated (HRP-labeled) secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. A DAB chromogenic substrate was added for the chromogenic reaction, and distilled water was used to stop the reaction. After counterstaining, dehydration, clearing and mounting, the sections were examined under a light microscope to reveal the target antigens.
