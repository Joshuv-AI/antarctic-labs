C3 Glomerulopathy With Occult Gammopathy Treated With Low-Dose, Fixed Duration Venetoclax
Authors: Chintan V Shah, Sherif Badra, Faiza Manji, Alejandro Best, Ansh Mehta, James W Lynch, David L Murray
Published: 2026

Introduction

C3 glomerulopathy (C3G), when associated with monoclonal gammopathy (MG, C3-MG), is now characterized as an MG of renal significance–associated disorder, in which the monoclonal Ig acts as a C3 nephritic factor or an anti–factor H antibody.1,2 Detection of monoclonal protein (M-protein) using serum protein electrophoresis and immunofixation remains a key criterion for diagnosing C3G-MG.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Here, we describe a case of C3G with occult gammopathy in which no M-protein was detected using serum and urine studies, including serum mass spectrometry. With high clinical suspicion, the patient underwent bone marrow evaluation and clone-directed treatment, with a dramatic kidney response. Key teaching points of this case are presented in Table 1.

Case Presentation

A 69-year-old male with a history of hypertension was referred to the onco-nephrology clinic for a second opinion for C3 glomerulonephritis.

The patient presented with leg swelling with a serum creatinine of 2.7 mg/dl and a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio of 4.6 g/g. Urine microscopy revealed 324 erythrocytes/hpf. The kidney ultrasound was unremarkable. The patient underwent a kidney biopsy, which yielded 41 glomeruli; light microscopy showed 28 glomeruli with mesangial and endocapillary hypercellularity with neutrophilic exudation, 30% interstitial fibrosis, and tubular atrophy. Immunofluorescence showed mesangial and capillary wall C3 deposits (2+) on frozen sections as well as on pronase-digested paraffin-embedded sections. Electron microscopy showed subepithelial hump-like electron-dense deposits. The findings were consistent with C3 glomerulonephritis (Figure 1a). Serological workup (occult infection, alternate complement pathway activity) and anti–factor H antibody were unremarkable. Genetic workup showed a single heterozygous complement factor H variant of unknown significance (Supplementary Table S1).

A further hematological evaluation was not offered by the local oncology team because of a negative serum protein electrophoresis, serum immunofixation, urine protein electrophoresis, urine immunofixation, and serum matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry. However, because of an elevated serum free light chain ratio (κ: 25.4 mg/dl, λ: 3.2 mg/dl, κ/λ ratio: 7.83) and a high index of suspicion, the patient was referred to the onco-nephrology service for a second opinion and underwent a bone marrow biopsy. Bone marrow immunohistochemical stains showed clonal B-cell aggregates occupying approximately 10% of marrow cellularity, and flow cytometry confirmed the presence of a clonal B-cell population with dim surface kappa restriction expressing CD19, CD20, bright CD5, CD23, and CD11c, without significant expression of CD10 or CD38. Peripheral blood flow cytometry confirmed CD5 (+) clonal B cells at 0.85 × 109/L (11.3% of white blood cells). Cytogenetics, fluorescence in situ hybridization, and next-generation sequencing showed no clonal aberrations or pathogenic variants. Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) was excluded because of the absence of lymphadenopathy, organomegaly, cytopenias, or symptoms attributable to a lymphoproliferative disorder. The overall findings were most consistent with a monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis (MBL) with a CLL-like phenotype.

Despite a negative serum M-protein, we hypothesized that an occult M-protein from the kappa-restricted MBL was driving C3G. The patient was treated with 2 doses of 1 g of rituximab 2 weeks apart. Two months later, although peripheral blood CD20 B-lymphocytes were 0 cells/μl, a monoclonal CD5+/CD23+ population persisted (11% of leukocytes), with CLL/SLL phenotype, positive for CD22 and CD19 and negative for CD10, CD38, and CD20 (absent CD20 reflecting anti-CD20 therapy). Four months after rituximab, serum creatinine was 2.3 mg/dl, urine protein-to-creatinine ratio of 4.3 g/g, and urine microscopy showed 174 erythrocytes/hpf. Later, the patient was started on the oral B-cell leukemia/lymphoma-2 (BCL-2) inhibitor, venetoclax (100 mg/d, ramped to and maintained at 200 mg/d for 3 months). Repeat peripheral blood flow cytometry showed a significant response with MBL (1% of leukocytes), without any significant changes in serum free light chain ratio (κ: 20.5 mg/dl, λ: 2.7 mg/dl, κ/λ ratio: 7.45) measured at 2 weeks and 3 months from starting venetoclax. This was followed by a dramatic kidney response, with urine protein-to-creatinine ratio declining to 0.3 g/g, serum creatinine of 2.2 mg/dl, serum albumin of 3.9 g/dl, and resolution of hematuria (0–2 erythrocytes/hpf; Figure 1b). Venetoclax was well-tolerated without cytopenias or infection, and the patient remains asymptomatic off diuretics. Venetoclax was electively discontinued after 3 months. At 6 months postdiscontinuation, the hematological response was stable (MBL at 1% of white blood cells) with persistent renal response (serum creatinine: 2 mg/dl, albumin: 4.2 g/dl, urine protein-to-creatinine ratio: 0.7, 0 red blood cells/hpf).

Discussion

We describe a patient with C3G with undetectable serum M-protein in whom a presumably benign hematologic condition (i.e., MBL) was identified and treated with low-dose, fixed-duration BCL-2 inhibitor, venetoclax, producing a dramatic kidney response.

This case highlights that the absence of a detectable serological M-protein should not exclude the possibility of an occult nephropathic Ig. Although serum free light chain ratio was elevated, the patient did not meet iSTOPMM criteria for light chain MG, and serum free light chain ratio was unchanged at the end of therapy.7,8 In fact, the absence of M-protein detection, when clinical suspicion is high, may instead reflect the limited sensitivity of currently available methodologies. Mills et al. previously demonstrated the limited sensitivity of immunofixation electrophoresis alone compared with mass spectrometry.9 In addition, current methodologies can only detect M-protein when expressed above the polyclonal background. A second possibility is the presence of a pathological nonmonoclonal autoantibody directed toward either the complement regulators or renal proteins. In such cases, the pathological antibody would not be monoclonal.

MBL has a prevalence of 5% to 12% in adults aged > 40 years and increases with age. High-count MBL (0.5 to <5 × 109/L), a CLL precursor, has a 1% to 2% annual progression risk; monitoring alone is typically recommended.9 Chauvet et al.4 showed that a kidney response correlates with hematologic response in C3G-MG.3 We hypothesized a causal link between C3G with occult gammopathy and kappa-restricted MBL, and successfully treated the patient with venetoclax. Fixed-duration venetoclax-based regimens are now first-line therapy for CLL.S1,S2 Although venetoclax has not been reported for MBL as of 2025, low-dose fixed-duration therapy may offer efficacy with limited toxicity.

Rituximab resistance (hematological and renal) is not uncommon, likely due to relatively low CD20 expression on pathological lymphocytes. As seen in our case, despite a CD20 count of 0 cells/μl after rituximab, the MBL burden remained unchanged. Venetoclax, by contrast, blocks the BCL2-mediated, TP53-independent survival signal and may offer a higher likelihood of response than retreatment with another CD20-directed agent such as obinutuzumab. In addition, as a once-daily pill, it is an attractive and convenient option for the patient.

In Chauvet et al.’s4 C3G cohort of 201 adults, approximately 30% had detectable M-protein overall, rising to approximately 60% among those aged > 50 years.3 Advanced, highly sensitive methods such as bone marrow next-generation sequencing could be helpful in the rest. In cases where defining a small B-cell clone is not feasible because of technical limitations or patient factors, a therapeutic trial of the oral BCL2 inhibitor venetoclax may offer a safe, convenient option warranting further investigation.

Conclusion

First, in patients with C3G, occult gammopathy can occur even if serum M-protein is undetectable, especially in older adults (aged > 40 years). Hematological work-up, like bone marrow evaluation, can reveal occult M-protein or hematological disease. Although the novel complement pathway blockers have shown promise,S3 treating the underlying clone, when present, may yield a more profound and more lasting kidney response, as seen in our case. Second, fixed-duration venetoclax-based regimens are now the backbone of frontline therapy for CLL. We suggest that low-dose, fixed-duration oral BCL2 inhibitor, venetoclax, may be a viable, safe, and convenient option for treating the lymphocytic clone associated with C3G with occult gammopathy (Table 1).
