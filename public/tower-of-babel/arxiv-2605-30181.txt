Generalized matrix nearness problems II
Rongbiao Thomas Wang, Chi-Kwong Li, Lek-Heng Lim

Abstract
. Given a matrix A A , a matrix nearness problem seeks an X X that most closely approximates A A in the sense of minimizing ∥ A − X ∥ \lVert A-X\rVert under a variety of constraints on X X . A generalized matrix nearness problem seeks the same but with three given matrices A , B , C A,B,C and ∥ A − B ​ X ​ C ∥ \lVert A-BXC\rVert in place of ∥ A − X ∥ \lVert A-X\rVert . We extend previous studies of the latter problem in three directions: incorporating an affine term, replacing matrix product by Kronecker product in various manners, and generalizing Frobenius norm to any orthogonally invariant norm. We will solve several of these in closed form. For the rest, we develop an iterative algorithm that works for any Schatten norm, proving that it converges to a global minimizer regardless of the initial point. In addition, the algorithm relies purely on numerical linear algebra, and notably does not compute any explicit gradients or subgradients. Along the way, we will also show that there is no Mirsky-type theorem for rank constrained generalized matrix nearness problems.

2.
Conventions and notations

We write all vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as column vectors, i.e.,
\mathbb{R}^{n}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
. When
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
are enclosed by parentheses, it denotes a column vector, i.e.,

(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

When enclosed by square brackets,
[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
is a row vector.

Throughout this article, when
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
, and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n}
, we assume without loss of generality that
m\geq p
and
n\geq q
, since otherwise we may simply add rows of zeros to
A
and
B
or columns of zeros to
A
and
C
to meet these requirements. We write
E_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
for the standard basis matrix with
1
in its
(i,j)
th entry and
0
elsewhere; when
n=1
, we write
e_{i}\coloneqq E_{i1}
for the standard basis vector. We use upper case letters for matrices and lower case letters for vectors and scalars.

Adhering to convention in numerical linear algebra, the vectorization operator
\vect:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{mn}
is defined by

\vect(X)\coloneqq(x_{11},\dots,x_{m1},x_{12},\dots,x_{m2},\dots,x_{1n},\dots,x_{mn}),

i.e., with respect to the reverse lexicographic ordering on indices. As a result of this we have the somewhat awkward interplay with the Kronecker product:

(2.1)

\vect(BXC)=(C^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\otimes B)\vect(X)

for any
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n}
, and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
. Note that if we had defined
\vect
with respect to the lexicographic ordering, it would simply have been “
\vect(BXC)=(B\otimes C)\vect(X)
.”

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. For
i=1,\dots,\min(m,n)
, we write
\sigma_{i}(X)
for the
i
th largest singular value of
X
. We view singular values as a
function

\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{\min(m,n)}
,

(2.2)

\sigma(X)\coloneqq\bigl(\sigma_{1}(X),\dots,\sigma_{\min(m,n)}(X)\bigr).

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. We view eigenvalues as a
set

\lambda(X)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We write
\lambda_{\max}(X)
and
\lambda_{\min}(X)
for the largest and smallest eigenvalues by magnitude.

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, the vector
p
-norm is denoted by
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{p}
and the Schatten
p
-norm is defined by

(2.3)

\lVert X\rVert_{\sigma,p}\coloneqq\Bigl(\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{\min(m,n)}\sigma_{i}(X)^{p}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{p}}=\lVert\sigma(X)\rVert_{p}.

In particular,
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\sigma,1}
is the nuclear norm,
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\sigma,\infty}
is the spectral norm, and
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\sigma,2}=\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}
is the Frobenius norm. The letter
p
is used both as matrix dimensions and as norm powers, but never in close proximity.

Given a finite-dimensional vector space
\mathbb{V}
with norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
, we denote its dual space and dual norm by

\mathbb{V}^{*}\coloneqq\{\varphi:\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{R}:\varphi\text{ is linear}\},\qquad\lVert\varphi\rVert^{*}\coloneqq\sup_{\lVert x\rVert=1}\varphi(x).

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
is called orthogonally invariant if it satisfies
f(UXV)=f(X)
for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and orthogonal matrices
U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. For example, rank, singular values, Schatten norms are all orthogonally invariant functions.

The subdifferential
[
5
]
of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
at
X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is denoted by

\partial f(X)\coloneqq\bigl\{G\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:f(Y)-f(X)\geq\tr\bigl(G^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(Y-X)\bigr)\text{ for all }Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\bigr\}.

For those unfamiliar with this notion, note that subdifferentials are set-valued; an element of
\partial f(X)
is called a subgradient.

3.1.
Affine generalized matrix nearness

We first address a simple case for later use. The closed form solution to the least squares problem
\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\lVert A-bx^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}
is standard, commonly required in mean centering
[
15
]
. It is straightforward to extend this to two-sided variants:

(3.1)

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;\lVert A-bx^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}C\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;\lVert A-Bxc^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}},

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By taking transpose, the two problems are easily seen to be identical and we will use the one on the right. Although a special case of (
1.1
) and follows from the closed-form solution in
[
13
, Equation 2.1]
, this simple case has a normal equation type closed form solution that avoids singular value decomposition:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
, and
0\neq c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

x_{*}=\frac{1}{c^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}c}B^{\dagger}Ac

gives a solution to (
3.1
).

Proof.

Since
\lVert A-Bxc^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}=\lVert A\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}-2\tr(A^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Bxc^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}})+\lVert Bxc^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}
, it is equivalent to minimizing

f(x)=-2\tr(A^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Bxc^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}})+\lVert Bxc^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}=\lVert c\rVert_{2}^{2}x^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}B^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Bx-2x^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}B^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Ac

Taking gradient
\nabla f(x)=2\lVert c\rVert_{2}^{2}B^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Bx-2B^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Ac=0
gives the required solution.
∎

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The affine orthogonal Procrustes problem

\min_{X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}X=I,\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\lVert A-BX+cx^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}

is well-known and closely related to the notion of centering matrices in statistics
[
15
]
. The affine generalized matrix nearness problem of this section may be regarded as a generalization to arbitrary constraints beyond orthogonality and allowing for extra matrix multipliers:

(3.2)

\min_{X\in\mathcal{S},\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\lVert A-BXC+dx^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}E\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\min_{X\in\mathcal{S},\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\lVert A-BXC+Dxe^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}.

Here
C\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n}
,
e\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and we assume that
E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
D\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
are nonsingular.
Since the Frobenius norm is invariant under taking transpose, the two problems in (
3.2
) are really one and the same. We will work with the version on the right. The following shows how such an affine generalized matrix nearness problem can be transformed into a (standard) generalized matrix nearness problem.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n}
, nonsingular
D\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
0\neq e\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
be a constraint set. If

(3.3)

\displaystyle X_{*}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{X\in\mathcal{S}}\;\biggl\lVert A\biggl(I-\frac{ee^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}{\lVert e\rVert_{2}^{2}}\biggr)-BXC\biggl(I-\frac{ee^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}{\lVert e\rVert_{2}^{2}}\biggr)\biggr\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}},

\displaystyle x_{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}e}D^{\dagger}(BX_{*}C-A)e,

then
(X_{*},x_{*})
is a solution to (
3.2
).

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, for each
X\in\mathcal{S}
,

\frac{1}{\lVert e\rVert_{2}^{2}}D^{\dagger}(BX_{*}C-A)e\in\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;\lVert A-BXC+Dxe^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\min_{X\in\mathcal{S},\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\lVert A-BXC+Dxe^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}

\displaystyle=\min_{X\in\mathcal{S}}\;\biggl\lVert A-BXC-DD^{\dagger}(A-BXC)\frac{ee^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}{\lVert e\rVert_{2}^{2}}\biggr\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}

\displaystyle=\min_{X\in\mathcal{S}}\;\biggl\lVert A\biggl(I-\frac{ee^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}{\lVert e\rVert_{2}^{2}}\biggr)-BXC\biggl(I-\frac{ee^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}{\lVert e\rVert_{2}^{2}}\biggr)\biggr\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}.\qed

Note that (
3.3
) takes the form of a generalized matrix nearness problem in (

3.2.
Nearest separable Kronecker product

One might wonder what happens when we replace the matrix product in (
1.1
) with Kronecker product, i.e.,

(3.4)

\min_{X\in\mathcal{S}}\;\lVert A-B\otimes X\otimes C\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}

for given
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}pm_{2}\times n_{1}qn_{2}}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times n_{1}}
, and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}\times n_{2}}
. We show that it may be transformed into a matrix nearness problem.

Let
\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}m_{2}p\times n_{1}n_{2}q}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}n_{1}m_{2}n_{2}\times pq}
be the unique linear operator satisfying

\mathcal{R}(Y\otimes X\otimes Z)=\vect(Y\otimes Z)\vect(X)^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}

for all
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times n_{1}}
,
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
, and
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}\times n_{2}}
. Linear operators of this form are called rearrangement operators (see Section
3.3
), convenient in problems involving Kronecker products. They entail nothing more than a rearrangement of the indices of matrix entries and are therefore invertible and isometries in the Frobenius norm.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}pm_{2}\times n_{1}qn_{2}}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times n_{1}}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}\times n_{2}}
, and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
. If
H\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
is the unique matrix with

\vect(H)=\frac{1}{\lVert B\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}\lVert C\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}}\mathcal{R}(A)^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\vect(B\otimes C),

then any

X_{*}\in\argmin_{X\in\mathcal{S}}\;\lVert X-H\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}

is a solution to (
3.4
).

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{R}
preserves Frobenius norms,

\displaystyle\lVert A-B

\displaystyle\otimes X\otimes C\rVert^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}=\lVert\mathcal{R}(A)-\vect(B\otimes C)\vect(X)^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\rVert^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathcal{R}(A)\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}-2\vect(B\otimes C)^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{R}(A)\vect(X)+\lVert B\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}\lVert C\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}\lVert X\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}

Let

\min_{\rank_{\otimes}(X)\leq r}\lVert A-(B_{1}\otimes B_{2})X(C_{1}\otimes C_{2})\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}.

As mentioned in the introduction, without the

We introduce another rearrangement operator. This terminology is due to

3.4.
Nearest prescribed partial trace

The use of Kronecker product also allows us to discuss notions with no nontrivial counterpart for matrix product. One example is partial trace, simple but ubiquitous in quantum computing
[
17
]
, indispensable for describing observable quantities of subsystems in a composite system.

The partial trace of a matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times p^{2}}
, partitioned as
X=(X_{ij})
with blocks
X_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
i,j=1,2,\dots,p
, is

\tr_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{P}}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\tr(X_{ii}),

i.e., it is a matrix-valued trace formed by taking sum of diagonal blocks and is compatible with Kronecker product:

\tr_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{P}}(Y\otimes Z)=\tr(Y)Z

for any
Y
and
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
.

We will consider the generalized nearness problem for prescribed partial trace, i.e., its value
\lambda
is prescribed a priori:

(3.11)

\min\;\{\lVert A-(B_{1}\otimes B_{2})X(C_{1}\otimes C_{2})\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}:\tr_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{P}}(X(W\otimes I))=\lambda W\text{ for some }W\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\},

for fixed
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}m_{2}\times n_{1}n_{2}}
,
B_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times p}
,
B_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}\times p}
,
C_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n_{1}}
,
C_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n_{2}}
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. This reminds us of the prescribed eigenvalue problem in
➃
on p.
➃
, although the latter is not a special case of (
3.11
). Nevertheless, our first method of solving (
3.11
) is by a reduction to the prescribed eigenvalue problem.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let

is a solution to

(3.12)

\min_{\lambda\in\lambda(\widehat{X})}\lVert\mathcal{R}(A)-(C_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\otimes B_{2})\widehat{X}(C_{1}\otimes B_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}})\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}.

Proof.

By the singular value decomposition of
\mathcal{R}(X)
, we have
\mathcal{R}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\vect(Z_{i})\vect(Y_{i})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
for some

and

On the other hand, by (

Therefore,

(3.13)

\mathcal{R}(X)\vect(W)=\lambda\vect(W)\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\tr_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{P}}(X(W\otimes I))=\lambda W.

The rest of the proof follows from the same calculation in (

4.
Norm dependence of generalized nearness problems

The closed form solutions in Section
3
, as well as those in
[
13
]
, are exclusively for generalized nearness problems in the Frobenius norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}
. From this section onwards, our focus would be on arbitrary orthogonally invariant norms
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
, occasionally restricted to Schatten
p
-norms
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\sigma,p}
,
p\in[1,\infty]
.

As we mentioned in Section
1.2
, we will show that one cannot expect a Mirsky-type result
[
16
, Theorem 2]
for rank constrained generalized nearness problems. Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
, and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n}
. Let

B=U_{B}\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{B}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}V_{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},\quad C=U_{C}\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{C}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}V_{C}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},

be the singular value decompositions of
B
and
C
with
\Sigma_{B}=\diag(\sigma_{1}(B),\dots,\sigma_{s}(B))
,
\Sigma_{C}=\diag(\sigma_{1}(C),\dots,\sigma_{t}(C))
,
\rank(B)=s
, and
\rank(C)=t
. For an orthogonal invariant norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
,

\lVert A-BXC\rVert=\biggl\lVert U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}_{B}AV_{C}-\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{B}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}V_{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}XU_{C}\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{C}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert.

Let

(4.1)

U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}_{B}AV_{C}\eqqcolon\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix},\quad V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}_{B}XU_{C}\eqqcolon\begin{bmatrix}X_{11}&X_{12}\\
X_{21}&X_{22}\end{bmatrix}

with
A_{11},X_{11}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}
. Then

(4.2)

\lVert A-BXC\rVert=\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}-\Sigma_{B}X_{11}\Sigma_{C}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert.

So

(4.3)

\begin{bmatrix}Y_{*}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\in\argmin_{Y\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}}\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}Y&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert\\
\Longleftrightarrow\quad V_{B}\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{B}^{-1}(A_{11}-Y_{*})\Sigma_{C}^{-1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}U_{C}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\in\argmin_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}}\lVert A-BXC\rVert.

If the left side of (
4.3
) admits different minimizer under different orthogonally invariant norms, then so does the right side. We now prove an optimality condition for the left side of (
4.3
). While the equivalence in (

) requires an orthogonally invariant norm, Proposition
4.1
below holds for any arbitrary norm.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
be a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert^{*}
be the dual norm. For any
S\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we write
\varphi_{S}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

, for the linear functional defined by

. Let
A_{12}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times(n-t)}
,
A_{21}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m-s)\times t}
,
A_{22}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m-s)\times(n-t)}
, and

.
Then

\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}Y_{*}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert\leq\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}Y&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert\quad\text{for all }Y\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}

if and only if there exists

(4.4)

S=\begin{bmatrix}0&S_{12}\\
S_{21}&S_{22}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

with
S_{12}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times(n-t)}
,
S_{21}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m-s)\times t}
,
S_{22}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m-s)\times(n-t)}
such that
\lVert\varphi_{S}\rVert^{*}=1
and

(4.5)

\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}Y_{*}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert=\tr\biggl(S^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}Y_{*}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr)=\tr\biggl(S^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}0&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr).

Proof.

Let
\beta\coloneqq\bigl\lVert\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Y_{*}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\bigr\rVert
. By its definition,
\beta\leq\bigl\lVert\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Y&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\bigr\rVert
for all
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}
. Consider the subspace

\mathbb{V}=\biggl\{\begin{bmatrix}Y&zA_{12}\\
zA_{21}&zA_{22}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:Y\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t},\,z\in\mathbb{R}\biggr\}

and the linear functional
\varphi:\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\varphi\biggl(\begin{bmatrix}Y&zA_{12}\\
zA_{21}&zA_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr)=\beta z

for all
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
.
We must also have
\lVert\varphi\rVert^{*}\leq 1
as

\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}Y&zA_{12}\\
zA_{21}&zA_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert=\lvert z\rvert\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}Y/z&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert\geq\lvert\beta z\rvert=\biggl\lvert\varphi\biggl(\begin{bmatrix}Y&zA_{12}\\
zA_{21}&zA_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr)\biggr\rvert

for all
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}
and
0\neq z\in\mathbb{R}
.
In fact,
\lVert\varphi\rVert^{*}=1
since
\beta=\varphi\bigl(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Y_{*}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\bigr)\leq\lVert\varphi\rVert^{*}\beta
. By Hahn–Banach Theorem,
\varphi
can be extended to a linear functional on
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with the same norm. By Riesz representation, any such linear functional must take the form
\varphi_{S}
for some
S\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. So
\lVert\varphi_{S}\rVert^{*}=1
and
\varphi_{S}\bigr\rvert_{\mathbb{V}}=\varphi
, i.e.,

\varphi_{S}\biggl(\begin{bmatrix}Y&zA_{12}\\
zA_{21}&zA_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr)=\tr\biggl(S^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}Y&zA_{12}\\
zA_{21}&zA_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr)=\beta z

for all
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
. Since

\tr\bigl(S^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}Y&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\bigr)=0

for all
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}
, the matrix
S
must be of the form (
4.4
) and satisfies (
4.5
).

Now, suppose that there exists
S\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
of the form (
4.4
) such that
\lVert\varphi_{S}\rVert^{*}=1
and satisfies (
4.5
). Then for any
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}
,

\displaystyle\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}Y_{*}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert

\displaystyle=\biggl\lvert\tr\biggl(S^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}Y_{*}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr)\biggr\rvert

\displaystyle=\biggl\lvert\tr\biggl(S^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}Y&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr)\biggr\rvert\leq\lVert S\rVert^{*}\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}Y&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert=\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}Y&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert.\qed

An alternative way, perhaps slightly shorter, to prove Proposition
4.1
would be to deduce it from
[
20
, Theorem 1.1]
; but we prefer a direct proof via the much better known Hahn–Banach Theorem.

With Proposition
4.1
in place, we may now construct an example to show that Mirsky Theorem
[
16
, Theorem 2]
does not extend to the generalized nearness problem.

Example 4.2
.

Consider the best rank-one generalized matrix nearness problem with

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

which gives

(4.6)

\min_{\rank(X)\leq 1}\;\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}X\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert_{\sigma,p}=\min_{\rank(X)\leq 1}\;\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}x_{11}&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert_{\sigma,p}.

In case this is not clear, in the minimization problem on the right of (
4.6
), the matrix
X
in the rank constraint is
not
the same matrix as the one in the norm objective, likewise in (
4.7
) below.
Recall that the dual norm of the Schatten
p
-norm is the Schatten
q
-norm where
1/p+1/q=1
. By Proposition
4.1
, we know that
X_{*}=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}x_{*}&y_{*}\\
z_{*}&w_{*}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
is a global minimizer of

(4.7)

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}\;\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}x_{11}&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert_{\sigma,p}

for
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
if there exists
S_{p}=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&b_{p}\\
c_{p}&d_{p}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
such that
\lVert\varphi_{S_{p}}\rVert_{\sigma,q}=1

and

\tr(S_{p}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}X_{*})=\tr\biggl(\begin{bmatrix}0&b_{p}\\
c_{p}&d_{p}\end{bmatrix}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}x_{*}&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\biggr)=\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}x_{*}&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert_{\sigma,p}.

We now show that the minima in (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) are the same, i.e., the minimum in (
4.7
) can be attained with rank-one matrices. For
p=1
, notice that

\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert_{\sigma,1}=\tr\biggl(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\biggr)=2,\quad\text{where }S_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}

satisfies
\lVert S_{1}\rVert_{\sigma,\infty}=1
. So any rank-one matrix of the form
X_{*}=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&y_{*}\\
z_{*}&w_{*}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
is a solution to (
4.7
).

For
p=2
, notice that

\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert_{\sigma,2}=\tr\Biggl(\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\Biggr)=\sqrt{3},\quad\text{where }S_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\end{bmatrix}

satisfies
\lVert S_{2}\rVert_{\sigma,2}=1
. So any rank-one matrix of the form
X_{*}=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&y_{*}\\
z_{*}&w_{*}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
is a solution to (
4.7
).

Similarly, for
p=\infty
,

\biggl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}-1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\biggr\rVert_{\sigma,\infty}=\tr\Biggl(\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\\
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\Biggr)=\sqrt{2}\quad\text{where }S_{\infty}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\\
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix}

satisfies
\lVert S_{\infty}\rVert_{\sigma,1}=1
. So any rank-one matrix of the form
X_{*}=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}-1&y_{*}\\
z_{*}&w_{*}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
is a solution to (
4.7
).

The minimizers for the
p=1
,
2
,
\infty
cases are all distinct. We conclude that the solution of

\min_{\rank(X)\leq r}\lVert A-BXC\rVert_{\sigma,p}

depend on the value of
p
; Mirsky Theorem does not hold here.

5.
Iterative solutions

Given that we do not have closed form solutions for norms other than the Frobenius norm, we now turn to iterative solutions. As mentioned in Section

, our proposed iterative algorithm is very versatile: it works with any of the constraints

The iterative algorithm in question is Algorithm

that attains the minimum

\min_{X\in\mathcal{S}}\;f(A-BXC).

Since treating this more general case requires no extra effort, we will establish our convergence results in Section

A word about the sublinear convergence in case it gives the reader pause: Such is the reality of working with norms that are not strongly convex. Note that the Schatten norm

is convex but not strictly convex for

and

and we lack that here. And we are not alone: sublinear convergence is typical of works involving

[

; without additional assumptions like the Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz property (which does not hold in our case), a sublinear rate is the best one can do
[

. Our silver lining is that, in practice, the numerical experiments in Section

5.1.
Convergence analysis

The idea that motivates our algorithm for (

Algorithm 2

Dykstra iteration

1:

Lemma 5.1

(Optimality condition)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
be subdifferentiable and attain its minimum on
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}\coloneqq\{A-BXC\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:X\in\mathcal{S}\}
. Then
X_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
is a minimizer of (
5.1
) if and only if there exists
G_{*}\in\partial f(A-BX_{*}C)
such that

(5.5)

\tr\bigl(G_{*}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}B(X_{*}-X)C\bigr)\geq 0\quad\text{for all }X\in\mathcal{S}.

Proof.

Let
\eta:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be the indicator function given by

\eta(Y)=\begin{cases}0&Y\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}},\\
\infty&Y\not\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}.\end{cases}

We rewrite (
5.1
) as

\min_{X\in\mathcal{S}}\;f(A-BXC)=\min_{Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}f(Y)+\eta(Y).

Since
\eta
is subdifferentiable:

0\in\partial\eta(Y)=\{G\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:\tr\bigl(G^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(Z-Y)\bigr)\leq 0\text{ for all }Z\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}\}

for all
Y\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
, the sum
f+\eta
is subdifferentiable on
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
. The function
f+\eta
attains its minimum at
Y_{*}
if and only if
0\in\partial(f+\eta)(Y_{*})=\partial f(Y_{*})+\partial\eta(Y_{*})
, i.e., there exists
G\in\partial f(Y_{*})
such that
-G\in\partial\eta(Y_{*})
. This is equivalent to the existence of
G_{*}\in\partial f(Y_{*})
with

(5.6)

\tr\bigl(G^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(Y-Y_{*})\bigr)\geq 0\quad\text{for all }Y\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}.

A matrix
X_{*}
minimizes (
5.1
) if and only if
Y_{*}=A-BX_{*}C
minimizes
f+\eta
. Since
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
is the surjective image of
\mathcal{S}
under
X\mapsto A-BXC
, (
5.6
) is equivalent to (
5.5
).
∎

We next demonstrate sufficient descent in Algorithm
2
.

Lemma 5.2

(Sufficient descent)
.

Let
Y_{*}=A-BX_{*}C
minimize (
5.1
) with
\Delta_{*}\in\partial f(Y_{*})
such that

(5.7)

\tr\bigl(\Delta_{*}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}B(X_{*}-X)C\bigr)\geq 0\quad\text{for all }X\in\mathcal{S}.

Then the iterate
(Y_{k},\Delta_{k})
of Algorithm
2
satisfies

\displaystyle\lVert Y_{k}-Y_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\lVert\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}-\lVert Y_{k+1}-Y_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle-\lVert\Delta_{k+1}-\Delta_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}

(5.8)

\displaystyle\geq\lVert Y_{k}-Y_{k+1}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\lVert\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

Proof.

For each
k
, it follows from (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) that
\mu\Delta_{k}\in\partial f(Y_{k})
.
Since
\mu\Delta_{k}\in\partial f(Y_{k})
and
\mu\Delta_{k+1}\in\partial f(Y_{k+1})
,

(5.9)

\begin{split}\tr\bigl((\mu\Delta_{k}-\mu\Delta_{k+1})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(Y_{k}-Y_{k+1})\bigr)&=\tr\bigl(\mu\Delta_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(Y_{k}-Y_{k+1})\bigr)-\tr\bigl(\mu\Delta_{k+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(Y_{k}-Y_{k+1})\bigr)\\
&=\tr\bigl(\mu\Delta_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(Y_{k}-Y_{k+1})\bigr)+\tr\bigl(\mu\Delta_{k+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(Y_{k+1}-Y_{k})\bigr)\\
&\geq f(Y_{k})-f(Y_{k+1})+f(Y_{k+1})-f(Y_{k})=0.\end{split}

By (
5.2
),
\lVert Y_{k+1}-A+BXC-\Delta_{k}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}-\lVert Y_{k+1}-A+BX_{k+1}C-\Delta_{k}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}\geq 0
for all
X\in\mathcal{S}
. By

(5.10)

\lVert a-c\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\lVert b-c\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}=2\tr\bigl((a-c)^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(a-b)\bigr)-\lVert a-b\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2},

we get
2\tr\bigl((Y_{k}-A+BXC-\Delta_{k})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}B(X-X_{k+1})C\bigr)\geq\lVert B(X-X_{k+1})C\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}
.
Plugging in (
5.4
) gives

(5.11)

\tr\bigl((Y_{k}-Y_{k+1}-\Delta_{k+1})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}B(X-X_{k+1})C\bigr)\geq 0.

Taking
X=X_{*}
and adding to (
5.7
), we get

\tr\bigl((Y_{k}-Y_{k+1}+\Delta_{*}-\Delta_{k+1})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(Y_{k+1}-Y_{*}+\Delta_{k+1}-\Delta_{k})\bigr)\geq 0
.
By (
5.4
) and (
5.9
),

\displaystyle\tr\bigl((\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(\Delta_{k+1}-\Delta_{*})\bigr)

\displaystyle+\tr\bigl((Y_{k+1}-Y_{*})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(Y_{k}-Y_{k+1})\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\tr\bigl((\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(Y_{k}-Y_{k+1})\bigr)\geq 0.

By (
5.10
) again,

\displaystyle\lVert Y_{k}-Y_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\lVert\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle-\lVert Y_{k+1}-Y_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}-\lVert\Delta_{k+1}-\Delta_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\lVert Y_{k}-Y_{k+1}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\lVert\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}\\
+2\tr\bigl((Y_{k}-Y_{k+1})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(Y_{k+1}-Y_{*})+(\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(\Delta_{k+1}-\Delta_{*})\bigr)\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\geq\lVert Y_{k}-Y_{k+1}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\lVert\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}.\qed

Theorem 5.3

(Global, provable, and sublinear convergence)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
be subdifferentiable and attain its minimum on
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}\coloneqq\{A-BXC\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:X\in\mathcal{S}\}
. For any
\mu>0
, the iterates
(Y_{k},\Delta_{k})
in Algorithm
2
will converge to
(Y_{*},\Delta_{*})
such that
Y_{*}=A-BX_{*}C
for some
X_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}
that minimizes (
5.1
) and
\Delta_{*}\in\partial f(Y_{*})
that satisfies (
5.7
). The convergence is sublinear as

(5.12)

\frac{\lVert Y_{k+1}-Y_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\lVert\Delta_{k+1}-\Delta_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}}{\lVert Y_{k}-Y_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\lVert\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}}\leq 1,\quad k=0,1,2,\dots.

If
B
has full column rank and
C
has full row rank, then
X_{*}
is unique and the iterates
X_{k}
in Algorithm
2
will converge to
X_{*}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.2
, the sequence
(\lVert Y_{k}-Y_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}+\lVert\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}})_{k=0}^{\infty}
is monotone decreasing and bounded below, thus convergent. It remains to show that the limit is zero. By passing through a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that
(Y_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
and
(\Delta_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
are convergent with limits
\widehat{Y}
and
\widehat{\Delta}
respectively. Taking limit in (
5.11

\tr\bigl(\widehat{\Delta}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(\widehat{Y}-A+BXC)\bigr)\geq 0
for all
X\in\mathcal{S}
.
By Lemma
5.1
,
\widehat{Y}
is a global minimizer. Let
Y_{*}=\widehat{Y}
and
\Delta_{*}=\widehat{\Delta}
. Since
(\lVert Y_{k}-Y_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}+\lVert\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{*}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}})_{k=0}^{\infty}
is convergent, we must have
(Y_{k},\Delta_{k})\to(Y_{*},\Delta_{*})
. We rearrange (
5.8
) to get (
5.12
).

If
B
has full column rank and
C
has full row rank, then

is injective, so
X_{*}
is unique. Taking limit in (

) gives

\lim_{k\to\infty}\lVert B(X_{*}-X_{k})C\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}=0
and thus
\lim_{k\to\infty}\lVert X_{*}-X_{k}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}=0
.
∎

Proposition 5.4

(Minima attainment)
.

Let

(2)

The function defined by

\displaystyle=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}p_{2}\times q_{1}q_{2}}:\rank_{\otimes}(X)\leq r\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times p^{2}}:\tr_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{P}}(X(W\otimes I))=\lambda W\text{ for some }W\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\}.

Let
\mathbb{V}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}:BXC=0\}

(1)

Lemma 5.5

(
Y_{k}
-update for nuclear norm)
.

Let
\mu>0
and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with singular value decomposition
M=U\Sigma V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
. Let
\sigma_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{\min(m,n)}
be given by

\sigma_{i}^{*}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{i}(M)-1/\mu&\text{if }\sigma_{i}(M)>1/\mu,\\
0&\text{if }\sigma_{i}(M)\leq 1/\mu,\end{cases}

for
i=1,\dots,\min(m,n)
. Then

Y_{*}\coloneqq U\diag(\sigma_{*})V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\in\argmin\bigl\{\lVert Y\rVert_{\sigma,1}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}\lVert Y-M\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}:Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\bigr\}.

Proof.

Choose
\ell\in\{1,\dots,\min(m,n)\}
such that

\begin{cases}\sigma_{i}(M)\geq 1/\mu&\text{if }i\leq\ell,\\
\sigma_{i}(M)<1/\mu&\text{if }i>\ell.\end{cases}

Let
g\in\mathbb{R}^{\min(m,n)}
be defined by

g_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i\leq\ell,\\
\mu\sigma_{i}(M)&\text{if }i>\ell.\end{cases}

Since
\lVert g\lVert_{\infty}=1
and

g^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\sigma_{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bigl(\sigma_{i}(M)-1/\mu\bigr)=\lVert\sigma_{*}\rVert_{1},

we obtain
g\in\partial\lVert\sigma_{*}\rVert_{1}
by (
5.14
). It follows that
U\diag(g)V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\in\partial\lVert Y_{*}\rVert_{\sigma,1}
by (
5.13
). So
Y_{*}
is a required minimizer as

U\diag(g)V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}+\mu(Y_{*}-M)=0\in\partial\bigl(\lVert Y_{*}\rVert_{\sigma,1}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}\lVert Y_{*}-M\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}\bigr).\qed

Lemma 5.6

(
Y_{k}
-update for spectral norm)
.

Let
\mu>0
and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with singular value decomposition
M=U\Sigma V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
. Let

(5.15)

\ell=\max\biggl\{i\in\{1,\dots,\min(m,n)\}:\sigma_{i}(M)\geq\frac{1}{i}\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\sigma_{j}(M)-\frac{1}{\mu}\biggr)\biggr\}

and
\sigma_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{\min(m,n)}
be given by

\sigma_{i}^{*}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\ell}\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\sigma_{j}(M)-\frac{1}{\mu}\biggr)&\text{if }i\leq\ell,\\
\sigma_{i}(M)&\text{if }i>\ell,\end{cases}

for
i=1,\dots,\min(m,n)
. Then

Y_{*}\coloneqq U\diag(\sigma_{*})V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\in\argmin\bigl\{\lVert Y\rVert_{\sigma,\infty}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}\lVert Y-M\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}:Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\bigr\}.

Proof.

Let
g\in\mathbb{R}^{\min(m,n)}
be given by

g_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\ell}+\mu\sigma_{i}(M)-\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mu\sigma_{j}(M)&\text{if }i\leq\ell,\\
0&\text{if }i>\ell.\end{cases}

By (
5.15
),

\mu\sigma_{i}(M)\geq\mu\sigma_{\ell}(M)\geq\frac{1}{\ell}\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mu\sigma_{j}(M)-1\biggr)

for all
i=1,\dots,\ell
. So
g
is a nonnegative vector. If
\ell<\min(m,n)
, then the maximality of
\ell
implies that

\frac{1}{\ell}\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sigma_{i}(M)-\frac{1}{\mu}\biggr)\geq\sigma_{\ell+1}.

The coordinates of
\sigma_{*}
are thus in nondecreasing order. By
[
23

\partial\lVert\sigma_{*}\rVert_{\infty}=\conv\{e_{i}:i=1,\dots,\ell\}.

Since

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\biggl(\frac{1}{\ell}+\mu\sigma_{i}(M)-\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mu\sigma_{j}(M)\biggr)=1,

we obtain
g\in\partial\lVert\sigma_{*}\rVert_{\infty}
. It follows that
U\diag(g)V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\in\partial\lVert Y_{*}\rVert_{\sigma,\infty}
by (
5.13
). So
Y_{*}
is a required minimizer as

U\diag(g)V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}+\mu(Y_{*}-M)=0\in\partial\bigl(\lVert Y_{*}\rVert_{\sigma,\infty}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}\lVert Y_{*}-M\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{F}}^{2}\bigr).\qed

7.
Conclusion

We expanded the study of generalized matrix nearness problems, deriving exact closed-form solutions in four new cases: affine objective, separable Kronecker product constraint, Kronecker rank constraint, and prescribed partial trace constraint. We showed that there is no result analogous to Mirsky Theorem for such problems. In part to address this, we proposed an iterative algorithm for generalized matrix nearness problems in any Schatten norm; the algorithm converges to a global minimizer from any starting point, and never computes a gradient or a subgradient explicitly.

As the numerical experiments show, our algorithm handles objectives like the Schatten
\frac{3}{2}
-norm, which are convex and yet lie outside any standard convex solver’s reach. For generalized nearness problems that do fall within the domain of such solvers, our algorithm is both faster and more accurate. This reaffirms a core premise alluded to in
[
13
, Section 7]
: Despite the popularity of general purpose convex optimization solvers, an old-school numerical linear algebra approach allows one to exploit structures far richer than mere convexity. This yields superior computational performance, and in several cases also extends to nonconvex problems.
