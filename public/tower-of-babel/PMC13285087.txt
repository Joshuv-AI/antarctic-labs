Non-destructive yield estimation of onion and garlic using UAV-based hyperspectral imaging and hybrid machine learning models
Authors: Yuqi Su, Jianchun Long, Juan Chen, Rui Zhou, Zhuolin Liu, Hao Deng, Ye Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate pre-harvest yield estimation of underground bulb crops such as onion and garlic is important for precision agriculture, harvest planning, and food-security-oriented decision-making. However, their harvestable organs develop below ground and cannot be directly observed using conventional remote sensing methods. This study aimed to develop a non-destructive yield estimation framework by integrating UAV-based hyperspectral imaging with hybrid machine learning models.MethodField experiments were conducted in Muan-gun, Korea, using onion and garlic as representative underground bulb crops. UAV-based hyperspectral images, crop growth traits, and destructive live bulb weight measurements were collected during the growing period. Hyperspectral images were processed through geometric correction, radiometric correction, and Savitzky–Golay spectral smoothing. Three dimensionality reduction methods, including genetic algorithm (GA), principal component analysis (PCA), and clustering, were used to reduce spectral redundancy. Five prediction models, including random forest (RF), XGBoost, partial least squares regression (PLSR), multilayer perceptron (MLP), and residual network (ResNet), were then evaluated for live bulb weight prediction.ResultSignificant spectral differences were observed in the 550–680 nm and 730–800 nm bands, which were closely associated with crop yield and below-ground bulb development. GA was the most effective feature selection method for extracting yield-related spectral bands. For onion yield prediction, the GA + RF model achieved the highest predictive accuracy, with an R2 of 0.9656 and an NRMSE of 18.55%. For garlic yield prediction, PLSR showed the best performance, with an R2 of 0.9260 and an NRMSE of 27.20%.ConclusionThe proposed UAV-based hyperspectral framework enables accurate, real-time, and non-destructive yield estimation for underground bulb crops. This approach reduces reliance on labor-intensive destructive sampling and provides a practical tool for precision crop monitoring and data-driven agricultural management.Keywords: Hyperspectral images, Machine learning models, Genetic algorithm, Live bulb prediction, Unmanned aerial vehicle

Introduction

Agriculture has long served as the backbone of human civilization, playing a vital role not only in ensuring food security and sustaining livelihoods but also in driving economic growth and technological advancement. However, with rapid population growth and increasing food demand, agricultural production is facing multiple challenges, including labor shortages, water scarcity, climate variability, and the reduction of arable land. In this context, improving crop productivity while reducing resource consumption has become a critical priority for precision agriculture and sustainable agricultural management.

The rise of intelligent agriculture provides a promising approach to addressing these challenges. By integrating automation, remote sensing, artificial intelligence, and data-driven decision-making, modern agricultural systems can improve crop monitoring efficiency, reduce labor dependence, and support timely management decisions. Among these technologies, hyperspectral imaging has emerged as a powerful tool in precision agriculture because it can capture fine-grained spectral information across hundreds of narrow and continuous bands. This capability enables the characterization of crop physiological and biochemical traits, including chlorophyll concentration, water status, canopy structure, and biomass accumulation.

In particular, the spectral range of 400–700 nm is sensitive to pigment absorption and chlorophyll-related changes, whereas the red-edge and near-infrared regions, especially 700–1300 nm, are closely associated with leaf internal structure, canopy vigor, and biomass-related traits. Therefore, hyperspectral imaging provides a useful non-destructive approach for evaluating crop growth status and predicting yield-related traits.

Recent studies have shown that crop yield estimation can be improved by integrating agronomic properties, remote sensing information, and machine learning models. Soil properties, optical and radar remote-sensing variables, phenological-stage information, and optimized feature-selection strategies have all been reported to contribute to yield prediction in crops such as date, wheat, and pistachio. These findings indicate that reliable yield prediction depends not only on the prediction algorithm itself but also on the rational selection of crop traits, spectral variables, environmental factors, and evaluation criteria.

However, most previous studies have focused on above-ground crops, orchard crops, soil-property-based yield modeling, or satellite-scale field monitoring. The non-destructive yield estimation of underground bulb crops remains less explored because the harvestable organs are hidden below the soil surface and cannot be directly observed by UAV-mounted sensors. Therefore, this study focuses on onion and garlic and investigates whether UAV-based hyperspectral canopy information can be used to estimate live bulb weight through appropriate image preprocessing, feature selection, and machine learning modeling.

To address this issue, this study developed a non-destructive yield estimation framework by integrating UAV-based hyperspectral imaging with hybrid machine learning models. Onion and garlic were selected as representative underground bulb crops. UAV-based hyperspectral data were collected across key growth stages, including leaf expansion, bulb formation, and senescence. The spectral data were then processed using geometric correction, radiometric correction, Savitzky–Golay spectral smoothing, dimensionality reduction, and predictive modeling to estimate live bulb weight without uprooting the plants.

Specifically, this study compared three dimensionality reduction methods, including genetic algorithm (GA), principal component analysis (PCA), and clustering, to reduce spectral redundancy and extract yield-related spectral features. Five prediction models, including partial least squares regression (PLSR), random forest (RF), XGBoost, multilayer perceptron (MLP), and residual network (ResNet), were further evaluated to identify optimal model combinations for onion and garlic yield prediction.

Related work

Precision agriculture has become an important approach for improving crop productivity, resource-use efficiency, and food-security-oriented agricultural decision-making. With the development of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), spectral imaging, machine learning, and deep learning, crop monitoring has gradually shifted from manual field investigation to non-destructive and data-driven phenotyping. Among these technologies, hyperspectral imaging is particularly valuable because it can capture continuous spectral information across hundreds of narrow bands, providing detailed information related to crop pigments, canopy structure, water status, nitrogen status, and biomass accumulation.

Hyperspectral imaging has been widely used in different agricultural applications. In crop disease monitoring, Zhao et al. used a rice blast vegetation index to improve early disease detection under UAV hyperspectral conditions [1], while Liu et al. optimized a GA-Mask R-CNN method for automatic disease and pest identification [2]. In soil monitoring, Tsakiridis et al. proposed a localized multi-channel one-dimensional convolutional neural network for soil property estimation [3], and Li et al. established a hyperspectral grey estimation model for predicting soil organic matter content [4]. Intelligent agricultural equipment and field management have also been investigated. Asefpour et al. developed an autonomous machine-vision-based system for nitrogen fertilizer management [5], and Benavides et al. explored robotic harvesting based on computer vision [6]. In addition, precision irrigation and pesticide application are important components of intelligent agricultural management. Thompson et al. investigated irrigation and nitrogen management practices related to nitrate leaching loss in intensive vegetable production systems [7], and Zheng et al. designed an intermittent herbicide spray system for open-field cabbage and evaluated its plant-protection effects [8]. These studies demonstrate that spectral sensing and intelligent algorithms can support crop monitoring, field management, and precision agricultural decision-making.

The integration of artificial intelligence and agricultural remote sensing has further improved the ability to analyze complex agricultural datasets. Machine learning has been used for heavy-metal source analysis in soil [9], fruit freshness estimation [10], water resource availability classification [11], automatic weed recognition [12], and wheat yield prediction using long short-term memory networks [13]. Deep learning has also been applied to hyperspectral image reconstruction and feature extraction [14]. These studies indicate that intelligent models can capture nonlinear relationships between spectral, image, and environmental features. However, many existing studies focus on a single model or a specific agricultural task, while systematic comparisons among different dimensionality reduction methods and prediction models remain limited.

Recent studies have further emphasized the value of hybrid and advanced learning models in intelligent agriculture. Reddy et al. proposed a crop yield estimation framework integrating Naive Bayes and convolutional neural networks, showing that probabilistic learning and deep feature extraction can be combined to improve agricultural prediction performance [15]. For crop health monitoring, Maruthai et al. developed a Hybrid Vision Graph Neural Network for early detection and protection against pest diseases in coffee plants, achieving a detection accuracy of 93.6625% using 2,850 labeled coffee plant images [16]. Vision-based models have also been extended to phenological and reproductive-stage monitoring. Sivasubramanian et al. proposed an IoU-based Cascade R-CNN framework for coffee flowering detection and pollination-transfer inspection, reporting flower detection accuracy ranging from 94.77% to 85.34% across different blooming stages [17]. In addition to prediction and detection, reliable agricultural monitoring also requires trustworthy data management. Vinayagam et al. proposed a blockchain-based crop-monitoring framework using the InterPlanetary File System, which improved data integrity, confidentiality, and monitoring reliability [18]. These studies show that intelligent agriculture is moving toward integrated systems that combine prediction accuracy, early-warning capability, phenological monitoring, and reliable data-driven decision support.

Feature reduction is a key issue in hyperspectral data analysis because hyperspectral images contain many highly correlated bands. Direct modeling with all bands may introduce redundancy, increase computational cost, and reduce model generalization. Existing dimensionality reduction methods can generally be divided into feature extraction and band selection. Principal component analysis transforms high-dimensional spectral data into low-dimensional components while retaining major variance information. Clustering-based methods group similar bands and select representative features. Band-selection methods, such as mutual-information-based selection and genetic algorithms, aim to identify the most informative wavelength bands for specific prediction tasks [19, 20]. Previous studies have also used hyperspectral imaging to estimate crop biophysical variables, such as leaf area index, chlorophyll content, nitrogen status, and biomass, which are closely related to crop growth and yield formation [21, 22].

Despite these advances, non-destructive yield estimation of underground crops remains challenging. Most remote-sensing-based yield estimation studies focus on above-ground crops, where yield-related organs or canopy traits can be directly observed. In contrast, onion and garlic develop their harvestable organs underground, making direct observation difficult using UAV-based sensors. Conventional yield estimation for these crops still relies heavily on destructive sampling, which is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and unsuitable for continuous field-scale monitoring. Therefore, a key scientific question is whether canopy-level hyperspectral features can indirectly reflect below-ground bulb development.

The physiological basis for this approach is that canopy reflectance is closely associated with chlorophyll concentration, leaf structure, water status, and biomass accumulation. Spectral regions in the visible range, especially 400–700 nm, are sensitive to pigment absorption, while the red-edge and near-infrared regions, especially 700–1300 nm, are closely related to canopy structure and crop vigor [23, 24]. Therefore, although the underground bulb cannot be directly observed, canopy hyperspectral information may still provide useful indicators for estimating live bulb weight.

Based on the above literature, several research gaps remain. First, UAV-based hyperspectral estimation of underground bulb crop yield has not been sufficiently explored. Second, most existing studies use a single prediction model or a limited modeling strategy, whereas systematic comparisons among dimensionality reduction methods and prediction models remain limited. Third, the reliability of hyperspectral yield prediction depends not only on model accuracy but also on standardized image preprocessing, spectral noise reduction, feature selection, and quantitative model evaluation. To address these gaps, this study integrates UAV-based hyperspectral imaging, geometric and radiometric correction, Savitzky–Golay spectral smoothing, three dimensionality reduction methods, and five prediction models to estimate the live bulb weight of onion and garlic in a non-destructive manner.

Experimental design and data collection

Experimental design

In this experiment, onions and garlic were planted and monitored at the Onion Vegetable Research Institute in Muan-gun, Jeollanam-do, Korea. Onions are planted from March 2024 to May 31, 2024, and garlic was planted from November 2023 to May 21, 2024. In order to ensure the scientific and comparative nature of the experiment, the experimental field was divided as follows: Regional division: The experimental field was divided into two large areas, the left side planted onions and the right planted garlic. Each large region is further subdivided into two control groups, the upper-Block A and lower-Block B groups, forming four main experimental regions. Each experimental area was further divided into 16 small experimental units, and 32 experimental units were set up for onion and garlic, for a total of 64 experimental units. Fertilizer levels: Fertilizer application was applied at four levels of 1/4, 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 times the standard rate. Each fertilizer level was distributed among all experimental units to ensure representative and extensive data. Hyperspectral image preprocessing

Geometric correction

Since the hyperspectral data used in this study were raw and unprocessed, geometric correction was first performed to rectify geometric distortions in the images. This step ensured that each pixel in the hyperspectral image corresponded accurately to a specific ground location. Geometric correction was conducted using ENVI software based on the hyperspectral image file and the corresponding geometric reference file. The geometric reference file provided spatial coordinate information, whereas the hyperspectral image file contained the original spectral data.

The specific processing steps were as follows: first, the hyperspectral image and geometric reference files were imported into ENVI. Second, the “Georeference from IGM” function was selected in the toolbox. Third, the hyperspectral image file was imported as the input file. Fourth, Band 2 was selected as the input X geometry band and Band 1 was selected as the input Y geometry band. Fifth, the datum was set to WGS-84 and the zone was set to 52. Finally, the output pixel size was set to 0.03. The comparison of hyperspectral images before and after geometric correction is shown in Fig. 4.

Rationale for factor and criterion selection

The selection of experimental factors, crop properties, spectral features, dimensionality reduction methods, prediction models, and evaluation criteria was based on agronomic relevance, non-destructive measurability, spectral sensitivity, and modeling robustness.

Onion and garlic were selected as representative underground bulb crops because their harvestable organs develop below the soil surface and cannot be directly observed by UAV-mounted sensors. Crop type, field block, and nitrogen fertilizer level were selected as the main experimental factors to account for crop differences, spatial field variation, and management-induced growth variation. Nitrogen fertilizer level was included because nutrient supply affects leaf growth, chlorophyll formation, canopy structure, biomass accumulation, and final yield formation. Previous studies have shown that yield prediction can be improved when agronomic and environmental properties are properly selected and modeled [6, 8].

Plant height, leaf length, leaf width, and number of leaves were selected as non-destructive crop growth indicators because they reflect vegetative growth, canopy development, and photosynthetic potential. Live bulb weight was selected as the target variable because it directly represents the yield of onion and garlic and provides the ground-truth reference for model training and evaluation.

Hyperspectral features were selected because canopy reflectance contains information associated with pigment absorption, leaf structure, water status, biomass accumulation, and crop growth. Previous remote-sensing studies have shown that optical indices, soil-vegetation indices, and radar-derived information can contribute to crop yield prediction when appropriate phenological stages and variables are selected. In this study, UAV-based hyperspectral imaging was used because it provides high-resolution canopy spectral information suitable for plot-scale monitoring.

PCA, clustering, and GA were selected to represent different dimensionality reduction strategies. PCA represents feature extraction, clustering represents unsupervised band grouping, and GA represents supervised band selection. These methods were used because hyperspectral datasets usually contain many highly correlated and redundant bands. PLSR, RF, XGBoost, MLP, and ResNet were selected to cover statistical regression, ensemble machine learning, and deep learning approaches. Model performance was evaluated using NRMSE and R2 because these metrics allow quantitative comparison of relative prediction error and explanatory ability. The final model selection was based on the joint consideration of lower NRMSE and higher R2.

Intelligent models

For the processing of hyperspectral images, the geometrically corrected hyperspectral data were first subjected to noise reduction and dimensionality reduction to remove noise from the data and reduce the data dimensionality to extract key spectral features. The noise reduction was used by Savitzky-Golay filter, and the dimensionality reduction was processed by principal component analysis (PCA), clustering method and genetic algorithm (GA), respectively. After noise reduction and dimensionality reduction, the following five prediction models were used to predict the output of the data, respectively: (1) a statistical method: partial least squares regression (PLSR); (2) two machine learning methods: random forest (RF) and XGBoost; (3) two deep learning methods: multilayer perceptron (MLP) and residual network (ResNet). By combining multiple intelligent models through dimensionality reduction algorithms and prediction models, this study aims to explore the optimal combination of hyperspectral data and intelligent models to achieve accurate prediction of live crop bulbs. Noise reduction methods

The Savitzky-Golay filter is a data smoothing method based on local polynomial fitting. Unlike the spline function, which achieves smoothing by fitting a low-order polynomial over a global or segmented range, and unlike the moving smoothing method, which simply moves the window to compute the mean or weighted average of data points, the Savitzky-Golay filter focuses more on retaining the details in the signal. The core idea is to use the least squares method to fit polynomials in the local scope of the sliding window to achieve data smooth while maximizing the retention of spectral features and detailed information. Especially for hyperspectral data, while removing noise, the Savitzky-Golay filter can retain the detailed spectral features well, making it an ideal noise reduction method for hyperspectral data analysis. Dimensionality reduction methods

In order to reduce the dimensionality of hyperspectral data and extract the main features, three dimensionality reduction methods are adopted: principal component analysis (PCA), cluster analysis and genetic algorithm (GA). The principles and applications of these three methods are described in detail below, respectively.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis is a dimensionality reduction method based on linear transformation, the core idea of which is to project high-dimensional data onto a new set of orthogonal coordinate axes to obtain new a new low-dimensional representation. Through this linear transformation, PCA can reduce the dimensionality while preserving the primary variance information of the data. The principal components generated by PCA are orthogonal to each other, thus minimizing the redundant information between dimensions.

In this paper, the hyperspectral data contains 150 bands, and the data is compressed into the first 30 principal components by PCA, which can explain most of the spectral information variation, thus realizing the purpose of dimensionality reduction. The advantage of this method is that the main information of the data is retained after dimensionality reduction, while the computational efficiency is high.

Clustering method

Clustering analysis is a dimensionality reduction method for grouping based on data similarity. The core idea is to divide the data into several clusters and use the representative features of each cluster as a new dimension to complete the dimensionality reduction. The reduced data not only retains the main features of the original data, but also significantly reduces the dimensional complexity, so it is especially suitable for analyzing high-dimensional sparse data. However, since the clustering method selects only one representative feature for each cluster, it may lead to the omission of some key information.

In this paper, the 150 bands in the hyperspectral data were clustered into 30 clusters, and representative feature bands in each cluster were selected for prediction to achieve dimensionality reduction. The advantages of this method are its visualization and simplified analysis of high-dimensional data, but the model performance needs to be weighed in terms of information loss.

Prediction methods

To realize effective prediction of hyperspectral data, this paper adopts five intelligent prediction methods: partial least squares regression (PLSR), random forest (RF), eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), multilayer perceptron (MLP) and residual network (ResNet). The five models selected in this study—partial least squares regression (PLSR), random forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), multilayer perceptron (MLP), and residual network (ResNet)—represent a comprehensive mix of statistical, ensemble learning, and deep learning approaches. PLSR provides a classical regression baseline that is particularly suitable for high-dimensional and collinear data, such as hyperspectral information. RF and XGBoost are robust ensemble models known for their high accuracy and ability to handle non-linear relationships and noise, making them ideal for complex agricultural datasets. MLP, as a traditional deep learning model, captures non-linearities in spectral patterns, while ResNet introduces deep architectures with skip connections that address issues like vanishing gradients, thereby enhancing prediction performance on structured high-dimensional data. Together, these models allow for a balanced and thorough evaluation of predictive capabilities in hyperspectral-based yield estimation.

Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR)

Partial least squares regression is a dimensionality reduction method that simultaneously considers the correlation between the independent variables and the dependent variable, and unlike traditional principal component analysis, PLSR not only reduces the dimensionality of the independent variables, but also retains the maximum covariance information with the dependent variable. The calculation process of PLSR mainly includes the following three steps: firstly, the principal components are extracted based on the covariance matrices of the dependent variable and the independent variable; secondly, the extracted principal components are used as the new independent variables and construct a linear regression model to predict the dependent variable; the residuals of the unexplained portion are analyzed, anew principal components are further extracted from the remaining independent variables, and the modeling process is repeated until the set stopping criteria is satisfied. In this study, the number of principal components is set to 12 when PLSR is used alone; and the number of principal components is set to 10 when it is used in combination with genetic algorithm to balance the complexity of the model with the prediction performance.

Random forest

Random Forest (RF) is a prediction method based on integrated learning, which generates multiple decision trees by autonomously sampling the data and combines the outputs of all trees to obtain the final prediction. Its advantages lie in its high accuracy and strong generalization ability, which is especially suitable for processing complex data and high-dimensional feature scenarios. In this paper, 100 trees are selected to form a random forest model, and other parameters are set by default to ensure the stability and prediction performance of the model.

XGBoost

XGBoost is a machine learning algorithm based on gradient boosting, the core idea of which is to take the residuals as the goal, gradually add trees to optimize the prediction ability of the model, and the model also introduces regularization to control the complexity of the model to avoid model overfitting. In this experiment, the maximum depth of the tree is taken to be 6 and the learning rate is 0.1 for the prediction of onion, and the maximum depth of the tree is taken to be 5 and the learning rate is 0.09 for the prediction of garlic. The sample sampling rate and the feature sampling rate of the two prediction experiments are both set to 0.8.

Multi-layer Perceptron (MLP)

Multi-layer Perceptron is a classical feedforward neural network that consists of an input layer, multiple hidden layers, and an output layer. It was used in this study to capture nonlinear relationships between hyperspectral features and live bulb weight.

In this study, the input dimension of the MLP depended on the dimensionality reduction method. Specifically, the input dimension was 30 for PCA- and clustering-based features, 51 for GA-selected onion bands, and 41 for GA-selected garlic bands. The MLP contained five hidden layers with 128, 64, 32, 16, and 8 neurons, respectively. The Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) function was used as the activation function for all hidden layers. A dropout layer with a dropout rate of 0.2 was added after the hidden layers to reduce overfitting. The output layer contained one neuron for continuous live bulb weight prediction.

The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001. The loss function was mean squared error (MSE). The maximum number of training epochs was set to 300, and the batch size was set to 16. Early stopping was applied based on the validation loss, with a patience of 30 epochs. The best model weights were restored after early stopping.

Residual Network (ResNet)

Residual Network is a deep learning architecture that uses shortcut connections to alleviate gradient vanishing and performance degradation during training. In this study, a one-dimensional ResNet structure was used to process hyperspectral feature vectors for live bulb weight prediction.

Each residual block consisted of two one-dimensional convolutional layers. The number of filters was set to 64, and the kernel size was set to 3. Batch normalization was applied after each convolutional layer, followed by the ReLU activation function. A shortcut connection was used to add the block input to the output of the second convolutional layer. When the input and output dimensions were inconsistent, a 1 × 1 convolution was used for dimension matching. After the residual blocks, global average pooling was applied, followed by a fully connected output layer with one neuron for live bulb weight prediction.

In this study, six residual blocks were used for onion data, and five residual blocks were used for garlic data. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001. The loss function was MSE, the batch size was 16, and the maximum number of epochs was 300. Early stopping was applied based on the validation loss, with a patience of 30 epochs, and the best model weights were restored. Performance evaluation and reliability assurance

Data splitting, cross-validation, and leakage prevention

To avoid over-optimistic model evaluation and prevent data leakage, the dataset for each crop was divided into a training set and an independent test set before dimensionality reduction, feature selection, and model training. Specifically, the onion and garlic datasets were randomly split into training and testing subsets at a ratio of 70:30. The training set was used for model development, including dimensionality reduction, feature selection, model fitting, and parameter optimization, whereas the independent test set was used only for final performance evaluation.

Within the training set, five-fold cross-validation was used to optimize model development and reduce the influence of random data partitioning. For each cross-validation fold, the training subset was used to fit the dimensionality reduction or feature-selection procedure and train the prediction model, while the validation subset was used to evaluate model performance during development.

To prevent information leakage, the independent test set was not used at any stage of feature selection, dimensionality reduction, model training, or hyperparameter tuning. For PCA-based analysis, the PCA transformation was fitted only on the training data and then applied to the test data. For clustering-based dimensionality reduction, band clustering and representative band selection were performed using only the training data. For GA-based feature selection, the fitness evaluation and optimal band selection were conducted only within the training set using cross-validation. After the optimal bands or transformations were determined from the training data, the final model was trained on the full training set and evaluated on the independent test set.

This procedure ensured that the reported model performance reflected the predictive ability on unseen data rather than information learned from the test set.

Food-security relevance and reliability assurance of the proposed framework

In this study, “security” refers to food-security-oriented agricultural decision support rather than cyber security. The proposed UAV-based hyperspectral framework contributes to food security by providing non-destructive and timely yield estimation for underground bulb crops. This enables early assessment of crop productivity, supports harvest planning, and assists resource allocation without the need for extensive destructive sampling.

The reliability of the proposed framework was ensured through several procedures. First, the field experiment was designed with two crop types, two blocks, four nitrogen levels, and 64 experimental plots, which improved the representativeness of the dataset. Second, repeated measurements of crop growth traits and destructive bulb-weight measurements were conducted to provide reliable ground-truth data. Third, hyperspectral images were processed using geometric correction and radiometric correction to reduce spatial and radiometric errors. Fourth, Savitzky–Golay filtering was applied to suppress spectral noise while preserving important spectral features. Finally, three dimensionality reduction methods and five prediction models were systematically compared, and model reliability was evaluated using RMSE and R2. These procedures collectively improved the robustness, reproducibility, and reliability of the yield prediction framework.

Discussion

Comparison with previous yield estimation studies

Remote-sensing-based crop yield estimation has been widely investigated in precision agriculture. Previous studies have demonstrated that multispectral, hyperspectral, and RGB image-derived features can be used to predict yield or yield-related traits in crops whose productive organs or canopy characteristics are directly observable. For example, machine learning and deep learning methods have been applied to wheat, rice, maize, fruit crops, and other above-ground crops, where visible canopy traits, ears, grains, or fruits can provide direct or semi-direct indicators of yield formation [26, 27]. Recent studies have further shown that hybrid learning strategies can improve agricultural prediction. Reddy et al. integrated Naive Bayes and convolutional neural networks for crop yield estimation, indicating that combining different learning paradigms can improve agricultural prediction performance [25].

Compared with these studies, the present work focuses on a more challenging scenario: non-destructive yield estimation of underground bulb crops. Onion and garlic develop their harvestable organs below the soil surface, and the target yield organs cannot be directly observed by UAV-mounted sensors. Therefore, this study differs from most above-ground crop yield studies by estimating below-ground live bulb weight indirectly from canopy-level hyperspectral information. The results showed that this indirect prediction strategy was feasible. For onion, the GA + Random Forest model achieved the best performance, with a high R2 of 0.9656 and a low NRMSE of 18.55%. For garlic, the direct PLSR model achieved the best performance, with an R2 of 0.9260 and an NRMSE of 27.20%. The low normalized prediction errors and high explanatory ability indicate that UAV-based hyperspectral imaging can provide useful yield-related information for non-destructive estimation of underground bulb crops.

Comparison of model performance and feature-reduction strategies

A major contribution of this study is the systematic comparison of dimensionality reduction methods and prediction models. Previous studies have often focused on a single model or a limited set of algorithms, whereas hyperspectral data usually contain highly correlated and redundant bands. Without effective feature reduction, model performance may be affected by noise, multicollinearity, and overfitting. In this study, PCA, clustering, and GA were compared as representative dimensionality reduction strategies. The results showed that GA-based band selection performed better than PCA and clustering in onion yield prediction, suggesting that supervised band selection can retain more yield-related spectral information.

The superior performance of GA + Random Forest and GA + XGBoost is consistent with previous findings that ensemble learning models are effective for nonlinear agricultural prediction tasks [26]. Random Forest and XGBoost can handle complex interactions among spectral features and are relatively robust to noise. In contrast, the MLP model showed weaker performance in several cases, possibly because the sample size was relatively limited for deep neural network training. This finding suggests that deep learning models do not always outperform traditional machine learning models when the dataset is small or medium-sized.

For garlic yield prediction, PLSR achieved the best overall performance, indicating that linear latent-variable modeling remains highly useful for hyperspectral datasets with strong collinearity. It is worth noting that PLSR alone achieved better performance than GA + PLSR for garlic, with a higher R2 value and lower NRMSE. Specifically, PLSR achieved an R2 of 0.9260 and an NRMSE of 27.20%, whereas GA + PLSR achieved an R2 of 0.9180 and an NRMSE of 28.64%. This difference may be explained by the intrinsic characteristics of PLSR and the crop-specific spectral response of garlic.

PLSR extracts latent variables from the full spectral space while maximizing the covariance between spectral predictors and the response variable. For garlic, yield-related information may be distributed across multiple correlated wavelength bands rather than concentrated in a small number of highly informative bands. Therefore, using the full spectral information through PLSR alone may preserve weak but complementary spectral signals that contribute to live bulb weight prediction. In contrast, GA-based band selection may remove some correlated bands that individually appear less important but collectively improve the latent-variable structure of PLSR. This could explain why GA + PLSR performed slightly worse than PLSR alone for garlic.

For onion, however, yield-related spectral information may be more concentrated in specific bands, allowing GA-based feature selection to remove redundant bands and improve model performance. Therefore, the optimal model should be selected according to crop type, spectral-response characteristics, sample size, and feature structure rather than relying on a single universal algorithm.

Spectral interpretation and physiological relevance

The spectral differences associated with yield were mainly located in the 550–680 nm and 730–800 nm wavelength ranges. These regions are physiologically meaningful. The 550–680 nm region is related to pigment absorption, especially chlorophyll-related reflectance, while the 730–800 nm red-edge and near-infrared regions are associated with canopy vigor, internal leaf structure, and biomass accumulation [27]. This supports the biological plausibility of using canopy hyperspectral data to estimate below-ground bulb development.

Climatic and illumination conditions should also be considered when interpreting UAV-based hyperspectral data. Cloud cover can change incoming solar radiation, atmospheric scattering, and the proportion of direct and diffuse light reaching the crop canopy. These changes may alter the magnitude and shape of canopy reflectance, especially when images are acquired on different dates or under variable sky conditions. Although radiometric correction was applied in this study to reduce inter-date radiometric inconsistency, residual effects from cloud cover, illumination angle, atmospheric conditions, and canopy shadowing may still influence spectral measurements. Therefore, future UAV hyperspectral acquisition should preferably be conducted under stable illumination conditions, and weather metadata, downwelling irradiance measurements, and calibration panels should be incorporated to further improve spectral consistency.

Plant nutritional status is another important factor affecting spectral interpretation. Nitrogen supply influences chlorophyll synthesis, leaf expansion, canopy structure, photosynthetic capacity, biomass accumulation, and final bulb development. Therefore, variations in nutritional status can affect both canopy reflectance and live bulb weight. In particular, the visible region around 550–680 nm is sensitive to pigment and chlorophyll-related changes, while the red-edge and near-infrared regions around 730–800 nm are closely associated with canopy vigor and biomass-related traits. Thus, the spectral regions identified in this study may reflect yield-related bulb development partly through nutrient-driven changes in canopy physiology. Since nitrogen levels were included as an experimental factor, the dataset captured part of this nutritional variation. However, direct measurements of leaf nitrogen concentration, SPAD values, chlorophyll content, or soil nutrient dynamics were not incorporated into the current modeling framework. Future studies should include these quantitative nutritional indicators to better distinguish yield-related spectral signals from nutrient-status-driven spectral variation.

Although onion and garlic bulbs cannot be directly detected by UAV sensors, their development is closely linked to canopy photosynthetic capacity, leaf expansion, nutrient status, and assimilate accumulation. Therefore, canopy reflectance can serve as an indirect indicator of bulb yield formation. This explains why the selected bands produced high prediction accuracy in this study. Compared with vision-based detection studies that rely on directly visible objects, such as pest-disease symptoms or flowers, the present study addresses a more indirect prediction problem. For example, Maruthai et al. used Hybrid Vision Graph Neural Networks for coffee pest-disease detection based on visible plant images, and Sivasubramanian et al. used an IoU-based Cascade R-CNN framework for coffee flowering and pollination monitoring. These studies demonstrate the value of computer vision for visible crop traits, whereas our work extends intelligent crop monitoring to below-ground yield estimation using canopy spectral responses.

Reliability of the proposed framework compared with recent intelligent monitoring systems

Recent precision agriculture studies have increasingly emphasized not only prediction accuracy but also monitoring reliability, traceability, and decision-support value. Vinayagam et al. proposed a blockchain-based crop-monitoring framework using the InterPlanetary File System to improve data integrity, confidentiality, and monitoring reliability [23]. Although the present study does not involve blockchain-based data storage, the reliability of the proposed framework was strengthened through experimental and methodological controls, including standardized field sampling, geometric correction, radiometric correction, Savitzky–Golay spectral smoothing, feature selection, multi-model comparison, and quantitative evaluation using NRMSE and R2. The geometric and radiometric correction procedures were particularly important for reducing spatial and radiometric errors in UAV hyperspectral images.

Compared with many image-recognition studies that report classification accuracy, this study used regression-based metrics to evaluate continuous live bulb weight prediction. NRMSE reflects the relative prediction error after normalization, while R2 reflected the proportion of yield variation explained by the model. This is more appropriate for yield estimation than classification accuracy alone. Therefore, the reliability of this framework was supported not only by high prediction performance but also by a complete preprocessing, modeling, and evaluation pipeline.

Advantages, limitations, and future perspectives

This study has several advantages. First, it provides a non-destructive approach for estimating the yield of underground bulb crops, whose harvestable organs cannot be directly observed by UAV-mounted sensors. Second, UAV-based hyperspectral imaging enables rapid field-scale acquisition of canopy spectral information, making the framework suitable for precision agricultural monitoring. Third, this study systematically compared three dimensionality reduction methods and five prediction models, which allowed the identification of crop-specific optimal model combinations. Fourth, the reliability of the proposed framework was supported by standardized field sampling, geometric and radiometric correction, spectral noise reduction, feature selection, multi-model comparison, and quantitative evaluation using NRMSE and R2.

However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the experiment was conducted at a single research site in Muan-gun, Korea, and within only one growing season. Therefore, the transferability of the proposed models to other regions, soil types, climatic conditions, crop varieties, and management practices requires further validation. Although an independent test set was used for model evaluation, external validation using datasets from different locations, years, or cultivars was not performed in the present study. Multi-location and multi-season validation will therefore be necessary to evaluate the generalizability and robustness of the proposed framework.

Second, environmental factors were not fully quantified in the current prediction framework. Cloud cover, solar radiation, rainfall, temperature, canopy wetness, and soil moisture may influence UAV-based hyperspectral reflectance and introduce inter-date variability. In particular, cloud cover can alter incoming solar radiation, atmospheric scattering, and the balance between direct and diffuse light, thereby affecting the magnitude and shape of canopy reflectance. Although geometric correction, radiometric correction, and spectral smoothing were applied to reduce spatial, radiometric, and spectral inconsistencies, residual environmental effects may still influence model stability under variable field conditions.

Third, plant nutritional status was not directly measured in this study. Nitrogen status can affect chlorophyll content, leaf expansion, canopy structure, photosynthetic capacity, biomass accumulation, and bulb development, thereby influencing both spectral signals and yield prediction. Although nitrogen fertilizer levels were included as an experimental factor, direct measurements of leaf nitrogen concentration, SPAD values, chlorophyll content, soil nutrient dynamics, and nutrient uptake were not incorporated into the current modeling framework. Therefore, some yield-related spectral features may partly reflect nutrient-driven canopy physiological variation rather than bulb development alone.

Fourth, the sample size was relatively limited, especially for training deep learning models such as MLP and ResNet. This may partly explain why deep learning models did not consistently outperform traditional machine learning models in this study. In addition, although GA-selected bands were used to identify important spectral features, further visualization and physiological interpretation of the selected wavelength regions would help clarify which spectral regions contributed most to yield prediction.

Future studies should expand the dataset across multiple locations, growing seasons, cultivars, and management conditions. Detailed weather records, downwelling irradiance measurements, rainfall data, soil moisture, soil nutrient information, leaf nitrogen content, and SPAD or chlorophyll measurements should be incorporated to improve model interpretation and external applicability. In addition, multi-source data such as multispectral imagery, LiDAR-derived canopy structural traits, soil properties, and meteorological variables should be integrated with hyperspectral features. Adaptive learning and transfer learning strategies should also be explored to improve model transferability across different regions and growing seasons, thereby supporting real-time and reliable agricultural decision-making.

Conclusion

This study developed a non-destructive yield estimation framework for underground bulb crops by integrating UAV-based hyperspectral imaging with hybrid machine learning models. By combining three dimensionality reduction methods, including Genetic Algorithm (GA), Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and clustering, with five prediction models, including Random Forest, XGBoost, PLSR, MLP, and ResNet, this study systematically evaluated the feasibility of estimating onion and garlic live bulb weight from canopy-level hyperspectral information.

The results showed that GA was the most effective feature selection method for reducing hyperspectral data dimensionality and extracting yield-related spectral bands. For onion yield prediction, the GA + Random Forest model achieved the best performance, with an R2 of 0.9656. For garlic yield prediction, PLSR showed the highest predictive accuracy, with an R2 of 0.9260. These findings demonstrate that although the yield organs of onion and garlic are located underground and cannot be directly observed by UAV sensors, canopy spectral characteristics can still provide reliable information for estimating below-ground bulb development.

The reliability of the proposed framework was supported by standardized field sampling, geometric and radiometric correction, spectral noise reduction, feature selection, multi-model comparison, and quantitative evaluation using NRMSE and R2. Overall, the proposed framework provides an accurate, real-time, and non-destructive approach for yield estimation of underground crops. It can reduce the dependence on labor-intensive destructive sampling and provide technical support for precision agriculture, harvest planning, and food-security-oriented agricultural decision-making. By extending UAV-based hyperspectral yield estimation from visible above-ground crop traits to non-visible underground bulb development, this study provides a promising framework for non-destructive monitoring of crops with erect growth and reduced canopy morphology.

Future work will focus on improving the generalizability and practical applicability of the proposed framework. First, multi-location and multi-season field experiments should be conducted to validate the robustness of the models under different soil types, climatic conditions, crop varieties, and management practices. Second, the frequency of hyperspectral data acquisition should be increased to better capture dynamic crop growth information throughout the growing season. Third, multi-source data, including soil properties, meteorological variables, irrigation information, multispectral imagery, and LiDAR-derived structural traits, should be integrated to further improve prediction accuracy. Finally, adaptive learning and transfer learning strategies should be explored to update the prediction models across different environments and support real-time agricultural decision-making.
