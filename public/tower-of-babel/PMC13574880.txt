Prediagnosis frailty and survival after cancer in middle-aged and older adults: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Guangzhi Wan, Chunyan Ma, Chunqiao Ma, Jingzhi Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrailty is a multidimensional syndrome reflecting diminished physiological reserve and has been associated with adverse outcomes in middle-aged and older adults with cancer. However, its long-term prognostic significance when assessed prior to cancer diagnosis remains insufficiently characterized.MethodsWe conducted a population-based prospective cohort study using data from the 2006 wave of the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), with follow-up through December 31, 2020. Participants were middle-aged and older adults aged 52 years or older, were cancer-free at baseline, and subsequently developed incident cancer. Frailty was assessed a median of 6.0 years before diagnosis using a 33-item deficit accumulation frailty index (FI) and categorized as non-frail (≤ 0.10), pre-frail (> 0.10 to ≤ 0.21), and frail (> 0.21). The primary outcome was all-cause mortality after cancer diagnosis. Weighted Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, residential setting, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart disease, psychiatric disorders, rheumatic disease, and persistent pain.ResultsAmong 1,882 participants, 819 deaths occurred over a median follow-up of 8.7 years after diagnosis. In the fully adjusted model, both pre-frailty and frailty were independently associated with higher mortality compared with non-frailty (HR 1.35, 95% CI 1.05–1.73; and HR 1.87, 95% CI 1.34–2.59, respectively). When modeled continuously, each 0.01-unit increase in FI was associated with an approximately 2% higher mortality risk (HR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03). At baseline, 740 participants (39.3%) were younger than 65 years, whereas 1,142 participants (60.7%) were aged 65 years or older. Age-stratified fully adjusted weighted Cox analyses demonstrated that each 0.01-unit increment in FI was associated with elevated mortality in the total population (HR 1.022, 95% CI 1.015–1.030; P < 0.001), adults aged ≥ 65 years (HR 1.017, 95% CI 1.008–1.026; P < 0.001), and participants < 65 years (HR 1.023, 95% CI 1.011–1.036; P = 0.003). Significant nonlinear associations were observed for the total population and those aged ≥ 65 years (both P-nonlinear < 0.001), whereas nonlinearity was not statistically significant among participants < 65 years (P-nonlinear = 0.124; Fig. 3, Supplementary Table S10).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that prediagnosis frailty may serve as a marker of underlying vulnerability and may provide modest incremental prognostic information for longitudinal risk assessment in geriatric oncology. Future interventional studies are needed to test whether frailty-targeted assessment and management before or after cancer diagnosis can improve survival, functional outcomes, or quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40520-026-03440-1.Keywords: Frailty, Cancer, Middle-aged and older adults, Mortality, Frailty index, Cohort study, Health and Retirement Study

Introduction

Cancer incidence and mortality increase sharply with age, and older adults now represent a growing proportion of the oncology population worldwide [1, 2]. However, this population is highly heterogeneous with respect to physiological reserve, functional status, multimorbidity, and treatment tolerance. Consequently, chronological age alone is insufficient for prognostic assessment or treatment decision-making in oncology [3, 4].

Frailty has emerged as a key construct in geriatric medicine, representing a multidimensional state of vulnerability arising from cumulative decline across multiple physiological systems [5]. In oncology, frailty has been associated with postoperative complications, treatment intolerance, hospitalization, functional decline, and mortality [6–10]. These observations have contributed to increasing interest in geriatric assessment and frailty-informed care pathways for older adults with cancer [10, 11].

Despite this growing body of evidence, several important limitations remain in the existing literature. Many prior studies have been based on selected hospital-based or disease-specific cohorts, which may limit generalizability to the broader population of older adults who develop cancer [6, 8]. Second, follow-up periods have often been relatively short, with emphasis on perioperative outcomes or early mortality rather than longer-term survival [6, 9]. Third, and most importantly, frailty has frequently been measured after cancer diagnosis or during treatment, when it may already reflect tumor burden, treatment toxicity, or both, thereby raising concerns about reverse causation. In addition, relatively few population-based studies have evaluated whether prediagnostic frailty shows a graded association with long-term mortality across the full range of vulnerability.

In this context, the frailty index (FI) is particularly suitable for the present research question. Based on the deficit-accumulation framework proposed by Rockwood and Mitnitski, the FI quantifies vulnerability as a continuous burden of multisystem health deficits rather than as a dichotomous or purely phenotypic state [12, 13]. Compared with the Fried frailty phenotype, which emphasizes a narrower set of physical characteristics, the deficit-accumulation FI captures broader domains including comorbidity, function, symptoms, and psychosocial deficits, making it well suited to population-based prognostic modeling and dose-response analyses in adults who later develop cancer [12–16].

This choice is further supported by contemporary geriatric oncology guidelines and systematic reviews, which emphasize multidimensional assessment of functional status, comorbidity, cognition, psychological health, nutrition, social support, and physical performance in adults with cancer [10, 14–16]. These domains are clinically relevant in oncology, where treatment tolerance, multimorbidity, functional decline, and competing mortality risks are central determinants of outcomes. However, evidence specifically evaluating prediagnostic frailty in population-based cancer cohorts remains limited.

Using data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), we examined whether frailty assessed before incident cancer diagnosis was associated with long-term all-cause mortality after diagnosis. We hypothesized that greater prediagnosis frailty would be associated with poorer post-diagnosis survival in a graded manner.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study used a population-based prospective cohort design based on the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey of older adults in the United States [17]. The HRS is conducted biennially. We used the 2006 wave as baseline because it provided the variables required for FI construction and covariate assessment. Participants were followed through December 31, 2020. The HRS was approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent [17]. The present study was based on de-identified secondary data.

Study population

Participants were eligible if they completed the 2006 h interview, reported no history of cancer at baseline, subsequently developed incident cancer during follow-up, had sufficient information to construct the baseline FI, and had available follow-up data for survival analysis. Participants with prevalent cancer at baseline, insufficient FI data, or missing key follow-up information were excluded.

All exposure, covariate, and health assessment data were obtained at the 2006 interview, preceding the recorded date of cancer diagnosis for all included participants. This design ensured temporal ordering between exposure and outcome. To characterize the separation between baseline assessment and diagnosis, we examined the interval from baseline to cancer diagnosis. The median interval was 6.0 years (interquartile range [IQR], 2.0–9.0 years). The flow of participant selection is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram of participant selection

Because the HRS includes middle-aged and older adults, not all participants were aged 65 years or older at baseline. Therefore, we reported the number and proportion of participants aged < 65 years and conducted a sensitivity analysis restricted to participants aged ≥ 65 years to evaluate the robustness of the findings in a strictly older population.

Frailty index construction

The FI was constructed according to the standard deficit accumulation approach described by Rockwood and Mitnitski and operationalized using published procedures for FI development [12, 13]. A total of 33 health deficits were included, spanning seven domains: general health, psychological status, comorbidities, physical function, activities of daily living (ADL), instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), and geriatric syndromes. The full list of deficits is presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Each deficit was coded on a 0 to 1 scale, where 0 indicated absence of the deficit and 1 indicated full presence of the deficit. Variables with intermediate severity were coded proportionally where appropriate. Self-rated health was treated as an ordinal variable and rescaled to a 0–1 range according to standard FI procedures [13].

For each participant, the FI was calculated as the number of deficits present divided by the number of deficits assessed, yielding a continuous score from 0 to 1. For descriptive purposes, the same value was also expressed on a 0–100 scale. Thus, an FI of 0.20 corresponds to 20 on the 0–100 scale.

Variables with more than 30% missingness were excluded from primary FI construction. In total, 109 participants were excluded because they had fewer than 80% non-missing FI items. Item-level missingness for included FI components ranged from 0.0% to 2.1%.

For descriptive analyses, FI was categorized as non-frail (≤ 0.10), pre-frail (> 0.10 to ≤ 0.21), and frail (> 0.21), based on commonly used thresholds in the FI literature and their clinical interpretability [12, 13]. FI was also analyzed continuously, and continuous and spline-based analyses were prioritized for inference.

Outcome ascertainment

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality after incident cancer diagnosis. For survival analyses, time zero was defined as the recorded date of incident cancer diagnosis in the HRS follow-up data. Specifically, cancer onset was identified from the first follow-up interview at which a participant reported a new physician diagnosis of cancer after being cancer-free at baseline, and the diagnosis date recorded in the HRS dataset—derived from the participant’s reported time of physician-diagnosed cancer during biennial interviews, rather than an imputed midpoint or a registry-linked exact date—was used as the event date for cohort entry. Follow-up accrued from that recorded diagnosis date to death or censoring, whichever occurred first, and only post-diagnosis person-time was included in the risk set.

Because HRS cancer diagnoses are ascertained within biennial survey intervals and are partly based on participant report, some imprecision in the recorded diagnosis date is possible. However, the same operational definition was applied uniformly across all participants. Mortality status and follow-up time were derived from HRS records with linkage to the National Death Index and other validated sources [17].

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori on the basis of clinical relevance and previous literature [3, 4, 10]. These included age, sex, race, education, residential setting, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), heart disease, psychiatric disorders, rheumatic disease, and persistent pain.

We attempted to identify harmonized cancer-site variables in the available HRS files for the analytic cohort. However, cancer-site information was not sufficiently complete and consistently harmonized across waves to support reliable broad cancer-category stratification. Data on stage, pathological subtype, treatment modality, and treatment intensity were also unavailable or incomplete; therefore, cancer-type-specific sensitivity analyses were not performed.

Missing data

Missing data were addressed at two levels. For FI construction, variables with more than 30% missingness were excluded from the primary FI, and participants with fewer than 80% non-missing FI items were excluded from the main analysis. For non-FI covariates, missingness was low overall, with the highest residual missingness being less than 1% among included analytic variables (Supplementary Table S8). Therefore, analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach. The direction and magnitude of selection differences between included and excluded participants were further assessed using standardized mean differences (Supplementary Table S8). Alternative approaches, such as multiple imputation, may be explored in future studies.

Statistical analysis

Primary Cox proportional hazards models incorporated the 2006 h respondent-level sampling weight to improve population representativeness [17]. Baseline characteristics were summarized using weighted descriptive statistics and compared across frailty categories using appropriate weighted tests.

Survival functions were estimated using Kaplan-Meier methods and compared using the log-rank test. Weighted Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate HRs and 95% CIs for the association between FI and all-cause mortality. FI was modeled both categorically and continuously. Three models were specified: Model 1, unadjusted; Model 2, adjusted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity; and Model 3, additionally adjusted for smoking status, alcohol consumption, education, hypertension, diabetes, COPD, heart disease, psychiatric conditions, rheumatic conditions, urban-rural classification, and pain.

To characterize the dose-response relationship, restricted cubic spline models were fitted with 4 knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the FI distribution. This knot specification follows a commonly used strategy for Cox regression spline modeling, balancing flexibility in capturing potential nonlinearity while avoiding overfitting in the tails of the distribution. The reference value was set at FI = 0.10. Nonlinearity was tested by comparing the spline model with a model containing only the linear FI term using a likelihood ratio test. Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s C-index. To evaluate the incremental prognostic value of FI, we compared the C-index of a baseline multivariable model including conventional covariates with that of the full model additionally including FI, and calculated the change in C-index (ΔC-index). Confidence intervals for ΔC-index were estimated using bootstrap resampling. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were used to estimate area under the curve (AUC) values at 1, 3, 5, and 10 years after diagnosis.

Restricted cubic spline models were further stratified by baseline age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years) to explore subgroup-specific dose-response relationships (Fig. 3; Table 3).

To further test robustness in a strictly older population, we performed restricted cubic spline sensitivity analyses in the total population and among participants aged ≥ 65 years at baseline. Log-transformed HR curves were plotted to optimize visual comparison of dose-response trends across age strata (Fig. 3). Consistent with the primary analysis, all Cox regression models for the ≥ 65-year-old sensitivity subgroup were also weighted using HRS respondent-level sampling weights.

Model assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals and graphical diagnostics. Additional model diagnostics included Cox-Snell residual plots for overall fit and deviance residual plots to assess influential observations. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate consistency across demographic and clinical strata and were considered exploratory. Candidate FI cut-off values were also explored using a threshold scan based on model log-likelihood; these analyses were regarded as exploratory. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

After multistage screening, 1,882 participants who were cancer-free at baseline and subsequently developed incident cancer were included in the analytic cohort. The minimum age at baseline was 52 years. At baseline, 740 participants (39.3%) were younger than 65 years, whereas 1,142 participants (60.7%) were aged 65 years or older.

The median interval between baseline FI assessment and cancer diagnosis was 6.0 years (IQR, 2.0–9.0 years). The distribution of this interval was broadly similar across frailty categories, although slightly shorter intervals were observed among more frail participants.

At baseline, 34.4% of participants were classified as non-frail, 33.4% as pre-frail, and 32.3% as frail. Greater frailty burden was associated with older age, lower educational attainment, higher comorbidity burden, and poorer functional status (Table 1). Frailty category distributions were broadly comparable between participants aged < 65 and ≥ 65 years, suggesting that inclusion of middle-aged participants did not materially distort the overall frailty distribution.

Survival according to frailty category

Over a median post-diagnosis follow-up of 8.7 years, 819 deaths occurred. Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated marked differences in survival probability across the three frailty groups (Fig. 2). Participants categorized as frail had the poorest survival, followed by the pre-frail group, whereas the non-frail group had the most favorable survival throughout follow-up. The overall difference among groups was statistically significant (log-rank P < 0.0001).

Sensitivity and supplementary analyses

Restricted cubic spline analyses showed a significant overall association (P-overall < 0.001), with significant nonlinear association (P-nonlinear < 0.001), suggesting a nonlinear monotonic dose-response relationship between FI and post-cancer mortality (Fig. 3). In the weighted sensitivity analysis restricted to participants aged ≥ 65 years, each 0.01-unit increase in FI remained significantly associated with higher post-diagnosis all-cause mortality in the fully adjusted weighted Cox model (HR 1.017, 95% CI 1.008–1.026; P < 0.001), with evident nonlinearity (P-nonlinear < 0.001; Supplementary Table S10, Fig. 3).

The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residual tests for both the main and sensitivity models. In the main analysis, FI satisfied the proportional hazards assumption (P = 0.202), and global model testing did not indicate clear overall violation of the proportional hazards assumption (P = 0.065; Supplementary Table S6). In sensitivity analyses excluding participants diagnosed in 2020, the association between FI and all-cause mortality remained essentially unchanged (Supplementary Table S5), and the proportional hazards assumption for FI remained satisfied (P = 0.174; Supplementary Table S7). Minor violations of the proportional hazards assumption were observed for some covariates, including age, diabetes, and heart disease (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). However, the proportional hazards assumption for FI itself was satisfied in both the main model and the age-restricted sensitivity model.

Age-stratified dose-response spline analyses further demonstrated consistent positive linear associations between FI and mortality across age strata (Fig. 3). Stratified analyses by baseline-to-diagnosis lag time also yielded consistent results across all lag subgroups, further supporting robustness (Supplementary Figure S6).

Dose-response relationship and model performance

Restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated a significant overall association between continuous FI and mortality (P-overall < 0.001), with significant nonlinearity (P-nonlinear < 0.001; Fig. 3). The association therefore appeared broadly monotonic across the observed FI range. The positive graded association between FI and post-diagnosis mortality was consistent in direction across all age subgroups. Nonlinearity was significant in the total population and adults aged ≥ 65 years (both P-nonlinear < 0.001), whereas the nonlinear trend was non-significant among participants aged < 65 years (P-nonlinear = 0.124).

Associations were generally consistent across prespecified subgroups, with subgroup-specific hazard ratios mostly above 1.0 and ranging from approximately 1.02 to 5.37 (Fig. 4). In the COPD subgroup, the association was attenuated (HR 1.40, 95% CI 1.12–1.74). Because multiple interaction tests were conducted, these subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

Prespecified subgroup analyses of the association between frailty and all-cause mortality after incident cancer. Subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory because multiple interaction tests were conducted

The fully adjusted model yielded a Harrell’s C-index of 0.689 (95% CI 0.669–0.709). Compared with the baseline model including conventional covariates, the addition of FI resulted in a modest but statistically significant improvement in discrimination (C-index 0.682 vs. 0.689; ΔC-index = 0.007, 95% CI 0.002–0.013; P = 0.023; Supplementary Table S9). Time-dependent AUCs for the full model were 0.671 at 1 year, 0.691 at 3 years, 0.709 at 5 years, and 0.747 at 10 years (Fig. 5), indicating modest-to-moderate discrimination. FI provides modest incremental prognostic information within a multivariable framework rather than serving as a standalone predictive tool.

Discussion

The findings of this population-based cohort study demonstrate that greater prediagnostic frailty burden is independently associated with worse long-term survival after cancer diagnosis among middle-aged and older adults. Recent geriatric oncology guidelines and systematic reviews have consistently demonstrated that frailty and multidimensional geriatric assessment are associated with treatment tolerance, postoperative complications, and survival outcomes in older adults with cancer [14–16]. Our results extend this literature by demonstrating that frailty assessed years before cancer diagnosis already carries prognostic significance for subsequent cancer survival.

These findings extend prior literature in several important ways. Previous studies have largely focused on frailty assessed at or after cancer diagnosis, often in selected clinical populations [6–10, 18]. Although such studies have consistently shown that frailty is associated with adverse cancer outcomes, they are inherently limited by the possibility that frailty reflects tumor burden, treatment toxicity, or acute deterioration present at the time of assessment. By contrast, our study established a clear temporal sequence, with frailty measured a median of six years before diagnosis. This design strengthens the interpretation of frailty as a marker of underlying vulnerability rather than a consequence of clinically apparent cancer or its treatment.

The use of a nationally representative cohort also enhances external validity compared with many prior hospital-based studies. In addition, the extended duration of follow-up allowed us to examine long-term survival rather than only peri-treatment outcomes or short-term mortality. The incorporation of continuous and spline-based modeling further provided a more nuanced characterization of the frailty-mortality relationship and suggests that risk may increase progressively across the FI continuum.

Several biological and clinical mechanisms may underlie the observed association. Frailty reflects multisystem physiological dysregulation, including impairments in inflammatory, endocrine, immune, neuromuscular, and metabolic pathways [5, 19]. These deficits may reduce resilience to cancer-related stressors, limit treatment tolerance, and accelerate functional decline. At the same time, because the outcome in this study was all-cause mortality in an older and multimorbid population, the observed association likely also reflects vulnerability to major non-cancer causes of death, including cardiovascular events, organ failure, infections, and other consequences of reduced physiologic reserve. Frailty is closely linked to multimorbidity, sarcopenia, chronic inflammation, and catabolic vulnerability, all of which may contribute both to poorer cancer outcomes and to higher non-cancer mortality risk [20–22].

Frailty added limited but statistically detectable complementary prognostic information beyond conventional clinical covariates by summarizing functional limitations, comorbidities, symptoms, geriatric deficits, and competing mortality risk. However, the small improvement in discrimination (Delta C-index = 0.007) indicates that FI should be interpreted as an incremental risk marker rather than a clinically sufficient standalone prediction tool.

From a clinical perspective, our findings support prediagnostic frailty as a long-term prognostic marker of underlying vulnerability. Given the observational design and modest discriminative improvement, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as direct clinical recommendations. Future interventional studies are needed to test whether prediagnostic health optimization, preventive monitoring, or frailty-targeted supportive care can improve survival, functional outcomes, or quality of life. Once cancer is diagnosed, prediagnostic frailty information may complement contemporary geriatric assessment for individualized risk stratification, rather than directly guiding definitive clinical interventions.

Our findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, frailty was measured once at baseline, a median of 6.0 years before cancer diagnosis, and may have changed over time; accordingly, some exposure misclassification is possible despite the advantage of temporal ordering. Sensitivity analyses stratified by baseline-to-diagnosis interval demonstrated consistent associations across all lag periods, supporting result stability.

Second, the primary outcome was all-cause mortality without cause-specific adjudication. Therefore, we could not distinguish cancer-specific deaths from non-cancer deaths. In older and multimorbid cancer populations, non-cancer causes—including cardiovascular disease, infections, and organ failure—may account for a substantial proportion of total mortality. Because frail individuals are more vulnerable to these competing risks, the observed association may partly reflect excess non-cancer mortality rather than cancer progression alone. If interpreted as a cancer-specific prognostic effect, the direction of bias would likely be upward. However, the magnitude of this bias cannot be quantified without adjudicated cause-of-death data or competing-risk modeling. Accordingly, the present estimates should be interpreted as associations with overall post-diagnosis mortality, not as estimates of cancer-specific mortality.

Third, cancer diagnosis timing in HRS may be subject to minor self-report imprecision, though uniform operational definitions minimized differential bias.

Fourth, a major limitation is the lack of sufficiently complete cancer-specific information, including cancer site, stage, pathological subtype, treatment modality, and treatment intensity. Because prognosis varies substantially across cancer types, residual confounding by tumor prognosis cannot be excluded. If frail participants were more likely to develop or present with poorer-prognosis cancers, due to delayed diagnosis, immune senescence, differential screening, or other mechanisms, the observed frailty-mortality association may have been biased upward. Conversely, differential screening or diagnosis patterns could introduce additional uncertainty in the direction of this bias. Therefore, the association should not be interpreted as a purely direct effect of frailty on cancer survival.

Fifth, subgroup analyses were exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously. All findings represent prognostic associations rather than causal inference.

In addition, partial overlap exists between the FI’s rheumatic disease deficit item and the adjusted rheumatic disease covariate, which may cause mild overadjustment and influence cross-group descriptive comparisons.

Conclusions

In conclusion, frailty assessed a median of 6.0 years before cancer diagnosis was independently associated with worse long-term survival after diagnosis in middle-aged and older adults with incident cancer. These findings highlight prediagnosis frailty as a marker of underlying vulnerability and suggest that it may provide modest incremental information for longitudinal risk assessment, particularly in geriatric oncology contexts. Future studies with adjudicated cause-of-death and cancer-registry data should examine these associations using cause-specific or competing-risk approaches, and prospective trials are needed to test whether frailty-targeted management improves clinical or patient-centered outcomes.
