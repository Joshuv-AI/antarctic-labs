Thyroid storm triggered by perianal abscess: clinical overlap with severe thyrotoxicosis
Authors: Pedro Arthur da Rocha Ribas, Guilherme Martins, Cleo Otaviano Mesa Junior, Gisah Amaral, Fabíola Yukiko Miasaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThyroid storm is a rare and potentially fatal endocrine emergency, most commonly precipitated by infections, withdrawal of antithyroid drugs, or acute events in patients with Graves disease. Reports associated with thyroiditis or with unusual infections, such as perianal abscess, are exceptionally uncommon. We describe the case of a 61-year-old previously healthy man admitted with a perianal abscess complicated by atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response and thyroid storm. The patient received intensive treatment with propylthiouracil, Lugol solution, propranolol, corticosteroids, antibiotics, and electrical cardioversion, resulting in sustained reversion to sinus rhythm. During follow-up, he developed permanent hypothyroidism, requiring levothyroxine replacement. Beyond the perianal infection as an evident trigger, the progression to definitive hypothyroidism and positive antithyroid peroxidase antibodies also raises the uncommon possibility of hashitoxicosis as the underlying etiology. This case illustrates an atypical presentation of thyroid storm, underscoring the importance of early recognition and multidisciplinary management.Keywords: thyroid storm, thyroiditis, perianal abscess, hashitoxicosis, permanent hypothyroidism

Introduction

Thyroiditis encompasses a heterogeneous spectrum of inflammatory disorders of the thyroid gland, with diverse etiologies and clinical presentations. Most cases course with transient phases of thyrotoxicosis, which are usually self-limited and mild. However, in rare situations, they may progress to thyroid storm, a severe complication more commonly associated with untreated Graves disease [1-4].

Thyroid storm is characterized by multiorgan systemic decompensation and carries a high mortality rate, although its incidence is low. Common precipitating factors include infections, withdrawal of antithyroid medications, and acute medical events. Although usually milder, hashitoxicosis is a transient thyrotoxic manifestation of Hashimoto thyroiditis that, in extremely rare circumstances, can present with severe forms culminating in thyroid storm [5-10].

Reports of thyroid storm in the setting of autoimmune thyroiditis or triggered by unusual infections, such as perianal abscess, are exceptionally rare. In such cases, it is crucial to consider both the infectious precipitant and the possibility of underlying autoimmune etiologies in order to guide appropriate management and follow-up [4, 9, 11].

Case presentation

A 61-year-old man, previously healthy, with no history of chronic medication use and no recent exposure to iodinated contrast agents, presented with a 15-day history of profound asthenia, loss of appetite, anhedonia, fever (38 °C), night sweats, dyspnea, and anterior cervical pain. He also reported localized perianal pain and discomfort preceding admission, which prompted evaluation at an urgent care facility where a perianal abscess was diagnosed and ceftriaxone therapy was initiated. Laboratory tests revealed a thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) of 0.01 µIU/mL (SI: 0.01 mIU/L) (normal reference range: 0.35-4.94 µIU/mL; SI: 0.35-4.94 mIU/L) and free thyroxine level (free T4) > 5 ng/dL (SI: > 64.4 pmol/L) (normal reference range: 0.7-1.48 ng/dL; SI: 9-19 pmol/L), consistent with thyrotoxicosis (Table 1), prompting referral to the Clinical Hospital of the Federal University of Paraná.

Diagnostic assessment

On admission, the patient was in atrial fibrillation with a ventricular rate of 160 beats per minute and a temperature of 37.5 °C. He exhibited significant central nervous system involvement, with marked somnolence, slowed responses, and disorientation, and was arousable to verbal stimuli (Glasgow Coma Scale score 12: eye 3, verbal 4, and motor 5). Pulmonary and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. A 1 cm ulcerated perianal lesion with granulation tissue and no inflammatory signs was observed.

Based on these findings, the Burch-Wartofsky point scale score (BWPS) was calculated at 45 points (10 for atrial fibrillation, 10 for the precipitating factor [perianal abscess], 20 for lethargy, and 5 for temperature between 37.2 and 37.7 °C), meeting the diagnostic threshold for thyroid storm.

Additional testing revealed a positive antithyroglobulin antibody level (29.54 IU/mL; normal reference range: <4.1 IU/mL), negative thyrotropin receptor antibody (TRAb), which includes stimulating antibodies in contemporary assays (thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin was not separately measured), and negative viral and syphilis serologies.

Treatment

Immediate treatment was initiated with propylthiouracil (PTU) (1000 mg loading dose followed by 200 mg every 6 hours), Lugol solution (10 drops every 8 hours), propranolol (60 mg every 6 hours), and hydrocortisone (100 mg every 8 hours). The arrhythmia remained refractory to deslanoside, magnesium sulfate, metoprolol, and amiodarone during the hospital course. Transesophageal echocardiography excluded intracavitary thrombi, allowing electrical cardioversion with 200 J, which achieved sustained reversion to sinus rhythm. The perianal abscess was treated with ceftriaxone and metronidazole for 7 days.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient remained hospitalized for 12 days, including time in the intensive care unit, due to management of the perianal abscess with intravenous antibiotics and persistent atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response refractory to medical therapy. Transesophageal echocardiography was performed to exclude intracardiac thrombus before electrical cardioversion, followed by monitoring to ensure sustained sinus rhythm and appropriate anticoagulation planning. He was discharged on methimazole 10 mg/day and rivaroxaban 20 mg/day. Although total T3 had declined by day 12, free T4 remained in the upper-normal range. Given the recent severe thyrotoxic state with systemic instability, methimazole was temporarily continued at a low dose with close follow-up and discontinued 1 month later after biochemical hypothyroidism was confirmed.

At outpatient endocrinology follow-up, biochemical hypothyroidism was confirmed 1 month later (TSH 50 µIU/mL [SI: 50 mIU/L]; free T4 0.72 ng/dL [SI: 9.3 pmol/L]; total triiodothyronine [T3] 102 ng/dL [SI: 1.57 nmol/L] {normal reference range: 64-152; SI: 1.0-2.3 nmol/L}), and levothyroxine 100 mcg/day was initiated. After 3 months of replacement therapy, thyroid function normalized (TSH 2.04 µIU/mL [SI: 2.04 mIU/L]; free T4 1.14 ng/dL [SI: 14.7 pmol/L]; total T3 107 ng/dL [SI: 1.64 nmol/L]), with positive antithyroid peroxidase antibodies (6.19 IU/mL; normal reference range: <5.6 IU/mL). During cardiology follow-up, the patient maintained sustained sinus rhythm without documented recurrence of atrial fibrillation. Rivaroxaban was discontinued 4 months after discharge given a CHA2DS2-VASc score of 0 and stable rhythm control. The patient remained on continuous levothyroxine replacement, which was maintained beyond 6 months after the thyroid storm episode. Graves disease was considered less likely in light of negative TRAb, rapid hormonal decline, and subsequent progression to permanent hypothyroidism. However, we acknowledge that seronegative Graves disease has been described.

Discussion

Thyroiditis comprises a heterogeneous group of inflammatory disorders, most commonly autoimmune (Hashimoto thyroiditis). It may manifest with a transient thyrotoxic phase (hashitoxicosis), generally milder and self-limited compared to Graves disease, and frequently progresses to permanent hypothyroidism due to parenchymal destruction. Silent thyroiditis and hashitoxicosis are overlapping forms of autoimmune destructive thyroiditis and may be indistinguishable during the hyperthyroid phase. In this case, progression to permanent hypothyroidism with positive antithyroid peroxidase antibodies supported an underlying chronic autoimmune process consistent with Hashimoto thyroiditis presenting as hashitoxicosis [1, 2, 10].

Other causes of thyroiditis include subacute (Quervain) thyroiditis, typically postviral, characterized by cervical pain, elevated inflammatory markers, and a transient thyrotoxic phase followed by temporary hypothyroidism and recovery. Painless thyroiditis, of autoimmune origin, follows a triphasic course (thyrotoxicosis, hypothyroidism, and recovery) and may result in permanent hypothyroidism in up to 20% of cases. Amiodarone can also induce thyrotoxicosis through increased hormone synthesis (type 1) or a destructive process (type 2). Management varies according to etiology and may include anti-inflammatory agents, corticosteroids, or thyroid hormone replacement [1-3].

Thyrotoxicosis due to thyroiditis is usually self-limited and less severe, but rarely may progress to thyroid storm. This complication is most often linked to untreated Graves disease, with only sporadic reports in subacute or painless thyroiditis, particularly in older patients or those with comorbidities [4]. In this case, its occurrence in a previously healthy 61-year-old man underscores its atypical nature. Negative TRAb and rapid hormonal decline made Graves disease less likely, although about 5% of cases may be seronegative [12]. Radionuclide imaging was not performed during the acute phase because of clinical instability requiring urgent treatment. Subsequent progression to permanent hypothyroidism with positive antithyroid peroxidase antibodies supported a destructive autoimmune process.

When thyrotoxicosis reaches its most severe form, it may culminate in thyroid storm, an endocrine emergency characterized by multiorgan decompensation due to failure of metabolic, cardiovascular, and thermoregulatory compensation. Although rare, estimated at 0.20 per 100 000 annually and in 0.22% of hospitalized thyrotoxic patients, it carries high mortality (8%-25%), particularly among those requiring intensive care [5, 6]. In this case, the severity of presentation likewise necessitated intensive care management.

Given its severity, the diagnosis of thyroid storm is primarily clinical, based on thyrotoxicosis with systemic decompensation. The BWPS, summarized in Table 3, and the Japanese Thyroid Association criteria are the most commonly used diagnostic tools. Typical features include fever, marked tachycardia, arrhythmias, heart failure, neurologic changes, and gastrointestinal symptoms, with identification of a precipitating factor being essential [5, 7-9]. In this case, the patient presented with fever, altered mental status, and atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, reaching 45 points on the BWPS and meeting the accepted diagnostic threshold for thyroid storm.

Nevertheless, this diagnosis carries inherent challenges, particularly with concurrent infection. Although the patient met the BWPS threshold, overlap between thyroid storm and severe thyrotoxicosis complicated by infection is well recognized. Infection may precipitate thyroid storm but can also independently cause fever, tachycardia, and neurologic changes (key components of BWPS scoring). Moreover, thyroid hormone levels do not reliably distinguish thyroid storm from uncomplicated thyrotoxicosis, and scoring systems have limited specificity. Thus, the diagnosis remains fundamentally clinical [13, 14]. In this case, marked thyrotoxicosis, systemic instability, and an identifiable precipitant justified management according to thyroid storm protocols. At presentation, differentiation between Graves disease and destructive thyroiditis was not established; therefore, PTU was initiated per guideline recommendations. Whether representing overt thyroid storm or a storm-spectrum presentation, the therapeutic approach was consistent with recommended management for patients with BWPS ≥ 45 and systemic decompensation.

After diagnosis, identifying the precipitating factor is essential. Common triggers include infections, withdrawal of antithyroid drugs, surgery, trauma, amiodarone use, iodine exposure, and cardiovascular events. Infections are particularly relevant, as the associated inflammatory response may precipitate thyroid storm. In this case, the perianal infection likely acted as the trigger. Although rarely linked to thyroiditis, cutaneous or pilonidal infections have occasionally spread hematogenously and caused acute suppurative thyroiditis, especially in patients with predisposing factors such as immunosuppression or anatomic abnormalities (eg, pyriform sinus fistula). Transient thyrotoxicosis may result from follicular destruction, and, though uncommon, progression to thyroid storm can occur, particularly with extensive glandular injury, delayed diagnosis, or severe infection, highlighting the importance of early recognition [5-9, 11].

Given its severity, thyroid storm requires intensive management, ideally in an intensive care unit, to rapidly inhibit thyroid hormone synthesis, release, and peripheral action while treating the precipitating factor. PTU is administered as a 500-1000 mg loading dose followed by 250 mg every 4 hours, with high-dose methimazole (60-80 mg/day) as an alternative. Iodine therapy (Lugol solution or potassium iodide) should be given at least 1 hour after antithyroid drug initiation [9, 15]. Propranolol (60-80 mg every 4 hours) is used for adrenergic control, with cardioselective beta-blockers considered in selected cases [9, 16]. Corticosteroids, such as hydrocortisone (300 mg IV bolus followed by 100 mg every 8 hours), reduce peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion and address relative adrenal insufficiency [9, 15]. Supportive care includes fluid resuscitation, temperature control, electrolyte correction, and ventilatory support, with electrical cardioversion reserved for refractory instability [7, 9, 17, 18]. In this case, the patient received PTU, Lugol solution, propranolol, hydrocortisone, antibiotics, and electrical cardioversion, achieving sustained sinus rhythm. Improvement was likely multifactorial, reflecting the natural course of destructive thyroiditis, infection resolution, and adjunctive corticosteroid effects rather than a single intervention [13, 19].

This case illustrates an unusual presentation of thyroid storm in a previously healthy patient, likely precipitated by a perianal abscess. The clinical course, an initial severe thyrotoxic phase followed by permanent hypothyroidism with positive antithyroid peroxidase antibodies, supports an autoimmune destructive thyroiditis evolving from hyperthyroidism to hypothyroidism. Although definitive etiologic distinction may be difficult in the acute setting, this evolution favors a chronic autoimmune process as the underlying substrate. This case highlights the importance of early recognition and intensive management of atypical and diagnostically challenging presentations of severe thyrotoxicosis, underscoring the clinical complexity of suspected thyroid storm in real-world settings.
