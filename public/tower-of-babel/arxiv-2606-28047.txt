A combinatorial nerve theorem
Sucharita Barik, Anupam Mondal, Sajal Mukherjee, Pritam Chandra Pramanik, Arundhati Rakshit

Abstract
The celebrated (homological) nerve theorem makes use of spectral sequences to determine the homology of a space. However, this theorem cannot effectively compute the homology in every circumstance. In this paper, we develop an effective version of the nerve theorem. Our theorem enables us to compute the homology of a simplicial complex explicitly using the combinatorial information of its subcomplexes and their non-trivial intersections using discrete Morse theory. Suppose X X is a simplicial complex with subcomplexes A 1 , A 2 , … , A k A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k} such that X = ∪ i = 1 k A i X=\cup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i} . Then the main theorem of this paper states that we can explicitly compute the homology of X X using the information of given gradient vector fields on A i A_{i} for each i ∈ [ k ] i\in[k] , and on their possible non-trivial intersections. Our approach is purely combinatorial, in the sense that it does not involve any notions of geometric realization, continuity or homotopy.



Keywords:
simplicial complex, simplotopal complex, nerve complex, discrete Morse theory, Morse homology.

Introduction

Suppose
X
is a simplicial complex with subcomplexes
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
such that
\cup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}=X
. In this setup, the (homological) nerve theorem can be used to determine the homology of
X

A_{i}
(e.g., they are acyclic), using Leray spectral sequence (see
[
1
,
5
]
). However, in general cases, i.e., when the
A_{i}
-s are not necessarily acyclic, this approach does not always offer an explicit way to compute the homology of the simplicial complex.
In this setup, we develop an
effective
combinatorial nerve theorem, which provides an algorithmic procedure to explicitly determine the homology groups of
X
using some combinatorial information of the subcomplexes
A_{i}
and their all possible intersections. Our theorem works without any additional conditions or restrictions on the subcomplexes
A_{i}
. We mention here, that our approach throughout this paper, is purely combinatorial, i.e., we do not use any notions of geometric realisation, continuity or homotopy anywhere.

In order to proceed to the setup of our main result, we need to introduce a few terminologies.

Next, we introduce the

The essence of our theorem can be formulated in the following manner. Suppose,
X

A_{1},\dots,A_{k}

Our paper is organised in the following manner. In Section

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Simplicial complex

An
(abstract) simplicial complex

X
is a collection of finite sets with the property that for each
\tau\in X
, if
\sigma\subseteq\tau
, then
\sigma\in X
. The elements of
X
are called
simplices
. The
dimension of a simplex

\sigma
is defined as
\dim(\sigma):=|\sigma|-1
. An
i
-dimensional simplex is also referred to as an
i
-simplex. The
dimension of
X
is defined as,
\dim(X):=\max_{\sigma\in X}\dim(\sigma)
. A simplicial complex
Y
is called a
subcomplex
of
X
if
Y\subseteq X
.

Definition 2.1
.

(Orientation on a simplex)
Let
X
be a simplicial complex and
\sigma=\{v_{0},\dots,v_{n}\}\in X
. We define an equivalence relation on the set of all possible orderings of
v_{0},\dots,v_{n}
such that for any two orderings
\gamma,\delta
,
\gamma\sim\delta
if and only if
\gamma
and
\delta
differ by an even permutation. An orientation on
\sigma
is defined as an ordering which represents an equivalence class under this relation. Thus, there are two distinct orientations of a simplex. We represent a simplex
\{v_{0},\dots,v_{n}\}
with an orientation represented by the ordering,
v_{0},\dots,v_{n}
as
[v_{0},\dots,v_{n}]
and call this an
oriented
simplex.

We denote a simplex
\{v_{0},\dots,v_{i-1},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{n}\}
equipped with the orientation given by the ordering
v_{0},\dots,v_{i-1},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{n}
, as
[v_{0},\dots,\hat{v}_{i},\dots,v_{n}]
.

Definition 2.2
.

(Incidence number)
Let
\tau,\sigma\in X
such that
\tau=[v_{0},\dots,v_{n}]
and
\dim(\sigma)=\dim(\tau)-1
. Then, the incidence number of
\sigma
with respect to
\tau
is defined as,

\langle\tau,\sigma\rangle:=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\sigma\nsubseteq\tau,\\
(-1)^{i}&\text{ if }\sigma=[v_{0},\dots,\hat{v}_{i},\dots,v_{n}]\\
\end{cases}

Let
S_{q}(X)
represent the set of all
q
-dimensional oriented simplices of
X
.
Now, for each
q\geq 0
, the
q^{th}

simplicial chain group

C_{q}(X)
is defined as the free module generated by
S_{q}(X)
over
\mathbb{Z}
. The
(q+1)^{th}
boundary map
\partial_{q+1}:C_{q+1}(X)\rightarrow C_{q}(X)
as,

\partial_{q+1}(\tau)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q}(X)}\langle\tau,\sigma\rangle\sigma,\text{ for each }\text{ }\tau\in S_{q+1}(X),

and extend it linearly to
C_{q+1}(X)
.

It can be shown that
\partial\circ\partial=0
, i.e.,
(C_{\#},\partial_{\#})
is a chain complex. The
q^{th}
simplicial homology group
is defined as,

H_{q}(X):=\frac{\operatorname{Ker}(\partial_{q})}{\operatorname{Im}(\partial_{q+1})}.

Suppose, we have two chain complexes
(P_{\#},\partial^{P}_{\#})

(Q_{\#},\partial^{Q}_{\#})

Let
f_{\#}
and
g_{\#}
be two chain maps between the chain complexes
(P_{\#},\partial^{P}_{\#})
and
(Q_{\#},\partial^{Q}_{\#})
. Then
f_{\#}
and
g_{\#}
are called
homotopic
, denoted by
f_{\#}\simeq g_{\#}
, if there exists a sequence of module homomorphisms
\lambda_{n}:P_{n}\rightarrow Q_{n+1}
such that the following holds.

\lambda_{n-1}\circ\partial_{n}^{P}+\partial_{n+1}^{Q}\circ\lambda_{n}=f_{n}-g_{n},\text{ for all }n\geq 0.

(P_{\#},\partial^{P}_{\#})
and
(Q_{\#},\partial^{Q}_{\#})
are called

, if there exists chain maps
f_{\#}:P_{\#}\rightarrow Q_{\#}

such that

and
g_{\#}\circ f_{\#}=\operatorname{id}_{P_{\#}}

2.3
Simplotopal complex

Let
V_{1},\dots,V_{m}
be a collection of finite mutually disjoint sets. A
simplotopal complex

K
is defined (see
[
6
]
) as a collection of subsets of
V_{1}\times V_{2}\times\dots\times V_{m}
of the form
\tau_{1}\times\tau_{2}\times\dots\times\tau_{m}
, where
\emptyset\neq\tau_{i}\subseteq V_{i}
for each
i\in[m]
, with the property that for each
i\in[m]
, if
\emptyset\neq\sigma_{i}\subseteq\tau_{i}
, then
\sigma_{1}\times\sigma_{2}\times\dots\times\sigma_{m}
is also a member of this collection. Elements of
K
are termed as
simplotopes
. We represent a simplotope
\tau
of the form
\tau_{1}\times\dots\times\tau_{m}
as an ordered m-tuple
(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{m})
. For each
\tau_{i}\subseteq V_{i}
, we define dimension of
\tau_{i}
as
\dim(\tau_{i}):=|\tau_{i}|-1
. The
dimension
of
\tau
is defined as

\dim(\tau):=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\dim(\tau_{i}).

The dimension of the simplotopal complex
K
is defined as
\max\{\dim(\tau)\mid\tau\in K\}
. Let
\tau=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots,\tau_{m})\in K
, and
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m})\subseteq\tau
such that
\emptyset\neq\sigma_{i}\subseteq\tau_{i}
for each
i\in[m]
. Then
\sigma
is said to be a
face
of
\tau
. If
\dim(\sigma)=\dim(\tau)-1
, then
\sigma
is said to be a
facet
of
\tau
. A
0
-dimensional face of
\tau
is said to be a
vertex
of
\tau
. A
q
-dimensional simplotope
\tau
is denoted as
\tau^{(q)}
.

A simplotopal complex
Y
is said to be a
subcomplex
of a simplotopal complex
K
if
Y\subseteq K
.

Let
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m})
be a simplotope, where
\sigma_{i}=\{v_{i0},v_{i1},\dots,v_{ip_{i}}\}
for each
i\in[m]
. Then
\sigma
is often represented as
\{v_{10},v_{11},\dots,v_{1p_{1}}\mid v_{20},v_{21},\dots,v_{2p_{2}}\mid\dots\mid v_{m0},v_{m1},\dots,v_{mp_{m}}\}
.

Let
\tau=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{m})\in K
, where
\tau_{i}=\{v_{i0},v_{i1},\dots,v_{ip_{i}}\}
for each
i\in[m]
. Let
\sigma
be a facet of
\tau
. Then
\sigma
must be of the form
(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m})
, where
\sigma_{j}=\tau_{j}\setminus\{v_{ji}\}
,
i\in\{0,\dots,p_{j}\}
for a unique
j\in[m]
, and
\sigma_{k}=\tau_{k}
for all
k\neq j
, i.e.,
\sigma=\{v_{10},v_{11},\dots,v_{1p_{1}}\mid\dots\mid v_{j0},\dots,\widehat{v_{ji}},\dots,v_{jp_{j}}\mid\dots\mid v_{m0},v_{m1},\dots,v_{mp_{m}}\}
.

We make a note here, that we will sometimes denote a simplotope
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m})
as simply
\sigma
, for convenience. This will not cause any notational ambiguity, as the meaning will be made clear from the context.

Definition 2.3
.

(Orientation on a simplotopal complex.)
Let
K
be a simplotopal complex. Suppose
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\times\dots\times\sigma_{m}\in K
. Then we define a
\sigma
-compatible ordering
as an ordering on
\sigma_{1}\cup\sigma_{2}\cup\dots\cup\sigma_{m}
such that the vertices of each
\sigma_{i}

\sigma_{i}
remains unaltered. Let
A_{\sigma}
denote the collection of all
\sigma
-compatible orderings. Now we define an equivalence relation on
A_{\sigma}
as follows. Let
\gamma,\delta\in A_{\sigma}
. Then
\gamma\sim\delta
iff
\gamma
and
\delta
differ by an even permutation. Thus, if
\dim(\sigma)>0
, then there are precisely two equivalence classes in
A_{\sigma}
, each of which represent an orientation of
\sigma
.

Let
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m})
, where
\sigma_{i}=\{v_{i0},v_{i1},\dots,v_{ip_{i}}\}
for each
i\in[m]
. Then the orientation of
\sigma
represented by the ordering
v_{10},v_{11},\dots,v_{1p_{1}},v_{20},v_{21},\dots,v_{2p_{2}},\dots,v_{m0},v_{m1},\dots,v_{mp_{m}}
is denoted by
[v_{10},v_{11},\dots,v_{1p_{1}}\mid v_{20},v_{21},\dots,v_{2p_{2}}\mid\dots\mid v_{m0},v_{m1},\dots,v_{mp_{m}}]
. A simplotope equipped with an orientation is said to be an
oriented simplotope
. With a slight abuse of notation, we also represent an oriented simplotope
\sigma
with the orientation given by the ordering
v_{10},v_{11},\dots,v_{1p_{1}},v_{20},v_{21},\dots,v_{2p_{2}},\dots,v_{m0},v_{m1},

\dots,v_{mp_{m}}
as
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m})
where each
\sigma_{i}=\{v_{i0},v_{i1},\dots,v_{ip_{i}}\}
has the ordering
v_{i0},v_{i1},\dots,v_{ip_{i}}
. We note that this will not cause any terminological ambiguity. The collection of all
q
-dimensional oriented simplotopes of
K
is denoted as
\mathbb{S}_{q}(K)
.

Let
\tau=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{m})
be a simplotope where
\tau_{j}=\{v_{j0},v_{j1},\dots,v_{jp_{j}}\}
for each
j\in[m]
, and
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m})
be a facet of
\tau
. Now as we have discussed earlier,
\sigma
must be of the form

(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{j}\setminus\{v_{ji}\},\dots,\tau_{m})
. Then
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m})
denotes the oriented simplotope with the orientation represented by the ordering
v_{10},v_{11},\dots,v_{1p_{1}},\dots,v_{j0},\dots,\widehat{v_{ji}},\dots,v_{jp_{j}},\dots,v_{m0},v_{m1},\dots,v_{mp_{m}}
.

Definition 2.4
.

(Incidence Number.)
Let

. Suppose

. Let

\langle\tau,\sigma\rangle:=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\left(\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\dim(\sigma_{k})\right)+i}&\text{ if }j\neq 0\\

The next observation follows naturally from the above definition.

Observation 2.5
.

Let
\tau=(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})

However, if

with respect to

For each

, we define

over

.

.

This gives us the

of

3
Discrete Morse theory on simplotopal complex

Let
K
be a simplotopal complex. A
discrete vector field

\mathcal{V}
on
K
is defined as a collection of pairs of simplotopes
(\sigma,\tau)
in
K
such that
\sigma
is a facet of
\tau
and every simplotope in
K
appears in atmost one such pair.

Now, we define
\mathcal{V}
-trajectories in
K
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
\mathcal{V}
-trajectory is defined as a sequence of simplotopes of
K
as follows.

P:\tau_{0}^{(q)},\sigma_{1}^{(q-1)},\tau_{1}^{(q)},\dots,\sigma_{k}^{(q-1)},\tau_{k}^{(q)},

where
(\sigma_{i}^{(q-1)},\tau_{i}^{(q)})\in\mathcal{V}
for each
i\in[k]
, and
\sigma_{i}
is a facet of
\tau_{i-1}
,
(\sigma_{i}^{(q-1)},\tau_{i-1}^{(q)})\notin\mathcal{V}
for each
i\in[k]
.

We say that
P
is
non-trivial
if
k>0
and
closed
if
\tau_{0}=\tau_{r}
for
r>1
.

A discrete vector field
\mathcal{V}
is defined as a
gradient vector field
if there are no closed
\mathcal{V}
-trajectories.

An
extended
\mathcal{V}
-trajectory
is defined as a sequence of simplotopes in
K
of the following form.

P:\tau_{0}^{(q)},\sigma_{1}^{(q-1)},\tau_{1}^{(q)},\dots,\sigma_{k}^{(q-1)},\tau_{k}^{(q)},\sigma_{k+1}^{(q-1)},

where
(\sigma_{i}^{(q-1)},\tau_{i}^{(q)})\in\mathcal{V}
for each
i\in[k]
, and
\sigma_{i}
is a facet of
\tau_{i-1}
,
(\sigma_{i}^{(q-1)},\tau_{i-1}^{(q)})\notin\mathcal{V}
for each
i\in[k+1]
.

We refer to
\tau_{0}
as the
initial simplotope
of
P
and
\sigma_{k+1}
as the
terminal simplotope
of
P
.
Here onwards, we will refer to an (extended)
\mathcal{V}
-trajectory as simply a trajectory and note that this will not create any additional ambiguity.

The set of all
\mathcal{V}
-trajectories in
K
with the initial simplotope as
\tau
and the terminal simplotope as
\sigma
is denoted by
\Gamma(\tau,\sigma)
.
Next we define the weight of a trajectory.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
P
be the following
\mathcal{V}
-trajectory.

P:\tau_{0}^{(q)},\sigma_{1}^{(q-1)},\tau_{1}^{(q)},\dots,\sigma_{k}^{(q-1)},\tau_{k}^{(q)},\sigma_{k+1}^{(q-1)}.

Then the
weight
of
P
is defined as,

w(P):=\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)\langle\tau_{i},\sigma_{i+1}\rangle\langle\tau_{i+1},\sigma_{i+1}\rangle\right)\langle\tau_{k},\sigma_{k+1}\rangle.

A simplotope which does not appear in
\mathcal{V}
is termed as
\mathcal{V}
-critical
. The set of all
q
-dimensional critical simplotopes is denoted by
\operatorname{Crit}^{\mathcal{V}}_{q}(K)
.

Next, for each
q\geq 0
, we define the
q^{th}

Thom–Smale chain group
of
K
with respect to
\mathcal{V}
,
\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{V}}_{q}(K)
, as the free module generated by
\operatorname{Crit}^{\mathcal{V}}_{q}(K)
over
\mathbb{Z}
. Then we define the
(q+1)^{th}
boundary operator
\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{V}}:\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{V}}_{q+1}(K)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{V}}_{q}(K)
as follows. Let
\tau\in\operatorname{Crit}_{q+1}^{\mathcal{V}}(K)
. Then,

\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{V}}(\tau):=\sum_{\sigma\in\operatorname{Crit}^{\mathcal{V}}_{q}(K)}\left(\sum_{P\in\Gamma(\tau,\sigma)}w(P)\right)\sigma.

Now we extend this map linearly to
\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{V}}_{q+1}(K)
. Thus, we obtain the chain complex
(\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{V}}_{\#}(K),\partial_{\#}^{\mathcal{V}})
, which is defined as the Thom–Smale chain complex on
K
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The following,

\dots\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{V}}_{q+1}(X)\xrightarrow{\partial^{\mathcal{V}}_{q+1}}\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{V}}_{q}(X)\xrightarrow{\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{V}}}\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{V}}_{q-1}(X)\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{V}}_{0}(X)\rightarrow 0.

is a chain complex.

Thus, we obtain the chain complex
(\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{V}}_{\#}(K),\partial_{\#}^{\mathcal{V}})
, which is referred to as the Thom–Smale chain complex on
K
. We define the
q^{th}
Thom–Smale homology group as,

\mathbb{H}_{q}^{\mathcal{V}}(K):=\frac{\operatorname{Ker}(\partial_{q}^{\mathcal{V}})}{\operatorname{Im}(\partial_{q+1}^{\mathcal{V}})}.

The following result states a version of Forman’s immensely powerful result (see
[
2
,
3
]
), in the simplotopal setup.

Theorem 3.4
.

The Thom–Smale complex of
K
is homotopy equivalent to the simplotopal chain complex of
K
. Hence,
\mathbb{H}^{\mathcal{V}}_{\#}(K)\cong\mathbb{H}_{\#}(K)
.

This theorem follows from the idea of similar results in
[
4
]
.

4
The main theorem

In this section, we will prove our main result in two parts.

Let
X
be a simplicial complex with subcomplexes
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
such that
X=\cup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{N}(X)
be the nerve complex of
X
. We recall that for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{N}(X)
,
A_{\alpha}=\cap_{i\in\alpha}A_{i}
. Suppose
\mathcal{W}_{\alpha}
is a given gradient vector field on
A_{\alpha}
for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{N}(X)
. In the first part, we construct a simplotopal complex
\widetilde{X}
from
X
and show that the the simplotopal homology of
\widetilde{X}
is isomorphic to the simplicial homology of
X
. In the second part, we construct a new chain complex
\mathcal{D}_{\#}
on
X
and use the gradient vector fields
\mathcal{W}_{\alpha}
on
A_{\alpha}
for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{N}(X)
to show that the homology of
\mathcal{D}_{\#}
is isomorphic to the simplotopal homology of
\widetilde{X}
.

4.1
Construction of a simplotopal complex
\widetilde{X}

We recall that
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
are subcomplexes of
X
where
X=\cup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
.
Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{N}(X)
, where
\alpha=\{i_{0},\dots,i_{r}\}
,
0\leq i_{0}<\dots<i_{r}\leq k
. We also recall
A_{\alpha}=\cap_{i\in\alpha}A_{i}
. Now, for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{N}(X)
, we define,

\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}:=\{(\sigma,\alpha)\mid\sigma\in A_{\alpha}\}.

Next we define a simplotopal complex
\widetilde{X}
as,

\widetilde{X}:=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{N}(X)}\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}.

Next, we construct a gradient vector field
\mathcal{V}
on
\widetilde{X}
such that the Thom–Smale complex of
\widetilde{X}
with respect to
\mathcal{V}
is isomorphic to the simplicial chain complex of
X
.

4.2
Construction of a gradient vector field
\mathcal{V}
on
\widetilde{X}

For each
\sigma\in X
, first let us define
\Lambda_{\sigma}:=\{i\in[k]\mid\sigma\in A_{i}\}
. Thus
\Lambda_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{N}(X)
for each
\sigma\in X
. Next, we define, for each
\sigma\in X
,
P_{\sigma}:=\{(\sigma,\alpha)\mid\alpha\subseteq\Lambda_{\sigma}\}
. Therefore, it can be observed that,

\widetilde{X}=\bigcup_{\sigma\in X}P_{\sigma},

where
P_{\sigma}\cap P_{\tau}=\emptyset
for
\sigma\neq\tau
,
\sigma,\tau\in X
.

Now we define a collection of pairs
\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}
on
P_{\sigma}
for each
\sigma\in X
as follows.

Suppose,
\Lambda_{\sigma}=\{i_{0},\dots,i_{r}\}
, where
0\leq i_{0}<i_{1}<\dots i_{r}\leq k
. Let
(\sigma,\alpha)\in P_{\sigma}
. If
i_{0}\in\alpha
, then
\left((\sigma,\alpha\setminus\{i_{0}\}),(\sigma,\alpha)\right)\in\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}
.

We define
\mathcal{V}:=\cup_{\sigma\in X}\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}
.

For each
\sigma\in X
, let
i_{\sigma}=\min\{i\mid i\in\Lambda_{\sigma}\}
. Therefore the unpaired simplotopes in
\widetilde{X}
are given by
\{(\sigma,\{i_{\sigma}\})\mid\sigma\in X\}
.

Observation 4.1
.

Let
\sigma\subseteq\tau
,
\sigma,\tau\in X
. Then
\Lambda_{\tau}\subseteq\Lambda_{\sigma}
. Consequently,
i_{\sigma}\leq i_{\tau}
.

The following proposition characterizes the
\mathcal{V}
-trajectories in
\widetilde{X}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
P:\tau_{0}^{\prime},\sigma_{1}^{\prime},\tau_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\sigma_{k}^{\prime},\tau_{k}^{\prime},\sigma_{k+1}^{\prime}
be a
\mathcal{V}
-trajectory in
\widetilde{X}
, where
\sigma_{j}^{\prime}=(\sigma_{j},\alpha_{j})
,
\tau_{j}^{\prime}=(\tau_{j},\beta_{j})
,
\sigma_{j},\tau_{j}\in X
,
\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}\in\mathcal{N}(X)
. Then
P
is of the following form.

(i)

Either of the following holds.

(a)

\sigma_{1}
is a facet of
\tau_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}=\beta_{0}
,

(b)

\sigma_{1}=\tau_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}=\beta_{0}\setminus\{x\}
,
x\neq i_{\tau_{0}}
.

(ii)

For each
j\in[k]
,
\tau_{j}=\sigma_{j}
,
i_{\sigma_{j}}\notin\alpha_{j}
and
\beta_{j}=\alpha_{j}\cup\{i_{\sigma_{j}}\}
.

(iii)

For each
j\in[k-1]
,
\sigma_{j+1}
is a facet of
\tau_{j}
such that
i_{\sigma_{j+1}}<i_{\tau_{j}}
and
\alpha_{j+1}=\beta_{j}
.

Proof.

The first condition (condition
(i)
) of the proposition follows from the fact that
(\tau_{0}^{\prime},\sigma_{1}^{\prime})\notin\mathcal{V}
. Indeed, if
\sigma_{1}=\tau_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}=\beta_{0}\setminus\{i_{\tau_{0}}\}
, then
(\sigma_{1}^{\prime},\tau_{1}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}
. The second condition (condition
(ii)
) follows from the fact that
(\sigma_{j},\tau_{j})\in\mathcal{V}
. Now, for each
j\in[k-1]
, there are two possibilities, either
\sigma_{j+1}=\tau_{j}
and
\alpha_{j+1}
is a facet of
\beta_{j}
, or
\sigma_{j+1}
is a facet of
\tau_{j}
and
\alpha_{j+1}=\beta_{j}
. In the first case, we know from
(ii)
that
\alpha_{j+1}=\beta_{j}\setminus\{x\}
,
x\neq i_{\tau_{j}}
, which means
i_{\tau_{j}}\in\alpha_{j+1}
. Consequently, from the construction of
\mathcal{V}
,
\sigma_{j}^{\prime}
is paired with a lower dimensional simplotope which is not possible. Therefore,
\sigma_{j+1}
is a facet of
\tau_{j}
and
\alpha_{j+1}=\beta_{j}
. Now, if
i_{\sigma_{j+1}}=i_{\tau_{j}}

\displaystyle=\langle(\tau_{1,0},\alpha_{0}),(\tau_{1,0},\alpha_{1})\rangle\prod_{l=1}^{t-1}\langle(\tau_{l+1,j_{l}},\alpha_{l}),(\tau_{l+1,j_{l}},\alpha_{l+1})\rangle\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j_{1}-2}(-1)\langle(\tau_{2,k+1},\alpha_{1}),(\tau_{1,k},\alpha_{1})\rangle\right.

\displaystyle\left.\langle(\tau_{2,k+1},\alpha_{1}),(\tau_{1,k+1},\alpha_{1})\rangle\right)(-1)\langle(\tau_{2,j_{1}},\alpha_{1}),(\tau_{1,j_{1}-1},\alpha_{1})\rangle

\displaystyle\prod_{l=2}^{t-1}\left(\left(\prod_{k=j_{l-1}}^{j_{l}-2}(-1)\langle(\tau_{l+1,k+1},\alpha_{l}),(\tau_{l,k},\alpha_{l})\rangle\langle(\tau_{l+1,k+1},\alpha_{l}),(\tau_{l,k+1},\alpha_{l})\rangle\right)(-1)\langle(\tau_{l+1,j_{l}},\alpha_{l}),(\tau_{l,j_{l}-1},\alpha_{l})\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{k=j_{t}}^{j_{t}+r-1}(-1)\langle(\tau_{t+1,k+1},\alpha_{t}),(\tau_{t,k},\alpha_{t})\rangle\langle(\tau_{t+1,k+1},\alpha_{t}),(\tau_{t,k+1},\alpha_{t})\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{t}(-1)^{i+l-1}\prod_{l=1}^{t}\langle\alpha_{l-1},\alpha_{l}\rangle\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j_{1}-2}(-1)\langle\tau_{2,k+1},\tau_{1,k}\rangle\langle\tau_{2,k+1},\tau_{1,k+1}\rangle\right)(-1)\langle\tau_{2,j_{1}},\tau_{1,j_{1}-1}\rangle

\displaystyle\prod_{l=2}^{t-1}\left(\left(\prod_{k=j_{l-1}}^{j_{l}-2}(-1)\langle\tau_{l+1,k+1},\tau_{l,k}\rangle\langle\tau_{l+1,k+1},\tau_{l,k+1}\rangle\right)(-1)\langle\tau_{l+1,j_{l}},\tau_{l,j_{l}-1}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{k=j_{t}}^{j_{t}+r-1}(-1)\langle\tau_{t+1,k+1},\tau_{t,k}\rangle\langle\tau_{t+1,k+1},\tau_{t,k+1}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\phi(i,t)}\prod_{l=1}^{t}\langle\alpha_{l-1},\alpha_{l}\rangle\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j_{1}-2}(-1)\langle\tau_{2,k+1},\tau_{1,k}\rangle\langle\tau_{2,k+1},\tau_{1,k+1}\rangle\right)(-1)\langle\tau_{2,j_{1}},\tau_{1,j_{1}-1}\rangle

\displaystyle\prod_{l=2}^{t-1}\left(\left(\prod_{k=j_{l-1}}^{j_{l}-2}(-1)\langle\tau_{l+1,k+1},\tau_{l,k}\rangle\langle\tau_{l+1,k+1},\tau_{l,k+1}\rangle\right)(-1)\langle\tau_{l+1,j_{l}},\tau_{l,j_{l}-1}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{k=j_{t}}^{j_{t}+r-1}(-1)\langle\tau_{t+1,k+1},\tau_{t,k}\rangle\langle\tau_{t+1,k+1},\tau_{t,k+1}\rangle\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\left(\text{ where }\phi(i,t)=it+\frac{t(t-1)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=w_{G}(\psi(P)).

Hence
w(P)=w_{G}(\psi(P))
for each
P\in\Gamma((\tau,\beta),(\sigma,\alpha))
.
∎

Proposition 4.12
.

The following diagram commutes.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{W}}_{q+1}(\widetilde{X})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{W}}_{q}(\widetilde{X})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{\mathcal{L}}_{q+1}(X)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{L}_{q}(X)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial^{\mathcal{W}}_{q+1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{q+1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{q}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial^{\mathcal{L}}_{q+1}}
\scriptstyle\circlearrowleft

Hence,
(\mathcal{L}_{\#},\partial^{\mathcal{L}}_{\#})
is a chain complex and
(\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{W}}_{\#},\partial^{\mathcal{W}}_{\#})\cong(\mathcal{L}_{\#},\partial^{\mathcal{L}}_{\#})
. Consequentially,
\mathbb{H}^{\mathcal{W}}_{\#}(\widetilde{X})\cong H^{\mathcal{L}}_{\#}(X)
.

Proof.

We recall from
4.8
that
f_{q+1}:\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{W}}_{q}(\widetilde{X})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{q}(X)
is an isomorphism given by
f_{q+1}((\sigma^{(i)},\alpha^{(q+1-i)}))=\sigma_{\alpha}
, where
0\leq i\leq(q+1)
.

Now, we show that
f_{q}\circ\partial^{\mathcal{W}}_{q+1}=\partial^{\mathcal{L}}_{q+1}\circ f_{q+1}
.
Let
\tau^{\prime}=(\tau^{(i)},\beta^{(q+1-i)})\in\operatorname{Crit}_{q+1}^{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{X})
for a fixed
i\in\{0,\dots,(q+1)\}
. For each
t\in\{0,\dots,(q+1-i)\}
, let
(\sigma_{t},\alpha_{t})=(\sigma^{(i+t-1)},\alpha^{(q-i-t)})\in\operatorname{Crit}_{q}^{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{X})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle f_{q}(\partial^{\mathcal{W}}_{q+1}((\tau,\beta)))

\displaystyle=f_{q}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{q+1-i}\sum_{(\sigma_{t},\alpha_{t})\in\operatorname{Crit}_{q}^{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{X})}\left(\sum_{P\in\Gamma(\tau^{\prime},(\sigma_{t},\alpha_{t}))}w(P)\right)(\sigma_{t},\alpha_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{q+1-i}\sum_{(\sigma_{t},\alpha_{t})\in\operatorname{Crit}_{q}^{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{X})}\left(\sum_{P\in\Gamma(\tau^{\prime},(\sigma_{t},\alpha_{t}))}w(P)\right)(\sigma_{t})_{\alpha_{t}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{q+1-i}\sum_{(\sigma_{t},\alpha_{t})\in\operatorname{Crit}_{q}^{\mathcal{W}}(\widetilde{X})}\left(\sum_{\psi(P)\in\operatorname{GT}(\tau_{\beta},(\sigma_{t})_{\alpha_{t}})}w_{G}(\psi(P))\right)(\sigma_{t})_{\alpha_{t}},

\displaystyle\text{ (follows from \autoref{bij} and \autoref{weight})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{q+1-i}\sum_{(\sigma_{t})_{\alpha_{t}}\in L_{q+1}(X)}\left(\sum_{\psi(P)\in\operatorname{GT}(\tau_{\beta},(\sigma_{t})_{\alpha_{t}})}w_{G}(\psi(P))\right)(\sigma_{t})_{\alpha_{t}},

\displaystyle\text{(follows from \autoref{chiso})}

\displaystyle=\partial^{\mathcal{L}}_{q+1}(\tau_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\partial^{\mathcal{L}}_{q+1}(f_{q+1}((\tau,\beta))).

Thus,
(\mathcal{L}_{\#},\partial^{\mathcal{L}}_{\#})
is a chain complex and
f_{\#}
is a chain isomorphism between
(\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{W}}_{\#},\partial^{\mathcal{W}}_{\#})
and
(\mathcal{L}_{\#},\partial^{\mathcal{L}}_{\#})
. Hence, the result follows.

∎

Therefore, we obtain the following isomorphisms,

H_{\#}(X)\cong\mathbb{H}_{\#}(\widetilde{X})\cong\mathbb{H}^{\mathcal{W}}_{\#}(\widetilde{X})\cong H^{\mathcal{L}}_{\#}(X),

where the first isomorphism follows from
4.5
, the second isomorphism follows from
3.4
while the third follows from
4.12
. This establishes our main result,
1.2
.

5
An application of the combinatorial nerve theorem

In this section, we prove a version of the usual homological nerve theorem using our combinatorial nerve theorem,
1.2
. Suppose,
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
are subcomplexes of
X
such that
\cup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}=X
and
A_{\alpha}
is collapsible for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{N}(X)
. Then, this theorem states that the homology groups of
X
are isomorphic to the respective homology groups of
\mathcal{N}(X)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
X
be a simplicial complex with subcomplexes
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
such that
X=\cup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{N}(X)
be the nerve complex of
X
. Further, suppose that
A_{\alpha}
is collapsible for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{N}(X)
. Then,

H_{\#}(X)\cong H_{\#}(\mathcal{N}(X)).

Proof.

Since,
A_{\alpha}
is collapsible for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{N}(X)
, therefore each
A_{\alpha}
can be assigned a gradient vector field such that only one
0
-simplex is critical in
A_{\alpha}
with respect to that gradient vector field. For each
\alpha\in\mathcal{N}(X)
, let
\bar{A}_{\alpha}
be a disjoint copy of
A_{\alpha}
and
\overline{\mathcal{W}}_{\alpha}
be a gradient vector field on
\bar{A}_{\alpha}
such that the only
\overline{\mathcal{W}}_{\alpha}
-critical simplex in
\bar{A}_{\alpha}
is a
0
-simplex of
\bar{A}_{\alpha}
.

We show that,
H_{\#}^{\mathcal{L}}(X)\cong H_{\#}(\mathcal{N}(X))
. Then, it will follow from our theorem
1.2
that
H_{\#}(X)\cong H_{\#}^{\mathcal{L}}(X)\cong H_{\#}(\mathcal{N}(X))
. We note that, for each
q\geq 0
,

L_{q}(X)=\bigcup_{\alpha\in S_{q}(\mathcal{N}(X))}\operatorname{Crit}_{0}^{\overline{\mathcal{W}}_{\alpha}}(\bar{A}_{\alpha}).

Therefore,
\mathcal{L}(X)=\mathbb{Z}\langle L_{q}(X)\rangle
.

First, we construct an isomorphism
f_{q}
between
C_{q}(\mathcal{N}(X))
and
\mathcal{L}_{q}(X)
, for each
q\geq 0
. Then we will show that this is also a chain map, i.e., the following diagram commutes.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{L}_{q+1}(X)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{L}_{q}(X)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{C_{q+1}(\mathcal{N}(X))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{C_{q}(\mathcal{N}(X))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial^{\mathcal{L}}_{q+1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{q+1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{q}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial_{q+1}}
\scriptstyle\circlearrowleft

We define
f_{q}:\mathcal{L}_{q}(X)\rightarrow C_{q}(\mathcal{N}(X))
on the set of generators. Let,
\sigma_{\alpha}\in L_{q}(X)
. Then,

\sigma_{\alpha}\longmapsto\alpha.

Let us now define
g_{q}:C_{q}(\mathcal{N}(X))\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{q}(X)
as,

\alpha\longmapsto\sigma_{\alpha}

where
\alpha\in S_{q}(\mathcal{N}(X))
and
\operatorname{Crit}^{\overline{\mathcal{W}}_{\alpha}}(\bar{A}_{\alpha})=\{\sigma_{\alpha}^{(0)}\}
. Both
f_{q}
and
g_{q}
are defined on the generators whence they are linearly extended to their domains. The well-definedness of
g_{q}
follows from the fact that each
\bar{A}_{\alpha}
, being collapsible, has a unique
\overline{\mathcal{W}}_{\alpha}
-critical simplex. Again, following the same line of reasoning, it can now be easily verified that
f_{q}\circ g_{q}=\operatorname{id}
and
g_{q}\circ f_{q}=\operatorname{id}
on the set of generators and hence on their respective domains.

Now, we prove,
f_{q}\circ\partial^{\mathcal{L}}_{q+1}=\partial_{q+1}\circ f_{q+1}.

First, we observe that, for any
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}\in L_{q+1}(X)
, all the generalised trajectories beginning from
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}
terminate at some
\sigma^{\prime}_{\alpha}\in L_{q}(X)
, where
\alpha\subseteq\alpha_{0}
. Now, we characterize these generalised trajectories more precisely and compute their weights, by proving the following two claims.

Claim 1:
Let
\alpha_{0}^{(q+1)},\alpha_{1}^{(q)}\in\mathcal{N}(X)
such that
\alpha_{1}<_{f}\alpha_{0}
. Let
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}\in L_{q+1}
,
\sigma^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}\in L_{q}
. Then, there exists a unique generalised trajectory,
P_{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}}
from
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}
to
\sigma^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}
. Moreover, any generalised trajectory beginning from
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}\in L_{q+1}(X)
and ending at some simplex in
L_{q}(X)
is of the form
P_{\alpha_{0},\alpha}
, where
\alpha<_{f}\alpha_{0}
.

Proof of Claim 1.

Since,
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}
is a
0
-simplex, therefore, the only possible generalised trajectory emanating from
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}
is of the second kind. Therefore, a generalised trajectory initiating from
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}
is of the form,
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}},\sigma_{\alpha},\dots
, where,
\alpha<_{f}\alpha_{0}
. Now, if
\alpha=\alpha_{1}
, then a generalised trajectory starting from
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}
is always of the form,

\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}^{(0)},((\sigma_{0})_{\alpha_{1}}=)~\sigma_{\alpha_{1}}^{(0)},(\tau_{0})^{(1)}_{\alpha_{1}},(\sigma_{1})^{(0)}_{\alpha_{1}},\dots,(\sigma_{k})^{(0)}_{\alpha_{1}},(\tau_{k})^{(1)}_{\alpha_{1}},(\sigma_{k+1})^{(0)}_{\alpha_{1}}~(=\sigma^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}).

This follows from the following observation. Since there are no
\overline{\mathcal{W}}_{\alpha}
-critical
1
-simplex in any
\bar{A}_{\alpha}
, therefore these generalised trajectories cannot continue further, into facets of
\alpha_{1}
. Hence, these generalised trajectories must end at the unique
\overline{\mathcal{W}}_{\alpha_{1}}
-critical
0
-simplex of
\bar{A}_{\alpha_{1}}
, namely
\sigma^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}
(since
\bar{A}_{\alpha_{1}}
is collapsible).

Next, we observe that each
(\sigma_{i})_{\alpha_{1}}
is paired uniquely with
(\tau_{i})_{\alpha_{1}}
. Further, each
(\tau_{i})_{\alpha_{1}}
being an
1
-simplex, the facet
(\sigma_{i+1})_{\alpha_{1}}
of
(\tau_{i})_{\alpha_{1}}
, following
(\tau_{i})_{\alpha_{1}}
in the sequence, is unique. This makes this generalised trajectory unique. We denote this unique generalised trajectory with initial simplex
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}
and terminal simplex
\sigma^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}
as
P_{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}}
.
∎

Claim 2:

w_{G}(P_{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}})=\langle\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}\rangle
.

Proof of Claim 2.

Next, we compute the weight of the trajectory
P_{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}}
.

\displaystyle w_{G}(P_{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{0}\langle\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}\rangle\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k}(-1)\langle\tau_{i},\sigma_{i}\rangle\langle\tau_{i},\sigma_{i+1}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{0}\langle\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}\rangle\left(\prod_{i=0}^{k}-(-1)(1)\right),\text{ (since, $\tau_{i}$ is a $1$-simplex.)}

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}\rangle.

∎

Now, let
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}\in L_{q+1}(X)
, where
\alpha_{0}\in S_{q+1}(\mathcal{N}(X))
. Therefore, from the property of the trajectories from
\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}
, we can write,

\displaystyle f_{q}(\partial^{\mathcal{L}}_{q+1}(\sigma_{\alpha_{0}}))

\displaystyle=f_{q}\left(\sum_{\alpha<_{f}\alpha_{0}}\left(\sum_{\sigma_{\alpha}\in L_{q}(X)}w_{G}(P_{\alpha_{0},\alpha})\sigma_{\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=f_{q}\left(\sum_{\alpha<_{f}\alpha_{0}}\left(\sum_{\sigma_{\alpha}\in L_{q}(X)}\langle\alpha_{0},\alpha\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}\right)\right),\text{ (since, $w_{G}(P_{\alpha_{0},\alpha})=\langle\alpha_{0},\alpha\rangle$)}

\displaystyle=f_{q}\left(\sum_{\alpha<_{f}\alpha_{0}}\langle\alpha_{0},\alpha\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}\right),\text{ (since, there exists a unique $\sigma_{\alpha}\in L_{q}$ for each $\alpha<_{f}\alpha_{0}$,)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha<_{f}\alpha_{0}}\langle\alpha_{0},\alpha\rangle f_{q}(\sigma_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha<_{f}\alpha_{0}}\langle\alpha_{0},\alpha\rangle\alpha

\displaystyle=\partial_{q+1}(\alpha_{0})=\partial_{q+1}(f_{q+1}(\sigma_{\alpha_{0}})).

Hence, the result follows. ∎
