A feasibility study of an Evidence-informed, Competency-based, Accredited, Compassion Training (EnACT) program
Authors: Shane Sinclair, Cara MacInnis, Amanda Roze des Ordons, Swati Dhingra, Daranne Harris, Shelley Raffin Bouchal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study assessed the feasibility and impact of an Evidence-informed, Competency-based, Accredited, Compassion Training (EnACT) program on both healthcare providers’ confidence and competence in compassion, professional fulfillment, and burnout; and patients’ experiences of compassion.DesignA two arm non-randomized controlled feasibility trial to evaluate the feasibility and impact of the program over a nine-month period.Settings and ParticipantsThe study was conducted between July 2023 and August 2024 in two long term care settings and two hospices in Canada. A total of 163 eligible healthcare providers participated, and 634 patients completed the Sinclair Compassion Questionnaire (SCQ).ResultsAll feasibility targets were met, apart from the proportion of patients who skipped any one item on the SCQ being slightly higher than predicted. In addition to high sustained levels of learner satisfaction, professional fulfillment significantly increased in the intervention group. Compassion confidence also improved significantly in the intervention group immediately post-training and remained high nine-months post-training, with a similar, albeit weaker, trend observed for compassion competence. Notably, one-month post-training, patients being cared for by participants in the intervention group, reported a statistically significant increase in experiences of compassion in comparison to patients in the control group. These results and subsequent power analysis suggest that a full randomized controlled trial is feasible.ConclusionsCompassion has been long regarded as a key component of quality care that patients want and healthcare providers desire to provide. Despite its reputed importance, healthcare providers are given limited training and healthcare organizations are provided little guidance on how to cultivate compassion in their organizational cultures. The EnACT program addresses existing limitations associated with compassion training programs, while also positively impacting both learner outcomes associated with compassion and also importantly their patients’ experiences of compassion.

Background

Compassion is a virtuous response that seeks to address the suffering and needs of a person through relational understanding and action [1]. In healthcare, compassion is a significant predictor of patients’ quality care ratings [2]; physician ratings [3]; and the overall patient experience [4–6]. Deficits in compassion are associated with increased malpractice suits, patient/family complaints, healthcare costs and poorer patient outcomes [5, 7, 8]. Healthcare providers (HCPs) who work in organizations that prioritize and exhibit compassion to employees and clients, and are supported to provide compassion to their patients report higher workplace wellbeing, job satisfaction, compassionate care, and lower burnout and absenteeism [4, 8–11].

While compassion is increasingly considered a core competency of healthcare education [4, 7, 8], a patient right [12], and a central value of professional codes of ethics [13–15] that can be taught [4, 16, 17], evidence-based compassion training programs are nascent within healthcare, with existing programs outside of healthcare having significant limitations that diminish their clinical relevance and utility [4, 18, 19]. Limitations include a lack of: conceptual clarity; valid measures; clinical relevancy; content addressing workplace barriers; and patient reported outcomes. In addition, existing programs typically focus on a small facet of compassion (self-compassion, affective compassion, compassion fatigue, etc.) superimposed from disciplines outside of healthcare (Education, Social Psychology, Philosophy, Religious Studies). For compassion education interventions to be effective they need to be evidence-based and clinically relevant; integrate a strength-based approach; focus on the development of learners’ practical skills, knowledge and attitudes; address organizational factors; include relational and reflective skills training; and include cultural safety and anti-racism training [4, 18, 20].

To address these limitations, we developed an Evidence-informed, Competency based, Accredited Compassion Training (EnACT) program [21] based on our programmatic research on the topic [1, 6, 7, 11, 22–25]. The training program was directly informed by a patient and family advisory council [21]; qualitative studies exploring the perspectives of patients [16], healthcare providers [17], and leaders in compassion education [19]; assessing the impact of measuring compassion at a systems level [2, 3, 25]; and systematic reviews on compassion education and the conditions required to sustain compassion in clinical care [4, 18]. The overall aim of this study was to assess the feasibility and initial impact of the EnACT program from the perspective of both healthcare providers and their patients (See Table 1).

Methods

Design

A 2-arm non-randomized controlled feasibility trial (NRCFT) [26, 27] consistent with Stage 1B of the NIH model [28] was designed to closely simulate and inform a future clinical trial of the EnACT program and to evaluate its initial impact [21]. NRCFTs often involve: an iterative approach to data collection; qualitative as well as quantitative components; and running and evaluating a smaller version of the larger study, in which participants and sites are not randomized, in order to assess the feasibility and impact of a larger subsequent trial [29–31]. The study was approved by the Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (#20–0907) at the University of Calgary.

Intervention

The EnACT program is described in detail elsewhere [21]. In short, the program was developed from over a decade of programmatic research on compassion in healthcare [1, 4, 7, 8, 18, 21, 25]. EnACT can be delivered in-person or online (https://www.compassiontraining.com) and consists of one mini-module (30 min) overviewing the topic, evidence, and impact of compassion and four core modules (1.5 h. each) that map onto the domains of the Compassion Model [1] and the Sinclair Compassion Questionnaire (SCQ) [25]: (1) Cultivating the Virtues and Values Associated with Compassion; (2) Establishing an In-Depth Understanding of the Person and Their Suffering; (3) Developing Compassionate Communication Skills; (4) Addressing Suffering and Integrating Compassion into Practice. Using a competency-based medical education approach [32–34], the modules consist of a mix of evidence informed clinically relevant teaching, experiential learning, self-reflective exercises, clinical case studies, and a learner toolkit, each of which aim to equip learners with the attitudes, knowledge, and skills [33] to enhance compassion in their clinical practice and within their organizations. The learner toolkit contains learner and organizational resources introduced in the modules and is intended to sustain learnings over time: (1) Program Workbook; (2) ‘Compassion Compass’ lanyard placard; (3) a 52-week Compassion Journal; (4) ‘Compassion Checkpoint’ workplace posters; and (5) Patient ‘Compassion and Me’ Posters.

Participants

Recruitment and consent

The study was conducted between July 2023 and August 2024. The training program was delivered in-person at two publicly funded long term care (LTC) centres (approximately 200 beds each) and two hospices (approximately 20 beds each), each located in an urban setting in Alberta, Canada. These settings were intentionally chosen due to the prevalence of suffering—an antecedent to compassion, and recent research, healthcare reviews, and media reports suggesting compassion is particularly challenged in long-term care settings [7, 25]. To maximize recruitment, we first engaged site leaders to inform them of the program, garner organizational support, and identify contextual challenges. Site leaders approved attendance with pay for all staff, as part of their sites annual continuing professional development accreditation requirement [35, 36]. Healthcare provider participants were recruited via study posters in their workplace and the organization’s email listserv. There were 163 eligible staff, and they all consented to participate (intervention and control), representing a 100% recruitment rate (Fig. 1).

Healthcare providers

Healthcare providers (HCPs) were recruited in September 2023. HCPs were broadly defined in this study as any member of the organization, who interacted with patients directly in the care setting and by virtue, were able to provide compassion, whether as a clinician, a unit clerk, or a member of the housekeeping staff. Inclusion criteria were: ≥18 years old; able to read and speak English; provide care for patients in either LTC or hospice; and have decision-making capacity to provide written consent.

Data were collected from HCPs 1 month pre-intervention, immediately after the intervention, and 9 months post-intervention.

Patients

Patient data was collected between July 2023 – June 2024. There were 724 eligible participants, with 634 participating in the study. Participants, who were not cognitively impaired, could read and speak English, and whose symptoms were well controlled were administered the SCQ on a monthly basis as a standard measure of care within participating sites. To obtain the monthly SCQ score, eligible patients were visited each week by a volunteer of the organization or a member of the clerical staff until a completed SCQ was obtained for the month, as patients may have been unable to complete the SCQ on a given week (off-unit, sleeping, medical procedure, etc.). Monthly collection of the SCQ scores for the purpose of this study, began 3 months prior to training and continued 9 months post training. Since participant information was required to be de-identified as per REB approval, a single patient may have completed the questionnaire multiple times, or completed it only once over the course of the study.

Procedures

The LTC and hospice with the greatest number of staff and patients were intentionally assigned to the intervention group in order to obtain as much feedback about the acceptability, feasibility, and impact of the intervention. Staff in the control group working on other units continued with usual practice, were offered the training after study completion, and were not exposed to any of the elements of the training. While masking was impossible as the training is distinguishable from the control, to minimize contamination across sites, participants in the training group were asked to not share information about the study with their colleagues at other sites.

After consenting, HCP participants in both arms of the study were administered questionnaires via Qualtrics one month prior to the training intervention. HCP participants were administered baseline surveys which included a learner needs assessment [37], confidence and competence in compassion questionairres, burnout/professional fulfillment questionairres, and demographic variables (See Table 2). The needs assessment indicated that while learners felt that compassion was vital to their care and was espoused as an organizational value, evidence-informed training that spanned both personal practice and the broader organization was lacking, and they identified a number of personal, relational, and organizational challenges that inhibited their ability to provide compassion.

HCP learner demographics and characteristics

The one-day training (6.5 h) intervention was repeated over 4 consecutive days in October 2023. On the day of the training, participants in the intervention arm (n = 103) were administered a set of post-training surveys immediately after the training which included a workshop satisfaction survey, confidence and competence in compassion questionnaires, and a commitment to change form inviting them to make three commitments to change in their future practice based on their learnings from the training. The same post-training surveys (excluding the workshop satisfaction survey and commitment to change statement) were also administered to the control group participants (n = 45) over the same 4-day period. At 9 months, both control (n = 45) and intervention (n = 103) participants were asked to complete a final set of follow-up surveys including the confidence and competence in compassion questionnaires; the burnout/professional fulfillment survey; and the workshop satisfaction survey which was only administered to the intervention group. Additionally, a purposive sample of 21 participants from the intervention arm—selected to ensure diverse representation in ethnicity, gender, discipline, years of experience—participated in qualitative interviews at 9 months post-training to further explore the impact, integration, challenges and sustainability of the program over time, which is reported in a separate manuscript [38]. Qualitative participants received a $25 gift card for their participation in the study.

Measures

Healthcare provider measures

The primary aim of feasibility studies is to identify whether the research protocol requires adaptation before expanding it as a larger clinical trial, by assessing the outcomes of interest, recruitment strategies, data collection procedures, intervention adherence, attrition rates, data completion rates, effect size estimates, and the estimated sample size for a larger clinical trial [29–31]. A secondary aim of feasibility studies is to assess the initial impact of the intervention on clinical outcomes.

In order to assess the impact of the program on learner outcomes, we measured learners’ compassion confidence and competence, professional fulfillment and burnout, satisfaction, and demographic variables. The Sinclair Compassion Questionnaire Healthcare Provider Competency Self-Assessment (SCQ-HCPCSA) is a 15-item validated measure (original scale α = 0.94) assessing the degree to which HCPs feel they possess the necessary knowledge, judgment, and skills to provide compassion. Utilizing a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all competent) to 5 (completely competent) [9], a mean of all items is computed, with higher scores indicating greater competence (current sample α = 0.93–0.96). To determine whether there was a difference between clinical competence and personal confidence [39], a measure of compassion confidence–assessing individuals’ self-assurance in providing compassion arising from an appreciation of one’s abilities and qualities–was created for this study. The Sinclair Compassion Questionnaires Confidence Self-Assessment (SCQ-HCPCfSA), utilizes the same 15 questions as the SCQ-HCPCSA, but differs in the preamble (Please rate your confidence in the following aspects of compassion) and the responses of the 5-point Likert scale which ranges from 1 (not at all confident) to 5 (very confident), with a mean of all items being computed, with higher scores indicating greater confidence (current sample α = 0.95–0.98). The Professional Fulfillment Index (PFI) is a validated 16-item measure comprised of two scales assessing HCPs professional fulfillment and burnout. Professional Fulfillment is measured on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (not at all true) to 4 (completely true) (current sample α = 0.86–0.89), while burnout is measured from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely) (current sample α = 0.92–0.93) [40]. A mean of all items in each respective scale is computed, with higher scores in each corresponding scale indicating greater professional fulfillment or greater burnout. Learner satisfaction with the program was assessed using a 15-item survey. Twelve items assessed satisfaction with the content and facilitators using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Not Satisfied and 5 = Very Satisfied), while three items assessed whether the workshop met the stated learning objectives, increased personal knowledge, and enhanced skills related to compassion, using a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) (current sample α = 0.90–0.98).

Patient measures

To assess the impact of the compassion training program on patients, we collected patients’ de-identified SCQ scores on a monthly basis from 3 months prior to the training intervention up to 9 months after the intervention (12 months total). The SCQ is a valid and reliable 15-item patient reported compassion measure [6, 25] that assesses patients experience of compassion from their healthcare team with items mapping onto each of the domains of compassion [1, 25]. It has been validated in long term care and hospice (original scale α = 0.96) [25] and in other patient populations and languages [2, 3, 41–44].

Analytic approach

Due to the anonymous nature of our patient data, we could not nest individual patients within HCPs or determine whether patients completed the SCQ at a single time point or across multiple time points. Thus, HCP and patient data were analyzed separately and not linked. An ANOVA approach was adopted as a preliminary test of efficacy in this feasibility study. We explored whether mixed model approaches would be warranted (i.e., by examining intraclass correlation coefficients) which they largely were not, save one exception. There was evidence in the HCP data of clustering by participant over time, demonstrating justification to test mixed models whereby observations were nested within participants. The results of these models were identical to those obtained via ANOVA, as reported below.

Patient and public involvement statement

A patient and family member advisory group, were involved across several stages of the trial, including the design and evaluation of the curriculum and learners resource kit, recruitment materials, and conduct of the trial. We intentionally partnered with patients and family members who had significant interactions with the healthcare system and had first-hand experience related to the importance of compassion. Involvement included, but was not limited to, presenting the draft curriculum to them, receiving feedback, and modifying the program. We also partnered with them to assess patient burden, modifying the data collection timepoints accordingly.

Results

Healthcare provider results

Feasibility

Of the 163 HCPs eligible, 163 consented to participate and completed baseline surveys. This 100% recruitment rate exceeded the target proportion of 80%. One hundred and three of the participants took part in the intervention, including the post-intervention survey. The post-intervention surveys (excluding the workshop satisfaction survey) were successfully administered to 45 control participants. In total, 148 participants completed the post-intervention survey, reflecting an attrition rate < 10% between the baseline survey and training. A total of 134 of the 148 participants completed 9-month post-intervention surveys, reflecting an adherence rate of 91%.

The proportion of missing data per item was between 0% − 3.4% for the baseline survey, 0% − 1.4% for the post-intervention survey, and 0% − 0.7% for the 9-month post-intervention survey. This met the targets of less 10% of data missing on any scale item. The proportion of participants skipping any one item was at maximum 6.1% for the baseline survey, 2% for the post-intervention survey, and 1.5% for the 9-month follow up survey. This was well under the target of < 10% of participants skipping any one scale item. The survey contained an open-ended question, asking participants to share any concerns about the training program (e.g., content, delivery, clinical relevance, etc.). Participants reported no concerns about the intervention, well under the target of < 10%. We created a filter whereby only those who completed the post-intervention survey as well as those with complete data for compassion confidence and competence (the primary outcomes of interest) were retained for subsequent analyses (N = 125)1.

Impact of the intervention

Learner satisfaction

Immediately after the workshop, participants reported high satisfaction with the workshop (M = 4.92, SD = 0.24). Satisfaction remained high 9 months later (M = 4.56, SD = 0.65), albeit lower than immediately post-workshop, t (82) = 4.78, p < 0.001, d = 0.522. A 2 (immediately versus 9 months post-intervention, within-subjects factor) X 2 (long term care versus hospice, between-subjects factor) mixed ANOVA to examine potential main effects or interactions with care setting identified no such effects, ps > 0.178.

Professional fulfillment

A 2 (pre-intervention versus 9 months post-intervention, within-subjects factor) X 2 (intervention versus control, between-subjects factor) X 2 (long term care versus hospice, between-subjects factor) mixed ANOVA was conducted to examine potential differences in professional fulfillment as a function of time, intervention group, or care setting, and interactions between these factors. There were no main effects of time, intervention group, or care setting. There was one interaction between time and intervention group F (1, 121) = 6.51, p = 0.012, ηp2 = 0.05, with follow-up analyses revealing that professional fulfillment was significantly higher post- (M = 3.21, SD = 0.12) versus pre- (M = 2.95, SD = 0.08) intervention, in the intervention group (p = 0.004), but not in the control group, p = 0.285.

Burnout

A 2 (pre-intervention versus 9 months post-intervention, within-subjects factor) X 2 (intervention versus control, between-subjects factor) X 2 (long term care versus hospice, between-subjects factor) mixed ANOVA to examine potential differences in burnout as a function of time, intervention group, or care setting, as well as any interactions between these factors identified no main effects or interactions, ps > 0.208.

Compassion confidence

A 3 (pre-intervention versus immediately post-intervention versus 9 months post-intervention, within-subjects factor) X 2 (intervention versus control, between-subjects factor) X 2 (long term care versus hospice, between-subjects factor) mixed ANOVA was conducted to examine potential differences in compassion confidence as a function of time, intervention group, or care setting, as well as any interactions between these factors. A main effect of time was present, F (2, 242) = 8.62, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.067 as well as a quadratic3 effect of time, F (1, 121) = 7.23, p = 0.008, ηp2 = 0.056. These effects were qualified by an interaction between time and intervention group, F (2, 242) = 4.05, p = 0.019, ηp2 = 0.032, and a quadratic interaction between time and intervention group, F (1, 121) = 9.19, p = 0.003, ηp2 = 0.071. There was also a quadratic interaction between time and care setting, F (1, 121) = 4.13, p = 0.044, ηp2 = 0.033, and an interaction between intervention group and care setting, F (1, 121) = 7.20, p = 0.008, ηp2 = 0.056. No additional main effects or interactions were significant.

Follow-up analyses revealed that in the intervention group compassion confidence was higher immediately post-intervention (M = 4.81, SD = 0.31) than pre-intervention (M = 4.46, SD = 0.55), d = 0.76, but lower at 9 months post-intervention (M = 4.65, SD = 0.55) than immediately post-intervention (M = 4.81), d = 0.30. Compassion confidence at 9 months post-intervention was higher than pre-intervention compassion confidence (M = 4.46), d = 0.35, ps < 0.003 one-tailed (0.007 two-tailed). These differences were only present in the intervention group and not in the control group (Fig. 2).

Examining care setting, follow-up analyses revealed that for participants working in hospice settings, the same overall pattern reported above was present namely, that in the intervention group, compassion confidence was higher immediately post-intervention (M = 4.76, SD = 0.36) than pre-intervention (M = 4.34, SD = 0.60), d = 0.82. Compassion confidence at 9 months post-intervention was lower than immediately post-intervention, d = 0.33, but higher than pre-intervention (M = 4.57, SD = 0.60), d = 0.25, ps < 0.039 (one-tailed). These differences were not present for those in the control group.

For long term care settings, in the intervention group compassion confidence was higher immediately post-intervention (M = 4.87, SD = 0.20) than pre-intervention (M = 4.62, SD = 0.43), d = 0.68, p < 0.001, but compassion confidence at 9 months post-intervention (M = 4.76, SD = 0.45) was not significantly different from immediately post-intervention or pre-intervention ps > 0.05. For those in the control group, compassion confidence was not significantly different from pre-intervention (M = 4.52, SD = 0.50) to immediately post-intervention (M = 4.54, SD = 0.56), ps > 0.426, but was higher at 9 months post-intervention (M = 4.73, SD = 0.43) than at both of the other timepoints, ds = 0.36–0.37, ps < 0.036 (one-tailed).

Compassion competence

A 3 (pre-intervention versus immediately post-intervention versus 9 months post-intervention, within-subjects factor) X 2 (intervention versus control, between-subjects factor) X 2 (long term care versus hospice, between-subjects factor) mixed ANOVA examining potential differences in compassion competence as a function of time, intervention group, or care setting, as well as any interactions between these factors identified no significant main effects or interactions. Despite this, we opted to examine mean differences in compassion competence over time as a function of intervention group, to explore whether a similar pattern to that observed for confidence—albeit below the threshold of a statistically significant interaction —was present. One difference was observed in the intervention group whereby competence was higher immediately post-intervention (M = 4.71, SD = 0.36) versus pre-intervention (M = 4.62, SD = 0.47), t (82) = 1.85, p = 0.027, d = 0.21. In the intervention group, competence at 9 months post-intervention (M = 4.68, SD = 0.50) was not significantly different from pre-intervention or immediately post-intervention. There were no mean differences observed in the control group. Overall, this is a similar but weaker pattern to that observed for compassion confidence, given the lack of a significant interaction.

Sample size estimate for a full RCT

Based on the effect sizes observed in the current study, several a priori power analyses were conducted using G*power [45] to estimate the required sample size for a full RCT. Employing the effect sizes for the interaction between time and intervention group (linear ηp2 = 0.032 and quadratic ηp2 = 0.071 converted to Cohen’s f = 0.18–0.28) for compassion confidence for a repeated measures ANOVA including a within-between factors interaction with alpha = 0.05, power = 0.95, 2 groups (i.e., intervention and control), and 3 measurements (i.e., pre-intervention, immediately post-intervention, and 9 months post-intervention), a sample size in the range of 58 to 136 would be required for an RCT. A sample size within a similar range (N = 84) would be required for the same analysis when employing the effect size observed for the time by intervention group interaction observed for professional fulfillment (ηp2 = 0.032, converted to Cohen’s f = 0.23).

Patient results

Of the 724 participants who were deemed eligible to complete the SCQ by organizational staff members, 634 participants completed the questionnaire (87.5%). The proportion of missing data per item of the SCQ was between 0.3 and 3.8%, with a lower proportion of missing data on the first 5 items, presumably due to participant fatigue. Nonetheless, this was well below the target: <10% of data missing on any scale item, and is consistent with previous patient research using the SCQ [25]. The proportion of patients who skipped any one item on the SCQ was 13.1% (n = 83 participants). This was slightly beyond the target of < 10% of patients skipping any one scale item. We created a filter whereby only those with complete data were retained for subsequent analyses (final n = 551 [intervention n = 338, control n = 213, long term care n = 387 (control n = 153, intervention n = 234), hospice n = 164 (control n = 61, intervention n = 103)]).

Impact of the intervention

Mean patient SCQ scores were computed with the measure demonstrating strong internal consistency (α = 0.97). A 2 (intervention versus control) X 2 (long term care versus hospice) X 2 (pre- versus post-intervention) univariate ANOVA was conducted to examine potential differences in patient reported compassion as a function of intervention group, care setting, or time, as well as any interactions between these factors. The only difference observed was that greater compassion was reported in hospice (M = 4.63) versus long term care (M = 3.48), F (1, 543) = 135.63, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.20, representing a large effect size [46, 47].

Given that there were multiple means by which to conceptualize time, this analysis was repeated, conceptualizing time as month of data collection. That is, we examined potential differences in patient reported compassion as a function of data collection month, as well as any potential interaction of this with intervention group or care setting (i.e., a 2 × 2 × 12 ANOVA). Although this analysis revealed no main effects or interactions beyond the already observed main effect of care setting, a pattern was observed whereby compassion appeared to differ between intervention and control groups in month 5 (i.e., the month immediately following the training intervention). To further explore this observation, patient SCQ scores were compared separately for each month of data collection. As shown in Table 3, patient reported experiences of compassion were significantly different at month 5; p = 0.048, representing a medium effect size [46], Cohen’s d = 0.58.

Discussion

The current study demonstrates that the EnACT Program is feasible, with initial results suggesting that it has a positive impact on both HCP learners and their patients. In terms of feasibility results, all targets (See Table 1) were met, apart from the proportion of patients who skipped any one item on the SCQ which was slightly higher than the 10% target (13.1%). In terms of learner outcomes, HCP learner satisfaction after the training day was high (M = 4.92/5) and was sustained 9 months after training (M = 4.56/5). Historically, learner satisfaction within healthcare education [48, 49], including for compassion training programs [4], has a relatively short duration, with initial high levels of satisfaction post-intervention rapidly dissipating thereafter. Although it is difficult to determine the exact reasons why EnACT learner satisfaction was sustained over time, the qualitative results from a smaller subset (n = 21) of the current sample identified a number of additional factors that participants felt contributed to their ongoing satisfaction [38]. These included but were not limited to the learner resources (compassion toolkit), the applicability and relevance of learnings to participants’ clinical practice, and the involvement of senior leaders in the integration, spread, and evaluation (e.g., adopting the SCQ as standard clinical measure) of the program.

Recognizing both the limitations of existing compassion training programs and the inherent limitations of learner satisfaction scores in general (i.e., subjectivity, variability, and weak associations to retention and learner outcomes) [4, 50, 51], we also assessed additional learner outcomes over time. In terms of workplace wellbeing, participants in the intervention group reported significantly higher and sustained (9 months post-intervention) levels of professional fulfillment in comparison to the control group. In addition to replicating the results of our previous pilot study [21], these expected results add to recent evidence challenging the notion of compassion fatigue that claims that compassion is a finite resource and that providing compassionate care makes individuals more susceptible to burnout and work-related stress [11, 52]. In fact, our results suggest that compassion training and importantly, the integration of learnings into professional practice over a 9-month period, may actually enhance professional fulfillment and buffer against burnout, which needs to be verified through additional research.

Of note, the EnACT program positively impacted both learners’ knowledge and skills related to compassion, and their patients’ experiences of compassion, with statistically significant effects observed at one-month post training—those individuals who arguably are the ultimate beneficiaries and determinants of the success of compassion training in healthcare. Although we anticipated a positive trend in patients’ experiences of compassion, patients of EnACT trained HCPs in fact reported a meaningful improvement at that time point. This improvement coincided with a statistically significant increase in HCP confidence in providing compassionate care, in comparison with the control group –results that persisted, albeit slightly lower, 9 months post training. Collectively these results suggest that the knowledge and skills disseminated within the training program were retained over time and integrated into learners' care. Although these results are promising, as knowledge and skills decay over time, additional learning strategies and resources post-training would nonetheless be beneficial in further bolstering retention and sustaining patient compassion ratings over time. As reported elsewhere [38], a subset of learners from this study suggested offering a refresher course or ‘lunch and learn’ six months post-training to re-instill learnings; establishing a ‘compassionate community of practice’ to sustain and contextualize learnings; and identifying a ‘compassion champion’ within each learning cohort to co-facilitate training sessions and operationalize learnings into clinical and organizational practice thereafter. Additional strategies to further bolster learnings over time could include making optional components of the program, such as embedding compassionate care plans in patient rounds and the compassion journal, a formal requirement.

Collectively, the feasibility results and impact on learner and patient outcomes suggest that further validation of the training program through a full RCT is warranted. While trials on compassion training are lacking, a meta-analysis of 13 RCTs investigating whether the patient-clinician relationship has a beneficial effect on healthcare outcomes have reported small-to-moderate sizes (d = 0.23–0.66) [53], consistent with those reported here. Power analyses revealed that for the next stage of testing EnACT, a sample size in the range of 58–136 would be required, with the lower end of the range sufficient to detect larger effects and the upper end of the range being sufficient for small-to-medium effects. It is our recommendation that an RCT be conducted with a final target sample size of 150, oversampling by at least 10% (i.e. n = 150 vs. 136) in order to account for attrition. In light of our low rate of HCP learner attrition across each study timepoint and to assist with recruitment, we highly recommend that researchers conducting similar education trials within healthcare settings proactively engage site leaders prior to the study to generate enthusiasm, ensure buy-in, and strategize on how to maximize enrollment and retention. For example, while our high recruitment rate for the training program was due in part to learner interest and the importance of the topic, it was likely equally attributable to our recruitment strategy and pragmatic approach, including having all site leaders suggest and agree to paid time for staff to attend the training, and in doing so fulfilling a component (staff education) of their site annual accreditation requirements [35, 36]. This approach affirms the results of recent reviews that report that effective organizational culture change requires inclusive participation and leadership across all levels [54, 55].

Strengths and limitations

A novel aspect of this study was the collection of patient-reported experiences of compassion, which to our knowledge is the first compassion training study that included reports from the recipients of compassion (patients). The results of this study demonstrated that a brief (1-day) compassion training program had a statistically significant impact on patient experiences of compassion. Although these effects were most evident in the month following training, this period represents a critical window of impact and an encouraging indication of the program’s potential to enhance compassionate care. Additional work is needed to identify how to best sustain these results over time. Importantly, we recommend that future compassion training and other educational interventions aimed at enhancing patient care (e.g., clinical communication, person centered care, cultural sensitivity training, etc.), include patient reported outcome measures as a standard component. In addition to including the ultimate and intended target population of these educational interventions, including patient reported outcomes addresses a persistent limitation in compassion training programs—an over reliance on learner self-assessments that measure learners’ compassionate intentions and feelings toward others without determining whether learnings actually translate to practice. Inclusion of a learner needs assessment, conducting a previous pilot study, and integrating feedback from learners and senior leaders prior, during, and after the trial were essential factors in successful implementation.

Finally, in addition to assessing patients’ experiences of compassion, we also evaluated healthcare providers’ competence and confidence in their compassionate knowledge, skills, and judgement (SCQ-HCPCfSA), to capture both recipients and providers perspectives. We recommend that future RCTs also include measures of self-reported compassionate behaviour to determine the extent to which increased confidence in compassion translate into compassionate action. This would help further elucidate whether provider confidence in their compassionate abilities is reflected in their clinical behaviours, or whether it fails to cross what we have termed the ‘heart-hand barrier’—a conceptual threshold, akin to the blood-brain barrier, that separates compassionate intention and compassionate behaviour. Exploring this concept is particularly important in light of emerging research suggesting that higher self-assessed confidence in compassion may, paradoxically, mask or exacerbate deficits in compassionate behaviour [21, 56]. Another direction for future work is to examine the potential influence of gaining experience more generally or other emotional skills one may be obtaining (including outside of work), especially in light of the pattern in the control group, whereby compassion confidence did not change from pre-to-post intervention, but was higher at the 9-month assessment.

We also recommend that future research address some of the limitations of our patient sample – as in order to protect anonymity, we were unable to nest individual patients within healthcare provider learners or track individual patient experiences over time. Because we used de-identified patient data, we could not determine whether patients completed the SCQ at a single time point or across multiple time points. For these reasons we recommend obtaining patient consent to collect identifiable data in future studies, which would allow tracking individual compassion scores over time and to confirm unique participants. Additionally, patient completion rates did fluctuate from month to month which seemed to be due to a number of factors (acuity, death, fatigue, off-unit tests or appointments, etc.), that we unfortunately were unable to track and analyze for the aforementioned reasons. Another limitation of the study was that most learners/staff were primarily from the discipline of nursing, which while being representative of the composition of typical care teams in each setting, would benefit from a wider range of disciplinary representation in a future trial. Specifically, in light of our findings, further research on physician learners would be beneficial to better determine the effectiveness of the training program within this group and to explore potential barriers to participation, such as limited time, interest, or patient caseload. In our study, we suspect that physician enrollment was influenced by their employment status—namely that physicians were contracted service providers rather than employees of the organization and therefore were not provided paid release time to attend the training. In addition, the training program was not accredited as a continuing medical education (CME) activity at the time of the study, which likely was a further contributor. This limitation has since been addressed, as the EnACT program is now accredited with the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada, with credits being fully transferrable to the American Medical Association, which may enhance physician engagement in future trials. With these limitations addressed, we speculate that stronger differences would be observed among patients.

Conclusion

Healthcare providers are increasingly expected and mandated to provide compassion to their patients. Most healthcare providers desire to, and benefit from, delivering compassion to the patients and families they serve, but are given little support and training on providing this reputedly vital component of quality care. The EnACT program has begun to address these limitations and the gaps of existing compassion training programs, holding promise for improving compassion on a personal, clinical, and organizational level.
