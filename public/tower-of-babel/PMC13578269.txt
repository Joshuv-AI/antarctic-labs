The genomic footprint of myna invasion in Oman showcases desert isolation and urban connectivity
Authors: Qais Al Rawahi, Abdullahi Aliyu, Mazen M. Al-Obaidi, Karim Khalil, Masooma Al-Lawati, Tahani A. Al-Jidaili, Basel S. Al-Maskari, Ahmed M. Al-Shakili, Jihad A. Al-Toubi, Maisaa S. Al-Saadi, Adamu Abdul Abubakar, Jose L. Mijangos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Invasive species threaten global biodiversity, often adapting rapidly to new environments while outcompeting native species. The common myna (Acridotheres tristis) exemplifies this challenge in the Sultanate of Oman, where its aggressive behaviour and adaptability have displaced native bird species and disrupted ecosystems. Despite increasing concern, the genetic structure of this invasion remains unclear. Here we show, using 17,460 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from 311 common mynas across four governorates, that this invasive species forms two distinct genetic clusters: one comprising Dhofar individuals isolated by desert expanses, and another uniting populations from Muscat, North Batinah, and South Sharqiyah with notable substructure. We find that North Batinah harbours a substantially larger effective population size than the other governorates, suggesting it may be an initial invasion source and a critical target for management. Our findings clarify the routes and scale of common myna dispersal in Oman, highlighting how localised control in Dhofar may be feasible. In contrast, an integrated approach is needed for the closely connected northern governorates. This study underscores the power of genomic monitoring to inform targeted eradication efforts and managing invasive bird populations.

Introduction

Native to South Asia, the common myna (Acridotheres tristis) is recognised globally as one of the most invasive bird species owing to its remarkable adaptability to anthropogenic landscapes1. In the Sultanate of Oman, common mynas have become widespread in urban and agricultural areas, where they exhibit aggressive behaviours that displace local species and compromise native trophic interactions2,3.

Evidence from Seychelles further indicates that mynas can interfere with breeding attempts, damage eggs and chicks during nest-site competition, attack adult endemic birds at nests, and may reduce the reproductive performance of native species4.

Their dietary flexibility and ability to exploit human food wastes have facilitated rapid population expansion. In addition to ecological repercussions, common mynas pose risks to human health by acting as vectors for diseases and causing agricultural losses through crop damage5.

The invasion history of common mynas in Oman dates back to the early 1980s6; since then, populations have expanded considerably, with the highest densities observed in urbanised northern regions but increasingly established populations in the southern Dhofar governorate. The geographic features of Oman, particularly the central desert expanses separating the northern governorates from Dhofar, potentially create natural barriers to dispersal, suggesting population connectivity may be complex and regionally variable.

Management efforts to eradicate invasive avian species are often implemented only after invasions become established, typically relying on culling without considering population dynamics or the potential for recolonisation. A robust understanding of the genetic structure of invasive populations is essential for developing effective, long-term control strategies7. Genetic analyses can uncover introduction pathways and identify source populations8 and have informed successful eradication programs by highlighting founder populations9 and likely dispersal corridors10. High-resolution markers, such as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), can detect subtle genetic structure11, which is especially useful in recent invasions. More broadly, population genetic approaches provide insights into invasion history, connectivity, and adaptive potential, which are vital for designing targeted and sustainable management strategies12.

Despite growing concern over their proliferation, the genetic structure of the common myna invasion in Oman, and consequently the most effective strategies for its management, remains poorly understood. Here, we address this gap by analysing 311 common myna individuals collected from four governorates in Oman (Muscat, Dhofar, A’Sharqiyah, and Al Batinah; (Fig. 1 and Table 1)) using single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Our goals are twofold: first, to determine the genetic structure and diversity underlying this invasion, and second, to inform evidence-based management policies that can mitigate the ecological and socioeconomic impacts caused by this avian invader.

Results

Sequencing provider DArT (Canberra) successfully genotyped 51,316 SNPs in 311 individuals. After filtering, the common myna dataset comprised 17,460 SNPs genotyped in 296 individuals, with 15 individuals excluded due to a call rate below 0.6.

Genetic structure

PCA results (Fig. 2a and b) indicate the presence of two distinct groups. The first principal component (i.e. PC1) explained 3.7% of the variance in the data and separated Dhofar from the other governorates. PC2 explained 1.7% of the variance and shows that North Batinah, Muscat and most of South Sharqiyah individuals overlap, and the remaining South Sharqiyah individuals form a distinct cluster. PC3 explained 1.1% of the variation and provides similar information as PC2. Three-dimensional visualisation of the first three principal components (Supplemental File S1) shows that the North Batinah cluster overlaps with Muscat but not with South Sharqiyah. The lowest variation is observed in Dhofar, while the highest is among South Sharqiyah individuals (Table 1).

The Bayesian clustering analysis using fastSTRUCTURE13 inferred that the optimal number of populations (K) best explaining the genetic structure of common mynas from Oman (Supplemental Fig. S1) dataset was two, as indicated by the highest mean marginal likelihood values at K=2 across ten replications.

fastSTRUCTURE results (Fig. 3) revealed a clear division, with individuals from Dhofar representing one distinct population and individuals from other governorates forming the other. No admixture was observed in any individual from Dhofar or South Sharqiyah, while individuals from North Batinah, especially those from Muscat, exhibited minor levels of admixture.

Genetic diversity within governorates

Analysis of genetic diversity parameters across the four governorates revealed relatively homogeneous levels of genetic variation throughout the sampled range (Fig. 5 and Supplemental Table S1). Observed heterozygosity (Ho) values ranged from 0.0900 to 0.0938, with unbiased expected heterozygosity (uHe) between 0.1152 and 0.1105. Inbreeding coefficients (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{IS}$$\end{document}) were consistently positive across all populations (0.1704-0.1564), suggesting a moderate level of inbreeding within the established invasive populations.

Inference of effective population size

Effective population size (Ne) was much higher in North Batinah (Ne = 1,973; CI 1,238–4,774), standing at an order of magnitude greater than in the other governorates, where Ne values ranged from 165 to 300 (Table 3).

Dispersal patterns

Spatial autocorrelation reveals positive autocorrelation among individuals separated by up to ~400 km and negative autocorrelation at larger distances (Fig. 6). The first x-intercept (~400 km) estimates the spatial extent of detectable positive genetic structure14; the transition into negative r at greater distances is consistent with restricted gene flow under both isolation by distance and the desert-mediated dispersal barrier between Dhofar and the northern governorates.

Genome-wide association studies

Genome-wide association (GWAS) analyses identified significant autosomal loci for six of the eight morphological traits assessed in common mynas from Oman (Supplementary Fig. S2). Z-linked markers were excluded prior to analysis using gl.drop.sexlinked15 to avoid biases from female hemizygosity. Body width showed the largest number of strong signals, with peaks on chromosomes 1 (-log10(p) = 56), 29 (52), 11 (48), 7 (28), 30 (16), 13 (14) and 22 (14; Supplementary Fig. S2b). Body length had a single major peak on chromosome 29 (-log10(p) = 41) and a secondary peak on chromosome 22 (8.5; Supplementary Fig. S2h). Tarsus length showed two highly significant peaks on chromosome 22 (-log10(p) = 26 and 20) and a third on chromosome 12 (12; Supplementary Fig. S2e). For weight, a significant signal was detected on chromosome 22 (-log10(p) = 9.9; Supplementary Fig. S2a). For wingspan, significant signals were detected on chromosome 1 (8.3) and chromosome 22 (7.8; Supplementary Fig. S2f). For beak length, a single significant signal was detected on chromosome 1 (-log10(p) = 8.2; Supplementary Fig. S2g). No autosomal signal exceeded the genome-wide significance threshold (-log10(p) = 7.3) for wing length or body circumference (Supplementary Fig. S2c and d).

Discussion

Our study provides insights into the genetic structure and invasion dynamics of the common myna in Oman, revealing patterns with implications for invasive species management. Genetic analyses revealed two distinct population structures across the sampled governorates. The first comprises an isolated population in Dhofar, separated from northern regions by extensive desert barriers that effectively limit gene flow. The second consists of a metapopulation spanning the northern governorates of Muscat, North Batinah, and South Sharqiyah, which are interconnected through continuous urban corridors.

Interestingly, while these northern governorates exhibit substantial connectivity, our principal component analysis (PCA; Fig. 2) revealed a more complex structure than expected. Rather than forming a homogeneous cluster, individuals from North Batinah and South Sharqiyah formed discrete genetic clusters, with Muscat individuals occupying an intermediate position. This pattern suggests that despite urban continuity, subtle barriers to gene flow exist between these regions, which could be associated with landscape features, dispersal limitations, or human-mediated movement patterns.

This pattern is consistent with the native-range structure reported by Atsawawaranunt et al.16. In India, most native common myna populations showed low genetic differentiation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{ST}$$\end{document} = 0.001–0.03), whereas the distinct Maharashtra subpopulation A showed more moderate differentiation from other Indian populations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{ST}$$\end{document} = 0.042–0.073), suggesting that strong genetic structure can arise from low connectivity or distinct demographic histories. In Oman, the low \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{ST}$$\end{document} values among Muscat, North Batinah, and South Sharqiyah (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{ST}$$\end{document} = 0.011–0.021) similarly suggest relatively high connectivity across the northern urban network, whereas the higher divergence between Dhofar and the northern governorates (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{ST}$$\end{document} = 0.060–0.073) falls within the upper range reported for the more distinct population in India.

An alternative explanation is that the Dhofar population might have resulted from a separate introduction event rather than from divergence that arose only after colonisation. For instance, Atsawawaranunt et al.16 showed that introduced common myna populations can originate from different source populations. However, our data cannot distinguish between these possibilities conclusively, this hypothesis should be explored in future studies with broader sampling.

In the same study, Atsawawaranunt et al.16 identified the native range in India as the source of myna invasions in Australia, Fiji, Hawaii, South Africa and New Zealand. Within New Zealand, they also recovered genetic structure consistent with mountain ranges acting as dispersal barriers. The split we observe between Dhofar and the northern governorates mirrors that finding at a different geographic scale, with central desert expanses acting as a dispersal barrier. The positive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{IS}$$\end{document} values (0.16–0.17) across all four governorates are of a magnitude commonly reported in recently established invasive bird populations, where similar elevations have been documented in house sparrows across multiple invaded ranges17.

The route and origin of the Oman invasion are harder to resolve from these data. The earliest published records for the Arabian Peninsula place the species in the Gulf states by the late 1970s and early 1980s, with introductions to the United Arab Emirates attributed to escapees and releases from the cage-bird trade; subsequent regional reviews favour secondary spread from the Arabian Peninsula into Iraq and the Levant over independent introduction6 and references therein. The simplest hypothesis for Oman is a mix of cage-bird escapes during the urbanisation of the northern coast in the 1980s and ongoing exchange with the Gulf metapopulation. Our data cannot distinguish this from alternative scenarios such as direct introductions from South Asia via the port economies. Resolving the geographic origin of the Oman invasion will require comparisons with samples from the native range in South Asia and from neighbouring invaded populations, particularly in the UAE and Saudi Arabia.

The Ne estimate for North Batinah (Ne = 1,919) was an order of magnitude larger than for the other governorates (Table 3). Several factors could produce this pattern. Repeated introductions could be plausible given its proximity to the United Arab Emirates where the common myna is also established. Ongoing immigration from unsampled populations is known to bias LD-based Ne estimates upward18. The extensive urban and agricultural matrix of North Batinah probably supports a larger carrying capacity than the more restricted habitats in other governorates. We note that across the PCA, fastSTRUCTURE and genomic relationship-matrix results, the three northern governorates form discrete groupings rather than Muscat and South Sharqiyah being nested within the variation of North Batinah, which is the pattern we would expect under a simple sole-source scenario. We therefore interpret the high Ne as a signal of contemporary demographic dynamics and not as direct evidence of a single invasion origin19.

Our genome-wide association (GWAS) analyses identified autosomal loci associated with morphological traits (Supplementary Fig. S2). Because our DArTseq panel samples only a small fraction of the genome, the exact causal SNPs or variants are unlikely to have been sequenced20. These associations should therefore be interpreted as signals arising from linked regions rather than as confirmed causal variants. Confirming and refining these signals will require denser sequencing around the identified SNPs.

Note that our conclusions should be interpreted with caution because departures from strict neutrality, fluctuating selection, linkage, and other sources of model misspecification may influence patterns of molecular variation and divergence.

The genetic structure revealed by our analyses requires differentiated management approaches across Oman. For the northern metapopulation, the substantial gene flow among governorates indicates that isolated control efforts in any single region would likely be undermined by recolonisation from adjacent areas. Therefore, an integrated management approach spanning all three northern governorates is essential, with particular emphasis on North Batinah, given its large effective population size which sustains the greatest reproductive output in the northern metapopulation. In contrast, the genetic isolation of the Dhofar population facilitates more localised control strategies, focusing on both population reduction and maintaining its isolation from northern populations.

Effective management should incorporate ongoing genetic monitoring to detect changes in population size, genetic structure, and migration patterns, enabling timely assessment of intervention efficacy. Physical interventions should be complemented by habitat modification to limit access to anthropogenic resources that facilitate myna population growth21. Additionally, regulatory frameworks should address intentional feeding, pet trade restrictions, and quarantine measures to prevent further introductions or spread.

Long-term success in controlling common myna populations in Oman will depend on sustained collaboration among government agencies, research institutions, and local communities, with emphasis on data sharing, early detection systems, and public engagement. Furthermore, regional cooperation with neighbouring countries is crucial to prevent reintroduction and coordinate cross-border management efforts22.

Our findings highlight the importance of understanding genetic structure when designing invasive species management programs and demonstrate how genetic analyses can inform tailored, evidence-based approaches to biological invasions. Future research should focus on temporal genetic monitoring, functional analysis of adaptive loci, and comprehensive assessment of ecological impacts to refine management strategies and protect Oman’s native biodiversity.

Methods

Fieldwork

This study was reviewed and approved by the Environment Authority of Oman (approval CP/ENV/ASU/2023/01), with approval granted prior to the commencement of any fieldwork. All procedures complied with relevant guidelines and regulations, and reporting follows the ARRIVE guidelines where applicable. Fieldwork and sampling formed part of the project “Phenotypical and Genotypical Characterization of Invasive Birds: common myna and Indian crow in the Sultanate of Oman”, conducted by A’Sharqiyah University and funded by the Environment Authority of Oman (Agreement No. 39-2022/3/6121, signed 24 December 2023). Sampling locations were chosen to ensure geographic representation across the Sultanate of Oman. The selected sample locations included four governorates: Muscat, Dhofar, A’Sharqiyah, and Al Batinah (Fig. 1). A proportional distribution approach was used to collect samples to ensure each region contributed a similar number of individuals. A total of 311 Individuals were captured across the four governorates (Table 1) using standard non-lethal methods and humanely euthanised upon procurement23.

Each bird’s morphological measurements were recorded to assess body structure and size. Weight was measured using a digital balance, while body width and circumference were determined using callipers and a plastic sash, respectively. Wing length was measured from the flexural line to the longest primary feather, and wingspan was recorded as the full extension between wing tips. Tarsus length was taken from the tibiotarsal articulation to the toe base. Tail length was measured from its base to the longest feather tip, while body length was recorded from the beak to the tail’s extremity. Beak length was also documented.

Laboratory work

Genomic DNA was extracted by Diversity Arrays Technologies (DArT Pty Ltd, Canberra, Australia, www.diversityarrays.com) using a NucleoMag 96 Tissue Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) on a Freedom Evo automated liquid handler (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland). Samples were then genotyped using DArTseq™ (DArT Pty Ltd), a technique combining genome complexity reduction by restriction enzymes, implicit fragment size selection, and next-generation sequencing to generate thousands of SNP markers across the genome24. Sequence data were processed via DArT’s proprietary analytical pipelines24, as detailed in Georges et al.25. Reads were subsequently aligned to the latest common myna reference genome (GenBank assembly accession: GCA_037013685.1) using BLAST26 as implemented in the gl.blast function in the R package dartR27.

Individuals and SNP filtering

SNP filtering was performed using dartR27. Firstly, sex-linked markers were removed using the gl.drop.sexlinked function15. Individuals with lower than 60% of call rate, without metadata information or were duplicated were removed. To reduce genotyping errors that might have arisen during library preparation and SNP calling28, we discarded SNPs with a reproducibility of lower than 99% (RepAvg), a call rate of lower than 90% or a read depth of lower than 5. Heterozygosity (He) is generally lower for SNPs with rare alleles than SNPs with common alleles29. Therefore, we did not filter out SNPs based on their allele frequency. Data analyses

Genetic structure

To visualise the spatial distribution of genetic variation of the collected individuals, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) using dartR27. PCA is a method that does not rely on any genetic model or principle; however, spatial patterns revealed by PCA are mathematically equivalent to coalescent analysis so that they are representative of evolutionary processes such as genetic structure, gene flow, and founder effects30.

To infer the genetic ancestry proportion of each individual, we used a Bayesian clustering model implemented in the software fastSTRUCTURE13. The algorithm of fastSTRUCTURE approximates the inference of the admixture model of the software STRUCTURE31. The admixture model assumes that each sample has a certain proportion of ancestry from one or more distinct populations or sources (K). The model’s Bayesian algorithm attempts to find the number of populations or sources (K) at which population genetics parameters (i.e., Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium within populations and linkage equilibrium between loci) are maximised. In fastSTRUCTURE, we modelled the number of populations (K) from one to six, with ten replications for each. We then used the Clumpak method32 to identify sets of highly similar runs among all the replicates of the same K. The method then separates the distinct groups of runs representing distinct modes in the space of possible solutions.

Genetic differentiation between governorates was estimated using pairwise \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{ST}$$\end{document}33 using the gl.report.fstat function in dartR27.

Additionally, we estimated pairwise probabilities of identity by descent. Two or more alleles are identical by descent (IBD) if they are identical copies of the same ancestral allele in a base population. IBD was calculated using an additive relationship matrix approach developed by Endelman34 as implemented in the R packages dartR27 and rrBLUP35. This additive relationship matrix is a theoretical framework for estimating a relationship matrix consistent with an approach to estimate the probability that the alleles at a random locus are identical in state. Genetic diversity within governorates

To assess genetic variation within governorates, we calculated observed heterozygosity (Ho), unbiased (i.e. corrected for sample size) expected heterozygosity under random mating (uHe), and the inbreeding coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{IS}$$\end{document}) using the gl.report.heterozygosity function in dartR27.

Inference of population size

We estimated effective population size (Ne) using the linkage disequilibrium (LD; i.e. non-random association of alleles at different loci) method based on NeEstimator V236 using the function gl.LDNe in dartR27.

Dispersal patterns

We conducted a multivariate spatial autocorrelation analysis to evaluate fine-scale spatial genetic structure using the function gl.spatial.autoCorr in dartR27. This approach integrates data from all polymorphic loci to assess the relationship between genetic and geographic distances among individuals. Specifically, it calculates an autocorrelation coefficient (r) for each predefined distance class, quantifying the genetic similarity between pairs of individuals separated by distances within that class37. The first x-intercept of the correlogram estimates the spatial extent of detectable positive genetic structure14: at smaller distances individuals are more genetically similar than expected under random sampling, while negative r at larger distances is the expected pattern under restricted gene flow14,37.

Genome-wide association studies

A genome-wide association analysis (GWAS) was performed for eight traits: body length, beak length, tarsus length, wing length, wingspan, body width, body circumference, and weight. The analysis was conducted using the R package GAPIT38using the BLINK model39 with two principal components included to account for population structure. For each SNP–trait association, the -log10(p) score was calculated, with higher values indicating stronger evidence of association. Manhattan plots were generated to visualise the genome-wide distribution of association signals by plotting the -log10(p) values for each SNP across the genome.
