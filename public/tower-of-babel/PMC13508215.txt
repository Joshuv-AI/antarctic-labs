Factors influencing fear of cancer recurrence in breast cancer survivors: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Weimin Liu, Juan Liu, Yali Su, Xiaoxin Ji, Junping Yu, Huaguo Zhang, Ping Lei Chui, Chong Chin Che
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeFear of cancer recurrence (FCR) is a prevalent psychological concern among breast cancer survivors, and identifying influencing factors is crucial for designing effective interventions. We aimed to investigate the relationship of FCR with sociodemographic characteristics, clinical features, physical and psychological symptoms, and social support among survivors of breast cancer.MethodsA cross-sectional study performed between November 2024, and January 2025. 387 potential participants were screened using convenience sampling. Fear of cancer recurrence, symptoms, and social support were assessed using the FCR Inventory-Short Form (FCRI-SF), the Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale (MSAS), and the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), respectively. Factors influencing FCR in survivors of breast cancer were determined using multiple linear regression.ResultsThe findings revealed that age < 40 years (B = 3.505, p = 0.012), severe medical burden (B = 5.079, p < 0.001), tumor stage III (B = 4.309, p = 0.018), cancer diagnosis time of 6–12 months (B = 2.174, p = 0.014), and chemotherapy (B = 2.077, p = 0.031) were significantly associated with higher FCR. Endocrine therapy was positively associated with FCR scores, although this association did not reach statistical significance (B = 2.300, p = 0.054). Among the psychological factors, worrying (B = 2.213, p < 0.001) and feeling nervous (B = 0.880, p = 0.006) were significantly associated with higher FCR. Among the physical factors, “I do not look like myself” (B = 0.796, p = 0.010) and lack of energy (B = 0.662, p = 0.018) were also significantly associated with higher FCR.ConclusionsFCR in this population is influenced by age, medical burden, cancer stage, duration of cancer diagnosis, treatment methods, and physical and psychological symptoms. Although social support was not significant in this study, its potential role, as reported in previous literature, warrants further attention.

Introduction

Breast cancer represents the most prevalent malignant neoplasm affecting women [1]. Owing to advancements in early diagnosis, screening, and precision treatment techniques, the average 5-year survival rate of patients with breast cancer has reached 80% [2]. However, breast cancer treatment and its associated side effects can profoundly influence both the physical and psychological health of patients. Among these, fear of cancer recurrence (FCR) is the most prevalent psychological distress among those who survive the disease [3], with approximately 34.2–64.89% of patients with cancer experiencing moderate to high levels of FCR [4, 5]. Moreover, FCR may remain stable after diagnosis [6]. Furthermore, when these individuals show significant clinical levels of FCR, they tend to excessively focus on changes in their condition and are more likely to develop negative behavioral changes [7].

FCR is defined as “fear, worry, or concern associated with the possibility that cancer relapses back or worsens [8]. Owing to the disease and its treatment, the patients often experience various physiological, psychological, and psychosocial issues [9], such as fatigue, pain, gastrointestinal symptoms, peripheral neuropathy, sleep disturbances, anxiety, and depression [10–12]. Studies have shown a correlation between FCR and both physical and psychological symptoms in patients with breast cancer [13]. In China, FCR is prevalent among survivors, which may be partly related to the uneven distribution of medical resources and differences in cancer awareness [14, 15]. Reportedly, concerns about medication-induced body damage, fear of disease progression, and anxiety about doctor visits and regular check-ups can all contribute to FCR for patients [16]. Moreover, cultural background may influence patients’ illness perceptions, coping styles, and emotional responses. In Chinese culture, cancer is often viewed as a taboo topic, which may lead to concealment, reduced emotional expression, and limited discussion with others, thereby intensifying feelings of isolation and fear. These individuals are often stigmatized, being perceived as having a bad temperament [17]. Such cultural influences may not only increase emotional distress but also discourage patients from actively seeking psychological help or social support [18], potentially exacerbating FCR. Given the diverse sociodemographic characteristics, disease duration, and unique cultural contexts in China, the ways in which FCR relates to physical and psychological symptoms may differ from those observed in other countries. However, evidence specific to Chinese breast cancer survivors remains limited. Therefore, an in-depth investigation of FCR and its influencing factors among survivors of breast cancer in China is of great practical significance for developing effective psychological intervention strategies.

The treatment of breast cancer includes surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted and endocrine therapy [19, 20]. However, no consensus exists on the impact of different treatments and cancer stages on FCR in clinical settings [21], and limited resources hinder the management of FCR. Although some studies have reported associations between FCR and psychological distress such as anxiety and depression [22], there is a lack of theory-based, integrated models that comprehensively examine the multiple factors contributing to FCR, particularly within the Chinese population. This study addresses this gap by using a theoretical framework to explore how clinical, psychological, and social variables interact to influence FCR.

Leventhal’s Self-Regulation Model [23] suggests that some patients may mistakenly interpret certain symptoms as signs of disease recurrence, thereby triggering cognitive responses related to FCR during treatment. In the Social-Cognitive Processing Model [24], it is further emphasized that an individual’s social network can either facilitate or impede cognitive processing of the cancer experience, thereby alleviating or exacerbating emotional distress. Based on these complementary frameworks, we selected sociodemographic characteristics, clinical features, physical and psychological symptoms, and social support as key domains for measurement and analysis. Considering the strengths of both frameworks, the current study integrates key concepts from these two models to construct a comprehensive theoretical framework for understanding FCR. In this study, sociodemographic characteristics may influence patients’ illness perceptions and coping strategies. In addition, disease-related clinical factors can affect symptom burden, which in turn influences the patient’s cognitive processing of recurrence risk [13]. Physiological and psychological characteristics serve as critical mediators, leading to misinterpretations of bodily sensations as signs of cancer recurrence. Finally, social support acts as either a protective or exacerbating factor that may moderate the impact of FCR [25]. By drawing on both the Self-Regulation Model and the Social-Cognitive Processing Model, this theoretical framework provides a solid foundation for understanding the mechanisms underlying FCR in breast cancer survivors.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the relationship of FCR with sociodemographic characteristics, clinical features, physical and psychological symptoms, and social support among survivors of breast cancer. Identifying the influencing factors is essential for developing multimodal interventions to mitigate the impact of FCR.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, multicenter, cross-sectional study was conducted between November 2024 and January 2025. Using convenience sampling, patients diagnosed with primary breast cancer were recruited from three major tertiary hospitals in Beijing, China: Beijing Shijitan Hospital, Capital Medical University; Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University; and Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital. In this study, cancer survivors referred to patients who have survived from the time of cancer diagnosis, during and immediately after treatment, and through the balance of their lives [26]. We included patients who were diagnosed with breast cancer according to the Breast Cancer Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines [27] with a confirmed pathological diagnosis; age ≥ 18 years; fully conscious and able to communicate independently; aware of their diagnosis and provided informed consent. We excluded those who had mental illness or cognitive impairment, had hearing or speech impairments, were diagnosed with metastatic cancer, or were suffering from severe physical illnesses or other malignant tumors requiring treatment.

The determination of the sample size was guided by the statistical criteria required for multiple regression analysis. Following Cohen’s (1988) recommendations for a small-to-medium effect size, we set ρ0² = 0 under the null hypothesis and ρ1² = 0.12 under the alternative hypothesis [28]. Assuming a power of 90% and an alpha of 0.05, the determination of the necessary sample size was conducted utilizing PASS 15.0.5 software to be 328 participants. Considering a potential 10% attrition rate, the final participant recruitment target was set at 365 participants.

Conceptual framework

Drawing on Leventhal’s Self-Regulation Model (SRM) and the Social-Cognitive Processing Model (SCPM), we organized our predictors into three theory-driven domains. Previous FCR research has drawn on Leventhal’s SRM to explain symptom interpretation, checking behaviors, and cognitive responses among cancer survivors [29], whereas the SCPM has been empirically tested to explain how inhibited disclosure, interpersonal processes, and social context shape FCR in breast cancer survivorship [30]. First, sociodemographic and clinical factors were chosen as antecedent constructs that shape initial illness perceptions. Second, physiological and psychological symptoms were included as the key cognitive cues that may trigger FCR. Third, social support was selected to capture the moderating resources posited by the SCPM. This framework directly informed our choice of measurement instruments and the structure of subsequent analyses.

Procedures

Eligible participants were consecutively approached and enrolled by nurses from the oncology and breast surgery units across three tertiary hospitals in Beijing during routine clinical visits, using convenience sampling. The researchers explained the purpose and procedures, ensuring participants that their information would remain confidential. Participants who consented to take part in the study provided informed consent before independently filling out the questionnaire. Data collection was conducted using the WeChat-based survey platform " Wenjuanxing,” a Chinese online survey platform [31].

Measures

Socio-demographic and clinical information

This section includes sociodemographic characteristics such as age, marital status, education level, and medical burden. The clinical variables were cancer stage, treatment methods, cancer diagnosis time, type of surgery, and patients’ self-assessment of their disease awareness. Sociodemographic variables were self-reported by participants, whereas clinical variables were collected and verified by trained nurses or researchers based on medical records.

Fear of cancer recurrence inventory-short form

The Fear of Cancer Recurrence Inventory-Short Form (FCRI-SF) was developed by Simard in 2015 by extracting the “severity” dimension from the full-scale version. The scale demonstrates strong internal reliability (α = 0.89) [32]. It consists of nine items, each of which is assessed using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4. The total score ranges from 0 to 36, with elevated scores reflecting heightened FCR. The Cronbach’s α of the Chinese version of the FCRI-SF was 0.912 [33], and in this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.718.

Memorial symptom assessment scale

The Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale (MSAS) evaluates the frequency, severity, and distress of common physical and psychological symptoms associated with cancer over the past week [34]. The Simplified Chinese version of the MSAS consists of two dimensions: the distress level of physical symptoms (28 items) and the frequency of psychological symptoms (four items). The scoring range for physical symptoms is from 0 to 4, whereas other items are scored from 1 to 4. Higher scores indicate greater distress. The Cronbach’s α of this scale ranges from 0.782 to 0.874 [35], and in this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.753.

Social support rating scale

The Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) [36] assesses participants’ levels of social support. This instrument comprises 10 items categorized into three dimensions, yielding a total score between 0 and 66, where elevated scores denote greater social support. The Cronbach’s α of SSRS ranges from 0.89 to 0.94 [36], and in this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.804.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Shijitan Hospital, Capital Medical University (IIT2024-135-003). Separate ethical approvals were also obtained from Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University (KY2024-362-01) and Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital (24638-0-02). The study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki and ethical guidelines for life sciences and medical research involving human subjects.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0 and R version 4.4.1 (2024-06-14). Mann–Whitney and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare differences in FCR and symptom and social support scores between groups. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to examine the associations among FCR, breast cancer symptoms, and social support. Items with a breast cancer symptom incidence of < 10% were excluded from the correlation analysis. Predictors of FCR in patients with breast cancer were identified using a multiple linear regression model. In this study, the selection of independent variables for multiple linear regression analysis was based on variables with a p < 0.05 in both the group difference analysis and correlation analysis. Considering that high correlations among independent variables may compromise the stability of the regression model and increase the estimation error of coefficients, multicollinearity was examined using the correlation coefficient matrix, tolerance, and variance inflation factor (VIF). Previous methodological guidance [37] suggests that a correlation coefficient > 0.8, tolerance < 0.1, and VIF ≥ 10 suggest severe multicollinearity. The Durbin-Watson statistic was used to test for autocorrelation of the dependent variable. To further explore the contribution of different sets of predictors, hierarchical multiple regression was used by entering variables in blocks based on theoretical relevance (e.g., demographic and clinical variables, symptom variables, and psychosocial variables). This approach allowed for the assessment of the incremental explanatory power of each block.

Results

Participants

In this cross-sectional study, of the 387 potential participants that were screened, 21 declined to participate, leaving 366 eligible participants. Among these, two were excluded due to incomplete questionnaires, and seven voluntarily withdrew during the study, resulting in a registration rate of 92.2%. Given the minimal extent of missing data, listwise deletion was applied, and analyses were conducted on the remaining complete cases. Overall, 357 participants met the eligibility criteria and were incorporated into the analysis (Fig. 1).

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

All participants were women, with a mean age of 54.99 ± 11.62 years. The largest age groups were 40–50 years (n = 114, 31.9%). Half of the participants were diagnosed with stage II breast cancer (n = 184, 51.5%) and the most common primary treatments were modified radical mastectomy (n = 100, 28.0%) and chemotherapy (n = 127, 35.6%). Differences in FCR according to sociodemographic factors, clinical characteristics, physical and psychological symptoms, and social support

The median total FCR score was 13.0 (8.0, 17.0), reaching the clinically significant level. The median overall symptom score was 14.0 (8.0, 21.0), with higher scores indicating greater symptom distress; the median SSRS score was 39.0 (34.0, 46.0), indicating a moderate level of social support. There were statistically significant differences in total FCR scores among different age, childbearing status, medical burden, and cancer stage groups (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the total MSAS scores significantly differed among different medical burdens, cancer diagnosis time, treatment method, Activities of Daily Living (ADL), and level of understanding of the disease groups (P < 0.05). Moreover, there were statistically significant differences in total SSRS scores among different marital statuses, employment status, childbearing status, personal monthly income, medical burden, and surgical method groups (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Correlations among FCR, physical and psychological symptoms, and social support

Correlation analysis showed that various physical symptoms were significantly positively correlated with FCR, including pain (r = 0.329, p<0.001), and shortness of breath (r = 0.319, p<0.001), among others. In addition, psychological symptoms such as worrying (r = 0.431, p<0.001), and feeling irritable (r = 0.318, p<0.001) were also significantly associated with FCR. In contrast, SSRS was negatively correlated with FCR (r=-0.111, p = 0.036). These results suggest that greater physical and psychological symptom distress was associated with higher FCR, whereas higher social support was weakly associated with lower FCR (Table 2).

Predictors of FCR

In the multiple linear regression analysis, categorical independent variables were coded with assigned values, establishing dummy variables and reference groups, while continuous variables were incorporated into the regression model in their original form. Additionally, the correlation coefficient matrix of the independent variables (Fig. 2A) showed that sexual interest or activity had a correlation coefficient of 0.8 with lack of energy and 0.74 with numbness/tingling, indicating potential severe collinearity. After excluding sexual interest or activity, no strong correlations were observed among the remaining independent variables, with all correlation coefficients below 0.8 (Fig. 2B). From the hierarchical multiple regression analysis, we found that the Durbin-Watson statistic was 2.056, and all independent variables had tolerance values > 0.1 and VIF < 10, indicating good model stability. Hierarchical regression was conducted to assess the incremental contribution of different sets of predictors, entered in theoretically meaningful blocks (demographic and clinical variables, symptom variables, and psychosocial variables). The R² value increased from 0.302 in the first model to 0.529 in the final model, with each step showing improvement in explanatory power (R² = 0.302, 0.488, 0.507, 0.520, 0.529), demonstrating the added value of each predictor set. The final model showed a good predictive effect, explaining 46.4% of the variance in FCR (F = 8.176, p < 0.001). Age < 40 years (B = 3.505, p = 0.012), severe medical burden (B = 5.079, p < 0.001), tumor stage III (B = 4.309, p = 0.018), cancer diagnosis time of 6–12 months (B = 2.174, p = 0.014), chemotherapy (B = 2.077, p = 0.031) were significantly associated with higher FCR. Endocrine therapy was positively associated with FCR scores, although this association did not reach statistical significance (B = 2.300, p = 0.054). Among the psychological factors, worrying (B = 2.213, p < 0.001) and feeling nervous (B = 0.880, p = 0.006) were significantly associated with higher FCR. Among the physical factors, “I do not look like myself” (B = 0.796, p = 0.010) and lack of energy (B = 0.662, p = 0.018) were also significantly associated with higher FCR (Table 3).

Discussion

This study, guided by an integrated theoretical model combining Leventhal’s Self-Regulation Model and the Social-Cognitive Processing Model, aimed to identify the factors associated with FCR by investigating the relationship of FCR with sociodemographic characteristics, clinical features, physical and psychological symptoms, and social support in survivors of breast cancer. Age, medical burden, cancer stage and diagnosis duration, treatment methods, and physiological and psychological symptoms were identified as significant predictors of FCR.

The results of this study indicated that survivors of breast cancer who are < 40 years old may experience more severe FCR. Previous studies have shown [4] that age is negatively correlated with FCR. For each additional year of age, the likelihood of experiencing moderate to high FCR decreases by 4% [38], which is consistent with our findings. Compared with older cancer survivors, younger individuals may perceive the disease differently and could be less equipped with effective coping strategies, which may be associated with higher FCR [39]. Furthermore, survivors may focus more on the future as the time between cancer diagnosis and treatment increases [40]. It is possible that younger survivors are more likely to face competing life demands, such as caregiving roles or career responsibilities, which may contribute to heightened concerns about the future. Therefore, healthcare providers should prioritize developing targeted disease awareness programs for young survivors to help alleviate FCR.

Our findings indicate that medical burden is significantly associated with FCR. Survivors of this disease who are experiencing moderate to severe medical burden reported significantly higher FCR scores compared with those with no medical burden. This underscores the association between financial and health-related stressors and FCR, consistent with previous research findings [41]. A possible explanation is that most of these individuals require long-term, systematic, and comprehensive treatment after diagnosis. Moreover, the high cost of medical care [42] imposes considerable economic strain, especially for households with limited financial resources, which may severely impact survivors’ daily lives and be associated with higher FCR [43]. Early cancer detection and timely treatment at earlier stages have been shown to lower overall treatment costs and reduce long-term financial strain, as earlier interventions often require less extensive surgery, shorter hospital stays, and fewer adjuvant therapies [44]. Therefore, at the national policy level, strengthening the healthcare security system—such as enhancing early cancer screening and reducing patients’ financial burden—is crucial.

Regarding disease characteristics, cancer stage is significantly associated with FCR in survivors of breast cancer. This finding is consistent with previous studies [4, 45]. A possible explanation is that survivors with more advanced cancer stages may perceive a higher risk of recurrence or metastasis because of greater disease severity, poorer prognosis, and more intensive or prolonged treatment. Therefore, survivors with more advanced stages may experience a heightened fear of recurrence and metastasis. Reportedly, chemotherapy [38] and endocrine therapy [3] are factors associated with FCR, which is consistent with our findings. Breast cancer survivors with moderate to high levels of FCR are more likely to worry about the potential harm caused by medical treatments [46]. Chemotherapy and endocrine therapy are primary treatment modalities for cancer; nevertheless, the side effects of these medications may adversely affect patients’ physical and psychological health. Additionally, common medications used in endocrine therapy include selective estrogen receptor modulators (such as tamoxifen) and aromatase inhibitors (such as exemestane and anastrozole) [47]. Patients with cancer on these medications often experience menopausal-like symptoms, such as joint pain, dry mouth, irritability, and night sweats [48], which may heighten concerns about disease progression or recurrence among survivors of breast cancer.

In this study, we found that a cancer diagnosis duration of 6–12 months is also associated with higher FCR. Standard treatments for breast cancer, such as surgery combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy, are typically completed within a year after diagnosis. During this treatment period, various symptoms and socioeconomic factors may be associated with higher FCR in those who survive the treatment. Findings from studies reveal that these survivors who report higher FCR scores at hospital admission tend to experience stabilization approximately 4 months after breast cancer surgery, followed by a slight increase at 6 months post-surgery [49]. However, there are still individual differences in FCR levels before surgery and 6 months after diagnosis. In future studies, breast patients with breast cancer who are most likely to experience heightened FCR during the disease, treatment, and post-treatment phases should be further identified.

Owing to disease diagnosis and treatment, these patients experience various physiological, psychological, and psychosocial issues [9]. In this study, we found that physical (lack of energy and “I do not look like myself”) and psychological (worrying and feeling nervous) factors were associated with higher FCR. Previous studies [50] have also identified a positive association between the intensity of FCR and the severity of the disease. In this regard, individuals reporting more severe physical symptoms or chemotherapy side effects tend to have higher levels of FCR. A possible explanation is that physical cues (the emergence of symptoms) may trigger beliefs about vulnerability, leading to heightened concerns among patients with cancer [50]. Other factors, such as body image issues after completing treatment, can also be associated with emotional distress for survivors of breast cancer [51]. This is particularly evident among younger cancer survivors, who may experience “I do not look like myself” concerns due to image-related anxiety before returning to work. Moreover, these individuals are most concerned about their family’s future, with the majority reporting feelings of nervousness before medical consultations [52], which may also be related to FCR. However, in some studies, it was suggested that a persistently high level of FCR is likely if it is initially elevated, suggesting a potential association between FCR and actual cancer recurrence or progression [53]. Therefore, it is crucial for healthcare providers to continuously address both the physical and psychological issues of these survivors in FCR management, further emphasizing the necessity of comprehensive care interventions [54].

Notably, in this study, social support showed no significant association with FCR in the regression analysis. Previous research [46] has shown that higher social support is associated with lower FCR by providing emotional, cognitive, and practical assistance throughout the treatment process. In this study, we did not confirm this hypothesis. Several possible explanations exist. First, patients’ subjective perception of social support may have a greater association with FCR than the actual level of support, and existing measurement tools may not fully capture this dimension. Second, the association between social support and FCR may vary across cultural contexts. In societies that emphasize family responsibilities or hold more conservative values, social support may be accompanied by emotional pressure or uncertainty, potentially influencing FCR in different ways [55]. The nature and impact of social support observed in our study may differ significantly from Western perspectives because of deeply rooted values such as collectivism, filial piety, and family-centered care. Moreover, we did not control for certain potential confounding factors, such as the stability and quality of social relationships. This may influence how individuals perceive and utilize social support, thereby affecting its role in FCR. Therefore, the findings related to social support should be interpreted with caution, as they remain inconclusive and may reflect cultural or methodological limitations.

In this study, we applied Leventhal’s Self-Regulation Model and the Social-Cognitive Processing Model to explore FCR in survivors of breast cancer. Consistent with Leventhal’s model, physical (lack of energy and “I do not look like myself”) and psychological (worrying and feeling nervous) symptoms were identified as significant predictors of FCR, supporting the notion that patients may misinterpret bodily sensations as signs of recurrence, thereby triggering fear. Additionally, age and cancer diagnosis duration were recognized as key factors, aligning with the model’s premise that illness perceptions evolve over time, influencing coping strategies. Furthermore, in accordance with the Social-Cognitive Processing Model, cancer stage and treatment methods were found to be associated with symptom burden, which in turn may be related to the cognitive processing of FCR. The association between medical burden and FCR highlights the role of external stressors in cognitive and emotional adaptation. Social support was not a significant predictor of FCR in this study; however, cultural factors may modulate its impact, warranting further investigation. The hierarchical regression results showed that each block of variables—demographic/clinical, symptom, and psychological—contributed incrementally to the explained variance in FCR, especially after psychological symptoms were added. Compared to a single regression model, this approach provided a clearer understanding of how different types of factors are associated with FCR. These findings reinforce the interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors in shaping FCR and emphasize the importance of theory-driven, multidimensional interventions in managing FCR among breast cancer survivors.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the participation of individuals from three major tertiary hospitals in Beijing, China, ensuring a diverse sample, a low dropout rate, and comprehensive measurement guided by a theoretical model. Nevertheless, acknowledging the limitations is essential. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the assessment of temporal changes in FCR. Future longitudinal studies with multiple time-point measurements are warranted to identify survivors at higher risk of worsening FCR across different disease stages. Second, this study did not account for potential cultural influences on the relationship between social support and FCR. Future research should employ a mixed-methods approach, integrating qualitative interviews to gain deeper insights into survivors’ lived experiences and to elucidate the differential impact of various forms of social support on FCR.

Conclusions

In conclusion, age, medical burden, cancer staging, duration since cancer diagnosis, treatment methods, and physiological and psychological symptoms were identified as major factors associated with FCR among survivors of breast cancer. However, the relationship between social support and FCR was not significant in this study, despite its importance in other literature. The role of social support in FCR may vary across different cultural contexts, and these factors should be considered when developing future intervention strategies.
