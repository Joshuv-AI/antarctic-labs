 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report documents the microstructural and surface characterization of two high-fluence baffle-former
bolts (BFBs) harvested from a commercial pressurized water reactor (PWR) after nearly four decades of
service. These bolts experienced extreme conditions including high neutron fluence, elevated temperatures,
corrosive primary water, and sustained mechanical loads, all of which contribute to degradation
mechanisms such as irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracking (IASCC). Using scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), the study assessed bulk and near-surface
microstructures, grain boundary characteristics, and in-service oxide layers. The results revealed significant
differences between bolts exposed to 41 dpa and 29 dpa, including differences in recrystallization extent,
deformation structures, and oxidation morphology. These findings establish critical baseline data for
integration with advanced synchrotron-based high-energy X-ray microscopy (HEXM) and computational
crystal plasticity models. Ultimately, the work supports improved mechanistic understanding and predictive
capability regarding the long-term performance of reactor core internal components.

 vii

 1. INTRODUCTION
Core internal structural components of pressurized water reactors (PWRs) are subjected to multiple
environmental extremes such as high radiation doses, high temperatures, corrosive environments, and
mechanical stresses. This combination of environmental extremes makes internal alloy components
susceptible to irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracking (IASCC), which may lead to in-service failure
and inability to extend the lifetime of existing PWRs. Thus, it is important to understand how the
microstructures evolve with irradiation and during service for evaluation of lifetime extension of existing
reactors. It is critical to resolve the need to understand the mechanisms for IASCC initiation and
development and determine potential avenues for mitigating this highly detrimental phenomenon.
Baffle-former bolts (BFBs) are an integral component within PWRs. While holding load and subjected to
a pressure gradient, the bolts are exposed to a high neutron radiation flux and gamma heating both of which
vary along the length of the bolt. On top of this, the transition region between the bolt head and bolt shank
is exposed to primary loop water. It is at this spot where cracking and subsequent failure are most likely to
occur due to the combined effects that lead to IASCC. Post-irradiation examination (PIE) of BFBs retrieved
from commercial PWRs enables validation of research reactor and laboratory-based research efforts but
also to provide a detailed analysis of the synergistic and combined effects of multiple extreme environments
like radiation, corrosion, and mechanical stress.
In the United States, degraded BFBs were first observed in 1999. To study the long-term operational
performance and reliability of BFBs, the US Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL), under the DOE Light Water Reactor Sustainability Program’s Materials Research
Pathway, attempted to harvest two high-fluence BFBs from a commercial Westinghouse two-loop
downflow-type PWR [1, 2]. These BFBs had the highest fluence among bolts withdrawn from service in
2011 and did not exhibit any indications of cracking during ultrasonic and visual inspection after removal
from service. However, the torque required to remove these bolts from the baffle structure was lower than
the original torque specified during installation. The goal was to study the BFB microstructure, mechanical,
and corrosion-related properties and predict the performance of the same components remaining in service.
This Nuclear Science User Facilities (NSUF) project seeks to further characterize these BFBs using
capabilities only available at NSUF facilities to develop a mechanistic understanding for strain localization
and IASCC in operation conditions. The main thrust of this project is to do a first of its kind experiment on
highly radioactive material through access to the High Energy X-Ray Microscopy (HEXM) beamline at the
Advanced Photon Source (APS) at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) provided by the new Activated
Materials Laboratory (AML) at ANL, an NSUF facility. This report presents the characterization results
using scanning electron microscopy (SEM)/electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) performed at the Low
Activation Materials Development and Analysis (LAMDA) lab at ORNL of the cut pieces of the two BFBs.
The results provide initial SEM/EBSD data of the microstructure to inform and compare with the HEXM
data collection at APS and the crystal plasticity finite element modeling being done as part of this project.
 2. MATERIALS

2.1 Harvested Baffle-Former Bolts, Specimen Cutting and Preparation, and Shipping

History of the harvesting of these bolts can be found in prior DOE reports [2-7]. The specimen machining
scheme and specimens’ IDs are shown in Figure 1. The two BFBs were in the reactor for about 40 years,
spanning over 35 cycles. The maximum dose (in the bolt head end or collar slice) was 41 displacements per
atom (dpa) for BFB #4412 and 29 dpa for BFB #4416. For each BFB, four bend-bar specimens and seven
thin-slice specimens were machined at the Irradiated Materials Examination and Testing (IMET) at ORNL.
The bend-bar specimens were used in the fracture toughness and fatigue crack growth rate studies [6],
whereas the thin-slice specimens were designated for microstructural analyses and advanced mechanical

testing like those described in the literature [5, 8-10]. The specimens for this work are from the collar slice
or CS section.

 slice was further cut at IMET for BFB #4412 and BFB #4416 as shown in Figure 2 and Figure 3,
respectively. This reduction in size was used to reduce exposure dose and reduce the size of the region of
 interest (ROI) which needs to be under 1 mm for the X-ray beam to penetrate at the HEXM beamline at
  APS. The primary ROI for both samples is the curved region where the bolt head meets the bolt shaft.
 This region was exposed to the primary water while in service and is the region that is susceptible to
IASCC attack. This ROI has a surface oxide that was characterized by SEM here. The final cut pieces as
 shown in Figure 2c and Figure 3c were polished to EBSD quality. The other small piece from each BFB
 slice was then annealed at 1050 °C for 30 minutes in an inert environment. This piece was not
characterized by SEM at ORNL but was shipped along with the ROI piece to the AML for a total of four
  specimens. The annealing was meant to provide a comparable lattice and grain structure similar to an
  unirradiated reference material, as there is unfortunately no reference material from the same heat for

2.2 SEM/EBSD Characterization
A slice of the bolt head sections for both bolts was polished to a mirror-finish with a final polishing step
using colloidal silica in a vibratory polisher. The cross-section of the near-surface area in the bolt
head/shank intersection curved region was characterized by SEM-electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD)
using a Tescan Mira3 SEM equipped with an Oxford Instruments Symmetry S2 high speed EBSD detector.
The SEM/EBSD analysis and mapping were performed using the Oxford Instruments Aztec Crystal
software. The polished cross-section and oxide surface were both characterized. The microstructure of
polished cross-section in the bulk and near the oxide surface was characterized by EBSD with inverse pole
figure (IPF) maps, kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps, and grain reference orientation deviation
(GROD) maps. The latter two provide an estimate for the extent of dislocations and strain within the
material – specifically geometrically necessary dislocations.
3. SEM/EBSD ANALYSIS OF BFB SPECIMENS
3.1 Higher Dose BFB 4412-CS2-A
Figure 4 shows the typical microstructure of the 4412-CS2-A specimen. Recrystallized grains with
negligible in-grain misorientation gradients dominate the structure. Most of the grains were equiaxial in
shape, slightly elongated along the vertical direction in the image plane. Most likely, such a shape reflects
influences of manufacturing process. Several much larger grains, having strong in-grain misorientation
gradients, are also visible. Most likely, these grains did not undergo recrystallization, suggesting that time
at the temperature during annealing was not sufficient to fully remove deformation defects during the
fabrication process. No retained ferrite was observed in the microstructure.

 Figure 4. Bulk microstructure of the 4412-CS2-A specimen.
Microstructure along the head-to-shank transition, Figure 5, is close to the bulk microstructure. A mix of
recrystallized grains is observed with few grains still having misorientation gradients. The KAM level along
the specimen edge is slightly higher compared to the bulk, probably reflecting plastic deformation during
bolt manufacturing and handling.

 Figure 5. Microstructure of the head-to-shank transition area for 4412-CS2-A specimen.
Figure 6 presents SEM images obtained at the specimen edge (head-to-shank transition). An oxide layer,
formed during service, is clearly visible. Its thickness is approximately 2 µm, with slight variations along
the specimen edge. At grain boundaries, the oxide layer is locally thicker and penetrates more deeply into
the substrate, producing characteristic triangular or delta-shaped features. In several locations, thin hairline
cracks were observed, Figure 6e, none exceeded 5 µm in length. Analysis of the oxidized surface (Figure
6f) revealed crack-like formations delineating structures reminiscent of grains. This observation suggests
that, at least to some extent, the oxide layer morphology replicates the underlying grain boundary network.

 Figure 6. Cross-section and oxidized specimen appearance, 4412-CS2-A specimen.
3.2   Lower Dose BFB 4416-CS2-A
Figure 7 shows the microstructure of the 4416-CS2-A specimen. In contrast to the 4412-CS2-A specimen,
the fraction of recrystallized grains is relatively small, comprising ~25% or less of the volume. The structure
is dominated by large grains exhibiting pronounced in-grain misorientation gradients. The deformed grains
are large and typically elongated along a specific direction (from the upper right toward the lower left in
the EBSD maps). In contrast, recrystallized grains are much smaller and generally form clusters
surrounding the large, deformed grains. This microstructural configuration is characteristic of partially
recrystallized steels subjected to insufficient annealing time at elevated temperature.

 Figure 7. Bulk microstructure of the 4416-CS2-A specimen.
Figure 8 shows the microstructure of the 4416-CS2-A specimen at the edge, i.e., in the region exposed to
high-temperature water during service. The microstructure is comparable to that of the bulk (Figure 7):
deformed grains with pronounced misorientation gradients dominate the area, while the fraction of small
recrystallized grains with low misorientation gradients is even lower than in the bulk. Notably, in both cases
no strain-induced twins were observed, consistent with the well-established disappearance of strain-induced
twinning at temperatures above approximately 200–250 °C [11].

 Figure 8. Microstructure of the head-to-shank transition area for 4416-CS2-A specimen.
Figure 9 presents SEM images obtained at the edge of the 4416-CS2-A specimen (head-to-shank transition
area). Similar to the previous specimen, an oxide layer formed during service is clearly visible. Its thickness
is approximately 2 µm. In many images, the complex internal structure of the layer can be discerned: inner
and outer oxides are separated by chains of voids, which in some cases coalesce into crack-like features
between the two oxide layers. Oxidation also propagates into the bulk along grain boundaries. In several
locations, pit-like formations and oxide pockets were observed.

 Figure 9. Cross-section appearance, 4416-CS2-A specimen.
Figure 10 shows the oxidized surface of the 4416-CS2-A specimen. Distinct granule- or grain-like features
are apparent, most likely corresponding to outer iron-rich oxides. In several cases, separation between the
outer and inner oxide layers was observed, which most probably resulted from specimen preparation.
Localized cracks and depressions were also detected at several locations, often aligned with the underlying
grain boundaries.

 Figure 10. In-service formed oxide layer. 4416-CS2-A specimen.
 4. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
This study provides preliminary SEM/EBSD microstructure characterization of two high-fluence baffle-
former bolts (BFBs) harvested from a commercial PWR after ~40 years of service. The 4412-CS2-A
specimen exhibited a largely recrystallized microstructure with limited deformation gradients, whereas the
4416-CS2-A specimen retained a high fraction of deformed grains with pronounced intragranular
misorientations, indicating incomplete recrystallization. At the bolt edges, both materials revealed service-
induced oxide layers (~2 µm thick) with complex internal structures, including pore chains, grain-boundary
penetration, and localized oxide pockets. These results provide essential microstructural benchmarks for
correlating in-service degradation with advanced HEXM measurements and crystal plasticity modeling.
Overall, the findings highlight the strong influence of irradiation and service environment on BFB
microstructure and oxidation behavior, offering key insights into IASCC initiation and informing predictive
models for the lifetime management of PWR internals.
