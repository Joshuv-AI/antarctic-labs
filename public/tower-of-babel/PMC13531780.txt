Availability of essential medicines in conflict-affected african regions: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sileshi Belew, Yesuneh Tefera Mekasha, Anne Kosgei, Anton De Spiegeleer, Evelien Wynendaele, Sultan Suleman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccess to essential medicines is a fundamental human right and a critical pillar of effective healthcare. In Africa, armed conflicts severely weaken health systems, disrupting the availability of essential medicine, leading to gaps in patients’ treatment and, in turn, posing a serious risk to overall public health. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the impact of armed conflicts on the availability of essential medicines as a cornerstone of healthcare across African regions, and quantify the extent of this disruption over time and by country.MethodsIn this systematic review and meta-analysis, data were retrieved from published articles accessible in PubMed, Semantic Scholar, and grey literature covering the period from 1985 to 2025 and 2001 to 2024 for system impact and medicines availability studies, respectively. The literature search was conducted from January to May 2025. Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis guidelines, studies were independently screened by two reviewers and included if they contained information on medicine availability or its relation to health systems in conflict zones and were published in English. The quality of studies was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute criteria. Pooled estimates of medicine availability and their 95% CIs were obtained using a random-effects analysis. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic. The risk of bias and small study effects were assessed with funnel plots and Egger’s test. Additionally, the leave-one-out sensitivity test and influence diagnostics test were considered to evaluate study-level impact.ResultsThe review included data from 1,581 health facilities and 989 essential medicines across eight conflict-affected African countries. The pooled availability of essential medicines calculated from the data obtained from 41 studies conducted in African countries shouldering frequent armed conflict was estimated at 55% (95% CI, 47–63), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (I² = 93%, p < 0.001). Country-level analysis showed the highest availability in the Central African Republic (79%; 95% CI, 49–95) and the lowest in Nigeria (40%; 95% CI, 1–78). A temporal decline in medicine availability was observed, from 76% (2001–2010) to 46% post-2020. No significant publication bias was detected (funnel plot asymmetry p = 0.9560; Egger’s test p = 0.494). Additionally, the influence diagnostic test and the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis indicated that observed heterogeneity likely reflects methodological differences and the sample size across the studies rather than bias or outliers. The systematic review revealed that armed conflicts have profoundly compromised health system functionality through the destruction of healthcare infrastructure, closure of medical facilities, displacement of the health workforce, pervasive insecurity and psychological distress among healthcare providers, and disruptions in supply chains and transportation networks, all of which have adversely affected the accessibility of essential medicines.ConclusionOn average, only 55% of essential medicines were available in conflict-affected regions of Africa, which was below the WHO benchmark of 80%. This finding underscores the severe threat that armed conflict poses to medicine availability and, consequently, to increased indirect morbidity and mortality. Additionally, the conflict undermines medicine availability through multiple, often interconnected mechanisms, indicating the need for a coordinated, multisectoral approach to ensure continuous access to essential medicines. Therefore, strengthening healthcare infrastructure in conflict settings is critical. Furthermore, the international community should enforce accountability mechanisms for violations affecting healthcare services, while donors and regional humanitarian assistance and emergency response mechanisms should increase investment and medicine supply in resilient medicine supply systems capable of maintaining availability during periods of armed conflict.Study protocol registrationThe study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251154811), the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews, maintained by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13031-026-00815-z.

Introduction

Medicines are essential to health care systems and play a crucial role in providing health care services and improve the quality of life [1]. Thus, to meet basic healthcare needs, a country must have a functioning health care system with an adequate supply of essential medicines, allowing everyone to exercise their right to health and well-being as outlined in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights [2]. Therefore, in Africa and the Middle East, where major barriers to accessing essential medicines severely affect health outcomes and hinder progress towards universal healthcare, advocating for policies that reduce barriers to essential and life-saving treatments, promoting fairness and justice in global health systems is critical [3–5]. Evidence from Tanzania indicated that in the peace period, most of the tracer medicines were relatively good, with continuous availability of 70%, close to the WHO’s 80% benchmark [6]. However, in most African countries, key challenges, including limited availability, high costs, weak health care infrastructure, insufficient budgets, and underdeveloped regulatory systems, influence the health care system and availability of essential medicines [7–9]. Armed conflicts exacerbate these issues by disrupting supply chains of essential medicines, leaving affected populations without the urgently needed medication. Evidence suggest that both inter-state (international) and intra-state (civil) wars may significantly contribute to the lack of access to safe, effective and quality medicines [10–18]. However, no study has quantitatively assessed the magnitude of medicine availability.

Health systems are often casualties or deliberate targets in times of war. For example, the Gaza conflict has severely restricted access to essential medicines and contributed to the deterioration of the healthcare system. Evidence indicated that approximately six months into the war, only 12 out of the 36 hospitals remained operational, while 70% of primary healthcare centers (62 out of 88) were non-functional [19, 20]. Moreover, among the 25 essential medicines, the WHO benchmark of 80% availability was achieved for only four in public facilities, four in humanitarian centers, and one in private pharmacies [18]. Similarly, the armed conflict in Northern Ethiopia severely damaged the health system, causing medicine shortages [19].

Despite the widespread eruption of armed conflicts across various regions of the world, there remains a striking lack of global, comprehensive data on the availability of safe, effective, and quality medicines for populations living in these conflict zones [21]. This critical knowledge gap hinders efficient efforts to safeguard one of the most fundamental human rights: access to essential medicines. Addressing this issue is vital to advancing the 2030 Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) of achieving Universal Health Coverage [22, 23]. This study seeks to fill that gap by assessing the impact of armed conflict on the availability of essential medicines in armed conflict regions in Africa.

Objectives and research questions

This study aims to address the following four questions: (1) What is the quantitative and qualitative impact of armed conflict on the availability of essential medicines, with a spatial and temporal influence? (2) How does armed conflict affect healthcare infrastructure such as hospitals, health centers, and pharmacies, and how does this influence medicine’s delivery? (3) What are the effects of armed conflicts on healthcare professionals involved in dispensing medicines and providing pharmaceutical care? (4) To what extent does armed conflict disrupt the transportation of medicines, medical devices, and the patients who need them to healthcare facilities?

Methods

Study protocol and literature search strategy

The study was conducted following the guidelines established by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 [24]. In addition, a mixed methods research synthesis framework was applied to collect and integrate data from both descriptive qualitative systematic review, and quantitative studies, allowing for a comprehensive analysis that incorporates diverse evidence types [25]. This systematic review and meta-analysis is registered in the PROSPERO database and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and supplementary file has been attached in the online system (Table 1 Supplementary appendix 1 A). Institutional review board approval was not required, as this study did not involve research with human subjects.

Boolean operators were used to construct search queries described as follows: “access” OR “availability” AND “essential medicines” OR “medicines” OR “drugs” AND “armed conflict” OR “war” OR “refugees” OR “professional migration” AND “challenge” AND “healthcare”, and the geographic identifiers “Ethiopia”, “Sudan”, “South Sudan”, “CAR”, “DRC”, “Nigeria”, “Burkina Faso” and “Cameroon”. PubMed and Semantic Scholar were used as main search engines. Different grey literature sources (n = 14) such as reports from UNICEF Humanitarian Situation Reports, Human Rights Watch, BBC, Al Jazeera, the World Food Program, WHO, the World Bank, the United Nations (UN), and Médecins Sans Frontières were used to extract additional information of the potential impact of armed conflict on health systems.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This review includes English-language articles published between 2001 and 2024 that examined the availability of essential medicines, as well as studies conducted between 1985 and 2025 that provided information regarding the destruction of healthcare infrastructure (such as hospitals, health centers, and pharmacies); the impact of armed conflict on healthcare professionals (including migration, death, and disability); and constraints on the transportation of patients, medical devices, and medicines to healthcare facilities. Additionally, a non-African country such as Gaza was included for contextual framing as a control group [18]. Only studies focused on conflict-affected countries in Africa were considered. The study area was selected based on the United Nations Global Humanitarian Report, which assesses the impact of war on specific countries [26]. Moreover, the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data project (ACLED) conflict index indicator (2025) was used [27]. Accordingly, the selected countries lie between extreme high and turbulent (i.e., Nigeria (extreme), Sudan (extreme high), Cameroon (high), Ethiopia (high), South Sudan (high), CAR (high), Burkina Faso, and DRC (turbulent)). In these countries, there is at least one internationally recognized group in conflict with regional governments or considered terrorists. This study focused exclusively on these countries due to their prolonged and continuous inter- and intrastate conflicts, examining how such conflict settings affect the availability of essential medicines and the functioning of health systems.

The ACLED Conflict Index ranks the 50 most severe conflicts worldwide, classifying them as extreme, high, or turbulent. From these 50 conflicts, we included African countries that experienced conflict and for which evidence on medicine availability was available in peer-reviewed journals. Of the 53 African countries assessed by ACLED, two were categorized as experiencing extreme conflict, nine as high, eight as turbulent, and 33 as low or inactive according to the Conflict Index rankings. Data extraction process, and standardization

The full texts of eligible articles were thoroughly screened by two reviewers (SB and YTM). Any disagreements during the screening process were resolved through evidence-based discussion until a consensus was reached. The authors conducted data extraction and evaluation considering information on the first author, publication year, study period, study location, number of health facilities examined, number of essential medicines included, availability of essential medicines, and healthcare system challenges in conflict-armed regions of Africa.

The data extraction process for medicine availability was based on standardized data collection tools such as WHO [29], logistics indicators assessment tool (LIAT) [30], Logistics System Assessment Tool (LSAT) [31], Integrated Management Assessment (IMAT) [32], and Logistic Management Information System (LMIS) [33]. For instances WHO data collection tool, the outcomes of this data collection tools were standardized through pre-defined list of essential medicine list with specified dosage forms and strengths. The results are expressed in standard metrics such as the percentage of facilities where a medicine is present on the day of the survey. This uniformity allows aggregation of results at the national and multi-country levels. The outcome of LIAT is standardized through measuring core logistic indicators such as data accuracy, order fulfillment, storage conditions, stockout status, and stockout frequency that are collected at each facility using a consistent survey protocol [30]. LSAT outcomes are standardized through a scoring framework. Each component of the logistics system, from LMIS utilization to procurement, has comparable summary scores across systems or over time [31]. The outcomes of IMAT are standardized through the collection of inventory data, for example, stock record accuracy, stock levels, and duration of stockouts, and it computes standard inventory management indicators [32]. The outcome of LMIS is assured through records and reports with standard data elements such as stock on hand, consumption, shipments, and adjustments. These elements are defined consistently so that routine reports across facilities feed into a harmonized dataset. Hence, the published articles of medicine availability included in the meta-analysis reported these data collection tools to ensure information validity.

Quality assessment of the studies included in systematic review and meta-analysis

The potential bias (quality) of the 41 articles included in the meta-analysis was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist, that comprises nine questions considering (1) study design, (2) study setting and period, (3) appropriateness of the sampling frame, (4) selection of study participants, (5) adequacy of the sample size, (6) data analysis coverage, (7) validity and reliability of outcome measurements, (8) standardization of participant measurement methods, (9) appropriateness of statistical analysis, and strategies to handle confounders [34]. Each question in the checklist comprises an answer “Yes” (scored 1) and “No/Unclear” (scored 0). The total score indicating study quality. Thus, scores above 6 (≥ 75%), between 4 and 6 (50–75%), and below 4 (< 50%) reflect high quality, medium quality, and low quality, respectively [34]. Additionally, the quality of the 93 studies included in the systematic review was assessed using Cochrane Risk of Bias and the AMSTAR 2 checklist [35].

Contextual definitions

In this study, the impact of armed conflict on the availability of essential medicines within the healthcare challenges is the primary focus.

Essential Medicines: A medicines that satisfy the priority healthcare needs of the population [36].

Availability of essential medicines: The mean proportion of medicine outlets where a specific essential medicine is found on the day of the survey [37].

Armed Conflict: A state of open hostilities that severely disrupts essential health services [16].

Healthcare Challenges: a broad spectrum of barriers including infrastructural, economic, and political factors that impede the delivery of quality, accessible, and affordable healthcare services encompassing medicines within a given setting [38].

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Quantities data analysis

Data extracted from eligible sources and compiled in Excel spreadsheet version 10 was analyzed using R software version 4.4.1. study-level estimates and R 4.4.1 analysis code were found in https://osf.io/xzm8k/files/vufqb. The reporting protocol for the availability of essential medicines in healthcare settings followed the WHO Drug Availability Index [37]. The guideline evaluates the availability of products that are ordered and received by supply agencies, as well as their stock levels on the day of data collection. A product is considered “available” if it is in stock at the health facility providing the service on the day of the visit or during a specified period. The availability of essential medicines was classified as: not available (availability = 0), very low (0 < availability < 30%), low (30%≤availability < 50%), reasonable (50%≤availability < 80%) and high (≥ 80%) [37]. The mean availability of essential medicine was estimated using meta-analysis. Both common-effect and random-effects models were calculated. The common effect model assumes a single underlying availability across studies, while the random-effects model accounts for between-study heterogeneity arising from differences in settings and study designs. Given the substantial heterogeneity observed, the random effect estimate is presented for comparison. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² and tau-squared (τ²) statistics [39]. Significant heterogeneity was indicated if I² or τ² exceeded 50% or if the P-value was ≤ 0.05, in which case a random-effects model was applied, as illustrated in the forest plot. Publication bias was evaluated through funnel plot inspection and Egger’s regression test, with a P-value < 0.05 denoting statistical significance in all analyses. Descriptive systematic review data analysis

The systematic review data from 93 articles, including grey literature sources and advocacy and media sources, were analyzed using a country-specific approach guided by a mixed-methods research synthesis framework [25]. Additionally, Tong et al. [41]. and France et al. [42] guidelines were considered during reporting for enhancing transparency. Using these guidelines, the collected data merged as textual data to identify recurring themes related to healthcare system disruptions in conflict settings. Key themes emerged across the included studies, including the destruction of health infrastructure, displacement, and trauma among healthcare workers. These themes were continuously refined through team discussion to ensure analytical rigor and depth. In the final synthesis stage, the qualitative themes were integrated with the quantitative meta-analysis findings to provide contextual explanation. For instance, qualitative descriptions of hospital attacks and health worker displacement helped explain the low pooled availability of medicines and its sharp decline after 2020, while narratives of supply route disruptions and regulatory collapse illuminated the country-level disparities observed in the subgroup meta-analysis. This integrated approach enabled a nuanced understanding of how armed conflict undermines medicine availability through interconnected systemic failures.

Results

The study selection process and search results are presented in Fig. 3. A total of 1,580 records were retrieved from the literature search, with 949 articles sourced from PubMed and 631 from Semantic Scholar (Fig. 3). After removing duplicates and screening for relevance based on titles, abstracts, and applied measures, 134 articles were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. Of these, 41 studies met the criteria outlined in the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist and were included in the meta-analysis to determine the pooled availability of essential medicine, which was the primary objective.

Meanwhile, systematic review of 93 articles was conducted using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 framework [24], and analyzed using a mixed methods research synthesis (MMRS) approach [25]. Descriptive data from the articles indicating the potential impact of armed conflict on different segments of healthcare systems were examined and incorporated into the qualitative systematic review.

Characteristics of studies describing availability of essential medicines in armed conflict African countries

A total of 41 studies conducted between 2001 and 2024 and published from 2009 to 2025 met the inclusion criteria for this review. These studies were carried out in African countries affected by armed conflict, with the majority originating from Ethiopia (n = 29). Nigeria, Sudan, South Sudan, and Cameroon each contributed two studies. Collectively, the included studies assessed 1,581 health facilities and evaluated the availability of 989 essential medicines. The medicines were classified according to the WHO Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) system or by their intended disease management purpose. Categories included antibiotics, anticancer agents, antipsychotics, antidiabetics, essential medicine for children, life-saving priority medicines, medicines for respiratory diseases and non-communicable diseases, and non-program tracer medicines. Non-program tracer medicines refer to drugs managed under the Revolving Drug Fund (locally used to define a self-sustaining health financing system where revenue generated from patient user fees for drugs is reinvested to replenish medicine stocks). In terms of facility coverage, Ethiopia accounted for the largest proportion of assessed facilities (n = 1,157), while each of the remaining countries had fewer than 120 facilities examined (Fig. 4).

These findings suggest that most of the research analyzed in the meta-analysis upheld a high level of methodological rigor, thereby strengthening the reliability of the conclusion regarding the availability of essential medicines in conflict-armed countries.

Publication bias and sensitivity test

Publication bias and small-study effects were assessed in the current meta-analysis evaluated through funnel plot observation (Fig. 5) and Egger’s regression test. The results, comparing the risk difference of effects against their standard errors and logit-transformed proportion, show no evidence of slope asymmetry (P = 0.9560). This p-value is well above the typical significance threshold of p < 0.05, indicating no funnel plot asymmetry and suggesting no publication bias. Additionally, Egger’s regression test produced a non-significant result (Beta = 0.52, SE = 0.758, z = 0.68, p = 0.4940), confirming the absence of significant publication bias in the included studies. A p-value of 0.4940 also suggests no significant small-study effects, supporting these findings. Heterogeneity and potential outliers were visually represented, with the expectation that most studies should fall within the 95% confidence interval range.

Meta-analysis

The meta-analysis included a total of 41 studies to assess the overall availability of essential medicines in armed conflict of selected Africa countries (Fig. 7). The findings revealed that, on average only 55% (95% CI: 47–63) of essential medicines were available in these settings, falling below the WHO recommended standard [37]. Furthermore, there was significant variation across studies in terms of time periods and geographic regions, leading to high heterogeneity (I² = 93%, p ≤ 0.001) (Fig. 6). These differences are likely due to genuine disparities in healthcare infrastructure, supply chain efficiency, conflict severity, localization, and regulatory frameworks, rather than to random variation. The substantial heterogeneity observed highlights the unequal distribution of essential medicines, with some conflict regions facing severe shortages while others may have better access.

The analysis was categorized by geographical location and study period to offer insights that are more specific. The forest plot for the subgroup meta-analysis by country has been attached as supplementary information in the online system (S1F1). It includes a solid vertical line representing the overall effect size. This visual aid enhances the interpretation by allowing readers to compare each subgroup’s confidence interval with the overall effect, highlighting potential differences. It is worth noting, however, that subgroup confidence intervals may be wider due to smaller sample sizes, should be taken into account when assessing significance.

Meta-regression

Meta-regression was conducted extensively by focusing on study year and geographical sub-regions. Conflict areas of Africa were categorized as East Africa (Ethiopia, Sudan, and South Sudan), Central Africa (CAR, DRC, and Cameroon), and West Africa (Nigeria and Burkina Faso). These regions were used as representatives of armed conflict settings, and study years were considered to identify key drivers of heterogeneity in medicine availability. The meta-regression findings show that conflict period significantly explains heterogeneity in medicine availability across armed conflict-affected African regions (QM = 9.67, p = 0.0079), accounting for 16.58% of the between-study variance. Compared with the reference period, studies conducted before 2011 reported significantly higher medicine availability, whereas post-2020 conflict settings were associated with a substantial and statistically significant decline. This indicates worsening the availability of essential medicines in more recent conflicts. The 2011–2020 period demonstrated a negative but non-significant association, suggesting a transitional phase in which availability began to deteriorate but had not yet reached the severity observed after 2020 (Table 4).

In contrast, country-level predictors did not explain heterogeneity (R² = 0.00%, QM = 1.88, p = 0.9662), indicating that disparities in medicine availability are not driven by country-specific factors alone, but rather by broader temporal and conflict-related dynamics. The persistence of significant residual heterogeneity (QE p < 0.0001) further suggests that unmeasured contextual factors, including conflict intensity, health system resilience, supply chain disruptions, and humanitarian access constraints, likely contribute to the observed variability in medicine availability.

Medicine availability and health system association in peace period

To establish a baseline for assessing the impacts of conflict, literature on medicine availability and health system performance during periods of peace in selected African countries was reviewed and narrated to supplement the meta-analysis findings, serving as a control group. However, a direct comparison of the magnitude of medicine availability between armed conflict and peace periods was not undertaken; as such, evidence was not sufficiently captured in the available studies. Available literature information indicated a dramatic deterioration of the availability of essential medicines during conflict periods compared to peace. Though the scope of this study did cover countries like Gaza, a comparative study conducted on the availability of medicines during periods of peace and conflict in Gaza reflects the impact of war on healthcare service coverage, including the availability of essential medicines [18].

The reviewed articles indicate that during times of peace countries such as Ethiopia, Sudan, Nigeria, Cameroon, and the DRC had functional healthcare systems with moderate to good availability of medicines (Table 6). However, conflict severely disrupted these systems. Health facilities were damaged or closed, local manufacturing capacity decreased, and access to essential medicines significantly decreased. During periods of relative peace, medicine availability and healthcare functionality varied across African countries. For instance, in Ethiopia, essential medicine availability ranged from 19% to 62% across regions between 2011 and 2013, with Oromia reporting 55.7% and other regions 62.2%. Additionally, in 2007 Sudan had a stable pharmaceutical system with 27 manufacturers supplying about 30% of basic medicines. Nigeria in 2013 had widely available primary healthcare services. Availability of essential medicines is often higher and more stable during periods of peace, but the onset and continuation of conflict disrupt supply chains, making access difficult. For instance, in 2012, Cameroon had only 29.6%–47.8% availability, while the Democratic Republic of Congo in 2011 had 82%, illustrating that even moderate supply in peace can become critical once conflict begins. In Burkina Faso in 2012, all tracer medicines were reported as available, indicating strong medicine access (Table 5).

Healthcare system challenges

This systematic review synthesizes an additional 93 publications, and Grey literatures (1989–2025) on conflict-affected countries, applying a systems-thinking lens to situate the availability of essential medicines within the broader healthcare system, which in war is profoundly affected not only by disruptions and shortages of medicines but also by the destruction of infrastructure, the displacement of healthcare professionals, and barriers to patient transportation for treatment. It includes the countries Burkina Faso (n = 7) [120–126], Cameroon (n = 6) [127–132], the Central African Republic (CAR) (n = 3) [133–135], the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) (n = 14) [136–149], Ethiopia (n = 23) [15, 19, 150–170], Nigeria (n = 10) [11, 171–179], South Sudan (n = 10) [180–189], and Sudan (n = 20) [12, 190–208].

The ongoing conflict in northern Ethiopia has severely disrupted healthcare delivery across multiple regions. For instance, in the Tigray region, only 3.6% of health facilities remained operational during the conflict, with none functioning at pre-war capacity due to factors such as looting, power outages, and loss of equipment [156]. Correspondingly, maternal health services were markedly affected, with only 34% of women receiving comprehensive abortion care [156]. In the Northeast Amhara region, studies reported that 64.6% of patients experienced interruptions in medication supply, largely driven by limited access to health services and psychological distress associated with the conflict [165]. More broadly, care-seeking behavior dropped significantly; in northern Ethiopia overall, only 21% of individuals reporting illness sought formal healthcare. These disruptions reflect a widespread collapse of the healthcare infrastructure, underscoring an urgent need for coordinated international intervention. Importantly, the degradation of health services has direct implications for essential medicines’ availability and utilization. Data indicate that disruptions in supply chains and healthcare delivery reduce access to vital medications, further exacerbating health outcomes in conflict-affected populations (Table 4; Supplementary appendix 4E).

This review of ten published studies from Nigeria (2015–2024) highlights the profound impact of armed conflict, particularly Boko Haram and farmer-herder violence, on the country’s health systems [172]. The insurgency has severely disrupted healthcare access, especially for internally displaced persons (IDPs), compromising vaccination programs, maternal care, and overall service delivery. Attacks on health workers and facilities, including kidnappings and destruction of health transport, have further crippled healthcare infrastructure. Mental health support remains critically lacking in IDP camps, with many affected individuals, especially youth, experiencing trauma, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) without adequate care [178]. The conflict has also jeopardized access to essential medicines, raising policy and humanitarian concerns. Addressing these challenges calls for strengthened healthcare policies, enhanced humanitarian aid, and innovative approaches to restore and improve health outcomes in conflict-affected regions (Table 5; Supplementary appendix 5 F).

The review summarizes six studies documenting the severe impact of armed conflict on Cameroon’s health system, especially in Anglophone regions. Healthcare workers face violence and insecurity, causing staff shortages and reduced service use, with increased malaria complications, malnutrition, and higher maternal and infant mortality. Key health services, including immunization coverage and maternal care, have sharply declined between 2016 and 2019, with Diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis (DPT3) vaccination dropping from 90% to 55% and facility deliveries from 46% to 26% [129]. Increased complications from malaria, malnutrition, and rising mortality rates were reported. The conflict destroyed 26 health facilities and killed 11 workers [131]. Beyond health, the conflict has damaged education, fueled economic decline, and caused mass migration, all hindering Cameroon’s nation-building (Table 6; Supplementary appendix 6G).

This review included 14 articles from 2007 to 2024, highlighting the severe impact of conflict on healthcare in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). Studies in North and South Kivu revealed that instability drastically reduced access to essential health services, worsening outcomes for women, children, and adolescents [209]. Conflict disrupted infrastructure and caused displacement, exacerbating health crises. Pregnant women received poor care due to the conflict’s negative effects on education, employment, and socio-economic status [146]. A qualitative study of healthcare workers in Eastern DRC exposed systemic issues such as staff shortages, poor management, and low pay compounded by a blame culture and insecurity, which undermined patient safety [145]. Despite challenges, health workers showed resilience. Post-conflict assessments revealed critical shortages of medical supplies, with only 26% of providers having adequate access [139]. A 2023 study found a severe shortage of doctors (0.35 per 10,000 people) and widespread lack of official registration and government salary payments among healthcare workers, especially in unstable districts [144], further weakening the healthcare system (Table 7; Supplementary appendix 7 H).

The study from Burkina Faso highlights how armed conflicts and terrorist attacks severely disrupt access to maternal healthcare [121]. Following attacks, the number of assisted deliveries in healthcare facilities drops by 3.8%, with multiple attacks causing even greater declines [121]. Each additional attack results in 7.7% reduction cesarean sections, a 2.5% decrease in assisted deliveries, and a 1.8% decline in antenatal visits. At Ouahigouya Regional Teaching Hospital, 38.6% of obstetric admissions were emergencies, 25.6% from insecure areas, where patients had fewer prenatal visits, were more often rural, multigravida, and experienced higher uterine rupture and perinatal mortality rates [122]. The January 2016 terrorist attack further strained health infrastructure and workers, emphasizing the need for resilient health systems [123]. UNICEF reports that insecurity has forced over 37% of health facilities to reduce or cease services, affecting 4 million people. With over 100,000 internally displaced persons in the North, Medicines Sans Frontiers provides mobile healthcare where access is limited [125]. In Titao district, many health centers operate below capacity or have closed due to ongoing violence and logistical barriers [124] (Table 8; Supplementary appendix 8I).

Only three reports from the Central African Republic (CAR) were included due to limited data. Study conducted in (2024) documented 126 attacks on healthcare, including killings, abductions, and arson, causing prolonged closures and shortages that disproportionately affect vulnerable groups. Healthcare workers faced psychological trauma [133]. Conflict since the 2013–2014 civil war has left much of CAR beyond government control, complicating humanitarian efforts. Human Rights Watch (2015, 2017) found that chaotic food distributions in displacement camps severely impacted people with disabilities, who also faced unsafe access to water and sanitation due to lack of accommodations, increasing health risks [135] (Table 9;Supplementary appendix 9 J).

This review includes 20 detailed case studies examining the potential impact of armed conflict in Sudan. This review indicates the severe impact of armed conflict in Sudan on healthcare systems. Prolonged violence, including the 2003 Darfur conflict, has jeopardized essential services, putting over 8 million children under five at risk [207]. Health disparities persist, with maternal mortality rising due to lack of skilled care. As of July 2024, only one-third of hospitals in conflict zones remain functional; many have been attacked, evacuated, or militarized. The conflict disrupts medical supplies and staff availability, with Médecins Sans Frontières unable to reach North Darfur. Over 3.2 million people are internally displaced, and nearly a million refugees strain neighboring countries’ health systems. A cholera outbreak worsened by conflict resulted in 9,581 cases and 238 deaths. Surgical services have drastically declined, with many practitioners displaced or unable to work [197]. Attacks on healthcare facilities and workers, hospital closures, and supply shortages continue, severely destabilizing Sudan’s health infrastructure and exacerbating disease burden and maternal health challenges [199] (Table 11; Supplementary appendix 11 K).

The reviewed articles describe 10 cases of the impact of armed conflict in South Sudan. The armed conflict in South Sudan severely damaged infrastructure, communication, and transportation, creating major barriers for people with disabilities [210]. Poor transport and inaccessible public facilities limit mobility and access to essential services. High transport costs and lack of accessible media deepen social exclusion. About 68% of women deliver at home, linked to low education, distance, and costs. Between 2013 and 2015, violent attacks on hospitals killed at least 58 people and led to looting and destruction. By 2016, nearly half of medical facilities were destroyed or looted, crippling healthcare delivery nationwide [189] (Table 12: Supplementary appendix 12 L).

Discussion

Access to essential medicines is a fundamental human right and critical to achieving optimal health outcomes. In many African countries affected by armed conflict, however, availability remains severely constrained, undermining efforts to address both communicable and non-communicable diseases. This study investigates the availability of essential medicines and related health system challenges in such settings, offering evidence-based insights into their scale and implications.The finding of this study revealed that only 55% (95% CI: 47–63) of essential medicines are available in health facilities across some selected conflict-affected areas, falling significantly below the WHO’s benchmark (80%) set for functional health systems [37]. This reflects the prevailing challenge in the region to achieve the Universal Declaration of Human Rights to medical care describe in Article 25 [211]. Similarly, data obtained from health facilities in Gaza indicate 55% mean availability of essential medicines [212]. Also the data on the availability of essential medicine in public (37.8% to 68.3%) and private (42.3% to 77.4%) sectors in 54 low- and middle-income countries across WHO regions do not comply the WHO bench mark [37]. This reflects that the low availability might be attributed to various contributing factors. Thus, there needs to be significant efforts in addressing the issue to ensuring access to life saving essential medicines and management of health conditions attributed to communicable and non-communicable diseases contributing to significant number of disease and morbidities [5, 213, 214]. Since low and middle income countries are facing relatively low access and availability to essential medicines [3, 5, 213, 215, 216], various disease conditions requiring pharmacological interventions might not be managed. Therefore, an international bodies such as WHO, UNICEF, and UN humanitarian agencies should play a significant role to safeguard human health and well-being through global and local networking and strategic collaboration with all potential stakeholders.

Conflict zones might create environments where regulatory oversight weakens, which could worsen the proliferation of low-quality drugs into the pharmaceutical market [217]. For example, Ethiopia and Sudan are neighboring countries where there are frequent conflicts that could create porous borders and disrupt law enforcement, contribute to the smuggling and circulation of illegal drugs, and cause public health issues [218]. When official supply chains collapse, unregulated outlets often flourish [219]. Additionally, armed conflict presents complex challenges and compromises the access to quality-assured healthcare, including clinical-based essential medicine prescriptions [220]. In many conflict-affected areas, informal vendors might be selling whatever drugs they can acquire cheaply. This practice might enhance the existence of poor-quality medicines and could aggravate the magnitude of the prevalence of poor-quality medicines that the region faces [221]. As falsified medicines and dispensing of antibiotics without providing full-course regimens favour resistant strains to survive, the illegal marketing could fuel antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and lead to increased therapeutic failure [222–224].

Conflicts are also contributing factors to shortages of medication attributed to a legal import decline, while the few surviving distributors often increase prices to compensate for risks and limited supply [225]. When essential medicine are in short supply, people are left with no choice but to spend their limited resources on whatever drugs are available [226]. As a result, they are compelled to purchase costly and unaffordable medicines simply to manage their illnesses. In Ethiopia, the foreign exchange crisis worsened by conflict forces importers to rely on black-market rates that are nearly double the official rate [227]. Consequently, even the price of commonly available drugs may exorbitantly price. This might push many buyers to depend on contraband medicines sold at inflated prices, which in turn increases the risk of substandard or falsified products circulating in the market [227]. Additionally, illegal importers may genuinely attempt to procure poor-quality medicine. Evidence from the United Kingdom highlights a case involving the illegal importation and misbranding of unlicensed medicines originating from India, the Middle East, and the Far East, for which the importer was subsequently convicted and sentenced [228]. In Sudan, the civil war has created a humanitarian catastrophe which may lead to low medicine availability due to a combination of supply shortages, rising demand, and weakened regulatory oversight [14].

The low availability of essential medicines might lead to treatment interruptions. For instance, evidence from Ethiopia indicates that 64.6% of patients discontinued their treatment due to limited access to medicines and the financial burden of their cost [165]. Evidence from the Amhara region of Ethiopia indicates a pooled prevalence of treatment interruption of 76.71% (95% CI: 76.63–76.78) [229]. Conflict has direct and indirect negative public health consequences. In Sudan, reports indicate challenges in providing essential medicines and vaccines due to conflicts and the associated collapse of the health care system [203]. Similarly, in conflict-affected regions of Ethiopia, hospitals re-used single-use items and even expired drugs, while oxygen plants failed [230].

The findings of this study indicated that there is a temporal trend and regional disparities in the availability of EM, while the limited data are pronounced. For instance, the subgroup meta-analysis revealed a sharp decline from 76% in pre-2011 to 46% post-2020.The high heterogeneity across periods (I² = 93%, τ² = 0.0642, P < 0.001) might be due to regional conflict dynamics and health system fragility. The pre-2011 period-specific trends reveal relatively higher availability. This might be a degree of pre-conflict stability in certain regions. However, as indicated in the literature after 2010, the escalation of protracted conflicts such as the Boko Haram insurgency in Nigeria and the Tigray war in Ethiopia severely disrupted supply chains for essential medicines. For example, in Tigray, by 2022, 96.4% of health facilities were rendered non-functional, effectively halting medicine distribution and access to healthcare [156]. In 2022, the United Nations reported that 3.8 million people in Tigray were in urgent need of health assistance due to the high burden of communicable diseases [231]. Similarly, WHO report indicate 80% of malaria infection in region, reflecting high prevalence amid conflict, which hinder health services. In Sudan, post-war local drug manufacturing capabilities have sharply declined, bringing domestic pharmaceutical production to a near halt and causing shortages of certain medicines in many regions [13]. As conflicts escalate, this could worsen thresholds of healthcare access fluctuate, child, and old people mortality rises, as armed conflicts severely disrupt the availability of essential medicines [19]. Essential medicine availability during conflicts is often severely limited, and the time differences observed between regions may be attributed to disruptions in medication supply. Chronically ill patients are particularly vulnerable, as insecurity, displacement, damage to healthcare facilities, and transport restrictions frequently interrupt their access to vital treatments [165].

The significant geographical disparities in essential medicine availability might be reflecting both the dynamics of conflict and underlying infrastructure asymmetries. The subgroup meta-analysis reveal low availability falling well below the minimum availability standards set by the WHO [232]. In Ethiopia, the significant reduction in availability of essential medicines (55% (95% CI: 44–66)) might be the detrimental impact of the northern war, particularly involving the Tigray People’s Liberation Front, the Ethiopian Ministry of Defense, and the Fano Amhara force, on access to life-saving medications [233]. This shortage of life-saving medicines could particularly affect children and older adults and pose a major public health challenge. Additionally, Nigeria’s low-pooled availability rate (40% (95% CI: 1.78)) might be linked to the Boko Haram insurgency, which has disrupted healthcare delivery through supply chain interruptions and infrastructure damage and the closure of transportation routes due to instability [234]. This might be attributed to a grave threat to health outcomes, particularly in vulnerable populations including women’s, children and geriatrics [216]. Different forms of conflict ranging from protracted barriers to insurgencies may have varying impacts on medicine availability depending on the extent of health system disruption and logistical challenges. This could result medicine shortages and lead to risks triggering service shutdowns, worsening humanitarian crises, and increasing morbidity and mortality. Identical findings from Gaza revealed geographical differences in the availability of medicines [18]. For instance, the mean availability in northern Gaza was 37%, compared to 54% in southern Gaza [18].

Post 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic has also posed a distinct and significant disruption to medicine availability in Africa. For instance, data from Ethiopia demonstrate a stark decline in the mean availability of selected essential medicines during the pandemic (43.3%) compared to pre-pandemic levels (67.4%) [60]. This decline reflects the potential challenges to manage public health emergencies and vulnerabilities and points to the need of international collaboration to build resilient health systems capable of withstanding shocks from conflict or other crises, and to ensure global access to life-saving treatments, particularly during pandemics [235]. In Ethiopia, apart from the impact of COVID-19 pandemic, the confrontation between the Ethiopian Federal Government and the Tigray Liberation Front caused healthcare system collapse, mass evacuation of health personnel, and severe shortages of medical supplies [236]. In addition, the conflict in Tigray region of Ethiopia resulted server infrastructure, medical supply and material damage in over 80% of health posts, health centers, and hospitals [237]. The expansion of conflict in Tigray region to Northeast Amhara resulted discontinuation of treatment for nearly two-thirds of patients. The conflict hinders patents access to facility and obtain refills for medicines and receive follow-up care, amplifying the risk of treatment failure and worsening health outcomes [238]. Various studies describe severe disruptions of conflicts to service delivery and workforce morale [236, 239–243]. This could be worsened in prolonged armed conflict resulting in the breakdown of health infrastructure, disruption of supply chains, and even acts of vandalism that further weaken the health system [240]. Thus, promoting peace at all levels is crucial to safeguards health workers and ensure the quality of healthcare services. Additionally, evidence shows that war has devastating effect on children; million are injured or killed, torn from their families, and forced to witness brutal violence [244]. Many also endure grave human rights abuses, including kidnapping, rape and being coerced into serving as child soldiers [244]. This study provides empirical evidence on medicine availability levels and the impact of armed conflict on the health care system. Though there is not adequate evidence to explore the real picture of the impact of armed conflict on medicines’ availability and healthcare system infrastructure. This study emphasizes an urgent need for meaningful action to ensure an uninterrupted availability of essential medicines during periods of armed conflict while promoting peace through effective conflict resolution and prevention strategies.

Strength of the study

The strength of this study lies in its comprehensive and systematic approach to assessing the availability of essential medicines in conflict-affected regions of Africa. By employing the Joanna Briggs Institute quality criteria, the study ensures the inclusion of high-quality data from diverse sources, enhancing the reliability and robustness of its findings. The use of subgroup analyses to explore regional differences and temporal trends provides deeper insight into how armed conflict disrupts healthcare infrastructure. Additionally, the study’s attention to both direct impacts, such as the destruction of health facilities, and indirect effects, including the displacement of medical personnel, offers a well-rounded understanding of the multifaceted causes behind medicine shortages. This holistic perspective makes the study valuable for informing policymaking and guiding humanitarian interventions in conflict settings.

Limitation of the study

This study is subject to several limitations. Despite being the first systematic review and meta-analysis to examine the intersection between armed conflict and essential medicine availability, the evidence base was characterized by extremely high heterogeneity (I² = 93%). The included studies differed substantially in methodology, study settings, healthcare systems, conflict contexts, and measurement approaches, which may limit comparability across studies and affect the precision of pooled estimates. Additionally, reliance on secondary data sources further limited the ability to verify the accuracy, completeness, and consistency of reported medicine availability. Furthermore, variability in the definition of essential medicines, medicine lists, and availability indicators across the studies may have influenced the pooled estimates.

The pooled availability estimates may not correspond exactly to the WHO availability indices used in the original studies, potentially affecting comparability. The review was also restricted to studies published in English. Although published evidence in other languages appears limited, relevant studies may have been missed. Due to resource constraints, the literature search was limited to PubMed and Semantic Scholar, and the exclusion of other databases may have resulted in the omission of relevant biomedical literature. Potential publication bias is a concern, particularly in conflict settings where research dissemination may be challenging. Additionally, unequal geographical representation among the included studies may affect the generalizability of findings. A substantial proportion of studies originated from Ethiopia, while evidence from many conflict-affected countries was limited or absent. Consequently, the pooled estimates may disproportionately reflect the experiences of a small number of countries rather than conflict settings globally. The study also observed a long period (1985–2025), during which conflict dynamics, healthcare reforms, pharmaceutical supply systems, humanitarian assistance mechanisms, and international policies changed considerably. These temporal variations may have influenced medicine’s availability and contributed to observed differences across studies.

Moreover, unmeasured contextual factors unrelated to armed conflict may have affected medicine availability. For example, disruptions associated with the COVID-19 pandemic, including global supply chain interruptions and logistical constraints, may have contributed to declines in medicine availability post-2020 and cannot be fully disentangled from the effects of armed conflict. Although the review sought to explore the broader effects of armed conflict on essential medicine availability, the available literature did not comprehensively address related dimensions such as individual Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) medicine categories, healthcare infrastructure, health workforce well-being, and supply chain functionality. As a result, the direct pathways through which armed conflict affects essential medicines’ availability could not be fully examined. Finally, the review did not specifically assess medicine accessibility among particularly vulnerable populations, including older adults, children, and pregnant women. Older adults who consume a substantial proportion of medicines and often experience barriers to access may be disproportionately affected during humanitarian crises. Therefore, the findings may not adequately capture disparities in medicine availability and access among these high-risk groups. Overall, these limitations, including high heterogeneity, reliance on secondary data, variability in measurement approaches, restricted databases, searching, potential publication bias, unequal country representation, and unmeasured contextual influences, should be considered when interpreting the findings and assessing their generalizability.

Conclusion and future direction

The study found that armed conflict substantially compromises the availability of essential medicines (EM) across conflict-affected African regions. The pooled availability of EM was only 55%, far below the WHO benchmark of 80%. This indicates a major gap in access to life-saving essential medicines. A marked temporal decline was observed with medicine availability decreasing from 76% pre-2011 to 46% post-2020. This suggests that prolonged and escalating conflicts have increasingly weakened EM access over time. Country-level differences were evident, although conflict-related disruption rather than country-specific factors appeared to be the primary drivers of variability. The narrative systematic review further revealed that destruction of healthcare infrastructure, closure of health facilities, displacement of health workers, insecurity, and breakdowns in medicine supply chains collectively undermine medicine availability in conflict settings. The findings highlight the urgent need for resilient health systems, protected humanitarian supply corridors, strengthened pharmaceutical supply chains, and greater international accountability for attacks on healthcare services. Ensuring uninterrupted access to EM should be prioritized as a critical component of humanitarian response and post-conflict health system recovery in Africa. For future direction, sustainable progress requires collaboration among governments, donors, humanitarian agencies, and conflict actors to align interventions with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly Universal Health Coverage (UHC). Additionally, investments in data-driven monitoring systems can help track medicine availability and target gaps in real time. Moreover, prioritizing the needs of high-risk groups such as women, children, and the elderly must remain central to all strategies. By addressing these priorities, stakeholders can mitigate the health impacts of conflict and move toward a future where essential medicines are accessible to all, even in the most challenging circumstances.
