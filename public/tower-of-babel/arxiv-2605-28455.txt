Convergence Rate Analysis of Ratio Consensus Algorithms with Column-Allowable Matrices Thanks: The research was supported by NRDI (National Research, Development and Innovation Office) grant KKP 137490.
Balázs Gerencsér, Levente Szemerédi

Abstract
We give almost sure convergence rate bounds of ratio consensus algorithms when the protocol can be reformulated to be linear updates of vector values on a possibly larger, augmented network. This is an improvement of the results of Gerencsér and Gerencsér from 2021 by allowing zero values on auxiliary nodes infinitely often which makes the technique applicable to a much larger family of algorithms.



1
Introduction

In distributed computing one fundamental subroutine is distributed averaging of initial values along a network using local communication
[
10
]
. It can be a building block for example to calculate linear synopses, random sampling and quantile computation
[
8
]
. Real world networks can have various imperfections which were addressed in previous works step-by-step. Usually some structure is assumed on the communication network in terms of matrices describing the linear update of the vector of values. For handling asynchronous, directed communication - when doubly stochasticity cannot be assumed for the underlying matrix process - the
push-sum
protocol was introduced by Kempe, Dobra and Gehrke in
[
8
]
and extended by Bénézit et al.
[
3
]
as
weighted gossip
. Later it was referred as
ratio consensus.
Most theory regarding ratio consensus discusses convergence without exact rates.

Gerencsér and Gerencsér in
[
6
]
proved that the rate of convergence was connected to the difference of the top two Lyapunov exponents of the process of transition matrices. However, their theorems need the transition matrices to be
allowable
(at every step there are both in- and outgoing communication) and the process of transition matrices being
sequentially primitive:
after an almost surely finite stopping time each node contains a portion of the original value of every other.

To handle other challenges with the communication, variants of the push-sum protocol were introduced. Hadjicostis et al. in
[
7
]
considered the cases when there can be packet losses which are not directly detected by neither the sender nor the receiver, and the one when the knowledge of outgoing edges is imprecise. As a solution, they provided algorithms using
running sums
which made the communication protocol nonlinear (in terms of the updates of the value vectors). Using an augmented network they constructed a Markov Chain formulation, which was linear on the augmented network. However, using
virtual buffers
, the condition of allowability of the transition matrices and sequential primitivity of the matrix process do not hold anymore, thus the results on the exact rate of convergence cannot be used.

Motivated by the example above, in this work we extend the results of Gerencsér and Gerencsér
[
6
]
to be able to handle the push-sum protocol when the allowability and sequential primitivity condition is weakened. Thus, we can connect the convergence rate of the running-sums push-sum protocol to the difference of the first two Lyapunov exponents of the linear process on the augmented network.

The paper is organized as follows. First, we discuss the fundamental definitions, previous results and state our results in the rest of Section
1
. In Section
2
we include a short summary on Lyapunov exponents. In Section
3
we provide the proofs for our results concerning the bounds on the convergence rates. In Section
4
we prove that the bounds are nonvanishing using the Birkhoff contraction coefficient. In Section
5
we present some numerical results and we conclude in Section
6
.

1.1
Push-sum protocol

The push-sum protocol can be used to calculate approximate averages over a network. Here we describe the formulation called weighted gossip by Bénézit et al. (
[
3
]
).

Let us have a directed graph (network)
G(V,E)
on vertex (node) set
V
of size
p=\lvert V\rvert
and edge (connection) set
E.
For a vertex
v\in V,
let us denote the set of its out-neighbors
N_{v}=\{u\in V:(v,u)\in E\}.
Initially, we have a real value vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and a nonnegative and nonzero weight vector
\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}.
Our aim is to approximate the ratio
(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{0})/(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{0}),
where
\mathbf{1}=(1,1,\ldots,1)^{\top}
of length
p.
If we choose
\boldsymbol{w}_{0}=\mathbf{1},
the ratio corresponds to the averages of the coordinates of
\boldsymbol{x}_{0},
but if
\boldsymbol{w}_{0}=(1,0,0,\ldots,0)^{\top},
then we get the sum of the values as the ratio.

At each iteration step
n,
we apply the nonnegative, possibly random, transition matrix
A_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}_{+}
to have the next value and weight vectors:

\boldsymbol{x}_{n}=A_{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{n-1},\quad\boldsymbol{w}_{n}=A_{n}\boldsymbol{w}_{n-1}.

We say that
ratio consensus holds
if for each node
v\in V
the ratio
x_{n}^{v}/w_{n}^{v}
converges to
(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{0})/(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{0})
almost surely, where
x_{n}^{v}
and
w_{n}^{v}
are the value and weight on node
v
after step
n,
respectively. Bénézit et al. proved in
[
3
]
, that ratio consensus holds when
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a stationary and ergodic sequence of stochastic matrices with positive diagonals, and the expected value
\mathbb{E}A_{1}
is irreducible. These conditions are not very strong, this formulation can be used for push-sum variants using simple messages, see also in
[
8
]
.

Gerencsér and Gerencsér in
[
6
]
proved, in a slightly different format, the following theorem which connects the rate of convergence to the difference of the first two Lyapunov exponents. We will provide in Section
2
an overview of the necessary theory regarding the Lyapunov exponents of stochastic matrix processes, and in Subsection
1.2
the further technical terms used in the theorem below.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
6
, Theorems 12, 14, 16]
)
.

Let
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a strictly stationary, ergodic process of
p\times p
matrices such that
\mathbb{E}\log\lVert A_{1}\rVert^{+}<\infty.
Denote
M_{n}=A_{n}A_{n-1}\cdots A_{1}
the products. Moreover assume that
A_{n}
is nonnegative and allowable for all
n,
the process
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is sequentially primitive, the first two Lyapunov-exponents are different:
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2},
and for any pair of row indices
i,j,
and any column index
k,

M_{n}^{ik}/M_{n}^{jk}
is subexponential. Then, ratio consensus holds and an explicit upper bound can be given for the rate of convergence as follows: for all
i=1,\ldots,p,

\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
nonzero we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left\lvert\frac{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\boldsymbol{w}}-\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\boldsymbol{w}}\right\rvert\leq-(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\quad\text{a.s.}

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
is the
i
th unit vector and
v^{1}
is from the first order approximation of
M_{n}.
Moreover, for all pairs of nonnegative vectors
(x,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
apart from a set of Lebesgue measure zero, it holds that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\max_{i}\left\lvert\frac{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\boldsymbol{w}}-\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\boldsymbol{w}}\right\rvert=-(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\quad\text{a.s.}

Hadjicostis et al. in
[
7
]
considered variants, where the messages are more complicated: instead of sending the values directly, so called running sums are communicated. For the case of possible message losses at each step the sender sends the sum of the values which would have been sent through the whole communication process according to the push-sum protocol. Using this convention, occasional message losses are not posing an issue: the information is just delayed until a successful delivery occurs. However, this protocol cannot be written using linear transformations directly on the network. But by using an augmented network, where some additional nodes representing buffer values are added and edges (connections) are modified accordingly, each step becomes a linear transformation of the values. However, the conditions of Theorem
1.1
are not satisfied for this matrix process.

1.2
Weakened definitions

We follow follow the notations of
[
6
]
. In particular,
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
denotes the stochastic process,
M_{n}=A_{n}A_{n-1}\cdots A_{1}
the product of the first
n
terms – i.e. the state after the
n^{th}
step,
\lambda_{i}
the
i^{th}
Lyapunov exponent of the process
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
,
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
is the set of nonnegative and nonzero
p
-dimensional vectors.

The most problematic conditions from
[
6
]
, which are clearly not true for the matrix processes defined on the augmented network for the running sums version, are requiring the transition matrices to be allowable and the stochastic matrix process to be primitive. Instead, we show that when we use matrices not having zero columns, called column-allowable matrices, with the following weakening of primitivity, convergence rate results still can be deduced.

Definition 1.2
.

A nonnegative matrix
A
is called
column-allowable
if each of its columns contains a strictly positive value. Similarly, it is called
row-allowable
if each of its rows contains a strictly positive value. Moreover,
A
is called
allowable
if it is both column-allowable and row-allowable.

Definition 1.3
.

A strictly stationary process of nonnegative column-allowable matrices
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is
weakly (forward) sequentially primitive
if there exists an almost surely finite stopping time
\tau,
such that each row of the matrix
M_{\tau}=A_{\tau}A_{\tau-1}\cdots A_{1}
is either strictly positive or zero.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a (random) sequence of nonnegative column-allowable matrices. We associate the rows and column indices with the nodes of an augmented network as follows. We call the subset of nodes

\left\{i\in\{1,\dots,p\}\mid\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\left\{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}=\boldsymbol{0}\right\}\right)\right)=0\right\}

the set of
real nodes
. The remaining nodes are called
virtual nodes
.

We say that there is an
edge from real node
i
to real node
j
if
\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\left\{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}^{\top}A_{n}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}>0\right\}\right)\right)=1.

To state our results and formulate the proofs, we need the following notion of weak subexponentiality.

Definition 1.5
.

A stochastic process
(\xi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is called
weakly subexponential
if for any
\varepsilon>0
we have for all
n
, with finitely many exceptions, a.s.
|\xi_{n}|\leq e^{\varepsilon n}
or
\xi_{n}=\infty
. Equivalently,
\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty,\\
\xi_{n}\neq\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n}\log\lvert\xi_{n}\rvert\leq 0.
When we do not allow the process to take infinity as value but
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\lvert\xi_{n}\rvert\leq 0
still holds, we call it
subexponential
.

Condition 1.6
.

Assume that
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a strictly stationary ergodic process of
p\times p
matrices such that
\mathbb{E}\log\lVert A_{1}\rVert^{+}<\infty.

Condition 1.7

(Generalization of
[
6
, Condition 26]
)
.

Let
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of (possibly deterministic)
p\times p
matrices and
M_{n}=A_{n}A_{n-1}\cdots A_{1}
. Then, let us assume that for any pair of row indices
i,j
, and any column index
k
, it holds that
M_{n}^{ik}/M_{n}^{jk}
is weakly subexponential.

Note that if
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a weakly sequentially primitive sequence, after time
\tau
from the definition of weak sequential primitivity is reached, the indices
n
for which the quantity is infinity only depend on the row indices
i
and
j,
and independent of the column index
k.

1.3
Main theorems

We now arrive to state our main theorems. First, the one that quantifies at what rate the value vector and the weight vector become asymptotically collinear.

Theorem 1.8

(Generalization of
[
6
, Theorem 8]
)
.

Let
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of nonnegative matrices which is weakly sequentially primitive. Moreover, assume that Condition
1.6
holds.
Then, for all pairs
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
, except for a set of Lebesgue measure zero, it almost surely holds that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left\lVert\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}-\frac{\boldsymbol{w}_{n}}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{TV}=-(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})

where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are the first and the second largest Lyapunov exponents associated with
(A_{n})
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}=M_{n}\boldsymbol{x},

\boldsymbol{w}_{n}=M_{n}\boldsymbol{w}
for
M_{n}=A_{n}A_{n-1}\cdots A_{1}.

In addition, for any fixed pair
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
with strictly positive components with no exception, it holds that the above limit exists almost surely, and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left\lVert\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}-\frac{\boldsymbol{w}_{n}}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{n}}\right\rVert_{TV}\leq-(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}).

The following two theorems give a bound on the rate of ratio consensus: the first in a more general case, the second in the more simple one for averaging.

Theorem 1.9

(Generalization of the end of
[
6
, Theorem 12,14,16]
)
.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
1.8
are satisfied, in addition
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}>0
, and Condition
1.7
holds.
Take an arbitrary vector of initial values
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and a nonnegative vector of initial weights
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
such that
\boldsymbol{w}\neq\boldsymbol{0}.
Then, ratio consensus takes place and an explicit upper bound for the rate of convergence can be given as follows: for all
i=1,\dots,p
, we almost surely have

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\\
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n}\log\left\lvert\frac{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\boldsymbol{w}}-\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\boldsymbol{w}}\right\rvert\leq-(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})

for some positive random vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}.

Theorem 1.10

(Generalization of
[
6
, Theorem 19]
)
.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
1.9
hold, and
A_{n}
is column stochastic for all
n
. Then, for any vector of initial values
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and any nonnegative vector of initial weights
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
such that
\boldsymbol{w}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, we have almost surely for all
i=1,\dots,p

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\\
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n}\log\left\lvert\frac{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\boldsymbol{w}}-\frac{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}}\right\rvert\leq\lambda_{2}<0.

Finally, the following theorem states that the upper bound provided by the previous theorems is sharp.

Theorem 1.11

(Generalization of
[
6
, Theorem 21]
)
.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
1.9
are satisfied. Then, for all pairs of nonnegative vectors
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
, such that
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{w}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, expect perhaps for a set of Lebesgue measure zero, it almost surely holds that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\max_{i,\text{ if }\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\left\lvert\frac{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}M_{n}\boldsymbol{w}}-\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\boldsymbol{w}}\right\rvert=-(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}).

2
Notes on Lyapunov exponents

In this section, we discuss some basic properties of the Lyapunov exponents, based on the articles
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
, and
[
6
]
.

Let
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a (deterministic) sequence of
p\times p
matrices. Then for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the Lyapunov exponent of
\boldsymbol{x}
w.r.t. the sequence
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is defined as

\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\lvert A_{n}A_{n-1}\cdots A_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\rvert.

Then for any extended real number
-\infty\leq\mu\leq\infty
let us define the set

L_{\mu}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\mid\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\mu\}.

L_{\mu}
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, monotone increasing in
\mu
and continuous from the right. Since there can only be finitely many strictly decreasing subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, there are only finitely many possible values of the Lyapunov exponents:
\infty\geq\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}>\dots\mu_{q}\geq-\infty
such that

\mathbb{R}^{p}=L_{\mu_{1}}\supsetneq L_{\mu_{2}}\supsetneq\cdots\supsetneq L_{\mu_{q}}\supsetneq\{0\}=:L_{\mu_{q+1}}

and
L_{\mu}
is a piecewise constant function of
\mu
with discontinuities exactly at
\mu_{i}.
So for
1\leq r\leq q,

x\in L_{\mu_{r}}\setminus L_{\mu_{r+1}}
implies
\lambda(x)=\mu_{r}.
Let us denote
i_{r}
the dimension of the subspace
L_{\mu_{r}}
with
1\leq r\leq q+1.
Then, the codimension of
L_{\mu_{r+1}}
relative to
L_{\mu_{r}}
is
i_{r}-i_{r+1}
which can be interpreted as the multiplicity of the Lyapunov exponent
\mu_{r}.
Then, the full spectrum of Lyapunov exponents is
\infty\geq\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}\geq-\infty
by setting
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{r}
if
i_{r}\geq i>i_{r+1}
for
1\leq i\leq p.

When we get
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
as a realization of a strictly stationary ergodic process, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 2.1

(Oseledec’s theorem
[
11
]
)
.

Under Condition
1.6
the following holds. There exists a subset
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega
with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega^{\prime})=1
such that for all
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
and for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},
the following limit exists:

\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\lvert A_{n}A_{n-1}\dots A_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\rvert.

Moreover the Lyapunov exponents
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{p},

possibly taking the value
-\infty,
do not depend on
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
either.
The mapping
\omega\mapsto L_{\mu_{r}}(\omega)

is measurable from
\Omega
to the Grassmannian manifold of linear subspaces of dimension
i_{r}.

In addition, we have with
M_{n}=A_{n}A_{n-1}\dots A_{1}
the following limit almost surely exists:

M^{*}=\lim(M_{n}^{\top}M_{n})^{1/2n}

The singular value decomposition of the matrix
M_{n}
gives

M_{n}=U_{n}\Sigma_{n}V_{n}

where
U_{n}
and
V_{n}
are orthonormal matrices, and
\Sigma_{n}
is a diagonal matrix with entries
\sigma_{n}^{1}\geq\sigma_{n}^{2}\geq\ldots\geq\sigma_{n}^{p}\geq 0
such that almost surely for all
k=1,\ldots,p
we have

\lambda_{k}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sigma_{n}^{k}

and the matrix
M_{n}
is asymptotically of rank-
1
which can be quantified as follows.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
12
, Lemma 5]
)
.

Under Condition
1.6
and assuming that there is a positive spectral gap, i.e.
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2},
the first row of the matrix
V_{n}
converges almost surely to a (random) vector:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{1}=\boldsymbol{v}^{1}.

The matrix product
M_{n}=A_{n}A_{n-1}\dots A_{1}
can be written as

M_{n}=\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{1}\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\sigma_{n}^{1}+O(e^{(\lambda_{2}+o(1))n}),

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{1}
is the first column of the matrix
U_{n}.

3
Bounds on the convergence rate

When only minor modifications are needed to generalize the proofs, we do not write them out here in their full length.

In this Section, we provide the proofs for the theorems regarding the bounds on the convergence speed: Theorems
1.8
,
1.9
, and
1.10
. The main novelty is in the proofs of the lemmas required for the theorems, they are combined to finish the proofs in a similar way as in
[
6
]
.

We first establish two of these technical lemmas.

Lemma 3.1

(Generalization of
[
6
, Lemma 27]
)
.

Under the Conditions
1.7
and
1.6
, and the additional assumption that
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
, it holds that for all
i
,
1/\boldsymbol{u}^{1i}_{n}
is weakly subexponential almost surely. Moreover, if in Condition
1.7
subexponentiality holds (i.e.
\infty
is not allowed) then
1/\boldsymbol{u}^{1i}_{n}
is subexponential almost surely.

Proof.

The structure of the proof is the same as of the original. Because there is a positive spectral gap, we almost surely have
M_{n}=\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{1}\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\sigma_{n}^{1}+O(e^{(\lambda_{2}+o(1))n})
by Lemma
2.2
. Take an arbitrary pair of row indices
i,j
, and compare the rows
M_{n}^{j}
and
M_{n}^{i}.
Choose a column index
k
such that
\boldsymbol{v}^{1k}>0.
Now we can write

\frac{M^{jk}_{n}}{M^{ik}_{n}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{j1}\boldsymbol{v}^{1k}\sigma_{n}^{1}+O(e^{(\lambda_{2}+o(1))n})}{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{i1}\boldsymbol{v}^{1k}\sigma_{n}^{1}+O(e^{(\lambda_{2}+o(1))n})}=\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{j1}+O(e^{(-\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+o(1))n})}{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{i1}+O(e^{(-\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+o(1))n})}.

Indirectly suppose that
1/\boldsymbol{u}^{i1}_{n}
is not weakly subexponential. Then for an
\varepsilon>0
, we have with positive probability
\infty>1/\boldsymbol{u}^{i1}_{n}\geq e^{\varepsilon n}
along an infinite sequence, which is the same as
0<\boldsymbol{u}^{i1}_{n}\leq e^{-\varepsilon n}.
Because of the orthogonality of the matrix
U_{n}
we can pick a
j
such that
\infty>\boldsymbol{u}^{j1}_{n}>1/\sqrt{p}
holds for an infinite subsequence. Combining these together we get that with positive probability on an infinite sequence the following inequality holds with some constant
C>0
:

Ce^{\varepsilon n}<\frac{M^{jk}_{n}}{M^{ik}_{n}}<\infty.

This is a contradiction to Condition
1.7
.

For the second claim, supposing that
1/\boldsymbol{u}^{i1}_{n}
is not subexponential gives
\infty\geq 1/\boldsymbol{u}^{i1}_{n}\geq e^{\varepsilon n}
. Combining this with the inequality
\infty>\boldsymbol{u}^{j1}_{n}>1/\sqrt{p},
we get

Ce^{\varepsilon n}<\frac{M^{jk}_{n}}{M^{ik}_{n}}\leq\infty.

holds with positive probability which gives a contradiction to the subexponential version of Condition
1.7
.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Generalization of
[
6
, Lemma 28]
)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1.8
, with the additional assumption that
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
, and Condition
1.7
, it holds that
v^{1i}>0
for all
i=1,\dots,p.

Proof.

In principle, the same proof works as in

. The difference is that we need to apply the weakly subexponential version of Lemma
3.1
instead of Lemma 27 to the reversed process
(\bar{A}_{n})
and for the contradiction we also have to notice that the vector
v_{n}^{1}
is strictly positive due to the Perron-Frobenius theorem.
∎

3.1
Proof of Theorem
1.8

Lemma 3.3

(Generalization of
[
6
, Lemma 9]
)
.

Assume that Condition
1.6
holds.
In addition, assume that
(A_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is nonnegative and
weakly sequentially primitive
. Then, there exists a subset
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega
with
P(\Omega^{\prime})=1
such that for all
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
it holds that any strictly positive vector
\boldsymbol{x}>0,\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is contained in
\boldsymbol{x}\in L_{\mu_{1}}\setminus L_{\mu_{2}}:

\lambda_{1}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\lvert A_{n}A_{n-1}\cdots A_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\rvert.

Moreover, if we also assume that the conditions of Lemma
3.2
hold, we can relax the condition of
\boldsymbol{x}
being strictly positive to be nonnegative and nonzero.

Proof.

The proof has the same structure as of
[
6
]
but has multiple differences in the details.

Take
M_{n}^{\top}M_{n}=V_{n}^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{2}V_{n},
where the matrices
V_{n}
and
\Sigma_{n}
are from the singular value decomposition of the matrix
M_{n}=U_{n}\Sigma_{n}V_{n}.
After a finite stopping time, this is a positive semidefinite matrix with strictly positive elements. Indeed, when each row of
M_{n}
is either zero or strictly positive, the entries of product
M_{n}^{\top}M_{n}
are strictly positive.

The eigenvalues of
M_{n}^{\top}M_{n}
are the diagonal elements
(\sigma_{n}^{i})^{2}
of
\Sigma_{n}^{2}
with corresponding eigenvectors
(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{i})^{\top}.
By the Perron-Frobenius theorem the eigenvalue of maximal modulus is unique and positive as is the corresponding eigenvector. So
(\sigma_{n}^{1})^{2}
is a single eigenvalue, and
\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{1}>0
elementwise.

Writing
\boldsymbol{x}
in the basis defined by the rows of
V_{n}
we have
\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{n}^{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{i}
with
\alpha_{n}^{i}=\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{i}\boldsymbol{x}.
Then,
\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}M_{n}^{\top}M_{n}\boldsymbol{x}=\sum_{i}(\sigma_{n}^{i})^{2}(\alpha_{n}^{i})^{2}.
Considering

\alpha_{n}^{1}=\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}=(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{1}-\boldsymbol{v}^{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}>0,

we have that
\alpha_{n}^{1}>\alpha_{1}>0
holds for some positive
\alpha_{1},
possibly with finitely many exceptions either when
\boldsymbol{x}
or
\boldsymbol{v}^{1}
is strictly positive. Note that
v^{1}
cannot be zero, because it is the (random) limit of vectors of length
1.
Thus,
\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}M_{n}^{\top}M_{n}\boldsymbol{x}>(\sigma_{n}^{1})^{2}\alpha_{1}^{2},
implying
\liminf_{n}\frac{1}{n}\log\lvert M_{n}x\rvert\geq\lambda_{1},
which concludes the proof.

∎

3.3
Exceptional set

The coordinates of values and weights corresponding to real nodes, defined in Definition

, are not spanning the whole
2p
-dimensional Eucledian space, so we need a stronger statement on the exceptional set than being measure zero with respect to the
2p

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose that the conditions of Lemma
3.4
hold. If additionally the conditions of Lemma
3.2
hold, then we can say the following about the exceptional set in Lemma
3.4
.

Let

be a
q
-dimensional linear subspace spanned by
q
coordinate vectors. If there exists an almost surely finite stopping time

such that the image of the matrix
M_{T}=A_{T}\cdots A_{1}
is
U^{q}
and its rank is maximal and shifting time by
T
steps is a measure preserving transformation of the underlying probability space, then the zero-measure exceptional set of
\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
cannot contain
U^{q}\times U^{q}
. In fact, inside
U^{q}\times U^{q},
the exceptional set is of
2q
-dimensional Lebesgue-measure zero.

Proof.

First, fix
\omega,
the randomness in the matrix sequence. In
\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p},
the exceptional set is a linear subspace because it is defined with linear maps. The first part of Lemma
3.4
states that the exceptional set is of Lebesgue-

-measure zero in
\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Consider the map
(M_{N},M_{N})
acting on

its image is

with the appropriate inclusion. As we assumed that the rank of

is

(M_{N},M_{N})
is

. The image of the exceptional set must be a proper subspace of

, otherwise the exceptional set would have to be
\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}.
Shifting the time with

- thus changing

The argument above works almost surely (in

4
Spectral gap is nontrivial

For our results to be meaningful, we need to have that the spectral gap is nonzero. We address this question in this part.
In
[
6
, Section VIII.]
, the properties of the Birkhoff contraction coefficients are used. One application of this quantity is the proof of the Perron-Frobenius theorem. One such proof can be found in
[
9
]
. In that article, the contraction coefficient is analyzed in a more general setting: in terms of partially ordered vector spaces. One can identify a partial ordering of a vector space with a cone by taking the nonnegative vectors. If this cone is convex then the theory for the Birkhoff contraction coefficient holds. In the article
[
6
]
, the partial ordering is the one associated to the positive cone. But we can use the nonnegative cone instead, which is just the closure of the positive cone. Moreover, the interior of the nonnegative cone is the positive cone hence the positivity of transformations is the same: a
positive matrix with respect to the nonnegative cone
can only contain positive elements.

We want to have an analogue of
[
6
, Theorem 36]
with the condition of weak sequential primitivity. First, we discuss how the parts on the Birkhoff contraction coefficients can be addressed, after that we check the effect of the weak allowability. From now on, we follow the convention
\infty/\infty=1
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
be nonnegative, nonzero vectors. Then their
Hilbert distance
is defined as

h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\log\frac{\inf\{\lambda\geq 0\mid\lambda\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\geq 0\}}{\sup\{\lambda\geq 0\mid\boldsymbol{x}-\lambda\boldsymbol{y}\geq 0\}}.

The Hilbert distance is almost a metric. The difference is that it can take the value
\infty
and it is zero whenever
\boldsymbol{y}=c\boldsymbol{x}
for some positive real number
c>0
. It is easy to see that the Hilbert distance of the vectors
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
is
\infty
if and only if for some index
k
exactly one of
x_{k}
and
y_{k}
is zero – we call this as
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
are not on the same face of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}.
When
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
are on the boundary of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
but on the same face then their Hilbert distance coincides with the appropriate lower dimensional Hilbert-distance. With this in mind, we have the following definition.

Definition 4.2
.

The
Birkhoff contraction coefficient
of a nonnegative matrix
A
is defined as

\tau(A)=\sup\left\{\frac{h(A\boldsymbol{x},A\boldsymbol{y})}{h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})}\middle|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p},0<h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right\}.

The Hilbert distance defined above can be calculated directly from the coordinates of the vectors via the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

The Hilbert distance of two nonnegative, nonzero vectors
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
which are on the same face can be calculated as

h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\log\max_{k,l\text{ nontriv}}\left(\frac{x_{k}}{y_{k}}/\frac{x_{l}}{y_{l}}\right),

where ”
k,l\text{ nontriv}
” means that
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
are not both zero on coordinates
k
or
l
. If the two vectors are not on the same face, their Hilbert distance is
\infty.

Proof.

Since
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
are on the same face, we can apply the theorem for the appropriate dimensional Hilbert distance on open cones.
∎

For the better understanding of the Birkhoff contraction coefficient, one can turn to Birkhoff’s theorem. Let us introduce the following quantity:
\varphi(A)=\max_{i,j}h(A^{i},A^{j})
where
A^{i}
and
A^{j}
denote the
i
th and
j
th column vectors of the matrix
A
, respectively. Naturally, if
A
has a row which has both positive and zero values, then
\varphi(A)=\infty
otherwise it is a nonnegative real number. Using the previous lemma, we can calculate
\varphi(A)
directly from the elements of
A
: if
A
has a row which contain both positive and zero values then
\varphi(A)=\infty
, otherwise

\varphi(A)=\log\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j,\\
k,l\text{ nontriv}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{A^{ki}}{A^{kj}}\right)/\left(\frac{A^{li}}{A^{lj}}\right),

where
k,l\text{ nontriv}
means that
A_{k}
and
A_{l}
are both nonzero (strictly positive) rows of
A
. Birkhoff’s theorem states (see
[
4
]
,
[
13
, Theorem 3.12]
or
[
5
]
) that if
A
is a strictly positive matrix, then

\tau(A)=\tanh\left(\frac{\varphi(A)}{4}\right)=\frac{e^{\varphi(A)/4}-e^{-\varphi(A)/4}}{e^{\varphi(A)/4}+e^{-\varphi(A)/4}}.

The extension of Birkhoff’s theorem to the nonnegative matrices is also proved in
[
13
]
, but here we give a different argument in the following two lemmas.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
A
be a nonnegative matrix which has a row containing both at least one positive value and one zero but the matrix
A
does not have a zero column. Then the contraction coefficient
\tau(A)=1.

Proof.

It is enough to show a sequence of vector pairs
(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that the Hilbert distances
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}_{n})
are positive and the ratio
\frac{h(A\boldsymbol{x}_{n},A\boldsymbol{y}_{n})}{h(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}_{n})}
tends to
1
as
n
tends to infinity.

We can reindex the rows and columns of the matrix
A
in such a way that the special condition holds for the first row and the first element is strictly positive and the second one is zero:
A^{11}>0
and
A^{12}=0.

Define the vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{n}
as follows:
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}^{\top}=(1,n+1,0,\dots,0)
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{n}^{\top}=(1,n,0,\dots,0).
These vectors are on the same face of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p},
so their Hilbert-distance is finite:

h(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}_{n})=\log\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right).

To calculate the Hilbert distance
h(A\boldsymbol{x}_{n},A\boldsymbol{y}_{n}),
we have to take a maximum over pairs of coordinates. Here, there are two different cases: either the first coordinate is chosen or not.

For a fixed
n
, when we consider the first coordinate with the
k
th, we have

\gamma^{k}:=\log\left(\frac{A^{k1}+(n+1)\cdot A^{k2}}{A^{k1}+n\cdot A^{k2}}\right).

When both coordinates are different to
1
, let say,
k
and
l
, we have

\delta^{kl}=\log\left(\frac{A^{k1}+(n+1)\cdot A^{k2}}{A^{k1}+n\cdot A^{k2}}/\frac{A^{l1}+(n+1)\cdot A^{l2}}{A^{l1}+n\cdot A^{l2}}\right).

But
\gamma^{k}\geq 0
and
\delta^{kl}=\gamma^{k}-\gamma^{l},
so the maximum must be
\gamma^{k}
for some
k.
From now on, we denote
k
the index such that
\gamma^{k}
is maximal, i.e.
h(A\boldsymbol{x}_{n},A\boldsymbol{y}_{n})=\gamma^{k}.
We can rewrite
\gamma
as follows:

\gamma^{k}=\log\left(1+\frac{1}{A^{k1}/A^{k2}+n}\right).

Because we assumed that
A
does not have a zero column we have that
A^{k2}>0,
so we can do the division. From the previous form of
\gamma^{k},
it is clear that
k
is independent of
n
– more precisely, we can choose the same
k
for all
n
.

Based on the previous calculations, we have that

\frac{h(A\boldsymbol{x}_{n},A\boldsymbol{y}_{n})}{h(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}_{n})}=\frac{\log\left(1+\frac{1}{A^{k1}/A^{k2}+n}\right)}{\log\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)}

with
k
chosen as before. This sequence tends to
1
as
n
tends to infinity as we wanted.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

The equality

\tau(A)=\tanh\left(\frac{\varphi(A)}{4}\right)

holds when
A
is a nonnegative matrix. In particular, it is strictly less than
1
, if and only if each row of
A
is either strictly positive or constant zero.

Proof.

Directly from the definition of
\tau
and
\varphi
, it is clear that if any row of
A
has both a zero and a positive element then
\varphi(A)=\infty
and
\tau(A)=1
by Lemma

The other part of the proof is by a perturbation argument. First, notice that if
\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha}=(\alpha x_{2},x_{2},\dots,x_{p})^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{\alpha}=(\alpha y_{2},y_{2},\dots,y_{p})^{\top}
then for all
\alpha\geq 0
the value
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha},\boldsymbol{y}_{\alpha})
is the same because in the definition of the function
h
the
\inf
and the
\sup
is taken over the same set no matter the value of
\alpha
. In particular, the ratio for the first and the second coordinates are the same, so when
\alpha=0
it is still considered.

Let us suppose that
A
has
l
zero rows and we have proven the equality up to
l-1
zero rows. Moreover, the first row of
A
is zero and the second is positive. Let
A_{\alpha}=(\alpha A_{2}^{\top},A_{2}^{\top},\dots,A_{p}^{\top})^{\top}
where
A_{i}
is the
i
th row of
A
. In words: take
A
and make the first row to be
\alpha
times the second one. Now, for any column vertices
i
and
j
the value
h(A_{\alpha}^{i},A_{\alpha}^{j})
is constant in
\alpha\geq 0
thus
\varphi(A_{\alpha})
is also independent of
\alpha\geq 0
. On the other hand, for any
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
nonnegative vectors, the structure of
A_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{x}
and
A_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{y}
are the same as before, so

is also independent of
\alpha\geq 0
. Thus
\tau(A_{\alpha})
is independent of
\alpha\geq 0
. Combining the fact that
\tau(A_{\alpha})=\tanh(\frac{\varphi(A_{\alpha})}{4})

and both the LHS and RHS is constant for

which means that the relation is also true for

.

Note that we used the continuity of

h(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{w}_{n})=h(A_{n}A_{n-1}\cdots A_{1}\boldsymbol{x},A_{n}A_{n-1}\cdots A_{1}\boldsymbol{w})\leq\prod_{k=1}^{n}\tau(A_{k})\cdot h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{w})

For the next step, we only consider those

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log h(x_{n},w_{n})\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log\tau(A_{k})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log\tau(A_{k})=\mathbb{E}\log\tau(A_{1}).

For positive vectors
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{w})
we can apply Theorem

\lVert\bar{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{w}_{n}}\rVert_{TV}\leq h(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}},\bar{\boldsymbol{w}_{n}}).
If
h
is small then apply the lemma, if
h
is big then it is naturally bigger then the total variation. Now we have for positive
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{w})
the almost sure inequality

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\lVert\bar{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{w}_{n}}\rVert_{TV}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log h(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}_{n}},\bar{\boldsymbol{w}_{n}})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log h(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{w}_{n})\leq\mathbb{E}\log\tau(A_{1}).

But by Theorem
1.8

(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{w})

-(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})

Note that in the proof we used strictly positive starting vectors. But the statement is independent of the actual starting vectors of our process since the Lyapunov exponents depend only on the matrix series.
