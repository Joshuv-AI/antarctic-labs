Global Optimization for Parametrized Quantum Circuits
Iosif Sakos, Antonios Varvitsiotis, Georgios Korpas, Wayne Lin

Abstract
In the absence of error correction, noisy intermediate-scale quantumdevices are typically operated by training parametrized quantum circuitsso as to minimize a suitable loss function. Finding the optimal parameters of those circuits is a hard optimization problem, where global guarantees are known only for highly structured cases of limited practical relevance, and, more broadly, zero- and first-order methods can fail to find even local minima due to the presence of barren plateaus. In this work, we study the training of practical classes of parametrized quantum circuits, namely polynomial-depth circuits with a constant number of trainable parameters. This captures widely used parametrized quantum circuitfamilies, including fixed-depth QAOA, hardware-efficient ansätze with a controlled number of parameters per layer, and Fixed Parameter Count QAOA. Our main technical result is a fully polynomial randomized approximation scheme (FPRAS), which, for every ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 , returns an ϵ \epsilon -approximate solution to the problem’s global optimum with high probability, and has runtime and query complexity polynomial in 1 / ϵ 1/\epsilon and the number of qubits. Unlike the standard hybrid quantum–classical training loop in variational algorithms, where the quantum device is queried repeatedly throughout the training, our approach separates the computation into two distinct stages: (1) an initial quantum data-acquisition phase, followed by (2) a classical global-optimization phase based on the trigonometric moment/sum-of-squareshierarchies of semidefinite programs. Under a standard flat-extension condition, which can be checked numerically, the method also supports the extraction of globally optimal circuit parameters. The existence of anFPRASimplies that the promise problem associated with the optimization of poly-depth constant-parameter parametrized quantum circuitis in ??. This imposes a limitation on the expressive power of the class, namely, it cannot encode combinatorial optimization problems whose objective values are separated by an inverse-polynomial gap.

Introduction

Quantum computing has emerged as a revolutionary computational paradigm, exploiting the properties of quantum mechanics to perform tasks that are believed to be intractable for classical computers.
Early theoretical breakthroughs, such as Shor’s factoring algorithm
[
38
]

These efforts have culminated in the advent of noisy intermediate-scale quantumdevices
[

These are digitally controlled quantum processors that operate without full fault-tolerant error correction and are therefore constrained by noise and finite coherence times.

A NISQdevice, commonly, executes a sequence of fixed and parametrized unitary gates, giving rise to a parametrized quantum circuit (PQC).
The choice of gates, qubit connectivity, and overall circuit architecture is typically dictated both by the available hardware and by the problem structure, and is commonly referred to as

Contributions.

In this work, we depart from the restrictive settings considered in the existing global optimization literature for the parametrized quantum circuitoptimization problem, and study a class of ansätze well aligned with practical implementations.
In particular, we consider ansätze with a constant (with respect to the number of qubits
n
) number of independent parameters
M

K

poly-depth constant-parameter
parametrized quantum circuits.
Our driving questions are:

Q1

Can we design methods that solve the ??problem in this setting to global optimality?

Q2

Are these ansätze expressive enough to capture hard problems, even approximately?

We answer both questions by means of our main technical result, namely that, for poly-depth, constant-parameter parametrized quantum circuits, and under mild assumptions on the observable and the generators, the ??problem admits a range-scaled additive fully polynomial randomized approximation scheme.
Specifically, for every
\epsilon,\delta>0

n

1/\epsilon

\log(1/\delta)

\probsymbol\Big\lparen\lvert\hat{f}^{\star}-f^{\star}\rvert\leq\epsilon\big\lparen\max_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}f(\theta)-f^{\star}\big\rparen\Big\rparen\geq 1-\delta.

(10)

As a consequence, we obtain a strong positive answer to
Q1
.
At the same time, the existence of this approximation scheme imposes strong restrictions on the expressivity of poly-depth constant-parameter parametrized quantum circuits.
Specifically, it implies that the gap-promise problem corresponding to this family of parametrized quantum circuitsis in the complexity class ??, i.e., the family of promise problems solvable in polynomial time in quantum computer, and thus, no hard promise problem can be reduced to it, unless

This yields a corresponding negative answer to

To prove our main technical result, namely the existence of anFPRAS, we proceed in three steps.
First, we show that the objective

can be formulated as a Hermitian trigonometric polynomial (HTP), i.e., a function of the form

thereby casting the parametrized quantum circuitoptimization problem as a trigonometric optimization problem, a class of problems that has been extensively studied in the optimization literature; see, e.g.,

Although this representation has been derived in special cases in the literature, to the best of our knowledge the required degree bounds for further analysis have not been derived before for poly-depth constant-parameter parametrized quantum circuits.
Second, we approximate the Hermitian trigonometric polynomialformulation of the ??using the quantum hardware to compute estimates of

Finally, we apply the complex trigonometric moment/sum-of-squares (SOS) hierarchy

Unlike the standard hybrid quantum–classical training loop in variational quantum algorithms, where the quantum device is queried repeatedly throughout the optimization process, our approach separates the computation into two distinct stages: an initial quantum data-acquisition phase, in which the required quantum measurements are performed, followed by a purely classical global-optimization phase based on the trigonometric sum of squareshierarchy.

Hermitian polynomials.

The dual formulation.

Lemma 1
.

Any Hermitian polynomial optimization problem over the
M
-torus
\mathbb{T}^{M}
is equivalent, via the substitution
z_{j}=\exp(i\theta_{j})
, to anHTPoptimization problem.

Proof.

Consider the Hermitian complex polynomial

g(z,\overline{z})=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}g_{\alpha,\beta}z^{\alpha}\overline{z}^{\beta},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{M}

(43)

with max degree
d
meaning that for all non-zero terms we have
\max\{\lVert\alpha\rVert_{\infty},\lVert\beta\rVert_{\infty}\}\leq d
.
Then, for
z\in\mathbb{T}^{M}
, substituting the parametrization
z_{j}=\exp(i\theta_{j})
with
\theta_{j}\in[-\pi,\,\pi]
, for all
j\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
, we obtain

g(z,\overline{z})=g\big\lparen\exp(i\theta_{j}),\exp(-i\theta_{j})\big\rparen=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}g_{\alpha,\beta}\,\exp\big\lparen i\langle\alpha-\beta,\,\theta\rangle\big\rparen.

(44)

Moreover, by setting
\gamma=\alpha-\beta
, we can rewrite the expression as the Fourier series

f(\theta)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}^{M}\\
\lVert\gamma\rVert_{\infty}\leq d\end{subarray}}f_{\gamma}\,\exp\big\lparen i\langle\gamma,\,\theta\rangle\big\rparen,\ \text{where}\ f_{\gamma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\alpha-\beta=\gamma}g_{\alpha,\beta}.

(45)

Finally, since
g
is Hermitian, we have
\overline{g_{\alpha,\beta}}=g_{\beta,\alpha}
which in turn implies that
f_{-\gamma}=\overline{f_{\gamma}}
; thus,
f
is anHTP, and therefore, the optimization of
g
over
\mathbb{T}^{M}
is equivalent to the optimization of
f
over
[-\pi,\,\pi]^{M}
.
∎

Max-degree trigonometric SOS hierarchy.

In view of
Lemma

1
, since any Hermitian trigonometric polynomialoptimization problem is equivalent to a Hermitian polynomial optimization problem over the
M
-torus, we immediately obtain a max-degree moment/SOS hierarchy for such problems, as described above.
We refer to the corresponding hierarchies as the
max-degree trigonometric moment/SOS hierarchies
.
Notably, the trigonometric SOS hierarchy was already developed earlier in the context of signal processing applications; see
[
13
]
.
Two main differences between that earlier work and the approach of
[
21
]
are that

(i)

the former focuses solely on the SOS side (no dual formulation), and

(ii)

it does not provide conditions under which an optimizer can be extracted from the hierarchy.

More recently,
3
provide explicit error bounds for the max-degree trigonometric sum of squareshierarchy, quantifying how fast
f_{d}^{\star}\to f^{\star}
as
d\to\infty
; see also
24
for an overview of convergence rates for SOS hierarchies.
Relative to the total-degree truncation described in
Section

2.1
, their analysis is stated for a
max-degree
(i.e.,
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
) truncation, which is also the natural choice for Hermitian trigonometric polynomialssupported on frequencies
\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{M}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\lVert\alpha\rVert_{\infty}\leq d\}
.
Concretely, letting
\mathbf{M}_{d}(y)\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be the truncated moment matrix with
N=(2d+1)^{M}
with entries

{\mathbf{M}_{d}(y)}_{\alpha,\beta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}y_{\alpha,\;\beta}\qquad\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{M}\text{with $\lVert\alpha\rVert_{\infty}$, $\lVert\beta\rVert_{\infty}\leq d$},

(46)

the order-
d
max-degree trigonometric moment relaxation is once again given by the semidefinite program

\displaystyle f_{d}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\inf_{y}&&\sum_{\alpha,\beta}f_{\alpha,\beta}\,y_{\alpha,\beta}\\
&\mathmakebox[r]{\text{s.t.}}&&\begin{aligned} &y_{0,0}=1;\\
&\mathbf{M}_{d}(y)\succeq 0\ \text{and Toeplitz}.\end{aligned}\end{aligned}

(47)

Equivalently on the sum of squaresside, this corresponds to restricting the Putinar-type certificate to max-degree
d
.

Specifically,
3
show that truncation at level
d
yields a quadratic decay of the approximation error, namely

\lvert f_{d}^{\star}-f^{\star}\rvert=\mathcal{O}(1/d^{2}),

(48)

without additional assumptions.
Moreover, if
f
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
and its derivatives satisfy the growth condition in
3
, then the hierarchy converges at an exponential rate.
In
Section

3
, we use the max-degree trigonometric SOS hierarchy to obtain the exponential convergence guarantees of
3
for parametrized quantum circuitoptimization.

2.3
Trigonometric polynomials and the Fast Fourier Transform

The Fast Fourier Transform
[
10
]
can be used as an efficient method for converting between the two canonical representations of a trigonometric polynomial
f
; these are,

(i)

the vector of coefficients
(f_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
, and

(ii)

the vector of evaluations of
f
on a
uniform sampling grid
.

Crucially, this conversion can be performed in
quasi-linear time

\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
, where
N
is the number of sampled points, i.e., the size of either of the representation vectors (which are of the same size).
For completeness, we outline this procedure in order to highlight two important aspects that are particular to our setting:

1)

Its application to trigonometric polynomials, where the natural sampling grid is on the unit circle.

2)

Its extension to the multivariate case, where Fast Fourier Transformsubroutines are performed in parallel along each coordinate.

Univariate case.

We begin with the univariate case in order to familiarize ourselves with the required notation.
Consider the univariate complex trigonometric polynomial (not necessarily Hermitian)

f(z)=\sum_{n=-d}^{d}f_{n}\,z^{n},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C},

(49)

and sample
f
at
N=2d+1
uniformly spaced points
z_{-d}
, …,
z_{d}
on the unit circle, i.e.,

z_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\exp(i\theta_{t}),\qquad\theta_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{2\pi t}{N},\qquad t=-d,\dots,d.

(50)

Let
\omega^{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\exp(-2\pi it/N)
, for
t=-d,\dots,d
, be the
t
-th root of unity, so that
z_{t}=\omega^{-t}
.
We define the vector of evaluations

\mathbf{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\big\lparen f(z_{-d}),\dots,f(z_{d})\big\rparen,\ \text{where}\ f(z_{t})=\sum_{n=-d}^{d}f_{n}\,\omega^{-tn},

(51)

and let
\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be the matrix defined by

\Phi_{t,n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\omega^{-tn},\qquad t,n=-d,\dots,d.

(52)

Then it is easy to see that the evaluation vector can be written as the matrix-vector product

\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{\Phi}\,\mathbf{f},

(53)

where
\mathbf{f}=(f_{-d},\dots,f_{d})
is the vector of coefficients of
f
.

Since
N=2d+1
, the index range
-d
, …,
d
corresponds to all distinct frequencies.
Thus
\mathbf{\Phi}
is a row and column reordering of the standard
N\times N
Discrete Fourier Transformmatrix
\mathbf{F}_{N}
given by
(\mathbf{F}_{N})_{j,k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\omega^{jk}
for
j
,
k=0,\dots,N-1
.
Hence,
\mathbf{\Phi}
is unitary up to the scalar
N
, i.e.

\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}=N\,\mathbf{I}_{N},

(54)

This gives the inverse mapping

\mathbf{f}=\frac{1}{N}\,\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}\mathbf{p},\ \text{and}\ f_{n}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=-d}^{d}f(z_{t})\,\omega^{tn}.

(55)

Moreover, using the Fast Fourier Transform
[
10
]
, the inverse Discrete Fourier Transform
\mathbf{f}=\frac{1}{N}\,\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}\mathbf{p}
can be computed in quasi-linear time
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
(versus
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
for vanilla matrix inversion).

Multivariate case.

Now, consider a trigonometric polynomial in
M
variables

f(z_{1},\dots,z_{M})=\sum_{n_{1}=-d_{1}}^{d_{1}}\dots\sum_{n_{M}=-d_{M}}^{d_{M}}f_{n_{1},\dots,n_{M}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{M}z_{j}^{n_{j}},\qquad z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{M})\in\mathbb{C}^{M},

(56)

supported in the multi-index range
-d_{j}
, …,
d_{j}
for each variable
z_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,M
, and let
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{M}}
be the tensor of coefficients of
f
indexed so that

\mathbf{F}[n_{1},\dots,n_{M}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}f_{n_{1},\dots,n_{M}},\qquad n_{j}=-d_{j},\dots,d_{j},\ j=1,\dots,M.

(57)

Define
N_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}2d_{j}+1
for each mode
j\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
.
We sample
f
on a tensor-product sampling grid of uniformly spaced points on the unit circle; that is, for each mode
j\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
, we set

z_{j,t_{j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\exp\big\lparen 2\pi it_{j}/N_{j}\big\rparen,\qquad t_{j}=-d_{j},\dots,d_{j}.

(58)

Thus, the sampling points in the
j
-th variable lie at
N_{j}
equally spaced angles around the unit circle.
The entire sampling grid is then the set of points

(z_{1,t_{1}},\dots,z_{M,t_{M}}),\qquad t_{j}=-d_{j},\dots,d_{j}.

(59)

For each
j
, set
\omega_{N_{j}}^{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\exp(-2\pi it/N_{j})
, for
t=-d_{j},\dots,d_{j}
, as the roots of unity of order
N_{j}
, so that
z_{j,t_{j}}=\omega_{N_{j}}^{-t_{j}}
, and let
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{M}}
be the tensor of evaluations of
f
on the sampling grid, indexed so that

\mathbf{P}[t_{1},\dots,t_{M}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}f(z_{1,t_{1}},\dots,z_{M,t_{M}})=\sum_{n_{1}=-d_{1}}^{d_{1}}\dots\sum_{n_{M}=-d_{M}}^{d_{M}}f_{n_{1},\dots,n_{M}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{M}\omega_{N_{j}}^{-t_{j}n_{j}}.

(60)

For each mode
j
, define the (reordered) square Discrete Fourier Transformmatrix
\mathbf{\Phi}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{j}\times N_{j}}
by entries

\mathbf{\Phi}^{(j)}_{t_{j},n_{j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\omega_{N_{j}}^{-t_{j}n_{j}},\qquad t_{j},n_{j}=-d_{j},\dots,d_{j}.

(61)

Then, since
N_{j}=2d_{j}+1
, each
\Phi^{(j)}
satisfies

\big\lparen\mathbf{\Phi}^{(j)}\big\rparen^{\dagger}\,\mathbf{\Phi}^{(j)}=N_{j}\,\mathbf{I}_{N_{j}}.

(62)

Thus, the multivariate evaluation tensor
\mathbf{P}
can be obtained from the coefficient tensor
\mathbf{F}
by applying the Discrete Fourier Transformalong each mode, i.e., by performing
M
sequential batches of independent univariate Discrete Fourier Transformsalong each mode.

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:TensorDFT}\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{F}\times_{1}\mathbf{\Phi}^{(1)}\times_{2}\mathbf{\Phi}^{(2)}\dots\times_{M}\mathbf{\Phi}^{(M)}.

(63)

Here
\times_{j}
denotes the
mode-
j
product
of a tensor with a matrix, a generalization of matrix multiplication to tensors given by

(\mathbf{F}\times_{j}\mathbf{\Phi}^{(j)})[t_{1},\dots,t_{M}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum_{n_{j}=-d_{j}}^{d_{j}}\mathbf{\Phi}^{(j)}_{t_{j},n_{j}}\,\mathbf{F}[t_{1},\dots,t_{j-1},n_{j},t_{j+1},\dots,t_{M}],

(64)

for all valid indices
t_{1},\dots,t_{M}
.
In other words, to apply
\mathbf{\Phi}^{(j)}
in mode
j
, we fix all indices except the
j
-th, view
\mathbf{F}
along that direction as a length-
N_{j}
vector (a mode-
j
fiber), and multiply it by
\mathbf{\Phi}^{(j)}
.
In vectorized form, (
63
) is equivalent to

\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{P})=\big\lparen\mathbf{\Phi}^{(M)}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{\Phi}^{(1)}\big\rparen\,\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{F}).

(65)

Using that
(\mathbf{\Phi}^{(j)})^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}^{(j)}=N_{j}\mathbf{I}_{N_{j}}
, it follows that the mapping in (
63
) is invertible; that is,

\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{P}\times_{1}\Big\lparen\frac{1}{N_{1}}\big\lparen\mathbf{\Phi}^{(1)}\big\rparen^{\dagger}\Big\rparen\times_{2}\Big\lparen\frac{1}{N_{2}}\big\lparen\mathbf{\Phi}^{(2)}\big\rparen^{\dagger}\Big\rparen\dots\times_{M}\Big\lparen\frac{1}{N_{M}}\big\lparen\mathbf{\Phi}^{(M)}\big\rparen^{\dagger}\Big\rparen.

(66)

Here, each mode-
j
multiplication applies
\big\lparen\mathbf{\Phi}^{(j)}\big\rparen^{\dagger}
to all mode-
j
fibers.

To understand the computational structure, recall that multiplication by
\mathbf{\Phi}^{(j)}
in mode
j
acts only along mode-
j
fibers.
For instance, in the case
j=1
, let

\mathbf{F}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{F}\times_{1}\mathbf{\Phi}^{(1)}.

(67)

Then, by definition, we have that

\mathbf{F}^{(1)}[t_{1},\dots,t_{M}]=\sum_{n_{1}=-d_{1}}^{d_{1}}\mathbf{\Phi}^{(1)}_{t_{1},n_{1}}\,\mathbf{F}[n_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{M}]\qquad\forall t_{1},\dots,t_{M}.

(68)

Thus, for fixed
(t_{2},\dots,t_{M})
, the slice
\mathbf{F}[\colon\!,t_{2},\dots,t_{M}]
(with all indices fixed except the first one) is a mode-
1
fiber of length
N_{1}
, and the multiplication is exactly a 1D Discrete Fourier Transformof length
N_{1}
.
Therefore, applying
\times_{1}\mathbf{\Phi}^{(1)}
performs
N_{2}N_{3}\cdots N_{M}

independent
1D Discrete Fourier Transforms, one along each mode-
1
fiber.
Since the Discrete Fourier Transformsare independent, each 1D Discrete Fourier Transformof length
N_{1}
can be computed using the 1D Fast Fourier Transformin
\mathcal{O}(N_{1}\log N_{1})
, and the total complexity of the mode-
1
step is

\mathcal{O}(N_{2}N_{3}\cdots N_{M}\,N_{1}\log N_{1}).

(69)

Repeating this reasoning for modes
j=2,\dots,M
, the complete inverse (or forward)
M
-dimensional Discrete Fourier Transformcan be computed by
M
sequential batches of independent 1D Fast Fourier Transforms, yielding overall complexity

\mathcal{O}\Big\lparen\sum_{j=1}^{M}\prod_{i=1}^{M}N_{i}\log N_{j}\Big\rparen,

(70)

or equivalently, by setting
N=N_{1}N_{2}\cdots N_{M}
, the total number of sampled points, we can rewrite the above complexity as

\mathcal{O}(N\log N)

(71)

with
full parallelism
across fibers.

In

The main challenge of this approach lies in bounding the order of the SOS relaxation required to approximate

up to polynomial accuracy.
This is addressed via the complexity guarantees in
2
and

discussed in

.

.

3.1
Representing the PQC objective as a Hermitian trigonometric polynomial

The following
Theorem
characterizes the objective functions induced by parametrized quantum circuitswhose generators have integer spectral differences.
We remark that, although related representation results have been obtained in the literature
[
37
,
16
,
30
]
,
Theorem

2
makes explicit how the max degree and coefficients of the Hermitian trigonometric polynomialrepresentation depend on the architecture of the parametrized quantum circuit
\mathbf{U}

\mathbf{V}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{V}_{K}
and the parameter-sharing matrix
\mathbf{A}
.
We use this explicit dependence in

3.2
to bound the order of the SOS relaxation needed to approximate

.

Theorem 2
.

Consider a PQC
\mathbf{U}
that satisfies
1
, i.e., the spectra
\mathcal{L}_{1}
, …,
\mathcal{L}_{K}
of, respectively, the generators
\mathbf{V}_{1}
, …,
\mathbf{V}_{K}

Then, for every Hermitian observable
\mathbf{O}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}

\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-})=\alpha\end{subarray}}\langle 0\,|\,\mathopen{}\mathbf{P}_{\lambda_{+}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{O}\mathbf{P}_{\lambda_{-}}\,|\,\mathopen{}0\rangle,\qquad\forall\alpha\in\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\Delta\mathcal{L}),

Moreover, for each independent parameter

Since every real-valued trigonometric polynomial is anHTPand the objective function

Step 1: Reformulation as anHTP.

To begin with, consider an arbitrary

\mathbf{V}_{k}=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{L}_{k}}\lambda\mathbf{P}_{k,\lambda}.

Hence, for each

(79)

Next, consider an arbitrary
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}

Substituting the above expression for each factor
\mathbf{U}_{k}

\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}_{K}\big\lparen\mathbf{A}_{K,\colon\!}\theta\big\rparen\mathbf{C}_{K}\dots\mathbf{U}_{1}\big\lparen\mathbf{A}_{1,\colon\!}\theta\big\rparen\mathbf{C}_{1}

(80a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda_{K}\in\mathcal{L}_{K}}\exp\big\lparen-i\lambda_{K}\mathbf{A}_{K,\colon\!}\theta\big\rparen\mathbf{P}_{K,\lambda_{K}}\mathbf{C}_{K}\dots\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}}\exp\big\lparen-i\lambda_{1}\mathbf{A}_{1,\colon\!}\theta\big\rparen\mathbf{P}_{1,\lambda_{1}}\mathbf{C}_{1}

(80d)

Then the adjoint

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:PQCAdjointExpansion}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}(\theta)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{L}}\overline{\exp\big\lparen-i\langle\beta,\,\theta\rangle\big\rparen}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\mathcal{L}\\

\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\lambda=\beta\end{subarray}}\mathbf{P}_{\lambda}^{\dagger}.

(82)

Consequently, substituting

82

\displaystyle f(\theta)

\displaystyle=\big\langle 0\,\big|\,\mathopen{}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}(\theta)\mathbf{O}\mathbf{U}(\theta)\,\big|\,\mathopen{}0\big\rangle

(83a)

\displaystyle=\Bigg\langle 0\,\Bigg|\,\mathopen{}\bigg\lparen\sum_{\beta_{+}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{L}}\exp\big\lparen i\langle\beta_{+},\,\theta\rangle\big\rparen\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{+}\in\mathcal{L}\\
\mathclap{\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\lambda_{+}=\beta_{+}}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{P}_{\lambda_{+}}^{\dagger}\bigg\rparen\mathbf{O}\bigg\lparen\sum_{\beta_{-}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{L}}\exp\big\lparen-i\langle\beta_{-},\,\theta\rangle\big\rparen\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{-}\in\mathcal{L}\\
\mathclap{\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\lambda_{-}=\beta_{-}}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{P}_{\lambda_{-}}\bigg\rparen\,|\,\mathopen{}0\Bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta_{+},\beta_{-}\in\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{L}}\exp\Big\lparen i\big\langle(\beta_{+}-\beta_{-}),\,\theta\big\rangle\Big\rparen\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-}\in\mathcal{L}\\
\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\lambda_{+}=\beta_{+}\\

(83c)

Finally, by setting
\alpha\equiv\beta_{+}-\beta_{-}
and grouping the terms accordingly, we obtain

(84)

So the coefficient

collects all pairs of spectral contributions whose projected difference equals
\alpha
.
Thus, since the entries of
\mathbf{A}
are integers and, by

\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{K}
have integer differences, we conclude that the frequencies
\alpha\in\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\Delta\mathcal{L})
of

are integers; therefore,

is a trigonometric polynomial.

Step 2: Degree bound.

Now consider an arbitrary independent parameter
\theta_{j}
.
Then, since all entries of
\mathbf{A}
are nonnegative, we have that

\displaystyle\max\big\lparen\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\Delta\mathcal{L})\big\rparen_{j}

\displaystyle=\max_{\alpha\in\Delta\mathcal{L}}\langle\mathbf{A}_{\colon\!,j},\,\alpha\rangle

(85a)

\displaystyle=\max_{\alpha\in\Delta\mathcal{L}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{A}_{k,j}\alpha_{k}

(85b)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{A}_{k,j}\max(\mathcal{L}_{k}-\mathcal{L}_{k})

(85c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{A}_{k,j}(\max\mathcal{L}_{k}-\min\mathcal{L}_{k})

(85d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{A}_{k,j}\Delta_{k}

(85e)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\Delta)_{j}.

(85f)

Furthermore, since
\Delta\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}
is symmetric around zero, we also have

\min\big\lparen\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\Delta\mathcal{L})\big\rparen_{j}=-\max\big\lparen\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\Delta\mathcal{L})\big\rparen_{j}\geq-(\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\Delta)_{j}.

(86)

Therefore, all frequencies in the

\big[-(\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\Delta)_{j},\,(\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\Delta)_{j}\big]

with respect to the independent parameter

is at most
(\mathbf{A}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\Delta)_{j}

This explicit degree formula is what later controls sampling grid size and the SOS order in

.

3.2.1
Proof of the additive FPRAS

We now present the formal proof of

At a high level, the proof splits the target error budget

Step 1: Representing the objective

Since the spectra of the generators
\mathbf{V}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{V}_{K}

2

(96)

(97)

Intuitively, we

Step 3: Interpolating via Fast Fourier Transformand bounding the approximation error.

of the coefficients

of the objective function

Since the complexity of computing the Discrete Fourier Transformwith the Fast Fourier Transformalgorithm on a sampling grid of size

\ell_{0}

Moreover, observe that, by the definition of
\hat{f}_{\alpha}
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{0\}}\lvert\hat{f}_{\alpha}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}}\big\lparen\lvert\hat{f}_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha}\rvert+\lvert f_{\alpha}\rvert\big\rparen

(120a)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}}\lvert f_{\alpha}\rvert

(120b)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert}\bigg\lvert\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{S}}f(\phi)\exp\big\lparen-i\langle\alpha,\,\phi\rangle\big\rparen\bigg\rvert

(120c)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\lvert\mathcal{K}\rvert}{\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert}\sum_{\phi\in\mathcal{S}}\lvert f(\phi)\rvert

(120d)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\lvert\mathcal{K}\rvert\max_{\phi\in\mathcal{S}}\lvert f(\phi)\rvert

(120e)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\lvert\mathcal{K}\rvert\max_{\phi\in\mathcal{S}}\Big\lvert\big\langle 0\,\big|\,\mathopen{}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}(\phi)\mathbf{O}\mathbf{U}(\phi)\,\big|\,\mathopen{}0\big\rangle\Big\rvert.

(120f)

In addition, since
\mathbf{U}(\theta)
is unitary for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, i.e., its spectrum lies in the unit circle, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have that

\Big\lvert\big\langle 0\,\big|\,\mathopen{}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}(\phi)\mathbf{O}\mathbf{U}(\phi)\,\big|\,\mathopen{}0\big\rangle\Big\rvert\leq\lVert\mathbf{O}\rVert\leq O_{\max},\qquad\forall\phi\in\mathcal{S}.

(121)

Thus, we have that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:CoefficientSumBound}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{0\}}\lvert\hat{f}_{\alpha}\rvert\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\lvert\mathcal{K}\rvert\,O_{\max}.

(122)

This coefficient-sum bound turns the abstract SOS error bound in
3
into an explicit bound computable in polynomial time in
n
and
1/\epsilon
.

Concretely, we require that the right-hand side in (
119
) is at most
\epsilon/2
.
Thus, rearranging terms, and assuming
\ell\geq\sqrt{2}\,\ell_{0}
(the condition used to obtain
\mathcal{O}(\ell^{-2})
decay), it suffices to choose
\ell
such that

\ell\geq\frac{2\ell_{0}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\sqrt{M2^{M}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{0\}}\lvert\hat{f}_{\alpha}\rvert}.

(123)

Furthermore, by (
122
), we have that

\frac{2\ell_{0}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\sqrt{M2^{M}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{0\}}\lvert\hat{f}_{\alpha}\rvert}\leq\frac{2\ell_{0}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\sqrt{M2^{M}\Big\lparen\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\lvert\mathcal{K}\rvert\,O_{\max}\Big\rparen}\leq\frac{2\ell_{0}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\sqrt{M2^{M}\Big\lparen\frac{1}{2}+\lvert\mathcal{K}\rvert\,O_{\max}\Big\rparen}.

(124)

Thus, it suffices to choose the relaxation order
\ell
such that

\displaystyle\ell

\displaystyle\geq\max\bigg\{\sqrt{2}\,\ell_{0},\frac{2\ell_{0}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\sqrt{M2^{M}\Big\lparen\frac{1}{2}+\lvert\mathcal{K}\rvert\,O_{\max}\Big\rparen}\bigg\}

(125a)

\displaystyle=\max\bigg\{\sqrt{2}\,\ell_{0},\frac{2\ell_{0}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\sqrt{M2^{M}\Big\lparen\frac{1}{2}+(2K\,\Delta_{\max}+1)^{M}\,O_{\max}\Big\rparen}\bigg\}

(125b)

Moreover, since

is constant with respect to
n
, and
K
,
\Delta_{\max}
, and

are polynomial in
n
, we have that the required order

of the SOS relaxation is at most polynomial in
n
, and
1/\epsilon
.
Furthermore, since the complexity of solving the SOS relaxation at order
\ell
is
\mathcal{O}\big\lparen(2\ell+1)^{M}\big\rparen
and

is constant with respect to

\ell
can be solved in polynomial time in
n
, and
1/\epsilon
.

Step 5: Computing the final guarantee and complexity.

Combining the conditioned probability event with the two
\epsilon/2
error contributions gives the final
(\epsilon,\delta)
-guarantee.
In particular, by the triangle inequality, we have that at relaxation order

satisfying (

\lvert f^{\star}-\hat{f}_{\ell}^{\star}\rvert\leq\big\lvert f^{\star}-\hat{f}^{\star}\big\rvert+\big\lvert\hat{f}^{\star}-\hat{f}_{\ell}^{\star}\big\rvert\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon.

Recalling that we conditioned on the event in (

(Flat Extension Theorem)

Suppose we solve level

denotes the truncation of

We defer the formal proof of

.

Proof sketch of
Theorem

5
.

Let us start with an optimal order-
(\ell+1)
moment sequence
y
that satisfies the flat extension condition in (
130
).
Because
y
is feasible for the moment relaxation, it satisfies the constraints in the definition of the trigonometric moment hierarchy (cf.
Equation

47
); that is:

•

The sequence is normalized, i.e.,
y_{0,0}=1
.

•

The moment matrix is positive semidefinite.

•

The moment matrix is Toeplitz.

We rely on those properties throughout the proof.

Step 1: Constructing the shift operators.

The main objects of interest are the shift operators
\mathbf{T}_{1}
, …,
\mathbf{T}_{M}\colon\mathbb{C}^{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{R}
, where
R
is the rank of the moment matrix.
These are linear operators defined to shift the monomial indices of the moment matrix
\mathbf{M_{\ell+1}}(y)
.
The positive semidefiniteand Toeplitz properties of the moment matrix ensure that these operators are well-defined.

Step 2: Showing that the shift operators admit a common eigenbasis.

Ultimately, we are going to construct a positive atomic representing measure for
y
from the spectral decomposition of the shift operators, but first we need to show that
\mathbf{T}_{1}
, …,
\mathbf{T}_{M}
are
simultaneously diagonalizable
, i.e., they have a common eigenbasis.

Indeed, by the Toeplitz property of the moment matrix, we can show that

\mathbf{T}_{i}\mathbf{T}_{j}=\mathbf{T}_{j}\mathbf{T}_{i},\qquad\forall i,j\in\llbracket M\rrbracket,

(131)

i.e., the shift operators pairwise commute.
Moreover, each
\mathbf{T}_{j}
preserves inner products, and therefore, it is unitary.
Taken together, these two properties imply that
\mathbf{T}_{1}
, …,
\mathbf{T}_{M}
are normal, and thus, simultaneously diagonalizable.

Step 3: Building an atomic representing measure.

Letting
\mathbf{T}_{j}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}_{j}\mathbf{P}^{*}
be the spectral decomposition of
\mathbf{T}_{j}
, where
\mathbf{P}
is unitary and
\mathbf{D}_{j}
is diagonal unitary, we can show that the convex combination of Dirac measures defined as

\mu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{R}\lvert\langle z_{0},\,\mathbf{P}_{\colon\!,k}\rangle\rvert^{2}\delta_{d^{(k)}}

(132)

is a positive atomic representing measure for
y
.
Here,
\mathbf{P}_{\colon\!,k}
denotes the
k
-th column of
\mathbf{P}
, i.e., the
k
-th eigenvector of
\mathbf{T}_{1}
, …,
\mathbf{T}_{M}
, while
d^{(k)}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
is an atom defined from the diagonal entries of
\mathbf{D}_{1}
, …,
\mathbf{D}_{M}
; that is,

d^{(k)}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\overline{(\mathbf{D}_{j})_{k,k}},\qquad\forall j\in\llbracket M\rrbracket,

(133)

the fact that
\mu
is a convex combination follows from the normalization of
y
.

Step 4: Certifying global optimality of the atoms.

It remains to show that each atom
d^{(k)}
is a global minimizer of the Hermitian trigonometric polynomialoptimization problem.
This is a consequence of the fact that each
d^{(k)}
lies on the
M
-torus.
Indeed, since each
\mathbf{D}_{j}
is diagonal unitary, we have that, for each
j\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
and
k\in\llbracket R\rrbracket
,

\lvert d^{(k)}_{j}\rvert^{2}=\big\lvert\overline{(\mathbf{D}_{j})_{k,k}}\big\rvert^{2}=\big\lvert(\mathbf{D}_{j})_{k,k}\big\rvert^{2}=1.

(134)

Then optimality of the atoms follows from a standard convexity argument, and with the Flat Extension Theorem and the necessary notation in place, we can now prove
Theorem

4
.

Proof of
Theorem

4
.

By
Theorem

5
, if the moment sequence
y
at order
\ell+1
of the hierarchy admits a flat extension, then there exists a positive atomic representing measure for
y
, supported on the
M

In particular, these atoms can be extracted from the shift operators
\mathbf{T}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{T}_{M}
, which can be computed from
\mathbf{M_{\ell+1}}(y)
in polynomial time in
\ell
.

To see this, for each
j\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
, take a full-rank principal submatrix
\mathbf{M}
of
\mathbf{M_{\ell}}(y)
of size

, indexed by some set
\mathcal{A}

Let
\mathbf{M}^{(j)}
denote the

\mathbf{M_{\ell+1}}(y)

\mathcal{A}

Then

\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{(j)}

is similar to the shift operator

Now construct

\mathbf{H}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\big\lparen c_{j}\,\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{j}+\overline{c_{j}}\,\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{j}^{\dagger}\big\rparen,

For generic

Since

For each

3.3.1
Proof of the Flat Extension Theorem

Consider an arbitrary Hermitian trigonometric polynomial
f
, and recall we may identify
f
either with its angle formulation
f(\theta)
or with its complex formulation
f(z,\overline{z})
via the reparametrization
z_{j}=\exp(i\theta_{j})
, for all
j\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
, so
f(z,\overline{z})=f(\exp(i\theta),\exp(-i\theta))
(cf.
Section

2.1
).
Since in this proof we rely heavily on the definition of the order-
(\ell+1)
trigonometric moment relaxation in (
47
), which is more naturally expressed in terms of the complex formulation, we adopt this formulation throughout.

Moreover, recall that at a feasible solution of the order-
(\ell+1)
relaxation, the moment sequence
y
satisfies the normalization
y_{0,0}=1
, and the moment
\mathbf{M_{\ell+1}}(y)
is a Toeplitz PSD matrix, i.e., it satisfies the Toeplitz constraints

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:ToeplitzConstraints}y_{\alpha+e_{j},\beta+e_{j}}=y_{\alpha,\beta}\qquad\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1},

(140)

We are going to rely on those properties throughout the proof.

We now split the argument into four steps.
In particular, we begin by defining the shift operators taking advantage of the above Toeplitz constraints, then we show they are commuting unitaries, and therefore they have a common eigenbasis.
Finally, we build a finite atomic representing measure from their joint spectrum, and certify that every support atom is globally optimal.

Step 1: Constructing the shift operators.

Throughout this proof we define the following index sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\big\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{M}\nonscript\;\big|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\alpha_{j}\leq\ell,\ \forall j\in\llbracket M\rrbracket\big\}

(141a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\big\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{M}\nonscript\;\big|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\alpha_{j}\leq\ell\ \text{and},\ \alpha_{i}\leq\ell+1,\ \forall i\in\llbracket M\rrbracket\ \text{with $i\neq j$}\big\},

(141b)

for which we have the inclusions

\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{\ell+1},\qquad\forall j\in\llbracket M\rrbracket.

(142)

Now, set
R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\rank\mathbf{M_{\ell+1}}(y)=\rank\mathbf{M_{\ell}}(y)
.
As
\mathbf{M_{\ell+1}}(y)
is anPSD matrix, it admits a Gram factorization

y_{\alpha,\beta}=z_{\alpha}^{\dagger}z_{\beta},\qquad\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell+1},

(143)

where
z_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{R}
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell+1}
.
Then by the rank condition we have that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:Span}\lin\{z_{\alpha}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\}\equiv\lin\{z_{\alpha}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell+1}\}\equiv\mathbb{C}^{R}.

(144)

For
j\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
, we define the
shift operators

\mathbf{T}_{j}\colon\mathbb{C}^{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{R}
by

\mathbf{T}_{j}z_{\alpha}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}z_{\alpha+e_{j}},\qquad\forall\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1},

(145)

which we extend by linearity to any vector in
\mathbb{C}^{R}
.
Indeed, this is possible because
\lin\{z_{\alpha}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\}\equiv\mathbb{C}^{R}
(by
Equation

144
), and
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}
.
Intuitively,
\mathbf{T}_{j}
increases the
j
-th index of a moment basis vector, i.e., it maps the Gram vector associated with
z^{\alpha}
to the one associated with
z^{\alpha+e_{j}}
.
However, as
\{z_{\alpha}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}\}
are not necessarily linearly independent, we need to show that the shift operators are well defined.

The only subtlety is that the family
\{z_{\alpha}\}
may be linearly dependent, so we must verify that this definition is independent of the chosen representation of a vector in
\mathbb{C}^{R}
.
For this, we show that
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}}\lambda_{\alpha}z_{\alpha}=0
implies that
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}}\lambda_{\alpha}\mathbf{T}_{j}z_{\alpha}=0
.
Specifically, we show that

\Big\lVert\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}}\lambda_{\alpha}z_{\alpha}\Big\rVert_{2}^{2}=\Big\lVert\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}}\lambda_{\alpha}\mathbf{T}_{j}z_{\alpha}\Big\rVert_{2}^{2}

(146)

for the Euclidean norm.
Indeed, by the Toeplitz constraint in (
140
), it follows that

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}}\lambda_{\alpha}z_{\alpha}\Big\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}}\overline{\lambda_{\alpha}}\lambda_{\beta}z^{\dagger}_{\alpha}z_{\beta}

(147a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}}\overline{\lambda_{\alpha}}\lambda_{\beta}y_{\alpha,\beta}

(147b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}}\overline{\lambda_{\alpha}}\lambda_{\beta}y_{\alpha+e_{j},\beta+e_{j}}

by
Equation

140

(147c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}}\overline{\lambda_{\alpha}}\lambda_{\beta}z^{\dagger}_{\alpha+e_{j}}z_{\beta+e_{j}}

(147d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}}\overline{\lambda_{\alpha}}\lambda_{\beta}(\mathbf{T}_{j}z_{\alpha})^{\dagger}\mathbf{T}_{j}z_{\beta}

(147e)

\displaystyle=\Big\lVert\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{(j)}_{\ell+1}}\lambda_{\alpha}\mathbf{T}_{j}z_{\alpha}\Big\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(147f)

Hence the shift preserves all linear relations among the generators, so
\mathbf{T}_{j}
is well defined.

Step 2: Showing that the shift operators admit a common eigenbasis.

Next, we show that the shift operators
\mathbf{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{T}_{M}
are simultaneously diagonalizable, i.e., they have a common eigenbasis.
At a high level, Toeplitz invariance gives isometric shifts, and commutativity then lets us diagonalize all shifts in one common basis.

First, we show that the shift operators are unitary.
In particular, we show that they are isometries, i.e., they preserve inner products, and therefore, they are unitary.
To see this, consider arbitrary
j\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
.
By the Toeplitz constraints in
Equation

140
, and since
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\equiv\bigcap_{r=1}^{M}\mathcal{A}^{(r)}_{\ell+1}
, we have that

y_{\alpha+e_{j},\beta+e_{j}}=y_{\alpha,\beta}\qquad\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell},

(148)

From this it follows, for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
, that

\langle\mathbf{T}_{j}z_{\alpha},\,\mathbf{T}_{j}z_{\beta}\rangle=\langle z_{\alpha+e_{j}},\,z_{\beta+e_{j}}\rangle=y_{\alpha+e_{j},\beta+e_{j}}=y_{\alpha,\beta}=\langle z_{\alpha},\,z_{\beta}\rangle,

(149)

and since, by (
144
), we also have
\lin\{z_{\alpha}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\}=\mathbb{C}^{R}

5
Discussion

This work studies parametrized quantum circuitoptimization for poly-depth constant-parameter ansätze under standard assumptions.
The main contribution is anFPRASfor this problem, which is the first such result to our knowledge.
The fully polynomial randomized approximation schemeconsists of a three-step classical–quantum pipeline consisting of a data-acquisition phase where queries to the quantum device are performed, a reconstruction phase where the optimization problem is formulated by using multidimensional Fast Fourier Transformon the queried data, and a classical optimization phase where the resulting Hermitian trigonometric polynomialoptimization problem is solved by a max-degree trigonometric moment/sum of squareshierarchy.
The key insight is that the optimization problem can be solved efficiently by using the structure of the generator spectra and the parameter-sharing matrix, which allows for a low-degree trigonometric moment/sum of squaresrelaxation to be exact and for the optimization problem to be solved in polynomial time.

This result leads to several practical implications.
First,
Algorithm

1
can always be used (under natural assumptions) for convergence to a global
\epsilon
-approximation of the optimal value with high probability, which is a stronger guarantee than what is typically provided by variational quantum algorithmoptimizers, which are local descent methods and can get stuck in local minima.
Second, given some parametrized quantum circuit
\mathbf{U}
with integer spectral differences, possibly after rescaling, and with observable
\mathbf{O}
fixed, our result can be used to bound the number of queries and the time complexity for reaching any target error
\epsilon
and success probability
1-\delta
.
This is because, for fixed
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{O}
, the assumptions are trivially satisfied at the instance level, and the complexity of solving that parametrized quantum circuitoptimization problem can be computed directly from
Theorem

3
.
Finally, as the method is non-adaptive and all queries to the quantum hardware are performed in bulk before the optimization step, the total number of queries is known a priori, which allows for scheduling access to the quantum device and for the classical optimization task to run separately or be outsourced.
Taken together, these practical implications provide a useful tool for computing the difficulty of optimizing parametrized quantum circuits.
