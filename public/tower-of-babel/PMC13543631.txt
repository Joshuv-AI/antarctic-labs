Factors influencing delay to diagnosis and treatment among pediatric oncology patients at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Aron Kiros Bipsue, Hussen Mekonnen Asfaw, Nete Tewfik Yusuf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood cancer represents a significant cause of morbidity and mortality among children under 15 years of age and is a growing public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia.ObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to assess factors Influencing delay to diagnosis and treatment initiation among pediatric cancer patients attending at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital oncology unit in Addis Abeba, Ethiopia, in 2019.MethodsAn institutional-based cross-sectional study involving 244 pediatric cancer patients was conducted between February and April of 2019. Data were collected from parents/caregivers using a structured questionnaire through face-to-face interviews and supported by review of medical records. Bivariate and multivariate analyses with adjusted odds ratios were employed to evaluate the association between dependent and independent factors. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA (Version 14) with a significance level of P < 0.05.ResultA total of 244 children participated, with an average age of 6.4 (± SD 3.2 years). One hundred twenty-seven (52.0%) were reported as patient delays (> 30 days), while 179 (73%) were reported as health system delays. Children aged 5–9 years AOR:2.98; 95% CI,1.35, 6.57; three times delayed than children 0–4 years; where as children from rural areas AOR:2.28; 95% CI, 1.07, 4.88; were about 2.28 delayed as compared to children who come from urban.Furthermore, parents of children who visited traditional healers AOR: 7.85, 95% CI; 3.88,15.89 were more likely to be delayed as compared to their counter’s parts. Health system-related factors, such as lack of medical insurance AOR: 5.52, 95%CI: 2.61, 11.69 and first visit to a health institution AOR: 16.13, 95%CI: 4.00, 65.03, were identified as the cause of delay.Conclusion and recommendationsIn conclusion, prolonged patient and health system delays were significantly associated with age AOR: 2.98; 95% CI: 1.35–6.57), rural residence AOR: 2.28; 95% CI: 1.07–4.88, use of traditional healers AOR: 7.85; 95% CI: 3.88–15.89), low disease awareness, and lack of health insurance at diagnosis (AOR = 5.52; 95% CI: 2.61–11.69). To reduce delays, targeted health education for parents and healthcare providers, improved early detection and referral systems, strengthened multisectoral collaboration, expansion and decentralization of pediatrics oncology care facilities and further qualitative research to explore underlying causes of delay are recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16365-9.

Introduction

Cancer is disease as malignant and uncontrolled proliferation of abnormal cells that can invade surrounding tissues and spread to distant organs [1]. pediatric cancer is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in children under the age of 15, it is an increasing public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where the majority of pediatric cancer deaths occur [1, 2]. Although many childhood cancers are highly curable when detected early and treated effectively, survival rates in Sub-Saharan Africa remain extremely low, fewer than 10% of children diagnosed with cancer survive, mostly due to delays in diagnosis and treatment initiation [1, 2].

Delayed diagnosis of childhood cancer is a major contributor to poor treatment outcomes [1]. Children often present to health facilities at advanced stages of the disease, limiting therapeutic options and reducing survival chances [1]. Multiple factors contribute to these delays, including low parental awareness of early warning signs, financial constraints, often delay care-seeking behavior. cultural beliefs and reliance on traditional healers, weak referral systems, misdiagnosis at primary healthcare levels, and limited oncology diagnostic and treatment facilities [1–5]. In addition, shortages of trained healthcare professionals and inadequate availability of essential anticancer medicines further compound the problem [1–10].

In Ethiopia, more than 68.8% of patients were detected with advanced disease, indicating a delay in seeking diagnosis and treatment [11]. Delayed diagnosis and treatment are critical determinants of poor outcomes in childhood cancer [1, 7–9].

Without a clear understanding of the factors that contribute to these these delays, policymakers and healthcare professionals will be unable to devise focused, evidence-based interventions to improve early detection and referral systems. However, there is a scarcity of local research on the precise factors that contribute to delayed cancer detection and treatment among children. As a result, there is a need to investigate the factors associated with delayed diagnosis and treatment among pediatric cancer patients in the study area. Identifying these characteristics will give information to drive public health initiatives, improve service delivery, boost early detection efforts, and, eventually, improve survival and quality of life for children with cancer.

Methods

Study area and period

The study was conducted at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH) pediatric oncology unit from February – April / 2019. Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital is the largest referral hospital in Ethiopia with bed capacity of 752. The hospital has been offering surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy and palliative care for pediatrics and adult cancer patients [12].

Study design and sampling procedures

Health facility based cross-sectional survey was conducted at Tikur Anbessa specialized hospitals in Addis Ababa, from February – April, 2019.

The sample size was estimated using a single population formula by taking 50% of the proportion because there had been no previous equivalent study on factors influencing delay to diagnosis and treatment among pediatric oncology patients, with a 5% level of significance and a margin of error of 0.05. Using these assumptions, the minimum sample size was 384. However, the total number of patient flow during data collection was 525, and adjustment was performed using correction formula and 10% non-response rate was added. Furthermore, the sample size for different factors was calculated, and the largest sample size 244 was taken to reduce sampling errors.

Respondents were selected primarily using the previous patient flow as a sampling frame, and cases who came for treatment initiation and those on follow-up during data collection were involved, then every two cases were selected using systematic random sampling to maximize the distribution of cases. The first number was identified from the list of appointment logbook drown by lottery methods.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected using interviewer-administered structured questionnaires translated to Amharic using language experts and back translated to English to maintain it consistency. The data collection tools were used from previous similar studies [13, 14], and adapted to the local context. Four BSc nurses and two MSc oncology nurses were involved in data collection and supervision respectively. The data collectors’ and supervisors were recruited based on their previous experience in data collection, relevance of qualification and ability of the local language. Multilingual data collectors were selected in order to capture study subjects coming from different language background. Training was given for three consecutive days to make them familiar with the tool and interview techniques. Emphasis was given to ethical issues, privacy, and safety of the study subjects and the interviewers. The data collection tools were pre-tested in the same units on 10% of the total sample size but the samples used for the pretest were excluded from the actual data collection. Unclear, ambiguous, or culturally inappropriate questions were revise accordingly.

A fieldwork manual was developed by the principal investigator, and used by all research teams. To ensure the quality of the data and minimize inter-interviewer variation, about 5% of the respondents were reinterviewed at random by the principal investigator and supervisors and checked for consistency. In addition, daily check and follow-up debriefing meetings were done by the supervisors and investigator.

Analysis

Pre-coded responses were double entered in EPI Info version 3.5.2 software, to check their consistency, cleaned and were exported to STATA for Windows version 14 for statistical analysis. statistical analysis used were percentage, frequency, bivariate, and multiple logistic regression analysis. Variables found to be significant at the bivariate level, (P < 0.05) were selected and included in multiple logistic regression analysis to control potential confounding variables. Prior to fitting the multivariable logistic regression model, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). A VIF value greater than 5 was considered indicative of moderate multicollinearity, while VIF values greater than 10 were considered indicative of serious multicollinearity. Variables exceeding these cut-off points were examined and addressed before inclusion in the final mode. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals were reported, and statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Eighty-four (34.4%) mothers and 55 (22.5%) fathers have no education. Parent’s level of awareness about cancer

One hundred seventy-seven (72.5%) of parents had never heard of cancer before their children’s diagnosis. However, 67 (27.5%) of them said that they had heard about cancer before their child was diagnosed, 144 (59%) of parents believed cancer was curable, 135(55.3%) visited traditional healers. The most prevalent reasons for patient delays were 115 (47.1%) presumption of healing by itself, and 70 (28.7%) reported consulting traditional healers (Table 2).

Discussion

A longer timeframe for pediatric cancer detection and treatment may increase the proportion of late stages. Delays in diagnosing and treating pediatric cancer patients may have an influence on their prognosis and quality of life [1]. This study identified contextual patient and health system delays.

Regarding the patient delay of the 244 children, 127 (52.0%) were classified as having patient delay with 120 median days of stay at home with their symptoms. These median days of delay were slightly comparable study conducted in Kenya with the median patient delay of 104 days [15], were as multi country study from Uganda and western Kenya, the patient delays were substantially shorter, at 30 and 63 median days, respectively [16]. The difference might be related to the influence of patient characteristics such as parent’s perceptions of illness, poor access to health care facilities, possibly limited availability of transportation and dependency on traditional practices may have contributed to a longer patient delay.

In our study, children aged 5–9 years were shown to be at a higher risk of lengthier patient delays than children aged 0–4 years. This is similar to a study from Egypt at 6.5 years (± SD 3.8) [17], Peru at 6.7 (± SD 4.6) [18], and Canada at 7.7 years (± SD 5.5) [19] which demonstrated that the age of the patient was a risk factor for patient delay. This may be due to, close-up parental observation of the child might help in the recognition of symptoms while among older children the detection of signs and symptoms may be more frequently initiated by the patients themselves, and may be more unwilling to disclose symptoms to their parents.

Lliving in rural area was found to be risk factor for patient delay. In contrast to these findings, studies done among Canadian residents did not show significant association with patient delayed [19]. This discrepancy might be due to difference in socio-demographic characteristics and in urban may have more access to health services than residents of rural areas.

In our study, more than half of 135(55.3%) parents reported the use of alternative treatment before coming to TASH. This is similar to previous study reported from Kenya were many parents use alternative medicine for their children [13] .This may be due to the accessibility and affordability of traditional medicine than the modern treatments, and cultural influence and trust might be the contributing factor.

In terms of awareness, 67 (27.3%) of parents had heard of cancer before their child was diagnosed. The finding is lower than study conducted in Bangladesh and Kenya, where 92% and 82% of parents reported that previously heard about the childhood malignancy and helped them to send to cancer clinic [13, 20]. The difference might be related to educational differences, place of residency, lack of information, culture and socioeconomic differences of the study subjects.

In this study, 179 (73%), were having health system delay with 210 median days. This is higher than the median health system delay in Kenya, which was 91 days [20]. The difference could be attributed to health professional personnel’ inability to suspect and make the appropriate diagnosis early. It could also be poor referral mechanisms and a lack of diagnostic and treatment facilities in the country.

Our finding indicated that more than half 144 (59%) of the patient with malignancies were initially misdiagnosed. This in line with a study conducted in South Africa that initial misdiagnoses were reported 58% [21]. It higher than a study conducted in Egypt that 39.5% of the patients were misdiagnosed [22]. The difference might be due to the type of cancer may influenced a health professional’s ability to make a correct diagnosis, and the patient’s first interaction with a primary care physician has a significant impact on early childhood cancer. The diagnosis and Lack of information by health care providers in recognizing early indications of childhood cancer.

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between variables. There is also a potential for social desirability bias. Additionally, the study included only patients attending a tertiary cancer center, which may limit generalizability, although participants were carefully selected from patients referred nationwide.

In conclusion, prolonged patient and health system delays were significantly associated with age AOR: 2.98; 95% CI; 1.35–6.57, rural residence AOR: 2.28; 95% CI; 1.07–4.88, use of traditional healers AOR: 7.85; 95% CI; 3.88–15.89, low disease awareness, and lack of health insurance at diagnosis AOR = 5.52; 95% CI: 2.61–11.69. To reduce delays, targeted health education for parents and healthcare providers, establish early detection and referral systems, strengthen multisectoral collaboration, expansion and decentralization of pediatric oncology service, and further qualitative research to explore the underlying causes of delay are suggested.
