A pH‑responsive hydrogel delivering ISOC1 modRNA attenuates disc degeneration by promoting BIRC6‑mediated MYC degradation and inhibiting SBSN expression
Authors: Kun Wang, Zhiqiang Wang, Yanzheng Gao, Rui Sun, Guowang Zhang, Xiaofeng Lian, Xin Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD), a principal cause of chronic low back pain, is characterized by nucleus pulposus cell (NPC) apoptosis and currently lacks effective therapies. Through integrated multi-omics and Mendelian randomization analyses, we identified isochorismatase domain-containing 1 (ISOC1) as a key therapeutic target. To achieve controlled, context-specific expression within the acidic disc microenvironment, we designed an injectable, pH-responsive polyvinyl alcohol–phenylboronic acid-functionalized chitosan (PVA–csPBA) hydrogel for delivery of modified ISOC1 mRNA (modRNA). Mechanistically, ISOC1 promotes BIRC6-mediated ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of MYC, thereby suppressing its transcriptional target SBSN. Suppression of the MYC/SBSN pathway directly ameliorates lactate-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis in NPCs, preserving cellular viability and extracellular matrix homeostasis. In a rat IDD model, localized delivery of PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA effectively maintained disc hydration, mitigated nucleus pulposus tissue degeneration, and reduced annulus fibrosus fibrosis without observable systemic adverse effects. Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation, a major contributor to spinal disability, arises from intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD). The condition’s hallmark symptoms—chronic low back pain and sciatica—impose substantial clinical and socioeconomic burdens on aging populations worldwide [1]. Current therapeutic approaches remain predominantly symptomatic, failing to halt or reverse the underlying degenerative cascade. A central pathological event in IDD is the progressive depletion of nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs) [2]. While IDD was historically viewed as a passive consequence of mechanical stress and aging, it is now recognized as an active process driven by programmed cell death and mitochondrial dysfunction [3, 4]. However, the upstream regulators governing NPC apoptosis remain poorly defined.

Through integrative analysis of single-cell transcriptomic, Mendelian randomization, and functional genomic data, we identified isochorismatase domain-containing 1 (ISOC1) as a critical regulator in the pathogenesis of IDD. ISOC1, a member of the isochorismatase hydrolase superfamily, has recently been characterized as a peroxisomal constituent implicated in regulating inflammation, apoptosis, and cancer cell migration [5–7]. Although the functional role of ISOC1 in apoptosis has been established in hepatobiliary and oncological contexts, its relevance to NPC survival during IDD has not been investigated. The intervertebral disc, as the body’s largest avascular structure, relies predominantly on glycolysis for energy metabolism. While the precise etiology of IDD remains elusive, the condition is strongly associated with aging and the accumulation of metabolic byproducts, particularly lactate [8, 9]. Studies have demonstrated markedly elevated lactate levels in degenerated discs, which actively promote NPC apoptosis—a pivotal event driving degenerative progression [10]. This unique acidic and avascular microenvironment presents a dual challenge: it intrinsically impairs tissue self-repair while extrinsically limiting the efficacy of conventional pharmacological interventions [11].

Chemically modified mRNA (modRNA) has gained considerable attention as a gene therapy platform, offering transient yet efficient protein expression without the risks of genomic integration [12]. Produced by in vitro transcription from DNA templates, modRNA exhibits enhanced resistance to RNase degradation and, unlike unmodified mRNA, does not provoke innate immune responses [13]. The clinical success of mRNA vaccines has stimulated interest in extending this technology to regenerative medicine, with promising results reported in cardiac repair [14]. Notable examples include PSAT1 modRNA, which augments the serine synthesis pathway to potentiate cardiac repair following myocardial infarction [15]. Similarly, human adipose-derived stem cells engineered to co-express VEGFA and bFGF modRNA have demonstrated the capacity to promote stable neovascularization and improve cardiac function after ischemic injury [16]. Although modRNA overcomes the instability and immunogenicity of native mRNA, efficient delivery to target cells remains a critical challenge—particularly within the avascular and acidic microenvironment of degenerated discs, where conventional delivery approaches are largely ineffective. To address this limitation, we leveraged pH-responsive hydrogel systems that enable minimally invasive administration and microenvironment-triggered release [17], thereby establishing a targeted delivery strategy specifically designed for the degenerative disc. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), a biocompatible and water-soluble polymer, demonstrates excellent mRNA encapsulation capacity through electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged mRNA backbone. Complexation with phenylboronic acid-functionalized chitosan (csPBA) induces a rapid sol–gel transition, forming stable hydrogels via dynamic boronate ester bonds [18]. These bonds remain stable under physiological conditions but undergo accelerated hydrolysis in acidic environments, enabling triggered payload release within degenerative disc tissue. The convergence of pH-responsive hydrogel technology with modRNA delivery thus presents an opportunity to develop IDD-specific therapeutics that exploit the acidic microenvironment for controlled, localized drug release.

Here, through an integrative multi-omics approach combining single-cell transcriptomics, Mendelian randomization, and functional genomics, we identified isochorismatase domain-containing 1 (ISOC1) as a previously unrecognized regulator of IDD pathogenesis. In this study, we not only establish ISOC1 as a critical mediator of NPC apoptosis but also delineate its mechanistic action within the degenerative disc microenvironment. Our findings reveal a targetable molecular node linking cellular metabolism to apoptosis execution in IDD, offering a novel therapeutic framework for combating this prevalent degenerative disorder.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The training datasets GSE230809 and GSE124272, together with the validation dataset GSE15227, were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The GSE230809 dataset comprised nucleus pulposus tissue samples from 3 non-degenerated controls and 8 patients with IDD. The GSE124272 training set included peripheral blood mononuclear cell samples from 16 individuals, encompassing both IDD patients and healthy controls. The GSE15227 validation set contained tissue samples from 3 patients with IDD (Pfirrmann grade IV) and 7 controls (Pfirrmann grades II and III).

scRNA-seq analysis to identify highly variable genes

Initial data preprocessing was conducted using the R package Seurat, including quality control, normalization, and identification of highly variable genes. Quality control thresholds were set as follows: nFeature_RNA between 350 and 6,000, and percent.mt < 15%. Data normalization was performed using the NormalizeData function. Highly variable genes were subsequently identified using the FindVariableFeatures function based on the mean–variance relationship.

Dimensionality reduction, clustering, cell annotation, and visualization

The selected highly variable genes were scaled using the ScaleData function, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) for linear dimensionality reduction. Statistically significant principal components were determined using the JackStraw and ScoreJackStraw functions. Graph-based clustering was performed using the FindClusters function, and cells were visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) at a resolution of 0.2. Cell type annotation was performed based on canonical marker genes reported in the literature, and annotated clusters were visualized on UMAP plots.

Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis

MR analysis was performed to evaluate the causal relationship between circulating plasma proteins and IDD, using plasma protein quantitative trait loci from genome-wide association study (GWAS) databases as exposure variables and IDD as the outcome. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) extracted from GWAS datasets served as instrumental variables (IVs), and genetic association data for IDD were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS database. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results, followed by Steiger directionality testing for comprehensive causal inference.

Mendelian randomization statistical analysis

The TwoSampleMR R package was used to extract instrumental variables for exposure and outcome traits via the extract_instruments function. Instrumental variables significantly associated with exposure traits were identified using a genome-wide significance threshold of p < 5 × 10⁻⁸. To eliminate IVs in linkage disequilibrium, clumping was performed with the following parameters: clump = TRUE, R² < 0.001, and clumping window = 10,000 kb. Based on GWAS outcome data and the selected IVs, variants significantly associated with the outcome were excluded (proxies = TRUE; r² = 0.8). The strength of each IV was assessed by calculating the F-statistic; values > 10 indicated adequate predictive strength and absence of weak instrument bias. Heterogeneity among IVs was evaluated using the mr_heterogeneity function, with Q_pval > 0.05 indicating no substantial heterogeneity. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the mr_pleiotropy_test function, where p > 0.05 indicated no evidence of directional pleiotropy. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed using the mr_leaveoneout function. Causal effects were estimated using the inverse variance–weighted (IVW) method, and MR-Steiger filtering was applied to infer the direction of causality between exposure and outcome; exposure traits with correct causal direction (Steiger test = TRUE) were retained for subsequent analysis.

Construction of gene–gene interaction (GGI) Networks and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Key IDD-associated genes were identified by integrating single-cell differentially expressed genes (DEGs), bulk transcriptomic DEGs, and MR analysis results. GGI networks for these key genes were constructed using the GeneMANIA database (https://genemania.org/). For GSEA, the MSigDB “c2.cp.kegg.v2023.1.Hs.symbols.gmt” gene set was used as the reference, and Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess gene–phenotype associations.

Sample collection

Venous blood was collected from 58 IDD patients and 58 age-matched healthy controls into EDTA-coated tubes. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation (3,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) and stored at − 70 °C until analysis. Inclusion criteria for IDD patients (aged 30–45 years) were MRI-confirmed lumbar disc herniation with severe low back pain and/or sciatica within 4 weeks prior to enrollment and no medication use within the preceding 3 months. Healthy controls (aged 30–45 years) had no spinal symptoms or recent medication use. Exclusion criteria included spinal deformities, trauma, metabolic or rheumatic disorders, congenital abnormalities, infections, tumors, and age > 60 years. Nucleus pulposus tissues (≥ 5 mm thickness) were obtained during spinal surgeries and stratified by Pfirrmann grading into control (n = 4 males with congenital scoliosis, Pfirrmann grade II) and IDD (n = 4 males with lumbar disc herniation, Pfirrmann grade V) groups. ISOC1 expression was analyzed by immunohistochemistry, RT–qPCR, and western blotting. Detailed demographic and clinical information is provided in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5.

Total rna extraction and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT–qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from plasma samples using the Total RNA Purification Kit with Spin Column Separation (BioTeke, Wuxi, China). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the HiScript III First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit with gDNA Wiper (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reactions were incubated at 50 °C for 15 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 85 °C for 5 s. RT–qPCR was performed using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) with cDNA diluted 1:1 for plasma samples and 1:10 for tissue samples. Reactions were conducted in a total volume of 20 µL under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 15 s. ISOC1 mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences were as follows: ISOC1 forward, 5′-TGTGCATCCAACAAACTGCC-3′; reverse, 5′-CTATGATCCCGGTTCGAGCG-3′.

Western blotting

Protein lysates were prepared from NP tissue samples using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0009, Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-ISOC1 (1:500, 25844-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-p-DRP1 (Ser616) (1:500, ab314755, Abcam), anti-COX IV (1:1,000, ab202554, Abcam), anti-DRP1 (1:1,000, ab184247, Abcam), anti-mitofusin-1 (1:1,000, 14739, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-caspase-3 (1:2,000, ab184787, Abcam), anti-Bax (1:1,000, ab32503, Abcam), anti-PI3K (1:10,000, ab302958, Abcam), anti-p-PI3K (1:1,000, ab278545, Abcam), anti-AKT (1:10,000, ab179463, Abcam), and anti-p-AKT (1:1,000, ab38449, Abcam). Membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (P10200, New Cell & Molecular Biotech, Suzhou, China), and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunohistochemical staining

NP tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h at room temperature, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking with serum for 1 h, primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with species-matched secondary antibodies for 60 min at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and identified as brown–yellow cytoplasmic staining under light microscopy.

Synthesis of phenylboronic acid-functionalized chitosan (csPBA)

The functionalized chitosan derivative was synthesized via carbodiimide-mediated coupling. Briefly, 4-carboxyphenylboronic acid (0.5 g), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS, 6.0 g), and N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCl, 4.5 g) were dissolved in 300 mL of distilled water, and the pH was adjusted to 5.5 using 0.1 M HCl. Following activation of carboxyl groups under continuous stirring at 25℃ for 20 min, chitosan (0.4 g) was added to initiate the conjugation reaction. The reaction proceeded for 4 h, after which the product was dialyzed extensively against distilled water using a regenerated cellulose membrane for 6 days. The purified product was lyophilized to obtain the final csPBA conjugate.

modRNA synthesis and transfection

ISOC1 modRNA was synthesized by GenScript Biotech Corporation (Shanghai, China). The open reading frame sequence of ISOC1 modRNA is provided in Supplementary Table 6. For in vitro transfection, 1 µL of modRNA and 2 µL of Lipofectamine MessengerMAX transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were separately diluted in serum-free Opti-MEM medium, combined, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min to allow RNA–lipid nanoparticle complex formation. After 4 h of transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh serum-supplemented medium. This transfection protocol was optimized for 2 × 10⁵ NPCs per well. For in vivo delivery, 2 µL of modRNA and 4 µL of Lipofectamine MessengerMAX were individually diluted in 2 µL of Opti-MEM, combined, and incubated at room temperature for 35 min to complete complex formation.

Hydrogel preparation and characterization

For in vitro studies, the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA hydrogel was prepared by first incorporating 100 µL of ISOC1 modRNA–lipid complex (prepared as described in Sect. 2.12; containing 2 µL of ISOC1 modRNA) into a csPBA solution. Subsequently, 250 µL of this intermediate mixture was combined with 250 µL of PVA solution to yield the final hydrogel (total volume: 500 µL). For in vivo applications, 10 µL of ISOC1 modRNA–lipid complex (containing 2 µL of ISOC1 modRNA) was incorporated into a csPBA solution. Then, 25 µL of the resulting mixture was combined with 25 µL of PVA solution to generate the final PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA hydrogel (total volume: 50 µL). The elemental composition of lyophilized hydrogels was analyzed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS).

Fourier-Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

The chemical structure of the hydrogel was characterized by FT-IR spectroscopy. Lyophilized hydrogel samples were analyzed using an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory equipped with a diamond crystal. Background spectra were collected from ambient air and automatically subtracted. Freeze-dried samples were uniformly pressed onto the crystal surface to ensure optimal optical contact. Spectra were acquired in the mid-infrared region (4,000–500 cm⁻¹) at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ with 32 accumulated scans. Interferograms were processed using Happ–Genzel apodization prior to Fourier transformation. Final spectra were baseline-corrected and examined for characteristic absorption bands to confirm the formation of expected chemical bonds within the hydrogel network.

Rheological testing and injectability assessment

The mechanical properties of the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA hydrogel were evaluated using a controlled-stress rotational rheometer equipped with parallel-plate geometry. Oscillatory frequency-sweep tests were performed over an angular frequency range of 0.1–100 rad/s at a constant strain amplitude of 1% and 37 °C to determine the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) as functions of frequency. Injectability was assessed by extruding the hydrogel through a 29-gauge needle at a constant rate of 50 mm/min, and filament continuity was visually evaluated.

pH-responsive degradation and modRNA release

The degradation profile and modRNA release kinetics of the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA hydrogel were assessed under acidic conditions simulating the degenerative disc microenvironment (lactate buffer, pH 6.5). Cumulative ISOC1 modRNA release was quantified in collected supernatants using the Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit (R-32700, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Residual hydrogel mass was measured in parallel to monitor dissolution over time.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. NPCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and cultured with either PVA–csPBA or PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA hydrogel for 1, 3, and 6 days. At each time point, CCK-8 working solution was added to each well, and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm to determine cell viability.

Cell culture and experimental groups

Human NPCs were obtained from SUNNCELL and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (KeyGEN BioTECH) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cellcook) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. To simulate the acidic microenvironment characteristic of IDD in vitro, NPCs were exposed to lactate (100 µM) for 48 h based on preliminary dose-response experiments (Supplementary Fig. 1); PBS-treated cells served as controls. Therapeutic efficacy of PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA. Four groups were established: (1) Control: NPCs cultured under standard conditions; (2) Lactate: NPCs treated with lactate; (3) Lactate + PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA: NPCs pretreated with lactate followed by PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA; (4) Lactate + PVA–csPBA: NPCs treated with lactate followed by PVA–csPBA. Role of SBSN in ISOC1-mediated protection. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

NPCs from all experimental groups were processed for TEM analysis. Cells were fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde for 2 h, followed by post-fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide. Samples were then subjected to en bloc uranyl acetate staining, graded ethanol dehydration, and epoxy resin embedding. Ultrathin sections (~ 70 nm) were prepared using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome, mounted on copper grids, and counterstained with lead citrate. Ultrastructural features, including mitochondrial morphology, were examined using a FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit transmission electron microscope.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

To elucidate the anti-apoptotic mechanisms of ISOC1 in NPCs, a lactate-induced acidic microenvironment was established to simulate disc degeneration conditions. Comparative transcriptomic profiling was performed between lactate-treated control cells and PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA-treated cells using RNA sequencing (GeneChem Biological Co., Shanghai, China). Following RNA extraction, library preparation, and quality control, differentially expressed genes were identified and subjected to functional annotation using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses.

TUNEL assay

Apoptotic cells were detected using a TUNEL Assay Kit (C1086, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following experimental treatments, NPCs cultured in 6-well plates (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 30 min, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 5 min at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark, washed, mounted with anti-fade medium, and imaged by fluorescence microscopy.

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (ΔΨm) assay

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using the Enhanced Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit with JC-1 (C2003S, Beyotime). After treatment, NPCs cultured in 6-well plates (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) were washed with PBS and incubated with JC-1 working solution diluted 1:1 in culture medium at 37 °C for 20 min. Following two washes with JC-1 buffer, cells were maintained in fresh culture medium and imaged using fluorescence microscopy.

Mitochondrial fluorescence staining

Mitochondrial distribution and morphology were evaluated using MitoTracker Deep Red FM (C1032, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. NPCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and subjected to the indicated treatments. The culture medium was removed and replaced with MitoTracker working solution, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 15 min. The staining solution was then replaced with pre-warmed (37 °C) fresh culture medium, and mitochondria were visualized by fluorescence microscopy.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) detection

Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using a Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime). DCFH-DA was diluted 1:1,000 in HBSS to a final concentration of 10 µmol/L. After removal of the culture medium, cells were incubated with 1 mL of diluted DCFH-DA solution at 37 °C for 20 min. Following three washes with HBSS, cells were maintained in fresh serum-free medium and immediately examined by fluorescence microscopy to evaluate intracellular ROS levels.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To investigate MYC-mediated regulation of SBSN promoter activity, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed. A fragment of the SBSN promoter region (− 119 to − 124) was cloned into the pGL3-Basic vector to generate the wild-type reporter construct pGL3-SBSN-WT (containing the sequence CAGTGCCTCACA). A mutant construct (pGL3-SBSN-MUT) was generated by introducing point mutations (CAGTAAGTCACA). Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per well and transfected with 600 ng of plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The pRL-TK vector was co-transfected as an internal control for normalization. After 24 h, cells were harvested and luciferase activities were measured using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). Relative promoter activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly to Renilla luciferase activity.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

Following lactate treatment, HEK293T cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, and the reaction was quenched with 125 mM glycine. Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, harvested in lysis buffer, and sonicated to generate DNA fragments of 500–1,000 bp. Chromatin lysates were pre-cleared with protein A/G Sepharose beads and subjected to immunoprecipitation using rabbit anti-MYC antibody or control rabbit IgG. Purified ChIP DNA was amplified by PCR using SBSN-specific primers: forward, 5′-AGACCAACTCAGTGCCTCAC-3′; reverse, 5′-CAATTACCCCTGTGAGGCAC-3′.

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-immunoprecipitation was performed as previously described [19]. Protein magnetic beads were washed three times with lysis buffer and incubated with antibodies. Purified antibodies (5 µg) against ISOC1, BIRC6, MYC, or normal IgG were added to 500 µL of cell lysate together with 20 µL of magnetic beads, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. After magnetic separation, the supernatant was discarded. The resulting immunocomplexes were mixed with 1× loading buffer, heated at 95 °C for 10 min, and analyzed by western blotting.

Lentiviral transduction

One day prior to transduction, NPCs and HEK293T cells were seeded into 6-cm culture dishes at a density of 2 × 10⁶ cells per dish. Lentiviral vectors encoding ISOC1, MYC, or SBSN, along with their respective negative control vectors, were obtained from Miaoling Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). Transduction was performed using HiTransG A transduction reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Constructs for overexpression of ISOC1 (ISOC1-OE), MYC (MYC-OE), and SBSN (SBSN-OE) were individually transduced into cells. Stable polyclonal cell populations were established by selection with 5 µg/mL puromycin and used for subsequent experiments.

Rat model of IDD

Six-week-old male Sprague–Dawley rats were obtained from Qinglongshan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Male rats were used exclusively to minimize sex-related variability. Sample size (n = 10 per group) was determined based on previous studies employing similar IDD models [19]. Rats were randomly assigned to four groups: (1) Control; (2) Lactate: intradiscal injection of lactate; (3) Lactate + PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA: intradiscal injection of lactate followed by PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA (40 µg ISOC1 modRNA); (4) Lactate + ISOC1 modRNA: intradiscal injection of lactate followed by ISOC1 modRNA alone (40 µg). Rat caudal intervertebral discs were selected because they are technically accessible for percutaneous injection without laminectomy, retain the essential structural organization of intervertebral discs, and allow multiple adjacent levels to be treated within a single animal, consistent with established rodent IDD models. A 33-gauge needle was inserted perpendicularly into the nucleus pulposus region of the Co5/6, Co6/7, and Co7/8 caudal discs for treatment administration. One month post-injection, rats were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital overdose. Tail vertebrae were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified, and embedded in paraffin for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Safranin O/Fast Green staining. Histological and imaging evaluations were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp.), R software (version 4.2.0), and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). For bioinformatics analyses, single-cell RNA sequencing data were processed using the Seurat R package, with dimensionality reduction and clustering performed via the UMAP algorithm. Mendelian randomization analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR R package, with the inverse variance–weighted method as the primary analytical approach. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test, and horizontal pleiotropy was assessed via MR-Egger regression. Differentially expressed genes from bulk and single-cell RNA-seq datasets were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. For in vitro and in vivo experiments, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple-group comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test for two-group comparisons or the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test for multiple-group comparisons. All in vitro experiments were performed independently at least three times, with representative results shown. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

Integration of multi-omics data identifies ISOC1 as a key therapeutic target in IDD

To systematically identify potential therapeutic targets for intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD), we integrated single-cell RNA sequencing, bulk transcriptomics, and Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis of plasma circulating proteins (Supplementary Fig. 2). By intersecting differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the single-cell dataset GSE230809, the bulk RNA-seq dataset GSE124272, and plasma circulating protein-related genes (PCPGs) identified through MR analysis, we identified two candidate genes—ISOC1 and SHMT1—that were significantly dysregulated across all three datasets (Fig. 1A).

To explore the functional networks of these candidate genes, we constructed a gene–gene interaction (GGI) network using GeneMANIA, which revealed the top 20 genes most strongly associated with ISOC1 and SHMT1, including ISOC2, TMLHE, and ALAS2 (Fig. 1B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further demonstrated that both ISOC1 and SHMT1 were significantly enriched in critical cellular pathways, including DNA replication, ribosome biogenesis, and spliceosome assembly, indicating their involvement in fundamental processes such as DNA replication, protein synthesis, and RNA processing (Fig. 1C).

To assess the diagnostic potential of these genes, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis in both training and validation datasets. In the training set (GSE124272), both ISOC1 and SHMT1 achieved area under the curve (AUC) values greater than 0.7, with significant differential expression between IDD and control samples (Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1D). However, in the independent validation cohort (GSE15227), only ISOC1 maintained robust diagnostic performance (AUC > 0.7) with consistent differential expression (Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05), whereas SHMT1 failed to replicate its diagnostic value (Fig. 1E). The superior and consistent performance of ISOC1 across both datasets established it as a more reliable biomarker and led to its selection as the primary target for subsequent experimental validation.

To establish causal relationships between plasma circulating proteins and IDD risk, we performed two-sample MR analysis using genome-wide association study (GWAS) data. After stringent quality control (p < 5 × 10⁻⁸, r² < 0.001, distance = 10,000 kb), we identified 24 genes with significant causal effects on IDD. Scatter plot analysis revealed that ISOC1 functioned as a risk factor for IDD (OR > 1), with consistent effects across multiple MR methods, including inverse variance–weighted (IVW), weighted median, and MR-Egger approaches (Fig. 1F). Forest plot analysis of individual SNPs confirmed the robustness of this causal relationship, with the overall IVW estimate indicating increased IDD risk associated with elevated ISOC1 levels (Fig. 1G). Sensitivity analyses, including heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy tests, confirmed the reliability of these findings (p > 0.05 for pleiotropy), ruling out potential confounding factors.

Given the importance of immune dysregulation in IDD pathogenesis, we investigated the relationship between candidate genes and immune cell infiltration using single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA). Analysis of 28 immune cell types revealed that 11 populations exhibited significant differential infiltration between IDD and control samples, including activated CD4⁺ T cells, activated CD8⁺ T cells, activated dendritic cells, macrophages, mast cells, and neutrophils. Correlation analysis demonstrated that ISOC1 exhibited the strongest positive correlation with activated CD8⁺ T cells (r = 0.88, p < 0.05), suggesting a potential role in modulating T cell–mediated immune responses in IDD (Fig. 1H).

To identify the specific cell populations driving ISOC1 expression in IDD, we examined its expression across different cell types in the single-cell dataset GSE230809. Among eight annotated cell types (progenitor cells, blood vessel signature cells, stem cells, fibroblasts, pre-chondrocytes, fibrochondrocytes, regulatory chondrocytes, and homeostatic fibroblasts), ISOC1 showed significant differential expression specifically in homeostatic fibroblasts (HomFibro) between IDD and control samples (Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1I). This finding identified HomFibro as the key cell population mediating ISOC1 effects in IDD.

To understand the dynamic regulation of ISOC1 during cell differentiation, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis on HomFibro cells using the Monocle algorithm. Branched expression analysis modeling (BEAM) revealed that ISOC1 exhibited dynamic expression changes along the differentiation trajectory (Fig. 1J).

Finally, we examined ISOC1 expression in clinical specimens. RT–qPCR analysis revealed significantly reduced ISOC1 mRNA levels in the serum of patients with IDD compared with healthy controls (Fig. 1K). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated significantly lower ISOC1 protein levels in degenerated NP tissues than in normal controls (Fig. 1L, M). Western blot analysis confirmed reduced ISOC1 protein expression in degenerated NP tissues, consistent with the immunohistochemistry findings (Fig. 1N, O). Notably, while MR analysis identified elevated circulating ISOC1 as a risk factor for IDD (OR > 1), clinical validation in tissue specimens revealed an inverse pattern—a paradox that is mechanistically reconciled in the Discussion.

Collectively, these multi-omics analyses establish ISOC1 as a promising therapeutic target for IDD, with validated diagnostic value across independent cohorts, a causal relationship to disease risk, specific expression in homeostatic fibroblasts, and involvement in immune modulation and cell differentiation. The failure of SHMT1 to validate in the external cohort, contrasted with the consistent performance of ISOC1, underscores the robustness and clinical relevance of ISOC1 as a therapeutic target.

Synthesis and characterization of PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA hydrogel

Previous studies have established that stable pH-responsive hydrogels can be formed through cross-linking of PVA with csPBA (Fig. 2A). The injectable pH-responsive hydrogel (PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA) was prepared by first complexing ISOC1 modRNA with lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), mixing with 2% PVA, and subsequently combining with an equal volume of 2% csPBA, resulting in rapid formation of a stable hydrogel (Fig. 2B). Figure 2C provides a schematic overview of the ISOC1 modRNA design and synthesis process. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed a porous three-dimensional network architecture in the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA hydrogel (Fig. 2D).

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy confirmed successful lipid nanoparticle (LNP) encapsulation within the hydrogel matrix. The spectrum of bare LNPs displayed characteristic absorption bands indicative of phospholipid composition: C–H stretching vibrations at 2,850–2,920 cm⁻¹, a C = O stretch at 1,737 cm⁻¹, and a composite band at 1,061–1,147 cm⁻¹ assigned to P = O and C–O stretching modes. In the spectrum of the hydrogel-LNP formulation, these LNP-specific signals were substantially attenuated, consistent with surface coating by the hydrogel matrix. Concurrently, a broad peak emerged at approximately 3,317 cm⁻¹, attributable to O–H stretching from water and N–H stretching from the cross-linked network of PVA and phenylboronic acid-functionalized chitosan (Fig. 2E).

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis revealed that the hydrogel was primarily composed of oxygen (53.4 wt%) and carbon (33.6 wt%), with minor amounts of phosphorus (8.1 wt%) and nitrogen (4.8 wt%). On an atomic basis, the fractions of C, N, O, and P were 41.48, 5.12, 49.51, and 3.90 at%, respectively. These results confirm that the hydrogel network is rich in C- and O-containing organic components while incorporating N- and P-bearing functionalities; no other elements were detected within the sensitivity limits of EDS measurement (Fig. 2F).

Following fabrication, the functional characteristics of the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA hydrogel were systematically evaluated. The hydrogel exhibited injectability through a syringe needle and retained shape adaptability after injection (Fig. 3A). The pH-responsive properties were investigated under conditions simulating the acidic microenvironment of degenerative intervertebral discs. Hydrogels were incubated in lactate solution (pH 6.5), with PBS (pH 7.4) serving as a control. Using the RiboGreen quantification assay, we assessed the release profile of ISOC1 modRNA. Under acidic conditions, approximately 70% of modRNA was released within the first 10 days, whereas in PBS, a similar release percentage was not achieved until day 20 (Fig. 3B). The degradation profile demonstrated rapid hydrogel degradation under acidic conditions (60% mass loss within 4 days in lactate) compared with sustained stability in PBS (over 40% remaining at day 14) (Fig. 3C). These findings demonstrate the pH-responsive characteristics of the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA hydrogel, with specific sensitivity to the acidic, lactate-rich microenvironment.

Rheological properties were subsequently characterized. Frequency sweep measurements (0.1–100 Hz) demonstrated that the storage modulus (G′) was consistently greater than the loss modulus (G″), confirming stable hydrogel formation (Fig. 3D). Strain amplitude tests identified a linear viscoelastic regime below 10% strain, beyond which progressive structural breakdown occurred, resulting in complete loss of gel characteristics (Fig. 3E). Oscillatory strain amplitude measurements at fixed frequency revealed complete structural collapse of the hydrogel network under high dynamic strain (100%), as demonstrated by the reduction of G′ below G″. Notably, immediate mechanical recovery was observed upon return to 1% strain, confirming the material’s rapid self-healing capacity (Fig. 3F).

Transfection efficiency of PVA–csPBA@GFP modRNA in NPCs under both PBS and lactate conditions was evaluated using fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h. Successful transfection was achieved under both conditions. The PBS-treated group exhibited substantial GFP signal enhancement within 24 h, whereas the lactate-treated group demonstrated a time-dependent increase in GFP expression throughout the observation period (Fig. 3G–I). Biocompatibility was assessed using CCK-8 assays, which demonstrated that neither PVA–csPBA nor PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA hydrogels exhibited cytotoxic effects, with no significant differences in cell viability among experimental groups (Fig. 3J). Western blot analysis confirmed that ISOC1 protein expression was significantly upregulated in NPCs co-cultured with PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA hydrogel (Fig. 3K).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the PVA–csPBA hydrogel effectively encapsulates ISOC1 modRNA and exhibits pH-responsive release kinetics, enabling targeted delivery of ISOC1 modRNA under acidic conditions characteristic of degenerative discs.

PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA alleviates lactate-induced apoptosis in NPCs

To assess the anti-apoptotic effect of PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA on NPCs, we established an acidic microenvironment simulating the degenerative intervertebral disc by treating cells with lactate. TUNEL staining revealed a significant reduction in the apoptosis rate of NPCs in the Lactate + PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA group compared with the Lactate group (Fig. 4A, B). NPCs co-treated with lactate and PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA exhibited pronounced recovery of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) and marked reduction in intracellular ROS levels compared with lactate-treated cells (Fig. 4C–F).

Mitochondrial morphology was assessed by live-cell imaging using MitoTracker Red. Control NPCs displayed an extensive, interconnected mitochondrial network. In contrast, lactate exposure—with or without PVA–csPBA carrier—induced severe mitochondrial fragmentation, manifesting as discrete, punctate structures. Notably, this fragmentation was largely prevented in the Lactate + PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA group, where mitochondria maintained their tubular architecture, demonstrating that ISOC1 modRNA treatment effectively preserves mitochondrial integrity under acidic stress (Fig. 4G, H). Consistent with these findings, transmission electron microscopy revealed marked attenuation of lactate-induced mitochondrial damage following PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA treatment (Fig. 4I).

Western blot analysis confirmed downregulation of the pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3, along with phosphorylated DRP1 (Ser616), concomitant with upregulation of mitofusin-1 (MFN1) in PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA-treated NPCs compared with the Lactate group (Fig. 4J). Collectively, these results indicate that PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA effectively mitigates lactate-induced apoptosis in NPCs by preserving mitochondrial integrity and function.

To delineate the downstream mechanisms by which ISOC1 modRNA inhibits apoptosis in NPCs, we performed RNA sequencing to compare transcriptomic profiles between the Lactate and PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA groups. Volcano plot analysis identified SBSN as a prominently downregulated gene upon modRNA delivery (Fig. 5A). Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses revealed that differentially expressed genes were associated with cellular stress responses and significantly enriched in the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 5B, C). Based on these findings, we identified SBSN as a key mediator of PVA–csPBA@ISOC1-mediated protection against lactate-induced apoptosis in NPCs.

Integrated analysis of transcription factor databases (JASPAR, KnockTF, hTFtarget, ENCODE, ChEA, and GTRD) identified MYC as a putative transcriptional activator of SBSN (Fig. 5D). To investigate whether ISOC1 suppresses SBSN expression by targeting MYC, we performed luciferase reporter assays. MYC overexpression significantly enhanced transcriptional activity of the wild-type (WT) SBSN promoter, and this transactivation was markedly attenuated by co-expression of ISOC1. In contrast, mutation of the MYC-binding site abolished both MYC-driven activation and the suppressive effect of ISOC1 (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays confirmed that ISOC1 co-expression substantially reduced MYC occupancy at the endogenous SBSN promoter (Fig. 5F). These results indicate that ISOC1 impairs MYC binding to the SBSN promoter, thereby downregulating SBSN transcription.

The suppression of SBSN by ISOC1 modRNA suggested additional regulatory complexity. Screening of the HitPredict database identified the ubiquitin ligase BIRC6 as a potential MYC-interacting protein and modulator of MYC proteostasis (Fig. 5G). To investigate whether ISOC1 promotes BIRC6-mediated ubiquitination of MYC, we first confirmed interactions among ISOC1, BIRC6, and MYC by co-immunoprecipitation (Fig. 5H). The reduction in MYC protein levels upon ISOC1 overexpression was rescued by the proteasome inhibitor MG-132, demonstrating that ISOC1 promotes proteasomal degradation of MYC (Fig. 5I). Immunoprecipitation assays targeting MYC demonstrated that ISOC1 overexpression promoted MYC ubiquitination, which was further enhanced by BIRC6 overexpression. Conversely, BIRC6 knockdown (si-BIRC6) reduced MYC ubiquitination levels (Fig. 5J).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that ISOC1 suppresses SBSN expression by promoting BIRC6-mediated ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of MYC.

PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA alleviates NPC apoptosis through suppression of SBSN

To define the role of SBSN in the anti-apoptotic effect of ISOC1 modRNA, we overexpressed SBSN to counteract its suppression by ISOC1 modRNA. Compared with the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA group, the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA + SBSN-OE group exhibited a significant increase in TUNEL-positive cells, loss of ΔΨm, and elevated ROS levels (Fig. 6A, B, D–G). Transmission electron microscopy revealed a greater abundance of damaged mitochondria in the SBSN overexpression group (Fig. 6C). Mitochondrial staining demonstrated that ISOC1 modRNA rescued lactate-induced network disruption, restoring tubular morphology. However, this rescue was abolished by SBSN overexpression, which promoted a punctate, fragmented phenotype (Fig. 6H, I).

Western blot analysis revealed that compared with the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA group, the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA + SBSN-OE group exhibited upregulation of Bax, caspase-3, p-DRP1 (Ser616), p-PI3K, and p-AKT in NPCs, whereas MFN1 expression was significantly downregulated (Fig. 6J). Taken together, these results demonstrate that PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA ameliorates lactate-induced apoptosis in NPCs through suppression of SBSN expression.

PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA ameliorates lactate-induced IDD in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic potential of PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA, we utilized a rat model of IDD induced by percutaneous caudal disc injection of lactate. MRI assessment demonstrated that PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA preserved disc integrity against lactate-induced damage, as evidenced by maintained hydration and disc height. Furthermore, compared with the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA group, the Lactate + ISOC1 modRNA group (without hydrogel carrier) exhibited reduced hydration, more severe disc height loss, and exacerbated degeneration (Fig. 7B).

Histological evaluation using H&E and Safranin O/Fast Green staining revealed that the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA group exhibited less NP tissue loss and reduced annulus fibrosus fibrosis compared with the Lactate group. Conversely, the Lactate + ISOC1 modRNA group showed more pronounced tissue loss and fibrosis relative to the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA group (Fig. 7C, D).

Discussion

The identification of effective therapeutic targets for IDD has been hampered by disease heterogeneity and limited understanding of causal molecular drivers. In this study, we implemented a multi-omics integration strategy combining single-cell RNA sequencing, bulk transcriptomics, and Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to systematically identify ISOC1 as a pivotal therapeutic target. This triangulation approach offers distinct advantages: scRNA-seq resolved cell type–specific expression patterns [20], bulk RNA-seq provided population-level validation, and MR analysis established a causal relationship between circulating ISOC1 levels and IDD risk, thereby distinguishing causative factors from secondary consequences of disease progression. Notably, while both ISOC1 and SHMT1 emerged from the initial intersection analysis, only ISOC1 demonstrated robust diagnostic performance in the independent external validation cohort (GSE15227), underscoring the necessity of multi-cohort validation in target discovery pipelines. Furthermore, pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed dynamic ISOC1 expression along homeostatic fibroblast differentiation, suggesting its involvement in cell fate determination during disc homeostasis. A noteworthy paradox emerged from our findings. MR analysis demonstrated that elevated circulating ISOC1 is associated with increased IDD risk (OR > 1), yet tissue analysis revealed decreased ISOC1 expression in degenerated NP tissues. We propose that ISOC1 functions as a compensatory protective factor released into the circulation during disc degeneration–associated stress. As NPCs undergo apoptosis, intracellular ISOC1 is released into the bloodstream—analogous to cardiac troponins in myocardial injury—reflecting ongoing tissue damage rather than pathogenic causation. The resulting depletion of tissue-resident ISOC1 compromises local protective mechanisms, further accelerating degeneration. This interpretation is supported by our functional data demonstrating that ISOC1 modRNA delivery effectively protects NPCs by replenishing the depleted local protein pool.

The unique pathophysiological microenvironment of degenerated intervertebral discs poses formidable challenges for conventional drug delivery [21, 22]. The avascular nature of disc tissue severely limits systemic drug penetration, while progressive acidification (pH 5.7–6.5) resulting from lactate accumulation creates a hostile environment that compromises drug stability and cellular uptake efficiency [23]. Previous delivery strategies, including microspheres, nanoparticles, and thermosensitive hydrogels, have achieved limited success in maintaining sustained therapeutic concentrations within the disc space [21, 22, 24]. Our pH-responsive PVA–csPBA hydrogel system addresses these challenges through an innovative design philosophy that exploits pathological acidification as a trigger for controlled payload release. The dynamic boronate ester bonds between PVA and phenylboronic acid-functionalized chitosan remain stable at physiological pH but undergo accelerated hydrolysis in the acidic disc microenvironment, enabling site-specific modRNA release precisely where therapeutic intervention is most needed [18]. This stimulus-responsive mechanism represents a significant advancement over passive diffusion-based delivery systems. The selection of chemically modified mRNA as the therapeutic cargo offers additional translational advantages. Unlike plasmid DNA or viral vectors, modRNA enables transient yet robust protein expression without risks of genomic integration or insertional mutagenesis [25–27]. Furthermore, the demonstrated success of modRNA in cardiac regeneration—exemplified by VEGFA modRNA for neovascularization and PSAT1 modRNA for metabolic reprogramming—provides compelling precedent for extending this platform to musculoskeletal regenerative medicine [15, 16]. Our characterization data confirm that the PVA–csPBA hydrogel preserves modRNA integrity while exhibiting favorable injectability, self-healing properties, and biocompatibility, collectively supporting its potential for minimally invasive clinical application.

A central finding of this study is the elucidation of a previously unrecognized ISOC1/BIRC6/MYC/SBSN signaling axis governing NPC apoptosis. ISOC1, a member of the isochorismatase hydrolase superfamily, has been characterized as a peroxisomal constituent implicated in apoptosis regulation and cancer cell migration [6]. However, its functional relevance to NPC survival and IDD pathogenesis had not been previously established. Our data demonstrate that ISOC1 expression is significantly downregulated in degenerated disc tissues and patient serum, and that restoration of ISOC1 levels through modRNA delivery effectively protects NPCs from lactate-induced apoptosis. Mechanistically, we identified MYC as a key downstream effector of ISOC1-mediated protection. MYC protein stability is tightly regulated by the ubiquitin–proteasome system, with several E3 ligases—including FBXW7 and SKP2—known to target MYC for degradation through distinct mechanisms [28, 29]. Our findings introduce BIRC6 as a novel E3 ubiquitin ligase for MYC in NPC biology. Co-immunoprecipitation experiments revealed that ISOC1 forms a ternary complex with BIRC6 and MYC, and that ISOC1 overexpression enhanced BIRC6-mediated MYC ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. This regulatory mechanism expands the repertoire of MYC-targeting E3 ligases and suggests potential therapeutic opportunities beyond IDD. The identification of SBSN as a direct MYC transcriptional target that promotes NPC apoptosis represents another novel finding. SBSN has been primarily studied in the context of epidermal differentiation and tumor progression [30, 31], but its role in disc biology was previously unknown. Our luciferase reporter and ChIP assays confirmed that MYC directly binds to and activates the SBSN promoter, and that ISOC1-mediated MYC degradation results in transcriptional suppression of SBSN. Rescue experiments demonstrating that SBSN overexpression abrogates the protective effects of ISOC1 modRNA firmly establish SBSN as a functional mediator of this signaling axis. Furthermore, pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement of PI3K–AKT signaling, consistent with the established role of this pathway in regulating cell survival across diverse cellular contexts [32–34]. While our data demonstrate that SBSN overexpression is associated with upregulation of p-PI3K and p-AKT, the precise mechanistic relationship between SBSN and PI3K–AKT signaling remains to be fully elucidated. Future studies employing PI3K-specific inhibitors in combination with SBSN modulation will be necessary to definitively establish the hierarchical position of PI3K–AKT within the ISOC1/MYC/SBSN signaling axis.

Mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a central pathogenic mechanism in IDD, with accumulating evidence demonstrating that impaired mitochondrial dynamics, elevated oxidative stress, and compromised bioenergetics contribute to NPC death [35, 36]. Our findings reveal that ISOC1 modRNA delivery effectively preserves mitochondrial integrity under lactate-induced stress, as evidenced by maintained mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced ROS accumulation, and preserved tubular mitochondrial network architecture. At the molecular level, ISOC1 modRNA treatment suppressed phosphorylation of DRP1 at Ser616—a modification that promotes mitochondrial fission—while simultaneously upregulating MFN1, a key mediator of mitochondrial fusion. This rebalancing of fission–fusion dynamics represents a mechanistically distinct approach compared with previously reported NPC protection strategies [37].Prior studies have explored direct pharmacological interventions targeting mitochondrial dysfunction in IDD, including NAD⁺ precursors to restore mitochondrial bioenergetics [38], mitochondria-targeted antioxidants such as MitoQ to scavenge ROS [39], and DRP1 inhibitors such as Mdivi-1 to prevent excessive fission [37]. While these approaches have shown promise, they typically address downstream consequences rather than upstream regulatory mechanisms. In contrast, our strategy modulates mitochondrial dynamics through transcriptional regulation of the ISOC1/MYC/SBSN axis, potentially offering more durable effects by targeting the root cause of mitochondrial dysregulation. The convergence of anti-apoptotic signaling and mitochondrial protection through a single therapeutic target underscores the potential of ISOC1 modRNA for comprehensive intervention in IDD pathophysiology.

The therapeutic efficacy of PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA was validated in a rat model of lactate-induced IDD, which recapitulates key pathological features of human disc degeneration, including an acidic microenvironment, NPC loss, and extracellular matrix degradation. MRI assessment demonstrated that hydrogel-mediated ISOC1 modRNA delivery effectively preserved disc hydration and height, while histological analysis revealed attenuated NP tissue loss and reduced annulus fibrosus fibrosis compared with untreated controls. Importantly, the superior therapeutic outcomes observed in the PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA group relative to the naked ISOC1 modRNA group underscore the critical contribution of the pH-responsive hydrogel delivery system to treatment efficacy, likely reflecting enhanced local retention and sustained release kinetics. At the molecular level, immunohistochemical analysis confirmed the expected modulation of downstream targets, with decreased expression of pro-apoptotic markers (Bax, caspase-3) and the matrix-degrading enzyme MMP3, alongside increased collagen II deposition. The absence of detectable histopathological changes in major organs supports the systemic biocompatibility of this therapeutic approach. These preclinical findings provide a foundation for future translational studies.

Several limitations of the present study warrant consideration. First, the simple use of lactate intervention for constructing the IDD model has certain limitations in vivo. The occurrence of IDD is a chronic and complex pathological process. Therefore, a better in vitro model needs to be further proposed. Second, while our in vitro release studies demonstrated accelerated modRNA release under acidic conditions, the in vivo degradation kinetics of the PVA–csPBA hydrogel within the intervertebral disc microenvironment remain to be characterized. Future studies employing real-time imaging techniques with fluorescently labeled hydrogels will be essential to establish the temporal profile of hydrogel retention and modRNA release in situ, which is critical for optimizing dosing regimens for clinical translation. Third, the 4-week follow-up period may not be sufficient to assess the long-term efficacy of the hydrogel’s in vivo treatment and delayed adverse reactions; future studies should extend observation to 8–12 weeks with serial MRI assessments to evaluate sustained therapeutic benefit, hydrogel clearance kinetics, and potential delayed complications. Fourth, while co-immunoprecipitation experiments revealed that ISOC1 forms a ternary complex with BIRC6 and MYC, the precise molecular mechanism by which ISOC1 enhances BIRC6-mediated ubiquitination of MYC requires further investigation. Domain mapping studies and in vitro ubiquitination assays would help determine whether ISOC1 functions as a scaffold protein or directly modulates BIRC6 E3 ligase activity. Finally, validation in large animal models with anatomical and biomechanical characteristics more closely resembling human intervertebral discs will be necessary to advance clinical translation.

Conclusion

In this study, we integrated single-cell RNA sequencing, Mendelian randomization, and transcriptomic profiling to identify ISOC1 as a key therapeutic target in intervertebral disc degeneration, with validation in human samples and a rat model confirming its translational relevance. To enable controlled gene delivery within the acidic disc microenvironment, we engineered a pH-responsive PVA–csPBA hydrogel for ISOC1 modRNA delivery. Mechanistically, ISOC1 promotes BIRC6-mediated MYC ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby suppressing SBSN expression and attenuating lactate-induced nucleus pulposus cell apoptosis. In vivo, PVA–csPBA@ISOC1 modRNA treatment preserved disc structure and hydration without systemic toxicity. These findings establish pH-responsive hydrogel-mediated ISOC1 modRNA delivery as a promising regenerative strategy for IDD.
