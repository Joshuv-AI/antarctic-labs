Study on rockburst mechanism and risk identification for isolated working face in extra-thick coal seam fold zone
Authors: Yongqin Xie, Anye Cao, Geng Li, Weiwei Zhao, Cheng Zhang, Zhongjun Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFaced with the challenges of high in-situ stress and elevated rockburst risk in isolated working faces within fold zones, this study takes an isolated working face in a specific coal mine as the engineering background. By integrating theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and field practice, we investigated the evolutionary characteristics of overlying strata structure, clarified the variation law of mining-induced stress, revealed the rockburst mechanism of isolated working faces in fold zones of extra-thick coal seams, and established a dynamic risk identification model for the working face.The results show that when the minimum width of the coal pillar (95 m) during mining in the fold zone is significantly larger than the critical width (36.5 m), the overlying strata of the working face form a unique long-arm “F” structure. The cantilever effect of this structure causes the peak stress of the coal-rock mass in the working face advance area to increase to 42.1 MPa, with a stress concentration factor of 2.34. The superposition of the high static load in the advance area and the intense dynamic load released by the fracturing of the “F” structure leads to a significant decrease in the minimum principal stress and a corresponding increase in the maximum principal stress. This pushes the coal-rock mass beyond its strength limit, ultimately triggering a rockburst.To address the limitation of low early-warning accuracy (81.4%) associated with traditional single-index methods, a multi-index collaborative identification model based on microseismic data was constructed. Utilizing a Back-Propagation (BP) neural network algorithm, the model adaptively optimizes the coupling weights of six key indicators: b-value (0.04), P(b)-value (0.009), ∆F-value (0.185), AC-value (0.218), EEM-value (0.256), and S-value (0.292). This realizes a paradigm shift from “single-index alarm” to “multi-index collaborative discrimination”. Field validation demonstrates that the model improves the early-warning accuracy to 94.9%, significantly enhancing the capability for dynamic risk identification of rockbursts in the working face. The research conclusions provide a valuable reference for the safe mining of isolated working faces under similar geological and mining conditions.Keywords: Composite key strata, Composite effect, Rockburst, High energy microseismic events, Static and dynamic loadSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

With the continuous increase in coal mining depth, the geological conditions for coal seam extraction have become increasingly complex, and rockburst disasters have shown a marked rising trend. Numerous studies have confirmed that isolated working faces formed due to unreasonable mining layouts exhibit an extremely high propensity for rockbursts1–3. Owing to their special spatial occurrence characteristics, such working faces typically bear abnormally high abutment pressure, which is prone to causing severe fracturing of roadway surrounding rock and greatly increasing the difficulty of roadway maintenance4,5. When an isolated working face is superimposed on a synclinal structural zone, the asymmetric caving of overlying strata exacerbates the inhomogeneous distribution of in-situ stress, further pushing the rockburst risk of the working face to a critical level. Therefore, clarifying the rockburst-induced mechanism of isolated working faces under the coupling effect of synclinal axis structure and irregular coal pillar confinement is of great theoretical significance and engineering application value for ensuring the safe and efficient production of deep coal mines.

At present, scholars have carried out systematic and in-depth research on the evolution of overlying strata spatial structure and stress distribution characteristics of conventional isolated working faces. For example, Dou Linming6, Jiang Fuxing7 et al. analyzed the evolution modes of overlying strata structures of different types of isolated working faces and revealed the degree and dynamic evolution law of stress concentration in surrounding rock. For isolated working faces adjacent to faults, Cao Anye8, Zhu Guang’an9et al. explored the characteristics of their overlying strata structures and the disaster-inducing correlation with fault slip instability. Gao Rui et al10. clarified that the superposition of the high static stress environment of isolated coal mass and the strong dynamic load disturbance induced by the fracturing of thick and hard overlying strata is the core controlling factor for the occurrence of rockbursts. Jia Chao et al11. elucidated the deformation and failure mechanism of roadways in isolated working faces under the condition of hard roof, while Liu Guolei et al12. attributed the high rockburst risk of isolated working faces to the static-dynamic load coupling effect generated by the overlying strata structure of adjacent goafs. Yang Sen et al13. studied the stress transfer mechanism of the lateral roof structure after the fracturing of overlying strata in isolated working faces, and Ji Ming et al14. proposed that the loading effect of the abutment pressure from large-scale pressure arches formed by overlying strata in extensive goafs on section coal pillars is the main inducement for the frequent occurrence of high energy mining tremors during the extraction of isolated working faces.

Meanwhile, the academic community has also made considerable progress in the research on the stress field characteristics of fold structures in coal mines. Xia Yucheng et al15. pointed out through the analysis of stress distribution in fold structures that buckle folds are mainly formed by horizontal compressive tectonic stress. Zhou Ye et al16. found in their research that the maximum principal stress is always compressive stress during the deformation of single-layer folds, and its amplitude increases continuously with the increase of horizontal strain of folds. Wang Shengben et al17. discovered an obvious stress stratification phenomenon in the synclinal core of single-layer folds, where tensile stress is concentrated in the lower strata and compressive stress dominates in the upper strata. Kang Hongpu et al18. emphasized that the overall stress level in the synclinal axis zone is significantly higher, and the increase amplitude of horizontal stress is much larger than that of vertical stress. For multi-layer folds, Wang Yugang et al19. confirmed that the maximum horizontal stress is concentrated in the upper and lower hard rock layers, while the stress level in the middle soft rock layer is relatively low. Tang Long et al20., Jia Housheng et al21. further explored the evolution law of mining-induced stress in fold zones and optimized roadway support parameters based on the plastic failure characteristics of surrounding rock.

Despite the fruitful achievements in the above two research fields, existing studies still have obvious limitations in the face of the composite geological condition of “synclinal axis + isolated working face”. Firstly, most studies on isolated working faces focus on regular coal pillar boundaries under simple geological backgrounds, and lack in-depth consideration of the superposition effect of synclinal tectonic stress and mining-induced stress. Secondly, research on synclinal structures is mostly aimed at roadway engineering or single working faces, and few studies involve the dynamic evolution of overlying strata structure, the static-dynamic load coupling disaster-inducing mechanism of isolated working faces under the confinement of irregular coal pillars, as well as the quantitative identification of rockburst risk under the coupling of multiple stress fields, and other core scientific issues have not been effectively solved.

To address the aforementioned research gaps, this study takes an isolated working face in the folded zone of an ultra-thick coal seam in a rockburst-prone coal mine in Gansu Province as the engineering background, and adopts an integrated research approach combining theoretical analysis, mechanical modeling, numerical simulation and field practice. It systematically reveals the dynamic evolution law of the spatial structure of overlying strata and the spatiotemporal distribution characteristics of surrounding rock stress in the folded zone of ultra-thick coal seams, and clarifies the formation mechanism of the unique long-arm F-shaped overlying strata structure and its stress concentration effect. Combined with the energy accumulation and release characteristics of key stratum fracturing, it quantitatively analyzes the static-dynamic load coupling disaster-causing mechanism of rockbursts in such isolated working faces. Aiming at the limitation of low early-warning accuracy of traditional methods, microseismic precursor indicators are selected from the temporal, spatial and intensity dimensions to construct a multi-index collaborative rockburst risk identification model based on a Back-Propagation (BP) neural network, realizing the adaptive optimization of indicator coupling weights and the quantitative classification of hazard levels. Finally, the engineering applicability of the research results is verified through field practice. The findings of this study can fill the theoretical gaps in the rockburst mechanism and risk identification under the composite geological condition of “folded zone + ultra-thick coal seam + isolated working face”, and provide important theoretical support and practical reference for the safe mining of deep ultra-thick coal seams under similar complex geological and mining conditions.

Engineering background

The present study was conducted in the 1504 mining district of a coal mine in Gansu Province, China. The district’s primary extraction is the No. 5 coal seam, which possesses an average thickness of 41.78 m and demonstrates a notable propensity for rockburst. The present study focuses on Panel 150,404, which is located in the south-western part of the district. The dimensions of the panel are such that its mining height is 12.1 m, with surface elevations ranging from + 1520 m to + 1665 m and corresponding seam floor elevations between + 1054 m and + 1163 m. This results in an average mining depth of approximately 480 m. The coal seam dips at an average angle of 10°. The mining advance rate is controlled at 3.2 m per day. The operation employs fully-mechanized top-coal caving mining technology, with support provided by ZFT14000/25/40D hydraulic supports in conjunction with advanced support measures. The ma-terial and haulage roadways for the panel are developed at an average elevation of 1090 m. The panel has an average strike length of 1448.9 m and a dip width of 160 m. During the preliminary mining stage (within 0 ~ 340 m from the setup room), the panel is located within the hinge zone of a syncline.The direction of the panel’s advance forms an angle of 13° with the synclinal axis. Isolated working face numerical simulation analysis

Establishment of numerical models

The mining process for Panel 150,404 was simulated using the FLAC3D numerical code. A numerical model was developed using integrated borehole geological data and the actual geological conditions of the mining district. The dimensions of the model are 2400 m × 1200 m × 420 m (Strike × Dip × Vertical). In the model coordinate system, the Y-axis is aligned with the strike of the coal seam, the X-axis is aligned with the dip direction and the Z-axis is vertical. The boundary conditions were set as follows: horizontal displacements were fixed at both ends. Based on in-situ stress measurements, a confining stress with a gradient of 0.025 MPa/m and an initial value of 15.75 MPa was applied to the base of the model and its X-direction sides. For the Y-direction sides, a confining stress with a gradient of 0.035 MPa/m and an initial value of 22.05 MPa was applied. The top of the model was set as a free surface and a gravitational stress of 2.5 MPa was applied, corresponding to the actual overburden depth. Stress evolution patterns at the working face

As shown in Fig. 7, the cloud diagrams illustrate the variation laws of the maximum and minimum principal stresses in the strike direction of the working face after excavation. Both theoretical derivation and numerical simulation reveal that the horizontal stresses σx and σy reach their maximum values at the synclinal axis, while the shear stress τxy peaks at the limbs of the syncline, showing consistent laws. Field in-situ stress measurements indicate that the maximum principal stress in the syncline area of the working face ranges from 23.5 MPa to 25 MPa. Quantitatively, the simulation shows that the peak maximum principal stress at the axis (42.1 MPa) deviates from the theoretical value (44.3 MPa) by approximately 5.2%, verifying the applicability of the theoretical model. In terms of shear stress, the simulation demonstrates that the shear strain energy density at the limbs is 23% ~ 31% higher than that at the hinge area, which confirms the theoretical judgment of the τxy peak. However, since the theoretical model does not consider the effects of rock mass joints and mining-induced stress superposition, the location of the extreme point shifts 15 ~ 20 m toward the hinge compared with the theoretical prediction.

With the advancement of the working face, the concentration zones of the maximum and minimum principal stresses are mainly distributed on both sides of the synclinal axis. The unique long-arm “F” structure formed in the overlying strata of the working face leads to a significant increase in the stress peak of the coal and rock mass ahead of the working face. The stress concentration degree between the synclinal axis and the material roadway is noticeably higher than that on the haulage roadway side, which is attributed to the different widths of the barrier pillars on the two sides. When the working face advances 250 m from the setup room, the distance between the synclinal axis and the haulage roadway is only 10 m, resulting in a remarkable rise in the surrounding rock stress. Subsequently, the high stress zone spreads over the entire working face, with the peak values of the maximum and minimum principal stresses reaching 50 MPa and 25 MPa, respectively. When advancing to 350 m, the increase in the stress peak becomes relatively gentle, but the scope of the stress concentration zone between the synclinal axis and the roadway expands significantly. The prominent difference in horizontal elevations between the two gateways, combined with the unequal pillar widths, jointly results in a distinctly non-uniform stress distribution across the working face. This enlarges the difference between the maximum and minimum principal stresses near the end of the material gateway, raising the risk of rockburst in working face.

Notably, the stress increase on the inner rib (the solid coal side of the working face) was significantly greater than that on the outer rib (the panel pillar side). Mechanism of rockburst in isolated working face

Key fracture energy accumulation characteristics

The preceding analysis demonstrates that the stress state of the surrounding rock changes as the synclinal axis moves closer to the haulage roadway. Despite the relatively modest increase in maximum principal stress on the haulage roadway side, the overall stress distribution in the panel shifts towards the haulage roadway side. The stress levels in the coal mass on the side of the material roadway are found to be sig-nificantly higher(by more than 50%)than in the central area of the working face.

As the area of the Goaf expands, the load borne by the key stratum above it con-comitantly increases. Prior to the fracturing of the key stratum, an augmentation in load transfer through the canti-levered strata results in an escalation of the static load level ahead of the face. This phenomenon is characterised by the presence of elevated, extensive stress concentration zones at both extremities of the working face. Substituting Eqs. (11) and (12) into Eqs. (13) and (15) allows for the determination of whether the coal-rock mass enters a failure state upon the initial fracture of the key stratum.

During the initial fracturing of the key stratum, the working face is located in the high static load zone of the synclinal axis. The ultimate fracture of the key stratum releases a substantial amount of energy, generating strong dynamic loads in the form of P-waves and S-waves. The P-waves first create tensile cracks, followed by S-waves that induce shear slip. The superposition of static and dynamic loads causes a significant reduction in the minimum principal stress, or even a shift to tensile stress, with a corresponding increase in the maximum principal stress. According to the Mohr–Coulomb criterion, the coal-rock mass thus reaches its strength limit and fails under a lower maximum principal stress, triggering a rockburst. Therefore, the core mechanism of rockburst incubation is the coupling effect of “high tectonic static load + strong dynamic load from fracturing + abrupt stress state transition”.

Research on rockburst risk identification in isolated working areas

Strong mine shock event confirmed

Rockburst is fundamentally a result of coupled static and dynamic loading. Isolated working face located within a synclinal axis are subject to elevated levels of static stress within the surrounding rock mass. In this critical context, strong mining-induced seismic events, which represent a pronounced increase over the regional microseismic background, release dynamic energy that further destabilises the stress equilibrium. This process has been shown to substantially elevate the risk of rockburst. Consequently, the systematic analysis of microseismic monitoring data to identify temporal evolution patterns and precursor characteristics of major seismic events has become a critical methodology for assessing rockburst hazard.

The preliminary identification of major mining-induced seismic events was facilitated by the analysis of microseismic monitoring data from Panel 150,404 (January to February 2025, Fig. 11). The data demonstrate a clear correlation between key microseismic parameters, with event frequency and released energy exhibiting an approximately linear relationship. This observed correlation is consistent with the energy-frequency distribution described by the Gutenberg-Richter law36, indicating statistical self-similarity in the seismic activity of this region. Consequently, this pattern provides a potential basis for identifying precursors to larger seismic events.

In the context of the neural network model, the term “training set” is employed to denote the set of data that is used to train the network. This set is specifically utilised for training the neural network model.

The sample proportions for the training, validation, and test sets were set to 7:2:1.

First, the candidate range for the number of hidden layer nodes (4 to 13) was determined based on empirical formulas. Subsequently, using a trial-and-error method, microseismic events from the 150,404 working face (January to February 2025) were taken as samples. With consistent training parameters (learning rate of 0.01, target error of 0.001), networks with different node counts were trained repeatedly 10 times, and the accuracy of the validation set was used as the evaluation metric. The trial calculation results showed that when the number of nodes increased from 4 to 8, the validation set accuracy continuously improved from 84.8% to 94.9%. However, when the number of nodes exceeded 8, the training set accuracy continued to rise while the validation set accuracy began to decline (dropping to 93.1% at 10 nodes), indicating significant overfitting. Balancing accuracy and generalization capability, the optimal number of hidden layer nodes was determined to be 8. This result is highly consistent with the optimization outcome, validating the rationality of the network design.

Discussion

Engineering applicability of the long-arm “F” structure theory and stress evolution model

The long-arm “F”-shaped overburden structure model proposed in this study, established based on the geological and mining characteristics of isolated working faces in synclinal hinge zones of extra-thick coal seams, exhibits clear engineering applicability for similar working conditions. The critical coal pillar width calculation method, derived from field measurements and theoretical analysis, can effectively determine the overburden structure morphology during mining and provide a quantitative basis for early-stage rockburst risk prediction. The model has been demonstrated to reveal stress distribution characteristics, including stress concentration at the synclinal core and asymmetric stress evolution in the two roadways. These characteristics can guide the zoning of rockburst prevention and control and the determination of key pressure relief locations. However, the application of the model is constrained by the matching degree of geological and mining parameters. Factors such as coal-rock mechanical parameters, syncline structural morphology, mining height, and advance speed all influence the simulation results. In practical engineering applications, parameter calibration should be conducted based on borehole geological data, in-situ stress measurements, and real-time monitoring data to avoid blind application. Furthermore, the current model primarily focuses on the static-dynamic load coupling effect during mining, while the impacts of dynamic disturbance factors such as blasting operations and hydraulic support movement on overburden stability require further investigation and refinement.

Optimization space and application extension of the multi-index collaborative identification model

The microseismic multi-index collaborative identification model based on BP neural network, which selects six indicators from the temporal, spatial, and intensity dimensions and achieves multi-source data fusion through adaptive weight optimization, has improved the early warning accuracy of rockburst risk in isolated working faces within folded zones to 94.9%, effectively addressing the low accuracy issue of traditional single indicator methods. The energy thresholds determined based on inflection points of the energy-frequency curve provide quantitative criteria for graded early warning and graded prevention and control of rockburst. The model still has optimization potential in the following aspects: the time window can be further refined according to mining advance speed to capture short-term precursors, future work should integrate multi-source monitoring data such as mine pressure monitoring (support resistance, roof subsidence), stress monitoring (coal mass stress, surrounding rock stress), and displacement monitoring to enhance the comprehensiveness and accuracy of risk identification, at the algorithmic level, the BP neural network can be optimized by incorporating particle swarm optimization and genetic algorithm to address issues of slow convergence and susceptibility to local optima. In terms of application extension, the model’s indicator system can be adjusted according to different rockburst-inducing mechanisms, enabling its popularization to other complex geological conditions such as isolated working faces near faults, working faces with thick and hard roofs, and deep high stress working faces. Furthermore, by integrating the model with mine safety monitoring and early warning platforms, real-time data input, automatic indicator calculation, and intelligent output of risk warning results can be achieved, forming a closed-loop technical system of “dynamic identification—graded early warning—targeted prevention and control” for rockburst risk, thereby providing more intelligent technical support for the safe mining of deep coal seams under complex geological conditions.

Conclusions

The present study systematically investigated the evolution of overburden struc-tures, the distribution of stress resulting from mining activities, and the mechanisms of rockburst in isolated working face within synclinal hinge zones. The primary conclusions that can be drawn from this analysis are as follows.

When the minimum width of the irregular barrier pillar exceeds the critical value of 36.5 m (95 m in the field application), the overlying strata fail to form a connected fracture zone with the adjacent goafs, thereby developing a unique long-arm “F”-shaped overlying structure. Within this structural model, the stress distribution of the surrounding rock exhibits distinct zonal characteristics: the normal stress components reach their peak values in the synclinal core, where the shear stress is zero, conversely, τxy attains its maximum in the synclinal limbs. Vertically, the absolute values of σx and σy peak at y = ± h/2 and exhibit opposite tensile-compressive characteristics, which substantially elevates the risk of rockburst incidents at the working face.

On the one hand, the long-arm “F”-shaped overlying structure establishes a high static stress environment in the surrounding rock of the working face. On the other hand, the fracturing of key overlying strata generates intense dynamic load disturbances. Under the combined effect of these factors, the minimum principal stress of the coal-rock mass decreases sharply, rendering the coal-rock mass highly susceptible to reaching the critical state of dynamic instability and ultimately triggering rockburst disasters.
