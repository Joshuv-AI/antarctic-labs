Systemic therapy for gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms: current landscape and future directions
Authors: Eiichiro So, Chigusa Morizane, Takuji Okusaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are a heterogeneous group of tumors arising from neuroendocrine cells located in various organs, most commonly the gastrointestinal tract and pancreas, followed by the lungs. According to the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification, NENs are categorized into well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) based on morphological differentiation, with NETs further subdivided into G1, G2, and G3 according to the Ki-67 index. Among these, gastroenteropancreatic NENs (GEP-NENs) account for most cases and have become an important area of therapeutic investigation. As understanding of the disease has deepened, various treatment modalities have been developed, and treatment strategies tailored to specific subtypes have become increasingly complex. Currently, the treatment selection for GEP-NENs is largely based on the WHO classification. Hormonal therapy, molecular-targeted therapy, cytotoxic chemotherapy, and peptide receptor radionuclide therapy are standard treatment options for unresectable GEP-NETs, whereas cytotoxic chemotherapy remains the standard of care for GEP-NECs. However, therapeutic development and clinical outcomes remain unsatisfactory in unresectable NENs because of their rarity, highlighting the need for further therapeutic advancements in systemic therapy. In this review, we summarize the current landscape of systemic therapies, recent therapeutic advances, and their underlying biological mechanisms in GEP-NENs, while discussing the ongoing challenges and future directions in the field.Keywords: chemotherapy, gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasm, neuroendocrine carcinoma, neuroendocrine neoplasm, neuroendocrine tumor

Introduction

Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are a heterogeneous group of tumors that arise from neuroendocrine cells distributed throughout various organs, most commonly in the gastrointestinal tract and pancreas, followed by the lungs. 1 According to the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database, the age-adjusted incidence rate of NENs increased 5.2-fold from 1975 (1.64 per 100,000 persons) to 2021 (8.52 per 100,000 persons). 1 In Japan, a nationwide survey on gastroenteropancreatic (GEP)-NENs reported an annual incidence rate of 2.69 per 100,000 persons in 2010, representing a 1.2-fold increase compared with that in 2005. 2 A recent population-based study demonstrated that the annual incidence rate of GEP-NENs increased to 3.56 per 100,000 individuals in 2016. 3 This gradual increase in incidence is thought to reflect advances in diagnostic modalities and increased awareness of the disease.4,5

According to the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification, NENs are categorized as well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs). 6 NETs are generally slow-growing tumors that are further classified according to the Ki-67 index as low-grade (G1, Ki-67 index: <3%, mitotic rate: <2 per 10 high-power field (HPF)), intermediate-grade (G2, Ki-67 index: 3–20%, mitotic rate: 2–20 per 10 HPF), and high-grade (G3, Ki-67 index: >20%, mitotic rate: >20 per 10 HPF).6,7 Most NETs occur sporadically, with a subset arising in hereditary settings, such as multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 syndrome, von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease, tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC), and neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). 7 GEP-NENs are also classified by the presence or absence of hormonal symptoms as functional or nonfunctional.6,7 GEP-NETs represent a heterogeneous group of tumors with variable biological behavior depending on tumor grade, ranging from indolent low-grade tumors to aggressive high-grade NETs. While surgical resection plays an important role in the management of localized disease, multidisciplinary team discussion is essential for each patient from diagnosis through treatment selection. 8 Multimodal systemic therapies have recently emerged for unresectable or advanced cases, including hormonal therapy, molecular-targeted therapy, cytotoxic chemotherapy, and peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT). Treatment selection is based on the Ki-67 index, embryonic origin, and functional status. Conversely, NECs are aggressive tumors that are frequently accompanied by massive distant metastasis. 9 For GEP-NECs, outcomes of surgery alone are unsatisfactory even in patients without distant metastases. 10 Platinum-based chemotherapy is often used in the first-line setting; however, treatment options remain limited after progression. The median overall survival (OS) period is approximately 1 year, indicating poor prognosis. 11

In this review, we provide an overview of the current systemic therapies, recent therapeutic advances, and underlying mechanisms in NENs. As this is a narrative review, it does not employ a systematic search strategy; we aim to integrate existing evidence and highlight future directions in this field.

Current systemic therapy for NETs

Somatostatin analogs (lanreotide, octreotide)

Somatostatin is a peptide hormone secreted by the hypothalamus and the delta cells of the pancreas and gastrointestinal tract. It inhibits the secretion of growth hormones, insulin, glucagon, gastrin, and vasoactive intestinal peptides by binding to the somatostatin receptor (SSTR). Somatostatin analogs (SSAs) were initially introduced to control hormone-related symptoms in functional NETs, such as carcinoid syndrome, by inhibiting the excessive release of bioactive substances, such as serotonin, tachykinins, and histamine, thereby alleviating flushing and diarrhea.12–18 In contrast, SSAs may worsen hypoglycemia by inhibiting counter-regulatory hormones, such as glucagon and growth hormones, in insulinoma. 19 SSAs also inhibit cell proliferation by regulating mitogen-activated protein kinase-extracellular signal-regulated kinase signaling (Figure 1).20,21 Among the SSTR family members, SSTR2 and SSTR5 are expressed in nearly 80% of NETs, making them potential therapeutic targets. SSTR expression depends on tumor grade, with G1 and G2 showing higher positivity rates than G3.22,23 NETs that are SSTR-positive on SSTR scintigraphy or immunohistochemistry are suitable candidates for antitumor treatment with SSAs. 24

PROMID is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase III trial of octreotide long-acting release (octreotide LAR) in patients with treatment-naïve unresectable midgut NETs (Table 1). 25 Most of the enrolled patients had a Ki-67 index <2%. The primary endpoint of time to progression was statistically longer with octreotide LAR than that with placebo (median, 14.3 months vs 6 months; hazard ratio (HR), 0.34; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.20–0.59; p = 0.000072). Stable disease (SD) was observed in 66.7% of patients, and the overall response rate (ORR) was 2.4%. Subgroup analysis suggested that the most favorable antitumor effects were observed in patients with a low hepatic tumor load (HTL) of 0%—10% and resected primary tumors.

CLARINET is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase III trial of lanreotide (LAN) in patients with unresectable, nonfunctioning, SSTR-positive NETs G1 or 2 with Ki-67 <10% (Table 1). 26 Primary tumors were located in the pancreas, midgut, or hindgut or were of unknown origin. The primary endpoint of progression-free survival (PFS) of LAN was statistically longer than placebo (median, not reached vs 18.0 months; HR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.30–0.73; p < 0.001). In a Japanese open-label phase II study, the ORR was 7.1%, and the SD was 71.4%, with an acceptable safety profile. 27

Increasing the dose of SSAs is one way of controlling the disease progression or worsening of hormone-related symptoms. CLARINET FORTE is a phase II trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of LAN administered at a reduced dosing interval in patients with progressive midgut or pancreatic NETs (pNETs) G1 and G2 after standard dose LAN. 28 The primary endpoint, the median PFS, was 8.3 and 5.6 months, respectively, and the safety profile was similar to that of the standard dose; Ki-67 ⩽10% was associated with longer PFS in both midgut or pNETs. Furthermore, high-dose octreotide LAR has been used as control arms in NETTER-1 and NETTER-2 trials, with the median PFS 8.3 and 8.5 months, respectively.29–31 Nonetheless, the primary endpoint of PFS was significantly shorter than PRRT in both trials, suggesting that although high-dose octreotide represents a therapeutic option, it is unlikely to be an optimal treatment strategy compared to PRRT.29–31

Several real-world studies have reported the efficacy of SSAs beyond the populations included in the PROMID or CLARINET trials. The median PFS ranged from 11.9 to 18 months in pNETs with Ki-67 ⩾10, which appears comparable to that reported in the PROMID and CLARINET trials.32,33 Meanwhile, NETs G3 and HTL have been consistently associated with shorter PFS.32,33 In light of their favorable toxicity profiles, SSAs can be considered as a therapeutic option for selected patients with NET G2, particularly in those unable to tolerate other systemic therapies.

Combination strategies involving SSAs have been explored in clinical practice due to their favorable toxicity profile; however, robust evidence supporting their use remains limited. 34 Certain combination regimens with cytotoxic agents have been investigated. SONNET is a noncomparative phase II trial to evaluate the efficacy of LAN plus temozolomide (TMZ) in patients with GEP-NETs G1 and G2, or of unknown primary. 35 The primary endpoint of disease control rate (DCR) at 6 months was 73.5%. Following 6 months of lanreotide maintenance, 54.5% of patients with functional NET and 71.4% with nonfunctional NET had SD or PR, whereas 41.7% with nonfunctional NET had SD or PR on observation only. 35 Octreotide LAR in combination with 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) has shown an ORR of 24.8% and drug tolerability in a phase II trial. 36 Octreotide LAR plus interferon alfa-2b has shown reduced risk of tumor progression compared to octreotide LAR monotherapy in a randomized controlled trial in metastatic midgut NETs but has not demonstrated prolonged survival. 37 In addition, combinations with molecular-targeted therapy have been investigated. Southwest Oncology Group (SWOG) S0518 is a phase III trial comparing the efficacy of octreotide LAR plus bevacizumab and octreotide LAR plus interferon alfa-2b; however, the primary endpoint of PFS was not significantly different between both arms. 38 Octreotide plus pazopanib was evaluated including metastatic or locally advanced GEP-NETs G1 and G2 in a single-arm phase II study, ORR of 21.9% was observed in pNETs; however, no responses were observed in extrapancreatic NETs in the first stage lead to the termination of this arm. 39 AXINET is a randomized phase II/III trial comparing the efficacy of octreotide LAR plus axitinib and octreotide LAR plus placebo in unresectable or metastatic extrapancreatic NETs G1 and G2. Although ORR was greater for axitinib compared to placebo (17.5% vs 4.6%, p = 0.001), the primary endpoint of PFS was not significantly different between both arms (median, 17.2 months vs 12.3 months; HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.65–1.15; p = 0.169). 40 Recently, everolimus (EVE) plus LAN (EVE/LAN) therapy has shown improved efficacy compared to EVE monotherapy in unresectable GEP-NETs with poor prognostic factors, as discussed later in section “Molecular targeted therapy: mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitor (everolimus).”

In summary, SSAs are effective in controlling hormone-related symptoms of functional NETs and are indicated for tumor control in GEP-NETs (Figure 2). Although several combination strategies have been investigated, the available evidence is largely derived from noncomparative studies except for EVE/LAN. Therefore, no consensus supporting their routine use of combinations other than EVE/LAN currently exists, and these approaches are generally considered on a case-by-case basis in clinical practice.

Molecular-targeted therapy: mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitor (everolimus)

The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) is an intracellular serine/threonine kinase that regulates cell growth, proliferation, angiogenesis, and metabolism.41–43 The mTOR pathway is activated in response to growth signals from the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) pathway. 43 The activation of the mTOR pathway has been implicated in the tumorigenesis of familial and sporadic NETs. Constitutive activation of the mTOR pathway has been observed in genetic syndromes, such as NF1, caused by loss of the NF1 gene, and tuberous sclerosis, caused by loss of TSC1 or TSC2.41,44 Moreover, whole-exome sequencing of sporadic pNETs identified somatic mutations in PTEN, TSC2, and PIK3CA within the mTOR pathway in approximately 15% of cases, supporting the rationale for mTOR as a therapeutic target (Figure 1). 45

RADIANT-3 is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase III trial of EVE in patients with unresectable, low-, or intermediate-grade pNETs (Table 1). 46 The primary endpoint of PFS was significantly longer with EVE than with placebo (median, 11.0 months vs 4.6 months; HR, 0.35; 95% CI, 0.27–0.45; p < 0.001). The ORRS were 5% and 2% in the EVE and placebo groups, respectively.

RADIANT-2 is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase III trial comparing the efficacy of EVE or matching placebo both in combination with octreotide LAR for low- or intermediate-grade unresectable NETs (Table 1).47,48 Although the primary endpoint of PFS tended to be longer with EVE plus octreotide LAR than placebo plus octreotide LAR, the prespecified significance boundary was not met partly because of the crossover design (median, 16.4 months vs 11.3 months; HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.59–1.00; p = 0.026). The use of octreotide LAR in both groups may have contributed to the difficulty in detecting the differences. Therefore, a clinical trial targeting nonfunctional NETs was conducted.

RADIANT-4 is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase III trial of EVE for unresectable nonfunctional NETs of gastrointestinal or lung origin (Table 1). 49 Among the enrolled patients, 55.6% had gastrointestinal NETs (7.0% in the foregut, 32.8% in the midgut, and 15.9% in the hindgut). Both G1 and G2 NETs were included. The primary endpoint of PFS was significantly longer with EVE than with placebo (median, 11.0 months vs 3.9 months; HR, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.35–0.67; p < 0.00001). The ORRs were 2% and 1% in the EVE and placebo groups, respectively. Subgroup analysis demonstrated consistent efficacy of EVE in both G1 (HR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.39–0.84) and G2 (HR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.29–0.83). Additionally, survival benefits were observed, regardless of primary tumor origin and pretreatment.

Based on these results, EVE is currently indicated for patients with NET G1 and G2 who are not candidates for SSAs or who have progressed on SSAs (Figure 2). Within NETs G1 and G2, Ki-67 of 5%—20% is a poor prognostic subset, with a 5-year survival rate of 51% compared with 89% in Ki-67 ⩽5%. 50 The R-GETNE study also showed that the presence of liver metastases was associated with a 5-year survival rate of 53.3%. 51 STARTER-NET (JCOG1901) was a randomized phase III trial in Japan that confirmed the superiority of EVE/LAN therapy over EVE monotherapy in patients with unresectable GEP-NETs with poor prognostic factors, defined as Ki-67 5%—20% or Ki-67 <5% with diffuse liver metastases (Table 1). 52 Because the mTOR pathway is frequently activated in NETs, the combination of EVE with SSAs that suppress mTOR signaling via PI3K may provide greater antitumor activity than EVE alone.45,53 In the planned interim analysis, the primary endpoint of PFS was statistically longer in EVE/LAN than in EVE (median, 29.7 months vs 11.5 months; HR, 0.38; 99.91% CI, 0.15–0.96; p = 0.00017). 54 The ORRs were 26.8% and 8.7%, respectively.

Furthermore, EVE is considered in NETs G3. In a single-arm phase II EVINEC trial evaluating the safety and efficacy of EVE monotherapy in NETs G3 and NECs, the median PFS and OS were 5.2 and 23.9 months, respectively, with limited efficacy observed in NECs and no new safety concerns identified. 55 In a phase II trial of EVE combined with TMZ in GEP-NETs G3 and NECs, the median PFS and OS were 12.6 and 31.4 months in GEP-NETs G3, respectively; however, grade 3 and 4 toxicities were reported in 43% and 38% of patients. 56 Considering the limited available evidence in NETs G3, EVE can be considered a therapeutic option.

Molecular-targeted therapy: multikinase inhibitor (sunitinib, cabozantinib, surufatinib)

Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) plays an important role in angiogenesis and the pathogenesis of NETs.57–59 pNETs express VEGF receptors (VEGFR-2 and VEGFR-3), platelet-derived growth factor receptors (PDGFR-α and PDGFR-β), and the stem-cell factor receptor (KIT), which are involved in tumor vessel stability and cell proliferation (Figure 1).59–62

Sunitinib is a multikinase inhibitor with antiangiogenic and antitumor activities through the inhibition of VEGFR1, VEGFR2, VEGFR3, PDGFRα, PDGFRβ, KIT, fms-related tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3), rearranged during transfection (RET), and colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1) receptor (Figure 1). 63 SUN1111 is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase III trial of sunitinib in patients with unresectable well-differentiated pNETs (Table 1). 64 The primary endpoint of PFS was statistically longer with sunitinib than with placebo (median, 11.4 months vs 5.5 months; HR, 0.42; 95% CI, 0.26–0.66; p < 0.001). The ORRs were 9.3% and 0% in the sunitinib and placebo groups, respectively. Based on these results, sunitinib can be a standard therapeutic option for patients with pNETs (Figure 2). However, its efficacy for the treatment of GI-NETs has not yet been established.

The inhibition of specific signaling pathways involved in tumor survival, proliferation, invasion, and distant metastasis induces acquired resistance. For example, hypoxic signaling by VEGF inhibitors induces the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α and hepatocyte growth factor receptor (MET), which can result in increased cell motility, invasion, and metastasis. 65 The MET pathway plays an important role in the development of resistance to VEGF pathway inhibition by sunitinib in preclinical models, suggesting that it is a promising target for overcoming resistance. 66 Anexelekto (AXL) is also thought to be induced by VEGF inhibition and is associated with resistance mechanisms. 67 Cabozantinib is a multikinase inhibitor that targets VEGFR2, MET, RET, tyrosine kinase with immunoglobulin-like and EGF-like domains 2, AXL, FLT3, KIT, and recepteur d’origine nantais and had promising efficacy in preclinical models (Figure 1).68–70 CABINET is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase III trial of cabozantinib in patients with previously treated, unresectable, or well- or moderately differentiated NETs G1–G3 of extrapancreatic or pancreatic origin (Table 1). 71 The primary endpoint of PFS was statistically longer with cabozantinib than that with placebo in patients with extrapancreatic NETs (median, 8.4 months vs 3.9 months; HR, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.25–0.59; p < 0.001) and patients with pNETs (median, 13.8 months vs 4.4 months; HR, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.12–0.42; p < 0.001). The ORRs with cabozantinib were 5% and 19% in patients with extrapancreatic and pNET, respectively, compared with 0% with placebo. Subgroup analysis showed that survival benefits were observed, regardless of prior treatments, including EVE and 177lutetium (Lu)-DOTATATE, and tumor origin. Notably, a favorable trend in PFS was observed even in NETs G3, where treatment options with high levels of evidence are limited, although the difference was not statistically significant. Notably, cabozantinib is the only drug that has shown efficacy in phase III study for previously treated NETs G1 to G3 (Figure 2).

Surufatinib is a novel small-molecule inhibitor that blocks tumor angiogenesis by targeting VEGFR1, VEGFR2, VEGFR3, fibroblast growth factor receptor, and immune evasion via the macrophage CSF1 receptor. In Chinese randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase III trials of SANET-P and SANET-EP, surufatinib demonstrated PFS benefits for pancreatic and extrapancreatic NETs.72,73 However, its global clinical adoption remains limited because of the lack of multinational studies.

Cytotoxic chemotherapy (streptozotocin, capecitabine plus temozolomide)

Cytotoxic chemotherapy generally shows a higher ORR than molecular-targeted agents or SSAs. It is often selected for patients with a large tumor burden or a high Ki-67 index, for whom other treatment options may not be suitable.

Streptozotocin (STZ), alone or in combination with 5-FU, is a classical cytotoxic chemotherapy for unresectable NETs, and most evidence supporting its efficacy has been derived from previous studies (Figure 2). In a randomized study comparing STZ plus 5-FU and STZ monotherapy, the ORRs were 63% and 36% and the median OS periods were 26.0 and 16.4 months, respectively (Table 1). 74 However, the ORRs reported in this earlier study are higher than those reported in a more recent real-world study; moreover, the definition of ORR differs. Additionally, the efficacy in subgroups, such as primary origin or WHO grade, was unclear. 74 A meta-analysis indicated that tumor response was more evident in patients with pNET than in those with non-pNET, most of which consisted of GI-NET. 75 In a Japanese multicenter retrospective study, the ORR of STZ-based chemotherapy (monotherapy, 81.8%; weekly regimen, 46.4%) was 21.8%, and PFS was 9.8 months. 76 Ki-67 >5% was a predictive marker of response to STZ-based therapy, whereas a combination of regimen, treatment line, and liver tumor burden was not associated with response. 76

Capecitabine plus TMZ (CAPTEM) is a combination chemotherapy of capecitabine and TMZ. E2211 is a multicenter, randomized, phase II trial comparing CAPTEM and TMZ monotherapy in patients with previously treated advanced low- or intermediate-grade pNETs (Table 1). 77 The primary endpoint of PFS was statistically longer in CAPTEM than that in TMZ (median, 22.7 months vs 14.4 months; HR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.36–0.93; p = 0.022). The ORRs were 39.7% and 33.8%, respectively. Alkylating agents, including TMZ, exert antitumor activity by inducing DNA methylation at O 6 -guanine sites, which is restored by the DNA repair enzyme O 6 -methylguanine DNA methyltransferase (MGMT). 78 Preclinical and early clinical data suggest the synergistic effect of capecitabine and TMZ through downregulation of MGMT.79,80 Therefore, MGMT deficiency has been proposed as a potential predictive biomarker for CAPTEM. In the E2211 trial, MGMT deficiency, defined as low immunohistochemistry (IHC) expression or positive promoter methylation, was associated with tumor response. 77 However, the absence of a control arm and discordance of MGMT IHC and promoter methylation limit the clinical utility of MGMT status as a predictive biomarker. Moreover, the predictive value of MGMT status remains controversial, and no standardized assessment methods have been established in real-world data analyses.81,82 Therefore, the evidence to support the routine use of MGMT testing in all patients is insufficient.

Peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (177Lu-DOTATATE)

PRRT is an internal radiation therapy that delivers radiolabeled SSAs to tumor cells expressing SSTRs. 177Lu-DOTATATE is a radiolabeled peptide in which the SSA [Tyr³]-octreotate is conjugated with the chelator DOTA and labeled with the β-emitter lutetium-177, allowing targeted irradiation of SSTR-positive tumor cells. 83

Assessment of SSTR expression is essential for determining eligibility for PRRT and evaluating the extent of disease, and has traditionally been performed using somatostatin receptor scintigraphy (SRS) with 111In-pentetreotide. 68Ga-DOTATATE, 68Ga-DOTATOC, and 68Ga-DOTANOC are SSAs labeled with positron-emitting radionuclides used in SSTR-positron emission tomography (PET) imaging. 84 SSTR-PET has demonstrated higher sensitivity and specificity for assessing extent and helps determine eligibility for PRRT, including detection of lesions that are negative on SRS due to higher spatial resolution and affinity for SSTR compared with SRS.85,86 However, given the intratumoral heterogeneity of NETs, SSTR-negative lesions sometimes coexist and are often associated with a high proliferative index.87,88 Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) has high sensitivity for detecting these lesions.87–89 Moreover, FDG-PET is useful for risk stratification and helps guide treatment strategies, as FDG-positivity is associated with aggressive tumor biology and poorer response to PRRT. As histopathological analysis cannot always reflect heterogeneity across the entire tumor burden or changes over time, FDG-PET is useful for assessing tumor biology.90–92 The use of FDG-PET to complement SSTR-PET has been proposed to characterize tumor heterogeneity by identifying both SSTR-positive and highly proliferative FDG-positive tumor components. 93 The combined use of SSTR-PET and FDG-PET (dual PET) helps identify metabolically active and SSTR-negative tumors and may guide treatment strategies in patients for whom PRRT alone is insufficient. The NETPET score has been proposed as a grading system for the interpretation of dual PET, integrating SSTR and FDG uptake patterns into a classification ranging from P0 to P5.94,95 It has been validated as a prognostic biomarker in patients with metastatic GEP-NENs and may also help guide treatment selection, including identification of candidates for PRRT. 93

NETTER-1 is a multicenter, open-label, phase III trial for SSTR-positive midgut NETs G1 and G2 pretreated with octreotide LAR to compare the efficacy of 177Lu-DOTATATE plus octreotide LAR and high-dose octreotide (Table 1).29,30 For the primary endpoint of estimated rate of PFS at 20 months, 177Lu-DOTATATE plus octreotide LAR was statistically longer than high-dose octreotide (65.2% vs 10.8%; HR, 0.21; 95% CI, 0.13–0.33; p < 0.001). The ORRs were 18% and 3%, respectively. The OS in the final analysis was not significantly different between the study groups (median, 48.0 months vs 36.3 months; HR, 0·84; 95% CI, 0.60–1.17; p = 0·30).29,30

NETTER-2 is a multicenter, open-label, phase III trial for newly diagnosed SSTR-positive GEP-NETs G2 (Ki-67 ⩾10% and ⩽20%) and G3 (Ki-67 >20% and ⩽55%) to compare the efficacy of 177Lu-DOTATATE plus octreotide LAR and high-dose octreotide LAR (Table 1). 31 The primary endpoint of PFS was statistically longer in 177Lu-DOTATATE plus octreotide LAR than in octreotide LAR (median, 22.8 months vs 8.5 months; HR, 0.276; p < 0.0001). The ORRs were 43.0% and 9.3%, respectively. Survival benefits were observed across subgroups, including Ki-67 status and primary tumors. This was the first phase III trial to show the efficacy of NETs G3.

177Lu-DOTATATE plus octreotide LAR is one of the first-line therapies for SSTR-positive G2 and G3 GEP-NETs and has been used as salvage line therapy for midgut NETs G1 (Figure 2). COMPETE is a phase III trial for SSTR-positive unresectable GEP-NETs G1–G2 comparing the efficacy of 177Lu-edotreotide and EVE, 96 and the superiority of 177Lu-edotreotide in PFS was reported in the European Society for Medical Oncology Congress 2025. These findings further support the role of PRRT in the first-line setting. The publication of these results is awaited. COMPOSE is an ongoing phase III trial for SSTR-positive GEP-NETs G2–G3 (Ki-67, 15%—55%) comparing the efficacy of 177Lu-DOTATATE and physician choice. 97 Accumulating evidence on PRRT is expected to expand its use in the future.

Multidisciplinary treatment strategies incorporating PRRT have been explored owing to PRRT’s relatively favorable ORR. In a retrospective study, 26.3% of patients with unresectable GEP-NETs were converted to resectable disease following PRRT, with encouraging survival outcomes. 98 In addition, the role of PRRT in the neoadjuvant setting has been investigated. In a multicenter retrospective study, neoadjuvant PRRT in patients with resectable or potentially resectable pNETs with high-risk features was associated with a lower incidence of postoperative pancreatic fistula and nodal metastasis at the time of resection, as well as longer PFS among patients who achieved R0 resection, compared with upfront surgery. 99 NEOLUPANET, a single-arm phase II trial in patients with resectable or potentially resectable nonfunctioning pNETs with high-risk features, reported a PR of 58.1%. 100 Among patients who underwent surgical resection, 85.7% achieved R0 resection, with grade 3 complications observed in 25.0%. Although these findings suggest a potential role of neoadjuvant PRRT, the available evidence is limited, and comparative trials evaluating long-term outcomes and safety are warranted.

Furthermore, PRRT retreatment has been explored in clinical practice. However, prospective data evaluating its long-term efficacy and safety are lacking. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, PRRT retreatment demonstrated median PFS of 12.5 months (7 studies, 414 patients), median OS of 26.8 months (2 studies, 194 patients), and DCR of 71.5% (7 studies, 314 patients). Grade 3–4 adverse events were reported in approximately 5% of patients, with no therapy-related myeloid neoplasms observed in the included studies. 101 In a retrospective study of 26 patients with GEP-NETs treated with 177Lu-DOTATATE retreatment after progression on 90Y-DOTATOC, the median PFS was 22 months, with one case each of grade 3 hematologic toxicity and nephrotoxicity. 102 In another retrospective study including 168 patients with GEP-NETs and 181 with thoracic NETs treated with 177Lu-DOTATATE retreatment, the median PFS was 14.6 months, and grade 3–4 hematologic toxicity was observed in 6.6% of patients. 103 The incidence of therapy-related myeloid neoplasms was reported to be 2.2%. Although these findings suggest that PRRT retreatment may provide clinical benefit in selected patients, the potential for cumulative hematologic and renal toxicity, as well as the lack of prospective data, should be carefully considered. NET RETREAT, a phase II trial comparing the efficacy of 177Lu-DOTATATE retreatment and molecular-targeted therapy including EVE, sunitinib, or cabozantinib is now recruiting (NCT05773274).

Molecular-targeted therapy for von Hippel–Lindau-associated pNETs (belzutifan)

VHL disease is a rare genetic disorder caused by germline pathogenic variants of the VHL gene, with an incidence rate of approximately 1 in 27,300–39,000 live births.104,105 VHL disease is associated with benign and malignant neoplasms, including clear cell renal cell carcinoma, pNETs, and hemangioblastomas of the central nervous system and retina.104,105 The VHL protein functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase that targets the alpha subunit of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) for oxygen-dependent ubiquitination and degradation. Pathogenic VHL variants impair this regulatory function, resulting in HIF stabilization and constitutive activation of HIF-dependent transcription, independent of oxygen levels, which contributes to the development of VHL-associated tumors. 106

The LITESPARK-004 study is a single-arm phase II trial of belzutifan, an HIF-2α inhibitor, for renal cell carcinoma associated with VHL disease. 107 In this study, patients with other VHL-associated tumors were included, and the outcomes were evaluated not only for renal lesions but also for other VHL-associated lesions. The ORR (primary endpoint) in renal cell carcinoma was 49%, whereas it was 91% in pNETs.107,108 Belzutifan is a therapeutic option for VHL-associated pNETs (Figure 2).

Systemic therapy for thoracic NETs

As per the 2021 WHO classification, thoracic NETs, including lung and thymic NETs, are stratified into two prognostic subtypes: typical carcinoids (Ki-67: up to 5%, mitotic rate: <2 per 10 HPF and no necrosis) and atypical carcinoids (Ki-67: up to 20%, mitotic rate: 2–10 per 10 HPF and/or punctate necrosis). 109 In the European Society of Neuroendocrine Tumors (ENETS) 2025 guidance paper for lung and thymic carcinoids, recommendations for systemic therapies are stratified by prognostic subtype (typical or atypical) and disease status (slowly progressive or progressive). 110

In patients with typical carcinoids or slowly progressive disease, SSAs are recommended for SSTR-positive NETs, while a watch-and-wait strategy can be an option. SPINET is a phase III trial comparing LAN and placebo in patients with lung NETs, which was stopped prematurely owing to slow accrual. 111 The primary endpoint of PFS was not significantly different between LAN and placebo (median, 16.6 months vs 13.6 months; HR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.48–1.95; p = 0.866), while the survival benefits were observed in typical carcinoids compared to atypical carcinoids. LUNA is a randomized phase II trial comparing long-acting pasireotide, EVE, and their combination in patients with thoracic NETs (typical carcinoids, 32%; atypical carcinoids, 68%). 112 The primary endpoint, the proportion of progression free at 9 months, was 39.0% with long-acting pasireotide, 33.3% with EVE, and 58.5% with the combination; however, the median PFS was 8.5, 12.5, and 11.8 months, respectively, suggesting that the benefit of combination therapy remains uncertain. Therefore, both SSAs and a watch-and-wait strategy are considered, particularly in slowly progressive typical carcinoids.

In patients with atypical or typical carcinoids with progressive disease, molecular-targeted therapy or cytotoxic chemotherapy are treatment options. 110 As in GEP-NETs, EVE demonstrated a significantly longer PFS compared with placebo in a subgroup analysis of lung NETs in RADIANT-4 trial (median, 9.2 months vs 3.6 months; HR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.28–0.88).49,113 EVE plus octreotide LAR showed a trend of longer PFS compared with placebo plus octreotide LAR in a subgroup analysis of lung NETs in RADIANT-2 trial; however, the difference was not statistically significant (median, 13.6 months vs 5.6 months; HR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.31–1.68).47,48,114 Cabozantinib is another therapeutic agent that has demonstrated PFS benefits compared with placebo in pretreated pNETs and extrapancreatic NETs G1–G3 in CABINET trial. 71 Furthermore, subgroup analysis of PFS also suggested benefit in thoracic NETs. 71 Cytotoxic chemotherapy, including alkylating- or platinum-based regimens, represents another therapeutic options. ATLANT is a single-arm phase II trial evaluating the efficacy of LAN plus TMZ in patients with thoracic NETs. 115 DCR at 9 months was 35.0% and the median PFS was 37.1 months, suggesting a promising efficacy of this combination; however, the trial lacked a control arm. Moreover, CAPTEM, gemcitabine plus oxaliplatin, and 5-FU plus oxaliplatin (FOLFOX) have demonstrated efficacy in lung NETs in retrospective study.116,117 177Lu-DOTATATE is another promising treatment option based on a single-arm phase II trial, with DCR of 62%, median PFS of 18.5 months, and median OS of 48.6 months. 118 A randomized phase III trial comparing the efficacy of 177Lu-edotreotide with EVE 119 and a randomized phase II trial comparing the efficacy of 177Lu-DOTATATE in SSTR-positive thoracic NETs (NCT04665739) are ongoing.

In summary, EVE, cabozantinib, and cytotoxic chemotherapy, including alkylating- or platinum-based regimens, are therapeutic options for patients with atypical or typical thoracic carcinoids with progressive disease. However, most of the available evidence is derived from studies of extrapancreatic NETs, single-arm trials, or retrospective studies, highlighting the need for prospective comparative studies focus on this population.

Current treatment sequencing in GEP-NETs

In the first-line setting, SSAs are appropriate for patients with GEP-NETs G1 and G2, particularly in those with slowly progressive disease, low tumor burden, and preferably SSTR-positive (Figure 2). In addition, the watch-and-wait strategy is an option in patients with GEP-NET G1, low tumor burden, and slowly progressive disease. 7 In patients with Ki-67 of 5–20% or <5% with high tumor burden, EVE/LAN is considered. Sunitinib is another therapeutic option for pNETs. 177Lu-DOTATATE is considered for patients with SSTR-positive GEP-NETs with Ki-67 >10%, particularly for those who need tumor shrinkage, given its relatively high ORR. In case these treatments are not appropriate, particularly in patients with high Ki-67 and high tumor burden, cytotoxic chemotherapy such as 5-FU plus STZ is considered.

In the second line or later line setting, 177Lu-DOTATATE is available in SSTR-positive GEP-NETs with Ki-67 ⩽10%. Moreover, CAPTEM is considered in pNETs G1 and G2, particularly when a tumor response is desired, including SSTR-negative cases. Cabozantinib is available for both pancreatic and extrapancreatic NETs G1–G3, with evidence supporting its use in patients who have progressed after prior 177Lu-DOTATATE. For VHL-associated pNETs, belzutifan has demonstrated a high ORR of 91%. Treatment regimens not used in the front line would be considered as second-line or later-line setting.

Current systemic therapy for NECs

First-line chemotherapy (cisplatin plus etoposide, cisplatin plus irinotecan)

Systemic chemotherapy is indicated for advanced NECs, and standard regimens for small cell lung cancer (SCLC) have been recommended by various guidelines, given the similarity in the pathological and clinical features of these tumors.7,120 TOPIC-NEC (JCOG1213) is an open-label, phase III randomized clinical trial that enrolled chemotherapy-naïve patients with unresectable GEP-NEC to compare the efficacy of cisplatin plus etoposide (EP) and cisplatin plus irinotecan (IP), the standard chemotherapy treatments for SCLC, prior to the clinical introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors (Table 1). 11 The primary endpoint of OS was comparable in EP and IP (median, 12.5 months vs 10.9 months; HR, 1.04; 90% CI, 0.79–1.37; p = 0.80). The median PFS was also comparable in each arm (median, 5.6 months vs 5.1 months; HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.78–1.45). The ORRs were 54.5% and 52.5% in the EP and IP groups, respectively. In a large retrospective review, the efficacy of carboplatin plus etoposide (EC) in NEC was equivalent to that of EP (ORR, 30% vs 31%; median PFS, 4 months vs 4 months; median OS, 11 months vs 12 months). 121 Therefore, EC is considered one of the treatment options for GEP-NECs, particularly in frail patients or those with comorbidities who may not tolerate EP, based on evidence extrapolated from SCLC. 122 Carboplatin may be more feasible in such patients given its more favorable toxicity profile, including the lack of requirement for intensive hydration compared to cisplatin.

CAPTEM is another therapeutic option that has been explored in GEP-NECs based on a single-arm phase II study and large retrospective analyses including patients with NET G3 and NEC123,124; however, the outcomes appear to be limited, with the median PFS of 3.5 months and the ORR of 14.3%. In addition, FOLFOX has been explored in clinical practice. 125 Although the median PFS of 9 months has been reported, the ORR remains 17.9%; outcomes tend to be poorer in NECs than in NETs G3, and ORRs were generally lower than those observed with EP, IP, and EC. Therefore, EP, IP, and EC remain the preferred first-line treatments.

Salvage line chemotherapy

Treatment options remain limited after the failure of first-line platinum-based chemotherapy. There are no established effective therapies in the salvage line setting for NEC, as no phase III trials with high-level evidence have been conducted to date. In clinical practice, platinum regimens, such as EP or IP, not used in the first-line setting might be an option after a treatment break, as patients who progress after the discontinuation of first-line treatment may still be platinum-sensitive. 126 Fluoropyrimidines administered in combination with irinotecan or TMZ are the most common clinical options. Several randomized phase II trials have been conducted with these regimens; however, satisfactory results have not been obtained.127–129 In clinical practice, FOLFOX and FOLFIRI have been used; however, median PFS was only 2.8 months and the PFS ratio after platinum-etoposide was low (0.46, 8% >1.3). 130

Current treatment sequencing in GEP-NECs

EP or IP is considered as first-line treatment for GEP-NECs (Figure 2). Moreover, EC is considered as first-line treatment for GEP-NECs as it does not require intensive hydration. Fluoropyrimidines administered in combination with irinotecan or TMZ, such as FOLFIRI or CAPTEM, may also be considered as alternative options. However, therapeutic options remain limited after failure of first-line platinum-based chemotherapy.

Ongoing challenges and future directions of systemic therapy in neuroendocrine neoplasms

Emerging therapeutic approaches for NETs

No-carrier-added 177Lu-DOTATATE agent for NETs

XTR008 is a no-carrier-added 177Lu-DOTATATE. Conventional carrier-added 177Lu production methods generate long-lived 177mLu (t1/2 = 160.4 days), which poses challenges to radiation safety and waste management. In contrast, no-carrier-added 177Lu contains minimal 177mLu of impurities and demonstrates equivalent targeting efficacy to conventional carrier-added 177Lu-DOTATATE. XT-XTR008-3-01 is an open-label, randomized, phase III trial comparing the efficacy of XTR008 monotherapy and high-dose octreotide LAR in previously treated G1 and G2. 131 In the interim analysis, the primary endpoint of PFS was significantly longer with XTR008 than that with octreotide LAR (median, not reached vs 5.8 months; HR, 0.06; 95% CI, 0.031–0.136; p < 0.0001). The ORRs were 55.6% and 2.1%, respectively. Survival benefits were observed across all subgroups, including Ki-67 status and primary tumor site. These findings support the efficacy of no-carrier-added 177Lu-DOTATATE beyond the midgut NETs G1, and the final results are awaited (Figure 2).

Targeted alpha therapy for NETs

212Pb-DOTAMTATE is a novel form of PRRT consisting of 212Pb isotope, a bifunctional metal chelator (DOTAM), and an SSTR-targeting peptide (TATE). 132 Compared with 177Lu-DOTAq, ²¹²Pb-DOTAMTATE delivers higher linear energy transfer over a shorter path length, leading to greater double-stranded DNA damage in tumor cells. Moreover, a shorter path length is expected to reduce off-target toxicity and minimize adverse effects in patients receiving targeted alpha therapy. 133 In a phase I dose-escalation study, 212Pb-DOTAMTATE was well-tolerated with manageable toxicity. 132 ALPHAMEDIX 02 is a phase II trial evaluating the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of 212Pb-DOTAMTATE in PRRT-naïve and PRRT-refractory patients with unresectable SSTR-positive GEP-NETs. 134 In PRRT-naïve patients, the initial ORR was 47.2%. The final results and publications are awaited (Figure 2). ACTION-1, a phase Ib/III trial of 225actinium-DOTATATE, which is another alpha therapy, for PRRT-refractory SSTR-positive unresectable GEP-NETs is currently ongoing (NCT05477576).

PRRT with SSTR antagonist

177Lu-DOTATATE is an SSTR agonist labeled with the beta-emitter 177Lu. The development of PRRT has shifted from conventional SSTR agonists to SSTR antagonists. SSTR antagonists, such as DOTA-JR11, DOTA-LM4, and DOTA-LM3, have recently emerged as promising approaches for PRRT. These antagonists exhibit faster association and slower dissociation from SSTRs, leading to higher tumor uptake, improved tumor-to-background ratio, and prolonged tumor retention.135–137 In a retrospective study evaluating the safety and efficacy of 225Ac-DOTA-LM3, an SSTR antagonist labeled with the alpha-emitter 225Ac, promising efficacy and tolerable safety were observed in heavily pretreated NETs. 138 The validation of large-scale prospective trials is eagerly awaited (Figure 2).

Emerging therapeutic approaches for NECs

Immune checkpoint inhibitors for NECs

For SCLC, immune checkpoint inhibitors and cytotoxic chemotherapy have shown efficacy in phase III clinical trials. In IMpower133, OS was statistically longer with atezolizumab plus EC compared with that with placebo plus EC (median, 13.9 months vs 12.3 months; HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.54; p = 0.02).139,140 In CASPIAN, durvalumab plus platinum-EP showed improvement in OS compared with that with platinum-EP (median, 12.9 months vs 10.5 months; HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.62–0.91; p = 0.0032).141,142

Considering the above phase III trial in SCLC, the combination of immune checkpoint inhibitors and platinum-based chemotherapy is a promising treatment strategy for GEP-NECs (Figure 2). CELEBRATE (JCOG2107E), a randomized phase III trial comparing the efficacy of durvalumab plus EC and EC, is now ongoing (jRCT2031230456). SWOG 2012 is another ongoing phase II/III trial evaluating the efficacy of platinum-EP alone, atezolizumab plus platinum-EP followed by atezolizumab maintenance, and atezolizumab plus platinum-EP followed by observation in patients with extrapulmonary NECs (NCT05058651).

Nivolumab plus ipilimumab is another emerging therapeutic option for NECs in the subsequent chemotherapy. 143 The GCO-001 NIPINEC trial was a noncomparative, open-label, phase II trial evaluating nivolumab monotherapy and nivolumab plus ipilimumab in lung and GEP-NECs. The primary endpoint of ORR at 8 weeks as assessed by investigators was 7.2% for nivolumab monotherapy and 14.0% for nivolumab plus ipilimumab. However, in the GEP-NECs subgroup, ORR at 8 weeks was only 7.1% and 9.5%, respectively, highlighting the limited efficacy of dual immunotherapy and the need for more effective therapeutic strategies.

Delta-like ligand 3-targeted therapy for NECs

Delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3) is an inhibitory ligand of the Notch pathway and is expressed in approximately 80% of SCLC and GEP-NEC.144–147 DLL3 expression is regulated by achaete-scute homolog 1, a transcription factor for oncogenic drivers in SCLC and GEP-NEC.148–151 DLL3 is typically localized intracellularly in normal cells but is abnormally expressed on the surface of SCLC, making it a promising therapeutic target not only for SCLC but also for GEP-NECs. 152 In SCLC, the development of DLL3-targeted therapies has already progressed, and their application in GEP-NECs is anticipated. Although the development of DLL3-targeting antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) has been discontinued owing to systemic toxicity,152–155 the DLL3-CD3 bispecific antibody is an emerging treatment modality.

Tarlatamab (AMG 757) is a half-life-extended bispecific T-cell engager immunotherapy targeting DLL3 and CD3 on T cells that induces the formation of cytolytic synapses in an MHC-independent manner and directs their cytolytic activity selectively to the DLL3-expressing tumor cells (Figure 3).156,157 DeLLphi-301 is an open-label phase II trial that evaluates the antitumor activity and safety of tarlatamab in patients with EC-SCLC previously treated with two or more lines of therapy. 158 The ORR (primary endpoint) was 40% in tarlatamab 10 mg group. Although careful monitoring of cytokine-release syndrome is required, tarlatamab 10 mg is now a treatment option in the salvage setting for SCLC. Tarlatamab, either as a monotherapy or in combination with cytotoxic chemotherapy, represents a promising therapeutic strategy for GEP-NECs (Figure 2), and several phase II trials are currently ongoing or in preparation (NCT06893783, NCT07061080).

Mechanism of DLL3-CD3 bispecific antibody-mediated cytotoxicity in neuroendocrine carcinoma.

DLL3-CD3 bispecific antibody is designed to bridge DLL3-positive tumor cells and CD3-positive T cells, thereby inducing the formation of a cytolytic synapse that leads to serial tumor cell lysis and T-cell proliferation.

CD3, cluster differentiation of 3; DLL3, delta-like ligand 3; Fc, fragment crystallizable; Fv, fragment variable.

BI 764532 is a novel half-life-extended DLL3/CD3 IgG-like T-cell-engaging bispecific antibody that may reduce immunogenicity risk in humans by utilizing an IgG-like architecture. 159 In the first human phase I trial, BI 764532 showed promising efficacy against both lung and extrapulmonary NECs after progression with prior chemotherapy (Figure 2).159,160 Phase II trial of BI 764532 monotherapy and phase I trial of combination therapy with cytotoxic chemotherapy (NCT06132113, NCT05882058) are currently ongoing.

Other therapeutic targets

4-1BB, also known as CD137 or tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 9, has emerged as a novel therapeutic target for NECs (Figure 2).161,162 4-1BB is expressed on the surface of activated CD8+ and CD4+ T cells and other immune cells, including B cells, natural killer cells, monocytes, and dendritic cells. 161 Activation of 4-1BB signaling enhances T-cell survival by inhibiting activation-induced cell death. 4-1BB agonists have demonstrated antitumor activity in preclinical models, highlighting 4-1BB as a promising therapeutic target.163,164 LBL-024 is a novel bispecific antibody targeting programmed death-ligand 1 and 4-1BB, and a phase Ib/II trial evaluating LBL-024 in combination with platinum-EP for extrapulmonary NEC is currently ongoing (NCT06157827).

Seizure-related 6 homolog (SEZ6) is another promising therapeutic target for NECs (Figure 2). SEZ6 expression is highly elevated in SCLC cell lines, 165 and immunohistochemical analyses have demonstrated SEZ6 positivity in SCLC specimens. 166 A phase I trial of ABBV-706, an SEZ6-directed ADC, for patients with SCLC, high-grade central nervous system tumors, and high-grade NECs is currently ongoing (NCT05599984).

Conclusion

The management of GEP-NENs has substantially evolved with advances in molecular biology and the introduction of targeted therapies and PRRT. For NETs, treatment selection is increasingly guided by the tumor grade and SSTR expression. In contrast, therapeutic options for NECs remain limited, and clinical outcomes remain unsatisfactory. Recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors and DLL3-targeted therapies have emerged as potential therapeutic approaches. Continued efforts to integrate biological understanding with clinical development are essential to improve patient outcomes.
