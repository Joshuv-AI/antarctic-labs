 1. BACKGROUND OF THIS RESOURCE

This resource was developed through the U.S. Department of Energy’s Sustainable Wastewater
Infrastructure of the Future (SWIFt) Initiative, which ran from 2016 to 2024. SWIFt was designed to help
municipal governments tackle the significant energy demands of drinking water and wastewater plants,
which often account for 30-40% of local energy consumption. SWIFt provided technical assistance,
training, and resources to help over 328 facilities across 43 states achieve energy savings between 5-25%,
with some partners identifying annual savings opportunities exceeding $130,000.

DOE’s work with facility partners produced a number of tools and resources that have since been
compiled and published into the Wastewater Energy Management Toolkit [1]. The toolkit contains dozens
of resources to help facilities track their energy performance, identify no-and low-cost savings strategies,
learn more about funding and financing mechanisms, and establish energy improvement master plans.
SWIFt concluded in 2024, but its impact endures through the Better Plants program [2], which continues
to support water utilities in pursuing long-term energy, water, and waste reduction goals.

 2. PURPOSE OF THIS DOCUMENT

This information sheet discusses the technology pillar, Energy Capture, as a pathway toward improving
wastewater infrastructure sustainability and resiliency. To supplement existing literature on current
technologies and policies for improving resiliency at wastewater treatment plants, this document aims to
accomplish:

• Summarize the existing technologies for the capture and integration of renewable energy and energy
 storage.

• Identify challenges, concerns, and limitations associated with the implementation of technologies.

• Serve as a repository for leading approaches to energy capture systems for wastewater utilities.

• Present case studies and examples of best practices.

 3. ENERGY CAPTURE AVENUES FOR WASTEWATER INDUSTRY

The wastewater (WW) industry is heavily reliant on electricity for its operations, as electrical power is
essential throughout the entire treatment process. The workflow begins with typical primary treatment
steps, which include screenings, solids removal, and clarification. Following primary treatment, aeration
is employed to promote microbial growth as part of the activated sludge process, leading into secondary
clarification. The final treatment stage involves disinfection, typically using chlorine or ultraviolet light,
before the treated water is discharged. In parallel, solids from both the primary and secondary treatment
processes undergo thickening to produce concentrated solids. Additionally, some larger treatment plants
incorporate anaerobic digesters, which further reduce solids while generating biogas for energy and
digestate for land application.

As the wastewater treatment process becomes increasingly energy-intensive, the sector is now looking
towards more sustainable solutions to reduce its reliance on traditional electricity sources. In this context,
renewables are projected to contribute 22.8% of the total electricity generation in 2024 in the U.S. [3].
The growing reliance on renewable energy sources in the energy and electricity market is expected to
extend into the water and wastewater (W/WW) sector soon. These technologies enable W/WW utilities to

 harness both new renewable sources like solar and wind, as well as existing renewable sources such as
 biogas and renewable electricity, to meet their energy and electricity demands.

                 4.    AVENUE 1 | CAPTURING AVAILABLE RENEWABLE ENERGY

The electricity and energy demand at a WWTP can be supplemented by adding renewable energy such as
solar and wind via on- and off-site generation.

4.1 SOLAR

WWTPs typically have ample land, including parking lots, which can be effectively used for solar energy
generation in areas with sufficient solar irradiance. Solar energy can be used for (1) electricity generation,
(2) water and space heating, and (3) sludge drying processes. Solar photovoltaics (PVs) and solar thermal
are the most widely used solar technologies. PVs can help meet 8%–30% [5] of a WWTP’s energy needs
in the southwestern U.S. by using accessible land area or rooftop space (e.g., on a garage or building)
where solar panels can be installed. Solar arrays produce power during the day (peak hours) and can be
used in two ways:

A. To meet a WWTP’s current electricity needs

B. To feed excess energy back to the power utility, enabling the WWTP to receive credit on its
 electricity bill by using net metering, if and where available (see this map [6] to find states where net
 metering is available)

Solar thermal technologies capture solar energy radiance for direct conversion to heat that can be used
immediately or stored for later use for WWTP process heating, water, and space heating. The application
of solar thermal heaters is best suited to areas with high solar thermal energy radiance.

Two types of solar thermal technologies are available:

• Concentrating—These systems use parabolic or curved surfaces to concentrate the solar radiation.
 They typically can be used for larger process heating to meet industrial-level heating demands.

• Non-concentrating—These systems use flat plate collectors to capture the solar radiation. They
 typically can be used for building water heating applications.

The temperature range for operation is up to 600C for solar thermal applications, with concentrating
systems operating at higher ranges (300C–600C and theoretically up to 1,200C) and non-concentrating

4.2 WIND

Like solar PV systems, wind turbines can be installed on available space at WWTPs to convert wind
energy directly to electricity. The produced electricity can power various unit treatment processes and
ancillary operations to supplement existing power generating sources at WWTPs. Typically, horizontal
axis wind turbines are deployed, in which the turbine rotates around a horizontal axis under the influence
of flowing winds and is further attached to a high-speed generator to produce electricity.

By harnessing wind energy, WWTPs can produce additional renewable electricity by using excess land
without additional fuel requirements for operations. This form of harnessing renewable energy is more
applicable at WWTPs with higher available wind velocities, such as those located closer to coasts and in
elevated mountainous regions. The 2021 levelized cost of electricity was $34/MWh for land-based
installations and $68–$94/MWh for commercial large, distributed wind energy projects [15]. Wind
turbines can be installed at $1,000–$2,000/kW.

 4

WWTPs can explore the potential to harness wind energy by looking at the geospatial data provided by
the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) [16].

EPA has provided an additional Renewable Energy Fact Sheet: Wind turbines for further resourcefulness
[17]. DOE’s Wind Energy Technologies Office provides a detailed guidance document on federal
incentives, funding, and partnership opportunities for implementing wind energy–based projects:
Advancing the Growth of the U.S. Wind Industry: Federal Incentives, Funding, and Partnership
Opportunities [18]. Additional resources are provided through DOE’s Onsite Energy Program’s
Technology Fact Sheets [19].

                5.   AVENUE 2 | RENEWABLE ELECTRICITY PROCUREMENTS

Renewable electricity procurements can enable W/WW utilities to integrate renewable-based energy in
their input electricity mix to diversify their energy portfolio. A Renewable energy certificate (REC) is a
market-driven entity that represents the ownership rights to the nonpower attributes of renewable
electricity generation, including environmental and social attributes of renewable electricity generation.
RECs are issued upon the generation and delivery of 1 MWh of renewable energy source to the electricity
grid. These tradable, nontangible commodities in the energy market represent the benefits associated with
1 MWh of generated renewable energy.

All renewable projects will produce two products: (1) low-cost electricity and (2) RECs. The renewable
electricity procurement options. If the project is owned by the WW utilities, as shown in Figure 2, they
can be sold off to the grid or nearby facilities to generate additional revenue. When a WW utility
purchases an amount of renewable electricity along with the RECs associated with that amount, the RECs
are known as bundled RECs. Otherwise, when the WW utility chooses to purchase only the
environmental benefits of renewable energy, the purchase takes place through an unbundled REC without
the associated energy stream. WW utilities can obtain RECs through the following options:

• On-site generation at the utility
• Power purchase agreements (PPAs)
• Utility programs (e.g., green tariffs)
• Purchase of unbundled RECs

              6.        AVENUE 3 | ENERGY CAPTURE FROM TERTIARY EFFLUENT

In-line hydropower electricity is generated from turbines installed inside water pipelines, usually with the
vertical axis configuration and attached to an electric generator outside the pipe. This can be achieved by
using a hydro-turbine to convert water’s kinetic energy (flow of water) and potential energy (head of
water) into mechanical work and thus generate electricity. WWTPs can use the available head from
tertiary effluent downstream of the last process channel and the outfall structure, where the outgoing flow
can be captured and directed to a channel with hydropower-generating turbines. The critical success
factors toward the applicability of in-line hydropower at WWTPs include:

• Available head and water flow velocity
• Turbine/generator unit tolerant of submergence
• Ease of installation/maintenance
• Proximity to facility power distribution and loads

Advantages and ease of using in-line hydropower include:

• Minimized space requirements to generate >30 kW of cost-efficient renewable energy, because it can
 be installed in the outfall pipe,

• Supports onsite energy generation at sites providing relatively high effective head (in cases of small
 flow and large head)

• Increased efficiency via effective use of residual pressure in previously unused water supply facilities

• Reduced installation product cost through an integrated water turbine and generator

A survey by the New York State Energy Research and Development Authority of 78 of that state’s
WWTPs noted that over 75% have the potential to use in-line hydro-turbines to generate hydropower and
renewable electricity [23]. The practical operational ranges that WWTPs can use as a reference to assess
the potential for in-line hydropower generation are provided in Table 2.

                   7.     AVENUE 4 | ENERGY CAPTURE FROM WASTED SLUDGE

The bioreactors, primary and secondary settlers, and clarifiers at WWTPs can be treated to produce
concentrated digested sludge and biogas through anaerobic digestion (AD), a technology for sewage
sludge treatment that allows for generation of renewable energy. During AD, microorganisms break down
the organic matter in the absence of oxygen contained in the sludge and convert it into biogas that can be

 7

used for producing electricity, heat, and biofuel. The pathways to repurpose wasted sludge to capture
energy are shown in Figure 4.

 Figure 4. Pathways to repurpose biomass or wastewater sewage sludge at WWTPs.

Generally, processing biomass via biochemical processes (i.e., AD) or thermochemical processes (i.e.,
gasification) will produce fuel gas, although the AD process is more commonly applied. It is a renewable
energy resource that is a blend of methane (50%–70% CH4), carbon dioxide (30%–49% CO2), and other
gases released when sludge decomposes anaerobically. The high methane content of biogas makes it
flammable and suitable as an energy source for heat and electricity generation. More than 16,000 WWTPs
in the United States can produce 1 ft3 of digestor gas (raw biogas) per 100 gal of WW [24]. WW utilities
can access NREL geospatial data to explore the methane generation [25]. If fully used, the energy
generated at WWTPs through biogas could reach 2.3 MMT/year [26]. The benefits of producing biogas at
WWTPs are presented in Figure 5.

7.1 BIOMETHANE OR RENEWABLE NATURAL GAS

The operational ranges affecting the AD process are shown in Table 3. For biogas produced from sewage
sludge (as well as from landfills), particular attention must be paid to the concentration of siloxanes,
which can lead to deposits in combustion equipment and deterioration of performance. During biogas
conditioning, contaminants such as sulfur species (hydrogen sulfide), silicon species (siloxane), and
moisture are removed from biogas to avoid damage to end use equipment, risks to human health, and
pollution of the environment. Post-conditioning, biogas upgrading increases the calorific value of biogas
by increasing its methane content to produce high-purity fuel for electricity, heat generation, and vehicles.
This process increases the CH4 content of the biogas (45%–65% CH4) to biomethane or renewable
natural gas (RNG, 95%–99% CH4) by removing moisture, particulates, contaminants, and other gases
(CO2 [35%–45%], O2 [0%–2%], N2, H2, H2S [at 0%–1%], and volatile organic compounds (VOCs)
[<1%]). RNG produced that meets pipeline specifications can be marketed and injected into the common
carrier pipeline or used locally to refuel transportation vehicles (~90% of current RNG production) or
supply CHP systems. The steps for converting untreated biogas to treated biogas/RNG are shown in
Figure 6. A summary of biogas upgrading technologies is provided in Table 3.

7.2 COMBINED HEAT AND POWER

Combined heat and power (CHP), also known as cogeneration, is the concurrent production of electricity
or mechanical power and useful thermal energy (heating and/or cooling) from a single source of energy.
This suite of technologies can use a variety of fuels to generate electricity or power at the point of use,
allowing the heat that would normally be lost in the power generation process to be recovered to provide
needed heating and/or cooling. It serves as a type of distributed generation, which, unlike central station
generation, is located at or near the point of consumption.

Biogas produced via AD can also be used directly in a CHP system to produce electricity and thermal
energy that can be used on-site by WWTPs to supplement their operational energy needs. CHP increases
energy efficiency for energy recovery from biogas at WWTPs. An estimated 81.6 GW of existing CHPs
are used at more than 4,700 industrial, institutional, and commercial facilities to help save 1.3 quadrillion
Btu of fuel and 215 MT CO2 annually [33]. About 15% of existing CHP generation capacity uses
biomass, biogas, or municipal and process waste fuel. CHP currently uses renewable fuels, low-carbon
waste fuels, and hydrogen where available, and will be ready to use higher levels of biogas, RNG, and
hydrogen in the future. A case study of a proposed CHP system is shown in Table 5.

                         8.   AVENUE 5 | STORING CAPTURED ENERGY

The adoption and implementation of energy capture technologies discussed in Sections 3–5 of this report
may require solutions for storing on-site generated energy and maintaining balanced electrical loads with
grid electricity. Energy storage involves accumulating energy produced at one time for later use, helping
to address mismatches between energy generation and demand. Energy storage systems (ESSs) enhance
energy security by providing reliable, on-site sources of stored energy. The need for stability of the
energy infrastructure has spurred growth in the ESS market, which was valued at approximately $46.4
billion is U.S. dollars in 2023 and is expected to reach $114.3 billion USD in 2030 [36]. This growth was
associated with policy incentives for renewable energy and storage systems. This is particularly pertinent
in the context of the extensive implementation and integration of variable renewable energy sources like
solar and wind into the grid. ESSs generate value by creating a more resilient energy infrastructure by
providing protection against short-term grid disruptions. Whereas ESSs typically use the electric power
grid for self-charging and are co-located with a renewable energy facility for efficient energy capture and
storage, they can also be deployed to capture grid electricity for current or delayed use.

Having an ESS may provide the following benefits to WW utilities:

• Increased energy efficiency by enabling reliable power supply and the use of stored energy as needed.

• Emission reductions via off-grid power generation from renewable energy sources such as solar and
 wind.

• Cost savings by helping electricity grids operate at a consistent average load rather than fluctuating
 peak and valley loads.

• Availability of back-up power and reduced end-user demand and demand charges.

• Potential integration with microgrids for remote, isolated, and small-size communities and industrial
 customers.

The electricity generation capacity of ESSs is measured in two primary ratings [37]:

• Power capacity: the maximum instantaneous amount of electric power that can be generated
 continuously, measured in units of watts (kilowatts, megawatts, or gigawatts)

• Energy capacity: the total amount of energy that can be stored in or discharged from the storage
 system, measured in units of watt-hours (kilowatt-hours, megawatt-hours, or gigawatt-hours)

In the U.S., the following ESS technologies are commercially available:

• Batteries (electrochemical): A battery ESS (BESS) stores energy in batteries such as lithium-ion,
 lead-acid, and other minerals such as vanadium and zinc for battery components, typically for
 residential, commercial, industrial, and directly connected systems [35].

• Solar electric with thermal storage: This ESS uses thermal energy storage solutions such as molten
 salts or water to trap and store solar energy for later conversion to electricity. The stored thermal
 energy can be used during periods without sunlight—such as nighttime—or during grid outages,
 improving system reliability and continuous energy availability.

 14

• Pumped-storage hydroelectric: These are the oldest and largest (in power and energy capacity)
 ESSs, where electricity from power grids is used to operate hydroelectric turbines that pump water to
 store in a reservoir. The stored water is later used to generate electricity.

• Compressed-air energy storage (CAES): Air is compressed and injected into an underground cavity
 during periods of lower electricity demand when rates are more economical. When energy is required,
 the compressed air is released from the underground storage and used for heating and generating
 electricity. CAES significantly enhances the energy output with efficiency ranges of 42%–55%.

• Flywheels: These provide effective load-leveling and load-shifting because of their long lifecycle,
 high energy density, low maintenance costs, and quick response speeds.

The following ESSs are under various stages of research, development, and commercialization:

• Electrical energy storage via capacitors, supercapacitors, and superconducting magnetic energy
 storage with electrical charge based electrochemical double layers to store energy.

• Chemical energy based storage that uses process-produced chemicals such as ammonia, hydrogen,
 methanol, and synthetic fuels to store the energy.

• Thermal storage systems that store energy in low process fluid temperatures using electricity during
 off-peak hours for dispensing electricity later.

The most developed ESS is the BESS, specifically the type with lithium-ion batteries. Battery prices have
declined in the past decade; however, further cost reductions will depend not only on technological
advancements but also on critical mineral availability and battery production costs. Flow batteries are
emerging as an alternative to lithium-ion batteries for stationary storage because of significantly lower
performance degradation over 25–30 years and sizing capabilities for supplying continuous power. The
storage capacity and discharge time for various ESSs are shown in Figure 8. ESSs that feature increased
storage capacity and extended discharge durations, such as pumped-hydro, compressed-air, and high-
capacity battery, can deliver a steady supply of electricity for prolonged durations, spanning several hours
or more. These systems are mainly deployed to mitigate the effects of daily and seasonal fluctuations.
Conversely, systems with smaller storage capacity and briefer discharge times, like battery storage
systems, flywheels, and supercapacitors, offer swift responses to fluctuations in electricity demand.

8.2 ENERGY STORAGE PERFORMANCE TESTING

Energy storage performance testing is required to develop and validate performance metrics to measure
the efficiency of ESSs for informed decision-making on installation, use, and decommissioning of ESSs.
The important metrics for ESS are:

• Cycle life: how long or how many cycles (discharge time) the ESS can provide until it fails or
 significantly degrades. This depends on the operating temperature and depth of discharge.

• Capacity: The determined amperage-hour capacity of the battery between a set maximum and
 minimum operating voltage at a specified discharge rate.

• Rate performance: the capacity the ESS can deliver under different rates of charge/discharge.

Component testing on cells, modules, or the entire packaged system warrants safe and efficient operation
of ESSs. Component testing measures charge rate, input and output current and voltage, thermal output,
system temperatures, and ambient conditions at a temporal resolution.

• Phosphorous management for side-stream treatment

Pima County has also explored phosphorous management through side-stream treatment. This option
enables polymer savings, struvite mitigation savings, reduced dryer cake volume, and recycle load with
improved centrifuge operation and maintenance. This not only allows increased process efficiency and
resource utilization but also allows the County to market a potential waste product.

The facility uses ANITA Mox System Configuration for nutrient sequestration and side-stream treatment.
The deammonification of centrate is done by retrofitting two zones in one of the existing clarifiers.
