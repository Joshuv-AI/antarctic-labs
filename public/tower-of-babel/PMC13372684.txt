Disease-specific drivers of sex- and locality-related disparities in life expectancy at age 65: two decades of progress before COVID-19
Authors: Galina Gorbunova, Arseniy P Yashkin, Julia Kravchenko, Svetlana Ukraintseva, Eric Stallard, Anatoliy Yashin, Igor Akushevich, Monique Janiel Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesThe distribution of life expectancy at age 65 (LE65) in the United States is characterized by profound sex- and locality-related disparities. Quantifying the disease-specific drivers of these disparities prior to the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic provides a critical baseline for understanding subsequent health shocks.MethodsUsing CDC WONDER data (1999-2018) and Pollard’s decomposition method, we analyzed cause-specific contributions to disparities in LE65, establishing a pre-pandemic baseline for trends in years of life lost (YLL) at age 65.ResultsSex-related disparities (YLLsex) narrowed, particularly in disadvantaged localities, driven by reductions in YLL from macrovascular diseases and lung cancer. Conversely, locality-related disparities (YLLloc) widened, especially for females, due to increasing YLL from Alzheimer’s disease and the persistent impacts of diabetes, chronic lower respiratory diseases, and heart failure. This created a pre-existing landscape of vulnerability concentrated in low-LE states.Discussion and ImplicationsThe 2 decades before COVID-19 saw a critical divergence: progress in reducing sex-based gaps was offset by rapidly widening geographic disparities. The systemic failures that drove the increasing burden of chronic conditions in disadvantaged regions likely predetermined the populations most vulnerable to the pandemic’s shock. Our findings highlight that building future resilience requires targeted investments to address these specific, pre-existing health challenges.Keywords: Mortality trends, State-level variation, Chronic disease burden

This study identifies the major disease drivers of disparities in life expectancy at age 65 across U.S. populations using an exact cause-of-death decomposition applied to national mortality data. By focusing on the final pre-COVID period, the analysis establishes a baseline structure of mortality disparities before pandemic disruptions. The results identify conditions responsible for the largest share of longevity gaps and provide actionable evidence to guide prevention strategies, chronic disease management, and public health resource allocation aimed at reducing disparities among older adults.

Introduction

Life expectancy (LE) is a critical indicator of population health and well-being, yet its distribution in the United States reflects profound differences. Although LE has risen significantly over the past century, stark disparities persist across demographic and geographic groups.1–11 These disparities extend beyond individual health outcomes, serving as indicators of systemic differences that determine who benefits from longer, healthier lives and who faces preventable, premature mortality. These disparities are reflected in the years of life lost (YLL) at age 65 due to differences in sex (YLLsex) and locality (YLLloc) and are driven by prevailing differences in determinants of health. The historical female advantage in longevity, while narrowing, reflects higher male mortality from modifiable risks like cardiovascular disease,3–5,8,10,12,13 alongside a higher burden of age-related chronic illness and disability among women due to their longer lifespans.14,15 Simultaneously, place of residence creates a powerful geographic gradient in health,1,2,11,16,17 where rural and disadvantaged regions face systemic barriers that worsen outcomes for chronic conditions.14,15,18–21

As individuals age, the effects of sex- and locality-related health disparities increasingly intersect. In later life, structural vulnerabilities tied to geography (eg, underfunded healthcare, limited specialist access, and higher poverty) play a greater role in shaping how biological and behavioral sex-based risk manifests, contributing to compounded risk profiles in low-LE states. Consequently, the 65+ population offers a crucial perspective on these disparities, as this group bears a disproportionate burden of chronic and age-related conditions that impact LE. Macrovascular diseases, defined here as major vascular and circulatory conditions including atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, acute myocardial infarction, chronic ischemic heart disease, heart failure, hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, and other circulatory diseases, become key contributors to life expectancy at age 65 (LE65) and underscore the transition from infectious to chronic diseases as the dominant causes of mortality in later life.8,22,23

This study quantifies trends in YLLsex and YLLloc in LE65, from 1999 to 2018 by decomposing the total YLL into the relative contributions of 37 adverse health conditions. A focus on disease-specific contributions offers actionable insights to guide public health strategies aimed at reducing preventable mortality among older adults. Furthermore, establishing the pre-2020 baseline is critical for a deeper understanding of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic’s differential impact. The pandemic reversed decades of progress in LE, with disproportionately higher mortality observed in rural and socioeconomically disadvantaged regions.24–26 We hypothesize that this was not a novel phenomenon, but rather the consequence of a pre-existing landscape of vulnerability, shaped by the very disparities in chronic and age-related disease impacts we analyze here. This study therefore provides an essential etiological baseline, quantifying the specific disease drivers whose pre-pandemic trajectories likely predetermined which populations and places were most susceptible to the shock of COVID-19.

Methods

Using the multiple cause-of-death data from the CDC WONDER27 database, this analysis applies the integral representation of Pollard’s decomposition described in detail in28 to quantify cause-specific contributions to disparities in LE65. Annual period life tables for ages 65 and older were constructed using age-specific mortality rates in single-year intervals, with an open-ended final age interval. Life expectancy at age 65 was calculated using standard period life table methods. Because the analysis relies on complete population death counts rather than sampled data, the resulting quantities are deterministic arithmetic values rather than statistical estimates. The method allows exact evaluation of these contributions without numerical integration, supports both registry and administrative claims data, and preserves additivity, thereby ensuring that all cause-specific effects sum precisely to the total disparity. It also avoids assuming independence among causes of death, improving accuracy when morbidities interact. Formally, the decomposition expresses the difference in life expectancy between two populations as an exact additive sum of cause-specific contributions derived using the integral representation of Pollard’s method. The approach is robust to discontinuities in survival or hazard functions and yields unbiased estimates even when deaths cluster at exact ages. These features are essential because LE disparities, though well documented, often obscure which causes of death drive them. The method clarifies this by quantifying each disease’s contribution, enabling decision-makers to prioritize conditions that account for the largest share of preventable mortality, with full accounting and internal consistency. Conceptually, each cause-specific component represents the portion of the total life expectancy disparity attributable to differences in mortality from that cause, consistent with the exact additive structure of the Pollard decomposition. In plain terms, each cause’s contribution represents the portion of the total longevity gap attributable to differences in mortality from that cause, that is, the amount of the gap that would be reduced if both groups had the same mortality from that condition.

The geographic dimension of disparities in LE65 was framed using the final pre-pandemic year as a reference point. We ranked all states by LE65 using 2018 CDC WONDER27 data and identified states at both extremes of the distribution to represent high- and low-LE profiles. To improve comparability, California and New York were excluded from the high-LE comparison group because their large, highly urbanized, and demographically heterogeneous populations make them less comparable to the other high-LE states. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that their exclusion did not materially alter the substantive conclusions. The final high-LE group included Hawaii, Florida, Arizona, Connecticut, Minnesota, and Colorado; the low-LE group included Arkansas, Tennessee, Louisiana, Oklahoma, Kentucky, Alabama, Mississippi, and West Virginia.

The total 37 causes of death (Table 1) analyzed were grouped into the broad categories of macrovascular diseases, cancers, and other conditions for summary presentation. The top 17 of these contributors (highlighted in italics in Table 1) were selected for detailed trend analysis in Figures 1 and 2. ICD-10 codes are provided and discussed in.28

The primary results in Table 1 report the change in YLL for the disadvantaged population within each disparity framework over the 1999-2018 period: males relative to females for sex-related disparities, and residents of low-LE states relative to residents of high-LE states for locality-related disparities. The table therefore presents results across both disparity dimensions, including sex-stratified locality comparisons. These values are not statistical estimates but are exact arithmetic quantities derived from the Pollard decomposition.28 In this table, a negative value indicates a reduction in YLL for the disadvantaged group (ie, a narrowing of the disparity), while a positive value indicates an increase in YLL for the disadvantaged group (ie, a widening of the disparity). This metric effectively functions as a difference-in-difference measure, quantifying how the gap for each cause changed over the study period. Similarly, in the figures, negative contributions represent YLL attributed to the disadvantaged group (males in Figure 1; low-LE states in Figure 2), while positive contributions represent YLL for the advantaged group. Consequently, a trend toward smaller negative values over time indicates a narrowing disparity for that cause, while a trend toward larger negative values reflects a widening gap.

Results

Over the 1999-2018 period, males in low-LE states gained 1.82 years (14.17-15.98) of LE65, compared to a 2.34-year (16.37-18.71) gain in the high-LE states. Improvement in female LE65 was more modest, though from higher starting positions, with females in low-LE and high-LE states gaining 1.06 years (17.54-18.60) and 1.89 years (19.35-21.24), respectively. YLLsex narrowed in both low-LE (−3.38 to −2.62 male YLL; −0.76 YLL) and high-LE states (−2.99 to −2.53 male YLL; −0.46 YLL), but YLLloc increased for both males (−2.29 to −2.73 YLL in low-LE states; +0.44 YLL) and females (−1.81 to −2.64 YLL in low-LE states; +0.83 YLL). These differences indicate that while YLLsex narrowed more in low-LE states, YLLloc widened more significantly for females relative to males. These descriptive life table summaries provide contextual background and are presented in narrative form because they are not part of the decomposition outputs shown in Table 1 and Figures 1 and 2.

In 2018, macrovascular diseases were the strongest contributors to disparities in LE65 collectively accounting for 39.41% (−1.02 YLL) of YLLsex and 36.31% (−0.97 YLL) of YLLloc. Chronic ischemic heart disease (CHD; YLLsex = −0.40 YLL; YLLloc = −0.02 YLL), acute myocardial infarction (MI; YLLsex = −0.20 YLL; YLLloc = −0.22 YLL), heart failure (HF; YLLsex = −0.08 YLL; YLLloc = −0.20 YLL), and hypertension (HT; YLLsex = −0.06 YLL; YLLloc = −0.17 YLL) were the main contributors; with cardiovascular (CAD; YLLsex = −0.12 YLL; YLLloc = −0.06 YLL), cerebrovascular (CVD; YLLsex = −0.04 YLL; YLLloc = −0.12 YLL), and other circulatory diseases (DCS; YLLsex = −0.13 YLL; YLLloc = −0.18 YLL) also playing significant roles. Since 1999, macrovascular conditions have demonstrated consistent reductions in associated YLL with a U.S.-wide mitigation of 0.29 YLL for males. High-LE states exhibited faster improvements. Among specific conditions, MI, CVD, CHD, and DCS contributed to reductions in YLLsex, with MI showing the greatest overall improvement for both YLLsex and YLLloc. In contrast, HF and HT were associated with increased YLL during the 1999-2018 period. The impact of HT was particularly pronounced in YLLloc, where it accounted for an additional −0.12 YLL among residents of disadvantaged states, affecting both sexes with comparable magnitude.

Cancers accounted for 28.48% (−0.75 YLL) and 15.25% (−0.41 YLL) of YLLsex and YLLloc, respectively. Lung cancer (LC; YLLsex = −0.18 YLL; YLLloc = −0.18 YLL), prostate cancer (PC; YLLsex = −0.25 YLL; YLLloc = −0.01 YLL), and other fast progressive cancers (FPC; YLLsex = −0.17 YLL; YLLloc = −0.06 YLL) were the main contributors. Breast cancer (BC; YLLsex =0.18 YLL; YLLloc = −0.02 YLL) is an overwhelmingly female condition, which reduced the size of the male/female disparity. All other cancers accounted for −0.32 and −0.13 YLL of total YLLsex and YLLloc, respectively.

Over the 1999-2018 period, the reduction in YLL to YLLsex from cancers was predominantly driven by LC, which accounted for the largest decrease in YLL among all study conditions. Over time, the impact of PC on male YLL decreased, while the adverse effects of BC on female YLL remained relatively constant. FPC showed the most significant adverse change in associated YLL, contributing increases of +0.04 YLL to YLLsex and +0.03 YLL to YLLloc.

Other conditions of note included chronic lower respiratory diseases (CRD), which accounted for 10.4% (−0.278 years) of the YLLsex and 6.2% (−0.111 years) of YLLloc. Injury and poisoning (I&P) contributed 7.0% (−0.187 years) to YLLsex and 8.5% (−0.187 years) to YLLloc. Diabetes mellitus (DM; −0.113 years, 4.2%) and renal failure (RF; −0.090 years, 3.4%) made moderate contributions to YLLsex, while Alzheimer’s disease (AD; −0.223 years, 8.4% for YLLsex and −0.076 years, 4.3% for YLLloc) had a smaller but notable effect. Influenza and pneumonia (PNEU) and other non-cancer conditions rounded out the disparities, with sex-related losses of −0.054 years (2.0%) and −0.386 years (14.4%), respectively. Locality-related disparities were smaller in these categories (−0.030 years [1.7%] for PNEU and −0.095 years [5.4%] for non-cancer conditions).

The widening of YLLloc was primarily driven by a cluster of conditions distinct from those behind the narrowing of YLLsex. While macrovascular diseases and cancers reduced disparities overall, AD, chronic lower respiratory diseases, and hypertension exerted a strong adverse impact, predominantly by widening the gap between high-LE and low-LE states. Other contributors had an overall adverse impact, predominantly acting through YLLloc, with a combined additional 0.6578 YLL to residents of disadvantaged states. AD was the high-LE contributor, accounting for an additional 0.2023 YLL to disadvantaged states, increasing to 0.2982 YLL when combined with other dementias. AD disproportionately affected female LE65, resulting in a beneficial effect on YLLsex (−0.0667 YLL). CRD exhibited a similar pattern, contributing +0.1929 YLL to YLLloc, with a disproportional effect on females and −0.1150 YLL to YLLsex.

Discussion

In the decades preceding the COVID-19 pandemic, the United States exhibited a dual trajectory in population health: sex-based disparities in LE narrowed, largely through gains in cardiovascular and oncological care, while locality-related disparities widened, reflecting persistent failures in chronic disease prevention and management. Pre-existing regional disparities in the burden of hypertension, diabetes, and heart failure were concentrated in under-resourced areas and plausibly heightened vulnerability to severe COVID-19 outcomes in those settings.29–31

Diverging trajectories within macrovascular diseases exposed a central limitation of recent advances: effectiveness was unequal across the care continuum. Acute interventions for myocardial infarction diffused widely and reached disadvantaged groups.30,32–35 In contrast, sustained management of hypertension and heart failure, which depends on coordinated longitudinal care, lagged in under-resourced regions. This produced a growing population in low-LE states that survived acute events yet remained at risk for progressive chronic disease.31,36 The pandemic then exploited this vulnerability, because the same structural barriers that impeded chronic disease management also constrained testing, outpatient monitoring, and timely escalation of care during COVID-19, particularly in high-need regions.37,38

Lung cancer’s diminishing role in sex differences in life expectancy reflects the success of tobacco control and treatment, but the benefits accrued unevenly. Men realized larger gains, whereas women, especially in disadvantaged regions, showed slower improvement consistent with cohort smoking histories and possible inequities in access to contemporary oncology.39–41 These patterns indicate that narrowing sex gaps can coincide with emergent geographic gaps when baseline risks interact with uneven care infrastructure.42,43 Prostate cancer shows a parallel mechanism: screening and targeted therapies improved outcomes overall, yet their diffusion varied across regions, likely contributing to widening geographic disparities in total cancer mortality.44,45

Contrasting trajectories in chronic respiratory disease and AD indicate a shift in disparity drivers. Although chronic respiratory disease disparities narrowed, their persistent burden in low-LE states signals the limits of broad public health measures when regional exposures remain entrenched.16,46–48 AD emerged as a defining driver of contemporary disparities. Its growing impact on women in disadvantaged regions reflects aging demographics, gaps in primary and specialty care, and unaddressed social determinants. The pre-pandemic rise of AD as a contributor to mortality disparities21,49 created highly vulnerable populations in low-LE states, notably older women with cognitive impairment in under-resourced long-term care settings, who proved especially susceptible to COVID-19 mortality.

We acknowledge several limitations. Determining the underlying cause of death is challenging in older adults, who often have multiple comorbid conditions at the time of death.50 Our analysis relied on official underlying cause-of-death determinations; the relative contributions of specific diseases to disparities might differ if all conditions listed on the death certificate were considered. Furthermore, the trends we identified, particularly for AD, reflect a combination of real mortality changes and temporal or geographic variation in diagnosis and cause-of-death coding practices. However, in the latter case, over-attribution to one cause implies offsetting under-attribution elsewhere, and our primary outcome makes the difference in life expectancy robust to such diagnostic transfer. Finally, the relationship between chronic diseases and life expectancy is complex. Reducing YLL from one cause does not guarantee a commensurate gain in overall life expectancy, due to competing risks from other conditions, though interventions targeting shared risk factors could have multiplicative benefits.

Conclusion

The 2 decades preceding the COVID-19 pandemic were characterized by a critical divergence in U.S. population health: significant progress in reducing sex-based disparities in life expectancy at age 65 was starkly offset by a rapid widening of geographic disparities. Our analysis demonstrates that this was not a monolithic trend, but the result of distinct disease pathways. While advancements against macrovascular diseases and cancers narrowed the sex gap, systemic failures in managing chronic conditions (most notably AD, hypertension, and heart failure) drove the geographic gap wider, creating a preexisting landscape of vulnerability concentrated in low-LE states.

This landscape of unequal chronic disease burden likely shaped the pandemic’s uneven impact, as the same structural inequities in healthcare access and infrastructure that contributed to these disparities may also have constrained an effective response to COVID-19. Strengthening the nation’s resilience against future health shocks therefore requires directly addressing these same vulnerabilities. We propose 4 complementary priorities: (1) Expand healthcare infrastructure in low-LE states by investing in rural hospitals, telemedicine, and specialized chronic disease management centers; (2) Enhance prevention and screening for cardiovascular diseases and cancers in underserved populations to sustain hard-won gains; (3) Prioritize dementia care, caregiver support, and sex-specific research on AD, especially for women in rural areas; and (4) Tackle foundational inequities in education, housing, and income that underlie differential resilience to health crises. Addressing these priorities is imperative not only to improve longevity but to build a more equitable and robust foundation for health in an aging nation.
