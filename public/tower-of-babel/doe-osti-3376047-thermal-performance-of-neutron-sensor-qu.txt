 SUMMARY

 The neutron sensor qualification device was developed to provide a
temperature-controlled environment for neutron sensors and dosimetry, enabling
irradiation in a neutron field at the Armed Forces Radiobiology Research
Institute (AFRRI) Training, Research, Isotopes, General Atomics (TRIGA)
reactor facility. The device, constructed from low-activation and low-neutron-
cross-section materials, features a modular tube furnace design with three
independently controlled heating zones for controlling the axial temperature
distribution. Laboratory testing validated the device’s thermal performance,
including a uniform temperature distribution with less than 6°C variation across
the central region, a steady-state operational temperature of 350°C achieved in
approximately 3 hours, and a cooling time constant of 3.5 hours. External surface
temperatures stayed safe for handling, with the surrounding aluminum structure
remaining at ambient conditions. The results confirm the device’s suitability for
neutron sensor qualification experiments, with potential for future operation at
higher temperatures and further optimization of performance.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Design of the neutron sensor qualification device was initiated in fiscal year 2024, with the goal of
housing neutron sensors and dosimetry in a temperature-controlled environment that could be irradiated
in a neutron field. It was designed for use in conjunction with the Armed Forces Radiobiology Research
Institute (AFRRI) irradiation facility. The AFRRI facility has exposure rooms adjacent to a 1.1 MW
Training, Research, Isotopes, General Atomics (TRIGA) reactor capable of housing large experiment
devices such as the one presented here. The system has been designed, assembled, and thermally tested in
a laboratory setting. This report documents the thermal testing and performance results obtained from the
laboratory testing.
 2. DESIGN
 The neutron sensor qualification device is essentially designed as a tube furnace built from low-
activation and low-neutron-cross-section materials. Additionally, the heater electronics can be located
remotely outside of the radiation area. A 3D model of the device design is shown in Error! Reference
source not found.. AFRRI has two exposure rooms in which the device can be irradiated. These rooms
feature either a cadmium- or lead-lined wall that separates the reactor from the exposure rooms, thus
allowing the two rooms to each provide a different neutron spectrum. To enable easier handling, the
furnace frame, made from extruded aluminum, is mounted on wheels.

Figure 1. 3D model of the neutron sensor qualification device.

 The furnace is heated by three cable heaters, each located in a separate zone. Each zone can be
controlled independently to better control the axial temperature distribution in the furnace. Grade 2
titanium plates are used for mounting alumina tubes in order to provide locating features for the sensors
inside the furnace and to afford a structure for installing the cable heaters on. Alumina-silica-based
insulation is then wrapped around the furnace and covered with a thin sheet of aluminum to protect the
insulation and hold it in place. The tubes inside the furnace position the sensors such that neutron

shadowing and albedo effects are minimized, as shown in Figure 2. The sensors are held in place axially
by a shaft collar that is inserted between two plates at the end of the furnace. Sensors can be installed
from both ends of the furnace, providing up to 20 positions for sensors in the device.

 3. ASSEMBLY AND TESTING
 The furnace system has been fully assembled in the Measurement Science Laboratory at Idaho
National Laboratory. Photographs from different stages of the assembly process are shown in Figure 3.
After the assembly was completed, shakedown thermal testing was conducted in a laboratory setting to
confirm correct operation and test the temperature characteristics observed in the furnace during
operation. Initial operation of the furnace will extend up to 350°C, with future testing potentially reaching
up to 700°C. The temperature distribution and heating/cooling rates presented herein were evaluated for
operation at 350°C. Exterior temperature was evaluated via an infrared camera and point measurement
with a thermocouple in order to evaluate the potential worker hazard surrounding the furnace. These
evaluations are presented in the following subsections.

 3.1. Temperature Distribution
 After the furnace had reached a thermal steady state, the axial temperature distribution was measured
by positioning a type K thermocouple at various axial locations along the furnace tube. Each position is
referenced based on the numbering system given in Figure 2. Figure 4 and Figure 5 provide the results for
the sensors positioned in tubes (1, 2, 3) and (4, 5, 6, 7), respectively. These temperature distributions are
slightly asymmetric. This is believed to be due to the tubes being filled with sensors only from one side,
thus allowing more air flow into the testing tubes from the opposite side of the furnace. Future testing will
confirm this and perhaps the configuration will be altered to further homogenize the temperature
distribution. Regardless of this asymmetry, the temperature distribution is remarkably uniform, with the
variation between tubes at the center of the furnace (i.e., positions 1–3 in the first column closest to the
reactor) being <2°C and the variation between all the tubes being approximately 5.6°C. The temperature
measurements obtained 6 in. away in either direction results in a temperature distribution of 5.8°C from
the closest column of tubes, and 15°C variation between all the tubes.

 3.2. Heating and Cooling Times
 For operational considerations, the furnace heating and cooling times were evaluated. Figure 6 plots
the furnace temperatures, which range from ambient temperature to 350°C. The furnace largely reaches a
steady state temperature of 350°C after about 3 hours, and achieves a somewhat more homogenized
distribution at about 4 hours. After stabilizing at 350°C, the furnace heaters were shut off to provide the
cool down time (Figure 7). This cool down generally follows Newton’s law of cooling, with a time
constant, τ, of approximately 3.5 hours.

 3.3. Structure Temperatures
 The temperatures of the structure were evaluated via an infrared camera and point temperature
measurements using a type K thermocouple. The image collected, as well as the point measurements, are
presented in Figure 8. These measurements were conducted while the internal temperature of the furnace
was at 350°C. The hottest point measured was 117.5°C on the inner titanium support plate. Where the
support plates meet the aluminum structure was approximately 35°C, and the surrounding structure
remained at ambient temperature.

 4. SUMMARY
The neutron sensor qualification device has been fabricated, assembled, and thermally tested in a
laboratory setting. The internal temperature distribution of the device was characterized axially for several
sensor positions. This characterization showed the axial distribution to be uniform across the central
region of the furnace, varying by less than 6°C. The external surface temperature of the device was
evaluated via an infrared camera and point measurements using a type K thermocouple. The maximum
exterior temperature was measured at 117.5°C near the insulation on the inner titanium plate. The
surrounding aluminum structure remained at ambient conditions. The device was able to ramp up to
operating temperature in about 3 hours, and cools with a time constant of approximately 3.5 hours. Since
the surrounding aluminum structure remains at ambient conditions, the furnace can be handled safely
while at temperature.
