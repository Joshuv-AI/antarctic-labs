Rotation-Invariant Vectorized Shape Representations
Hamid Shafieasl, Jeff M. Phillips

Abstract
We introduce a rotation-invariant representation of planar shapes. In particular, this representation encodes shapes as vectors such that the Euclidean distance between them serves as a valid shape distance. For standardized, star-shaped objects, we can deterministically create a sketched vector of dimension O ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) O(1/\varepsilon) in O ⁡ ( ( 1 / ε ) ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) ) O((1/\varepsilon)\log(1/\varepsilon)) time that approximates this shape distance to within ε \varepsilon . Moreover, because the representation is a standard Euclidean vector, we can directly and efficiently perform various data analyses, such as nearest neighbor search and clustering, in shape space, inherently invariant to the rotation of the shapes. We demonstrate this through a series of simple experiments. The key technical contribution operates on functions over 𝕊 1 \mathbb{S}^{1} , which we use to encode standardized objects. The most general rotation-invariant representation of these functions works through a map to an infinite-dimensional function space, parameterized by an offset parameter. By analyzing special discretized cases of these functions, we show that the representation is strictly injective up to the desired rotation and a mirror-flip-type operation we call reverse of complement (RoC). While RoC status can be controlled by how the function is defined, it is inherent to the representation and required to be handled in the analysis. Regardless, the vectorized representation is robust to small shape perturbations, and hence discretizing the angles leads to the efficient approximation and algorithm.

Introduction

The

of an object (a compact set

) refers to its properties modulo position and orientation. A core challenge when working in shape spaces
[
19
,

]

There have been three major approaches to this challenge.
First, and perhaps most popular in computational geometry
[
24
,
44
]
, is to compute a shape distance
\texttt{{d}}(X_{1},X_{2})=\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d},R\in\mathbb{SO}(d)}\texttt{{d}}^{\prime}(X_{1},R(X_{2}+t))
where

is a translation
[

,

]
,

a rotation
[
3
,
14
,
12
]
, and
\texttt{{d}}^{\prime}
is a traditional distance between sets (e.g., Hausdorff
[
15
]
or largest polygon placement
[
2
]
) which relies on the positional embedding of sets
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
. For some

variants where an alignment is known, the Procrustes approach
[
23
,
6
,
21
,
20
]
offers closed-form solutions. However, the minimization over choices of
t
and
R
is typically computationally challenging, although it has been carefully studied and formalized as in the citations above.
The second approach is to compute intrinsic shape characteristics that do not depend on positional information, and define a distance between these characteristics. Such approaches include, for instance, the turning function between curves
[
5
]
, the Gromov-Hausdorff distance
[
31
,
32
,
42
]
, and the Persistent Homology Transform
[
7
,

]
. These distances again typically require optimizing an alignment between the characteristic-bearing parts of the shapes, making them computationally demanding.
The third category comprises machine learning-driven heuristics
[

]
that compute rotation-invariant representations
[
37
]
. Classic examples include those based on spherical harmonics
[
26
]
, while modern methods often use the PointNet architecture
[

,
40

. While these representations are empirically robust and translation/rotation invariant, they lack formal analysis regarding which metric properties they preserve and how fine a resolution is needed to distinguish different objects. A major advantage of these heuristic approaches is that they encode the shape as a vector
[
38
]
, allowing for fast downstream computation without the need to optimize an alignment.

This paper

introduces a new representation for planar shapes that is:

1.

rotation invariant;

2.

a vector, and the shape distance is Euclidean distance between the sketched vectors;

3.

up to a controlled level of discretization, the Euclidean vector distance is a metric between the base shapes, modulo rotation and an inherent
\mathsf{RoC}

4.

can

Power of vectors.

We reiterate that the second property means we can embed shapes as (high-dimensional) vectors, and then no longer need to worry about optimizing an alignment between them. The ability to directly use the Euclidean distance means that this can immediately plug into all of the amazing infrastructure built for this setting. This includes recent advances in fast nearest neighbor search

]
using NHSW structures

]

[

While a full exploration of this power is beyond the scope of this paper, we do demonstrate the stability and convenience of this representation through some simple retrieval and clustering applications on
2

Another important aspect of such sketched representations is the computational cost to construct them. As with any Euclidean vectors, the dimensionality can be reduced to
O((1/\varepsilon^{2})\log n)
dimensions to preserve relative
(1+\varepsilon)

for the kernel methods

. However, more interestingly, we show how to deterministically create representations efficiently with a fast Fourier transform

]
. This leads to an
\varepsilon

Rotationally discretizing shapes.

The core analysis of our method operates in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and in fact a discrete version of it
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{m}
.
We convert a shape into a function on
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{m}
by considering

, which are objects where every point in the shape can be connected to the origin by a line segment fully contained within its interior. We discretize shapes by their polar coordinates uniformly in
m

\mathbb{S}^{1}_{m}

,
10
,
34
,
43
]
.

Sketching discrete
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

The core technical result is a rotation-invariant sketch for functions
f
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{m}
mapped to
V_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In this setting we can index
f:[m]\to\mathbb{R}
(more generally
f:[m]\to\mathbb{R}^{w}
) by an integer
j\in[m]=\{0,1,2,\ldots,m-1\}
.
The
k
th coordinate of
V_{f}
, denoted
V_{f}(k)\in\mathbb{R}
, encapsulates all pairs of entries in
f
with a difference of
k
in their index. We set
V_{f}(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Phi(f(j)-f(j+k))
where typically
\Phi(z)=\exp(-z)
. The characteristic nature of
\Phi
ensures that the finite sum of the terms in
V_{f}(k)
must originate from a unique set
\Delta_{f}(k)
of distances. Moreover, since this only compares offsets, it is invariant to rotation. The challenging aspect is showing that in whole (using each
V_{f}(k)
for
k\in[m]
) this sketch is
only
invariant up to rotation, and no other transformations; so difficult that it is actually not strictly true! It also permits a constant offset operation, as well as a natural new sort of mirror-flip transformation we call
reverse-of-complement (RoC)
. However, these operations are fairly simple, and the offset can be controlled by setting the scale in
V_{f}
(based on how we derive them from shapes).

2
Preliminaries: Families of Shapes and Functions Considered

To make our theorems precise, we describe some specific families of shapes and functions.

Shape families.

We consider
standardized shapes

\mathcal{S}
which are compact sets
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which have been mean centered so
\bar{x}=\int_{x\in X}x
is the origin
\textbf{0}=(0,\ldots,0)
, and has bounded radius of the shape:
\max_{x\in X}\|x\|\leq 1
. Any shape can be converted into this form by a simple standardization process: (1) compute its mean point
\bar{x}
and subtract it from all coordinates, and (2) compute its maximum radius
\tau
and divide all coordinates by
\tau
.
As a further restriction, we sometimes also consider
standardized star shapes

\mathcal{S}_{\star}
. These are standardized shapes
X\in\mathcal{S}
that are also star-shaped with respect to the origin. This means all points
x\in X
can be connected to the origin
0
with a line segment that does not leave the shape.

Note that by considering standardized shapes, the choice of translation (and scaling) is removed. This allows us to focus on the rotational-invariance property.

We focus on
d=2
dimensions, and consider a discrete version of rotation; for some positive integer parameter
m
, let
\mathcal{R}_{m}=\mathbb{SO}(2)_{m}
be rotations at
j\frac{2\pi}{m}
radians for
j\in[m]=\{0,1,2,\ldots,m-1\}
. We pair this rotational restriction with shape families
\mathcal{S}^{m}
which are assumed to be uniform within
m
wedges around the origin. Specifically, any ray
r_{\theta}
from the origin, with angle of
\theta\in\Theta_{j}=\left[j\frac{2\pi}{m},(j+1)\frac{2\pi}{m}\right)
from the horizontal direction, has the same intersection with
X
. That is, let
I(r_{\theta}\cap X)
describe a subset of
[0,1]
(recall standardized shapes ensure the extent of the intersection is at most a distance
1
; and for shape in
\mathcal{S}_{\star}
this subset is an interval
[0,a_{\theta}]
for some
a_{\theta}\in(0,1]
), and for two
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta_{j}
then
I(r_{\theta}\cap X)=I(r_{\theta^{\prime}}\cap X)
. This restriction ensures that if two shapes
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
are identical under some rotation
R\in\mathbb{SO}(2)
(i.e.,
X_{1}=R(X_{2})
) then, this rotation must be in the discrete rotation set
\mathcal{R}_{m}
. And for any shape
X\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
and rotation
R\in\mathcal{R}_{m}
, then we also have
R(X)\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
(it forms a group).

Moreover, let us consider
\eta
-Lipschitz
star-shaped standardized shapes
\mathcal{S}_{\star,\eta}
; these
X\in\mathcal{S}_{\star,\eta}
ensure that for two rays
r_{\theta},r_{\theta^{\prime}}
from the origin that define intersections
I(r_{\theta}\cap X)=[0,a_{\theta}]
and
I(r_{\theta^{\prime}}\cap X)=[0,a_{\theta^{\prime}}]
satisfy that
|a_{\theta}-a_{\theta^{\prime}}|\leq\eta\frac{1}{2\pi}|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|
.
For such
\eta
-Lipschitz
shapes
X\in\mathcal{S}_{\star,\eta}
, then we will be able to show that for any error parameter
\varepsilon
and a distance
d
, when
m=O(1/\varepsilon)
we can deterministically construct an
X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\star}^{m}
such that
\texttt{{d}}(X,X^{\prime})\leq\varepsilon
.

Function families on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

As previewed, we convert these shapes in a rotational invariant way into functions on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
We restrict to a discrete version of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, denoted
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{m}
which is indexed by
m
values in
[m]=\{0,1,2,\ldots,m-1\}
.
We again restrict the family
\mathcal{F}
of
2\pi
-periodic functions, so
f\in\mathcal{F}
is
f:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{A}
, where
\mathbb{A}
is the set of real algebraic numbers.
We also consider two easy to analyze families.
Let
\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
be the family where all values
f(j)
are distinct, and moreover all pairwise differences
f(j)-f(j^{\prime})
for
j\neq j^{\prime}\in[m]
are also distinct.
Second, let
\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\sigma}
be the family where
f:[m]\to[m]
and
f(j)\neq f(j^{\prime})
for
j\neq j^{\prime}
; this describes all permutations of the values in
[m]
. These families lead to different types of analysis:
\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
leverages uniqueness, while
\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\sigma}
contains many tied difference values but possesses structural symmetry that aids case analysis.

3
Sketching
\mathbb{S}^{1}

In this section, we describe how to sketch functions
f\in\mathcal{F}
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
to a function space
\psi_{f}\in\Psi
, which can be (approximately) compactly represented as vectors
V_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
. In particular, these representations will be invariant to rotations in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

The Sketch.

We will first define in the continuous setting, with infinite dimensional functional representation, but then focus on the easier to work with discrete case:
For parameter
\alpha\in[0,2\pi)
define

\psi_{f}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\theta\in[0,2\pi)}\Phi(f(\theta)-f(\theta+\alpha))\mathsf{d}\theta.

The mapping function
\Phi
we consider will primarily be
\Phi(z)=\exp(-z)
. However it will also be interesting to consider kernel functions such as the Laplace
\Phi(z)=\exp(-|z|)
and Gaussian
\Phi(z)=\exp(-z^{2})
. This can be interpreted as considering all function
f
values offset by
\alpha
, processing them through a characteristic function
\Phi
, and averaging over all
\theta
.

When considering
f\in\mathcal{F}^{m}
and only rotations in
\mathcal{R}_{m}
, then there are only
m
choices for
\theta
and for
\alpha
so the mapping simplifies to a result in a vector
V_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined so the
j
th coordinate (representing angle
\theta=j\frac{2\pi}{m}
), for
j\in[m]
is

V_{f}(k)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j\in[m]}\Phi(f(j)-f(j+k))

Recall that since
f\in\mathcal{F}^{m}
is described on indexes in
[m]
, but the input
j+k
of the second term might can be in
[2m]
, that
f
takes inputs modulo
m
, so
f(m+i)=f(i)
.

3.1
Invariances of the Sketch

We first provide a straightforward observation regarding invariance to rotations and shifts.

Lemma 3.1

(Rotation and Shift Invariance)
.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}
, any rotation
\beta\in[0,2\pi)
and any constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
, define
g\in\mathcal{F}
as

g(\alpha)=f(\alpha+\beta)+c.

Then the signature map satisfies

\psi_{g}(\alpha)=\psi_{f}(\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in[0,2\pi)
.

Proof.

By definition, for all
\alpha\in[0,2\pi)
we have

\displaystyle\psi_{g}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi\Bigl(g(\theta)-g(\theta+\alpha)\Bigr)d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi\Bigl(f(\theta+\beta)+c-\bigl(f(\theta+\alpha+\beta)+c\bigr)\Bigr)d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi\Bigl(f(\theta+\beta)-f(\theta+\alpha+\beta)\Bigr)d\theta

\displaystyle\{\text{Substitute }\gamma=\theta+\beta\}\qquad

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\beta}^{2\pi+\beta}\Phi\Bigl(f(\gamma)-f(\gamma+\alpha)\Bigr)d\gamma

\displaystyle\{f\text{ is a }2\pi\text{-periodic function}\}\ \qquad

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi\Bigl(f(\gamma)-f(\gamma+\alpha)\Bigr)d\gamma=\psi_{f}(\alpha).\qed

Reverse of Complement.

We next define a less intuitive function operation, which we call
reverse of complement (RoC)
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{M}
be the set of functions with
f(\theta)\in[0,M]
. Then
f^{\prime}=\mathsf{RoC}(f)
satisfies
f^{\prime}(\theta)=M-f(2\pi-\theta)
. In fact, this operation can use any value for
M
(including
M=0
), but will be useful later to define this way when considering
f\in\mathcal{F}_{M}
.

Lemma 3.2

(RoC, rotation and shift invariance)
.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}
, any rotation
\beta^{\prime}\in[0,2\pi)
and any constant
c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, define
g\in\mathcal{F}
as

g(\alpha)=\mathsf{RoC}(f(\alpha+\beta^{\prime}))+c^{\prime}.

Then the signature map satisfies

\psi_{g}(\alpha)=\psi_{f}(\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in[0,2\pi]
.

Proof.

First observe that we can simplify

g(\alpha)=\mathsf{RoC}(f(\alpha+\beta^{\prime}))+c^{\prime}=M-f(2\pi-\alpha-\beta^{\prime})+c^{\prime}.

Now setting
\beta=2\pi-\beta^{\prime}
and
c=c^{\prime}+M
we have
g(\alpha)=-f(-\theta+\beta)+c
. Hence

\displaystyle\psi_{g}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi\bigl(g(\phi)-g(\phi+\alpha)\bigr)\,d\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi\bigl(-f(-\phi+\beta)+c-\bigl(-f(-(\phi+\alpha)+\beta)+c\bigr)\bigr)\,d\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi\bigl(-f(-\phi+\beta)+f(-\phi-\alpha+\beta)\bigr)\,d\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi\bigl(f(-\phi-\alpha+\beta)-f(-\phi+\beta)\bigr)\,d\phi.

Make the change of variables
\zeta:=-\phi+\beta
. Then
d\zeta=-\,d\phi
. As
\phi
runs over
[0,2\pi)
,
\zeta
runs over an interval of length
2\pi
(specifically
[\beta,\beta-2\pi)
), and by
2\pi
-periodicity we may replace that interval by
[0,2\pi)
. Applying the change of variables,

\displaystyle\psi_{g}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\beta}^{\beta-2\pi}\Phi\bigl(f(\zeta-\alpha)-f(\zeta)\bigr)(-d\zeta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\beta-2\pi}^{\beta}\Phi\bigl(f(\zeta-\alpha)-f(\zeta)\bigr)\,d\zeta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi\bigl(f(\zeta-\alpha)-f(\zeta)\bigr)\,d\zeta.

Finally, relabel the dummy integration variable (or shift
\zeta\mapsto\zeta+\alpha
) to obtain

\psi_{g}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi\bigl(f(\theta)-f(\theta+\alpha)\bigr)\,d\theta=\psi_{f}(\alpha).

This proves the claim.
∎

Note that, when we also consider shift and rotational invariance, it is equivalent to think of
\mathsf{RoC}
as a form of
involution

\mathsf{Inv}(g(\alpha))=-f(-\alpha)
.
Moreover, the above proofs work for
\mathcal{F}^{m}
over
\mathcal{R}_{m}
; for the other direction which we describe next we restrict to this setting.

Periodic convolution.

A periodic convolution of two functions
p,q
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is defined
(p*q)(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}p(\theta)q(\alpha-\theta)\mathsf{d}\theta
. We show
\psi(\alpha)
can be decomposed this way.

Lemma 3.3
.

With
\Phi(z)=\exp(-z)
, then
\psi(\alpha)
is a periodic convolution of
p_{f}(\alpha)=\exp(-f(-\alpha))
and
q_{f}(\alpha)=\exp(f(\alpha))
.

Proof.

\displaystyle(p_{f}*q_{f})(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int_{\theta=0}^{2\pi}p_{f}(\theta)q_{f}(\alpha-\theta)\mathsf{d}\theta=\int_{\theta=0}^{2\pi}\exp(-f(-\theta))\exp(f(\alpha-\theta))\mathsf{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\theta=0}^{2\pi}\exp(-(f(\theta)-f(\alpha-\theta)))\mathsf{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\theta^{\prime}=0}^{2\pi}\exp(-(f(\theta^{\prime})-f(\alpha+\theta^{\prime})))\mathsf{d}\theta^{\prime}=2\pi\cdot\psi_{f}(\alpha).

The second to last equality can be seen by substituting
\theta^{\prime}=-\theta
, which does not change the periodic
[0,2\pi)
domain.
∎

This allows us to analyze
\psi_{f}
using the powerful tools of convolution theory, such as the Convolution Theorem
[
36
, Sections 3.4 & 8.2]
.
And in particular, implies that for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
if we define
g(\alpha)=-f(-\alpha)
then
\psi_{f}=\psi_{g}
; this implies the invariance to
\mathsf{RoC}
, by interpreting it as an involution
\mathsf{Inv}
.

3.2
Injectivity of the Sketch

We next show that for a discrete class of functions
\mathcal{F}^{m}
that the sketch is injective in that it is
only
invariant up to the rotation, constant offsets, and
\mathsf{RoC}
operations.

For a function
f\in\mathcal{F}^{m}
, let
\Delta_{f}(k)=\{f(j)-f(j+k)\mid j\in[m]\}
be the
multiset
of function differences offset by
k\in[m]
. Recall that we can define
V_{f}(k)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{z\in\Delta_{f}(k)}\Phi(z)
. We show that these
\{\Delta_{f}(k)\}_{k\in[m]}
are an isomorphic representation of the sketch
V_{f}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Consider two multisets
W,W^{\prime}
of algebraic real numbers with
|W|=|W^{\prime}|=m
, a rational number
\lambda\neq 0
and fix
p\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
\sum_{w_{i}\in W}\exp(\lambda w_{i}^{2p+1})=\sum_{w^{\prime}_{i}\in W^{\prime}}\exp(\lambda{w^{\prime}}_{i}^{2p+1})
, then
W=W^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the set of algebraic numbers. For
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, let
W(\alpha)=\{w\in W\ |w=\alpha\}
(resp.
W^{\prime}(\alpha)
). Then define
\delta_{\alpha}=|W(\alpha)|-|W^{\prime}(\alpha)|
. Let
L=\{\delta_{\alpha}\ |\ \delta_{\alpha}\neq 0,\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\}
. The equality condition in our lemma ensures that
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}|W(\alpha)|\exp(\lambda\alpha^{2p+1})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}|W^{\prime}(\alpha)|\exp(\lambda\alpha^{2p+1})
. Therefore,

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\delta_{\alpha}\exp(\lambda\alpha^{2p+1})=0
. Our goal is to show that
L=\emptyset
.

We now invoke a classical result from transcendental number theory, the

Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem
(
[
9
, Theorem 1.4]
),
which states that if
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
are distinct algebraic numbers,
then
e^{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,e^{\alpha_{n}}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Therefore, the finite set

\{\,\exp(\lambda\alpha^{2p+1}):\delta_{\alpha}\neq 0\,\}

is linearly independent, which forces
\delta_{\alpha}=0
for all such
\alpha
.
Hence
L=\emptyset
.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

For two functions
f,g\in\mathcal{F}^{m}
with algebraic real values, then when using
\Phi(z)=\exp(-z)
, then
V_{f}=V_{g}
is equivalent to have
\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{g}(k)
for all
k\in[m]
.

We say two functions
f,g\in\mathcal{F}^{m}
so that
\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{g}(k)
for all
k\in[m]
are
lag-homometric
. Since
\mathcal{F}^{m},\mathcal{R}_{m}
is a special case of
\mathcal{F},\mathbb{SO}(2)
(as elaborated on in Appendix
B.1
), then Lemma
3.2
established that lag-homometric functions are in the same equivalence class up to rotations, offsets, and potential
\mathsf{RoC}
operations.

Canonical representations.

We first establish a representation of a function
f\in\mathcal{F}^{m}
fixing the choice of rotation and constant offsets. We say a
f\in\mathcal{F}
is
canonical
if its smallest value is
0
, and this value is in the first position so
f(0)=0
. We can enforce the first condition by identifying
\alpha=\min_{j}f(j)
, and then, through a constant offset, subtracting
\alpha
from each entry in
f
so
f(j)\leftarrow f(j)-\alpha
. If
j_{\min}=\arg\min_{j}f(j)\neq 0
, then we need to perform a rotation. We set
f(j)\leftarrow f(j-_{m}j_{\min})
where
-_{m}
indicates subtraction modulo
m
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
be lag-homometric (which includes e.g.,
g=\mathsf{RoC}(f)
). If
\tilde{f},\tilde{g}
are the canonical forms of
f,g
, then

\max_{j\in[m]}\tilde{f}(j)=\max_{j\in[m]}\tilde{g}(j)\;\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\;\arg\max_{j\in[m]}\tilde{f}(j)=\arg\max_{j\in[m]}\tilde{g}(j).

Proof.

Let the maximum value
\beta=\max_{j\in[m]}\tilde{f}(j)
, and
j_{\max}=\arg\max_{j\in[m]}\tilde{f}(j)
. Since
\tilde{f}(0)=0
then
\beta=\tilde{f}(j_{\max})-\tilde{f}(0)\in\Delta_{f}(m-j_{\max})
. Since
f,g
are lag-homomorphic, which by Lemma
3.2
and Lemma
3.4
implies so are
\tilde{f},\tilde{g}
, since they only change by rotation and shift. Now because
\tilde{g}(0)=0
, and since
\beta\in\Delta_{g}(m-j_{\max})
, there must exist some
j
with
\tilde{g}(j)-\tilde{g}(j+m-j_{\max})=\beta
. Since
\beta
is the global maximum difference across all shifts, and all differences of
\tilde{g}
are at most
\max\tilde{g}-\min\tilde{g}=\max\tilde{g}
(because
\min\tilde{g}=0
), we must have
\max\tilde{g}\geq\beta
. But
\beta
must also be the maximum difference in
g
(since all multisets are shared), so
\max\tilde{g}=\beta
and
j_{\max}=\arg\max_{j\in[m]}\tilde{g}(j)
.
∎

This argument on
\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
only used the uniqueness of the maximum. Thus it also applies to the other function family we consider on permutations
\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\sigma}
. From here, the arguments needed to prove that
V_{f}=V_{g}
implies that
f
matches
g
up to rotation, shifts, and
\mathsf{RoC}
differs for this permutation family. These arguments are technical and leverage the strong symmetry of the sets of differences. We defer showing this invariance of
\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\sigma}
to Appendix
B.2
.

Next we resolve the choice of
\mathsf{RoC}
operation. Let
\Delta_{f}=\bigcup_{k\in[m]}\Delta_{f}(k)
.
Let
\beta_{2}
be the second largest values in
\Delta_{f}
(recall
\beta
is the largest).
We say
f
is in
\mathsf{RoC}
-canonical form
(so
f(0)=0
) then let
j_{1}=\arg\max_{j\in[m]}f(j)
and
j_{2}=\arg\max_{j\in[m],j\neq j_{1}}f(j)
, then
f(j_{1})=\beta
(by Lemma
3.6
) and
f(j_{2})=\beta_{2}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

If
f\in\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
we can always transform it into
\bar{f}
in
\mathsf{RoC}
-canonical form using shift, rotation, and
\mathsf{RoC}
operations.

Proof.

First transform
f
into canonical form
\tilde{f}
, and define
\beta
,
\beta_{2}
with respect to
\tilde{f}
. Now the second largest difference
\beta_{2}
can either be between
f(0)=0
and the second largest value of
f
(at
j_{2}
), or between the largest value
f(j_{1})
and the second smallest value of
f
at
j^{\prime}_{2}
. If it is the first case then
\beta_{2}=f(j_{2})-f(0)=f(j_{2})
and we are in
\mathsf{RoC}
-canonical form. If it is the second case, then we apply an
f^{\prime}=\mathsf{RoC}_{\beta}(\tilde{f})
operation, and then make
f^{\prime}
canonical as
\tilde{f}^{\prime}
. This
\mathsf{RoC}_{\beta}
operation makes
f^{\prime}((m-1)-j_{1})=0
,
f^{\prime}(m-1)=\beta
, and
f^{\prime}((m-1)-j^{\prime}_{2})
becomes the second largest value. Importantly, it preserves the second largest difference
\beta_{2}=f^{\prime}((m-1)-j^{\prime}_{2})-f((m-1)-j_{1})=f^{\prime}((m-1)-j^{\prime}_{2})
, satisfying
\mathsf{RoC}
canonical form (after rotation to canonical form) since
f^{\prime}((m-1)-j_{1})=0
.
∎

Inductive argument.

We are now ready for the inductive argument to show that once lag-homometric functions
f,g\in\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
are put in
\mathsf{RoC}
-canonical form they must be equal.
For this argument we introduce some more notation. Sort the values in
f
and in
g
and represent their sorted order as
\hat{f},\hat{g}
; this format implies
\hat{f}(j)<\hat{f}(j+1)
for all
j\in[m-1]
,
\hat{f}(j)=f(j^{\prime})
for some
j,j^{\prime}\in[m]
, and
\hat{f}(0)=0
; and the same for
\hat{g}
.

Now we define a subset of differences
\Delta_{f}^{s,t}\subset\Delta_{f}
. It excludes all differences formed by the
j\leq s
smallest values and the
j^{\prime}\leq t
largest values of
f
(it can pair two large or two small values). When we have established the location of the
s
smallest and
t
largest values in both
f
and
g
, then we have resolved these differences, and can examine the largest difference remaining in
\Delta_{f}^{s,t}=\Delta_{g}^{s,t}
. Note that by Lemma
3.6
and the definition of
\mathsf{RoC}
-canonical form, when we have this form for
f,g
then we have resolved the
s=1
smallest value and
t=2
largest distance.

We now state the most technical lemma, proved in Appendix
C
as Lemma
C.3
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Consider
f,g\in\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
which are lag-homometric and in
\mathsf{RoC}
-canonical form. Assume we have resolved the locations of the
s\geq 1
smallest and
t\geq 2
largest values in
f,g
, confirming they are the same. We can then confirm the location of either the
(s+1)
th smallest or
(t+1)
th largest by value of
f,g
and confirm that this value matches.

Proof Sketch.

Each of
f
and
g
has two options for which difference is the largest in
\Delta_{f}^{s,t}=\Delta_{g}^{s,t}
; using the
(s+1)
th smallest or
(t+1)
th largest value. If these match, we satisfy the lemma’s claim. Showing if they do not match it causes a contradiction is technical. We pick a location where
f
and
g
differ in this case, and compare the difference with
\hat{f}(m-2)=\hat{g}(m-2)
, which generates different differences
\delta_{f},\delta_{g}
both placed in some
\Delta_{f}(k)
and
\Delta_{g}(k)
, respectively. Since these sets
\Delta_{f}(k)
and
\Delta_{g}(k)
must match, and all difference are unique, we can argue that we cannot form
\delta_{g}
to put in
\Delta_{f}(k)
, and they cannot match.
∎

Starting with the base case of
s=1,t=2
from
\mathsf{RoC}
-canonical form, we can invoke Lemma
3.8
inductively. Each time it increases either
s
or
t
. This continues until
s+t=m
, in which case all positions of
f
and
g
are confirmed to be the same, completing the proof. This results in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.9
.

For
f,g\in F^{m}_{\neq}
which are lag-homometric in
\mathsf{RoC}
-canonical form, then they must be equal.

Theorem 3.10
.

Consider two functions
f,g\in\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
. Then
V_{f}=V_{g}
if and only if there exist a rotation
R\in\mathcal{R}_{m}
and a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
f=R(g)+c
or
f=R(\mathsf{RoC}(g))+c
.

Proof.

The forward implication follows from Lemma
3.2
.

For the converse, Corollary
3.5
shows
V_{f}=V_{g}
implies that
f,g
are lag-homometric.
Then Lemma
3.7
shows we can transform both
\bar{f}\leftarrow f
and
\bar{g}\leftarrow g
so both
\bar{f}
and
\bar{g}
are in
\mathsf{RoC}
-canonical forms and still lag-homometric with each other and

. Then by Theorem

we must have
\bar{f}=\bar{g}
. Hence, any other operation to

would change

violating it being lag-homometric, and would hence change the representations

, completing the proof.
∎

Let

denote the equivalence class of functions to
f\in\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
up to shifts, rotations, and

. Let
\mathcal{F}^{m}_{[\neq]}
be the family of distinct functions modulo this equivalence class.
Define function
\texttt{{d}}^{\prime}_{\Phi}(f,g)=\|V_{f}-V_{g}\|
for

being the sketched representation of

respectively.

Corollary 3.11
.

The distance

.

3.3
Inner Products for the Sketch

We now define an inner product on the space of the sketch signatures, which is the standard inner product of a Hilbert space and quantifies the similarity between two functions in
\mathcal{F}
.

The
invariant sketch kernel

K(f,g)
is defined as the inner product between two signature functions
\psi_{f}
and
\psi_{g}
for
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
as

K(f,g)=\langle\psi_{f},\psi_{g}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\psi_{f}(\alpha)\psi_{g}(\alpha)\,d\alpha.

The invariant sketch kernel
K(f,g)=\langle\psi_{f},\psi_{g}\rangle_{L^{2}}
provides a powerful tool for quantifying the similarity between functions in
\mathcal{F}
. By measuring the inner product of signature functions
\psi_{f}
and
\psi_{g}
, the kernel captures the degree of alignment between
f
and
g
across all possible shifts on the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
A common goal for a kernel to be used effectively within kernel methods in machine learning
[
42
]
, is for it to be
positive semi-definite
.

Lemma 3.12

(Positive Semi-Definiteness of the Sketch Kernel)
.

For functions
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
, the invariant sketch kernel
K(f,g)
is positive semi-definite.

Proof.

A positive semidefinite kernel is one that for any finite set of functions
\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{F}
and real coefficients
\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
, then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}K(f_{i},f_{j})\geq 0
.

We show this by first substituting our kernel into the sum

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}\langle\psi_{f_{i}},\psi_{f_{j}}\rangle_{L^{2}}.

The inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}}
is bilinear. This property allows us to move the summations and coefficients inside the inner product

=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\psi_{f_{i}},\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}\psi_{f_{j}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}
.
Now for a new function
V\in L^{2}([0,2\pi])
that is the linear combination of the signature functions

V(\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\psi_{f_{i}}(\alpha),

this becomes the inner product of
V
with itself as

\langle V,V\rangle_{L^{2}}=\|V\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
.
Therefore, as desired

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}K(f_{i},f_{j})=\|V\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\geq 0.\qed

3.4
Computing the Sketch

For computation, we focus on
f\in\mathcal{F}^{m}
over
\mathcal{R}_{m}
.
The sketch takes
O(m^{2})
time to compute directly, as it provides a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and each coordinate takes
O(m)
time to sum over all discrete rotations in
[m]
. In this section we provide two alternatives.

Fast FFT algorithm: near-linear time.

First we leverage that, via Lemma
3.3
, we can write the sketch vector
V_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, defined for any coordinate
j\in[m]
as the convolution of two discrete functions
p_{f}(j)=\exp(-f(-j\frac{2\pi}{m}))
and
q_{f}(j)=\exp(f(j\frac{2\pi}{m}))
. Specifically:

V_{f}(j)=\frac{1}{m}(p_{f}*q_{f})(j).

Then we can leverage the
discrete convolution theorem

[
16
,
36
]
. This states that given two discrete signals
p,q\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
that their convolution can be computed in the frequency domain

(p*q)[j]=\mathsf{DFT}^{-1}(\mathsf{DFT}(p)\odot\mathsf{DFT}(q))[j]

where
\mathsf{DFT}
is the Discrete Fourier Transform,
\mathsf{DFT}^{-1}
is its inverse, and
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication of the transformed signals.
Moreover, this approaches computes all
m
coordinates simultaneously:

1.

Compute the DFT of the signals
p_{f}
and
q_{f}
.

2.

Perform a single element-wise multiplication of their transformed representations.

3.

Compute the Inverse DFT of the resulting product to obtain
\psi_{f}
.

Using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)
[
16
]
, can compute
\mathsf{DFT}
and its inverse
\mathsf{DFT}^{-1}
, each in
O(m\log m)
time.

Theorem 3.13
.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}^{m}
we can build the sketch
V_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
using
\Phi(z)=\exp(-z)
in
O(m\log m)
time.

Randomized low-dimensional sketch.

We also note that given two functions
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
, one can view
K(f,g)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\psi_{f}(\alpha)\psi_{g}(\alpha)\mathsf{d}\alpha
as an expected value of
\alpha\in\mathsf{Unif}(0,2\pi]
. This implies one can estimate
K(f,g)
from a sample of
t=O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta})
so with probability at least
1-\delta
, it guarantees at most
\varepsilon M^{2}
error for
f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{M}
.
In particular, by consistently using
t\ll m
samples
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{t}\in(0,2\pi]
, this induces an approximate sketch
\hat{V}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
. This is akin to Random Fourier Features
[
41
]
; details are in Appendix
D.1
. However the same sort of guarantees can be achieved by applying a JL random projection
[
30
]
on
V_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Since our random sketch takes
O(tm)
time to build, this is not typically much faster than creating the full sketch
V_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and applying the Fast JL
[
25
]
in
O(m\log m+t/\varepsilon)
time.

\mathbb{S}^{1}

We consider families of standardized shapes
\mathcal{S}^{m}
which are discretized into
m

from the origin at angle

In practice (as we explore in Section

we determine the intersection

(for

of the extent of the shape in that direction. For function

we assign

as in

. Figure

Define our distance between standardized star shapes

\texttt{{d}}_{\Phi}(X_{1},X_{2})=\|V_{1}-V_{2}\|.

Define

or

(i.e., with a invertible map

to that on

. An illustration of this on a fish is shown in Figure

.
Let
\mathcal{S}^{m}_{[\star]}
as the space of these standardized star shapes modulo rotation and

Illustration of an

Theorem 4.1
.

For shapes
\mathcal{S}^{m}_{[\star]}
in general position then
\texttt{{d}}_{\Phi}
is a metric using
\Phi(z)=\exp(-z)
.

Proof.

By embedding in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and using the Euclidean distance, we automatically inherit symmetry, triangle inequality, and that the distance is at least
0
(non-negativity), making it a
pseudometric
. What remains is to show that
\|V_{1}-V_{2}\|=0
if and only if
X_{1}=R(X_{2})
under some rotation
R\in\mathcal{R}_{m}
or
\mathsf{RoC}
operation. This follows in two steps. First, as argued above setting
f(j)=X(\theta_{j})
is an isometric mapping for
X\in\mathcal{S}^{m}_{\star}
into
\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
. Then by Theorem
3.10
two functions
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{F}^{m}_{[\neq]}
satisfy
[f_{1}]=[f_{2}]
(up to rotation and
\mathsf{RoC}
), if and only if their corresponding sketch vectors
V_{1}=V_{2}
are equal.
∎

Star Distance.

For
X\in\mathcal{S}_{\star}
, and angle
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
let
X(\theta)=a_{\theta}
where for ray
r_{\theta}
the intersection
I(X\cap r_{\theta})=[0,a_{\theta})
.
Now consider two
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{\star}
, and define the following
star distance
:

\texttt{{d}}_{\star}(X_{1},X_{2})=\max_{\theta\in[0,2\pi)}|X_{1}(\theta)-X_{2}(\theta)|.

The star distance between two shapes under rotations, what we call the
R-star distance
:

\texttt{{d}}_{R\star}(X_{1},X_{2})=\min_{R\in\mathbb{SO}(2)}\texttt{{d}}_{\star}(X_{1},R(X_{2})).

Discrete Approximation.

We can show that
\texttt{{d}}_{\star}
is approximately preserved as we replace a
\eta
-Lipschitz shape
X\in\mathcal{S}^{\star,\eta}
with some
X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\star,\eta}^{m}
. In particular choose a set
\mathcal{T}=\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{m}\}
so
\theta_{j}=j\frac{2\pi}{m}+\frac{\pi}{m}
. That is each
r_{\theta_{j}}
is the ray in the middle of the wedge of angles in
\Theta_{j}
.
Define
X^{\prime}
as the
m
-discrete approximation of
X
by
X^{\prime}(\theta_{j})=X(\theta_{j})
; that is it takes the angular radius in the middle of each wedge
\Theta_{j}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}_{\star}
the
m
-discrete approximation of
X\in\mathcal{S}_{\star,\eta}
then
\texttt{{d}}_{\star}(X,X^{\prime})\leq\pi\eta/m
.

Proof.

For each
\theta\in\Theta_{j}
, we have
|X(\theta)-X(\theta_{j})|\leq\eta\frac{\pi}{m}
. Since for any
\theta\in\Theta_{j}
we have
X^{\prime}(\theta)=X(\theta_{j})
, the bound follows.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For two
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{\star}
if
\texttt{{d}}_{R\star}(X_{1},X_{2})\leq\varepsilon
, then the corresponding rotational invariant sketches
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
satisfy
\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{L_{2}}\leq(2e)\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality let the optimal rotation for
\texttt{{d}}_{R\star}
be the identity rotation
R(X_{2})=X_{2}
, so now
\texttt{{d}}_{\star}(X_{1},X_{2})\leq\varepsilon
.
Then we know that
\max_{\theta}|X_{1}(\theta)-X_{2}(\theta)|\leq\varepsilon
. So then the max difference over all choices of
\theta,\alpha\in[0,2\pi)
in

|(X_{1}(\theta)-X_{1}(\theta+\alpha))-(X_{2}(\theta)-X_{2}(\theta+\alpha)|\leq|X_{1}(\theta)-X_{2}(\theta)|+|X_{1}(\theta+\alpha)-X_{2}(\theta+\alpha)|\leq 2\varepsilon.

Then we observe that
\Phi(z)=\exp(-z)
is
e
-Lipschitz for
z\in[-1,1]
, and each of
f_{1},f_{2}
have range
(0,1]
, so
X_{1}(\theta)-X_{1}(\theta+\alpha)\in[-1,1]
and same for
X_{2}
. Thus for all
\alpha

\displaystyle|\psi_{1}(\alpha)-\psi_{2}(\alpha)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\Phi(X_{1}(\theta)-X_{1}(\theta+\alpha))-\Phi(X_{2}(\theta)-X_{2}(\theta+\alpha))|\mathsf{d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}|\Phi(X_{1}(\theta)-X_{1}(\theta+\alpha))-\Phi(X_{2}(\theta)-X_{2}(\theta+\alpha))|

\displaystyle\leq|\Phi(z)-\Phi(z+2\varepsilon)|\leq e\cdot 2\varepsilon.

As a result

\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|^{2}_{L_{2}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\psi_{1}(\alpha)-\psi_{2}(\alpha))^{2}\mathsf{d}\alpha\leq\max_{\alpha\in[0,2\pi)}(\psi_{1}(\alpha)-\psi_{2}(\alpha))^{2}\leq(2e\varepsilon)^{2}.\qed

Theorem 4.4
.

For two shapes
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{\star,\eta}
and
\Phi(z)=\exp(-z)
we can compute a distance
D
so that
|D-\texttt{{d}}_{\Phi}(X_{1},X_{2})|\leq\varepsilon
in
O((1/\eta\varepsilon)\log(1/\eta\varepsilon))
time.

Proof.

Set
m=\frac{4e}{\pi\eta\varepsilon}
so that when using
X^{\prime}
an
m
discrete approximation of
X\in\mathcal{S}_{\star,\eta}
that
\texttt{{d}}_{\star}(X,X^{\prime})\leq\pi\eta/m=\varepsilon/(4e)
, via Lemma
4.2
.
This step is the only approximation, and takes
O(m)
time for each shape
X_{1},X_{2}
. Then we represent them as functions
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{F}^{m}
, and convert them into sketches
V_{1},V_{2}
in
O(m\log m)
time, via Theorem
3.13
. By Lemma
4.3
and
\texttt{{d}}_{\star}(X_{1},X^{\prime}_{1})\leq\varepsilon/(4e)
and
\texttt{{d}}_{\star}(X_{2},X^{\prime}_{2})\leq\varepsilon/(4e)
then by triangle inequality we have
\|V_{1}-V_{2}\|\leq 2\cdot(2e)\frac{\varepsilon}{4e}=\varepsilon
as desired.
Computing
\|V_{1}-V_{2}\|
takes
O(m)
time, so the total time is
O(m\log m)=O((1/\eta\varepsilon)\log(1/\eta\varepsilon))
.
∎

Note that for two standardized star-shapes
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{\star}
if
X_{2}
is an
\varepsilon
-Hausdorff distortion of
X_{1}
(e.g.,
\texttt{{d}}_{H}(X_{1},X_{2})\leq\varepsilon
), then this implies that
\texttt{{d}}_{\star}(X_{1},X_{2})\leq\varepsilon
, and hence by Lemma
4.3
, their associated sketched functions
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
satisfy
\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{L_{2}}\leq(2e)\varepsilon
. This holds for shapes which are rotations of each other, with
\varepsilon
-Hausdorff perturbations. Thus, in this sense, in the rotation-shape space the sketch mapping is
2e
-Lipschitz with respect to Hausdorff distance.

Clustering.

To assess the robustness of the proposed sketch vector with respect to rotation, and in particular how it interacts with the discretization level, we conducted the following experiment on a subset of star-shaped objects from the SQUID dataset.

Appendix A
More Formal Definitions

Definition A.1

(Discrete circle and modular arithmetic)
.

Fix an integer
m\geq 1
.
We represent the discrete circle by the index set
\mathbb{Z}_{m}:=\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
with modular addition

a+_{m}b:=(a+b)\bmod m.

For each index
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
, define the discrete angle

\theta_{k}:=\frac{2\pi k}{m}.

The additive inverse of
\theta_{k}
is

-\theta_{k}:=\theta_{-k},\qquad\text{where }-k:=(-k)\bmod m=\begin{cases}0,&k=0,\\
m-k,&1\leq k\leq m-1.\end{cases}

All index operations are taken modulo
m
.

Definition A.2

(Discrete signature)
.

Let
f=(f_{0},f_{1},\dots,f_{m-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be any fixed function (e.g.,
\Phi(x)=e^{-|x|}
or
\Phi(x)=e^{-x^{2}}
).
The
discrete signature
of
f
is the function
V_{f}:\mathbb{Z}_{m}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

V_{f}[r]:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Phi\!\big(f_{j+_{m}r}-f_{j}\big),\qquad r\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}.

Theorem A.3

(Discrete rotation and shift invariance)
.

Let
f=(f_{0},\dots,f_{m-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
For any rotation
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
, define

g_{j}:=f_{j+_{m}b}+c,\qquad j\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}.

Then for all
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
,

V_{g}[r]=V_{f}[r].

Proof.

\displaystyle V_{g}[r]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Phi\!\big(g_{j+_{m}r}-g_{j}\big)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Phi\!\big(f_{j+_{m}r+_{m}b}+c-(f_{j+_{m}b}+c)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Phi\!\big(f_{j+_{m}r+_{m}b}-f_{j+_{m}b}\big)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\Phi\!\big(f_{i+_{m}r}-f_{i}\big)=V_{f}[r],

where we reindexed
i=j+_{m}b
.
∎

Theorem A.4

(Discrete involution, rotation, and shift invariance)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and fix
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Define

g_{j}:=-\,f_{-j+_{m}b}+c,\qquad j\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}.

Then for all
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
,

V_{g}[r]=V_{f}[r].

Proof.

\displaystyle V_{g}[r]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Phi\!\big(g_{j}-g_{j+_{m}r}\big)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Phi\!\big(-f_{-j+_{m}b}+c-(-f_{-(j+_{m}r)+_{m}b}+c)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Phi\!\big(f_{-j-_{m}r+_{m}b}-f_{-j+_{m}b}\big)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\Phi\!\big(f_{i-_{m}r}-f_{i}\big)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\Phi\!\big(f_{i}-f_{i+_{m}r}\big)=V_{f}[r],

where
i=-j+_{m}b
and indices are taken modulo
m
.
∎

Definition A.5

(Discrete Invariant Signature Kernel)
.

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be two discrete functions on the circle
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
,
and let
V_{f},S_{g}:\mathbb{Z}_{m}\to\mathbb{R}
denote their discrete signature functions defined by

V_{f}(r):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Phi\bigl(f_{j}-f_{j+_{m}r}\bigr),\qquad V_{g}(r):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Phi\bigl(g_{j}-g_{j+_{m}r}\bigr).

The
Discrete Invariant Signature Kernel
between
f
and
g
is defined as the discrete inner product

k(f,g)\;:=\;\langle V_{f},V_{g}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})}\;=\;\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}V_{f}(r)\,\overline{V_{g}(r)}.

This kernel quantifies the total similarity between
f
and
g
across all discrete shifts on the circle
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.

Remark A.6

(Invariance)
.

If
g_{j}=f_{j+_{m}b}+c
or
g_{j}=-\,f_{-j+_{m}b}+c
for some
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
then
V_{g}(r)=V_{f}(r)
for all
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.
Consequently, the discrete invariant signature kernel is unchanged:

k(f,g)=k(g,f)=k(f,f)=k(g,g).

Appendix B
Invariance of Permutation Functions

B.1
Lag-Homomorphism, and other Definitions

Definition B.1
.

Consider the additive group
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.
Every function
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
can be represented by the sequence

f=(f(0),f(1),\ldots,f(m-1)).

By periodicity,
f(m)=f(0)
and more generally
f(-j)=f(m-j)
for all integers
j
.

Definition B.2
.

For
0\leq x\leq m-1
, the right and left rotations of
f
by
x
are

\mathsf{Rot}_{x}^{r}(f)(i)=f(i-x),\qquad\mathsf{Rot}_{x}^{l}(f)(i)=f(i+x).

These definitions extend naturally to any integer
x
modulo
m
.

Definition B.3
.

For
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
and a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
, the
Reverse-of-Complement
of
f
with respect to
c
is defined by

\mathsf{RoC}_{c}(f)(i)=c-f(-i).

When
c=0
, we simply write
\mathsf{RoC}(f)
.

Definition B.4
.

For each shift
k\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
and any
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
, define the multiset of discrete differences

\Delta_{f}(k):=\{\,f(j+k)-f(j):j=0,\dots,m-1\,\}.

If
f,g\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
satisfy
\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{g}(k)
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
, then we say that
f
and
g
are
lag-homometric
sequences.

Theorem B.5
.

Let
f,g\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
be lag-homometric. Then for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
the sequences
f
and
\mathsf{RoC}_{c}(g)
are lag-homometric.

Proof.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.
Since
\mathsf{RoC}_{c}(g)(j)=c-g(-j)
, we compute

\mathsf{RoC}_{c}(g)(j+k)-\mathsf{RoC}_{c}(g)(j)=\big(c-g(-j-k)\big)-\big(c-g(-j)\big)=g(-j)-g(-j-k).

Setting
i=-j-k
(a permutation of indices modulo
n
) gives

\displaystyle g(-j)-g(-j-k)=g(i+k)-g(i),

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\Delta_{\mathsf{RoC}_{c}(g)}(k)=\{\,\mathsf{RoC}_{c}(g)(j+k)-\mathsf{RoC}_{c}(g)(j):j\,\}=\{\,g(i+k)-g(i):i\,\}=\Delta_{g}(k).

Since
f
and
g
are lag-homometric,
\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{g}(k)
for all
k
, and therefore

\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{\mathsf{RoC}_{c}(g)}(k)
for all
k
.
Thus
f
and
\mathsf{RoC}_{c}(g)
are lag-homometric.
∎

Theorem B.6
.

Let
f,g\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
be lag-homometric. Then for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
the sequences
f
and
\mathsf{Rot}_{x}(g)
are lag-homometric.

Proof.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.
Since
\mathsf{Rot}_{x}^{r}(g)(j)=g(j-x)
, we compute

\mathsf{Rot}_{x}^{r}(g)(j+k)-\mathsf{Rot}_{x}^{r}(g)(j)=g(j+k-x)-g(j-x).

Setting
i=j-x
(again a permutation of indices modulo
n
) yields

\displaystyle g(j+k-x)-g(j-x)=g(i+k)-g(i),

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathsf{Rot}_{x}(g)}(k)=\{\,\mathsf{Rot}_{x}^{r}(g)(j+k)-\mathsf{Rot}_{x}^{r}(g)(j):j\,\}=\{\,g(i+k)-g(i):i\,\}=\Delta_{g}(k).

Since
f
and
g
are lag-homometric,
\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{g}(k)
for all
k
, and therefore

\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{\mathsf{Rot}_{x}(g)}(k)
for all
k
.
Thus
f
and
\mathsf{Rot}_{x}^{r}(g)
are lag-homometric.
∎

Definition B.7
.

For
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
we define its equivalence class

[f]\;:=\;\{\,g\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})\;|\;g=\mathsf{Rot}_{m}^{r}(\mathsf{RoC}_{c}(f))\ \text{or}\ g=\mathsf{Rot}_{m}^{r}(f)\text{ for some }c\in\mathbb{R},\ m\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}\,\}.

Definition B.8
.

Let
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
and let
\Phi
be a fixed feature function.
The
discrete signature
of
f
is the function
V_{f}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
defined by

V_{f}(k)=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Phi\!\big(f(j)-f(j+k)\big),\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}.

Remark B.9
.

Let
\phi:[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}
be a function that is constant on each interval

\Big[\,\tfrac{i\,2\pi}{m},\ \tfrac{(i+1)\,2\pi}{m}\,\Big],\qquad i\in\mathbb{Z}_{m},

and denote this constant value by
t_{i}
. Then

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\phi(t)\,dt=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\int_{\frac{i2\pi}{m}}^{\frac{(i+1)2\pi}{m}}t_{i}\,dt=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}t_{i}\,\frac{2\pi}{m}=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}t_{i}.

Hence the integral of such a uniformly spaced step function is exactly proportional to
the sum of its step values. This shows that our discrete signature coincides with the
continuous signature on the space of these step functions.

Theorem B.10
.

If
f,g\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
are lag-homometric (in particular, if
g\in[f]
), then

V_{f}(k)=V_{g}(k)\qquad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}.

Proof.

For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
we may rewrite

V_{f}(k)=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Phi\!\big(f(j+k)-f(j)\big)=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{x\in\Delta_{f}(k)}\Phi(x),

since the sum enumerates precisely the elements of the multiset
\Delta_{f}(k)
.
Because
f
and
g
are lag-homometric, Theorems
B.5

and
B.6
together ensure that

\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{g}(k)\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}.

Therefore,

V_{g}(k)=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{x\in\Delta_{g}(k)}\Phi(x)=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{x\in\Delta_{f}(k)}\Phi(x)=V_{f}(k).

∎

Lemma B.11

(Injectivity of Integer Exponential Sums)
.

Let
m\geq 1
and let
\lambda\neq 0
be an algebraic real number.
Fix an integer
p\geq 0
.
Suppose

V=\{v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m-1}\},\ W=\{w_{0},w_{1},\ldots,w_{m-1}\}

are two multisets of rational numbers satisfying

\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}e^{\lambda\,v_{j}^{\,2p+1}}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}e^{\lambda\,w_{j}^{\,2p+1}}.

Then
V=W
as multisets.

Proof.

For each
k\in\mathbb{Q}
define

m_{k}=\big|\{\,i\mid v_{i}=k\,\}\big|,\qquad n_{k}=\big|\{\,i\mid w_{i}=k\,\}\big|.

Then
m_{k},n_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and only finitely many of them are nonzero.

In terms of these multiplicities, the assumptions become

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Q}}m_{k}=n=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Q}}n_{k},\qquad\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Q}}m_{k}\,e^{\lambda k^{\,2p+1}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Q}}n_{k}\,e^{\lambda k^{\,2p+1}}.

Define
d_{k}:=m_{k}-n_{k}
for each
k\in\mathbb{Q}
. Then
d_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Q}}d_{k}\,e^{\lambda k^{\,2p+1}}=0.

Let

S=\{\,k\in\mathbb{Q}:d_{k}\neq 0\,\}

be the support of
\{d_{k}\}
.
If
S=\emptyset
, we are done.
Assume
S\neq\emptyset
.
Then

\sum_{k\in S}d_{k}\,e^{\lambda k^{\,2p+1}}=0.

Now invoke the classical Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem
(
[
9
, Theorem 1.4]
), which asserts that if

\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
are distinct algebraic numbers, then

e^{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,e^{\alpha_{m}}
are linearly independent,
in particular linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Apply this theorem to the distinct algebraic numbers

\alpha_{i}=\lambda\,k_{i}^{\,2p+1},\qquad S=\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\}.

Then

\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{k_{i}}\,e^{\lambda k_{i}^{\,2p+1}}=0,\qquad d_{k_{i}}\in\mathbb{Q},

implies
d_{k_{i}}=0
for all
i
by linear independence.
This contradicts the definition of
S
.
Hence
S=\emptyset
.

Therefore
d_{k}=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Q}
, so
m_{k}=n_{k}
for all
k
.
Thus
V
and
W
have identical multiplicities, and therefore

V=W

as multisets.
∎

Corollary B.12
.

If
f,g\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
and
V_{f}=V_{g}
where
\Phi(x)=e^{\lambda x^{2p+1}}
for a fix
p\in\mathbb{Z}
, then they are lag-homometric.

Proof.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
. From
V_{f}(k)=V_{g}(k)
we have

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\exp\lambda(f(j)-f(j+k))^{2p+1}=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\exp\lambda(g(j)-g(j+k))^{2p+1}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\exp\lambda(f(j)-f(j+k))^{2p+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\exp\lambda(g(j)-g(j+k))^{2p+1}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\sum_{x\in\Delta_{f}(k)}\exp\lambda x^{2p+1}=\sum_{x\in\Delta_{g}(k)}\exp\lambda x^{2p+1}

By Lemma
B.11
, we see that
\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{g}(k)

∎

B.2
Uniqueness for Permutation Functions in
\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\sigma}

Definition B.13
.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}^{m}
, the position function
\pi_{f}
is defined by
\pi_{f}(f(i))=i\bmod m
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.

Theorem B.14
.

Let
f,g\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{m})
.
If
f
and
g
are lag-homometric and

\beta:=\max_{j}f(j),\qquad\alpha:=\min_{j}f(j),\qquad\beta^{\prime}:=\max_{j}g(j),\qquad\alpha^{\prime}:=\min_{j}g(j),

then
\beta-\alpha=\beta^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime}
.
Moreover, if
f
and
g
are injective, then

\pi_{f}(\beta)-\pi_{f}(\alpha)=\pi_{g}(\beta^{\prime})-\pi_{g}(\alpha^{\prime})\pmod{m}.

Proof.

Since all indices are taken modulo
m
, every ordered pair
(p,q)
appears as a
lagged difference: for any
p,q
there exists
k
with
q=p+k\pmod{m}
, and hence

f(q)-f(p)\in\Delta_{f}(k).

Therefore,

\beta-\alpha=\max\{d:d\in\Delta_{f}(k)\ \text{for some }k\}.

Because
f
and
g
are lag-homometric, we have
\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{g}(k)
for all
k
, so the same maximal value appears in
\bigcup_{k}\Delta_{g}(k)
. Hence

\beta-\alpha=\beta^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime}.

For the positional statement, assume
f
and
g
are injective.
Then the values
\beta,\alpha
occur at unique indices
i,j
with

f(i)=\beta,\qquad f(j)=\alpha,

and similarly
\beta^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}
occur at unique indices
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}
with

g(i^{\prime})=\beta^{\prime},\qquad g(j^{\prime})=\alpha^{\prime}.

Since
f(i)-f(j)=\beta-\alpha
is the maximal difference, this value occurs in

\Delta_{f}(k)
only for the unique shift

k=i-j\pmod{m}.

Similarly, the maximal difference
\beta^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime}
occurs in
\Delta_{g}(k^{\prime})
only for

k^{\prime}=i^{\prime}-j^{\prime}\pmod{m}.

Because the two functions are lag-homometric, the location (shift) of the maximal difference must be the same; hence

k=k^{\prime}\pmod{m}.

Substituting the definitions of
k
and
k^{\prime}
gives

i-j=i^{\prime}-j^{\prime}\pmod{m},

which in terms of position functions is exactly

\pi_{f}(\beta)-\pi_{f}(\alpha)=\pi_{g}(\beta^{\prime})-\pi_{g}(\alpha^{\prime})\pmod{m}.

This completes the proof.
∎

(Anchoring
1
and

Let

.

Example B.16
.

For
n=5
, if
f=(1,3,5,4,2)
and
g=(3,1,4,2,5)
, then:

•

In
f
:
\pi_{f}(1)=0
,
\pi_{f}(5)=2
, so cyclic distance is
2-0=2
and
5-1=4\in\Delta_{f}(2)

•

In
g
:
\pi_{g}(1)=1
,
\pi_{g}(5)=4
, so cyclic distance is
4-1=3
and
5-1=4\in\Delta_{g}(3)

Moreover,

\displaystyle 4\notin\Delta_{g}(2)=\{3-1=2,\ 4-3=1,\ 2-4=-2,\ 5-2=3,\ 1-5=-4\},

\displaystyle 4\notin\Delta_{f}(3)=\{4-1=3,\ 2-3=-1,\ 1-5=-4,\ 3-4=-1,\ 5-2=3\}.

Therefore,
V_{f}\neq V_{g}
.

Remark B.17

(Positions of the next extremes cannot be determined up to swap)
.

Let
f,g
be lag-homometric permutations of
\{1,\dots,m\}
with

\pi_{f}(1)=\pi_{g}(1)
and
\pi_{f}(m)=\pi_{g}(m)
.
One cannot in general conclude that

\{\pi_{g}(2),\;\pi_{g}(m-1)\}=\{\pi_{f}(2),\;\pi_{f}(m-1)\}.

The following simple counterexample shows the failure of this claim:

f=(1,2,4,3,5),\qquad g=(1,3,2,4,5).

For these permutations

\{\pi_{f}(2),\pi_{f}(4)\}=\{1,2\}\neq\{2,3\}=\{\pi_{g}(2),\pi_{g}(4)\}.

Hence additional hypotheses are required if one wishes to determine the positions
of
2
and
n-1
up to swapping.

Lemma B.18
.

Let
f,g
be lag-homometric permutations of
\{1,\dots,m\}
.
Assume

1.

\pi_{f}(1)=\pi_{g}(1)=0
,

2.

\pi_{f}(m)=\pi_{g}(m)
,
for every shift
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
,

Then

\pi_{f}(2)\neq\pi_{g}(2)\Longrightarrow\pi_{g}(m-1)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2)\pmod{m}.

Proof.

We prove this by contradiction. Suppose that

\pi_{g}(m-1)\not=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2)\pmod{m},

and set
d=m-2
,
k=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2)
and

C_{f}(k,d)=\text{Multiplicity of }d\text{ at shift }k\text{ in }\Delta_{f}(k)=|\{d\ |d\in\Delta_{f}(k)\}|

The only ordered pairs that can produce
d
are
(2,m)
and
(1,m-1)
.
Now, let’s compute
C_{f}(k,d)
and
C_{g}(k,d)
, which must be equal by the 4th hypothesis.

•

Computing
C_{f}(k,d)
:

Clearly,
(2,m)
contributes 1 to
C_{f}(k,d)
because
m-2\in\Delta_{f}(\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2))
. On the other hand, the pair
(1,m-1)
contributes 1 if
\pi_{f}(m-1)-\pi_{f}(1)=\pi_{f}(m-1)=k\pmod{m}
, and if not, it contributes 0 to
C_{f}(k,d)
. Therefore,
C_{f}(k,d)=1
or
C_{f}(k,d)=2
.

•

Computing
C_{g}(k,d)
:

We know that
m-2\in\Delta_{g}(\pi_{g}(m)-\pi_{g}(2))
. Now, if
\pi_{g}(m)-\pi_{g}(2)=k\pmod{m}
, then
\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2)=\pi_{g}(m)-\pi_{g}(2)\pmod{m}
, and because
\pi_{f}(m)=\pi_{g}(m)
, we get
\pi_{f}(2)=\pi_{g}(2)
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
\pi_{g}(m)-\pi_{g}(2)\not=k\pmod{m}
. Therefore, the pair
(2,m)
contributes 0 to
C_{g}(k,d)
. On the other hand, the pair
(1,m-1)
contributes 0 to
C_{g}(k,d)
because
(m-1)-(1)\in\Delta_{g}(\pi_{g}(m-1)-\pi_{g}(1))=\Delta_{g}(\pi_{g}(m-1))
and
\pi_{g}(m-1)\not=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2)=k\pmod{m}
. This implies that
C_{g}(k,d)=0
.

Therefore,
C_{g}(k,d)<C_{f}(k,d)
, which is a contradiction with being lag-homometric. Therefore, the assumption is false, and
\pi_{g}(m-1)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2)\pmod{m}
.
∎

Lemma B.19
.

Let
f,g
be lag-homometric permutations of
\{1,\dots,m\}
.
Assume

1.

\pi_{f}(1)=\pi_{g}(1)=0
,

2.

\pi_{f}(m)=\pi_{g}(m)
,

3.

\pi_{f}(2)\neq\pi_{g}(2)

Then

\pi_{f}(m-1)\neq\pi_{g}(m-1)\bmod m\ \;\text{ and }\;\;\pi_{g}(2)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(m-1)\bmod m.

Proof.

Using the Lemma
B.18
, we can see that

\pi_{g}(m-1)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2)\bmod m,\qquad\pi_{f}(m-1)=\pi_{g}(m)-\pi_{g}(2)\bmod m.

From 2nd hypothesis
\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2)\not=\pi_{g}(m)-\pi_{g}(2)\bmod m
and therefore,
\pi_{g}(m-1)\not=\pi_{f}(m-1)\bmod m
.
Moreover, by using the above equivalency we can write

\pi_{g}(2)=\pi_{g}(m)-\pi_{f}(m-1)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(m-1)\bmod m.

∎

Lemma B.20
.

Let
f,g
be lag-homometric permutations of
\{1,\dots,m\}
. Assume

1.

\pi_{f}(1)=\pi_{g}(1)=0
,

2.

\pi_{f}(m)=\pi_{g}(m)
,

3.

\pi_{f}(2)=\pi_{g}(2)
.

Then
\pi_{f}(m-1)=\pi_{g}(m-1)
.

Proof.

Assume, for contradiction, that
\pi_{f}(2)=\pi_{g}(2)
but
\pi_{f}(m-1)\neq\pi_{g}(m-1)
.
Set

d:=m-2.

Observe that among ordered label-pairs from
\{1,\dots,m\}
the only pairs whose label-difference equals
d
are

(1,m-1)\quad\text{and}\quad(2,m).

Hence the value
d
can occur in the shift-multisets only at shifts coming from these two pairs.

Write

k_{1}^{f}:=\pi_{f}(m-1),\qquad k_{2}^{f}:=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2)\bmod m,

and likewise

k_{1}^{g}:=\pi_{g}(m-1),\qquad k_{2}^{g}:=\pi_{g}(m)-\pi_{g}(2)\bmod m.

By construction, in permutation
f
the difference
d
appears (as an element of the multiset) exactly at the shifts in the multiset
\{k_{1}^{f},k_{2}^{f}\}
(counted with multiplicity), and in
g
it appears exactly at the shifts in
\{k_{1}^{g},k_{2}^{g}\}
.

Hypotheses (2) and (3) give
\pi_{f}(m)=\pi_{g}(m)\bmod m
and
\pi_{f}(2)=\pi_{g}(2)\bmod m
, hence

k_{2}^{f}=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2)=\pi_{g}(m)-\pi_{g}(2)=k_{2}^{g}\bmod m.

Being lag-homometric implies that the multiset of shifts where
d
occurs in
f
equals that for
g
, i.e.

\{k_{1}^{f},k_{2}^{f}\}=\{k_{1}^{g},k_{2}^{g}\}\quad\text{(as multisets).}

Since
k_{2}^{f}=k_{2}^{g}\bmod m
, two cases cover all possibilities:

•

Case 1:

k_{1}^{f}\neq k_{2}^{f}
:

Then the multiset equality forces
k_{1}^{f}=k_{1}^{g}
, i.e.
\pi_{f}(m-1)=\pi_{g}(m-1)\bmod m
, contradicting the assumption.

•

Case 2:

k_{1}^{f}=k_{2}^{f}
.

Then

\pi_{f}(m-1)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2)\bmod m.

By multiset equality we must also have
k_{1}^{g}=k_{2}^{g}\bmod m
, hence

\pi_{g}(m-1)=\pi_{g}(m)-\pi_{g}(2)\bmod m.

Using
\pi_{f}(m)=\pi_{g}(m)\bmod m
and
\pi_{f}(2)=\pi_{g}(2)\bmod m
we obtain

\pi_{g}(m-1)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(2)=\pi_{f}(m-1)\bmod m,

again contradicting the assumption.

In both cases we see a contradiction. Therefore the assumption
\pi_{f}(m-1)\neq\pi_{g}(m-1)\bmod m
is false, and
\pi_{f}(m-1)=\pi_{g}(m-1)\bmod m
.
∎

Lemma B.21
.

Let
f,g
be lag-homometric permutations of
\{1,\dots,m\}
. Assume

1.

\pi_{f}(1)=\pi_{g}(1)=0
,

2.

\pi_{f}(m)=\pi_{g}(m)
.

Then for all
p\in\{2,\ldots,m\}
:

\forall a\in\{1,2,\ldots,p\},\quad\pi_{f}(a)=\pi_{g}(a)\Longrightarrow\pi_{f}(m+1-p)=\pi_{g}(m+1-p)

Proof.

We will prove this by induction.

Base case

p=2
: Done in the Lemma
B.20
.

Induction hypothesis (IH)
:
For any
1\leq a\leq p-1
, the equation
\pi_{f}(a)=\pi_{g}(a)
implies that
\pi_{f}(m+1-a)=\pi_{g}(m+1-a)
.

Induction step
:
We prove that if
\pi_{f}(p)=\pi_{g}(p)
then
\pi_{f}(m+1-p)=\pi_{g}(m+1-p)
.
We set
d=m-p
. The only pairs that can generate the
d
are :

(1,1+d),\ (2,2+d),\ \ldots,\ (p,p+d).

Now, we define
k_{a}^{f}=\pi_{f}(a+d)-\pi_{f}(a),\ k_{a}^{g}=\pi_{g}(a+d)-\pi_{g}(a)
. Then we set

M_{f}=\{k_{a}^{f}\ |\ a\in\{1,2,\ldots,p\}\},\qquad M_{g}=\{k_{a}^{g}\ |\ a\in\{1,2,\ldots,p\}\}

Since we have
\Delta_{f}=\Delta_{g}
, we can claim
M_{f}=M_{g}
. This is true because the only possible shifts that generate the
d
are the shift values in these sets. By the hypothesis of the induction step, we know that
\pi_{f}(p)=\pi_{g}(p)
and by the third hypothesis of the statement of the lemma we know that
\pi_{f}(m)=\pi_{g}(m)
. Hence
k_{p}^{f}=k_{p}^{g}
. Now, we define
\alpha=a+d
and
\beta=m+1-\alpha
. Then we have

m+1-\beta=m+1-(a+(m-p))=p-a+1

and because
2\leq a\leq p-1
we get

2=p-(p-1)+1\leq p-a+1\leq p-2+1=p-1

Therefore,

\pi_{f}(m+1-\beta)=\pi_{g}(m+1-\beta)

which implies that
\pi_{f}(a+d)=\pi_{g}(a+d)
. Hence,

k_{a}^{f}=\pi_{f}(a+d)-\pi_{f}(a)=\pi_{g}(a+d)-\pi_{g}(a)=k_{a}^{g}.

Since we show that
k_{2}^{f}=k_{2}^{g},k_{3}^{f}=k_{3}^{g},\ldots,k_{p}^{f}=k_{p}^{g}
and
M_{f}=M_{g}
, therefore we can say that
k_{1}^{f}
has to be equal
k_{1}^{g}
which implies that

\pi_{f}(1+d)-\pi_{f}(1)=\pi_{g}(1+d)-\pi_{g}(1),

and from second hypothesis we get the
\pi_{f}(1+d)=\pi_{g}(1+d)
and that is

\pi_{f}(1+m-p)=\pi_{g}(1+m-p)
. Therefore, the induction step is proven and the result is as desired.
∎

Theorem B.22
.

Let
f,g
be permutations of
\{1,\dots,m\}
.
Assume

1.

\pi_{f}(1)=\pi_{g}(1)=0
,

2.

\pi_{f}(m)=\pi_{g}(m)
,

3.

\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{g}(k)
for every shift
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.

Then for every
p\in\{2,\ldots,m-1\}

\pi_{f}(p)\neq\pi_{g}(p)\Longrightarrow\pi_{g}(m+1-p)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(p)\pmod{m}.

Proof.

We prove this by induction on
p
.

Base case
(
p=2
): This is Lemma
B.18
.

Induction hypothesis
: For every
1\leq a\leq p-1
,

\pi_{f}(a)\neq\pi_{g}(a)\;\Longrightarrow\;\pi_{g}(m+1-a)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(a).

Induction step
: Assume
\pi_{f}(p)\neq\pi_{g}(p)
. We must show
\pi_{g}(m+1-p)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(p)
.

Set
d=m-p
. For each
a\in\{1,\dots,p\}
, define

k_{a}^{f}=\pi_{f}(a+d)-\pi_{f}(a),\qquad k_{a}^{g}=\pi_{g}(a+d)-\pi_{g}(a),

and collect these into multisets

M_{f}=\{k_{1}^{f},k_{2}^{f},\ldots,k_{p}^{f}\},\qquad M_{g}=\{k_{1}^{g},k_{2}^{g},\ldots,k_{p}^{g}\}.

Since
\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{g}(k)
for all
k
, we have
M_{f}=M_{g}
as multisets.

Since
\pi_{f}(p)\neq\pi_{g}(p)
and
\pi_{f}(m)=\pi_{g}(m)
, we compute

\displaystyle k_{p}^{f}=\pi_{f}(p+d)-\pi_{f}(p)

\displaystyle=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(p)

\displaystyle\neq\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{g}(p)=\pi_{g}(m)-\pi_{g}(p)=\pi_{g}(p+d)-\pi_{g}(p)=k_{p}^{g}.

Therefore
k_{p}^{f}\neq k_{p}^{g}
.

We now show that every
k_{a}^{g}
for
a\in\{2,\dots,p-1\}
is determined by the induction hypothesis, leaving
k_{1}^{g}
as the only free entry in
M_{g}
.

For each
a\in\{2,\dots,p-1\}
, define
\beta=p+1-a
. Since
2\leq a\leq p-1
, we also have
2\leq\beta\leq p-1
. A direct computation gives

a+d=m+1-\beta,\qquad\beta+d=m+1-a.

This means
k_{a}^{g}
involves
\pi_{g}(a+d)=\pi_{g}(m+1-\beta)
, and
k_{\beta}^{g}
involves
\pi_{g}(\beta+d)=\pi_{g}(m+1-a)
. These are exactly the quantities the IH provides formulas for when positions disagree. Since both
a<p
and
\beta<p
, the IH applies to each. We consider four cases depending on whether
f
and
g
agree or disagree at
a
and at
\beta
.

(i):
\pi_{f}(a)=\pi_{g}(a)
and
\pi_{f}(\beta)=\pi_{g}(\beta)
.
Both positions agree. From
\pi_{f}(\beta)=\pi_{g}(\beta)
, Lemma
B.21
gives
\pi_{f}(m+1-\beta)=\pi_{g}(m+1-\beta)
, i.e.,
\pi_{f}(a+d)=\pi_{g}(a+d)
. Combined with
\pi_{f}(a)=\pi_{g}(a)
:

k_{a}^{g}=\pi_{g}(a+d)-\pi_{g}(a)=\pi_{f}(a+d)-\pi_{f}(a)=k_{a}^{f}.

So
k_{a}^{g}
is determined. By the symmetric argument,
k_{\beta}^{g}=k_{\beta}^{f}
is also determined.

(ii):
\pi_{f}(a)=\pi_{g}(a)
and
\pi_{f}(\beta)\neq\pi_{g}(\beta)
.

Since
\pi_{f}(\beta)\neq\pi_{g}(\beta)
and
\beta<p
, the IH gives

\pi_{g}(m+1-\beta)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(\beta).

Since
a+d=m+1-\beta
, this says
\pi_{g}(a+d)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(\beta)
. We also know
\pi_{g}(a)=\pi_{f}(a)
. Therefore

k_{a}^{g}=\pi_{g}(a+d)-\pi_{g}(a)=\bigl(\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(\beta)\bigr)-\pi_{f}(a).

Everything on the right is known. So
k_{a}^{g}
is determined.

(iii):
\pi_{f}(a)\neq\pi_{g}(a)
and
\pi_{f}(\beta)=\pi_{g}(\beta)
.

Since
\pi_{f}(a)\neq\pi_{g}(a)
and
a<p
, the IH gives

\pi_{g}(m+1-a)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(a).

Since
\beta+d=m+1-a
, this says
\pi_{g}(\beta+d)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(a)
. We also know
\pi_{g}(\beta)=\pi_{f}(\beta)
. Therefore

k_{\beta}^{g}=\pi_{g}(\beta+d)-\pi_{g}(\beta)=\bigl(\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(a)\bigr)-\pi_{f}(\beta).

So
k_{\beta}^{g}
is determined. Moreover, from
\pi_{f}(\beta)=\pi_{g}(\beta)
, Lemma
B.21
gives
\pi_{f}(a+d)=\pi_{g}(a+d)
, so
k_{a}^{g}
is determined as well.

(iv):
\pi_{f}(a)\neq\pi_{g}(a)
and
\pi_{f}(\beta)\neq\pi_{g}(\beta)
.

Both disagree. The IH applies to both:

\pi_{g}(m+1-\beta)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(\beta),\qquad\pi_{g}(m+1-a)=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(a).

Since
a+d=m+1-\beta
and
\beta+d=m+1-a
, we get

k_{a}^{g}=\bigl(\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(\beta)\bigr)-\pi_{g}(a),\qquad k_{\beta}^{g}=\bigl(\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(a)\bigr)-\pi_{g}(\beta).

Both are determined.

In all four cases,
k_{a}^{g}
is determined for every
a\in\{2,\dots,p-1\}
. The entry
k_{p}^{g}=\pi_{g}(m)-\pi_{g}(p)
is also known. The only entry of
M_{g}
that is not yet determined is

k_{1}^{g}=\pi_{g}(1+d)-\pi_{g}(1)=\pi_{g}(m+1-p),

since
\pi_{g}(1)=0
.

Now,
M_{f}=M_{g}
as multisets. The value
k_{p}^{f}
appears in
M_{f}
, but
k_{p}^{g}\neq k_{p}^{f}
and all of
k_{2}^{g},\dots,k_{p-1}^{g}
are already fixed. The only entry that can match
k_{p}^{f}
is
k_{1}^{g}
. Therefore
k_{1}^{g}=k_{p}^{f}
, which gives

\pi_{g}(m+1-p)=k_{1}^{g}=k_{p}^{f}=\pi_{f}(m)-\pi_{f}(p).\qed

Theorem B.23
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.
Let
P
be the set of all permutations of any consecutive
m
numbers, and let

V_{f}^{P}
denote the restriction of
V_{f}
to
P
.
Then

g=\mathsf{Rot}^{r}_{x}(f)\text{ or }g=\mathsf{Rot}^{r}_{x}(\mathsf{RoC}(f))\quad\text{ for some }x\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}\Longleftrightarrow V_{f}^{P}=V_{g}^{P}

Proof.

The forward direction is immediate:
the discrete signature is invariant under shifts, rotations, and
Reverse-of-Complement, so any such transformation leaves
V_{f}^{P}
unchanged.

For the converse direction, assume
f,g\in P
and
V_{f}^{P}=V_{g}^{P}
.
By Lemma
B.11
, equality of the restricted signatures
forces
f
and
g
to be
lag-homometric
.

Since
f,g\in P
, each is obtained from some
f^{\prime},g^{\prime}\in P^{\prime}

by adding a constant and applying a rotation.
Both operations preserve lag-homometricity, so the corresponding elements

f^{\prime},g^{\prime}\in P^{\prime}
are also lag-homometric.

Now normalize each
f^{\prime},g^{\prime}
to a canonical representative in
P^{\prime}
:
let

i_{\min}:=\arg\min f^{\prime},\qquad f^{\prime\prime}:=\mathsf{Rot}^{r}_{\,m-i_{\min}}\!\bigl(f^{\prime}-\min f^{\prime}+1\bigr),

so that
f^{\prime\prime}(0)=1
and
f^{\prime\prime}
becomes a permutation of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
Apply the same normalization to
g^{\prime}
to obtain
g^{\prime\prime}
.
Again, all steps preserve lag-homometricity, hence

\Delta_{f^{\prime\prime}}(k)=\Delta_{g^{\prime\prime}}(k)\qquad\text{for all }k.

At this point,
f^{\prime\prime}
and
g^{\prime\prime}
are permutations in
P^{\prime}
that are
lag-homometric.
Therefore, by Theorem
B.22
,
the signature on
P^{\prime}
is injective up to rotation or rotation of
the Reverse-of-Complement, which yields

g^{\prime\prime}=\mathsf{Rot}^{r}_{x}(f^{\prime\prime})\quad\text{or}\quad g^{\prime\prime}=\mathsf{Rot}^{r}_{x}(\mathsf{RoC}(f^{\prime\prime}))\qquad\text{for some }x\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}.

Undoing the normalization and reversing the earlier shifts and rotations
shows that the same relation holds for
f
and
g
:

g=\mathsf{Rot}^{r}_{x}(f)\quad\text{or}\quad g=\mathsf{Rot}^{r}_{x}(\mathsf{RoC}(f)),

for some
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
, completing the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Invariance of General Position Functions

This section provides details on the proof of invariance of lag-homometric functions
f,g\in\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
that are in
general position
, in the sense that all values
f(j)\neq f(j^{\prime})
for
j\neq j^{\prime}\in[m]
, and also all differences
f(j)-f(j^{\prime})\in\Delta_{f}
for
j\neq j^{\prime}
are also distinct.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
let
\Delta_{f}=\bigcup_{k\in[m]}\Delta_{f}(k)
be the set of all differences. The difference of a function value
f(j)
with itself
f(j)-f(j)=0
, but by definition of
\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
, all other differences are distinct. Thus
\Delta_{f}
has
m(m-1)+1
distinct values (with the
1
coming from the
m
copies of difference of
0
).

Recall that we define the
hat
version
\hat{f}
of a function
f\in\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
as being in order by its index, so
0=\hat{f}(0)<\hat{f}(1)<\hat{f}(2)<\ldots<\hat{f}(m-2)<\hat{f}(m-1)=M
.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
f=[a_{0}=0,a_{1},\ldots,a_{m-1}]
be a function on
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and let

\hat{f}(0)=0<\hat{f}(1)<\cdots<\hat{f}(m-1)=M
be the increasing rearrangement of

\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{m-1}\}
. For
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
define

\Delta_{f}(k)=\{\,f(j+k)-f(j)\mid j=0,1,\ldots,m-1\,\}.

Then the second largest value in
\Delta_{f}
can only be one of

\hat{f}(m-1)-\hat{f}(1),\qquad\hat{f}(m-2)-\hat{f}(0).

Proof.

Each element of
\Delta_{f}(k)
is a difference of the form

\hat{f}(j)-\hat{f}(i)
for some
0\leq i,j\leq m-1
.
Any difference using only indices
0<i,j<m-2
is strictly smaller than every
difference involving either the minimum
\hat{f}(0)
or the maximum
\hat{f}(m-1)
.
Thus the second largest value cannot arise from such pairs.

Hence the second largest value must come from one of the two families

\hat{f}(m-1)-\hat{f}(i),\quad i=1,2,\ldots,m-2,\qquad\text{or}\qquad\hat{f}(i)-\hat{f}(0),\quad i=1,2,\ldots,m-2.

Within the first family,

\hat{f}(m-1)-\hat{f}(1)\;>\;\hat{f}(m-1)-\hat{f}(i),\qquad i=2,\ldots,m-2,

so the largest candidate there is
\hat{f}(m-1)-\hat{f}(1)
.

Within the second family,

\hat{f}(m-2)-\hat{f}(0)\;>\;\hat{f}(i)-\hat{f}(0),\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,m-3,

so the largest candidate there is
\hat{f}(m-2)-\hat{f}(0)
.

Thus the second largest value in
\bigcup_{k}\Delta_{f}(k)
can only be

\hat{f}(m-1)-\hat{f}(1)
or
\hat{f}(m-2)-\hat{f}(0)
.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
be lag-homometric, and in canonical form so

\hat{f}(0)=\hat{g}(0)=0
.
Then we must have

\hat{g}(m-2)=\hat{f}(m-2)\quad\text{XOR}\quad\Big(\hat{g}(m-2)=\hat{f}(m-1)-\hat{f}(1)\quad\&\quad\hat{f}(m-2)=\hat{g}(m-1)-\hat{g}(1)\Big).

Proof.

Let
M
be the unique maximum difference in
\Delta_{f}
. Because
f,g
are lag-homometric, they share the exact same multiset of differences for every lag
k
. Thus,
f
and
g
share the same overall maximum difference
M
, and the same second largest difference
d_{2}
.

By Lemma
C.1
, the second largest difference in
f
must be either
A_{f}=\hat{f}(m-1)-\hat{f}(1)
or
B_{f}=\hat{f}(m-2)-\hat{f}(0)=\hat{f}(m-2)
. Similarly, the second largest difference in
g
must be either
A_{g}=\hat{g}(m-1)-\hat{g}(1)
or
B_{g}=\hat{g}(m-2)
. Because
d_{2}
is uniquely the second largest difference in both functions,
d_{2}\in\{A_{f},B_{f}\}
and
d_{2}\in\{A_{g},B_{g}\}
.

Because
f\in\mathcal{F}^{m}_{\neq}
, all non-zero differences are uniquely generated by exactly one pair of indices. Therefore, the value
d_{2}
occurs at a unique lag
k_{2}
in
f
. Since
\Delta_{f}(k_{2})=\Delta_{g}(k_{2})
, the exact value
d_{2}
must also be generated in
g
at the exact same lag
k_{2}
.
The lag for
A_{f}
is
k_{A_{f}}=\pi_{f}(\hat{f}(m-1))-\pi_{f}(\hat{f}(1))\pmod{m}
.
The lag for
B_{f}
is
k_{B_{f}}=\pi_{f}(\hat{f}(m-2))-\pi_{f}(\hat{f}(0))\pmod{m}
.
(And similarly for
A_{g}
and
B_{g}
).

We have two possibilities for
f
:

1.

d_{2}=A_{f}
. Then
d_{2}
occurs at lag
k_{A_{f}}
. In
g
,
d_{2}
must be either
A_{g}
or
B_{g}
. If
d_{2}=A_{g}
, then
A_{f}=A_{g}

∎

Recall, that since we have identified the location of the second largest difference in

In general, define

. This notation will be useful when applied to

. So far, by having

\mathsf{RoC}

\pi_{f}(\hat{f}(m-1))=\pi_{g}(\hat{g}(m-1))

\pi_{f}(\hat{f}(m-2))=\pi_{g}(\hat{g}(m-2))

. This assumes we have identified the location of the

Lemma C.3
.

Consider

which are lag-homometric and in
\mathsf{RoC}
-canonical form. Assume we have resolved the locations of the

, confirming they are the same. We can then confirm the location of either the
(s+1)
th smallest or
(t+1)
th largest by value of

and confirm that this value matches.

Proof.

Let
\beta=\max\Delta_{f}^{s,t}=\max\Delta_{g}^{s,t}
. This difference must either be formed by

\beta=\hat{f}(m-1)-\hat{f}(s)\quad\text{ or }\quad\beta=\hat{f}(m-1-t)-\hat{f}(0)=\hat{f}(m-1-t)

in
f
, and similar in

\beta=\hat{g}(m-1)-\hat{g}(s)\quad\text{ or }\quad\beta=\hat{g}(m-1-t)-\hat{g}(0)=\hat{g}(m-1-t).

If in both

and

f(m-1)
and which
\Delta_{f}(k)=\Delta_{g}(k)

, resolving the location of the
(s+1)
th smallest.
Similarly, if both
f
and
g
are in the second case, then we can resolve the location of the
(t+1)
th largest.

What remains is to show that if they have a mismatch (
\beta=\hat{f}(m-1)-\hat{f}(s)=\hat{g}(m-1-t)
, or
\beta=\hat{f}(m-1-t)=\hat{g}(m-1)-\hat{g}(s)
), this causes a contradiction, so that case cannot happen. These cases are symmetric, so we analyze the condition
\beta=\hat{f}(m-1)-\hat{f}(s)=\hat{g}(m-1-t)
.

Define the position function
p_{f}(j)=\pi_{f}(\hat{f}(j))
and
p_{g}(j)=\pi_{g}(\hat{g}(j))
.
Now we must have
g(\pi_{g}(\beta))\neq f(\pi_{g}(\beta))
, since otherwise
f(\pi_{g}(\beta))-f(0)=\beta
and
f(0)-f(\pi_{g}(\beta))=-\beta
would both be in
\Delta_{f}
, but

.

Now consider the lag

. Since we established

\delta_{2}=g(p_{g}(m-2))-g(\pi_{g}(\beta))=\hat{g}(m-2)-g(\pi_{g}(\beta))\in\Delta_{g}(k).

. But in

Appendix D
Sampling from RKHS

D.1
Kernels and Hilbert Space

In this section, we define our representation and highlight some of its key properties. Specifically, the representation maps every bounded function into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), where the induced positive definite kernel allows us to compare functions in
L^{2}(S^{1})
in a principled and geometrically meaningful way.

We now define an inner product on the space of signatures, which is the standard inner product of a Hilbert space.

Definition D.1

(Invariant Signature Kernel)
.

The
Invariant Signature Kernel

k(f,g)
is defined as the inner product between two signature functions
V_{f}
and
V_{g}
in
L^{2}(S^{1})
:

k(f,g)=\langle V_{f},V_{g}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}V_{f}(\alpha)\overline{V_{g}(\alpha)}\,d\alpha.

This kernel quantifies the total similarity between the functions
f
and
g
across all possible shifts on
S^{1}
.

The invariant signature kernel
k(f,g)=\langle V_{f},V_{g}\rangle_{L^{2}}
provides a powerful tool for quantifying the similarity between functions in
L^{2}(S^{1})
. By measuring the inner product of signature functions
V_{f}
and
V_{g}
, the kernel captures the degree of alignment between
f
and
g
across all possible shifts on the unit circle
S^{1}
. For a kernel to effectively serve as a similarity measure, it must be
positive semi-definite
. Positive definiteness ensures that the kernel induces a valid inner product in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), which has several critical implications. First, it guarantees that the kernel defines a notion of distance via the induced norm, allowing meaningful comparisons of similarity: larger values of
k(f,g)
indicate greater similarity, while
k(f,f)=\|V_{f}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq 0
reflects the magnitude of
f
. Positive semidefiniteness ensures the distance is a pseudometric.
Second, positive definiteness ensures that the kernel matrix formed by evaluating
k(f_{i},f_{j})
for a set of functions is positive semi-definite, which is essential for applications in machine learning, such as kernel methods, where this property ensures numerical stability and well-defined optimization problems. Without positive semi-definiteness, the kernel could produce negative or undefined similarity measures, undermining its interpretability and utility. The following theorem establishes that our invariant signature kernel satisfies this crucial property.

Remark D.2

(Kernel as an Expectation)
.

Our kernel,
k(f,g)
, can be expressed as an expected value. Let
\alpha
be a random variable drawn from the uniform distribution on the interval
[0,2\pi)
. The probability density function (PDF) for this distribution is
p(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi}
for any
\alpha\in[0,2\pi)
.

By the definition of expected value, we have:

\mathbb{E}_{\alpha\sim U[0,2\pi]}\left[V_{f}(\alpha)\cdot V_{g}(\alpha)\right]=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(V_{f}(\alpha)\cdot V_{g}(\alpha)\right)\cdot p(\alpha)\,d\alpha=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}V_{f}(\alpha)\cdot V_{g}(\alpha)\,d\alpha.

Rearranging this equation gives a direct relationship between our kernel and the expectation:

k(f,g)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}V_{f}(\alpha)\cdot V_{g}(\alpha)\,d\alpha=2\pi\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\alpha\sim U[0,2\pi]}\left[V_{f}(\alpha)\cdot V_{g}(\alpha)\right].

This formulation allows us to use Monte Carlo methods, such as the one proposed by
[
41
]
, to approximate the kernel.

Remark D.3

(Approximation Bound via Hoeffding’s Inequality)
.

We can determine the number of random samples
D
required to ensure that our randomized kernel
Z_{f}^{T}Z_{g}
provides a good approximation to the true kernel
k(f,g)
by applying Hoeffding’s inequality (a form of the Chernoff bound for bounded random variables).

First, we define a practical, finite-dimensional representation. The
randomized signature

Z_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is constructed by sampling
D
i.i.d. shifts
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{D}\}
uniformly from
[0,2\pi]
and setting:

Z_{f}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{D}}\begin{bmatrix}V_{f}(\alpha_{1})&V_{f}(\alpha_{2})&\cdots&V_{f}(\alpha_{D})\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

The inner product is then:

Z_{f}^{T}Z_{g}=\frac{2\pi}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D}V_{f}(\alpha_{i})V_{g}(\alpha_{i}),

which serves as an unbiased Monte Carlo estimator of the true kernel
k(f,g)
. To bound the approximation error, we apply Hoeffding’s inequality with the following identifications:

•

The number of samples is
n=D
.

•

The i.i.d. random variables are
X_{i}=2\pi\,V_{f}(\alpha_{i})\,V_{g}(\alpha_{i})
.

•

The sample mean
\bar{X}=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D}X_{i}
equals the randomized kernel
Z_{f}^{T}Z_{g}
.

•

The expected value
\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}]
is the true kernel
k(f,g)
.

Since
f
and
g
are bounded, implying that the signature functions
V_{f}(\alpha)
and
V_{g}(\alpha)
are also bounded. Consequently, each
X_{i}
is bounded within an interval of length
\Delta
for some
\Delta>0
.

Substituting into Hoeffding’s inequality yields:

\Pr\left[|Z_{f}^{T}Z_{g}-k(f,g)|>\varepsilon\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}D}{\Delta^{2}}\right).

To achieve a confidence level of
1-\delta
, we set the right-hand side equal to
\delta
and solve for
D
:

D\geq\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right).

This demonstrates that the required number of samples scales as
O\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
.

Moreover, if we normalize the functions such that
\Phi(f(\theta)-f(\theta+\alpha))<\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}
for all
\alpha,\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, then:

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi(f(\theta)-f(\theta+\alpha))\,d\theta\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}\,d\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}},

which implies
V_{f}(\alpha)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
and similarly for
V_{g}(\alpha)
. Thus,
X_{i}\leq 1
, allowing us to set
\Delta=1
(assuming
X_{i}\geq 0
). For
\varepsilon=0.1
and
\delta=0.01
, this yields
D\geq 265
(approximately).

Remark D.4

(Bounding
X_{i}
for
\Phi(x)=e^{-\lambda x}
)
.

Fix
\lambda>0
. For

V_{f}(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-\lambda\,(f(\theta)-f(\theta+\alpha))}\,d\theta,\qquad X_{i}=2\pi\,V_{f}(\alpha_{i})\,V_{g}(\alpha_{i}),

we require a uniform bound
|X_{i}|\leq B
.

A direct estimate uses
\;|f(\theta)-f(\theta+\alpha)|\leq 2\|f\|_{\infty}\;
, giving

\|V_{f}\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{2\lambda\|f\|_{\infty}}\,d\theta=2\pi\,e^{2\lambda\|f\|_{\infty}},

and hence

|X_{i}|\;\leq\;B:=2\pi\|V_{f}\|_{\infty}\|V_{g}\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;8\pi^{3}\,e^{2\lambda(\|f\|_{\infty}+\|g\|_{\infty})}.

Appendix E
Error Bounds and Sampling Requirements for the FFT-Based Computation

Remark E.1

(Error Bound of the Discretized FFT Evaluation)
.

The continuous signature
V_{f}(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-\lambda(f(\theta)-f(\theta+\alpha))}\,d\theta
must be numerically approximated by a discrete periodic convolution computed via the FFT. Let

V_{f}^{(N_{\theta})}(\alpha_{k})=\Delta\theta\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\theta}-1}e^{-\lambda(f(\theta_{j})-f(\theta_{j+k}))},\qquad\theta_{j}=\tfrac{2\pi j}{N_{\theta}},\quad\Delta\theta=\tfrac{2\pi}{N_{\theta}}.

Then
V_{f}^{(N_{\theta})}
converges to
V_{f}
as
N_{\theta}\to\infty
. To quantify this convergence, assume
f
is
L
-Lipschitz and
2\pi
-periodic, i.e.,

|f(\theta_{1})-f(\theta_{2})|\leq L|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|
.
Then via mean value theorem for any fixed
\lambda>0
,

\bigl|e^{-\lambda(f(\theta)-f(\theta+\alpha))}-e^{-\lambda(f(\theta_{j})-f(\theta_{j+k}))}\bigr|\leq 2\lambda L\,e^{2\lambda L}|\theta-\theta_{j}|.

Standard trapezoidal-rule estimates for smooth periodic functions then yield

\|V_{f}^{(N_{\theta})}-V_{f}\|_{\infty}\leq C(\lambda,L)\,N_{\theta}^{-m},

(1)

for some constant
C(\lambda,L)
depending only on
\lambda
and the smoothness of
f
, where
m
is the number of continuous derivatives of
f
.
In particular, if
f\in C^{2}([0,2\pi])
, then
m=2
and

\|V_{f}^{(N_{\theta})}-V_{f}\|_{\infty}=O(N_{\theta}^{-2}).

For analytic
f
, the convergence is even faster, exponential in
N_{\theta}
, since the trapezoidal rule for periodic analytic integrands exhibits spectral accuracy.

Remark E.2

(Sampling Requirement for Prescribed Accuracy)
.

Given a target accuracy
\varepsilon>0
, we can select the minimal number of angular samples
N_{\theta}
such that

\|V_{f}^{(N_{\theta})}-V_{f}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{with confidence }1-\delta.

If
f
is assumed to be twice continuously differentiable and the discretization error follows the bound in (
1
), then

N_{\theta}\geq\left(\frac{C(\lambda,L)}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/2}.

The constant
C(\lambda,L)
can be estimated empirically by comparing successive resolutions or theoretically via the bound

C(\lambda,L)\approx 4\pi^{3}\lambda Le^{2\lambda L}
.
Under stochastic perturbations of
f
or additive measurement noise with variance
\sigma^{2}
,
Chebyshev’s inequality ensures that the probability of exceeding the target error is bounded as

\Pr\bigl(\|V_{f}^{(N_{\theta})}-V_{f}\|_{\infty}>\varepsilon\bigr)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}},

which corresponds to a confidence level
1-\delta
with
\delta=\sigma^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}
.
Thus, for a desired confidence
1-\delta
, it suffices to choose

N_{\theta}\geq\left(\frac{C(\lambda,L)}{\varepsilon\sqrt{1-\delta}}\right)^{1/2}.

Remark E.3

(Practical Implication)
.

In practice, this bound implies that doubling the sampling density improves the accuracy approximately by a factor of four when
f\in C^{2}
, and exponentially when
f
is smooth. Therefore, the FFT-based computation not only achieves
O(N_{\theta}\log N_{\theta})
complexity but also provides near-spectral convergence with respect to the number of angular samples, ensuring that high-precision computation of
V_{f}
is both fast and theoretically well-controlled.
