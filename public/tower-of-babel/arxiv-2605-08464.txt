The Geometric Structure of Models Learning Sparse Data Thanks: Now at Google Research
Thomas Walker, T. Mitchell Roddenberry, Ahmed Imtiaz Humayun, Randall Balestriero, Richard Baraniuk

Abstract
The manifold hypothesis (MH) is often used to explain how machine learning can overcome the curse of dimensionality . However, the MH is only applicable in regimes where the training data provides a sufficiently dense sample of the underlying low-dimensional data manifold, or where such a low-dimensional manifold is conceivably present. We describe the regimes where the MH is not applicable as sparse . In this paper, we demonstrate that models succeed in the sparse regime by exploiting a highly structured local geometry, a property we formalize as normal alignment . We prove that normal-aligned classifiers—whose input-output Jacobians are rank-one and align perfectly with the training data—minimize the training objective under norm constraints and achieve maximal local robustness under a non-zero Jacobian constraint. For continuous piecewise-affine deep networks, normal alignment manifests geometrically as centroid alignment within the network’s induced power diagram partition and results from the feature-learning regime. Motivated by these theoretical insights, we introduce GrokAlign , a regularization strategy that actively induces normal alignment. We demonstrate that GrokAlign significantly accelerates the training dynamics of deep networks relevant to the grokking phenomenon. Furthermore, we apply the principle of normal alignment to Recursive Feature Machines (RFMs) to introduce Recursive Feature Alignment Machines (RFAMs) . We show that RFAMs exhibit greater adversarial robustness compared to RFMs when trained on tabular data.



1
Introduction

The manifold hypothesis (MH) states that high-dimensional natural data has the majority of its structure in a low-dimensional subspace
[
37
,
43
]
.
Machine learning practitioners use this as a post-hoc explanation of how models can overcome the
curse of dimensionality
and extract patterns from high-dimensional datasets.

The MH assumes a continuous low-dimensional structure; thus, to be applicable in practice, it is necessary that the training data is a sufficiently dense sample.
Moreover, the assumed low-dimensional structure ought to be sufficiently regular with respect to the natural inductive biases of machine learning models.
Indeed, many common machine learning practices can be viewed as ensuring these conditions hold.
For example, data augmentation ensures a dense sampling of the manifold
[
27
,
10
,
36
,
16
,
11
,
6
]
, and model architectures are designed to complement the known structures of the data
[
4
,
1
]
.

Yet, machine learning models routinely succeed in regimes where the manifold hypothesis breaks down.
This occurs primarily under two conditions: first, in data-scarce settings where standard data augmentation cannot be readily applied, making a dense sample of the input space impossible to obtain.
Second, in inherently discrete domains—such as the algorithmic task of modular addition—the concept of a continuous underlying data manifold remains entirely inapplicable, even under infinite data assumptions.

In this paper, we show that the success of machine learning models in these
sparse
settings can be similarly attributed to the exploitation of low-dimensional structures.

Since the phenomenon of grokking – performance on the train set saturating well before performance on a test set saturates
[
14
]
– is a canonical example of a sparse setting, we use these insights to introduce the
GrokAlign
strategy for accelerating grokking training dynamics.
Similarly, as tabular data can be described as sparse, we introduce
Recursive Feature Alignment Machines (RFAMs)
to improve the robustness of Recursive Feature Machines (RFMs)
[
25
]
when trained on tabular data.

2
Sparse Datasets and Normal Aligned Classifiers

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{C}
be a classifier that predicts the class of an input
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\mathrm{argmax}(f(\boldsymbol{x}))
.
Let
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times d}
denote the input-output Jacobian of
f
at
\boldsymbol{x}
, with the
c^{\text{th}}
row denoted as
\boldsymbol{J}^{(c)}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denoting its
k^{\text{th}}
top right singular vector with corresponding singular value
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}
.
The output of the classifier can be decomposed as
f(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, where
\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
is the offset of
f
at
\boldsymbol{x}
.
Classifiers are trained on a data set
\mathcal{D}=\left\{\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
– where
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is its corresponding class – under some loss function
\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell\left(f\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right),y_{i}\right)=:\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}
.

Definition 1
.

A classifier
f
is
normal-aligned
to a dataset
\mathcal{D}
if for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
there exists
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
such that
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}=\boldsymbol{c}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}
.

That is, a model
f
is normal-aligned to
\mathcal{D}
if, at each training point
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
in
\mathcal{D}
, it only varies along the direction of
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.
In particular, the Jacobian of a model at
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is
rank-one
with rows that are scalar multiples of
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.

Recalling how the output of the classifier is reconstructed as
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}
, normal alignment resonates strongly with the concept of a
matched filter bank
from classical detection theory (radar and sonar, particularly)
[
39
,
38
]
.
A matched filter bank classifies a signal by selecting the template that maximizes the inner product between the signal and the template.
When the input signal
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, this is done optimally by setting the template equal to
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
(by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality).
Thus, a normal-aligned classifier precisely implements the program of an optimal match filter on the training data.

2.1
Sparse Datasets

The structure of a trained model can reveal information about the data it was trained on.
The geometry of a normal-aligned classifier is locally one-dimensional, since variations only occur along the directions of the training data.
Meaning, from a normal-aligned model’s perspective, there is no underlying structure connecting the dataset.
In other words, the dataset is
sparse
.

Definition 2
.

A dataset
\mathcal{D}
is
sparse
with respect to a class of classifiers
\mathcal{H}
if there exists a subclass of classifiers
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
normal aligned to
\mathcal{D}
such that for every
i=\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}
the value of
\ell_{i}
can be changed independently of
\ell_{j}
for
j\neq i
.

Normal-aligned classifiers are realizable for parameterized models such as deep networks.
In
Appendix

A
, we demonstrate how a single hidden-layer deep network can be constructed to be normal aligned to a data set
\mathcal{D}
.
The construction demonstrates that as the size of
\mathcal{D}
increases (i.e., it becomes more dense), a normal-aligned classifier becomes increasingly irregular from the perspective of weight norm.

2.2
Properties of Normal Aligned Classifiers

Optimizing the training objective.

Although normal alignment appears to be a restrictive property, it turns out to be optimal in the sparse regime.

.

\ell

\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\alpha
for

, the classifier which minimizes

is such that
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}=\boldsymbol{c}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and where
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}
only depends on
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
through its norm and
y_{i}
.

Theorem

1
says that at the optimum of the training objective – under a constraint on the norm of its Jacobian and offset terms at the training data – the classifier is normal-aligned.
The norm constraint of
Theorem

is analogous to the one imposed by weight-decay; it is also known to prevent “gradient explosion”

Exhibiting robustness.

In practice, it is desirable to have a robust classifier that correctly classifies the training data.
That is, a classifier that is invariant to small perturbations of the input.
To study this formally, fix an input point
\boldsymbol{x}
with true class
y
.
Let
\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{x},\lambda}
be the set of linear models
g(\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{b}
such that
\arg\max\left(g(\boldsymbol{x})\right)=y
and
\frac{\left|[\boldsymbol{b}]_{y}-[\boldsymbol{b}]_{c}\right|}{\left\|\boldsymbol{J}^{(y)}-\boldsymbol{J}^{(c)}\right\|_{2}}\leq\lambda
for
c\neq y
.

While this second condition prevents the trivial solution of a constant function with zero Jacobian, it naturally arises from standard regularized training.
Specifically, weight decay strictly bounds the magnitude of the local offset
\boldsymbol{b}_{x}
.
Consequently, to obtain a suitable classification margin, the network is forced to maintain non-zero Jacobians and satisfy this condition.
Forcing Jacobians to zero geometrically collapses the model to a bounded constant function within each activation polytope, destroying its expressivity and leading to severe performance degradation.
We outline this argument in more detail in
Appendix

D
as well as verify it empirically.

Let the local robustness of
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{x},\lambda}
be given by

\rho\left(\boldsymbol{x};g\right)=\inf\left(\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right\|_{2}:\arg\max(g(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}))\neq y\right\}\right),

such that the maximum local robustness is given by
\rho(\boldsymbol{x})=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{x},\lambda}}\left(\rho\left(\boldsymbol{x};g\right)\right)
.
For an arbitrary classifier
f
, we can speak of its local robustness at
\boldsymbol{x}
as
\rho\left(\boldsymbol{x};\tilde{f}\right)
where
\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{x},\lambda}
is given by
\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Theorem 2
.

The normal aligned classifier
f
of
Theorem

1
achieves maximal local robustness on
\mathcal{D}
.

To understand whether a normal-aligned classifier is achieved in practice, we consider the normal alignment and the effective rank of a model to the set
\mathcal{D}
to be

\mathrm{na}(f;\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}}\left|\frac{\left\langle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle}{\left\|\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(1)}\right\|_{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{2}}\right|,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{er}(f;\mathcal{D}):=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}}\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{C}\left(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(r)}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sigma^{(1)}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)^{2}},

respectively.

3
Normal Alignment for Different Model Architectures

Thus far, the theory of normal alignment has been agnostic of the model’s architecture.
Understanding the interplay between normal alignment and a model architecture can elucidate the
intrinsic
utility of different architectures, and inform strategies to improve their implementation.

In this section, we consider normal alignment in deep networks and recursive feature machines
[
25
]
.
We also consider the simpler example of a Gaussian kernel logistic regression model in
Appendix

C
.

3.1
Deep Networks

To develop the theory for deep networks, we focus on continuous piecewise-affine (CPA) deep networks.
Neoclassical classifiers like ReLU DNs are exactly CPA, while modern classifiers like transformers are approximately CPA
[
39
,
38
]
.
Since Jacobians are computable for arbitrary differentiable functions, conclusions in the CPA setting will transfer to the general setting.

3.1.1
Continuous Piecewise Affine Deep Networks

Any deep network built using affine transformations (e.g., convolution, matrix multiplication) and piecewise linear operations (e.g., ReLU activation, max pooling) is a CPA spline
[
39
,
38
]
.
CPA deep networks have two tightly interconnected features:
(i) An irregular tessellation (aka tiling or partition)
\Omega
of the
d
-dimensional input space into convex polytopes
[
17
]
.
(ii) A collection of affine mappings (one for each polytope) arranged such that the overall input-to-output mapping is continuous.
These polytopes and affine mappings combine together into the representation

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}(\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}\boldsymbol{x}+_{\omega})\mathbb{I}_{\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\omega\}},

(1)

where the “slopes” matrix
\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}
and “intercept” vector
ω
define the affine transformation mapping all inputs from tile
\omega\in\Omega
to the output.
While it is not explicit in (
1
),
\Omega
,
\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}
, and
ω
conspire such that the overall mapping
f
is continuous.
Clearly, we see that the Jacobian and offset of a CPA classifier at input point
\boldsymbol{x}
are given simply by
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}
and
{}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=_{\omega}
,
where
\boldsymbol{x}\in\omega
.
Of course, the Jacobian does not exist at the tile boundaries, but this set has measure zero in the input space.

The input-space tiling
\Omega
is implicitly defined in terms of the weights and biases of a CPA deep network.
In brief, for a ReLU DN (see
[
39
,
38
]
for more details), the inference computation at each neuron in a deep network layer involves the inner product of the layer’s input with the corresponding row of the layer’s weight matrix.
Combined with the additive bias term, this computation defines a hyperplane that divides the layer’s input space into two half-spaces.
The tiles are formed by the combinatorial intersections of these half-spaces.
Chaining layers together leads to a subdivision process that creates an increasingly fine tiling
[
20
]
.

To consider this more precisely, we introduce the following notation.
For a CPA deep network
f=\left(f^{(L)}\circ\dots\circ f^{(1)}\right)
, each layer
f^{d^{(l)}}:\mathbb{R}^{(l-1)}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{(l)}}
and sub-component
f^{(1\leftarrow l)}=\left(f^{(l)}\circ\dots\circ f^{(1)}\right):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{(l)}}
is also a CPA deep network.
Let
\boldsymbol{A}^{(l)}_{\omega^{(l)}}
,
{}^{(l)}_{\omega^{(l)}}
,
\omega^{(l)}
,
\Omega^{(l)}
and
\boldsymbol{A}^{(1\leftarrow l)}_{\omega^{(1\leftarrow l)}}
,
{}^{(1\leftarrow l)}_{\omega^{(1\leftarrow l)}}
,
\omega^{(1\leftarrow l)}
,
\Omega^{(1\leftarrow l)}
be analogous notation as introduced for CPA deep networks.

3.1.2
The Theory of Centroid Alignment

The
l^{\text{th}}
layer of a CPA deep network partitions its input space as a power diagram
\Omega^{(l)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(l-1)}
, making the partition of the input space of a deep network a power diagram subdivision
[
17
]
.
Power diagrams are closely related to Voronoi diagrams but employ a different defining distance
[
46
]
.

Definition 3
.

Given a collection of
Q

centroid-radius
pairs

\left\{\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{q},r_{q}\right)\right\}_{q=1}^{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
,
a
power diagram
partitions
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
Q
disjoint tiles
\Omega=\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{Q}\}
such that

\cup_{q=1}^{Q}\omega_{q}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
with each tile given by

\omega_{q}=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:q=\arg\min_{q^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,Q\}}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{q^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}^{2}-r_{q^{\prime}}\right)\right\}.

(2)

The distance minimized in (
2
) is called the Laguerre distance
[
30
]
.

The power diagram subdivision induced by the deep network is then constructed recursively.
The first layer of the DN partitions the input space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\Omega^{(1)}
.
The second layer then partitions the projections of the tiles
\omega^{(1)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{(1)}}
induced by
f^{(1)}
.
These are then pulled back to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to yield a finer partition of the input space
\Omega^{(1\leftarrow 2)}
which is a power diagram subdivision.
Eventually, the power diagram subdivision
\Omega=\Omega^{(1\leftarrow L)}
of the DN is constructed.
This process is analogous to hierarchical
k
-means
[
42
]
.
For more details, consult
[
17
]
, which shows that one can similarly obtain descriptors for a region
\omega_{q}^{(1\leftarrow l)}
of a power diagram division
\Omega^{(1\leftarrow l)}
, namely
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{q}^{(1\leftarrow l)}
and
r_{q}^{(1\leftarrow l)}
.
For simplicity, we will still refer to
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{q}^{(1\leftarrow l)}
and
r_{q}^{(1\leftarrow l)}
as centroids and radii; however, it is important to note that they do not reconstruct the partition
\Omega^{(1\leftarrow l)}
through (
2
).

While each tile
\omega
in the partition is defined implicitly through a combinatorial intersection of half-spaces, its centroid and radius are defined explicitly.
In a Voronoi diagram, centroids can be interpreted as elements of the input space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In contrast to Voronoi diagrams, in a power diagram (subdivision), a centroid is likely to lie outside its polytope.

Let
\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})=q
where
\boldsymbol{x}\in\omega_{q}
, and define the all-ones vector by
\mathbf{1}
.

Proposition 1
.

For a CPA deep network
f
, we have
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}\mathbf{1}
and
r_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}=\left\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}\right\|_{2}^{2}+2_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}\mathbf{1}
.

Proof.
See
Section

G.4
. ∎

In words,
the centroid of a polytope is the row-sum of its Jacobian
.

Proposition

1
enables us to access the centroid and radius of polytopes through an efficient Jacobian-vector product computation for arbitrary deep networks, including transformers
[
1
]
.

The connection established between a deep network’s power diagram parameters and its Jacobian enables us to examine the implications of normal alignment in deep networks.

Definition 4
.

A deep network
f
is
centroid-aligned
to
\mathcal{D}
if for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
there exists
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})}=c\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.

From Definition
1
and
Proposition

1
, it is clear that centroid-alignment is a weaker property than normal-alignment.

Corollary 1
.

A deep network normal-aligned to
\mathcal{D}
is centroid-aligned to
\mathcal{D}
.

The proof follows by direct calculation of the centroid of an aligned Jacobian:
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}=\boldsymbol{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=c\boldsymbol{x}
, where
c=\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}\mathbf{1}
.

3.1.3
Centroid Alignment Corresponds to Feature Learning

Suppose a deep network
f
has parameters (e.g., weights and biases)
\theta
.
In particular, consider a two-layer deep network of the form
f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\left(\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)
, where
\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{(2)}\times d^{(1)}}
,
\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{(1)}\times d}
, and
\sigma
is the ReLU nonlinearity.

Lemma 1
.

For
f_{\theta}
, we have
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}=\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}
, where
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}:=\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell\left(f\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right),y_{i}\right)
is an arbitrary differentiable loss function, and the deep network is being trained using full-batch gradient descent with a learning rate of
\eta
.
We denote the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the network output as
\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}:=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\ell\left(\boldsymbol{z},y_{i}\right)\big|_{\boldsymbol{z}=f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})}
.

Proposition 2
.

In the setting described above, we have

\partial_{t}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}\right\rangle\right)=\frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}^{\top}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\right)^{\top}\right)\left\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\rangle+\left(\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\right)^{\top}\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right)\right)\right]\mathbf{1}.

Proof.
See
Section

G.5
. ∎

The neural tangent kernel
[
31
]
between
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is taken to be
\Theta\left(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)=\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})\left(\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)\right)^{\top}
.
For the two-layer network considered here, the neural tangent kernel is given by

\Theta(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\left(\sigma(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\sigma(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\right)\boldsymbol{I}+\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right)\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\right)^{\top}.

It follows that the expression of
Proposition

2
can be written as

\partial_{t}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}\right\rangle\right)=\frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}^{\top}\Theta(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\mathbf{1}.

(3)

The
linear
and
feature
learning regimes of deep network training are characterized by having relatively static or dynamic neural tangent kernels, respectively
[
29
,
2
,
9
]
.
More specifically, a deep network is in the linear learning regime when for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have
\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\left(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right)\right)=0
for every
i=1,\dots,n
, and it is in the feature learning regime otherwise.
The former identifies when the deep network approximates a linear function, whereas the latter involves the deep network’s nonlinearities.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that the deep network
f
has memorized the training data (i.e.,
\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\right)=0
for every
i=1,\dots,n
).
Then, a changing rate of change of the centroid inner product (i.e.,
\partial^{2}_{t}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}\right\rangle\right)\neq 0
) implies the deep network is in the feature learning regime of training.

3.1.4
GrokAlign

From
Theorem

3
, it is apparent that encouraging centroid alignment is beneficial for inducing feature learning.
Thus, in this section, we explore how normal alignment (which implies centroid alignment) can be induced in deep networks trained via gradient-based methods.

Theorem

1
motivates the regularization of the Jacobian and offset norms during training, a method we introduce as GrokAlign.

However, a priori, it is unclear how this strategy is connected to, or more effective than, directly regularizing for normal alignment.
For simplicity, we henceforth consider bias-free models, such that
{}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{0}
for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
However, all derivations also hold for biased models by adding a dimension of ones to the input space.

One reason the Jacobian matrices of a classifier may exhibit a high effective rank and fail to align under standard training is the presence of orthogonal components.
More specifically, if
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times d}
is not aligned, then necessarily there exists non-zero vectors
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
and
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
along with a matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times d}
such that
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{c}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}
with
\boldsymbol{u}\perp\boldsymbol{x}
.
This orthogonal component
\boldsymbol{c}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}
of
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
does not influence the output of the classifier as
f(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{x}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{c}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{x}
.
Therefore, during training, the classifier will not observe any gradients to remove this component from the Jacobian.

To remove these orthogonal components, it is necessary for the Jacobian of
f
at
\boldsymbol{x}
to operate on an input that is not parallel to
\boldsymbol{x}
.
For a continuous piecewise affine (CPA) model, we can apply a small perturbation
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\boldsymbol{x}
such that
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
Hence,

f(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon})=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon})=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=f(\boldsymbol{x})+\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{c}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\right)\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(4)

meaning the orthogonal components we want to remove contribute to the classifier’s output.
Therefore, in practice, to remove the orthogonal components, it suffices to regularize
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\left(\frac{\left\|f(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon})-f(\boldsymbol{x})\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
for
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
drawn from a Gaussian distribution with covariance
\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}
.
As
\sigma\to 0
it follows that
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x})\to\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{F}^{2}=:\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x})
; meaning, GrokAlign regularization can be interpreted as a method for removing the orthogonal components of a classifier’s Jacobians.

, we review the practical implementation of GrokAlign and compare it with directly regularizing for normal alignment.

3.2.2
Recursive Feature Alignment Machines

While grokking and modular arithmetic represent explicit examples of sparse learning environments, tabular data presents a uniquely pervasive case of implicit sparsity in machine learning.
Tabular datasets typically fall into the sparse regime due to the breakdown of the MH.
Indeed, tabular datasets are fundamentally anisotropic; their dimensions represent features with distinct semantics, scales, and distributions.
Thus, defining a natural, continuous metric space or assuming a smooth underlying geometry between samples is challenging.
This geometric disconnect is compounded by the typically low sample-to-feature-complexity ratio inherent to tabular tasks.
Therefore, the theory of normal alignment seems particularly applicable to models training on tabular data.

As a result of
Proposition

3

\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{X})

\boldsymbol{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

4
Discussion

This work provides a rigorous geometric framework to explain how models succeed when classical assumptions, like the manifold hypothesis, fail.
We show that normal alignment serve as a structural signature of models trained in these sparse settings, offering a mechanistic explanation for how models successfully navigate data-scarce or discrete environments.

We actively translate this theoretical framework into actionable algorithms.
By regularizing for normal alignment via GrokAlign, we consistently accelerates grokking dynamics across a diverse set of tasks.
Similarly, by framing tabular datasets as fundamentally sparse, Recursive Feature Alignment Machines (RFAMs) leverage these geometric principles to achieve superior adversarial robustness over standard Recursive Feature Machines (RFMs).

A key direction for future work is mapping the phase transition between sparse and “dense” learning regimes, particularly since aggressive data augmentation diminishes a model’s reliance on explicit alignment.
Additionally, extending normal alignment regularization to domains dominated by discrete tokens and sparse signals, such as reinforcement learning and large language models, represents a promising frontier for accelerating feature learning at scale.

Constructing Normal Aligned Deep Networks

For simplicity, consider each
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
to be unit norm and let
f(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}+\right)
where
\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times n}
and
\sigma
is the ReLU activation function.
Let
m_{i}:=\max_{i\neq j}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right\rangle\right)
.
Then setting
\boldsymbol{W}_{1}^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
{}_{i}:=-\frac{1+m_{i}}{2}
is sufficient to yield a normal aligned deep network.
Intuitively, the deep network is constructed by positioning the activation level sets of each neuron (i.e., the hyperplane along which the input to the nonlinearity is zero) such that the
i^{\text{th}}
neuron is only active for
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and the normal of the hyperplane is parallel to the direction
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.
A visualization of this procedure is shown in
Figure

3
.

Since this construction is only dependent on
\boldsymbol{W}_{1}
and , the parameters
\boldsymbol{W}_{2}
do not contribute to the alignment property.
Instead,
\boldsymbol{W}_{2}
manipulates the output of the
i^{\text{th}}
neuron, which is
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}:=\frac{1-m_{i}}{2}
, to form the output of the model.
If
\mathcal{D}
is a
denser
sample, then
m_{i}
is closer to one, which means that
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
approaches zero.
Hence, for a fixed output,
\boldsymbol{W}_{2}
must have a larger norm, which means that under regularity constraints (e.g., weight decay) the normal-aligned solution is more challenging to learn.
In
Figure

we visualize this by coloring the scatter points in the second and fourth panel according to the norm of
\boldsymbol{W}_{2}
necessary to ensure that

.

Appendix D
Models with Jacobians Equal to Zero

Normal aligned classifiers are optimally robust amongst classifiers whose input-output Jacobians are non-zero.
Here, we demonstrate theoretically that this class of classifiers emerges under natural regularized training.
Furthermore, we show that classifiers with zero Jacobians suffer from performance degradation.

Suppose that weight decay is applied with a coefficient
\eta>0
.
Then there exists a
\mathcal{B}(\eta)
for the
\ell_{2}
-norm of
x
with the property that
\mathcal{B}(\eta)\to 0
as
\eta\to\infty
.
Thus,

\left|\left[{}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]_{y}-\left[{}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]_{c}\right|\leq\sqrt{2}\left\|{}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2}\mathcal{B}(\eta).

Now
Theorem

1
already demonstrates that there is an optimization pressure to have non-zero Jacobians.
However, we can deduce this more naturally by noting that a sufficiently well-trained classifier will satisfy some classification margin condition.
Namely,
f_{y}(\boldsymbol{x})-f_{c}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq\gamma
for some
\gamma>0
.
Consequently,

\left(\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(y)}-\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(c)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\gamma-\left(\left[{}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]_{y}-\left[{}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]_{c}\right).

Assuming
\boldsymbol{x}
is non-zero, some rearranging allows us to conclude that
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is non-zero as

\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(y)}-\boldsymbol{J}_{x}^{(c)}\right\|_{2}\geq\frac{\gamma-\sqrt{2}\mathcal{B}(\eta)}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}}.

Hence, for each
c\neq y
we have

\frac{\left|\left[{}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]_{y}-\left[{}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]_{c}\right|}{\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(y)}-\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(c)}\right\|_{2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\mathcal{B}(\eta)\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}}{\gamma-\sqrt{2}\mathcal{B}(\eta)}=:\lambda_{c}.

Therefore, taking
\lambda=\max_{c\neq y}\lambda_{c}
is sufficient to satisfy the ratio constraint of
Section

2.2
.

We can similarly use this argument to show that explicitly regularizing for zero Jacobians can degrade the classifier’s performance.
Indeed, if the Jacobians were zero, then
f(\boldsymbol{x})=_{x}
, which implies the margin is given by
\min_{c\neq y}\left(\left[{}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]_{y}-\left[{}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]_{c}\right)
.
However, due to weight-decay, this implies that the margin is bounded by
\mathcal{B}(\eta)
.
Since larger margins are associated with better classifiers, this implies that classifiers with zero Jacobians perform worse.

We can empirically verify that in practice, models do not learn classifiers with zero Jacobians, and that regularizing for zero Jacobians affects performance.
We train a fully connected deep network on a subset of MNIST
[
22
]
of size
1000
.
We train the network adversarially by applying PGD
[
12
]
to each training batch and weight decay.
Furthermore, we apply a Jacobian Frobenius norm penalty to the loss function with a weighting factor
\gamma
.
Throughout training, we monitor the model’s clean, robust accuracy on a held-out test set, as well as the norms of the Jacobians and offset terms evaluated on the training data.

In
Figure

6
, we see that for small values of
\gamma
the norms of the Jacobians increase during training and the size of the offset terms converge to a bounded value.
It is only for large values of
\gamma
for which solutions with Jacobians equal to zero are learned.
However, in these cases, the model’s performance severely degrades; perhaps because the corresponding offset terms still converge to bounded values.

Thus, it appears that the optimally robust solution of a classifier with Jacobians equal to zero suffers from not being optimal for the task.
This is a common trade-off when training adversarially robust models.
An optimally robust model is just the constant function; however, such a function is not able to learn the task
[
15
]
.

E.1
Directly Optimizing for Alignment

Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a unit vector.
Let
\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\perp}}=\boldsymbol{I}-\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}
be the orthogonal projector onto the subspace orthogonal to
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}
. Let
\mathcal{R}_{\perp}(\boldsymbol{x})=\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\perp}}\right\|_{F}^{2}
.
Intuitively,
\mathcal{R}_{\perp}(\boldsymbol{x})
measures how far
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
deviates from spanning the direction of
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}
.

Proposition 4
.

With notation as above,
\min_{\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}}\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{c}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\mathcal{R}_{\perp}(\boldsymbol{x})
with minimizer
\boldsymbol{c}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}
.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{c}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\perp}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}\right)-\boldsymbol{c}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\perp}}+\left(\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\boldsymbol{c}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\perp}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\boldsymbol{c}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}+2\left\langle\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}},\left(\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\boldsymbol{c}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}\right\rangle_{F}

\displaystyle\overset{(1)}{=}\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\perp}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\boldsymbol{c}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\perp}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\boldsymbol{c}\right\|_{2}^{2},

where in (1) we have used the fact that
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\perp}}
has columns orthogonal to
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
\left(\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\boldsymbol{c}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}
has columns in the span of
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}
.
Thus, the result follows.
∎

From
Proposition

4
it follows that regularizing
\mathcal{R}_{\perp}(\boldsymbol{x})
is equivalent to fitting the Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
to a matrix aligned to the vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}
.
Thus, to attain normal alignment, it is sufficient to replace
\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}
with
\frac{\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|}
.

E.2
Nuclear Norm Regularization.

The normal-aligned solution represent a deep network with rank-one Jacobians at the training.
Since, the nuclear norm constraint is the convex relaxation of minimizing rank
[
34
]
, it seems appropriate to consider nuclear norm regularization of a deep network’s Jacobian to induce normal alignment.

In
44
, it is shown that regularization of the Frobenius norms of the Jacobians of two sub-components of a classifier, whose composition yields the full input-output mapping, is equivalent to regularizing the nuclear norm of the full input-output Jacobian of the DN.

Theorem 4

(
44
)
.

Suppose
f=g\circ h
.
Then minimizing
\ell(f(\boldsymbol{x}),y)+\eta\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(f)\right\|_{\star}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\star}
denotes nuclear norm, is equivalent to minimizing
\ell(f(\boldsymbol{x}),y))+\eta\mathcal{R}_{\text{Nuc}}(\boldsymbol{x})
, where
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Nuc}}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{h(\boldsymbol{x})}(g)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h)\right\|_{F}^{2}\right)
.

E.3
Practical Implementation of Frobenius Norm Regularization

We have demonstrated that we can reduce the practical implementation of
\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{R}_{\perp}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Nuc}}
to understanding how to compute the Frobenius norms of a model’s Jacobians.
In particular, it is unnecessary to compute the full Jacobians of the classifier.
Computing Jacobians of classifiers is computationally expensive.
Similar to how stochasticity makes gradient descent tractable in practice (i.e., stochastic gradient descent), we can use stochasticity to make the above regularizers tractable in practice.
We leverage the fact that
\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}\right)=\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{F}^{2}
to note that we can use Hutchinson’s estimator to form an unbiased estimate of
\left\|\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{F}^{2}
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times d}
, and let
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy
\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\mathbb{E}\left(\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\right)=\boldsymbol{I}_{d}
.
Then,
\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{z}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{J}^{\top}\right)=\left\|\boldsymbol{J}\right\|_{F}^{2}
.
Equally, if
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left(\boldsymbol{u}\right)
and
\mathbb{E}\left(\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\right)=\boldsymbol{0}=\boldsymbol{I}_{C}
.
Then,
\mathbb{E}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{J}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{J}^{\top}\right)=\left\|\boldsymbol{J}\right\|_{F}^{2}
.

3
for estimating the Frobenius norm of
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
can be found in

.
However,

\mathbb{R}^{C}
.
The implementation of this estimation of the Frobenius norm of
\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
can be found in

.

These estimators are sufficient for implementing GrokAlign,
\mathcal{R}
, and nuclear norm regularization,
\mathcal{R}_{\text{Nuc}}
.
In practice, we utilize random vectors in the output space of classifiers, to obtain an approximation of the Frobenius norm of the classifier’s Jacobian, (i.e.,

The reason for this is that we can use the accumulated gradients from the forward pass to compute the Jacobian vector product

with

Using random vectors in the input space (i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=-\frac{\boldsymbol{W}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}+\alpha}{\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{2}^{2}}\boldsymbol{W}.

\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

Proof.

Consider any competing class

c
is given by

\left(\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(y)}-\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(c)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}+\left({}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(y)}-_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(c)}\right)=0.

Set
\boldsymbol{W}_{y,c}:=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(y)}-\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(c)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and write
\boldsymbol{W}_{y,c}=\alpha\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}+\beta\boldsymbol{u}
where
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}}
,
\boldsymbol{u}\perp\boldsymbol{x}
, and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, using
Lemma

5
, the minimal perturbation that moves
\boldsymbol{x}
onto that boundary is

\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{W}_{y,c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}+\left({}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(y)}-_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(c)}\right)\right|}{\|\boldsymbol{W}_{y,c}\|_{2}}=\frac{\left||\alpha|\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}+\left({}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(y)}-_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(c)}\right)\right|}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}+\lambda.

For fixed
\boldsymbol{J}
, the adversary will pick the minimizing
c
(the nearest boundary).
Therefore,

\rho(\boldsymbol{J};\boldsymbol{x})=\min_{c\neq y}\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{W}_{y,c}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\right|}{\|\boldsymbol{W}_{y,c}\|_{2}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}+\lambda.

(17)

This is true for every admissible
\boldsymbol{J}
.
Taking the maximum yields
\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}+\lambda
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

Equality in
Eq.

17
is only achieved when
\beta=0
and
{}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(y)}-_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(c)}=\lambda
for each
c\neq y
.
Clearly, the Jacobian aligned solutions of
Theorem

1
satisfy these conditions.

∎

G.5
The Dynamics of Centroid Alignment

Proof of
Proposition

2
.

From
Lemma

1
, observe that

\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}\right)=\left(\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\right)\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{W}_{1}+\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\right)+\boldsymbol{W}_{2}(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}.

Because
\sigma
is the ReLU nonlinearity, we have
\sigma^{\prime\prime}(z)=0
for all
z\neq 0
.
Consequently,
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=0
almost everywhere along the gradient flow, since
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}))
only changes when a pre-activation crosses zero.
Ignoring these measure-zero events, the dynamics simplify to

\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}\right)=\left(\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\right)\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{W}_{1}+\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\right)\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1},

where

\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{W}^{(j)}\right)=-\eta\nabla_{\boldsymbol{W}^{(j)}}\mathcal{L}

for
j=1,2
.
By the chain rule, one can show that

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{W}_{1}}\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}

and

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{W}_{2}}\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right)^{\top}.

Substituting these gradients into the dynamics equation yields

\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}\right)=\frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}\right)\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right)\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{1}\right).

To find the dynamics of the projection, we compute
\partial_{t}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}\right\rangle\right)=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}\right)
. Distributing
\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}
into the second term gives
\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Because
\sigma
is the ReLU function, it satisfies the identity
z\sigma^{\prime}(z)=\sigma(z)
. Consequently,
\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\left(\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\right)^{\top}=\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\right)^{\top}
.

Applying this simplification, the result follows:

\partial_{t}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}\right\rangle\right)=\frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}^{\top}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\right)^{\top}\right)\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+\left(\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\right)^{\top}\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right)\right)\right]\mathbf{1}.

∎

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

By assumption,
\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\right)=0
.
Note from
Equation

3
that,

\partial^{2}_{t}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}\right\rangle\right)=\frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}^{\top}\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\left(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right)\right)\mathbf{1}+\frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Theta}\left(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right)\mathbf{1}

for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Thus, if
\partial^{2}_{t}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})}\right\rangle\right)\neq 0
(i.e., an increasing rate of change of centroid inner product) then
\partial_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\left(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right)\right)\neq 0
for some
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
as
\boldsymbol{m}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
.
Hence, an increasing rate of change in the centroid inner product implies that the deep network is in the feature-learning regime. ∎

3
.

i\in\{1,\dots,n\}

.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}
