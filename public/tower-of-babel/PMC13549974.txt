Development and properties of composite coatings for surface repair of cement concrete pavements
Authors: Xiaoqing Yu, Peng Hu, Bo Peng, Zhipeng Tao, Zhanghong Liu, Hang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Repeated high-pressure water rubber removal can damage the surface texture of airport cement concrete pavements and reduce skid resistance. To address this problem, three composite coating systems, namely styrene-acrylic copolymer emulsion (H), water-based epoxy (S), and pure water (N), were developed and evaluated by laboratory tests. Within the tested thickness range, the pull-off force reached its maximum at a coating thickness of 3 mm, and the S coating showed the highest adhesion to concrete. Compared with the uncoated surface, the mean profile depth increased by 84.3, 60.0, and 32.8% for the H, S, and N coatings, respectively, while the slope spectral density peak wavelength shifted from 1.78 to 0.5–0.6 mm. The British Pendulum Number increased by 39.5, 31.8, and 21.5% for the H, S, and N coatings, respectively, and showed a strong positive correlation with mean profile depth (R2 = 0.959). After 100 freeze-thaw cycles, the S-series coatings showed the best durability, with coating retention rates up to 98% and a relative dynamic modulus of elasticity of about 89%. Overall, H was more effective for surface texture reconstruction and skid-resistance improvement, whereas S provided better bonding performance and freeze-thaw durability. These results demonstrate the potential of the proposed coating systems for rapid surface repair of cement concrete pavements.

Introduction

Airport cement concrete pavements may suffer from surface texture degradation due to repeated maintenance operations, aircraft loading, and environmental actions. In airport pavement maintenance, high-pressure water rubber removal is commonly used to remove rubber deposits and surface contaminants. However, while this technique effectively cleans pavement surfaces, frequent rubber removal may damage pavement texture, thereby reducing the surface friction coefficient1. Additionally, this process frequently induces surface defects, such as abrasion and freeze-thaw damage2, thereby posing a significant threat to aircraft operational safety.

To address the inadequate skid resistance of concrete pavements, common approaches involve the reconstruction of skid-resistant structures and the application of asphalt overlays3,4. However, these methods are often complex, time-consuming, and economically inefficient. In recent years, coating technologies have attracted increasing attention for pavement surface restoration. Arabzadeh et al.5 synthesized superhydrophobic coatings using nanomaterials, which provided water and ice repellency on cement-based concrete surfaces. Guo et al.6 selected two nanoparticles and three organic polymer coatings to investigate the influence of modified nanoparticles on the hydrophobicity of coated concrete, as well as the underlying improvement mechanisms. Xu et al.7 developed a colorful and durable superhydrophobic concrete (CSC) coating. Elnaggar et al.8 employed a novel asphalt polyurethane (As/PU) protective coating to improve the performance of concrete. Yuan et al.9 analyzed the impact of aggregate embedding techniques on both the skid resistance and abrasion resistance of concrete pavements, confirming that this process significantly enhances both properties. Li et al.10 developed MMA-modified unsaturated polyester resin (UPR) composite repair mortar as a high-performance, cost-effective thin-layer repair material for airport pavements, significantly enhancing their stability and performance. Ginneth et al.11 investigated the pull-off adhesion strength of mineral, epoxy, solvent-based, and water-soluble coatings applied to concrete slabs saturated with either oil or water, using two different drying processes. Their results indicated that the epoxy coating exhibited the best performance. Concurrently, rubber powder demonstrates exceptional elasticity and plastic deformation capacity, thereby improving the surface morphology and freeze resistance of concrete. Consequently, numerous researchers have integrated rubber powder into the surface layers of concrete12,13. Furthermore, the formation of a “positive texture” structure, characterized by textured peaks and interconnected surface voids on the pavement surface, can effectively enhance skid resistance during high-speed water accumulation14,15. However, the absence of theoretical guidance for optimizing positive texture pavements or wearing courses inhibits the full utilization of the advantages provided by positive textures in road skid resistance maintenance projects.

Although styrene-acrylic emulsion and epoxy-based coatings have been widely studied, their application as rapid texture-restoration coatings for cement concrete pavements remains insufficiently clarified. To address the surface texture reconstruction needs of apron and taxiway cement concrete pavements, this study proposes a composite coating system composed of custom-made functional powder, quartz sand, rubber powder, silane, and different liquid-phase components. The proposed coatings are designed to reconstruct positive pavement texture and improve skid resistance while maintaining sufficient interfacial bonding performance and freeze-thaw durability. Moreover, a multi-index evaluation framework is adopted to assess the bonding performance, texture reconstruction capability, skid resistance, and freeze-thaw durability of different coating systems, thereby clarifying their performance differences and providing a basis for coating selection under different repair requirements.

Materials and methods

Material composition and preparation process

In this experiment, coating materials were prepared by combining liquid components (Y) with solid components (G) in predetermined ratios. The solid component consisted of 98.4 g of a custom-made functional powder, 15 g of 100-mesh quartz sand, 1 g of rubber powder, and 0.6 g of silane, which were uniformly blended. The custom-made functional powder was mainly composed of 52.5 Portland cement, 52.5/72.5 sulphoaluminate cement, α-hemihydrate gypsum, silica fume (nano-SiO₂), nano-CaCO₃, and nano-TiO₂, with a representative mass ratio of 29:34:5:6:4:3, respectively. The incorporation of rubber powder and silane significantly enhanced the toughness and low-temperature performance of the coating materials. The liquid component included three types: styrene-acrylic copolymer emulsion (H, with a solid content > 45%, containing high-purity styrene emulsion, acrylic emulsion, and silica sol), water-based epoxy (S), and pure water (N). The N formulation, using water as the liquid phase, was included as a control group to distinguish the contribution of polymer binders in the H and S coatings. The coating preparation process was as follows: each component was weighed according to the designed ratio, the solid components were added to the liquid components, and the mixture was stirred for 5–6 min until homogeneous. During application, the flow properties of the coating materials were first tested, and the concrete substrate was cleaned. The mixture was then brushed onto the substrate at an application rate of 180–200 g/m². The coating cured in approximately 30 min, forming a thin functional layer with a microtextured surface. Coating performance evaluation methods

Specimen molding

The substrate concrete was designed according to the requirements for airport cement concrete pavement. P.O 52.5 Portland cement was used as the binder. The mixture consisted of cement, sand, two coarse aggregate fractions, water, and a water-reducing agent. The water-to-cement ratio was 0.40, and the dosage of the water-reducing agent was 2% of the cement mass. The unit dosages of cement, sand, coarse aggregate I, coarse aggregate II, water, and water-reducing agent were 340, 607, 850, 566, 136, and 7 kg/m³, respectively. Since airport cement concrete pavement is mainly designed to resist flexural stress under wheel loading, flexural strength was adopted as the key strength requirement. The substrate concrete was designed to meet the requirements of flexural strength greater than 5.0 MPa, cement dosage not less than 300 kg/m³, and frost resistance grade not lower than F300.

To meet the requirements for skid resistance testing, concrete slabs measuring 400 mm × 400 mm × 40 mm were cast. The surfaces were roughened using a 3 mm brush to simulate broom-finished airport pavement surfaces. After curing, the concrete slab was divided into eight zones labeled H1, H2, S1, S2, S3, S4, N, and M, each coated with the corresponding coating type, with M denoting the uncoated zone. To meet the requirements for pull-off testing, test blocks were cast using 4 cm × 4 cm × 16 cm triple-cavity steel molds. After casting, the blocks were cut into 4 cm × 4 cm × 1 cm test plates, which served as reference blocks. For freeze-thaw resistance testing, standard rectangular specimens measuring 100 mm × 100 mm × 400 mm were cast. All test specimens are depicted in Fig. 2.

Testing and characterization methods

Pull-off force is a commonly used indicator for evaluating the bond performance between coating materials and concrete substrates. In accordance with the MH/T 5006—2024 Technical Specification for Construction of Cement Concrete Pavements at Civil Airports17, the term “reference block” refers to a pre-cast concrete block with dimensions of 4 cm × 4 cm × 1 cm, which was cut from a 4 cm × 4 cm × 16 cm concrete specimen, as shown in Fig. 3a. During specimen assembly, the lower surface of the pull-off dolly was bonded to the reference block using a high-strength adhesive, while the opposite surface of the reference block was embedded into the uncured coating layer. In this configuration, the tensile load was transferred from the pull-off dolly to the concrete substrate through the reference block and the coating layer. This arrangement reduced the possibility of premature failure at the dolly–adhesive interface and allowed a more reliable evaluation of the interfacial bond strength between the coating and the substrate.

For the pull-off test, a 400 mm × 400 mm × 40 mm concrete slab was used as the substrate. To simulate the roughened surface condition of field-repaired pavement, the substrate was prepared using a layered-casting method, in which the lower five-sixths of the slab thickness served as the base layer and the remaining upper one-sixth formed the surface layer. The completed substrate is shown in Fig. 3b. The assembled pull-off specimen and test setup are shown in Fig. 3c,d, respectively. Pull-off tests were performed at 20 °C with a loading speed of 5 mm/min using an LBY-VI bond strength tester.

Results and discussion

Bonding performance

Pull-off force

To evaluate the interfacial adhesion between the coating and the concrete substrate and to investigate the influence of coating thickness on adhesion performance, pull-off tests were conducted on coatings of different thicknesses under different curing durations. The results are presented in Fig. 5. As shown in Fig. 5a, the pull-off force of the S coating was significantly higher than that of the H and N coatings. This may be attributed to the ability of the water-based epoxy resin to polymerize and react with the curing agent, thereby possibly forming a relatively continuous and dense three-dimensional network structure. This network may enhance the adhesion of the water-based epoxy resin to the concrete surface and improve its bonding performance21,22. Among the H coatings, H1 showed the highest pull-off force, whereas S2 showed the highest pull-off force among the S coatings. Based on these results, additional pull-off tests were conducted on H1, S2, and N under curing times of 3 h, 6 h, and 24 h, using controlled laboratory coating thicknesses of 3 mm and 6 mm, to further investigate the effects of curing time and coating thickness on adhesion performance.

Fig. 5Pull-off force results: (a) different coatings at 6 mm thickness; (b) different curing times.

Pull-off force results: (a) different coatings at 6 mm thickness; (b) different curing times.

As shown in Fig. 5b, the pull-off forces of the H1, S2, and N coatings under different curing times and coating thicknesses were measured and compared. At a coating thickness of 3 mm, the maximum pull-off forces after 3 h of curing were 958 N, 1128 N, and 752 N for the H1, S2, and N coatings, respectively. After 6 h of curing, the corresponding values increased to 1379 N, 1779 N, and 1025 N, and after 24 h, they further increased to 1676 N, 1989 N, and 1298 N. At a coating thickness of 6 mm, the maximum pull-off forces after 3 h of curing were 795 N, 992 N, and 573 N for the H1, S2, and N coatings, respectively. After 6 h, the corresponding values were 1093 N, 1365 N, and 895 N, and after 24 h, they were 1262 N, 1594 N, and 938 N. These results clearly indicate that the S coating provides advantages in terms of rapid curing and high adhesion. In general, both the type of liquid component and its blending ratio significantly affected interfacial performance. A lower liquid-component ratio and a higher solid-component ratio resulted in higher pull-off force and better interfacial adhesion. In addition, the pull-off force increased with curing time for all coating types, regardless of thickness, indicating that prolonged curing enhanced the overall bonding performance.

As the coating thickness increased, the pull-off force exhibited a decreasing trend, with the maximum value occurring at a coating thickness of 3 mm. This can be explained by the coating-substrate interaction at different thicknesses. When the coating thickness is too small, fine particles on the substrate surface may protrude beyond the coating layer, resulting in localized mortar deficiency and reduced pull-off force23. At a thickness of 3 mm, the coating more effectively fills the irregularities and voids at the coating-concrete interface, thereby eliminating existing gaps, increasing the contact area, and improving interfacial adhesion. However, as the coating thickness increases further, the possibility of interlayer slippage within the coating also increases, leading to a decrease in pull-off force. This is because thicker coatings may develop internal stresses that weaken the bond between layers24. Therefore, within the tested thickness range, 3 mm was identified as the coating thickness at which the pull-off force reached its maximum.

Bond flexural strength

Bond flexural strength serves as an indirect measure of the bond performance at the coating-concrete interface. Test results for the bond flexural strength of different coatings under various curing conditions are presented in Fig. 6.

Fig. 6The bonding flexural strength test results of different coatings.

The bonding flexural strength test results of different coatings.

As shown in Fig. 6, under 7-day curing conditions, the S coating exhibited the highest bond flexural strength of approximately 1.3 MPa, followed by the H coating at about 1.2 MPa, while the N coating demonstrated the lowest performance at 0.7 MPa. This may be primarily attributed to the significant changes in the microstructure of the aggregate and filler after incorporation into the epoxy resin. The aggregates and fillers are bonded by thin epoxy films. A prominent phenomenon involves network structures enveloping the lower parts and surroundings of acicular and lamellar particles. These films may bridge and fill some pores, thereby reducing their volume. The epoxy resin may interlock, forming a network structure25. During the curing and hardening process of epoxy mortar, the epoxy resin may permeate and form a film between the aggregates and fillers, possibly contributing to a relatively denser mortar structure and enhanced bond strength. This results in superior mechanical properties and permeability resistance, leading to the S coating exhibiting the best bond flexural strength. After extending the curing period to 14 days, the bond flexural strength of all coatings increased by more than double, indicating that the strength improves with extended curing cycles within the specified timeframe. Superior bonding performance helps minimize interface defects between the coating and concrete substrate, thereby inhibiting moisture and harmful medium ingress, ultimately enhancing the durability of the structure.

Skid resistance

Texture scanning results and analysis

(1) Mean Profile Depth.

MPD is a widely used parameter for characterizing the texture structure of asphalt pavements26. The Ames Texture Scanner professional software was used to obtain surface texture data for uncoated surfaces H1, S1 and N coatings’ surfaces. The MPD distribution characteristics are shown in Fig. 7. In the MPD visualization images, color variations directly correspond to MPD magnitude, following this order: red > yellow > green > blue > purple. Red regions indicate the maximum MPD values, while purple regions represent the minimum values.

Fig. 7MPD visualized 3D morphology: (a) Uncoated; (b) H1 coating; (c) S1 coating; (d) N coating.

MPD visualized 3D morphology: (a) Uncoated; (b) H1 coating; (c) S1 coating; (d) N coating.

As shown in Fig. 7, the MPD visualization images of the uncoated specimens predominantly display blue and purple hues, while the H1, S1, and N coatings display blue, green, and red tones, respectively. Compared to the uncoated specimens, the proportion of red and green areas in the MPD visualization images increases significantly after coating application. This change visually reflects the enhanced MPD values resulting from the application of the coatings. Furthermore, compared to the S1 and N coatings, the H1 coating exhibits the most extensive distribution of red areas. The red coverage of the S1 coating also exceeds that of the N coating, indicating that the H1 coating possesses the highest MPD value, while the N coating has the lowest. To more precisely quantify the MPD differences among the coatings, the mean MPD values for each coating type were calculated, and the results are presented in Fig. 8.

Fig. 8MPD values of different formulation coatings.

MPD values of different formulation coatings.

As shown in Fig. 8, the average MPD of the uncoated specimen was 0.235 mm, while the average MPD values for the H, S, and N-coated specimens were 0.433, 0.376, and 0.312 mm, representing increases of approximately 84.3, 60.0, and 32.8%, respectively. This demonstrates that coating technology can effectively enhance MPD. Furthermore, the type of liquid component plays a significant role in surface texture reconstruction. Compared to the S and N coatings, the H coating exhibited an average MPD approximately 15.2 and 38.8% higher, respectively. This difference may be attributed to the liquid components used in the H coating formulation. The primary components of the liquid phase in the H coating include high-purity styrene emulsion, acrylic emulsion, and silica sol. These components exhibit excellent adhesion and film-forming properties, facilitating the formation of a uniform film during the coating curing process27. Additionally, the high-purity acrylic emulsion and silica sol enhance the surface structure of the coating, promoting texture reconstruction and significantly increasing the MPD value. Overall, coating technology demonstrates effective performance in reconstructing surface texture to improve anti-skid properties.

(2)Slope Spectral Density.

Regarding the correlation between texture wavelength and skid resistance, it is widely recognized that wavelengths below 1 mm are positively correlated with skid resistance, with a correlation coefficient as high as 80%. The influence of wavelengths between 1 and 1.25 mm on skid resistance decreases, while wavelengths between 1.25 and 2.5 mm exhibit a negative correlation with skid resistance28. The wavelength corresponding to the peak of the SSD curve is typically considered the characteristic wavelength of the texture structure within the scanned area20. The SSD distribution curves for different coatings are shown in Fig. 9.

Fig. 9SSD distribution curves of different formulation coatings.

SSD distribution curves of different formulation coatings.

As shown in Fig. 9, the characteristic wavelength of the uncoated surface is 1.78 mm, while those of the H, S, and N coatings are 0.53 mm, 0.56 mm, and 0.63 mm, respectively. It is evident that the uncoated surface exhibits a characteristic wavelength exceeding 1.25 mm, whereas the SSD peaks of the coated surfaces cluster around 0.5 mm. The underlying reason is that the coatings may form multiple micro-protrusions on the concrete surface, enriching the microscopic texture structure and creating a positive texture effect. Compared to negative textures, positive textures effectively enhance slip resistance29. Particularly on wet surfaces, coarser textures can break through the surface water film, promoting contact between the pavement and tires to generate friction and improve slip resistance30. This finding provides an indirect texture-based explanation for how coatings improve concrete surface slip resistance. In summary, if slip resistance is the sole consideration, H coating should be prioritized. Furthermore, the concrete surfaces in this study were roughened, inherently possessing favorable texture structures. If the post-wear pavement is used as the baseline, the improvement effect of coating technology becomes even more pronounced.

BPN results and analysis

BPN is widely used to evaluate the skid resistance of road surfaces. The average BPN values measured at five locations for each coating type are shown in Fig. 10a. To further quantify the relationship between surface macrotexture and skid resistance, a linear regression analysis was conducted using paired individual MPD and BPN measurements obtained from the same test locations, and the results are shown in Fig. 10b.

Fig. 10BPN results and MPD–BPN correlation: (a) BPN values; (b) MPD–BPN correlation.

BPN results and MPD–BPN correlation: (a) BPN values; (b) MPD–BPN correlation.

As illustrated in Fig. 10a, the average BPN of the uncoated specimen was 61.40, whereas the average BPN values of the H, S, and N coated specimens increased to 85.65, 80.91, and 74.62, respectively. Compared with the uncoated specimen, the corresponding increases were 39.5, 31.8, and 21.5%, respectively. Among the coated specimens, the H coating exhibited the highest BPN, followed by the S coating, while the N coating showed the lowest value. This improvement may be attributed to the reconstruction of surface texture by the coating materials. In particular, the effective combination of the styrene-acrylic copolymer emulsion and solid particles in the H coating contributed to increased surface roughness, thereby improving skid resistance.

As shown in Fig. 10b, MPD and BPN exhibited a significant positive linear correlation based on all paired individual measurements (R2 = 0.959, n = 40, p<0.01). This result indicates that the increase in surface macrotexture depth was closely associated with the improvement in skid resistance. Therefore, optimizing coating formulations to reconstruct surface texture provides an effective approach for enhancing the skid resistance of cement concrete pavement surfaces.

Freeze-thaw resistance testing was conducted to evaluate the protective durability of the coatings on concrete. After 100 freeze-thaw cycles, the surface damage states and coating retention rates of different specimens are presented in Table 2. To quantitatively assess the protective effect of the coatings, the relative dynamic modulus of elasticity of the specimens was calculated, and the results are shown in Fig. 11.

Surface damage state and coating retention rate.

As shown in Table 2, all coating formulations, including H1, H2, S1, S2, S3, S4, and N, were subjected to 100 freeze-thaw cycles. Clear differences in freeze-thaw resistance were observed among the different coatings. The S-series coatings exhibited the best overall coating retention performance. Among them, S1 and S2 showed the highest retention rates, reaching 96% and 98%, respectively, indicating that the water-based epoxy system effectively reduced freeze-thaw-induced surface damage. S3 and S4 also maintained relatively high retention rates of 87 and 89%, respectively, although slightly lower than those of S1 and S2. In comparison, H1 and H2 showed moderate retention rates of 59% and 58%, respectively, accompanied by visible surface peeling. The N coating showed the lowest coating retention rate of 25%, indicating that the coating without an effective polymer liquid binder had limited resistance to freeze-thaw damage.

Fig. 11Relative dynamic modulus of elasticity of different coating types.

Relative dynamic modulus of elasticity of different coating types.

As shown in Fig. 11, the relative dynamic modulus of elasticity decreased with increasing freeze-thaw cycles for all specimens. The uncoated specimen (M) deteriorated most rapidly, decreasing to approximately 65% after 100 cycles, whereas the coated specimens maintained higher values. This indicates that the coatings improved the freeze-thaw durability of concrete, owing to their barrier effect and the enhanced toughness and low-temperature resistance provided by silane and rubber powder31. Among the coated specimens, the S-series coatings performed best, retaining relative dynamic moduli of approximately 88–89% after 100 cycles. This superior performance may be associated with the water-based epoxy system, which may have formed a relatively denser surface layer and improved waterproofing and interfacial bonding, thereby reducing water ingress and freeze-thaw-induced internal damage. The H-series coatings showed moderate performance, with final relative dynamic moduli of approximately 78–79%, while the N coating decreased to approximately 72%. This difference may be attributed to the weaker interfacial compactness and water resistance of the H-series coatings compared with the S-series coatings, as well as the absence of an effective polymer binder in the N coating. It should be noted that the coating retention rate and relative dynamic modulus reflect different aspects of freeze-thaw durability. The former mainly characterizes the surface integrity of the coating layer, whereas the latter represents the internal stiffness degradation of the whole specimen. Therefore, although S1 and S2 showed higher coating retention rates, the highest relative dynamic modulus observed for S3 indicates that it was more effective in mitigating internal freeze-thaw damage. Overall, the S-series coatings provided the most effective freeze-thaw protection among the tested formulations.

Conclusions

The bonding performance at the coating-concrete interface was influenced by both coating thickness and material type. Among the three coating systems, namely styrene-acrylic copolymer emulsion (H), water-based epoxy (S), and pure water (N), the S coating exhibited the highest pull-off force and bond flexural strength, while the N coating showed the lowest bonding performance. Within the tested thickness range, the pull-off force reached its maximum at a coating thickness of 3 mm.The coatings effectively reconstructed the surface texture and improved skid resistance. Compared with the uncoated surface, the MPD values of the H, S, and N coatings increased by 84.3, 60.0, and 32.8%, respectively, while the BPN values increased by 39.5, 31.8, and 21.5%, respectively. The characteristic wavelength shifted from 1.78 mm to approximately 0.5–0.6 mm after coating application. A significant positive correlation was observed between MPD and BPN (R2 = 0.959, n = 40, p < 0.01), indicating that texture reconstruction contributed to skid resistance enhancement.The coatings improved the freeze-thaw resistance of concrete. After 100 freeze-thaw cycles, the uncoated specimen showed severe surface damage, whereas the coated specimens maintained better integrity. The S-series coatings showed the best overall freeze-thaw performance, with S1 and S2 achieving the highest coating retention rates of 96% and 98%, respectively. In addition, S3 maintained the highest relative dynamic modulus of approximately 89%, indicating that the S coatings provided the most effective freeze-thaw protection.These results indicate that different coating systems exhibited different performance advantages and should be selected according to specific repair objectives. The H coating was more suitable for surface texture reconstruction and skid-resistance enhancement, whereas the S coating was more suitable for applications requiring higher interfacial bonding and freeze-thaw durability. Overall, the proposed coating systems show potential for rapid surface repair of cement concrete pavements.Based on the current laboratory results, the proposed coating systems are mainly intended for rapid surface repair of apron and taxiway cement concrete pavements, whereas their applicability to runway areas with more demanding service conditions still requires further verification. This study still has several limitations. First, the results were mainly obtained from laboratory tests, and field validation under actual airport pavement service conditions has not yet been conducted. Second, the 3 mm coating thickness was selected mainly based on the bond strength results within the tested thickness range, while the effects of coating thickness on skid resistance, freeze-thaw durability, and long-term service performance were not systematically investigated. Third, the freeze-thaw evaluation was limited to 100 cycles. In addition, several service-related properties were not systematically evaluated, including abrasion resistance, high-temperature stability, UV/oxidative aging resistance, fuel resistance, repeated rubber removal effects, jet blast resistance, and aircraft braking-induced shear resistance.
