Recommendations for pre-analytical urine sample handling to allow optimal assessment of albuminuria and proteinuria
Authors: Stefan Ridderbos, Dominique van Mil, Bengt Fellström, Barbara S Gillespie, William G Herrington, Lilio Hu, Gaetano La Manna, Maaike Mepschen, W Greg Miller, Mauro Panteghini, Michele Provenzano, Jesse C Seegmiller, Joseph A Vassalotti, Hiddo J L Heerspink, Ron T Gansevoort
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlbuminuria and proteinuria are key biomarkers for diagnosing, staging, and monitoring of chronic kidney disease. Both are also strongly associated with renal and cardiovascular risk. Given their important diagnostic role, reliable measurement of urinary albumin and protein is essential for clinical practice and trials. However, variability in the methodology presently used to collect, store, and prepare urine samples may influence assay performance and contribute to inconsistent results. In response to a call for harmonization, the United States of America National Kidney Foundation organized a workshop in April 2025 to define optimal methods and practices for measuring albuminuria and proteinuria. As part of this initiative, the available evidence on pre-analytical factors affecting urinary albumin and protein measurements was discussed.For this purpose, a narrative literature review was conducted to identify relevant studies focusing on urine collection strategies, including 24-h versus single-void samples, timing of sample collection, the need for repeated measurements, clinical conditions that may cause false-positive results, and the effects of sample storage and handling under various temperature conditions. Given heterogeneity in study designs and outcome measures, findings were synthesized qualitatively.This consensus report summarizes the findings of this literature review and the resulting discussion. It highlights the importance of standardized urine collection and handling procedures to minimize biological and analytical variability. In addition, it provides practical recommendations.

INTRODUCTION

Plasma proteins, of which albumin is the most abundant, are usually not present in urine in more than trace amounts in healthy individuals. Due to their large molecular size and net negative electric charge at physiological pH, only a small fraction of plasma proteins are filtered by the glomeruli into the pre-urine that enters the renal tubules. Of the proteins that are filtered, most are then reabsorbed in the proximal tubules by receptor-mediated endocytosis, a process that has a maximal capacity of around 2 g per day [1]. In general, increased urinary protein concentrations (UPCs) indicate glomerular damage when more protein is lost in pre-urine than can be reabsorbed [2]. Increased urinary protein loss may reflect specific kidney diseases, intraglomerular hypertension, or systemic vascular damage, e.g. in case of diabetic or hypertensive kidney disease. During the past three decades, it has been shown that increased urinary protein excretion (UPE) is strongly associated with kidney and cardiovascular prognosis in cohorts of patients with specific kidney diseases, patients with diabetes or hypertension, as well as in the general population [3]. Moreover, medication–induced reductions in urinary albumin or protein excretion are generally predictive of positive long-term outcomes [4–7]. These characteristics have made measurements of albuminuria and proteinuria essential for accurate diagnosis, staging, and monitoring of kidney disease in clinical practice [8].

In April 2025, the National Kidney Foundation conducted a workshop to define optimal methods and practices for measuring albuminuria and proteinuria for clinical practice and trials, in response to a call for harmonization. The workshop participants agreed that, as part of this effort, the optimal pre-analytical handling of urine samples for albumin and protein measurement should be described. This report summarizes the literature discussed during the workshop, critically reviewing the current evidence on pre–analytical factors that determine assay performance and identifying knowledge gaps that require further investigation.

METHODOLOGY

This focussed meeting report adopted a systematic narrative approach to identify, evaluate, and synthesize the existing literature relevant to the various clinical questions that are discussed. A comprehensive literature search was conducted to retrieve publications from January 2000 to November 2025. The electronic database PubMed was systematically searched using keywords ‘albuminuria’, ‘proteinuria’, ‘urinary albumin excretion’, or ‘urinary protein excretion’, combined with either ‘febrile illness’, ‘fever’, ‘urinary tract infection’, ‘bacteriuria’, ‘exercise’, ‘physical exertion’, ‘menstruation’, ‘hematuria’, ‘haematuria’, ‘spot urine sample’, ‘first morning void’, ‘early morning void’, or ‘storage’.

This broad search strategy aimed to ensure adequate coverage of the various topics, while maintaining relevance to the study objectives. Titles and, when deemed appropriate, abstracts of the retrieved records were screened. When eligible, full-text assessment of potentially relevant articles followed and cross-references from included studies were examined to identify additional relevant literature. Studies were included if they addressed the core concepts of the review, were published in peer-reviewed journals, and added novelty or had a comprehensive, conclusive character.

Given the heterogeneity of study designs, methodologies, and outcome measures across the included literature, a narrative synthesis was deemed most appropriate. The findings were therefore summarized qualitatively, with emphasis on identifying key themes, trends, methodological approaches, and gaps in the existing body of research.

How should a urine sample be taken/collected for measurement of urinary albumin and protein?

How does a 24-h urine sample compare to single-void urine samples?

Excretion of protein and albumin in urine follows a circadian rhythm, resulting in fluctuations throughout the day [9]. Due to this variability, the gold standard for assessing urinary albumin excretion (UAE) and UPE is measuring the total excretion of albumin or protein over a 24-h period (expressed in mg/24 h) [10]. Additionally, a 24-h sample accounts for hydration status and consequent urinary dilution/concentration, which cannot be fully taken into account when measuring just urinary albumin concentration (UAC) or UPC in a single urine void. Moreover, a 24-h urine sample provides important information about dietary intake of sodium, potassium, and protein. Despite its value, collecting a 24-h sample is often cumbersome for patients and prone to incomplete collection. For instance, a study by Miler and Šimundić reported that more than half of patients who received detailed instructions on how to collect a 24-h urine sample failed to do so properly: some volumes were discarded, or improper containers were used [11]. Recognising these limitations, the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2021 guideline for glomerular diseases indicates that, if an intended 24–hour urine collection for UPE or UAE cannot be completed reliably, a reasonable compromise is to assess the urinary protein to creatinine ratio or urinary albumin to creatinine ratio in an aliquot of the collected urine [12].

The inconvenience and unreliability of collecting complete 24-h samples prompted the adoption of a surrogate method involving measuring UAC or UPC in single-void urine samples (Table 1). Single-void urine samples can be collected as a first–morning void (FMV), an early–morning void (EMV), or a random spot sample. Although more convenient, these measurements are influenced by hydration status and subsequent urine dilution and concentration, as well as by circadian variation of protein excretion.

In contrast to urinary protein and albumin excretion, the excretion of creatinine is relatively stable over time [9]. Therefore, if UAC and UPC are expressed as a ratio to urinary creatinine concentration, the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR; expressed in mg/mmol or mg/g) and the urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR; mg/mmol or mg/g) show good correlation with UAE and UPE [13, 14]. Notwithstanding, variability between the various methods of single–void urine samplings exists, which is discussed in next section.

Since muscle mass is the main determinant of urinary creatinine excretion, urinary creatinine concentration is generally higher in males than females, increases at higher body mass index, and decreases with ageing [15]. Based on these data, age, sex, and body mass index-specific UACR thresholds have been proposed [15]. These proposals have not been adopted in the KDIGO guidelines. In contrast, these guidelines recommend unified UACR thresholds, recognizing that although this may introduce some bias, a unified definition will facilitate widespread adoption to screen and monitor for albuminuria and foster clinical implementation. This recommendation is supported by studies from the general population and patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD) demonstrating that the associations of UACR from spot urine samples with risk for various outcomes are essentially similar in age, sex, or body weight-specific subgroups and similar compared to 24-h UAE [16].

Recommendation 1.1: When albuminuria or proteinuria cannot be reliably measured over 24 h, urinary protein and albumin concentration should be measured in a single-void urine sample and expressed as a ratio to the urinary creatinine concentration (mg/mmol) to adjust for urine concentration/dilution.

Should measurements of UACR/UPCR be done in a spot, an EMV, or an FMV urine sample to replace measurement of UAE/UPE in a 24-h urine sample?

Random spot urine sampling is considered convenient as it can be collected at any time without preparation. However, convenience compromises accuracy because UACR measured in random spot urine samples shows higher intra-individual variability and consequently a lower agreement with 24-h UAE compared to UACR measurement in an FMV [10, 17]. Thus, their higher variability limits the reliability of spot samples in clinical use.

In a study by Witte et al. that included 241 subjects with early stages of CKD, albuminuria assessed in FMV and spot urine samples was compared with values from 24-h urine collections [10]. The prevalence of moderately increased albuminuria based on an FMV measurement (7.5%, measured as UAC and UACR) matched that determined by 24-h UAE (10.0%) better than when measured in a random spot urine sample (25.4% for UAC and 22.4% for UACR). In the overall cohort, the intra-individual coefficient of variation of UACR in an FMV (19.1%) did not differ from that of 24–h UAE (19.0%; P = .64). Regression analysis demonstrated that the UACR in FMV showed a better agreement with 24-h UAE than UACR in spot urine samples. This suggests that FMV offers a more consistent estimate of 24 -h albuminuria compared with random spot samples, particularly when expressed as UACR [10, 18].

The accuracy of UACR measured in an FMV to reflect 24–h UAE has been evaluated, and FMV UACR has also been compared with 24–h UAE to assess its predictive value for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. In a study including 3414 subjects representing the general population in Groningen (the Netherlands), UACR measured in FMV predicted cardiovascular morbidity and mortality with similar performance as 24-h UAE [19]. Another study including 701 patients with type 2 diabetes and nephropathy concluded that FMV UACR not only matches but may even outperform 24-h UAE in predicting kidney disease progression [20]. The authors proposed two explanations for this finding. First, the UACR incorporates urinary creatinine excretion, which reflects muscle mass. Muscle mass could subsequently reflect overall health status and may therefore add prognostic information beyond albuminuria alone. Second, errors or incomplete 24–h urine collections may reduce the predictive accuracy of 24–h UAE compared with UACR measurements in an FMV [20].

In addition to an FMV, collecting an EMV for measuring UACR may be an acceptable and more convenient alternative for estimating 24-h UAE [21]. Two studies using EMV samples reported within–subject biological variability of UACR comparable to that of 24–h UAE and UACR measured in FMVs [22, 23]. Another study of 31 people with UAC <18 mg/L did not differ in expected biological or analytical variability between UACR in EMV versus 24-h collections [22]. However, the predictive performance of UACR in an EMV compared with a 24-h UAE requires further formal assessment. Evidence for UPCR measured in FMV or EMV as an alternative for UPE in 24-h urine collections is more limited than for UACR. However, several studies demonstrated correlation between FMV/EMV UPCR and 24–h protein excretion [24–26], but to our knowledge no studies directly compared intrapatient day-to-day variation or predictive performance of FMV/EMV UPCR with 24-h UPCR or UPE. More research is therefore needed to clarify whether FMV UPCR provides similar reliability and prognostic value to UPE.

Recommendation 1.2.1: Urine albumin to creatinine ratio measured in a first morning void urine sample can replace measuring UAE in a 24-h or timed urine collection because it has low intrapatient day-to-day variation and good diagnostic agreement with 24-h UAE.

How many samples should be taken to estimate albuminuria concentrations?

A single measurement is insufficient for an accurate evaluation of albuminuria, especially when it concerns moderately increased albuminuria. Transiently increased albuminuria, combined with relatively high intra-individual variation in the amount of albuminuria, requires multiple measurements to avoid false-positive results and to allow accurate categorization of albuminuria status [17, 21, 27]. Analysis of 5247 participants in the 2009–2010 US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey showed that only 43.5% of adults with moderately increased albuminuria in an initial random urine sample also had elevated UACR in an FMV collected within 10 days. This finding highlights the greater variability and higher likelihood of false–positive results when relying on random spot urine samples and supports the recommendation to use an FMV for confirmation [28, 29]. It is generally agreed that an abnormal UACR result should therefore be confirmed by at least one additional FMV sample. Only in case the confirmation test shows a normal value, a third and decisive test is advised. To establish a diagnosis of CKD, confirmation of increased albuminuria should by definition be performed after at least 3 months to confirm chronicity [30].

From a clinical perspective it is acceptable that spot urine samples are used for screening for increased albuminuria. Albuminuria in these samples has higher variability, but in general albuminuria is overestimated. Therefore, while the chance of false-negative results is small any abnormal values should be confirmed preferably using an FMV. In an analysis of three randomized clinical trials that tested various interventions in patients with type 2 diabetes and nephropathy (AVOID, VITAL, and RADAR), Kröpelin et al. demonstrated that increased sampling frequency improved the precision, but not the magnitude of estimated albuminuria–lowering treatment effects [31–34]. Averaging two to three consecutive FMV samples reduced the standard error of the treatment effect, while the estimated mean remained almost unchanged compared to using a single sample. Simulation analyses in the same study showed that increasing the number of consecutive urine collections beyond three yielded no appreciable gain in precision [31]. Incorporating albuminuria measurements from all follow–up visits, instead of relying only on baseline and end–of–treatment values, produced a four– to six–fold reduction in required sample size to detect a 30% albuminuria–lowering effect with 80% power, again without altering the estimated treatment effect. These data support averaging the UACR results of at least two, and preferably three, urine samples and using repeated assessments over time instead of a single urine sample in albuminuria trials to increase statistical precision [31]. Although not formally studied, it is likely that this will also hold true for clinical practice.

Recommendation 1.3.1: For clinical use, a single increased urine albumin to creatinine ratio result should be confirmed by a second sample, preferably a first morning void, to establish a diagnosis of increased albuminuria.

Are there circumstances that may lead to false-positive UACR/UPCR results?

Should the presence of urinary tract infection or febrile illness be considered before measuring UACR/UPCR?

A comprehensive systematic review by Carter et al. examined whether urinary tract infections (UTIs) can lead to increased proteinuria and albuminuria, potentially resulting in false-positive diagnoses of CKD [35]. This review indicated that symptomatic UTIs can indeed increase both proteinuria and albuminuria, with levels decreasing following antibacterial treatment [35]. Non-UTI febrile illnesses are also reported to cause transient elevations in UPE, especially of tubular origin, and albumin excretion [36]. UACR/UPCR measurements should therefore be avoided in the presence of symptomatic UTI or febrile illness.

In contrast, asymptomatic UTIs have not consistently been associated with elevated UAC and UPC [37, 38]. An in vitro study, in which urine samples contaminated with >107 bacteria per litre were stored for 14 days at room temperature, did not show a difference in UAC compared with samples that were presterilized by microfiltration [39]. In another study, which included 81 patients with diabetes who had urine screened for UACR at two occasions <6 months apart, UACR was not different in urine samples that did or did not contain bacteria [40]. In another group of 59 patients with diabetes and asymptomatic UTI, UACR was not different before versus after antimicrobial treatment [38]. Of note, there are also some studies that did suggest an association between asymptomatic UTI and proteinuria [41, 42]. However, as Carter et al. argued, there is no evidence to suggest a causal link [35]. Based on a thorough review of the available evidence, they concluded it is likely that individuals with asymptomatic UTI and increased albuminuria or proteinuria may have underlying diseases such as diabetes or nephropathy that are independently associated with an increased risk for both common clinical events.

Recommendation 2.1.1: Refrain from sampling urine for measurement of urine albumin to creatinine ratio or urine protein to creatinine ration in the setting of symptomatic UTIs or other febrile illnesses until these are resolved to avoid falsely elevated results.

Recommendation 2.1.2: Asymptomatic UTIs do not in general cause significant changes in urine albumin to creatinine ratio, so routine screening for bacteriuria or leukocyturia to exclude an asymptomatic UTI when measuring urine albumin to creatinine ratio is not needed.

Should strenuous exercise or dietary exposures be avoided prior to measuring UACR/urine protein to creatinine ration (UPCR)?

Strenuous physical activity, defined by high-intensity training like treadmill exercise to maximal heart rate or exhaustion rather than lower-intensity exercise of prolonged duration, has been found to temporarily increase UAC and UPC in healthy individuals [43, 44]. Exercise–induced proteinuria is thought to result from a combination of transient increases in glomerular permeability and limited proximal tubular handling of the increased filtered protein load [45]. Metabolic disturbances such as lactic acidosis and organic acid accumulation may further impair glomerular and tubular function, contributing to increased proteinuria [46]. The reported duration of transient exercise-induced proteinuria and albuminuria varies across studies. In general, however, the rise in urinary albumin is only of short duration and returns to pre-exercise levels within 24 h [43]. A 24-h rest period after strenuous exercise is therefore recommended to minimize false-positive results of UACR and UPCR measurements.

Dietary factors can also influence UACR and UPCR. For instance, lowering dietary intake of sodium reduces albuminuria, with the greatest reductions occurring in individuals treated with renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors [47, 48]. Similarly, reducing protein intake lowers UPE additive to (but not potentiating) the proteinuria-lowering effect of RAAS inhibitors [49, 50]. Therefore, when unexpected changes in albuminuria or proteinuria are observed, changes in dietary intake of protein and especially sodium should be taken into account as possible causal factor. Moreover, when collecting urine, individuals should be advised not to change their routine dietary habits.

Recommendation 2.2.1: Refrain from urine sampling for measurement of urine albumin to creatinine ratio or urine protein to creatinine ration for 24 h after strenuous physical activity.

Should measurement of UACR/UPCR be avoided during menstruation or in patients with haematuria?

Current guidelines recommend postponing urine collection for UPCR and UACR measurements in women during menstruation [8]. The rationale behind this recommendation is not that pathophysiological changes may occur during the menstrual cycle, but rather that contamination of urine with menstrual blood could lead to falsely elevated results. The admixture with plasma proteins, combined with the low creatinine concentration in blood relative to urine, may cause an overestimation of UACR and UPCR values that are supposed to reflect glomerular damage, leading to a misclassification of kidney disease and unnecessary further investigations [51, 52]. Although this recommendation is generally shared, systematic studies directly quantifying the impact of menstruation on UACR/UPCR are scarce. The limited evidence that is available, however, does support the recommendation to refrain from UACR and UPCR measurements in women during menstruation [51].

As with menstrual blood contamination, gross haematuria has not been widely studied to determine whether associated elevations in urinary protein or albumin are transient and directly subject to the presence of haematuria. A classic study showed that when blood was added to urine to simulate haematuria, visible haematuria produced marked elevations in UPC, and haemoglobin was identified as the responsible protein, indicating that false-positive proteinuria can result from red blood cell lysis in haematuric samples [53]. While the admixture of plasma protein contamination during gross haematuria might result in overestimation of UPCR, there is less evidence for the effects on UACR. Haematuria has been found to cause false-positive results on spot albumin tests, especially when using dipstick or nonspecific assays, and less when using quantitative laboratory methods [54]. Notwithstanding, it seems prudent to avoid measurement of UACR in urine samples with visible haematuria, because of the theoretical chance of falsely increased results, especially when UACR is in the lower range [53, 54].

Recommendations 2.3: Refrain from urine sampling for measurement of urine albumin to creatinine ratio or urine protein to creatinine ration in women during menstruation and in individuals with visible haematuria.

How should a urine sample be stored before UACR/UPCR assessment?

Does storage of urine samples at maximal ambient temperatures (30°C) influence UACR or UPCR results?

Herrington et al. investigated the influence of storing urinary albumin samples at six different temperatures for varying time periods, with or without boric acid as a preservative [55]. These authors demonstrated that UAC measured in urine samples stored at 30°C increased gradually over time with little difference between samples with or without the preservative added. The upper limit of the 95% confidence interval (CI) exceeded the predefined restrictive ±5% stability threshold after 2 days with no preservatives added and after 4 days with preservatives added [55]. Urinary creatinine measurements showed no increase in average value, although a slight increase in variability was observed with longer storage. The increase in UAC over time was suggested to be caused by bacterial contamination of a small number of samples, in which hydrolysis by bacterial proteases increased the apparent UAC. The fact that the increase in UAC occurred similarly in samples with and without preservative, however, argues against this explanation.

Similar findings have been reported by Sompark et al., who recommended limiting storage at 30°C to a maximum of 2 days to prevent clinically significant deviations in UACR [56]. In their study, adding a preservative helped to prevent an increase in UAC beyond this period of 2 days. For UPCR, the effects of delayed processing after storage at higher temperatures have not been systematically investigated.

Recommendation 3.1: When urine samples are stored at the maximum ambient temperature of 30°C, they should be processed within 2 days from collection to obtain reliable urine albumin to creatinine ratio results when no preservative is added. If a preservative is added they should be processed within 4 days from collection.

Does storage of urine samples at ambient temperature (18°C–20°C) influence UACR or UPCR results?

Some studies have shown that UAC remains stable for up to 7 days when stored at room or ambient temperatures of 18°C–20°C, reporting no significant decrease nor increase in UAC compared with UAC measured in fresh samples, regardless of whether preservatives were used [56, 57]. In the more recent study by Herrington et al., the use of preservatives was recommended to prevent bacterial growth when urine was stored at ambient temperature to preserve albumin and creatinine concentration [55]. In this study, when boric acid was added as a preservative UACR changes during 7 days of storage at 18°C remained within the ±5% stability threshold of the study. Samples without preservative showed a mean percentage change of +2.7% (95% CI: −2.0% to +5.4%) at day 7. Although the upper bound slightly exceeded the predefined criteria to indicate stability, statistical testing for equality revealed no significant difference in UACR between the groups with and without a preservative added. With a preservative, the increase in UACR was +2.1% (95% CI: 0.3%–3.8%). Graphs in the Supplementary material show that the higher upper bound at day 7 was caused by two outlier results, but that these outliers were already present at day 1 with similar values that did not increase further with longer storage. This suggests that adding a preservative did not particularly mitigate UAC instability, but rather that these results may have been a chance finding. In line with this latter assumption are the aforementioned findings that asymptomatic UTI does not influence UACR results and that urine samples with more than 107 bacteria per litre can be stored for 14 days at room temperature without a change in UACR when compared to the same urine samples that were sterilized [40].

Therefore, while it has been recommended to add a preservative when urine is to be stored for a longer period at 18°C–20°C, there is limited evidence for this, with additional research warranted. Again, no articles related to UPCR were found that studied the effect of delayed processing after storage at 18°C–20°C of human urine samples.

Recommendation 3.2: When urine samples are stored at ambient temperatures of 18°C–20°C, urine albumin to creatinine ratio can be measured reliably for up to 7 days of storage. Further research is needed to determine definitively whether adding a preservative is necessary to obtain reliable results.

Recommendation 3.2: When urine samples are stored at ambient temperatures of 18°C–20°C, urine albumin to creatinine ratio can be measured reliably for up to 7 days of storage. Further research is needed to determine definitively whether adding a preservative is necessary to obtain reliable results.

Does storage of urine samples in a refrigerator (4°C–6°C) influence UACR or UPCR results?

Short-term storage at refrigerated temperatures is considered an acceptable option to maintain sample integrity when immediate analysis of UACR or UPCR is not possible. While urinary creatinine remains stable up to 28 days when refrigerated, urinary albumin has been shown to remain stable for at least 7 days when refrigerated or shipped under refrigerated conditions without preservatives [21, 56, 57]. The study by Herrington et al. with 102 urine samples with moderately to severely increased albuminuria likewise reported no significant change in UACR at 4°C for up to 7 days, with mean percentage changes that remained well within the predefined 5% stability threshold, and no evidence that adding preservatives provided additional stability under refrigerated conditions [55]. The stability of UPCR in human urine under refrigerated conditions has not been reported in the literature. However, the current KDIGO CKD guideline mentions to apply similar conditions for urine storage to UPCR measurement as for UACR measurement [8].

Recommendation 3.3: When urine samples are stored in a refrigerator (4°C–6°C), reliable measurements can be obtained for urine albumin to creatinine ratio for up to 7 days of storage with or without adding a preservative.

Recommendation 3.3: When urine samples are stored in a refrigerator (4°C–6°C), reliable measurements can be obtained for urine albumin to creatinine ratio for up to 7 days of storage with or without adding a preservative.

Does storage of urine samples in a standard lab freezer (−20°C) influence UACR or UPCR results?

Several studies have demonstrated that storage at −20°C can result in clinically relevant underestimations of UAC when urine samples are stored for long periods of time, especially in samples with slight or moderate elevations in albumin concentrations [58–61]. A significant decrease in UAC was noted after 1 month of storage at −20°C while urinary creatinine remained stable after 6 months of storage at −20°C [55]. Brinkman et al. investigated whether poststorage handling could improve UAC results. They thawed the stored samples to room temperature, then mixed them prior to analysis to prevent aggregation or sediment formation of albumin in the tube influencing results [58]. They showed that the mixed samples maintained the original UAC values for up to 5 months, whereas unmixed samples showed a significant decrease. However, mixing could not prevent an increase in variability of UAC results at month 2 of storage at −20°C, nor an average decrease in UAC beyond month 2. These results indicate that post-storage mixing may improve medium-term UAC stability, but also that such a procedure cannot prevent an increase in variability and cannot extend storage time beyond two months. Studies describing the effects of storage in a standard lab freezer for UPCR in human urine were not found.

Recommendation 3.4: Urine samples should not be stored at −20 °C for prolonged periods for the measurement of urine albumin concentration (urine albumin concentration).

Does storage of urine samples in an ultralow temperature freezer (≤−70°C) influence UACR or UPCR results?

Long-term storage of urine samples at ≤−70°C is considered the best option for preserving sample integrity for UAC testing over extended periods of time [55, 56, 62–64]. One study investigated urinary albumin, protein, and creatinine stability in samples stored at −70°C [65]. Investigators found that both UPCR and UACR levels were fairly stable, with only a minor decrease after ∼2.5 years of storage, whereas urinary creatinine concentration remained completely stable. The observed minor reductions in UACR and UPCR did not substantially affect kidney disease classification and results remained within usual laboratory variability. Additionally, UACR remained stable even after three freeze–thaw cycles during a 6-month storage period, indicating that repeated thawing does not significantly impact measurement accuracy [55]. In all these studies, samples were mixed after thawing and before assessment which is routine recommended laboratory practice.

Differences were reported regarding the detection method for UAC measurement. Brinkman et al. demonstrated that UAC results measured by immunonephelometry were less affected by storage at −80°C than results measured by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), which showed a considerable decrease in UAC [66]. The authors hypothesized that HPLC may detect additional forms of albumin, including immunochemically nonreactive albumin, which could be more susceptible to loss during prolonged frozen storage [66, 67]. Because of these considerations and because of the complexity of this measurement technique and associated cost, assessment of urinary albumin by HPLC is no longer in use and has been replaced by immunoassays.

Recommendation 3.5: When urine samples are stored at ≤−70°C, reliable results can be obtained for urine albumin to creatinine ratio and urine protein to creatinine ration for at least 2.5 years. Mixing after sample thawing is required.

Is it necessary to use pre-analytical sample stabilization methods to accurately measure UACR/UPCR after prolonged storage?

Early studies suggested that alkalinization can be used to preserve albumin stability in urine when stored frozen [39, 68]. The proposed mechanisms for improved stability include reduced albumin aggregation at higher pH due to its protein isoelectric point of 4.7, which minimizes entrapment in precipitates and enhances antibody binding during immunochemical assays, as well as preventing conformational changes caused by frozen storage [64]. Additionally, alkalinization may inhibit protease activity found at lower urinary pH and prevent degradation of albumin [62]. The influence of pH was definitively demonstrated by Brinkman et al., who reported that UAC changes after 12 months of storage at −20°C were greater in samples with pH <5 compared to those with a pH of 8 [69]. Subsequently, Heerspink et al. demonstrated in 90 patient samples that alkalinization of urine samples to a pH of 8 before storing urine at −20°C for up to 12 months is a feasible and practical strategy to get reliable UAC results in urine samples with moderately to severely increased albuminuria [64]. Alkalinization of urine samples prior to storage at −20°C led to comparable results when samples were stored at −80°C for 12 months in patients with diabetes mellitus [64].

Alkalinization is not needed for samples stored at −80°C. No significant difference in UAC was found between alkalized and untreated samples [64]. This was later confirmed in another study, showing that alkalinization did not influence UACR stability when urine was stored at −80°C for up to 12 months [70].

Other pre-analytical sample treatments were found not to improve storage stability at −20°C and ≤−70°C. At both temperatures, boric acid used as preservative or the use of low-protein binding cups (special uncoated cups that minimize protein sticking) did not prevent urinary albumin loss that was found in untreated samples after 6 months at −20°C [55, 70]. The pre-analytical handling for sample storage has not been specifically studied for UPCR.

Recommendation 3.6.1: Alkalinization to pH 8 enables storage of urine samples at −20°C up to 12 months, with average urine albumin concentration remaining stable but with an increase in variability of individual urine albumin concentration results.

Recommendation 3.6.2: Alkalinization of urine samples or adding a preservative is not necessary to obtain reliable results for urine albumin to creatinine ratio when urine is stored at ≤−70°C.

Is it necessary to use poststorage sample handling methods to preserve the stability of UACR/UPCR results?

Detailed descriptions of poststorage sample handling are often lacking in research articles even though these factors can significantly influence results. Differences in sample handling have been reported as a source of variability between studies [55, 68]. Mixing after thawing has been demonstrated as an important poststorage sample handling step influencing assay results [58]. Changes in the environment, such as temperature, can promote protein–protein interactions, leading to reversible albumin aggregation in urine samples. These aggregates may sediment or remain incompletely solubilized after thawing, leading to an uneven albumin concentration within the sample. Brinkman et al. demonstrated in a study of 1785 samples that those that were thawed after being stored for 3–5 months at −20°C and not mixed before measurement showed larger changes in UAC compared to samples that were mixed by either vortexing or hand inversion (−34.9% vs. −5.3% and −2.6%, respectively; both P < .001) [58]. Another study containing 90 samples also emphasized that thorough mixing of urine samples after thawing, prior to assay measurement, substantially reduced apparent variation in albumin concentration [64]. Together, these findings highlight mixing after thawing as an important posthandling step to rehomogenize urine samples and reduce variation in UAC between samples at all storage temperatures.

Recommendation 3.7: Once thawed, urine samples that have been stored frozen should be mixed thoroughly prior to measurement to obtain optimal results for urine albumin concentration.

RESEARCH GAPS

Although the recommendations for pre-analytical sample handling for optimal measurement of UACR are in general supported by a substantial body of evidence, some uncertainties remain. Further research is needed to formally assess the agreement and the predictive performance of UACR measured in EMV urines samples when compared to UACR in FMV and UAE in 24-h urine samples. In addition, the quantitative impact of specific transient conditions, especially menstruation, has been poorly evaluated. Also, the data regarding the value of adding preservatives to allow optimal assessment of UACR in urine samples stored at ambient temperature remain limited. Furthermore, research is needed to assess how often such misclassification may alter clinical management or outcomes.

In contrast, the evidence defining the pre-analytical sample handling for optimal measurement of UPCR is remarkably sparse. Data on biological variability, optimal sampling strategies, stability under different storage conditions, and poststorage handling are largely lacking. Moreover, no studies have rigorously assessed the agreement of FMV or EMV UPCR with 24-h proteinuria or its prognostic relevance. Addressing these research gaps is essential to enable evidence-based recommendations for the accurate assessment of proteinuria in both clinical practice and research.

CONCLUSIONS

The pre-analytical handling of urine samples for measurement of urinary proteins is in general well defined in literature, with more evidence for UACR than UPCR. Only a few uncertainties remain, e.g. how long urine samples can be kept at room temperature with or without preservatives for accurate assessment of UACR and UPCR. Best practices recommend using an FMV urine sample from individuals who have abstained from exercise for 24 h, are free from symptomatic UTI or febrile illness, are not menstruating and do not have visible haematuria (Table 2). In the absence of an FMV, EMV or random spot urine samples may be used. Samples should be analysed preferably promptly to avoid variability due to storage conditions. In case storage is needed, this can be done for up to 7 days at room temperature or in a refrigerator, and for prolonged storage preferably at ≤−70°C without a need to add preservatives or alkalinization. Prior to analysis, frozen samples should be thoroughly mixed after thawing. Due to intra-individual variability, results showing increased UACR or UPCR should be confirmed. Confirmation over at least 3 months is required to allow a definitive diagnosis of CKD. Adherence to these recommendations for pre-analytical sample handling is important to minimize analytical variability and to support accurate diagnosis, staging, and monitoring of CKD in clinical practice as well as in a research setting.
