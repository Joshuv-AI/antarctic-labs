Stellate ganglion block for attenuating postoperative sore throat after double-lumen tube intubation in thoracic surgery: a randomized clinical study
Authors: Shaowen Zhang, Shaoshuang Wang, Zongzhao He, Yun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFor thoracic surgery patients who undergo double-lumen endotracheal intubation, postoperative sore throat (POST) remains one of the most common postoperative complication.ObjectiveTo evaluate whether preoperative ultrasound-guided stellate ganglion block (SGB) can reduce the incidence and severity of POST, and decrease the postoperative throat VAS score in patients undergoing double-lumen tube (DLT) intubation for thoracic surgery.MethodsThis prospective, randomized, double-blind, controlled clinical trial enrolled 120 patients undergoing elective thoracic surgery with DLT intubation. Participants were randomized into two groups: the SGB group (n = 60) received 2–3 ml of 0.25% ropivacaine under ultrasound guidance on the right stellate ganglion, while the control group (n = 60) received no regional anesthetic intervention prior to anesthesia induction. The primary outcomes included the incidence and severity of POST and the visual analogue scale (VAS) scores for throat pain. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of postoperative hoarseness, cough, and postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs), as well as the levels of serum inflammatory markers before and 1 day after surgery.ResultsThe incidence of POST was significantly lower in the SGB group at 1 h, 6 h, and 24 h postoperatively compared to the control group (10.3%, 13.8%, and 5.2% vs. 44.1%, 35.6%, and 20.3%, respectively, p < 0.05). The severity grading of POST in the SGB group was also lower than that in the control group at all three time points (p < 0.05). Additionally, the throat VAS scores in the SGB group at 1 h and 6 h postoperatively (0.45 ± 1.45 and 0.34 ± 1.05, respectively) were significantly lower than those in the control group (1.88 ± 2.54 and 0.93 ± 1.41, respectively; p < 0.05). Cough incidence was significantly lower in the SGB group at 1 h and 6 h (both 5.2%) than in the control group (both 20.3%, P = 0.014).ConclusionsPreoperative ultrasound-guided SGB significantly alleviates POST and reduces the early postoperative cough incidence in thoracic surgery patients undergoing DLT intubation.Trial registrationThe study was registered on Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, (ChiCTR2500106117) on 17/07/2025.Keywords: Stellate ganglion block, Double-lumen tube, Thoracic surgery, Postoperative sore throat, Enhanced recovery after surgery

Introduction

In recent years, the advancement of pulmonary nodule detection techniques has led to a growing number of patients requiring minimally invasive thoracic surgery. One-lung ventilation (OLV) has consequently become a central component of anesthesia management in thoracoscopic procedures. Among airway management strategies, the double-lumen tube (DLT) is widely regarded as the gold standard due to its reliable lung isolation. However, its application is not without drawbacks. The relatively large outer diameter and curved bronchial lumen of DLTs predispose patients to mechanical trauma during insertion and extubation, resulting in postoperative sore throat (POST), hoarseness, and cough (Knoll et al. 2006). The lateral decubitus position is commonly employed in thoracic surgery. During patient repositioning from supine to lateral decubitus, the double-lumen tube (DLT) inevitably migrates, which induces marked alterations in bronchial cuff pressure and aggravates injury to the airway mucosa (Maruyama et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2021). OLV and the associated surgical manipulations can also trigger complex pathophysiological changes in lung tissue, substantially increasing the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) (Marret et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2011). The combination of POST and PPCs not only reduces patient satisfaction but also prolongs hospital stay, thereby posing a significant challenge to perioperative management and enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) for thoracic surgery patients (Liu et al. 2022; Baar et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2022). However, current preventive measures for POST, such as the topical application of ketamine, magnesium sulfate, and dexamethasone, often yield suboptimal therapeutic effectiveness (Mazzotta et al. 2023).

As a part of the cervical sympathetic chain, the stellate ganglion (SG) is formed by the combination of the inferior cervical ganglion and the first thoracic ganglion. SGB is a specific type of peripheral nerve block technique involving the injection of local anaesthetic drugs into the area surrounding and adjacent to the stellate ganglion. Conventionally, this technique has been primarily used for managing various pain syndromes (Qingyang et al. 2022), due to the established role of the sympathetic nervous system in the initiation and maintenance of chronic pain. In recent years, research on SGB has focused on its postoperative analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects. Several investigations propose that the way SGB alleviates pain may be associated with modifying the sympathetic influence on neuroinflammation controlled by glia. Furthermore, SGB may exert lung-protective effects through multiple mechanisms, including the suppression of inflammatory responses and improvement of pulmonary microcirculation (Chen et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2016; Ückert et al. 2021; Lynch et al. 2021; Jiang et al. 2022). However, there remains a paucity of high-quality clinical data evaluating the efficacy of preoperative SGB in reducing POST among patients undergoing DLT-facilitated OLV.

This prospective, randomized, double-blind clinical trial aims to evaluate the impact of preoperative ultrasound-guided SGB on the incidence and severity of POST in patients undergoing thoracic surgery with DLT intubation. Secondary outcomes include assessment of SGB’s efficacy in reducing postoperative hoarseness, cough, and PPCs. The objective of the findings is to present new clinical insights for applying SGB in the perioperative thoracic surgery patients and within ERAS procedures.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethics

This study was a prospective, randomized, double-blind controlled trial. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qinghai Provincial People's Hospital (Approval No. (2025)−014–03) on 25/06/2025 and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2500106117) on 17/07/2025. This randomized controlled trial adheres to the CONSORT guidelines. The CONSORT checklist is included as supplementary material. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Study population and sample size estimation

Patients scheduled for elective thoracic surgery requiring intraoperative one-lung ventilation (OLV) at Qinghai Provincial People's Hospital between June and September 2025 were screened for inclusion. Based on previous literature (Li et al. 2023), the incidence of postoperative sore throat (POST) was approximately 55.43%. Assuming that stellate ganglion block (SGB) could reduce POST by 50% to 27.72%, with a two-sided α = 0.05 and power (1–β) = 0.8, a sample size of 46 patients per group was calculated using PASS 2021 software. Considering a 20% attrition rate, the sample size was expanded to 60 per group. Participants were randomised using a computer-generated sequence of random numbers. The sequence was generated by an anaesthetist who was not involved in recruitment or outcome assessment, and was placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. When a suitable participant was enrolled, the next envelope in the sequence was opened to reveal the allocation.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: age 18–75 years; BMI 18–28 kg/m2; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III; no history of severe cardiovascular disease or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); elective lobectomy, segmentectomy or wedge resection.

Exclusion criteria: contraindications to SGB (e.g., clotting abnormalities, infection at the puncture location, and allergic reactions to local anesthetics); history of glucocorticoid or NSAID use within one month; upper respiratory tract infection, sore throat, or hoarseness within two weeks preoperatively; severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; preoperative respiratory failure, pneumonia, or other PPCs.

Withdrawal criteria: failed SGB (absence of Horner’s syndrome), serious complications during the study (e.g., local anesthetic toxicity, syncope, high spinal/epidural block), or patient/family request for withdrawal.

Anesthesia and intervention procedures

All patients were instructed to fast for at least 6 h and abstain from drinking for 4 h before surgery. Upon arrival in the operating room, a peripheral intravenous line was established on the non-operative upper limb, and Ringer's lactate solution was administered at a rate of 10 mL/(kg·h). Standard monitoring included electrocardiography (ECG), non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), pulse oximetry (SpO₂), and bispectral index (BIS). After confirming baseline stability, an ultrasound examination of the right cervical region was performed using a high-frequency linear probe to identify an appropriate and safe needle path for stellate ganglion block (SGB).

SGB Group (Interventional Group)

Ten minutes before anesthesia induction, patients received an ultrasound-guided right-sided SGB in the supine position with the head slightly turned to the left and a thin pillow placed under the shoulders. The ultrasound probe was positioned transversely at the level of the cricoid cartilage to visualize the C6 vertebral level. At this level, characteristic anatomical landmarks, including the anterior tubercle of the C6 transverse process, the longus colli muscle, the C6 nerve root, the common carotid artery, and the internal jugular vein, were identified. The stellate ganglion was located between the prevertebral fascia and the surface of the longus colli muscle (Fig. 1) (2022). Under in-plane needle guidance, a 22-G needle was advanced into the potential space between the longus colli and the prevertebral fascia at the C6 or C7 level. After confirming negative aspiration for blood or air, 2–3 mL of 0.25% ropivacaine was injected slowly. The block was considered successful when the patient exhibited at least three signs of Horner's syndrome on the ipsilateral side—miosis, ptosis, enophthalmos, facial flushing, increased facial skin temperature, or conjunctival hyperemia (Zhu et al. 2021).

Control group

Ten minutes prior to induction of anesthesia, the patient’s stellate ganglion was located under ultrasound guidance, and a “non-puncture sham block” was performed: specifically, patients in the control group were placed in the same position, disinfected, and covered with dressings, and a needle-free syringe was used to simulate an injection at the puncture site.The same experienced anesthesiologist performed all SGB and sham procedures. Subsequent anesthesia management and data collection were carried out by an independent anesthesiologist who was unaware of the group assignments.

Standardized general anesthesia protocol

Anesthesia was induced with intravenous midazolam (0.05 mg/kg), propofol (2.0 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.5 μg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) after preoxygenation for 3–5 min. DLT size and positioning were determined based on patient height, weight, surgical site, and CT measurements.

Postoperative analgesia and recovery

Postoperative pain was managed using patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) with a solution of hydromorphone (8 mg), dezocine (10 mg), and ondansetron (8 mg) diluted in 100 ml of saline. PCIA settings: background infusion 2.5 ml/h, bolus 2.5 ml, lockout interval 20 min.

Patients were extubated in the PACU upon full recovery of consciousness, spontaneous breathing, and protective airway reflexes. After 30 min of stable monitoring, they were transferred to the general ward and followed daily until discharge.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes

Postoperative Sore Throat (POST)

Assessed by anaesthetists unaware of patient groupings at 1 h, 6 h, and 24 h post-extubation. A four-grade scale was used to record POST incidence and severity.(Grade 0: no sore throat; Grade 1: mild sore throat, mentioned only when actively asked; Grade 2: moderate sore throat, actively reported but does not affect swallowing; Grade 3: severe sore throat, significantly affecting swallowing or causing dysphagia) (Kuriyama et al. 2020).

Throat Pain Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)

At the same time points as above, instruct the patient to mark their pain level on a 10 cm-long ruler (0 points: no pain; 10 points: severe pain that is difficult to bear).

Secondary outcomes

Hoarseness and cough

Assessed at the same timepoints post-extubation.

Postoperative Pulmonary Complications (PPCs)

Chest radiographs or CT scans were performed preoperatively and on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7 (or before discharge). PPCs, including new or worsened pneumonia, pleural effusion, or atelectasis, were assessed independently by two blinded radiologists according to European Perioperative Clinical Outcome (EPCO) definitions (Jammer et al. 2015).

Inflammatory markers

Serum IL-6 (by ELISA), hs-CRP (immunoturbidimetry), and WBC counts were measured preoperatively and on postoperative day 1.

Hemodynamic parameters (MAP, HR) were recorded at T0 (pre-induction), T1 (post-intubation), T2–T5 (OLV and recovery timepoints). Respiratory variables (PIP, PaO₂, PaCO2, OI, pH) were recorded during OLV and analyzed via arterial blood gases. We also conducted a safety assessment of patients in the SGB group, including the average time to onset, duration of effect and time to resolution following injection.

Statistical analysis

The analysis of data was carried out using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) based on distribution normality. Independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests served as the methods for comparing groups. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square (χ2) tests. Repeated measures ANOVA was applied to time-series data. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 125 patients were initially screened for eligibility, of whom five were excluded due to ineligibility or refusal to participate. The remaining 120 patients were assigned to either the SGB group (n = 60) or the control group (n = 60). During the study, one patient in the control group required reoperation due to postoperative hemorrhage and was excluded. In the SGB group, two patients were excluded due to failed blockade (absence of definitive Horner’s syndrome). Therefore, 117 patients finished the trial and were part of the final analysis (SGB group: n = 58; control group: n = 59) (Fig. 2).

The flow of participants through the study

No significant statistical differences were found between the two groups regarding age, sex distribution, BMI, ASA physical status, Mallampati classification, type of surgery, duration of OLV, DLT size and laterality, number of intubation attempts, cricoid pressure application, perceived resistance during intubation, or duration of DLT placement (all P > 0.05), indicating good baseline comparability (Table 1).

Primary outcomes

Postoperative Sore Throat (POST) and Throat Pain Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)

At 1 h, 6 h, and 24 h post-extubation, the incidence of POST was significantly lower in the SGB group (10.3%, 13.8%, 5.2%, respectively) than in the control group (44.1%, 35.6%, 20.3%; all p < 0.05). The severity of POST, assessed using a 4-point scale, was also significantly reduced at each time point (p < 0.05).

VAS scores for throat pain were significantly lower in the SGB group at 1 h (0.45 ± 1.45) and 6 h (0.34 ± 1.05) compared to the control group (1.88 ± 2.54 and 0.93 ± 1.41, respectively; both p < 0.05). No significant difference was observed at 24 h (P = 0.468) (Table 2).

Postoperative Pulmonary Complications (PPCs)

The overall incidence of PPCs was significantly lower in the SGB group (31.0%, 18/58) compared to the control group (81.4%, 48/59) (χ2 = 30.120, p < 0.001). Specifically, the incidence of postoperative pneumonia was 24.1% in the SGB group versus 47.5% in the control group (χ2 = 6.912, P = 0.009). The incidence of pleural effusion was also significantly lower in the SGB group (13.8% vs. 64.4%, χ2 = 31.404, p < 0.001). No significant difference was observed in the incidence of atelectasis (3.4% vs. 8.5%, χ2 = 1.314, P > 0.05) (Table 4).

Other dates

No statistically significant differences were found between groups in MAP and HR at T0–T5, or in intraoperative PIP, PaO2, PaCO2 oxygenation index (OI), or pH at the specified time points (all P > 0.05).

Safety evaluation

All 58 patients in the SGB group exhibited successful blockade as evidenced by Horner’s syndrome, with a mean onset time of 5.2 ± 1.3 min post-injection and a mean duration of 3.5 ± 0.8 h, resolving fully postoperatively.

No serious complications related to SGB (e.g., hematoma, pneumothorax, local anesthetic systemic toxicity, or neurological injury) were observed. The incidence of other perioperative adverse events (e.g., nausea, vomiting, arrhythmias) was comparable between groups (P > 0.05).

Discussion

One-lung ventilation (OLV) is a critical technique in minimally invasive thoracic surgery, enabling optimal surgical exposure by isolating the operative lung (Chen and Xiong et al. 2018). As the gold standard for OLV, DLT has a relatively large diameter and hard material, frequently cause mechanical irritation or trauma to the oropharyngeal, laryngeal, and tracheal mucosa during intubation and extubation, and trigger inflammatory response, predisposing patients to postoperative sore throat (POST), cough, and hoarseness (Knoll et al. 2006). Thoracic surgery typically requires patients to remain in the lateral decubitus position throughout the procedure, facilitating adequate exposure of the surgical site, alleviating cardiac and mediastinal pressure, and aiding respiratory management during surgery (Chiumello et al. 2020). During anaesthetic induction, patients undergo supine intubation followed by transfer to the lateral decubitus position while maintaining endotracheal tube placement. Studies indicate that the majority of patients experience endotracheal tube displacement during this repositioning, accompanied by significant variations in bronchial cuff pressure, which exacerbates damage to the airway mucosa (Maruyama et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2021). Consequently, patients undergoing thoracic surgery with DLTs experience a higher incidence and severity of airway-related complications compared to single-lumen tube intubations (Maertens and Blot 2019). POST linked to tracheal intubation can be affected by multiple factors, such as gender, Mallampati classification, DLT size, number of intubation attempts, cricoid pressure application, perceived resistance during intubation and type or duration of surgery (Chiumello et al. 2020). In the study, these risk factors showed no statistical difference.

Our study found that SGB significantly reduced the incidence and severity of POST, early postoperative cough, and throat pain, especially within the first 6 h after surgery. These effects may stem from SGB’s neuromodulatory properties. The stellate ganglion (SG) sends sympathetic fibers to the head, neck, upper chest, and upper limbs, including the mucosa of the larynx and trachea (Elas 2000; Vinod et al. 2018). By blocking these fibers, SGB can attenuate sensory input from the recurrent and superior laryngeal nerves, providing localized analgesia. Additionally, its anti-inflammatory effects may reduce mucosal edema and pain by inhibiting cytokine release in response to tissue injury (Luo et al. 2023). Research indicates that SGB may suppress microglial activation and neuroinflammation by modulating the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signalling pathway. This mechanism may also play a role in regulating pharyngeal inflammation (Yu et al. 2013). Luo et al. (Luo et al. 2023) similarly reported that ultrasound-guided SGB alleviated POST and postoperative sleep disturbance in lumbar surgery, potentially by suppressing sympathetic excitation and inflammation in the laryngeal mucosa.

Moreover, OLV itself and its associated pathophysiological alterations significantly contribute to the high incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs). Lung collapse in the non-ventilated side, potential overdistension of the ventilated lung, mechanical traction during surgery, and ischemia–reperfusion injury upon lung re-expansion can collectively trigger the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, such as IL-6, IL-8, interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) (Schilling et al. 2005; Conno et al. 2009; Baudouin 2003; Fernández-Pérez et al. 2006; Bethmann et al. 1998). These cytokines can directly damage alveolar epithelial and vascular endothelial cells, disrupt the alveolar-capillary barrier, and recruit neutrophils that exacerbate the inflammatory cascade, ultimately causing acute lung injury (ALI) and potentially acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) (Cantor and Boyse 1975; Craig et al. 2001). Among these, IL-6 is the pivotal mediator of the systemic inflammatory response triggered by surgical trauma and is closely linked to the development of PPCs. (Baudouin 2003). Our study demonstrated that patients in the SGB group exhibited lower postoperative incidences of pneumonia and pleural effusion, along with reduced serum IL-6 levels. These findings suggest that SGB may exert its pulmonary protective effects in part by suppressing the inflammatory response during OLV.

Furthermore, the results of this study show that the overall incidence of postoperative PPCs in the control group was as high as 81.4%, of which the incidence of pleural effusion was 64.4%, significantly higher than that in the observation group (31.0% versus 13.8%), and also markedly higher than the commonly reported range of postoperative PPCs incidence in thoracic surgery patients (30–50%) in the literature (Kaufmann and Heinrich 2021). We conducted an in-depth analysis of this significant difference. Firstly, we believe that long-term exposure to a high-altitude environment may be a key factor contributing to the increased baseline risk of PPCs among the patients included in this study. Our research centre is located in the eastern part of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, at an altitude of over 2,200 m, which is classified as a moderate-altitude region. Individuals residing long-term in this area, due to prolonged exposure to a hypoxic environment, have undergone a series of complex physiological changes, including secondary polycythaemia and pulmonary vascular remodelling. Such patients have relatively lower pulmonary reserve capacity and are more prone to perioperative pulmonary oedema and impaired gas exchange. Some studies have shown that the incidence of postoperative PPCs in patients living at high altitudes is significantly higher than in those living at sea level, suggesting that the high incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications in this study is closely related to the patients’ long-term exposure to a high-altitude environment (Zhu 2023). Secondly, this study adopted a more stringent definition of PPCs based on clinical intervention criteria. By defining strict endpoints aligned with the ERAS philosophy and guided by clinical intervention, we aimed to more accurately identify postoperative pulmonary complications affecting the recovery process and demonstrate the true clinical benefits of SGB. This may be a key reason why SGB was found to significantly improve the overall incidence of postoperative PPCs and pleural effusions in thoracic surgery patients undergoing double-lumen endotracheal intubation.

Formed by the fusion of the inferior cervical and first thoracic sympathetic ganglia, the stellate ganglion (SG) is a key structure for autonomic regulation. SGB induces a shift in sympathetic tone by delivering local anesthetics to the SG, thereby restoring homeostasis across the nervous, cardiovascular, endocrine, and immune systems (Lee et al. 2022; Wittwer et al. 2020; Kerzner et al. 2021; Wen et al. 2021; Eugene and Cameron 2015). Previous studies suggest that right-sided SGB may offer superior autonomic regulation, stress response attenuation, and hemodynamic stabilization (Dai et al. 2021). Therefore, all SGB interventions in this study were performed on the right side.

The advent of ultrasound-guided techniques has greatly enhanced the precision and safety of SGB. Real-time visualization of anatomical landmarks, needle trajectory, and local anesthetic spread minimizes the risk of complications such as vascular puncture, neural injury, and pneumothorax (Ghai et al. 2016; Kim et al. 2017; Pu et al. 2018). In our study, all SGB procedures were completed without any major adverse events, further validating the safety of this approach.

Postoperative sore throat remain key challenges in thoracic anesthesia. Our findings indicate that preoperative ultrasound-guided SGB not only effectively mitigates DLT-intubation-related pharyngeal complications but also contributes to reducing the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications and exerts a certain inhibitory effect on the early postoperative inflammatory response. These results support the integration of SGB into multimodal perioperative management strategies. Particularly within the context of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols, SGB offers a promising, minimally invasive adjunct that may facilitate smoother recovery trajectories.

Our study also has several limitations. First, owing to its origin at a single institution and the modest number of participants, the external validity of the results should be interpreted with caution. Then, the study only evaluated the short-term effects of SGB, without long-term follow-up to assess its impact on the incidence of POST, chronic pain, or patients' quality of life. Finally, although this study provides preliminary evidence for the anti-inflammatory effect of SGB through observed reductions in IL-6 levels, the limited panel of inflammatory markers measured constrains definitive conclusions regarding its specific anti-inflammatory mechanisms. The development of POST and PPCs likely involves a more intricate network of inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and tissue injury.

Conclusion

For patients undergoing thoracic surgery with double-lumen tube intubation, the preoperative administration of a right-sided, ultrasound-guided stellate ganglion block appears to be a safe and effective perioperative strategy. Stellate ganglion block significantly alleviates postoperative sore throat, reduces the incidence of early postoperative cough, and may help reduce the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications, suppressing the early postoperative inflammatory response. These findings underscore the clinical utility of stellate ganglion block in the perioperative management of thoracic surgery patients and in the context of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery, and warrant further exploration in larger-scale studies.
