Near-projective GHZ certification from disjoint Bell measurements
Hyunho Cha, Jungwoo Lee

Abstract
Certifying multipartite entangled states is a basic task in quantum information processing, but the achievable copy complexity depends crucially on the measurements available to the verifier. The strongest possible certification measurement for a known pure target state | ψ ⟩ \lvert\psi\rangle is the two-outcome projector { | ψ ⟩ ⟨ ψ | , 𝖨 − | ψ ⟩ ⟨ ψ | } \{\lvert\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi\rvert,\mathsf{I}-\lvert\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi\rvert\} , which is copy-optimal but often experimentally unrealistic or outside the intended measurement model. In this work, we introduce Bell-Matching Certification (BM-Cert), a single-copy verification protocol for the n n -qubit Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger state using only disjoint two-qubit Bell-basis measurements, together with one single-qubit X X -basis measurement when n n is odd. Surprisingly, a simple combinatorial effect yields perfect completeness and a verification spectral gap ν BM ​ ( n ) = 1 − O ⁡ ( 1 / n ) \nu_{\mathrm{BM}}(n)=1-O(1/n) , so the protocol approaches the ideal projective verification asymptotically as n n grows. This contrasts with local Pauli GHZ verification, whose optimal spectral gap remains bounded away from 1 1 . Thus, allowing only two-qubit entangling measurements on disjoint pairs is already enough to achieve asymptotically ideal projective certification.



1
Introduction

A quantum computer or quantum network is often designed to output a particular state. In an experiment, noise and calibration errors can make the actually produced state differ from the intended one.
State-preparation certification
asks for a statistically justified assessment of whether the produced states are close to the target state.
The target in this work is the GHZ state

\lvert G_{n}\rangle=(\lvert 0^{n}\rangle+\lvert 1^{n}\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
, a standard multipartite entangled state with roles in quantum networks, secret sharing, teleportation, nonlocality, and entanglement certification
[
4
,
7
,
5
]
. Full tomography learns an entire density matrix and is generally far more expensive than merely checking closeness to one target. Direct fidelity estimation
[
3
]
, classical shadows
[
6
]
, and quantum state verification
[
9
,
11
,
10
]
are major alternatives.

If there were no measurement restriction, the verifier would simply measure

\{\lvert G_{n}\rangle\!\langle G_{n}\rvert,\mathsf{I}-\lvert G_{n}\rangle\!\langle G_{n}\rvert\}
. One copy then passes with probability exactly equal to the fidelity
\langle G_{n}\rvert\rho\lvert G_{n}\rangle
. This is information-theoretically optimal but hides almost all experimental difficulty inside one global projector. Recent work also shows that nondemolition or memory-assisted models can approach the performance of global measurements by using stronger resources
[
8
,
1
]
. We study a more restricted and explicit family:

On each copy, the verifier may only perform disjoint two-qubit Bell measurements, plus one single-qubit
X
-basis measurement if one qubit is unmatched. The verifier may randomize the pairing and may postprocess the classical outcomes arbitrarily, but may not measure across different copies and may not implement a global projector or inverse GHZ-preparation circuit.

Within this family we give an optimal strategy. The main consequence is that by allowing only disjoint two-qubit Bell measurements, the Bell-matching strategy achieves spectral gap
\nu_{\mathrm{BM}}(n)=1-O(1/n)
, which converges to the ideal projective value
1
as
n
grows. This gives a qualitative improvement over the local Pauli benchmark for qubit GHZ states, whose optimal spectral gap is
2/3

[
7
,
2
]
. In particular, the Bell-matching strategy has a strictly larger spectral gap for all odd
n\geq 5
and even
n\geq 6
.

2
Preliminaries

For a bit string
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, write

\lvert a\rangle=\lvert a_{1}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes\lvert a_{n}\rangle.

2.1
Fidelity

Definition 1

(Fidelity with a pure target)
.

Let
\lvert\psi\rangle
be a unit vector and let
\rho
be a density matrix on the same Hilbert space. The fidelity of
\rho
with
\lvert\psi\rangle
is

F_{\psi}(\rho):=\langle\psi\rvert\rho\lvert\psi\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(\lvert\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi\rvert\rho).

The infidelity is
1-F_{\psi}(\rho)
.

The fidelity lies in
[0,1]
. It equals
1
exactly when
\rho=\lvert\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi\rvert
, and it equals
0
when
\rho
is completely supported on the subspace orthogonal to
\lvert\psi\rangle
.

2.2
Pauli operators

The Pauli
X
and
Z
matrices are

X=(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix})

and

Z=(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}).

If an operator acts on qubit
i
, we write it with a subscript. For example,

Z_{i}Z_{j}
means
Z
on qubits
i
and
j
, and the identity on all other qubits. The operator

X^{\otimes n}:=X_{1}X_{2}\cdots X_{n}

flips every computational-basis bit.

2.3
The GHZ target state

Definition 2
.

For
n\geq 2
, the
n
-qubit GHZ state is

\lvert G_{n}\rangle:=\frac{\lvert 0^{n}\rangle+\lvert 1^{n}\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},

where
0^{n}=00\cdots 0
and
1^{n}=11\cdots 1
.

The GHZ state is stabilized by the following observables:

X^{\otimes n}\lvert G_{n}\rangle=\lvert G_{n}\rangle,\qquad Z_{i}Z_{j}\lvert G_{n}\rangle=\lvert G_{n}\rangle\quad\text{for all }i\neq j.

2.4
Measurements, pass effects, and verification operators

A two-outcome quantum test is described by a positive semidefinite operator
E
satisfying
0\leq E\leq\mathsf{I}
. The probability that state
\rho
passes the test is
\operatorname{Tr}(E\rho)
. The complementary failure probability is
1-\operatorname{Tr}(E\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}((\mathsf{I}-E)\rho)
.

Definition 3
.

Let
\lvert\psi\rangle
be the target. A test effect
E
has perfect completeness for
\lvert\psi\rangle
if

E\lvert\psi\rangle=\lvert\psi\rangle.

Equivalently,
\lvert\psi\rangle
passes the test with probability
1
.

A randomized verification strategy chooses a test effect
E_{s}
with probability
p_{s}
. Its average pass effect, or verification operator, is

\Omega:=\sum_{s}p_{s}E_{s}.

If every
E_{s}
has perfect completeness, then
\Omega\lvert\psi\rangle=\lvert\psi\rangle
.

Definition 4
.

Let
\Omega
be a verification operator with
\Omega\lvert\psi\rangle=\lvert\psi\rangle
. Its second eigenvalue relative to
\lvert\psi\rangle
is

\beta(\Omega):=\bigl\|(\mathsf{I}-\lvert\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi\rvert)\Omega(\mathsf{I}-\lvert\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi\rvert)\bigr\|,

where
\|\cdot\|
is the largest singular value, which equals the largest eigenvalue for positive semidefinite matrices. The spectral gap is

\nu(\Omega):=1-\beta(\Omega).

The spectral gap is the standard copy-complexity parameter in quantum state verification
[
9
,
11
,
10
]
, as explained by the following lemma.

Lemma 1

(One-copy soundness from the spectral gap)
.

Let
\Omega
be a verification operator with perfect completeness for
\lvert\psi\rangle
, second eigenvalue
\beta
, and spectral gap
\nu=1-\beta
. For every density matrix
\rho
,

\operatorname{Tr}(\Omega\rho)\leq 1-\nu\bigl(1-F_{\psi}(\rho)\bigr).

In particular, if
F_{\psi}(\rho)\leq 1-\varepsilon
, then

\operatorname{Tr}(\Omega\rho)\leq 1-\nu\varepsilon.

Proof.

Let
P=\lvert\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi\rvert
and
P^{\perp}=\mathsf{I}-P
. Because
\Omega\lvert\psi\rangle=\lvert\psi\rangle
and
0\leq\Omega\leq\mathsf{I}
, the vector
\lvert\psi\rangle
is an eigenvector of
\Omega
with eigenvalue
1
, and the restriction of
\Omega
to the orthogonal subspace has largest eigenvalue
\beta
. Therefore, as an operator inequality,

\Omega\leq P+\beta P^{\perp}.

Taking the trace against the density matrix
\rho
preserves the inequality:

\operatorname{Tr}(\Omega\rho)\leq\operatorname{Tr}(P\rho)+\beta\operatorname{Tr}(P^{\perp}\rho).

Now

\operatorname{Tr}(P\rho)=F_{\psi}(\rho),\qquad\operatorname{Tr}(P^{\perp}\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)-\operatorname{Tr}(P\rho)=1-F_{\psi}(\rho).

Hence

\operatorname{Tr}(\Omega\rho)\leq F_{\psi}(\rho)+\beta\bigl(1-F_{\psi}(\rho)\bigr)=1-(1-\beta)\bigl(1-F_{\psi}(\rho)\bigr)=1-\nu\bigl(1-F_{\psi}(\rho)\bigr).

If
F_{\psi}(\rho)\leq 1-\varepsilon
, then
1-F_{\psi}(\rho)\geq\varepsilon
, and the final inequality gives
\operatorname{Tr}(\Omega\rho)\leq 1-\nu\varepsilon
.
∎

Lemma 2

(Many-copy certification bound)
.

Suppose the verifier tests
N
independent copies, using the same verification operator
\Omega
with spectral gap
\nu
, and accepts only if every copy passes. Let the
j
-th copy have state
\rho_{j}
and infidelity

\varepsilon_{j}:=1-F_{\psi}(\rho_{j}).

If the average infidelity obeys

\bar{\varepsilon}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{j}\geq\varepsilon,

then the probability that all
N
tests pass is at most

(1-\nu\varepsilon)^{N}.

Consequently, significance level
\delta\in(0,1)
is guaranteed whenever

N\geq\left\lceil\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{-\ln(1-\nu\varepsilon)}\right\rceil.

Proof.

By Lemma
1
, copy
j
passes with probability at most
1-\nu\varepsilon_{j}
. Independence of the copies and of the measurements gives

\Pr[\text{all pass}]\leq\prod_{j=1}^{N}(1-\nu\varepsilon_{j}).

Taking logarithms,

\ln\Pr[\text{all pass}]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ln(1-\nu\varepsilon_{j}).

The function
f(x)=\ln(1-\nu x)
is concave on the interval where it is defined.
By Jensen’s inequality,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ln(1-\nu\varepsilon_{j})\leq\ln\!\bigl(1-\nu\bar{\varepsilon}\bigr).

Since
\bar{\varepsilon}\geq\varepsilon
and
x\mapsto\ln(1-\nu x)
is decreasing,

\ln\!\bigl(1-\nu\bar{\varepsilon}\bigr)\leq\ln(1-\nu\varepsilon).

Combining the inequalities yields

\ln\Pr[\text{all pass}]\leq N\ln(1-\nu\varepsilon),

so

\Pr[\text{all pass}]\leq(1-\nu\varepsilon)^{N}.

To make this at most
\delta
, it is enough that

(1-\nu\varepsilon)^{N}\leq\delta.

Because
0<1-\nu\varepsilon<1
, taking natural logarithms gives

N\ln(1-\nu\varepsilon)\leq\ln\delta.

Dividing by
\ln(1-\nu\varepsilon)<0
gives

N\geq\frac{\ln\delta}{\ln(1-\nu\varepsilon)}=\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{-\ln(1-\nu\varepsilon)}.

∎

3
Main algorithm

3.1
Bell measurements

The four two-qubit Bell states are

\lvert\Phi^{+}\rangle=\frac{\lvert 00\rangle+\lvert 11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},\quad\lvert\Phi^{-}\rangle=\frac{\lvert 00\rangle-\lvert 11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},

\lvert\Psi^{+}\rangle=\frac{\lvert 01\rangle+\lvert 10\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},\quad\lvert\Psi^{-}\rangle=\frac{\lvert 01\rangle-\lvert 10\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}.

A Bell-basis measurement on qubits
i,j
distinguishes these four states. Equivalently, it jointly measures the commuting observables
Z_{i}Z_{j}
and
X_{i}X_{j}
. Its classical outcome is a pair of signs

(z_{ij},x_{ij})\in\{+1,-1\}^{2},

where
z_{ij}
is the measured eigenvalue of
Z_{i}Z_{j}
, and
x_{ij}
is the measured eigenvalue of
X_{i}X_{j}
.

Definition 5
.

Let
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. A quasi-perfect matching
Q
of
[n]
is:

•

if
n
is even, a perfect matching, meaning a partition of
[n]
into
n/2
unordered pairs;

•

if
n
is odd, a near-perfect matching, meaning a partition of
[n]
into
(n-1)/2
unordered pairs and one singleton.

We write
M(Q)
for the set of pairs in
Q
. When
n
is odd, we write
s(Q)
for the unique singleton.

The number of perfect matchings on
n
labeled vertices, for even
n
, is

(n-1)!!=(n-1)(n-3)\cdots 3\cdot 1.

The number of near-perfect matchings on
n
labeled vertices, for odd
n
, is

n(n-2)!!=n(n-2)(n-4)\cdots 3\cdot 1.

The double-factorial convention
(-1)!!=1
will be used later.

4
Operator form of one Bell-matching test

For a pair
e=\{i,j\}
, define the projector onto the
+1
eigenspace of
Z_{i}Z_{j}
by

P^{Z}_{e}:=\frac{\mathsf{I}+Z_{i}Z_{j}}{2}.

Define also the projector onto the
+1
eigenspace of the global
X
-parity by

P_{X}:=\frac{\mathsf{I}+X^{\otimes n}}{2}.

For a quasi-perfect matching
Q
, define

\Pi_{Q}:=P_{X}\prod_{e\in M(Q)}P^{Z}_{e}.

All projectors in this product commute, so the product is again a projector.

Lemma 3
.

For every quasi-perfect matching
Q
, the pass effect of the BM-Cert measurement with matching
Q
is

\Pi_{Q}=P_{X}\prod_{e\in M(Q)}P^{Z}_{e}.

Proof.

We first consider even
n
. Let the pairs of
Q
be
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
, where
m=n/2
. The Bell measurement on pair
e_{r}
jointly measures
Z_{e_{r}}:=Z_{i}Z_{j}
and
X_{e_{r}}:=X_{i}X_{j}
for
e_{r}=\{i,j\}
. The round accepts those outcomes for which

Z_{e_{r}}=+1\quad\text{for all }r,\qquad\prod_{r=1}^{m}X_{e_{r}}=+1.

The first condition contributes the projector
\prod_{r=1}^{m}P^{Z}_{e_{r}}
. Because the pairs are disjoint,

\prod_{r=1}^{m}X_{e_{r}}=X_{1}X_{2}\cdots X_{n}=X^{\otimes n},

so the second condition contributes
P_{X}=(\mathsf{I}+X^{\otimes n})/2
. These observables commute, and hence the joint pass effect is

P_{X}\prod_{r=1}^{m}P^{Z}_{e_{r}}=\Pi_{Q}.

Now consider odd
n
. Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
be the pairs, where
m=(n-1)/2
, and let
s=s(Q)
be the singleton. The pair measurements again impose
Z_{e_{r}}=+1
for every
r
. The total
X
-parity condition is now

X_{s}\prod_{r=1}^{m}X_{e_{r}}=+1.

Since the pairs together with the singleton cover every qubit exactly once,

X_{s}\prod_{r=1}^{m}X_{e_{r}}=X_{1}X_{2}\cdots X_{n}=X^{\otimes n}.

Thus the same joint projector
P_{X}\prod_{e\in M(Q)}P^{Z}_{e}
is obtained.
∎

Lemma 4
.

For every quasi-perfect matching
Q
,

\Pi_{Q}\lvert G_{n}\rangle=\lvert G_{n}\rangle.

Therefore BM-Cert has perfect completeness.

Proof.

The GHZ state obeys

X^{\otimes n}\lvert G_{n}\rangle=\lvert G_{n}\rangle

because
X^{\otimes n}\lvert 0^{n}\rangle=\lvert 1^{n}\rangle
and
X^{\otimes n}\lvert 1^{n}\rangle=\lvert 0^{n}\rangle
. Therefore

P_{X}\lvert G_{n}\rangle=\lvert G_{n}\rangle.

For any pair
e=\{i,j\}
, the two strings
0^{n}
and
1^{n}
have equal bits at positions
i,j
. Hence

Z_{i}Z_{j}\lvert 0^{n}\rangle=\lvert 0^{n}\rangle,\qquad Z_{i}Z_{j}\lvert 1^{n}\rangle=\lvert 1^{n}\rangle,

and therefore

Z_{i}Z_{j}\lvert G_{n}\rangle=\lvert G_{n}\rangle,\qquad P^{Z}_{e}\lvert G_{n}\rangle=\lvert G_{n}\rangle.

Because every factor in
\Pi_{Q}=P_{X}\prod_{e\in M(Q)}P^{Z}_{e}
fixes
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
, their product fixes
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
.
∎

The BM-Cert verification operator is the uniform average

\Omega_{\mathrm{BM}}:=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\,\Pi_{Q},

where
Q
is a uniformly random quasi-perfect matching.

5
Diagonalization in a GHZ basis and the spectral gap

For a bit string
a\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, define its bitwise complement by

\bar{a}:=a\oplus 1^{n},

where
\oplus
means bitwise addition modulo
2
. Thus
\bar{a}_{i}=1-a_{i}
. Choose one representative from each unordered pair
\{a,\bar{a}\}
, and call the representative set
R
. For each
a\in R
and each sign
\tau\in\{+1,-1\}
, define

\lvert a,\tau\rangle:=\frac{\lvert a\rangle+\tau\lvert\bar{a}\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}.

The target is
\lvert G_{n}\rangle=\lvert 0^{n},+\rangle
when the representative of
\{0^{n},1^{n}\}
is chosen to be
0^{n}
.

Lemma 5

(GHZ-basis eigenvectors)
.

The vectors
\lvert a,\tau\rangle
, with
a\in R
and
\tau\in\{+1,-1\}
, form an orthonormal basis of
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
. Moreover,

X^{\otimes n}\lvert a,\tau\rangle=\tau\lvert a,\tau\rangle,

and for every pair
e=\{i,j\}
,

Z_{i}Z_{j}\lvert a,\tau\rangle=(-1)^{a_{i}\oplus a_{j}}\lvert a,\tau\rangle.

Proof.

First, for a fixed pair
\{a,\bar{a}\}
, the two vectors

\frac{\lvert a\rangle+\lvert\bar{a}\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},\qquad\frac{\lvert a\rangle-\lvert\bar{a}\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}

are orthonormal. There are
2^{n-1}
complement pairs and two signs
\tau
, hence
2^{n}
orthonormal vectors. The Hilbert space dimension is
2^{n}
, so these vectors form an orthonormal basis.

Next,

X^{\otimes n}\lvert a\rangle=\lvert\bar{a}\rangle,\qquad X^{\otimes n}\lvert\bar{a}\rangle=\lvert a\rangle.

Therefore

X^{\otimes n}\lvert a,\tau\rangle=\frac{\lvert\bar{a}\rangle+\tau\lvert a\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}=\tau\frac{\lvert a\rangle+\tau\lvert\bar{a}\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}=\tau\lvert a,\tau\rangle,

where the equality
\tau^{2}=1
was used.

For the
Z_{i}Z_{j}
statement, note that

Z_{i}Z_{j}\lvert a\rangle=(-1)^{a_{i}\oplus a_{j}}\lvert a\rangle.

Also,

\bar{a}_{i}\oplus\bar{a}_{j}=(1-a_{i})\oplus(1-a_{j})=a_{i}\oplus a_{j},

so

Z_{i}Z_{j}\lvert\bar{a}\rangle=(-1)^{a_{i}\oplus a_{j}}\lvert\bar{a}\rangle.

Thus the same eigenvalue multiplies both terms in
\lvert a,\tau\rangle
, proving

Z_{i}Z_{j}\lvert a,\tau\rangle=(-1)^{a_{i}\oplus a_{j}}\lvert a,\tau\rangle.

∎

For a bit string
a
, let

A(a):=\{i\in[n]:a_{i}=1\},

and let

\operatorname{wt}(a):=|A(a)|.

A pair
\{i,j\}
has
a_{i}=a_{j}
precisely when it does not cross the cut
(A(a),[n]\setminus A(a))
.

Lemma 6

(Eigenvalue as a cut-avoidance probability)
.

Let
Q
be uniformly random among quasi-perfect matchings and let

\Omega_{\mathrm{BM}}=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\Pi_{Q}.

For every
a\in R
and
\tau\in\{+1,-1\}
,

\Omega_{\mathrm{BM}}\lvert a,\tau\rangle=\lambda(a,\tau)\lvert a,\tau\rangle,

where

\lambda(a,\tau)=\begin{cases}\Pr_{Q}\bigl[\textnormal{every pair in }M(Q)\textnormal{ has equal }a\text{-bits}\bigr],&\tau=+1,\\[3.0pt]
0,&\tau=-1.\end{cases}

Equivalently, for
\tau=+1
,
\lambda(a,+)
is the probability that the random matching avoids the cut
(A(a),[n]\setminus A(a))
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5
,
\lvert a,\tau\rangle
is an eigenvector of
X^{\otimes n}
with eigenvalue
\tau
. Hence

P_{X}\lvert a,\tau\rangle=\frac{1+\tau}{2}\lvert a,\tau\rangle.

Thus
P_{X}\lvert a,+\rangle=\lvert a,+\rangle
, while
P_{X}\lvert a,-\rangle=0
. This proves
\Pi_{Q}\lvert a,-\rangle=0
for every
Q
, and hence
\lambda(a,-)=0
.

Now assume
\tau=+1
. For a pair
e=\{i,j\}
, Lemma
5
gives

Z_{i}Z_{j}\lvert a,+\rangle=(-1)^{a_{i}\oplus a_{j}}\lvert a,+\rangle.

Therefore

P^{Z}_{e}\lvert a,+\rangle=\begin{cases}\lvert a,+\rangle,&a_{i}=a_{j},\\
0,&a_{i}\neq a_{j}.\end{cases}

Because
\Pi_{Q}=P_{X}\prod_{e\in M(Q)}P^{Z}_{e}
, and
P_{X}\lvert a,+\rangle=\lvert a,+\rangle
, we obtain

\Pi_{Q}\lvert a,+\rangle=\begin{cases}\lvert a,+\rangle,&a_{i}=a_{j}\text{ for every }\{i,j\}\in M(Q),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Averaging over uniform
Q
proves the stated eigenvalue formula. The phrase “avoids the cut” is the condition that no matched pair has one endpoint in
A(a)
and one endpoint outside
A(a)
.
∎

Lemma 7

(Cut-avoidance probability for even
n
)
.

Let
n\geq 4
be even, and let
Q
be a uniformly random perfect matching of
[n]
. Fix a subset
A\subset[n]
with
|A|=k
. The probability that every pair of
Q
is contained entirely in
A
or entirely in
[n]\setminus A
is

E_{n}(k)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{(k-1)!!(n-k-1)!!}{(n-1)!!},&k\textnormal{ even},\\[10.0pt]
0,&k\textnormal{ odd}.\end{cases}

For nontrivial cuts, meaning
1\leq k\leq n-1
, the largest possible value is

\max_{1\leq k\leq n-1}E_{n}(k)=\frac{1}{n-1}.

Proof.

The total number of perfect matchings of
[n]
is
(n-1)!!
. A perfect matching avoids the cut
(A,[n]\setminus A)
when it separately matches the vertices inside
A
and the vertices inside
[n]\setminus A
. If
k
is odd, this is impossible because an odd number of vertices cannot be partitioned into pairs. Thus
E_{n}(k)=0
for odd
k
.

If
k
is even, the number of perfect matchings inside
A
is
(k-1)!!
, and the number of perfect matchings inside the complement is
(n-k-1)!!
. Multiplying these independent choices gives

E_{n}(k)=\frac{(k-1)!!(n-k-1)!!}{(n-1)!!}.

It remains to maximize
E_{n}(k)
over
1\leq k\leq n-1
. Odd
k
gives zero, so only even
k
matters. Also
E_{n}(k)=E_{n}(n-k)
, so it is enough to consider even
k
with
2\leq k\leq n/2
. For such
k
, compute

\frac{E_{n}(k+2)}{E_{n}(k)}=\frac{(k+1)!!(n-k-3)!!}{(k-1)!!(n-k-1)!!}=\frac{k+1}{n-k-1}.

When
2\leq k\leq n/2-1
, we have
k+1\leq n-k-1
, so this ratio is at most
1
. Thus
E_{n}(k)
is nonincreasing as
k
moves from
2
toward the middle. By symmetry, the maximum is attained at
k=2
or
k=n-2
. Finally,

E_{n}(2)=\frac{1!!(n-3)!!}{(n-1)!!}=\frac{1}{n-1}.

Therefore
\max_{1\leq k\leq n-1}E_{n}(k)=1/(n-1)
.
∎

Lemma 8

(Cut-avoidance probability for odd
n
)
.

Let
n\geq 3
be odd, and let
Q
be a uniformly random near-perfect matching of
[n]
, meaning one singleton and
(n-1)/2
pairs. Fix a subset
A\subset[n]
with
|A|=k
. The probability that every pair of
Q
is contained entirely in
A
or entirely in
[n]\setminus A
is

O_{n}(k)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{k(k-2)!!(n-k-1)!!}{n(n-2)!!},&k\textnormal{ odd},\\[12.0pt]
\dfrac{(n-k)(k-1)!!(n-k-2)!!}{n(n-2)!!},&k\textnormal{ even}.\end{cases}

For nontrivial cuts, meaning
1\leq k\leq n-1
, the largest possible value is

\max_{1\leq k\leq n-1}O_{n}(k)=\frac{1}{n}.

Proof.

The total number of near-perfect matchings is

n(n-2)!!,

because there are
n
choices for the singleton and then
(n-2)!!
perfect matchings of the remaining
n-1
vertices.

A near-perfect matching avoids the cut if, after removing the singleton, the remaining vertices inside
A
can be paired among themselves and the remaining vertices outside
A
can be paired among themselves.

First suppose
k
is odd. Then the singleton must lie in
A
. There are
k
choices for the singleton,
(k-2)!!
matchings of the remaining vertices in
A
, and
(n-k-1)!!
matchings of the complement. Therefore

O_{n}(k)=\frac{k(k-2)!!(n-k-1)!!}{n(n-2)!!}

when
k
is odd.

Now suppose
k
is even. Then the singleton must lie in the complement
[n]\setminus A
. There are
n-k
choices for the singleton,
(k-1)!!
matchings of
A
, and
(n-k-2)!!
matchings of the remaining complement. Therefore

O_{n}(k)=\frac{(n-k)(k-1)!!(n-k-2)!!}{n(n-2)!!}

when
k
is even.

It remains to maximize
O_{n}(k)
over
1\leq k\leq n-1
. We use symmetry
O_{n}(k)=O_{n}(n-k)
.
For even
k
, compare consecutive even values:

\frac{O_{n}(k+2)}{O_{n}(k)}=\frac{(n-k-2)(k+1)!!(n-k-4)!!}{(n-k)(k-1)!!(n-k-2)!!}=\frac{k+1}{n-k}.

As
k
moves from
2
toward the middle, this ratio is at most
1
until the midpoint is reached. Hence among even nontrivial
k
, the maximum is at
k=2
(by symmetry, the same value is attained at
k=n-2
). Its value is

O_{n}(2)=\frac{(n-2)1!!(n-4)!!}{n(n-2)!!}=\frac{1}{n}.

For odd
k
, compare consecutive odd values:

\frac{O_{n}(k+2)}{O_{n}(k)}=\frac{(k+2)k!!(n-k-3)!!}{k(k-2)!!(n-k-1)!!}=\frac{k+2}{n-k-1}.

As
k
moves from
1
toward the middle, this ratio is at most
1
until the midpoint is reached. Hence among odd nontrivial
k
, the maximum is at
k=1
(by symmetry, the same value is attained at
k=n-1
). Its value is

O_{n}(1)=\frac{1\cdot(-1)!!(n-2)!!}{n(n-2)!!}=\frac{1}{n}.

Both parity classes have maximum
1/n
, so the overall nontrivial maximum is
1/n
.
∎

Theorem 1

(BM-Cert spectral gap)
.

For
n\geq 3
, the BM-Cert verification operator
\Omega_{\mathrm{BM}}
has perfect completeness for
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
. Its second eigenvalue is

\beta_{\mathrm{BM}}(n)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{n-1},&n\geq 4\textnormal{ even},\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{n},&n\geq 3\textnormal{ odd},\end{cases}

and its spectral gap is

\nu_{\mathrm{BM}}(n)=1-\beta_{\mathrm{BM}}(n)=\begin{cases}1-\dfrac{1}{n-1},&n\geq 4\textnormal{ even},\\[8.0pt]
1-\dfrac{1}{n},&n\geq 3\textnormal{ odd}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Perfect completeness follows from Lemma
4
. By Lemma
6
, the GHZ-basis vectors
\lvert a,\tau\rangle
diagonalize
\Omega_{\mathrm{BM}}
. The target vector
\lvert 0^{n},+\rangle=\lvert G_{n}\rangle
has eigenvalue
1
, because the empty cut is always avoided. The vector
\lvert 0^{n},-\rangle
has eigenvalue
0
, because
\tau=-1
.

All other basis vectors correspond to nontrivial cuts:
1\leq|A(a)|\leq n-1
. For
\tau=-1
, Lemma
6
gives eigenvalue
0
. For
\tau=+1
, Lemma
6
says that the eigenvalue is the cut-avoidance probability. If
n
is even, Lemma
7
shows that the largest nontrivial cut-avoidance probability is
1/(n-1)
. If
n
is odd, Lemma
8
shows that it is
1/n
. Therefore the largest eigenvalue on the subspace orthogonal to
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
is the claimed
\beta_{\mathrm{BM}}(n)
.
∎

Corollary 1

(Certified sample complexity)
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. Suppose BM-Cert is run for

N_{\mathrm{BM}}(n,\varepsilon,\delta)=\left\lceil\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{-\ln(1-\nu_{\mathrm{BM}}(n)\varepsilon)}\right\rceil

independent rounds, accepting only if all rounds pass. If the average infidelity of the tested copies with
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
is at least
\varepsilon
, then the probability of accepting is at most
\delta
. If every tested copy is exactly
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
, then the probability of accepting is
1
.

6
Optimality inside the Bell-matching family

We show that the spectral gap in Theorem
1
cannot be improved without leaving the stated measurement family or allowing completeness error.

Definition 6
.

A perfect-completeness Bell-matching strategy is any randomized single-copy strategy of the following kind.

(i)

It chooses any quasi-perfect matching
Q
according to any probability distribution.

(ii)

It performs Bell-basis measurements on all pairs in
M(Q)
, and, when
n
is odd, an
X
-basis measurement on the singleton.

(iii)

It accepts or rejects using any classical randomized or deterministic function of the outcomes.

(iv)

The target
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
is accepted with probability
1
.

Lemma 9
.

Fix a quasi-perfect matching
Q
. In the measurement associated with
Q
, every outcome accepted by BM-Cert occurs with positive probability when the input state is
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
. Therefore, any perfect-completeness postprocessing rule for this fixed measurement must accept all BM-Cert pass outcomes. Consequently, its pass effect
E_{Q}
satisfies the operator inequality

E_{Q}\geq\Pi_{Q}.

Proof.

First let
n=2m
be even. For each pair, write

\lvert 00\rangle=\frac{\lvert\Phi^{+}\rangle+\lvert\Phi^{-}\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},\qquad\lvert 11\rangle=\frac{\lvert\Phi^{+}\rangle-\lvert\Phi^{-}\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}.

For a sign vector
r=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})\in\{+1,-1\}^{m}
, let
\lvert\Phi^{r_{j}}\rangle
mean
\lvert\Phi^{+}\rangle
if
r_{j}=+1
and
\lvert\Phi^{-}\rangle
if
r_{j}=-1
. Expanding
\lvert 0^{n}\rangle
and
\lvert 1^{n}\rangle
across the matched pairs gives

\lvert 0^{n}\rangle=2^{-m/2}\sum_{r\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\lvert\Phi^{r_{j}}\rangle,

\lvert 1^{n}\rangle=2^{-m/2}\sum_{r\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}r_{j}\right)\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\lvert\Phi^{r_{j}}\rangle.

Therefore

\lvert G_{n}\rangle=\frac{\lvert 0^{n}\rangle+\lvert 1^{n}\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}=2^{(1-m)/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}\\
\prod_{j}r_{j}=+1\end{subarray}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\lvert\Phi^{r_{j}}\rangle.

This shows that exactly the outcomes with all
Z_{i}Z_{j}
signs equal to
+1
and total
X_{i}X_{j}
sign product
+1
occur, and each occurs with probability
2^{1-m}>0
. These are the BM-Cert pass outcomes.

Now let
n=2m+1
be odd, and let the singleton be
s
. Use the additional identities

\lvert 0\rangle=\frac{\lvert+\rangle+\lvert-\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},\qquad\lvert 1\rangle=\frac{\lvert+\rangle-\lvert-\rangle}{\sqrt{2}},

where
X\lvert\pm\rangle=\pm\lvert\pm\rangle
. The same expansion gives

\lvert G_{n}\rangle=2^{-m/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{0},r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\in\{\pm 1\}\\
r_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{m}r_{j}=+1\end{subarray}}\lvert r_{0}\rangle_{s}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\lvert\Phi^{r_{j}}\rangle,

where
\lvert r_{0}\rangle_{s}
means
\lvert+\rangle
if
r_{0}=+1
and
\lvert-\rangle
if
r_{0}=-1
. Thus exactly the outcomes with all pairwise
Z_{i}Z_{j}
signs
+1
and total
X
-parity
+1
occur, each with probability
2^{-m}>0
. Again these are the BM-Cert pass outcomes.

If a postprocessing rule with positive probability of using this measurement rejected any outcome that occurs with positive probability on
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
, then the conditional acceptance probability for
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
under this measurement would be less than
1
, contradicting perfect completeness of the strategy. Therefore all BM-Cert pass outcomes must be accepted. The pass effect is a sum of orthogonal outcome projectors. Since it includes every outcome projector appearing in
\Pi_{Q}
, it satisfies
E_{Q}\geq\Pi_{Q}
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Among all perfect-completeness Bell-matching strategies, the minimum possible second eigenvalue is

\beta_{\min}(n)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{n-1},&n\geq 4\textnormal{ even},\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{n},&n\geq 3\textnormal{ odd}.\end{cases}

Equivalently, the maximum possible spectral gap in this family is
\nu_{\mathrm{BM}}(n)
from Theorem
1
. Uniform BM-Cert attains this optimum.

Proof.

Theorem
1
proves that uniform BM-Cert attains the second eigenvalue
\beta_{\min}(n)
. It remains to prove that no perfect-completeness Bell-matching strategy can have a smaller second eigenvalue.

First let
n\geq 4
be even. Consider an arbitrary strategy in the family. It chooses a perfect matching
Q
from some distribution and then uses a perfect-completeness postprocessing rule. Let
\Omega
be its verification operator. For any unordered pair
\{i,j\}
, let
a^{ij}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be the bit string with ones only at positions
i,j
. The vector
\lvert a^{ij},+\rangle
is orthogonal to
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
.

For a fixed matching
Q
, the minimal BM-Cert projector
\Pi_{Q}
accepts
\lvert a^{ij},+\rangle
if and only if
\{i,j\}\in M(Q)
. Indeed, if
i
and
j
are paired together, then that pair has equal
a^{ij}
-bits, both equal to
1
, and every other pair has equal
a^{ij}
-bits, both equal to
0
. If
i
and
j
are not paired together, then the qubit
i
is paired with a zero bit or the qubit
j
is paired with a zero bit, so some measured
Z
-parity is
-1
. Thus

\langle a^{ij},+\rvert\Pi_{Q}\lvert a^{ij},+\rangle=\mathbf{1}\{\{i,j\}\in M(Q)\}.

By Lemma
9
, the actual pass effect for matching
Q
is at least
\Pi_{Q}
. Therefore

\langle a^{ij},+\rvert\Omega\lvert a^{ij},+\rangle\geq\Pr_{Q}[\{i,j\}\in M(Q)].

Average this inequality over all
\binom{n}{2}
unordered pairs:

\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\langle a^{ij},+\rvert\Omega\lvert a^{ij},+\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}|M(Q)|.

Every perfect matching has
|M(Q)|=n/2
, so

\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}|M(Q)|=\frac{n/2}{n(n-1)/2}=\frac{1}{n-1}.

Hence at least one pair
\{i,j\}
satisfies

\langle a^{ij},+\rvert\Omega\lvert a^{ij},+\rangle\geq\frac{1}{n-1}.

Since
\lvert a^{ij},+\rangle
is a unit vector orthogonal to
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
, the largest eigenvalue of
\Omega
on the orthogonal subspace is at least this expectation value. Thus
\beta(\Omega)\geq 1/(n-1)
.

Now let
n\geq 3
be odd. For each vertex
i
, let
a^{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be the bit string with a single one at position
i
. The vector
\lvert a^{i},+\rangle
is orthogonal to
\lvert G_{n}\rangle
. For a fixed near-perfect matching
Q
, the minimal projector
\Pi_{Q}
accepts
\lvert a^{i},+\rangle
if and only if
i
is the singleton. If
i
is the singleton, then all paired vertices have zero
a^{i}
-bits, so every pairwise
Z
-parity is
+1
, and the global
X
-parity condition is satisfied because the state has
\tau=+1
. If
i
is paired with any other vertex, that pair crosses the cut and has
Z
-parity
-1
. Hence

\langle a^{i},+\rvert\Pi_{Q}\lvert a^{i},+\rangle=\mathbf{1}\{s(Q)=i\}.

Again Lemma
9
implies

\langle a^{i},+\rvert\Omega\lvert a^{i},+\rangle\geq\Pr_{Q}[s(Q)=i].

Averaging over
i\in[n]
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle a^{i},+\rvert\Omega\lvert a^{i},+\rangle\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pr_{Q}[s(Q)=i]=\frac{1}{n},

because every near-perfect matching has one singleton. Therefore some
i
has expectation at least
1/n
, and since
\lvert a^{i},+\rangle\perp\lvert G_{n}\rangle
, we get
\beta(\Omega)\geq 1/n
.
∎

8
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced BM-Cert, a single-copy verification protocol for the
n
-qubit GHZ state using only disjoint two-qubit Bell-basis measurements, together with one single-qubit
X
-basis measurement when
n
is odd.
The remarkable point is that once we allow only the additional capability of two-qubit entangling measurements, the spectral gap improves to
1-O(1/n)
. Consequently, the leading copy coefficient approaches that of the ideal projective certification asymptotically.

Several directions remain open. The present analysis assumes uniformly random matchings on the complete interaction graph, which requires either the ability to Bell-measure arbitrary pairs or the ability to route qubits so that arbitrary pairings can be realized. Restricted hardware connectivity would require a different analysis. Another limitation is that memory-assisted and collective-copy strategies are outside the present model. Such strategies can be stronger, as demonstrated by recent work on quantum-memory-assisted verification
[
1
]
. Finally, while we prove optimality inside the Bell-matching family, it remains open whether BM-Cert is optimal among all perfect-completeness verification strategies based on at most two-local measurements.
