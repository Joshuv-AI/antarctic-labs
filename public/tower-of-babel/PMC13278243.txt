99mTc-methoxy-isobutyl-isonitrile Scintigraphy Demonstrates Enhanced Foot Perfusion Following Dellon Decompression in Patients With Diabetic Neuropathy
Authors: Sofija Pejkova, Sofija Tusheva, Gordana Georgieva, Blagoja Srbov, Katerina Jovanovska, Stefania Azmanova Mladenovska, Tanja Makazlieva, Savetka Paljoskovska Jordanova, Sinisa Stojanoski, Anamarija Jankulovska, Nevena Manevska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Diabetic neuropathy, a prevalent complication of diabetes mellitus, is frequently associated with peripheral vascular insufficiency and diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs). Dellon decompression of the tarsal tunnel has demonstrated efficacy in restoring sensory function and improving microcirculation in affected limbs. This prospective study aimed to evaluate changes in foot perfusion following Dellon tarsal tunnel decompression using both 99mTc-methoxy-isobutyl-isonitrile (99mTc-MIBI) scintigraphy and Doppler ultrasonography.Methods:Ten patients with diabetes with chronic DFUs (mean duration, 17.1 mo) underwent Dellon tarsal tunnel decompression. Sensory nerve function was assessed via 2-point discrimination (TPD) testing at baseline and 12 months postoperatively. Perfusion was quantified at 12-month follow-up using Doppler ultrasonography to measure peak systolic velocity in the posterior tibial artery and 99mTc-MIBI scintigraphy to compare perfusion in the operative versus contralateral foot.Results:Postoperative evaluation showed significant improvement in sensory function (TPD, P < 0.001) and posterior tibial artery flow (mean velocity increased from 43.2 to 79.4 cm/s, P < 0.0001). Scintigraphy revealed increased uptake in the operative foot during both early (16–150 versus 8–80 counts) and delayed phases (33–290 versus 15–165 counts), along with shorter time-to-peak activity and a mean uptake increase of 38.5% (rest) and 36.8% (stress). All DFUs healed by the 12-month follow-up.Conclusions:Dellon decompression significantly enhances sensory function and microvascular perfusion in patients with diabetes. Improved perfusion, confirmed by both 99mTc-MIBI scintigraphy and Doppler ultrasonography, suggests a potential role for local sympathectomy in promoting DFU healing.

Takeaways

Question: Does Dellon decompression of the tarsal tunnels improve foot perfusion and sensory function in patients with diabetes with chronic foot ulcers?

Findings: In 10 patients, 12 months after decompression, sensory function improved (2-point discrimination, P < 0.001). Posterior tibial artery flow increased (43.2 → 79.4 cm/s, P < 0.0001), and 99mTc-methoxy-isobutyl-isonitrile scintigraphy showed higher uptake and faster time-to-peak in the operative foot. All ulcers healed by follow-up.

Meaning: Dellon decompression of the tarsal tunnels enhances sensory recovery and local microvascular perfusion, supporting its use in promoting diabetic foot ulcer healing.

INTRODUCTION

In patients with diabetes, poor microcirculation and endothelial dysfunction significantly contribute to nerve degeneration and subsequent loss of sensation, which in turn leads to diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) formation.1,2 Tissue degeneration and delayed healing make vascular assessment a crucial part of their care.3–5 This study aimed to evaluate the impact of Dellon decompression on foot perfusion by performing 99mTc-methoxy-isobutyl-isonitrile (99mTc-MIBI) scintigraphy as the primary imaging technique and Doppler ultrasonography as a supplementary method.6–8

We hypothesized that patients with diabetic neuropathy who undergo Dellon decompression would show an increase in foot perfusion, evaluated by radiotracer uptake, and thus an increase in the arterial flow. Additionally, we sought to correlate these imaging results with clinical outcomes, particularly improvements in sensory nerve function and the healing of DFUs.

Few studies have used a multimodal imaging approach to examine the vascular impact of Dellon decompression. The innovative use of advanced nuclear imaging techniques for evaluating muscle perfusion in diabetic foot complications encompasses the assessment of both perfusion and blood flow.

This research addresses this gap by combining quantitative perfusion imaging with 99mTc-MIBI scintigraphy and clinical assessments to determine whether improvements in microcirculation lead to sensory recovery and ultimately to the resolution of DFUs.9 Additionally, this study sought to investigate the potential mechanisms by which nerve decompression influences vascular dynamics, particularly through local sympathectomy. This study is designed to investigate the dual role of the Dellon decompression technique on both neural and vascular functions in patients with diabetes mellitus who have chronic neuropathic ulcers.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Faculty of Medicine, University Clinic St. Cyril and Methodius (no. 0302-251/1).

Study Design and Selection of Patients

This prospective study was conducted at our clinic on 10 patients (n = 10) diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus, all of whom had DFUs. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a clinical diagnosis of diabetic neuropathy confirmed by a positive Tinel sign over the tarsal tunnel10; (2) presence of DFUs (mean duration preoperatively, 17.1 mo; mean size, 3.4 cm3; 80% of the ulcers were grade 2 according to the Wagner scale); (3) impaired sensory function in the foot as demonstrated by the 2-point discrimination (TPD) test; and (4) presence of peripheral arterial disease assessed by Doppler ultrasonography. Patients with severe renal impairment, active infection, or prior vascular surgery that could confound imaging or clinical outcomes were excluded.

The procedure includes nerve decompression, neurolysis, and local sympathectomy of the posterior tibial artery to enhance microvascular perfusion. The same experienced surgical team performed the procedure to ensure consistency in technique and postoperative care.

Dellon decompression and neurolysis of the posterior tibial nerve with local sympathectomy of the posterior tibial artery in a patient with diabetic neuropathy.

Patients were clinically assessed for parameters indicative of DFU healing: wound closure, decreased local inflammation, and restoration of tissue integrity. Data on the mean healing time for DFUs were collected and compared with preoperative records. Clinical assessments were conducted to evaluate sensory function before and 12 months after the operation using the TPD test, in accordance with the Medical Research Council scale.11 The TPD test was measured using the Dellon Discriminator disk at standardized sites on the plantar aspect of the foot. A reduction in the minimum distance required for TPD was considered indicative of improved sensory function.12

Additionally, Doppler ultrasonography was used with a high-frequency linear probe (7–12 MHz) both before and 12 months after surgery to assess macrovascular changes. This technique is particularly relevant for evaluating the posterior tibial artery, one of the primary vessels supplying the plantar aspects of the foot. Peak systolic velocity (PSV) measurements were taken at standardized sites. Postoperatively, an increase in PSV was interpreted as an enhancement in arterial blood flow, potentially leading to improved tissue perfusion.13

We used 99mTc-MIBI scintigraphy to assess tissue perfusion of the feet 12 months after Dellon decompression of the tarsal tunnels.14 The imaging protocol involved an intravenous injection of 99mTc-MIBI in the rest phase (dose: 300 MBq) and in the stress phase (600 MBq).

For dynamic imaging, sequential images were acquired in 2 phases: the vascular phase (early phase), which involved capturing high-resolution dynamic images immediately postinjection, and the tissue phase (delayed imaging), which assessed tracer distribution and retention in the foot tissues. Regions of interest were manually delineated on both the operative and contralateral (control) feet. Quantitative data were extracted, including counts (a surrogate for radiotracer uptake) and time-to-peak activity.

Specific parameters documented included the ranges observed in the vascular phase (counts) and tissue phase; time-to-peak activity, defined as the time from injection to peak activity measurement; and percentage of uptake increase. The average increase in radiotracer uptake was determined for the operative foot compared with baseline and for the contralateral foot. Foot and calf perfusion were evaluated based on the images, with a focus on the perfusion reserve of the operative foot.15,16

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD. Pre- and postoperative TPD values, Doppler ultrasound findings, and scintigraphy counts were compared using paired t tests. Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05. Analyses were performed with SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Preoperative Doppler ultrasonography revealed that the mean PSV of the posterior tibial artery was 43.2 ± 5.6 cm/s before surgery, which significantly increased to 79.4 ± 6.1 cm/s 12 months postoperatively (P < 0.0001). This substantial rise in arterial blood flow suggests that the Dellon decompression procedure of the tarsal tunnels led to enhanced macrovascular circulation through decreased sympathetic tone and reduced nerve-related vasoconstrictor activity.

The 99mTc-MIBI scintigraphy method confirmed an increase in vascularization and tissue perfusion postoperatively following the Dellon decompression procedure, showing enhancement in the operative foot (33–290 counts), compared with the control foot, which ranged from 15 to 165 counts. These differences were statistically significant (P < 0.005) (Fig. 2).

Additionally, a shorter time-to-peak activity was observed in the operative foot. In the rest phase, the time-to-peak was 36–96 seconds in the operative foot and 57–174 seconds (mean ± SD) in the control foot; in the stress phase, the time-to-peak was 54–84 seconds in the operative foot and 66–144 seconds in the control foot.

To assess the relationship between perfusion parameters in the operative and control feet, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed, which is more suitable for small-sample, nonnormally distributed data. Spearman rank order correlation coefficient was used to analyze the perfusion values in the vascular, delayed tissue, rest, and stress phases and a strong positive correlation was found (ρ = 0.881, P = 0.0039), suggesting that higher radiotracer uptake was associated with better perfusion parameters in the operative foot (Fig. 3). This result is statistically significant and strengthens the hypothesis that, after Dellon decompression, perfusion dynamics are consistent, and that scintigraphy with 99mTc-MIBI can be relied on for the assessment of the perfusion. The results show that, although there are individual differences, the changes in perfusion in the operative foot are proportional to the contralateral control foot perfusion. This supports the idea that decompression is effective in improving vascular dynamics and can be quantified as an improvement in tissue perfusion over time. Quantitative analysis revealed that radiotracer uptake in the operative foot increased by an average of 38.5% during the rest phase and 36.8% during the stress phase compared with baseline values.

Spearman correlation analysis between perfusion parameters in the operative and control feet. A strong positive correlation (P = 0.0039) indicates significantly improved perfusion following Dellon decompression.

A significant correlation between enhanced foot perfusion and DFU healing was observed. The most substantial increases in scintigraphy parameters were associated with the fastest rates of DFU resolution.

The plot shows the proportion of healed DFUs over time in patients with high (solid orange) and low (dashed red) 99mTc-MIBI perfusion. Higher perfusion was associated with a significantly faster healing rate (HR = 1.85, P < 0.01). The gray-shaded area represents the difference in healing trajectories, emphasizing the prognostic value of perfusion imaging in DFU recovery (Fig. 4).

Cumulative incidence of DFU healing by 99mTc-MIBI perfusion.

Patients who had high 99mTc-MIBI perfusion had faster DFU healing than those with low perfusion. The likelihood of wound closure was markedly greater in the high-perfusion group, with a hazard ratio of 1.85 (P < 0.01), which means that the probability of healing is 1.85 times higher than that during the follow-up period. The variation in healing rates was apparent early and remained constant throughout, which indicates that perfusion is a key driver of ulcer healing. These findings suggest that 99mTc-MIBI perfusion may be a predictor of DFU healing outcomes.

The observed improvement in perfusion, as evidenced by both Doppler ultrasonography and 99mTc-MIBI scintigraphy, was correlated with positive clinical outcomes 12 months after Dellon decompression. Notably, the local sympathectomy effect—confirmed by improved posterior tibial artery flow—played a crucial role in promoting the healing process of chronic DFUs.

In this group of 10 patients, linear regression analysis showed a significant positive correlation between blood flow and 99mTc-MIBI uptake (r = 0.97, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5). The regression equation y = 4.29x + 39.99 suggests that radiotracer uptake increases by about 4.29 counts for every 1 cm/s increase in blood flow. The model had a good fit (R2 = 0.94), which means that 94% of the variation in the radiotracer uptake can be explained by changes in blood flow. Although the sample was small, the results of the present study confirm the possibility of using 99mTc-MIBI as a quantitative tool for assessing tissue perfusion.

DISCUSSION

Pathophysiologic Basis for Decompression in Diabetic Neuropathy

Diabetic sensorimotor polyneuropathy (DSPN) results from complex metabolic, ischemic, and microvascular mechanisms, remaining one of the most challenging complications of diabetes. Studies have demonstrated that focal nerve entrapment can coexist with generalized neuropathy in up to 30% of cases, despite DSPN being primarily a systemic metabolic disease.17 Chronic compression exacerbates endoneurial ischemia and impairs axonal transport at anatomically constrained sites such as the tarsal tunnels, which are the most common site of entrapment.6,18 This phenomenon provides the rationale for surgical decompression in selected patients with diabetes who exhibit clinical evidence of focal entrapment, such as a positive Tinel sign or localized sensory deficit pattern.10

Dellon Decompression of the Tarsal Tunnels: Mechanisms and Rationale

Surgical decompression via the Dellon technique acts as a dual-action intervention that concurrently restores neural conduction and rejuvenates regional microcirculation. The underlying physiological rationale hinges on the reversal of chronic, ischemia-induced autonomic dysfunction; by alleviating focal mechanical entrapment, the procedure normalizes localized sympathetic tone. This shifts the downstream microvasculature away from persistent vasoconstriction toward sustained vasodilation, ultimately enhancing distal blood flow, increasing tissue oxygenation, and establishing an ideal metabolic environment for wound healing.19,20

Evolving Perspective and Clinical Evidence Supporting Surgical Decompression

The notion that surgical treatment cannot alter a metabolic process has fueled skepticism in the past.21 Over the years, evolving research has accumulated clinical and histological evidence that decompression relieves the superimposed ischemic component and facilitates nerve regeneration. Initially demonstrated by Dellon et al,22 decompression of multiple lower extremity nerves improves pain, sensation, and balance, and can even prevent ulceration and amputation in carefully selected patients with diabetes. Subsequent studies have further supported the integration of surgical management into the multidisciplinary care of diabetic neuropathy through improvements in sensibility, walking balance, and ulcer healing.23–26

Quantitative Perfusion Imaging With 99mTc-MIBI

During the past decade, advances in imaging have deepened the understanding of the link between neural function and microvascular perfusion. Although Doppler ultrasonography remains valuable for assessing arterial flow, it offers limited insight into capillary-level perfusion. In contrast, 99mTc-MIBI scintigraphy allows quantitative evaluation of tissue microcirculation with high spatial resolution and has recently been applied to peripheral vascular assessment in diabetic neuropathy.27,28

Our findings support the hypothesis that tarsal tunnel decompression not only alleviates neuropathic pain and restores sensory discrimination but also improves microvascular perfusion by reducing sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone. The observed enhancement in posterior tibial arterial flow confirms the local sympathectomy effect expected after decompression.29–32

99mTc-MIBI scintigraphy provided objective quantification of these changes. Compared with Doppler ultrasonography, scintigraphy more sensitively detected increased capillary perfusion, demonstrated by higher tracer uptake and reduced time-to-peak activity in the operative foot. Dynamic imaging captured both early vascular and delayed tissue phases, revealing sustained perfusion improvement correlated with ulcer healing.33–35

At 12-month follow-up, the operative foot exhibited greater perfusion and complete DFU healing, whereas the contralateral foot showed persistent deficits (Fig. 6). The dual-phase increase indicates both enhanced acute blood supply and improved long-term microvascular retention—key components of tissue repair.36,37 The sympathectomy effect, confirmed by improved posterior tibial arterial flow, further supports the mechanism of enhanced perfusion contributing to healing. In our cohort, ulcers healed on average 9 months postoperatively despite a preoperative duration of 17.1 months. The positive correlation between perfusion values and wound healing underscores the therapeutic value of Dellon decompression.

Mechanisms of Improved Blood Flow After Decompression

Several mechanisms can be offered for enhanced perfusion following Dellon decompression. First, the procedure may improve axonal function, thereby improving sensibility and decreasing neuropathic pain. Second, it may decrease dysfunctional sympathetic outflow, which is responsible for vasoconstriction in diabetic neuropathy. Finally, improved neural function may also modulate local inflammatory responses, leading to decreased tissue edema and, therefore, an improvement in perfusion. Although the exact timing and the combination of these mechanisms need to be further investigated, the results suggest that a combination of these factors is responsible for the observed benefits.

Comparison With Previous Studies

Previous studies have mainly focused on clinical outcomes in terms of sensory improvement rather than blood perfusion, but our study offers a quantitative assessment of vascular improvement.38,39

Relationship Between Perfusion and Ulcer Healing

This study is based on previous research by applying advanced imaging methods for the quantification of changes in blood flow. However, the same advantages in sensory function and pain relief have been observed after Dellon decompression, and our study is one of the first to show a significant relationship between improved perfusion assessed by 99mTc-MIBI scintigraphy and the healing of DFUs (Fig. 8).

Clinical Implications

A multimodal approach adds value to the previous studies and suggests that this procedure should be considered as a bilateral strategy targeting both neural and vascular pathophysiological processes.

Limitations and Future Directions

Future work should focus on larger numbers of patients and randomized controlled trials to confirm these findings. Electrophysiological testing was not included, as nerve conduction studies in advanced DSPN often yield absent or noninterpretable results due to severe axonal degeneration, leading to false–negative findings. Instead, we relied on clinical parameters and quantitative perfusion imaging as functional indicators of improvement.10

Understanding the molecular and cellular events that are associated with enhanced nerve function and improved vascular perfusion, as well as the prophylactic value of the Dellon procedure in patients with diabetic neuropathy at risk of developing DFUs, are important areas for future research. Because the perfusion reserve in the operative foot was almost the same as in the contralateral foot, a bilateral or early intervention may help to avoid the formation of ulcers in patients with neuropathic changes.40

CONCLUSIONS

The results of this study show that Dellon decompression is capable of restoring sensibility and enhancing blood flow in the feet. 99mTc-MIBI scintigraphy and Doppler ultrasonography provide an overall and quantifiable evaluation of these effects and could be used to inform the decision-making process. This approach has the potential to enhance patient care, decrease the incidence of DFUs, and lower the likelihood of lower limb amputation through the management of both nerve and vascular pathology.
