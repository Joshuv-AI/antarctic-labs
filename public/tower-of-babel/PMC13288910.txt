The mouse-lethal H5N1 influenza virus carrying the PB2-384L/443R/460M characteristics acquired in avian hosts can effectively utilize human ANP32A/B proteins
Authors: Weipeng Lin, Guohua Deng, Xin Xing, Jianzhong Shi, Huihui Kong, Liling Liu, Xianying Zeng, Li Jiang, Chengjun Li, Yuntao Guan, Pengfei Cui, Hualan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe species-specific acidic nuclear phosphoprotein 32 kDa members (ANP32A/B) play a key role in restricting the function of avian influenza virus polymerase in mammalian host. Avian influenza viruses, upon replicating in humans, may acquire critical amino acid substitutions in the polymerase basic 2 (PB2) protein (e.g. E627 K and D701N) that enhance the viral polymerase’s functional compatibility with human ANP32A and ANP32B proteins, thereby augmenting viral replication efficiency and increasing pathogenic potential in humans. However, certain H5N1 avian influenza viruses have demonstrated the capacity to establish productive human infections in the absence of the canonical PB2-627 K or PB2-701N adaptive mutations; the underlying molecular mechanism remains incompletely characterized. In this study, we found that the two H5N1 viruses, namely A/chicken/LN/SD035/2018 (LN35) and A/duck/JL/S1261/2019 (JL261), were genetically similar, but their pathogenicity for mice was different. By evaluating a series of single-gene reassortant viruses and mutants in mice, we confirmed that the PB2-384L/443R/460M characteristics are crucial for LN35 to maintain high lethality in mice. We further revealed that the polymerase of H5N1 avian virus bearing PB2-384L/443R/460M signature could efficiently utilize human ANP32A/B. Moreover, we found that this PB2-384L/443R/460M signature positively regulates H5N1 vRNP-human ANP32A/B interaction and vRNP assembly. Our findings indicate that key amino acid substitutions in the PB2 gene of the H5N1 virus may emerge in avian host and then augment its capacity to interact with human ANP32A/B protein, underscoring the substantial zoonotic and public health risks associated with continued circulation and evolution of H5N1 in avian populations.

Introduction

Influenza A virus (IAV), a member of the Orthomyxoviridae family, is a single-stranded, negative-sense RNA virus. The viral genome comprises eight segments encoding at least 10 essential proteins, including polymerase subunits (PB2, PB1, PA), nucleoprotein (NP), surface glycoproteins (HA, NA), matrix proteins (M1, M2), and non-structural proteins (NS1, NS2). IAV is classified into 19 HA (H1-H19) and 12 NA (N1-N12) subtypes [1–5] based on the antigenic differences in its surface glycoproteins. Influenza viruses are significant pathogens that pose substantial threats to both human and animal health [6], causing seasonal epidemics and sporadic pandemics in humans, with the potential to affect various animal species. While waterfowl have traditionally been considered as natural reservoir of avian influenza viruses (AIVs), numerous AIV subtypes (e.g. H5N1, H5N6, H5N8, H7N9, H9N2, H10N3) have documented the capacity to infect humans [7–15]. This phenomenon signifies a risk of AIVs breaking the host barrier and potentially initiating a pandemic. Therefore, elucidating the fundamental mechanisms underlying AIV interspecies transmission and pathogenicity in mammals is of paramount importance.

The RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP) of influenza virus is a heterotrimer consisting of PA, PB1, and PB2 subunits and functions in the context of the viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) to direct viral genome transcription and replication [16]. Taft et al. reported that threonine switch to isoleucine at position 97 (T97I) in PA of H5N1 increased viral replication in mammalian cells and virulence in infected mice [17]. Fan et al. demonstrated that the K185R mutation in PA significantly increased the viral polymerase activity of H5N1 virus in mammalian cells and its virulence in mice [18]. In addition, S224P and N383D substitutions in PA were reported to synergistically enhance H5N1 virulence in mice [19]. Feng et al. revealed that the D622G mutation in PB1 strengthened PB1-vRNA binding, leading to enhanced polymerase activity and increased virulence of H5N1 AIV in mice [20]. The single-amino-acid mutation E627K and D701N in the PB2 protein are two well-established markers to facilitate the adaptation of AIVs in mammalian hosts [21–23]. Besides, three amino acid mutations I147 T, K339 T, and A588 T in PB2 were reported to synergistically increase polymerase activity in mammalian cells and enhance avian H5N1 virulence in mice [24]. More recently, the M631L substation in PB2 was reported to enhance polymerase activity and promote H5N1 adaptation in cattle [25–27].

The acidic nuclear phosphoprotein 32 kDa (ANP32) family members, ANP32A/B, have been identified as essential host cofactors for IAV polymerase [28–34]. Species-specific differences in ANP32A/B determine the restriction of AIV polymerase in mammalian hosts [30]. Specifically, chicken ANP32A (chANP32A), characterized by an additional 33 amino acids compared to its mammalian ortholog, can be efficiently used by both AIV and mammalian-adapted influenza polymerase. Conversely, the shorter mammalian ANP32A/B proteins are poorly utilized by most AIV polymerase, creating a molecular barrier in mammalian cells. This barrier can be overcome by the ectopic expression of chANP32A or by acquisition of canonical polymerase adaptive mutations, such as PB2-E627K or PB2-D701N [29,30,35]. Previous study demonstrated that the intrinsically low activity of AIV polymerase in mammalian hosts drives the acquisition of these adaptive mutations [36], which enables the AIV polymerase to efficiently use mammalian ANP32A/B for productive infections [29,30,33,36]. Intriguingly, a significant proportion of human isolates of highly pathogenic H5N1 viruses lack the PB2-E627 K or PB2-D701N substitution yet still cause productive infections in humans, with some strains exhibiting high pathogenicity in mouse models [37]. These observations suggest the existence of previously unidentified alternative strategies employed by H5N1 AIV to enhance adaptation to mammalian hosts during cross-species transmission.

Xing et al. previously investigated the evolution and biological diversity of H5N1 influenza viruses bearing the clade 2.3.2.1 HA gene, and found two viruses, A/chicken/LN/SD035/2018(H5N1) (LN35) and A/duck/JL/S1261/2019(H5N1) (JL261), belonged to the same genotype, but displayed significant differences in the virulence in mice [37]. In this study, using these two viruses as models, we identified three amino acids in PB2 gene that jointly contribute to the difference of the pathogenicity of these two viruses in mice, and we further revealed that the PB2-384L/443R/460M signature obtained in avian hosts enables H5N1 virus to efficiently use mammalian ANP32A/B.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement and facility

This study was carried out in strict accordance with the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Ministry of Science Technology of the People’s Republic of China. Experiments involving highly pathogenic H5N1 AIVs were conducted in animal biosafety level 3 (ABSL3) laboratory approved for such use by the Chinese Ministry of Agriculture. Protocols were approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of the Harbin Veterinary Research Institute (HVRI) of the Chinese Academy of Agriculture Sciences (CAAS).

Cells and viruses

HEK293T, TKO (huANP32A, huANP32B, and huANP32E triple knocked out HEK293 T cells) [38], and MDCK-chANP32A cells (MDCK cells stably expressing chicken ANP32A protein) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, HyClone). A549 cells were cultured in nutrient mixture F-12 Ham Kaighn’s modified medium (F-12 K, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cells were maintained at 37℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The H5N1 AIVs A/chicken/LN/SD035/2018(H5N1) (LN35) and A/duck/JL/S1261/2019(H5N1) (JL261) were isolated during our routine surveillance [37]. Virus stocks were propagated in the allantoic cavities of 10-day-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated chicken eggs, and stored at −70℃ until use.

Antibodies

The primary antibodies used for Western blotting (WB) in this study were obtained from commercial sources and are shown below: rabbit anti-PB2 (1:5,000 for WB; GeneTex, GTX125926), rabbit anti-Flag (1:2,000 for WB; Sigma-Aldrich, F7425), rabbit anti-V5 (1:2,000 for WB; Proteintech, 14440-1-AP) and rabbit anti-β-actin (1:100,000 for WB; ABclonal, AC026). The mouse anti-PB1, anti-PA, and anti-NP monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were produced and stored by our laboratory. The DyLight 680 goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:10,000 for WB; ImmunoWay, RS23710) and DyLight 800 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:10,000 for WB; ImmunoWay, RS23920) were used as secondary antibodies.

Plasmid construction

The non-coding regions (NCRs) of LN35 and JL261 were sequenced as previously described [39], and homologous recombination primers were designed based on these sequences. The eight segments of the two viruses were individually cloned into the vRNA-mRNA bidirectional vector pBD for virus rescue. For protein expression, the open reading frames (ORFs) of PB2, PB1, PA, and NP from both viruses were inserted into the pCAGGS vector, either untagged or with a C-terminal Flag tag. Additionally, the ORFs of huANP32A (Homo sapiens, NM_006305.4), huANP32B (Homo sapiens, NM_006401.3) and huANP32E (Homo sapiens, NM_030920.5) were cloned into pCAGGS with a C-terminal Flag or V5 tag. All constructs were generated using a homologous recombination strategy with the ClonExpress II One-Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). Indicated mutations were introduced into the PB2 segment within the relevant plasmids. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing to confirm the absence of undesired mutations. The reporter plasmids pPolI-Luc (expressing influenza-virus-like-RNA which carries the firefly luciferase gene and is under the regulation of the human RNA polymerase I promoter) and pRL-TK (expressing Renilla luciferase as an internal control, Promega) were maintained in our laboratory.

Virus rescue

Reassortant and mutant viruses were generated using an eight-plasmid reverse genetics system as previously described [23]. All rescued viruses were sequence-verified and stored at −70℃ until use. Viral titres were determined as the 50% egg infectious dose (EID50) for subsequent experiments.

Mouse study

To assess viral replication in vivo, groups of three 6-week-old (42-day-old) female BALB/c mice (Beijing Experimental Animal Center, Beijing, China) were lightly anesthetized and intranasally inoculated with 106 EID50 of virus in 50 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). On day 3 post-inoculation, the mice were euthanized, and their lungs, nasal turbinates, brain, spleen, and kidneys were collected for virus titration. For determining the 50% mouse lethal dose (MLD50), groups of five mice were anesthetized and intranasally inoculated with serial 10-fold dilutions of the test virus containing 101–107 EID50. The mice were monitored for weight loss and death (mice that lost more than 25% of their initial body weight were regarded as reaching the humane endpoint, euthanized, and recorded as dead) for 14 days, and the MLD50 values were calculated using the method of Reed and Muench.

Minigenome assay

HEK293T or TKO cells seeded in 24-well plates were co-transfected with the following plasmids: pPolI-Luc (40 ng), pRL-TK (5 ng), and pCAGGS constructs expressing PB2 or PB2 mutants (40 ng), PB1 (40 ng), PA (40 ng), and NP (60 ng), along with any other indicated plasmids. The polymerase activity of negative control was measured in the absence of PB2. At 24 h post-transfection, cells were lysed, and luciferase activity was measured using a Dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Promega). Polymerase activity was expressed as firefly luciferase activity normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Viral replication kinetics

The A549 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and infected with viruses at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1. After 1 h of adsorption at 37℃, the inoculum was then removed. Cells were washed three times with PBS, and then cultured in Opti-MEM medium (Gibco) at 37°C. Culture supernatants were collected at specified time points post-infection, and viral titres were titrated using MDCK-chANP32A cells.

Co-immunoprecipitation

TKO cells were seeded in 60 mm dishes and transfected with indicated plasmids using Polyethylenimine (PEI, Polysciences) transfection reagent. For the vRNP-ANP32 interaction assay, pCAGGS constructs expressing PB2 or PB2 mutants (400 ng), PB1 (400 ng), PA (400 ng), NP (600 ng), huANP32A/B-Flag (400 ng), and pPol I-Luc (400 ng) were co-transfected. For the vRNP assembly assay, pCAGGS constructs expressing PB2 or PB2 mutants (400 ng), PB1 (400 ng), PA (400 ng), NP-Flag (600 ng), huANP32A/B-V5 (400 ng), and pPol I-Luc (400 ng) were co-transfected. Empty pCAGGS plasmids were used in place of the Flag-tagged constructs as a negative control. At 24 h post-transfection, cells were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (APExBIO). Cleared lysates were incubated with anti-Flag M2 Magnetic Beads (Sigma-Aldrich) overnight at 4°C. The beads were washed with PBS and bound proteins were eluted by treatment with 3×Flag peptide (APExBIO). Both input and co-immunoprecipitated (co-IP) samples were analyzed by WB. Equal input was confirmed by consistent β-actin (ACTB) levels.

Western blotting

Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (GE Healthcare). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h, incubated with indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, and then incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were visualized using an Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences), and analyzed with Image Studio software. The intensities of individual protein bands were quantified using ImageJ software, normalized to the respective IP-Flag band for each sample, and then expressed as a ratio relative to the wild-type control (set as 1.00).

Isothermal titration calorimetry

The PB2 cap-binding domain (CBD) was expressed in a prokaryotic system. Purified CBD protein was desalted into the specific buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4), and the concentration was determined using a BCA assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was performed at 20 °C using an Affinity ITC instrument (TA Instruments, USA). The sample cell contained 70 μM CBD protein, and the syringe contained 0.7 mM m⁷GTP. An initial injection of 0.5 μL was followed by 34 injections of 2 μL each. Data were fitted to a single-binding-site model using NanoAnalyze Data Analysis Software (version 3.11.0) to obtain the dissociation constant (Kd).

Homology modelling

A homology model of the LN35-CBD was generated using the SWISS-MODEL server (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/), using the crystal structure of the H5N1 PB2 CBD in complex with m7GTP (PDB: 4CB4) as a template. The resulting model was visualized and rendered using PyMOL.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance was determined by unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test using GraphPad Prism 7.0 software. Significance levels are denoted as follows: ns, not significant (P > 0.05); *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; and ****, P < 0.0001.

Results

Replication and pathogenicity of rescued viruses LN35 and JL261 in mice

Our previous study indicated that the virus LN35 was highly pathogenic in mice, with an MLD50 of 1.75 log10EID50, whereas the virus JL261 was not lethal in mice at a dose as high as 106 EID50 [37]. In this study, we rescued these two viruses by using reverse genetics as described previously [23], and investigated their replication and virulence in mice. As shown in Figure 1A, the rescued virus Re-LN35 replicated systemically, with high viral titres detected in the lungs, nasal turbinates, brain, spleen, and kidneys of intranasally inoculated mice. Consistently, the virus Re-LN35 exhibited high lethality, with an MLD50 of 1.75 log10EID50 (Figure 1B). In contrast, the rescued virus Re-JL261 was detected only in the lungs and nasal turbinates at lower titres and caused no lethality even at a dose of 107 EID50, with an MLD50 of greater than 7.50 log10EID50 (Figure 1A, B). These results indicated that the virulence of Re-LN35 and that of Re-JL261 in mice differ by no less than 560,000-fold (MLD50 of 1.75 versus ≥7.50 log10EID50). PB2 is the key determinant of LN35 pathogenicity

The two viruses share a highly similar genetic background, differing by only 26 amino acids across the eight essential viral proteins encoded by seven gene segments (excluding the NP gene) (Figure 2B). To identify the primary gene responsible for the substantial difference in pathogenicity between LN35 and JL261, we generated a series of reassortant viruses using a single-gene reassortant strategy. With the lethal LN35 as backbone, we rescued seven reassortants, each of them bears one JL261 gene segment that differs from the LN35 virus, and evaluated their replication and virulence in mice. Strikingly, the reassortant bearing the JL261_PB2 segment (LN35-JL261_PB2) was severely attenuated: the virus titres were significantly reduced in both the lungs and nasal turbinates, and the virus was undetectable in the brain and kidneys, present only in the spleen of 1 out of the 3 inoculated mice (Figure 2A). Furthermore, LN35-JL261_PB2 showed a 2,570-fold reduction in lethality compared to Re-LN35 (MLD50 of 5.16 versus 1.75 log10EID50) (Figure 2C). In contrast, the other six reassortants that bearing the PB1, PA, HA, NA, M, or NS gene of JL261 in the LN35 background replicated systemically in mice, and showed only slight attenuation in pathogenicity, with a 1.2- to 28.2-fold reduction in lethality compared with Re-LN35 (MLD50 ranging from 1.83 to 3.20 log10EID50 versus 1.75 log10EID50) (Figure 2). Moreover, the recombinant virus JL261-LN35_PB2 produced significantly higher viral loads in various mouse tissues relative to its parental strain JL261, and the introduction of the LN35_PB2 gene increased the pathogenicity of the JL261 by 1,318-fold in mice (Figure 2). Collectively, these results clearly demonstrate that the PB2 segment is the key determinant governing the differential replication efficiency and pathogenicity between LN35 and JL261 in mice. The PB2-384L/443R/460M signature determines LN35 pathogenicity in mice

The PB2 proteins of LN35 and JL261 differ by only three amino acids, at positions 384, 443, and 460 (Figure 2B). To pinpoint which amino acid residues are responsible for the difference in virulence, we generated a series of mutant viruses and evaluated their replication and virulence in mice. Firstly, we introduced single mutations (L384M, R443 K, or M460 V) of PB2 into the highly pathogenic LN35 background. As shown in Figure 3, the three single mutants (LN35-PB2 L384M, LN35-PB2 R443 K, and LN35-PB2 M460 V) replicated to slightly lower titres than Re-LN35, with lethality changes within a 10-fold range (MLD50 values ranging from 1.67 to 2.67 log10EID50 versus 1.75 log10EID50). These results indicated that individual mutations exert only minimal effects on virulence. Secondly, we introduced double mutations (L384M/R443 K, L384M/M460 V, or R443 K/M460 V) of PB2 into the highly pathogenic LN35 background. Compared with single mutants, introduction of double mutations of PB2 further impaired the replication and virulence of LN35 in mice (Figure 3). The virulence of the three double mutants (LN35-PB2 L384M/R443 K, LN35-PB2 L384M/M460 V, and LN35-PB2 R443 K/M460 V) showed a 120-, 562- and 2,630-fold reduction in lethality compared to Re-LN35, respectively (Figure 3B). Notably, among the three double mutations in PB2 protein, the R443 K/M460 V mutation exerted the strongest attenuation of LN35 virulence, and its attenuating effect on the virulence of LN35 was comparable to that of the triple mutation L384M/R443 K/M460 V (Figure 3). These results suggested that double mutations and triple mutations overall exhibited a more significant attenuating effect than any single mutation, indicating a synergistic contribution of residues 384, 443, and 460 on viral pathogenicity. To further verify the synergistic effect of residues 384, 443, and 460 on viral pathogenicity, we introduced the reciprocal double mutations (M384L/K443R, M384L/V460M, or K443R/V460M) and triple mutation (M384L/K443R/V460M) of PB2 into the low-pathogenic JL261 background, respectively. As shown in Figure 3A, compared with Re-JL261, the double mutants exhibited varying degrees of increased viral titres in the lungs and nasal turbinates of mice, and virus was detected in the spleen and kidneys of mice inoculated with JL261-PB2 M384L/V460M. The triple mutant JL261-PB2 M384L/K443R/V460M replicated systemically in mice and exhibited higher viral titres in mouse organs compared with the double mutants (Figure 3A). In terms of virulence, three double mutants (JL261-PB2 M384L/K443R, JL261-PB2 M384L/V460M, and JL261-PB2 K443R/V460M) exhibited 32-, 132-, and 100-fold increases in lethality, respectively, compared with Re-JL261, whereas the triple mutant (JL261-PB2 M384L/K443R/V460M) displayed a much greater increase in pathogenicity, with a 1,318-fold increase (Figure 3B). In summary, these in vivo data demonstrate that the PB2-384L/443R/460M signature is responsible for the high pathogenicity of avian H5N1 viruses in mice.

Amino acid substitutions L384M/R443 K/M460 V in PB2 impair H5N1 polymerase activity and replication in mammalian cells

PB2 is an essential component of the vRNP complex that directs transcription and replication of the viral genome [40]. To determine whether the enhanced replication of LN35 is attributable to the superior polymerase activity conferred by its PB2 protein, we performed minigenome assays in HEK293 T cells. As shown in Figure 4A, LN35 exhibited markedly higher polymerase activity than that of JL261 (2,283-fold higher). The triple substitution PB2-L384M/R443 K/M460 V conferred a pronounced effect of 695-fold attenuation in LN35 context (Figure 4A), demonstrating that the L384M/R443 K/M460 V substitutions in PB2 impair H5N1 polymerase activity. To examine whether these PB2-mediated changes in polymerase activity correlate with viral replication, multicycle replication assays were performed in A549 cells. As shown in Figure 4B, Re-LN35 replicated to higher titres than Re-JL261, and introduction of the triple substitution PB2-L384M/R443 K/M460 V into LN35 severely impaired viral replication. Collectively, these findings confirm that the PB2 amino acid substitutions L384M, R443 K, and M460 V impair polymerase activity and suppress the in vitro replication of the H5N1 virus in mammalian cells. The PB2-384L/443R/460M signature enables efficient utilization of human ANP32A/B by the H5N1 polymerase

Species-specific differences between mammalian ANP32A/B and chANP32A are known to restrict AIV polymerase activity in mammalian cells [29,30]. Significantly higher LN35 polymerase activity was observed in HEK293 T cells. Does this indicate that LN35 polymerase can utilize human ANP32A/B proteins more effectively than JL261 polymerase? To answer this question, we performed minigenome assays in TKO cells (human ANP32A/B/E triple knockout HEK293 T cells) [38] and TKO cells reconstituted with huANP32A, huANP32B, or huANP32E. In the TKO cells and TKO cells expressing huANP32E, the polymerase activities of LN35, JL261, and LN35-PB2 Mut were all comparable (Figure 5), however, in the presence of either huANP32A or huANP32B, the LN35 polymerase activities were significantly higher than that of JL261 (Figure 5). Of note, introduction of the triple substitution PB2-L384M/R443 K/M460 V into LN35 (LN35-PB2 Mut) severely impaired its polymerase activity in both the TKO cells expressing huANP32A or huANP32B. The PB2-384L/443R/460M signature is important for H5N1 vRNP-ANP32A/B effective interaction and vRNP assembly

Previous studies have demonstrated that the interaction of ANP32 proteins with vRNP is essential for their polymerase-supporting function, and that stronger binding between ANP32 proteins and vRNP is associated with higher polymerase-supporting activity [41–43]. To determine how the PB2-384L/443R/460M signature enhances utilization of huANP32A/B by H5N1 polymerase, we first examined its impact on the interaction between vRNP and huANP32A/B proteins. Co-IP assays were performed in TKO cells reconstituted with Flag-tagged huANP32A or huANP32B. We found that more LN35-vRNP was co-precipitated with huANP32A/B compared to JL261-vRNP or the LN35-PB2 Mut vRNP (Figure 6A, B). Given that defective vRNP assembly contributes to the restriction of AIV polymerase in mammalian cells [44], we next investigated how the PB2-384L/443R/460M signature affect avian H5N1 vRNP assembly in human cells. We found that, in the presence of either huANP32A or huANP32B, vRNP assembly of LN35 was markedly more efficient than that of JL261. Disrupting the signature by introducing the triple substitution L384M/R443 K/M460 V into the LN35 context reduced vRNP assembly to levels comparable to JL261 (Figure 6C, D). Collectively, these results demonstrated that the PB2-384L/443R/460M signature promotes adaptation of the avian H5N1 polymerase to huANP32A/B by strengthening the vRNP-ANP32A/B interaction and facilitating efficient vRNP assembly. Discussion

Cross-species transmission of AIVs to humans often leads to severe infections. While AIV replication in mammalian cells is typically impaired due to host restrictions, certain highly pathogenic AIVs (e.g. H5N1 and H7N9) can overcome these barriers and successfully infect mammals using various strategies [11,30,42,45]. Here, we identified a signature-384L/443R/460M-in the PB2 protein of avian H5N1 isolates that facilitates efficient utilization of huANP32A/B by the viral polymerase, thereby enhancing adaptation to mammalian cells and leading to increased replication and pathogenicity in mice. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that this PB2 signature exerts its polymerase-enhancing function by promoting H5N1 vRNP-huANP32A/B interactions and facilitating the assembly process of H5N1 vRNP in human cells. Collectively, we have uncovered a genetic signature in PB2 that serves as a virulence factor and represents a potential alternative strategy for H5N1 viruses to break the species barrier.

After AIVs cross the species barrier to infect mammalian host cells, their viral polymerases must overcome the functional restrictions imposed by mammalian ANP32A/B proteins to achieve efficient replication. The most prevalent strategy is the acquisition of adaptive mutations within the viral polymerase complex, among which the well-characterized PB2-E627K and PB2-D701N substitutions represent canonical examples. AIV polymerases harbouring these mutations can effectively exploit mammalian ANP32A/B proteins to sustain robust activity, thereby enabling high-level replication in mammalian hosts and laying a critical molecular foundation for cross-species transmission [29,30,35]. Notably, recent outbreaks of H5N1 viruses in cattle further highlight the important role of such polymerase adaptive mutations. The mammalian-adaptive mutation PB2-M631L greatly enhances the polymerase activity of cattle-derived H5N1 viruses by promoting their utilization of both bovine and human ANP32 proteins, thereby improving viral replication and virulence in mammals [26,27]. Although the exact emergence time of this mutation and the process of cross-species transmission remain unclear, this mutation clearly facilitates AIV adaptation to mammals and establishment in cattle populations. The PB2-384L/443R/460M signature identified in this study functions through a similar mechanism: it markedly enhances the polymerase activity of avian H5N1 viruses by improving their utilization of huANP32A/B proteins, suggesting that H5N1 viruses carrying this signature display elevated zoonotic risk and pose a persistent threat of cross-species infection.

To analyze the prevalence of PB2-384L/443R/460M residues in nature, we downloaded all available IAV PB2 sequences from the GISAID database up to April 30, 2026 (n = 394,730). Among these viruses, 585 isolates carry the PB2-384L/443R/460M signature, including 298 human isolates, 141 avian isolates, and 146 mammalian isolates. The signature can be detected in multiple prevalent H5 clades, including 2.3.2.1a, 2.3.2.1f, and the currently dominant 2.3.4.4b. Beyond the H5 subtype, the signature exists in multiple influenza A subtypes, including H1, H2, H3, H4, H6, H7, H8, H9, H10, H11, and H12 (Table S1). For the H5N1 viruses, the prevalence of this signature is higher in human isolates (8/519) than in avian isolates (40/32,459) (Table S1), implying that this signature confers an adaptive advantage for H5N1 viruses to infect human hosts and may facilitate cross-species spillover from avian reservoirs to humans. Moreover, continuous emergence of H5N1 strains harbouring this signature in multiple mammalian species has been reported in recent years, including raccoons, foxes, blue foxes, cats, and raccoon dogs across North America, Europe, and Asia during 2022–2026. Notably, PB2-384L/443R/460M can co-occur with other adaptive markers, such as the canonical human-adaptive marker PB2-627 K. Among the eight human H5N1 isolates harbouring this triple signature, three strains simultaneously carry PB2-627 K. The co-occurrence of multiple mammalian adaptive mutations may synergistically enhance viral polymerase activity, mammalian adaptation, and pathogenicity, further elevating the zoonotic and pandemic potential of circulating H5N1 viruses.

Given that the PB2-384L/443R/460M signature resides within the cap-binding domain (CBD), which is primarily responsible for cap recognition and binding during IAV transcription [46], we initially considered whether it might alter cap-binding affinity. However, our isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assays showed no significant difference in m7GTP binding between the LN35-CBD and the triple-mutant CBD (P > 0.05) (Figure S1A-C). Consistent with structural modelling, the residues 384, 443, and 460 are located distal to the cap-binding pocket (Figure S1D). Instead, we found that this signature promotes efficient utilization of huANP32A/B by the H5N1 polymerase.

In recent years, the host factors ANP32A and ANP32B have been identified to be key cofactors for the IAV polymerase [29–32]. Although the precise molecular mechanism by which ANP32A/B support viral polymerase function remains elusive, studies have confirmed that the binding of ANP32A/B to the viral polymerase is indispensable for their function [28,29,31,32,41,47–49]. In addition, several studies have shown that ANP32A/B proteins are also involved in the process of AIV vRNP assembly [41,42]. For example, over-expression of chANP32A in mammalian cells can rescue defects in both AIV polymerase activity and vRNP assembly [30,42]. Moreover, recent evidence has revealed that the AIV non-structural protein NS2 facilitates the exploitation of huANP32A/B by the AIV polymerase [42]. Mechanistically, NS2 enhances the interaction between AIV vRNP and huANP32A/B and promotes vRNP assembly, without affecting the binding of huANP32A/B to viral RdRP [43]. Consistently, our present study reveals that the PB2-384L/443R/460M signature enables the H5N1 polymerase to efficiently exploit huANP32A/B via strengthening H5N1 vRNP-huANP32A/B interaction and accelerating viral vRNP assembly.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that the PB2-384L/443R/460M signature enables H5N1 polymerase to function efficiently in human cells by adapting it to huANP32A/B. Acquisition of this signature in avian hosts may therefore serve as an alternative strategy for H5N1 viruses to cross the species barrier from birds to mammals. Consequently, as a new determinant associated with AIV interspecies transmission, the PB2-384L/443R/460M signature should be considered in risk-assessment frameworks.
