Alzheimer’s disease risk prediction from clinical and social determinants of health: a machine learning cohort study in UK Biobank
Authors: Junming Hu, Simon Lu, Qi Zhang, Kehan Qian, Habbiburr Rehman, Congcong Zhu, John Farrell, Julio E Castrillon-Candas, Rhoda Au, Lindsay A Farrer, Wei Q Qiu, Jinying Chen, Xiaoling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSocial determinants of health (SDOH) may improve Alzheimer’s disease (AD) risk prediction by capturing upstream contextual risk beyond routinely measured clinical variables. We aimed to develop and validate an accurate, interpretable machine-learning pipeline for AD risk prediction in UK Biobank using routinely collected data.MethodsUsing data from 13 076 participants in the UK Biobank, we developed an automated machine-learning pipeline for AD risk prediction with feature selection and a C5.0 boosted-tree classifier. Data were split into training, development and test sets (7:2:1); missing values were imputed in the training data only, and feature selection, tuning and threshold calibration were performed using the training/development data, with final evaluation on the independent test set. Internal validation used repeated subsampling without replacement.ResultsDuring up to 16 years of follow-up, 927 participants developed AD. Feature selection reduced 3590 variables to 26 predictors spanning age, APOE4, SDOH, medical history and routine clinical measures. The final model showed good discrimination (area under the precision–recall curve 0.89) and adequate calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow p=0.71), with stable performance under repeated subsampling. Sex-stratified models showed similar patterns.DiscussionSDOH contributed useful predictive information, but their associations should be interpreted as predictive rather than causal and may reflect socioeconomic confounding and healthcare access.ConclusionsThis model could support scalable AD risk screening using routinely collected data, but external validation and recalibration in non-UK populations are needed before broader application.

Introduction

Late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder predominantly impacting individuals aged 65 and older.1 2 AD pathology may accumulate silently for decades before the emergence of clinical symptoms.3 4 Current biomarkers, including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), positron emission tomography (PET) and plasma phosphorylated tau (p-tau), are primarily useful for diagnosis4 but are costly, invasive and not scalable for population-level prediction.5 Plasma p-tau is safer and more affordable, with growing potential for early prediction.6 Plasma p-tau217 predicts AD progression (area under the curve (AUC) ~83%) and distinguishes AD from non-AD or cognitive impairment with high accuracy (AUC ~89%).79 However, assays remain unstandardised and provide little insight into modifiable risk factors. Risk prediction models incorporating cognitive function and neuroimaging data, such as brain volume,10 and genetic factors require additional tests and expert training to obtain data, which is costly and time-consuming. The global rise in AD cases underscores the urgent need for accurate, cost-effective tools to screen for risk in the asymptomatic stage. Leveraging real-world data routinely collected from primary care, nursing homes, electronic health records and large-scale epidemiological studies offers a promising path towards scalable early detection. Recent machine learning (ML)-based AD prediction studies have leveraged routinely collected real-world data, such as electronic health records and linked administrative data, to support scalable risk stratification years before clinical diagnosis.11 12 However, social determinants of health (SDOH) are often inconsistently captured in routine data and are therefore rarely evaluated in a systematic way for their added value beyond clinical and healthcare utilisation variables, which limits their utility for profiling potentially modifiable upstream risk contexts.13

Existing dementia risk models based on routinely collected data often rely on a limited set of predefined factors.14 15 Yet AD has a complex, largely sporadic aetiology influenced by diverse clinical, lifestyle and social features.16 17 Many of these routinely collected traits show predictive value but including them all risks overfitting. Automated selection of clinically relevant predictors is therefore needed to build accurate and interpretable models.

To address this gap, we aimed to develop and validate a fully automated ML pipeline using UK Biobank data18 to build an accurate and interpretable model for AD risk prediction with C5.0 boosted tree algorithm.19 Our approach systematically integrates SDOH with clinical and genetic predictors using an automated, reproducible feature-selection pipeline to support interpretable AD risk prediction. Leveraging the power of ML and the extensive UK Biobank (UKBB) dataset, we aimed to uncover phenotypic and behavioural factors indicative of AD risk, which could inform clinical decision-making for early AD detection and prevention.

Methods

Overview

After comprehensive preprocessing of the UKBB data,18 we developed an ML model to predict AD risk. We built and evaluated the model on a subset of 13 076 participants from the full UKBB cohort of 502 505 participants, as detailed in online supplemental method 1. Feature selection followed an automated three-step pipeline integrating statistical and ML methods, with expert validation based on clinical experience.

Study participants

As shown in figure 1, we selected study participants from the UKBB dataset through the following steps. Participants were excluded based on sex inconsistencies, age <50, pre-baseline AD diagnosis, or entirely missing features, yielding 13 076 participants (12 149 controls, 927 cases), as detailed in online supplemental method 2. Data extraction and preprocessing were performed using R and Python, adhering to UKBB data use policies and ethical guidelines.

Primary outcome and candidate predictors

The primary outcome was incidence of AD over the 16-year follow-up period. A total of 927 AD cases were identified using the UKBB data field indicating AD diagnosis date (f.42020). The mean time from baseline to AD diagnosis among cases was 8.3 ± 0.9 years. Candidate predictor variables, assessed at the baseline visit, included participants’ demographics (eg, sex, age, race and education), APOE4 status, disease history, lifestyle factors (eg, smoking and drinking habits), and cognitive, physical and blood/urine test results. Education was represented as imputed years of education (online supplemental method 4).

Data preparation

UKBB variables were harmonised to a consistent analytic format, and only baseline measurements were used as predictors to maximise completeness and reduce selection bias (online supplemental method 3).

Experimental datasets

We categorised the UKBB participants into two groups: (1) AD groups: developed AD during the follow-up period and (2) control group: those without developing AD during the follow-up period. Participants were randomly split into training, test and development sets (7:2:1), preserving class balance representative of the population. The development set was used for hyperparameter tuning and threshold calibration; final results were reported on the test set. Random sampling was performed using the R function createDataPartition(). The final division included 9155 participants in the training dataset, 2615 in the test dataset, and 1306 in the development dataset. All model development steps (including imputation, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, and threshold calibration) were performed using the training and development sets only; the test set was reserved for final evaluation. Missing values were imputed within the training set (including sex-specific subsets) for feature selection; further details, including sensitivity analyses using the MICE package, are provided in (online supplemental method 4).

Feature selection

We adopted a three-step pipeline to identify predictors for the AD risk model. First, statistical screening excluded features with weak associations or high missingness. Second, the Boruta algorithm was applied to retain features with predictive importance. Finally, a modified backward selection procedure further reduced the feature set, optimising performance and interpretability. Additional details are provided in the online supplemental methods: the overall three-step procedure (online supplemental method 5), modified backward selection process (online supplemental methods 6 and 7) and ML model implementation (online supplemental method 8) (see also figure 2).

Workflow for enhancing the backward feature selection. Dynamic sampling: To accelerate backward feature selection, dynamic feature sampling was introduced to reduce the number of features per iteration. Two-phase elimination process: An initial aggressive elimination phase, controlled by the AET parameter, excluded the bottom 10% of features based on importance. Once a predefined threshold was reached, the process shifted to examining remaining features individually. As fewer features remained, the elimination strategy became more conservative. The modified backward feature selection pipeline was implemented using R, leveraging parallel processing to enhance computational efficiency. AET, aggressive elimination threshold.

Evaluation metrics

We validated model performance on the test set using accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and the area under the precision–recall curve (PR-AUC). As AD is a low-prevalence condition, F1 and PR-AUC were emphasised because they are more sensitive to performance under class imbalance. Full definitions of these metrics, including the formula for the F1 score, are provided in the online supplemental method 9.

Optimisation and training of the C5.0 boosted tree model

Because a large number of iterative model fits (along with hyperparameter tuning) are required by the backward feature selection procedure, we chose C5.0 as the ML model to balance interpretability and computational efficiency. C5.0 is highly interpretable because it produces human-readable decision trees and rule sets, making it easy to trace which features drive each prediction. It also has a relatively small hyperparameter space. In preliminary analysis against commonly used ML models, C5.0 achieved the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) (0.95; online supplemental figure S1; online supplemental method 8) and was therefore used in the formal experiments with the full feature-selection pipeline. The optimisation of the C5.0 boosted tree model involved hyperparameter tuning using R’s caret package. The process focused on determining the optimal number of boosting trials (ranging from 1 to 100) and winnowing option (which refers to the internal feature selection algorithms used in C5.0) using grid search optimisation. Model performance under each hyperparameter configuration was evaluated and compared using the F1 score. For model training, we repeated 10-fold cross-validation (5 repeats). We applied 1:1 down-sampling to address class imbalance and used parallel processing with fixed random seeds to ensure efficient and reproducible model training. Down-sampling was chosen for computational efficiency. Prior studies show that ensemble classifiers can effectively reduce information loss due to down-sampling and that RUSBoost performs comparably to SMOTEBoost while being simpler and faster.20 21

Classification threshold selection

Due to the disproportionate representation of individuals who developed AD in both the general population and our study sample, we recalibrated the trained model on the development set to determine an optimal classification threshold. We calibrated thresholds using the development data to optimise the F1 score, as shown in online supplemental table S1 and method 10. The predictive model’s calibration quality on the test set was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. We additionally evaluated the Brier score and calibration slope on the test set after recalibrating the raw predicted probabilities using the development data. The stability of feature selection and the robustness of primary model performance were evaluated through a subsampling-based analysis, with detailed procedures and results provided in online supplemental method 11. To assess the impact of hospital-related variables—whose timing relative to AD diagnosis could not be fully established—we evaluated a reduced model (ie, excluding these variables from the selected predictors) using the same subsampling protocol.

Sex-stratified risk prediction

The impact of sex on AD risk varies throughout the clinical and neuropathological progression of the condition. In a secondary analysis, we assessed sex-specific risk factors and risk prediction performance. We divided the dataset by sex and, following the same study protocol described above, conducted feature selection and built risk prediction models separately for males and females. For females, there were 4671 individuals in the control group and 455 in the AD group, while for males there were 7478 individuals in the control group and 472 in the AD group. These sex-stratified analyses aimed to identify both shared and sex-specific predictors of AD risk, providing deeper insights into how risk factors differ between sexes. To ensure the reliability of the sex-specific findings, the robustness of the sex-stratified models was further validated using a simplified repeated subsampling approach, as detailed in online supplemental method 11.

Results

Population characteristics

A total of 13 076 UKBB participants were eligible for our study, which included 927 cases who developed AD during the follow-up period. Most of these participants were of white ethnicity (99.4%), with an average age of 60.63 years (SD=5.21). Females comprised 39.2% (5126) of the participants, while males accounted for 60.8% (7950). The participants’ average educational level was 16.70 years (SD=2.33), while the imputed education average was 18.43 years (SD=3.14). Among the AD cases who developed dementia, 95.9% were of white ethnicity, with an average age of 64.61 years (SD=4.11), and females comprised 49.1% (n=455) of this subgroup. Feature selection and AD risk prediction

As detailed in the Feature selection section and figure 1, 636 features were initially identified through statistical analyses (step 1), among which 128 were further selected using the Boruta algorithm (step 2).

Performance metrics for various hyperparameter settings in the backward selection procedure (aggressive elimination threshold (AET), dynamic sampling threshold (DST), and conservative elimination threshold (CET)) are presented in online supplemental table S2. The model with AET=55, DST=0.6, and CET=40 achieved the best overall performance. We referred to this model as 55_0.6_40. Its iteration-wise performance on the development set is shown in online supplemental figure S2. The 55_0.6_40 model achieved the highest F1 score for most iterations, particularly after ten iterations, and was therefore selected for further investigation (see online supplemental method 7 for full definitions of AET, DST and CET).

Through backward feature selection, we identified 26 key predictors (table 2), which were categorised into four complementary domains: (1) clinical indicators and biometrics; (2) medical and hospital care-related data; (3) SDOH; and (4) genetics (specifically APOE genotype). Variable-importance scores extracted from the final C5.0 model are shown in online supplemental figure S3. These predictors underscore the multi-dimensional nature of AD risk predictors, reflecting the interplay between physiological, clinical, environmental and genetic factors. A detailed breakdown of these features and their relative importance is provided in online supplemental result 1, while their interpretation and comparisons with existing literature are presented in the Discussion section.

Discussion

Advances in AD risk models have increasingly incorporated clinical assessments, neuroimaging, genetic markers and lifestyle factors.22 23 Prior ML-based AD prediction studies using routinely collected data (eg, EHR or population cohorts) have consistently identified demographic, clinical history and lifestyle predictors as informative for dementia risk.11 Our results are consistent with prior evidence, APOE genotype remains a strong predictor of late-onset AD risk.24 A strength of this study is our systematic feature curation and preprocessing, in which we identified and categorised the full candidate feature set in UKBB and used a multistage feature selection strategy to prioritise an interpretable set of predictors, including SDOH-related markers. This provides additional context beyond clinical measures and supports more comprehensive risk profiling. The complexity of the UKBB data requires careful data preparation. Robust predictions require complete and reliable input data. In this study, standard preprocessing practices were applied to address missing data, categorical encoding and integration of diverse variables. These steps were essential given the scale and heterogeneity of the UKBB dataset (see online supplemental method 4).

The automated pipeline for feature selection was designed to manage the complexity of prediction models built from thousands of candidate features. By combining statistical screening, the Boruta algorithm and a modified backward elimination within the C5.0 framework,25 the model reduced predictors to a manageable set while retaining relevance to AD risk. The backward elimination procedure incorporated strategies to address data imbalance and improve computational efficiency. These adaptations supported both accuracy and interpretability, demonstrating the feasibility of scalable AD risk prediction in large datasets. Implementing this risk model in current electronic health record systems may be challenging because SDOH-related variables are often inconsistently captured in routinely collected clinical data.13

Prior studies have applied ML models to routinely collected data for AD risk prediction.11 12 Building on this literature, we evaluated SDOH-related markers alongside clinical and genetic predictors in UKBB. In our final model, SDOH-related variables contributed to prediction accuracy in addition to established clinical predictors, with eight of the 26 selected predictors falling within the SDOH category. Our findings align with previous reports, reinforcing the importance of incorporating diverse factors into AD risk prediction models.22

The observed associations between SDOH-related predictors and the AD outcome variable are informative for risk profiling, but should not be interpreted as causal. They may reflect confounding and/or mediation effects between SDOH (eg, health behaviours, socioeconomic status) and clinical variables (eg, comorbidity burden, healthcare access/utilisation and coding). For example, a participant may quit drinking due to poor baseline health condition, and both factors may be linked to high risk of developing AD in our model. In addition, early-life socioeconomic status (eg, lower educational attainment) may shape late-life health behaviours and disease risk, including AD, and thus may confound our analyses. Disentangling these pathways will require dedicated causal-inference or mediation analyses with appropriate longitudinal designs. Long-term exposure to air pollutants (eg, nitrogen oxides and PM2.5) has been reported to be associated with dementia outcomes in prior studies.26 27 In our model, these exposures acted as predictive markers; the observed associations may reflect a mixture of biological effects, residual confounding and correlated social or healthcare factors. Additional SDOH factors and factors that could be influenced by SDOH, such as home location,28 household size, dietary habits—particularly cereal intake,29 alcohol consumption, mobile phone usage30 and family status (eg, father alive), were SDOH-related predictors. While alcohol consumption was selected as a feature, as discussed previously, its association with AD is complex. In addition, excessive intake has been linked to increased dementia risk.2 These findings provide context for risk profiling and hypothesis generation, and motivate future causal-inference analyses.

Our multidomain feature selection identified AD risk predictors spanning clinical, medical and social domains. Notably, impaired pulmonary function (peak expiratory flow, forced vital capacity) showed the link between respiratory health and neurodegeneration. In the medical category, predictors such as specialty consultations and procedural codes proved significant; their routine availability and ease of measurement offer cost-effective screening opportunities. While sex-specific modelling identified consistent core predictors like APOE status alongside subgroup variations in lifestyle and clinical factors, these differences should be interpreted with caution due to potential sample size constraints and distribution shifts. A comprehensive interpretation of these predictors and their alignment with literature is provided in online supplemental discussion 1.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the model was developed in a UK-based cohort (UKBB), which is predominantly White (99.4%), and cohort characteristics and selection bias may limit generalisability to other populations and healthcare settings, particularly for predictors related to healthcare access and coding practices. External validation and recalibration in independent cohorts are therefore needed. Second, this is a predictive modelling study. The SDOH-related predictors selected by our model should be interpreted as associative risk markers; the observed associations may reflect residual confounding. For certain hospital-related variables, temporal ordering relative to AD diagnosis could not be fully established. However, reduced models excluding these variables yielded similar performance, suggesting they were not the primary drivers of the results. Third, the extensive feature set complicated missing data handling and contributed to participant exclusions, which may affect representativeness. Fourth, the initial statistical prescreening and subsequent feature-selection steps may have omitted relevant predictors. In the sex-stratified analyses, performance was compared descriptively, and formal tests were conducted for performance metrics. Calibration was also assessed separately by sex. It was adequate in females but less well calibrated in males, suggesting that improving calibration in males is an important direction for future work. Finally, systematic benchmarking against additional ensemble algorithms (eg, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) was not performed. Future work should prioritise external validation, formal cross-sex comparisons, broader model benchmarking and incorporation of longitudinal predictors to assess temporal trajectories of AD risk.

Conclusions

This study employed ML methods, including the C5.0 boosted tree with automated feature selection, to develop an interpretable AD risk model. Using a three-step feature selection procedure, the analysis identified key predictors from UKBB data, including SDOH. Separate sex-specific models were also developed, and the models showed strong predictive performance in the test set. Our findings highlight that more systematic collection of SDOHs in future EHR systems could meaningfully improve risk prediction and facilitate potential integration into routine clinical practice. However, external validation in more diverse cohorts is needed before broader application.
