Beyond the Catheter: Contemporary Aspiration Pump Systems in Neurointerventional Stroke Therapy
Authors: Sai Sanikommu, Connor Berger, Alejandro N. Santos, Caleigh Roach, Victor M. Lu, Emade Jaman, Sima M. Vazquez, Ahmed Abdelsalam, Romil Singh, Tiffany Eatz, Ali Mortezaei, Alick P. Wang, Evan Luther, John W. Thompson, Christopher P. Kellner, Robert M. Starke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute ischemic stroke is the greatest cause of morbidity and mortality among cerebrovascular pathologies. Over time, several therapeutic options have been developed for treating patients with stroke, including IV r-tPA (intravenous recombinant tissue-type plasminogen activator) and IV-TNK (intravenous tenecteplase) thrombolysis, and endovascular mechanical thrombectomy, with the latter showing favorable recanalization rates. Stent retrievers and mechanical aspiration, the 2 types of mechanical thrombectomy, are used for acute ischemic stroke due to large-vessel occlusions. The first reported aspiration thrombectomy dates back to 2002, during which a 50 mL syringe was used to generate negative pressure and aspirate a clot. Since then, extensive research and development have transformed aspiration thrombectomy. Penumbra’s first system paved the way for present-day aspiration systems in neurointerventional surgery, such as the Penumbra engine and Thunderbolt aspiration systems. Ongoing innovations have focused on improving access to challenging vascular locations and enabling the removal of large thrombi. Over time, aspiration techniques have also evolved. However, despite successful first-pass complete revascularization, a subset of patients continues to experience poor functional outcomes, highlighting the multifactorial nature of stroke recovery. In this review, we outline in detail the historical development of aspiration pump systems in neuroendovascular surgery, describe the different systems and their technical characteristics, highlight the clinical trials evaluating their efficacy, with particular attention to distinctions between devices, and discuss avenues for future growth.

Among various cerebrovascular pathologies, stroke is the primary cause of morbidity and mortality in the United States, with 795 000 individuals suffering from stroke every year.1 Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) accounts for 87% of strokes, making timely and effective clot removal treatment essential to decrease morbidity and mortality among these patients.1 Treatment options for AIS include thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy, with endovascular thrombectomy and medical therapy being the first choices when feasible.2–4 For decades, IV r-tPA and IV-TNK (intravenous recombinant tissue-type plasminogen activator and intravenous tenecteplase) have been the mainstay therapy, recommended within 4.5 hours of symptom onset and, in selected cases, up to 9 hours.2,5 However, IV-rtPA has been shown to have poor recanalization rates in cases, such as large-vessel occlusions (LVO), especially with clots larger than 8 mm, and carries a risk for intracranial hemorrhage in up to 2% to 7% of patients.5–7 In these scenarios, endovascular thrombectomy has become the cornerstone of therapy, typically performed with stent retrievers or aspiration techniques.8

Thrombo-aspiration was first reported in 2002, marking a major step in AIS treatment.9,10 Chapot et al described 2 successful basilar artery aspirations using a 50 mL syringe,11 paving the way for aspiration pumps and catheters.10,12 The ADAPT study (A Direct Aspiration First-Pass Technique) confirmed the safety and efficacy of aspiration thrombectomy in achieving Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (TICI) 2B-3 revascularization.10,12 While several landmark studies have demonstrated the efficacy of stent retrievers for endovascular thrombectomy,3,13,14 aspiration remains a noninferior option, with similar recanalization and clinical outcomes in LVO-AIS.15–18 Registry data further suggest that direct aspiration can be fast and effective, sometimes requiring fewer passes and shorter procedure times, though complication rates remain comparable.19 In practice, device choice is individualized, and combined strategies (eg, aspiration plus stent retriever) are commonly employed.20

Despite these advances, achieving consistent first-pass effect (FPE) and durable clot-catheter integration remains challenging in clinical practice. Catheter tip occlusion, variable clot composition, and vessel anatomy contribute to procedural inefficiency and incomplete clot retrieval. Although much of the focus has been on catheter design and procedural technique, pump-level characteristics, including pressure magnitude, waveform, and responsiveness, may independently modulate clot engagement and aspiration performance at the catheter–clot interface. In this review, we outline the evolution of aspiration thrombectomy, describe current aspiration pump systems and their technical properties, and summarize available clinical trial evidence, with particular attention to distinctions among devices.

Mechanisms of Aspiration in Mechanical Thrombectomy

Syringe-Based Aspiration Systems

Syringe aspiration systems, inspired by standard injection syringes, were the precursors of modern aspiration pumps (Figure 1A). Initially, air was expelled from the barrel using a piston with the proximal tip of the syringe connected to the aspiration catheter. The piston was then slowly and consistently pulled back to generate negative pressure, drawing the clot into the catheter at its distal end.21 This process requires the operator to use both hands (one to hold the barrel and the other to pull the piston backward to generate negative pressure). More recently, a new multisyringe model was developed to address the limitation of filling a single barrel before the procedure was complete22 (Figure 1B). Still, many neurointerventionalists continue to use syringes due to limited cost and often note that the data evidence supporting mechanical aspiration pumps is limited or flawed.

Syringe aspiration systems. A, Aspiration using a single syringe. B, Aspiration using multiple syringes to extend suction capacity.

Transition to Aspiration Pumps

Although syringe pumps are affordable and widely available, the difficulty in maintaining constant negative pressure led to the development of aspiration pumps. Unlike syringes, aspiration pumps provide continuous suction through a collection canister, which accommodates larger volumes of blood and clot.21 Successful aspiration usually depends on the thrombus composition, vessel characteristics, catheter used, properties of the aspiration pump, and the magnitude of the pressure used in the aspiration method.21 The only modifiable factor in this case is the pressure. Care must be taken to prevent mechanical damage to the vessel wall and collapse of the parent vessel and requires monitoring the volume of blood aspirated during the procedure.21 Given recent advances in the field, neuroendovascular surgeons have developed several new techniques to optimize procedural performance in aspiration thrombectomy.23 This review provides a detailed overview of technology advances in aspiration systems, approaches in aspiration, and evidence for adoption regarding outcomes in aspiration systems.

Continuous Negative Pressure Aspiration

Continuous negative pressure aspiration systems represent the most widely adopted approach in direct aspiration thrombectomy. The Penumbra aspiration ENGINE (Penumbra Inc, Alameda, CA) is the most established system in this category and serves as the foundation for the ADAPT strategy. In this technique, a large-bore catheter is advanced to the proximal face of the thrombus and connected to a suction pump that provides continuous negative pressure. This method enables en bloc removal of thrombus or aspiration of clot fragments into the catheter lumen. The initial feasibility study of ADAPT by Turk et al12 demonstrated high safety and efficacy, achieving TICI 2b/3 reperfusion in 78% of patients, with 40% achieving a favorable 90-day outcome (modified Rankin Scale score 0–2). More recently, the multicenter COMPLETE registry (International Acute Ischemic Stroke Registry With the Penumbra System Aspiration Including the 3D Revascularization Device) reported successful reperfusion in 87.8% of patients treated with aspiration-first approaches, with good functional outcomes in 55.8% at 90 days.24 While constant aspiration has proven effective and is now widely available, an important limiting factor includes corking, where the clot becomes lodged at the catheter tip, often requiring multiple passes, and potentially leading to distal embolization. These challenges have driven the development of cyclic aspiration systems, designed to optimize suction dynamics, enhance complete clot ingestion (CCI), where the entire clot is pulled into the aspiration catheter, and improve the FPE beyond what constant negative pressure pumps can achieve.25–27

Cyclic and Pulsatile Aspiration Systems

The terms cyclic and pulsatile are used interchangeably among interventionalists. Pulsatile aspiration systems have gained increasing attention in recent years, as preclinical and early clinical studies suggest potential advantages over continuous aspiration.25,28 The waveforms produced by these pumps are depicted in Figure 2.

Different types of mechanisms used in present-day aspiration pumps. APA indicates adaptive pulsatile aspiration.

Cyclic aspiration differs from continuous suction in that it introduces time-varying pressure at the catheter-clot interface. This oscillatory pressure profile may enhance clot ingestion by periodically reducing interfacial resistance, thereby promoting incremental clot deformation and advancement into the catheter.29–31 In contrast to steady suction, pulsatile aspiration may also mitigate catheter tip occlusion by intermittently relieving negative pressure, thereby reducing the likelihood of complete flow arrest.29–31 The effectiveness of cyclic aspiration is influenced by waveform characteristics, including amplitude, frequency, and duty cycle, which together determine the efficiency of pressure transmission and clot engagement.29–31

These pumps produce pulsatile aspiration pressure through various modulation techniques, with novel pumps being developed and tested at various levels.25,28,32 Bench studies further demonstrated that achieving CCI, rather than partial corking, could be a key determinant of FPE success and reduced distal embolization.25,28,33 A recent in vitro bench top study comparing constant and pulsatile aspiration showed that pulsatile aspiration may improve clot ingestion dynamics and reduce clot fragmentation under simulated conditions.28 Another study by Arslanian et al demonstrated that cyclical ADAPT enhanced CCI, leading to higher first-pass recanalization rates and fewer distal emboli.25 Inspired by such preclinical findings, Kalousek et al34 conducted the first clinical experience study using the CLEAR Aspiration System (Insera Therapeutics Inc, Dallas, TX) in 40 patients, achieving high TICI 3 FPE in large-vessel strokes. Bajrami et al34 later confirmed good recanalization rates with the Rapidpulse cyclic aspiration system (Rapid Pulse, Miami, FL) in a multicenter prospective study. More recently, Silva et al demonstrated in a preclinical model that the ALGO system (Von Vascular Inc, Sunrise, FL), which employs adaptive catheter-specific pulsatile aspiration (adaptive pulsatile aspiration [APA]), achieved faster recanalization, improved FPE, and fewer distal emboli in simulated LVO models.26,32,33,35 It also switches to static mode for continuous aspiration if corking is detected. APA fundamentally differs from traditional cyclic aspiration by using real-time feedback to customize each suction pulse at the catheter-clot interface.26,32,33,35 Although cyclic aspiration applies fixed, preset oscillations that remain the same regardless of clot resistance or behavior,28 APA continuously adjusts the amplitude, frequency, and waveform of suction to improve clot engagement and maintain steady flow.26,32,33,35 This adaptive adjustment prevents catheter tip stall, reduces sudden pressure changes that can fragment the clot, and boosts overall clot removal efficiency.26,32,33,35 Essentially, APA offers intelligent, responsive aspiration, whereas cyclic aspiration provides a uniform, nonadaptive pattern in all conditions.26,28,32,33,35 These findings are derived from preclinical models, and their translation into clinical outcomes in patients remains to be established.

Pressure Reporting

Unless otherwise specified, pressure values are reported as negative gauge pressure (vacuum) relative to atmospheric pressure, in mm Hg, and represent the manufacturer-reported maximum pump suction rather than catheter tip pressure. For pulsatile systems, reported values reflect peak negative pressure; time-varying waveform characteristics are described where available.

Penumbra Aspiration Systems (Penumbra Inc, CA)

Penumbra Aspiration Systems were first introduced in 2009 after receiving Food and Drug Administration clearance for the revascularization of patients with AIS due to LVO within 8 hours of symptom onset. This system was comprised of a catheter, separator, and an aspiration pump capable of generating high negative pressure, connected to a canister for collecting aspirated material.39 Over time, Penumbra introduced a variety of different aspiration catheters and pumps, giving rise to different Penumbra Aspiration Systems39 (Figure 3).

Timeline of aspiration thrombectomy systems (2005–2025), showing the progressive introduction of Penumbra devices and other platforms (Gomco, Riptide, AXS, CLEAR, Zoom, RapidPulse, and ALGO), reflecting continuous advances in aspiration technology for stroke treatment. ACE indicates angiotensin-converting enzyme.

Original Penumbra Aspiration System (Penumbra Inc, CA)

The original Penumbra Aspiration System was a first-generation Penumbra device, primarily designed for occlusions in the internal carotid artery, the M1 and M2 segments of the middle cerebral artery, and the vertebral and basilar arteries.40,41 This system consists of a Penumbra Gomco aspiration pump, a thrombus removal ring, a reperfusion catheter, and a separator, which comes in various sizes (026, 032, 041, and 054)41 (Table S1). Catheter and separator size selection is based on the diameter of the target vessel lumen.39,41

The Gomco aspiration pump is the source of vacuum pressure in original Penumbra systems and can create a continuous static pressure of −20 mm Hg. The Penumbra Pivotal Stroke Trial, conducted by the Penumbra Pivotal Stroke Trial Investigators, evaluated the safety and efficacy of the system and reported an 81.6% treated vessels were successfully revascularized to Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 2 to 3.42 At 90 days, 25% of patients achieved a modified Rankin Scale of 0 to 1.42 These results demonstrate favorable revascularization performance, which is associated with improved neurological outcomes, although a direct causal relationship specific to device performance cannot be established from these data.

Pump Max Aspiration Systems (Penumbra Inc, CA)

After adoption of the original system, Penumbra introduced the MAX aspiration catheters. Their advantage was single-operator, over-the-wire delivery without requiring a proximal balloon guide. Catheters came in 3 sizes: 3MAX, 4MAX, and 5MAX, along with MAX aspiration tubing.39 With these large-bore designs and new catheter technology, ADAPT emerged.12,42 The ADAPT FAST trial (A Direct Aspiration First Pass Technique for Acute Stroke Thrombectomy) using 5MAX and 5MAX ACE catheters achieved successful revascularization (TICI ≥2b) in 78% of cases.12

Between 2013 and 2016, Penumbra introduced ACE family aspiration catheters and the Pump MAX pump, which is compatible with both MAX and ACE catheters and can generate a continuous pressure of −27.8 mm Hg.39,43 ACE catheters offered larger distal diameters and came in sizes ACE60, ACE64, and ACE68. In the PROMISE study (Prospective, Real-World Observational Multicenter Ischemic Stroke Study With ACE Reperfusion Catheters), ACE64/68 achieved successful revascularization (TICI ≥2b) in 93.1% of patients.44

Penumbra 3-Dimensional Revascularization Device (Penumbra Inc, CA)

In 2017, Penumbra introduced a 3-dimensional revascularization device to capture the clot easily along with reperfusion catheters.39 It featured intraluminal chambers, petal-like clot traps, 4 distal markers for positioning, and minimized vessel wall contact. The device was compatible with RED, JET 7, and ACE catheters.39 The COMPLETE registry, a global multicenter study across 42 centers, reported 87.8% successful revascularization and 55.8% good functional outcomes (modified Rankin Scale score ≤2) at 90 days.24

Penumbra Aspiration System (Penumbra Inc, CA; Penumbra Engine, JET, and RED Family Catheters)

In 2019, Penumbra introduced a new aspiration pump, Penumbra ENGINE, along with JET family reperfusion catheters, giving rise to Penumbra Aspiration System. Penumbra ENGINE can deliver and maintain a continuous static pure vacuum of −29.2 mm Hg, which helps in aspirating large clots easily.39 JET catheters (JET 7, JET D) featured large distal tips, progressive distal coil winding for flexibility, and Quad-Wire technology for shaft stability.45 It is validated by progressive distal coil wind, which enhances flexibility and incorporates 20 transitions, from the proximal shaft to distal tip, to facilitate trackability and navigation. The proximal shaft is equipped with Quad-Wire technology, providing easier catheter advancement. A multicenter observational study using JET 7 with ADAPT showed 92.6% successful revascularization, 42.9% good outcomes (modified Rankin Scale score 0–2) at 90 days, serious adverse effects were seen in 5.6%, and mortality occurred in 10.9%.46

Penumbra later introduced RED catheters with REDglide coating for improved delivery in tortuous anatomy. RED catheters ranged from RED 43 to RED 72, with the latter offering the highest aspiration force.39 These catheters are highly flexible, equipped with precision tips, an articulating marker band, and REDglide coating, making them suitable for small vessel occlusions in complex vascular anatomy. SENDIT technology was later added to RED 72 to simplify preparation and facilitate delivery.39 The INSIGHT registry, a prospective multicenter study stratifying patients by clot type (firm red, soft red, firm white, and soft white), demonstrated that 95.5% of firm red clots and 100% of soft red, firm white, and soft white clots achieved a final modified TICI score >2b with RED catheters.36

Penumbra System Including Thunderbolt Aspiration Tubing (Thunderbolt; Penumbra Inc, CA)

Penumbra developed integrated computer-assisted vacuum thrombectomy, specifically Thunderbolt aspiration tubing, into the existing Penumbra Aspiration System. This new aspiration tubing facilitates modulated cyclic aspiration in AIS patients with LVO. Thunderbolt uses a solenoid valve based on an on-off mechanism to generate cyclic aspiration. A multicenter, prospective, single-arm study, THUNDER (Acute Ischemic Stroke Study With the Penumbra System Including Thunderbolt Aspiration Tubing), is currently underway, involving a clinical event committee and a core laboratory for the independent assessment of safety and imaging outcomes of this novel device (URL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT05437055).47 Many centers across the United States are currently enrolled in this study, patient enrollment has been completed, and awaiting for final results.

Gomco 405 Aspiration Pump (Microvention Terumo Inc, CA)

The Gomco 405 aspiration pump (Microvention Terumo Inc, CA) is a vacuum aspirator suction pump manufactured by Microvention Terumo.48 It features a pressure control knob delivering continuous static suction up to −27 mm Hg and uses a 1500 mL plastic canister. In combination with the SOFIA Flow Plus Aspiration Catheter and MicroVention Tubing Kit, it is indicated for revascularization in AIS secondary to LVO within 8 hours of symptom onset.48 A prospective, multicenter study by Gandhi et al37 SOFAST (SOFIA Aspiration System as First-Line Technique) showed SOFIA Flow Plus catheter aspiration system, is safe and effective with high revascularization rates (modified TICI >2b in 97.2% of the patients) in LVOs.

AXS Universal Aspiration Set (Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, CA)

Two companies, Stryker and Medela, joined together to develop the AXS Universal aspiration system. It is comprised of a Medela Dominant flux surgical suction pump which provides continuous static pressure between −68 and −95 kPa, AXS Universal liner set and aspiration tubing, and AXS Catalyst distal access catheter.49 The catheter facilitates delivery of neurointerventional devices into target vessels and can also serve as a conduit for retrieval devices. The system is indicated for use in LVO involving the internal carotid artery, M1 and M2 segments of the middle cerebral artery, as well as the basilar and vertebral arteries, within 8 hours of symptom onset.49

Riptide Aspiration System (Medtronic Inc, Irvine, CA)

Introduced by Medtronic in 2017, the Riptide aspiration system comprises an aspiration pump, a collection canister with intermediate tubing, large-bore aspiration tubing, and React 68/71 and ARC-132 catheters.50 The pump generates a continuous static vacuum of 20 to 25 mm Hg.50 The recommended blood vessel diameter is >2.5 mm for ARC-132 and >2.7 mm for React 68 and 71 catheters, for their respective uses.50

Zoom Stroke Solution (Imperative Care, Campbell, CA)

Food and Drug Administration–approved in April 2021, the Zoom system combines aspiration with mechanical clot retrieval. It includes an aspiration pump, Zoom POD aspiration tubing, aspiration canister, and catheters in 4 sizes (Zoom 35, 45, 55, and 71).51 The pump generates −29.5 mm Hg continuous static suction.51 The system incorporates a dual-aspiration tubing configuration, with the Zoom POD designed to capture clots in the sterile field while maintaining continuous aspiration flow.51 Unlike most systems that divert clot and blood to a remote canister, the Zoom POD captures clot at the sterile field, providing immediate visual confirmation of retrieval and allowing embolic material analysis.51 One study showed shorter puncture-to-procedure times with Zoom catheters (46.42 minutes) compared with other devices (62.38 minutes).52 Clot capture also makes it easier to send the embolic material for analysis. Additionally, the Zoom aspiration catheter is noted to be flexible and adaptable in design, allowing for efficient navigation through tortuous cerebrovasculature.

The system is supported by 3 insert types (Zoom 6F Insert Catheter SIM [Simmons], VTK [Vertebral], and VRT [Vitek]) and 3 access catheters (Zoom 88 Large Distal Platform, Zoom 88 Support, Zoom RDL Radial Access).51 Mack et al18 recently published the results of the Imperative Trial, showing that successful reperfusion was achieved in 83% (216/259 [95% CI, 78%–89%]) within 3 passes. Final reperfusion was obtained in 92% of cases, with 5.5% requiring rescue devices. First-pass modified TICI 2b-3 success was achieved in 57%, and the median puncture-to-reperfusion time was 19 minutes.18 At 90 days, 55% of patients achieved functional independence (modified Rankin Scale score 0–2).18 The rate of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage was 1.9%, and overall 90-day mortality was 12.7%.18

CLEAR Aspiration System (Insera Therapeutics Inc, Dallas, TX)

The CLEAR Aspiration System is among the first commercial thrombectomy platforms to offer both continuous negative pressure and cyclic (pulsatile) aspiration. Unlike conventional pumps, CLEAR modulates suction pressure and timing, producing cycles of vacuum designed to enhance clot-catheter interaction and reduce tip corking. It generates up to −98.2 kPa mm Hg and allows adjustment of duty cycle according to clot type and catheter size. This design emphasizes CCI as the mechanism to improve FPE and reduce distal embolization.

In the CLEAR study, 40 patients with LVO stroke treated with cyclic aspiration achieved high first-pass TICI 3 rates with a favorable safety profile.34 This study provided the first-in-human confirmation that cyclic aspiration could translate bench findings, improve clot ingestion, and reduce clot fragmentation into clinical benefit, achieving a TICI 3 first-pass rate of 68% (76% TICI 2c/3) and excellent final reperfusion outcomes.34 The device has obtained CE (Conformité Européenne or European Conformity) Mark approval in Europe for use in ischemic stroke, pulmonary embolism, and deep vein thrombosis, although it is not yet widely available in the United States.

Rapid Pulse Aspiration System (Rapid Pulse, Miami, FL)

Rapid Pulse Cyclic Aspiration System is a mechanical thrombectomy cyclical aspiration system, delivering high-frequency pulsed vacuum forces to increase the efficiency of aspiration thrombectomy.53 Its oscillating suction is regulated by a valve box with a proprietary algorithm. Developed by Rapid Pulse, the system includes a Rapid Pulse aspiration pump, a collection canister with intermediate tubing, large-bore tubing, and a Rapid Pulse catheter. The catheter shaft has a hydrophilic coating to reduce friction during navigation.53 The system is currently enrolling patients in prospective multicenter clinical studies evaluating its safety and efficacy. A prospective multicenter 2-arm study comparing Rapid Pulse to controls reported modified TICI ≥2c after first pass in 53.9% of patients, versus 38.5% in controls.38 While preliminary data are promising, further development and trials are needed.

ALGO Smart Pump (Von Vascular) With APA

The ALGO Smart Pump is a compact, operator-side aspiration pump that operates in either APA mode (noncontinuous, algorithmic cycling of suction) or smart static mode (continuous). The engineering improves clot-catheter interaction and thereby increases the likelihood of CCI, and thereby boost first-pass recanalization and reduces distal emboli by tailoring the suction waveform delivered to the catheter-clot interface rather than relying solely on higher steady vacuum. It also provides adaptive catheter-specific cyclic aspiration and automatically switches to static mode if corking or disruption in flow is detected. Unlike conventional cyclic aspiration systems that generate suction cycles using solenoid valve–based mechanisms, the ALGO pump employs motorized vacuum modulation with internal waveform shaping, enabling adaptive, catheter-specific cyclic aspiration. This methodology is completely distinct from Penumbra thunderBOLT, Rapid Pulse, Insera Therapeutics, and other cyclical pumps, although comparative clinical data is limited.

Several preclinical benchtop studies showed the favorable performance of attaining CCI and FPE with this pump in both APA and Static Continuous aspiration.27,32,33,35 In a comparative study of 25 aspiration catheters, APA delivered via ALGO demonstrated consistent clot ingestion under simulated LVO conditions, with CCI in the majority of trials, supporting the notion that a pulsatile profile improves clot ingestion mechanics across different catheter types and sizes.32 Another study by Lu et al showed the APA mode of the ALGO pump was associated with higher rates of CCI and reduced clot fragmentation compared with conventional continuous aspiration in vitro when compared with the Penumbra pump (80% versus 38.6%).26 It also showed higher CCI rates in static mode than the Penumbra pump (85.6% versus 75.6%).35 To date, the evidence is limited to preclinical models; rigorous clinical trials and real-world studies are still needed.

Although these findings highlight the importance of pump-level innovation, aspiration efficacy is not determined by suction waveform alone. The effectiveness of clot ingestion depends critically on the catheter-to-artery ratio, which governs the degree of flow arrest and local pressure transmission at the clot interface.54 Larger catheter diameters relative to the target vessel enhance aspiration efficiency by improving seal formation and reducing antegrade flow, thereby facilitating clot capture.55–57 In contrast, suboptimal catheter sizing may limit even advanced pulsatile systems from achieving consistent CCI.54,58 Additionally, clot composition and vessel compliance further modulate the response to applied suction, underscoring that aspiration performance arises from the interaction among pump dynamics, catheter geometry, thrombus characteristics, and vascular anatomy rather than any single component in isolation.30,58–60 Emerging data from adaptive pulsatile systems such as the ALGO pump suggest that waveform modulation may partially mitigate these limitations, but remains dependent on adequate catheter-vessel matching.26,27,32,33,35,61

Conclusions

The evolution of aspiration systems has substantially advanced the technical capabilities of mechanical thrombectomy, with modern devices showing improved clot-catheter integration and higher rates of first-pass recanalization in preclinical and early clinical studies. Innovations such as large-bore catheters and APA systems have enhanced clot ingestion dynamics and broadened the feasibility of treating more complex and distal occlusions. However, despite these technical advances, translation into consistent improvements in angiographic and patient-centered outcomes remains variable. Procedural success continues to depend on the interplay among device design, catheter-vessel compatibility, clot composition, and vascular anatomy.

Emerging systems, including RapidPulse, the ALGO pump, and Penumbra platforms such as Thunderbolt, are undergoing clinical evaluation. While these technologies show promise for optimizing aspiration efficiency, robust evidence demonstrating superiority in functional outcomes, complication rates, and long-term recovery remains limited. Future studies should focus on integrating device innovation with patient-specific and anatomic factors to better define the conditions under which technical improvements translate into meaningful clinical benefit.
