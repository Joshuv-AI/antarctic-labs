Behavioral and psychosocial correlates of awake bruxism behaviors in esports athletes: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Caio Sberni Pinheiro Souza, Luiz Guilherme Spadon-Brito, Maria Amália Dias Pereira Calças, Rafael Chadud Matoso-Filho, Nykolas Jorge Silva Castaldi, Melissa Oliveira Melchior, Fabiane Carneiro Lopes-Olhê, Lais Valencise Magri, Jardel Francisco Mazzi-Chaves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAwake bruxism (AB) has been little explored in esports. This study estimated the frequency of self-reported AB behaviors, described behavioral features, and identified associated factors in esports practitioners. In this cross-sectional study, 447 esports players from São Paulo, Brazil, completed self-report questionnaires using items derived from the BruxScreen protocol and the Standardized Tool for the Assessment of Bruxism (STAB). AB behaviors were assessed using a frequency-based approach, and participants were stratified into low- and high-frequency groups. Overall, 35% met the criterion for frequent AB. Compared with low-frequency individuals, high-frequency participants more often reported clenching (46.7% vs. 9.1%) and daily mandibular pain (39.1% vs. 7.2%). Behavior frequency correlated with previous AB history (ρ = 0.49), psychological distress (ρ = 0.42), and gastroesophageal symptoms (ρ = 0.37) (all p < 0.001), while male sex showed an inverse correlation (ρ = − 0.27; p < 0.001). In multivariable regression, previous AB history (B = 0.75; 95% CI 0.55–0.95), psychological distress (B = 0.06; 95% CI 0.03–0.10), and gastroesophageal symptoms (B = 0.24; 95% CI 0.11–0.36) remained independently associated (p < 0.001). The model explained 36% of the variance. AB behaviors are common in esports and reflect a multifactorial phenomenon influenced by psychosocial and contextual factors, supporting the need for preventive and educational strategies in this population and clinical awareness.Keywords: Esports, Self-reported behaviors, Psychosocial factors, Gaming-related factorsSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Psychology, Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

Esports, or electronic sports, refer to competitive virtual gaming1–3. Unlike traditional sports, their dynamics depend on electronic systems that mediate interactions in digital environments, requiring high levels of cognitive and motor precision4,5. Originally conceived as entertainment, Esports have evolved into a global phenomenon, with increasing professionalization, structured leagues, international tournaments, and an expanding economic impact valued in billions of dollars4,6,7.

Worldwide, more than 2.2 billion individuals engage in digital gaming, of whom approximately 150 million participate in organized competitions8. Since their inclusion as a demonstration event at the 2018 Asian Games and as an official discipline in 2023, esports have expanded across genres such as first-person shooters, sports simulations, and multiplayer online battle arenas9. This growth reflects not only technological progress but also sociocultural shifts, particularly among younger populations1,10.

In Brazil, the Pesquisa Game Brasil11 reported that 73.9% of the population plays digital games, and 20% are directly engaged in esports. Competitive players are predominantly men (62.9%), whereas women represent the majority of gamers overall (50.9%). The highest proportion of players falls between 30 and 39 years of age (33%), with 52.3% identifying as Black or mixed-race, emphasizing the inclusivity and cultural significance of the Brazilian esports scene11. Popular titles include Free Fire, FIFA, GTA, League of Legends (LoL), and Counter-Strike: Global Offensive (CS:GO). Access differs according to profile: casual players favor smartphones, while competitive practitioners rely on computers and consoles11.

Despite this diversity and accessibility, competitive esports environments have been linked to health risks. Evidence points to the frequent use of stimulants and psychoactive substances to enhance attention, reduce fatigue, and improve performance4,9,12. Although such supplementation can improve accuracy and reaction times4,9, excessive use raises concerns about physical, mental, and social health12.

Psychological impacts are equally significant. esports have been associated with anxiety, depression, stress, isolation, and mental and physical fatigue8,10,13. Prolonged training sessions, often conducted at night, combined with extended screen exposure and high luminance, compromise sleep quality8,13. Recent studies show that blue light and irregular schedules reduce REM sleep, increase fragmentation, and elevate muscular hyperactivity during wakefulness, conditions linked to bruxism14,15.

In addition to sleep-related pathways, awake bruxism may also be sustained by prolonged low-level isometric activation (“bracing”) of the masticatory muscles during wakefulness. In this perspective, emotional or cognitive load may be transferred into increased muscle tension, potentially contributing to fatigue, related symptoms and functional overload of myofascial and articular tissues16,17. Recent evidence also supports a linear association between the frequency of specific awake bruxism behaviors and psychological distress indices (e.g., anxiety/depression screening), reinforcing the relevance of stress-related vulnerability in wake-time masticatory muscle activity18. Competitive esports practice requires prolonged vigilance, attentional engagement, and performance pressure, which may plausibly increase the likelihood of sustained jaw muscle tension during wakefulness; therefore, esports athletes may be predisposed to this cascade of events, similarly to other performance domains that demand sustained concentration17,19. The concomitant use of stimulants and psychosocial stress may exacerbate behaviors recognized as risk factors for bruxism20,21.

Bruxism is currently conceptualized primarily as a behavior rather than a disorder or disease entity, and its clinical relevance depends on the context in which it occurs, its consequences, and associated comorbidities22–25. From this perspective, bruxism has been described as a “medical gateway” for dentistry, promoting a broader interpretation of masticatory muscle activities within general health frameworks24. Bruxism may occur during sleep (sleep bruxism, SB) or during wakefulness (awake bruxism, AB)22–25. SB is characterized by rhythmic or non-rhythmic masticatory muscle activity during sleep26,27, whereas AB is defined by repetitive or sustained tooth contact and/or sustained mandibular postures during wakefulness, including phenotypes such as clenching, light tooth contact, and muscle bracing20,22. In the present study, we specifically focus on AB phenotypes during wakefulness, particularly sustained muscle tension/bracing, as the esports environment is characterized by prolonged vigilance and performance-related demands that may promote wake-time masticatory muscle activity.

Epidemiological studies provide frequency estimates of self-reported awake bruxism (AB) behaviors in adults, typically ranging from 15 to 25%28–30, with higher estimates reported in some athlete populations (e.g., 29.3%)31. In South America, estimates may reach ~ 30% 30, suggesting potential cultural and behavior influences. Importantly, AB behaviors may fluctuate within individuals and may vary across clinical contexts, which limits the interpretation of a single cross-sectional estimate as a stable trait29.

Both SB and AB can lead to muscle fatigue, headache, temporomandibular pain, may act as a risk factor for a negative outcomes such as mechanical tooth wear and potentially for technical complications or failures of fixed prosthodontic restorations and implant-supported rehabilitations, although comparative clinical evidence across restorative materials remains limited, impairing quality of life13,16,24,25,32. These consequences may be intensified by prolonged gaming and stimulant use, affecting not only oral and systemic health but also cognitive and emotional performance in competition 13,16.

Although associations between esports, psychosocial symptoms, and sleep alterations are well documented, evidence on awake bruxism behaviors (AB) in esports players remains limited and is based on few cross-sectional studies using different self-report frameworks and outcome operationalization’s33,34. Therefore, the present study aimed to determine AB behaviors frequency in esports practitioners, characterize behavior and phenotypic features, and evaluate associated predictors in a larger Brazilian sample, thereby supporting preventive and interventional strategies to promote health and optimize competitive outcomes.

Methods

Ethical aspects

This cross-sectional observational study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the School of Dentistry of Ribeirão Preto, University of São Paulo (FORP/USP) (CAAE nº 85129824.3.0000.5419), in accordance with the ethical principles governing human research. All participants provided written informed consent after being informed of the study objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits, as required by Resolution No. 466/12 of the National Health Council. Data collection was conducted through Google Forms, and analyses were performed at the Laboratory of Research in Temporomandibular Disorders and Orofacial Pain, FORP/USP.

Sample

A total of 447 esports practitioners (men and women) participated in the study between November 2024 and June 2025. Eligible participants were required to have engaged in esports activities for at least six months, with a minimum frequency of three sessions per week; reside in the state of São Paulo; demonstrate the cognitive ability to complete the questionnaires; provide informed consent; and have internet access. Individuals undergoing active interventions for bruxism were excluded. These eligibility criteria were adopted to enhance sample homogeneity and ensure the reliability of the findings.

AB behaviors were assessed using items derived from the BruxScreen protocol35. Participants reported the frequency of the following behaviors during wakefulness: (a) teeth clenching; (b) teeth grinding; (c) non-functional tooth contact, defined as touching, pressing, or holding the teeth together outside of eating; and (d) jaw muscle bracing, characterized by firm contraction of the facial muscles without tooth contact. Responses were recorded on a five-point ordinal Likert scale ranging from never to always33.

To capture awake bruxism as a behavior continuum, responses were numerically coded from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“always”), and an score was calculated as the mean of the four items. Scores were computed when at least three responses were available, while “don’t know” responses were treated as missing. This dimensional approach was adopted to improve statistical sensitivity and avoid arbitrary diagnostic thresholds33.

For descriptive and interpretative purposes, participants were additionally stratified according to the sample median of the score into low-frequency awake bruxism behaviors (L-AB) and high-frequency awake bruxism behaviors (H-AB). Individuals with scores above the median were allocated to the H-AB group, whereas those with scores at or below the median were assigned to the L-AB group. This stratification was used exclusively to enhance clinical interpretability and did not replace the primary dimensional analyses.

BruxScreen-derived self-report items were administered in Brazilian Portuguese using a non-official translation/adaptation guided by established cross-cultural procedures for self-report measures36. No EMA monitoring or instrumental component was used.

Clinical assessments

Data collection instruments included:

Sociodemographic and behaviour questionnaire: A semi-structured questionnaire gathered information on gender, age, socioeconomic level, occupation, education, alcohol, tobacco, drug, juice and soda consumption, medication use, comorbidities, physical activity, daily gaming time, and gaming platforms.

The items were administered using a non-official Brazilian Portuguese translation/adaptation for questionnaire administration, guided by established cross-cultural adaptation procedures for self-report measures35,36.

Both instruments are internationally proposed, consensus-informed assessment frameworks for bruxism. In the present cross-sectional survey, we implemented selected subject-based items derived from the BruxScreen (self-report module) and STAB (Axes A and B), suitable for large-scale epidemiological research.

Statistical analysis

Group comparisons were performed using chi-square tests (χ2) for categorical variables and Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, according to data distribution. Awake bruxism was subsequently analyzed as a behavior continuum using the Awake Bruxism Frequency Score. Bivariate associations between bruxism frequency and the investigated variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Variables showing significant associations and clinical relevance were then entered into a multivariable linear regression model to identify factors independently associated with awake bruxism frequency while minimizing multicollinearity. The final model demonstrated moderate explanatory capacity, accounting for approximately 36% of the variance in behavior frequency (adjusted R2 ≈ 0.35). Prior awake bruxism history, psychological distress, and gastroesophageal symptoms remained independently associated with higher bruxism frequency after adjustment, whereas psychotropic medication use, sex, caffeine intake, alcohol consumption, and gaming time were not independently associated. All analyses were conducted using JASP software (version 0.18.3), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and behavior characteristics

Significant differences were observed between groups with high and low frequency of AB behaviors. Sex was associated with high frequency of AB behaviors (H-AB) (p = 0.01): females predominated among individuals with high frequency of AB behaviors (H-AB) (58.6%), whereas males were more frequent in the non-AB group (71.7%), suggesting a protective effect of male sex. Although household income and physical activity did not differ significantly (p > 0.05), Higher frequency of AB behaviors was observed among those in lower income brackets (≤ three minimum wages). Moreover, a higher proportion of individuals with high frequency of AB behaviors (H-AB) reported regular physical activity, although this was not a risk factor. Age also showed a strong association (p < 0.0001); with high frequency of AB behaviors (H-AB) more common among participants over 30 years (43.9%), while younger individuals (< 24 years) predominated in the non-AB group (Table 1).

Esports practice-related variables

Chi-square analysis showed that H-AB participants were more intensively engaged in esports. A total of 37.3% of individuals with high frequency of AB behaviors (H-AB) had practiced for > 5 years, compared with 20.3% of L-AB (p = 0.0001). Similarly, 20% of H-AB participants played > 6 h/day, versus 7.9% of L-AB participants (p = 0.0003). Multiple platform use was also higher among the H-AB group, with 22.7% using ≥ 3 devices compared with 7.9% of the L-AB group (p = 0.0001) (Table 2). These findings suggest that prolonged exposure and multitasking during gaming may predispose to AB behaviors through mechanisms involving stress, attentional hyper focus, and sustained muscular tension.

Orofacial behaviors and symptoms

Participants with high frequency of AB behaviors (H-AB) reported significantly higher frequencies of clenching, grinding, light tooth contact, and facial muscle tension. For example, 46.7% of H-AB individuals reported clenching frequently, compared with 9.1% in the L-AB group, while 22% of L-AB individuals reported never clenching versus only 0.7% of those with high frequency of AB behaviors (H-AB). Muscle tensing without occlusion was also highly prevalent, with only 1.3% of H-AB individuals reporting never engaging in this behavior, compared with 21.6% of L-AB participants. Notably, 43.0% of the H-AB group selected “Don’t know” for tooth grinding, suggesting limited awareness of this behavior (Table 3; Fig. 1).

Psychosocial and behavior factors

Psychosocial factors were also significantly associated with high frequency of AB behaviors (H-AB). Anxiety/depression scores were higher in the H-AB group (mean = 2.29 ± 0.66) compared with L-AB participants (mean = 1.67 ± 0.73; p < 0.00001). Gastroesophageal reflux scores were similarly elevated in H-AB individuals (mean = 1.65 ± 0.57 vs. 1.34 ± 0.54; p < 0.0001). Substance use also differed significantly. Psychotropic medication use was more common in H-AB participants (24.6%) than in L-AB participants (12.6%; p = 0.0044). Alcohol consumption was reported by 44.0% of H-AB individuals versus 30.8% of L-AB individuals (p = 0.0097), and coffee consumption by 70.1% versus 52.8%, respectively (p = 0.0008). A previous history of AB behaviors was also more frequent among H-AB participants (38.2% vs. 20.6%; p = 0.0001) (Table 6).

Correlation teste and regression model

Correlation analyses demonstrated significant associations between awake bruxism frequency and several behavior and clinical variables (Table 7). Higher Awake Bruxism Frequency Scores were moderately correlated with previous bruxism history (ρ = 0.49, p < 0.001), psychological distress as measured by the PHQ-4 (ρ = 0.42, p < 0.001), and gastroesophageal symptoms (ρ = 0.37, p < 0.001). A weaker but statistically significant positive correlation was observed for psychotropic medication use (ρ = 0.20, p < 0.001). Conversely, male sex showed a significant inverse association with bruxism frequency (ρ = − 0.27, p < 0.001), indicating lower reported behavior frequency among men. Gaming time exhibited a small negative correlation with awake bruxism (ρ = − 0.14, p = 0.005). No significant associations were identified for daily caffeine intake (ρ = 0.07, p = 0.159) or alcohol consumption (ρ = − 0.05, p = 0.292) (Fig. 3).

Multivariable linear regression analysis identified factors independently associated with awake bruxism frequency (Table 8). Previous awake bruxism history emerged as the strongest predictor (B = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.55–0.95, p < 0.001). Higher psychological distress, as measured by the PHQ-4 total score, was also significantly associated with greater behavior frequency (B = 0.06, 95% CI: 0.03–0.10, p < 0.001), as were gastroesophageal symptoms (B = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.11–0.36, p < 0.001). Psychotropic medication use, male sex, daily caffeine consumption, gaming time, and alcohol consumption were not independently associated with awake bruxism after adjustment for covariates (p > 0.05). Notably, although gaming time showed a small correlation with bruxism frequency in the bivariate analysis, this association did not remain significant in the adjusted model. Overall, the regression model demonstrated moderate explanatory capacity, accounting for approximately 36% of the variance in awake bruxism frequency.

Discussion

Using a frequency-based, self-report operational approach derived from adapted BruxScreen/STAB items, 35% of participants met the operational frequency-based criterion for frequent awake bruxism (AB) behaviors at the time of assessment. Higher AB behaviors reporting was associated with psychosocial and demographic variables, including anxiety/depression, gastroesophageal reflux, psychotropic medication use, alcohol and coffee intake, and a previous history of AB. In addition, longer daily gaming time and longer esports career duration were associated with higher AB behaviors reporting, suggesting that prolonged exposure to competitive demands may favor sustained jaw muscle tension during wakefulness. Male sex appeared as a protective factor, possibly reflecting differences in stress responses and coping profiles. Overall, these findings support AB behaviors as a multifactorial, context-dependent phenomenon and should be interpreted within current frameworks that conceptualize bruxism primarily as a behavior with phenotype specificity and context-dependent clinical significance22–25. In line with the “gateway” perspective, these findings may support considering targeted screening and educational strategies for esports practitioners within broader health-oriented dental care24.

When compared with the general population, the frequency-based proportion observed in this study appears higher than several published estimates; however, comparisons should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in instruments, assessment approaches, and case definitions across studies. Archer et al.28 reported a global estimate of 15.4% in adults, while Oliveira et al.37 reported values ranging from 16 to 32% depending on study design. Stanisic et al.29 estimated 25.9% in the general population, with higher rates in women, and Zieliński et al.30 estimated 23% worldwide, with South America showing the highest estimate (30%). Among athletes, Sales et al.31 reported 29.3%, particularly in contact sports.

In esports populations, recent cross-sectional studies have likewise reported high frequencies of AB-related behaviors and highlighted psychosocial/behavior correlates; however, direct comparisons remain constrained by heterogeneity in questionnaires, recall windows, and operational criteria33,34. In a related gaming context, Güzel et al.38 reported that video game addiction was associated with self-reported awake and sleep bruxism, whereas perceived stress showed no significant association with bruxism-related outcomes. Importantly, AB behaviors are time varying and may differ across clinical contexts; for example, AB frequency has been reported to be substantially higher (≈50%) in people with temporomandibular disorders (TMD) in a recent systematic review and meta-analysis29.

An important methodological aspect of the present study relates to the operationalization of awake bruxism (AB) behaviors. Currently, there is no universally accepted cutoff point to classify individuals as having or not having awake bruxism, a limitation explicitly acknowledged in the most recent international consensus, which conceptualizes bruxism as a behavior rather than a dichotomous diagnosis25. In this context, the present study adopted a frequency-based scoring approach derived from the BruxScreen items, converting ordinal responses into a continuous score to reflect the behavioral spectrum of AB. This strategy was informed by previous work in esports populations33 and is consistent with contemporary recommendations emphasizing dimensional rather than categorical assessment of bruxism behaviors25. For descriptive and interpretative purposes, participants were further stratified into low- and high-frequency AB behavior groups based on the sample median, allowing clinical interpretability while avoiding arbitrary diagnostic thresholds. This approach acknowledges intra-individual variability and context dependence of AB behaviors and aligns with current consensus-driven frameworks for bruxism research.

Psychosocial factors played a central role in the present findings. Elevated levels of anxiety and depression observed among participants with awake bruxism (AB) behaviors are consistent with evidence from other high-stress contexts. Levartovsky et al.39 reported increased AB behaviors frequency among dental students during periods of high academic demand, while Machado et al.40 observed similar associations in orthodontic patients. Câmara-Souza et al.41 further demonstrated correlations between AB behaviors, perceived stress, depressive symptoms, and poorer quality of life in pre-university students. More recent evidence supports a dose–response relationship between psychological distress and AB behaviors frequency, with Saracutu et al.18 reporting a linear association between the frequency of AB behaviors and PHQ-4 scores. These observations are also consistent with earlier findings indicating that stress-related vulnerability traits, including characteristics associated with heightened stress sensitivity, may differentiate individuals exhibiting bruxism behaviors42.

Within the esports context, Lee et al.43 associated competitive gaming with poorer sleep quality and depressive symptoms, whereas Gündoğdu et al.44 and Aonso-Diego et al.45 reported associations with mental fatigue and anxiety. Collectively, these findings reinforce the notion that competitive and cognitively demanding environments may facilitate the expression of AB behaviors. Importantly, the psychological correlates of sleep bruxism appear to be more complex and may involve distinct underlying mechanisms; therefore, the present interpretation is restricted to wake-related AB behaviors rather than sleep bruxism.

Beyond psychosocial stress, the specific demands of esports may exacerbate bruxism. Prolonged concentration during gaming can lead to sustained jaw clenching or muscle tension, accompanied by sympathetic nervous system activation. Additionally, long exposure to high-luminance screens emitting blue light suppresses melatonin, delays sleep onset, and fragments sleep cycles14,46, while irregular training schedules reduce REM sleep and increase lighter stages, both associated with bruxism43,47,48. The frequent use of caffeine and psychotropic substances, often employed to optimize performance, may further intensify these behaviors4,49,50.

A previous history of bruxism also emerged as a key predictor. Participants with high frequency of AB behaviors (H-AB) reported more frequent past episodes of clenching, grinding, and bracing, suggesting continuity of parafunctional patterns. Current literature emphasizes that bruxism assessment should include both present and past behaviors, as susceptibility may persist over time51. This reinforces the notion that previous AB constitutes a marker of vulnerability to recurrence in high-stress environments, such as esports.

The present results also corroborate the biopsychosocial framework proposed by Stanisic et al.29 and Soto-Goñi et al.52, highlighting the role of stress, anxiety, and individual vulnerability traits in AB behaviors. Importantly, early work discussed stress-related vulnerability (including stress sensitivity-related characteristics) in clinically diagnosed bruxers and paved the way for later developments of this construct42. This perspective also supports the interpretation that coping profiles and engagement in structured activities may modulate stress-related arousal and, consequently, AB-related behaviors, with adaptive coping being potentially protective52. The observed association with gastroesophageal reflux may be explained bidirectionally, since stress and irregular habits increase reflux symptoms, which in turn may trigger microarousals and daytime hyperactivity52. Likewise, stimulants such as caffeine and psychotropic drugs alter excitability and muscle tone, potentially exacerbating AB behaviors53,54.

The findings have relevant clinical implications. The high frequency of AB behaviors and its association with modifiable risk factors emphasize the importance of preventive and interventional strategies in the esports community. Dentists and healthcare professionals should screen competitive gamers for AB behaviors, particularly those reporting anxiety, depression, reflux, or prolonged gaming exposure. In addition, recent evidence highlights persisting gaps in bruxism-related knowledge and the need for consistent educational and terminological strategies among both trainees and practitioners, to ensure that contemporary concepts (bruxism as a behavior, phenotype specificity, and context-dependent clinical significance) are correctly understood and communicated55,56. Preventive approaches may include ergonomic guidance, education on oral habits, and behavior interventions. Notably, mindfulness-based strategies have shown promise in reducing parafunctional behaviors56 and could be incorporated into digital self-monitoring tools tailored for esports athletes. When temporomandibular disorder (TMD) symptoms are present, clinical management should follow contemporary standard-of-care recommendations, prioritizing patient-centered education and conservative, reversible approaches, and avoiding irreversible procedures in the absence of clear indications57.

Strengths include the large sample size and the use of standardized, consensus-informed subject-based items derived from BruxScreen and STAB to operationalize awake bruxism behaviors on a frequency continuum. However, limitations should be acknowledged: the cross-sectional design provides a single-time snapshot and cannot capture within-person fluctuations; assessments relied on self-report without clinical examination or instrumental confirmation; and the EMA-based longitudinal monitoring recommended to characterize awake bruxism dynamics was not implemented and, according to the literature, did make a difference in the rigors AB diagnostic58. Future works should include this instrumental diagnostic part. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as a frequency-based self-report snapshot rather than a stable trait estimate, and repeated/longitudinal measurements are needed to characterize individual trajectories over time.

In addition, although culturally appropriate adaptations were used, an official Brazilian Portuguese validated version of the BruxScreen is not yet available; while recent international consensus supports the use of standardized instruments and permits culturally appropriate translations/adaptations following established cross-cultural guidelines17,36, the use of a non-official adaptation may still introduce measurement uncertainty and should be considered when interpreting these frequency-based self-report estimates19.

Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as screening-oriented, frequency-based self-report estimates rather than diagnostic measures. Future research should employ longitudinal and ecological designs (e.g., EMA) to clarify causal pathways and capture within-person fluctuations in awake bruxism (AB) behaviors across days and competitive contexts59. In the specific context of esports, future protocols should also incorporate objective assessments, such as portable surface electromyography (sEMG) recordings of the masseter and/or temporalis muscles during standardized and/or real-world gaming sessions, to quantify masticatory muscle activity under performance pressure and prolonged gameplay. This recommendation aligns with videogame-related research reporting associations between gaming-related factors and bruxism-related outcomes and highlighting the value of objective bruxism assessment37,38,60,61.

In parallel, the growing focus on bracing suggests that sustained low-level jaw muscle activity may represent a clinically relevant and frequently overlooked AB phenotype60–62. A long-term EMA investigation further indicates that, in healthy young adults, the overall frequency of AB behaviors can remain relatively stable over a six-month period, with teeth contact and mandible bracing reported more frequently than clenching, while grinding is rarely reported during wakefulness63,64. These findings support repeated in-home measurements to establish reference values and to enable meaningful comparisons in populations exposed to potential risk factors (e.g., competitive gaming stress) or reporting clinical consequences.

Conclusion

This study provides the first investigation into the frequency-based self-report estimate and predictors of awake bruxism (AB) behaviors in Esports practitioners. The findings support AB as a multifactorial behavior phenomenon associated with psychosocial and behavior factors within the esports environment. In the context of the rapid global growth and professionalization of Esports, frequent AB behaviors should be recognized as a relevant health-related Behavior in this population. Dentists and healthcare professionals should remain vigilant to the risk factors identified, as their management may contribute not only to the preservation of oral health but also to overall well-being and to the optimization of competitive performance.
