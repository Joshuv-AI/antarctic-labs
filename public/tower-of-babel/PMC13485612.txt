Transforming Systematic Reviews: Evaluating a Fine-Tuned Large Language Model for Abstract Screening in Uveitis and Retinal Vasculitis
Authors: Carlos Cifuentes-González, Maxwell B. Singer, William Rojas-Carabali, Germán Mejía-Salgado, Maria Vittoria Cicinelli, Jyotirmay Biswas, Sapna Gangaputra, Alejandra de-la-Torre, Vishali Gupta, Jose S. Pulido, Rupesh Agrawal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the classification performance of UveAItis, a domain-specific large language model (LLM) fine-tuned for automated title and abstract screening in systematic reviews, using retinal vasculitis as a prototype.DesignComparative evaluation study embedded within a registered systematic review and meta-analysis (PROSPERO: CRD42023489232).SubjectsA total of 1030 randomly selected articles from an initial search of 5533 records related to retinal vasculitis.MethodsArticles were independently screened by 2 uveitis experts (gold standard), final-year medical students, and 3 LLMs: UveAItis (fine-tuned Generative Pre-trained Transformer [GPT]-4o), base GPT-4o, and Claude Sonnet 3.5. Screening followed a 2-question binary logic regarding human subjects and primary empirical research design. Discrepancies were resolved through expert adjudication.Main Outcome MeasuresClassification accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and Cohen Kappa coefficient for inter-rater agreement.ResultsUveAItis achieved the highest performance with an accuracy of 93.3%, area under the curve (AUC) of 0.887, and Kappa of 0.77. It significantly outperformed base GPT-4o (AUC: 0.805, P = 0.021), Claude Sonnet 3.5 (AUC: 0.669, P < 0.0001), and medical students (AUC: 0.585, P < 0.00001). The fine-tuned model correctly identified 65.4% of expert-included articles postconsensus, whereas students only identified 22.3%. UveAItis also demonstrated the lowest rate of ambiguous “Need Consensus” outputs (3.4%) compared to experts (16.7%).ConclusionsUveAItis demonstrated expert-level performance, significantly outperforming general-purpose LLMs and nonexpert human reviewers. These findings validate the potential of domain-specific fine-tuning to enhance the efficiency, scalability, and reproducibility of evidence synthesis in specialized medical fields like ophthalmology.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.

Generating high-quality evidence through systematic reviews and meta-analyses is essential for informing clinical decision-making, particularly in complex and rapidly evolving fields. However, the process of conducting systematic reviews remains highly resource-intensive, with literature screening representing one of the most time-consuming and labor-intensive steps. As the volume of published biomedical literature continues to grow exponentially, identifying relevant studies efficiently while maintaining methodological rigor has become an increasing challenge for research teams.

These challenges are particularly pronounced in specialized fields such as uveitis1 and retinal vasculitis2, where the literature is heterogeneous, terminology is complex, and expert interpretation is often required. Although systematic reviews are frequently conducted by multidisciplinary teams—including clinicians, epidemiologists, and public health researchers—the application of consistent inclusion and exclusion criteria remains difficult, especially for nonspecialist reviewers.

In parallel, clinicians and subspecialists face an increasing need to remain up to date with rapidly expanding evidence bases, further compounding the burden of literature appraisal. Together, these challenges highlight the need for scalable, accurate, and efficient tools to support both systematic review workflows and continuous knowledge synthesis.

The global shortage of uveitis specialists further compounds these challenges. In the United States, a limited number of specialists serve a large population, while in low- and middle-income countries, this disparity is even more pronounced.3,4 For example, in Colombia, only a handful of uveitis specialists serve millions of patients, contributing to significant delays in diagnosis and care.5 Beyond clinical implications, this shortage also limits the capacity for high-quality evidence synthesis, reinforcing the need for scalable tools to support systematic review workflows.

To address these challenges in evidence synthesis and literature screening, artificial intelligence (AI), specifically large language models (LLMs), have emerged as promising solutions to support or automate key steps in the systematic review workflow. Recent studies have demonstrated that tools such as Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT)-4, Claude Sonnet 3.5, and platforms like DistillerSR, Abstrackr, and Rapid Medical Evidence Synthesis can assist in literature screening with high accuracy.6,7 For example, GPT-4 demonstrated an accuracy of 0.91, a sensitivity of 0.91 for excluded papers, 0.76 for included papers, and a prevalence-adjusted and bias-adjusted Kappa of 0.96 when compared to expert reviewers.7 Additionally, in cancer-related systematic reviews, tools like Abstrackr eliminated 20% and 88% of citations from manual screening without missing any included studies, saving between 7 and 86 hours of human effort.6 Moreover, the rise of living evidence syntheses, which integrate AI-based surveillance with human expertise, has revolutionized systematic reviews, enabling continuous updates of evidence bases.8

Despite these advances, no prior study has assessed the performance of a fine-tuned LLM specifically designed for a structured systematic review screening process. In this study, we introduce UveAItis (GPT-4o-uveitis), a domain-specific LLM trained using data from a systematic review on retinal vasculitis, serving as a prototype model applicable to broader systematic review and meta-analysis workflows. This is the first model to be benchmarked against general-purpose LLMs such as GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet 3.5 in systematic review title and abstract screening. Screening decisions from uveitis specialists with >20 years of experience served as the gold standard, establishing a robust benchmark for evaluating AI-driven systematic review methodologies.

By demonstrating expert-level performance in a highly specialized domain, this study highlights the potential of fine-tuned LLMs as scalable tools for improving efficiency, accuracy, and reproducibility in systematic reviews. This framework can potentially be extended across diverse medical and scientific disciplines.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This comparative evaluation study was conducted as part of a registered systematic review and meta-analysis (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews: CRD42023489232) focused on retinal vasculitis. The objective was to assess the classification performance of a domain-specific LLM (UveAItis) that was fine-tuned on uveitis literature. This model was compared with 2 general-purpose language models (GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet 3.5) and a group of final-year medical students. The evaluation was benchmarked against the decisions of expert uveitis reviewers, each with >20 years of clinical experience and regular involvement in systematic reviews.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. As the study exclusively used publicly available published data and did not involve human participants or identifiable personal information, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required.

Literature Search and Eligibility Criteria

A comprehensive literature search was performed across 5 major databases: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane (via Ovid), Virtual Health Library, and ProQuest. The search included all articles published up to June 14, 2023. Indexing vocabularies were adapted for each platform (Medical Subject Headings, Emtree, Descriptors in Health Sciences). Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: primary research with ≥10 patients diagnosed with retinal vasculitis, and study designs including cohort studies, case series, cross-sectional studies, case-control studies, or clinical trials. Case reports, systematic reviews, secondary data studies, and economic evaluations were excluded.

Patients of all ages and demographics were eligible as long as the diagnosis of retinal vasculitis was clearly defined in the original study. Studies that reported only eye-level data without corresponding patient-level metrics were excluded. Detailed inclusion/exclusion definitions and search strategies are provided in the Supplementary Methods Sections 1 and 2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org.

Reference Standard and Screening Process

A total of 5533 unique records were retrieved from the database searches. From these, 1030 articles were randomly selected for evaluation. Each article was independently screened by 2 uveitis experts using predefined criteria. Any discrepancies (e.g., Include vs. Exclude) were resolved through consensus or adjudicated by the principal investigator (R.A.) and a senior reviewer (A.D.L.T.). The final binary classifications served as the reference standard. A complete description of the labeling process and adjudication framework is available in Supplementary Methods Section 3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org.

Development and Fine-Tuning of UveAItis (GPT-4o-Uveitis)

UveAItis (GPT-4o-uveitis) was fine-tuned on 3786 PubMed abstracts related to uveitis, selected using a structured search strategy based on disease-specific keywords and indexing terms. The training data set was constructed independently from the retinal vasculitis systematic review used for model evaluation.

To ensure strict separation between training and evaluation data sets, all abstracts included in the evaluation set (n = 1030) were explicitly excluded from the training corpus by cross-referencing unique identifiers (e.g., PubMed IDs). Additionally, to minimize the risk of near-overlap, records with identical or highly similar titles were excluded during data set curation.

A custom data set of structured question-and-answer (Q/A) pairs was generated using Claude Sonnet 3.5 to simulate the decision-making logic for screening. The data set was formatted for supervised instruction tuning and fine-tuned using OpenAI’s application programming interface. Full details on the training data set, token counts, and fine-tuning parameters are outlined in Supplementary Methods Section 4, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org.

The Q/A pairs used for fine-tuning were generated using Claude Sonnet 3.5 based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. While this approach enabled scalable data set construction, it may introduce potential biases or artifacts related to model-generated reasoning patterns. To mitigate this, the decision framework was strictly guided by expert-defined screening criteria. However, no formal human validation of each generated Q/A pair was performed, and this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Articles were classified as Include, Exclude, or Need Consensus based on their combined scores. Medical students received structured training sessions and reviewed the same set of articles under masked conditions. Consensus on their decisions was achieved through group discussions moderated by an expert. The complete evaluation logic, including the scoring system and harmonization of probabilistic outputs, is provided in Supplementary Methods Sections 5 and 6, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org.

Statistical Analysis

Only articles with definitive binary outcomes (Include = 1, Exclude = 0) were included in the final performance evaluation. Descriptive statistics were used for data set characterization. Model performance was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) calculated using the pROC package in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). DeLong test was applied to assess the statistical significance of AUC differences between models.

Sensitivity (recall), specificity, precision, harmonic mean of precision and recall (F1 score), and Youden index were calculated to determine diagnostic performance thresholds. Inter-rater agreement with the expert standard was measured using Cohen Kappa, interpreted via the Landis and Koch scale. Confusion matrices were generated for each LLM and the student group to examine classification accuracy and error patterns.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.3.1) and Jamovi (v2.3). Additional technical details, including software packages and code logic, are presented in Supplementary Methods Section 7, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org.

Results

Of the 1030 articles screened, the proportion of studies marked for inclusion varied substantially across evaluators. The average agreement rate among expert reviewers (with definitive inclusion/exclusion decisions) was 79.3%, ranging from 75.6% to 82.9% among the 3 expert pairs. For student reviewers, the average agreement across reviewer pairs was slightly higher at 82.3%, although with a broader range across pairs (64.6% to 97.6%).

In Terms of Inclusion Rates

The UveAItis identified the highest proportion of articles for inclusion (17.3%), followed by expert reviewers (12.4%), GPT-4o (12.1%), students (8.4%), and Claude Sonnet 3.5 identifying the fewest (6.9%). The need for consensus also differed widely: 16.7% for expert reviewers, 11.6% for students, and only 3.4% for both UveAItis and GPT-4o. Claude Sonnet 3.5 generated almost no ambiguous cases (0.1%). Most articles were ultimately classified as “Not included” by all evaluators, with the highest exclusion rate from Claude Sonnet 3.5 (93.0%) and the lowest from the experts (70.9%). Unresolved or ambiguous classifications by LLMs (“Need Consensus”) were excluded from the binary analysis. A total of 361 articles (35.0%) were initially classified as “Need Consensus” by ≥1 evaluator, reflecting a substantial proportion of borderline cases in clinical settings screening scenarios. Students correctly identified 42 of 188 articles (22.3%), misclassifying 146 relevant articles. UveAItis again showed the best performance, identifying 123 of 188 articles (65.4%) with 65 misclassifications. Discriminative Performance (ROC, AUC, and Accuracy Analysis)

To assess the discriminative capacity of each evaluator in identifying relevant articles, we conducted ROC curve analyses and calculated key performance metrics, including AUC. Among all evaluators, the UveAItis achieved the highest AUC (0.887), with strong sensitivity (81.7%), high specificity (95.7%), and the best overall accuracy at 93.3%. Generative Pre-trained Transformer-4o also demonstrated robust performance, with an AUC of 0.805, specificity of 97.9%, but lower sensitivity (63.2%), resulting in an overall accuracy of 91.9%. Claude Sonnet 3.5 exhibited moderate performance, with an AUC of 0.669, low sensitivity (34.6%), excellent specificity (99.3%), and accuracy of 87.5%. Pairwise AUC Comparisons

UveAItis demonstrated significantly superior performance compared with the other LLMs, with all differences reaching statistical significance. The difference in AUC between UveAItis and GPT-4 was change in area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ΔAUC) = 0.075 (P = 0.021), and the difference between UveAItis and Claude Sonnet 3.5 reached ΔAUC = 0.205 (P < 0.0001). The largest difference was observed between UveAItis and students (ΔAUC = 0.301, P < 0.00001). Generative Pre-trained Transformer-4o also outperformed Claude Sonnet 3.5 (ΔAUC = 0.118, P < 0.001) and students (ΔAUC = 0.217, P = 0.0012). Notably, the difference between Claude Sonnet 3.5 and students was not statistically significant (P = 0.185) (see pairwise model comparisons in Table 4).

Discussion

This study highlights the strong performance of the fine-tuned UveAItis model with domain-specific literature. Among the 1030 articles screened from a previously published systematic review on retinal vasculitis,9 the UveAItis model consistently outperformed the evaluated general-purpose LLMs and nonexpert human reviewers, achieving an AUC of 0.89, sensitivity of 81.7%, specificity of 95.7%, and accuracy of 93.3%.

Cohen Kappa coefficient further reinforced these findings, with UveAItis showing the highest agreement with expert reviewers (Kappa = 0.77), outperforming GPT-4o (Kappa = 0.68), Claude Sonnet 3.5 (Kappa = 0.45), and medical students (Kappa = 0.21). These results underscore the ability of fine-tuned, disease-specific LLMs to approximate expert-level decision-making in systematic review screening, suggesting their potential utility in resource-constrained settings or rare disease contexts where expert time and knowledge may be limited.

The findings highlight both the power and the necessity of domain adaptation in LLM applications. Despite receiving focused training and reference materials, medical students exhibited low sensitivity (22.3%) and accuracy (81.5%), emphasizing the limitations of relying on nonexpert reviewers. This supports integrating specialized AI models to augment or replace early-stage human screening, particularly for rare diseases like retinal vasculitis, where domain expertise is often limited.

Our findings not only align with but also build upon previous research that has evaluated the use of AI and LLMs in systematic review workflows. For instance, Yao et al (2024) demonstrated that ChatGPT-4 achieved moderate sensitivity and specificity when screening abstracts for cancer epidemiology reviews, with an AUC around 0.83, but the model was not fine-tuned for the task nor specialized by domain. Similarly, the BioSift tool evaluated by Kartchner et al (2023) for literature screening in biomedicine achieved an F1 score of 0.77, yet was not focused on rare diseases and showed variable performance across different conditions.10 In contrast, our fine-tuned model UveAItis was trained on the literature on uveitis and retinal vasculitis—a highly specialized and rare inflammatory eye condition. This disease-specific training may partially explain the improved performance observed of the model compared to general LLMs.

This is further supported by findings from Sugiura et al (2024), who demonstrated improved performance among models adapted to subdomains via prompt engineering or fine-tuning.11 Morever, the comparative evaluation conducted by Marshall et al (2023) using general tools like RobotReviewer, Abstrackr, and Rayyan found limited agreement with expert reviewers, with Kappa values ranging from 0.30 to 0.56.8

To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly evaluate and statistically compare the performance of a disease-specific fine-tuned LLM against both general LLMs and human screeners in the context of a rare disease. These results underscore the potential of domain adaptation in maximizing the utility of LLMs for medical evidence synthesis, offering hope for improved systematic review workflows.

Importantly, this study evaluated a single fine-tuned model based on GPT-4o and did not include fine-tuned versions of other architectures such as Claude or LLaMA. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as model-specific and task-specific rather than evidence of universal superiority of fine-tuned models across different architectures. Instead, they should be considered as a proof-of-concept demonstrating the potential benefits of domain-specific adaptation within a given model framework.

The results of this study have important implications for clinical research and the operational execution of systematic reviews, particularly in fields where expert reviewers are scarce and time-consuming screening processes remain a bottleneck. In rare and immune-mediated diseases like retinal vasculitis, where the number of trained specialists is minimal and delays in patient diagnosis and treatment are common, domain-specific LLMs offer a transformative opportunity. The UveAItis model, by outperforming general-purpose LLMs and trained medical students, illustrates how customized AI tools can meaningfully contribute to early-stage evidence appraisal.

The model’s high sensitivity and specificity profile makes it particularly well-suited to serve as a primary screener in large-scale systematic reviews or as a triage tool in living evidence syntheses, which are continuous and iterative updates of systematic reviews as new evidence becomes available. By prioritizing relevant literature for expert review and minimizing missed inclusions, the model helps maintain methodological rigor without compromising efficiency. Despite lower sensitivity, Claude Sonnet 3.5 and GPT-4o demonstrated excellent specificity and may thus be employed in hybrid screening strategies—either to reduce the workload in low-risk exclusions or provide second-pass validation.

This approach aligns with current global efforts to make evidence synthesis more sustainable and responsive. Fabiano et al (2023) emphasized that AI tools should not only improve speed but also maintain reproducibility and transparency when applied to systematic review pipelines.12 Another paper mapped >100 available tools intended to improve systematic review efficiency, many of which focused on early-phase screening, the initial stage of systematic review where large volumes of literature are screened for relevance, but had limited domain-specific adaptation or clinical settings validation.13 Chen et al (2024) further highlighted the potential of LLMs to reduce cognitive workload and promote the democratization of high-quality evidence in complex disciplines such as epidemiology and public health.14

Our findings directly support this vision, showing that fine-tuned, disease-focused models can be trained to mirror expert-level screening with high agreement. Moreover, by reducing reliance on large expert panels and improving the consistency of inclusion criteria application, such models have the potential to significantly improve equity in research participation for under-represented diseases and regions, inspiring a more inclusive research landscape.

Given these promising results, future implementations should aim to integrate these LLMs directly into commonly used systematic review platforms such as Covidence or DistillerSR, allowing research teams to test real-time performance, error patterns, and reviewer confidence. Importantly, such models not only enhance the efficiency and equity of research but also serve an educational function, offering feedback and consistency checks for less experienced screeners in research teams, providing a safety net for learning and growth.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of this study—including a robust gold standard, rigorous statistical comparisons, and clinical settings data set integration—several limitations must be acknowledged. First, although the study analyzed 1030 articles, these were drawn from a single systematic review focused on retinal vasculitis. While this allowed for highly contextualized fine-tuning and evaluation, the generalizability of our findings to other disease domains, especially those outside ophthalmology or immunology, remains untested. Potential contributions to future validation across multiple rare disease areas are crucial to confirm external applicability.

Second, articles labeled as “Need Consensus” (coded as 0.5) were excluded from the primary ROC and Kappa analyses to preserve the validity of binary classification metrics. While methodologically appropriate, this approach may have simplified the classification task relative to clinical settings screening conditions, particularly in borderline cases where expert disagreement occurs. Sensitivity analyses in which these cases were alternatively classified as “Included” and “Not Included” did not materially change the relative ranking of model performance. However, the exclusion of these cases may have led to a slight overestimation of absolute performance metrics and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Third, the use of model-generated Q/A pairs for fine-tuning may introduce subtle biases related to the underlying generative model (Claude Sonnet 3.5). Although these pairs were structured based on predefined expert criteria, no formal human validation was performed for each instance. This may introduce artifacts in reasoning patterns that were not fully captured in this study and should be considered when interpreting model performance.

Fourth, although medical students received training sessions and reference material, their performance may have been affected by variability in prior experience, engagement, or interpretation of inclusion criteria. This limitation, however, is reflective of clinical settings heterogeneity in reviewer teams, which underscores the relevance of our study and further supports the utility of LLMs in enhancing standardization and reproducibility in your own work.

Finally, this study focused solely on title and abstract screening. Other critical systematic review stages—such as full-text review, risk of bias assessment, and data extraction—were not evaluated. It remains unclear whether the same level of accuracy and agreement would be achieved in these more nuanced and interpretative phases of the review process.

Conclusions

This study provides compelling evidence supporting the use of domain-specific, fine-tuned language models to enhance literature screening in systematic reviews. UveAItis outperformed the evaluated general-purpose models and nonexpert human reviewers in this study setting, suggesting that domain-specific fine-tuning may improve performance in specialized tasks. These findings highlight the potential of domain-adapted AI tools to improve the efficiency, consistency, and scalability of evidence synthesis, particularly in rare and complex medical fields. In such contexts, where expert availability is limited, these tools may support evidence-based decision-making and reduce the burden on specialized reviewers. UveAItis demonstrated high accuracy and agreement with expert reviewers while reducing screening workload, underscoring the value of AI-assisted approaches in optimizing systematic review workflows. Future studies should further evaluate the integration of fine-tuned LLMs into systematic review platforms and across diverse clinical domains.
