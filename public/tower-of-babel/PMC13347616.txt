A cross-sectional study of oxidative stress pathway genotypes and their interactions with environmental pollutant levels identifies associations with gene expression and lung function
Authors: Shuangjia Xue, Seyed Mehdi Nouraie, Jiebiao Wang, Xingnan Li, Deborah Meyers, Eugene R. Bleecker, Victor E. Ortega, Mario Castro, Loren C. Denlinger, Serpil Erzurum, Elliot Israel, Prescott Woodruff, Wendy C. Moore, Stephanie Christenson, John Fahy, Kaharu Sumino, Nizar Jarjour, Bruce Levy, Sally E. Wenzel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAsthma is a heterogeneous disease influenced by genetic and environmental factors. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) exacerbates asthma, likely through oxidative stress pathways, but whether genetic variation modifies this effect remains unclear.MethodsWe analysed data on 948 adults with asthma from the Severe Asthma Research Program (SARP), linking ZIP-code-level PM2.5 exposure with whole-genome sequencing data. We tested 4337 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in 120 oxidative stress pathway genes for gene–environment (GxE) interactions with PM2.5 on lung function (forced expiratory volume in 1 s [FEV1] % predicted) using weighted linear regression. Gene expression data from bronchial epithelial cells (n = 170) were used to assess cis-expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs).FindingsHigher PM2.5 exposure was associated with lower FEV1% predicted (β per μg/m3 = −0.7, p = 0.01). We identified 20 SNPs across seven genes (OXSR1, PXDN, TPO, LRRK2, APP, MSRA, MSRB2) with significant GxE interactions after multiple-testing correction. Five SNPs were also eQTLs, linking PM2.5-modified gene expression to lung function. Minor alleles in OXSR1 and PXDN were associated with reduced gene expression and worsened FEV1% under high PM2.5 exposure. Conversely, TPO variants were associated with higher baseline expression and lower lung function, but under increasing PM2.5 exposure, minor allele carriers showed suppressed TPO expression and improved FEV1%.InterpretationThis study identified 20 SNPs in oxidative stress pathway genes that modify the effect of PM2.5 on lung function in asthma. These findings highlight the importance of integrating environmental context in genetic studies and suggest potential therapeutic targets for pollution-sensitive asthma phenotypes.FundingSupported by NIH grants.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Asthma is a complex and heterogeneous disease shaped by both genetic and environmental influences. Air pollution, particularly fine particulate matter (PM2.5), is a well-recognised risk factor for airway obstruction and asthma exacerbations, with oxidative stress pathways thought to mediate much of its harm. Prior gene–environment (GxE) studies have largely focused on a small number of candidate genes, such as the glutathione S-transferase family, and on childhood asthma susceptibility, rather than lung function in adults. Moreover, these studies were often underpowered, with limited sample sizes and genetic resolution, leaving a gap in our understanding of how individual genetic variation in oxidative stress pathways modifies the respiratory response to air pollution.

Added value of this study

This study represents one of the most comprehensive GxE analyses of lung function in asthma to date, combining whole-genome sequencing, ZIP code–level PM2.5 exposure, and bronchial epithelial gene expression profiling in a well-characterised cohort of 948 adults with asthma. By systematically evaluating 4337 SNPs in 120 oxidative stress pathway genes, we identified 20 SNPs across seven genes (OXSR1, PXDN, TPO, LRRK2, APP, MSRA, MSRB2) that significantly interacted with PM2.5 exposure to influence lung function. Furthermore, five SNPs also functioned as cis-expression quantitative trait loci, supporting biologically plausible mechanisms through altered gene expression. These findings not only expand the catalogue of pollution-sensitive genetic variants in asthma, but also link them to functional gene regulation in airway epithelium.

Implications of all the available evidence

The findings of this study advance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying interindividual susceptibility to air pollution in asthma. The identified gene–environment interactions highlight oxidative stress pathways as potential mediators of pollution-related lung function impairment and identify specific genes that may serve as targets for therapeutic intervention. These results underscore the necessity of incorporating environmental exposures into genetic and genomic investigations of asthma and support the potential utility of genetic markers to inform risk stratification. Future research should aim to replicate these findings in independent populations, elucidate the causal pathways linking genotype, gene expression, and lung function, and assess whether targeted modulation of oxidative stress pathways can mitigate the adverse effects of air pollution on lung function in susceptible individuals.

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways characterised by bronchial hyper-responsiveness and reversible airflow limitation.1 Forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) is a key physiologic parameter that assesses airflow limitation, and is an important tool for both asthma diagnosis and assessment of control and severity.1 Asthma is also now recognised as a highly heterogeneous disease for which both genetic and environmental risk factors are involved.2 Genetic factors related to airway structure, T helper type 2 (Th2) immunity, the HLA locus, and oxidative stress pathways have been reported to predispose individuals to greater airflow limitation (airway obstruction).3 Patients with asthma appear to be particularly sensitive to air pollution, as exposure increases airway obstruction both acutely and chronically while worsening asthma control and severity.4

Particulate matter, less than or equal to 2.5 μM diameter (PM2.5) exposure has also been reported to be associated with lowered lung function in patients with asthma.4 Airborne pollutants can originate from anthropogenic sources, such as vehicular exhaust, industrial sources, and coal combustion in urban centres, as well as from organic sources like biomass burning and agricultural activities in rural areas.5 Gaseous pollutants including ozone (O3), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), carbon monoxide (CO), and sulphur dioxide (SO2) can combine with PM to form mixes of solids, liquids, and vapours that can be inhaled.6 As these pollutants penetrate deep into the respiratory system, they generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) through chemical components or metal-mediated Fenton reactions, leading to persistent oxidative stress and inflammation. These changes have been reported to worsen airway hyperresponsiveness, remodelling, and airway obstruction.4

Enhancement of oxidative stress pathways likely plays a central role in the biologic and clinical effects of PM exposure. Previous studies have evaluated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in selective candidate genes for their potential to drive GxE interactions.7,8 SNPs in the glutathione S-transferase family have been extensively investigated for their interactive effects with traffic-related air pollutants in childhood asthma onset.7,9 Interactions of PM10 exposure with SNPs in oxidative stress pathway genes (NFE2L2 (Nrf2), superoxide dismutase) have also been reported with hospital admissions.10 Although GxE interactions have been studied in asthma development and exacerbations, evidence linking GxE to lung function in asthma patients is limited, in contrast to well-established findings in COPD and occupational exposure.11,12 These previous studies have also been constrained to a small number of candidate genes.13

The aim of this study was to comprehensively investigate whether known polymorphisms in a wide range of oxidative stress response pathway genes modulate the effects of PM2.5 on lung function (specifically FEV1% predicted) in patients from the well-characterised Severe Asthma Research Program (SARP). We then incorporated airway epithelial brushing gene expression profiles to explore whether these polymorphisms may have influenced lung function by serving as expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) for gene expression. The objective of this study was to investigate how genetic variation in oxidative stress–related pathways modifies the association between long-term PM2.5 exposure and lung function in adults with asthma.

Methods

Study subjects

SARP is a multi-centre study to investigate the pathobiology of asthma phenotypes using standardised protocols. The initial cohort, encompassing SARP 1 and 2, was cross-sectional and included 1685 individuals. The subsequent cohort, SARP 3, was designed as a longitudinal study and included 763 individuals. Sex was self-reported at enrolment and included as a biological variable in regression models. No sex-based exclusions were applied. Only participants with both 5-digit zip codes (US only) (n = 1457, excluding n = 740 for missing zip code data) and whole genome sequencing (WGS) (n = 1688, excluding n = 760 for missing genotype data) were included in the analysis (total n = 948) (see Flowchart in Fig. 1). As this was a convenience sample derived from available SARP participants with complete WGS and exposure data, no formal sample size calculations were performed a priori. The cohort was further categorised into traditional industrialised and non/less-industrialised sites. Cleveland, St. Louis, and Pittsburgh were considered industrialised, while Boston, Denver, Galveston, Madison, San Francisco, and Winston–Salem were considered less industrialised. FEV1% predicted values were calculated using the GLI Global 2022 reference equations, which provide race-neutral predicted values.14Fig. 1Study flowchart showing participant selection and exclusions. Ethics

All research activities were conducted with the approval of the respective institutional review boards at the participating research sites, and informed consent was obtained from all study participants. This study was conducted as part of the Severe Asthma Research Program IV (SARP IV): Immunometabolic phenotypes in adult severe asthma and disease progression. The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Wake Forest University Health Sciences under protocol number IRB00066576. All study participants provided written informed consent prior to participation in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments.

Genetic and genomic studies

WGS was performed in SARP (Freeze 6; dbGaP accession: phs001446.v3.p2) through the NHLBI Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) program, primarily using Illumina HiSeq X Ten platforms with PCR-free library preparation and ≥30 × coverage.15 Bronchial epithelial cell (BEC) gene expression was profiled from RNA extracted via bronchoscopic airway brushings in asthma patients. SARP 1–2 Microarray gene expression (GSE63142) was processed using Agilent workflows.16,17 Background correction and probe filtering were performed, followed by quality control using array-wide intensity distributions, MA plots, and detection of outlier samples. Expression values were log2-transformed and normalised using cyclic-LOESS with Bioconductor, as documented in the original SARP dataset.

SARP 3 RNA-seq data underwent standard quality control, including assessment of library size, mapping rate, and gene count distributions. Low-count genes were filtered before normalisation. Gene expression was normalised using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method in edgeR, followed by log2 transformation to obtain normalised expression values suitable for downstream analyses.18

Following platform-specific QC and normalisation, gene expression values were Z-score–normalised within each platform to place microarray intensities and RNA-seq–derived expression on a comparable scale. This approach preserves within-platform relative expression while enabling integrated gene expression analyses across platforms. Z-score transformation has been previously demonstrated as an effective and practical strategy for cross-platform harmonisation of transcriptomic data.19, 20, 21

Air pollution estimation

To estimate ambient air pollution exposure for SARP participants, we calculated annual average concentrations of five pollutants: PM2.5, NO2, PM10, O3, and SO2. The year 2011 was selected as it represents the midpoint of the SARP1–3 recruitment period. Pollutant data were obtained from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's Air Quality System (AQS). Exposure levels were assigned to participants based on a nearest-neighbour interpolation method, using the closest monitoring station to the geographic center of each participant's ZIP code. The distance between the ZIP code centroid and the nearest monitor was calculated and categorised into three groups (<3 miles, 3–10 miles, >10 miles), and these distance-based weights were incorporated into subsequent GxE analyses. This approach assigns PM2.5 exposure using annual averages at each participant's 5-digit ZIP-code centroid and applies distance-based weighting of monitoring stations to help reduce spatial misclassification. When a primary monitoring station had incomplete annual data, we assigned exposure using the nearest available monitor within the same distance tier, averaging values across monitors when multiple candidates were available.

Selection of SNPs and eQTL analysis

To examine gene–environment interactions, SNPs in oxidative stress–related genes (436 genes) were prioritised using the GO:0006979 pathway. Only the 120 with available eQTL data in GTEx were selected, as accessed in 2021. SNPs were extracted from WGS database using PLINK 1.9. Expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) analysis was performed using a linear additive model in R to associate SNP genotype with gene expression. eQTL analyses were restricted to cis-eQTLs, defined as SNPs located within ±1 Mb of the transcription start site of the gene. For SNPs showing significant G × E interactions, we queried the GTEx v8 Human Lung tissue dataset to assess whether the same SNPs demonstrated significant SNP–gene expression associations in lung tissue. Associations with FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 were considered evidence of tissue-specific regulatory relevance.

Statistical analysis

The association between pollution exposure and FEV1% predicted was evaluated using linear regression to estimate the effect size (β) for each pollutant. The standard error of the β coefficient was calculated based on the residual sum of squares from the model and the variance of the exposure variable, with 95% confidence intervals computed using the corresponding t-distribution. Associations between individual pollutants and FEV1% predicted were visualised in a forest plot, presenting β estimates and their 95% confidence intervals. Gene-environment interactions were assessed using weighted linear regression, which accounted for the distance between the nearest air quality monitor and the participant's ZIP code centroid, to improve exposure estimate precision. Interaction terms between SNPs and pollutant levels were included to quantify the joint effect of genetic variation and environmental exposure on lung function. We first identified SNPs located within ±50 kb of genes in the oxidative stress response pathway, followed by filtering using a minor allele frequency >1% and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium p > 1 × 10−6. Linkage disequilibrium pruning was conducted at r2 < 0.8 using PLINK. After quality control, 4337 candidate SNPs were retained for gene–environment interaction testing. SNPs were coded additively (0, 1, 2 copies of the effect allele), and PM2.5 was treated as a continuous variable. Age, sex, and height are incorporated in the calculation of FEV1% predicted, and all participants were non-smokers (<10 pack-years and no smoking in the prior year). Limited socioeconomic variables (e.g., education) were tested but did not materially influence results, and BMI was not included to avoid over-adjustment. Analyses were conducted in the combined cohort. Genetic ancestry was characterised using the TOPMed ancestry pipeline, which computes principal components with PC-AiR and PC-Relate on high-quality, LD-pruned biallelic SNPs, accounting for relatedness and population.15,22 Models were adjusted for the top five principal components derived from genetic ancestry analyses to account for potential confounding due to population structure. A directed acyclic graph illustrating the assumed causal relationships among PM2.5 exposure, genetic variation, lung function, and key covariates is shown in Supplementary Figure S1. P-values were derived from two-sided Wald tests of regression coefficients in linear models. For gene–environment analyses, p-values correspond to the SNP × PM2.5 interaction term. For eQTL analyses, p-values were obtained from linear additive models testing genotype–expression associations. To account for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Associations with FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. Significant interaction results were visualised in a volcano plot, displaying the β estimates and log10-transformed p-values of the interaction terms for all tested SNPs. Normality of residuals was evaluated using Q–Q plots (Supplementary Figure S2). Residuals demonstrated approximate normality with minor deviation in the tails, and no major violations of model assumptions were observed. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2).

PM2.5 associates with lower baseline FEV1% predicted

To determine the impact of various air pollutants on lung function in patients with asthma, linear regression was conducted on race-neutral baseline FEV1% predicted (95% confidence interval). Exposure to higher PM2.5 associated with a lower FEV1% predicted [beta = −0.7 (CI: −1.23 to −0.16, p-value = 0.01)]. Thus, for each 1 μg/mm3 increase in PM2.5, there is a 0.7% decrease in FEV1% predicted, with a CI of 0.16% and 1.23% (Fig. 2). SNPs influenced by PM2.5 exposure alter cis-gene expression

To determine whether identified SNPs influence FEV1% predicted by modulating expression of their corresponding genes in response to PM2.5 exposure, we conducted an eQTL analysis. PM2.5 was dichotomised at the median (10.3 μg/m3) due to limited sample size. Analysis included the 20 SNPs showing GxE interactions with FEV1%. Five SNPs significantly affected both FEV1% predicted and cis-gene expression in the presence of PM2.5 (before correction for multiple comparisons, Supplementary Table S1). Allele differences at most of these SNPs did not independently affect FEV1% predicted (Fig. 4, Fig. 5). However, the minor “A” allele at the rs2011 locus in OXSR1 associated with worsening FEV1% predicted as PM2.5 increased (Fig. 4b). In the smaller subgroup with gene expression data, the A allele at rs2011 in OXSR1 tended to associate with a lower FEV1% predicted, particularly in the AA homozygotes when PM2.5 exposure was high (Fig. 4c), consistent with findings in the overall group. These physiologic changes associated with lower expression of OXSR1 mRNA in AA homozygotes, but only under conditions of high PM2.5 exposure (Fig. 4d). Similarly, rs1330782395 heterozygotes in PXDN showed reduced FEV1% and PXDN expression under high PM2.5 (Fig. 4E–H). Discussion

The findings reported here represent one of the broadest scale gene–environment interactions studies in asthma to date. While this study replicated earlier findings that exposure to PM2.5 linked to lower FEV1% predicted,26 it significantly expands on those findings by identifying interactions of zip code defined PM2.5 exposures with personal genetic sequencing variation, specifically among 120 genes well recognised to modulate intracellular redox balance (GO:0006979), which may affect changes in lung function. Our findings complement a large genome-wide gene–air pollution interaction study in UK Biobank, which identified loci influencing pollution-related lung function in the general population. Unlike that study, we focused on oxidative stress pathways in a well-characterised asthma cohort and integrated airway epithelial gene expression data to provide mechanistic insight.27 20 SNPs from 7 redox-related genes (LRRK2, MSRA, MSRB2, OXSR1, PXDN, TPO, and APP) were identified for their GxE interactions. Additional gene expression data in a smaller subset identified 5 of these SNPs (in 4 of the 7 genes) as eQTLs affecting cis-gene expression, and particularly under higher PM2.5 exposure, the observed effects occurred in biologically meaningful and predictable directions; that is, genetic variants associated with reduced expression of oxidative stress–related genes under high PM2.5 exposure corresponded to expected decreases (or protection) in lung function, consistent with known mechanisms of particulate matter–induced oxidative injury.

Perhaps not surprisingly, we observed higher PM2.5 (and various other atmospheric pollutant) levels in the zip codes associated with the industrialised SARP sites as compared to the other centres. Additionally, emitting sources (factories/power plants) in these industrialised cities are likely to be sources for sulfates and heavy metals, which can interact with PM2.5 to increase its toxicity and the risk for respiratory diseases.28 However, like cigarette smoking, environmental exposure to atmospheric pollutants can have varying effects dependent on each individual's genetics. Indeed, atmospheric pollution is widely believed to have a greater clinical impact on patients with respiratory diseases.4 Therefore, a deeper understanding of the complex interactions of the environment with the genome is needed.

Twin and family-based studies estimate that heritability of lung function ranges from 26% to 81%, highlighting the contribution of genetic factors to FEV1.29 However, genetic studies have traditionally overlooked the role of environmental factors in asthma. Most existing gene–environment interaction studies in asthma are limited to a small set of candidate genes, particularly the glutathione-S transferase (GST) family,30,31 and have primarily focused on susceptibility to childhood onset asthma. For example, studies have shown that the risk of childhood-onset asthma varies based on the interaction of GSTM1 genotype with maternal smoking during pregnancy and environmental tobacco smoke exposure (ETS) in childhood.32 However, few studies have performed comprehensive GxE analyses focused on lung function.33

Major challenges in conducting GxE studies include the lack of large geocoded populations with WGS. Of 30 GxE studies in asthma, only 9 included more than 500 subjects.34 Although the SARP population started with over 2400 patients, after limitations from WGS and zip code data availability, <1000 patients were included in the final GxE analysis. Yet this still represents one of the largest GxE studies to date in asthma, allowing us to explore an expanded candidate gene approach. Even this larger sample size is grossly underpowered to detect GxE interactions across the genome that survive multiple comparison testing. Although oxidative stress is a well-established mechanism of PM2.5-related injury, air pollutants also affect epithelial barrier function, immune signalling, and epigenetic regulation. As the small sample size of this study drastically limited power for a genome-wide agnostic GxE scan, we used a supervised pathway approach, examining thousands of variants across 120 oxidative stress–related genes. This expands upon traditional targeted studies of single genes while maintaining statistical power and enabling biologically interpretable findings, while leaving open opportunities to examine other pathways. Future studies in larger cohorts (or targeted analysis of other pathways) will be needed to evaluate genome-wide GxE signals and to investigate additional pathways beyond oxidative stress. Thus, we chose a candidate pathway approach, focussing on 120 oxidative stress-related genes to compare SNPs, their relation to FEV1% predicted and the modification by PM2.5 exposure. Including the top five principal components as covariates accounts for subtle differences in genetic ancestry across participants, allowing analyses in the combined cohort without race stratification.

This study identified 20 SNPs in 7 oxidative stress-related genes that appear to interact with PM2.5 to influence lung function in patients with asthma. These genes can be broadly and biologically categorised as potentially beneficial (antioxidant) or harmful (oxidative). One of the genes (with 4 identified SNPs) is thyroid peroxidase (TPO). TPO encodes a haem-containing peroxidase that catalyses the oxidation of substrates by hydrogen peroxide, playing a critical role in thyroid hormone synthesis.35 Importantly, TPO expression has been reported in the lung, where it could contribute to the formation of ROS and oxygen radicals which worsen oxidative stress.35 Thus, although everyone carries this genetic locus, the impact on lung function is modified by the specific allele present at each site; notably, the minor T alleles of TPO SNPs (rs62107964 and rs12471599) appear to interact with PM2.5 levels with a dampening of TPO expression, preserving lung function despite increasing PM2.5 exposure.

A similar trend was observed with leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) SNP (rs73102291). LRRK2 contributes to ROS production, causing mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis, and impaired cellular integrity under oxidative stress.36,37 Although the A allele was not associated with baseline lung function, it correlated with lower LRRK2 expression and improved lung function under high PM2.5 exposure. These findings suggest LRRK2 and TPO may be potential therapeutic targets in asthma, particularly in individuals with high oxidative stress and pollutant exposure.

In contrast, OXSR1 (Oxidative Stress Responsive Kinase 1) is recognised for its role in antioxidant stress-response pathways, whereas PXDN (Peroxidasin) functions as a haem peroxidase in the extracellular matrix.38,39 OXSR1 facilitates cellular adaptation to oxidative stress by regulating antioxidant defences,39 while PXDN catalyses collagen IV cross-linking through a haem peroxidase–mediated reaction within the basement membrane.40,41 Increased expression of these proteins likely enhances the cell's ability to adapt to oxidative stress (OXSR1), maintain extracellular matrix integrity (PXDN) and maintain lung function, especially under environmental challenges like PM2.5 exposure. Importantly, genetic variation affects this protection: the OXSR1 rs1384006 T allele is linked to asthma exacerbations, reduced expression, and weaker defences.39 Although specific PXDN SNPs are less defined, certain variants similarly reduce expression and lung function, potentially impairing basement membrane stability.40,42 Together, these findings emphasise OXSR1 and PXDN as key protective factors against pollutant-induced oxidative stress in asthma, suggesting that boosting their stress-response and matrix-stabilising functions could help mitigate oxidative damage and improve clinical outcomes.

Similar gene–environment interactions with PM2.5 exposure and lung function were identified for SNPs in APP (amyloid precursor protein), MSRA (methionine sulfoxide reductase A), and MSRB2 (methionine sulfoxide reductase B2), all of which play key roles in oxidative stress response.43, 44, 45 However, their expression in airway epithelial cells was not affected, suggesting that expression changes in other asthma-relevant cell types, such as immune or structural cells, may be involved. While the interaction p-values are modest, the consistent directionality across multiple SNPs and concordant eQTL findings support the biological relevance of these novel PM2.5–gene interactions. Notably, several SNPs showed qualitative interaction patterns, where minor-allele carriers exhibited opposite directionality relative to the reference genotype. Given the modest sample size and risk of false-positive qualitative interactions, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating. Future studies with larger samples and independent cohorts will be needed to validate these observations. Although the SNPs we identified all reside within oxidative stress–related pathways, the directions of interaction effects were not uniform. This variation is biologically plausible because oxidative stress pathways include both antioxidant and pro-oxidant processes, and individual genes have distinct, sometimes opposing mechanistic roles. As shown in Figs. 4 and 5, reduced expression of antioxidant genes such as OXSR1 and PXDN under higher PM2.5 exposure was associated with lower FEV1, whereas reduced expression of the pro-oxidant enzyme TPO was associated with improved lung function. These context-dependent, pathway-consistent effects support biologically coherent mechanisms rather than random pleiotropy or analytic artifact.

Other pollutants, including PM10, did not show significant associations with FEV1% predicted. PM10 was only moderately correlated with PM2.5 in our dataset (r ≈ 0.47, Supplementary Figure S6) and represents total particulate mass ≤10 μm, which includes but does not isolate the fine fraction. PM2.5, with its small (2.5 μm size or less), is more likely to impact bronchial airways and beyond. It has also been more strongly linked to redox changes, small-airway pathology and disease.46 This pattern is consistent with prior literature where PM2.5 demonstrates stronger associations with asthma morbidity than PM10.

The results of this study are limited by the imprecision of individual-level environmental exposures. Assigning exposure at the ZIP-code level introduces spatial imprecision, particularly in areas with heterogeneous PM2.5 distributions, but this misclassification is expected to be non-differential and likely biases results toward the null. Participants were localised to 5-digit U.S. ZIP codes, which cover broad geographic areas—averaging 82.25 square miles. Distance-based weighting from the nearest air quality monitor estimated PM2.5 levels, introducing potential spatial error of up to 5 miles.47 Notably, previous research has employed buffer radii ranging from 6.4 km to 50 km around monitors, supporting the validity of our approach within the commonly accepted spatial range in air pollution epidemiology.48 We also note that this ZIP-code/monitor–based assignment induces primarily Berkson-type spatial measurement error, in which individuals’ true exposures vary around the assigned ZIP-level mean. This form of non-differential error with respect to genotype is expected to attenuate exposure–response and GxE estimates toward the null and reduce statistical power rather than create spurious interactions. To address temporal misclassification, we repeated the analyses using average annual PM2.5 for both 2008 and 2015. β estimates remained directionally consistent, with smaller p-values in 2008 (higher PM2.5 levels) and larger p-values in 2015 (lower PM2.5 levels), supporting a longer-term, more chronic effect of PM2.5 rather than acute results driven by a single exposure year (Supplementary Table S2). These results may reflect the nature of lung function in adult asthma where variations from day to day or even year to year are modest.49 Despite these limitations, the strengths of our study include a novel approach to identifying biologically meaningful gene–environment interactions, which encompass over 400 oxidative stress pathway genes, that may worsen asthma in genetically susceptible individuals.

In addition to exposure limitations, our findings may be confounded by race and socioeconomic status (SES). While we stratified analyses by racial group (Black and White), residual differences in genetic ancestry, exposure, and structural factors may still bias results. PM2.5 exposure is disproportionately higher in racially and economically marginalised communities.50 Excluding SNPs with discordant effects between groups may improve reliability but limits generalisability. The absence of individual-level SES data, such as income and education, and reliance on area-level proxies may obscure within-group differences.50 Healthcare disparities could also confound associations between pollution, genetics, and lung function. Future studies with refined ancestry, exposure, SES, and treatment data are needed to better understand these complex, intersecting influences.

In conclusion, our study represents one of the largest GxE studies to date in asthma and the first to integrate airway gene expression data. Our approach expands previous GxE research in asthma by analysing thousands of known SNPs in 120 oxidative stress-related genes. Using this approach, we identified 20 SNPs across seven oxidative stress-related genes that interact with PM2.5 exposure to influence asthmatic lung function and airway gene expression. These findings could begin provide critical insights into the mechanisms behind the effects of pollution on human health while identifying novel therapeutic targets for individuals at high risk due to PM2.5 exposure.
