RmTIL, a cysteine-rich protein as a putative microbiota modulator from Rhipicephalus microplus tick
Authors: Gabriel C. A. Costa, Gabriel F. Goulart, Fernando A. A. Silva, Ricardo J. S. Torquato, Luís F. Parizi, Itabajara da Silva Vaz, Lucas Tirloni, Aparecida S. Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rhipicephalus microplus, also known as the “cattle tick”, presents worldwide distribution in tropical and subtropical regions and causes significant economic losses in livestock activity. At the present work, an immunogenic cysteine-rich protein produced in salivary glands was identified through an association of phage display and third generation sequencing (TGS) high-throughput techniques. This protein presenting a trypsin inhibitor-like (TIL) domain from R. microplus, named here as RmTIL, was expressed as a recombinant protein and submitted to a functional characterization as a serine protease inhibitor. The recombinant protein corresponding to its TIL domain portion (rRmTIL-D) inhibited human neutrophil elastase, cathepsin G, and subtilisin A, displaying inhibition constant (Ki) values of 7.0, 32.7 and 182.5 nM, respectively. RNA interference (RNAi) was employed to evaluate the effect of RmTIL-gene expression over reproductive parameters in engorged female ticks, revealing that eggs viability was significantly affected after gene silencing. Moreover, RmTIL gene silencing led to upregulation of 16S bacterial rRNA levels in ovary and eggs, while experimental infection of ticks with Gram-positive bacteria resulted in upregulated RmTIL transcripts. Following the increased bacterial burden, genes encoding proteins related to embryo development, lipid, redox, and energy metabolism pathways were modulated in eggs laid by RNAi-silenced females. Data presented here indicate that RmTIL is an immunogenic protein playing the two important roles of inhibiting host proteases and regulating tick microbiota, both crucial to the R. microplus life cycle.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00436-026-08712-3.

Introduction

Rhipicephalus microplus, commonly called “cattle tick”, is a tick species well known for infesting livestock animals and distributed worldwide in tropical and subtropical regions (Estrada-Peña et al. 2006; Garcia et al. 2019). During the blood-feeding process, ticks ingest significant amounts of blood from hosts, resulting in stress and a decrease in milk and meat production (Borges et al. 2002). Moreover, R. microplus can transmit different pathogens to cattle, such as Babesia spp. and Anaplasma marginale, which may cause severe disease to the animals (Jonsson et al. 2008). Additionally, it has been shown that repeatedly infested bovines develop acquired tick resistance (ATR), which results in decreased weight and number of engorged ticks in subsequent infestations. Moreover, ATR can reduce the risk of pathogen transmission from ticks to hosts (Karasuyama et al. 2020). Sera from repeatedly infested bovines were able to block R. microplus antihemostatic mechanisms, thereby impacting the effect of tick saliva on blood coagulation and endothelium activation modulation (Reck et al. 2009). Since repeatedly infested cattle develop resistance to the pharmacological effects of tick saliva, hindering successful blood feeding, the search for immunogenic salivary antigens offers a promising approach for identifying targets that may confer protection against tick infestations. Therefore, several antigens produced in salivary glands or in other tick tissues have been tested in vaccine formulations. However, many of those showed variable efficacy against tick strains in different countries, emphasizing that new antigens should be identified to develop an effective vaccine against several R. microplus strains (Pereira et al. 2022).

It is well known that tick saliva contains hundreds of different proteins, many of which are potent enzymes and enzyme inhibitors essential for successful blood-feeding by disrupting host hemostasis and immunity (Francischetti et al. 2009; Jmel et al. 2021). To protect themselves from microbial invasion at the feeding site, ticks also secrete protease inhibitors (Blisnick et al. 2017) and other antimicrobial peptides which possess microbicidal activity and help to maintain the sterility of the ingested blood meal (Lu et al. 2010). Tick molecules, including a trypsin inhibitor-like (TIL) protein (Fogaça et al. 2006) were shown to perform both inhibitory activity against proteases and antimicrobial activity. Therefore, due to their great importance for tick blood-feeding, salivary protease inhibitors and antimicrobial proteins should be investigated and considered as targets for the development of novel tick-control strategies.

At the present study, a transcript encoding a cysteine-rich immunogenic salivary protein containing TIL domain, herein referred to as RmTIL, was identified. This was achieved by screening a phage display library constructed from salivary glands cDNA of partially fed R. microplus female ticks, using IgG antibodies elicited in cattle repeatedly exposed to tick infestations. To address the importance of this protein on tick blood-feeding and reproduction, the characterization of the TIL domain region of RmTIL was carried out, revealing that it exhibits inhibitory activity against serine proteases, while RNA interference (RNAi) followed by 16S rRNA metatranscriptome and gene expression analysis in ovary and eggs suggest that RmTIL plays a regulatory role in microbiota in ovary and eggs. Collectively, these findings suggest that RmTIL is involved in tick-host interactions and the control of symbiont microbiota and reproductive metabolism, being a promising candidate for development of anti-tick strategies.

Materials and methods

Rhipicephalus microplus ticks

Rhipicephalus microplus ticks (Porto Alegre strain) used in the present study were fed in Hereford cattle and collected from the colony maintained at the Faculdade de Veterinária, Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul (UFRGS), Brazil. The research was conducted in accordance with the International and National Directives and Norms of ethics and methodological guidance and approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul (UFRGS) (projects numbers 27559 and 24651).

Phage display

The screening of immunogenic proteins and peptides expressed in salivary glands of R. microplus ticks was performed through the phage display technique. First, an ORFeome M13 phage display library was constructed using double-stranded cDNA fragments of salivary glands of R. microplus female ticks partially fed on cattle (average weight = 45 ± 0.7 mg) according to Becker et al. (2015). First, salivary glands (n = 5) were dissected in 0.9% NaCl using a steromicroscope and fine tip tweezers and homogenized in 1.5 mL microtubes containing 200 µL of TRIzol® Reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). Total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions, treated with TURBO™ DNase (Invitrogen, CA, USA) and quantified in a NanoVue Plus (GE Healthcare, NJ, USA) spectrophotometer. One µg of total RNA was employed in cDNA synthesis using the SMARTer PCR cDNA synthesis kit (Takara Bio, CA, USA). Double-stranded cDNA was amplified by PCR using the Platinum SuperFi II Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, IL, USA) in reactions in a Veriti™ 96-Well Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) set to 98 °C for 30 s followed by 26 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 10 s and 72 °C for 3 min, and a final cycle of 72 °C for 5 min. Amplified cDNA was fragmented using the DES500 ultrasonic system (Unique, SP, Brazil) at 50% power and 2 min pulses on ice (5x). Then, fragments ends were repaired using the Fast DNA End Repair Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, IL, USA). DNA was loaded on 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and fragments between 100 bp and 1 kb were recovered and purified using the Wizard® SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up System (Promega, WI, USA). Blunt-ended cDNA fragments were cloned into pHORF3 phagemid (previously linearized with PmeI), kindly provided by Dr. Michael Hust of the Department of Medical Biotechnology at Technische Universität Braunschweig, Germany (Kügler et al. 2008). Then, the M13 phage library was amplified and selected in three biopanning rounds for IgG elicited in a Hereford cattle exposed to several experimental infestations by R. microplus ticks according to Costa et al. (2024). Following the last biopanning round, all the eluted phages were recovered to have their immunogenic peptides identified through DNA sequencing.

DNA sequencing

Phages presenting salivary peptides recognized by anti-tick saliva IgG on the 3rd biopanning round were utilized to infect E. coli TOP10F´ cells. The pHORF3 phagemids containing tick cDNA sequences were purified from bacteria using the Fast-n-Easy Plasmid Mini-Prep Kit (Cellco Biotec, SP, Brazil). Then, using 50 ng of DNA from minipreps corresponding to the libraries pre and post screening as templates, the cloned DNA sequences were amplified by PCR using the primers pHORF3_seq_Fw 5’ TTAGCTCACTCATTAGGCACCCC 3’ and pHORF3_seq_Rv 5’ CATTCCACAGACAGCCCTCATAG 3’, and Platinum SuperFi II Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, IL, USA) in reactions in thermal cycler set to 98 °C for 30 s followed by 18 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 10 s and 72 °C for 30 s, and a final cycle of 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR reactions were loaded on agarose gel and DNA bands corresponding to amplicons were removed and purified as previously described. After quantification using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, IL, USA), DNA was used for production of sequencing libraries. First, end-prep was performed using NEBNext End repair/dA-tailing Module (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), and then barcodes and adapters were added using the Native Barcoding Kit 24 V14 SQK-NBD114.24 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). Following, libraries were pooled, and the material was loaded on a R10.4.1 flow cell attached to a Nanopore MinION device, and Nanopore sequencing was performed for 24 h. The obtained reads were basecalled at super accurate (SUP) model, demultiplexed and filtered (Phred Q-score > 11) using MinKNOW software v.23 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). Remaining reads were trimmed to remove the primers regions using the Porechop tool (Wick et al. 2017), dereplicated, and clustered (minimum abundance = 4 and 8 for pre and post screening, respectively) using UNOISE algorithm on VSEARCH tool (Rognes et al. 2016). Translated DNA sequences corresponding to salivary peptides were identified using the local sequence aligner DIAMOND tool(Buchfink et al. 2021) against arthropods protein sequences in non-redundant (nr) and Transcriptome Shotgun Assembly (TSA) databases from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI).

Relative gene expression quantification

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed to investigate the relative RmTIL-encoding gene expression in salivary glands of female ticks during blood feeding. Additionally, expression levels were also evaluated in salivary glands, ovary, midgut, and Gené’s organ of engorged females. Total RNA from salivary glands of partially engorged R. microplus female ticks in different weight ranges were obtained as previously described. One µg of RNA was employed for cDNA synthesis reactions using the ImProm-II™ Reverse Transcription System (Promega, WI, USA). Total RNA samples from salivary glands, midgut, ovary and Gené’s organ from the engorged females (> 200 mg) up to 24 h after natural detachment were also extracted and used as templates for cDNA synthesis. At least four different biological samples obtained from individual ticks were utilized in each experimental group. The reactions were prepared with 5 µL of Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix kit (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA), 1 µL of cDNA (diluted 10x) as template, RmTIL qPCR primers, and Milli-Q water to a final volume of 10 µL. Negative controls were performed in absence of template or 1 µL of DNAse-treated RNA samples (diluted 5x) to confirm the absence of genomic DNA contamination. The reactions were conducted in 96-well plates using a StepOnePlus™ System (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) set to 95 °C for 10 min followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Relative quantification data were achieved according to the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001), and using elongation factor 1-α (ELF1α) (XM_037421720.1) as a reference gene (Nijhof et al. 2009). The evaluation of bacterial 16S rRNA expression levels in ovaries was performed as above, using ovary cDNA samples diluted 3x and bacterial 16S rRNA universal primers (Bacchetti De Gregoris et al. 2011). Primers used in qPCR experiments are described in supplementary Table 1.

Cloning, expression and purification of recombinant RmTIL and RmTIL-D

The coding sequence (CDS) corresponding to RmTIL (GenBank accession number: GJSL01013457.1) without signal peptide was predicted using SignalP 6.0 (Teufel et al. 2022), while its protein structure was predicted and analyzed using the RoseTTAFold method (Baek et al. 2021) in Robetta protein structure prediction web server (robetta.bakerlab.org) and the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System v2.5.4 (Schrödinger Inc, NY, USA), respectively. The sequence coding for RmTIL was synthetized and cloned into EcoRI and NotI restriction sites of pcDNA™3.1 + vector containing a signal peptide of human tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) followed by a 6x histidine tag (6xHis tag) and an enterokinase site (Bio Basic Inc, ON, Canada). Plasmid DNA was purified using NucleoBond PC 2000 plasmid megaprep kits (Takara Bio, CA, USA). Expi293 human embryonic kidney cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, IL, USA) were transfected with endotoxin-free plasmid DNA at the Protein Expression Laboratory of Leidos Biomedical Research, Inc. (Frederick, MD, USA). Supernatant was collected 72 h post-transfection, centrifuged at 1,000 x g for 15 min, frozen, and shipped to our laboratory.

Purification of recombinant RmTIL (rRmTIL) was performed in two different steps. First, by affinity to 6xHis tag, using the AmMag™ Ni Magnetic beads (GenScript, NJ, USA), according to manufacturer’s instructions. Then, eluted proteins were loaded on a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 75 pg size exclusion column previously equilibrated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4 and connected to a ÄKTA™ chromatography system (GE Healthcare, Uppsala, Sweden). Fractions containing the rRmTIL were pooled, concentrated using Amicon® Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter (Merck Millipore, MA, USA), quantified using Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, IL, USA), and stored at 4 °C.

The region corresponding to the TIL domain of RmTIL (RmTIL-D) was amplified by PCR in a 25 µL reaction using 1 µL cDNA of salivary glands from partially fed female ticks as template and 200 nM of the following primers: RmTIL-D-Fw (presenting an EcoRI restriction site followed by a 6xHis tag and an enterokinase cleavage site sequence) 5’ TTCAGGAATTCATCACCATCACCACCACGATGATGATGATAAATCACCTCAAAAACGATGCTGG 3’ and RmTIL-D-Rv (presenting a XbaI restriction site) 5’ GGATTTCTAGACTAATAAGCCTTGCACTGCCATG 3’. The PCR and the 255 pb amplicon purification were carried out as previously described. After digestion with EcoRI and XbaI restriction enzymes, the DNA insert was cloned into pPICZα B vector (Invitrogen, CA, USA) previously digested with the same enzymes. The pPICZαB-RmTIL-D construct (10 µg) was linearized with SacI and used for transformation of Pichia pastoris GS115 competent cells in a MicroPulser Electroporator (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Transformed P. pastoris containing the RmTIL-D DNA was inoculated in 50 mL of BMGY medium [1.0% yeast extract, 2.0% peptone in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer pH 6.0, 1.34% YNB, 4 × 10 − 5 (w/v) D-biotin and 1% (v/v) glycerol] and incubated at 30 °C and 200 RPM until OD600 = 5.0. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (15 min, 3,000 g, 16 °C) and resuspended in BMMY medium [1.0% yeast extract, 2.0% peptone in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer at pH 6.0, 1.34% YNB 4 × 10 − 5% (w/v) D-biotin and 0.5% (v/v) methanol] for an OD600 = 1. Recombinant protein expression was carried out at 30 °C and 200 RPM, induced by methanol (0.5% final concentration) every 24 h for 96 h. The culture was centrifuged for 15 min at 3,000 g and the supernatant was collected. Recombinant RmTIL-D was precipitated by adding 3 M (NH4)2SO4, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. After solubilization in PBS, pH 7.4, RmTIL-D was purified by affinity chromatography in a HisTrap column (Cytiva, MA, USA) previously equilibrated with PBS, pH 7.4 and connected to the ÄKTA™ chromatography system (GE Healthcare, Uppsala, Sweden). Washing and elution steps were performed in 40 mM and 200 mM imidazole concentrations, respectively. Fractions containing purified RmTIL-D were pooled, had imidazole removed in Spectrum™ 3 kDa dialysis membrane tubing (Thermo Fisher Scientific, IL, USA), and proteins were quantified and stored as described above.

Enzymatic inhibition assays

The rRmTIL and rRmTIL-D were evaluated according to their enzymatic inhibitory activities against different serine proteases. Human neutrophil elastase (HNE) (30 nM), human neutrophil cathepsin G (98 nM) or subtilisin A (18 nM) were preincubated with 300 nM of each inhibitor in 20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.01% Tween-20, pH 8.0 buffer for HNE, 100 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5 buffer for cathepsin G or 100 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, pH 8.6 buffer for subtilisin A at 37 °C for 10 min. The chromogenic substrates (200 µM) MeOSuc-Ala-Ala-Pro-Val-pNA (HNE) or MeOSuc-Ala-Ala-Pro-Phe-pNA (cathepsin G and subtilisin A) were added, and incubated at 30 °C for 15 min. Reactions were monitored at 405 nm wavelength in Synergy HT (BioTek Instruments, VT, USA), and the enzyme residual activities were determined by using the Vmax values in the presence and absence of inhibitors. The experiment was performed three times. The inhibition constant (Ki) values were calculated by fitting the plot of the residual Vmax values obtained under crescent concentrations of inhibitors to the equation for a tight-binding inhibitor using nonlinear regression analysis (Morrison 1969).

RmTIL RNAi

RmTIL gene expression affect by RNAi technique was performed to evaluate the reproductive parameters in engorged female ticks. First, the following primers containing the T7 polymerase promoter sequence at their 5’ ends were designed using the Primer3 tool (Rozen and Skaletsky 1999) and checked for specificity to RmTIL CDS using the Primer-BLAST tool (NCBI): Forward – 5’ TAATACGACTCACTATAGGGAGATCTGTCTCTTGCTGTGGTCC 3’ and Reverse – 5′ TAATACGACTCACTATAGGGAGATTTCAGGGTATGTGGTGGGG 3’. To check the specificity of the putative small interfering RNAs (siRNA) to RmTIL messenger RNA (mRNA), off-target prediction was carried out using the si-Fi21 tool (Lück et al. 2019) and a transcriptome of R. microplus female ticks (GenBank accession number: GHWJ00000000.1) as database. After PCR using the described primers and salivary glands cDNA as template, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) was synthetized using the 413 bp RmTIL amplicon and the T7 RiboMAX Express RNAi System (Promega, WI, USA). The dsRNA was purified, quantified in NanoVue Plus spectrophotometer (GE Healthcare, NJ, USA) and diluted in sterile PBS, pH 7.4 at 2 µg/µL. The dsRNA corresponding to GFP (green fluorescent protein) DNA (MN114103.1) was used as non-related control. Engorged female ticks (average weight 319 ± 33 mg) were washed in ethanol 70% for 30 s, rinsed in sterile water and injected with 1 µL (2 µg) of RmTIL or GFP dsRNA, using insulin syringes (5 mm x 0.23 mm), approximately 24 h after the end of blood feeding. Ticks were individually kept on sterile 12-well plates, under controlled temperature (28 °C) and semi-controlled conditions of humidity (> 80% relative humidity). Sixteen or 48 h ater dsRNA injection, part of the females had the ovaries dissected (n = 5) in sterile Petri dishes under stereomicroscope and total RNA was extracted as previously described to be used in cDNA synthesis. The rest of the ticks were kept on plates during oviposition. Eggs masses produced by each female (n = 10 per group) were weighed and the reproductive efficiency index was calculated according to the following formula: weight of eggs/weight of engorged female X 100 (%) (Bennett 1974). Additionally, eggs viability based on larval hatching was also assessed by the formula: number of larvae/number of eggs X 100 (%).

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed to investigate the relative expression of different genes in eggs laid by R. microplus ticks following RNAi treatment. Total RNA was extracted from masses of eggs produced by individual female ticks (n = 5; 20 mg of eggs per female) collected approximately 5 days after the start of oviposition. The cDNA synthesis reactions and qPCR using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix kit (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) were carried out as previously described.

Experimental bacterial infection

Engorged females (> 200 mg) up to 24 h after natural detachment were experimentally infected by Gram-negative Escherichia coli 35,218™ or Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus 23,235™ bacteria (ATCC, VA, USA). Cells were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C and 180 RPM to exponential phase (OD600 = 0.5), centrifuged and resuspended in sterile PBS, pH 7.4. Experimental infection was based on methodology described by (Rechav et al. 1999). Ticks were washed, submitted to subcuticular injection at the dorsal region with 2 µL (1 × 105 cells) of bacteria suspension and kept under the same temperature and humidity conditions as previously described. cDNA libraries corresponding to ovaries 48 h after bacterial infection were synthesized and RmTIL transcripts were quantified by qPCR.

16S bacterial rRNA metatranscriptomic analysis

The bacterial microbiota in eggs laid by RNAi treated females were evaluated through bacterial 16S rRNA taxonomic analysis after Nanopore sequencing. First, cDNA libraries corresponding to masses of eggs produced by individual female ticks (n = 8 per group; 20 mg of eggs per female) collected between 5 and 10 days after the start of oviposition were synthetized to be used as PCR templates. The amplicons (~ 1,500 bp) comprising the V1-V9 regions of full-length 16S rRNA were produced by PCR, using the following primers: 27 F – 5’ AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG 3’ (Lane 1991) and 1492R – 5’ GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT 3’ (Turner et al. 1999) and Platinum SuperFi II Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, IL, USA). DNA amplification was performed in thermal cycler under the following cycles: 98 °C for 30 s, followed by 35 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 50 °C for 15 s, 72 °C for 45 s, and a final step of 72 °C for 3 min. Amplified DNA samples were loaded in agarose gel and amplicons were recovered and purified. PCR samples were prepared and sequenced on MinION device as previously described. The obtained duplex reads were basecalled at super accurate (SUP) model and demultiplexed using Dorado software v.0.5.2 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). Reads were mapped (minimap2), filtered by size (1200–1800 bp) and quality (Phred Q-score > 20), and the taxonomic assignment (97% identity cutoff) were performed using the 16S workflow of EPI2ME software (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) using Silva database as reference. Abundance matrix and alpha diversity indexes were calculated by EPI2ME software, while Bray-Curtis dissimilarity beta diversity metrics, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) statistical test and a Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) plot were generated using Vegan (Oksanen et al. 2013) and ggplot2 (Wickham 2011) packages in Rstudio (RStudio Team 2022).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). The statistically significant differences between two groups with parametric distribution data were determined by Student’s T test, while Mann‒Whitney tests were employed for nonparametric data. ANOVA test and Tukey’s post hoc test were used to determine significant differences between three or more groups with parametric distribution data, while the Kruskal‒Wallis test and Dunn’s post hoc test were used for nonparametric data.

Results

Salivary immunogenic proteins screening

The DNA sequencing of the R. microplus salivary ORFeome library generated 956,861 Nanopore reads, comprising the non-screened and the screened libraries after three biopanning rounds of phage display. The non-screened library’s reads were clustered in 8586 individual sequences. Nevertheless, after biopanning, the number was reduced to 110 sequences (Supplementary Table 2) corresponding to peptides with high affinity to the anti-tick saliva IgG antibodies elicited in the infested cattle, or 1.28% of total sequences available in the ORFeome library (Table 1).

Rhipicephalus microplus trypsin inhibitor-like protein

The amino acid sequence of the translated CDS revealed the presence of 10 conserved cysteine residues and a TIL cysteine rich domain (cd19941). The protein named here as RmTIL presented a predicted molecular weight and pI of 15.6 kDa and 8.86, respectively, excluding the signal peptide (Fig. 1).

The amino acid sequence of mature RmTIL was aligned to sequences of Ixodidin and BmSI-7, serine protease inhibitors from R. microplus. Although all the sequences presented the conserved cysteine residues of TIL domain, the alignment revealed low identity values to RmTIL of 36.92% and 43.28% for Ixodidin and BmSI-7, respectively. Nevertheless, if considered only the TIL domain region, comprised of the 10 cysteine residues, the identity levels were higher, of 44% and 50%, respectively. Moreover, both inhibitors, with 65 and 67 amino acid residues were significantly smaller than RmTIL, with 137 residues length, due to the large N and C-terminal portions surrounding the conserved cysteine rich domain, absent in the former proteins. Consequently, the screened peptide by phage display, which is part of the C-terminal portion of RmTIL, is not conserved in the other TIL proteins sequences and the elicited antibodies for this region should only recognize RmTIL. Finally, RmTIL presented an alanine residue at position P1, similarly to the human neutrophil elastase inhibitor BmSI-7 (Fig. 2).

Recombinant RmTIL and RmTIL-D

Recombinant RmTIL was expressed in HEK293 mammalian cells system fused to a 6xHis tag and enterokinase cleavage site at the N-terminal region, presenting a predicted molecular weight and pI of 20 kDa and 10.88, respectively. Additionally, a second recombinant protein in which N and C-terminal ends were absent, resulting in a sequence composed mostly of the TIL cysteine rich domain of RmTIL, was expressed in P. pastoris cells system. This recombinant protein, named here as rRmTIL-Domain (rRmTIL-D), presented molecular weight and pI of 9.1 kDa and 8.5, respectively. The amino acid sequence within the TIL domain of rRmTIL was identical to rRmTIL-D, except for three residues (Supplementary Fig. 1). These, in addition to two residues at n-terminal region of mature RmTIL, diverged from the translated sequence of a full-length transcript amplified by PCR using cDNA of ticks from the same strain (data not shown), what could be explained as a natural polymorphism within tick population.

Purification of soluble rRmTIL was performed by nickel affinity and size exclusion chromatography, resulting in a major protein band slightly below the 20 kDa marker, corresponding to RmTIL predicted molecular size (Fig. 4a). Recombinant RmTIL-D was also purified by affinity chromatography, resulting in a single band between the 10 and 15 kDa in SDS-PAGE (Fig. 4b).

Enzymatic inhibition assays

The inhibitory activity of rRmTIL was evaluated for human neutrophil elastase in enzymatic kinetic assays, but did not show significant inhibition, since HNE presented 93% residual activity in the presence of the recombinant tick protein. Nevertheless, rRmTIL-D presented over 99% inhibitory activity towards HNE, or 80% for cathepsin G, under the same conditions. Moreover, it partially inhibited subtilisin A activity, reducing over 50% of its catalytic activity (Supplementary Fig. 2). Then, the inhibition constant (Ki) values of RmTIL-D for HNE, cathepsin G and subtilisin A were calculated and presented at nanomolar range. While the 7.04 ± 4.7 nM and 32.70 ± 5.7 nM values were estimated for HNE (Fig. 5a) and cathepsin G (Fig. 5b), respectively, a higher value of 182.58 ± 2.7 nM was obtained for subtilisin A (Fig. 5c).

RNAi and tick reproductive performance

RmTIL-encoding gene expression was detected in ovary of engorged female ticks. Therefore, RNAi was performed to investigate the putative role of RmTIL in reproductive parameters. RmTIL gene silencing did not cause tick mortality before eggs production and all RmTIL dsRNA-treated ticks laid eggs, while one specimen from control group died before the start of oviposition. The reproductive efficiency index was assessed and revealed that, for both groups, 42% of the body mass of engorged females was converted in eggs, suggesting that RmTIL RNAi did not impair fertility (Fig. 6a). Nevertheless, although it did not affect eggs production, a significant decrease in viability of eggs laid by RmTIL RNAi group was observed. While there was a 46% hatching rate among the eggs produced by ticks from control group, only 12% of those from RmTIL silencing group presented viability (Fig. 6b).

A bacterial 16S rRNA metatranscriptomic analysis in eggs laid by silenced ticks was performed to investigate possible effects of RmTIL RNAi over microbial composition during embryo development. Gene expression in eggs from RNAi treated ticks

To investigate the effects of RmTIL silencing over embryo development in eggs laid by ticks after dsRNA injection, a preliminary transcriptomic analysis was generated and indicated differential expressions in genes related to several metabolic pathways after RNAi treatment (data not shown). Then, relative expression levels of these genes were evaluated by qPCR. First, RmTIL and 16S rRNA transcript levels were accessed to be compared to those observed in ovaries of silenced engorged females. Opposing from ovaries, eggs did not present significant reduction in RmTIL transcript levels (Fig. 11a), indicating that the RmTIL silencing did not persist in eggs. Discussion

A cysteine-rich protein containing a TIL domain, named here as RmTIL, was identified as an essential protein in R. microplus physiology. RmTIL transcripts were detected in salivary glands and ovary, and RNAi-mediated silencing impaired embryogenesis and egg viability. Finally, the protein was functionally characterized as a serine protease inhibitor that is also involved in tick microbiota regulation.

An ORFeome phage display library was constructed using salivary transcripts from partially fed female R. microplus ticks and successfully screened against purified anti-tick saliva IgG elicited in cattle submitted to successive infestations by R. microplus ticks. Previous studies have demonstrated that saliva composition varies throughout the feeding process, as an evolutionary strategy to evade vertebrate host’s immune system (Ribeiro and Mans 2020; Mans 2023; da Silva Vaz Junior et al. 2024). Therefore, the present work focused on screening proteins whose the correspondent encoding genes were expressed in salivary glands of ticks with an average weight of 45 mg. At this stage, ticks are still on the slow feeding phase, when the saliva content was shown to be rich in secreted proteins, including proteases and protease inhibitors, important to form the feeding site and oppose the host homeostatic and immune responses (Kemp et al. 1982; Tirloni et al. 2014). Then, the aim was to identify molecules that could be functionally affected through IgG binding at the feeding site, disturbing tick performance on blood acquisition.

Transcripts corresponding to the screened RmTIL were detected in salivary glands extracted from females ranging from 30 to 265 mg. Silva Vaz Junior et al. (2024) performed a proteomic analysis in salivary glands from R. microplus female ticks throughout the slow and rapid feeding phases and detected TIL proteins during the whole process. Surprisingly, only five TIL proteins were identified and RmTIL was not among them. Therefore, it is possible that RmTIL concentration was lower than the other TIL proteins in saliva content, which could explain why it was not detected. Besides salivary glands, RmTIL transcripts were detected in ovaries of engorged female ticks, suggesting the presence of the protein in this organ and a possible function related to tick reproduction, specifically eggs production and embryo development. Moreover, many protease inhibitors secreted by arthropods were demonstrated to act not only as inhibitors of proteolytic activity, but also as antimicrobial molecules (Gulley et al. 2013; Blisnick et al. 2017; Parizi et al. 2018), including TIL proteins secreted by ticks, such as Ixodidin and Rhamp1 inhibitors. The former was shown to inhibit the growth of E. coli and Micrococcus luteus (Fogaça et al. 2006), while the latter presented antimicrobial activity against several Gram-negative bacteria like Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella typhimurium and E. coli (Zhang et al. 2015). Therefore, the role of RmTIL in tick physiology was addressed by investigating its capacity to inhibit proteases and affect tick microbiota.

TIL proteins like Ixodidin (Fogaça et al. 2006) and BmSI-7 (Sasaki et al. 2008) from R. microplus, or Ixochymostatin (Martins et al. 2025) from I. scapularis ticks were previously characterized as serine protease inhibitors. Thus, rRmTIL was also tested as an inhibitor in enzymatic assays. However, despite presenting the conserved TIL domain and the same alanine residue at P1 position (Schechter and Berger 1967) as the BmSI-7 elastase inhibitor, rRmTIL did not show significant inhibitory activity against human neutrophil elastase. Unlike the previous inhibitors, whose amino acid sequences are largely restricted to the conserved TIL loop, mature RmTIL sequence is more than twice as long, with large extensions on N- and C-terminal regions. Therefore, to investigate a possible inhibitory function of the domain portion, the recombinant RmTIL domain was expressed and evaluated in enzymatic assays. In the absence of the terminal regions, the protein inhibited neutrophil elastase and subtilisin A, similarly to BmSI-7 (Sasaki et al. 2008). Moreover, the inhibition of cathepsin G was also observed, similarly to that described for Ixochymostatin, a TIL protein presenting tyrosine at P1 (Martins et al. 2025). Hence, these results suggest that one or both terminal ends of RmTIL might impair the access to the binding site on enzyme’s cleft, what could be confirmed by protein structure analysis. Alternatively, the misfolding of recombinant RmTIL in mammalian cells could also explain the low inhibitory activity, and expression in arthropoda heterologous systems could be done as an attempt to obtain higher inhibitory activities. The peptide from C-terminal region of RmTIL, displayed in phage surface, was recognized by anti-tick saliva antibodies, indicating that the protein is secreted in saliva and capable of stimulating the host immune system. Therefore, as the inhibitory activity was achieved only after the removal of N and C-terminal ends, it would be possible that after being secreted, the native protein could be processed by other tick proteases or at the blood feeding site by host proteases. It would generate an active inhibitor able to modulate diverse host responses such as inflammation, hemostasis and wound healing, regulated by elastase and cathepsin G released by neutrophils (Kessenbrock et al. 2011; Zhu et al. 2021). Additionally, after cleavage, the terminal ends could also play a different role in tick-host interaction. The C-terminal region presents a high content of proline residues, also common in proline-rich antimicrobial peptides (PrAMPs) (Welch et al. 2020; Stączek et al. 2024), what could represent an additional function in controlling tick microbiota or bacteria present at the feeding site. Hence, antimicrobial experiments should be conducted in the presence of the C-terminal region of RmTIL.

RNAi was used in the present study to investigate the effect of RmTIL gene silencing on R. microplus physiology. Although RmTIL gene expression was detected in salivary glands and rRmTIL-D inhibited host proteases, RNAi was not employed in this study to evaluate the effects of RmTIL silencing on tick-host interactions during blood feeding due to the technical limitations in delivering controlled amounts of dsRNA to immature ticks before infestation. Instead, engorged female ticks were treated to explore the effects over reproductive parameters. The assay revealed that RmTIL silencing did not affect eggs production but severely impacted their viability. The effect over egg development was also observed in other studies in which RNAi technique was applied to silence tick genes (Hussein et al. 2019; Costa et al. 2024). Additionally, RmTIL RNAi led to upregulated microbiota levels in ovary. A reverse relationship between microbiota abundance and TIL proteins was previously described by Guizzo et al. (2022), that observed the downregulation of RmTIL and other TIL proteins gene expression in R. microplus metanymphs after the removal of Coxiella endosymbiont bacteria. Finally, the disturbance of tick microbiota by experimental infection with Gram-negative S. aureus also led to RmTIL-encoding gene upregulation. Similar result was observed for a TIL expressed by the Asian corn borer, Ostrinia furnacalis, 12 h after bacterial infection. Nevertheless, the stimulation was caused by E. coli (Guan et al. 2022). Hence, the observation that RmTIL gene expression was influenced by endosymbiotic bacteria levels or bacterial infection in ovary suggests that the protein could play a secondary role as a microbiota modulator. However, this effect might not be due to a direct antimicrobial activity, as observed for the Ixodidin (Fogaça et al. 2006), since preliminary antimicrobial assays were performed and both rRmTIL and rRmTIL-D did not show activity towards E. coli and S. epidermidis bacteria (data not shown). Therefore, further experiments should be performed to investigate whether RmTIL could modulate tick microbiota by inhibiting the bacterial proteases secreted to evade host immunity and necessary to dissemination (Armstrong 2001).

The 16S rRNA metranscriptomic data obtained from eggs of RNAi silenced ticks revealed that RmTIL regulation did not lead to significant modifications in microbiota richness and overall composition, in comparison to control group. The major bacterial taxon found in eggs from both groups was identified as the endosymbiont Coxiella sp. Previous studies have described a high relative abundance of this endosymbiont in R. microplus microbial composition (Andreotti et al. 2011; Rojas-Jaimes et al. 2021; Hernandez et al. 2023). Guizzo et al. (2017) estimated a 98% relative abundance of Coxiella sp. in eggs, value comparable to the present study. Moreover, the importance of this endosymbiont to diverse physiological processes during R. microplus development was demonstrated, especially on blood feeding (Guizzo et al. 2022). Therefore, novel studies should be conducted to improve the understanding of the interaction between RmTIL and symbiont bacteria, and to investigate if blocking RmTIL activity would affect tick fitness at the engorgement phase.

The gene expression analysis revealed that RmTIL transcript levels were restored in eggs from silenced females, but 16S bacterial rRNA transcripts remained significantly increased, as observed in ovaries. Then, the microbiota imbalance caused by Coxiella sp. proliferation in RmTIL RNAi eggs was probably the causative agent for the regulation observed for many genes encoding proteins from different metabolic pathways. Transcripts identified as a papilin, coding a secreted protein containing Kunitz BPTI domains, were upregulated in eggs after RmTIL RNAi treatment. There is not vast information about the functional role of these proteins in tick physiology, but they were demonstrated to be upregulated in response to Theileria annulata infection in Hyalomma anatolicum ticks (Abbasi et al. 2024). In other invertebrates, as Drosophila melanogaster and Caenorhabditis elegans, papilins were described as glycoproteins present in the extracellular matrix and modulators of metalloproteinases, essential for embryo development, and RNAi treatment in these species affected cell arrangements, causing embryonic death (Kramerova et al. 2000, 2003). The upregulation of the gene encoding a perilipin-2 like intracellular protein was observed in RmTIL RNAi eggs. The homologous mammalian protein demonstrated a key role in fat metabolism by regulating deposition and mobilization in lipid storage droplets (Souza et al. 2002). The function of perilipins in tick immunity is still unclear, but the downregulation of LSD-1, Drosophila homolog of perilipin-1 (Miura et al. 2002), in response to the immune deficiency (IMD) pathway activation protected the insects from oxidative stress during bacterial infections (Wang et al. 2021). Another upregulated gene was identified as a GST, related to oxidative stress control in tick eggs during embryogenesis (Freitas et al. 2007). The redox metabolism has been pointed out as one of the mechanisms for tick immunity (Alberdi et al. 2019; Fogaça et al. 2021; Hernandez et al. 2022; Groth et al. 2022) and its role in R. microplus embryonic cells protection has been demonstrated (Kalil et al. 2017). Previous studies demonstrated the upregulation of GST gene expression in I. scapularis cells after A. marginale infection, indicating the favoring of bacterial survival in intracellular environment (de la Fuente et al. 2007; Kalil et al. 2017). Finally, genes encoding IDH3 and NDUFS2 mitochondrial proteins were also overexpressed in eggs in response to RmTIL RNAi treatment. IDH3 and NDUFS2 are involved in the maintenance of NADH/NAD+ balance within the cell due to NADH production by IDH3 in citric acid cycle (Gálvez and Gadal 1995) and consumption by NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase composed by NDUFS2 in oxidative phosphorylation (Hirst 2013). As the unbalance in NADH/NAD+ ratio can result in hydrogen peroxide production (Murphy 2009), upregulation of genes encoding these mitochondrial proteins could indicate an increased oxidative stress in eggs produced by RmTIL silenced females. Altogether, the observed transcriptional profiles of the evaluated genes indicate that the reduced hatching rates in eggs laid by silenced ticks could be explained by the generation of an oxidative environment capable of interrupting embryo development, what should be evaluated in further experiments.

Conclusion

In the present study, the combination of high-throughput phage display and TGS techniques was employed to identify antigenic proteins in R. microplus saliva. RmTIL, an immunogenic cysteine-rich protein was selected and characterized as a serine protease inhibitor against host proteases related to inflammation, hemostasis and wound healing responses that oppose tick blood-feeding. Moreover, RmTIL gene silencing affected tick immunity against bacteria, resulting in endosymbiont bacteria proliferation in eggs, disturbance of embryo development and reduction of egg viability. Thus, due to its inhibitory activity against host proteases and tick microbiota regulation, RmTIL should be essential to blood-feeding and tick immunity, representing a possible target for R. microplus control.
