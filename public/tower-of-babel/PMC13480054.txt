Developing diagnostic reasoning through point-of-care ultrasound in emergency medicine training: a qualitative study of international medical students
Authors: Hsiang-Chin Hsu, Shao-Chung Chu, Tzu-Ching Sung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTechnology-enhanced learning is increasingly applied in medical education to support complex cognitive processes, including diagnostic reasoning. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), as a portable and real-time imaging modality, has been widely integrated into clinical training. However, limited research has explored how learners engage with POCUS to construct diagnostic understanding, particularly within international medical education contexts involving students from resource-variable healthcare environments. This study aimed to investigate international medical students’ learning experiences with POCUS as a technology-enhanced learning tool.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted involving 28 third- and fourth-year international medical students who participated in a structured POCUS learning activity. The intervention combined guided instruction with supervised hands-on practice in cardiac and abdominal ultrasound within an emergency medicine training context. Following the activity, participants completed written reflective narratives. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis to identify recurring patterns in learners’ experiences and meaning-making processes.ResultsFour major themes were identified. First, POCUS facilitated diagnostic understanding through real-time visualisation of anatomical structures and physiological processes. Second, integration of ultrasound within problem-based clinical scenarios supported the development of diagnostic reasoning. Third, collaborative learning and instructor scaffolding enhanced engagement and knowledge construction. Fourth, participants recognised the limitations of POCUS and highlighted the importance of reflective practice and continued skill development. These findings demonstrate that learners actively integrate visual, experiential, and contextual information when engaging with POCUS.ConclusionPOCUS functions as an effective technology-enhanced learning tool that supports diagnostic reasoning by linking visualisation with contextualised and reflective learning processes. The findings extend current understanding of technology-enhanced medical education by demonstrating how domain-specific technologies facilitate meaning-making and cognitive integration in diverse and cross-cultural learning environments. These insights have implications for the design of ultrasound-based curricula in emergency medicine education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09406-1.

Introduction

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) has become increasingly integrated into undergraduate medical education as a bedside diagnostic tool that supports clinical assessment, clinical reasoning, and decision-making. Within the broader expansion of technology-enhanced learning, medical education has increasingly adopted digital and simulation-based approaches to promote experiential learning and clinically situated knowledge application [1]. These approaches enable learners to connect theoretical concepts with real-time clinical information, supporting the development of diagnostic thinking within authentic healthcare contexts. Educational strategies that combine supervised instruction with hands-on technological engagement may therefore facilitate deeper learning by allowing students to interpret clinical findings through direct observation and contextualised practice.

Among technology-enhanced learning modalities, POCUS represents a particularly relevant tool for bridging diagnostic knowledge and bedside application. Over the past decade, POCUS has evolved from a specialised imaging modality into an increasingly routine component of clinical practice across emergency medicine, internal medicine, and critical care [2]. Its portability and immediacy allow clinicians to incorporate real-time imaging into patient assessment, thereby supporting rapid bedside evaluation in situations characterised by diagnostic uncertainty and time-sensitive decision-making [2]. Recognising its growing clinical relevance, international consensus recommendations advocate for early exposure to ultrasound education and longitudinal integration across undergraduate medical curricula [3]. In resource-variable healthcare settings, portable ultrasound technologies may further support timely assessment when access to advanced diagnostic imaging is limited, highlighting their potential relevance across diverse clinical environments [4].

Reflecting these developments in clinical practice, many medical schools worldwide have incorporated ultrasound education into undergraduate curricula. International consensus statements recommend early exposure to ultrasound, longitudinal integration across training stages, and competency-based progression aligned with clinical reasoning development [3, 5, 6]. Although ultrasound education has been increasingly adopted within undergraduate medical programmes, evidence regarding its educational effectiveness remains heterogeneous. Systematic reviews suggest that ultrasound-supported learning may contribute to anatomical understanding, learner engagement, and confidence when implemented within structured educational contexts [7].

Evidence from specific educational settings further suggests that ultrasound integration may support anatomy learning and physical examination skills by enabling real-time visualisation of anatomical structures and reinforcing clinically relevant spatial understanding [8, 9]. So et al. reported that ultrasound-assisted teaching may improve medical students’ understanding of anatomy and physical diagnosis through active visual engagement and contextualised learning [9]. Similarly, Oteri et al. found that ultrasound-integrated instruction may contribute to improved physical examination performance when embedded within structured undergraduate curricula [8].

However, critical evaluations have also highlighted that many commonly cited educational benefits remain supported by context-dependent or limited evidence, warranting cautious interpretation of broad outcome claims [10]. Surveys nevertheless indicate widespread adoption of ultrasound teaching within medical programmes and strong perceived educational value among both students and faculty [11]. Despite increasing curricular integration, much of the existing literature has focused primarily on measurable technical outcomes—such as image acquisition skills, procedural competence, or learner satisfaction—while offering comparatively limited insight into how learners experience ultrasound-supported education as a broader process of learning, sense-making, and professional development [7].

From a health professions education perspective, POCUS-supported learning represents more than the acquisition of technical skills. Effective use of ultrasound in clinical practice requires learners to integrate visual information with clinical reasoning, interpret findings within conditions of diagnostic uncertainty, and apply knowledge within dynamic patient contexts. Learning theories grounded in experiential and social learning suggest that such competence develops through cycles of practice, reflection, and interaction with peers and expert clinicians [12–14]. Within this framework, technology-enhanced learning tools such as ultrasound may function as pedagogical resources that facilitate deeper conceptual understanding and the development of adaptive clinical expertise.

Despite the growing integration of ultrasound into undergraduate medical education, much of the existing literature has focused primarily on measurable technical outcomes, procedural competence, and learner satisfaction [7, 10, 11]. Comparatively less attention has been directed toward understanding how learners interpret, internalise, and construct meaning from ultrasound-supported educational experiences [7, 10, 15]. Although previous studies demonstrate increasing curricular adoption and perceived educational value [3, 11], l limited insight exists into how learners integrate ultrasound into clinical reasoning processes and how diagnostic understanding develops within technology-enhanced learning environments [7, 10, 15]. Consequently, the cognitive and interpretive dimensions of ultrasound-supported learning remain insufficiently understood, particularly in relation to how learners connect technical performance with diagnostic thinking and clinical meaning-making.

From the perspective of scholarly argumentation, a meaningful research contribution requires not only identifying what remains unknown but also explaining why that absence of understanding matters [16]. Applying a problem–gap–hook framework to the current literature highlights an important gap: although ultrasound education has become increasingly embedded within undergraduate curricula, the mechanisms through which learners translate ultrasound-supported experiences into diagnostic reasoning remain underexplored. Addressing this gap is particularly important because diagnostic reasoning is not acquired solely through technical exposure or procedural repetition. Rather, it develops through situated learning, reflective interpretation, and the contextual integration of clinical information within authentic practice settings [12–14]. Understanding these interpretive and cognitive dimensions may therefore provide a more comprehensive account of how ultrasound-supported learning contributes to the development of clinical reasoning in undergraduate medical education and may inform curriculum design that extends beyond technical competence to support deeper cognitive integration and clinically meaningful learning [16].

These learning processes may be particularly significant in international medical education contexts involving students preparing for practice across healthcare systems with differing diagnostic resources. Many international medical students are trained within transnational educational programmes while anticipating future practice across healthcare systems with varying diagnostic infrastructure, resource availability, and clinical practice conditions. In resource-limited or geographically dispersed healthcare systems, access to advanced imaging modalities such as computed tomography may be constrained by logistical, financial, or infrastructural factors [17]. In such environments, portable diagnostic tools like POCUS can play an important role in supporting early clinical decision-making and patient triage. Exposure to ultrasound-supported learning during medical training may therefore influence how international students develop clinical reasoning, diagnostic strategies, and preparedness for practice across diverse healthcare settings [3]. From the perspective of socially accountable medical education, integrating technology-enhanced diagnostic tools within international curricula may also contribute to educational equity and workforce responsiveness to global health needs [18].

Despite growing institutional adoption of ultrasound education and ongoing innovation in curricular design—including vertically integrated curricula, short-term intensive training models, and technology-supported learning environments [19–22] —important gaps remain in the literature. Systematic reviews highlight a lack of qualitative research examining how learners conceptualise the clinical value of POCUS, translate technological skills into diagnostic reasoning, and interpret the role of ultrasound within their developing professional identities [15]. Furthermore, relatively little research has explored these learning processes among international medical students preparing for future practice across resource-variable healthcare settings.

To address these gaps, the present study adopted a qualitative approach to explore international medical students’ reflective learning experiences within a cross-cultural, technology-enhanced medical education context. By examining students’ perspectives following participation in a structured POCUS learning activity, this study aims to illuminate how technology-enhanced clinical learning contributes to the development of clinical reasoning, learning processes, and perceptions of preparedness for future practice. Insights from this study may inform curriculum design and support the development of globally responsive and educationally meaningful approaches to technology-enhanced medical education.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative design using reflexive thematic analysis to explore how participation in a structured point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) learning activity shaped international medical students’ learning experiences, development of clinical reasoning, and perceptions of preparedness for future clinical practice. The study was informed by an interpretive qualitative paradigm and was designed to align closely with the research questions, which focused on learners’ subjective experiences and meaning-making within a cross-cultural, technology-enhanced medical education context.

A qualitative design was selected to explore how learners interpreted and integrated ultrasound-supported experiences into evolving understandings of diagnostic reasoning within a technology-enhanced educational context. Guided by an interpretive qualitative paradigm, the study focused on participants’ subjective experiences, meaning-making processes, and perceptions of how POCUS-supported learning contributed to clinical reasoning and professional development. By foregrounding students’ reflective accounts, this study sought to examine how technology-enhanced clinical learning was experienced and how ultrasound-supported instruction was interpreted and integrated into clinical reasoning and emerging professional identity.

Written reflective narratives were selected as the primary data source becauseWritten reflective narratives were selected as the primary data source because they enabled participants to articulate their experiences, perceptions, and interpretations in their own words while providing time for post-session reflection and meaning-making. Compared with interviews or focus groups, written reflections were considered particularly appropriate for capturing individual cognitive and interpretive processes, reducing potential peer influence, and supporting participants’ ability to reflect on evolving diagnostic understanding following the learning activity. This approach allowed participants to describe how they interpreted the educational and clinical relevance of ultrasound-supported learning within their own developmental contexts. Written reflection was also well suited to a cohort-based educational setting, as it enabled all students to contribute equally and supported broad representation of perspectives across the participant cohort.

Data were analysed using the six-phase reflexive thematic analysis framework described by Braun and Clarke, which supported systematic identification of patterns and development of themes grounded in participants’ accounts [23]. This approach enabled an in-depth examination of how learners negotiated technical skill acquisition, clinical meaning-making, and professional self-positioning within the context of ultrasound-supported education.

POCUS context and instructional framework

The POCUS learning activity formed part of the clinical skills curriculum for international medical students and was delivered in collaboration with emergency medicine faculty from a tertiary teaching hospital. The sessions were facilitated by board-certified emergency physicians with extensive experience in bedside ultrasound education and clinical supervision. The instructional approach positioned ultrasound as an extension of the physical examination and as a technology-enhanced tool to support bedside clinical reasoning. Teaching was structured to balance foundational knowledge acquisition with experiential learning, guided practice, and immediate feedback. The learning activity combined focused didactic instruction with supervised hands-on scanning practice in cardiac and abdominal ultrasound, reflecting contemporary competency-based approaches to undergraduate ultrasound education.

To approximate authentic bedside learning conditions, the instructional session incorporated both peer-assisted scanning and simulated patient practice within a controlled educational environment. During the hands-on component, students were divided into two groups and rotated through structured scanning stations. Participants practised ultrasound examination using both peer classmates and simulated patients, allowing exposure to varied scanning contexts and body habitus while promoting familiarity with bedside assessment techniques. Simulated patients were trained to support standardised educational interaction and maintain consistency across learning stations. Each student completed all instructional stations and participated in supervised cardiac and abdominal scanning exercises under faculty guidance. This rotational format ensured that all learners engaged in probe handling, image acquisition, image interpretation, and observational learning while receiving immediate corrective feedback. The use of peer and simulated patient scanning supported experiential learning by enabling repeated practice and reinforcing anatomical orientation, clinical reasoning, and contextual interpretation of ultrasound findings.

All participants received standardised instructional content, scanning protocols, and supervised practice opportunities. Faculty-to-student ratios were maintained to allow individualised guidance and real-time correction of scanning technique. Portable ultrasound devices were used to approximate bedside clinical conditions within a controlled educational environment. The instructional sequence included introductory ultrasound principles, faculty-led demonstration, supervised scanning practice, and facilitated discussion focused on integrating ultrasound findings with clinical reasoning. Further details of the curriculum, instructional sequence, and faculty roles are provided in Supplementary Material S1.

Participants

Participants were third- and fourth-year international medical students enrolled in a post-baccalaureate medical programme at a Taiwanese medical university. At the time of the study, students were in the pre-clinical or early clinical phases of training and were preparing for subsequent hospital-based clerkships and future practice across diverse healthcare systems. At the time of participation, students had completed foundational biomedical coursework and introductory clinical skills training. More than half of the participants had prior exposure to introductory bedside clinical assessment, history taking, and basic diagnostic reasoning exercises during pre-clinical skills training, although formal ultrasound instruction remained limited.

The cohort comprised students from varied national, cultural, and educational backgrounds, reflecting the cross-cultural and transnational nature of the programme. Many participants anticipated future clinical practice in settings where access to advanced diagnostic imaging may be limited, making bedside ultrasound learning particularly relevant to their anticipated professional contexts.

The POCUS learning activity was delivered during the academic semester as a scheduled component of the clinical skills curriculum rather than as an optional workshop. All eligible third- and fourth-year students enrolled in the programme attended the instructional session as part of standard coursework, and 28 students consented to inclusion of their reflective narratives for qualitative analysis. Students who completed the learning activity and submitted a reflective narrative were eligible for inclusion in the study. Participation in the research component was voluntary. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and all data were anonymised prior to analysis.

Data collection

Data consisted of written reflective narratives submitted by students following completion of the POCUS learning activity. Reflection prompts were developed by faculty members with expertise in medical education and reflective practice and were aligned with the educational objectives of the ultrasound programme.

The prompts invited students to reflect on their learning experiences, technical and cognitive challenges, emotional responses, perceived clinical relevance, and evolving understanding of bedside diagnostic reasoning. Specifically, students were asked to consider: (1) their overall learning experience, (2) challenges encountered during scanning, (3) perceived changes in clinical reasoning, (4) insights into the role of POCUS in bedside assessment, and (5) the influence of the learning activity on professional development and preparedness for future practice.

Reflections were submitted one week after the session to allow time for reflection and consolidation of learning. Narratives ranged from approximately 500 to 1,000 words. All submissions were anonymised prior to analysis. A full list of reflection prompts is provided in Supplementary Material S2.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis following the six-phase framework [23]. Analysis was reflexive and iterative, with themes developed from the data rather than imposed a priori.

Two researchers read and coded the reflective narratives independently during the initial stages of analysis to enhance depth of engagement with the dataset. Initial coding prioritised participants’ language and experiential meanings, with close attention to both semantic content and underlying interpretive patterns. The researchers then met iteratively to compare coding, discuss emerging interpretations, and refine the developing thematic structure. Where differences in interpretation arose, these were explored through discussion rather than treated as errors requiring mechanical resolution, consistent with the reflexive orientation of the analysis.

Reflexive memo-writing was used throughout the analytic process to document interpretive decisions, emerging ideas, and considerations related to researcher positionality. Sample adequacy was considered using the principle of information power, with thematic sufficiency judged to have been reached when no substantively new interpretive patterns emerged and additional accounts served primarily to deepen or enrich existing themes.

Although experiential and social learning theories informed interpretation of findings, coding and theme development remained reflexive and grounded in participants’ narratives rather than guided by predetermined theoretical categories. Theoretical concepts were therefore used to support interpretive understanding during later stages of analysis rather than to structure initial coding or theme generation.

Analytic procedure

Analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach [23]. First, all reflective narratives were read repeatedly to facilitate familiarisation with the dataset, during which initial impressions, recurring ideas, and salient excerpts were noted.

Second, the data were systematically coded to capture core experiential meanings, including both technical aspects of learning, such as probe handling difficulties and uncertainty in image interpretation, and broader experiential dimensions, such as increasing confidence, perceived clinical relevance, and reflective awareness of limitations.

Third, codes were examined for conceptual relationships and clustered into preliminary themes that represented patterned meanings across participants’ accounts. Fourth, candidate themes were reviewed against the full dataset to ensure internal coherence, conceptual distinctiveness, and alignment with the study aims. Fifth, themes were refined, defined, and named to reflect their conceptual scope and educational significance. Finally, the themes were synthesised into a coherent analytic narrative, supported by representative quotations selected to preserve participants’ voices and illustrate salient patterns of meaning.

Coders’ backgrounds and reflexivity

The analytic team comprised two researchers with complementary areas of expertise. One researcher had formal training in medical education and qualitative research methodology, while the other had extensive experience in clinical education and ultrasound-based skills training. Prior to analysis, both researchers engaged with Braun and Clarke’s framework to establish a shared analytic orientation and support methodological coherence [23].

Reflexivity was actively maintained throughout the study. The researchers acknowledged their dual roles as educators and facilitators within the ultrasound learning programme and recognised that their professional backgrounds and prior assumptions could shape data interpretation. The researchers also recognised that their prior involvement in ultrasound education and generally positive orientation toward technology-enhanced learning could shape interpretation of participant narratives. Reflexive memo-writing and ongoing analytic dialogue were used to critically examine assumptions, document interpretive decisions, and minimise unexamined influence on theme development. Additional strategies included peer debriefing and consultation with an experienced qualitative researcher in medical education.

Data management and coding were supported using ATLAS.ti (version 24; Scientific Software Development GmbH), which facilitated systematic organisation, retrieval, and transparency of analytic procedures.

Trustworthiness and rigor

Multiple strategies were employed to enhance the trustworthiness and methodological rigour of the study, consistent with established qualitative research standards. Credibility was supported through iterative peer debriefing, during which emerging interpretations and thematic structures were critically examined and refined within the research team. These discussions encouraged analytic reflexivity and helped ensure that interpretations remained grounded in the dataset.

To further strengthen interpretive credibility, preliminary thematic interpretations were reviewed and discussed within the broader research team, allowing alternative perspectives to be considered. Dependability and confirmability were supported through maintenance of a detailed audit trail documenting coding decisions, theme development, and reflexive considerations throughout the analytic process. This audit trail enhanced transparency and provided a clear record of how interpretations evolved over time.

Theme 1: clinical value of point-of-care ultrasound

Students consistently emphasised the clinical relevance of POCUS as a bedside diagnostic adjunct within technology-enhanced clinical learning environments, often describing ultrasound as a tool that extended beyond technical imaging toward clinical interpretation and decision-making. Many participants described ultrasound as an extension of the traditional physical examination that allowed them to visualise internal structures and physiological processes in real time. This capacity for immediate visualisation was perceived as supporting more informed and timely clinical decision-making, particularly in acute care settings.

Participants also highlighted the potential value of ultrasound in healthcare settings characterised by variable access to diagnostic resources. Students originating from countries with limited access to advanced imaging modalities reported that delays in obtaining computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging often complicate diagnostic processes in their home healthcare systems. Within this context, POCUS was viewed as a cost-effective and accessible diagnostic tool capable of improving early patient assessment and facilitating diagnostic decision-making. These reflections underscore the perceived relevance of ultrasound-supported learning for international medical students preparing to practise across diverse healthcare environments.

Collectively, these reflections suggest that students viewed ultrasound not only as a diagnostic technology but also as a clinically meaningful extension of bedside assessment that supported contextual interpretation and early diagnostic reasoning.

Theme 2: experiential and hands-on learning

Hands-on scanning practice emerged as a central component of the technology-enhanced learning experience. Participants described substantial gains in confidence related to probe manipulation, image acquisition, and spatial orientation of anatomical structures. The opportunity to directly apply theoretical knowledge during real-time ultrasound scanning enabled students to bridge classroom-based learning with clinical application.

Peer-assisted learning further enriched the experiential learning process. Collaborative scanning sessions encouraged shared problem-solving and collective troubleshooting of image acquisition challenges. Students frequently described a supportive learning environment in which peer feedback facilitated skill development and helped normalise the uncertainties associated with early-stage clinical skill acquisition. Such collaborative learning experiences were perceived as enhancing both technical competence and diagnostic understanding.

These experiences illustrate how hands-on engagement facilitated active meaning-making and supported integration between theoretical and clinical knowledge.

Theme 3: development of diagnostic understanding

Participants reported that structured exposure to ultrasound views and scanning protocols enhanced their understanding of cardiac and abdominal anatomy and pathology. Learning key cardiac windows—including the parasternal long-axis, apical four-chamber, and subcostal views—improved students’ ability to recognise clinically significant findings such as ventricular dysfunction and pericardial effusion.

Similarly, abdominal ultrasound training strengthened students’ understanding of systematic scanning approaches and relevant anatomical landmarks. Many participants described an increased ability to correlate sonographic findings with clinical scenarios, thereby reinforcing diagnostic reasoning and pattern recognition skills. Through these experiences, ultrasound-supported learning was perceived not only as a technical skill but also as a means of integrating anatomical knowledge with developing diagnostic interpretation.

Participants’ reflections suggest that ultrasound-supported learning contributed to developing diagnostic interpretation through visual and contextual integration.

Theme 4: recognition of challenges and limitations

Alongside recognising the educational and clinical benefits of ultrasound-supported learning, participants also acknowledged several challenges associated with the use of POCUS. The operator-dependent nature of ultrasound was frequently mentioned, with students emphasising the need for repeated practice and ongoing skill development in order to achieve reliable image acquisition and interpretation.

Participants also demonstrated awareness of the diagnostic limitations of ultrasound. While POCUS was viewed as a valuable tool for initial assessment and clinical triage, students recognised that comprehensive imaging modalities remain necessary for definitive diagnosis in complex clinical cases. This balanced perspective reflected an emerging understanding of the appropriate role of ultrasound within clinical decision-making and the need to integrate multiple diagnostic approaches when interpreting patient presentations.

These reflections demonstrate an emerging awareness of the role of diagnostic boundaries within responsible clinical reasoning.

Integrative interpretation across themes

Rather than reflecting isolated categories, the themes demonstrated interconnected dimensions of students’ learning experiences. Participants’ reflections suggested that experiential engagement with ultrasound supported not only technical skill acquisition but also broader interpretive processes involving clinical reasoning, contextual judgement, and evolving clinical awareness. Across narratives, students moved between recognising ultrasound as a practical bedside tool, reflecting on its limitations, and considering how it may influence future clinical practice. This thematic interconnectedness illustrates how POCUS-supported learning was experienced as both a technical and meaning-making process within an international medical education context involving students from resource-variable healthcare settings.

Discussion

POCUS as a cognitive and epistemic tool for clinical reasoning

This study demonstrates that technology-enhanced clinical learning activities such as point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) can offer multidimensional educational benefits that extend beyond technical skill acquisition. Within the broader shift in health professions education toward competency-based and practice-relevant learning, POCUS may function not only as a procedural skill but also as a clinically meaningful tool that supports interpretation, diagnostic reasoning, and bedside decision-making. Students’ reflections suggest that engagement with ultrasound enabled a transition from surface-level pattern recognition toward more clinically grounded diagnostic reasoning.

This transition appeared most evident when scanning activities were explicitly connected to diagnostic questions and bedside decision-making rather than taught as isolated psychomotor tasks. Such findings reinforce the importance of contextualised clinical learning in technology-enhanced medical education. They also support conceptualisations of POCUS as a clinician-performed imaging modality whose educational value depends on the integration of image acquisition, interpretation, and clinical application [2, 3]. Teaching these elements as an integrated construct—rather than as discrete or sequential competencies—may therefore be essential for meaningful learning and safe clinical use.

Experiential learning and reflective integration in ultrasound education

From a learning theory perspective, students’ emphasis on hands-on practice and iterative meaning-making aligns closely with experiential learning models, which suggest that deep conceptual understanding emerges through cycles of concrete experience, reflective observation, and active experimentation [12]. Within technology-enhanced clinical education, ultrasound-supported learning provides opportunities for learners to visualise physiological processes and integrate theoretical knowledge with real-time clinical interpretation. These patterns align closely with experiential learning theory, particularly the cyclical relationship between experience, reflection, and application [12].

These findings suggest that technical exposure alone is insufficient to support meaningful learning in ultrasound education. Rather, supervised scanning experiences appear most educationally valuable when paired with opportunities for reflection, guided feedback, and discussion of diagnostic interpretation. Processes such as facilitated debriefing, comparison of image quality, and explicit articulation of clinical reasoning may help learners connect procedural performance with broader cognitive understanding. In this context, hands-on ultrasound practice functions not merely as repetitive skill acquisition but as an experiential learning process that supports integration of technical competence with clinical interpretation and diagnostic reasoning. Existing literature similarly indicates that extended supervised practice and reflective facilitation may strengthen the relationship between technical learning and clinical reasoning development in ultrasound education [24, 25].

Social and situated learning through peer-supported practice

Students’ descriptions of collaborative learning and peer-supported skill development reflect social and situated learning processes, in which competence develops through observation, participation, shared language, and progressive engagement within a community of practice [13, 14]. Within this perspective, learning occurs not only through direct instruction but also through interaction with peers and instructors and through participation in authentic clinical activities. In ultrasound-supported learning environments, observing peers and faculty integrate imaging findings into clinical reasoning may support learners’ development of interpretive judgement, reinforce professional standards, and encourage safe diagnostic practice.

In practical terms, these findings support curriculum designs that incorporate small-group scanning sessions, near-peer teaching, and structured role rotation (e.g., scanner, interpreter, team leader). Such strategies operationalise social learning principles while promoting equitable participation, learner confidence, and collaborative professional identity formation [13, 14].

Learning to recognise diagnostic limitations

A particularly salient finding of this study was students’ recognition of the limitations of ultrasound-supported diagnostics. Rather than reflecting diminished confidence, this awareness may indicate developing recognition of diagnostic boundaries and emerging clinical judgement, including an appreciation of when ultrasound findings are clinically useful, when additional diagnostic modalities may be required, and how diagnostic uncertainty can be managed within appropriate clinical limits.

Participants’ recognition of uncertainty and diagnostic limitation suggests increasing awareness that ultrasound findings must be interpreted within a broader clinical context. Rather than relying solely on imaging results, students appeared to recognise the importance of integrating sonographic findings with clinical assessment and acknowledging when additional diagnostic evaluation may be required. This developing awareness may reflect an emerging capacity to navigate diagnostic uncertainty within supervised educational settings. Such observations are broadly consistent with the concept of uncertainty tolerance described by Stephens and Lazarus, who emphasise that learners develop the ability to engage constructively with incomplete or ambiguous clinical information through guided experience, reflection, and supported decision-making [26]. Within technology-enhanced medical education, structured exposure to diagnostic uncertainty—accompanied by supervision and reflective discussion—may therefore contribute to more balanced clinical reasoning and context-sensitive judgement.

Framing POCUS as a form of diagnostic decision support with bounded reliability—rather than as a definitive diagnostic test—may support the development of appropriate clinical judgement. Such an approach encourages learners to critically appraise sonographic findings within the broader clinical context and to recognise when additional imaging or specialist consultation may be warranted [2, 26]. Early exposure to the strengths and limitations of ultrasound may contribute not only to technical competence but also to more thoughtful, context-sensitive clinical decision-making.

International medical education context among students from resource-variable healthcare settings

The international composition of this cohort provides insight into the educational value of technology-enhanced learning within a transnational medical education programme. Many international medical students anticipate practising in healthcare systems characterised by variable access to diagnostic infrastructure and specialist resources. In such contexts, portable imaging technologies such as POCUS may play an important role in supporting early clinical assessment and triage [17].

Students’ reflections suggest that ultrasound-supported learning may foster adaptable diagnostic strategies relevant to healthcare settings characterised by variable diagnostic resources. These findings highlight the potential role of technology-enhanced medical education in preparing learners for practice in resource-variable environments. Integrating ultrasound-supported learning within international medical curricula may therefore help prepare students to apply diagnostic reasoning across diverse clinical contexts and healthcare systems [18].

Implications for curriculum design and future research

The findings of this study align with international consensus recommendations advocating structured and longitudinal integration of ultrasound education within undergraduate medical curricula [3, 6, 27]. Beyond the development of technical competence, ultrasound-supported learning may contribute to clinical reasoning when embedded within curricula that emphasise reflective learning, supervised practice, and integration of imaging findings into clinical assessment and decision-making.

Curriculum designers may therefore consider several principles. These include longitudinal scaffolding that progresses from foundational ultrasound skills to clinically integrated reasoning, deliberate practice supported by structured feedback, and learning opportunities that encourage interpretation of sonographic findings within authentic clinical contexts. Assessment strategies may also benefit from evaluating how learners integrate imaging information into diagnostic reasoning rather than focusing solely on isolated technical performance [28].

Future research should prioritise longitudinal designs examining how technology-enhanced learning experiences influence skill retention, transfer to clinical practice, and patient care outcomes. Comparative studies across educational contexts may also provide valuable insight into how ultrasound-supported learning can be adapted for diverse healthcare environments.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. By adopting a qualitative approach centred on students’ reflective narratives, it addresses a recognised gap in the ultrasound education literature, which has historically focused on quantitative performance outcomes. The analysis provides insight into how learners experience technology-enhanced clinical education as a process of meaning-making, professional development, and clinical reasoning formation. The international composition of the cohort further enhances the relevance of the findings for global medical education.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size and single-institution design may limit the transferability of the findings to other educational settings. Although participants represented diverse international backgrounds, all students were enrolled within a single post-baccalaureate medical programme and participated in a structured curricular learning activity. Their experiences may therefore differ from those of learners in programmes with alternative curricular structures, institutional resources, or levels of clinical exposure. Although participants represented an international cohort, most students originated from Saint Lucia and Belize, resulting in a geographically concentrated sample. As such, the findings may not be transferable to students from substantially different cultural, linguistic, or healthcare training backgrounds, nor to other forms of international or cross-cultural medical education programmes. Because participants were primarily in pre-clinical or early clinical phases of training, their interpretations of diagnostic reasoning may reflect developing rather than fully established clinical judgement.

In addition, participation in the instructional session formed part of the standard curriculum, whereas inclusion in the research component was voluntary through submission of reflective narratives. Although this approach supported broad representation across the cohort, students who elected to submit reflections may have been more engaged or more willing to articulate their learning experiences, potentially influencing the perspectives represented in the dataset.

Second, the study relied on self-reported reflective narratives as the primary data source. Although written reflection allowed participants time for individual meaning-making and supported broad participation across the cohort, this approach may have limited opportunities for clarification, probing, and deeper exploration of emerging ideas. Interviews or focus groups may have generated richer insight into how learners interpreted uncertainty, integrated ultrasound findings into clinical reasoning, and negotiated evolving understanding during the learning process. Additionally, reflective narratives may be subject to social desirability and recall bias, with participants potentially emphasising positive learning experiences or underreporting challenges due to perceived academic expectations.

Third, reflections were collected shortly after the learning activity and therefore do not capture longer-term outcomes such as sustained skill retention or transfer to clinical practice. Longitudinal studies will be important for examining how technology-enhanced learning experiences influence clinical reasoning development over time.

Finally, as with all qualitative research, interpretation remains inherently contextual and reflexive. Continued methodological transparency and reflexive practice remain essential to strengthening credibility in qualitative health professions education research.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), when implemented as a structured technology-enhanced learning intervention, can support the development of diagnostic reasoning beyond technical skill acquisition. By integrating real-time visualisation with guided clinical scenarios, collaborative learning, and reflective processes, POCUS facilitates learners’ ability to connect theoretical knowledge with clinical interpretation and decision-making. The findings suggest that the educational value of POCUS lies not only in procedural competence but also in its role as a cognitive tool that supports clinical reasoning and contextualised understanding, particularly within international medical education contexts involving learners from resource-variable healthcare settings. These results highlight the importance of aligning domain-specific technologies with pedagogically grounded instructional design to maximise learning effectiveness in emergency medicine education. From an educational and clinical perspective, incorporating POCUS into medical curricula may enhance learners’ preparedness for real-world clinical practice by promoting integrative and reflective reasoning processes. Future research should investigate the longitudinal impact of such technology-enhanced learning approaches on diagnostic performance, knowledge retention, and clinical application across different training settings.
