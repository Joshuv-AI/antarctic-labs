Equity challenges in public health responses during the COVID-19 Pandemic: mixed-methods study of Travellers’ experiences in Nouvelle-Aquitaine, France
Authors: Aude Mondeilh, Ana Rivadeneyra-Sicilia, Léa Dallemane, Leila Saboni, Marc Ruello, Stéphanie Vandentorren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, socially disadvantaged populations in France faced higher risks of infection and severe forms of COVID-19, particularly among marginalized groups such as Travellers, a population predominantly composed of nationals residing in either static or mobile caravans. In France, few studies have investigated Travellers’ experiences and preventive behaviours, with existing research limited to a small number of epidemiological outcomes prior to the pandemic. This study addresses this gap by exploring how living conditions and social exclusion shaped Travellers’ experiences of the pandemic and their responses to government measures.MethodsWe conducted a sequential explanatory mixed-methods study, combining questionnaires and semi-structured interviews among Travellers in the region of Nouvelle-Aquitaine, France. Quantitative data assessed health status, prevention practices including COVID-19 vaccination and living conditions. Qualitative data provided deeper insight into Travellers’ experiences, perceptions and attitudes towards the government’ handling of the health crisis. Questionnaire data were analysed using descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regression to identify factors associated with COVID-19 vaccination (≥ 1 dose). Interview data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis framework.ResultsAmong 406 participants, 52.6% reported probable COVID-19 infection (based on a positive test or clinical symptoms) since the onset of the pandemic, of whom 9.2% reported to have been hospitalized. 30.0% of participants reported COVID-19 vaccination compared with 85% in the French general population. Vaccination was associated with being a woman, older age, living in stable housing, prior measles-mumps-rubella vaccination, receiving clear information from health authorities, and trust in the French government’s pandemic response. Interviewees reported that overcrowded, underserved living conditions hindered adherence to preventive measures, while fear of infection disrupted social interactions, increased feelings of confinement, and heightened psychological distress.ConclusionsThe COVID-19 pandemic disproportionately impacted Traveller communities in France, with structural vulnerabilities increasing both exposure and severe outcomes. These findings highlight the need for inclusive public health responses to avoid increasing preexisting health inequalities in future pandemic crisis. Effective strategies should integrate community perspectives, engage healthcare and social professionals, and tailor vaccination approaches to the specific vulnerabilities and socio-historical contexts shaping behaviours in marginalised populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-026-02918-8.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated pre-existing health inequalities and caused a disproportionate number of adverse consequences on socially-disadvantaged groups [1–3]. Numerous epidemiological studies have shown that during the crisis, these groups had a higher risk of infection and of developing severe forms leading to hospitalisation and death [4–8]. The underlying mechanisms behind this higher risk were complex, multi-dimensional, and frequently intertwined with health and socioeconomic inequalities. More specifically, a higher prevalence of chronic diseases and other comorbidities combined with poor social and material living conditions resulted in higher levels of risk exposure to COVID-19 [9–12]. Additionally, these conditions hampered socially-disadvantaged groups’ ability to implement prevention behaviours (such as handwashing, physical distancing, use of masks, etc.) and to access health care services for screening, treatment and vaccination [2].

Traveller communities have been identified as a socially disadvantaged minority groups at increased risk of infection in France [13]. These communities are largely composed of nationals predominantly living in static or mobile caravans and sharing a tradition of regular or occasional nomadism, even though they are increasingly transitioning to more sedentary lives [14]. As elsewhere in Europe, French Travellers have long been identified as one of the most discriminated minority groups, with a long history of institutional prosecution and repression that have undermined their trust in the State and its institutions [13, 15]. Due to this social exclusion, those living in the most disadvantaged environments face intersecting vulnerabilities including poverty, barriers to accessing and sustaining employment and education, substandard and overcrowded housing conditions as well as deprived living conditions in underserved areas which constrain their access to social services and healthcare use (including vaccination), and limited access to essential services such as water and sanitation [16–20]. Moreover, Travellers have poorer health status than the general population [21–23], including a markedly lower life expectancy of approximately 10 years in France [24]. They also present a higher prevalence of adverse health conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity and respiratory disorders, all of which have been identified as comorbidities associated with COVID-19 [25].

These overlapping and interacting disadvantages are known to increase the risk of COVID-19, and severe forms of the disease. Therefore, we might expect that they have also heightened Travellers’ vulnerability and exposure to COVID-19 infection [26–31]. However, empirical evidence on the experiences and risk exposure of Travellers in France during the COVID-19 crisis remains limited, thereby challenging equity in the design and implementation of public health responses.

In France, the Traveller population was estimated at 250 000-350 000 persons in 2012 [32] and to our knowledge, only two epidemiological studies have investigated the impact of the pandemic on Travellers in France. The first was conducted in three socially deprived neighbourhoods in the city of Perpignan, where a large number of Travellers live in settled accommodation. It revealed a significantly higher seroprevalence of antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 (the virus causing COVID-19) during the first months of the pandemic compared to the general population [33]. This study was complemented by a qualitative inquiry involving health and social care professionals as well as Travellers, which highlighted the adverse impacts of the pandemic in terms of increased barriers to accessing basic services, loss of income, and heightened psychological distress among Traveller families [34]. The second epidemiological study, which targeted marginalised urban populations in the Ile-de-France region (Greater Paris area) and in the city of Marseille, included a small sample of Travellers. It found that, despite their overexposure to COVID-19 infection, vaccination coverage among these groups in December 2021 was low compared to the national average [35]. While these studies, provide some evidence on Travellers’ exposure and effect of the pandemic, they offer limited insight into how structural factors, such as poor living conditions and social exclusion, shaped Travellers’ prevention attitudes and behaviours during the pandemic.

The present study addresses this gap by examining the interplay between poor living conditions, social exclusion and preventive behaviours, including COVID-19 vaccination, among Travellers in France. By doing so, it offers a more comprehensive understanding of these communities’ realities and coping strategies to inform more equitable and effective public health responses in future crises.

Methods

Study design and population

The mixed-methods study reported here is part of a larger multi-dimensional epidemiological study launched in October 2019 to analyse the consequences of living conditions on health, healthcare access and prevention - including vaccination in general - among Travellers living in the Nouvelle Aquitaine region in France [25]. A participatory approach was used, the study being designed and implemented through collaboration between researchers, decision makers, grassroots associations through the French National Federation of Solidarity Associations for Action with Gypsies and Travellers network (FNASAT-Gv), and Travellers themselves [36].

That study was interrupted in March 2020 because of the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. It was relaunched in October 2021, using a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design as an opportunity to document Travellers’ experiences of the pandemic. In the first phase, from October 2021 to march 2022, structured questionnaires were administered to measure participants’ self-reported COVID-19 infection rate and health-related consequences, prevention practices (self-testing, isolation and vaccination), attitudes towards government measures, and living conditions. In the second phase, from June to September 2022, semi-structured interviews were conducted with a distinct sample of participants to complement and further interpret the quantitative data, and to provide a deeper insight on Travellers’ realities and coping strategies during the pandemic. More specifically, the goals of the study, were to: (i) identify participants’ main daily challenges and coping strategies during of the pandemic; (ii) characterize their views and attitudes towards government measures to combat the spread of COVID-19; and (iii) understand factors influencing their engagement and compliance with preventive behaviours, with a particular focus on COVID-19 vaccination uptake.

Eligibility criteria were as follows: being an adult (18 years and older), French language proficiency, currently residing or previously residing in a caravan or other mobile structure, and living in a housing location known by one of the ten partner associations affiliated to FNASAT-Gv and engaged in the territories covered by the study.

Data collection and analysis

Quantitative component using face-to face questionnaires

A three-stage random sample design was implemented. The sampling frame was based on an estimated census of all Travellers’ housing locations conducted by the ten partner associations engaged in the territories covered by the study. The census also provided information on the number of households and the type of housing at each location. In the first stage, housing locations were drawn at random from all the housing locations in our sampling frame (n = 993). Each location was classified by partner associations into one of four housing categories: (1) precarious & unauthorized housing (mobile homes installed on land or halting sites without a temporary occupation agreement and subject to eviction orders); (2) precarious housing (mobile homes or mixed housing on public or private land with an occupation agreement); (3) inadequate housing (legally occupied mobile home or mixed housing that is overcrowded or does not meet housing standards); and (4) adequate housing (legal and safe housing, without overcrowding that meets established standards, whether it be mobile or fixed) (Supplementary Table 1). This classification was adapted from the European Typology on Homelessness and housing exclusion (ETHOS) grid by FNASAT-Gv for the specific context of Travellers’ housing. In the second stage, households were drawn at random from the total households present in the housing locations. Finally, in the third stage, one adult was randomly drawn from all adults present in each randomly selected household. The second and third stages were carried out by partner associations on the day the questionnaire was administered. The full methodology of the study has been described in detail elsewhere [25]. Pseudonymised standardized face-to-face questionnaires were administered by trained social workers from partner associations.

Categorical variables were presented as percentages and 95% confidence intervals (CI). We assessed the prevalence of COVID-19 vaccination coverage (i.e., at least one dose) according to demographic characteristics, health characteristics and living conditions identified in the literature as being associated with vaccine uptake. Comparisons of percentages were tested using Pearson’s Chi-squared test. Given the small numbers of persons in inadequate housing, we combined this category with adequate housing, as these two categories were closest in terms of two main characteristics: legal occupancy and secure tenure. Hereafter, this combined category is referred to as stable housing.

To assess factors associated with COVID-19 vaccination, we first conducted univariate logistic regressions analyses and then a multivariable logistic regression. Only variables with statistically significant p-value in the univariate analysis (p ≤ 0.05) were included in the multivariate model: age (as continuous variable), sex, education level (elementary/lower secondary school/upper secondary school or third level), type of housing (Stable/Precarious/Precarious & unauthorized), self-reported measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccination (yes/no), the perception of having received clear information on COVID-19 from health authorities (not at all or not really/ somewhat or absolutely), trust in the government’s and other public authorities’ actions to resolve the health crisis (not at all or not really/ somewhat or absolutely). Sex was not significant in univariate analysis but was nonetheless included in the multivariate model due to its theoretical relevance. Our analyses were conducted on complete-case data. All estimates were weighted by the inverse probability of inclusion at the individuals, households and housing locations levels to account selection probability according to the sampling design. Analyses were post-stratified by sex (using the 2021 French general population census as a reference [37], to restore balance between the numbers of women and men in the sample). All analyses were conducted using R software (R Core Team, 2022) and the survey package, designed for the analyse of weighted data. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Qualitative component using semi-structured interviews

Participants were recruited using purposeful sampling, targeting adults living in hazardous and insecure housing corresponding to the two most disadvantaged categories identified in the quantitative component of the study, Precarious and Precarious & unauthorised housing. This sampling design aimed to ensure greater insight into the impact of the crisis on the most vulnerable groups within the Traveller community. Initial contact with participants was facilitated by trained social workers from two of the ten partner associations (see above). A snowball sampling method was subsequently used to expand the sample and enhance diversity in age, gender, and living conditions. Semi-structured interviews were conducted face-to-face at participants’ housing locations, with some involving more than one participant (see below). Interviews were audio-recorded with verbal consent of participants and supplemented by observational notes. For participants who declined audio-recording, detailed notes were taken. All recordings were transcribed and pseudonyms were assigned to ensure anonymity. The length of the interviews ranged from 25 to 75 min. Interview data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis, encompassing familiarization with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes and sub-themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report [38]. More precisely, two researchers independently read all transcripts and interview notes to become familiar with the content, and to outline the overarching themes. Initial coding of three interviews was performed and discussed to develop a consistent coding framework. All discrepancies were resolved through open discussion until consensus was reached. The full dataset was manually coded and reviewed. Subsequently, interview data were charted in a matrix linking codes and thematic categories, aligned with illustrative quotes. The thematic structure of the report was progressively refined to ensure coherence with the research questions and the study goals. We used the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist for analysing and reporting collected data.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Among the 1030 adults included in the epidemiological study, 418 were finally surveyed across 188 housing locations, in the latter stages of the COVID-19 health crisis (between October 2021 and March 2022). For the present study, quantitative analyses focused on the subsample of participants with valid responses to the question “Are you vaccinated against COVID-19?” (n = 406) (Fig. 1).

With regards to the qualitative study, 23 interviews involving 33 adults (including some with 2 or 3 participants) were conducted between June and September 2022. The sample comprised 21 women and 12 men, aged 18 to 63 years (Table 2). With few exceptions, most men reported being self-employed in activities such as green space maintenance, roofing, and casual jobs. All women were housewives except one employee. In terms of social benefits, 8 men and 19 women received the French minimum income benefit. Study findings

The results reported here integrate both the quantitative and qualitative data analysed. Three main themes emerged: the first two address participants’ experiences of the pandemic and perceptions of government’s handling of the health crisis. These two themes contextualise the third, which examines COVID-19 vaccine uptake and underlying motivations.

Travellers’ experiences of the pandemic and day-to-day prevention measures.

Travellers’ experiences of the pandemic and day-to-day prevention measures.

Travellers’ experiences of the pandemic and day-to-day prevention measures.

Half (52.6%) the survey participants reported they had probably been infected with COVID-19 (i.e., positive test or clinical symptoms) since the beginning of the pandemic; of these, 9.2% reported they had been hospitalized (Supplementary Table 2).

With regard to COVID-19 testing, 86.4% said they had been tested at least once since the beginning of the pandemic. The main cited reasons for testing were: being a contact case (48.5%), having symptoms of the disease (39.9%), and obtaining a health pass (19.8%).

In terms of prevention measures, 65.8% of those who declared probable infection, said they had isolated themselves. This proportion was highest among those residing in stable housing (81.6%), compared to those in precarious (61.8%) and precarious & unauthorised housing (61.4%) (p = 0.3).

Interview participants highlighted difficulties in maintaining physical distancing in overcrowded council-run campsites. Specifically, they complained of tightly packed caravans and shared sanitary and reception areas, which increased the risk of transmission: “We live together. We live in caravans. So, we get a bit stuck. And on top of that, we infect each other”. Interviewees also reported a disruption in social life due to fear of contagion., with many families self-isolating by creating physical separations between caravans, sometimes by cordoning off their area. These measures acted as a sort of symbolic barricade to keep away anyone who was not a close family member: “There were a lot of us like that; all together as a family, but at the same time, we all had ropes around [the caravan] and nobody crossed them, even though we’re a family”.

Day-to-day social interactions were also disrupted. Interviewees described how people stopped having morning coffee with neighbours, eating together, paying unplanned visits to each other’s caravans, and helping others out: “It was a question of cutting ties”; “We didn’t have coffee together anymore”; “Before, we were a bit more ‘everyone together’, but since COVID, it’s true that we’ve all become a bit… “. Despite these difficulties, several interviewees emphasized the special attention given to elderly members of the community, perceived to be particularly vulnerable to severe forms of COVID-19 and death: “We used to yell at the elderly. My mother went outside, and I told off her for that, because if she died, we couldn’t even have a wake; we couldn’t do anything”.

With regard to individual prevention behaviours, 38.4% of survey respondents reported they had encountered difficulties obtaining masks at the beginning of the pandemic, whereas 39.1% declared they had received masks free of charge. Qualitative data support this finding: all interviewees reported widespread mask shortages, notably at the start of the pandemic, leading to coping strategies such as reusing masks, making fabric ones, or relying on social networks to obtain them.

With regards to cleaning and disinfecting practices at home, less than half (43.7%) of the survey participants declared they cleaned their home more frequently since the onset of the pandemic; while a quarter (22.8%) reported using bleach more frequently. These findings are corroborated by qualitative data, which reveal that some women maintained their usual cleaning routines, whereas others increased the frequency or intensity of such practices (e.g., using bleach more frequently). In addition, families living in informal camps highlighted increased challenges in cleaning and disinfection practices due to limited access to water and sanitation.

Experiences and attitudes toward the government’s measures to reduce COVID-19 transmission.

Survey participants declared that the most disruptive lockdown measures were social isolation (83%), travel bans (83%), and reduced public services (69%); 64%, 39% and 35% of the study sample, respectively, considered these measures to be useful (Fig. 2). The most useful measures were school closures (68%), bans on large gatherings (67%), social isolation (64%), and isolation of sick relatives in hospital (59%). School closures were considered the measure which had the least personal impact on participants during the pandemic (46%).

Interview data provide further insight into how these restrictions were experienced. Interviewees noted that their crowded living conditions made physical distancing impossible, but no one openly questioned these measures. Several interviewees adhered to mobility restrictions, a measure judged to be legitimate and useful despite the disruption caused to their day-to-day lives: “It was actually much better, because sometimes, if we hadn’t done that, there would have been even more deaths. So, for me, yes, absolutely, it was appropriate and it was even something good all that [i.e., the restrictions] “. Some of them criticized people who did not respect safety procedures during the most critical periods of the pandemic and even regretted the interruption of mobility restrictions in the summer of 2020: “Well, I don’t think they should have stopped [the measures] from one day to the next just like that, that’s for sure”.

With regard to attitudes toward government’s containment measures, a large majority of survey respondents (79.4%) expressed distrust in the government’s handling of the pandemic. Quantitative confidence scores corroborate this, with only 12.7% of participants giving a score between 7 and 10 (out of 10) for these measures, 26.8% between 4 and 6, and 60.4% between 0 and 3. Furthermore, 44.1% declared that the information provided by public authorities was not clear enough (Table 1). Their main sources for advice or help were as follows: a family member or a close friend (45.0%), a member of an association (i.e., Traveller specific or other) (19.0%), a doctor or other health professional (18.0%), and a clergyperson (4.3%). Interview participants also cited the media, especially television, as the main source of information during the most critical stages of the pandemic, alongside informal exchanges with social workers, doctors, nurses and police officers who visited their living locations.

Moreover, most interviewees expressed fatigue with the constant reminders and repeated messaging of prevention measures. Lockdown restrictions, were often perceived as unclear, complex, inconsistent and characterised as “useless”, “lacking common sense” or “hard to comply with”. More specifically, several interviewees mentioned the fact that large gatherings were banned while access to shops and restaurants was allowed under certain conditions. These contradictions increased confusion, prompting some people to say they “no longer understood anything”. Participants also complained about the bans on family gatherings at religious ceremonies, weddings, funerals, and other extended family events. Finally, some interviewees noted occasional police checks at the campsite entrances when a positive case was reported. These actions exacerbated families’ anxiety, as they feared increased risk of infection and led some to want to leave the site: “It was like a prison in here”. They also felt local media reports of positive cases combined with these police checks, reinforced mistrust towards Traveller communities and blamed them for spreading the virus. Some said that these events might have also brought back memories of racism and prosecution.

3.COVID-19 vaccination practices and associated factors.

3.COVID-19 vaccination practices and associated factors.

COVID-19 vaccination practices and associated factors.

Overall, 30.0% of survey participants were vaccinated against COVID-19 (at least one dose), including 27.9% for men and 32.0% of women (Table 1). Of those, 98.3% stated they had no difficulty making an appointment to get vaccinated. The vaccination sites most often reported included specific COVID-19 centres set up during the health crisis (51.7%), general practitioner offices (22.8%), pharmacies (20.4%), and hospitals (12.0%).

Vaccination coverage increased with age, rising from 22.5% among persons 18–24 years of age to 45.0% among those over 65 (Table 1). Vaccination coverage also differed according to social conditions, rising from 25.0% among those living in precarious & unauthorized housing to 45.2% among those in stable housing (p = 0.02). The following participants also had higher COVID-19 vaccination coverage: those with MMR vaccination (35.8% vs. 14.2%, p = 0.01), participants who perceived they received clear information about the disease from health authorities (35.4% vs. 23.1%, p = 0.02), and individuals who replied they ‘somewhat’ or ‘absolutely’ trusted the government in its response to the health crisis (50.9% vs. 24.2%, p < 0.001).

Multivariate analyses (Table 3) of the quantitative data highlighted that the following factors were associated with COVID-19 vaccination coverage: being a woman (ORa: 2.08; 95% CI: 1.00–4.31), older age (ORa: 1.35 for an increase of 10 years; 95% CI: 1.06–1.72), living in stable housing (ORa: 2.63; 95% CI: 1.0–6.93), self-reported MMR vaccination (ORa: 4.55; 95% CI: 1.25–16.5), receiving clear information on COVID-19 from health authorities (ORa: 2.55; 95%CI: 1.33–4.90), and trust in the government’s actions (ORa: 4.15; 95% CI: 1.76–9.75).

With regard to attitudes towards COVID-19 vaccine uptake, one third of the respondents (32.3%) perceived that vaccination was useful (Supplementary Table 2). Among those already vaccinated, the most frequently cited reasons for doing so were: to protect themselves (53.2%), to protect their loved ones (42.7%), to obtain a health pass (22.7%), and mandatory circumstances (e.g., for work, etc.) (13.7%).

Findings from interviews data provide important insights into the impact of the COVID-19 health pass, a measure introduced in July 2021 to encourage people to get vaccinated. One of the reasons cited for getting the pass was to be able to enter public hospitals, especially in the event of an emergency hospitalization for a child. Mothers felt that without a pass, the time required for testing and getting results before being allowed to visit their child would be unbearable. Some interviewees noted that the health pass was useful for mobility, as it allowed them to visit family living elsewhere.

Interestingly, no participant mentioned being unable to go to restaurants, cinemas or other public spaces as a problem, likely because such leisure and social activities are less common among Travelers than that in the general population. According to one interviewee: “It must have been pretty hard for people who were used to going out (…) but us, we’re not used to doing all that. We go shopping, eat, come home and do the housework, cook, wash the kids, put them to bed in front of the TV. That’s what we did, and that’s what we still do”.

The desire to avoid infection was frequently reported by interviewees with a chronic disease and those who had lost someone close to them due to COVID-19: “personally, I have… someone I know very well who died of COVID. And that was a trigger. It made me… I got vaccinated… Yeah, he had [the] Delta [variant]. He just had a little problem with diabetes. It was quick. Last December, on the 15th, he invited me to his son’s birthday. We ate together and all that…He died the 25th. Ten days”.

Among unvaccinated survey participants, 71.6% declared they had no intention of being vaccinated against COVID-19. The reasons most frequently mentioned for vaccine refusal were: perceiving it as dangerous (46.8%), being afraid of its immediate side effects (28.1%), waiting for more information about it (25.2%), knowing someone who had experienced adverse side effects (20.0%), loved ones being against vaccination (13%), and considering that they themselves were not at risk of infection or that no one around them was at risk (9.7%).

Interview data shed further light on factors underlying vaccine refusal. Some participants made a clear distinction between “old vaccines” developed and approved over a long period, and COVID-19 vaccines, which were developed, tested and rolled-out in an unprecedentedly short timeframe. Others reported concerns about all kinds of dangerous side effects, based on personal experiences or on information from community members, internet, or social media. Mistrust also arose from the generalized expectation that COVID-19 vaccines would provide absolute protection, whereas each new infection further fuelled doubts about their effectiveness. For example, one participant complained that protection against infection “only lasted a month” while others argued that even vaccinated people could contract the disease and infect others: “You’re less at risk [if you get vaccinated], but you still give it to others if you have it, so there’s no point in getting it”; “All my sisters-in-law have had the vaccine, and they all got Covid”. Two interviewees reported that conspiracy beliefs exacerbated their suspicion and made them more hesitant about vaccination. Mainly spread through social media, these claims suggested harmful or hidden interests including that the vaccines had been made for profit, contained a microchip to track people, or had components that could cause death or sterility. One of the interviewees mentioned conspiracy theories, expressed in apocalyptic terms, linking the vaccine to “the mark of the beast” and the Antichrist.

Discussion

Our study found a higher prevalence of probable COVID-19 infection among survey respondents compared to the general population: just over half of the former reported probable infection, compared to one-third of the latter over the same period (52.6% vs. 31.9%) [39]. The risk of hospitalisation (i.e., severe forms of COVID-19) was also higher: 9.2% of our respondents versus only 2.9% of the general population during the first wave of the pandemic [40]. These findings align with the few epidemiological data previously available on Traveller communities’ experience of the COVID-19 health crisis in France [33, 35], and with existing evidence for other minority groups in Europe [41, 42]. These higher infection and hospitalisation prevalences may be the result of cumulated vulnerabilities such as pre-existing comorbidities and deprived social and material conditions, specifically precarious and overcrowded campsites, limited access to water and sanitation, and difficulties respecting physical distancing.

These conditions also constrained adherence to recommended protective measures; for example, effective self-isolation after a positive test was almost impossible among families living in the most precarious dwellings. More generally, the spatial layout of caravans and shared living areas hindered their ability to isolate and to respect physical distancing. Consistent with other studies [31], these results show that difficult living conditions, worsened by poverty and competing essential needs, increased Travellers’ exposure to COVID-19 and their risk of infection during the most acute periods of the pandemic.

Our results also highlight that social isolation and bans on family and religious gatherings were experienced as the most disruptive contention measures during the pandemic. Despite the communal values and strong solidarity that bind Travellers’ communities, we can expect that more socially isolated families and individuals were disproportionally affected by feelings of isolation and despair. Moreover, our findings reveal that police checks at campsite entrances combined with the media portrait of Travellers as a source of contagion, awakened collective feelings of prosecution and blame, and probably increased psychological burden among some persons. Unlike studies of other minority groups, which did not report high rates of mental health problems during the pandemic [43], our findings highlight the need of targeted interventions to reduce isolation and psychological distress among Travellers in the most precarious conditions during future pandemics. Previous studies have associated intentional non-adherence to distancing measures with both, underlying mistrust towards public authorities and a lack of knowledge of prevention measures and rules [44, 45]. Our results suggest that Travellers’ mistrust was largely driven by perceptions of health policy clarity and effectiveness, with government‘s measures often seen as unclear, inconsistent and ineffective. These results highlight the importance of clear, consistent, and well-communicated public health measures, suggesting that improving transparency and clarity could strengthen Travellers’ adherence and public trust in future health crises. In addition, conventional communication channels may be ineffective in the context of the social and material deprivation experienced by some Traveller communities. As pointed out by Stenroos et al. [46], information campaigns must be accessible and culturally tailored to effectively reach this and other marginalised minority groups.

COVID-19 vaccination coverage in our study sample was low (30%), and significantly lower than that in the French general population (85%) [47] despite the vaccine being freely available to all persons aged 18 years and over on 12 May 2021. This difference in vaccination coverage was also observed in two other studies focusing on the MMR vaccine. The first found that only 45.3% of Traveller children had completed the full MMR vaccination schedule by 24 months, compared to 89% in the general population [25]. The second found that 74% of adults had had the MMR vaccination compared to 84.0% in the general population [48]. It is important to point out that vaccination patterns may vary depending on specific vaccines, and between adult and child vaccines, irrespective of the social status of the population concerned. Given the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established, however, factors associated with COVID-19 vaccination were identified. In France, the vaccination campaign initially prioritized older adults before extending to younger groups. As a result, we might expect age to be a factor strongly associated with vaccination uptake. However, our study shows relatively low COVID-19 vaccination uptake among Travellers when compared to the general population for all age groups, including individuals over 65 years (45.5% vs. 94.0%) [47] and despite the former’s greater risk of comorbidities. Interestingly, Traveller women were more likely to be vaccinated than men, contrasting with other studies in the general population at the beginning of the pandemic [49, 50]. Women are often seen as the main source of knowledge and experience regarding health matters, including vaccination [51]. This perception may have influenced greater vaccination uptake in women. Our results also indicate a social gradient in vaccine coverage, consistent with other studies among the general population in France and elsewhere [35, 52, 53]. As expected, this social gradient mainly concerned housing conditions: the more precarious the living environment, the lower the vaccination coverage. In terms of individual decision-making, numerous studies have shown that a positive attitude toward vaccination in general was associated with acceptance of the COVID-19 vaccine [49]. In particular, Haidar et al. showed that general vaccination uptake and acceptability in adult Travellers - such as prior MMR vaccination and getting recommended vaccines - were significantly correlated with favourable attitudes toward COVID-19 prevention measures [48]. Consistent with these findings, we found that a history of MMR vaccination was positively associated with COVID-19 vaccination.

Perceived benefit of individual and collective protection was also a key driver, while mobility restrictions played a more limited role, echoing research among Traveller communities in the United Kingdom [29]. In contrast to the general population [54, 55], the health pass policy was less effective among Travellers. As highlighted in our interviews, many Travellers did not perceive the pass was as essential, as their communities were already relatively self-contained within formal or informal settlements and made limited use of public venues such as restaurants, cinemas, and other facilities where the pass was required.

As for the French general population [47] and other disadvantaged groups [35], two of the main reasons for vaccine hesitancy were individuals’ doubts about its efficacy and safety, and the fear of side effects. Mistrust in the government was also strongly associated with COVID-19 vaccination, reflecting research among the general population in France [53] and other marginalised communities elsewhere [46, 56, 57]. Such mistrust may be considered within a broader context of longstanding discrimination, institutional harassment and diverse forms of surveillance and sanitaire control suffered by Travellers in France, portrayed as vectors of epidemic spread on the solely basis of historic prejudice and their nomadic status [15]. This legacy continues to shape their relationships with public institutions and, while our findings do not fully capture this dimension, it may have influenced perceptions of vaccination campaigns. This effect may have been exacerbated in the context of the COVID-19 health crisis, during which vaccination campaigns were essentially driven by government authorities.

Our quantitative data reveal that coverage among Travellers was lower than in the general population, with a clear social gradient according to type of settlement. Taken together, these findings suggest that structural conditions, together with other attitudinal, informational or contextual influences related to the government’s handling of the pandemic, may have contributed to observed vaccination inequities compared with the general population.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, data were collected between October 2021 and September 2022, a period which saw several changes in prevention attitudes and vaccination uptake reflecting the changes in the dynamic of the pandemic and government lockdown measures. Consequently, one might expect that participants’ attitudes and vaccination practices evolved over this time, perceptions towards the end of this period more likely being influenced by a context of ever-increasing fatigue regarding the health crisis and the long-lasting disruptions prevention measures had on day-to-day activities.

Second, administration of questionnaires by social workers familiar with participants may have introduced social-desirability bias in certain responses, resulting in either an overestimation or underestimation of the results. For instance, although COVID-19 vaccination was lower than that observed in the general population, it may have been overestimated due to desirability bias. Nevertheless, this potential bias has been mitigated through triangulation with our qualitative findings and the available literature. Furthermore, the involvement of local associations in data collection, along with the study’s participatory approach likely contributed to its success [36]. In particular, longstanding trust between these associations and the Travellers’ communities certainly accounts for the study’s high participation rate, despite a broader context of mistrust towards governmental and other public authorities’ measures to combat the COVID-19 crisis.

Third, the data collected were self-reported and retrospective in nature, possibly leading to reporting and recall bias. Our results should therefore be interpreted taking in light of these limitations. For instance, the prevalence of probable COVID-19 infection may have been either overestimated or underestimated. However, the criteria used to define probable infection—clinical symptoms or a positive test—likely limited potential information bias. Moreover, the same criteria were used to estimate prevalence in the general population, supporting the validity of comparisons. Notwithstanding, the large number of study participants and the fact that most of them were living in social and material deprivation, provided us with a very detailed insight into this understudied population in France.

Fourth, our interviewees were recruited using purposive and snowball sampling. While these approaches are well suited to accessing hard-to-reach populations, they may have overrepresented individuals more stable and connected, with greater access to resources or support, at the expense of the most isolated or transient individuals. To minimise this bias, recruitment proceeded through multiple community and social service networks, following a range of perspectives across age, gender, settlement type, geographic location and income. Furthermore, it was continuously adjusted until a sample of diverse backgrounds and profiles was achieved.

Fifth, the fact that a few interviews were conducted collectively may have influenced individual responses, as some persons could have been reluctant to share sensitive information or influenced by others’ views. However, this approach was necessary for including those who would not have agreed to an individual interview, capturing otherwise excluded perspectives.

Conclusions

Our study highlights the disproportionate impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on Traveller communities in France, who were more exposed to and less protected from infection than the general population. They experienced a higher prevalence of probable COVID-19 infection and related hospitalizations, as well as lower COVID-19 vaccination coverage. Suboptimal housing conditions, particularly overcrowding, limited the feasibility of key prevention strategies, including effective self-isolation. In addition, government responses were widely perceived as unclear, inconsistent and often ineffective, particularly in the context of Travellers deprived social and material conditions and generalised mistrust of the government’s ability to handle the crisis. Together, these structural and contextual factors help explain lower vaccination uptake in this population.

These findings reveal the unequal impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on marginalised communities and underscore the need for inclusive public health responses to avoid increasing preexisting health inequalities. This leads us to conclude that, in the context of major health crisis, public authorities must ensure clear, consistent and culturally tailored information and recognize the role of community cohesion, solidarity and shared responsibility within these groups. In this regard, preventive approaches mobilising formal and informal networks and including healthcare professionals, social workers and community associations, will be essential for achieving effective and sustainable outcomes. Finally, while the COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated the effectiveness of mass vaccination in reducing transmission in the general population, vaccination strategies targeting marginalised groups must take into account the living conditions and socio-historical context shaping vaccination decision-making at both individual and community levels. For instance, health mediation, an intervention based in trust with communities, could be widely implemented by combining different practices to adapt to diverse populations and evolving social contexts.

More generally, preventive interventions targeting socially excluded populations should not only support individuals’ capacity to make health-promoting decisions, but also address the structural determinants of health by developing measures that ensure sustainable access to quality housing, employment, healthy food and education, as well as by combating discrimination and social exclusion.

Indeed, our findings are consistent with the WHO model on the social determinants of health, which highlights how personal, social, economic, and environmental factors influence health at both the individual and community level. Therefore, in the event of future pandemics, particular emphasis should be placed on the structural determinants of health, including political, cultural, economic, and social contexts shaping Travellers’ disadvantaged social position and helping to explain their unequal experiences and the equity challenges arising from government responses to the crisis.
