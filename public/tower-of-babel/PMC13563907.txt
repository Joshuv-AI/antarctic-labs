Decentralized point-of-care HBV viral load testing for HBs Ag positive pregnant women in Mali, a prospective study
Authors: Djeneba B. Fofana, Mountaga Diallo, Hamadi Sissoko, Tenin A. Coulibaly, Mohamed S. Sissoko, Amadou Fomba, Soumaila Diallo, Alassane Traore, Bassirou Diarra, Almoustapha I. Maiga, Yacouba Cissoko, Seydou Doumbia, Mahamadou Diakité, Christophe Martinaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major public health challenge, particularly in sub‑Saharan Africa, where mother‑to‑child transmission (MTCT) is a leading cause of chronic infection. Perinatal HBV acquisition carries a high risk of chronic disease, yet prevention efforts are limited by restricted access to HBV DNA testing. Simplified point‑of‑care viral load assays, such as Xpert® HBV Viral Load, may strengthen MTCT prevention in resource‑limited settings.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between June 2024 and May 2025 among pregnant woman attending antenatal care at different levels of the health system pyramid in Mali. The study sites included Referral Health Centers in Commune III of Bamako, Bla, and Sikasso, as well as Community Health Centers in Djibouria (Kéniéba district) and Badialan (Commune III, Bamako). Pregnant women testing positive for HBs Ag by a rapid test were enrolled. Venous blood (4 mL) was collected for HBV viral load quantification using the Xpert® assay. Samples from Bla and Sikasso were analyzed in decentralized laboratories, while others were tested at the central UCRC laboratory.ResultsAmong 2,952 pregnant women screened, 205 were HBsAg positive (prevalence of 6.9%). After excluding hemolyzed samples, 191 women underwent HBV viral load testing. Overall, 64.0% had detectable HBV DNA; among these, 69.9% had viral loads < 2,000 IU/mL, while 5.7% had levels > 200,000 IU/mL. Viral load was not associated with maternal age or gestational age but was significantly associated with antiviral treatment status (p < 0.001). Same-day results were more frequent in decentralized laboratories with on-site GeneXpert testing than in the central laboratory (23.5% vs. 4.4%). However, in both settings, most results were returned after more than four days, with a significant difference in turnaround time distribution (p < 0.001).ConclusionHBV infection remains highly endemic among pregnant women in Mali, with a meaningful proportion presenting with high viral loads associated with increased risk of mother-to-child transmission. Decentralized Xpert® HBV viral load testing is feasible and improves turnaround time compared with centralized testing, although operational challenges persist. Expanding decentralized access and addressing system-level barriers could enhance timely clinical decision-making and strengthen mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13867-6.

Introduction

Hepatitis B is caused by the hepatitis B virus (HBV), which infects the liver and may lead to chronic liver disease, including cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [1]. According to current estimation by the World Health Organization (WHO) report, in 2024, about 254 million were living with hepatitis B in the world. The estimated number of people newly infected by viral hepatitis declined from 3 million in 2019 to 2.2 million in 2022, of whom approximately 1.2 million were hepatitis B infections. This reduction is likely associated with multiple prevention interventions, including hepatitis B vaccination, improved injection safety. The African Region accounts for 63% of new hepatitis B infections [2].

Most chronic HBV infection are due to vertical transmission via Mother To Child Transmission (MTCT). This put pregnant women at the center of HBV infection elimination [3]. Without prophylaxis, the transmission rate for HBeAg-positive mothers is about 70%-90%, vs. 10%-40% for HBeAg-negative mothers [4].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), all pregnant women should be screened for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) during pregnancy to identify those at risk of mother-to-child transmission (MTCT). For HBsAg-positive pregnant women, HBV DNA quantification (viral load testing) is recommended to assess the risk of MTCT and guide antiviral prophylaxis. The WHO recommends tenofovir prophylaxis from the 28th week of gestation until at least delivery for HBsAg-positive pregnant women with an HBV DNA level ≥ 200,000 IU/mL (or, where HBV DNA testing is unavailable [5].

Despite screening pregnant women and vaccinating newborns has reduced the rate of perinatal transmission in some regions, HBV remains a significant health concern [6]. Indeed, in highly endemic countries in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), HBV is commonly acquired through perinatal transmission from HBV-infected mothers [7, 8], particularly those who have a high viral load and/or are positive for the hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) [9]. Approximately 370,000 newborns are perinatally infected with HBV in SSA annually [10].

In Mali, national antenatal care guidelines recommend systematic screening of pregnant women for HBsAg using rapid diagnostic tests; however, access to HBV DNA quantification remains limited and largely centralized, which constrains timely identification of women eligible for antiviral prophylaxis. This gap represents a critical barrier to the implementation of effective prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) strategies.

Prevention of vertical transmission of HBV is an important goal, as this type of transmission is a major cause of new infections and is associated with an increased risk of progression to liver disease compared with other transmission routes [11]. The WHO has set the goal of HBV eliminating viral hepatitis as a major public health threat that includes improving its diagnosis among infected individuals, with the aim of reducing new infections by 90% and deaths by 65% by 2030 [12, 13]. To achieve this goal, the problem of vertical transmission of HBV must be addressed. This requires scaling up screening, ensuring timely assessment of antiviral prophylaxisis eligibility by making HBV DNA quantification assays widely available and easily accessible and deployable instead of depending on antiviral prophylaxisis eligibility by gestation age of more or equal to 28 weeks. More importantly, universal birth dose vaccination must be accessible. In Africa, current screening tools rely mainly on serology, rapid point-of-care tests and real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). The latter is a molecular assay used to quantify HBV genomic DNA and is often restricted to well-equipped laboratories that are often based in large cities or capitals. In this context, PCR assay platforms require dedicated infrastructure and highly trained laboratory technicians, which often results in a sample turn-around time (TAT) of days or even weeks and high testing costs for patients [14].

In this context, the GeneXpert system has transformed the management of infectious diseases in low- and middle-income countries, particularly for tuberculosis, where it has been successfully implemented in decentralized test-and-treat strategies. Building on this success, the Xpert HBV Viral Load test has been developed as a simplified tool to facilitate HBV viral load testing in resource-limited settings. Several studies have demonstrated its good performance and feasibility as a near point-of-care test [15].

Furthermore, since 2019, national programs targeting HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and viral hepatitis have been integrated under the support of the Global Fund, providing an opportunity to leverage existing diagnostic infrastructure [16]. In Mali, GeneXpert instruments are already widely deployed for tuberculosis diagnosis, offering a unique opportunity to expand their use for HBV management. The present study aims to evaluate how decentralized access to HBV viral load testing using existing GeneXpert infrastructure could complement current national strategies by facilitating timely treatment decisions in pregnant women at high risk of transmission.

Materials and methods

Study sites

This study was conducted at multiple levels of Malian public health system to reflect both centralized and decentralized care settings. Study sites included Referral Health Centers, in Commune III of Bamako, Bla, and Sikasso, as well as Community Health Centers in Djibouria (Kéniéba district) and Badialan (Commune III of Bamako). The Malian public health system is organized hierarchically, comprising Community Health Centers (CSCOM), at the community level, Reference Health Centers (CSREF) providing secondary care with specialist doctors, regional hospitals, and 3rd-level public hospital establishments, affiliated which university centers and equipped with advanced diagnostic platforms and qualified personnel. GeneXpert instruments are available nationwide in regional hospitals, 3rd-level public hospital establishments, and selected CSREFs enabling decentralized testing.

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional prospective study between June 2024 and May 2025. Data were collected consecutively over a 12-month period among pregnant women attending antenatal care services at the selected study sites who provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Study participants and procedures

All pregnant women of reproductive age attending routine prenatal consultations were screened for HBsAg using the Abbott Determine™ rapid diagnostic test, in accordance with the national guidelines. Women who tested positive for HBsAg and provided informed consent were enrolled in the study. For each enrolled woman, approximately 4 mL of venous blood was collected in EDTA tube for HBV viral load quantification using the Xpert HBV Viral Load test (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Samples collected at sites in Bla and Sikasso were analyzed on-site in peripheral laboratories equipped with GeneXpert instruments. Sample from the remaining sites were transported to the central laboratory at the UCRC for testing. Data were collected using tablets via KoBoCollect through a pretested electronic questionnaire, ensuring real-time data capture and reducing transcription errors.

Measured variables

Data collected included sociodemographic, obstetric, clinical, virological, and operational variables. Sociodemographic variables included maternal age, marital status, occupation, and place of residence. Obstetric variables included gestational age at screening, gravidity, parity, and history of previous pregnancies. Others variables included prior knowledge of HBV infection, hepatitis B vaccination status, family history of HBV infection, HIV status, history of blood transfusion, and receipt of antiretroviral (ARV) treatment for HBV during pregnancy, recorded at the time of study inclusion irrespective of the timing of HBV viral load testing. Virological variables included HBsAg positivity, HBV DNA detectability, and viral load categories defined as < 2,000 IU/mL, 2,000–200,000 IU/mL, and > 200,000 IU/mL. Operational variables included study site, laboratory type (central or peripheral), and turnaround time for viral load results, defined as the interval between sample collection and availability of test results. In addition, we collected information on the prescribing clinician’s decision regarding initiation of antiviral therapy following HBV DNA test results.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into a secure database and analyzed using R software version 4.5.2. Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. The prevalence of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) among pregnant women was calculated overall and by study site as the proportion of women testing positive among those screened. Comparisons of HBsAg prevalence across sites were descriptive. HBV viral load results were categorized as undetectable or detectable, and detectable viral loads were further classified into three clinically relevant categories: <2,000 IU/mL, 2,000–200,000 IU/mL, and > 200,000 IU/mL. Associations between viral load categories and maternal characteristics (age group, gestational age at screening, and antiviral treatment status post AgHBs testing) were assessed using the chi‑square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Turnaround time for HBV viral load testing was categorized by number of days to result availability and compared between central and peripheral laboratories using the chi‑square test. All statistical tests were two‑sided, and a p‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

The research protocol was approved in Mali by the Ethics Committee of the University of Sciences, Techniques and Technologies of Bamako (USTTB) (N°2023/273/CE/USTTB). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines and regulations. Prior to participation during the field activities, informed consent was obtained from each participant. Participant confidentiality was strictly maintained; all data were anonymized for analysis and shared using a unique identification number for each respondent.

Results

Between June 2024 and May 2025, a total of 2,952 pregnant women were screened for HBV infection across different levels of the health system in Mali. Overall, 205 women tested positives for HBsAg, corresponding to a prevalence of 6.9% (95%CI: 6.1–7.9%). Of these HBs Ag-positive samples, 24 were hemolyzed and therefore excluded from further analysis, leaving 191 samples that were successfully analyzed for HBV viral load using the GeneXpert platform (Fig. 1).

The characteristic of the 205 HBsAg positive pregnant women are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 28.7 years (IQR: 21–33), with more than half (56.1%) younger than 30 years. Most participants were housewives (69.8%), and nearly all were married (98.5%). Screening occurred predominantly during the second trimester (49.8%), and third (45.8%) trimesters of pregnancy, and the majority of women (87.8%) had at least one prior pregnancy. More than half (57.6%) were unaware of their HBsAg status prior to screening, and vaccination coverage against hepatitis B was extremely low (1.0%). A family history of hepatitis B (7.3%), HIV positivity (1.5%), and previous blood transfusion (2.4%) were uncommon. Overall, 95.6% of HBsAg positive women were screened during the second and third trimester, indicating limited early pregnancy detection.

Among the 191 participants who underwent HBV viral load testing, 123 (64%) had detectable HBV viral load, whereas 68 (36%) had undetectable viral loads (Table 3). Among women with detectable HBV viral load, the majority had low viral loads (< 2,000 IU/mL, 69.9%, n = 123). Intermediate viral loads (2,000–200,000 IU/mL) were observed in 24.4% of cases, while 5.7% of women had high viral loads (> 200,000 IU/mL), a level associated with an increased risk of mother-to-child transmission. Women with HBV DNA > 200,000 IU/mL represented 3.9% of all HBsAg positive women corresponding to those eligible for antiviral prophylaxis according to international guidelines, particularly WHO recommendations.

Association between maternal characteristics and viral load categories among women with detectable HBV are presented in Table 4. Viral load was not significantly associated with maternal age (p > 0.9) or gestational age at screening (p = 0.2). However, higher viral load categories were more frequently observed among women screened in the third trimester, with 71.4% of women with viral loads > 200,000 IU/mL screened during this period. HBV treatment status was significantly associated with viral load categories (p < 0.001), with higher proportions of women on treatment observed in the higher viral load groups. Among women with viral loads > 200,000 IU/mL, only 57.1% were receiving antiviral treatment, indicating that nearly half of women at highest risk of mother-to-child transmission was not on therapy at the time of testing.

Turnaround time for HBV viral load differed significantly by laboratory type (Table 5). Peripheral laboratories equipped with on-site GeneXpert instruments returned results within one day more frequently than the central laboratory (23.5% vs. 4.4%, p < 0.001). This corresponds to an absolute increase of 19.1% points and an approximately five-fold relative improvement in same-day result availability with decentralized testing. However, in both laboratory types, most results were reported after more than four days (63.7% and 75%, respectively), highlighting persistent operational delays.

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection and evaluated the use of HBV viral load testing using GeneXpert in the management of HBsAg positive pregnant women in decentralized settings in Mali. It also described viral load profiles and operational aspects of viral load testing across different settings.

The overall prevalence of HBsAg among pregnant women in this study was 6.9%, confirming that HBV infection remains highly endemic in Mali. However, substantial variations were observed across study sites. The highest prevalence was reported in Kenieba/Djibouria (11.6%), while the lowest prevalence was observed in Bla (2.0%). The HBsAg prevalence observed in this study is broadly consistent with findings from other studies conducted among pregnant women in Africa, although variations in magnitude persist [17–22]. Similarly, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 91 studies from 28 African countries involving pregnant women reported a pooled prevalence of 5.89%. The highest prevalence was found in West and Central Africa, followed by East Africa [23]. These variations may reflect differences in HBV transmission dynamics, vaccination coverage, socio-demographic factors, access to healthcare, and methodological approaches across study settings.

The predominance of screening during the second (49.8%) and third (45.8%) trimesters of pregnancy likely reflects delayed prenatal care in the study context. This could be explained in Mali, where women who have already been pregnant (87.8% of our study participants) are often less likely to begin prenatal care early, and by other factors, including financial, geographic, and sociocultural ones. This can lead to delays in screening and antiviral prophylaxis for pregnant women with hepatitis B.

Regarding virological characteristics, 68% of women who underwent viral load testing had detectable HBV DNA, indicating active viral replication in a substantial proportion of infected pregnant women. Among those with detectable viral load, the majority had low viral loads (< 2,000 IU/mL), suggesting a low-replicative chronic HBV infection in this population. However, 5.7% had viral loads exceeding 200,000 IU/mL, which is clinically significant because this level is associated with an increased risk of mother-to-child transmission (MTCT). This proportion represents a critical subgroup requiring targeted therapeutic intervention. According to international guidelines, these women should receive antiviral prophylaxis during pregnancy, typically with tenofovir, to reduce the risk of perinatal transmission [24]. The predominance of low viral load levels observed in this study is consistent with the natural history of chronic HBV infection in highly endemic settings, where most infections are acquired early in life and evolve into an inactive carrier state during adulthood [25–26]. However, the presence of a subset of women with high viral load underscores the ongoing risk of vertical transmission in the absence of systematic screening and antiviral prophylaxis. Although this study did not directly assess mother-to-child transmission, previous studies have clearly demonstrated that high maternal viral load is a major determinant of MTCT. In this context, our findings provide indirect evidence of a residual risk of transmission in this population. Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis revealed wide variation in HBV prevalence, in children aged 0–16 years in West Africa showing a decrease in prevalence over the past two decades [27]. Nevertheless, the persistence of higher prevalence in high-risk child populations, suggests that transmission is still ongoing, and insufficient prevention of MTCT, particularly among women with high viral load, may contribute to this residual burden.

The analysis of factors associated with viral load categories showed no significant association with maternal age (p > 0.05) or gestational age (p > 0.05) at screening. However, HBV viral load results were systematically available before clinical decision-making, and antiviral therapy was initiated based on these results. Clinicians’ decision to initiate antiviral therapy was significantly associated with viral load levels (p < 0.05). Practitioners seem more likely to initiate treatment for pregnant women with higher HBV DNA levels. This finding may reflect the fact that women with higher viral loads are more likely to be identified and initiated on treatment, or that treatment may have been started in response to clinical indications.

GeneXpert system has transformed the management of infectious diseases in low- and middle-income countries, particularly for tuberculosis, where it has been successfully implemented in decentralized test-and-treat strategies. Building on this success, the Xpert HBV Viral Load test has been developed as a simplified tool to facilitate HBV viral load testing in resource-limited settings. This study assessed whether access to GeneXpert platforms with HBV viral load cartridges at peripheral laboratories could improve the timeliness of viral load testing and facilitate clinical management. A significant difference in turnaround time was observed between central and peripheral laboratories. Peripheral laboratories equipped with GeneXpert systems were more likely to provide viral load results within one day compared with central laboratories. However, despite the availability of GeneXpert in peripheral sites, a large proportion of results were still obtained after more than four days. This suggests that, although decentralization of viral load testing has the potential to improve access and reduce delays, other operational factors such as sample flow, workload, logistics, or communication of results may still affect the timely return of results. Indeed, decentralization of HBV viral load testing and scale-up of HBV treatment programs remain significant challenges across most HBV endemic countries. There is therefore an urgent need to identify the gap to access those cost-effective alternatives to molecular HBV diagnostics, particularly in Africa, due to its high disease burden and the lack of context-adapted guidelines [28].

Conclusion

The findings demonstrate that decentralized HBV viral load testing using the GeneXpert platform is feasible in routine antenatal care settings and can significantly reduce turnaround times compared with centralized testing. However, persistent delays in result availability highlight ongoing operational challenges related to sample management, laboratory workload, logistics, and result communication that must be addressed to fully realize the benefits of decentralization.

Overall, expanding access to decentralized HBV viral load testing, strengthening early antenatal screening, and improving linkage to timely antiviral treatment for eligible pregnant women are critical steps to reducing vertical transmission of HBV. This study confirms that hepatitis B virus infection remains highly endemic among pregnant women in Mali, with marked geographical heterogeneity across health districts. A substantial proportion of HBsAg‑positive women had detectable HBV DNA, and an increased risk of mother‑to‑child transmission, underscoring the need for systematic viral load assessment during pregnancy to guide antiviral prophylaxis.
