Patient health literacy as a critical missing component in the PLAN to address the Parkinson pandemic
Authors: Paula Abola, Benjamin Wolden, Alessandra Swarowsky, Mitchell Wolden
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read the article “A PLAN to Address the Parkinson Pandemic” by Dorsey, Okun, and Bloem with great interest. Their framework offers an essential and timely vision for addressing the rising global burden of Parkinson's Disease (PD). However, the PLAN could be strengthened by more explicitly acknowledging patient health literacy as a foundational determinant of its success. Health literacy plays a central role in how well individuals understand their condition, interpret treatment options, and engage meaningfully in shared decision-making (SDM). 1 Highlighting this factor aligns the PLAN more closely with current principles of patient-centered care and highlights why it should be considered a core component of effective PD management.

Evidence suggests wide and persistent disparities in patient knowledge regarding both pharmacological and non-pharmacological management options, as well as disparities in how patients perceive their involvement in shared decision-making compared to their healthcare providers.2,3 These findings mirror broader evidence from chronic disease research showing that health literacy systematically shapes how patients access, interpret, and use health information.4–6 In this context, health literacy is not simply an educational consideration; it is an enabling condition for equitable care.

Dorsey, Okun, and Bloem go on to highlight the importance of amplifying the voices of those affected by PD. Yet amplification requires more than opportunities to speak; it requires the ability to understand options, evaluate evidence, and articulate informed preferences. Similarly, greater attention to SDM could further strengthen the PLAN. In PD care more broadly, discrepancies are often observed between how much SDM providers believe they are delivering and how much patients feel they experience. Factors associated with higher patient-perceived SDM, such as educational background, social support, and access to information, are closely linked to health literacy. 7 Recognizing these dynamics highlights the importance of integrating SDM principles more explicitly into the PLAN to ensure that patients can meaningfully participate in treatment decisions. Without explicit attention to these structural factors, existing disparities in communication and engagement will persist, even within well-intentioned frameworks.

Moreover, each component of the PLAN presupposes a certain level of patient understanding. Prevention efforts rely on the capacity to recognize risk factors and early symptoms. Learning from research requires access to information presented in understandable formats, amplifying voices assumes informational readiness, and navigating emerging treatments depends on the ability to interpret increasingly complex therapeutic choices. In this sense, health literacy intersects with every aspect of the PLAN and functions as a prerequisite rather than a complementary idea.

To support and extend the authors’ proposal, we suggest integrating health literacy as a cross-cutting dimension across all four elements: (1) prevention – develop public-facing educational resources adapted to diverse literacy levels and linguistic contexts to ensure equitable understanding of environmental risks and early symptoms; (2) learning – promote accessible dissemination of research findings and greater transparency in clinical trial reporting so that emerging evidence becomes usable for patients and families; (3) amplification – implement literacy-appropriate decision aids, enhance clinician communication training, and increase access to reliable patient education materials; (4) navigation – incorporate structured health literacy interventions into routine care to support informed participation, treatment adherence, and meaningful SDM.

Evidence from other chronic diseases demonstrates that targeted health literacy interventions can improve patient engagement, adherence, and overall outcomes.1–3 Incorporating similar approaches to PD care would support the goals outlined in the PLAN and ensure that its benefits reach individuals who currently face the greatest informational and structural barriers.

We commend the authors for their ambitious and necessary proposal to address the PD pandemic. Explicitly integrating health literacy into this framework would enhance its feasibility and promote a more equitable and patient-centered approach to PD care.

Sincerely,

Dr. Paula Abola, Dr. Benjamin Wolden, Dr. Alessandra Swarowsky, and Dr. Mitchell Wolden
