Executive Summary
The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit end-
use load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover a majority of the high-impact,
market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. “Measure” is another name for an upgrade or
retrofit that can be applied to a building.

An “end-use savings shape” is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building
and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It
results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas,
propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data
sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the
annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States.
The baseline model intends to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018.
Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is
available in Reyna et al. (2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are
available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (Wilson et al. 2022).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape upgrade—“Reference Space
Heating and Air Conditioning Upgrade Circa 2025.” This upgrade replaces space heating and
cooling equipment in homes with existing ductwork with units that meet minimum efficiency
standards reflective of circa-2025 requirements.

1 Technology Summary
This work evaluates a reference case where existing space heating and air conditioning
equipment in homes with existing ductwork is upgraded to the minimum efficiency available
(circa 2025)⎯or like-for-like replacements for above-minimum equipment—across the U.S.
residential building stock. The purpose of this reference case is to provide a comparison point for
other measures and packages that simulate heating and cooling equipment replacements at the
end of the equipment’s useful life (also called “at wear out”). This enables estimates of
incremental energy savings and incremental upfront cost of measures compared to an assumed
alternative.

1.1 Introduction to Technology
Fuel-fired furnaces—including propane, fuel oil, and natural gas—are used to heat indoor
environments. Fuel ignites in the burner, and the flame heats a metal heat exchanger. A blower
fan moves air over the heat exchangers, which warms the air. Most furnaces use ducts to
distribute heat, while combustion exhaust is vented outside. In comparison, gravity furnaces do
exist but are older and not as common; as such in this study, we only model forced air furnaces.

Fuel-fired wall or floor furnaces are directly installed within walls or floors to serve one or a few
rooms. They burn natural gas, propane, or fuel oil in a combustion chamber, transferring heat
through a heat exchanger. Heated air rises by convection into occupied spaces, and combustion
byproducts should be vented outside the dwelling. Unlike central furnaces, they typically do not
use ducts and instead provide localized heating.

Electric furnaces use resistance heating elements rather than combustion to produce heat. When
an electric current passes through the elements, they generate heat, which is transferred to air
moving across them. A blower fan distributes this heated air through ducts.

Fuel boilers heat water instead of air. Natural gas, propane, or fuel oil is burned in a combustion
chamber to heat water. The hot water (or steam in some systems) is then circulated through pipes
to radiators, baseboard heaters, or radiant floor systems. Boilers can provide both space heating
and domestic hot water. Combustion gases are vented outside the building.

Central air conditioners typically use the same duct network as the heating system. They remove
heat from buildings by operating a vapor-compression cycle: an outdoor unit compresses and
condenses refrigerant, which then absorbs heat from indoor air as it passes over an evaporator
coil. A blower fan circulates the cooled air through the ducts, and the absorbed heat is expelled
outdoors; system efficiency is measured by the seasonal energy efficiency ratio, version 2
(SEER2), with higher ratings indicating more efficient cooling.

Room air conditioners are self-contained cooling units designed to cool a single room or small
area. They are typically mounted in windows or through walls and operate using a vapor-
compression cycle: they draw in warm indoor air, cool it by passing it over the evaporator coil,
return it to the room, and expel heat outside. Room air conditioners are rated by their combined
energy efficiency ratio (CEER).

 3

Air-source heat pumps (ASHPs) provide both heating and cooling using a reversible vapor-
compression cycle. In cooling mode, they operate like a central air conditioner, transferring heat
from indoors to outdoors, but in heating mode, the cycle reverses and they extract heat from
outdoor air and deliver it indoors. Even at cold temperatures, heat pumps can capture usable
energy from the outdoor air. Distribution is typically through the same duct network used for
cooling, though ductless systems deliver conditioned air directly to rooms. Efficiency is rated
using heating seasonal performance factor, version 2 (HSPF2) for heating and SEER2 for
cooling.

1.2 Sizing
HVAC sizing is automatic across all HVAC system types and sizes following the ACCA Manual
J/S methodology. ACCA Manual J (ACCA, 2024) provides guidelines for calculating the design
heating and cooling loads in a building, and ACCA Manual S (ACCA, 2014) provides a guide
for selecting the size of residential HVAC systems to meet those loads. For a full description of
sizing and system selection for ASHPs, reference the Dual Fuel Heat Pump System
documentation (White et al. 2025).

1.3 Efficiency
Efficiency is rated using annual fuel utilization efficiency (AFUE) for natural gas furnaces,
SEER2 for air conditioning systems, HSPF2 for heat pumps, CEER for room air conditioners.
Importantly, SEER2 and HSPF2 include fan energy but AFUE does not.

1.3.1 Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency
AFUE is the standard rated efficiency for how efficiently a furnace converts fuel into heat at
rated conditions. The higher the AFUE rating, the more efficient the system is at converting fuel
into usable heat. Energy lost through exhaust gases, radiation, and other components decreases
efficiency.

AFUE is the total annual heat output divided by the total annual fuel input—on a scale of 0% to
100%. Note that unlike SEER2 and HSPF2, AFUE does not include the electricity used by the
air handler blower, combustion blower, ignition system, or controls. ANSI/ASHRAE 103-2022
provides a procedure for determining AFUE of residential central furnaces and boilers, which is
a test cycle structured to simulate ordinary heating conditions throughout the year—including
seasons (ANSI/ASHRAE 2022).

“Residential furnaces manufactured and distributed in commerce, as defined by 42 U.S.C.
6291(16) must meet the energy conservation standards specified in the Code of Federal
Regulations at 10 CFR 430.32” (DOE 2025b). 80% AFUE furnaces are the minimum for non-
weatherized gas furnaces (i.e., natural gas and propane) manufactured from 2015 through Dec.
18, 2028. After Dec. 18, 2028, the minimum is 95% AFUE. If an investigator would like to
understand equipment standard changes over time, a useful approach could blend data from this
study with data from the Propane and Fuel Oil Furnaces upgrade (Stenger et al. 2026b) as well as
the Natural Gas Furnaces upgrade (Stenger et al. 2026a), both of which model 95% AFUE for
propane and natural gas furnaces, respectively. In this study, we model an 80% AFUE as
minimum efficiency natural gas furnace when existing efficiency is rated lower than 80% AFUE,
and a like-for-like replacement when the existing natural gas furnace is rated above 80% AFUE.

 4

1.3.2 Combined Energy Efficiency Ratio
CEER is the standard rated efficiency for room air conditioners under specific test conditions. It
measures how effectively the unit converts electrical energy into cooling capacity; the higher the
CEER rating, the more efficient the system.

CEER is defined as the ratio of the steady-state cooling capacity (i.e., Btu/hr) to the electrical
power input (i.e., kW) under rated conditions (Code of Federal Regulations 2025). Because it is
measured at a single set of conditions, energy efficiency ratio (EER) provides a snapshot of
performance rather than a seasonal metric. For portable and room air conditioners, CEER
accounts for standby and off-mode power consumption, but has different test procedures.

1.3.3 Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio, Version 2
SEER2 is a metric for representing the cooling efficiency of central air conditioners and heat
pumps as they would perform over an entire cooling season. It is defined as the total cooling
output (in British thermal units, Btu) during a typical cooling season divided by the total
electrical energy input, including the blower fan energy (in watt-hours) consumed over that same
period. Higher SEER2 values indicate more efficient systems, since they deliver more cooling
per unit of electricity. Energy losses due to on-off cycling, blower waste heat, and refrigerant line
heat transfer all reduce SEER2.

SEER2 is determined under standardized test conditions specified by ANSI/AHRI Standard
210/240-2024 (and DOE’s Appendix M1), which simulates a range of outdoor temperatures and
indoor loads to approximate real-world operation throughout the cooling season. Ratings
typically range from about 13 SEER2 for older equipment up to 26 SEER2 for more advanced
centrally ducted air-conditioner systems, and up to 35.1 SEER2 for non-ducted systems.

In this study, dwellings with existing air‐conditioning systems below the regional minimums are
modeled with central air conditioners or heat pumps rated at 14.3 SEER2 in the South region and
13.4 SEER2 in the North region, and with central ASHPs rated at 14.3 SEER2 and 7.5 HSPF2.
For dwellings whose current cooling equipment already meets or exceeds these thresholds, we
simulate a like-for-like replacement at the higher efficiency.

The South region includes the following states: Alabama, Arizona, Arkansas, California,
Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Hawai‘i, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maryland, Mississippi, Nevada, New
Mexico, North Carolina, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia, and the District
of Columbia. The North region includes all states not listed in the South region.

1.3.4 Heating Seasonal Performance Factor, Version 2
HSPF2 is a rating representing the heating efficiency of heat pumps as they perform over an
entire heating season. It is defined as the total heating output (in Btu) during a typical heating
season divided by the total electrical energy input, including the energy consumed by the indoor
blower fan (in watt-hours) and outdoor unit over that same period. Higher HSPF2 indicates more
efficient systems, since they deliver more heating per unit of electricity. Factors such as on-off
cycling, coil defrost cycles, and standby power consumption can reduce HSPF2.

HSPF2 is determined under standardized test conditions, which simulate a range of outdoor
temperature and part-load conditions to approximate real-world performance throughout the

heating season. Residential central air conditioning heat pumps manufactured and distributed in
commerce, as defined by 42 U.S.C. 6291(16), must meet the energy conservation standards
specified in the Code of Federal Regulations at 10 CFR 420.32(c)(5) (DOE 2025a).

In this study, dwellings with existing systems below the regional minimums are modeled with
ducted heat pumps rated at 7.5 HSPF2. For dwellings whose current heating equipment already
meets or exceeds these thresholds, we simulate a like-for-like replacement at the higher
efficiency.

includes:

3 Modeling Approach
This section outlines how the upgrade is modeled in ResStock using the OpenStudio-HPXML
workflow. In this scenario, dwellings meeting the applicability logic receive the upgrade.
ResStock uses probabilistic sampling logic based on national housing stock data to assign each
dwelling unit a set of baseline building characteristics, including existing heating fuel, system
type, and duct configuration. Ducts remain unchanged when heating and cooling systems are
upgraded, which can yield inefficiencies. The model assumes consistent operation patterns
across households and scales furnace capacity based on heating load requirements determined by
building envelope characteristics and climate zone. While equipment brand and installation
quality are not modeled explicitly, the total heating energy demand and fuel consumption are
recalculated to reflect the standardized furnace and fuel use. This scenario represents a reference
case where all space heating and air conditioning equipment is upgraded to the minimum
efficiency available (or like-for-like replacements for above-minimum equipment) across the
U.S. residential building stock. The purpose of this reference case is to provide a comparison
point for other measures and packages that simulate heating and cooling equipment replacements
at the end of the equipment’s useful life (also called “at wear out”). This enables estimates of
incremental energy savings and incremental upfront cost of measures compared to an assumed
alternative.

These probability distributions are sampled to create a synthetic stock where each sample has an
assigned option for each characteristic for each dwelling unit to be simulated. ResStock utilizes
the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building
models ready to be simulated via OpenStudio® and EnergyPlus® through the use of an HPXML
file. For reference, the state of the OpenStudio-HPXML repository when the ResStock 2025
Release 1 dataset was created can be found here. Assumptions about the ASHPs upgrades that
are not explicitly defined in ResStock are taken care of in the BuildResidentialHPXML measure.

3.1 Upgrade Modeling Details
In ResStock, building characteristics are translated to OpenStudio-HPXML parameters. Measure
upgrades, such as installing natural gas furnaces and air conditioning, are modeled as changes to
baseline building characteristics. This section outlines the technical details of how the federal
minimum heating and cooling upgrade is implemented in the ResStock workflow.

slightly based on the return water temperature, whereas for furnaces the efficiency is always
constant and equal to the AFUE of the unit.

4.3 Stock Energy Impacts
Out of 550,000 model building simulation (i.e., models), 413,611 models are applicable for the
reference space heating and air conditioning upgrade (75.2%). For the 413,611 models, the total
baseline consumption is 2.87 trillion kWh annually, and after the upgrade is 2.80 trillion kWh, a
2% savings. Figure 6 shows the total site energy consumption between the baseline and upgrade.

 Figure 6. Annual total site energy consumption for housing stock

 16

 Figure 7. Annual energy consumption by HVAC heating type and fuel end use
Cooling from electricity in ducted heating systems accounts for 42,000 GWh of energy
reduction, the greatest energy reduction after the upgrade; in comparison, heating from natural
gas in ducted heating systems was reduced by 6,000 GWh. Cooling fans and pumps (i.e., air
handler fan energy) also yielded 6,400 GWh in energy savings, whereas heating fans and pumps
yielded 1,300 GWh in energy savings. Energy savings from fans and pumps are primarily a
result of more advanced motors and controls.

Given that this is a reference case and relatively few HVAC systems are below minimum
efficiency, only modest energy savings are expected: 4% for electricity-heated homes, 2% for
natural gas, 3% for fuel oil, and 1% for propane.
