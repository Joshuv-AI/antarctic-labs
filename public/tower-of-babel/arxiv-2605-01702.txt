Floating-Point Networks with Automatic Differentiation Can Represent Almost All Floating-Point Functions and Their Gradients
Sejun Park, Yeachan Park, Geonho Hwang

Abstract
Theoretical studies show that for any differentiable function on a compact domain, there exists a neural network that approximates both the function values and gradients. However, such a result cannot be used in practice since it assumes real parameters and exact internal operations. In contrast, real implementations only use a finite subset of reals and machine operations with round-off errors. In this work, we investigate whether a similar result holds for neural networks under floating-point arithmetic, when the gradient with respect to the input is computed by the automatic differentiation algorithm D 𝙰𝙳 D^{\mathtt{AD}} . We first show that given a floating-point function ϕ \phi (e.g., a loss function), arbitrary function values and gradients can be represented by a floating-point network f f and D 𝙰𝙳 ​ ( ϕ ∘ f ) D^{\mathtt{AD}}(\phi\circ f) , respectively. We further extend this result: given ϕ 1 , … , ϕ n \phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n} , D 𝙰𝙳 ​ ( ϕ i ∘ f ) D^{\mathtt{AD}}(\phi_{i}\circ f) can simultaneously represent arbitrary gradients while f f represents the target values, under mild conditions. Our results hold for practical activation functions, e.g., ReLU \mathrm{ReLU} , ELU \mathrm{ELU} , GELU \mathrm{GELU} , Swish \mathrm{Swish} , Sigmoid \mathrm{Sigmoid} , and tanh \tanh .



1
Introduction

Modern neural network applications often require not only the function values but also the gradients with respect to the input.
For example, in the field of scientific machine learning, neural networks are employed as surrogates for physical models or solutions to partial differential equations
(
21
)
.
Gradients are also used in various problems, such as sensitivity analysis
(
23
)
, inverse problems
(
2
)
, and reinforcement learning
(
15
)
, where decisions depend on how outputs change in response to small perturbations of inputs.
Furthermore, gradients are often used to revise inputs, e.g., for adversarial attacks
(
6
;
4
)
and generation
(
5
)
. They are also used to improve the performance of trained networks, e.g., several studies show that fitting the gradient together with the function value can increase the final performance of networks
(
3
;
9
)
.

Theoretical results show that neural networks can approximate complex functions when the networks use real parameters and exact operations (e.g., addition and multiplication).
Early results focused on fully-connected networks
(
20
)
, which have been extended to modern architectures such as convolutional neural networks
(
25
)
, recurrent neural networks
(
22
)
, residual networks
(
16
)
, and transformers
(
24
)
.
While most of these results focus on approximating function values only, it is also known that neural networks can approximate function values and gradients at the same time
(
14
;
12
)
.

In practice, neural networks are executed on computers using floating-point arithmetic
(
13
)
. Namely, all network parameters and intermediate values are represented by a finite set of floating-point numbers, and internal operations follow the rules of floating-point computations, involving round-off errors.
Hence, a practical network is an algorithm determined by a sequence of floating-point operations rather than a real-valued function in theory.
Likewise, gradients in such systems are computed by applying chain-rule-based automatic differentiation algorithms (e.g., backpropagation) to this computational graph
(
8
)
.
Therefore, this gradient is not the derivative of a differentiable, real-valued function, but rather the output of a computational procedure.

A few recent studies have investigated the representability of floating-point neural networks that only use floating-point parameters and operations.

19
show that floating-point networks can represent all functions from floating-point vectors to floating-point values when the activation function is
\mathrm{ReLU}
or the binary step function.
This result has been extended to
(
11
)
and floating-point interval arithmetic
(
10
)
.
Nevertheless, as in most existing results under exact arithmetic, these results aim to fit the function values only, not the gradients.

1.1
Contributions

In this work, we study the representability of floating-point networks together with automatic differentiation (AD).
Specifically, we investigate the following problem:

\begin{gathered}\textit{Can floating-point networks with AD represent}\\
\textit{arbitrary function values and arbitrary gradients?}\end{gathered}

To better describe our results, we briefly introduce a floating-point network
f
consisting of floating-point affine transformations
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{l}
and a pointwise activation function
\sigma
:

f(x)=\rho_{l}\circ\sigma\circ\rho_{l-1}\circ\sigma\circ\cdots\circ\sigma\circ\rho_{1}(x).

For a floating-point function
\phi_{x}
(e.g., a loss function that may depend on the input
x
) AD for
\phi_{x}\circ f
is computed as follows: for predefined derivatives
\phi_{x}^{\prime}
,
\rho^{\prime}_{i}
,
\sigma^{\prime}
of
\phi_{x}
,
\rho_{i}
,
\sigma
,

\displaystyle D^{\mathtt{AD}}(\phi_{x}\circ f,x)\!=

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{x}(f(x))\!\otimes\!\rho_{l}^{\prime}(z_{l})\!\otimes\!\sigma^{\prime}(y_{l-1})\!\otimes\!\cdots\!\otimes\!\rho_{1}^{\prime}(x)

where
z_{i}
denotes the input to
\rho_{i}
,
y_{i}
denotes the input to
\sigma
in the layer
i
, and

\otimes
denotes the floating-point multiplication. Importantly, the multiplications here should be computed from left to right; otherwise, the final value can be different due to the non-associativity of floating-point operations.

In our first result sketched below, we prove that floating-point networks with AD can represent almost all function values and gradients,
where
\mathbb{F}
denotes the set of finite floating-point numbers.

Theorem 3.1

(informal)
.

For almost all
f^{*}:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
,
g^{*}:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
, and
\phi^{\prime}_{x}:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
such that
g^{*}(x)=0
for all
x
with
\phi^{\prime}_{x}(f^{*}(x))=0
, there exists a floating-point network
f
such that
f=f^{*}
and
D^{\mathtt{AD}}(\phi_{x}\circ f,x)=g^{*}(x).

Theorem
3.1
states that given some function
\phi^{\prime}_{x}
,
f
and
D^{\mathtt{AD}}(\phi_{x}\circ f)
can represent the target function
f^{*}
and the gradient
g^{*}
, respectively.
Here, we do not consider the antiderivative
\phi_{x}
of
\phi^{\prime}_{x}
since it does not affect
D^{\mathtt{AD}}(\phi_{x}\circ f)(x)
.
Furthermore,
\phi^{\prime}_{x}
can be arbitrarily chosen. For example, if
\phi^{\prime}_{x}(x)=1
for all
x
, then
D^{\mathtt{AD}}(\phi_{x}\circ f)
corresponds to the gradient of
f
computed by AD.
For general
\phi^{\prime}_{x}
, Theorem
3.1
shows that floating-point networks can represent the gradient of
\phi_{x}\circ f
, as long as the value of
\phi^{\prime}_{x}
is non-zero.

Theorem
3.1
implies that floating-point networks can solve problems that require fitting both function values and gradients simultaneously, e.g.,
(
21
;
3
)
.
Furthermore, Theorem
3.1
shows that floating-point networks can successfully perform target tasks (by fitting function values) while having manipulated gradients (e.g., zero for all inputs).
Such manipulation can be useful when one does not want to leak information from gradients, e.g., to protect against gradient-based attacks and reasoning
(
6
;
23
)
.

Theorem
3.1
applies to various practical activation functions, including
\mathrm{ReLU}
,
\mathrm{ELU}
,
\mathrm{GELU}
,
\mathrm{Swish}
,
\mathrm{Sigmoid}
, and
\tanh
. Here, the activation function determines the possible range of
g^{*}
and
\phi^{\prime}_{x}
. For example, for
\mathrm{ReLU}
,
\mathrm{ELU}
,
\mathrm{GELU}
, and
\mathrm{Swish}
, Theorem
3.1
covers all
f^{*},g^{*}:[-\Omega/8,\Omega/8]^{d}\to\mathbb{F}
and
\phi_{x}^{\prime}:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
where
\Omega
denotes the largest finite float, e.g.,
\Omega\approx 2^{128}
and
2^{16}
for the 32-bit single-precision and 16-bit half-precision formats
(
13
)
.
For
\mathrm{Sigmoid}
, Theorem
3.1
covers
f^{*},g^{*}:[-\Omega/4,\Omega/4]^{d}\to\mathbb{F}
and
\phi_{x}^{\prime}:\mathbb{F}\to[-\Omega/4,\Omega/4]
, while
\tanh
covers a slightly smaller range of
\phi_{x}^{\prime}
.

The floating-point network
f
in
Theorem
3.1
is a function of
\phi_{x}^{\prime}
. Hence, given
f
,
D^{\mathtt{AD}}(\phi_{x}\circ f,x)
is a function of
x
only (i.e.,
g^{*}(x)
), and it may output an arbitrary gradient for a different choice of
\phi_{x}^{\prime}
(e.g., when
\phi_{x}
attached to
f
changes).
Then, can we construct a floating-point network
f
so that
D^{\mathtt{AD}}(\phi_{x}\circ f,x)
is a function of
\phi_{x}^{\prime}
and
x
given
f
?
We provide a positive answer in our next result.

Theorem 3.2

(informal)
.

For almost all
f^{*}:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
and
g^{*}:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
such that
g^{*}(x,-y)=-g^{*}(x,y)
, there exists a floating-point network
f
such that for almost all
\phi_{x}^{\prime}:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
,

f=f^{*}
and
D^{\mathtt{AD}}(\phi_{x}\circ f)(x)=g^{*}(x,\phi^{\prime}_{x}(f(x)))
.

Theorem
3.2
implies that there exists a single floating-point network
f
such that, even when
\phi^{\prime}_{x}
changes,
D^{\mathtt{AD}}(\phi_{x}\circ f,x)
changes accordingly, following
g^{*}(x,\phi_{x}^{\prime}(f(x)))
.
Importantly, we can choose an arbitrary
g^{*}(x,\phi^{\prime}_{x}(f(x)))
as long as
g^{*}(x,-y)=-g^{*}(x,y)
; such a restriction cannot be bypassed due to the symmetry inherent in the definition of
D^{\mathtt{AD}}(\phi_{x}\circ f,x)
.
We note that an analogous result cannot hold under exact arithmetic since

\nabla(\tilde{\phi}\circ\tilde{f})(x)=\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}(\tilde{f}(x))\times\nabla f(x)

should be proportional to
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}(\tilde{f}(x))
for real differentiable functions
\tilde{\phi}
and
\tilde{f}
.
Nevertheless, under floating-point arithmetic, it is possible to represent gradients that are not proportional to
\phi_{x}^{\prime}
by exploiting the non-associativity of floating-point multiplication.
That is, floating-point networks can perform the target task by fitting function values while having artificially manipulated gradients, even if
\phi_{x}^{\prime}
can change over time.
We note that Theorem
3.2
also applies to floating-point networks using activation functions that we discussed after Theorem
3.1
.
However, our results are not limited to these activation functions; we also provide similar results for general activation functions in
Theorems

3.6
and
3.8
.

In this paper, we follow the IEEE-754 standard for floating-point arithmetic

The set of finite floating-point numbers

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p,q}\coloneq\{

When it is clear from the context, we omit

We say that “

(i.e.,

or
s(i/{2^{p}})2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\min}}

The set of all floating-point numbers

contains some non-finite numbers:

{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\coloneq\mathbb{F}\cup\{-\infty,\infty,\text{NaN}\}

For

To define floating-point operations, we define the floating-point rounding operation

\left\lceil{x}\right\rfloor\coloneq\begin{cases}\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{F}}|x-y|&\text{if }|x|<\Omega+2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}-p-1},\\
-\infty&\text{if }x\leq-\Omega-2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}-p-1},\\

If a tie occurs in

For

2.3
Floating-Point Neural Network

We consider an activation function
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
and its derivative
\sigma^{\prime}:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
.
One may regard
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
as the rounded versions of a real activation function and its derivative.
Nevertheless, we do not impose any restrictions on

{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
to
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
.
We often apply
\sigma

\sigma^{\prime}
to vectors: for

\sigma(x)=(\sigma(x_{1}),\dots,\sigma(x_{d}))

\sigma^{\prime}(x)

Throughout this paper, we assume that
\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbb{F})\subset\mathbb{F}

Let

For each

We define a floating-point neural network

as

f(x;\theta)\coloneq\rho_{L}\circ\sigma\circ\rho_{L-1}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma\circ\rho_{2}\circ\sigma\circ\rho_{1}(x).

(1)

We call such an

For convenience, we also define variants of floating-point networks.

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\rho_{L}\circ\sigma\circ\cdots\circ\sigma\circ\rho_{1}\circ\sigma(x),

\displaystyle f_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma\circ\rho_{L}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma\circ\rho_{2}\circ\sigma\circ\rho_{1}(x),

\displaystyle f_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma\circ\rho_{L}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma\circ\rho_{1}\circ\sigma(x),

f_{1}
“a network starting with
\sigma
”,

“a network ending with
\sigma
”, and

For these variants, we count the number of layers as the number of affine transformations they contain (i.e., all

,

,

For two

\displaystyle(g_{1}\#g_{2})(x;\tilde{\theta})=\tilde{\rho}_{L}\circ\sigma\circ\cdots\circ\sigma\circ\tilde{\rho}_{1}(x)

\tilde{\rho}_{l}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1l}&0\\
0&A_{2l}\end{bmatrix}\otimes x\oplus(b_{1l}||b_{2l})

2.4
Automatic Differentiation

To derive the gradients of floating-point networks computed by the (reverse-mode) automatic differentiation algorithm, we first define the derivatives of two types of
primitive functions

\rho:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}^{d_{1}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}^{d_{2}}

\mathcal{D}_{\rho,x}(g)\coloneq g\otimes A~~\text{and}~~\mathcal{D}_{\sigma,x}(g)\coloneq g\otimes\sigma^{\prime}(x).

Here,

can be viewed as the gradient of

.
Likewise,

These notations are useful when defining the gradient of a composite function: for

and

By using this definition, we can characterize the gradient (computed by automatic differentiation) of a neural network defined in

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{f,x}(g)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{\rho_{1},x}\circ\mathcal{D}_{\sigma,y_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{D}_{\sigma,y_{L-1}}\circ\mathcal{D}_{\rho_{L},z_{L-1}}(g)

\displaystyle=g\otimes A_{L}\otimes{\sigma^{\prime}}(y_{L-1})\otimes\cdots\otimes{\sigma^{\prime}}(y_{1})\otimes A_{1}

where

and

.
We note that the operations in the second line

computed in a left-associative way, unlike in the exact arithmetic case.
Note that if

.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we show that floating-point neural networks can represent arbitrary function values and gradients computed via automatic differentiation.
We further extend this result to the case where the target gradient is a function of
\phi_{x}^{\prime}
, and
\phi_{x}
denotes the function composed with the network.
Our results show the discrepancy between exact and floating-point arithmetic: the target gradient should be proportional to the input gradient under exact arithmetic, but this is not the case under floating-point arithmetic.
We believe our results help in understanding the properties of real implementations of neural networks.

Impact Statement

This paper analyzes the representation power of floating-point neural networks. We could not find any notable potential societal consequences of our work.

\sigma
such that
f(x_{i})=y_{i}

for all
i\in[n]
.

The following result is a powerful tool for characterizing the representational power of sequential additions.

Lemma A.3

(Lemma 4.6 of
11
)
.

Let
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
and suppose that
\sigma
satisfies
1
.
Then, for any
y\in[-2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}},2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}})_{\mathbb{F}}
and
x_{1},x_{2}\in[-2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}},2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}]_{\mathbb{F}}
such that
x_{2}-x_{1}\in(0,2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}]_{\mathbb{F}}
,
it holds that

(-2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}},y,y^{+},2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}){\mathrel{\to}^{\sigma}}{}(x_{1},x_{1},x_{2},x_{2}).

Note that Condition 1 in
11
is replaced here by
1
. Since any activation function satisfying
1
also satisfies Condition 1 of
11
, this replacement is valid.

Corollary A.4
.

Let
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}\to{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}
and suppose that
\sigma
satisfies
1
.
Then, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{F}
,

(x){\mathrel{\to}^{\sigma}}{}(y).

Proof.

By
Lemma

A.3
, we get the desired result for
|x|,|y|\leq 2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}
. Therefore, it is sufficient to prove that

(x){\mathrel{\to}^{\sigma}}{}\left({2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}}\right),\text{ and }\left({2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}}\right){\mathrel{\to}^{\sigma}}{}(y),

for
|x|,|y|>2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}
.

First suppose
x,y>2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}
.
By the second item of
1
, we have

[\mathfrak{e}_{\min}-p,\mathfrak{e}_{\max}-p]_{\mathbb{Z}}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\nu}[\mathfrak{e}_{\sigma(\gamma_{i})}+\mathfrak{e}_{\min},\mathfrak{e}_{\max}-\kappa+\min(\mathfrak{e}_{\sigma(\gamma_{i})},k_{i})]_{\mathbb{Z}}.

Hence there exists
\gamma_{i}
such that

\mathfrak{e}_{\max}-p\leq\mathfrak{e}_{\max}-\kappa+\min(\mathfrak{e}_{\sigma(\gamma_{i})},k_{i})
leading to
\min(\mathfrak{e}_{\sigma(\gamma_{i})},k_{i})\geq-p
. and
2^{-p}\leq|\sigma(\gamma_{i})|
. Therefore, there exists
w\in\mathbb{F}
such that

\frac{1}{2}\times 2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}-p}<w\otimes\sigma(\gamma_{i})<\frac{3}{2}\times 2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}-p}.

Then, since
z\oplus\left((-w)\otimes\sigma(\gamma_{i})\right)=z^{-}
for
z>2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}
and
z\oplus\left(w\otimes\sigma(\gamma_{i})\right)=z^{+}
for
z\geq 2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}
, there exists
n_{x},n_{y}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle x\oplus\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n_{x}}(-w)\otimes\sigma(\gamma_{i})

\displaystyle=2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}},

\displaystyle 2^{\mathfrak{e}_{\max}}\oplus\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n_{y}}\left(w\otimes\sigma(\gamma_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=y.

Similar arguments apply to other cases.
This completes the proof.
∎

Definition A.5
.

Now let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},w\in\mathbb{F}

\alpha_{1}\otimes w,\;\alpha_{2}\otimes w\in\mathbb{F}.

Lemma B.4
.

Consider
-k\in[-p,0]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
For every
i\in[0,p)_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
g\in[2^{i}\omega,2^{i+1}\omega)
,
there exists
x\in\left(2^{k-1},2^{k}\right]_{\mathbb{F}}
such that

g\otimes x\leq 2^{i}\omega,

and

g^{+}\otimes x=(2^{i}+1)\omega.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\{1+\sum_{i=1}^{p}b_{i}2^{-i}:b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in\{0,1\}\}\cup\{2\}
.
Then, there exists
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
g=\alpha\times 2^{i}
,
g^{+}=\alpha^{\prime}\times 2^{i}
, and
\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha=2^{-i}
.
Let
d
be the largest number in
\mathcal{M}
such that
\alpha\times(d/2)\leq(1+2^{-i-1})
. Then, it is easy to observe that such
d
always exists and
1<d<2
.
Furthermore, since
\alpha<2
,
d<2
, and
d^{+}-d\leq 2^{-p}
, it holds that

(1+2^{-i-1})-\alpha\times(d/2)<\alpha\times(d^{+}/2)-\alpha\times(d/2)<2^{-p}.

This implies that
\alpha\times(d/2)>1+2^{-i-1}-2^{-p}
, and hence,

\alpha^{\prime}\times(d/2)=\alpha\times(d/2)+(d/2)\times 2^{-i}>\alpha\times(d/2)+2^{-i-1}>1+2^{-i}-2^{-p}

as
d>1
.
Let
x=d\times 2^{k-1}
.
Then,
g\otimes x\in[2^{i+k},(1+2^{-i-1})\times 2^{i+k}]_{\mathbb{F}}
and
g^{+}\otimes x\in((1+2^{-i-1})\times 2^{i+k},(1+2^{-i})\times 2^{i+k}]_{\mathbb{F}}
. This implies
g\otimes x\otimes 2^{-k}=2^{i}\omega
and
g^{+}\otimes x\otimes 2^{-k}=(1+2^{i})\times\omega
, and
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma B.5

(To

)
.
