Testican-1 activates Wnt/β-Catenin signaling to drive colorectal cancer progression
Authors: Yi Li, Chuying Huang, Chu Zhang, Lian Feng, Huiyun Lin, Jiannan Yao, Shu Luo, Jinsen Shi, Yuping Yang, Zhiyun Cao, Nathaniel Weygant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks second in cancer-related mortality worldwide. Its clinical intractability and chemoresistance pose significant challenges, often driven by cancer stem cells (CSCs). Wnt/β-catenin signaling is a lynchpin of CSC self-renewal, but targetable upstream drivers remain elusive. Using bioinformatics, we identified the proteoglycan SPOCK1 (encoding the protein Testican-1) as a Wnt-associated marker. High SPOCK1 gene expression predicted poor patient prognosis and reduced progression-free survival following first-line chemotherapy. In vitro assays demonstrated that Testican-1 levels modulated Wnt/β-catenin activity. Testican-1 downregulation inhibited β-catenin nuclear translocation and TCF/LEF-mediated transcription, leading to concurrent suppression of CRC proliferation and spheroid-forming potential. Conversely, Testican-1 overexpression promoted β-catenin nuclear translocation and TCF/LEF-mediated transcription, which enhanced CRC malignant properties. This Testican-1-driven pro-tumorigenic effect was reversed by the β-catenin/TCF/LEF inhibitor iCRT3, demonstrating Testican-1’s dependence on Wnt transcriptional activation. We validated this functional role in vivo using a Testican-1-overexpressing xenograft model and ex vivo using patient-derived organoids (PDOs), both confirming increased growth and β-catenin nuclear localization. Furthermore, data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry of Testican-1-overexpressing tumors showed enrichment in Wnt/β-catenin and pro-tumorigenic pathways, including c-Myc and oxidative phosphorylation. Notably, Testican-1 expression was associated with remodeling of the tumor microenvironment, characterized by desmoplastic features. In summary, our findings establish a novel Testican-1/Wnt/β-catenin axis driving CRC tumorigenesis and progression, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target to overcome Wnt-mediated progression and resistance.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the most common gastrointestinal tumor type. Among all cancers, CRC ranks third in incidence and second in mortality [1]. Overall treatment efficacy has improved due to the use of multiple modalities, including surgical intervention, cytotoxic agents, radiation, targeted therapies, and immunotherapies. However, the diverse and often atypical clinical manifestations of CRC result in many patients being diagnosed at advanced stages, posing significant challenges for clinical treatment [2]. Approximately 35% of CRCs are metastatic at diagnosis, and 50% of non-metastatic advanced-stage patients may also develop metastasis [3]. Moreover, the considerable heterogeneity of CRC complicates the selection of treatment strategies [4]. Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are a key source of genomic and cellular heterogeneity in CRC due to their capacity for self-renewal and multipotent differentiation. These properties lead to variant cell lineages with distinct phenotypes and resistance characteristics that play a crucial role in CRC progression and drug resistance [5, 6].

To maintain homeostasis and barrier integrity, intestinal stem cells (ISCs) undergo continuous self-renewal and lineage commitment, driving the dynamic renewal of the epithelial compartment [7]. However, when ISCs accumulate mutations and other harmful genetic alterations, they can transform into tumor-initiating cells, ultimately leading to CRC development [8]. Schepers et al. used an Lgr5Cre; ApcFl/fl; R26RConfetti lineage tracing mouse model to prove that Lgr5⁺ adenoma crypt base cells intermingle with Paneth cells and form a stem cell niche similar to normal crypts. From here, these cells self-renew, producing Lgr5+ cells and other adenoma cell types, and continuously fueling epithelial tumor growth [9]. Additional studies using Dclk1CreERT2/+ or Dclk1BAC-Cre lineage tracing and ablation models proved that this tuft cell marker also represents a specific, Lgr5-derived tumor-initiating cell in conditions of Apc loss with or without inflammatory injury [10, 11].

Recent findings have moved beyond the hierarchical model to redefine CSCs and stemness through the lens of cell state plasticity. Importantly, these studies implicate the tumor microenvironment (TME) in the dedifferentiation of non-stem cancer epithelia, induction of Wnt signaling, and the reemergence of CSCs after engineered ablation [12]. Shimokawa et al. used knock-in reporter CRC organoids to demonstrate that, while LGR5+ cells give rise to the complete tumor lineage, after their ablation, differentiated KRT20+ cells are able to revert to LGR5+ CSCs and support tumor regrowth [13]. Similarly, de Sousa e Melo et al. showed that Lgr5- proliferative cells rapidly replace depleted Lgr5+ populations [14]. Single-cell technologies have further enriched our knowledge of the importance of dynamic cell states. Single-cell RNA-Sequencing in a human-like CRC model identified Emp1+/Lgr5- “high relapse cells”, which give rise to Lgr5+ cells and drive metastasis [15]. More recently, Mzoughi et al. demonstrated that in conditions of Apc loss, Wnt signaling triggers a phenotypic spectrum where Lgr5+ cells give rise to Lgr5- progeny that are reprogrammed into an embryonic-like, “oncofetal” state. Moreover, the tumor is able to survive chemotherapy by dynamically switching towards this state [16]. Collectively, these findings suggest that while CRC initiation often depends on a Wnt/LGR5 axis, the progression of established tumors is sustained by dynamic dedifferentiation and plasticity programs.

SPARC/osteonectin, CWCV, and Kazal-like domains proteoglycan 1 (SPOCK1), encoding the protein Testican-1, is a highly conserved proteoglycan with diverse biological functions. It facilitates the binding of low-affinity calcium, which may underlie its role in cellular signal transduction and intercellular communication [17]. In gastric cancer, Testican-1 regulates epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through the transcription factor SNAI2 (Slug), leading to increased invasive and metastatic properties [18]. Similarly, in pancreatic cancer, it enhances metastasis via NF-κB-dependent EMT [19]. In glioma and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), Testican-1 induces Wnt/β-catenin pathway markers and promotes migration and proliferation [20, 21]. In CRC, Testican-1 has also been reported to promote growth via PI3K/Akt signaling [22], while silencing Testican-1 can inhibit proliferation and invasion [23]. Furthermore, high expression of Testican-1 is a prognostic marker in CRC and is associated with advanced clinical stage, T stage, and N stage [24]. In summary, Testican-1 is closely associated with pro-cancer functions in a variety of tumors, but its specific molecular function in CRC is yet to be fully understood.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is among several that regulate activation of ISCs [25]. Additionally, it is highly conserved in embryonic development and paramount to CSC self-renewal, stemness maintenance, differentiation capacity, drug resistance, and immune evasion [26]. In CRC, about 90% of patient tumors show Wnt/β-catenin signaling dysregulation, with most demonstrating APC or β-catenin alterations [27]. These alterations block β-catenin degradation, which, in turn, promotes its nuclear translocation and the activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, thereby initiating oncogenesis. Prior studies show that Testican-1 expression is elevated in CRC tissues compared with peritumoral normal specimens [22]. Herein, we demonstrate that Testican-1 promotes CRC progression both in vitro and in vivo by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, which drives β-catenin nuclear translocation, and potentiates TCF/LEF-dependent transcription. Collectively, the findings presented here reveal a novel Testican-1/Wnt/β-catenin axis driving CRC malignancy, and provide new insight into the molecular mechanisms underpinning CRC occurrence and progression.

Materials and methods

Details on cell lines and culturing conditions, plasmid transfection, TOPFlash reporter assay, CCK8 assay, colony formation assay, spheroid assay, nuclear fractionation, Western blot, immunofluorescence/immunohistochemistry/hematoxylin & eosin staining, lentivirus infection, tumor xenografts, RNA-Sequencing, proteomics analysis, and others can be found in the Supplementary Methods.

Colorectal cancer patient-derived organoid culture

Three CRC patient-derived organoids (PDOs), T1101, T0528, and T0714, were established with the approval of the Ethics Committee of Fuzhou Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital (AF/SC-08/03.3, approval date: 15 April 2021). PDOs were cultured in 50% reduced growth factor Matrigel domes with Complete IntestiCultTM Organoid Growth Medium (OGM; #6005, STEMCELL) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. When the PDOs matured, the Matrigel domes were washed with 1 ml D-PBS (#37350, STEMCELL). Organoids were then digested using Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (GCDR; #7174, STEMCELL). The dissociated PDOs were then scraped with a sterile pipette tip and centrifuged (290 × g, 5 min). To ensure dissociation, the collected PDOs were mechanically disrupted by pipetting 1 ml of DMEM/F12 (#36254, STEMCELL) supplemented with 1% BSA (#A8010, Solarbio). The PDO crypts were then counted and seeded at 2000 crypts/well into 50 µL of 50% Matrigel on preheated 24-well plates and incubated at 37 °C. After solidification, 500 μL of complete IntestiCultTM OGM was added to each well. OGM was refreshed every other day to maintain PDO growth.

Establishment of stable overexpression PDOs

Lentiviruses for oeCtrl and oeSPOCK1 were purchased from GeneChem (see Supplementary Methods). For infection, PDOs were digested with TrypLE (12604013, Thermo Scientific), passed through a 70 μm strainer, and seeded in 24-well plates (2 × 105 cells per well). Cells were cultured in OGM with 10 µM Y-27632 and infected with 1 × 107 TU of lentivirus overnight. On the following day, PDO cells were collected using TrypLE and seeded into 50% Matrigel at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. After Matrigel solidification, OGM supplemented with 10 µM Y-27632 and 1 µg/ml puromycin dihydrochloride (HY-B1743A, MCE) was added. Stable selection was confirmed by fluorescence microscopy and western blot analysis.

CRC PDO growth assay

Matured PDOs were digested to single cells using TrypLE. After digestion, the enzyme was neutralized with DMEM-F/12 supplemented with 1% BSA. Afterward, cells were centrifuged (290 × g for 5 min) and seeded at densities of 500 (T0528) or 800 (T1101) cells per well in 50% reduced growth factor Matrigel within 96-well plates. After solidification, 100 μL of complete IntestiCultTM OGM was added to each well with medium replacement every 2 days. Images were acquired with a multifunctional live cell imaging system (PerkinElmer, Muvicyte). When PDOs reached maturity, organoid viability was assessed by adding 100 μL of 3D-ATP solution (C0061S, Beyotime) mixed at a 1:1 ratio with culture medium. Following complete lysis, the Matrigel was scraped with a 10 μL pipette tip and dissolved (25 min, room temperature). Then the liquid from the wells was transferred into a white-bottomed 96-well plate, and luminescence was detected using a multifunctional plate reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Bioinformatics

For TCGA data, batch-effects normalized mRNA data for SPOCK1 expression, ssGSEA analysis of PARADIGM pathways (Wnt_Signaling_Network), and gene program data (GP4_MES/ECM and GP21_Anti-apoptosis/DNA-stability) were downloaded from the TCGA PANCAN datasets available from xenabrowser.net and analyzed in GraphPad Prism. For RNA-Seq of SPOCK1 overexpressing cell lines, the DESeq2 and fgsea R packages analysis were used to determine dysregulated genes and pathways. RNA-Seq counts are deposited at NCBI’s Gene Expression Omnibus (accession no. GSE309532).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Parametric comparisons used the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test or two-way ANOVA. Non-parametric comparisons used the Mann–Whitney U test. Survival analyses were performed in R using the survival, coxphf, and survminer packages. Survival in relation to chemotherapy response was determined as previously described [28]. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Testican-1 is associated with Wnt signaling and poor outcomes in human CRC patients

Prior studies in NSCLC and glioma have linked Testican-1, encoded by the SPOCK1 gene, to Wnt signaling pathway activation [20, 21], and it was among the candidate markers identified using an intestinal stem cell signature-based screening procedure in our previous work [28]. Given the connection between ISCs and Wnt signaling, we compared Wnt signaling network activity in CRC patients from TCGA’s COAD dataset. Wnt activation was significantly increased in COAD tumors with high SPOCK1 expression (>75th percentile) compared with low or moderate expression (P < 0.0001; Fig. 1A). Notably, SPOCK1 expression was also strongly associated with ECM/Mesenchymal and pro-survival/anti-apoptosis gene programs in agreement with previous reports in CRC [23, 29] (Fig. S1A, B). To assess these findings at the protein level, we performed IHC on human CRC surgical specimens and a human CRC tissue microarray and found that Testican-1 was strongly expressed in the CRC epithelium compared with matched paracancer tissue (Fig. 1B, Fig. S1C), significantly increased in T4 stage tumors (Fig. 1C), and strongly correlated with nuclear β-catenin (Fig. 1D, E). Survival analysis using the TCGA CRC dataset (COADREAD) showed that high expression of SPOCK1 predicts reductions in cancer-specific survival (CSS; HR:3.97, 95% C.I. [2.05–7.69], P < 0.0001), progression-free survival (PFS; HR:2.38, 95% C.I. [1.60–3.52], P < 0.0001), and disease-free survival (DFS; HR:2.39, 95% C.I. [1.00–5.70], P < 0.05) (Fig. 1G, Fig. S1D). To assess the potential influence of SPOCK1 on therapy response, we separated the CRC patients from TCGA’s COADREAD dataset with drug therapy information to stratify survival among SPOCK1 low and high patients receiving first-line chemotherapy or adjuvant therapies (Fig. 1F). Patients expressing high levels of SPOCK1 had significantly lower PFS after first-line chemotherapy (HR:2.21, 95% C.I. [1.11–4.40]) and significantly lower CSS with adjuvant therapy (HR:11.01, 95% C.I. [1.05–1487.69]) (Fig. 1G). Testican-1 regulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling and functional phenotypes in CRC cells

Consistent with this finding, western blot analysis demonstrated decreased expression of Wnt downstream targets CD133, LGR5, c-Myc, and Cyclin D1, as well as β-catenin, following SPOCK1KD in both cell lines (Fig. 2B). In order to assess the effect on β-catenin subcellular localization, we performed an immunofluorescence experiment following SPOCK1KD. To investigate whether Testican-1 activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, we transiently overexpressed Testican-1 (SPOCK1oe) in HCT116 and SW480 cells and performed TCF/LEF-based reporter assay (TOPFlash). SPOCK1oe significantly activated downstream TCF/LEF-mediated transcription compared to the control group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2E). Consistent with this finding, western blot analysis revealed that Testican-1 upregulation increased protein expression of CSC and Wnt pathway markers, including CD133, LGR5, c-Myc, β-catenin, and Cyclin D1 (Fig. 2F). Next, we performed IF staining for β-catenin, using the agonist Wnt-3a as a positive control. Imaging revealed that the intensity of β-catenin in the SPOCK1oe group was clearly stronger than the vector control group, and comparable to the Wnt-3a positive control group (Fig. 2G, Fig. S2A). Quantifications showed that nuclear β-catenin significantly increased by more than 80% in HCT116 and more than 60% in SW480 SPOCK1oe cells. Moreover, cytoplasmic β-catenin was significantly increased in HCT116 cells but showed no significant change in SW480 SPOCK1oe cells (Fig. 2H, I). Nuclear fractionation assays further confirmed increased nuclear β-catenin after SPOCK1oe in HCT116 and SW480 cell lines, in agreement with the IF assay (Fig. 2J, Fig. S2B). Consistent with these findings, SPOCK1KD inhibited the expression of nuclear β-catenin in HCT116 and SW480 cell lines (Fig. S2C). Since the SW480 cell line contains a truncated, but partially functional APC gene [30] and the HCT116 cell line harbors a heterozygous β-catenin mutation [31], we repeated TOPFlash and nuclear fractionation assays in the RKO cell line, which lacks these prevalent alterations. Similar to the mutant lines, SPOCK1oe promoted Wnt activation and nuclear β-catenin, while SPOCK1KD inhibited these processes (Fig. S2D–F). In combination, these findings demonstrate that Testican-1 regulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling in CRC cells independent of mutational status.

We next assessed the functional consequences of Testican-1 overexpression or knockdown in CRC cells. In colony formation experiments, the clonogenic capacity of the SPOCK1oe group was enhanced more than 1.5-fold compared to controls in HCT116 and SW480 cell lines (P < 0.01). In conditions of SPOCK1KD, colonies decreased by 40–50% on average compared to controls in both cell lines (P < 0.001). In 3D spheroid formation assays, we observed that the number of spheroids was increased by more than 50% in both SPOCK1oe CRC cell lines (P < 0.001), while they were decreased to a comparable extent by SPOCK1KD (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2K, L). Together, these findings suggest that, in CRC, Testican-1 activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling and positively regulates proliferation and 3D spheroid formation capacity.

Testican-1 promotes tumorigenesis downstream of β-catenin in CRC

To more clearly establish the role of Testican-1 in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, we used Wnt ligand secretion inhibitor LGK-974, β-catenin degrader MSAB, and the β-catenin-TCF/LEF-complex inhibitor iCRT3 to treat HCT116, SW480, and RKO CRC cells after SPOCK1oe. TOPFlash assays demonstrated that all three inhibitors significantly attenuated Testican-1-activated TCF/LEF transcription in all cell lines (Fig. 3A–C). Consistent with this finding, western blot analysis confirmed that inhibitor treatment strongly attenuated the Testican-1-mediated upregulation of direct, Wnt target proteins c-Myc and Cyclin D1 in all 3 cell lines. Moreover, SPOCK1oe leads to increased expression of Wnt/β-catenin/c-Myc downstream target and pluripotency factor SOX2 and decreased expression of the essential β-catenin destruction complex protein, AXIN2. Notably, SPOCK1oe induced increased expression of active (Ser-33/37/Thr-41 dephosphorylated) β-catenin in both β-catenin wild-type cell lines, but not in HCT116. To supplement our TOPFlash reporter and Western blot findings, we performed iCRT3 treatment on HCT116 and SW480 SPOCK1oe cells and performed IF, colony formation, and spheroid assays. Imaging analysis revealed that β-catenin was translocated after SPOCK1oe (P < 0.01), but there was no statistically significant change in localization or intensity after iCRT3 treatment (Fig. 3G, Fig. S2G, H). In colony formation assays, iCRT3 reduced the amount of SPOCK1oe induced colonies by nearly 3-fold in HCT116 (P < 0.001) and approximately 2.5-fold in SW480 (P < 0.0001) cells. Similarly, 3D spheroid assays revealed that iCRT3 attenuated Testican-1-driven spheroid formation by more than 30% in both CRC cell lines (HCT116: P < 0.001, SW480: P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3H). These findings demonstrate that Testican-1’s pro-tumorigenic effects rely, in part, on TCF/LEF-dependent transcription.

Testican-1 can act extracellularly to stimulate Wnt/β-catenin activation

Since Testican-1 is a secreted protein [32, 33] and its effects on Wnt/β-catenin signaling were reversible by Wnt ligand inhibitor LGK-974, we sought to determine if it could act in a paracrine fashion. In confirmation of Testican-1’s secreted status, ELISA assay of SPOCK1oe conditioned medium (CM) revealed plentiful baseline expression of Testican-1 in control HCT116, SW480, and RKO cells with concentrations ranging from approximately 0.35 to 2.5 ng/ml, with increased detection after overexpression in all three lines (Fig. S2I). TOPFlash reporter activation was significantly induced by SPOCK1oe CM and abrogated by iCRT3, MSAB, and LGK-974 in tested HCT116 (P < 0.001) and SW480 (P < 0.01) cell lines (Fig. 3I). To determine if this effect was present in Wnt wild-type-like conditions, we also tested CM stimulation in RKO cells with or without LGK-974, revealing an equivalent result (Fig. S2J).

Given our findings with SPOCK1oe CM, we sought to determine if the recombinant Testican-1 protein (rTestican-1) itself was capable of inducing Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation or whether it depended on co-factors secreted due to Testican-1 expression in parallel. After confirming rTestican-1 by Coomassie blue staining and immunoblotting (Fig. 3J) and testing concentrations ranging 0–500 ng/ml for their ability to induce TOPFlash reporter activity in the SW480 cell line (Fig. S2K), we performed the TOPFlash assay in HCT116 and SW480 cells with 50 ng/ml rTestican-1 with or without Wnt inhibitor treatment. In HCT116 cells, rTestican-1-mediated TOPFlash activity was completely reversed by iCRT3, MSAB, and LGK-974 (P < 0.0001). Comparatively, rTestican-1-induced TOPFlash activity was only significantly attenuated by iCRT3 in the SW480 cell line (Fig. 3K). These results suggest that Testican-1 can modulate Wnt/β-catenin signaling through distinct extracellular mechanisms. While the complete reversal of CM-mediated induction by all inhibitors suggests post-translational modification or secreted co-factor dependency, rTestican-1 appears to promote signaling via a mechanism that is uniquely sensitive to nuclear-level iCRT3 inhibition in APC-compromised backgrounds.

Testican-1 promotes CRC tumorigenesis in vivo

To study Testican-1’s functional effect in vivo, we established a stable SW480 cell line overexpressing Testican-1 (oeSPOCK1) or a control vector (oeCtrl), and confirmed its molecular and functional properties (Fig. S3A–E). RNA-Seq analysis of oeSPOCK1 cells revealed distinct transcriptomic profiles by principal component analysis (PCA) and confirmed SPOCK1 as the most significantly upregulated gene. Differentially expressed genes were consistent with a coordinated Wnt/Myc signaling program, characterized by the downregulation of Wnt antagonists (DKK4, LRP1/2) and the induction of Myc-responsive transcripts (NIBAN1, MANF). GSEA using the Hallmark database [34] revealed that in oeSPOCK1 cells, c-Myc targets (MYC_V1/2), along with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) pathways, were highly enriched (Fig. S3F, G). Given c-Myc’s identity as a Wnt-transcribed gene and pluripotency/stemness factor [24, 35, 36], these findings support Testican-1’s function in activating Wnt signaling and promoting malignancy. Moreover, they concur with prior findings linking Testican-1 to EMT in CRC [23, 37].

Subcutaneous xenografts were established in athymic nude mice using 1 × 106 cells per injection. After 10 days of measurements, oeSPOCK1 tumors showed a significant increase in volume compared to oeCtrl (P < 0.0001; Fig. 4A), with no significant changes in bodyweight (Fig. S3H). After excising the tumor, oeSPOCK1 tumors exhibited approximately two-fold increases in weight (P < 0.01) and volume (P < 0.001) relative to controls (Fig. 4B). IHC analysis confirmed strong Testican-1 overexpression in tumor epithelia of the oeSPOCK1 group. Importantly, β-catenin staining was more intense, frequent, and nuclear in oeSPOCK1 compared to oeCtrl tumors (Fig. 4C, D). To test whether CRC epithelia-derived Testican-1 can alter fibroblast structure in vitro, we performed a transwell co-culture assay using oeSPOCK1 SW480 and NIH-3T3 fibroblast cells, followed by immunofluorescence staining for F-actin (phalloidin) and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), and neural network-enabled quantification of fiber length (Fig. 5E). Collectively, these findings associate Testican-1 expression with features of desmoplasia and fibrosis, suggesting that it may play a role in the remodeling of the cancer microenvironment.

Therefore, our in vivo findings demonstrate that Testican-1 is associated with β-catenin activation and its downstream effector c-Myc, and that its expression can promote tumorigenesis in vivo. In addition to TME remodeling, these results also suggest the potential for a powerful metabolic and immunoregulatory role for Testican-1, which has not previously been explored in CRC.

Discussion

While Wnt/β-catenin signaling is a core driver of CRC, which drives cell proliferation and maintains the stemness of CSCs [39–42], its classical role in the CSC hierarchy is now understood within the context of cellular plasticity. Wnt-activated LGR5+ CSCs are essential for primary growth, yet tumors often bypass this dependency through dedifferentiation during progression or therapy resistance [15, 16]. Extensive research into Wnt/β-catenin mediators has identified key oncology targets, including LGR5, R-spondins, and PORCN [43–45]. However, clinical trials targeting these components have not yet yielded an approved drug candidate, potentially due to the ability of plastic cell states to circumvent traditional Wnt-dependency. This underscores the need to identify novel regulators, such as Testican-1, that may support the signaling programs necessary for tumor regeneration.

Our current findings identify Testican-1 as a Wnt/β-catenin signaling mediator in CRC for the first time. Prior research on Testican-1 in various tumors, including CRC, has primarily focused on its close association with EMT [19, 21, 46, 47]. Additional proposed molecular mechanisms for Testican-1-mediated tumor development and progression include PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation [22, 47]. Our study confirms Testican-1 overexpression in CRC compared to paired normal specimens, as well as its association with chemotherapy resistance, in agreement with previous findings [29]. Furthermore, we found that mesenchymal/ECM gene signatures are associated with SPOCK1 gene expression in TCGA’s COAD dataset (Fig. S1A), which is consistent with Testican-1’s role in EMT [22].

We identified Testican-1 as a candidate associated with the LGR5 + ISC signature in CRC based on a screening analysis in our previous study [28]. Since this signature is highly dependent on Wnt/β-catenin, we assessed Testican-1’s relationship with this key signaling pathway. Prior research in NSCLC showed that β-catenin, c-Myc, and Cyclin D1 expression were decreased after Testican-1 silencing, and cell proliferation was reduced [20]. Similarly, in glioma, silencing Testican-1 also decreased β-catenin, c-Myc, and Cyclin D1 expression, as well as inhibiting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [21]. Our experiments show that Testican-1 is a positive regulator of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in CRC, and that it promotes tumorigenesis downstream of β-catenin. Knockdown of Testican-1 expression via shRNA reduces Wnt pathway activity, as determined by the TopFlash reporter assay, Western blot, and immunofluorescence staining. Conversely, overexpression of Testican-1 activates the pathway. Further mechanistic studies revealed that Testican-1 drives Wnt signaling pathway activation by promoting the nuclear translocation of β-catenin, which we confirmed in cells, organoids, and tumor xenografts.

Previous studies unequivocally demonstrate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway as central to ISC-mediated homeostasis [41, 42]. When ISCs accumulate mutations, they may transition into tumor-initiating cells that fuel CRC progression [9]. Wnt signaling is also a significant factor in the regulation of CSC self-renewal [48–50]. Notably, the CSC marker LGR5 is a central receptor in R-spondin-mediated Wnt signaling amplification, and markers such as LGR5, CD133, SOX2, and c-Myc are established downstream targets of TCF/LEF signaling [6, 36, 41, 51]. This regulation is crucial for maintaining the stemness, proliferation, and survival of CSCs [6, 36, 43]. Our study evaluated whether Testican-1-mediated Wnt activation correlates with an enrichment of CSC markers and associated 3D growth and regenerative capacity.

To our knowledge, only two prior studies have investigated Testican-1 in this context. In breast cancer, Testican-1 silencing inhibits spheroid formation and impairs orthotopic tumor growth [52]. In NSCLC, silencing Testican-1 reduces cell proliferation and spheroid formation [53]. Here, we observed that Testican-1 overexpression drives the expression of LGR5 alongside CD133, SOX2, and c-Myc. The functional consequence of this molecular shift is a significant increase in clonogenic capacity, as evidenced by our 3D spheroid and PDO growth assays. While the 3D growth in these models represents a functional surrogate for stem-like properties rather than a definitive proof of asymmetric division capacity, the concordance between the marker data, unbiased proteomics (e.g., IGFBP2, LRIG1), and the pharmacological reversal with iCRT3 provides a consistent narrative of Testican-1-mediated promotion of a stem-like phenotype.

A compelling question raised by these data is whether Testican-1 may directly or indirectly dictate hallmarks of true CSC maintenance, such as asymmetric division. While our study focuses on the bulk population impact of Testican-1 signaling, the observed increase in spheroid and organoid growth may suggest a shift in the self-renewal program. However, given overlapping Wnt-mediated properties of proliferation and stemness, it will be necessary to elucidate the relative contribution of Testican-1 to proliferative expansion versus self-renewal. To fully resolve the single-cell kinetics and potential dedifferentiation programs driven by Testican-1, future studies utilizing label-retention assays or real-time lineage tracing in PDOs will be essential.

Among our findings, Testican-1’s strong association with Wnt-transcribed transcription factor c-Myc is notable. In cell models, silencing and overexpression of Testican-1 can respectively decrease and increase c-Myc protein expression, while Wnt inhibitor treatment can attenuate Testican-1-mediated c-Myc protein expression. In CRC PDO models, Testican-1 overexpression was also able to increase c-Myc protein expression. Furthermore, using Testican-1-overexpressing cells and xenografted tumors, we performed RNA-seq analysis and LC/MS proteomics, respectively. Analysis of the Hallmark Database revealed c-Myc targets as strongly enriched. Combined, these results suggest that c-Myc is induced downstream of the Testican-1/Wnt/β-catenin axis.

Interestingly, our proteomics results imply that Testican-1 has a powerful metabolic role in CRC. Hypoxia in the TME is known to cause a shift from OXPHOS to aerobic glycolysis [54], thus enhancing glucose transport in tumor cells [55]. A prior study identified Testican-1 as a target of miR-135B. Treatment of human CRC PDOs with miR-135b mimic suppressed SPOCK1 promoter reporter activity, Testican-1 expression, glucose uptake, and lactate secretion [56]. H&E staining of tumor-associated stroma in our xenograft model revealed strong eosin staining around Testican-1-overexpressing tumors compared to controls, consistent with a desmoplastic/fibrotic TME. Proteomic analysis of the tumor epithelia revealed activation of OXPHOS and tumor growth pathways, alongside canonical WNT signaling. Proteomics analysis of the stroma revealed increased hypoxia, EMT, glycogen and lactate metabolism, and myofibroblast characteristics. Combined, these findings suggest that Testican-1 in tumor epithelia may induce EMT in the TME, fueling desmoplasia/fibrosis and hypoxia.

While our data establish Testican-1 as a driver of β-catenin nuclear translocation and associated transcriptional activity, future studies will need to delineate its effect on β-catenin protein stability versus nuclear trafficking. Furthermore, our data in genetically diverse CRC models demonstrate that Testican-1 can activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling regardless of APC or β-catenin mutational status. Pharmacological inhibition across multiple signaling locales, from Wnt ligand secretion (LGK-974) to transcriptional activation (iCRT3), confirms that Testican-1 acts as an upstream modulator of β-catenin nuclear accumulation. Interestingly, while the Wnt-inducing activity of Testican-1-conditioned medium is reversed by all three inhibitors, recombinant Testican-1 exhibits selective sensitivity to iCRT3 in APC-mutant SW480 cells, suggesting that Testican-1 activity may be modulated by post-translational modifications or the recruitment of extracellular co-factors. Moreover, transwell co-culture of oeSPOCK1 SW480 cells with NIH-3T3 fibroblasts promoted stress fiber elongation, consistent with our proteomics data supporting a paracrine role for Testican-1 in myofibroblast-like activation in the TME. These paracrine functions are perhaps the ripest area for future Testican-1 research in CRC. Additionally, untangling potential interactions between Testican-1, Wnt/β-catenin, and EMT may clarify the role of Testican-1 in CRC chemoresistance and among its consensus molecular subtypes. Overall, our findings provide new insight into the tumorigenic mechanisms of Testican-1 and reveal a potentially targetable interface for preclinical therapeutic development.
