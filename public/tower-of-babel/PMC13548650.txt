Caregiver guilt as an underrecognised burden in family cancer caregiving: a scoping review and implications for palliative care support
Authors: Han Xu, Xiaojuan Han, Wenxiang Fan, Yunhuan Sun, Kaixia Gao, Xiaoxia Liu, Xiaoyan Liu, Yawen Li, Yuqi Zhang, Wangting Du, Wenying Li, Zhe Zhou, Linping Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFamily caregivers play a central role in cancer and palliative care but often experience substantial emotional burden. Caregiver guilt has increasingly been recognised as clinically relevant; however, existing research has largely examined guilt as a secondary component of broader psychosocial outcomes. Evidence regarding how caregiver guilt is conceptualised, measured, and addressed across cultural contexts remains fragmented. This scoping review aimed to synthesise current evidence on caregiver guilt among family caregivers of patients with cancer and to examine its conceptualisation, measurement, and implications for palliative care.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following the Arksey and O’Malley framework and reported in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Thirteen English and Chinese databases were searched from database inception to 20 October 2025. Qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies examining guilt as a primary or secondary outcome among family caregivers of patients with cancer were included. Data were synthesised narratively to identify key themes across studies.ResultsTwenty-seven studies were included. The studies used qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods designs and were conducted across diverse cultural and care contexts. Caregiver guilt was reported in relation to perceived inadequacy in caregiving, difficult care-related decisions, self-care, family and relational responsibilities, emotional suppression, and uncertainty around prognosis or end-of-life care. Measurement approaches were heterogeneous, including standardised scales, brief guilt items, study-specific questionnaires, and qualitative methods. Six studies were conducted in direct palliative, hospice, end-of-life, or bereavement contexts, and five addressed advanced, incurable, prognosis-related, or otherwise palliative-relevant cancer caregiving. Limited intervention-related evidence was identified, including one feasibility study that included caregiver guilt as an outcome and one single-case intervention component reported in a master’s thesis.ConclusionsCaregiver guilt is a recurring but inconsistently conceptualised experience in cancer caregiving. Based on the reported patterns across included studies, this review proposes an interpretive framework comprising two interrelated dimensions of caregiver guilt: action-oriented guilt and identity-oriented guilt. Future research should develop and test conceptually clear measures of caregiver guilt and evaluate interventions that address guilt-related distress across palliative and broader cancer caregiving contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-026-02203-z.

Background

Family caregivers, including unpaid relatives, partners, friends, or other close persons who provide emotional, practical, physical, or decision-making support, play an indispensable role in cancer care by improving patient outcomes and bolstering family well-being [1]. In line with recent recommendations on caregiving language, this review uses “family caregivers” or “caregivers” rather than “informal caregivers” where possible, to recognise the substantial and complex work performed by unpaid caregivers [2, 3]. This role is particularly prominent in collectivist cultures, where family-based caregiving is deeply embedded in social norms and ethical obligations [4, 5]. Family caregivers often face substantial emotional, physical, and financial burdens, which may compromise their well-being and capacity to provide effective care. Among these emotional challenges, guilt has emerged as a clinically relevant yet comparatively underexplored phenomenon [6]. Caregiver guilt is defined as a self-critical emotional response arising from caregivers’ perceptions that they have failed to meet their own or others’ expectations regarding patient care [7, 8].

The experience and expression of caregiver guilt are strongly shaped by cultural norms. In Western individualistic societies, guilt is often associated with perceived personal inadequacy, reflecting the belief that one has not done enough relative to personal standards or capabilities [9]. By contrast, in collectivist cultures influenced by Confucian values, such as China, Korea, and Japan, caregiver guilt may be more closely linked to family roles and moral obligations. In these contexts, guilt may arise not only from specific caregiving actions or omissions, but also from perceived failure to fulfil responsibilities associated with one’s role as a spouse, child, or parent [10]. Filial piety (孝, xiào) refers to a Confucian-informed moral expectation that adult children should respect, support, and care for their parents, often placing family obligation above individual preference. Together with values such as family harmony and self-sacrifice for the collective good, filial responsibility may reinforce role-related guilt when caregivers feel unable to provide sufficient care [11, 14].

China provides a salient context for examining these dynamics. With a population exceeding 1.4 billion and a rapidly ageing demographic, family caregivers bear a substantial proportion of the cancer care burden [12]. National health policies, including the “Healthy China 2030” initiative, have increasingly emphasised the importance of caregiver well-being [13]. At the same time, traditional Confucian values continue to shape caregiving expectations, creating a complex intersection between contemporary healthcare delivery and long-standing cultural norms [14]. Within this context, family caregivers may feel responsible not only for meeting caregiving demands after a cancer diagnosis, but also for the perceived origins of the illness itself. Caregivers frequently report self-blame related to not recognising early symptoms, not encouraging timely medical examinations, or not intervening in unhealthy behaviours. Rather than attributing cancer to biological, environmental, or stochastic factors, caregivers may internalise responsibility for the illness, perceiving it as a consequence of insufficient attentiveness or care. This illness-attribution pattern may intensify self-blame and moral guilt, shaping caregivers’ emotional responses and caregiving behaviours throughout the illness trajectory [15]. One practical manifestation of these cultural expectations is the difficulty of implementing “no-caregiver” hospital policies (wards without family caregivers) in China [16]. Many families perceive the absence of direct, hands-on caregiving as a moral failure, even when professional care is available [17]. Caregivers may experience intense guilt and fear social or moral censure if they do not fulfil these perceived responsibilities [18]. Such expectations can amplify caregiver distress and complicate the adoption of hospital models that rely primarily on healthcare professionals for comprehensive inpatient care [14, 19].

Despite growing recognition of caregiver guilt as clinically relevant, the existing literature remains diverse in scope, methodology, and conceptual focus. Previous reviews have primarily examined caregiver burden, anxiety, or depression [20], with caregiver guilt often embedded within broader psychosocial outcomes rather than examined as a central construct [21]. In addition, the ways in which caregiver guilt is conceptualised, measured, and addressed across different cultural contexts (particularly in non-Western settings) have not yet been comprehensively mapped [22]. A scoping review is therefore warranted to systematically identify and synthesise the range of existing evidence, clarify key concepts, and describe measurement approaches and intervention strategies related to caregiver guilt in oncology caregiving.

Although caregiver burden has been widely studied in palliative care, caregiver guilt has received comparatively limited attention as a distinct emotional dimension requiring targeted support. Understanding how guilt emerges, how it is shaped by cultural contexts, and how it may influence caregivers’ help-seeking behaviour is essential for developing effective caregiver support strategies. Mapping the current evidence on caregiver guilt may therefore help inform palliative care services seeking to better recognise and address caregivers’ emotional needs.

By situating caregiver guilt within the broader spectrum of emotional, social, and cultural challenges in cancer caregiving, this scoping review aims to inform the development of culturally sensitive, caregiver-centred support strategies, ultimately contributing to more equitable, effective, and family-integrated palliative care worldwide.

Methods

Study design

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [23]. The review was reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [24]. A scoping review was chosen because the literature on caregiver guilt is conceptually heterogeneous and includes diverse qualitative and quantitative methodologies.

Protocol registration

The review protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/KVHYZ). The PRISMA-ScR checklist is provided in Additional File 2.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were developed by the research team using the Participants, Concept, and Context (PCC) framework [25].

Participants

This review considered studies involving family caregivers of adult or paediatric patients with cancer, including unpaid relatives, partners, friends, or other close persons providing emotional, practical, physical, or decision-making support. Studies using terms such as “informal caregiver,” “unpaid caregiver,” “family caregiver,” “care partner,” or “carer” were considered eligible if the caregiving role was unpaid and based primarily on a personal relationship with the patient. No restrictions were applied regarding caregivers’ age, sex, ethnicity, or relationship to the patient.

Concept

Studies were included if they addressed guilt as a primary or secondary outcome, or explored guilt experiences qualitatively. Eligible studies had to report on the conceptualization, measurement, associated factors, or interventions related to caregiver guilt.

Context

Studies conducted in any care setting (e.g., hospital, community, home-based care) and cultural context were eligible, with a focus on unpaid family caregiving across the cancer care trajectory, including palliative and end-of-life care.

Types of sources: This review included primary empirical studies with qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods designs. Dissertations or theses indexed in the searched databases were eligible if they reported original empirical data and met the PCC criteria. Review papers, conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, protocols, and non-English or non-Chinese language publications were excluded. Reviews were excluded because this scoping review focused on primary empirical studies; however, reference lists of relevant reviews were screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across thirteen English and Chinese electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, MEDLINE, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, APA PsycNET, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, the VIP Database, SinoMed, and the Chinese Medical Journal Full-text Database. Searches were conducted between 17 and 20 October 2025, covering records from database inception to 20 October 2025, with no restrictions on publication year. Search strategies were developed in consultation with experienced research librarians and combined controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., MeSH and Emtree) with free-text keywords related to cancer, informal caregivers, and guilt. The term “informal caregiver” was retained in the search strategy because it remains widely used in database indexing and empirical cancer caregiving literature. In the narrative text, however, we use “family caregiver” or “caregiver” where possible to better reflect the substantial and complex work performed by unpaid caregivers. The strategy was adapted for each database according to its indexing system and search interface. The complete search strategies for all databases are provided in Additional File 1. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were also screened to identify additional eligible records. A dedicated grey literature search was not conducted. However, dissertations or theses indexed in the searched databases were considered eligible if they met the PCC criteria and reported original empirical data. All records were imported into EndNote X9 for reference management, duplicate removal, and screening.

Study selection

After duplicate removal, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Records considered potentially eligible by either reviewer were retrieved for full-text assessment. The same reviewers independently assessed the full texts to determine final eligibility. Final inclusion decisions and reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were recorded using a structured Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer when necessary. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data were charted using a standardised Microsoft Excel extraction form developed by the research team. Two reviewers independently extracted key information from each included study, including author, year, country, publication type, study design, sample characteristics, care context, palliative or end-of-life relevance, conceptualisation and measurement of caregiver guilt, associated factors, and reported interventions or support strategies. Publication type was extracted to distinguish peer-reviewed journal articles from database-indexed grey literature, such as dissertations or theses. The extraction form was piloted on a small number of studies and refined before full extraction. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Extracted data were synthesised narratively to summarise patterns across studies. Findings were organised around caregiver guilt as a reported experience, measurement approaches, associated factors, intervention-related evidence, and palliative, hospice, end-of-life, or bereavement relevance [26].

Quality appraisal

Consistent with the scoping review methodology, no formal quality appraisal of the included studies was undertaken. The objective of this review was to map the breadth and characteristics of the available evidence rather than to evaluate the effectiveness or methodological quality of individual studies. Accordingly, the findings are presented as a descriptive map of the evidence rather than as estimates of effect.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

The systematic search of thirteen Chinese and English electronic databases yielded a total of 1,253 records. After removing 187 duplicate records, 1,066 articles remained for title and abstract screening. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria, excluding 970 records. The remaining 96 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 71 were excluded for the following reasons: 32 did not focus on caregiver guilt as a primary or secondary outcome; 18 were not focused on cancer caregivers; 12 were review articles, conference abstracts, or editorials; 5 were not available in English or Chinese; and 4 were duplicate publications not identified during initial deduplication. Manual screening of reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews identified two additional eligible articles that met the inclusion criteria. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of included studies

The 27 included studies used qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods designs. Based on the primary methodological approach reported in each publication, studies were classified as qualitative studies (n = 16), quantitative studies (n = 8), and mixed-methods studies (n = 3). Twenty-six studies were peer-reviewed journal articles, and one was a database-indexed master’s thesis reporting original empirical data. The studies were conducted across 12 countries: the United States (n = 6), Canada (n = 5), China (n = 4), South Korea (n = 3), Brazil (n = 2), and one study each from the United Kingdom, Denmark, Sweden, Morocco, Malaysia, Japan, and Australia. Participants were predominantly family caregivers, most commonly spouses or partners, adult children, parents, or other relatives of patients with cancer.

To clarify palliative care relevance, six studies were conducted in direct palliative, hospice, end-of-life, or bereavement contexts; five addressed advanced, incurable, prognosis-related, or otherwise palliative-relevant cancer caregiving; and the remaining studies focused on broader cancer caregiving contexts, including active treatment, post-treatment or survivorship, paediatric oncology, and supportive intervention settings. Caregiver guilt as a reported experience

Across the included studies, caregiver guilt among family caregivers of patients with cancer was reported in relation to multiple caregiving situations and psychosocial experiences. Reported sources of guilt included perceived inadequacy in caregiving, difficulty meeting patients’ needs, delayed recognition of symptoms, inability to relieve patient suffering, difficult care-related decisions, self-care, neglect of other family members, role conflicts, emotional suppression, and uncertainty around prognosis or end-of-life care. Quantitative studies most commonly reported caregiver guilt as a measured psychosocial outcome and examined its associations with caregiver burden, psychological distress, quality of life, self-efficacy, hope, family functioning, caregiving duration, patient dependency, and disease severity. Qualitative studies described guilt through caregivers’ narratives of caregiving responsibility, moral obligation, relational tension, self-blame, perceived failure, and difficulty seeking support.

Measurement of caregiver guilt

Across the 27 included studies, caregiver guilt was assessed using a diverse range of approaches, including standardised instruments, brief screening measures, study-specific questionnaires, and qualitative methods such as interviews and audio diaries (Table 2). These approaches captured multiple dimensions of guilt, including perceived inadequacy in care provision, role-related conflicts, self-care neglect, and culturally or contextually specific manifestations.

Measurement strategies varied substantially across studies. Quantitative studies more commonly relied on brief or multidimensional self-report scales to assess guilt as part of broader psychosocial distress, whereas qualitative studies explored guilt through narrative accounts of caregiving experiences. This variation suggests that caregiver guilt has not yet been measured in a conceptually consistent manner across cancer caregiving research. In particular, the diversity of tools and assessment methods indicates that existing measures may capture different dimensions of guilt, with some focusing on behavioural inadequacy and others reflecting relational, moral, or context-specific meanings. However, none of the included studies explicitly operationalised caregiver guilt according to the action-oriented and identity-oriented distinction proposed in this review. This distinction should therefore be understood as a review-derived interpretive framework rather than as a classification already validated by existing measurement instruments.

Factors associated with caregiver guilt

Across the included studies, caregiver guilt was reported alongside individual, relational, and contextual factors. At the individual level, higher guilt was observed among younger caregivers, those juggling multiple roles, and those with lower perceived caregiving competence. Guilt was also described alongside anxiety, depressive symptoms, and empathic involvement (No. 4, 6, 7, 10, 11, 15, 16, 23). Relational factors were identified in several studies. Spousal caregivers reported guilt linked to changes in intimacy, decision-making, and perceived failure to protect their partner. Parental caregivers described guilt associated with perceived responsibility for the illness and inability to alleviate their child’s distress. Communication difficulties with patients, family, and healthcare professionals were also noted (No. 3, 8, 9, 15, 19, 20, 22, 24, 27). Contextual and illness-related factors were reported across multiple studies. Greater disease severity, functional decline, and proximity to end-of-life were observed alongside elevated caregiver guilt. Limited support services, unclear role boundaries, and restrictive policies were also described as healthcare system factors contributing to caregiver guilt (No. 3, 6, 9, 12, 16, 21, 26).

Interventions and strategies addressing caregiver guilt

Two included sources reported intervention-related content concerning caregiver guilt. Study No. 7 evaluated the Male Transition Toolkit (MaTT), a self-administered online psychosocial support tool for male spouses of women with breast cancer. Participants in the intervention group accessed MaTT over a four-week period. The toolkit included sections on personal stories and roles, expected changes, frequently asked questions, resources, calendar functions, and health information. Caregiver guilt was measured alongside hope, quality of life, and self-efficacy at baseline and follow-up time points. The study reported that MaTT was feasible and acceptable to participants. Caregiver guilt was also analysed in relation to caregiver quality of life, with lower guilt associated with higher quality of life. Study No. 17, a database-indexed master’s thesis involving family caregivers of patients with advanced cancer, included a single-case intervention component based on virtual guilt theory. The intervention component focused on excessive empathic distress, self-responsibility attribution, negative self-evaluation, and cognitive reconstruction. The reported intervention evidence was limited to this single case and was described at the case level.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesised 27 studies examining caregiver guilt among family caregivers of patients with cancer. Across the included studies, caregiver guilt was reported in relation to perceived inadequacy in caregiving, difficult care-related decisions, self-care, family and relational responsibilities, emotional suppression, and uncertainty around prognosis or end-of-life care. These findings show that caregiver guilt has been examined across heterogeneous cancer caregiving contexts, but often without a consistent conceptual or measurement approach. Based on these reported patterns, this review proposes an interpretive framework comprising two interrelated dimensions of caregiver guilt, action-oriented guilt and identity-oriented guilt, to organise existing evidence and guide future research on measurement, intervention development, and palliative care support.

Two interrelated dimensions of caregiver guilt

To organise these reported patterns, this review proposes two interrelated dimensions of caregiver guilt: action-oriented guilt and identity-oriented guilt. This distinction was not an existing classification used or validated in the primary studies, but was developed through the narrative synthesis of reported findings. Studies contributing to this review-derived framework are summarised in Table 3, and the conceptual relationship between the two dimensions is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Fig. 2Review-derived conceptual model of action-oriented and identity-oriented caregiver guilt

Review-derived conceptual model of action-oriented and identity-oriented caregiver guilt

Action-oriented guilt refers to guilt linked to perceived inadequacy in caregiving behaviours, decisions, omissions, or care-related actions. This dimension was reflected in studies describing guilt about not doing enough for the patient, making difficult or perceived wrong care decisions, failing to recognise symptoms early, being unable to relieve patient suffering, leaving the patient alone, or not managing care demands adequately [7, 30, 36, 38, 40, 43, 48, 51]. Identity-oriented guilt refers to guilt linked to perceived failure to meet relational, moral, familial, cultural, or role-based expectations within caregiving roles. This dimension was reflected in studies describing guilt associated with being a spouse, parent, adult child, or family member, as well as guilt related to emotional suppression, competing social roles, family obligations, moral responsibility, self-sacrifice, or difficulty seeking support [36, 38, 42, 44, 46, 47, 49, 50, 52].

These dimensions should be understood as overlapping rather than mutually exclusive. Several studies described situations in which caregivers reported both perceived action failure and perceived role or identity failure, particularly in parental caregiving, spousal caregiving, competing family responsibilities, and end-of-life decision-making contexts [40, 42, 44, 51]. Although cultural patterning was observed, identity-oriented guilt was not limited to East Asian studies. Studies from Brazil, the United Kingdom, Denmark, Sweden, Malaysia, and Australia also described guilt in relation to moral expectations, relational roles, identity disruption, and emotion work [29, 44, 46, 47, 49, 50, 52].

Action-oriented guilt refers to guilt linked to perceived shortcomings in caregiving behaviours or decisions. Identity-oriented guilt refers to guilt linked to perceived failure to fulfil relational, moral, familial, cultural, or role-based expectations within caregiving roles. These dimensions may overlap across caregiving contexts and cultural settings. This model should be interpreted as a review-derived organising framework rather than as a validated classification.

Measurement implications

Caregiver guilt should be assessed as a distinct construct while also being examined alongside related caregiver outcomes. Several included studies measured or analysed guilt together with caregiver burden, psychological distress, quality of life, hope, self-efficacy, family functioning, coping, social support, caregiving competence, or patient dependency, suggesting that guilt is closely connected with broader caregiver well-being [7, 28, 31–33, 35, 40, 41, 43]. However, guilt should not be treated as interchangeable with burden, anxiety, or depression, because it reflects a specific self-evaluative and relational emotional experience that may add explanatory or clinical value beyond general distress measures [7, 28, 33].

Existing measures captured important aspects of caregiver guilt, including perceived caregiving inadequacy, guilt about leaving the patient alone, guilt about self-care, guilt about neglecting other family members, and guilt about failing to manage care demands [7, 31, 32, 35, 40–43, 52]. However, no included instrument explicitly operationalised the action-oriented and identity-oriented distinction proposed in this review. Further work is therefore needed to examine whether existing tools, such as the Caregiver Guilt Questionnaire, the Guilt about Parenting Scale, and brief guilt measures, adequately capture both behavioural and relational or role-based dimensions of guilt. New or adapted measures may also be needed for culturally specific guilt related to filial obligation, spousal commitment, parental responsibility, emotional suppression, self-care, and help-seeking [18, 36, 38, 44, 46, 47, 49, 53, 65, 66].

Guilt and support: a feedback loop

Across multiple studies, inadequate support systems were linked to elevated caregiver guilt [49], which in turn appeared to inhibit help-seeking [53, 55]. This pattern is consistent with findings from other caregiving populations, where the use of non-professional or family-based support has been associated with higher levels of guilt and emotional burden [55], suggesting that guilt may paradoxically increase when support is available but perceived as undeserved or inappropriate. Caregivers who felt guilty often hesitated to access support, perceiving themselves as undeserving or fearing moral censure [54, 56]. This may contribute to a self-reinforcing cycle, where support deficits amplify guilt and in turn limit caregivers’ engagement with potentially beneficial resources. These findings suggest a potential focus for future interventions aimed at addressing caregiver guilt and improving support engagement within palliative care contexts.

The silent experience of caregiver guilt in family caregiving

Qualitative studies, particularly in Asian contexts, highlighted caregivers’ tendency to suppress or conceal guilt. Caregivers reported feeling they had “no right” to experience distress, or that expressing guilt might burden the patient or disrupt family harmony [36, 44, 49]. This silent experience may lead to underrecognition of caregiver distress and delay timely support. The phenomenon reflects how cultural norms, for example the primacy of family harmony and relational understandings of the self, shape emotional expression among caregivers [56]. Recognising these “silent sufferers” as a priority population within palliative care may facilitate earlier identification of caregiver distress and inform the development of targeted support interventions [57–60].

Guilt trajectory across the illness course

While most included studies were cross-sectional, the findings indicate that caregiver guilt may follow a provisional trajectory across the illness course. At diagnosis, guilt often centres on attribution, specifically self-blame for missed early symptoms. During active treatment, guilt often relates to perceived competence and adequacy of care. In palliative phases, decision-related guilt intensifies around treatment withdrawal and advance care planning [61]. During bereavement, guilt may manifest as regret over unspoken concerns [62]. This provisional trajectory framework may inform the timing of interventions, such as attribution-focused support at diagnosis, skill-building during treatment, decision support at end-of-life, and bereavement follow-up addressing regret. Recognising these shifting patterns of guilt has direct implications for clinical practice, suggesting that support should be tailored to the specific challenges emerging at each stage of the illness journey [63, 64].

Culture and the meaning of caregiver guilt

Culture appeared to shape not only how caregiver guilt was expressed, but also how it was understood by caregivers. In studies from East Asian settings influenced by Confucian values, guilt was often described in relational terms, particularly as perceived failure to fulfil family roles, filial responsibilities, or moral obligations [18]. In contrast, studies from more individualistic contexts more often framed guilt in relation to personal inadequacy, caregiving competence, or failure to meet self-imposed standards of care [65]. This pattern suggests that caregiver guilt should not be understood only as an individual emotional response, but also as a relational and culturally embedded experience.

This cultural patterning has implications for both measurement and support. If guilt is shaped by local understandings of family responsibility, obligation, and moral care, then simply translating measures developed in Western settings may not fully capture guilt in non-Western contexts [65]. Culturally adapted versions of existing tools, such as the Caregiver Guilt Questionnaire, or new measures designed to assess guilt arising from filial obligations and family role expectations may therefore be needed [66]. Similarly, support strategies for caregivers experiencing identity-oriented guilt may need to address family roles, moral expectations, emotional suppression, and culturally shaped meanings of being a “good” caregiver. Narrative, family-based, peer-support, or culturally adapted meaning-centred approaches may be relevant, although their effectiveness for reducing caregiver guilt remains to be tested [67].

Interventions: current evidence and future directions

Evidence on interventions addressing caregiver guilt remains limited. This review identified one peer-reviewed feasibility study that included caregiver guilt as an outcome [32] and one database-indexed master’s thesis that reported a single-case intervention component [42]. The available evidence should therefore be interpreted as preliminary intervention-related evidence rather than as evidence of established intervention effectiveness.

Future intervention development should be aligned with the different ways caregiver guilt is experienced. Action-oriented guilt may be addressed through psychoeducation, caregiving skills training, communication support, and cognitive reframing of perceived caregiving failures [68]. Identity-oriented guilt may require approaches that attend more explicitly to relational roles, moral expectations, family obligations, and cultural meanings of care, such as narrative therapy, family-based counselling, peer support, or culturally adapted meaning-centred interventions [67, 68]. Future studies should use longitudinal and adequately powered designs to evaluate whether these approaches reduce guilt-related distress and improve caregiver outcomes, including burden, anxiety, depression, quality of life, help-seeking, and support use. Flexible or technology-facilitated approaches may also be useful for caregivers who are reluctant to seek face-to-face support because of guilt, stigma, or fear of burdening others [69].

Supporting caregivers experiencing guilt

Caregivers experiencing guilt may not readily disclose distress, particularly when guilt is linked to moral obligation, fear of burdening the patient, family role expectations, or self-judgement [36, 38, 44, 46, 47, 49]. Clinicians should therefore be attentive to indirect signs of guilt, such as reluctance to seek help, persistent self-blame, avoidance of self-care, emotional suppression, or difficulty discussing care-related decisions [36, 38, 42, 44, 60].

Supportive conversations should avoid reinforcing blame and should instead normalise guilt as a common response to demanding caregiving situations. Clinicians may help caregivers distinguish between realistic caregiving responsibilities and unrealistic self-blame, discuss acceptable limits of caregiving, and identify support that does not threaten caregivers’ sense of responsibility or family role. For caregivers whose guilt is shaped by cultural or family expectations, support may need to include family-based conversations, peer support, or culturally sensitive discussion of obligation, responsibility, and self-care [42, 44, 46, 47, 48, 67, 68].

Implications for palliative care practice and policy

These findings have implications for palliative care practice and policy, particularly because caregiver guilt was reported in direct palliative, hospice, end-of-life, and bereavement contexts, as well as in broader cancer caregiving settings with palliative relevance [35, 36, 38, 44, 51, 54]. At the clinical level, palliative care teams could consider incorporating attention to caregiver guilt into psychosocial assessment and communication at key transition points, such as referral to palliative care, treatment withdrawal, advance care planning, loss of patient function, terminal anorexia, and bereavement. Rather than relying only on general distress screening, clinicians may need to ask about self-blame, perceived caregiving failure, difficulty accepting support, guilt related to self-care, and competing family responsibilities.

The action-oriented and identity-oriented distinction may offer a provisional guide for clinical assessment, but it should not be treated as a fixed diagnostic classification. Action-oriented guilt may be explored through questions about specific caregiving behaviours, decisions, omissions, or perceived lack of competence. Identity-oriented guilt may require attention to relational roles, moral responsibility, family expectations, and cultural meanings of being a “good” caregiver. Given the relational nature of guilt, assessment and support may also need to consider the caregiver and patient as a unit of care [68].

At the service and policy level, integrating caregiver guilt assessment into community-based cancer care pathways, rather than confining it to specialist palliative settings, could extend reach and enable earlier support, aligning with a public-health approach to caregiver well-being [54]. Flexible support models, including family-based counselling, peer support, stepped psychosocial care, and moderated online communities, may improve access for caregivers who are reluctant to seek face-to-face help [70]. Attention to caregivers’ digital health literacy will be important to ensure equitable access to technology-facilitated support, particularly for caregivers who may prefer anonymous or flexible engagement [71]. Recognising caregivers as co-clients [60] and investing in culturally sensitive support systems may help strengthen family-centred palliative care [72]. Given the limited intervention evidence identified in this review, any implementation of guilt-focused assessment or support should be accompanied by evaluation of feasibility, acceptability, cultural appropriateness, and caregiver outcomes [32, 42].

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. Although this review searched multiple English and Chinese databases, a dedicated grey literature search was not conducted. One database-indexed master’s thesis was included because it met the PCC criteria and reported original empirical data, but other non-indexed grey literature may have been missed. The included studies were also heterogeneous in design, population, cancer type, disease stage, care context, and measurement approach, which limited direct comparison across studies. In addition, caregiver guilt was often reported as part of broader psychosocial distress rather than as a primary research focus. The action-oriented and identity-oriented distinction proposed in this review should therefore be interpreted as a synthesis-derived organising framework rather than as a validated classification. Finally, because intervention-related evidence was limited to one feasibility study and one single-case intervention component, no conclusions can be drawn regarding the effectiveness of guilt-focused interventions in cancer care.

Conclusion

This scoping review mapped current evidence on caregiver guilt among family caregivers of patients with cancer. Across 27 included studies, caregiver guilt was reported across diverse cancer caregiving contexts, including direct palliative, hospice, end-of-life, and bereavement settings, as well as broader treatment and survivorship contexts. The literature remains heterogeneous in definitions, measurement approaches, populations, and care contexts. Based on the synthesis of reported findings, this review proposes an interpretive framework comprising two interrelated dimensions of caregiver guilt: action-oriented guilt and identity-oriented guilt. These dimensions may overlap and should be interpreted as a review-derived organising framework rather than as a validated classification. Future research should clarify the conceptual boundaries of caregiver guilt, test whether these dimensions can be reliably measured, and examine whether guilt provides explanatory or clinical value beyond related constructs such as burden, anxiety, depression, and quality of life. Intervention evidence remains limited. Future studies should evaluate culturally sensitive and contextually appropriate approaches to identifying and supporting caregivers experiencing guilt in palliative and broader cancer care.
