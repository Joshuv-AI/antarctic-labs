Factors associated with breast self-examination and mammography screening among women attending primary healthcare centers in central Tunisia
Authors: Cyrine Bennasrallah, Yesmine Ben Youssef, Mariem Kacem, Amani Maatouk, Wafa Dhouib, Manel Ben Fredj, Ines Bouanène, Hela Abroug, Imen Zemni, Asma Belguith Sriha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer remains a major public health concern in Tunisia. Despite national efforts, significant gaps persist in women’s knowledge and uptake of early detection practices. Understanding the determinants of these behaviors is critical for improving screening coverage. This study aimed to identify factors associated with breast self-examination (BSE) and mammography screening among women attending primary healthcare centers in Monastir, Tunisia.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study in the governorate of Monastir from May to October 2023. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire specifically developed for the study objectives. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of BSE and mammography uptake. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS statistics version 21.ResultsAmong 439 women included, 80.6% reported awareness of BSE as an early detection method and 53.1% reported performing it monthly. In multivariate analysis, awareness of BSE (Adjusted odds ratio: AOR = 6.38; 95% CI: 3.41–11.90), having a family history of breast cancer (AOR = 2.03; 95% CI: 1.07–3.87) and older age (AOR = 1.042; 95% CI: 1.024–1.060) were independent associated factors with BSE practice. Among 192 women aged over 45 years, 35% had undergone mammography at least once. In multivariate analysis, the factors associated with mammography screening were BSE practice (AOR = 2.60; 95% CI: 1.22–5.55) and the knowledge of the recommended age for mammography initiation (AOR = 2.28; 95% CI: 1.17–4.45).ConclusionThe practice of BSE and especially mammography among participants remains relatively low. Targeted education and awareness-raising efforts are therefore essential to increase the use of breast cancer screening and early detection methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04623-8.

Background

Breast cancer is the predominant cancer among women all over the world [1]. Its global incidence is estimated at 2.1 million per year and this incidence is expected to increase to around 3.2 million new cases per year by 2050 [2]. In 2022, a total of 670 000 deaths globally were attributed to breast cancer and mortality rates are increasing in developing an developed countries [1, 3]. Breast cancer is also the most common cancer-affecting women in Tunisia with incidence rates varying across regions [4]. In 2023, the age-standardized incidence rate (ASR) of breast cancer was 55.4 per 100,000 people, and it is projected to more than double by 2040 [5]. Indeed, breast cancer is a major public health concern, necessitating effective programs for control and prevention [6]. In Tunisia, the national breast cancer screening program is opportunistic based on early detection through breast self-examination (BSE), clinical breast examination by a healthcare professional and mammography screening starting at the age of 45 for women without identified risk factors [7, 8].

Mammography continues to be the best screening modality and has demonstrated a decrease in breast cancer mortality by 25–31% in women between the ages of 50 and 69 years [9]. In spite of mammography benefits some studies demonstrated poor knowledge, attitudes and use among the target population in the developing world [10, 11]. Previous studies inspecting breast cancer screening barriers has revealed that mammography use are associated with many factors such as socioeconomic status, education level, and access to healthcare services [12, 13] .

BSE is an early detection method that empowers women to monitor their own health by regularly checking their breasts for any changes or abnormalities. Although BSE is not a substitute for, it plays a complementary role in promoting early detection, especially in low-resource settings where access to medical imaging may be limited. Despite its benefits, several studies have reported low levels of knowledge and practice of BSE among women in developing countries, often due to lack of education, cultural beliefs, or lack of knowledge about breast cancer [14]. Understanding factors associated with mammography screening practice is of a key importance to plan interventions for women in low resource settings. In this context, the aim of our study was to assess the prevalence of breast self-examination (BSE) and mammography screening practices and to identify factors associated with these practices among women attending primary healthcare centers in Monastir, Tunisia.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was carried out in the Monastir governorate from May to October 2023, including women who attended primary health care centers (PHCCs) for medical consultations or who came specifically to participate in breast cancer awareness campaigns organized by the Department of Epidemiology and Preventive Medicine of Monastir.

The governorate of Monastir is an urban area comprising 13 delegations. Our study is multicentric including four primary health care centers from four delegations (Moknine, Khnis, Sidi Ameur, Menzel Kamel).

Participants

The study included women aged 20 years and above, as BSE is recommended from this age in Tunisia. These women had to be present in the visited PHCCs at the time of the survey and willing to participate. Women with a personal history of breast cancer or who provided incomplete responses were excluded.

Data collection

Data was collected using an Arabic, interviewer-administered questionnaire including sociodemographic information’s and questions related to BSE and mammography screening practices and its barriers. A pilot test was conducted to assess the readability of the questionnaire. The final version was finalized after incorporating minor amendments based on participants’ feedback during the pilot testing phase.

The following formula: n = [(Zα/2) 2 × p x (1-p)]/i2 was used to calculate the required sample size. A proportion (p) of BSE practice of 36% [15], a precision (i) of 5%, a type one error (α) of 5% were considered. The minimum required sample size was 354. The sample was increased by 25% to account for nonresponse and recording errors. A total of 439 women were included. As for mammography screening, the minimum required sample size was 139, based on the assumption that the mammography screening utilization rate among women was 10%, the precision was 5%, and 95% confidence level [15]. The larger sample size calculated for BSE was then used to ensure adequate power for detecting differences in either outcome. A convenience sample was taken including patients attending PHCCs during breast cancer awareness campaigns. Four PHCCs were purposively selected based on their active participation in breast cancer awareness campaigns and their accessibility to the target population. Our study included eligible women attending the included PHCCs for either routine clinic visits or breast cancer awareness campaigns.

Study variables

The survey questionnaire included:

Outcome variable

Breast self-examination and mammography practices:

BSE practice was assessed by the following question: “Do you practice breast self-examination monthly?” With binary answers (yes/no).

Mammography screening practices: A single construct (i.e., Have you ever get a mammography screening? ) was used to assess the mammography use with binary responses (yes/no). Women were categorized into “screeners” if they had ever used mammogram screening and “never screeners” if not.

Independent variable

Socio-demographic informations: Socio-demographic informations were recorded during the survey including age, the number of children, marital status (unmarried/ married/Divorced/Widowed) and economic level (low/moderate/high). Economic level was assessed using self-reported perceptions of household income.

Educational level was also reported in four categories (illiterate/ primary/secondary and Higher education): To enhance standardization and comparability with other studies, these categories correspond approximately to International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED 2011) levels: Analphabet (ISCED 0), primary (ISCED 1–2), secondary (ISCED 3–4), and higher education (ISCED 5–8) [16].

Breast cancer and screening awareness

Breast cancer awareness was assessed using the questions: “Have you ever heard about breast cancer?” and “Have you heard about the symptoms of breast cancer?” (Responses: Yes/No).

Awareness of screening methods was explored through several items:

Breast self-examination (BSE) awareness was assessed with the question “Are you aware of BSE?” (Responses: Yes/No). Awareness of BSE periodicity was assessed using the question “Did you know that BSE should be performed monthly?” (Yes/No).

Mammography: The knowledge of mammography was evaluated using two questions: “Have you heard about mammography?” and “Did you know that mammography is recommended from the age of 45?” (Yes/No).

Barriers to mammography screening

Barriers to mammography screening were assessed using two items addressing potential obstacles: lack of access to radiology centers and lack of financial support.

Statistical analysis

In the descriptive analysis, categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and proportions, and continuous variables as means (with standard deviations) or medians (with interquartile range (IQR)) depending on their distribution. The association between categorical variables were compared using the chi-square statistical method. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test before applying parametric tests. Differences between groups for continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test when variables were normally distributed and the Mann-Whitney U test for variables that were not .

Univariate then multivariate analyses using the logistic binary regression were performed in order to identify the factors associated with BSE and mammography screening practices. Observations with missing data about variables that were used in regression models were deleted. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test served to check for the adequacy of the final model.

Variables with p-value ≤ 0.20 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model [17]. Adjusted ddds ratios (AOR) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. For all statistical analyses, differences were considered significant at p-value < 0.05. Data was analyzed by SPSS 21.0 software.

Results

Study population characteristics

A total of 439 women were included in the study, with a mean age of 43.7 ± 13.6 years-old (range: 20–74 years). The most common age was 32, and most of the women 56.3% (247/439) were under 45 years old. Among our study population, 76.9% (337/438) were married and 69.3% (293/423) have three children and less. A total of 44.4% (182/410) had a secondary education level and 73.1% (305/417) reported having a moderate economic level. In terms of breast cancer awareness, 91.4% (394/431) of respondents said they had heard of breast cancer and 14.8% (61/413) reported a family history of breast cancer.

Breast self-examination and its associated factors

Breast self-examination prevalence and barriers

A total of 80.6% (337/418) women reported being aware of BSE as a method for early detection of breast cancer and 50.8% (212/417) reported performing it every month. A total of 53% participants indicated that not having symptoms was the main barrier to self-examination, followed by a lack of information about BSE (35.3%), and fear of being diagnosed with breast cancer (28.7%) and embarrassment and shyness (11.6%).

Family history of breast cancer

Univariate analysis showed that age, marital status, education level, number of children, family history of breast cancer, BSE awareness and knowledge of its recommended frequency were significantly associated with BSE practice. Of the 439 participants, 192 aged over 45 are eligible for mammography screening. These people are included in the following analysis.

Mammography screening utilization and its barriers

Most of study participants 65% (115/177) were aware about mammography as a screening tool and only 45.4% (79/174) knew that the recommended age for mammogram screening is 45 years-old. In terms of participants’ breast cancer screening practices, only 35% (62/177) of our participants had ever had a mammogram. About 52.7% (n = 57) have addressed the lack of knowledge as the main barrier to practice mammography followed by the lack of economic support and the absence of radiology center nearby (37.9% and 19.4% respectively).

Discussion

Main results

According to our study, 53.1% of women reported performing BSE monthly. Multivariate analysis identified three independent predictors of BSE: awareness of BSE as a method for early breast cancer detection (AOR = 6.38; 95% CI: 3.41–11.90), having a family history of breast cancer (AOR = 2.03; 95% CI: 1.07–3.87), and older age (AOR = 1.042; 95% CI: 1.024–1.060). Mammography screening utilization was reported by 35% of participants. Factors independently associated with mammography use included practicing BSE (AOR = 2.60; 95% CI: 1.22–5.55) and knowledge of the recommended age for screening (AOR = 2.28; 95% CI: 1.17–4.45).

Interpretation

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women worldwide, and its incidence is constantly on the rise, both in developed and developing countries, where it is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage [18].

The majority of participants (80.6%) recognized BSE as a means of early detection, but only 53.1% practiced it every month. These results are similar to those observed in Sub-Saharan Africa [19]. However, in other studies carried out in Pakistan [20] and Vietnam [21] a lack of awareness of BSE was noted. In fact, regardless of how much women know about BSE, the importance of early detection, as a preventive measure should always be highlighted.

Furthermore, studying the main factors associated with BSE is crucial for a better understanding of women’s attitudes towards the early detection of breast cancer. The presence of a family history of breast cancer was an independent factor associated with BSE practice. This finding is consistent with a study in north-west Ethiopia, which found that the odds of BSE in women with a family. history of breast cancer were 6.5 times higher than in those without [22]. Indeed, a family history of breast cancer can have a significant psychological impact on individual’s well-being and practices.

Being aware of BSE as a mean of early detection was also a factor associated with BSE. This result is consistent with a similar study of female teachers in Ethiopia, which showed that the greater the total knowledge score, the greater the likelihood of performing BSE [23]. Indeed, the awareness of risks, early signs and importance of early detection motivates them to adopt BSE as a preventive behavior [24].

Moreover, age is considered in our study as a factor independently associated with BSE. This result is similar to that observed in a study carried out in Nigeria [25], which revealed that the practice of BSE increased with age, and that the majority of those performing BSE were aged between 40 and 49. This could be explained by the fact that at these stages of life, women pay more attention to their reproductive health, leading them to become more aware of the importance of breast health. Despite BSE being an easy, inexpensive and painless examination, there are many barriers to women’s involvement. In our study, three main barriers to BSE were identified: the majority of participants indicated that the absence of symptoms was the main barrier, followed by a lack of information, fear of being diagnosed with breast cancer, and embarrassment and shyness. These barriers are similar to those found in studies from Ethiopia [26] and Sub-Saharan Africa [27]. Training, support and awareness-raising can help women feel more comfortable and confident in practicing BSE.

Mammography is a vital breast cancer screening tool that saves lives and enable rapid and effective management. In our study the prevalence of mammography awareness was 65% lower than the 86% rate reported in Egypt [28] and higher than the 40.5% in Nigeria [29]. This could be attributed to disparities in study population. Surprisingly, only 35% of the participants reported having a mammogram screening. Our findings were lower than those reported in Saudi Arabia (40%) [28] and very lower than rates in Sweden and Denmark (92% and 79% respectively) [30]. The lack of knowledge was the most commonly identified barrier to mammography in our study in line with what was reported in previous studies [2, 31]. Women having a lack of knowledge about mammography were less likely to participate in mammography screening in univariate analysis but not multivariate analysis. However being aware of the recommended age increased the adherence to mammography. Indeed, limited knowledge can contribute to a negative attitude, reflecting the importance of education [32]. This finding highlights the importance of implementing community-based interventions on breast cancer awareness and screening strategies [33]. Indeed, education programs for female is recommended especially for those living in rural area to improve their knowledge about breast cancer and mammogram. In this context, primary health care facilities in Tunisia should play a key role in promoting behavioral changes to reduce risk factors, as well as through the implementation of screening practices for early detection of cancer. Lack of economic support was reported as a barrier to mammography screening by women but was not found to be an independent associated factor. However, in Tunisia, women have to pay for mammography, as it is not covered by medical insurance. Lack of reimbursement may affect breast cancer screening [32]. This suggests that the Tunisian government should consider extending health insurance coverage to include breast cancer screening, and future studies are needed to explore the economic feasibility. In addition, according to our findings, practicing BSE was significantly associated with higher rates of mammography use. Indeed, women who perceive a risk of breast cancer may be more likely to engage in breast cancer-related screening behaviors.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. First, the sample was drawn from a single region using a non-randomized method, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to all Tunisian women. Secondly, using a convenience sample of women attending PHCCs during breast cancer awareness campaigns may have resulted in the inclusion of women with a higher level of awareness, which could limit the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, the small number of women who reported having a mammography screening may have slightly increased the risk of overfitting in the multivariate analysis. Future research should adopt a multi-regional, population-based approach with probabilistic sampling to better identify determinants of mammography screening utilization and inform national public health strategies.

Conclusion

Despite high levels of knowledge about breast cancer screening methods, the prevalence of breast self-examination (BSE) and mammography remained low. Knowledge of BSE, a family history of breast cancer, and older age were significantly associated with BSE practice, while regular BSE and awareness of the recommended age for mammography were associated with mammography screening. These findings highlight the need for culturally and socially appropriate educational interventions that not only increase knowledge but also motivate women to engage in regular screening. Integrating such programs into community and primary healthcare settings, with reminders and follow-ups, may improve early detection and ultimately reduce breast cancer-related morbidity and mortality.
