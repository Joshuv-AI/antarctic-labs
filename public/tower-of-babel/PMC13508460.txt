The diagnostic performance of salivary gland shear wave elastography to differentiate Sjögren disease from type 2 diabetes mellitus with sicca: a cross-sectional controlled study
Authors: Erdem Ozkan, Abdulvahap Kahveci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundXerostomia is a shared clinical manifestation of both Sjögren disease (SjD) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM), but their underlying pathophysiology differs markedly. The differentiation between autoimmune glandular damage and metabolic dysfunction is of clinical significance. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of shear wave elastography (2D-SWE) for discriminating SjD from DM patients with sicca symptoms.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included three groups: 61 patients with SjD, 42 patients with type 2 DM presenting with sicca symptoms, and 43 age-matched healthy controls (HC). All participants underwent bilateral parotid and submandibular gland ultrasonography. Parenchymal inhomogeneity was graded using the Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) scoring system, and tissue stiffness (Young’s modulus, kPa) was quantified via 2D-SWE. Pairwise comparison between groups were conducted, and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to assess the performance of SWE in identifying SjD patients.ResultsThe SjD group demonstrated higher stiffness values in the bilateral parotid and submandibular glands compared with both the DM and control groups. Additionally, the OMERACT total scores for all salivary glands were elevated in patients with SjD compared with the DM and HC groups (p < 0.001 for both groups). In contrast, the DM group exhibited tissue stiffness and gray-scale ultrasound profiles that were comparable to those of the HC group (p > 0.0167 for all comparisons). The ROC analysis indicated that SWE possessed moderate discriminatory power for distinguishing SjD from DM (Area Under the Curve (AUC) range for glands: 0.598–0.628).ConclusionThe combined use of SWE and gray-scale assessment may serve as a multiparametric approach that could contribute to differentiating autoimmune sialadenitis in SjD patients from metabolic sialosis in DM patients presenting with dry mouth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02485-y.Keywords: Salivary gland, Shear wave elastography, Sjögren disease, Diabetes mellitus, Ultrasonography

Introduction

Xerostomia is a debilitating clinical manifestation that significantly impairs quality of life. While it is the hallmark feature of Sjögren disease (SjD), a chronic systemic autoimmune disorder characterized by lymphocytic infiltration and progressive glandular destruction [1, 2]. Xerostomia is also a common complication of metabolic disorders, particularly type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) [3]. Differentiating between these two etiologies in patients presenting with dry mouth is clinically critical, as their therapeutic management and long-term prognoses differ substantially.

The recent international consensus has shifted the nomenclature from “syndrome” to “disease” to better reflect the systemic and biologically unified nature of SjD [4]. The pathogenesis of SjD is now understood as a dynamic “epithelial-stromal-immune ecosystem” where epithelial cells actively license immune responses and stromal fibroblasts scaffold tertiary lymphoid structures [5, 6]. This complex microenvironment leads to structural inhomogeneity and increased tissue stiffness, which are distinct from those observed in other pathologies. On the other hand, DM-associated glandular dysfunction, often termed diabetic sialosis, involves acinar hypertrophy, adipose infiltration (steatosis), and microvascular alterations and lacks the aggressive lymphocytic destruction characteristic of autoimmune diseases [7, 8].

Salivary gland ultrasonography (SGUS) has emerged as a pivotal tool for assessing glandular structure [9]. In order to standardize SGUS assessment, the Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) ultrasound working group developed a consensus scoring system for parenchymal inhomogeneity, which has demonstrated high reliability [10, 11]. While SGUS features in SjD are well-defined, recent studies have begun to characterize the sonographic changes in diabetic patients, noting glandular enlargement and vascular alterations that differ from those observed in SjD [12, 13].

Shear wave elastography (SWE) further enhances diagnostic capability by quantifying tissue stiffness (Young’s modulus). Numerous studies have demonstrated that SWE values are elevated in SjD patients compared with healthy controls attributable to fibrosis [14, 15]. The utility of SWE has also been explored in other rheumatic conditions presenting with sicca, such as ankylosing spondylitis, highlighting its potential as a broad discriminator of glandular pathology [16]. However, a significant gap exists in the literature regarding the direct comparison between SjD and DM. Most existing studies compare diseased groups solely against healthy controls. To our knowledge, few comprehensive studies simultaneously evaluate SjD patients, DM patients with sicca symptoms, and healthy volunteers to determine whether SWE can reliably distinguish the underlying etiologies of xerostomia.

This study aims to bridge this gap by evaluating the diagnostic utility of 2D-SWE and the OMERACT gray-scale scoring system in a three-group comparative design. We investigated whether quantitative stiffness could serve as a discriminatory biomarker between autoimmune (SjD) and metabolic (DM) glandular impairment.

Material and methods

Participants, study design and clinical outcomes

The study was performed in a tertiary research and education hospital between January 2025 and November 2025. This cross-sectional study consisted of three groups: (1) patients with SjD [meeting the disease criteria [2016 American Colleges of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) Classification Criteria for pSS] [17], (2) age-matched type 2 DM patients with sicca symptoms (xerostomia and/or xerophthalmia) [fulfilled 2011 American Diabetes Association (ADA) Criteria for DM] [18], and (3) age matched healthy controls. Exclusion criteria included a history of head and neck ionizing radiation, hepatitis C infection, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, graft versus host disease, immunoglobulin G4-related disease. Also, patients with concomitant secondary rheumatic disease, malignant or pre-malignant disease of the major salivary glands, current use of drugs that might decrease salivary gland function, history of the salivary gland surgery, and concomitant SjD and type 2 DM were excluded from the study. The simple random method was used in the sampling process.

Demographic and clinical characteristics included age, body mass index (BMI), sex, tobacco use, disease and symptom duration, current symptoms (sicca symptoms, arthralgia etc.) and signs (sialomegaly, tongue hyperlobulation etc.), drugs and comorbidities. Laboratory measurements of the patients including rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), anti-Ro (SS-A) and anti-La (SS-B), the results of unstimulated whole saliva flow rate (ml/15 min), Schirmer’s test (mm/5 min), and minor labial gland biopsy (focus score) were recorded from the electronic health records. Disease activity in patients with SjD was measured using the EULAR Sjögren’s syndrome disease activity index (ESSDAI), and the EULAR Sjögren’s Syndrome Patient Reported Index (ESSPRI) [19, 20]. Also, the ESSPRI-dryness score was used for grade sicca symptoms in patients with type 2 DM.

The present study was formally approved by the Kastamonu University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Protocol number and date: 2024-KAEK-102 and 6 November 2024). During the study period, the World Medical Association Helsinki Declaration and Good Clinical Practices Guidelines followed. All participants were informed about the aim of the study and provided written informed consent.

Ultrasound and shear-wave elastography of the salivary glands

All ultrasonographic examinations were performed using a high-end ultrasound system (Resona R9, Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics, Shenzhen, China) equipped with a multifrequency linear array transducer (Model L15-3WU) and integrated Sound Touch Elastography (STE) software. The thyroid preset program was selected for all examinations, as it provides optimized image resolution and penetration depth appropriate for the evaluation of superficial glandular structures. To ensure methodological consistency, all assessments were performed by a single radiologist with 8 years of experience who was blinded to the participants’ clinical data and group assignments.

Participants were examined in a supine position with the neck hyperextended and the head slightly rotated to the contralateral side to optimize the acoustic window [11]. Bilateral parotid and submandibular glands were systematically evaluated. First, conventional B-mode ultrasonography was performed to assess morphological characteristics, including parenchymal echogenicity, homogeneity, and the presence of hypoechoic areas or hyperechogenic reflection. The structural changes were graded according to the OMERACT scoring system (grades 0–3) [11, 21]. According to this system, grade 0 indicates normal parenchyma; grade 1 indicates mild inhomogeneity without anechoic/hypoechoic areas; grade 2 indicates moderate inhomogeneity with focal anechoic/hypoechoic areas; and grade 3 indicates severe inhomogeneity with diffuse anechoic/hypoechoic areas occupying the entire gland, or a fibrotic gland [11]. A representative image of a normal parotid gland (OMERACT grade 0) is shown in Fig. 1A.

After the gray-scale assessment, 2-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE) was employed to quantify glandular stiffness. The elastographic mode was activated to display a color-coded map of tissue elasticity superimposed on the B-mode image, where stiffer tissues were coded in red and softer tissues coded in blue. The transducer was applied perpendicularly to the skin surface with a sufficient amount of coupling gel to ensure acoustic contact without manual compression, thus avoiding pre-compression artifacts [22, 23]. Participants were instructed to breathe normally but to remain strictly motionless and refrain from speaking or swallowing during image acquisition. To ensure high diagnostic fidelity, dual quality control indices were strictly utilized. First, the Motion Stability (M-STB) index was monitored in real-time to mitigate motion artifacts; measurements were acquired only when the index was rated ≥ 4 stars (out of 5), indicating optimal tissue stability. Second, the Reliability (RLB) index was assessed to verify the validity of shear wave propagation. Only elastographic maps maintaining an RLB index exceeding 80% inside the region of interest (ROI) were considered valid for statistical analysis, thereby ensuring that the calculated stiffness values were free from signal decorrelation or noise (Fig. 1B).

Quantitative stiffness measurements were obtained using circular ROIs with a fixed diameter of 4 mm. The ROIs were placed within the glandular parenchyma, carefully avoiding visible vascular structures and dilated ducts (Fig. 1C). For each gland, five valid measurements were acquired, and the mean elasticity value (kPa) was calculated and used for statistical analysis.

Intra-observer analysis

The reliability of the measurements was assessed by repeating US elastography examinations in 12 participants one week later. The same radiologist performed the repeat examinations. The reliability of the measurements was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis.

Sample size analysis

In this study, the sample size was calculated a priori using the G*power program [24]. The study was powered at 80% with a type I error rate of 0.05, with the primary outcome measure being mean SWE stiffness (kPa). A sample size of 42 individuals per group (SjD, DM, and healthy controls) was required to detect an effect size of 0.6 (Cohen’s d).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were employed to assess the normality of the data. Numerical data with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For the non-normally distributed variable, the median, interquartile range (25th and 75th percentiles), and minimum and maximum values were presented, as were the frequencies for the categorical data. For pairwise comparison among the SjD, DM and control groups, independent T-test (for normal distributed variables) and Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed variables) were used to assess differences in numerical variables. The Pearson Chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact test (expected value < 5) were conducted to compare categorical data between groups. To control Type I error due to multiple testing, Bonferroni correction was applied, resulting in an adjusted significance threshold of p = 0.0167 (0.05/3). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was employed to determine whether the US elastography results of major salivary glands could differentiate patients with SjD from DM patients. The area under the curve (AUC), with a confidence interval (CI) for each salivary gland, was estimated from the ROC curves. The statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

In total, 61 SjD patients, 42 DM patients with sicca, and 43 healthy controls were enrolled in the study. The mean age was similar across groups (53.95 ± 11.93, 54.07 ± 10.83, and 51.30 ± 8.88 years, respectively; p values > 0.05 for all comparisons). The DM group exhibited a higher BMI (33.89 ± 6.03 kg/m²) than the SjD group (30.05 ± 7.06 kg/m²; p = 0.005) and controls (29.58 ± 5.35 kg/m²; p = 0.001).

Clinically, xerostomia was present in 100% of the DM group and 86.9% of the SjD group (p = 0.015). However, positivity of autoimmunity markers was predominantly observed in the SjD group: ANA positivity (80.3%) and Anti-Ro/SSA positivity (42.6%), were significantly lower or absent in the DM group (p < 0.001). The median symptom duration was 4.0 (1.0–15.0) years for SjD and 8.0 (1.0–20.0) years for DM (p < 0.001). The detailed clinical variables were presented in Table 1.

Gray-scale ultrasound findings (OMERACT scores)

According to the OMERACT total scores, the SjD group demonstrated elevated pathological scores in all salivary glands than DM and healthy groups (p < 0.001). For the right parotid gland, 100% of DM patients (42; 42) and healthy controls (43; 43) were classified as score 0 (normal), whereas the OMERACT scores were found to be higher in the SjD group (p = 0.040 and p = 0.042 for binary group comparison, respectively). Similar patterns were observed in the left parotid gland (p = 0.042 and p = 0.038, respectively) and left submandibular gland (p = 0.050 and p = 0.053). No statistically significant differences were found between the OMERACT scores of the DM group and the healthy group in across glands (p > 0.0167 for all comparisons) (Table 2).

Shear wave elastography (SWE) assessment

Figure 2; Table 2 showed SWE results for three groups. The mean stiffness of the right parotid gland in SjD patients was higher (8.88 ± 3.15 kPa) than DM patients (7.86 ± 1.65 kPa; p = 0.035) and controls (7.89 ± 1.93 kPa; p = 0.039). Similarly, the left parotid gland showed higher stiffness in SjD group than DM group (9.27 ± 4.04 vs. 7.63 ± 1.66 kPa; p = 0.031). Submandibular gland stiffness was higher bilaterally in SjD patients than in DM patients (right: 12.27 ± 4.51 vs. 10.66 ± 3.41 kPa, p = 0.035; left: 13.44 ± 5.67 vs. 11.49 ± 5.70 kPa, p = 0.027). Despite the elevated stiffness levels observed in the SjD group, subsequent pair-wise comparisons between the groups did not reveal any statistically significant disparities (according to p value with Bonferroni correction; p < 0.0167). Diagnostic performance

The ROC analysis indicated moderate discriminatory power for SWE in differentiating SjD from DM, with AUC values of 0.598 (95% CI: 0.488–0.788; p = 0.081) for right parotid gland, 0.628 (95% CI: 0.520–0.736; p = 0.020) for the left parotid gland, 0.614 (95% CI: 0.505–0.723; p = 0.040) for the right submandibular gland, and 0.625 (95% CI: 0.518–0.733; p = 0.023) for the left submandibular gland (Table 3and Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study represents one of the few investigations to concurrently evaluate the diagnostic performance of SWE and the OMERACT gray-scale ultrasound scoring system for the major salivary glands across three distinct cohorts: patients with SjD, patients with type 2 DM presenting with sicca symptoms, and healthy volunteers. While existing literature predominantly compares diseased populations solely against healthy controls [13, 25–28], our study design addresses a critical clinical dilemma: differentiating the etiology of xerostomia in complex patient populations. The most significant finding of this study is the quantitative demonstration that, despite overlapping clinical symptoms, the elastographic and morphological profiles of salivary glands in SjD and DM are fundamentally distinct.

Sjögren’s disease and tissue stiffness: the role of fibrosis

In our cohort, mean elasticity (kPa) in the parotid and submandibular glands of SjD patients was higher than those in both the DM and healthy control groups. Although it is not statistically significant, the higher glandular stiffness of SjD is consistent with the established pathophysiology of SjD, which is driven by chronic lymphocytic infiltration and progressive glandular fibrosis. Özer et al. similarly reported that SWE parameters provide excellent diagnostic performance (AUC > 0.90) and often complement gray-scale findings by quantifying parenchymal stiffness [27]. Likewise, previous investigations by Arslan et al. and Prata et al. demonstrated significantly increased shear wave velocity (SWV) values in SjD patients compared with healthy controls, attributing these increases to the loss of glandular elasticity [14, 15]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Freitas et al. further corroborated this pattern, reporting pooled mean differences of 12.37 kPa in the parotid gland and 9.09 kPa in the submandibular gland between SjD patients and controls [28]. Mechanistically, this increased stiffness likely reflects the complex “epithelial-stromal-immune ecosystem” described by Chatzis et al., in which the interplay between epithelial cells, stromal fibroblasts, and immune cells drives structural remodeling and tissue rigidity [29].

Diabetes mellitus and the “sialosis” paradox

A pivotal contribution of our study is the characterization of the “sialosis paradox” in diabetic patients. Although 100% of our DM cohort reported xerostomia, their glandular stiffness values were lower than those of the SjD group and remained closer to those of healthy controls. Conversely, in DM patients, a statistically significant lower ESSPRI dryness score predicts lower stiffness elastography. Badarinza et al. previously noted that while salivary glands in diabetic patients may be enlarged, they do not exhibit significantly increased stiffness; the authors attributed this to fatty infiltration (steatosis) rather than inflammatory fibrosis [30]. Adipose tissue has a lower elastic modulus compared to fibrotic tissue. Recent studies by Ozturk and Yalcin, as well as Erdem and Tosun, have characterized salivary glands in diabetic patients as having increased volume and altered microvascularity but lacking the specific focal inhomogeneity and high stiffness typical of autoimmune sialadenitis [13, 26]. Our data aligned with these findings, demonstrating that DM patients had significantly lower OMERACT scores compared to SjD patients. This distinction was also highlighted by Kahveci et al., who reported that ultrasonographic scoring systems designed for SjD yield significantly lower scores in diabetic patients despite the presence of sicca symptoms [31]. Thus, a “soft” or “mildly stiff” glandular structure in a patient with dry mouth strongly points towards a metabolic etiology rather than an autoimmune process.

The impact of symptom duration on diagnostic accuracy

While SWE successfully differentiated SjD from DM, the discriminatory power observed in our study was moderate (AUC range = 0.628 − 0.598). A critical explanatory factor may be a short symptom duration in our SjD cohort (median 4.0 years). Glandular damage in SjD is cumulative; early-stage disease is dominated by lymphocytic infiltration and edema, whereas dense fibrosis which significantly elevates stiffness develops in later stages [28, 32–35]. Ma et al. reported that the sensitivity of SWE for diagnosing pSS was notably lower (70.3%) in patients with a disease duration of less than 5 years compared to those with longstanding disease [35]. Consequently, our SjD cohort may largely represent the “inflammatory phase” rather than the “fibrotic phase,” resulting in stiffness values that are elevated but not yet distinct enough to sharply separate from the metabolic changes seen in DM. In patients with SjD, the low-to-moderate disease activity (ESSDAI = 4.57 ± 3.12) may also be a factor explaining the relatively low stiffness. Furthermore, Dai et al. emphasized in their meta-analysis that patient age and disease duration are significant covariates affecting the diagnostic performance of elastography [34]. This finding serves as a critical caveat for clinicians: in early-stage SjD, elastography values may remain in a “gray zone,” necessitating careful interpretation as suggested by Hoffman et al. regarding the interpretation of “normal” stiffness [36].

The power of the multiparametric approach

The limitations of elastography in early-stage disease can be mitigated by integrating gray-scale findings (OMERACT score). In our study, whereas OMERACT scores were significantly elevated in SjD patients, indicating structural inhomogeneity, the DM group predominantly presented with grade 0 (normal) scores. This finding reinforces that diabetic sialosis could not manifest as the focal structural inhomogeneity typical of autoimmune sialadenitis. Recent research by Cheng et al. demonstrated that combining SGUS scores with SWE values significantly improves diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.883) compared with either modality alone [37]. Similarly, Luo et al. proposed a modified diagnostic model incorporating both OMERACT scores and SWE, which achieved an AUC of 0.963, further supporting the synergistic use of these modalities [38]. Furthermore, the utility of SWE extends to other rheumatic conditions; Atik et al. demonstrated its effectiveness in assessing glandular involvement in ankylosing spondylitis, reinforcing the versatility of this modality in the broader context of autoimmune exocrinopathy [16].

Limitations

Our study has certain limitations. First, due to the cross-sectional design, we could not establish a causal relationship between disease duration and the progression of glandular stiffness. Second, ethical considerations precluded histopathological confirmation in the diabetic group; therefore, although the literature and sonographic patterns strongly suggest steatosis, definitive histological evidence differentiating fatty infiltration from microvascular fibrosis in this cohort is lacking. The relatively short symptom duration in our SjD group may have limited the observation of advanced fibrotic changes that would likely yield higher elastographic contrast. A further potential limitation is the high BMI observed in the DM group. While this could act as a confounding factor in the results, it may also be another consequence of metabolic syndrome, which is the cause of salivary gland pathology in patients with DM. Finally, the study’s limitation can be stated as its single-centre design, single operator dependency and the omission of multiple regression analysis for potential influential factors on the results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study suggests that SWE may provide useful biomarker information for differentiating xerostomia associated with SjD from that related to DM. SjD often presents with increased tissue stiffness and structural inhomogeneity due to autoimmune-mediated fibrosis, whereas DM tends to show lower stiffness values and relatively preserved parenchymal architecture. Nevertheless, clinicians should be cautious in early-stage SjD, where incomplete fibrosis may lead to moderate stiffness values that overlap with metabolic conditions. Accordingly, SWE should not be used as a standalone diagnostic tool but rather as part of a multiparametric approach combining clinical data and B-mode ultrasonography to ensure optimal diagnostic accuracy.
