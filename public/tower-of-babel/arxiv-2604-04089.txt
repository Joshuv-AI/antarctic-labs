From Paper to Program: Accelerating Quantum Many-Body Algorithm Development via a Multi-Stage LLM-Assisted Workflow
Yi Zhou

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) can generate code rapidly but remain unreliable for scientific algorithms whose correctness depends on structural assumptions rarely explicit in the source literature. We introduce a multi-stage LLM-assisted workflow that separates theory extraction, formal specification, and code implementation. The key step is an intermediate technical specification—produced by a dedicated LLM agent and reviewed by the human researcher—that externalizes implementation-critical computational knowledge absent from the source literature, including explicit index conventions, contraction orderings, and matrix-free operational constraints that avoid explicit storage of large operator matrices. A controlled comparison shows that it is this externalized content, rather than the formal document structure, that enables reliable code generation. As a stringent benchmark, we apply this workflow to the Density-Matrix Renormalization Group (DMRG), a canonical quantum many-body algorithm requiring exact tensor-index logic, gauge consistency, and memory-aware contractions. The resulting code reproduces the critical entanglement scaling of the spin- 1 / 2 1/2 Heisenberg chain and the symmetry-protected topological order of the spin- 1 1 Affleck–Kennedy–Lieb–Tasaki model. Across 16 tested combinations of leading foundation models, all workflows satisfied the same physics-validation criteria, compared to a 46% success rate for direct, unmediated implementation. The workflow reduced a development cycle typically requiring weeks of graduate-level effort to under 24 hours.



I
Introduction

A central challenge in computational science is the translation of formal theory into reliable and scalable code. Although this step is essential for scientific progress, it is often slow, error-prone, and dependent on substantial tacit expertise. Large language models (LLMs) now offer a compelling possibility: direct conversion of research papers into executable scientific software. In practice, however, this promise remains difficult to realize for algorithms whose correctness depends on precise mathematical structure. The challenge is not only that scientific code must be syntactically correct, but that it must also preserve structural assumptions—including index conventions, contraction logic, gauge constraints, numerical stability, and memory scaling—that are rarely made fully explicit in the source literature.

Quantum many-body algorithms provide a stringent test of this problem. Tensor-network methods

Yet implementing these methods from the literature remains difficult even for experienced researchers. Beyond understanding the formalism, one must convert compact mathematical expressions into explicit array operations, maintain canonical gauge conditions, manage multi-index tensor contractions, and avoid severe memory overhead in applications of the effective Hamiltonian. As a result, developing a working DMRG codebase from first principles often requires weeks to months of graduate-level effort.

This gap between theoretical exposition and computational realization suggests that the failure of direct scientific code generation is fundamentally a
knowledge-externalization
problem. In this context, knowledge externalization refers to the active process of transforming the tacit domain expertise required for numerical coding—such as assumed index conventions, memory management, and operational constraints—into an explicit, structured format. Research papers are written for human interpretation, not for unambiguous machine execution. Important implementation details are often compressed into notation, omitted as tacit knowledge, or left to the reader’s technical judgment. When these implicit constraints are not made explicit, even highly capable models must infer them, and the resulting code becomes fragile. In this view, the central obstacle is not code generation alone, but the absence of an intermediate document that makes explicit the computational knowledge required for implementation—knowledge that is present in no single source but must be actively externalized from the interplay of theory, convention, and numerical practice.

In this work, we introduce a multi-stage, human-in-the-loop (HITL) workflow for AI-assisted scientific programming that addresses this knowledge-externalization gap. Rather than treating an LLM as a monolithic code generator, we separate the process into three stages: theory extraction, formal specification, and implementation. The workflow is organized as a “Virtual Research Group” composed of specialized agents supervised by a human Principal Investigator (PI): a Junior Theorist (LLM-0) for theory extraction, a Senior Postdoc (LLM-1) for formal specification, and a Research Assistant (LLM-2) for code implementation. This staged structure introduces explicit review and refinement between the theoretical source and the final program, thereby reducing ambiguity across the development pipeline.

The central innovation is the introduction of an intermediate technical specification between the source literature and the final code. This specification serves as a rigorous implementation document: it fixes universal tensor-index conventions, makes contraction logic explicit, and enforces memory-efficient implementations of the effective Hamiltonian. Crucially, the specification externalizes implementation-critical computational knowledge—including explicit index naming, contraction orderings, and architectural constraints—that is not present in the source literature but must be inferred from the interplay of theory and numerical practice. II.5

We benchmark this workflow on the automated construction of an MPS/DMRG engine in Python. This is a deliberately demanding test case. Tensor-network algorithms require simultaneous control of symbolic consistency, numerical stability, and asymptotic memory scaling, making them an exacting benchmark for LLM-assisted code generation. A successful implementation must not only run without errors, but also reproduce physically meaningful observables. To test this, we validate the generated code on two canonical one-dimensional quantum systems: the spin-

From the perspective of AI-assisted scientific computing, tensor-network algorithm implementation presents an unusually demanding test of LLM code-generation capabilities. Standard code-generation benchmarks primarily test syntactic correctness and API usage on self-contained programming tasks. By contrast, DMRG implementation requires simultaneous satisfaction of symbolic index consistency, algebraic gauge invariance, and asymptotic memory constraints—none of which are fully explicit in the source literature. As we show in Sec.
III.1
, direct DMRG code generation remains unreliable: while some current models can succeed, others fail systematically even after extensive human feedback, and the outcome is not predictable in advance. This positions tensor-network programming as a challenging benchmark at the frontier of AI-assisted scientific code generation.

Our contribution is not a replacement for mature tensor-network libraries such as ITensor
5

7

Instead, we identify a broader methodological principle: explicitly externalizing implementation-critical computational knowledge—through a structured multi-stage pipeline—can dramatically improve the reliability, reproducibility, and efficiency of AI-assisted scientific programming. Across 16 combinations of leading foundation models used in the specification and implementation stages, all tested workflows satisfied the same physics-validation criteria within the defined HITL protocol. More broadly, the workflow reduced a development cycle that typically requires weeks of graduate-level effort to under 24 hours of wall-clock time, including approximately 14 hours of active human-AI collaboration.

These results suggest that reliable AI-assisted scientific programming may depend less on ever-larger monolithic models than on structured workflows that externalize the tacit computational knowledge required for implementation. A controlled comparison further shows that it is the

Although demonstrated here for tensor-network algorithms, the same principle may extend more broadly to other scientific programming tasks in which theoretical correctness depends on implicit computational knowledge that must be made explicit before implementation. In this sense, the present work treats tensor-network programming not only as an application domain, but also as a controlled setting for studying how the explicit externalization of computational knowledge can improve LLM behavior in scientific computing.

II
Methodology: The “Virtual Research Group” Workflow

Translating quantum many-body theory into discrete, scalable array operations requires computational knowledge that is often only implicit in the literature. To address this gap, we organized our LLM-assisted development pipeline to mirror the functional structure of a traditional academic research group. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, the workflow partitions algorithm development into three distinct LLM roles supervised by a human Principal Investigator (PI): theory extraction, formal specification, and code implementation.

In practice, this first-stage output captures the broad mathematical structure of the algorithm but often remains incomplete as an implementation document. As shown schematically in Fig.

If translated directly into executable code, such drafts can lead to incorrect tensor wiring or unfavorable memory scaling, including dense intermediate constructions with cost well above the intended matrix-free design.

II.2
Stage 2: Expert Specification (LLM-1 / the “Senior Postdoc”)

The central methodological step is Stage 2. Rather than translating the Stage 1 draft directly into Python, we insert an explicit specification stage in which the draft is reviewed for computational consistency and implementation readiness. This task is assigned to a second model, LLM-1, which acts as the specifying agent and produces a detailed technical specification for the final code.

The role of LLM-1 is to externalize computational knowledge that is implicit in the source literature but essential for numerical implementation. As illustrated in Fig.
1
(c), the resulting specification standardizes tensor notation, makes contraction order explicit, and records implementation constraints required for scalability. The key elements introduced at this stage include:

•

Universal index conventions:
A fixed nomenclature for tensor legs (for example,
b/B
for MPO bonds,
x/X
for bra bonds, and
y/Y
for ket bonds), reducing the risk of broadcasting and contraction mismatches.

•

Matrix-free scalability:
Replacement of dense effective-Hamiltonian constructions by iterative
scipy.sparse.linalg.LinearOperator
representations, so that Hamiltonian application is performed through sequential tensor contractions without explicitly materializing the full operator matrix in memory.

•

Memory-aware tensor operations:
Explicit use of contraction and reshaping patterns compatible with efficient NumPy execution, including
np.tensordot
for gauge shifts and careful distinction between memory views and deep copies.

Crucially, these implementation constraints were not hand-coded or micro-managed by the human Principal Investigator. The explicit prompt provided to LLM-1 was strictly high-level: “
This is a latex note generated from the attached review article. As a computational and theoretical physicist, could you review the latex note equation by equation, then check whether it is ready for practical coding?
” The fact that LLM-1 autonomously deduced the necessity of standardizing indices and adopting a matrix-free approach demonstrates that foundation models possess the requisite tacit computational knowledge. This knowledge, however, remains latent during direct implementation and is only successfully externalized when the model is explicitly tasked with drafting an intermediate structural blueprint.

The output of Stage 2 is a detailed specification that functions as a model-agnostic intermediate representation for the final implementation stage. Because it makes computational knowledge explicit in a transferable form, the specification can be consumed by different foundation models with consistent results, effectively serving as a shared knowledge interface between diverse AI ecosystems. As we show in Sec.
II.5

II.3
Stage 3: Code Implementation and HITL Mentorship (LLM-2 and Human PI)

In the final stage, the technical specification is provided to the implementation agent, denoted LLM-2. In our tests, this role was filled by different foundation models, including Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, GPT 5.4, Claude Opus 4.6, and Kimi Agent. Because the specification explicitly constrains tensor shapes, contraction logic, and algorithmic structure, the implementation stage is reduced from open-ended scientific reasoning to a more localized translation task: generating object-oriented Python code that follows the prescribed blueprint.

At this stage, the human researcher acts as the Principal Investigator within a human-in-the-loop (HITL) workflow. The PI does not manually rewrite the generated code; instead, the code is executed and assessed through physically meaningful diagnostics. For example, if a two-site DMRG update produces an unphysical collapse of the bond dimension to
D=1
, the PI provides physics-based feedback indicating that the result is inconsistent with the expected algorithmic behavior. This feedback is then incorporated by LLM-2 in subsequent revisions. In this sense, the HITL component operates primarily at the level of scientific supervision and error interpretation rather than low-level code editing.

II.4
Protocol and Evaluation Criteria

For each workflow path, the stage definitions and benchmark tasks were fixed in advance. The human intervention was strictly constrained: during the HITL debugging rounds, the human PI’s feedback was exclusively limited to copying and pasting Python error tracebacks and reporting high-level physical diagnostics (e.g., “
The code executes, but the entanglement entropy collapses to zero
”). At no point did the PI read the generated code to locate bugs, point out specific line numbers, or provide explicit mathematical corrections to tensor-contraction axes. The implementation model (LLM-2) was required to autonomously map these runtime and physical failures back to the underlying algorithmic logic.

The number of HITL feedback rounds was not prescribed in advance and varied across workflow paths, from 1 to 13 debugging rounds after initial code delivery (with the Kimi Agent framework handling debugging autonomously), depending on the implementation model and the specific errors encountered. Thus, while the form of human intervention was fixed, the number of interaction cycles was allowed to vary. For the direct implementation baseline (Sec.
III.1
), sessions were typically terminated after approximately 20 HITL rounds if no convergence was evident, unless the model self-terminated earlier by declaring its inability to produce a correct implementation. Throughout this work, HITL feedback rounds are counted from the first code delivery by LLM-2, including only interactions involving debugging, physics diagnosis, or correction of implementation errors. The initial prompt providing the source document is not counted. To ensure transparency, all prompts, responses, and revision histories were recorded and are available in the public repository
21
.

To ensure comparability, the prompts used for specification-guided and direct generation were deliberately matched in structure and requirements. The specification-guided prompt read: “
This is a latex note on MPS and DMRG. Could you generate python codes according to it? Please compute both S=1/2 Heisenberg model and AKLT model, and make sure that a ‘scalable’ matrix-free approach with LANCZOS is adapted. I work with Jupyter. Please show all figures and save them in directory ‘figureAKLT’. Please stay strictly with the latex note implementation.
” The direct implementation prompt read: “
This is a review article on MPS-based DMRG. Could you generate python codes according to it, by using MPS/MPO finite-DMRG implementation. Please build Python codes from scratch, adhere strictly with the review article, and use only numpy + scipy + matplotlib. Please compute both S=1/2 Heisenberg model and AKLT model using two-site DMRG algorithm, and make sure that a ‘scalable’ matrix-free approach with LANCZOS is adapted. I work with Jupyter and prefer to fewer cells. Please show all figures and save them in directory ‘figureAKLT’. Begin with a quick test on small systems, then go to larger systems.
” The only substantive difference was the input document: the technical specification in the guided case versus the review article in the direct case.

Furthermore, to prevent target leakage, the prompts never contained the exact analytical physical targets (such as the exact AKLT energy
E=-2/3
or the string order value
-4/9
). The models generated the measurement routines autonomously, and the PI evaluated the results against the analytical benchmarks only
post facto
.

A workflow path was considered successful if the final codebase simultaneously satisfied three criteria: (1) the code executed without shape-mismatch or memory-allocation failures; (2) the implementation employed matrix-free application of the effective Hamiltonian, avoiding explicit construction of large operator matrices; and (3) the engine successfully reproduced the benchmark physical observables detailed in Sec.
III
and Sec.
IV
.

Results: Systematic Reproducibility and Workflow Acceleration

III.1
The Direct Implementation Baseline

To assess whether the multi-stage workflow addresses a genuine limitation of current models, we performed a direct implementation baseline test with all four foundation models. Each model was provided with the same source review article
15
and prompted to generate a complete two-site DMRG engine for both the spin-
1/2
Heisenberg chain and the spin-
1
AKLT model using only NumPy and SciPy. The prompt explicitly requested a scalable, matrix-free approach with Lanczos iteration. As detailed in Sec.
II
, this prompt was closely matched in structure and requirements to the prompt used for specification-guided generation—the only substantive difference was the input document: the theoretical review article in the direct case versus the technical specification produced by LLM-1 in the guided case.

The results revealed a sharp capability divide across model families (Table
1
). Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview succeeded in all three attempts, producing correct matrix-free implementations within 1–3 rounds of HITL feedback. Claude Opus 4.6 succeeded in three of four attempts, requiring 7–11 debugging rounds when successful; its one failure consumed 32 rounds of increasingly circular debugging—with the model repeatedly rediscovering the same bugs in environment initialization and index contraction order—before the session was terminated without a correct result. By contrast, GPT 5.4 (0/3) and the Kimi Agent framework (0/3) failed in every attempt even after extended HITL debugging sessions exceeding 20 rounds each. The dominant failure mode for these models was consistent: despite the explicit instruction to use a matrix-free approach, they constructed the effective Hamiltonian as an explicit dense matrix, leading to memory overflow at moderate bond dimensions. In one GPT session, after extended interaction the model itself concluded: “
I failed to produce a correct implementation under your constraints in this session.
”

The qualitative character of the debugging trajectories differed markedly between successful and failed attempts. In failed direct runs, debugging was circular: models would fix one index error only to reintroduce a previously corrected one, suggesting that without explicit global constraints, local fixes destabilize other parts of the interdependent contraction logic. By contrast, in successful runs, each debugging round addressed a localized error without disrupting previously validated components. This distinction between convergent and non-convergent debugging trajectories suggests that the direct implementation failures reflect a systematic inability to maintain global consistency across interdependent tensor operations, rather than a marginal shortfall that additional rounds would resolve.

V
Discussion and Conclusion

The integration of large language models into scientific computing has the potential to accelerate the translation of theory into executable code, yet direct, unmediated code generation remains unreliable for advanced quantum many-body algorithms. In this work, we showed that a major part of this difficulty arises from workflow design rather than from code generation alone. By separating theory extraction, rigorous mathematical specification, and implementation, we constructed a pipeline that bridges abstract literature and validated numerical code in a more structured and reproducible way.

V.1
The Role of Externalized Computational Knowledge

The central methodological finding of this work is that explicitly externalizing implementation-critical computational knowledge—through an intermediate technical specification—dramatically improves the reliability of LLM-assisted scientific code generation. Providing an implementation model with raw theoretical equations often leaves too many ambiguities unresolved for robust code generation, particularly in tensor-network settings where index conventions, contraction order, and memory scaling must all be specified explicitly. In contrast, a specification that makes this knowledge explicit constrains the implementation problem to a much narrower translation task.

The cross-model results support this interpretation. Although the behavior of the models differed at the extraction and implementation stages, all 16 tested combinations of specification model and coding model satisfied the same validation criteria within the defined HITL workflow. The direct implementation baseline (Sec.
III.1
) sharpens this point. The same two models that failed every baseline attempt—GPT and Kimi—succeeded in all specification-guided attempts. The specification did not change what these models could do in principle; it changed what they could do reliably. This distinction between capability and reliability is central to the practical utility of LLM-assisted scientific programming.

An additional implication of the baseline results concerns the relative quality of different source documents for code generation. In direct testing, the implementation models received the original review article
15
—a complete, authoritative, and pedagogically detailed theoretical source. Yet the direct success rate (6/13) was substantially lower than the specification-guided success rate (16/16), even though the specification was derived from an LLM-0 draft that is itself a lossy extraction of the same review article. This implies that the specification stage does not merely recover information lost during extraction, but adds implementation-critical structure—such as explicit index conventions, matrix-free constraints, and contraction ordering—that was never present in the original source. In this sense, the specification is not a summary of the review article but a fundamentally different kind of document: one that externalizes the tacit computational knowledge required for machine translation rather than for human understanding.

This result indicates that the primary driver of the workflow’s effectiveness is the externalized computational content, not the formal document structure. The critical transition is from the review article (which contains the theory but not the implementation-critical knowledge) to the specification (which externalizes that knowledge), rather than from informal to formal notation. V.3
Evidence of In-Context Algorithmic Reasoning

A natural concern for LLM-generated scientific code is data contamination: the possibility that a model reproduces memorized open-source implementations, such as ITensor
5
or TeNPy
7
, rather than generating solutions from the local prompt context. Our results do not eliminate this concern completely, but they provide several observations consistent with genuine in-context symbolic reasoning.

1. Custom-defined syntax:
The generated Python adopted
numpy.einsum
expressions defined within the intermediate specification, including contraction strings such as
’bxy,ytY,bBst,xsX->BXY’
. These strings are not standard fragments copied from common tensor-network libraries, suggesting that the models were translating the local specification rather than simply retrieving memorized implementations.

2. Divergent derivations of the AKLT MPO:
While all tested models reproduced the standard
D_{W}=5
Heisenberg MPO, their treatment of the more involved spin-
1
AKLT biquadratic interaction,
\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{i+1}+\frac{1}{3}(\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{i+1})^{2}
, differed substantially. Gemini and GPT produced a
D_{W}=14
expansion, Claude generated a compressed
D_{W}=11
representation, and Kimi preferred procedural construction rules. This diversity of valid outputs is more consistent with local derivation under shared constraints than with simple retrieval of a single memorized template.

3. Correction of inconsistent specifications:
The intermediate
L
a
T
e
X
documents occasionally contained typographical or logical inconsistencies, such as mismatched bra/ket conventions or flawed contraction strings. In multiple cases, the implementation models produced code that repaired these inconsistencies rather than following the flawed local expression literally. Some models silently corrected the indices in code; others explicitly surfaced an “Errata”-like note to flag the inconsistency. These behaviors suggest that the models were not only parsing the specification, but also checking it against broader structural constraints of tensor-network theory.

Taken together, these observations support the view that the models were performing nontrivial in-context algebraic synthesis during implementation. At the same time, broader and more controlled studies would be needed to distinguish more sharply between reasoning, retrieval, and hybrid behavior.

More directly, the central finding of this study—that the same models fail systematically in direct implementation but succeed reliably with the specification—is itself difficult to explain by memorization alone. If the models had memorized a working DMRG implementation from their training data, they should be able to reproduce it under direct prompting, particularly given the explicit and detailed instructions provided (Sec.
II
). The fact that GPT and Kimi failed every direct attempt suggests that their training-data exposure to DMRG code, if any, was insufficient for reliable reproduction without additional guidance. The specification succeeds not by triggering retrieval of a memorized template, but by providing the explicit computational constraints needed to guide generation in real time. In this sense, the data-contamination concern and the knowledge-externalization thesis are complementary rather than contradictory: even if partial memorization contributes to the models’ baseline familiarity with tensor-network concepts, reliable implementation still requires the explicit externalization of computational knowledge that the specification provides.

V.4
A Pedagogical Interpretation of HITL Scientific Programming

The human role in this workflow is closer to scientific supervision than to conventional software engineering. Rather than manually writing boilerplate code or debugging library syntax, the PI provides the source literature, evaluates the intermediate specification, and diagnoses physically inconsistent outputs during execution. In this respect, the workflow resembles the training of junior researchers: the human supplies the scientific curriculum and conceptual feedback, while the AI agents perform the intermediate translation and implementation steps.

This pedagogical interpretation is useful because it shifts expectations about what LLMs can reliably do in scientific settings. They are most effective not as infallible oracles, but as highly capable assistants operating within a structured framework of explicit computational knowledge and human oversight. Such a workflow can reduce the amount of low-level implementation effort required from the researcher and redirect attention toward algorithmic design, physical interpretation, and benchmarking.

The 24-hour development timeline should be interpreted with appropriate caveats. The workflow assumes a human PI who already understands the underlying theory well enough to provide meaningful physics-level feedback—for example, recognizing that a bond-dimension collapse to
D=1
indicates an algorithmic error rather than a physical result. The relevant baseline is therefore not the full training period of a beginning graduate student, which includes learning the theory itself, but the implementation effort required of an experienced researcher working without LLM assistance. For an algorithm of DMRG’s complexity, this effort might range from days to a few weeks depending on the target level of generality and optimization. Even under this more conservative comparison, the workflow represents a significant acceleration, and it additionally produces a fully documented development history—including the intermediate specification and complete interaction transcripts—that would not typically accompany a manually developed codebase.

V.5
Limitations and Scope

Several limitations of the present study should be noted. First, the workflow was validated on a single algorithm family (DMRG/MPS); while we expect the principle of knowledge externalization to generalize, this has not been demonstrated for other tensor-network geometries or unrelated scientific algorithms. Second, the theoretical source used here
15
is an exceptionally detailed pedagogical review; the workflow’s effectiveness starting from more compressed source material, such as original research papers with terse notation, remains to be tested. Third, the number of HITL feedback rounds varied across workflow paths (from 1 to 13 for specification-guided runs, Table
2
), indicating that the human supervision component is not yet fully standardized. Fourth, the controlled comparison (Sec.
III.3
) was conducted with a single model (GPT) and two attempts; while the result is clear and consistent with the broader pattern, extending the comparison to other models would strengthen the conclusion. Fifth, the direct implementation baseline comprises 13 total attempts across four models; while the qualitative pattern is stark, the per-model sample sizes (3–4 attempts each) are too small for precise statistical comparison, and additional trials would be needed to establish reliable per-model success rates. Sixth, the physics benchmarks test ground-state properties of small systems with known exact solutions; the numerical stability and performance of the generated code under more challenging conditions—such as frustrated systems, near-degenerate spectra, or large bond dimensions—remain to be assessed. Finally, because LLM capabilities evolve with model updates, the specific HITL dynamics reported here may not reproduce exactly with future model versions, although the externalized knowledge artifact itself remains version-independent.

Looking forward, the present results suggest that knowledge-externalization workflows may be extensible beyond the DMRG example studied here. Natural next targets include Time-Dependent Variational Principle (TDVP) engines
6
, infinite Matrix Product States (iMPS)
18
, infinite-system DMRG (iDMRG)
11
, Projected Entangled Pair States (PEPS)
16
, and hybrid approaches such as Gutzwiller-guided DMRG
9
;
8
. More broadly, our results point to a simple but consequential insight: the central bottleneck in AI-assisted scientific programming is not model capability but knowledge externalization—making explicit the tacit computational knowledge that links theory to implementation.
