Apple Vision Pro as the Intended Primary Intraoperative Visualization Platform for Complete Cataract Surgery: A Prospective Single-Center Feasibility Study
Authors: Tommy S Korn, Cheyenne Santiago, Yvonne Fuller, Bishoy Said
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the feasibility and early safety observations of Apple Vision Pro as the intended primary intraoperative visualization platform during cataract surgery.Patients and MethodsThis prospective, single-center, single-arm feasibility study evaluated Apple Vision Pro integrated with a ZEISS ARTEVO 850 three-dimensional digital surgical microscope. Forty-five patients undergoing cataract surgery were enrolled. Apple Vision Pro was used as the intended primary visualization platform, with conventional microscope oculars and an ARTEVO 850 three-dimensional monitor available as backup. Primary endpoints were procedure completion without device-related conversion and device-related intraoperative complications. Secondary endpoints included surgeon-reported workload and usability, operating room staff experience, and postoperative visual outcomes.ResultsForty-four of 45 procedures (97.8%) were completed using Apple Vision Pro without device-related conversion. One procedure required conversion to backup visualization after a transient image interruption perceived as image freezing; no patient harm or surgical complication occurred. No device-related intraoperative surgical complications were observed, although the study was not powered to assess rare complications. Mean patient age was 70.6 ± 8.8 years, and mean operative time was 15.4 ± 4.9 minutes. Mean best-corrected visual acuity improved from 0.50 ± 0.40 logMAR preoperatively to 0.02 ± 0.07 logMAR at 1 month. Surgeons reported low perceived workload on the modified NASA Task Load Index, and operating room staff reported minimal workflow disruption.ConclusionIn this prospective single-center feasibility cohort, Apple Vision Pro enabled completion of most selected cataract procedures using the intended primary visualization pathway within a defined workflow with immediate backup visualization. These preliminary findings suggest that wireless, head-mounted stereoscopic visualization may be feasible in a specialized, backup-supported surgical environment and support further controlled evaluation. However, these data cannot guide universal routine practice or establish comparative safety, clinical advantage, ergonomic benefit, routine clinical suitability, cross-specialty applicability, or detection of rare complications.Keywords: Apple Vision Pro, cataract surgery, spatial computing, digital surgery, stereoscopic visualization, surgical visualization, head-mounted display

Introduction

Cataract surgery is one of the most commonly performed surgical procedures worldwide, with demand expected to increase as populations age and access to surgical care expands.1 Advances in surgical technology have contributed to improved outcomes, efficiency, and patient safety. Visualization remains a critical component of surgical performance, particularly in ophthalmology, where micron-level precision is required within the confined anatomical spaces of the eye.

Traditional operating microscopes provide high-quality stereoscopic visualization but require surgeons to maintain prolonged fixed postures while viewing through microscope oculars. Prolonged use of optical microscopes and slit-lamp biomicroscopes has been associated with a high prevalence of work-related musculoskeletal disorders among ophthalmologists, most commonly affecting the neck, lower back, and shoulders.2–5 These symptoms have been linked to sustained non-neutral posture, forward head positioning, repetitive movements, high clinical and surgical volume, and prolonged use of ophthalmic equipment.2–5 Musculoskeletal symptoms may negatively affect clinical workload, productivity, surgeon well-being, and career longevity.5 Similar ergonomic challenges have been reported across other procedural specialties, where work-related musculoskeletal disorders remain highly prevalent among surgeons and interventionalists.6

Prior work evaluating visual ergonomics in microsurgery has also described the phenomenon of visual tunnel vision, in which microscope-based visualization may limit awareness of the surrounding operative environment and contribute to ergonomic and cognitive burden during prolonged procedures.7

To address these limitations, digital three-dimensional surgical systems have been developed, allowing surgeons to operate while viewing a high-resolution stereoscopic display rather than microscope eyepieces. These systems have demonstrated favorable visualization quality, surgical performance, surgeon ergonomics, cognitive workload, and safety outcomes compared with conventional microscope ocular viewing.8–13 However, surgeons remain dependent on a fixed external display positioned within the operating room environment. Although digital three-dimensional systems may improve ergonomics compared with conventional microscope oculars, visualization remains dependent on monitor positioning, viewing distance, and operating room configuration. A recent cataract surgery study using the ZEISS ARTEVO 800 demonstrated reduced neck flexion and lower musculoskeletal discomfort compared with a conventional microscope, but the system still required viewing on an external three-dimensional display.13 The external display and microscope arrangement may introduce line-of-sight and workflow considerations that require individualized setup and adjustment.13 In addition, ophthalmic surgery requires simultaneous coordination of hand instrumentation and multiple foot controls while maintaining continuous visualization through the surgical microscope, factors that may further contribute to work-related musculoskeletal strain among ophthalmologists.5 Surgeons may also need to intermittently review ancillary diagnostic information, including biometry, corneal topography, imaging, and other intraoperative data that are typically displayed separately from the surgical image within the operating room environment.

Head-mounted display technologies may offer an alternative way to position surgical images within the surgeon’s field of view. Spatial computing platforms such as Apple Vision Pro can display stereoscopic images in adjustable virtual windows while allowing awareness of the surrounding operating room environment. However, these potential workflow characteristics have not been shown to provide ergonomic, clinical, or safety advantages over established microscope ocular or external three-dimensional display systems.

For intraocular microsurgery, wireless transmission of a live stereoscopic surgical image introduces additional technical considerations, including image delay, frame interruption, signal instability, and loss of visualization. Even brief interruptions may be clinically consequential during delicate intraocular maneuvers. Therefore, any evaluation of a wireless head-mounted visualization pathway requires predefined backup visualization options and careful assessment of device-related workflow interruptions.

Recently, a pilot series reported Apple Vision Pro use during eyelid malposition repair, but these extraocular procedures did not evaluate continuous intraocular microsurgery.14 Mixed-reality headsets have also been explored in cataract surgery and telementoring applications; however, these reports did not evaluate Apple Vision Pro as the intended primary visualization pathway throughout complete cataract surgery or provide controlled comparisons with conventional microscope oculars or external three-dimensional monitor visualization.15

To our knowledge, this study is the first prospective, single-center evaluation of Apple Vision Pro as the intended primary visualization pathway throughout complete cataract surgery. The study was designed as a single-arm feasibility assessment within a defined workflow that included a dedicated local wireless network and immediate access to conventional microscope oculars and an external three-dimensional monitor. It was not designed to compare Apple Vision Pro with standard visualization modalities or to establish comparative safety, ergonomic benefit, clinical advantage, or suitability for routine use.

The purpose of this study was to describe the feasibility of this workflow, including successful completion without device-related conversion, device-related technical events, intraoperative and early postoperative safety observations, surgeon-reported workload, and operating room staff experience. Because cataract surgery requires continuous stereoscopic visualization during delicate intraocular maneuvers, the study also evaluated whether the workflow could be used with predefined backup pathways when device-related concerns arose.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a prospective, single-center, single-arm feasibility study evaluating Apple Vision Pro as the intended primary intraoperative visualization pathway during cataract surgery. The study was not randomized and did not include a concurrent control group using conventional microscope oculars or external three-dimensional monitor visualization. The study was designed to describe feasibility and device-related workflow events within a defined clinical setting rather than to establish comparative effectiveness, ergonomic benefit, noninferiority, or safety for rare surgical complications. No formal sample-size calculation was performed because this was an exploratory feasibility cohort. The sample size was not intended to provide statistical power for uncommon adverse events or comparative analyses. The study was conducted at Sharp Otay Lakes Surgery Center in Chula Vista, California. This study was approved by the Sharp HealthCare Institutional Review Board, Sharp HealthCare, San Diego, California, USA (IRB #2511901; approved March 11, 2026). All study procedures adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. This was an investigator-initiated study supported by a grant from the Sharp HealthCare Foundation.

Participants

Forty-five adult patients (≥18 years) undergoing cataract surgery were enrolled. Patients were recruited from the clinical practices of the participating surgeons and screened for eligibility before surgery. Key inclusion criteria included elective ophthalmic surgery and the ability to provide informed consent. Key exclusion criteria included ocular conditions that could increase surgical risk or interfere with visualization, including zonular instability, pseudoexfoliation syndrome, prior vitreoretinal surgery, and significant corneal opacity, as well as cognitive impairment limiting the ability to provide informed consent. Accordingly, the enrolled cohort primarily represented patients undergoing routine cataract surgery and did not include many conditions associated with greater operative complexity or reduced visualization. These selection criteria were intended to support initial feasibility assessment and limit generalizability to more complex cataract surgery. All procedures were performed by two board-certified ophthalmic surgeons specializing in cataract and corneal surgery who had extensive experience in anterior segment microsurgery. The limited number of participating surgeons was consistent with the exploratory single-center design and limits generalizability to surgeons with different experience levels or practice environments.

Surgical System and Visualization Platform

All procedures were performed using a ZEISS ARTEVO 850 three-dimensional (3D) digital surgical microscope (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany), which provides high-resolution stereoscopic visualization of the operative field. The microscope’s digital output was transmitted to Apple Vision Pro (Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA), a head-mounted display utilizing second-generation M5 hardware, through a spatial computing interface consisting of the ClearSphere application and its proprietary hardware transmission system, ClearSync (ClearSurgery, Lyon, France). This system captured the microscope’s stereoscopic output and wirelessly streamed the signal to the Apple Vision Pro in real time over a dedicated local wireless network. The Apple Vision Pro communicated exclusively with the ClearSync transmission system through this dedicated local wireless network. The visualization system operated independently of internet connectivity during surgical procedures (Figure 1).

Surgical visualization system architecture and workflow. Live stereoscopic video from the ZEISS ARTEVO 850 digital surgical microscope was transmitted through the ClearSync video transmission system and a dedicated local wireless network to Apple Vision Pro, which served as the intended primary visualization platform. ARTEVO 3D monitor visualization and conventional microscope oculars remained continuously available as first-line and secondary backup visualization pathways, respectively.

The Apple Vision Pro displayed the operative field as a high-resolution stereoscopic 3D image within a spatial computing environment, allowing the surgeon to dynamically position and scale the visual display within their field of view while maintaining binocular depth perception and visualization of the surrounding operating room environment (Figure 2). The Apple Vision Pro displayed the same stereoscopic surgical image simultaneously available on the ARTEVO 850 external 3D monitor (Figure 3). No augmented reality overlays, telementoring functions, or additional applications were utilized during surgical procedures. Apple Vision Pro was configured as the intended primary surgeon visualization display for the entirety of each procedure and received the live stereoscopic surgical image transmitted from the ARTEVO 850 for evaluation of intraoperative visualization, safety, and feasibility. The surgical assistant and operating room personnel utilized the ARTEVO 850 external 3D monitor for their visualization and workflows.

Simultaneous visualization was available on the ARTEVO 850 external 3D monitor for operating room personnel and as a backup visualization pathway.

To minimize distraction and preserve procedural focus, notification functions, background application activity, and nonessential device services within the study device were disabled during all cases. The study device was not connected to personal cloud services and was configured in accordance with institutional privacy and security practices. No patient-identifiable information was transmitted to or stored on the Apple Vision Pro study device.

The standard ARTEVO 850 external 3D monitor remained active throughout all cases for operating room team visualization and as a safety backup. Surgeons were able to immediately transition to either conventional microscope ocular visualization or standard ARTEVO 850 3D monitor visualization if deemed necessary for patient safety or technical reasons. Backup Apple Vision Pro battery packs for the study device were available throughout all procedures.

Prior to clinical implementation, participating surgeons completed device familiarization using ophthalmic surgical training eye models and operating room simulation exercises. Before clinical use, the complete visualization platform, including the ARTEVO 850 digital microscope, ClearSphere application, ClearSync transmission system, dedicated local wireless network, and Apple Vision Pro, was evaluated under operating room conditions to verify image quality, stereopsis, signal stability, and workflow functionality. Before each procedure, functionality of the visualization platform and wireless stereoscopic transmission pathway was verified. Verification included assessment of image quality, stereoscopic depth perception, and signal stability before patient entry into the operating room.

Infection prevention, device handling, and device reprocessing procedures were performed in accordance with Sharp HealthCare operating room policies and institutional infection-prevention procedures. The Apple Vision Pro was cleaned and disinfected between cases according to institutional procedures for reusable ophthalmic operating room equipment. Standardized barrier techniques were used during device positioning and adjustment to maintain surgeon sterility and preserve the integrity of the sterile field throughout the procedure. Apple Vision Pro served as the intended primary visualization platform throughout each procedure, while conventional microscope oculars and standard ARTEVO 850 three-dimensional monitor visualization remained immediately available as backup modalities.

Latency and Technical Event Monitoring

No formal end-to-end latency measurement, frame-rate analysis, or quantitative image-quality testing was performed. During each procedure, the operating surgeon and surgical team monitored the live stereoscopic image for perceived delay, image freezing, loss of stereopsis, signal interruption, or any visualization concern that could affect surgical confidence or patient safety. Conventional microscope oculars and the ARTEVO 850 external three-dimensional monitor were immediately available as backup visualization pathways. Conversion from Apple Vision Pro to backup visualization was performed whenever the live image was considered insufficient for safe continuation. No prespecified numerical latency threshold or duration-based risk grading system was used; any perceived image delay, image freezing, signal instability, loss of stereopsis, or visualization concern affecting surgeon confidence or patient safety prompted immediate conversion to backup visualization at the surgeon’s discretion. Device-related technical events, backup conversion, and associated intraoperative outcomes were prospectively documented.

Surgical Procedure

Use of Apple Vision Pro did not alter surgical technique, instrumentation, medications, anesthesia management, or intraoperative patient care. All procedures were performed using routine perioperative safety time-out protocols and standard surgical techniques appropriate for the planned procedure.

Immediately following the surgical safety time-out, the ARTEVO 850 digital surgical microscope was positioned and focused on the operative eye. Operating room personnel then assisted with placement of the Apple Vision Pro on the surgeon in accordance with the study workflow and sterile field procedures described above. The surgeon subsequently adjusted the position and size of the stereoscopic surgical display within the spatial computing environment to optimize visualization and ergonomic comfort. The virtual display was positioned within the surgeon’s field of view such that it did not obstruct visualization of the patient, surgical microscope, operative field, instrumentation, operating room personnel, or surrounding environment. This configuration permitted continuous awareness of the operating room environment and routine interaction with the surgical team, including instrument exchange and intraoperative communication. Once the desired display location and size were established, the virtual display was locked in position using the ClearSphere application to prevent unintended movement during surgery (Figure 3).

Cataract surgery was then performed under local topical anesthesia with monitored anesthesia care provided by an anesthesiologist. A temporal clear corneal approach was utilized in all cases, followed by standard phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation according to surgeon preference and routine clinical practice.

Apple Vision Pro served as the intended primary visualization platform from initial incision through completion of surgery. Study procedures were planned for continuous Apple Vision Pro-assisted visualization throughout the procedure unless conversion to a backup modality was deemed necessary. Surgeons utilized Apple Vision Pro throughout all operative stages, including wound creation, capsulorhexis, phacoemulsification, cortical removal, posterior capsule polishing, intraocular lens implantation, and wound closure. Conventional microscope ocular viewing and the ARTEVO 850 external three-dimensional (3D) monitor remained immediately available throughout all procedures as backup modalities. If image quality, signal stability, latency, or any other device-related concern was identified, surgeons were permitted to immediately transition to a backup system at their discretion.

Device-related conversion was defined as transition from Apple Vision Pro viewing to conventional microscope oculars or the ARTEVO 850 3D monitor because of image quality, latency, signal instability, hardware malfunction, or surgeon preference related to device performance. Any device-related workflow deviations, technical issues, interruptions in viewing, or conversions to backup systems were documented in the study device deviation log and included in safety monitoring analyses.

Formalized Training and Workflow Implementation

Formalized training protocols were implemented for participating surgeons and operating room staff prior to study initiation. Surgeon training included structured instruction in device setup, visualization workflow, ergonomic positioning, troubleshooting, and safe conversion to conventional microscope visualization. Training was followed by operating room simulation exercises and supervised dry-run sessions to confirm familiarity with the Apple Vision Pro visualization workflow and conversion procedures.

Operating room staff received structured training in device handling, maintenance of sterility, intraoperative support, battery management, and conversion procedures. Training emphasized preservation of the sterile field, safe device placement and removal, and rapid response to surgeon-initiated conversion to backup visualization modalities. Training activities were documented using standardized checklists to promote consistency and adherence to study procedures.

Outcome Measures

Primary Endpoints

The primary endpoints were feasibility and safety. Feasibility was defined as successful completion of surgery using Apple Vision Pro as the primary visualization platform without device-related conversion to conventional microscope ocular visualization or ARTEVO 850 3D monitor visualization. Safety was assessed by the occurrence of intraoperative and postoperative adverse events potentially attributable to Apple Vision Pro-assisted visualization. Intraoperative safety outcomes included posterior capsule rupture, vitreous loss, corneal or scleral injury, device-related workflow interruptions, conversion to backup visualization, and other intraoperative complications potentially attributable to Apple Vision Pro-assisted visualization. Postoperative safety outcomes included cystoid macular edema (CME), endophthalmitis, corneal edema, retained lens fragments, elevated intraocular pressure, wound leak, retinal detachment, unexpected return to the operating room, and other adverse events identified during postoperative follow-up. Primary feasibility outcomes were completion of cataract surgery using Apple Vision Pro as the intended primary visualization pathway without device-related conversion and the occurrence of device-related technical events or intraoperative surgical complications.

Secondary Endpoints

Secondary outcomes included surgeon-reported workload and usability, operating room staff experience, operative time, and early postoperative visual and safety observations, including best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA). Surgeon workload, usability, and staff experience were exploratory subjective outcomes. Formal ergonomic testing, objective posture assessment, quantitative image-quality analysis, end-to-end latency measurement, and comparative workflow-efficiency assessment were not performed.

Survey Instruments and Data Collection

Study data were collected using structured surveys administered to surgeons and operating room staff immediately following each procedure. Operating room staff respondents included the circulating nurse and surgical technician or surgical assistant involved in each case. The surgeon survey evaluated visualization quality, ergonomics, workflow integration, perceived safety, and the need for conversion to standard surgical visualization systems (Supplementary Appendix 1). Surgeon workload was additionally assessed using a modified version of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration Task Load Index (NASA-TLX), including the domains of mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration. All NASA-TLX domains were recorded using 21-point Likert scales, with lower scores indicating lower workload for mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, effort, and frustration, and higher scores indicating greater perceived surgical performance. NASA-TLX results were collected only after Apple Vision Pro-assisted procedures and were not paired with surgeon workload assessments during conventional microscope ocular or external three-dimensional monitor visualization.

The operating room staff survey assessed workflow integration, intraoperative communication, sterility concerns, staff comfort, and confidence in Apple Vision Pro-assisted procedures using Likert-scale responses and open-ended qualitative feedback (Supplementary Appendix 2). All surgeon and operating room staff surveys were completed within 15 minutes of case completion and before initiation of the subsequent procedure to minimize recall bias and capture immediate procedural impressions while the operative experience remained fresh in participants’ memory.

Additional study data, including screening information, operative details, and postoperative outcomes, were obtained from the Sharp HealthCare electronic medical record and study-specific data collection forms developed for this project. Surveys were completed electronically using institution-issued iPads operating in secured kiosk mode through Microsoft Forms as part of Sharp HealthCare’s secure Microsoft 365 enterprise platform.

All study data were coded prior to analysis. Identifiable patient information was accessible only to authorized study personnel for enrollment tracking and linkage of study data to surgical outcomes and was not included in the final analysis dataset. Survey and study data were stored within password-protected, encrypted, access-controlled institutional systems accessible only to the study team in accordance with institutional research and privacy policies.

Safety Monitoring, Oversight, and Data Integrity

Ongoing safety monitoring was performed by the Principal Investigator and an independent reviewer under oversight of the Sharp Center for Research and the Sharp Institutional Review Board (IRB). Intraoperative safety events, workflow disruptions, and device-related technical issues were documented using standardized device deviation logs and study-specific safety tracking forms. Safety monitoring included assessment of intraoperative complications, device-related workflow interruptions, conversion to backup visualization, and postoperative adverse events potentially attributable to Apple Vision Pro-assisted visualization.

For each procedure, the surgeon documented whether any safety event, workflow disruption, or device-related technical issue occurred and whether the event was considered unrelated, possibly related, or probably related to Apple Vision Pro use. All device-related technical issues and conversions to backup visualization were reviewed according to predefined study device deviation procedures. Device-related events and cumulative feasibility outcomes were reviewed at predefined intervals throughout the study period.

To promote data integrity and objective assessment, study data, including surgeon and operating room staff survey responses, device deviation logs, operative documentation, and recorded safety endpoints, were independently reviewed throughout the study period. The independent reviewer assessed data completeness, consistency, and concordance between study records and reported outcomes. All unanticipated problems, protocol deviations, and adverse events were reported in accordance with institutional IRB policies and required reporting timelines.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistical methods were used to summarize demographic characteristics, procedural outcomes, safety events, and survey responses. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Given the exploratory feasibility design of the study, no formal hypothesis testing was performed. No formal sample-size calculation was performed because this was an exploratory single-center feasibility study rather than a hypothesis-testing, noninferiority, or comparative safety trial. The 45-procedure cohort reflected feasible prospective enrollment within the single-center study setting and was intended to provide useful information about workflow feasibility, device-related technical events, backup conversion procedures, and early safety observations. This approach is consistent with published guidance that pilot and feasibility studies are used to assess feasibility, safety, procedures, and implementation before larger definitive studies.16 The cohort was not powered to detect rare complications, assess long-term reliability, or compare outcomes with conventional microscope ocular or external three-dimensional monitor visualization. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Postoperative Follow-up and Safety Assessment

Patients were monitored through the 1-month postoperative visit. This follow-up period was intended to capture early visual and safety observations and was not designed to evaluate longer-term ocular outcomes or delayed device-related effects. Postoperative assessments included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and evaluation for complications, including cystoid macular edema (CME), endophthalmitis, corneal edema, retained lens fragments, elevated intraocular pressure, wound leak, retinal detachment, unexpected return to the operating room, and other adverse events. All postoperative safety events were documented and assessed for potential relationship to Apple Vision Pro-assisted surgical visualization.

Results

Patient and Procedure Characteristics

A total of 45 cataract surgeries were included in the study (Table 1). The mean patient age was 70.6 ± 8.8 years (range, 49–84 years). Thirty-one patients (68.9%) were female and 14 (31.1%) were male. Twenty-four procedures (53.3%) were performed on the right eye and 21 (46.7%) on the left eye. Mean preoperative best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 0.50 ± 0.40 logMAR, improving to 0.02 ± 0.07 logMAR at 1 month postoperatively. Mean operative time was 15.4 ± 4.9 minutes (range, 8–29 minutes).

Feasibility and Device-Related Technical Events

Apple Vision Pro was used as the intended primary visualization pathway in all 45 procedures. Forty-four procedures (97.8%) were completed from initial incision through completion of surgery without device-related conversion to backup visualization (Table 2). One cataract procedure required immediate conversion to the ARTEVO 850 external three-dimensional monitor during the fifth study case after a transient interruption of the Apple Vision Pro image feed, perceived by the surgeon as image freezing for approximately 1 second. The interruption occurred before capsulorhexis, while the surgeon was awaiting the capsulorhexis needle before initiating capsulotomy. The event was considered a clinically consequential device-related technical event because continuous visualization is required during intraocular surgery. The surgeon immediately transitioned to the backup monitor, and the procedure was completed without a recorded intraoperative surgical complication or patient harm. The event was reviewed according to the study device deviation protocol in collaboration with on-site technical personnel. The contemporaneous study records did not permit formal quantification of end-to-end latency, frame loss, or the technical cause of the interruption. No additional image interruptions, perceived delay, or signal-instability events were observed during the remaining 40 procedures.

Intraoperative Safety Outcomes

No device-related intraoperative surgical complications were observed in this cohort. No cases of posterior capsule rupture, vitreous loss, dropped nucleus, or other vision-threatening intraoperative complications occurred. Given the sample size, selected study population, and absence of a comparator group, these observations do not establish safety for uncommon complications or comparative safety relative to standard visualization methods. One cataract surgery required implantation of a capsular tension ring because of intraoperative zonular laxity. The procedure was completed without posterior capsule rupture, vitreous loss, or device-related complications. This case was distinct from the procedure requiring conversion to backup visualization for the transient image-interruption event.

Postoperative Outcomes

At the 1-month postoperative visit, mean best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) improved to 0.02 ± 0.07 logMAR (range, −0.1 to 0.4). No cases of cystoid macular edema, endophthalmitis, retained lens fragments, wound leak, retinal detachment, or unexpected return to the operating room were observed during postoperative follow-up. One patient developed transient postoperative intraocular pressure elevation at 1 week, presumed secondary to a steroid response. This event was not considered related to the Apple Vision Pro visualization pathway.

Surgeon Workload Assessment

Participating surgeons reported low perceived workload across the modified NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) domains while using Apple Vision Pro as the intended primary visualization pathway (Table 3). These descriptive, self-reported findings were not compared with workload during conventional microscope ocular or external three-dimensional monitor visualization. On the modified 21-point scale used in this study, mean scores were 1.24 ± 0.68 for mental demand, 1.07 ± 0.25 for physical demand, 1.02 ± 0.15 for temporal demand, 1.24 ± 0.57 for effort, and 1.18 ± 0.54 for frustration. Perceived performance was high, with a mean score of 20.96 ± 0.30 on the same 21-point scale.

Operating Room Staff Experience

A total of 87 operating room staff surveys were completed, including responses from circulating nurses and ophthalmic surgical technicians/assistants (Table 4). For workflow integration, 84 of 87 respondents (96.6%) strongly agreed and 3 (3.4%) agreed that Apple Vision Pro use did not disrupt normal operating room workflow. Effective communication between the surgeon and operating room staff was reported by all respondents, with 83 (95.4%) strongly agreeing and 4 (4.6%) agreeing. Regarding safety and sterility, 84 respondents (96.6%) strongly agreed and 3 (3.4%) agreed that use of the headset and associated equipment did not raise safety or sterility concerns. Similarly, 83 respondents (95.4%) strongly agreed and 4 (4.6%) agreed that they felt comfortable working in the operating room during Apple Vision Pro-assisted procedures. For the four Likert-scale items, no respondents selected neutral, disagree, or strongly disagree. All respondents answered yes when asked whether they would feel confident assisting with future surgeries using the Apple Vision Pro visualization pathway. These responses reflect immediate staff perceptions in the study setting and were not compared with staff experience during conventional microscope ocular or external three-dimensional monitor workflows.

Discussion

This prospective, single-center, single-arm study evaluated the feasibility of using Apple Vision Pro as the intended primary intraoperative visualization pathway throughout complete cataract surgery within a defined workflow with immediate backup visualization. In the present study, the ClearSphere application and ClearSync transmission system enabled wireless stereoscopic image transmission from the ARTEVO 850 digital microscope to Apple Vision Pro during live surgery. In this cohort, 44 of 45 procedures (97.8%) were completed using Apple Vision Pro from initial incision through completion of surgery without device-related conversion. No device-related intraoperative surgical complications were observed, and mean best-corrected visual acuity improved from 0.50 ± 0.40 logMAR preoperatively to 0.02 ± 0.07 logMAR at 1 month. These findings support feasibility of the evaluated workflow in a selected cataract surgery cohort but do not establish comparative safety, clinical advantage, reliability, or suitability for routine clinical use.

A central finding of this study is that Apple Vision Pro was used as the intended primary surgeon visualization pathway throughout the procedure rather than as an intermittent adjunct. Cataract surgery requires continuous stereoscopic visualization, depth perception, and image stability during delicate intraocular maneuvers such as capsulorhexis, phacoemulsification, cortical removal, intraocular lens implantation, and wound closure. Completion of most procedures using the intended primary visualization workflow supports further evaluation of head-mounted visualization during intraocular microsurgery. However, this study did not include objective latency measurement, image-quality analysis, or comparison with microscope oculars or external three-dimensional monitor visualization.

One procedure required immediate conversion to the ARTEVO 850 external three-dimensional monitor during the fifth study case after a transient interruption of the Apple Vision Pro image feed, perceived by the surgeon as image freezing for approximately 1 second. The interruption occurred before capsulorhexis, while the surgeon was awaiting the capsulorhexis needle before initiating capsulotomy. Because continuous visualization is required during intraocular surgery, this event was clinically consequential and prompted immediate transition to backup visualization. The procedure was completed without a recorded intraoperative surgical complication or patient harm. Although no additional image interruptions, perceived delay, or signal-instability events were observed during the remaining 40 procedures, end-to-end latency, frame loss, and the technical cause of the interruption were not formally quantified. Therefore, the observed event rate should be interpreted cautiously.

No device-related intraoperative surgical complications were observed in this cohort, and early postoperative observations through 1 month were reassuring with mean best-corrected visual acuity at 1 month and no cases of cystoid macular edema, endophthalmitis, retained lens fragments, wound leak, retinal detachment, or unexpected return to the operating room. One procedure required a capsular tension ring because of zonular laxity, and this was not considered device-related. One patient developed transient postoperative intraocular pressure elevation presumed secondary to steroid response and not related to the visualization pathway. Given the selected cohort, small sample size, absence of a comparator group, and limited follow-up period, these observations do not establish safety for uncommon complications, long-term safety, or comparative safety relative to standard visualization methods.

Participating surgeons reported low perceived workload across the modified NASA Task Load Index domains and high perceived performance while using Apple Vision Pro. These findings are exploratory and subjective. Because the study did not include objective ergonomic assessment, posture measurement, fatigue measurement, or comparison with conventional microscope ocular or external three-dimensional monitor visualization, the results do not demonstrate an ergonomic advantage, reduced fatigue, or lower workload relative to existing visualization systems.

Prior studies have reported ergonomic benefits of some digital three-dimensional visualization systems compared with conventional microscope ocular viewing.8–13 However, the present NASA-TLX findings cannot be directly benchmarked against those studies because of differences in surgical procedure, visualization platform, study design, survey timing, workload instrument implementation, and absence of a paired control condition. A recent cataract surgery study using the ZEISS ARTEVO 800 demonstrated reduced neck flexion and lower musculoskeletal discomfort compared with conventional microscope viewing.13 Apple Vision Pro allowed the surgeon to position, scale, and lock the stereoscopic surgical image within the visual field (Figure 4), but this was a design characteristic of the evaluated workflow rather than evidence of improved ergonomics, reduced fatigue, or improved surgeon comfort. The present study was not designed to determine whether Apple Vision Pro offers ergonomic or performance advantages beyond established digital three-dimensional systems. Future comparative studies should use objective ergonomic and performance measures, including posture and cervical angle assessment.

The evaluated Apple Vision Pro workflow differs from established external three-dimensional monitor visualization in several important ways. External three-dimensional monitor systems are integrated within the microscope display pathway, provide shared visualization for the operating room team, and are established in ophthalmic surgical practice. In contrast, the evaluated Apple Vision Pro workflow required wireless transmission of the live stereoscopic image to a head-mounted display used by the surgeon. This wireless step may introduce risks of image delay, freezing, signal interruption, or loss of visualization that are not identical to the operational considerations of fixed external monitor viewing. Therefore, the present study does not establish ergonomic, workflow, cost, safety, or clinical advantages of Apple Vision Pro over established external three-dimensional visualization systems.

Operating room staff responses were favorable regarding communication, perceived workflow disruption, comfort, sterility concerns, and confidence assisting with future procedures using the Apple Vision Pro visualization pathway. These findings reflect immediate perceptions from staff working within the study setting and were not compared with staff experience during conventional microscope ocular or external three-dimensional monitor workflows. They support feasibility of implementing the evaluated workflow at the participating center but do not establish workflow, communication, safety, or efficiency advantages over standard visualization methods. Prior reports of three-dimensional surgical viewing systems have also described favorable staff perceptions of operating room integration and communication.17

This study adds prospective clinical data on Apple Vision Pro used as the intended primary visualization pathway throughout complete cataract surgery. To our knowledge, it represents the first prospective, single-center evaluation of this workflow during complete cataract surgery. The study was conducted under institutional review board oversight with prospective data collection, structured safety monitoring, and predefined backup visualization pathways.

Previous reports involving Apple Vision Pro in ophthalmology have largely focused on educational applications, communication tools, adjunctive visualization, or extraocular procedures rather than continuous visualization during complete intraocular microsurgery.14 Mixed-reality technologies have also been explored for cataract surgery and telementoring, but these reports have provided limited evaluation of head-mounted spatial computing platforms as primary visualization systems during complete cataract surgery.15 The present findings extend that literature by describing preliminary feasibility in a selected single-center cohort; they do not establish comparative clinical benefit, routine clinical applicability, or broader clinical translation.

Clinical Implications

The clinical implication of this study is narrow. The findings support only the feasibility of the evaluated wireless, head-mounted stereoscopic visualization workflow during selected cataract surgery procedures at a single center with experienced anterior segment surgeons, a dedicated local wireless network, trained operating room personnel, and immediate backup visualization available. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that Apple Vision Pro can replace conventional microscope oculars or established external three-dimensional monitor visualization for routine cataract surgery.

The evaluated workflow may be most applicable, if at all, to specialized high-resource surgical environments able to provide dedicated information-technology support, workflow training, device management, disinfection procedures, and immediate backup visualization pathways. The study did not evaluate routine high-volume cataract surgery, community surgical centers, low-resource settings, complex cataract cases, or environments without dedicated technical infrastructure. Operational considerations including latency risk, device fit, battery management, device handling, disinfection workflow, cost, staff training, and wireless-network reliability were not formally evaluated and may limit broader implementation.

Apple Vision Pro was used exclusively to display the live stereoscopic image transmitted from the digital microscope during selected cataract surgery procedures. The study did not evaluate neurosurgery, reconstructive surgery, other ophthalmic procedures, or other surgical specialties that use digital or optical microscopes. Therefore, any potential application to other specialties, augmented reality overlays, telementoring, navigation, electronic health record integration, artificial intelligence, or other spatial-computing workflows should be considered untested and hypothesis-generating only. Further comparative evaluation is required before broader clinical translation can be considered.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center with two experienced anterior segment surgeons, which may limit generalizability to other practice settings, surgical teams, and surgeon experience levels. Second, the enrolled cohort primarily represented routine cataract surgery because patients with several conditions associated with greater operative complexity or reduced visualization were excluded. Therefore, the findings may not generalize to more complex cataract cases. Third, the sample size was modest, and no formal sample-size calculation was performed because this was an exploratory feasibility cohort. The study was not powered to detect rare complications, establish noninferiority, or assess comparative safety relative to conventional visualization systems. Fourth, the study did not include a randomized control group; therefore, conclusions regarding superiority, equivalence, comparative effectiveness, operative efficiency, visualization quality, or ergonomic benefit relative to microscope oculars or digital heads-up visualization systems cannot be drawn. Fifth, surgeon workload and operating room staff experience were assessed using subjective survey instruments, and formal objective ergonomic measurements, including posture or cervical angle assessment, were not performed. NASA-TLX workload scores were not collected in a paired cross-modality design comparing Apple Vision Pro with conventional microscope ocular or external three-dimensional monitor visualization. Sixth, objective end-to-end latency measurements, frame-loss analysis, and quantitative image-quality assessment were not performed. The evaluated workflow used a dedicated local wireless network and immediate backup visualization pathways; therefore, findings may not generalize to operating rooms with greater wireless interference, different information-technology infrastructure, limited backup visualization, or operational constraints related to device fit, battery management, device handling, disinfection workflow, cost, and staff training. Wireless transmission of a live stereoscopic surgical image may introduce risks of image delay, freezing, signal interruption, or loss of visualization that require further objective evaluation. Finally, the 1-month follow-up period was intended to capture early postoperative observations and cannot assess longer-term ocular outcomes or delayed device-related effects. These limitations inform the future-study priorities outlined below, including multicenter controlled comparison, broader case complexity and surgeon participation, longer follow-up, objective latency and image-quality testing, objective ergonomic assessment, and evaluation of operational implementation requirements.

Future Directions

Future multicenter studies should evaluate the Apple Vision Pro visualization pathway and directly compare it with conventional microscope ocular viewing and established digital three-dimensional heads-up visualization during cataract surgery. Such studies should incorporate objective measures of end-to-end latency, frame loss, image stability, image quality, surgeon posture, cervical angle, ergonomic outcomes, workflow efficiency, cost, surgeon workload, and clinical outcomes. They should also include more diverse surgeons, surgical teams, and cataract case complexity, with longer postoperative follow-up. Future technical studies should also evaluate redundant, wired, or otherwise fail-safe transmission pathways to reduce the risk of image interruption during intraocular surgery.

Potential applications beyond live stereoscopic image display, including augmented reality overlays, telementoring, navigation, electronic health record integration, artificial intelligence, other ophthalmic procedures, and other surgical specialties, were not evaluated in this study. These applications should be considered untested hypotheses and should be assessed separately only after the safety and performance of the primary visualization pathway have been established through adequately powered comparative studies.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective clinical evaluation of Apple Vision Pro used as the intended primary wireless, head-mounted visualization pathway for viewing a live stereoscopic surgical image throughout complete cataract surgery. In this prospective, single-center, single-arm feasibility cohort, 44 of 45 procedures were completed using the intended visualization pathway within a defined workflow that included a dedicated local wireless network and immediate backup visualization. One procedure required conversion after a transient image interruption.

These preliminary findings support further controlled evaluation of wireless, head-mounted stereoscopic visualization for cataract surgery under strict dedicated safeguards. They do not establish comparative safety, reliability, ergonomic benefit, clinical advantage, suitability for routine clinical use, replacement of conventional microscope oculars or external three-dimensional monitor visualization, cross-specialty applicability, or the ability to detect rare complications. Larger multicenter randomized or controlled comparative studies with objective latency, image-quality, ergonomic, workflow, cost, and clinical outcome measures are needed before broader clinical translation can be supported.
