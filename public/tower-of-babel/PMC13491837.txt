Comparison of the effect of internet-based and in-person cognitive behavioral therapy on the fear of childbirth in primiparous pregnant women: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Kosar Piri, Maryam Gholamzadeh Jofreh, Mohammad Hosein Haghighizadeh, Poorandokht Afshari, Somayeh Makvandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionFear of childbirth (FOC) is a significant factor contributing to requests for elective cesarean sections. This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of internet-based cognitive behavioral therapy (ICBT) versus in-person cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) on fear of childbirth and delivery mode preference among primiparous pregnant women.MethodsIn this randomized controlled trial, 72 primiparous pregnant women with high fear of childbirth (based on the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire [W-DEQ-A]) were randomly allocated into three equal groups: in-person CBT, internet-based CBT (ICBT), and a control group. The interventions were delivered in eight 60–90-minute sessions. Data were collected using demographic questionnaires, W-DEQ-A, and a delivery mode preference question at three time points: before, immediately after, and one month after the intervention. Data were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA, chi-square, and one-way ANOVA in SPSS version 25.ResultsAt baseline, groups were similar in W-DEQ-A scores. Both in-person CBT and ICBT significantly reduced total scores and most subscales compared with the control group, with large effect sizes. In-person CBT showed greater improvement than ICBT in total score, lack of self-efficacy, and lack of positive anticipation, while other subscales were comparable. Effects persisted at follow-up. Both interventions also increased preference for vaginal delivery, especially in the in-person CBT group (8.7% to 60.9%), whereas no statistically significant change was observed in the control group over time. Analysis accounting for repeated measures confirmed these group-level differences over time. These findings demonstrate that both CBT modalities effectively reduce fear of childbirth and positively influence delivery mode preference.ConclusionBoth in-person and internet-based CBT effectively reduce fear of childbirth and improve related psychological outcomes. In-person CBT shows slightly greater benefits in self-efficacy and positive anticipation, while other domains are similarly improved by both modalities. Effects persist at follow-up, and both interventions increase preference for vaginal delivery. These findings support integrating CBT, particularly blended or flexible approaches, into routine prenatal care to enhance maternal psychological well-being and childbirth outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04903-w.

Introduction

Fear of childbirth (FOC) is a significant health concern affecting a substantial proportion of pregnant women worldwide, with reported prevalence rates varying from 5% to over 40% [1, 2]. In Iran, it is estimated that approximately 25% of pregnant women experience FOC [3]. This fear is characterized by intense anxiety and worry about the process of childbirth and its associated experiences, often leading to substantial distress and impairment in daily functioning [4]. Primiparous women are particularly vulnerable, reporting higher levels of FOC compared to multiparous women, primarily due to the uncertainty and lack of prior experience [5]. The consequences of severe FOC are far-reaching. It is strongly associated with an increased risk of adverse psychological outcomes, such as postpartum depression and post-traumatic stress disorder [6].Furthermore, from a clinical perspective, FOC is a well-documented, powerful predictor for requesting elective cesarean sections (CS) without medical indications [7, 8]. The rising rate of CS, especially in Iran, where a significant proportion of elective CS are attributed to FOC, poses a major public health challenge due to the associated higher risks for both maternal and neonatal health compared to vaginal delivery [9, 10]. Addressing FOC is therefore a clinical priority. Among various psychological interventions, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) has emerged as a gold standard for anxiety-related disorders, including FOC [11, 12]. CBT helps individuals identify and challenge distorted cognitions and maladaptive behaviors related to childbirth, thereby reducing fear and enhancing coping self-efficacy [13]. Traditionally delivered in-person, CBT has proven effective in reducing FOC and decreasing the preference for CS [14]. However, barriers such as geographical limitations, cost, and stigma often limit access to face-to-face psychological services. The advent of digital health has introduced Internet-based CBT (ICBT) as a promising alternative. ICBT delivers the core components of CBT through online platforms, offering greater accessibility, flexibility, and potential anonymity [15–17]. While preliminary studies, primarily from European countries, have shown the effectiveness of ICBT for FOC [15], direct comparisons between ICBT and traditional in-person CBT are scarce. Furthermore, the effectiveness of these interventions within the specific cultural and healthcare context of Iran remains largely unexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to fill this gap by conducting a randomized controlled trial to directly compare the effects of in-person CBT and ICBT with a control group on FOC and delivery mode preference in a sample of Iranian primiparous women.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a three-arm, randomized controlled trial with a pretest-posttest-follow-up design conducted from March to June 2025. The study was performed in urban health centers of Abdanan, Iran. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences, Ahvaz, Iran (Code: IR.AJUMS.REC.1403.421, Approval date: 2024.11.02) and prospectively registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (Code: IRCT20241111063668N1, Registration Date: 2024.11.24, https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir).

Participants

The study population consisted of primiparous pregnant women attending selected urban health centers in Abdanan, Iran. Inclusion criteria were: primiparity, gestational age between 20 and 24 weeks, a score above 65 on the Wijma Delivery Expectancy Questionnaire Version A (W-DEQ-A), age 18–45 years, literacy, access to a smartphone and the internet, and a singleton, low-risk pregnancy. Exclusion criteria included: current or history of psychiatric disorders or use of psychotropic medications, a score indicating depression on the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), medical indications for cesarean section, and development of any pregnancy complications during the study.

Sample size and sampling

The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome of the study, namely FOC, using data from a previous similar study (Uçar et al., 2019) [18]. The calculation was performed using standard formulas for comparison of means between groups, with a confidence level of 95% and a statistical power of 90% (α = 0.05, β = 0.10). The required sample size was estimated to be 20 participants per group. To account for a potential 20% attrition rate, the final sample size was increased to 24 participants per group, resulting in a total sample of 72 women who were randomly allocated into three groups (two intervention groups and one control group).

A quota sampling method was initially used to recruit eligible participants from health center records. Subsequently, eligible and willing participants were randomly allocated into one of the three groups using permuted block randomization with variable block sizes of 3, 6, and 9. The random allocation sequence was generated using an online randomization tool (www.sealedenvelope.com). Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Each envelope contained the group assignment and was opened only after the participant had been enrolled and baseline assessments had been completed.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and intervention providers was not feasible. The same individual who delivered the intervention also conducted the outcome assessments. Outcome measures were completed by participants using standardized self-administered questionnaires. Therefore, blinding of outcome assessment was not implemented in this study.

Intervention fidelity and adherence

The CBT sessions were delivered by a trained midwife (M.Sc. in Midwifery Counseling) who had completed specialized CBT training in addition to her academic coursework. The intervention protocol was developed based on established CBT principles and previous studies [15, 19], under the supervision of a counseling expert. Adherence to the protocol was ensured through session checklists, recording of sessions, and continuous oversight by the supervising faculty. The same therapist conducted both in-person and internet-based sessions, maintaining consistent content, structure, and homework assignments.

Description of interventions

In-person CBT group (n = 24)

In this group, the intervention consisted of group counseling sessions conducted in-person using CBT methods. The 24 participants were divided into three groups of eight. Sessions were held weekly over eight consecutive weeks on predetermined days, in the counseling rooms of the local health centers where the study took place. Each session lasted 60 to 90 min.

The teaching methods in the in-person sessions included presentation of educational content via PowerPoint, interactive lectures, group discussions, question-and-answer sessions, and the use of brainstorming techniques to actively engage participants in the process of identifying and modifying their thoughts (See supplementary file 1 for session content). The group setting encouraged empathy, sharing of experiences, and facilitated cognitive restructuring.

At the end of each session, participants were asked to complete CBT homework assignments related to the session, including recording automatic thoughts, analyzing anxiety-provoking situations, practicing mindfulness skills, and relaxation techniques in their personal notebooks, and to present them in the following session. These assignments were designed to consolidate learning, facilitate internalization of skills, and enhance self-monitoring among participants.

ICBT group (n = 24)

In this group, the intervention involved conducting CBT sessions online via a virtual platform (Skype). Participants were divided into three groups of eight to maintain group interaction and ensure sufficient opportunities for active participation, thereby enhancing the quality of counseling and learning.

Before the intervention, participants received in-person training on installing, setting up, and using Skype to ensure that all individuals could effectively access the sessions and utilize the virtual platform. Although the study was conducted in Abdanan, a relatively small city, the majority of participants had at least a high school or university education (Table 2) and predominantly resided in urban areas. These characteristics suggest a relatively favorable level of digital literacy and access to internet-based technologies among the study population, supporting the feasibility of delivering the intervention via an online platform.

The intervention consisted of eight weekly group sessions, each lasting 60 to 90 min, held at a predetermined day and time. Groups joined the online sessions regularly, utilizing Skype features such as group video calls, screen sharing, and group chat.

The session content, similar to the in-person group, was based on CBT principles and delivered through interactive lectures, question-and-answer, group discussions, cognitive exercises, thought restructuring, and training in coping and relaxation skills. Before each ICBT session, internet connectivity and Skype functionality were verified for each participant to ensure smooth participation and full access to group video calls, screen sharing, and chat features. To address potential technical issues, participants were instructed to report any connectivity problems, and in cases of temporary internet disruption, they were encouraged to rejoin the session as soon as possible. All sessions were recorded and made available to participants, allowing them to review missed content. In addition, brief follow-up support was provided when needed to ensure continuity of participation.

To maintain participant engagement, sessions were conducted interactively using group discussions, real-time questions and answers, and direct prompts to encourage participation.

At the end of each session, participants were asked to complete assignments related to that session throughout the week, including recording thoughts and emotions, practicing learned skills, and documenting their mental feedback. These assignments were reviewed and analyzed at the beginning of the next session to maintain continuity of learning and active engagement.

Control group (n = 24)

Participants in the control group did not receive any educational or psychological intervention and only received routine prenatal care according to the standard protocols of the local health centers.

To comply with ethical research principles and ensure fairness in accessing scientific knowledge, after completion of the intervention and data collection, the educational content from CBT sessions was compiled into educational files (CDs) and provided to the control group participants. This allowed them, if desired, to benefit from the materials for better emotional management and improved mental preparation for childbirth.

Participant assignments

Assignments were designed to reinforce learning and facilitate the application of session content. These included cognitive exercises (identifying and restructuring negative thoughts), daily recording of thoughts and emotions, and practicing relaxation, meditation, and breathing techniques. Participants documented their experiences and submitted brief reports after each session. Feedback was provided by the researcher to support progress and guide necessary adjustments.

Outcome measures and data collection tools

The primary outcome of the study was the total score of FOC, measured using the W-DEQ-A. Secondary outcomes included W-DEQ-A subscale scores and delivery mode preference. Data were collected at three time points: baseline, immediately after the 8-week intervention, and one month after intervention completion (follow-up).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software version 25. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency, percentage) were used to describe the data. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. One-Way ANOVA and Chi-square tests were used to compare baseline demographic and clinical characteristics across groups.

Because one participant in the in-person CBT group withdrew after the first intervention session and did not provide post-intervention outcome data, a per-protocol approach was used for all analyses.

For FOC and its subscales, repeated measures ANOVA was used as the primary analytical method to assess changes over time and between groups. Within-group effects were reported with p-values and partial eta-squared (η²), and between-group differences over time were assessed using the group × time interaction effect.

For delivery mode preference, between-group comparisons at each time point were performed using Chi-square tests. Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were additionally used as a supplementary longitudinal analysis to account for within-subject correlations across repeated categorical measurements over time.

A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Fear of childbirth outcomes

As shown in Table 3, there were no statistically significant differences among the control, in-person CBT, and ICBT groups in the total W-DEQ-A score at baseline (p = 0.08), indicating baseline homogeneity across the study groups. Following the intervention, significant between-group differences emerged (p < 0.001). Both intervention groups demonstrated a substantial reduction in total W-DEQ-A scores (p < 0.001), whereas no statistically significant change was observed in the control group. These differences remained statistically significant at the follow-up assessment (p < 0.001), indicating the persistence of treatment effects over time. Repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant within-group time effect for both the in-person CBT and ICBT groups (p < 0.001), with large effect sizes, reflecting the effectiveness of both interventions in reducing W-DEQ-A scores. In contrast, the control group showed no significant change over time (p = 0.32). Moreover, a significant time × group interaction effect was observed (p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.53), suggesting that the pattern of change in W-DEQ-A scores over time differed significantly between groups.

Pairwise comparisons indicated that the in-person CBT group had significantly lower total W-DEQ-A scores compared with both the ICBT (MD = -4.25, 95% CI = -8.10 to -0.44, p = 0.03) and control (MD = -38.47, 95% CI = -43.41 to -33.53, p < 0.001) groups. In addition, the ICBT group also showed a significantly greater reduction in W-DEQ-A scores compared with the control group (MD = -34.22, 95% CI = -39.11 to -29.33, p < 0.001).

Regarding lack of control, baseline scores did not differ significantly across groups (p = 0.55). Post-intervention and follow-up analyses showed significant reductions in both intervention groups (p < 0.001), whereas no statistically significant change was observed in the control group (p = 0.32). The time × group interaction was significant with a large effect size (p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.57). However, no statistically significant difference was observed between the in-person CBT and ICBT groups (MD = 0.24, 95% CI = -0.45 to 0.93, p < 0.999), indicating comparable effectiveness of the two interventions for this subscale.

For the loneliness subscale, both in-person CBT and ICBT resulted in significant and sustained reductions compared with the control group (p < 0.001). Large within-group effect sizes were observed for both interventions (partial η2 = 0.77 and 0.66, respectively), and no significant difference was found between the two intervention groups (MD = -0.52, 95% CI = -1.62 to 0.57, p = 0.74), suggesting similar effectiveness in reducing loneliness. A significant time × group interaction effect was observed (p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.67), suggesting that the changes in loneliness over time differed significantly between groups, with both intervention groups demonstrating greater reductions compared to the control group.

In the fear subscale, both CBT and ICBT groups exhibited significant reductions from baseline to post-test and follow-up, with large effect sizes (partial η² = 0.68 and 0.81, respectively, p < 0.001), whereas the control group showed an increase in fear over time (partial η² = 0.38, p < 0.001). The time × group interaction was significant with a large effect size (p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.80). Pairwise comparisons revealed no statistically significant difference between in-person CBT and ICBT, indicating that both modalities were equally effective in reducing this component of W-DEQ-A (MD = -0.49, 95% CI = -1.87 to 0.89, p < 0.999).

Regarding lack of positive anticipation, baseline scores did not differ significantly across groups. Post-intervention and follow-up analyses demonstrated significant reductions in both intervention groups (p < 0.001), whereas no statistically significant change was observed in the control group over time. The time × group interaction was significant with a large effect size (p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.56). Pairwise comparisons indicated that both in-person CBT and ICBT were significantly more effective than the control group (p < 0.001). Additionally, a statistically significant difference was observed between the in-person CBT and ICBT groups (MD = -1.56, 95% CI = -2.78 to -0.34, p = 0.007), indicating greater effectiveness of in-person CBT for improving positive anticipation.

Regarding fear of fetal injury, post-intervention and follow-up analyses showed significant reductions in both intervention groups (p < 0.001), whereas the control group exhibited an increase in fear over time (p = 0.19). The time × group interaction was significant with a large effect size (p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.35). Pairwise comparisons indicated that both in-person CBT and ICBT were significantly more effective than the control group (p < 0.001). However, no statistically significant difference was observed between the in-person CBT and ICBT groups (MD = -0.27, 95% CI = -0.75 to 0.20, p = 0.48), suggesting comparable effectiveness of the two interventions for this subscale.

With respect to lack of self-efficacy, no significant differences were observed between groups at baseline. Over time, both intervention groups demonstrated marked reductions at post-test and follow-up (p < 0.001), whereas the control group showed minimal change. The interaction between time and group was statistically significant and associated with a large effect size (p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.83). Post hoc analyses revealed that both CBT formats outperformed the control group (p < 0.001). However, the difference between in-person CBT and ICBT reached statistical significance (MD = -1.63, 95% CI = -3.10 to -0.16, p = 0.03), indicating that in-person CBT was more effective than ICBT in improving self-efficacy.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated the significant effectiveness of both in-person and internet-based Cognitive Behavioral Therapy in reducing fear of childbirth and increasing the preference for natural vaginal delivery among Iranian primiparous women. The findings align with and significantly extend the existing literature by providing a direct, nuanced comparison between the two modalities.

Our analysis of demographic and baseline characteristics confirmed the homogeneity of the three groups, ensuring that the observed effects can be attributed to the interventions. The lack of significant differences in variables such as age, education, economic status, and depression scores (BDI) at baseline aligns with findings from studies like those of Nieminen et al. and Karimi et al., suggesting that targeted psychological interventions, rather than demographic factors, are the primary drivers of change in FOC [17, 22].

Only one participant dropped out of the study, resulting in a very high retention rate (98.6%). Several factors likely contributed to this low attrition. First, the participant recruiter was a well-known practicing midwife at the local health center in Abdanan, a small city, and her classes were generally well-received by the community, leading to high motivation to participate. Second, the research team conducted regular follow-ups and provided continuous support to minimize dropout, including offering internet access for online participants when needed. These combined organizational and motivational strategies helped maintain consistent engagement throughout the intervention.

The core finding of this study is the powerful and sustained reduction in the total FOC score and all its subscales in both intervention groups compared to the control group, with large effect sizes. This result is consistent with the established effectiveness of CBT for anxiety disorders, wherein cognitive restructuring and exposure techniques help women challenge catastrophic thoughts and reduce avoidance behaviors related to childbirth [23, 24].

A nuanced and critical finding was the differential effectiveness of two modalities across specific domains of FOC. While both in-person CBT and ICBT were effective, in-person CBT demonstrated a modest but statistically significant advantage over ICBT in improving “Lack of Self-Efficacy” and “Lack of Positive Anticipation” subscales. We propose that this advantage may be attributed to the enhanced interactive and experiential components inherent in the in-person sessions, such as live discussion, real-time feedback, and group-based exercises, which likely strengthened engagement and self-reflective learning. Although both groups viewed the same labor and delivery videos, the in-person format allowed for immediate clarification of concepts, guided practice, and social modeling through peer interactions, consistent with Bandura’s Social Learning Theory [25]. This aligns with the fact that observational and interactive experiences, even when mediated via video, may be more impactful when embedded within a supportive, face-to-face context. Nevertheless, given that ICBT is more cost-effective, scalable, and accessible—particularly for women in remote areas or with limited access to trained therapists—the relatively small difference in self-efficacy and positive anticipation may be acceptable in practice. Thus, ICBT represents a practical alternative to in-person CBT, while in-person sessions may provide subtle additional benefits through richer social interaction and observational learning opportunities [26].

However, for other subscales—“Fear,” “Fear of Fetal Injury,” “Loneliness,” and “Lack of Control”—no statistically significant difference was found between the two intervention groups. This indicates that ICBT is just as effective as in-person therapy in mitigating the intense emotional and affective components of FOC, as well as fears for the baby’s safety. These results corroborate the findings of Nieminen et al. and Shahsavan et al., who reported significant reductions in FOC and its associated psychological symptoms through internet-based interventions [17, 27]. The ability of ICBT to effectively address these core emotional fears is a testament to its robustness as a therapeutic modality.

One of the most clinically significant outcomes was the dramatic shift in delivery mode preference. The proportion of women preferring vaginal delivery increased from 8.7% to 60.9% in the CBT group and from 12.5% to 50% in the ICBT group. Although the GEE analysis showed no significant group × time interaction, the overall trend confirms that both CBT modalities effectively influenced this key behavioral intention. By addressing the cognitive and emotional roots of FOC, both interventions enabled women to make choices based on reduced anxiety rather than fear. This finding is important for public health, highlighting that the scalable and accessible ICBT model can be nearly as effective as in-person therapy in potentially reducing non-medically indicated cesarean sections. The stability of these improvements at one-month follow-up indicates a lasting enhancement in cognitive and emotional preparedness for childbirth.

Practical implementation and future directions

Beyond demonstrating the effectiveness of both in-person and internet-based CBT for FOC, the findings also raise important considerations for practical implementation in the Iranian maternal health context. The scalable nature of ICBT offers the potential to reach a larger number of pregnant women, particularly in regions with limited access to trained therapists. However, challenges such as varying levels of digital literacy, internet accessibility, and cultural acceptability of online interventions must be addressed [28, 29]. In the present study, most participants had a relatively high educational level and were predominantly from urban areas, which likely facilitated the acceptability and feasibility of ICBT. In addition, providing initial face-to-face training on the use of the Skype platform helped ensure that all participants were able to effectively engage in the online sessions. Nevertheless, potential barriers such as lower digital literacy in less-educated populations, limited internet access in rural areas, and cultural preferences for face-to-face interactions should be considered when generalizing these findings to other settings. Strategies to enhance engagement could include preliminary orientation sessions on platform use, interactive multimedia content, and remote support from trained counselors.

Moreover, the development of blended care models—integrating core ICBT modules with targeted in-person sessions—may optimize both effectiveness and accessibility [30, 31]. For example, online modules could deliver psychoeducation, cognitive restructuring, and skill practice, while a limited in-person session could provide supervised experiential learning, peer interaction, or demonstration videos of labor coping techniques. Such a hybrid approach could combine the flexibility and reach of ICBT with the experiential richness of face-to-face support, potentially enhancing outcomes such as self-efficacy and positive anticipation.

Incorporating these pathways into existing maternal health infrastructure—such as routine prenatal visits, health center counseling services, or community health worker programs—could facilitate sustainable implementation. Future studies should explore the feasibility, acceptability, and cost-effectiveness of these models across diverse regions of Iran, with attention to digital equity and cultural tailoring.

Strengths and limitations

This study is one of the few randomized controlled trials to directly compare in-person and ICBT for FOC. Its strengths include a methodical design, a follow-up period, and assessment of a key behavioral outcome (delivery mode preference).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and the therapist was not feasible. In addition, outcome assessment relied on self-reported measures, which may have introduced bias related to expectancy effects and social desirability. The intervention was delivered by a single therapist, which may also have introduced potential therapist-related bias. Furthermore, the follow-up period was relatively short (one month), which does not capture outcomes up to childbirth. Finally, the single-region design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies with longer follow-up periods and more diverse populations are recommended to confirm the durability and external validity of the results.

Conclusion

Both in-person CBT and ICBT were effective in reducing fear of childbirth compared with the control group, showing substantial effect sizes across most W-DEQ-A subscales. In-person CBT showed additional superiority over ICBT only in total W-DEQ-A score, lack of self-efficacy, and lack of positive anticipation, while the two interventions were comparable on other subscales. Importantly, both interventions also led to a significant shift in delivery mode preference, with the proportion of women preferring vaginal delivery increasing markedly, particularly in the in-person CBT group (from 8.7% to 60.9%), whereas the control group’s preference for cesarean section remained high. These findings highlight that ICBT provides an effective and accessible alternative, while in-person CBT may offer extra benefits for enhancing overall childbirth confidence, positive expectations, and preference for natural delivery.
