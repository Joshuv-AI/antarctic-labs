Long-term Outcomes of Gender Decisions in Differences of Sex Development (DSD) from an Indian Perspective: A Multicenter Study
Authors: Shilpa Sharma, Pradnya S. Bendre, Sandip Kumar Rahul, Amar Shah, Santosh B. Kurbet, Ramesh Babu, Arka Banerjee, Rupesh Keshri, Anirudh Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Gender assignment is a central and often urgent decision in the management of differences of sex development (DSD). With growing emphasis on patient-centered care, there is an increasing need to evaluate how early gender assignment and surgical decisions influence long-term outcomes. This national multicenter study aimed to describe the DSD spectrum in India and assess gender trajectory, psychosocial well-being, and clinical outcomes into adolescence.Methods:This retrospective cohort included individuals who underwent surgical correction for DSD during infancy or childhood. Clinicians completed a structured pro forma capturing demographic characteristics, DSD diagnosis, treatment, complications, final sex assignment, gender identity outcomes, adolescent and parental agreement scores, psychological comorbidities, and fertility status. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Chi-square tests, nonparametric correlations, and simplified logistic regression, with significance set at P < 0.05.Results:Thirty patients (median age of 13 years) were included. Sex of rearing was significantly associated with final sex (χ2 = 14.46, P = 0.00014), and DSD type showed an even stronger association (χ2 = 26.19, P = 2.9 × 10−5). Parental agreement correlated strongly with adolescent gender agreement (ρ = 0.66, P = 0.0012), while gender dysphoria correlated negatively with parental agreement (ρ = −0.39, P = 0.033). Gender agreement did not differ by sex of rearing (P = 0.51). Surgical complications occurred in 26.7% and psychological comorbidities in 23.3%. Overall, 86.7% of adolescents affirmed with their assigned gender.Conclusion:This study provides essential long-term outcome data from India, demonstrating that most early gender assignments remain stable, while a minority experience dysphoria requiring reassessment. The findings underscore the need for ongoing family-centered, multidisciplinary follow-up into adolescence and beyond.

INTRODUCTION

Differences of sex development (DSD) encompass a heterogeneous group of congenital conditions in which chromosomal, gonadal, or anatomical sex is atypical, often manifesting at birth or around puberty through ambiguous genitalia, discordant pubertal development, or infertility. The 2005–2006 International Consensus Conference replaced earlier, often stigmatizing terms such as “intersex” and “hermaphroditism” with the umbrella term DSD, emphasizing multidisciplinary, family-centered care, shared decision-making, and long-term outcome evaluation as central pillars of management.[12] Subsequent guidelines and reviews have refined diagnostic algorithms, underscored the importance of early yet thoughtful sex assignment, and advocated for care within specialized centers equipped with expertise in pediatric endocrinology, urology, surgery, psychology, and ethics.[345]

Over the past two decades, outcome research in DSD has broadened to include gender identity, body image, sexual function, fertility potential, and quality of life (QoL) alongside traditional surgical and hormonal endpoints.[36789] Large European initiatives such as dsd-LIFE and more recent international reviews on long-term outcomes in 46,XX and 46,XY DSD have shown that while many individuals report stable gender identity and satisfactory QoL, a considerable subset experience psychosocial difficulties, sexual dysfunction, and mental health challenges.[10111213] Persistent questions remain regarding the optimal timing and extent of genital surgery, the risk–benefit balance of early feminizing or masculinizing procedures, and the need for systematic, lifelong follow-up.

Data from low- and middle-income countries – where sociocultural pressures surrounding binary gender, marriage, and fertility are particularly strong – remain scarce. Early Indian studies on ambiguous genitalia and DSD identified diagnostic delays, limited access to multidisciplinary care, and challenges in sex assignment within the framework of family expectations and cultural norms. Subsequent clinic-based series from dedicated pediatric intersex/DSD clinics described gender assignment patterns, decision-making algorithms, molecular diagnosis, and outcomes of male genitoplasty, providing valuable local evidence to guide clinical practice.[14151617] More recent Indian studies have evaluated long-term psychosocial adjustment, stakeholder perceptions, and adult QoL, highlighting the impact of sociocultural and economic factors on outcomes and care pathways. Despite this progress, robust long-term outcome data from India remain sparse, especially those integrating surgical results, gender trajectories, psychosocial health, and fertility within a single cohort. Many existing reports are restricted to specific etiologies (e.g., 46,XX congenital adrenal hyperplasia [CAH]) or age groups, are retrospective in nature, and include incomplete psychosocial data. Further, there is a growing need to understand how earlier Indian approaches to sex assignment and genitoplasty, now reconsidered in light of international discourse on autonomy and bodily integrity, have translated into adult outcomes in our context.

The present multicenter national study was undertaken to address the paucity of long-term outcome data in individuals with DSD in India. Its objectives were to characterize the spectrum of DSD managed across participating centers; document patterns of sex of rearing, sex assignment, and final gender identity; and evaluate long-term surgical, psychosocial, sexual, and fertility outcomes as reported by treating clinicians. Specifically, we aimed to assess concordance between sex of rearing and final gender, examine complications following early surgical interventions, and quantify psychiatric and psychological morbidity during adolescence and early adulthood. These findings are contextualized within global literature and previous Indian studies. The overarching aim was to determine whether early decisions made during infancy or childhood have a measurable impact on long-term outcomes and adult well-being.

METHODS

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institute Ethics Committee (AIIMSA00863/05.03.2024, RP-29/2024).

This was a multicentric, retrospective cohort study conducted across several medical centers with established expertise in the management of DSD. This multicentric study included six tertiary care institutions spanning the northern, eastern, western, and southern regions of India (New Delhi, Jharkhand, Mumbai, Ahmedabad, Belagavi, and Chennai), ensuring broad geographic representation. Eligible participants were individuals who had undergone surgical correction for DSD during infancy or childhood and had a minimum follow-up of 6 years from initial presentation; patients or parents who declined participation were excluded.

Treating clinicians completed a structured pro forma for each patient, capturing demographic details, DSD diagnosis, treatments received, surgical procedures and complications, gender outcomes, psychosocial well-being, fertility information, and patient and parental agreement with the assigned gender. Data collection was conducted over 6 months. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, subgroup comparisons, Chi-square tests, and simplified logistic regression models, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

The study included 30 patients with a mean age at last follow-up of 15.5 ± 8.6 years (median 13, interquartile range: 8.4–23.1). Twenty-eight patients had a follow-up of more than 10 years. Sex assignment occurred predominantly at birth, most often decided by the family (n = 16), followed by general practitioners (n = 5) and DSD specialists (n = 4), with an additional five children assigned in childhood by DSD experts. The diagnostic spectrum was dominated by 46,XX DSD due to 21-hydroxylase deficiency (n = 19), with smaller groups of 46,XY DSD including partial androgen insensitivity syndrome (PAIS) (n = 5), complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (CAIS) (n = 1), other uncommon variants (n = 4), and a single case of 46,XX ovotesticular DSD. Final sex outcome was predominantly female, with 21 (70%) individuals ultimately accepted, assigned, or choosing a female sex and 9 (30%) identifying as male [Figure 2]. By adolescence, 26 (86.7%) participants reported a gender identity concordant with their sex of rearing or initial assignment, while 2 (6.7%) underwent gender conversion and 2 (6.7%) remained undecided, indicating a high degree of alignment between early sex assignment and later gender identity. There was strong overall concordance between sex of rearing and final gender: of 20 individuals reared female, 19 identified as female, and 1 transitioned to male, whereas among 10 reared male, 8 remained male, and 2 transitioned to female. In total, 27 of 30 participants (90%) retained the same gender as their sex of rearing, and 3 (10%) showed discordance or reassignment, including one childhood reassignment in a 46,XX CAH case initially reared as a boy and two adolescent changes (one PAIS initially assigned female who virilized at puberty and one 46,XX CAH individual reared male who later affirmed a female identity despite prior male genitoplasty). The association between sex of rearing and final sex was strong and statistically significant (χ2 = 14.46, P ≈ 0.00014). Final sex outcomes were closely related to underlying DSD diagnosis. All individuals with 46,XX CAH (21-hydroxylase deficiency) were living as female (19/19; 100%), indicating complete concordance between diagnosis and final gender. Among those with 46,XY PAIS, 4 of 5 (80%) were living as male, and participants with other uncommon 46,XY variants and ovotesticular DSD were also predominantly male. The association between DSD type and final sex was highly statistically significant (χ2 = 26.19, P ≈ 0.000029), highlighting the strong influence of biological diagnosis on eventual gender outcome. Gender agreement scores in adolescence indicated generally high satisfaction: 46.7% reported the maximum score of 5 and 20% scored 4, with final gender approval evident in 86.7% of adolescents; only one adolescent reported a low score,[2] and 30% had no assessment recorded. Parental agreement scores were slightly higher overall, with 60% scoring 5, 16.7% scoring 4, 10% reporting moderate agreement, and 13.3% not assessed. A moderately strong positive correlation was observed between adolescent gender agreement and parental agreement (Spearman ρ = 0.66, P ≈ 0.0012), suggesting that greater parental acceptance is associated with higher adolescent gender comfort [Figure 3].

Most participants received combined medical and surgical treatment, with 24 individuals (80%) undergoing both modalities, 5 (16.7%) having surgery alone, and 1 (3.3%) managed with medical therapy only [Table 2]. Feminizing genitoplasty was the most frequent procedure (18 patients; 60%), followed by gonadal surgery with hypospadias repair (13.3%), hypospadias repair alone (10%), and less common combinations such as feminizing plus gonadal surgery, gonadal surgery alone, and feminizing plus hypospadias repair (each 3.3%); two individuals (6.7%) had no surgery. Surgical complications were documented in 8 (26.7%) patients, while 22 (73.3%) reported no postoperative complications [Table 2]. Table 3 summarizes long-term gender, psychosocial, sexual, and fertility outcomes. Psychiatric or psychological comorbidity was present in 7 (23.3%) patients, with gender dysphoria and generalized anxiety disorder each accounting for 10% and depression for 3.3%. The presence of psychological concerns appeared relevant to final gender decisions, as both individuals who underwent gender conversion had documented gender dysphoria, whereas most of those who remained aligned with their assigned or reared gender (21/26) had no psychiatric diagnosis, although a minority reported anxiety or depression.

The first logistic regression model assessed whether sex of rearing, final sex, and adolescent gender agreement predicted psychiatric or psychological comorbidity, but overall model fit was modest and nonsignificant (pseudo R2 = 0.12, LLR P = 0.26), and no individual predictor reached significance. Gender agreement showed a positive but nonsignificant trend (β = 0.37, P = 0.18), suggesting a possible but unconfirmed relationship between lower agreement and higher psychological problems. Most individuals were unmarried at assessment (25; 83.3%); 2 (6.7%) were married and happy, 2 (6.7%) were in unmarried relationships, and 1 (3.3%) was married but separated or divorced. Sexual function data were sparse: 24 (80%) participants had no assessment recorded, while among the six with documented scores, five reported a maximum score of 5 and one a score of 1, suggesting good function in most cases. Fertility outcomes were largely unknown or not applicable: for 20 (66.7%) participants, fertility assessment was not relevant, 8 (26.7%) had unknown status, 1 (3.3%) PAIS had become a father, and 1 CAIS (3.3%) had a child through surrogacy from an egg donor. Fertility and sexual outcomes were evaluable in only a minority of patients, largely reflecting the young age of the cohort and the consequent inability to perform meaningful long-term assessment.

An inverse association between gender dysphoria scores and parental agreement (ρ = −0.39, P ≈ 0.033) indicated that higher dysphoria tended to occur where parental alignment with the adolescent’s expressed gender needs was lower. In line with this, both individuals with gender conversion had gender dysphoria, whereas stable, approved gender outcomes were more common among those without significant psychiatric comorbidity, suggesting that persistent distress and limited parental support may contribute to gender identity reevaluation in DSD care.

DISCUSSION

This multicenter national study offers much-needed long-term data on Indian individuals with DSD, delineating patterns of sex assignment, gender outcomes, and the downstream consequences of early clinical decisions. It shows how childhood management – including sex of rearing and early surgical interventions – can influence later surgical, psychological, sexual, and fertility outcomes.

This national study adds to the limited long-term outcome data on DSD from India and extends earlier single-center intersex/DSD clinic reports.[37] The cohort was predominantly composed of individuals with 46,XX CAH, in keeping with international series where CAH is the most common cause of DSD presenting with genital ambiguity.[81819202122] High concordance was observed between sex of rearing and final gender identity, with 90% of individuals ultimately living in the gender in which they were reared and 86.7% demonstrating gender approval in adolescence; both the association between sex of rearing and final sex (χ2 = 14.46, P = 0.00014) and between DSD diagnosis and final sex (χ2 = 26.19, P = 0.000029) were statistically significant, and prior Indian work has shown that most parents (95%) prefer sex assignment soon after birth.[23] These results align with long-term data in 46,XX CAH, where the vast majority of patients reared as female retain a female gender identity in adulthood, and support the durability of gender assignment decisions made in multidisciplinary, high-volume centers in the Indian context.[24]

Gender outcomes and mental health

The small subgroup that underwent gender conversion carried a disproportionate burden of gender dysphoria and other psychiatric morbidity, echoing European data in which gender-incongruent individuals report poorer mental health and lower QoL. These findings underscore the importance of shared decision-making at initial sex assignment, including explicit discussion of uncertainties and the need to preserve space for later reassessment. There was clear, statistically significant concordance between adolescents’ gender agreement and parental agreement, with most families showing high mutual acceptance and only a minority reporting lower scores; in general, parental views tracked adolescents’ experiences rather than opposing them. Psychiatric and psychological problems – including gender dysphoria, anxiety, and depression – were documented in nearly one-quarter of the cohort, and although the simplified logistic model did not identify significant predictors (likely due to limited power), higher gender disagreement tended to cluster with psychiatric diagnoses. Adolescent gender agreement correlated moderately with parental agreement (ρ = 0.60, P = 0.00049), while higher dysphoria scores were inversely associated with parental agreement (ρ = −0.39, P = 0.033), consistent with international evidence that family support and parental acceptance are key protective factors for mental health in DSD and with Indian data showing that diagnostic uncertainty and dilemmas around gender assignment heighten parental stress.[15162526] These observations strongly support routine psychosocial screening and structured family counseling as core components of DSD care, in line with contemporary psychosocial frameworks.[27]

Surgical and functional outcomes

From a surgical perspective, just over a quarter of patients experienced documented complications such as fistula, stenosis, or scarring. Complication rates appeared highest in those undergoing combined hypospadias and gonadal surgery and lowest in isolated procedures, although the association between broad surgical category and complications was not statistically significant (P = 0.81). Overall rates were comparable to those reported after complex feminizing or masculinizing genitoplasty in other cohorts, and long-term follow-up of feminizing surgery in 46,XX CAH has shown that even in expert centers, up to one-third of women may report cosmetic or functional concerns or unsatisfactory sexual life despite general acceptance of early surgery. Sexual function was rarely assessed formally, with scores documented in only a minority of adults, mirroring global concerns about underreporting and underassessment of sexual health in DSD; this is in line with non-CAH long-term outcome reports and other international series.[282930]

Unmet needs and ethical landscape

Reassuringly, the cohort overall showed stable gender identity and high levels of gender approval, particularly among 46,XX CAH individuals reared female, concordant with contemporary long-term outcome data.[11121315] Nevertheless, the presence of nontrivial psychiatric burden, constrained fertility options, and limited documentation of sexual function and relationship outcomes highlights persisting unmet needs as DSD individuals transition into adulthood. Recent Indian young-adult DSD cohorts have further emphasized suicidal ideation, body image concerns, and ambivalence about previous surgery and its timing, especially in complex 46,XY DSD, and the present study, although smaller, supports these concerns and underlines the need for structured transition services linking pediatric and adult care. These findings must also be interpreted within an evolving ethical landscape: international debates increasingly question routine irreversible genital surgery in infancy when gender outcome is uncertain and advocate deferring nonessential procedures until the individual can participate in decisions. In India, where sociocultural pressures for early gender “normalization” and marriage are strong, balancing early surgery for social integration against respect for bodily autonomy is particularly challenging; historical Indian series show that carefully planned early genitoplasty can yield acceptable outcomes in many cases,[567] but newer long-term data and adult cohorts point to the need for transparent risk communication, robust psychosocial support, and mechanisms for ongoing review.[89101819]

Over the 15-year span of this cohort, shared decision-making in differences of sex development transitioned from a predominantly clinician-directed model to a structured, multidisciplinary, patient-centered process. Earlier, decisions were often led by surgical teams, with parents making early choices based on limited counseling and minimal involvement from psychology, genetics, or ethics services. More recently, shared decision-making has incorporated coordinated multidisciplinary evaluations, repeated family counseling, psychosocial support, and greater emphasis on informed consent, assent from maturing patients, and the option to defer irreversible interventions. This “then versus now” shift reflects evolving ethical standards and strengthens the study’s relevance to contemporary multidisciplinary DSD care. The findings in this study should therefore be interpreted within the context of rapidly evolving ethical and multidisciplinary standards in DSD care.

Strengths and limitations

The findings of this cohort must be interpreted within the context of rapidly evolving paradigms in differences of sex development (DSD), particularly the transition from clinician-directed models toward multidisciplinary, shared decision-making frameworks that emphasize patient autonomy and longitudinal psychosocial support. Historically, most children in this cohort underwent sex assignment in infancy or early childhood, consistent with prevailing practice at the time; however, contemporary consensus increasingly supports developmentally appropriate disclosure and, when feasible, participation of the affected individual in decision-making. Disorder-specific outcome data further highlight the heterogeneity of gender trajectories in DSD. For example, gender dysphoria in virilized 46XX CAH remains relatively uncommon (~3%–5%), although increased rates of male-typical behavior and modestly higher prevalence of nonheterosexual orientation have been reported.[3132] In contrast, individuals with 5α-reductase type 2 deficiency-assigned female demonstrate some of the highest rates of later gender transition (approximately 40%–60%), likely reflecting prenatal androgen imprinting, while gender variability in androgen insensitivity syndromes correlates with the degree of virilization.[3334] These observations reinforce the importance of individualized counseling, cautious prognostication, and long-term follow-up when supporting families through complex sex-assignment decisions.[35]

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design spanning 15 years, a period during which diagnostic capabilities, surgical philosophies, ethical frameworks, and shared decision-making practices in DSD care evolved substantially, thereby limiting direct temporal comparisons. The cohort predominantly reflects early childhood sex assignment and was not designed to evaluate outcomes following delayed or adolescent assignment. Psychological comorbidities were derived from documented psychiatric assessments rather than uniformly applied validated instruments, potentially introducing assessment variability. In addition, incomplete historical documentation restricted detailed genotype–phenotype correlations, including Prader staging and salt-wasting status in CAH. The relatively small representation of conditions such as mixed gonadal dysgenesis and androgen insensitivity syndromes further constrained disorder-specific inference regarding long-term gender identity, fertility potential, and psychosexual outcomes, while limited follow-up precluded definitive conclusions about adult gender trajectories. Its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias. The modest sample size limits the power of subgroup and regression analyses and leads to wide confidence intervals and convergence issues. Moreover, clinician-reported, partly retrospective data resulted in missing information for sexual function, fertility, and psychosocial domains. The absence of systematic patient-reported outcome measures and standardized quality-of-life instruments – now widely recommended in DSD research – is another important limitation.[1011121319] This retrospective cohort spans 15 years during which diagnostic capabilities, surgical philosophies, ethical frameworks, and shared decision-making practices in DSD care evolved substantially; these temporal shifts may limit direct comparison across the study period. Despite these constraints, this multicenter study provides one of the few integrated overviews of surgical, gender, and psychosocial outcomes from India, extending earlier single-institution clinic-based work and broadly aligning local experience with global trends.

Future directions

Going forward, there is a clear need for prospective, longitudinal registries of DSD patients in India that integrate genetic, endocrine, surgical, and psychosocial data, including patient-reported outcomes with standardized psychological metrics to better inform evidence-based, patient-centered DSD care. Patients of DSD should be managed only by those who have experience with a large number of patients, preferably in tertiary centers so that reliable follow-up data are available.[3637] There is a need for collaboration with international initiatives such as dsd-LIFE to benchmark Indian outcomes against global cohorts. Developing structured transition programs, culturally sensitive counseling, and sustained partnerships with patient advocacy groups will be critical for improving long-term QoL, and the present study offers an important baseline against which to evaluate future shifts in practice – such as more cautious early surgery, stronger emphasis on shared decision-making, and expanded fertility options.

Long-term follow-up studies of feminizing surgery in 46,XX CAH have shown that even in expert centers, up to one-third of women may report suboptimal cosmesis, functional limitations, or unsatisfactory sexual life, despite overall acceptance of early surgery.[27] Sexual function was seldom formally assessed in this cohort, with outcomes documented for only a small subset of adults – echoing global concerns about persistent underreporting and inadequate evaluation of sexual health in DSD.[2930]

The management of DSD should follow structured, context-appropriate guidelines, and there remains a clear need for more data on fertility outcomes to inform counseling and preservation strategies. For future long-term outcome and longitudinal studies, the authors propose the use of validated, anonymous tools like standardized QoL scales to benchmark Indian outcomes against international cohorts. The Indian Association of Pediatric Surgeons guidelines emphasize multidisciplinary, individualized care for children with DSD, providing consensus recommendations on diagnostic workup, sex assignment, timing of surgery, and long-term follow-up in the Indian setting.[38] Complementing these practice guidelines, emerging work on testicular cytology and semen analysis in individuals with 46,XY DSD underscores that spermatogenesis and ejaculate parameters are variably preserved, highlighting the importance of systematically evaluating gonadal function and incorporating fertility considerations into routine DSD management and future research agendas.[39]

This study contributes important long-term outcome data from India, showing that early gender assignment decisions are generally sustained over time, with only a smaller subset later experiencing gender dysphoria and needing reevaluation. These observations reinforce the imperative for continued, family-centered, multidisciplinary follow-up through adolescence and adulthood, so that emerging concerns about gender identity or psychosocial adjustment can be identified and addressed promptly.

CONCLUSION

This study offers valuable long-term outcome data from the Indian context, highlighting that the majority of early gender assignments remain consistent over time. However, a small but significant proportion of individuals develop gender dysphoria, necessitating careful reassessment and individualized management. These findings emphasize that gender assignment in early life should not be viewed as a one-time decision, but rather as part of a dynamic, longitudinal process. They reinforce the importance of sustained, family-centered care delivered through a multidisciplinary team—encompassing pediatric surgeons, endocrinologists, psychologists, and social support systems—with continued follow-up extending into adolescence and adulthood to ensure optimal psychosocial and clinical outcomes.
