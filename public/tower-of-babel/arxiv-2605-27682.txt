Abstract
We study the problem of determining a matrix whose k k th multiplicative compound is a prescribed matrix M M . The cardinality of the set of matrices whose k k th multiplicative compound equals M M is characterized in terms of rank ⁡ ( M ) \rank(M) . On the one hand, if rank ⁡ ( M ) ≤ 1 \rank(M)\leq 1 , it is shown that there exist infinitely many such matrices for which a complete characterization is determined. On the other hand, if rank ⁡ ( M ) > 1 \rank(M)>1 , then there exists a unique matrix — up to an overall sign — whose compound is M M . An algorithm for finding a matrix whose compound equals M M is detailed, and its time complexity is analyzed.

Introduction

Compound matrices are a classical object in linear and multilinear algebra, appearing in a range of applications, including geometry
[
10
]
, graph theory
[
9
,
4
]
, algebraic topology
[
21
]
, numerical analysis
[
26
,
1
]
, and electric network theory
[
6
]
. More recently, they have proven useful in dynamical systems and control theory
[
5
]
. Following the seminal works of Schwarz
[
32
]
and Li and Muldowney
[
25
,
22
]
, which introduced compound matrices into this setting, there has been growing interest in their use for studying linear and nonlinear systems. System operators with so-called variation-diminishing and -bounding properties have been studied in
[
16
,
12
,
14
,
15
]
with applications to model order reduction
[
13
]
, frequency domain analysis
[
11
]
, bounds on self-sustained oscillations
[
33
]
, as well as sparse optimization
[
23
]
. Autonomous system analysis brought forward new results on the global stability of systems with multiple equilibrium points
[
37
,
3
]
, with applications ranging from power systems
[
7
]
, to opinion dynamics
[
28
]
and neural networks
[
31
]
.

All of these results follow a common paradigm: a classical condition formulated in terms of a matrix (e.g., the Jacobian of the vector field of an autonomous system or the system operators associated with an input-output system) is lifted to a condition on its
k
th compound. As a concrete example, a linear time-invariant system
x(t+1)=Ax(t)
has been called
k
-positive if all entries of the
k
th multiplicative compound of
A
have the same sign
[
2
]
. Since the signs of the entries of a matrix and its compounds are not invariant under coordinate transformations, one may ask whether there exists a coordinate transformation that makes a given system
k
-positive (see also
[
35
]
which studies a related problem in the continuous-time setting and for a restricted class of coordinate transformations). This naturally leads to the question of whether a given matrix
M
can be realized as the
k
th multiplicative compound of some matrix
A
, and, if so, how such a matrix can be recovered from
M
.

Despite the long history of compound matrices, this inverse problem has not been considered before to the best of the authors’ knowledge. Given a matrix
M
of appropriate dimensions, two fundamental questions arise:
(i) what are necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a matrix
A
whose
k
th multiplicative compound is equal
M
?
(ii) assuming such a matrix exists, how can one characterize and compute all matrices
A
satisfying this relation?

In this paper, we address the second question. We provide a complete characterization of the preimage of the
k
th multiplicative compound operator, showing in particular that the preimage is unique — up to an overall sign — under certain conditions, and that otherwise it contains infinitely many matrices. We then develop a constructive algorithm for recovering all matrices
A
whose
k
th compound equals
M
, and show that the computational complexity of the algorithm is polynomial in the dimension of the matrix. A MATLAB implementation of the proposed algorithms is available at
https://github.com/debojyoti23/inverse-compound
.

\det(X_{\{2,3\},\{1,2\}})&\det(X_{\{2,3\},\{1,3\}})&\det(X_{\{2,3\},\{2,3\}})\end{pmatrix}

\comp_{k}(AB)=\comp_{k}(A)\comp_{k}(B)\ \ (k\leq\min\{m,n,p\})

(1)

For a compound matrix

M(I,J)

]

Lemma 1
.

Let
A\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times m}
.

1.

If
n=m
, then
A
is non-singular if and only if
\comp_{k}(A)
is non-singular, in which case
\comp_{k}(A^{-1})=\comp_{k}(A)^{-1}
.

2.

For any
t\in{\mathds{R}}
,
\comp_{k}(tA)=t^{k}\comp_{k}(A)
.

3.

If
n=m
, then the eigenvalues of
\comp_{k}(A)
are given by

\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}(A)
,
I\in{\mathcal{I}}_{n,k}
.
The same holds for singular values and arbitrary
n,m
.

4.

If
\rank(A)=r
, then
\rank(\comp_{r}(A))=1
.

5.

For
\rank(A)<k\leq\min\{n,m\}
,
\comp_{k}(A)=0
.

6.

If
A
is diagonalizable, and

is a linearly independent set of eigenvectors of

, then
\{\comp_{k}(\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}&\cdots&u_{k}\end{bmatrix}):I\in{\mathcal{I}}_{n,k}\}
is a linearly independent set of

\comp_{k}(A)
. In particular,
\comp_{k}(A)
is diagonalizable.

7.

If
n=m

\det(\comp_{k}(A))=(\det(A))^{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}
. This is also known as the

.

An immediate consequence of item 3 of

is the following identity between the rank of a matrix and its compound.

Lemma 2
.

Let
A\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times m}
. For every
1\leq k\leq\rank(A)

\rank(\comp_{k}(A))=\rank(A)^{\binom{\rank(A)}{k}}.

The following result from

of a given adjugate matrix

.

Lemma 3

Let

, then
\adj(M)=(\det(M))^{\frac{n-2}{n-1}}A
.

Since
\adj(A)

(n-1)
-th order minors of

, we further get the following reciprocal relation between the adjugate matrix
\adj(A)
of

\comp_{n-1}(A)
.

Lemma 4
.

For
A\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times n}
,

\adj(\adj(A))=\comp_{n-1}(\comp_{n-1}(A))

2.2
Exterior Algebra and Compound

The
k
th exterior power
V^{(k)}
of an
n
-dimensional vector space
V
is a vector space of dimension
\binom{n}{k}
spanned by vectors of the form,

z=u_{1}\wedge u_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{k}\quad(u_{i}\in V)

where the product above is called an exterior or wedge product
[
36
]
.
In this paper, we focus on
V={\mathds{R}}^{n}
. Given the
canonical basis

\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
of
{\mathds{R}}^{n}
, the set

\{e_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_{k}}:(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in{\mathcal{I}}_{n,k}\}

is a basis of the
k
th exterior power
V^{(k)}
, where each basis vector corresponds to a
k
-dimensional oriented subspace. Let
U=[u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}]\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times n}
,
u_{i}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
, then the
i
th coordinate of
z
, as defined above, will be given by the
Grassmann notation
as

\displaystyle z_{I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})}=\det(U_{\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\},1:k})

(2)

where
I
is the
i
th element of
{\mathcal{I}}_{n,k}
. Thus,
\comp_{k}(U)\in{\mathds{R}}^{\binom{n}{k}\times\binom{n}{k}}
is a matrix with each column representing the coordinates of
k
-wedge
u_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge u_{i_{k}}
for all
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in{\mathcal{I}}_{n,k}
, lexicographically ordered.
The two primary properties
[
20
, Section
0.iii
]
of the wedge product are:

1.

multi-linearity, i.e.,
(\alpha u_{1}+\beta u_{2})\wedge u_{3}=\alpha(u_{1}\wedge u_{3})+\beta(u_{2}\wedge u_{3})
;

2.

anti-symmetry, i.e.,
u_{1}\wedge u_{2}=-u_{2}\wedge u_{1}
, giving us
u\wedge u=0
for any
u\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
.

By the Cauchy-Binet formula,
\comp_{k}(A)
can also be seen as a coordinate representation of

\comp_{k}(A)(x_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{k})=Ax_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge Ax_{k}

for all
x_{i}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
(see
[
20
, Section 0.iii]
). Wedge products are also closely related to linear independence, since
u_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{k}=0
if and only if
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
are linearly dependent.

A vector
z\in V^{(k)}
is called
decomposable
if it can be written as the wedge product of
k
vectors in
V
, i.e. if
z=u_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{k}

[
18
]
. A decomposable vector does not have a unique decomposition, but if a vector is non-zero and decomposable, then all decompositions span the same
k
-dimensional subspace in
V
. To be precise, suppose
z\in V^{(k)}
is decomposable and non-zero, then if
u_{1},\dots,u_{k},v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in V
are such that

z=u_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{k}=v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k},

then

\spanop\{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\}=\spanop\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}.

This follows from the fact that
x\wedge z=0
if and only if
x
is in the span of any decomposition of
z
.

3
Main Results

The main objective of this study is to compute the preimage of the multiplicative compound. To be precise, for fixed
n,m
and
k\leq\min\{n,m\}
, we call a matrix
M\in{\mathds{R}}^{\binom{n}{k}\times\binom{m}{k}}

compound-decomposable
if there exists a matrix
A\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times m}
such that
M=\comp_{k}(A)
. We also call
A
an
inverse-compound
of
M
. Our goal is to find an inverse-compound of a given compound-decomposable matrix. We begin by posing the question:

Does a compound-decomposable matrix always have a unique inverse-compound matrix?

If
k
is even, then by item 2 of
1
,
C_{k}(-A)=(-1)^{k}C_{k}(A)=C_{k}(A)
, i.e., for even
k
uniqueness is at most up to an overall sign. The following
1
shows that, in some cases, there are infinitely many matrices that all have the same compound.

Proposition 1
.

Let
A,B\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times m}
, and
r=\rank(A)
. Then, the following hold:

1.

if
k>r
, then
\comp_{k}(A)=\comp_{k}(B)\iff\rank(B)<k
.

2.

if
k=r
and
A=UV^{\top}
, with
U\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times r},V\in{\mathds{R}}^{m\times r}
, be a rank-revealing factorization of
A
, then
\comp_{k}(A)=\comp_{k}(B)
if and only if

B\in\{UTV^{\top}:T\in{\mathds{R}}^{r\times r}\text{ with }\det(T)=1\}.

(3)

Remark 1
.

1
can also be stated in terms of compound-decomposable matrices as follows. Let
M\in{\mathds{R}}^{\binom{n}{k}\times\binom{m}{k}}
be compound-decomposable. If
\rank(M)=0
, then
\comp_{k}(A)=M
if and only if
\rank(A)<k
. If
\rank(M)=1
, then there exist infinitely many matrices whose
k
th multiplicative compound equals
M
.

Proof.

If
k>r
, then all minors of order
k
of
A
are zero, so
C_{k}(A)=0
. The same is also true for every
B\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times m}
with
\rank(B)<k
.

Suppose now that
k=r
. We first show that for every rank-revealing factorization of
A
, the set in (
3
) remains the same. Let
A=UV^{\top}=\tilde{U}\tilde{V}^{\top}
, with
U,\tilde{U}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times r}
and
V,\tilde{V}\in{\mathds{R}}^{m\times r}
full column rank matrices, be two rank-revealing factorizations of
A
. Theorem 2 of
[
30
]
asserts that there exists an invertible matrix
R\in{\mathds{R}}^{r\times r}
such that
\tilde{U}=UR
and
\tilde{V}^{\top}=R^{-1}V^{\top}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\{\tilde{U}\tilde{T}\tilde{V}^{\top}:\tilde{T}\in{\mathds{R}}^{r\times r}\text{ with }\det(\tilde{T})=1\}

\displaystyle=\{UR\tilde{T}R^{-1}V^{\top}:\tilde{T}\in{\mathds{R}}^{r\times r}\text{ with }\det(\tilde{T})=1\}

\displaystyle=\{UTV^{\top}:T\in{\mathds{R}}^{r\times r}\text{ with }\det(T)=1\},

where the second equality uses the fact that
\det(R\tilde{T}R^{-1})=\det(R)\det(R^{-1})\det(\tilde{T})=\det(\tilde{T})
for every
\tilde{T}\in{\mathds{R}}^{r\times r}
. Hence, it remains to show that
B\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times m}
is such that
\comp_{r}(B)=\comp_{r}(A)
if and only if

B\in{\mathcal{S}}=\{UTV^{\top}:T\in{\mathds{R}}^{r\times r}\text{ with }\det(T)=1\}.

Suppose
B\in{\mathcal{S}}
, then by the multiplicativity of the compound

\comp_{r}(B)=\comp_{r}(UTV^{\top})=\comp_{r}(U)\det(T)\comp_{r}(V^{\top})=\comp_{r}(UV^{\top})=\comp_{r}(A),

where the second equality uses the fact that
\comp_{r}(T)=\det(T)
since
T\in\mathds{R}^{r\times r}
.

Conversely, let
\comp_{r}(B)=\comp_{r}(A)
. By
2
,
\rank(B)
is such that
\binom{\rank(B)}{r}=\binom{\rank(B)}{\rank(A)}=1
, i.e.,
\rank(B)=\rank(A)=r
. Let
B=U_{B}V_{B}^{\top}
, where
U_{B}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times r},V_{B}\in{\mathds{R}}^{m\times r}
have full column rank, be a rank-revealing factorization of
B
. Note that
\comp_{r}(U),\comp_{r}(U_{B}),\comp_{r}(V),\comp_{r}(V_{B})
are all column vectors. Furthermore, multiplicativity of the compound implies that

\comp_{r}(A)=\comp_{r}(U)\comp_{r}(V)^{\top}=\comp_{r}(U_{B})\comp_{r}(V_{B})^{\top}.

(4)

So the above are rank-revealing factorizations of
\comp_{r}(A)
.
Since
\comp_{r}(A)
has rank 1, its rank-revealing factorization is unique up to scaling, and so

\comp_{r}(U_{B})=c\comp_{r}(U)\quad\text{and}\quad\comp_{r}(V_{B})=c^{-1}\comp_{r}(V),

for some non-zero
c\in{\mathds{R}}
.
The vector
\comp_{r}(U)\in{\mathds{R}}^{\binom{n}{r}}
is a non-zero decomposable column vector, so
\spanop(U)=\spanop(U_{B})
and hence, there exists an invertible matrix
T_{1}\in{\mathds{R}}^{r\times r}
such that

U_{B}=UT_{1}.

However, since
\comp_{r}(U_{B})=c\comp_{r}(U)
, it follows that
\det(T_{1})=c
. Applying the same arguments to
V_{B}
implies that
V_{B}=VT_{2}
for some invertible
T_{2}\in{\mathds{R}}^{r\times r}
with
\det(T_{2})=c^{-1}
and we conclude that

B=U_{B}V_{B}^{\top}=UT_{1}T_{2}^{\top}V^{\top}.

Since
\det(T_{1}T_{2}^{\top})=1
, it follows that
B\in{\mathcal{S}}
, which completes the proof.
∎

.

.

which is why

We continue by turning our attention to finding an inverse-compound
A
for a prescribed compound-decomposable
M
. Consider first the special case where
k=n-1
and
M
is invertible. Then,
A
is also invertible by item 1 of
1
, and a closed-form expression for all inverse-compounds can be derived by
combining item
5
of
1
,
3
, and
4
as follows.

Corollary 1
.

Let
M\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times n}
,
n>1
, be compound-decomposable and
\det(M)\neq 0
, and

B:=\det(M)^{-\frac{n-2}{n-1}}\comp_{n-1}(M)\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times n}.

Then
A\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times n}
is an inverse-compound of
M
, i.e.,
\comp_{n-1}(A)=M
, if and only if
A=B
when
n
is even and
A=\pm B
when
n
is odd.

Unfortunately, for a general
A\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times m}
and
k\leq\min(m,n)
, we are unaware of any existing closed-form solution to
A
given
M=\comp_{k}(A)
. In lieu of closed-form solutions, our main result is a constructive proof for the uniqueness, up to overall sign, of the inverse-compound of
M
when
\rank(M)>1
, and an algorithm to recover the set of all inverse-compounds of
M
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
A\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times m}
and
r:=\rank(A)
. Then, the following hold:

1.

if
k<r
and
B\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times m}
fulfills
C_{k}(A)=C_{k}(B)
, then
A=B
if
k
is odd and
A=\pm B
if
k
is even. Moreover, there exists an algorithm whose input is
C_{k}(A)
and returns as an output
cA
for any
c\in\{-1,1\}
fulfilling
c^{k}=1
. The time complexity of the algorithm is given by

(a)

\mathcal{O}\left(\max_{p\in\{m,n\}}\left[\binom{p}{k}^{3}+\binom{p}{k}^{2}\binom{r}{k}\right]\right)
if
k\leq\lceil\rank(A)/2\rceil

(b)

\mathcal{O}\left(\max_{p\in\{m,n\}}\left[\binom{p}{k}^{3}+\binom{p}{k}^{2}\binom{r}{k}+\binom{r}{r/2}^{2}pk^{2}\log r\right]\right)

(otherwise)

2.

For
k=r
, there exists an algorithm that determines an element from the set of inverse-compounds, as defined in
1
, in time complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\max_{p\in\{m,n\}}\left[\binom{p}{k}^{2}\binom{r}{k}\right]\right)
.

Note that given
\comp_{k}(A)
, any of its inverse-compounds has the same rank due to
2
. In particular, the assumption
k<\rank(A)
is equivalent to
\rank(M)>1
.

Remark 2
.

For the special case of a square matrix, where
\comp_{k}(A)\in{\mathds{R}}^{\binom{n}{k}\times\binom{n}{k}}
is invertible and
k>n/2
, there is an alternative method based on
[
8
]
for computing an inverse compound with better time complexity than applying
1
directly. Let
U\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\binom{n}{k}\times\binom{n}{k}}
be the matrix from
[
8
, Definition 3]
, which is orthonormal, i.e.,
U^{\top}U=UU^{\top}=I
. Then, by
[
8
, Theorem 8]
,

\comp_{k}(A)^{\top}U^{\top}\comp_{n-k}(A)U=\det(A)I,

(6)

so

\left(\comp_{n-k}(A)\right)^{-1}=\det(A)^{-1}U\comp_{k}(A)^{\top}U^{\top}.

(7)

Finally, since
k>n/2
, i.e.,
n-k<n/2
, computing an inverse-compound for
\left(\comp_{n-k}(A)\right)^{-1}
using
1
has better time complexity, and the matrix inverse of the resulting inverse-compound is an inverse-compound of
\comp_{k}(A)
by item 1 of
1
.

The algorithm in
1
is based on a recovery method that exploits the multiplicative property of compounds and the

reduced SVD
(also known as compact SVD): for
A\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times m}
with
\rank(A)=r

A=V\Sigma W^{\top}\implies M:=\comp_{k}(A)=\comp_{k}(V)\comp_{k}(\Sigma)\comp_{k}(W)^{\top},

(8)

where
\Sigma\in{\mathds{R}}^{r\times r}
has positive real singular values of
A
on its diagonal, and the corresponding left and right singular vectors are the columns of the orthonormal matrices
V\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times r}
and
W\in{\mathds{R}}^{m\times r}
, respectively.
The main idea is to recover the singular vectors (left and right) and singular values of an inverse-compound matrix
A
from those of the given compound-decomposable matrix
M
, which then allows us to compose
A
as above.
A detailed proof of
1
is deferred to
Section

4
, following our discussion of all the involved components.

To begin with, we explain why it is important and, without loss of generality, possible to assume that the non‑zero singular values of
M
are distinct.

Remark 3
.

While every wedge product of
k
linearly independent singular vectors of
A
is a singular vector of
\comp_{k}(A)
, if
\comp_{k}(A)
has repeating singular values, then it will have singular vectors which are
not
decomposable. This is because if
u,v
are linearly independent singular vectors to the same singular value, every orthonormal basis of
\spanop\{u,v\}
consists of singular vectors, which are generally not decomposable.
2
illustrates this fact.

Example 2
.

For
M=I_{6}
, it follows from
I_{6}=\comp_{2}(I_{4})
that
M
is compound-decomposable. However, if
Q\in{\mathds{R}}^{6\times 6}
is an orthonormal matrix whose first column is

q=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

then
QIQ^{\top}
is an SVD of
I_{6}
, where
Q
is not compound-decomposable, i.e, there is no matrix
S\in{\mathds{R}}^{4\times 4}
such that
\comp_{2}(S)=Q
. To see this, assume by contradiction that such a matrix does exist, and let
u,v\in{\mathds{R}}^{4}
be its first two columns, i.e.,

\comp_{2}(\begin{bmatrix}u&v\end{bmatrix})=q.

In particular, it must hold that

\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}&v_{1}\\
u_{3}&v_{3}\end{bmatrix}\right)=0,

i.e.,
\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}&u_{3}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
and
\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}&v_{3}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
are linearly dependent. The same must be true for
\begin{bmatrix}u_{2}&u_{3}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
and
\begin{bmatrix}v_{2}&v_{3}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
. However, this implies that
\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}&u_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
and
\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}&v_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
are linearly dependent and, therefore,

\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}&v_{1}\\
u_{2}&v_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)=0\neq q_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},

which is a contradiction.

To avoid getting non-decomposable singular vectors of
M=\comp_{k}(A)
by reduced SVD, it is sufficient to have
M
with distinct positive singular values. In case
M
does not satisfy the requirement, the following
2
provides a preprocessing routine to ensure this property.

Proposition 2
.

Let
M\in{\mathds{R}}^{\binom{n}{k}\times\binom{m}{k}}
be a compound-decomposable matrix. Then, for almost all
Q\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times n}
, the non-zero singular values of
\comp_{k}(Q)M
are distinct.

Proof.

The claim is trivial for
M=0
. Therefore, let us suppose that
\comp_{k}(A)=M\neq 0
with
\rank(M)\geq 1
. Since it follows from
2
that
r=\rank(A)\geq k
, let a reduced SVD of
A
be given by
A=U\Sigma V^{\top}
, i.e.,
U^{\top}U=V^{\top}V=I
and
\Sigma\in\mathds{R}^{r\times r}
is diagonal and positive definite. Then, since for any
Q\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times n}
the singular values of
\comp_{k}(Q)M
are the square roots of the eigenvalues of

(\comp_{k}(Q)M)^{\top}(\comp_{k}(Q)M)=\comp_{k}(V)\comp_{k}(\Sigma U^{\top}Q^{\top}QU\Sigma)\comp_{k}(V)^{\top},

where
\comp_{k}(V)^{\top}\comp_{k}(V)=I
, our claim follows if we can show that
B:=\comp_{k}(\Sigma U^{\top}Q^{\top}QU\Sigma)
has no repeated eigenvalues for almost all
Q\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times n}
.

To this end, note that as each entry of
\Sigma U^{\top}Q^{\top}QU\Sigma
is a polynomial of the entries of
Q
, the same must apply to
B
. Hence, by
[
29
]
the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial of
\comp_{k}(B)
is also a polynomial of the entries of
Q
. In particular, if we can show that the this polynomial is not identical to zero, then it follows by
[
24
]
or
[
17
, Coroally 10, p. 9]
, that the set of
Q
that make the discriminant zero, i.e., cause repeated eigenvalues, is of Lebesgue measure zero. To see this, we will show that there exists an
\alpha\in\mathds{R}^{r}
such that
Q=S\Sigma^{-1}U^{\top}
with

S=\begin{bmatrix}D\\
0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathds{R}^{n\times r}\quad\text{and}\quad D:=\diag(\exp(\alpha))\in\mathds{R}^{r\times r},

leads to non-repeated eigenvalues of
B=\comp_{k}(S^{\top}S)
. In particular, by item 3 of
1
,
B
has repeated eigenvalues
e^{2\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}}
,
I\in{\mathcal{I}}_{r,k}
, if and only if

\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}=\sum_{j\in J}\alpha_{j}

(9)

for some
I,J\in{\mathcal{I}}_{r,k}
such that
I\neq J
. Since
{\mathds{R}}^{r}
is not the union of a finite number of subspaces, such
I
and
J
cannot exist for all
\alpha\in{\mathds{R}}^{r}
, which is why our claim follows.
∎

2
facilitates the pre-processing of
M
, i.e., one can first solve the inverse-compound problem for an alternative compound-decomposable
\tilde{M}=\comp_{k}(Q)M
, with an arbitrary invertible
Q\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times n}
such that
\tilde{M}
has distinct non-zero singular values. We can then obtain
A=Q^{-1}\tilde{A}
by finding a matrix
\tilde{A}
with
\tilde{M}=\comp_{k}(\tilde{A})
, which solves our original inverse-compound problem.
In our discussion on finding an inverse-compound, we, therefore, make the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The non-zero singular values of
\comp_{k}(A)
are distinct.

This
1
ensures that the left and the right singular vectors of
\comp_{k}(A)
coincide with the columns of
\comp_{k}(V)
and
\comp_{k}(W)
, respectively, up to simultaneous sign reversal, as formally stated in the following.

Lemma 5
.

Let
A\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times m}
have
\rank(A)=r
, and
A=V\Sigma W^{\top}
be a reduced SVD. Further, let
k\leq r
, such that
M=\comp_{k}(A)=LSR^{\top}
is a reduced SVD with
S
having distinct non-zero diagonal entries. Then
L=\comp_{k}(V)PS
and
R=\comp_{k}(W)PS
for a permutation matrix

and diagonal sign matrix

such that
s_{ii}\in\{1,-1\}
for all
i
.

The following outlines the main steps of our algorithm for finding an inverse-compound of

with some

,

, where

and

are the reduced SVDs:

1.

Wedge-decomposition:
Given a set of non-zero

-wedges

such that

, where

is the

\{\pm u_{1},\ldots,\pm u_{n}\}
with
\|u_{j\in[n]}\|_{2}=1
.
Leveraging this fundamental step, we recover
\hat{V}=\{\pm V_{:,i}\}_{i\in[r]}
and
\hat{W}=\{\pm W_{:,i}\}_{i\in[r]}
from

and

\hat{V}
and
\hat{W}
are not guaranteed to have sign-consistent columns.

2.

Ordering singular values:
Since

and

, such that

.

3.

Sign-consistency restoration:
Adjust the column signs of

,

and

, giving
\tilde{W}
as output.
\tilde{W}
is consistent in column signs with

.

4.

Singular values recovery:
Finally, from the diagonal of
\comp_{k}(\hat{\Sigma})
recovered in step

\hat{\Sigma}
by solving a linear system of equations.

Composing the components as determined above, it follows that
\tilde{V}\hat{\Sigma}\tilde{W}^{\top}
is an inverse-compound of

.

3.1
Recovering Singular Vectors from Wedge Decompositions

Let
M\in{\mathds{R}}^{\binom{n}{k}\times\binom{m}{k}}
be compound-decomposable such that
M=LSR^{\top}
is a
reduced SVD
, and
M
follows
1
. As discussed in
5
,
L
and
R
may not be compound-decomposable themselves, but only up to a column permutation and simultaneous sign reversal of a subset of their columns. This section shows that it is enough to decompose the columns of
L
(resp.
R
) to recover the left and right singular vectors of
A
such that
\comp_{k}(A)=M
.

To this end, let
U=[u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}]\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times r}
with
1<r\leq n
be such that each column has unit norm. For a given
k<r
,
\comp_{k}(U)=[z_{1},\ldots,z_{\binom{r}{k}}]
, such that the column
z_{i}=u_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{i_{k}}
where the index tuple
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
is the
i
th element of
{\mathcal{I}}_{r,k}
.
The key to our algorithm is to find a decomposition of each of the
k
-wedges
\{\pm z_{i}\}_{i\in[\binom{r}{k}]}
as a
k
-dimensional subspace and recover the columns of
U
, up to the sign, by intersecting the subspaces.
The following theorem gives a sufficient condition for the recovery.

Theorem 2
.

Let
U=[u_{1},\cdots,u_{r}]
be such that any
k<r
columns of
U
are linearly independent, and
\|u_{\ell}\|_{2}=1
for all
\ell\in[r]
. Then
algorithm

1
, having as input a set of
k
-wedges
\{\pm z_{i}\,:\,z_{i}=u_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{i_{k}},1\leq i\leq\binom{r}{k}\}
,

1.

for each
z_{i}
computes the subspace
\spanop\{u_{i_{1}},\ldots,u_{i_{k}}\}
. The total time complexity of computing all the subspaces is
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}\binom{n}{k+1}\binom{r}{k}\right)
.

2.

computes all one-dimensional pairwise intersections among the subspaces generated in step
1
, returning
\hat{u}_{\ell}=\pm u_{\ell}
for all
1\leq\ell\leq r
as output. The total time to compute
\hat{U}=[\hat{u}_{1},\cdots,\hat{u}_{r}]
is of complexity

(a)

\mathcal{O}\left(\binom{r}{k}^{2}nk^{2}\right)
if
k\leq\lceil r/2\rceil
.

(b)

\mathcal{O}\left(\binom{r}{r/2}^{2}nk^{2}\log r\right)
if
\lceil r/2\rceil<k<r
.

The two steps of the above theorem are elaborated in the next two subsections.

3.1.1
Creating Wedge Matrices

This section provides a constructive proof of step
1
of
2
.
Recall that for every non-zero and decomposable vector
z
in the
k
th exterior space
V^{(k)}
, there exists a unique
k
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{U}
in
V
such that if
z=u_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{k}
, then
\spanop\{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\}=\mathcal{U}
. The following lemma provides a method that uses the entries of the vector
z
to construct a matrix whose kernel is exactly
\mathcal{U}
. Given an element
s
in an ordered set
S
, we will use
\indexof(s,S)
to denote the index of
s
in
S
. For example

\indexof(3,(1,3,4))=2,\quad\text{and}\quad\indexof((1,3,4),{\mathcal{I}}_{4,3})=3.

Lemma 6
.

Let
k<n
,
z\in{\mathds{R}}^{\binom{n}{k}}
be non-zero and decomposable, and
{\mathcal{U}}\subset{\mathds{R}}^{n}
denote the unique
k
-dimensional subspace in
{\mathds{R}}^{n}
such that if
z=u_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{k}
then
\spanop\{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\}={\mathcal{U}}
. Define the matrix
M_{z}\in{\mathds{R}}^{\binom{n}{k+1}\times n}
by

(M_{z})_{i,j}=\begin{cases}0,&j\notin I,\\
(-1)^{\indexof(j,I)-1}z_{\indexof(I\setminus\{j\},{\mathcal{I}}_{n,k})},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(10)

where
I
is the
i
th element of
{\mathcal{I}}_{n,k+1}
. Then,
\ker(M_{z})=\mathcal{U}
.

Remark 4
.

To compute
\indexof(I,{\mathcal{I}}_{n,k})
, one can either perform a simple linear search on the ordered set
{\mathcal{I}}_{n,k}
, or use the explicit formula in the appendix of
[
27
]
.

Proof.

Recall that
x\in\mathcal{U}
if and only if
x\wedge z=0
. Since the exterior product is multilinear, the mapping
f:{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}^{\binom{n}{k+1}}
defined by
f(x):=x\wedge z
is linear, i.e., there exists a matrix
M_{z}\in{\mathds{R}}^{\binom{n}{k+1}\times n}
such that
f(x)=M_{z}x
, and
\ker(M_{z})=\mathcal{U}
. We will prove that the matrix defined in (
10
) is a matrix representation of
f(x)
for the canonical basis, i.e., the
j
column of
M_{z}
is equal to
f(e_{j})
.

To this end, let
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
be such that
z=u_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{k}
. Fix
i\in\{1,\dots,\binom{n}{k+1}\},\;j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
In Grassmann notation, the
i
th entry of
f(e_{j})
is given by

(f(e_{j}))_{i}=\det(W_{I,1:k+1})

where
W=\begin{bmatrix}e_{j}&u_{1}&\dots&u_{k}\end{bmatrix}
and
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1})
is the
i
th element of
{\mathcal{I}}_{n,k+1}
. If
j\notin I
, all entries of the first column of the submatrix
W_{I,1:k+1}
are zeros, so
(f(e_{j}))_{i}=0
. Suppose now that
j\in I
and
\ell
is such that
I_{\ell}=j
, or equivalently
\ell=\indexof(\ell,I)
. Then
W_{I,1:k+1}
takes the form

W_{I,1:k+1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&u_{i_{1}1}&\dots&u_{i_{1}k}\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
0&u_{i_{\ell-1}1}&\dots&u_{i_{\ell-1}k}\\
1&u_{i_{\ell}1}&\dots&u_{i_{\ell}k}\\
0&u_{i_{\ell+1}1}&\dots&u_{i_{\ell+1}k}\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\end{bmatrix}

By the properties of determinants,

\displaystyle\det(W_{I,1:k+1})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell-1}\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}1&u_{i_{\ell}1}&\dots&u_{i_{\ell}k}\\
0&u_{i_{1}1}&\dots&u_{i_{1}k}\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
0&u_{i_{\ell-1}1}&\dots&u_{i_{\ell-1}k}\\
0&u_{i_{\ell+1}1}&\dots&u_{i_{\ell+1}k}\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell-1}\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}u_{i_{1}1}&\dots&u_{i_{1}k}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
u_{i_{\ell-1}1}&\dots&u_{i_{\ell-1}k}\\
u_{i_{\ell+1}1}&\dots&u_{i_{\ell+1}k}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell-1}z_{\indexof(\{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1},i_{\ell+1},\dots,i_{k}\},{\mathcal{I}}_{n,k})}.

Since
I_{\ell}=j
by definition, i.e.,
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1},j,i_{\ell+1},\dots,i_{k})
, it follows that
(f(e_{j}))_{i}=(M_{z})_{i,j}
as claimed.
∎

Using the construction presented in the above

\comp_{k}(U)
, thus proving step

of

.

Remark 5
.

The time complexity for the wedge matrix construction given by

for a decomposable vector

.

Lemma 7

Proof.

and such that
\comp_{k}({\mathcal{D}}^{\prime})=\comp_{k}({\mathcal{D}})
. Consider an index set

such that

and let

and similarly for

Conclusion

In this paper, we presented an algorithm, which, given a compound-decomposable matrix
M\in{\mathds{R}}^{\binom{n}{k}\times\binom{m}{k}}
, provably finds a matrix
A\in{\mathds{R}}^{n\times m}
such that the
k
th multiplicative compound matrix of
A
equals
M
. The algorithm is based on singular value decomposition of
M
, and its time complexity is polynomial in the dimensions of
M
.

There are several directions for future work. First, the application of this algorithm to questions in dynamical systems and control theory will be the focus of follow-up work. Second, the question of whether a given matrix is compound-decomposable remains open. Finally, assuming a given matrix is not compound-decomposable, it might still be useful to find matrices whose
k
th multiplicative compound is the best possible approximation, and designing an algorithm to find such matrices will be done in future work.
