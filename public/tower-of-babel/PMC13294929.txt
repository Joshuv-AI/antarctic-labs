Upper gastrointestinal bleeding in a term neonate requiring haemostasis by laparotomy
Authors: Yu Yamamoto, Hiroshi Mizumoto, Hiroaki Fukuzawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe report a case of a term neonate who developed massive haematemesis at 15 hours of life without identifiable risk factors. Despite conservative management, the bleeding persisted, resulting in haemorrhagic shock and anaemia. Endoscopy was not feasible due to a large intragastric haematoma, so surgical haemostasis was performed, revealing diffuse gastric erosion and multiple bleeding ulcers and the neonate recovered well postoperatively. This report shows that life-threatening bleeding can occur even in term neonates without identifiable risk factors. In cases of ongoing massive bleeding in which conservative and endoscopic management are unsuccessful or not feasible, surgical haemostasis may need to be considered.

Background

Upper gastrointestinal bleeding occurs in approximately 1.2% of healthy term neonates within the first 4 days of life.1 In most cases, the condition spontaneously improves within a few days with conservative management, such as fasting and administration of antihistamines.2 3 Here, we report a case of severe upper gastrointestinal bleeding with haemorrhagic shock in a term neonate without identifiable risk factors which ultimately required surgical haemostasis by laparotomy.

Case presentation

The mother was a mid-30s multiparous woman (gravida 2, para 2) diagnosed with marginal placenta praevia, but her pregnancy course was otherwise uneventful. There was no significant family history. A female neonate was delivered at 37 weeks’ gestation by elective caesarean section. Birth weight was 2910 g, and Apgar scores were 8 at 1 min and 9 at 5 min. Umbilical arterial blood pH was 7.31, and the amniotic fluid was clear. The neonate received routine care in the well-baby nursery, including oral vitamin K administration, and was exclusively breastfed. At 15 hours of life, the neonate vomited a large amount of fresh blood and was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU).

Investigations

On admission, her vital signs were stable: temperature 36.5℃, heart rate 124/min, respiratory rate 46/min and blood pressure 75/45 mm Hg. Physical examination revealed no abnormal findings. Initial blood tests showed haemoglobin (Hb) of 206 g/L. Platelet count, coagulation profile and C-reactive protein were within normal limits. Blood cultures were obtained and remained negative, and neither the white blood cell count, the immature-to-total neutrophil ratio, nor the C-reactive protein level suggested infection. Ultrasonography demonstrated no cardiac or hepatobiliary abnormalities, and no evidence of haemorrhage in the brain or adrenal glands. Subsequent investigations showed that a stool antigen test for Helicobacter pylori, performed on day 2 of life, was negative, and serum pepsinogen levels measured at that time were within the normal range (table 1).

Treatment

Management was initiated with fasting and intravenous antihistamines (figure 1). At 21 hours of life, massive haematemesis recurred. Heart rate increased to 160/min, blood pressure dropped to 48/27 mm Hg and Hb decreased to 113 g/L, indicating haemodynamic deterioration. While transfusion was being prepared and gastric lavage was being performed, the neonate passed a large amount of dark red bloody stool. Haemostasis could not be achieved with saline lavage; therefore, a diluted epinephrine solution, prepared by adding 0.5 mL of 1:1000 epinephrine to 100 mL of normal saline, was administered intragastrically. Gastric lavage was then performed 11 times using 10 mL aliquots of this solution. In addition, thrombin (500 U) dissolved in 50 mL of normal saline was administered intragastrically, with 5 mL given in total; however, bleeding was not sufficiently controlled. By 22 hours of life, Hb had further decreased to 56 g/L. Following rapid transfusion with packed red blood cells, a decrease in platelet and fibrinogen levels was observed, necessitating administration of platelet concentrates and fresh frozen plasma.

At 40 hours of life, massive haematemesis and melaena recurred. Hb dropped to 26 g/L, and the serum lactate rose to 22.0 mmol/L. Ultrasound revealed that the stomach was filled with haematoma, suggesting that endoscopic evaluation and treatment would be impossible. Therefore, laparotomy was performed. At the beginning of the operation, a large amount of intragastric haematoma was evacuated. After removal of the clots, diffuse gastric mucosal erosion was observed, with multiple ulcerative lesions showing active bleeding, which were sutured to achieve haemostasis. The ulcerative lesions observed intraoperatively were consistent with Forrest classification III (figure 2). Gastric mucosal biopsy was not performed during the procedure because haemostasis was prioritised. Postoperatively, fasting and intravenous nutrition were maintained, and a proton pump inhibitor was administered. Bloody gastric residue had resolved by day 3, allowing enteral feeding to resume on day 4, and intravenous nutrition was discontinued on day 8. The proton pump inhibitor was discontinued on day 16.

Outcome and follow-up

The neonate was discharged on day 26. Brain MRI prior to discharge showed no evidence of hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy, and growth and development were normal at 9 months of age.

Discussion

Rare causes such as H. pylori infection and elevated serum pepsinogen have also been reported.1 9 In the absence of these known risk factors, this neonate developed severe bleeding resulting in hypovolaemic shock during the early neonatal period, ultimately requiring surgical haemostasis. Reports of such severe clinical courses without identifiable risk factors are extremely rare but warrant particular attention.

Treatment for neonatal upper gastrointestinal bleeding typically involves conservative management with short-term fasting and administration of proton pump inhibitors or a histamine-2 (H2) receptor antagonists, leading to favourable outcomes in most cases (figure 3).2 3 Although clear evidence is lacking, gastric lavage with saline may be performed to evaluate bleeding volume, assess ongoing bleeding or prepare for endoscopic or surgical intervention. In adults with gastric variceal bleeding, intragastric administration of epinephrine or thrombin has been proposed as a haemostatic treatment, and these methods may also be effective for upper gastrointestinal bleeding.1012 Thrombin powder has been reported to be useful in the treatment of gastric variceal bleeding and refractory bleeding in adults.10 11 Diluted epinephrine solution has also been reported to be effective when used in combination with other haemostatic methods, such as bipolar electrocoagulation or clipping.12 However, because this condition is extremely rare in the paediatric population, we selected these treatments on the basis of evidence derived from adult cases. In addition, although it is described in a Japanese-language textbook, these interventions are listed as treatment options for upper gastrointestinal bleeding in a major NICU manual.13 However, some cases fail to respond to conservative treatment, requiring transfusion or progressing to shock or death.14 15 When conservative treatment fails, endoscopic observation and haemostasis are reportedly attempted.14 As in the present case, massive blood clots may completely obstruct the visual field, in which surgical haemostasis may be the only effective treatment.
