Combining stated and revealed preferences to define smallholder cattle breeding objectives in northwest Ethiopia: Evidence for breeding program design

Abiy Shenkut Abebe, Belete Yazie, Melaku Menale

ABSTRACT
Identifying high-priority traits is essential for designing effective breeding programs in smallholder systems. This study employed both stated and revealed preference methods to identify priority traits for cattle breeding objectives among smallholders in northwest Ethiopia. Stated preferences were assessed using a discrete choice experiment (DCE), while revealed preferences were examined through a hedonic pricing approach. The two datasets were analyzed separately and their results compared to provide complementary insights. Data were collected between January and April 2024: 384 farmers participated in the choice survey across three districts (Farta = 91, Simada = 133, and Lay Gayint = 160), and 200 cattle transactions were recorded in three marketplaces—Debre Tabor, Simada, and Nefas Mewucha. Choice data were analyzed with a mixed logit model, and market data with a log-linear ordinary least squares regression. Results showed both convergence and divergence in trait preferences between methods. Farmers’ strong preference for large body size in the DCE (p < 0.001) aligned with the significant positive effects of body condition and live body weight on market price (p < 0.001). However, traits not visually observable at sale—such as milk yield, docility, disease tolerance, libido, calving interval, and calf vigor—were highly valued in stated preferences but not captured in market prices due to observability constraints and information asymmetry. Conversely, the hedonic model revealed market structure effects not captured by the DCE, including higher prices paid by traders and premiums for slaughter animals. These findings demonstrate that employing both methods provides a more comprehensive evidence base for defining breeding objectives—capturing both invisible livelihood-critical traits valued by farmers and market dynamics that shape real-world prices. The identified priority traits provide a foundation for subsequent research to estimate genetic parameters and economic weights to design a breeding program.

Introduction Cattle are the primary livestock species in Ethiopia, supporting the livelihoods of millions of smallholder farmers. They provide essential draft power for rural communities and are valued for their meat, milk, organic fertilizer, and cash income [ 1 – 3 ]. With an estimated 70.9 million head, Ethiopia has the largest cattle population in Africa and ranks fifth worldwide [ 4 ]. Indigenous breeds constitute 97.4% of the national herd, while crossbreds and exotic breeds account for the remainder. Although the cattle population has grown significantly over the past decade [ 4 ], the productivity of indigenous cattle remains low. Given that livestock—particularly cattle—are major contributors to agricultural greenhouse gas emissions, maintaining fewer but more productive animals is critical for supporting smallholder livelihoods, fostering national economic growth, and mitigating climate change. To enhance productivity, crossbreeding with exotic breeds such as Holstein Friesian and Jersey has been practiced for decades [ 5 , 6 ]. Yet, despite these efforts, performance remains limited. For instance, during a typical seven-month lactation period, cows produce an average of only 1.5 liters of milk per day [ 7 ]. Consequently, annual per capita milk consumption in Ethiopia is about 19 liters—far lower than in neighboring Uganda (50 liters) and Kenya (90 liters) [ 8 ], and well below the World Health Organization’s recommended intake of 200 liters per year. In addition to low milk yield, indigenous cattle face reproductive challenges, including extended days open, late age at first calving, long calving intervals, and short lactation lengths [ 7 , 9 ]. Despite these constraints, Ethiopian indigenous cattle exhibit considerable within-breed genetic diversity [ 10 , 11 ], highlighting strong potential for genetic improvement through selective breeding. They are also well adapted to the low-input production systems typical of northwest Ethiopia [ 12 ]. However, systematic selective breeding programs that leverage this adaptive potential and genetic diversity are virtually absent, particularly in the northwest. A critical step in designing such programs is defining the breeding objective—identifying the specific traits to be improved through selection [ 13 , 14 ]. Yet, up-to-date information on smallholder breeding objectives for indigenous cattle in northwest Ethiopia is lacking. Moreover, given Ethiopia’s diverse agro-ecologies, community groups, and livestock production systems, smallholders’ preferences for cattle traits are likely to vary across contexts. At the smallholder level, participatory methods such as discrete choice experiments (DCEs) and hedonic pricing have increasingly been used to identify livestock trait preferences in Ethiopia [ 12 , 15 , 16 ] and elsewhere [ 17 , 18 ]. A DCE is a stated preference method in which individuals reveal preferences through hypothetical choice sets, while hedonic pricing is a revealed preference method that infers preferences from actual market transactions. Both approaches have strengths and limitations [ 19 , 20 ]. DCEs are prone to hypothetical bias, arising from inconsistencies between stated and actual behavior, whereas hedonic pricing often suffers from endogeneity bias due to omitted variables and limited purchasing choices. Importantly, the limitations of one method are not necessarily constraints for the other. This complementarity suggests that combining both methods can harness their respective strengths while mitigating weaknesses. Accordingly, the present study employed both DCE and hedonic pricing to define smallholder cattle breeding objectives in northwest Ethiopia. The findings aim to inform the design of a conservation-based breeding program for indigenous cattle. Materials and methods Ethics approval statement This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Debre Tabor University (reference number: Ref: DTU/CAES/RCC/08/115/24). The study did not involve any experimental or clinical trial on live animals. The survey and data handling followed the required ethical guidelines and were carried out from January 5 to April 30/2024. Participants were first informed about the purpose of the study, after which informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the interview. Participation was entirely voluntary, and all participants were 18 years of age or older. No sensitive information (health status, religious beliefs, political views, or illegal behavior) was collected, and all data were anonymized. Morphometric data (heart girth and body length) were collected non-invasively from 200 traded cattle using a flexible measuring tape in an open market, ensuring animal welfare and causing no stress. Description of the study areas This study was conducted in the Farta, Simada, and Lay Gayint districts of the South Gondar Zone, located in northwest Ethiopia. These districts were selected because they host relatively large cattle populations compared to other districts in the zone and represent the natural habitat of the Simada cattle breed. The study aimed to investigate smallholders’ trait preferences to generate inputs for designing a conservation-based genetic improvement program for this breed. Agricultural practices in the study districts are characterized by mixed crop–livestock farming systems, with cattle serving as the principal source of draft power for cropland preparation [ 12 ]. Agro-ecologically, Farta and Lay Gayint are predominantly highland areas, while Simada is mainly mid-altitude. According to the Central Statistical Agency of Ethiopia (CSA) [ 21 ], the South Gondar Zone has approximately 1.9 million cattle, with the three study districts collectively accounting for about one-third of this population. Study approach This study employed both DCE and hedonic pricing methods to examine smallholder preferences for indigenous cattle traits. The target population for both methods was smallholder farmers in the selected districts. Choice data for the DCE were collected through a cross-sectional survey administered to randomly selected farmers, while transaction data for the hedonic pricing method were obtained by observing cattle sales in the major markets of the study districts. Although both methods focused on the same target population, the specific respondents and data sources differed. Consequently, the datasets were analyzed separately and independently rather than merged, and their results were subsequently compared and integrated to provide complementary insights. This study therefore represents a comparative and complementary integration of evidence, not a statistical integration of the two datasets. Experimental design and data collection Stated preference data A key step in designing a DCE is the identification of relevant attributes and their levels [ 12 , 26 ]. For this study, appropriate cattle attributes were identified through a combination of methods, including Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA) and consultations with researchers and livestock experts. In each study district, PRA was conducted in the form of focus group discussions with key informants and livestock experts. The key informants were experienced farmers with in-depth knowledge of the socio-economic and cultural values of indigenous cattle, identified with the assistance of district livestock experts. During the discussions, various cattle attributes were listed and prioritized using a pairwise ranking method. The six highest-ranking attributes for bulls and the six for cows were selected for the choice design. In addition, market price was included as a seventh attribute for both bulls and cows, given its importance in determining farmers’ marginal willingness to pay (MWTP) for genetic improvements in other traits. Employing a full factorial design consisting of six attributes at two levels and one attribute at three levels ( 2 6 X 3 1 ) yielded 192 possible choice profiles. However, presenting all combinations was impractical for smallholder respondents. Therefore, a fractional factorial design was used to reduce the number of alternatives. Sixteen choice alternatives, categorized into four blocks, were generated using JMP statistical discovery software [ 27 ]. The design achieved a D-efficiency score of 98.85, indicating high efficiency in estimating the effects of the study variables. To avoid forced choices, an opt-out option was included in each block. Thus, each block contained five alternatives for both bulls and cows, with respondents required to make a single choice decision per block. Pictures were used to illustrate attribute levels for both bulls and cows. A sample choice card for a bull is presented in Fig 1 . The choice survey was conducted between January and April 2024 by trained enumerators under close supervision of researchers. A total of 384 smallholder farmers randomly selected from the three districts (Farta = 91, Simada = 133, Lay Gayint = 160) participated. Farmers were first briefed on the study objectives and given sufficient time to understand the hypothetical choice alternatives. Choice cards from block one were introduced first, with participants proceeding sequentially through the remaining three blocks after selecting the most preferred alternative in each preceding task. Although an opt-out option was available, none of the participants selected it, suggesting that the presented alternatives were plausible and realistic. 10.1371/journal.pone.0359944.g001 Fig 1 A sample choice card for a bull, presented to respondents. The zero selection rate for the opt-out alternative, while uncommon in consumer-market choice experiments, is both behaviorally rational and methodologically documented within smallholder farming systems. For instance, Abebe et al. [ 12 ] reported an opt-out frequency below 0.5% for sheep traits, while Kassie et al. [ 28 ] observed a 1.4% opt-out rate for indigenous cow traits in Ethiopia. Because the explicit goal of the choice task was to define the breeding objective, the opt-out alternative was framed to farmers as: “no genetic improvement or trait modification is needed for your herd relative to the presented options.” Given the subsistence constraints of the crop-livestock production system, selecting the status quo was viewed as unviable by the respondents, illustrating how local context dictates choice behavior. Consequently, due to this complete lack of statistical variation, the opt-out alternative was excluded from the final econometric estimation matrix to ensure model convergence. Revealed preference data Cattle transaction data were collected between January and April 2024 from three major marketplaces—Debre Tabor, Simada, and Nefas Mewucha—which represent the Farta, Simada, and Lay Gayint districts, respectively. In Ethiopia, the absence of a standardized livestock marketing system means that prices are determined through mutual agreement between sellers and buyers. Under such conditions, transaction prices are expected to be influenced by multiple factors. To identify cattle price determinants, data were collected on buyers’ characteristics, purchasing purpose, and several observable cattle attributes, including sale price (see Table 3 ). These data were recorded only after the seller and buyer had reached full agreement, without their prior knowledge of the data collection, to avoid influencing negotiations. During data collection, cattle age was determined using dentition, and only animals with at least one pair of well-developed permanent incisors were included. In total, market data for 200 cattle transactions were collected. Of these, 56 transactions were observed in the Simada market, 69 in Debre Tabor, and 75 in Nefas Mewucha. The market transaction dataset includes 10 categorical and one continuous variables, with 11 predictors in total. According to Harrell [ 29 ], the ratio of sample size (m) to number of predictors (p) should satisfy p < m/10 (liberal), p < m/15 (good average), or p < m/20 (conservative). With m = 200 and p = 11, our study satisfies both the liberal rule (11 < 20) and the good average rule (11 < 13.33), implying the sample size was reasonably adequate to estimate the effects of 11 predictors without introducing overfitting. Statistical modeling Stated preference. Choice data were analyzed using a mixed logit model implemented in the STATA software package [ 30 ]. The model is grounded in random utility theory, which posits that an individual’s choices can be modeled as a function of the attributes that define the available options [ 31 ]. According to this theory, selecting one option over others indicates that the chosen option provides higher utility [ 32 ]. In this study, smallholders are assumed to maximize their utility by selecting their most preferred alternatives for breeding bulls and cows. The mixed logit model accounts for correlations among choices by incorporating random parameters [ 26 ]. A key advantage of this model is that it does not require the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) assumption, which is a limitation of the standard multinomial logit model. In the mixed logit framework, the utility ( U i j ) for smallholder i deriving from alternative j can be expressed by the following equation: U i j = β i X i j + γ w i j + ϵ i j Where β i are the random coefficients that vary over individuals, and X i j is a vector of alternative-specific variables, γ are fixed coefficients on w i j , a vector of alternative-specific variables, and ϵ i j is a random error term that follows the Gumbel distribution. The probability of choosing alternative j for respondent i is the standard logistic probability integrated over a density of random parameters, f ( β | θ ) . Stated more explicitly, the choice probabilities of mixed logit model take the following form: P i j = ∫ P i j ( β i ) f ( β ) d β Where P i j ( β i ) = e β i X i j + γ w i j ∑ j = 1 5 e β i X i j + γ w i j The integral ∫ … d β indicates that the standard logistic probabilities are averaged over all possible values of β , weighted by the density of each β Given that price was included as an attribute in the DCE, it was possible to compute MWTP for each characteristic. The MWTP estimates the amount smallholders are willing to pay for a one-unit improvement in a specific attribute of a bull or cow. The MWTP was calculated using the following formula: M W T P = − 1 X ( C o e f f i c i e n t o f t h e a t t r i b u t e C o e f f i c i e n t o f p r i c e ) To identify the key driver of smallholder preferences, we calculated the relative importance of the attributes from the MWTP. The relative importance for a specific attribute k was calculated as follows: R e l a t i v e i m p o r t a n c e k = ( M W T P k ∑ M W T P f o r a l l a t t r i b u t e s ) * 1 0 0 The MWTP is suitable for calculating relative importance because it converts utility coefficients into a common monetary scale, allowing direct comparison across attributes with different units. This approach was applied consistently to both the bull and cow models, ensuring that relative importance values are directly comparable. Revealed preference The relationship between observed cattle prices and various influencing factors was analyzed using a hedonic pricing model. This model is based on the premise that the price of a heterogeneous good, such as livestock, is a function of the utility derived from its intrinsic attributes and relevant external factors [ 33 ]. In the present study, the hedonic regression provides estimates of the implicit prices (marginal values) for each variable included in the model. The analysis employed the following log-linear functional form of the hedonic price function: ln ( P r i c e i ) = β 0 + β 1 ( B o d y W e i g h t i ) + ∑ k = 2 K β k D i k + ε i Where ln ( P r i c e i ) is the natural logarithm of the observed price for cattle transaction i , β 0 is the constant term, β 1 is the coefficient for the continuous variable live body weight, β k are the coefficients to be estimated for the k – 1dummy ( D i k ), and D i k represents a set of categorical dummy variables presented in Table 3 . For each categorical variable with m level, m − 1 dummies are included in the model to avoid perfect multicollinearity. ε i is the idiosyncratic error term, assumed to be independently and identically distributed. Homoscedasticity of the error variance is a key assumption for the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression used in the hedonic model. To test this, a Breusch-Pagan-Godfrey test was conducted. The results F ( 2 0 , 1 7 9 ) = 0 . 84 , p = 0 . 667 indicate that the null hypothesis of homoscedasticity cannot be rejected, confirming that the log-linear functional form of the model is appropriate and that heteroscedasticity is not a concern. Furthermore, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was examined to assess multicollinearity. The VIF values ranged from 1.3 to 3.1, with a mean of 1.8, attesting the absence of severe multicollinearity among the independent variables. Finally, a Ramsey Regression Equation Specification Error Test (RESET) was performed to check for potential model misspecification due to omitted variables. The results F ( 1 , 1 7 8 ) = 2 . 76 , p = 0 . 0 . 096 demonstrate that the effect of omitted variables is not statistically significant, supporting the adequacy of the specified model. Discussion Harnessing indigenous cattle genetic resources as a pathway to improve the livelihoods of smallholders in developing countries, such as Ethiopia, largely depends on efforts to enhance their overall productivity [ 2 , 35 ]. As an initial step toward designing a conservation-based breeding program, this study investigated smallholders’ preferences for cattle traits and assessed the internal and external factors influencing cattle prices in northwest Ethiopia. The results of the choice modeling revealed that multiple attributes significantly influenced smallholders’ preferences for indigenous cattle. The hedonic regression analysis also identified that both cattle biological characteristics and external market factors, such as buyer type and purchase purpose, were significant determinants of price variation in the local market. Synthesis of stated and revealed preferences for trait prioritization This study integrated DCE and hedonic pricing approaches to identify valuable cattle traits from both producer preference and market perspectives. The DCE revealed that large body size was the first and second most preferred trait for bulls and cows, respectively. This strong preference for body size was strongly corroborated by the hedonic pricing analysis, in which superior body condition and greater live body weight exerted a significant positive influence on cattle market prices. This convergence between stated and revealed preferences validates smallholders’ trait prioritization and provides robust evidence for including body size as a primary breeding objective. Scarpa et al. [ 36 ], employing a similar mixed-methods approach with indigenous cattle in Kenya, found that larger body size significantly influenced both choice preference and market price. Similarly, Woldu et al. [ 16 ] reported convergence between DCE and hedonic model results with respect to body size in Ethiopian indigenous goats. These consistent findings across species and geographic contexts suggest that body size is a robustly valued trait in smallholder livestock systems. Milk yield was the most preferred cow trait identified in the DCE. However, unlike body condition and live body weight—which are visually apparent at the market—milk yield is not directly observable at the point of transaction and as a result, could not be incorporated into the hedonic pricing model. This absence reflects a measurement limitation rather than a lack of market valuation. The high MWTP for milk in the DCE indicates that farmers value this trait strongly, but it cannot be priced through observable characteristics at the point of sale. This finding underscores the value of stated preference methods in capturing traits that are important to farmers but cannot be measured through market transactions. Therefore, while market data validates the economic importance of body size, the DCE results emphasize that breeding objectives must also prioritize milk production to align with smallholders’ livelihood strategies. The consistent emphasis on body size and milk yield aligns with previous reports indicating that indigenous cattle are crucial sources of food (milk and meat) and cash income at the smallholder level [ 2 , 3 , 37 ]. Apart from milk yield, traits such as docile temperament, disease tolerance, strong draft power, active libido, strong calf vigor, and short calving interval are not directly observable at the point of sale and were therefore not captured in the hedonic model. Their absence from the hedonic model does not imply that they are undervalued by the market; rather, it reflects the inherent difficulty of pricing such traits through observable transaction characteristics. In contrast, the hedonic model revealed market structure effects invisible to the DCE—for example, that traders pay higher prices and that slaughter animals command a premium. These divergences suggest that breeding for higher-value slaughter markets could increase profits but may conflict with smallholders’ draft power needs. Beyond observability constraints, the absence of record-based livestock transactions in Ethiopia contributes to significant information asymmetries in the market, particularly regarding reproductive and adaptive performance. Without formal records or transaction histories, buyers cannot verify an animal's health status, reproductive history, or productivity potential at the point of sale. This information asymmetry—not a lack of buyer interest—explains why traits such as disease resistance, reproductive efficiency, and temperament are not reflected in market prices. Taken together, these findings clearly highlight the value of integrating stated and revealed preference methods to identify robust breeding objective traits. Notwithstanding the merits of combining both strategies, certain methodological warrant attention. First, while the DCE and hedonic pricing analyses targeted the same population of smallholder cattle keepers in northwest Ethiopia, the actual samples differed in composition and context: the DCE was administered to randomly selected farmers in their villages, whereas the hedonic model relied on transaction data collected at livestock markets. Consequently, the market sample included traders and other buyers —such as farmers acting as buyers or consumers—who were not fully represented in the village-based DCE sample. This difference may partly explain some of the observed divergence between the two preference measures. As such, future research should consider integrated sampling designs—collecting stated preferences from farmers and subsequently tracking their actual market transactions—to minimize such divergence. Second, body live was estimated using Schaeffer's formula—a widely used method in field-based livestock studies [ 38 – 40 ]. Despite its popularity, this approach may introduce measurement error and lead to attenuation bias [ 41 ]. Our estimate of the price premium is therefore likely conservative, though the coefficient remains highly significant (p < 0.001). Implications for designing a conservation-based breeding program The convergence and divergence between stated and revealed preferences observed in this study provide a robust empirical foundation for defining breeding objectives that balance smallholder priorities with market realities. The strong alignment between farmers’ preferences for large-framed cattle and market premiums for superior body condition and live body weight suggests that growth rate and mature size should be primary breeding objectives. Genetic improvement of these traits would simultaneously satisfy breeder preferences and enhance market value, thereby creating a stronger economic incentive for sustainable resource conservation and utilization. However, the high stated preference for milk yield—despite its invisibility in local market transactions—highlights the need for breeding objectives that extend beyond what prices alone reveal. Milk production is vital to smallholders’ household consumption, nutrition, and daily income generation [ 42 , 43 ], yet it cannot be captured in market prices because it is not directly observable at the point of animal sale. Thus, adopting a dual-purpose breeding objective that incorporates both body size and milk yield is vital for a conservation-based breeding program within smallholder systems. In addition, breeding objectives should weigh these traits based on their relative contributions to smallholder livelihoods, rather than solely on their market premiums. Furthermore, the finding that buyer type and purchase purpose significantly influence prices suggests that market segmentation offers opportunities for niche breeding strategies. For instance, cattle destined for slaughter commanded higher prices, indicating that selecting for traits valued in the beef market—such as conformation and finish—could target this premium segment. However, such an approach must account for the continued need for draft power and milk production within smallholder systems. Beyond the traits discussed above, the DCE revealed that smallholders value several other invisible traits—including docile temperament, disease tolerance, strong draft power, active libido, strong calf vigor, and a short calving interval—which are not captured in market prices due to observability constraints at the point of sale.. While incorporating all of these into a breeding objective may be challenging, breeding programs should, at a minimum, avoid selecting against them. Care must be taken to monitor potential genetic correlations between visible and invisible traits to prevent unintended declines in these livelihood-critical attributes. The relatively small standard deviations indicate broad consensus among farmers on the importance of body size, temperament, and milk yield. While the standard deviation for calf vigor was somewhat larger, it remains modest. These findings suggest that a single breeding objective emphasizing these traits would serve the majority of smallholder farmers. This is particularly important in the context of smallholder breeding programs, where collective action and a large breeding population are required, making individualized breeding objectives impractical. Conclusion Integrating stated and revealed preference approaches has enabled the identification of priority traits that can inform breeding objective definition by reconciling farmers’ preferences with market incentives. The observed convergence suggests that body size should be considered a primary candidate breeding objective, while divergence on milk yield and other non-observable traits (e.g., docile temperament, disease tolerance, libido, calving interval, and calf vigor) reveals that these traits are not captured in market prices due to observability constraints and information asymmetry. Combining both methods is therefore valuable—hedonic models alone miss invisible traits, while DCE alone misses real market structures such as price premiums for traders and slaughter animals. These findings provide empirical inputs for breeding objective definition and suggest that breeding programs consider multi-trait objectives that balance market competitiveness with household livelihood needs, incorporating both visible and invisible traits. Developing a formal selection index for these traits will require additional steps—namely, estimating genetic parameters and economic weights—which represent important directions for future research. This integrated approach can help ensure that conservation-based breeding programs enhance, rather than compromise, the full range of smallholder livelihood strategies.
