Examine the effects of the developed Pender’s model constructs on medication compliance and adherence to a low-salt diet in patients with uncontrolled hypertension
Authors: Saeid Bashirian, Majid Barati, Erfan Ayubi, Saman Talebi, Mostafa Jiba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:One of the challenges for patients with high blood pressure is the lack of proper control. The aim of this study was to investigate the influence of the developed Pender’s model structures on medication compliance and adherence to a low-salt diet in patients with uncontrolled hypertension in the city of Ahvaz.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This cross-sectional study was conducted among patients with uncontrolled blood pressure in Ahvaz city. The participants were selected through multistage sampling. The data collection instrument was a researcher-designed questionnaire based on the constructs of the developed Pender’s model, which included three sections with demographic information, questions about adherence to a healthy and low-salt diet, and medication adherence. The data were analyzed using PLS SMART software.RESULTS:The results of the study revealed that the constructs self-efficacy (path coefficient = 0.25, P < 0.00), perceived sensitivity (path coefficient = 0.17, P < 0.02), and interpersonal influences (path coefficient = 0.09, P < 0.02) had a positive and significant influence on adherence to a low-salt diet, and the structure of perceived benefits (path coefficient = −0.09, P < 0.00) had a negative and reverse effect on this behavior. Additionally, the results of the study revealed that the self-efficacy construct (path coefficient = 0.11, P < 0.02) had a positive and significant effect on medication adherence, and the perceived barrier construct (path coefficient = −0.13, P < 0.00) had a negative and reverse effect on this behavior.CONCLUSION:By adding perceived sensitivity to the Pender’s health promotion model, there is the possibility of better control of hypertension in patients with uncontrolled blood pressure.

Introduction

Hypertension is a preventable disease that negatively affects human health.[1] Every year, more than 10 million deaths and 200 million disabilities are caused by high blood pressure worldwide.[2] Approximately 83% of deaths and 76% of disease burden in Iran in 2019 were due to non-communicable diseases, including high blood pressure.[345] This disease is one of the most preventable diseases, and its progression to cardiovascular and kidney diseases can be prevented by lifestyle changes.[67] One of the greatest challenges in treating high blood pressure is the lack of control.[8] Patients with hypertension have problems controlling their blood pressure. Among patients receiving drug treatment, only 43.7% have optimal blood pressure control.[9] Keeping blood pressure below 140/90 mmHg in patients with high blood pressure can prevent costly medical procedures such as hemodialysis, cardiovascular surgery, and death in the future.[10] Self-care is an important method for preventing and treating non-communicable diseases such as hypertension.[11] Many studies have shown that self-care plays a very important role in controlling high blood pressure.[1213] Self-care offers patients the opportunity to better control systolic blood pressure and diastolic.[14] To manage hypertension, modifiable factors such as patients’ adherence to a healthy and low-salt diet and medication adherence must be considered.[1516] Therefore, it is very important to use models such as Pender’s health promotion model that can predict, change, and correct unhealthy behaviors.[17] This model is a comprehensive model used to implement health-promoting behaviors and provides a theoretical framework for discovering effective health promotion factors.[18] Pender’s health promotion model is effective in achieving ideal behavior change and strengthening the structures to control hypertension in patients.[19] Studies have shown that the perceived severity and sensitivity of patients to the complications of hypertension are low, which may be important factors for patients’ lack of control of hypertension.[20] Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the influence of the developed Pender’s model structures on medication compliance and adherence to a low-salt diet in patients with uncontrolled blood pressure in Ahvaz city.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study is a cross-sectional study conducted in Ahvaz city in 1402 patients with high blood pressure whose blood pressure could not be controlled.

Study participants and sampling

The studied samples were selected via simple random sampling from health centers in East and West (two urban comprehensive health centers and one rural health center) and Karun Health Center (one urban comprehensive health center and one rural comprehensive center) from among patients with high blood pressure Ahvaz city who did not control their blood pressure and had health records in comprehensive health centers. After patients were selected using simple random sampling from comprehensive health centers, health posts, and health homes, they were invited to participate in the study by telephone. After the individuals attended comprehensive health centers, written consent was obtained to participate in the study. A total of 580 participants (282 men, 298 women) between the ages of 18 and 70 years who were literate and had uncontrolled blood pressure (blood pressure over 140 at 90 mmHg) despite having taken blood pressure medication for a year or more participated in the study. The exclusion criteria were: Patients who did not complete the questionnaire, physical or mental health problems preventing the individual from participating in the research, and incomplete records at comprehensive health care centers.

Data collection tool and technique

The data collection tool was a researcher-designed questionnaire that included three sections: demographic information (gender, age, body mass index, disease duration, first and second systolic blood pressure, first and second diastolic blood pressure, education status, marital status, employment status, history of hypertension in first-degree family members, history of smoking and drug use, history of kidney disease, history of heart attack, previous history related to surgery with heart disease, history of anxiety and stress, status of aspirin use, status of statin use, status of doctor-prescribed blood pressure medication use and insurance coverage [Table 1]. Questions about a healthy and low-salt diet are based on the constructs of the developed Pender’s model (perceived sensitivity structure: 3 items, perceived severity structure: 3 items, perceived benefit structure: 3-item, perceived barriers construct: 4-item, self-efficacy construct: 5-item, activity-related effect: 3 items, interpersonal influences construct: 3 items, situational influences construct: 5-item, structure of commitment to plan: 6 item and construct of the structure of the behavior of following a healthy and low-salt nutrition: 4-item [Figures 1 and 2] and questions about medication adherence developed based on the Pender’s model structures (perceived sensitivity structure: 3 items, perceived severity structure: 3 items, perceived benefit structure: 5 items, perceived barriers structure: 5 items, perceived self-efficacy structure: 4 items, activity-related effect: 3 items, structure of interpersonal influences: 3 items, structure of situational influences: 3 items, structure of commitment to plan: 5 items and structure of medication adherence behavior: 4 items. The questions were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale [Figures 3 and 4].

Validity and reliability of the questionnaire

After an extensive search and numerous studies, 134 items were written on the basis of the constructs of the developed Pender’s model. In the qualitative validation stage, 43 items from 134 items were eliminated after qualitative interviews were conducted with four health professionals. The questions were corrected at this stage from the aspects of the writing and wording. For quantitative validity, the remaining 91 items were assessed by 10 health professionals in terms of the content validity index and content validity ratio. The content validity index of ≤0.8 and content validity ratio of ≤0.75 were considered acceptable; 13 items were removed, and a total of 77 items remained. To assess the reliability of the questionnaire, it was completed by 25 participants in the first phase and completed again by the same participants 15 days later in the second phase. The intraclass correlation coefficient for the entire questionnaire was 0.87. The correlation coefficient before and after for each of the structures related to the questions of the section “Healthy and low-salt diet” were perceived sensitivity (before = 0.86, after = 0.82), perceived severity (before = 0.90, after = 0.75), perceived benefit (before = 0.72, after = 0.85), perceived barriers (before = 0.78, after = 0.76), perceived self-efficacy (before = 0.90, after = 0.91), activity-related effect (before = 0.76, after = 0.71), interpersonal influences (before = 0.74, after = 0.79), situational influences (before = 0.74, after = 0.71), commitment to plan (before = 0.85, after = 0.87), and diet compliance behavior (before = 0.76, after = 0.70), and the constructs related to medication adherence questions were perceived sensitivity (before = 0.89, after = 0.87), perceived severity (before = 0.88, after = 0.87), perceived benefit (before = 0.89, after = 0.77), perceived barriers (before = 0.89, after = 0.92), perceived self-efficacy (before = 0.89, after = 0.86), activity-related effect (before = 0.70, after = 0.71), interpersonal influences (before = 0.90, after = 0.85), situational influences (before = 0.71, after = 0.75), commitment to plan (before = 0.92, after = 0.91), and medication adherence behavior (before = 0.87, after = 0.89).

Statistical analysis

Data were recorded in SPSS21 and analyzed using PLS Smart software. In this section, validity, reliability, model fit, Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability, convergent validity or average variance extracted, and discriminant validity indices were calculated using Smart PLS software. Additionally, the regression coefficients, R2 values, and factor loadings were obtained in this section.

Also model fit, the normed-fit index (NFI) and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), significance levels (P values) and path coefficients indices were calculated using the PLS Smart. Using SPSS-21 software, the Pearson correlation coefficient of the model was calculated; the overall questionnaire correlation coefficient was 0.95.

Ethics consideration

The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hamedan University of Medical Sciences, Hamedan, Iran. (IR.UMSHA.REC.1402.391). Oral consent was obtained from all participants in the study, and all provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed in this research.

Results

A total of 580 people participated in the study; 51.4% of the respondents were female, and 48.6% were male. The dominant ethnicity was reported to be Arab (42% of the participants). The means and standard deviations of the participants’ age, body mass index, and disease duration were reported as follows: age = 53 (±11), body mass index = 53 (±11), and disease duration = 6.45 (±4.40). In terms of literacy, 32.4% had a college degree, 26.6% had a primary-school degree, 22.1% had a middle-school degree, and 19% had a university degree. Approximately 90% of the participants were married. In terms of employment status, 30.2% were housewives, and 22.8% were unemployed. Approximately 70% reported a family history of hypertension, and approximately 43% reported a history of anxiety and stress. Regarding blood pressure medication use, only 37.1% of the participants took medication regularly [Table 1].

The results of the study showed that the constructs of the developed Pender’s model explained a total of 53.6% of the behavior of following a healthy and low-salt diet. The self-efficacy construct with a regression coefficient (significance level) of 0.25 (0.001), the perceived sensitivity construct with a regression coefficient (significance level) of 0.17 (0.02), the interpersonal influences construct with a regression coefficient (significance level) of 0.25 (0.001), and the commitment to plan construct with a regression coefficient (significance level) of 0.09 (0.02) were ranked as the most effective constructs in influencing healthy and low-salt diet behavior. Overall, commitment to a low-salt diet had a 41% impact on behavior. The perceived barrier construct, with a regression coefficient (significance level) of 0.18 (0.001), and the activity-related effect construct, with a regression coefficient (significance level) of 0.08 (0.02), had positive and significant effects on behavior, and the situational influence construct had a positive effect on commitment to plan, with a regression coefficient (significance level) of 0.23 (0.001). The perceived benefits construct had negative and reverse effects on behavior and commitment to plan, with a regression coefficient (significance level) of −0.09 (0.001) and −0.10 (0.001), respectively. The results of the study showed that the construct of perceived severity, with a regression coefficient (significance level) of −0.128 (0.09), had no significant association with behavior in a healthy, low-salt diet [Table 2].

Discussion

Self-care behaviors play a significant role in controlling hypertension in patients.[22] The primary objective of this study was to examine the effect of constructs from the expanded Pender’s model on medication adherence and compliance with a healthy, low-salt diet in patients with uncontrolled hypertension. Adherence to a healthy, low-salt diet among 53.6% and medication adherence among 47.6% of patients with uncontrolled hypertension indicate moderate health literacy among participants regarding the importance of self-care, particularly in medication adherence.

Health awareness and literacy can substantially influence individuals’ beliefs and attitudes, ultimately affecting self-care behaviors.[22] A study in Bhutan demonstrated that patients’ knowledge and literacy about hypertension are key determinants of hypertension self-care.[23] Research by Obirikorang et al. further confirmed a significant positive correlation between health literacy and self-care behaviors in hypertension control, supporting the present study’s results.[24]

A strong association exists between self-care behaviors and patients’ ability to manage hypertension.[25] The current study found that the expanded Pender’s model constructs—perceived self-efficacy, interpersonal influences, and perceived susceptibility—had a significant positive impact on commitment to plan and adherence to a healthy, low-salt diet. Similarly, Mirkarimi et al. reported that perceived self-efficacy and interpersonal influences significantly improve dietary compliance, corroborating these findings.[26]

Chacko et al., in a cross-sectional study conducted in India titled “The Role of Family Support and Self-Care Practices in Hypertension Control among Hypertensive Patients,” highlighted that family support critically enhances self-care behaviors in hypertensive patients.[27] Patients with hypertension require family assistance to manage their condition effectively.[28] The family serves as a pivotal foundation for treatment adherence.[29] Studies confirm that culturally tailored, family-based interventions effectively promote self-care in hypertensive patients. Thus, family support—particularly from spouses—plays a vital role in hypertension self-care.[30]

Patients’ beliefs and attitudes significantly influence self-care behaviors and hypertension control.[31] Ma et al. found that perceived susceptibility improves self-care in young and middle-aged hypertensive patients.[32] In the expanded Pender’s model, perceived susceptibility was a key construct affecting dietary adherence in uncontrolled hypertension. Firmansyah et al. similarly demonstrated that perceived susceptibility enhances self-care, aligning with this study’s results.[33]

Financial constraints are another individual barrier to self-care.[31] This study revealed that perceived barriers and activity-related effect positively influenced dietary adherence but negatively impacted commitment to plan. Important barriers to following a healthy, low-salt diet in patients with hypertension that affect self-care behaviors and high blood pressure control include high costs and lack of sufficient time to prepare healthy, low-salt foods,[34] which were confirmed and supported by the present study. However, some studies conflict, reporting that activity-related effect and perceived barriers positively affect commitment to plan.[26]

Patients aware of their daily salt intake achieve better blood pressure control.[35] Perceived benefits lead patients with hypertension to exhibit better self-care behaviors to control their blood pressure.[36] In a study titled “Self-management of hypertension in patients with hypertension in Iran: A theory-based study,” Zareban et al. showed that the construct of perceived benefits is a strong predictor of self-care behaviors in patients with hypertension.[37] The results of the present study showed that the construct of perceived benefits has a negative effect on the commitment to plan and behavior regarding adherence to a healthy, low-salt diet, which is inconsistent with the findings of previous studies.

The present study revealed that the constructs of perceived self-efficacy, situational influences, and activity-related effect from the expanded Pender’s model had a significant positive effect on commitment to plan and medication adherence. Among these, perceived self-efficacy emerged as the strongest predictor of commitment to plan and medication adherence in hypertensive patients. Higher levels of self-efficacy were associated with greater success in blood pressure control.[38] This aligns with findings by Goudarzi et al. and Ghorbani et al., who demonstrated that patients with greater perceived self-efficacy exhibit better hypertension management and medication adherence,[3940] thus corroborating the results of the current study.

Negative emotions, such as stress, are influential factors in exacerbating hypertension.[41] In this study, approximately 43% of participants reported a history of anxiety, suggesting that stress—potentially induced by elevated blood pressure—may adversely affect medication adherence. Similarly, Mirkarimi et al. and Ghorbani et al. identified situational influences as a significant predictor of medication adherence [2640] further supporting our findings.

To avoid hypertension-related complications, patients typically adhere to prescribed antihypertensive regimens.[20] However, promoting self-care behaviors requires enhancing patients’ beliefs and perceptions about the risks of uncontrolled hypertension.[42] The construct of activity-related effect, by influencing commitment to action, was found to significantly predict medication adherence—a result consistent with Goudarzi et al.’s study, which identified behavioral feelings as the strongest predictor of adherence.[39]

For patients with uncontrolled hypertension, perceived barriers—such as personal costs or structural obstacles—may hinder healthy behaviors or reduce health-promoting actions.[43] This study observed that perceived barriers negatively impacted commitment to plan and medication adherence, a finding supported by Goudarzi et al.[39] However, conflicting evidence from Ghorbani et al. reported a positive association between perceived barriers and adherence, diverging from our results.[40] Notably, patients with greater awareness of the consequences of non-adherence tend to comply more rigorously with treatment.[20] While Hu et al.’s study in China identified perceived severity and perceived benefits as strong predictors of self-care in type 2 diabetes, these constructs showed no significant effect on medication adherence in our cohort.[44] Joho et al. emphasized that knowledge of complications strengthens the role of perceived susceptibility and severity in treatment adherence.[20] The discrepancy between studies may stem from participants’ low awareness of the benefits of a low-salt diet or the risks of non-adherence. Pan et al. showed in a study that cultural and economic differences influence the self-care behavior of patients with hypertension.[45] Given the ethnic and cultural diversity of Ahvaz, variations in economic and cultural contexts may explain inconsistencies in certain constructs across studies.

The results of this study demonstrated that commitment to plan, perceived self-efficacy, perceived sensitivity, interpersonal influences, situational influences, and activity-related effect can effectively modify the behavior of patients with uncontrolled hypertension regarding adherence to a low-salt diet and medication adherence. As this is the first study to predict healthy, low-salt dietary behavior and medication adherence using the developed Pender’s model constructs in patients with uncontrolled hypertension, the results of this study may provide useful practical information for researchers, policymakers, and healthcare providers working in the field of uncontrolled hypertension.

Conclusion

In low- and middle-income countries, non-communicable diseases create large economic costs for societies, especially the health sector. In addition, about 70% of deaths in the economically active population occur due to non-communicable diseases.[19] Thus, it is essential to use behavior change models to change attitude and increase patients’ perceived sensitivity and intensity to adhere to treatment and use a healthy, low-salt diet to prevent the high prevalence of non-communicable diseases and reduce the complications caused by these diseases. Results of the present study revealed that the perceived sensitivity structure of the developed Pender’s model has a strong effect on compliance with a healthy and low-salt diet in patients with uncontrolled blood pressure; thus, researchers, policymakers, decision-makers, and healthcare providers in future high blood pressure control programs can use the structure of perceived sensitivity to increase compliance with a low-salt diet to better control high blood pressure.

Study innovation

Several studies have recommended using the health belief model to increase self-care behavior for controlling high blood pressure in patients with hypertension.[4647] Given that the constructs of perceived sensitivity and severity are not included in the Pender’s health promotion model, the research team decided to add these constructs to the model to improve the control of high blood pressure in patients. In the present study, the constructs of perceived sensitivity and severity were added to the Pender’s model to predict the effect of these constructs on the behavior of following a healthy, low-salt diet and medication adherence behavior. The results of the study with the expanded Pender’s model showed that the construct of perceived sensitivity has a positive and significant effect on the behavior of following a healthy, low-salt diet.

Suggestions for future research

The cross-sectional design limits causal inferences. Future studies should adopt longitudinal or experimental designs to test targeted interventions (e.g. self-efficacy training programs) and assess their long-term effects on blood pressure control, medication adherence, and dietary compliance.

The homogeneous sample (predominantly Arab ethnicity, Ahvaz residents) restricts generalizability. Replication studies in diverse populations (e.g. varying ethnicities, rural vs. urban settings) and countries with different healthcare systems could validate the model’s universality or identify culturally specific mediators.
