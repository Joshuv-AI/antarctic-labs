Evaluation of the accuracy of a custom-made plate in maxillary repositioning
Authors: Wafaa A Mohamed, Susan A Hassan, Fatma I Mohamed, Ahmed A Barakat, Hatem H Alahmady, Mahmoud M Fata, Ahmed A Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Orthognathic surgery has become increasingly accurate with the integration of computerized technologies. This study aimed to evaluate the accuracy of custom-made plates in transferring the three-dimensional virtual plan to intraoperative maxillary repositioning.Methods This study included 10 patients. A digital surgical simulation technique was used for preoperative planning. The surgical components—osteotomy/plates, locating guides, and customized titanium plates—were fabricated using computer-aided design and manufacturing techniques, replacing the need for a surgical splint. Postoperative outcomes were assessed using unaltered anatomical landmarks from the preoperative virtual plan. Absolute discrepancies in translational and rotational movements between the planned and actual postoperative positions were measured through superimposition analysis. This evaluation assessed the precision of custom-made plates in transferring the virtual surgical plan, identifying deviations in the cephalocaudal, mediolateral, and anteroposterior directions that could influence functional and esthetic outcomes.Results No statistically significant differences were observed for any landmarks across the axial, coronal, and sagittal planes. The greatest variability was noted at the posterior nasal spine landmark in the axial plane (1.7 mm), compared with the coronal (0.9 mm) and sagittal (0.5 mm) planes.Conclusion Customized titanium plates effectively transferred the virtual surgical plan for maxillary repositioning without the need for an occlusal splint. However, potential posterior interference should be considered during surgical execution.Keywords: Computer-aided design, Data accuracy, Dental implants, Orthognathic surgery

INTRODUCTION

Successful orthognathic surgery should achieve stable occlusion as well as balanced facial morphology and proportions for the patient [1]. Conventional planning techniques for orthognathic surgery have several limitations, any of which may contribute to suboptimal surgical outcomes. Although these issues may appear minor when considered individually, their combined effects can lead to significant complications [2]. In the classic method of maxillary repositioning, an intermediate splint is used. However, this approach presents inherent challenges, including inaccuracies in vertical alignment and the need for multiple complex laboratory procedures [3]. Understanding the challenges associated with maxillary positioning is particularly important because patients undergoing orthognathic surgery— an elective procedure—typically have high expectations and are often highly sensitive to postoperative facial changes [4].

Recent technological advancements have enabled surgeons to use digital methods to enhance surgical precision and outcomes. Currently, three-dimensional (3D) surgical splints, computer- assisted surgical planning, and fully digital workflows are commonly employed in orthognathic surgery. Virtual treatment- planning protocols allow surgeons to accurately diagnose pitch, roll, yaw, and translational deformities; assess centric relation, and simulate multiple surgical strategies to identify the most effective approach for achieving the desired outcome [5].

Accordingly, new treatment modalities have been investigated to further improve the precision of orthognathic surgery. These include customized surgical drill guides, surgical navigation systems, 3D virtual planning, and patient-specific osteosynthesis (PSO) plates produced through milling or laser sintering [6]. Over the past 5–10 years, custom-made, patient-specific implants have become increasingly common, particularly in complex orthognathic procedures [7].

These custom implants—used widely in cranial and maxillofacial surgery— are manufactured from materials such as titanium, hydroxyapatite, polymethyl-methacrylate, polyether ketone, epoxide acrylate hydroxyapatite, polypropylene–polyester, and various acrylic-based resins [8].

Personalized plates offer a significant advantage in orthognathic surgery by eliminating the need for intermediate occlusal splints and enabling precise jaw repositioning based on the virtual design [9,10]. However, since these interocclusal guides do not provide osteotomy guidance, achieving the precise amount of bone removal required for superior maxillary repositioning via the Le Fort I osteotomy can be challenging. Moreover, condylar instability may influence the final postoperative position of the maxilla when interocclusal wafers are used, as the outcome may depend on mandibular autorotation [11,12].

Despite their advantages, patient-specific plates also present certain limitations in Le Fort I osteotomies, including longer fabrication times, limited flexibility in repositioning screws in thin maxillary bone, an inability to modify the surgical plan intraoperatively if the plates do not fit as intended, uncertainty regarding transverse maxillary stability in segmental cases, and higher costs—although these may be partially offset by improved accuracy and reduced operative adjustments [13].

Evaluating surgical outcomes is essential for ensuring highquality patient care. Although patient-reported outcomes are crucial, objective quantitative assessments are necessary to determine whether the surgical plan has been executed correctly [14]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate how precisely customized plates can transfer the 3D virtual plan to intraoperative maxillary repositioning.

METHODS

Patients

Ten patients were included in the study for bimaxillary orthognathic surgery. They were recruited from the outpatient clinic of the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Faculty of Dental Medicine for Girls, Al-Azhar University, Al-Azhar University Hospital. The same surgical team performed all stages of care—digital planning, preparation, and surgery—for each patient. The study population consisted of 10 young adults (five males and five females) with skeletal malocclusion who underwent the same surgical procedures: Le Fort I osteotomy combined with bilateral sagittal split ramus osteotomy. All cases were managed using the surgery-first approach.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) individuals with skeletal Class II or III malocclusion requiring orthognathic surgery; (2) presence of clinical facial asymmetry; (3) presence of occlusal plane canting; and (4) age between 18 and 40 years, with the ability to read and sign informed consent. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of maxillofacial trauma, (2) presence of maxillofacial tumors, (3) requirement for segmental maxillary surgery, (4) oral soft tissue defects, infections, and bone metabolism disorders, (5) allergy to titanium implants, and (6) presence of systemic disease or immunocompromised status. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Dental Medicine for Girls, Al-Azhar University. A comprehensive medical and surgical history was taken and documented during the initial clinical assessment.

Extraoral examination included evaluation of the mandible and maxilla with respect to anteroposterior relationships, facial asymmetry, nasal dimensions, lip length, facial shape, and tooth– lip relationships. Intraoral assessment involved examination of arch form, occlusion relationships, and any associated malocclusion. Impressions for diagnostic casts were obtained, and standardized intraoral and extraoral photographs were taken.

Preoperative work-up

Diagnostic cast impressions were obtained. The dental casts and registration wafer were laser scanned using an intraoral scanner (Medit i500; Medit Corp., Seoul, Korea) to generate STL files for both the preoperative and anticipated postoperative occlusions. Preoperative computed tomography (CT) scans of the head and neck region were acquired using a multislice CT machine (Aquilion Prime SP; slice thickness 0.5 mm). To ensure proper condylar positioning during scanning, patients were instructed to maintain their mouths in a closed position. The acquired DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) data were imported into Mimics Medical 21.0 (Materialise NV), rendered into 3D form, and used to create a virtual skull model. The orientation of the skull was adjusted according to the Frankfort horizontal, coronal, and midsagittal reference planes. The resulting models were then transferred to 3-matic Medical 14.0 for virtual surgical planning and analysis. Surgical instructions were generated using the program’s available planning and design tools. A 3D composite skull model was created. STL files derived from the scanned dental casts were imported into 3-matic Medical 14.0 and aligned with the dentition of the 3D skull model. The teeth from the scanned digital casts were used to replace the corresponding teeth of the skull model to ensure accuracy. The final (predicted) occlusion was registered by superimposing the scanned final occlusion digital casts onto the post-planned maxillary segment.

Fabrication of a custom-made plate

The screw drilling positions are critical in fabricating a custom plate, as they serve as the connection points between the surgical guide and the plate. After completing the custom-made plate design, the post-planned maxillary segment was duplicated and superimposed onto the pre-planned maxillary model along with the planned screw positions. The surgical guide was then fabricated on the pre-planned model, and the screw positions were digitally subtracted from the guide to create the predictive drill holes. Both the guide and the custom plate were exported from the 3-matic program as STL files. A patient-specific plate was fabricated using Medical Titanium Grade 4, and bone-supported surgical guides for osteotomy and plate positioning were produced using Formlabs Surgical Guide Resin. The combined use of the guide and custom plate facilitated accurate transfer of the final virtual treatment plan to the surgical field (Fig. 1).

Surgical process

Under general anesthesia with nasotracheal intubation and controlled hypotension, local anesthesia was administered using mepivacaine with adrenaline, and standard skin preparation was performed with Betadine. Following an intraoral vestibular incision, the maxilla was fully exposed. A patient-specific drill guide was passively fitted and temporarily secured to the bone using four 1.5 mm screws. The surgical guide included a drilling sleeve to control drill direction and a slit that defined the shape and level of the osteotomy (Fig. 2A).

(C) Fixation of the maxillary Le Fort I segment with customized bone plates.

Drilling through the guide was performed before the osteotomy to create the predictive screw holes for the custom-made plate. A reciprocating saw was then used to perform the Le Fort I osteotomy, guided by the slit in the surgical guides. The osteotomy extended laterally toward the pterygoid plate and anteriorly from the pyriform aperture, with an apical and medial orientation to facilitate separation and mobilization of the maxillary segment (Fig. 2B).

After completing the osteotomy, a controlled down-fracture of the maxilla was performed. Any bony interferences preventing proper placement of the custom-made plate were removed. The maxillary segment was then positioned according to the virtual plan and fixed using the patient-specific plate by inserting screws into the predetermined screw holes (Fig. 2C). Finally, alar base cinching was performed using 2.0 Prolene sutures. Closure of the vestibular incision was achieved using a V-Y suturing technique with 3-0 resorbable braided sutures composed of polyglycolic acid.

Data management and postoperative records

A CT scan obtained 3 days postoperatively was used to compare the virtual surgical plan with the postoperative 3D model using DICOM files. The skull was assessed in three reference planes: the axial plane (Frankfort horizontal), the coronal plane (perpendicular to both the axial and sagittal planes), and the sagittal plane (defined using midline craniofacial landmarks, such as the Nasion, anterior nasal spine [ANS], posterior nasal spine [PNS], and Glabella). Surface-based registration was performed to identify fixed anatomical landmarks on both the postoperative maxilla and the virtually planned model. These landmarks included A-point, ANS, PNS, right and left canine cusp tips, and the mesio-buccal cusps of the right and left maxillary first molar. Linear distances between these points and the reference planes were measured for both the postoperative results and the virtual plan. Angular measurements were obtained by constructing two bounding boxes representing the planned and postoperative 3D maxillary segment. By comparing linear measurements from the preoperative virtual plan with those from actual postoperative results, and by evaluating angular discrepancies (Fig. 3), we assessed the precision of the custom-made plate in transferring the virtual surgical plan to the operative field. This analysis also enabled the detection of deviations in mediolateral, anteroposterior, and cephalocaudal directions, which could influence the final esthetic and functional outcomes. All objective measurements were statistically analyzed using a paired t-test.

Statistical analysis

For each test group, mean and standard deviation (SD) values were calculated. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro– Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, both of which indicated a parametric (normal) distribution. Paired sample tests were employed to compare two related groups. For comparisons involving more than two related groups, a repeated-measure analysis of variance was applied. The level of statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.

RESULTS

The study included 10 patients (five males and five females) aged 18–22 years with Class III skeletal deformity, all of whom underwent maxillary advancement and impaction. Maxillary advancement ranged from 4 mm to 8 mm, while maxillary impaction ranged from 2 mm to 5 mm.

Minimal deviations were observed between the virtual surgical plan and the postoperative outcomes when using custom-made plates for maxillary repositioning. Mean linear deviations were 0.8 mm, 0.5 mm, and 0.7 mm in the axial, sagittal, and coronal planes, respectively. Seven landmarks (A, ANS, CT UPL3, CT UPR3, MBC UL6, MBC UR6, and PNS) were evaluated. Most landmarks showed no statistically significant differences; however, a significant difference was observed at the PNS landmark (p=0.015).

No significant differences in accuracy were detected among the planes for most landmarks (p≥0.05), except at the PNS point, where a significant difference was noted between the axial, sagittal and coronal planes (p=0.015) (Table 1, Fig. 4). Mean angular discrepancies were 2.45° for pitch, 2.25° for yaw, and 1.43° for roll (Table 2, Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

Advances in 3D imaging and computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technologies have transformed traditional approaches for orthognathic surgery. Fabricating an intermediate splint to modify the sagittal and transverse positions of the maxillary segments is time-consuming and prone to technical errors. In particular, vertical maxillary repositioning can be challenging during surgery, whether using a traditional or a CAD/CAM-generated intermediate wafer [15,16].

All patients in the present study underwent 3D diagnosis and surgical planning, which provide enhanced diagnostic information such as dental root position, bone thickness, and bone morphology at the intended osteotomy sites. This approach facilitates identification of anatomical abnormalities—such as sinus pathology and thin bones—and supports comprehensive preoperative preparation. Mimics simulation software was used for virtual planning due to its accuracy, user-friendly interface, and broad applicability across maxillofacial and oral surgery.

Given the importance of accurate maxillomandibular interocclusal relationships, a surface-based registration technique was employed to achieve precise superimposition and to generate a composite skull model.

Han et al. [14] demonstrated that surface-based registration is accurate and reliable, with no significant differences compared to voxel-based registration when constructing composite skull models. In this study, 3D-printed patient-specific surgical guides were used to determine the osteotomy line and to create predictive drilling holes for the custom-made plate. These predictive screw holes were carefully planned to ensure that the cutting guide could be secured in a stable position. As the same holes were replicated virtually in the patient-specific plate, the plate could be positioned with high precision according to the virtual plan [17].

A single bone-supported surgical guide customized for each patient and extending across the midline was used in this study to ensure optimal fit and precise positioning. This approach is consistent with the findings of Hanafy et al. [18], who employed a one-piece cutting guide that crossed the midline. In contrast, another study used two separate guides—one for each side— along with an additional verification step using image-guided navigation [19].

However, such devices may be heavy, obstruct the surgeon’s field of view, and make it difficult to maintain the Le Fort I segment in the planned position until fixation plates are applied [20]. The surgical guides used in the present study were boneborne and fabricated from resin. Resin-based guides, however, may undergo deformation under manual pressure. Other studies have reported advantages of bone-borne titanium guides, such as improved drill alignment and enhanced stability. Nonetheless, even a small mismatch on one side of a single titanium guide may produce greater inaccuracies on the opposite side [21].

To balance these considerations, the current study combined the benefits of both materials by using resin-based guides equipped with metallic sleeves. These sleeves provided stable drill guidance and minimized heat-related distortion of the resin. In the present study, postoperative CT scans were performed 3 days after surgery.

The objective was not to assess long-term skeletal stability but rather to verify the accuracy of surgical transfer by comparing the planned and immediate postoperative positions. This rationale explains the early timing of imaging. By contrast, other studies performed postoperative CT imaging after longer intervals because their primary aim was to evaluate skeletal stability rather than surgical transfer accuracy [1,22].

The accuracy of maxillary repositioning was assessed using surface-based registration to compare planned and actual postoperative positions. No significant differences were detected across the five landmarks or the three spatial planes. Mean deviations were greatest in the axial (0.8 mm) and coronal (0.7 mm) planes in comparison to the sagittal (0.5 mm) plane. These findings are consistent with the results of Amarista and Ellis [23], who evaluated absolute differences without considering the direction of deviation [24].

Kraeima et al. [21] reported that the splintless PSO method is clinically relevant for planned anteroposterior translations greater than 3.70 mm and improves the accuracy of maxillary translation in orthognathic surgery. However, this study used a more substantial tooth–bone-supported guide. In the present study, the only statistically significant difference was observed at the PNS landmark in the axial plane (1.7 mm) compared with the sagittal (0.4 mm) and coronal (0.9 mm) planes.

This likely reflects increased posterior interference during repositioning of the maxillary segment, particularly because no guide was used to aid in posterior bone removal. This finding emphasizes the need for careful assessment and removal of posterior bony interferences, particularly during maxillary impaction. Since the patient-specific implant is attached to the anterior maxillary surface, determining whether sufficient posterior bone has been removed can be challenging.

This conclusion is consistent with the findings of Kraeima et al. [21], who reported that the PSO approach did not improve the accuracy of maxillary impaction, likely due to the surgeondependent removal of bone interferences.

Conversely, Heufelder et al. [25] found that anteroposterior maxillary positioning exhibited the greatest deviations between the planned and actual postoperative outcomes. Regarding angular discrepancies, the mean differences between planned and postoperative orientations showed the smallest variation in roll (1.43°), followed by yaw (2.25°), while the largest difference occurred in pitch (2.45°). All angular deviations were less than 4°, and no statistically significant differences were observed. Variations of less than 4° were considered clinically nonsignificant in this study [12].

Clinical application should be evaluated with consideration of several practical limitations in addition to the posterior interference observed at the PNS landmark. These include the comparatively high cost of designing and fabricating patient-specific guides and plates—although resin surgical guides are less expensive than metal ones—the lengthy manufacturing process, which may delay surgery and increase workload, and the limited intraoperative flexibility once the guide and plate have been fabricated. Additionally, technical challenges during 3D design or printing may occur and have the potential to disrupt the surgical workflow. Moreover, the relatively small sample size and short follow-up period of the present study limit the generalizability of the findings. Although these limitations do not undermine the accuracy demonstrated in this investigation, they underscore the need to balance feasibility and precision to ensure broader clinical adoption.

In conclusion, the findings of this study indicate that patientspecific plates combined with 3D planning enable accurate maxillary repositioning without the use of splints. This approach is clinically feasible but requires further evaluation of cost, workflow requirements, and technical limitations before widespread implementation.
