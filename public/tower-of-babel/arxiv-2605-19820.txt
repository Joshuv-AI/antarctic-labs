Geometric curvature driven by many-body collective fluctuations
Alejandro S. Miñarro, Gervasi Herranz

Abstract
Quantum geometry characterizes the variation of wavefunctions in momentum space through their overlaps and relative phases, providing a general framework for understanding many transport and optical properties. It is generally formulated in terms of interband matrix elements, which, entering the response functions, allow obtaining experimental access to the quantum geometric tensor. Recently, it has been emphasized that quantum geometry can also be interpreted in terms of quantum dipole fluctuations in the ground state driven by interband mixing. Here, we extend this picture to include contributions from many-body collective fluctuations, in which propagators and response vertices are dressed dynamically by the interaction with collective modes. Focusing on the Berry curvature, we show that contributions from collective fluctuations can be experimentally distinguished from bare band-geometric contributions, via specific antisymmetric channels in inelastic scattering spectra. We further identify the non-commutative properties of transverse quantum fluctuations as well as non-local-time interactions as the generators of this dynamical curvature in the susceptibility response.

I.2
Geometric curvature driven by dynamical fluctuations

We analyze how the interaction with a fluctuating field provides a distinct mechanism to generate spectral
\Omega(\mathbf{k},\omega)
and geometric
\Omega({\mathbf{k}})
curvatures in momentum-frequency space. Ignoring fluctuations, the effective momentum gauge structure in
\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{T}
systems is trivial and the curvature vanishes identically in the response function. To fully isolate the geometric contribution from fluctuations, we first define a reference response by describing the linear susceptibility in terms of instantaneous current vertices
j_{\mathbf{k}}(t,t^{\prime})\propto\delta(t-t^{\prime})
connected by two fermionic lines, corresponding to the bare (undressed) susceptibility bubble diagram shown in Figure
1
a. Because of the constraints imposed by
\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{T}
symmetries, both geometric and optical spectral curvatures vanish throughout the
(\mathbf{k},\omega)
space of the susceptibility response (except for singularities associated with isolated band degeneracies where it is ill-defined, such as in Dirac nodes at specific momenta, see Figure
1
b).

A different situation, discussed more in depth in section
II.2
, arises as soon as coupling to a fluctuating field is considered, leading to a dressing of propagators and vertices and a distinctive dynamical curvature. In this situation, the interaction with collective fluctuations can generate a self-energy
\Sigma(\mathbf{k},\omega)
with nontrivial momentum and frequency structure (Figure
1
c), allowing localized regions of finite curvature in the linear response function (Figure
1
d). Crucially, the exchange mediated by bosonic propagators provides momentum-dependent vertex corrections
\delta j_{\mathbf{k}}(t,t^{\prime})\not\propto\delta(t-t^{\prime})
that introduce time-nonlocality and memory effects in the effective interactions, driving nontrivial geometry in
(\mathbf{k},\omega)
space. This dynamically generated curvature requires all of the following ingredients: (i) a nontrivial momentum-frequency structure that gives rise to non-commutative parallel transport in
(\mathbf{k},\omega)
space due to the emergence of a fluctuation-driven gauge (
1
e); (ii) a multiband structure that can host nontrivial internal phases; and (iii) a mechanism that generates such phases. Spin-orbit coupling is the most natural mechanism in
\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{T}
symmetric systems, since it can create internal complex phases without breaking time-reversal symmetry.

The emergence of this effective gauge structure can be understood intuitively in terms of the non-commutative properties of the operators governing the propagation of quantum fluctuations. In Figure
2
we illustrate this point by considering ladder operators for transverse spin fluctuations
S_{+},S_{-}
applied in the space of momenta and imaginary times. In this context, the curvature arises from differences in the geometric phases accumulated when the fluctuation operators are propagated between two endpoints. Along each path in the
(\mathbf{k},\tau)
manifold, the transverse quantum operators
S_{+}
versus
S_{-}
cannot diagonalize on the same spin-orbital basis, resulting in a nontrivial phase mismatch that can be interpreted as the flux of an enclosed spectral curvature. Thus, the geometric structure does not arise from propagation alone, but from the interplay between time-nonlocal propagation and the non-commutative character of the transverse fluctuation operators. Interestingly, this observation is consistent with the interpretation of anomalous Hall velocities and Berry curvature in terms of non-commuting effective position operators
39
;
122
;
25
. The role of time-nonlocality and non-commutativity in the emergence of dynamical curvature is developed in section
II.2
.

In Section
II.1
, we start by discussing the linear response of
\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{T}
symmetric systems based on
t_{2g}
states. The latter are relevant to describe the properties of a large family of heavy transition metal compounds that display strongly correlated states and large strong-spin-orbit coupling, which favors the emergence of unconventional phases
105
;
54
;
10
;
71
;
88
;
81
. Due to the imposed space inversion and time reversal symmetries, the antisymmetric transverse response is forced to vanish. In Section
II.2
, focusing on spin fluctuations, we show that an antisymmetric transverse component emerges in the response function due to the retardation effects and momentum dependence arising from coupling to collective modes. In Section
III

IV

II.2
Dynamical curvature from time-nonlocal vertices and non-commutative fluctuation operators

We now show that a finite spectral curvature emerges as soon as retarded (time-nonlocal) contributions and non-commutativity of quantum transverse fluctuations are taken into account. An intuition into these mechanisms is gained from the formulation of Hall-like responses in the Ishikawa-Mastuyama model for dc transport

terms (which would make the structure traceless). In contrast, when vertices are instantaneous (as in Equation
1
), the cyclical properties of the trace can be used to map the two fermionic lines to each other in the antisymmetrized bubble, making the corresponding algebraic structure traceless.

These observations show that non-local time correlations are an essential ingredient to allow non-zero antisymmetric susceptibility. Additionally, as mentioned previously, transverse quantum fluctuations are also a crucial ingredient. The latter can be intuitively understood by observing that transverse spin ladder operators
S_{+}
,
S_{-}
are necessary to give a nontrivial structure to spin-orbit coupling, otherwise its matrix reduces to the diagonal sector coming from
L_{z}S_{z}
products, for which propagators and vertices, even if dressed by interactions, can be represented in the same spin-orbital basis, suppressing any relevant commutators. Therefore, both non-commutative transverse quantum fluctuations as well as time-non-locality are necessary ingredients to obtain nonzero spectral weight for the antisymmetric susceptibility.

ANTISYMMETRIC SUSCEPTIBILITY AND DYNAMICAL BERRY CURVATURE

III.1
Spectral and geometric Berry curvature

Henceforth, we explore the connection between the antisymmetric susceptibility channel and the geometric curvature, establishing a rigorous connection with the geometry in the response function. For that, we distinguish between the spectral Berry curvature and the dressed geometric curvature, the latter being the geometric object of reference. Since the emergence of spectral weight
\bm{\chi}^{as}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
is driven by fluctuations, the associated curvature must be described with geometric models in which interaction effects are included
99
;
86
;
93
;
50
;
84
;
14
. Among possible extensions of quantum geometry to interacting systems
50
;
103

14
seems to provide good approximations to the quantum metric, at least for flat-band and weakly dispersive systems
103
. We therefore follow the interaction-dressed quantum geometry, which relies on the charge polarization susceptibility to define self-energy corrections
14
. Following this approach, a frequency-resolved spectral curvature can be defined from the antisymmetric component of the conductivity
\Omega^{\gamma}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=\dfrac{1}{\omega}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\Im\left[\sigma^{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\right]
. Its integration in frequency gives rise to a dressed curvature
\Omega^{\gamma}(\mathbf{k})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\Omega^{\gamma}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
, which reduces to the conventional Berry curvature in the noninteracting zero-temperature limit
14
.

In view of these considerations, we therefore connect the spectral curvature
\Omega^{\gamma}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
with the antisymmetric susceptibility
\bm{\chi}^{as}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
defined in Equation
3

\bm{\chi}^{as}(\mathbf{k},\tau)=\bm{\chi}^{as}(\mathbf{k},\beta-\tau)

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}(\mathbf{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Im\left[\bm{\sigma}^{as}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\right]}{\omega}

(4)

In turn, the dressed geometric curvature can be obtained from the (adiabatic) low-frequency limit of the spectral curvature
\bm{\Omega}(\mathbf{k},\omega\to 0)=\Im\left[d\bm{\sigma}^{as}(\mathbf{k},\omega)/d\omega\right]_{\omega=0}

14

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\Omega(\mathbf{k})}&=\pi\Re\left[\bm{\sigma}^{as}(\mathbf{k},\omega=0)\right]\\
&=\pi\left.\dfrac{d}{d\omega}\Im\left[\bm{\chi}^{as}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\right]\right|_{\omega=0}\end{split}

(5)

IV
PREDICTED RIXS SIGNATURES OF DYNAMICAL CURVATURE

So far, we have shown that the spectral curvature generated by fluctuations emerges in localized regions of the momentum-frequency space of the antisymmetric linear optical susceptibility. However, as mentioned above, optical spectroscopies do not allow to probe this curvature due to the nearly vanishing momentum transfer
\mathbf{q}\approx 0
of optical photons. Here we address the following question: can other probes, in particular, resonant inelastic x-ray scattering spectroscopy (RIXS), enable the direct detection of spectral curvature generated genuinely from coupling to collective fluctuations?

\pm 45^{\circ}

V
CONCLUSION

In this work, we have shown that inelastic spectroscopies can be used to detect excitations related to the emergence of geometric curvature driven exclusively by dynamical coupling to fluctuations. A key ingredient is finite-
\mathbf{q}
momentum transfer, which enables the detection of regions of finite spectral weight in momentum frequency space where fluctuation-driven geometries are generated. Thus, we show that manybody contributions to quantum geometry, which have recently received attention
103
;
14
;
50
;
84
;
30
;
46
, can be selectively detected by specific antisymmetric channels of the inelastic susceptibility. We further identify the non-commutative properties of transverse quantum fluctuations as well as non-local-time interactions as the generators of the effective gauge structure associated with the fluctuation-driven curvature.

Our interpretation in terms of geometry is crucial: without it, we may lose an essential framework to understand some deep physical implications. For example, recent work emphasizes the relation between quantum Fisher information and quantum metric, which can be measured experimentally by resonant inelastic x-ray spectroscopy, providing information on the entanglement of electronic orbitals
25
;
90
. This is an example of how response functions allow access to quantum metric and quantum information
25
;
124
. Our approach is complementary: rather than targeting contributions from the real part of the geometric tensor (the quantum metric), we focus on antisymmetric contributions that generate a dynamical curvature structure in the response functions, giving access to the imaginary part of the geometric tensor and the geometric phases driven by interactions with fluctuations. As a future perspective, one may also address how to detect manybody quantum contributions to the quantum metric, rather than curvature. Though the measurement of quantum metric is usually more challenging, its investigation may benefit from recent developments in angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy
48
;
46
;
59
or ultrafast techniques
111
.

The work presented here should be viewed as a minimal theoretical approach. More quantitative models should rely on the inclusion of
e_{g}
states
87
;
101
;
76
;
75
as well as contributions from charge transfer processes. Also, to explore these higher energy excitations, RIXS models should extend beyond the ultrafast collision approximation and include the effects of core-hole potentials
4
;
64
. In addition, material-specific contributions to the electronic structure should also be considered. Despite these limitations, the work described here shows that RIXS is a promising route to probe dynamical fluctuation-driven responses and associated geometric structures in correlated spin-orbit-coupled systems.

A.1
Computation of the optical susceptibility

To compute the longitudinal and antisymmetric transverse part of the optical susceptibility, we work within the Kubo formalism. We focus on obtaining linear responses in inversion (
\mathcal{P}
) time-reversal (
\mathcal{T}
) symmetric systems. The susceptibility is determined from the retarded current-current correlation function. The optical conductivity tensor is defined as

\sigma_{ij}(\omega)=\frac{1}{i\omega}\chi_{ij}^{R}(\omega),

(12)

where
\chi_{ij}^{R}(\omega)
is the retarded current–current propagator. The longitudinal optical conductivity corresponds to the diagonal components of this tensor,

\sigma_{ii}(\omega)=\frac{1}{i\omega}\left[\chi_{ii}^{R}(\omega)-\dfrac{e^{2}\langle\hat{n}\rangle}{m}\right],

(13)

In addition to the longitudinal response, we consider the antisymmetric component of the optical susceptibility

\displaystyle\chi_{ij}^{as}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\big[\chi_{ij}(\omega)-\chi_{ji}(\omega)\big].

(14)

This antisymmetric contribution encodes Hall-like optical responses and is directly connected to the spectral Berry curvature.

To evaluate these quantities, we first obtain the current–current correlator in imaginary time in the Matsubara branch

\chi_{\mu\nu}(\tau)=\left\langle\hat{j}^{\mu}(\tau)\hat{j}^{\nu}(0)\right\rangle.

(15)

where
\hat{j}^{\mu}(\tau)
is the
\mu
component of the current operator in imaginary time. Within the diagrammatic expansion, the current-current correlator can be written as

\displaystyle\chi_{\mu\nu}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\hat{j}^{\mu}\right\rangle\left\langle\hat{j}^{\nu}\right\rangle

(16)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\hat{j}^{\mu}_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\hat{j}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{k}}\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(-\tau)\right].

where, in equilibrium, the first term vanishes in the absence of external fields. Then, Fourier transforming to Matsubara frequencies and performing analytic continuation
i\omega_{n}\rightarrow\omega+i0^{+}
yields the retarded propagator
\chi^{R}_{\mu\nu}(\omega)
, from which we obtain the optical conductivity and susceptibility as a function of real frequencies. In the following, we first describe how we obtain the propagators in imaginary time and, subsequently, how we continue analytically to obtain the propagators in real frequencies.

A.2
Lattice model in the Matsubara formalism

To obtain the propagators in imaginary time, we define the Green’s functions, which are solved self-consistently as follows

[\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)]_{\alpha\sigma,\alpha^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}=-\langle\mathcal{T}_{\tau}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}\alpha\sigma}(\tau)\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\alpha^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}(0)\rangle.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{latt}+\mathcal{H}_{JT}+\mathcal{H}_{ph}+\mathcal{H}_{SO}+\mathcal{H}_{ee}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{latt}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\sum_{\mu,\nu}\sum_{\sigma}t_{i\mu,j\nu}c_{i\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{j\beta\sigma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\mu,\nu}\sum_{\sigma}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k})c_{\mathbf{k}\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k}\beta\sigma}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{ph}

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}\sum_{i}\!\left(b_{i\phi}^{\dagger}b_{i\phi}+b_{i\theta}^{\dagger}b_{i\theta}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{JT}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,\eta\in\{3,8\},\alpha,\beta,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}g_{\eta}\lambda^{\eta}_{\alpha\beta}c_{i\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{i\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\left(b_{i\eta}+b_{i\eta}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{SO}

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\sum_{\mu,\nu\in d}\langle\mu|\mathbf{l}|\nu\rangle\!\cdot\!\langle\sigma|\bm{\sigma}|\sigma^{\prime}\rangle\,c_{i\mu\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{i\nu\sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{ee}

\displaystyle=U\sum_{i,\alpha}n_{i\alpha\uparrow}n_{i\alpha\downarrow}+U^{\prime}\sum_{i,\alpha\neq\gamma}n_{i\alpha\uparrow}n_{i\gamma\downarrow}

\displaystyle\quad+(U^{\prime}-J)\sum_{i,\alpha<\gamma,\sigma}n_{i\alpha\sigma}n_{i\gamma\sigma}

\displaystyle\quad-J\sum_{i,\alpha\neq\gamma}c_{i\alpha\uparrow}^{\dagger}\left(c_{i\alpha\downarrow}c_{i\gamma\downarrow}^{\dagger}+c_{i\gamma\downarrow}c_{i\alpha\downarrow}^{\dagger}\right)c_{i\gamma\uparrow}

(17)

This model describes

To solve the Hamiltonian, we iterate the following self-consistent equations

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{G}(\imath\omega_{n})&=\left[\hat{\mathfrak{G}}^{-1}(\imath\omega_{n})-\left(\hat{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{HF}}+\hat{\Sigma}^{(2)}(\imath\omega_{n})\right.\right.\\
&\phantom{=}\left.\left.+\hat{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{ep}}(\imath\omega_{n})\right)\right]^{-1}\end{split}

(18)

A.3
Analytic continuation

To obtain the optical responses, we perform an analytic continuation from the imaginary to the real frequency domain. For that, we use the intermediate representation (IR) framework
16
;
96
;
113
, using the fact that the susceptibility is related to the optical conductivity through a Fredholm integral equation of the first kind
42
;
31
,

\chi^{p}_{ij}(\tau)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,K(\tau,\omega)\,\Re[\sigma^{p}_{ij}(\omega)],

Here,
\chi^{p}_{ij}(\omega)
and
\sigma^{p}_{ij}(\omega)
are the paramagnetic susceptibility and conductivity, while the bosonic kernel is
K(\tau,\omega)=\omega e^{-\tau\omega}/(1-e^{-\beta\omega})
. To solve this inverse problem, the kernel is compressed via singular value decomposition
16
;
96
;
113
,

K(\tau,\omega)=\sum_{l}U_{l}(\tau)S_{l}V_{l}(\omega)
,
where
U_{l}(\tau)
and
V_{l}(\omega)
are orthonormal bases and
S_{l}
decay exponentially, so that only a few modes contribute significantly. In the IR basis, the integral equation becomes a linear system,

\chi^{p}_{ij;l}+\sum_{m}\mathcal{K}_{lm}\,\Xi_{ij;m}=0,

where
\chi^{p}_{ij;l}
are the IR coefficients of the imaginary-time data,
\mathcal{K}_{lm}
are kernel matrix elements, and the spectral function is defined as
\Xi_{ij}(\omega)=\Re[\sigma_{ij}(\omega)]/\pi
. The system is solved with Tikhonov regularization
28
;
94
to stabilize the inversion, and the full conductivity is then reconstructed, with its real and imaginary parts related by the Kramers–Kronig relation.

Appendix B
DENSITY CURRENT OPERATORS

We now discuss the current operators that enter the current correlation functions defined in Equation
16
. For that, let us consider the density current operator
\hat{\mathbf{j}}(t)
defined as the variation of the Hamiltonian with respect to the vector potential
\mathbf{A}(t)
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{j}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\,\frac{\delta\hat{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta\mathbf{A}(t)}.

Introducing a vector potential in an electronic system requires a minimal substitution of the canonical momentum to preserve the gauge invariance,

\hat{\mathbf{p}}\rightarrow\hat{\mathbf{p}}+e\mathbf{A}(t)
,
where
e
is the electron charge.
This substitution introduces additional terms in the Hamiltonian that depend explicitly on
\mathbf{A}(t)
, from which one obtains the expression for the density current:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{j}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\,\frac{e}{m}\hat{\mathbf{p}}-\frac{e^{2}}{m}\hat{n}\,\mathbf{A}(t),

(20)

where the first and second terms correspond to the paramagnetic and diamagnetic contributions, respectively.
In this expression,
m
denotes the effective mass and
\hat{n}
the particle–density operator.
Both terms contribute to the linear susceptibility, defined as the variation of the current expectation value with respect to the vector potential,

\displaystyle\chi_{ij}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{\delta\langle\hat{J}_{i}(t)\rangle}{\delta A_{j}(t^{\prime})}.

(21)

On the other hand, if the variation is done with respect to the electric field,
\mathbf{E}(t)=-\partial_{t}\mathbf{A}(t)
we obtain the conductivity

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\delta\left\langle\hat{J}_{i}(t)\right\rangle}{\delta E_{j}(t^{\prime})}.

(22)

The relation between the vector potential and the electric field gives way to a relation between current susceptibility and conductivity.

\displaystyle\chi_{ij}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t^{\prime}}\sigma_{ij}(t,t^{\prime}).

(23)

In the absence of dispersion, the response depends only on the time difference
t-t^{\prime}
, allowing a Fourier representation in frequency space. Equation (
23
) then becomes

\displaystyle\chi_{ij}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\imath\omega\,\sigma_{ij}(\omega).

(24)

To compute the susceptibility, we can split the total current
\hat{\mathbf{j}}(t)
(Equation
20
) into paramagnetic and diamagnetic contributions. The diamagnetic term of the current is

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{\mathbf{j}}^{d}(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{e^{2}}{m}\left\langle\hat{n}\right\rangle\mathbf{A}(t),

(25)

so, this term contributes to the susceptibility with

\displaystyle\chi_{ij}^{d}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{e^{2}}{m}\left\langle\hat{n}\right\rangle\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta_{ij}

(26)

and transformed in the frequency domain as

\displaystyle\chi_{ij}^{d}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{e^{2}}{m}\left\langle\hat{n}\right\rangle\delta_{ij}.

(27)

The diamagnetic contribution
\chi^{d}_{ij}(\omega)
is instantaneous (time-local), symmetric in the spatial indices, and time-reversal even. Consequently, it does not contribute to the antisymmetric, time-reversal-odd components of the optical response. Therefore, only paramagnetic current correlations enter the evaluation of the antisymmetric susceptibility
\chi^{as}_{ij}
defined in Equation
3
. This paramagnetic contribution arises from the perturbation
-\hat{\mathbf{j}}^{\,p}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{A}(t)
term in the Hamiltonian. Its contribution to the susceptibility is obtained from the Kubo formula, which, written in imaginary time, can be expressed as

\displaystyle\chi_{ij}^{p}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{\tau}\hat{\tilde{j}}^{p}_{i}(\tau)\hat{\tilde{j}}^{p}_{j}(0)\right\rangle

(28)

After analytical continuation, the imaginary part of the conductivity can be obtained from the real part through the Kramers–Kronig relations,

\displaystyle\Im\left[\sigma(\omega)\right]

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\dfrac{\Re\left[\sigma(\omega^{\prime})\right]}{\omega^{\prime}-\omega}.

(29)

Using the relation between conductivity and current susceptibility in Eq. (
24
), the imaginary part of the conductivity can be written in terms of the real part of the paramagnetic susceptibility together with the diamagnetic contribution in Eq. (
27
),

\displaystyle\Im\left[\sigma_{ij}(\omega)\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\omega}\left\{\dfrac{e^{2}\left\langle\hat{n}\right\rangle}{m}\delta_{ij}-\Re\left[\chi^{p}_{ij}(\omega)\right]\right\}.

(30)

In the dc limit (
\omega\to 0
) the conductivity must remain finite.
Since the above expression contains a
1/\omega
prefactor, the diamagnetic term must cancel the zero–frequency paramagnetic susceptibility, leading to

\displaystyle\Re\left[\chi^{p}_{ii}(0)\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{2}\left\langle\hat{n}\right\rangle}{m}.

(31)

This condition follows from gauge invariance and corresponds to the well-known
f
–sum rule for the current response.

Appendix C
NON-LOCAL-TIME CORRELATIONS AND THE ANTISYMMETRIC SUSCEPTIBILITY

C.1
Symmetry constraints and the antisymmetric response

Cubic symmetry
O_{h}
forces the current response tensor to be isotropic,
\chi_{ij}(\omega)=\chi(\omega)\delta_{ij}
, which therefore suppresses any antisymmetric component of the susceptibility. Consequently, to obtain a finite antisymmetric response, we require a symmetry below
O_{h}
. This can be achieved, for instance, through anisotropic intersite hopping or lattice distortions.

In our calculations, we consider Jahn-Teller effects as the source of anisotropy for the hopping amplitudes. For that, we compute the self-energy generated by stabilizing Jahn-Teller distortions using the Galitzkii-Migdal approximation
73
;
24
;
82
(we discuss the procedure in Ref.
74
). We choose a Jahn-Teller distortion with
E_{g}
symmetry (allowed in the vibronic coupling to
t_{2g}
states
54
;
7
;
102
), characterized by an angle
\theta
and modify the Green functions obtained self-consistently by introducing an orbital polarization consistent with the stabilized Jahn-Teller mode

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\imath\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\left[\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}^{-1}(\imath\omega_{n})-\mathcal{E}_{jt}\left(\hat{\lambda}^{3}\sin\theta+\hat{\lambda}^{8}\cos\theta\right)\right]^{-1}

(32)

where
\mathcal{E}_{jt}
is the energy of Jahn-teller mode computed using the Galitskii-Migdal formula
73
. To simplify the notation, we use
\hat{G}
in the main text to refer to the orbitally-polarized Green function
\hat{\tilde{G}}
defined by Equation
32
. The two diagonal Gell–Mann matrices,

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{3}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\hat{\lambda}_{8}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&-2\end{pmatrix},

(33)

describe orthorhombic (
\hat{\lambda}_{3}
) and tetragonal (
\hat{\lambda}_{8}
)
E_{g}
Jahn-Teller modes. In the
t_{2g}
basis
(d_{yz},d_{zx},d_{xy})
, they measure the imbalance in the
orbital populations
\hat{\lambda}_{3}\sim n_{yz}-n_{zx},\hat{\lambda}_{8}\sim n_{zx}+n_{yz}-2n_{xy}
.

The stabilization of a Jahn-Teller mode implies an anisotropy in the bond lengths
R^{\alpha}=R_{0}+\delta R^{\alpha}
which, in turn, induce the anisotropic hopping amplitudes.
Using Harrison scaling for Slater-Koster hopping amplitudes,
t(R)\propto R^{-3}

32
;
97
, a small distortion gives

\delta t^{\alpha}\equiv t^{\alpha}-t\simeq-3t\,\frac{\delta R^{\alpha}}{R_{0}}.

(34)

We can expand conveniently the orbitally-polarized Green function into separate pure orbital and spin-orbital mixed spaces as

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}}\Big[g_{\mathbf{k}}^{\alpha}(i\omega_{n})|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\otimes\hat{\sigma}^{0}

(35)

\displaystyle-\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\alpha}(i\omega_{n})\hat{l}^{\alpha}\otimes\hat{\sigma}^{\alpha}\Big].

where
\hat{l}^{\alpha}
are orbital momentum operators and
\hat{\sigma}^{\alpha}
Pauli spin matrices. Expression
35
allows to make explicit separation between spin-independent contributions from orbital degrees of freedom (first term) and spin-orbit entanglement (second term). The orbital polarization can then be extracted from the equal-time Green function using the Matsubara identity for the density matrix
\hat{\rho}=\hat{G}(\beta)
.
Projecting the density matrix onto the Gell–Mann generators gives

\langle\hat{\lambda}_{\gamma}\rangle=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}g_{\mathbf{k}}^{\gamma}(\beta).

(36)

This follows from

\langle\hat{\lambda}_{\gamma}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\lambda}_{\gamma}\hat{\rho}]

together with
\hat{\rho}=-\hat{G}(\beta)
and the traceless property of the Gell–Mann matrices,
\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{\lambda}_{\gamma})=0
, which implies

\langle\hat{\lambda}_{\gamma}\rangle=-\,\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\lambda}_{\gamma}\hat{G}(\beta)]
.
The corresponding Jahn–Teller distortion amplitude is therefore

Q^{\gamma}=R_{0}\langle\hat{\lambda}_{\gamma}\rangle.

(37)

where
\hat{\lambda}_{\gamma}=\hat{\lambda}^{3}\sin\theta+\hat{\lambda}^{8}\cos\theta
denotes an orbital polarization along the unit vector

\hat{\gamma}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
in the
(\lambda_{3},\lambda_{8})
plane. By imposing volume conservation
\sum_{\alpha}\delta R^{\alpha}=0
, the change in bond-length along the axis
\alpha
can be written in terms of the components of orbital polarization
g_{\mathbf{k}}^{\gamma}(\beta)
as

\displaystyle\delta R^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{2R_{0}}{3\mathcal{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\gamma}g_{\mathbf{k}}^{\gamma}(\beta)\left[2\delta_{\alpha\gamma}-(1-\delta_{\alpha\gamma})\right].

(38)

Using the Harrison scaling of the Slater–Koster hopping amplitudes, the corresponding change in the hopping amplitude becomes

\displaystyle\delta t^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2t}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\gamma}g_{\mathbf{k}}^{\gamma}(\beta)\left[2\delta_{\alpha\gamma}-(1-\delta_{\alpha\gamma})\right].

(39)

Our calculations provide values for the anisotropy of the hopping amplitude in the order of
t^{\alpha}/t\approx 10^{-3}-10^{-2}
. Taking into account these values, we compute our lattice model assuming tetragonal elongations along
z
, where
t^{x}=t^{y}\neq t^{z}
and

\frac{t^{z}-t^{x}}{t^{x}}=\frac{t^{z}-t^{y}}{t^{y}}\approx 10^{-3}\text{--}10^{-2}.

C.2
Derivation of the antisymmetric susceptibility tensor

Once the symmetry is reduced below
O_{h}
, a finite antisymmetric component is allowed in the susceptibility. As discussed in the main text, the key ingredient is the coupling to dynamical fluctuations, where the non-commutativity of transverse fluctuation operators together with time non-local corrections to the current vertex generate the finite spectral curvature. It is therefore important to understand the properties of correlations involving time non-local fermionic operators, and how these effects enable a finite curvature. Bearing this in mind, we first briefly review the formalism of non-local-time operators and, subsequently, explain why the properties of non-local matrices, particularly the cyclical properties of the trace, enable a finite, nonzero antisymmetric response.

C.2.1
Non-local time correlations

Let us consider two time non-local operators
\hat{\mathcal{P}},\hat{\mathcal{Q}}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int d\tau^{\prime}\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}(\tau)\hat{p}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\hat{\mathbf{c}}(\tau^{\prime})

(40a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{Q}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int d\tau^{\prime}\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{\dagger}(\tau)\hat{q}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\hat{\mathbf{c}}(\tau^{\prime})

(40b)

whose correlation in imaginary time is expressed as

\displaystyle\Pi(\tau)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{P}}(\tau)\hat{\mathcal{Q}}(0)\right\rangle.

(41)

This expression can be expanded through quantum indices

\displaystyle\Pi(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\sum_{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\iint d\tau^{\prime}d\tau^{\prime\prime}\,p_{\mu\nu}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})q_{\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}(-\tau^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\times\left\langle\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mu}(\tau)\hat{c}_{\nu}(\tau^{\prime})\hat{c}_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(0)\hat{c}_{\nu^{\prime}}(\tau^{\prime\prime})\right\rangle.

(42)

These correlators can be computed with two-particle Green’s function

\displaystyle\begin{split}G^{(2)}_{\mu\nu\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3},\tau_{4})&=\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{T}}\,\hat{c}_{\mu}^{\dagger}(\tau_{1})\hat{c}_{\nu}(\tau_{2})\hat{c}_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(\tau_{3})\hat{c}_{\nu^{\prime}}(\tau_{4})\right\rangle\\[3.0pt]
&=G_{\nu\mu}(\tau_{2}{-}\tau_{1})G_{\nu^{\prime}\mu^{\prime}}(\tau_{4}{-}\tau_{3})\\[3.0pt]
&\quad-\,G_{\nu\mu^{\prime}}(\tau_{2}{-}\tau_{3})G_{\nu^{\prime}\mu}(\tau_{4}{-}\tau_{1})\\[3.0pt]
&\quad+\,\Upsilon_{\mu\nu\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3},\tau_{4}).\end{split}

(43)

where the second term corresponds to a correction beyond the mean-field approximation represented by the first term. In this work, we consider mean-field correlations, so the last term is neglected, i.e.
\Upsilon=0
.

Time non-local matrices
\hat{p}(\tau),\hat{q}(\tau)
in Eq(
42
) are defined in
0\leq\tau<\beta
with
\tau>\tau^{\prime}
and
0>\tau^{\prime\prime}
. The mean-field correlation is then expressed with Green’s functions as

\displaystyle\Pi(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int d\tau^{\prime}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left[\hat{p}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\hat{G}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)\right]\int d\tau^{\prime\prime}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left[\hat{q}(-\tau^{\prime\prime})\hat{G}(\tau^{\prime\prime})\right]-\iint d\tau^{\prime}d\tau^{\prime\prime}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left[\hat{p}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\hat{G}(\tau^{\prime})\hat{q}(-\tau^{\prime\prime})\hat{G}(\tau^{\prime\prime}-\tau)\right]

(44)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{\left[\hat{p}*\hat{G}\right](\beta)\right\}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{\left[\hat{q}*\hat{G}\right](\beta)\right\}+\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{\left[\hat{p}*\hat{G}\right](\tau)\left[\hat{q}*\hat{G}\right](\beta-\tau)\right\}.

where
\left[A*B\right](\tau)
denotes convolution in imaginary time.

C.2.2
Cyclical properties of the trace and finite antisymmetric susceptibility

The cyclical properties of the trace in non-local-time correlations are crucial to understand why a finite antisymmetric response is enabled when coupling to dynamical fluctuations is included. To illustrate this point, we take the second term in Equation
44
, whose convolution is written explicitly as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{[\hat{p}*\hat{G}](\tau)[\hat{q}*\hat{G}](\beta-\tau)\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\!\left[\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau_{1}d\tau_{2}\,\hat{p}(\tau-\tau_{1})\hat{G}(\tau_{1})\hat{q}(\beta-\tau-\tau_{2})\hat{G}(\tau_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau_{1}d\tau_{2}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left[\hat{G}(\tau_{2})\hat{p}(\tau-\tau_{1})\hat{G}(\tau_{1})\hat{q}(\beta-\tau-\tau_{2})\right]\neq\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{[\hat{G}*\hat{p}](\tau)[\hat{G}*\hat{q}](\beta-\tau)\right\}.

Crucially, functions that are convolved cannot be interchanged freely. Similarly, we can apply this property to the expression of the antisymmetric susceptibility response described by Equation
3
, to the following term:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left\{\hat{A}(\tau)\left[\hat{q}*\hat{G}\right](\beta-\tau)\right\}.

In this case, there is only one convolution. Developing this expression, we find

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{tr}\left\{\hat{A}(\tau)\right.&\left.\left[\hat{q}*\hat{G}\right](\beta-\tau)\right\}\\
&=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau^{\prime}\mathrm{tr}\left[\hat{A}(\tau)\hat{q}(\beta-\tau-\tau^{\prime})\hat{G}(\tau^{\prime})\right]\\
&=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau^{\prime}\mathrm{tr}\left[\hat{G}(\tau^{\prime})\hat{A}(\tau)\hat{q}(\beta-\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right]\\
&\neq\mathrm{tr}\left\{\hat{G}(\tau)\left[\hat{A}*\hat{q}\right](\beta-\tau)\right\}.\end{split}

(45)

This expression is a generic illustration of a single imaginary-time convolution under the trace. Equation
3
of the main text involves terms such as

\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{\hat{\mathscr{F}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)[\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\tilde{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}](-\tau)\right\}

which corresponds to the expression in Equation
45
with

\hat{A}(\tau)=\hat{\mathscr{F}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
,

\hat{q}=\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}
, and

\hat{G}(\tau)=\hat{\tilde{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)
,
and other terms like

\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{\hat{\tilde{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)[\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{F}}_{\mathbf{k}}](-\tau)\right\}

which is obtained by exchanging
\hat{\mathscr{F}}_{\mathbf{k}}
and
\hat{\tilde{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}
. Equation
45
shows that the cyclicity of the trace reorders matrices inside the integrand, but crucially, it does not exchange imaginary times
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
. Therefore, the two terms related by exchanging
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathbf{k}}
and
\hat{G}_{\mathbf{k}}
are not algebraically identical, and therefore the antisymmetric combination in Equation
3
is not constrained to vanish, wich explains why the antisymmetric component of the susceptibility (and thus, the spectral curvature) can be nonzero.

C.2.3
Second order expansion in the vertex correction of the antisymmetric susceptibility

The antisymmetric part of the paramagnetic susceptibility can be written using convolution in imaginary time as

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\chi}^{as}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k},\tau)&={\chi}^{p}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k},,\tau)-{\chi}^{p}_{\beta\alpha}(\mathbf{k},,\tau)\\
&=-\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{\left[\hat{\Gamma}^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](\tau)\left[\hat{\Gamma}^{\beta}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](-\tau)\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\left.-\left[\hat{\Gamma}^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](-\tau)\left[\hat{\Gamma}^{\beta}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](\tau)\right\}.\end{split}

(46)

At a given momentum
\mathbf{k}
, the contribution to the antisymmetric susceptibility can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{\chi}^{as}(\mathbf{k},\tau)=-\,\mathrm{tr}\left\{\left[\hat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](\tau)\times\left[\hat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](-\tau)\right\}.

(47)

We expand perturbatively in the self-energy generated by the coupling to spin fluctuations. Such a perturbation enters both through the vertex correction and the Von Neumann expansion of the Green’s function
\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{n})
. To leading order, the susceptibility vector can be expanded by decomposing the full vertex into the bare current operator and the vertex correction,

\hat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{k}}=\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}+\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}.

Substituting this decomposition into the previous expression yields

\displaystyle\bm{\chi}(\mathbf{k},\tau)

\displaystyle=-\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}\times\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(-\tau)\right\}

(48)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{\left[\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](\tau)\times\left[\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](-\tau)\right\}

\displaystyle-\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\left[\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](-\tau)+\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(-\tau)\left[\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](\tau)\right\}.

The first term vanishes identically since
\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{k}}=\mathbf{0}
.
The second term also vanishes, because under the transformation
\tau\rightarrow-\tau
the cross product changes sign due to its anticommutativity, while the bosonic symmetry of the correlator requires the expression to be even in
\tau
. These two properties are incompatible, and therefore this contribution cancels.

Taking these symmetry constraints into account, the susceptibility vector reduces to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\chi}^{as}(\mathbf{k},\tau)&=-\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathrm{tr}\!\left\{\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\left[\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](-\tau)\right.\\
&\quad\left.+\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(-\tau)\left[\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](\tau)\right\}.\end{split}

(49)

Furthermore, odd orders in the perturbative expansion are traceless due to the matrix structure: the corresponding operators are Hermitian and odd under time-reversal symmetry, and matrices with both properties must have zero trace. Consequently, the leading contribution arises at second order. After defining

\hat{\mathscr{F}}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{n})=\hat{\tilde{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{n})\hat{\Sigma}^{s}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{n})\hat{\tilde{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\omega_{n}),

we arrive at

\bm{\chi}^{as,(2)}(\mathbf{k},\tau)=-\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathrm{tr}\!\Big\{\hat{\mathscr{F}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\!\left[\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\tilde{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](-\tau)+\hat{\mathscr{F}}_{\mathbf{k}}(-\tau)\!\left[\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\tilde{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](\tau)+\hat{\tilde{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(\tau)\!\left[\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{F}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](-\tau)+\hat{\tilde{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(-\tau)\!\left[\delta\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}*\hat{\mathscr{F}}_{\mathbf{k}}\right](\tau)\Big\}.

(50)

which corresponds to Equation
3
of the main text. This Equation therefore implies that (in addition to non-commutative transverse quantum fluctuations) non-local time correlations are an essential ingredient to allow non-zero antisymmetric susceptibility and explain the emergent dynamical curvature.
