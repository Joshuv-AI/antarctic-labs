ABSTRACT

 Many climate-risk tools rely on ensemble-mean projections or endpoint climate snapshots to

 characterize future hazards. Although convenient for communication, these representations

 remove the statistical, temporal, and physical information that real infrastructure systems

 respond to. Infrastructure degradation and failure arise from extremes, sequences,

 cumulative stress, compound hazards, and nonlinear fragility relationships—none of

 which survive ensemble averaging or temporal compression. Power-system failure

 statistics and cascading-failure models further show that infrastructure risk is dominated

 by tail events and path-dependent dynamics rather than by mean conditions. This

 paper demonstrates why ensemble-mean or endpoint-only climate representations are

 mathematically and physically inconsistent with engineering-grade risk analysis. We

 outline a model-resolved, time-series-based workflow that preserves extremes, variability,

 and sequencing by propagating each climate model realization independently through

 hazard formation, exposure, fragility, and cascading failure mechanisms. We then test this

 workflow using an event-based backcasting methodology that INL developed to assess

 impacts from extreme events including heat stress, wind, flooding, and extreme cold. In

 this instance, backcasted data from the 2022 Los Angeles heat dome is used to evaluate the

 ability of climate ensemble and impact ensemble methods to capture climatological hazards

 based on real world events. Taking the ensemble of impacts—rather than the ensemble of

 climate—provides a defensible, physically coherent foundation for infrastructure resilience

 planning, regulatory compliance, and long-term investment decisions.

Executive Summary
 Many climate-risk assessment tools simplify future climate information into ensemble-mean projections
and endpoint snapshots. While these choices aid communication, they remove the temporal and statistical
structure that real infrastructure systems respond to. Critical assets experience degradation and failure
through extremes, sequences, persistent stress periods, nonlinear thermal and mechanical responses,
and compound hazards—not long-term averages. Recent research demonstrates that relying on annual-
mean climate indicators systematically underestimates risk by excluding variability, extremes, and path-
dependent mechanisms. Additional studies across climate science, impact modeling, and transformer-
aging research likewise emphasize that physical systems respond to full time-series signals rather than
averages or endpoints. This report shows why engineering-grade infrastructure risk analysis requires
model-resolved, time-dependent climate data and why ensemble-mean endpoint approaches cannot
support credible resilience decisions.

 Infrastructure risk lives in the tails, sequences, and compound stressors that ensemble
 means erase, requiring computation of the ensemble of impacts rather than the ensemble of
 climate.

1 Introduction
 Infrastructure systems—electric grids, transportation networks, pipelines, and other lifeline
systems—operate in environments characterized by continuous, evolving, and often compounding stresses.
Their degradation and failure mechanisms are inherently time dependent and nonlinear. Heatwaves,
prolonged humidity, back-to-back stress cycles, and sequences of extreme conditions all influence materials,
components, and system stability. Empirical work in global-impact modeling shows that climate-impact
estimates based solely on annual mean temperature significantly underestimate losses by ignoring
precipitation variability, temperature variability, and extreme-event contributions [1]. Additionally,
ensemble climate-impact modeling demonstrates that extreme impacts frequently arise from combinations
of moderate meteorological conditions rather than singular meteorological extremes, underscoring the
need to analyze full distributions of weather realizations rather than endpoint snapshots [2]. Together, this
evidence reinforces that infrastructure resilience assessment must be grounded in time-resolved, physically
coherent climate data rather than ensemble-mean or endpoint representations.

 Definition: Climate Model Ensemble
 A climate-model ensemble is a collection of independent climate model realizations—such as the
 full NA-CORDEX Global Climate Model (GCM)–Regional Climate Models (RCM) matrix—each
 representing an equally plausible trajectory of future climate. Ensemble members differ due to
 structural model differences, internal variability, and initialization conditions. In climate science,
 ensemble statistics (means, medians, percentiles) are often used to summarize projected climate
 trends. For engineering risk analysis, however, ensemble statistics remove the extremes, variance,
 and temporal sequencing that drive degradation, fragility, and failure. In this work, each ensemble
 member is treated as a physically coherent trajectory and each one is propagated independently
 through the hazard-to-impact workflow. An ensemble of impacts is taken rather than an ensemble of
 climate.

1.1 Relationship to NOAA and IPCC Ensemble Practices
 It is important to note that this definition aligns fully with National Oceanic and Atmospheric
Administration (NOAA) and Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) usage of the term
ensemble. National climate assessments, including NOAA’s Fifth National Climate Assessment (NCA5),

 1

rely on multi-model ensembles from Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP)-class global climate
models and their regional downscaling products to characterize future climate conditions [3]. In these
assessments, ensemble statistics such as means, medians, and percentile ranges are used appropriately for
the purpose of communicating large-scale climate trends and uncertainty. These summaries, however, are
not intended to represent physically coherent trajectories suitable for engineering-risk analysis. For
applications requiring the preservation of extremes, temporal sequencing, and compound stressors
(e.g. infrastructure degradation, thermal aging, and cascading failure propagation), ensemble statistics
remove the very features that drive system fragility. The workflow therefore uses each model realization
independently and aggregates impacts only after full hazard-to-damage propagation, maintaining
consistency with climate-science ensemble structure while addressing the distinct requirements of
engineering systems.

2 Problems with Endpoint-Only Climate Snapshots
2.1 Degradation Over Time
 Material aging processes in infrastructure components accumulate over time as a function of the entire
sequence of environmental conditions. Continuous thermal exposure, humidity cycles, and temperature-
driven chemical changes cause degradation even under moderate conditions—effects that cannot be
inferred from isolated endpoint values. Laboratory studies of insulating liquids show that transformer
oil deteriorates progressively as a function of both temperature and exposure duration, with breakdown
voltage, acidity, water content, and dielectric dissipation factor all changing nonlinearly across thermal
histories [4]. These results illustrate that degradation arises from sustained, cumulative stress, requiring
continuous time-series data. Numerous studies demonstrate that GCM monthly or annual mean outputs
are structurally incapable of representing the variability and extremes required by process-based impact
models. Semonov and Stratonovitch show that Fourth Assessment Report (AR4) GCM outputs are typically
available only as monthly means, cannot produce daily temperature or precipitation statistics, and lack
the extremes needed for local-scale or nonlinear systems. Their evaluation highlights that GCM daily
fields, when available, are too coarse and uncertain for hazard-relevant impact modeling, confirming that
endpoint climate summaries remove precisely the information needed for engineering-grade assessments
[5].

2.2 Extremes and Tail Events
 Infrastructure components often fail during the rarest and most intense conditions. Global economic
modeling shows that extremes and temperature variability increase climate damages beyond what
annual-mean indicators imply, contributing several percentage points of additional loss at +3◦ C warming
[1]. Power-system failure statistics exhibit a similar structure: large blackouts follow heavy-tailed,
approximately power-law distributions. The statistics demonstrate that rare, extreme events dominate total
risk [6]. This well-established feature of cascading failures confirms that infrastructure systems respond
primarily to tail behavior rather than to central tendencies. Time-averaged endpoints cannot describe the
magnitude, persistence, or clustering of extremes that drive engineering failures.

2.3 Path Dependence and Temporal Sequences
 Sequence matters. The order, timing, and persistence of meteorological conditions can amplify or
mitigate impacts. Ensemble climate-impact modeling shows that 85% of extreme impact events in a 2000-
year ensemble resulted from non-extreme meteorological seasons rather than arising from specific temporal
sequences of otherwise moderate conditions [2]. Such relationships cannot be seen in endpoint-only climate
snapshots.

2.4 Internal Variability
 Internal climate variability contributes significantly to year-to-year and decade-scale changes in
temperature and precipitation. Large-ensemble regional climate modeling shows that internal variability
and structural model uncertainty interact to produce wide spreads in regional climate signals [7]. Endpoint
snapshots suppress this variability, removing operationally relevant information.

2.5 Compound Hazards
 Compound events arise when multiple stressors co-occur. Thermal aging of oils depends jointly on
temperature and moisture, both of which evolve continuously [4]. This aligns with cascading-failure
observations in transmission systems, where interacting stresses accumulate and propagate nonlinearly.
Dobson et al. demonstrate that blackout cascades are not the result of isolated extremes but emerge
from combinations of load, stress, and local failures that interact across the network [6]. Such compound
mechanisms cannot be reproduced by endpoint or mean-based climate representations. Climate-impact
ensembles show that moderate conditions, when combined, produce severe impacts [2]. Capturing these
interactions requires synchronized time-series inputs across variables.

2.6 Capital Planning and Replacement Timing
 Infrastructure owners require an understanding of when temperature thresholds are crossed, how often
exceedances occur, and how trends accelerate. Climate-damage studies show that incorporating variability
and extremes shifts both the magnitude and timing of damages relative to mean-only projections [1]. Only
time-resolved trajectories enable realistic planning for asset replacement or refurbishment.

2.7 Regulatory and Compliance Analysis
 Regulatory frameworks often require validation under full distributions of stressors, including tail
events, persistent heat periods, or specific exceedance frequencies. Endpoint values cannot meet these
requirements.

2.8 Design Standards and Load Calculations
 Design standards rely on extreme values—e.g., 1-in-50-year heat, wind, or load levels. Dynamic
transformer models confirm that peak hot-spot temperatures, not means, govern insulation aging and
overload capability [8, 9]. Likewise, climate-impact studies show that focusing on long-term averages
hides the extremes relevant for design [2].

3 Limitations of Ensemble Means
 Ensemble-mean climate trajectories smooth away the spatial, temporal, and statistical information
that assets respond to. This is directly analogous to cascading failures in electric grids, where nonlinear
propagation mechanisms cause risk to scale disproportionately with system stress. Dobson et al. show that
blackout sizes arise from nonlinear, load-dependent chain reactions and follow power-law size distributions,
indicating that the largest events emerge from the most extreme—and least averaged—conditions [6].
Averaging away such conditions necessarily erases the very inputs that drive large failures.

 Jensen’s Inequality and Infrastructure Risk
 Nonlinear fragility relationships ensure that:

 whenever f is nonlinear. Damage estimated from ensemble-mean climate conditions systematically
 underestimates true risk relative to model-by-model computations [1, 2]. This mathematical fact is
 not a modeling choice—it is a property of the physics. Any tool that averages climate inputs before
 applying fragility functions produces biased results.

 Studies of incomplete climate model ensembles show that averaging across models obscures structural
differences and reduces variance in ways that bias downstream impact estimates [10]. Regional Analysis of
Variance (ANOVA) decomposition likewise finds that ensemble means fail to represent dominant sources
of uncertainty—especially the overarching influence of driving GCMs on projected changes [7]. For these
reasons, ensemble-mean endpoints are mathematically inconsistent and empirically unsupported as inputs
to engineering risk models. Figure 1 shows this dramatically, where the ensemble mean of air temperature
for Los Angeles rarely exceeds 80◦ F.

 Semenov and Stratonovitch arrive at the same conclusion from an impacts perspective; they treat each
GCM in the AR4 ensemble as an equally plausible trajectory and explicitly reject the use of ensemble-mean
climate fields. Their methodology computes impacts separately for each model and only aggregates impacts,
not climate, noting that combining climate fields before impact propagation erases extremes, smooths
distributions, and produces misleading assessments of uncertainty [5].

4 Applications That Require Full Time Series
 Infrastructure systems operate in a world shaped by continuous, time-dependent environmental
stress. They respond not to abstract averages or distant endpoint snapshots, but to the full sequence of
temperatures, humidity levels, wind events, and compounding hazards that unfold hour by hour and year
by year. Figure 2 illustrates a representative two-week heat-stress sequence, showing how multiple climate
variables evolve jointly to create compound stress conditions that cannot be captured by endpoint snapshots
or ensemble means. A substantial body of work in power-system reliability shows that transmission-grid
failures are governed by nonlinear propagation mechanisms and heavy-tailed statistics. Dobson et al.
provide a foundational analysis, demonstrating that large blackouts arise from cascading sequences of
dependent failures and that blackout size distributions follow approximate power-law behavior across

multiple countries and grid topologies [6]. These results establish that risk in electric-power systems is
dominated by tail events and path-dependent dynamics rather than by characteristics of the mean state,
reinforcing the need for time-resolved, variance-preserving hazard inputs.

 For this reason, engineering-grade climate risk analysis requires high-temporal-resolution data from
each climate model; preserving the extremes, variability, persistence, and timing that drive real failures.
Any approach that reduces future climate to ensembles or isolated events inevitably strips away these
dynamics and cannot capture the conditions under which infrastructure degrades, approaches its limits,
or fails. The applications discussed in this section illustrate why full time series are essential for realistic
assessment, planning, and investment.
 Furthermore, quantitative uncertainty analysis shows that climate projections include significant
internal variability and model-structural divergence that manifest at sub-annual time scales [7, 10]. Asset
performance models require full trajectory inputs to accurately propagate these variations into exposure,
fragility, and damage outcomes.

4.1 Asset-Level Degradation Modeling
 The degradation of transformers, conductors, pavements, pipelines, and other components depends on
their exposure to time-varying environmental stress. Detailed thermal modeling of power transformers
demonstrates that winding and oil temperatures evolve dynamically with load and ambient conditions,
exhibiting transient overshoot, nonlinear resistance effects, and viscosity-dependent time constants [8, 9].
Transformer aging and insulation loss-of-life depend on the integral of instantaneous hot-spot temperature
over time and not on long-term averages. Experiments with oil samples further show nonlinear degradation
driven by hours-to-days temperature history [4]. These results confirm that component degradation cannot
be meaningfully modeled without time-resolved climate sequences.

 5

4.2 Temporal Alignment and Sequence-Dependent Impacts
 Process-level impacts respond to the alignment of hazard trajectories with the internal state of the
system, rather than responding to long-term averages or endpoint climate fields. The risk emerges from
sequences. A striking demonstration is provided by Semenov and Stratonovitch, who examined the heat
stress in wheat as a function of the day-by-day seasonal temperature curve and the timing of anthesis. As
shown in Figure 3, shifting anthesis by approximately ten days alters the effective warming experienced
by the crop; the temperature offset at a late anthesis date (∆1 ) is substantially larger than the offset at an
early anthesis date (∆2 ). Because maximum temperature follows a seasonal trajectory, even modest shifts
in timing can fully compensate for, or strongly amplify, projected warming.

Figure 3: From Semonov & Stratonovitch [5, p. 9]. “Maximum temperature for the baseline (solid curve line) and
future (dashed line) scenarios (a hypothetical case). The offset of temperature ∆1 as a result of an annual cycle for
a late anthesis date (e.g. 171) is smaller than the offset of temperature ∆2 for an early anthesis date (e.g. 141). An
increase in temperature in the future could be fully compensated by the advance in anthesis date for an early but not
late anthesis date.”

 This example illustrates a general principle. When the system has internal dynamics (i.e. thermal inertia
in transformers, multi-day stress accumulation in conductors, humidity-moderated nighttime cooling, or
operational loading cycles on feeders) the impact is determined by when the stressor occurs relative to
the system’s state. Temporal ordering matters. A multi-day sequence of high-humidity nights during a
heat event produces transformer thermal runaway risk that cannot be inferred from monthly means. The
onset of a coastal heat dome relative to the diurnal wind reversal determines conductor capacity. Likewise,
cascade propagation depends on the hour-scale evolution of voltages, flows, and protection actions—none
of which can be reconstructed from climatological indicators.

4.3 Extreme Event Stress Testing
 Short-duration extremes often drive failure. Dynamic thermal tests show that rapid load increases
create hot-spot temperature overshoots far beyond steady-state predictions, underscoring that transient
peaks govern mechanical and thermal failure modes [8]. Climate models’ full hourly or sub-hourly
trajectories are required to test upper-bound performance.

4.4 Cascading Failure and Grid Stability
 Grid instability and cascading failures evolve over hours to days. Cascading failure theory shows that
each failure increases system stress and raises the probability of subsequent failures, producing escalating,
path-dependent sequences of outages [6]. These dynamics exhibit critical-point behavior in which small
perturbations can trigger large outcomes, underscoring the necessity of preserving full, time-ordered input
trajectories when assessing climate-driven grid risk. Thermal inertia causes transformer temperatures to
lag behind ambient conditions, meaning that sequences of warm nights and sustained heatwaves impose
cumulative burdens [9]. Ensemble-average or endpoint temperatures cannot reconstruct these sequences.

4.4.1 Cascading Failure and Heavy-Tailed Infrastructure Risk
 Electric power systems exhibit strongly nonlinear responses to environmental and operational stress.
When individual components fail, flows redistribute according to network topology and protection
behavior, often increasing loading on distant elements and triggering further outages. This load-dependent
propagation creates cascades in which severity is sensitive to the temporal ordering and magnitude of
stressors.
 Statistical analyses of historical outage data show that blackout sizes follow heavy-tailed, approximately
power-law distributions across multiple national grids, indicating that rare, extreme events contribute
disproportionately to the total system risk [6]. Such heavy-tailed behavior arises because each failure
weakens system margins and increases the probability of subsequent failures, which is precisely the kind
of nonlinear interaction that is erased by ensemble-mean climate representations.
 Because cascading failures emerge from sequences of stress accumulation rather than from single
isolated extremes, engineering-relevant modeling must retain the full time-series of hazard inputs.
Endpoint statistics or averaged climate fields cannot reproduce the load-dependent amplification
mechanisms that generate large blackouts. Preserving model-resolved trajectories is therefore essential for
correctly propagating climate stress into infrastructure fragility and cascade behavior.

4.5 Compound Hazard Modeling
 Compound hazards arise when multiple stressors occur simultaneously. Thermal aging of oils depends
jointly on temperature and moisture, both of which evolve continuously [4]. This aligns with cascading-
failure observations in transmission systems, in which interacting stresses accumulate and propagate
nonlinearly. Dobson et al. demonstrate that blackout cascades are not the result of isolated extremes
but emerge from combinations of load, stress, and local failures that interact across the network [6].
Such compound mechanisms cannot be reproduced by endpoint or mean-based climate representations.
Climate-impact ensembles show that moderate conditions, when combined, produce severe impacts [2].
Capturing these interactions requires synchronized time-series inputs across variables.

4.6 Capital Planning and Replacement Timing
 Infrastructure owners require an understanding of when temperature thresholds are crossed, how often
exceedances occur, and how trends accelerate. Climate-damage studies show that incorporating variability
and extremes shift both the magnitude and timing of damages relative to mean-only projections [1]. Only
time-resolved trajectories enable realistic planning for asset replacement or refurbishment.

4.7 Regulatory and Compliance Analysis
 Regulatory frameworks often require validation under full distributions of stressors, including tail
events, persistent heat periods, or specific exceedance frequencies. Endpoint values cannot meet these
requirements.

4.8 Design Standards and Load Calculations
 Design standards rely on extreme values (e.g., 1-in-50-year heat, wind, or load levels). Dynamic
transformer models confirm that peak hot-spot temperatures, not means, govern insulation aging and
overload capability [8, 9]. Likewise, climate-impact studies show that focusing on long-term averages
hides the extremes relevant for design [2].

5 A Correct Workflow for Infrastructure Risk Modeling
 Infrastructure risk modeling is only valid when the climate data driving it preserves the physical and
statistical characteristics that assets respond to. Avoiding ensemble-mean climates and endpoint-only
snapshots requires processing each climate model realization separately through the hazard and damage
pipeline.

5.1 Preserve Full Time-Series Data
 Each climate model contains a physically consistent trajectory of temperatures, humidity, wind, and
precipitation. Engineering models must receive these trajectories directly. Transformer thermal models, for
example, require continuous ambient and load inputs because the hot-spot temperature at each moment
depends on prior conditions through thermal inertia [8, 9]. Likewise, empirical degradation of oils and
other materials is tied to cumulative time exposure [4].

5.2 Model-by-Model Hazard to Damage Propagation
 Each climate model realization should be propagated through hazard formation, exposure, fragility, and
damage without averaging at intermediate steps. Studies of incomplete GCM–RCM matrices show that
structural differences between models drive significant uncertainty, and combining models prematurely
obscures this variance [10]. Fully populated ANOVA matrices demonstrate that GCM identity is a dominant
driver of climate-change signal, reinforcing the need to treat each model independently [7].

5.3 Ensemble of Impacts, Not Ensemble of Climate
 After each model’s full trajectory has been propagated through the engineering workflow, the resulting
impacts can be aggregated to create distributions of damages—not distributions of climate inputs.
Ensemble climate-impact modeling shows that aggregating impacts preserves nonlinear relationships
and reveals extreme outcomes that regression or mean-based approaches miss [2]. This approach avoids
mathematical errors associated with applying fragility models to ensemble-mean climates.

5.4 Why This Matters
 Nonlinear fragility curves, temporal dependence, and compound hazards ensure that true risk resides
in the combined distribution of high-frequency and low-frequency variations. Economic-damage modeling
shows that variability and extremes add materially to losses beyond mean-temperature projections [1].
Ensemble-impact modeling reveals that rare combinations of moderate conditions produce severe impacts
[2]. These findings demonstrate that only model-resolved impact processing yields defensible infrastructure
risk assessments. Infrastructure-level evidence reinforces this point. Observed blackout statistics exhibit

 8

heavy-tailed, power-law size distributions; models of cascading failure reproduce this behavior only when
full nonlinear and temporal dynamics are preserved [6]. This confirms that the dominant risk in power
systems arises from extreme trajectories and their propagation—not from mean conditions or smoothed
representations.

5.5 Capabilities Enabled by This Workflow
 A model-by-model time-series workflow supports detailed degradation modeling, high-fidelity failure
probability estimation, realistic cascade simulation, lifecycle cost analysis, and prioritization of asset
hardening. It connects climate science to engineering practice in a defensible and reproducible manner.

6 Case Study: Ensemble-Resolved Heat-Stress Analysis Framework for the
  2022 Los Angeles Heat Dome
 This section defines the analytical framework and event context used to examine ensemble-resolved
heat-stress impacts in the Los Angeles Basin, shown in Figure 4. The objective is to establish the climate-
model ensemble, spatial configuration, event definition, and impact-mapping methodology used in the
subsequent analysis. This case study is methodological rather than reconstructive. It does not attempt to
reproduce observed meteorological conditions during the 2022 Los Angeles heat dome; instead it evaluates
how ensemble climate model realizations representing heat-dome-like conditions propagate through a
time-resolved impact modeling pipeline. While this case study evaluates heat stress, we believe that it can
be replicated for a wide range of climatological hazards such as flooding, extreme cold, and high winds.
Methods could also expand to areas including drought and wildfire risks.

6.1 Climate Ensemble Context and Event Definition
 The analysis employs a 16-member NA-CORDEX ensemble under the Representative Concentration
Pathway (RCP) 8.5 scenario, with each ensemble member treated as an equally plausible and internally
consistent climate trajectory. As shown in Figure 4, climate model output is evaluated at Grid 7, a
representative land-based grid cell in the Los Angeles Basin (33.75–34.00◦ N, 118.25–118.00◦ W), selected to
ensure the availability of land-surface diagnostics required for heat-stress modeling.
 To focus on impact-relevant conditions and to support interpretable diagnostics, the analysis is
temporally subset to a nine-day window corresponding to peak heat-dome conditions in late summer
2022 (August 31 to September 9). This event window is used consistently for exploratory data analysis,
composite evaluation, and probabilistic impact mapping in the results that follow.
 While the 2022 Los Angeles heat dome is used as a reference trajectory, the ensemble members do
not reproduce the exact magnitude or timing of the observed event. Regional climate models often
smooth short-lived synoptic extremes and may underrepresent peak daily intensities. However, the
purpose of this case study is not deterministic event replication. Rather, observations are incorporated as a
conditional realization within the ensemble framework, enabling comparison in impact space. This framing
allows evaluation of how physically coherent model trajectories—whether under- or over-estimating peak
magnitudes—propagate through nonlinear hazard and fragility mappings.

 Within the event window, ensemble members exhibit elevated but moderate absolute temperatures
relative to the observed trajectory. TASMIN and TASMAX, HI, and WBGT values in the ensemble median
remain below conventional severe thresholds, and hot-night or inadequate-cooldown criteria are generally
not exceeded at this grid cell.

would not be classified as extreme. This provides a useful test case for examining whether impact-relevant

6.3 Composite Heat-Stress Indices and Relative Extremeness
 Despite moderate absolute values, ensemble members exhibit periods of elevated relative stress when
evaluated against their own climatologies. Derived heat-stress metrics are standardized on a per-member
basis and combined into composite indices representing distinct damage pathways, including cooling
failure, cumulative thermal damage, human heat stress, grid operational stress, and a unified physics-based
index.
 This relative normalization preserves temporal structure, persistence, and compound stress effects
while avoiding reliance on absolute thresholds that may be poorly represented in climate models. The
resulting composites provide the basis for probabilistic impact mapping described in the next section.

6.4 Probabilistic Impact Mapping Framework
 Composite heat-stress indices are mapped to impact probabilities using nonlinear logistic damage
functions. Ensemble statistics are computed after the impact transformation, yielding an ensemble of
impacts rather than an ensemble of climate variables. This framework enables the identification of
impact-relevant risk signals arising from time-dependent stress patterns and ensemble variability.
 The results of this impact mapping, including ensemble-resolved probability trajectories and
comparative risk across damage pathways, are presented and interpreted in the following section.

7.4 Interpretation
 These event-conditioned results illustrate the methodological advantage of the ensemble-of-impacts
framework. Although climate model realizations underrepresent the observed magnitude of the 2022 Los
Angeles heat dome, the impact pipeline identifies elevated operational vulnerability through persistence,
compound stressors, and nonlinear response. The ordering of pathway risk (i.e. grid operational stress
exceeding human heat stress at this location) emerges only after time-resolved composite construction and
probabilistic mapping.
 Accordingly, the probabilities reported here should be interpreted as conservative, model-conditioned
estimates rather than reconstructions of observed impacts. The results demonstrate that engineering-
relevant risk cannot be inferred from ensemble-mean temperature trends or endpoint thresholds alone, but
must be computed from model-resolved trajectories and aggregated in impact space.

8 Recommendations for Agencies and Tool Developers
 Climate data intended for infrastructure resilience analysis must preserve the physical and statistical
properties that determine asset degradation, fragility, and cascading behavior. Simplified ensemble-mean
fields and endpoint summaries are insufficient for engineering-grade assessment. Agencies and tool
developers should adopt the following practices:

8.1 Provide Full Time-Series Outputs
 Deliver daily or sub-daily climate variables rather than only endpoint or long-term mean values.
Thermal aging, material fatigue, and operational stress models depend on cumulative exposure, diurnal
cycling, and transient peaks. Transformer insulation loss-of-life, for example, is governed by the integral of
hot-spot temperature over time rather than annual averages [4, 8]. Time-resolved trajectories are therefore
necessary to evaluate degradation pathways and overload conditions.

8.2 Retain Individual Model Realizations
 Provide access to individual ensemble member trajectories rather than only ensemble means or medians.
Structural differences among GCMs and their regional downscalings dominate uncertainty in projected
change [7]. Premature aggregation suppresses variance and eliminates physically coherent sequences that
may drive nonlinear failure modes. Impact modeling should propagate each realization independently
and aggregate outcomes only in impact space.

8.3 Preserve Variance and Extremes During Bias Correction
 Bias correction and statistical downscaling methods must preserve variance, persistence structure,
and tail behavior. Smoothing extremes or constraining variance can materially alter downstream damage
estimates [1]. Corrections that improve mean climatology but truncate peak intensities or multi-day
persistence may inadvertently reduce estimated infrastructure risk.

8.4 Support Hazard-Ready Variables
 Provide variables required for direct hazard construction rather than only canonical meteorological
outputs. Infrastructure-relevant stress frequently depends on interacting parameters such as humidity,
wind speed, radiation, multi-day heat persistence, and nighttime recovery conditions. Thermal loading and
insulation aging are driven by compound and duration-dependent effects [4, 9]. Datasets should therefore
support computation of derived indices (e.g., wet-bulb metrics, cumulative degree-hours, persistence
counters) without requiring reconstruction from incomplete fields.

8.5 Enable Asset-Level and Geospatial Integration
 Facilitate direct integration with asset inventories, geospatial infrastructure layers, and fragility
databases. High-resolution regional structure matters because model configuration and driving GCM
identity introduce geographically varying uncertainty patterns [7]. Climate datasets should be structured
to allow spatially explicit hazard-to-asset propagation, not merely grid-cell summary statistics.

8.6 Report Impact-Space Diagnostics Alongside Climate Metrics
 In addition to reporting temperature or precipitation projections, agencies should provide example
hazard-to-impact transformations illustrating how ensemble variability propagates through nonlinear
fragility relationships. Presenting impact distributions alongside climate distributions clarifies the
distinction between ensemble climate spread and ensemble damage spread, reinforcing that engineering
risk is determined by nonlinear mapping rather than by central climate tendencies.

9 Conclusion
 Ensemble-mean climate representations and endpoint snapshots provide an incomplete and often
misleading view of future climate risk. They remove the extremes, variability, timing, and compound
interactions that drive real-world infrastructure degradation and failure. Evidence from climate-impact
modeling, regional climate variability studies, transformer aging and thermal-inertia research, and power-
system cascading-failure analysis all converges on the same conclusion: engineering-relevant climate
risk assessment must retain full time-series information and treat each model realization as a physically
coherent trajectory.
 By propagating each climate model independently through hazard formation, exposure, fragility, and
cascade dynamics, the ensemble-of-impacts workflow captures the nonlinear relationships and tail-driven
behaviors that dominate system risk. This approach avoids the mathematical inconsistencies of applying
fragility models to ensemble-mean climate fields and preserves the heavy-tailed structure observed in
both climate impacts and infrastructure failures. It also provides a transparent and reproducible basis
for evaluating resilience investments, regulatory compliance pathways, design standards, and long-term
infrastructure planning.
 A shift to model-resolved, time-series climate data is therefore not optional—it is required for credible,
defensible estimates of infrastructure risk under extreme weather. As climate extremes intensify and
systems operate closer to their physical limits, the importance of preserving the full statistical and temporal
structure of climate forcing will only grow. Taking the ensemble of impacts, rather than the ensemble
of climate, offers a scientifically grounded foundation for the next generation of infrastructure-climate
assessment tools.
