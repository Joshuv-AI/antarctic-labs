Quantum simulation of lattice gauge theories coupled to fermionic matter via anyonic regularization
Mason L. Rhodes, Shivesh Pathak, Riley W. Chien

Abstract
The optimal regularization of infinite-dimensional degrees of freedom is a central open problem in the tractable simulation of lattice gauge theories on quantum computers. Here, we consider regularizing the gauge field by replacing the gauge group G G with a braided fusion category whose objects correspond to Wilson lines of the associated Chern-Simons theory G k G_{k} , with the level k k serving as the regularization parameter. We demonstrate how to couple these regularized U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) and S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) gauge groups to fermionic matter using the framework of fusion surface models, which treats matter and gauge field excitations as interacting anyons. We then address the simulation of the Hamiltonians we construct on fault-tolerant quantum computers, providing explicit quantum circuit constructions for implementing the primitive gates in this model, namely, the F F and R R symbols of the U ​ ( 1 ) k U(1)_{k} and S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) k SU(2)_{k} anyon theories, which may be of independent interest.

One of the main difficulties in simulating LGTs, which remains an open problem to date, is that of the regularization of the Hilbert space of the gauge fields. The gauge groups relevant to Yang-Mills theory are Lie groups, meaning any computational encoding of these groups must be made finite in order to even store the system in the memory of a standard quantum computer. A variety of different techniques have been proposed for performing this regularization, in which there are two goals. II.1
The Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian

In a seminal 1975 paper, Kogut and Susskind developed the now-standard Hamiltonian formulation of lattice gauge theories (LGTs)
59
. The Hamiltonian is defined on a
d
-dimensional cubic spatial lattice consisting of
L^{d}
vertices labeled by
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Fermionic matter is hosted on the vertices and the gauge field degrees of freedom are hosted on edges.

The Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian can be explicitly written as

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=g_{M}H_{M}+g_{K}H_{K}+H_{YM}\,,

(1)

where the coupling coefficients
g_{M}=m
and
g_{K}=1/2a
are defined in terms of the fermion mass
m
and the lattice spacing
a
. The mass term
H_{M}
and kinetic term
H_{K}
act on the fermionic fields in the LGT and the Yang-Mills term
H_{YM}
acts purely on the gauge fields, with each defined by

\displaystyle H_{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{v}}(-1)^{\bm{v}}a_{\bm{v}}^{\dagger}a_{\bm{v}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\,,

(2)

\displaystyle H_{K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{e}}a_{\partial\bm{e}_{1}}^{{\dagger}}W_{1}(\bm{e})a_{\partial\bm{e}_{0}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+\text{H.c.}\,,

(3)

\displaystyle H_{YM}

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}}{2}\sum_{\bm{e}}E_{\bm{e}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2a^{2}g^{2}}\sum_{\bm{p}}\Tr W_{1}(\partial\bm{p})+W_{1}(\partial\bm{p})^{{\dagger}}\,.

(4)

where
a_{\bm{v}}
are the fermionic annihilation operators acting on each vertex
\bm{v}
.
In the mass term,
H_{M}
, the
(-1)^{\bm{v}}
coefficient staggers fermionic matter and antimatter on the lattice, a result of splitting the Dirac spinors into their upper and lower components, which is necessary to mitigate fermion doubling. The operator
W_{1}(\bm{e})
is the elementary Wilson link in the fundamental representation and the Wilson loop
\Tr W_{1}(\gamma)=\prod_{\bm{e}\in\gamma}W_{1}(\bm{e})
is the product of Wilson links along an ordered path of edges
\gamma
around the plaquette
\bm{p}
.
E_{\bm{e}}^{2}
is the quadratic Casimir operator for the associated gauge group, which is diagonal in the irrep basis. The term
H_{K}
contains the fermionic hopping terms which must be dressed by a Wilson link for gauge invariance. This also couples the matter and the gauge field with coupling strength
g_{K}
.

The associated Hilbert space is spanned by all possible configurations of fermionic and gauge fields. However, we are only interested in the physical subspace which corresponds to field configurations satisfying Gauss’s law at each vertex in the lattice. In particular, Gauss’s law annihilates nonphysical states according to

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{e}\in\delta\bm{v}}G(\bm{e})|\psi\rangle=0

(5)

where
G(\bm{e})
is the Gauss operator. All of the physical operators must also commute with the Gauss’s law operator, which ensures that states remain in the physical subspace. Even then, the physical subspace is infinite-dimensional and the theory must be regularized. We present our regularization scheme in Sec.
III
.

II.2

Here we provide a brief introduction to fusion categories and anyon models. In this work, we consider only braided fusion 1-categories, so we restrict our attention to these relevant properties. For additional details see Ref.

A braided fusion category

2

\displaystyle a\times b=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}N_{ab}^{c}c\,.

II.3
Deformed Yang-Mills Gauge Theory

To simulate the Hamiltonian lattice gauge theory (or any system) on a quantum computer, we require a finite-dimensional Hilbert space. When the gauge group of the lattice gauge theory is a Lie group, the relevant case to particle physics, this will require regularizing the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space to a finite-dimensional space.

In the
q
-deformed regularization of the pure (
i.e.
, with no matter) lattice gauge theory considered in Refs.
89
;
48
;
49
, we replace the representations of the Lie group with the representations of the quantum group obtained from a
q
-deformation of the universal enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra at a root of unity determined by an integer
k
which serves as our control parameter. In this case, the representation theory truncates to a finite number of irreps. When
k\to\infty
the original representation theory is recovered. Another route to the same destination is to replace the irrep labels on the edges of the group with labels on the objects in the anyon model of the Chern-Simons theory with gauge group
G
at level
k
(see the left side of Fig.
1
). This route also allows us to demonstrate our warm up, the case of
U(1)_{k}
for which there is no corresponding quantum group, but we can use the
U(1)_{k}
anyon model as the basis for the construction.

The Hamiltonian still has the same form consisting of an electric field strength acting on the edges and a magnetic term on plaquettes. The electric field term is the quadratic Casimir operator of the gauge group

\displaystyle E_{\bm{e}}^{2}\ket{j}_{\bm{e}}=C(j)\ket{j}_{\bm{e}}.

(14)

For
SU(2)_{k}
, some authors have taken this to be the
q
-deformed quadratic Casimir operator
E_{\bm{e}}^{2}\ket{j}_{\bm{e}}=[C(j)]_{q}\ket{j}_{\bm{e}}
. The magnetic term also has the same form, although now it can be given the interpretation of fusing a loop of the anyon corresponding to the Wilson line in the fundamental representation into the edges around the plaquette. The matrix elements of the plaquette operator are computed from the
F
symbols of the anyon model in a simplification of the expression in Eq. (
25
). Such operators first appeared in the Levin-Wen string-net models
64
. The difference here is that they are not being used to enforce a zero-flux constraint but rather to assign an energy to a flux. These plaquette operators do not commute with the electric field operators and their interplay generates the gauge field dynamics.

This formulation can in fact be thought of as a perturbation of a Levin-Wen type model by the electric field term. For any gauge group, we actually have a sequence of perturbed Levin-Wen models indexed by the regularization parameter
k
, with the undeformed lattice gauge theory Hamiltonian being recovered as
k\to\infty
.

This regularization has a number of features that make it attractive for potential use in lattice gauge theory simulations, particularly on a quantum computer. First, the regularization comes with an integer control parameter
k
that precisely controls how large we take our local Hilbert spaces to be. This should be contrasted with the approximations of non-abelian Lie groups by discrete subgroups
61
;
4
;
52
;
53
;
5
where there is a largest discrete subgroup beyond which one is not able to improve the approximation. Second, we retain a notion of gauge invariance. At each vertex, we constrain the edge labels to obey the fusion rules of the category, implementing a version of Gauss’s law. Third, as we will show later, compiling the quantum circuits necessary to implement the algorithmic primitives of modern quantum simulation algorithms is quite natural due to the fact that the electric field strength term is diagonal in the electric basis (as with other regularizations) and the magnetic term is expressed explicitly in terms of the
F
(and later
R
) symbols which are tensors that can be interpreted in terms of unitary matrices.

Currently, the deformed lattice gauge theory is only formulated for the case of pure gauge theory, with a proposal for coupling to hard-core bosonic matter given in Ref.
89
. In order to be potentially useful to particle physics, a formulation which couples the gauge fields to fermionic matter is necessary. Here, we present such a framework using
fusion surface models
and provide explicit quantum circuit primitive constructions for realizing quantum simulation of anyonic-regularized LGTs coupled to fermionic matter.

III
Coupling Anyonic-Regularized Gauge Theories to Fermionic Matter

Coupling the anyonic-regularized gauge theory to electrically charged fermionic matter will require us to expand our Hilbert space to include states with charged particles. From the point of view of the Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian, it would be natural to place fermionic modes at the vertices of the lattice and to enforce Gauss’s law saying that the net electric flux out of a vertex should be equal to the charge at the vertex. One issue with this is that it would complicate the implementation of the plaquette operator which is defined in terms of
F
and
R
symbols. Since these symbols are only defined when the vertices obey the fusion rules, allowing for violations of the fusion rules would lead to an ambiguity as to how to proceed with defining the plaquette operator.

After regularizing the gauge theory, the Wilson lines become the worldlines of anyons of a corresponding Chern-Simons theory. The Hilbert space of our gauge-matter system will therefore have the interpretation as the state space of a system of interacting anyons. Models with precisely this interpretation are known in the literature as
anyon chains
in 1d
35
and as
fusion surface models
in 2d
51
and we will utilize the technology developed in these papers to construct the state space of our system and some of the operators. Note that other recent works have considered interpreting the anyonic content of a topological phase as a kind of charged matter in a gauge theory in a different context
92
;
91
;
90
.

As in the string-net models and deformed gauge theory without matter discussed earlier, our state space will consist of labelings of the edges of a trivalent graph by the objects of a category. We will however need our category to be not just a fusion category but a braided fusion category whose objects are anyons. We will attach additional “dangling” edges labeled by a fixed anyon
\rho
, as in Fig.
1
.

The braided fusion category we will take as input to our construction will be the product of
G_{k}
, the anyon model of the Chern-Simons theory for the gauge group
G
at level
k
, and the braided fusion category
\{1,\psi\}
consisting of just the vacuum and a fermion,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=G_{k}\boxtimes\{1,\psi\}.

(15)

We can think of this as taking a “stack” of the two anyon models. The objects of this product category are then pairs of objects from each layer with the first layer carrying the charges and the second layer providing fermionic statistics.

We will bind the charges to the fermionic particles by taking the anyon
\rho
to be the following semi-simple object

\displaystyle\rho=(g_{0},1)\oplus(g_{1},\psi)

(16)

where
g_{0}
is the vacuum particle corresponding to the trivial representation of the gauge group,
g_{1}
is the anyon corresponding to the fundamental representation,
1
is the absence of a fermion, and
\psi
is the fermion. Taking
\rho
to be the semisimple object
\rho=(g_{0},1)\oplus(g_{1},\psi)
means that at each dangling edge, we can have either
(g_{0},1)
, no charged particle, or
(g_{1},\psi)
, a charged particle. The dynamical degrees of freedom live on the non-dangling edges of the lattice and have basis states labeled by the objects of
\mathcal{B}
. The fusion rules of
\mathcal{B}
are then enforced at every vertex.

Note that this formulation obscures the internal degrees of freedom of the representations carried by charged particles. We interpret this as a feature of this formulation, rather than a bug, as we are minimally encoding the gauge invariant states and operators of the system. Facets that are subject to gauge transformation are not locally accessible and should be interpreted as being dissolved into the fusion spaces of the anyon model.

III.1
Fermionic Hamiltonians

As a warm-up to coupling the anyonic-regularized gauge theory to fermionic matter, we will demonstrate the construction of a purely fermionic Hamiltonian within the fusion surface model framework. This will hopefully also serve as a demonstration of how a spin Hamiltonian can be obtained from the more abstract diagrammatic operators to come. We will show explicitly how generators for the even-parity subalgebra of fermionic operators can be constructed in terms of the diagrammatic operators presented in Sec.
III.2
. We will find that the obtained operators reproduce those of the Bravyi-Kitaev superfast encoding/exact bosonization
22
;
27
.

Recall that the fermionic operators
a_{x}^{\dagger}
and
a_{x}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}
create and annihilate a fermion at site
x
, respectively, and satisfy the anticommutation relations

\displaystyle\{a_{x}^{\dagger},a_{y}^{\dagger}\}=\{a_{x}^{\vphantom{\dagger}},a_{y}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\}=0\,,\quad\{a_{x}^{\vphantom{\dagger}},a_{y}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{xy}\mathbb{I}\,.

(17)

Fermionic Hamiltonians in general are represented via sums of even-parity products of these operators. Alternatively, one can define Majorana operators

\displaystyle\gamma_{x}^{\phantom{\overline{}}}=a_{x}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+a_{x}^{\dagger}\,,\quad\overline{\gamma}_{x}=-i(a_{x}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}-a_{x}^{\dagger})\,,

(18)

which satisfy the anticommutation relations

\displaystyle\{\gamma_{x}^{\phantom{\overline{}}},\gamma_{y}^{\phantom{\overline{}}}\}=\{\overline{\gamma}_{x},\overline{\gamma}_{y}\}=2\delta_{xy}\mathbb{I}\,,\quad\{\gamma_{x}^{\phantom{\overline{}}},\overline{\gamma}_{y}\}=0\,.

(19)

A useful (overcomplete) generating set of even-parity fermionic operators consists of the following operators along edges,
\bm{e}
, of the lattice

\displaystyle O_{\bm{e}}^{\psi}=-i\overline{\gamma}_{\partial\bm{e}_{0}}\gamma_{\partial\bm{e}_{1}}^{\phantom{\overline{}}}\,,

(20)

which flip the fermion parity of the modes at the endpoints of the edge
\bm{e}
and the following single-site fermion parity operator associated to the vertex
\bm{v}

\displaystyle P_{\bm{v}}^{\psi}=-i\gamma_{\bm{v}}^{{\phantom{\overline{}}}}\overline{\gamma}_{\bm{v}}

(21)

Since we are focused solely on the fermionic algebra here, we take as input the braided fusion category of supervector spaces
1
,
\mathcal{B}=\{1,\psi\}
, consisting only of the vacuum particle and a fermion. The anyons obey
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
fusion rules,
\psi\times\psi=1
, and the
F
symbol is trivial, with all elements being just zero or one as allowed by the fusion rules. The
R
symbol has a single nontrivial element,
R^{\psi\psi}_{1}=-1
, which provides the fermionic statistics of
\psi
. Correspondingly,
\psi
has a topological spin of
\theta_{\psi}=-1
.

Since the braided fusion category we use has two simple objects, each edge of our lattice will carry a single qubit. Due to the triviality of the
F
symbols and the Abelian nature of the fusion rules, we will also neglect the trivalent resolution for simplicity, so the lattice we consider for this warm-up consists of a square lattice with dangling edges at vertices. Since we want to allow each vertex to be either occupied by a fermion or unoccupied, we take the dangling edge to be labeled by the semi-simple object
\rho=1\oplus\psi
. The
1
state is interpreted as the empty mode, and the
\psi
state is the occupied mode. Once the state of the four edges incident to a given vertex is specified, the uniqueness of the fusion channels fixes the fermion parity of the vertex.

Fermionic operators are defined by gluing on diagrams in the space
\Hom(\rho^{\otimes(L\times L)},\rho^{\otimes(L\times L)})
to the dangling edges for an
L\times L
lattice. One then uses the
F
and
R
symbols to absorb or remove the extra lines in the diagram, recovering the form of the original diagram that defines the Hilbert space. In doing so, the elements of the
F
and
R
symbols provide the matrix elements of the operator on the Hilbert space. As long as the fusion rules are obeyed at each vertex in the diagrammatic operator (which they must be) the operator will preserve the total fermion parity. A consequence of having all of the operators defined in terms of anyon diagrams glued in from the bottom is that any Hamiltonian we construct will obey the 1-form symmetry obtained by gluing in closed loops of the fermion line from above, as in the bottom of Fig.
2
. Having this 1-form symmetry with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
fusion rules and the nontrivial braiding phase is equivalent to the fact that the spin system we obtain has an emergent fermion.

We define the operators
O_{\bm{e}}^{\psi}
by gluing on a
\psi
string attached to two dangling edges as in Eqs. (
30
) and (
31
). Using the
F
and
R
moves to resolve multiple such strings into the lattice, we can determine the commutation relations of these operators. With these commutation relations in hand, we can make the simple identification that if a
\psi
line enters the dangling edge from the left side of the page, it corresponds to the Majorana operator
\gamma
and if the
\psi
line enters a dangling edge from the right side of the page, it corresponds to the Majorana operator
\overline{\gamma}
. This allows one to identify the hopping operator with the Majorana bilinear

\displaystyle O_{\bm{e}}^{\psi}=-i\overline{\gamma}_{\partial\bm{e}_{0}}\gamma_{\partial\bm{e}_{1}}^{\phantom{\overline{}}}\,,

(22)

where
\partial\bm{e}_{i}
are the boundaries, or vertices, of the associated edge
\bm{e}
. The single-site fermion parity operator can be realized by attaching a small
\psi
line to one side of the dangling edge, crossing over, and attaching to the other side. The nontrivial sign in the
R
symbol provides the
(-1)
phase when
P_{\bm{v}}^{\psi}=-i\gamma^{\phantom{\overline{}}}_{\bm{v}}\overline{\gamma}_{\bm{v}}
acts on an occupied mode.

III.2
Anyonic-Regularized Hamiltonian

We now generalize our construction for fermionic Hamiltonians to that of the full Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian. Taking as a starting point the Kogut-Sussind Hamiltonian of Eq. (

), we will demonstrate how to construct each of these interactions in terms of the

\mathcal{B}
.

IV
Quantum Simulation of Anyonic-Regularized Hamiltonian

With the general formulation of the Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian in terms of our diagrammatic operators, we will now discuss the details of simulating this Hamiltonian on a quantum computer. Here we consider explicit implementations for
U(1)_{k}
and
SU(2)_{k}
LGTs, leaving further generalizations to future work. Since the Hamiltonian of Eqs. (
32
)–(
34
) can be written as a linear combination of unitary operators using the
F
and
R
symbols, we can take advantage of this structure to realize any state-of-the-art eigenstate preparation or time-dynamics quantum simulation algorithm.

As an example we can consider the time dynamics algorithm of Ref.
83
which yields asymptotically near-optimal scaling in all relevant system parameters, and demonstrated remarkable improvements in explicit resource estimates for
U(1)
,
SU(2)
, and
SU(3)
LGTs
75
. In this algorithm, one works in the interaction picture, for which the time-dependent Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H_{I}(t)=e^{itH_{E}}(H_{M}+H_{K}+H_{B})e^{-itH_{E}}\,,

(35)

and we have explicitly decomposed the Yang-Mills Hamiltonian
H_{YM}
into its electric,
H_{E}
, and magnetic,
H_{B}
, terms.

Since the electric term, corresponding to the quadratic Casimir operator, is diagonal in the electric basis, its time evolution can be fast-forwarded
40
. This only requires constructing unitary operators to prepare the eigenstates of the quadratic Casimir operators, namely,
[a^{2}]_{q}
for
a\in U(1)_{k}
and
[j(j+1)]_{q}
for
j\in SU(2)_{k}
, and a series of phase gates
31
.

The remaining three terms can then be block encoded using the LCU oracles
prepare
and
select

67
. Since each of the Hamiltonian terms is written in the form
H=\sum_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell}U_{\ell}
, it can be encoded via queries to the oracles

\displaystyle\textsc{prepare}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{\ell}}{\|\alpha\|_{1}}}|\ell\rangle\,,

(36)

\displaystyle\textsc{select}|\ell\rangle|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=|\ell\rangle U_{\ell}|\phi\rangle\,,

(37)

where the number of Hamiltonian terms
\ell
will be functions of the number of vertices, edges, and plaquettes in the lattice. The
prepare
oracle generates a superposition of index states, weighted by the coefficients of the Hamiltonian and the
select
oracle applies the unitary Hamiltonian terms
U_{\ell}
to the basis states
|\phi\rangle
, controlled on the index states
|\ell\rangle
.

In order to implement
prepare
, we can make use of the techniques developed in Ref.
75
and absorb the nontrivial phases of the Hamiltonian terms into the unitary
F
and
R
symbols, such that
prepare
amounts to generating uniform superpositions of index states. Then the action of
select
is simply to apply singly-controlled
F
and
R
symbols using the circuit primitives we will construct, which makes use of the unary iteration subroutine for indexing the states
6
.

Upon successfully block encoding the Hamiltonian by querying these oracles, one can then simulate time dynamics via the truncated Dyson series techniques for time-dependent Hamiltonian simulation
68
;
18
.

As we will see, the structure of the circuit primitives for the
F
and
R
symbols makes the post-Trotter simulation algorithm we sketched more enticing. However, we remark that any state-of-the-art Trotter simulation could also be applied using the circuit realizations of the
F
and
R
symbols. Thus, by providing only the circuit primitives, we open the door to detailed resource estimates and comparisons of various dynamical simulation algorithms, which we leave to future work. It only remains to show how to construct the quantum circuits realizing the
F
and
R
symbols of the input anyon model. We now provide these constructions for
U(1)_{2}
,
U(1)_{k}
, and
SU(2)_{k}
LGTs.

IV.0.1
U(1)_{2}

The
U(1)_{2}
anyon model is an Abelian gauge theory describing the semion edge theory of topological phases. It is equivalent to
SU(2)_{1}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{(1/2)}
braided fusion categories. The input braided fusion category is

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=U(1)_{2}\boxtimes\{1,\psi\}\,,

(38)

where the topological excitations of

are
\{1,s\}
, consisting of the vacuum and a semion. Thus, the composite system contains four anyons

\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{(1/2)}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}

\theta_{(1,1)}=1
,
\theta_{(s,1)}=i
,
\theta_{(1,\psi)}=-1
, and
\theta_{(s,\psi)}=-i
. On the dangling edges, we take the fixed lines
\rho
to be

\displaystyle\rho=(1,1)\oplus(s,\psi)\,.

(39)

In this model, the Hamiltonian terms of Eqs. (
32
)-(
34
) become

\displaystyle H_{M}=\sum_{\bm{v}}P_{\bm{v}}^{\psi}=\sum_{\bm{v}}(-1)^{\rho_{\bm{v}}}\,,

(40)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} H_{K}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{e}}(i\gamma_{\partial\bm{e}_{0}}^{\phantom{\overline{}}}\overline{\gamma}_{\partial\bm{e}_{1}}-i\overline{\gamma}_{\partial\bm{e}_{0}}\gamma_{\partial\bm{e}_{1}}^{\phantom{\overline{}}})O_{\bm{e}}^{(s,1)}\\

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!H_{YM}=\frac{g^{2}}{2}\sum_{\bm{e}}E_{\bm{e}}^{2}-\frac{1}{a^{2}g^{2}}\sum_{\bm{p}}T_{\bm{p}}^{(s,\psi)}\,,

(42)

where for

O_{\bm{e}}^{\alpha{\vphantom{\dagger}}}=O_{\bm{e}}^{\alpha\dagger}
. Thus,
O_{\bm{e}}^{\alpha}
only facilitates hopping when one vertex is occupied by a fermion and the other is not. For the electric term, the
q
-deformed electric field strength is

.

In the
U(1)_{2}
anyon model, each composite anyon can be stored in a two-qubit register with the first qubit storing the anyon from the
U(1)_{2}

\{1,\psi\}

\{1,\psi\}

\mathbb{Z}_{2}
fusion rules, the nonzero

U(1)_{2}

F

\displaystyle[F^{abc}_{d}]_{e,f}^{\phantom{a}}=[F^{abc}_{a\oplus b\oplus c}]_{a\oplus b,b\oplus c}^{\phantom{a}}=e^{\frac{\pi i}{2}a(b+c-(b\oplus c))}\,,

\displaystyle|a,b;a\oplus b\rangle|a\oplus b,c;a\oplus b\oplus c\rangle\mapsto e^{\frac{\pi i}{2}a(b+c-(b\oplus c))}|a,b\oplus c;a\oplus b\oplus c\rangle|b,c;b\oplus c\rangle\,,

U(1)_{k}

We now generalize from

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=U(1)_{k}\boxtimes\{1,\psi\}\,,

(47)

\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{(1/2)}

\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{(n+1/2)}

\mathbb{Z}_{k}^{(1/2)}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}

\overline{a}=(-m\!\!\mod k,n)

\theta_{(m,1)}=e^{2\pi i(m\!\!\mod k)^{2}/2k}

\theta_{(m,\psi)}=-e^{2\pi i(m\!\!\mod k)^{2}/2k}

\displaystyle\rho=(0,1)\oplus(1,\psi)

(48)

\displaystyle H_{M}=\sum_{\bm{v}}P_{\bm{v}}^{\psi}=\sum_{\bm{v}}(-1)^{\rho_{\bm{v}}}\,,

q

V
Summary and Outlook

In this work we have constructed explicit Hamiltonians coupling fermionic matter to anyonic-regularized gauge fields in order to simulate LGTs on fault-tolerant quantum computers. Regularization of the gauge group is a necessary step in the tractable encoding and simulation of any gauge field on a computational device, and a variety of techniques have been proposed for accomplishing this task for quantum computers. The anyonic-regularized gauge groups we construct here preserve the underlying symmetries of the full Lie algebra, which allows for a systematic bound on the error in the extrapolation to the undeformed algebra, in which observables correspond to physically meaningful quantities. This motivates additional work on numerical and analytical bounds for the convergence of of these gauge groups in such a limit, and in particular, how these bounds compare to other popular regularization schemes such as direct truncation and the use of discrete subgroups.

While prior work has proposed classical simulation algorithms of
q
-deformed gauge theories for both
SU(2)

89
and
SU(3)

48
;
50
LGTs, these studies have been restricted to pure-gauge theories, with a proposal for coupling to hard-core bosons provided in Ref.
89
. Here we generalize these results by taking as input a braided fusion category, allowing us to simulate Lie groups for which there is no corresponding
q
-deformation, such as
U(1)
, and demonstrate how to couple these deformed gauge fields to fermionic matter using the framework of fusion surface models. The application of fusion surface models to quantum simulation is novel, and the techniques developed here may be of broader interest to the quantum simulation community. While we only provided Hamiltonian constructions for
U(1)_{k}
and
SU(2)_{k}
, the extension to
SU(3)_{k}
and beyond is an interesting route to consider for future work. While the pure-gauge simulation is a direct generalization of our construction, albeit with additional degrees of freedom at vertices storing multiplicities, coupling to fermionic matter has a number of subtleties which need to be addressed. An alternative line of future work is the generalization to higher dimensions. In particular, formulating
3+1
d LGTs in a similar framework may make use of Walker-Wang models
85
, the higher dimensional generalization of Levin-Wen models.

Finally, the Hamiltonian constructions we provided have a simple structure as a linear combination of unitary operators, but require a new primitive gate set for simulation in terms of the
F
and
R
symbols of the underlying anyon model. We constructed efficient quantum circuit subroutines for these operators in
U(1)_{k}
and
SU(2)_{k}
LGTs. With the circuit primitives in hand, any state-of-the-art Trotterization or post-Trotter simulation protocol may be applied to obtain resource estimates for state preparation or time evolution of these LGTs. We note that there is significant room for improvement in the implementation of the
SU(2)_{k}

F
symbols, contingent upon an efficient quantum algorithm for computing the (deformed) Wigner-
6j
symbols, in which one could modify the Clebsch-Gordan transform to realize this operator, but we leave this development for future work.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}j_{1}&j_{2}&j_{5}\\

Appendix B
Convergence of Anyonic-Regularized Hamiltonian

In order to validate our model, we must ensure that the anyonic-regularized Hamiltonian converges to the Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian in the limit
q\to 1
, or equivalently, as
k\to\infty
, in which the braided fusion category
G_{k}
converges to the Lie group
G
. Since the anyonic-regularized Hamiltonian is constructed from the product of
F
and
R
symbols, we consider the convergence of these quantities in this limit.

B.1
U(1)_{k}

Recall that in the
U(1)_{k}
LGT, the
F
and
R
symbols are defined by

\displaystyle[F_{a\oplus_{k}b\oplus_{k}c}^{abc}]_{a\oplus_{k}b,b\oplus_{k}c}^{\phantom{a}}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{\pi i}{k}a(b+c-(b\oplus_{k}c))}\,,

(87)

\displaystyle R^{ab}_{c}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{\pi i}{k}ab}\,,

(88)

where
\oplus_{k}
denotes addition modulo
k
. Taking products of these operators for a fixed labeling of the state to construct the various Hamiltonian terms will generate an overall phase of the form
e^{\pi if(a,b,c)/k}
for some function
f
(independent of
k
), which in the limit yields

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}e^{\frac{\pi i}{k}f(a,b,c)}=1

(89)

Moreover, the quantum dimension which also appears as a coefficient in Eqs. (
30
) and (
31
) is trivial since
\text{d}_{1}=1
for the generating Wilson line of
U(1)_{k}
. Therefore, in the limit
k\to\infty
, the link operators of the full gauge group
U(1)
are recovered:

\displaystyle W_{1}=\sum_{a\in U(1)_{k}}|a+1\rangle\langle a|\,,\quad W_{1}^{\dagger}=\sum_{a\in U(1)_{k}}|a-1\rangle\langle a|\,.

(90)

The convergence of the Casimir operator is also straightforward since we need only check the convergence of the coefficient
[a^{2}]_{q}
. Note that the limit
q\to 1
is equivalent to the limit
k\to\infty
in the
q
-numbers, and by definition in Eq. (
76
), we have

\displaystyle\lim_{q\to 1}[a^{2}]_{q}=a^{2}\,,

(91)

which recovers the exact Casimir operator of the
U(1)
LGT. Lastly, we note that since the parity operator
P_{\bm{v}}
acts only on the fermionic layer, we need not check its convergence.

B.2
SU(2)_{k}

Recall that in the
SU(2)_{k}
LGT, the
F
and
R
symbols are defined by

\displaystyle[F_{j_{4}}^{j_{1}j_{2}j_{3}}]_{j_{5}j_{6}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}+j_{4}}\sqrt{[2j_{5}+1]_{q}[2j_{6}+1]_{q}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}j_{1}&j_{2}&j_{5}\\
j_{3}&j_{4}&j_{6}\end{array}\right\}_{q}\,,

\displaystyle R_{j_{3}}^{j_{1}j_{2}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j_{3}-j_{1}-j_{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}(j_{3}(j_{3}+1)-j_{1}(j_{1}+1)-j_{2}(j_{2}+1))}\,,

(94)

where
q=e^{2\pi i/(k+2)}
is the deformation parameter. In the limit
k\to\infty
, or equivalently
q\to 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{q\to 1}[F_{j_{4}}^{j_{1}j_{2}j_{3}}]_{j_{5}j_{6}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}+j_{4}}\sqrt{(2j_{5}+1)(2j_{6}+1)}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}j_{1}&j_{2}&j_{5}\\
j_{3}&j_{4}&j_{6}\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\lim_{q\to 1}R_{j_{3}}^{j_{1}j_{2}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j_{3}-j_{1}-j_{2}}\,,

(97)

for which the usual Wigner-
6j
symbols are recovered, but there is a persistent phase in the
R
symbols which must be carefully accounted for.

The convergence of the
SU(2)_{k}
pure-gauge theory was demonstrated in Ref.
89
for the plaquette and Casimir operators. In this case the only
F
symbols appear in the Hamiltonian, whereas our model additionally contains a single
R
symbol. However, the particular structure of the
R
symbol in Eq. (
25
) ensures a trivial contribution since

\displaystyle R^{\bar{\alpha}\rho^{\prime}}_{\rho}=\begin{cases}R_{(1/2,\psi)}^{(1/2,\psi)(0,1)}=1\\
R_{(0,1)}^{(1/2,\psi)(1/2,\psi)}=1\end{cases}\,,

(98)

and the contributions from the fermionic layer cancel out any nontrivial contribution from the
SU(2)_{k}
layer. Thus, the plaquette operator recovers the usual Kogut-Susskind plaquette operator and the Casimir operator converges in the same way as the
U(1)_{k}
LGT:

\displaystyle\lim_{q\to 1}[j(j+1)]_{q}=j(j+1)\,.

(99)

The only remaining term to consider is the kinetic term. Unfortunately, the
R
symbols appearing in Eqs. (
30
) and (
31
) do not always have a trivial contribution since they depend on non-dangling edges in the lattice which are unconstrained. The application of the kinetic term for
SU(2)_{k}
LGTs in Eq. (
60
), which acts on the enlarged Hilbert space of anyon states and total fermion numbers, ensures we always produce the correct final states. See Fig.
6
.

If we compare our model to that of the spin-network representation of the Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian, it is clear that the nontrivial matrix elements of the Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian will depend only on
j_{1}
,
j_{2}
, and
j_{3}
. However, in our anyonic-regularized model, the coefficients will depend on
j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{5}
, with the additional dependencies arising due to the
F
symbols which are functions of
j_{4}
and
j_{5}
. Thus, our hopping operator will need to be multiplied by a prefactor which depends only upon
j_{4}
and
j_{5}
in order to correct the amplitudes in our model such that they converge to the same matrix elements as the Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian.

One could compute the hopping amplitudes in the spin-network representation of the Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian and compare to the sequence of
F
symbols appearing in our anyonic-regularized Hamiltonian to attempt to identify the functional form of these prefactors. If they are simple functions, one could then implement them directly via quantum circuits for arithmetic. However, since there are at most
O(k^{2})
unique coefficients, we can instead classically pre-compute these factors and load them into the computation via a QROM
6
. The asymptotic scaling incurred here is still subleading with respect to the cost of computing the
F
symbols themselves, which scales as
O(k^{3})
, so using a QROM will not increase the computational complexity. However, it is interesting to note that since we have included the fermionic layer
\{1,\psi\}
, the amplitudes of our model have the same sign as the usual Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian, and differ in magnitude only.

Thus, after applying these corrective prefactors to the Hamiltonian
H_{K}
in Eq. (
60
), each matrix element of the anyonic-regularized Hamiltonian will converge to the true Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian in the limit
k\to\infty
.
