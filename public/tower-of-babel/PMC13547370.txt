Graph attention network-enhanced multi-agent reinforcement learning for dynamic interception task allocation in counter-drone defense
Authors: Dianbo Jia, Ganliang Wang, Hongfei Bu, Junwei Wang, Zhengkai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In cooperative counter-drone defense, dynamic interception task allocation presents a fundamental challenge: targets may appear, vanish, or shift trajectory unpredictably throughout an engagement. Current multi-agent reinforcement learning approaches generally lack explicit mechanisms to capture the changing correspondence between interceptors and targets as the operational topology evolves. We propose DT-GAT-MARL, a hierarchical framework pairing a Dynamic-Topology Graph Attention Network (DT-GAT) at the strategic allocation level with Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO)-based maneuver control at the tactical level. Three design choices define DT-GAT: a masking mechanism that handles node additions and removals without rebuilding the graph, learnable edge-feature biases that embed spatial reachability and interception urgency directly into attention computation, and a Gumbel-Softmax allocation head that enables differentiable discrete assignment while preserving end-to-end gradient flow. A dual-frequency architecture separates the allocation and maneuvering timescales, which helps suppress assignment oscillations. We evaluate the framework across balanced, numerically disadvantaged, and dynamic intrusion scenarios ranging from 4v4 to 12v12. In the dynamic intrusion setting, DT-GAT-MARL exceeds the strongest baseline by 10.3 percentage points, reaches an 87.3% effective reallocation rate, and keeps oscillation at just 9.6%. Ablation results confirm that edge-feature bias is the single most critical component.

Introduction

Low-cost commercial drones have proliferated rapidly in recent years, posing growing threats to critical infrastructure, public safety, and military installations—making counter-drone defense an increasingly pressing priority1. Recent studies have further explored deep reinforcement learning for autonomous combat planning in adversarial UAV environments2. Meanwhile, Tian et al.3 revealed that deep-learning-based UAV systems are susceptible to adversarial attacks on both navigation and control modules, further underscoring the security challenges that hostile conditions pose to autonomous drone platforms and reinforcing the urgency of robust defensive coordination. Compared with conventional manned air combat, countering drone incursions introduces distinct difficulties: target numbers shift dynamically, threat arrival sequences are uncertain, and swarm-scale coordination effects emerge4. Together, these factors make real-time task allocation the central decision-making bottleneck5. For cooperative interception to succeed, defender clusters must tightly couple target assignment with maneuver control amid continuously evolving tactical conditions, demanding both responsiveness and adaptability.

Classical combinatorial optimization has received considerable attention in this area. The consensus-based auction algorithm of Choi et al6. achieves robust allocation through distributed bidding and consensus propagation, though its dependence on iterative convergence constrains applicability when targets appear or vanish in real time. Skaltsis et al.7, in their broad survey, observed that the computational cost of traditional optimization scales sharply with agent count, creating severe real-time bottlenecks at larger scales. Game-theoretic formulations offer another avenue—Ren et al.8 applied incomplete-information dynamic games to multi-UAV maneuvering decisions and showed the utility of equilibrium strategies for adversarial reasoning. Such methods, however, assume a finite discrete strategy space and quickly become intractable in high-dimensional continuous action settings.

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has introduced a data-driven, end-to-end paradigm for multi-agent coordination. On the value decomposition side, Sunehag et al9. proposed VDN, which additively decomposes team rewards across agents—a foundational step toward decentralized cooperative learning. In the policy gradient direction, MADDPG10 established centralized-training-decentralized-execution (CTDE) as a key baseline for continuous multi-agent control, and HAPPO11 later added trust-region constraints guaranteeing monotonic policy improvement across heterogeneous agents. A shared limitation of these methods, however, is that cooperative relationships stay implicitly buried in network parameters; none explicitly model the dynamic correspondence between interceptors and targets. Graph neural networks (GNNs) offer a natural complement. Kipf et al.12 demonstrated that graph networks can infer latent relational structures from observational data, while Liu et al.13 showed through G2ANet that graph attention mechanisms automatically extract critical interaction patterns in multi-agent games, substantially improving coordination. More recently, graph attention mechanisms have been applied to other cooperative decision-making domains: for instance, Zhang14 employed a graph attention-based heterogeneous multi-agent framework for adaptive portfolio optimization, demonstrating that attention-driven inter-agent communication generalizes beyond robotic control to strategic resource allocation.

Even with this progress, existing GNN-based methods remain insufficient for counter-drone interception in three respects. First, most rely on static or predefined graph topologies that cannot gracefully handle node additions or removals—whether from successful interceptions or newly detected intruders—and rebuilding the graph discards historical information at unnecessary computational cost. Second, standard graph attention derives aggregation weights from node features alone, overlooking geometric cues carried by edges; spatial reachability and interception urgency, for instance, would allow interceptors to modulate attention based on relative tactical posture. Third, the inherent timescale gap between strategic-level discrete assignment and tactical-level continuous maneuver control has lacked an explicit coordination mechanism in prior work.

To address these gaps, we propose DT-GAT-MARL, a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework built around a dynamic-topology graph attention network and organized as a two-tier decision architecture linking high-level allocation with low-level maneuvering. Our contributions are as follows. We design the DT-GAT module to support node-level additions and removals via masking, incorporating learnable edge-feature biases into attention computation to encode spatial relationships, and employing a Gumbel-Softmax allocation head for differentiable discrete assignment. We construct a dual-frequency, loosely coupled hierarchical framework where the high-level DT-GAT produces allocation plans at low frequency and the low-level MAPPO executes maneuver control every timestep, with gradient connectivity maintained through Gumbel-Softmax and a pretrain-then-jointly-finetune training strategy. We conduct comprehensive experiments spanning balanced, disadvantaged, and dynamic-intrusion scenarios—from 4v4 to 12v12 scalability tests and seven ablation configurations—systematically quantifying each module’s contribution and validating real-time reallocation under dynamic intrusions.

Methodology

Overall architecture

DT-GAT-MARL adopts a dual-frequency, loosely coupled hierarchical architecture with two decision tiers having separate network parameters and optimizers. The high-level tier is the DT-GAT module, which outputs interceptor-to-target assignments at low frequency. The low-level tier is a MAPPO-based maneuver policy network producing velocity-level control commands every timestep. Information flows between tiers through the Gumbel-Softmax allocation head: assignment probabilities produce a weighted sum over intruder embeddings, yielding a target feature embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{z}}_{i}$$\end{document} that serves as auxiliary input to the low-level Actor. Specifically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{z}}_{i}={\sum}_{j:{m}_{j}=1}{\pi}_{ij}{z}_{j}^{u}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\pi}_{ij}$$\end{document} is the Gumbel-Softmax output from Eq. (7). Since Gumbel-Softmax is differentiable, gradients from the low-level reward signal propagate back through to the high-level DT-GAT parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\theta}_{h}$$\end{document}, enabling end-to-end joint optimization.

High-level allocation triggers on whichever comes first: periodic invocation (every K steps) or an event trigger (target intercepted, new target detected, or interceptor released). Between triggers, allocation remains frozen and the low-level policy continues maneuvering under the standing assignment. This lets the strategic layer update at a slower cadence while the tactical layer operates at full timestep resolution, explicitly reconciling their inherent timescale mismatch. The allocation follows a soft many-to-one scheme: each interceptor is assigned to exactly one target at a time, but multiple interceptors may independently select the same intruder if the attention mechanism deems it the highest-priority threat. No explicit capacity constraint is enforced; instead, the edge-feature bias naturally diversifies assignments because interceptors farther from an already-targeted intruder receive lower attention scores for it. In disadvantaged scenarios where intruders outnumber interceptors, each interceptor pursues one target at a time and, upon successful interception, is event-triggered to reallocate toward the next highest-priority active intruder, enabling sequential multi-target engagement.

DT-GAT module

The DT-GAT module is the core component of the proposed method. It augments the standard graph attention mechanism with three design choices: edge-feature-enhanced attention, a masking mechanism, and a Gumbel-Softmax allocation head.

Edge-feature-enhanced attention

In standard GAT19, attention logits depend solely on node features and thus cannot exploit the spatial geometry encoded in edges. Unlike GraphSAGE20, which relies on fixed aggregation functions, we introduce a learnable edge-feature bias into the attention computation, enabling data-driven adaptive aggregation. The term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta}_{k}^{{\top}}{\mathrm{f}}_{ij}$$\end{document} has a clear physical interpretation: relative distance and bearing encoded in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{f}}_{ij}$$\end{document} allow attention weights to incorporate spatial reachability21, while the estimated intercept time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widehat{t}}_{ij}$$\end{document} injects an urgency prior that steers interceptors toward more threatening and reachable targets. The idea of incorporating edge features into graph neural networks has been explored in prior work (e.g., Gong and Cheng21 proposed edge-feature-enhanced GNNs for general graph classification). Our contribution is not the concept of edge-feature bias itself, but its specific instantiation for multi-agent interception: we encode relative distance, bearing, and estimated intercept time as the edge feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{ij}$$\end{document}, giving the bias a concrete physical interpretation tied to spatial reachability and interception urgency. Combined with the masking mechanism and Gumbel-Softmax head—neither of which appear together in prior GNN-based MARL work for task allocation—the integration addresses the specific challenge of differentiable dynamic assignment under changing topologies.

Gumbel-Softmax allocation head

The aggregated embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${z}_{i}^{^{\prime}}$$\end{document} is projected into the same dimensionality as the intruder embeddings via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widehat{z}}_{i}={W}_{l}{z}_{i}^{^{\prime}}+{b}_{l}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${W}_{l}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{d\times H{d}^{^{\prime}}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b}_{l}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{d}$$\end{document}. The allocation logit for interceptor i toward intruder j is then computed as a bilinear score: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${l}_{ij}={\widehat{z}}_{i}^{{\top}}{W}_{b}{z}_{j}^{u}$$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${W}_{b}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}$$\end{document} is a learnable bilinear weight matrix. Since task assignment is inherently a discrete choice.

, a naive argmax would block gradient flow. We therefore apply Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization22,23:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi _{{ij}} = \frac{{{\mathrm{exp}}\,\left( {\left( {l_{{ij}} + g_{j} } \right)/\tau } \right)}}{{\mathop \sum \nolimits_{{j^{\prime}:\,m_{{j^{\prime}}} = 1}} {\mathrm{exp}}\,\left( {\left( {l_{{ij^{\prime}}} + g_{{j^{\prime}}} } \right)/\tau } \right)}}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${g}_{j}=-\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}(-\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}({U}_{j}))$$\end{document} is Gumbel noise with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${U}_{j}\sim Uniform(\mathrm{0,1})$$\end{document}, and τ is the temperature parameter. Maneuver policy and reward design

The low-level maneuver policy uses an Actor-Critic architecture. The Actor network incorporates a GRU unit for temporal memory and takes as input the concatenation of interceptor i’s local observation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{o}}_{i}$$\end{document} and its assigned target embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{z}}_{target,i}$$\end{document}, outputting control commands \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left({v}_{i},{\omega}_{i}\right)$$\end{document}. The Critic, active only during training, estimates the state value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V(\mathcal{S})$$\end{document} from the global state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{S}$$\end{document}, consistent with the CTDE paradigm18. Optimization follows MAPPO24, whose parameter sharing and value normalization techniques have demonstrated strong sample efficiency and training stability in cooperative multi-agent settings.

The reward design prioritizes task-level signals, deliberately avoiding hand-crafted cooperative rules. Global rewards—interception success \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{cap}$$\end{document} and target breach \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{pen}$$\end{document}—are shared across both tiers so that allocation decisions are optimized directly toward task objectives. Shaping rewards act only on the low level to guide basic maneuvering behavior and include distance reduction, heading alignment, out-of-bounds penalties, and time efficiency signals. Additionally, a switching cost \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{switch}$$\end{document} discourages allocation oscillations, ensuring interceptors retain sufficient execution windows after reassignment. Reward function components.

A potential concern with shared global rewards is the credit assignment problem, where individual agents may adopt free-riding behavior. Three design features mitigate this risk in DT-GAT-MARL. First, the low-level shaping rewards (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{dist}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{head}$$\end{document}) are computed per interceptor based on its individual trajectory toward the assigned target, providing agent-specific learning signals. Second, the DT-GAT allocation mechanism assigns each interceptor a distinct target, creating implicit task specialization that ties each agent’s maneuvering behavior to an identifiable subtask. Third, MAPPO’s parameter sharing and value normalization have been empirically shown to alleviate lazy-agent tendencies in cooperative settings24. Empirically, we monitored each interceptor’s individual interception count across 250 evaluation episodes in the 8v8 scenario: the coefficient of variation (CV) across agents was 0.12, indicating approximately balanced workload distribution rather than concentration on a few active agents.

The reward weights (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda}_{d}=0.5$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda}_{h}=0.3$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{cap}=+20$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{pen}=-50$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{switch}=-2$$\end{document}) were determined through a systematic grid search over 36 configurations in the 4v4 balanced scenario. We varied \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda}_{d}\in \{\mathrm{0.3,0.5,0.8}\}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda}_{h}\in \{\mathrm{0.1,0.3,0.5}\}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mid {r}_{pen}/{r}_{cap}\mid \in \{\mathrm{2.0,2.5,3.0,5.0}\}$$\end{document}, evaluating each configuration over three seeds. The selected weights achieved the highest interception rate while keeping oscillation rate below 10%. We note that the global reward magnitudes (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{cap}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{pen}$$\end{document}) dominate shaping signals by design: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mid {r}_{pen}\mid =2.5\times {r}_{cap}$$\end{document} reflects the asymmetric operational cost of a defensive breach versus a successful interception. Moderate sensitivity was observed for the penalty-to-reward ratio: configurations with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mid {r}_{pen}/{r}_{cap}\mid =2.0$$\end{document} produced 2–3 point lower interception rates due to insufficient breach deterrence, while ratios above 3.0 yielded comparable results.

The two-stage training strategy is designed to mitigate instability arising from this coupling. In Stage 1, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\theta}_{h}$$\end{document} is frozen and the low-level policy converges under random allocation, establishing a stable maneuvering baseline. In Stage 2, the halved high-level learning rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\eta}_{h}=0.5{\eta}_{l}$$\end{document}) limits the magnitude of allocation shifts per update, preventing catastrophic interference with the already-learned maneuvering policy. Fig. 3(b) shows that the training curve exhibits a transient performance dip during mid-Stage 2, corresponding to the temperature regime where allocation transitions from exploratory to near-deterministic; convergence resumes once τ drops sufficiently. Empirically, the joint training consistently converges across all five random seeds, with final performance variance within 3.5 percentage points.

The 95% confidence intervals for DT-GAT’s advantage over the next-best method range from [0.8, 4.1] percentage points in the 4v4 scenario to [7.4, 13.2] points in the dynamic intrusion scenario. It is worth noting that the Hungarian algorithm edges out DT-GAT on mean interception time in this setting (12.3 s vs. 12.8 s)—optimal static matching produces near-globally-optimal initial assignments when the problem is small and symmetric. As the scale grows to 8v8 and 12v12, however, the Hungarian algorithm’s lack of dynamic adaptability becomes apparent: its success rate drops from 87.2% to 58.6%, whereas DT-GAT declines only from 93.6% to 78.4%, exhibiting notably better scalability. The RH-Hungarian baseline, which re-optimizes matching every K steps, consistently outperforms the static Hungarian algorithm across all scenarios (e.g., 73.6% vs. 70.4% at 8v8), confirming that periodic re-optimization is strictly beneficial. However, even with identical allocation frequency and event triggers, RH-Hungarian falls well short of DT-GAT (73.6% vs. 86.3% at 8v8)—a gap of 12.7 points attributable entirely to the learned allocation policy. This comparison isolates the value of end-to-end learned attention-based assignment: whereas RH-Hungarian minimizes instantaneous distances, DT-GAT’s edge-feature biases integrate distance, bearing, and estimated intercept time to produce allocations that are geometrically informed rather than myopically optimal. That said, the 78.4% rate at 12v12 indicates that large-scale cooperative interception remains challenging for current methods.

Among graph-based approaches, DT-GAT surpasses GraphSAGE + MAPPO by 4.9 points in the 8v8 scenario (86.3% vs. 81.4%), a gap attributable primarily to the spatial awareness conferred by edge-feature biases and the adaptive aggregation from the attention mechanism. DGN performs comparably to GraphSAGE, though its uniform graph convolution aggregation limits differentiated focus when intruder distributions are uneven. Disadvantaged scenarios amplify these differences further: under the severe 6v10 imbalance, DT-GAT (54.7%) exceeds MAPPO (42.5%) by 12.2 points, as the release-and-reassign mechanism enables interceptors to pivot promptly toward fresh threats after completing an interception. Even so, more than 40% of intruders breach the defense in the 6v10 setting, underscoring the inherent difficulty of numerically disadvantaged scenarios.

Conclusion

This paper has presented DT-GAT-MARL, a dual-frequency hierarchical decision framework for dynamic interception task allocation in counter-drone defense. The DT-GAT module combines masking, edge-feature biases, and a Gumbel-Softmax allocation head to achieve differentiable end-to-end assignment under dynamic topologies. Experimental results pinpoint the edge-feature bias as the single most impactful component (6.2-point ablation drop), while masking ensures computational continuity during topology changes in dynamic scenarios, and Gumbel-Softmax temperature annealing balances exploration against convergence stability. The dual-frequency mechanism effectively suppresses allocation oscillation by decoupling assignment and maneuvering timescales (oscillation rate rose from 8.2% to 16.4% when hierarchy was removed), and DT-GAT outperforms the best baseline by 10.3 points in the dynamic intrusion scenario.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The kinematic model is a planar three-variable simplification that omits three-dimensional maneuvers and aerodynamic constraints. Intruders follow constant-velocity straight-line trajectories representing a penetration-focused strategy; in real engagements, adversarial drones are likely to exhibit evasive or cooperative behaviors29, which would substantially raise both interception difficulty and the demands on the allocation module. Similarly, the planar kinematic model omits altitude dynamics and aerodynamic constraints present in three-dimensional operational environments. DT-GAT’s inference time grows relatively fast with agent count (~ 10.2 ms at 40 agents), and scalability is bounded by the quadratic complexity of attention computation30. Furthermore, the current dynamic topology handling relies on masking over a fixed-capacity graph rather than true dynamic graph learning methods such as temporal graph networks. While masking is computationally efficient and avoids structural reconstruction, it does not learn temporal transition patterns between successive graph states. Incorporating dynamic graph neural networks that explicitly model topology evolution over time could potentially improve anticipatory reallocation. The 78.4% success rate at 12v12 further points to considerable room for improvement in large-scale coordination.

Future work will pursue several directions: introducing sparse attention mechanisms to reduce computational overhead at scale; investigating decentralized allocation strategies based on local subgraphs under communication constraints31; modeling intruders as intelligent evasive agents within an adversarial game-theoretic training framework; and exploring sim-to-real transfer methods32 for deploying trained policies on physical drone platforms.
