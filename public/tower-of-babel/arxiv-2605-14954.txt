Introduction

Zero-sum Ramsey theory asks for copies of a prescribed graph whose edge labels sum to the identity element of a finite Abelian group.
The subject was initiated by Bialostocki and Dierker
[
3
,
4
]
and is closely tied to additive zero-sum results such as the Erdős-Ginzburg-Ziv theorem
[
16
]
and the generalized Cauchy-Davenport theorem
[
12
,
15
]
.

Unless otherwise stated, let
q\geq 2
be an integer.
We work throughout with the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.
For a graph
G
, the
zero-sum Ramsey number

R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{q})
is the least integer
n
such that every edge-labeling
w\colon E(K_{n})\to\mathbb{Z}_{q}
contains a copy
G^{\prime}
of
G
with
\sum_{e\in E(G^{\prime})}w(e)=0
in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.
Note that the divisibility condition
q\mid e(G)
is necessary for any general zero-sum Ramsey statement.
In fact, if
q\nmid e(G)
, then for every
n
, in the edge-labeling of
K_{n}
defined by
w(e)=1
for every
e
, every copy
G^{\prime}
of
G
satisfies
\sum_{e\in E(G^{\prime})}w(e)=1\cdot e(G)\neq 0
in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.
Whenever
q\mid e(G)
, the existence of the zero-sum Ramsey number is guaranteed by the standard multicolor Ramsey theorem.
Indeed, any monochromatic copy of
G
arising from a
q
-coloring is necessarily zero-sum.

General upper bounds have been established for certain families of graphs, such as complete graphs
[
1
,
6
,
8
]
, stars
[
7
]
, forests
[
14
]
, bounded degree graphs
[
17
]
and
d
-degenerate graphs
[
20
]
.
However, the exact value of
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{q})
is known for only a few special instances of
G
and
q
, such as when
G
is a star and
q
is even, or when
G
is a matching
[
4
]
.
Other notable results on zero-sum Ramsey numbers include the complete determination of
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})

[
9
]
and the determination of
R(F,\mathbb{Z}_{3})
for forests
[
11
,
2
]
.
For further background and early developments in this area, we refer the reader to the survey by Caro
[

and the seminal work of Alon and Caro

In this paper, we study zero-sum cycles with lengths prescribed as multiples of the modulus.
Cycles are a natural test case; determining the exact value of

depends on the complex interaction between the cycle length, the modulus, and the underlying zero-sum structure.
Recent work on zero-sum cycles in related settings includes
[
5
,
13
]
.

Throughout this paper, all congruences and edge-label sums are taken over
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.
For a given graph
H

A subgraph

For integers

and

denote the cycle on

The primary object of this paper is the zero-sum Ramsey number:

Our first main result reveals that

.

For every

.

We briefly outline our proof strategy.
Theorem
1.1
follows from an insertion lemma: given a zero-sum cycle of length at least
2q-1
and at least
2q-1
unused vertices, the classic Erdős-Ginzburg-Ziv theorem guarantees that we can insert
q
vertices such that the total change to the cycle’s weight is zero.
We prove Theorem
1.2
by applying Pikhurko’s even-cycle Turán bound to the largest color class and invoking the theorem of Katz et al. on zero-sum Ramsey numbers for bounded degree graphs.
The lower bounds in Theorem
1.3
arise from a specific cut labeling in which every long cycle crosses the cut a positive even number of times.
The proof of Theorem
1.6
relies on a different proof strategy.
By the Cauchy-Davenport theorem, having many independent non-zero switches would yield a zero-sum copy; thus, in any counterexample, all switch defects must be confined to a bounded number of vertices.
Then, the congruence condition
s+2\equiv 0\pmod{p}
guarantees that any sufficiently large set of the same type contains a zero-sum copy.
Finally, the exact results for cycles and wheels modulo
3
require a more structural analysis of labelings that avoid zero-sum copies.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 contains the proofs of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
.
The proof of Theorem
1.3
is presented in Section 3.
In Section 4, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.6
.
Sections 5 and 6 establish the exact results for cycles and wheels modulo 3, proving Theorems
1.7
and
1.8
, respectively.

2
Preliminaries and Proofs of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2

In this section, we first introduce a key lemma, which we apply to prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
.
Our approach relies on the following version of the Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv theorem
[
16
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv
[
16
]
)
.

Every sequence of
2q-1
elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
contains a subsequence of length
q
whose sum is
0
in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
q\geq 2
. Suppose that
K_{n}
has an edge-labeling

w\colon E(K_{n})\to\mathbb{Z}_{q}
,
which contains a zero-sum cycle
C_{\ell}
with
w(C_{\ell})=\sum_{e\in E(C_{\ell})}w(e)\equiv 0\pmod{q}
for some
\ell\geq 2q-1
.
If there are at least
2q-1
vertices outside
C_{\ell}
, then
K_{n}
contains a zero-sum cycle
C_{\ell+q}
.

Proof.

Choose
2q-1
distinct edges of
C_{\ell}
, say

e_{i}=a_{i}b_{i}
, for
i\in[2q-1]
,
and choose
2q-1
distinct vertices outside
C_{\ell}
, say
u_{1},\dots,u_{2q-1}
.
For each
i
, define the insertion increment

d_{i}=w(a_{i}u_{i})+w(u_{i}b_{i})-w(a_{i}b_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}.

By Theorem
2.1
, there is an index set

I\subseteq[2q-1]
with
|I|=q
,
such that
\sum_{i\in I}d_{i}=0
in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.

For each
i\in I
, replace the edge
a_{i}b_{i}
of
C_{\ell}
by the two-edge path
a_{i}u_{i}b_{i}
.
Subdividing distinct edges of a simple cycle by distinct new vertices produces another simple cycle. Thus, we get a cycle of length
\ell+|I|=\ell+q
.
Moreover, the edge-label sum is
w(C_{\ell})+\sum_{i\in I}d_{i}=0+0=0
in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.
Hence the resulting cycle is a zero-sum
C_{\ell+q}
.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We first claim that for every
t\geq 3
,

R(C_{qt},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leq\max\{R(C_{q(t-1)},\mathbb{Z}_{q}),qt+q-1\}.

Indeed, let
n\geq\max\{R(C_{q(t-1)},\mathbb{Z}_{q}),qt+q-1\}

and label the edges of
K_{n}
arbitrarily by elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.
Since
n\geq R(C_{q(t-1)},\mathbb{Z}_{q})
, there is a zero-sum cycle of length
q(t-1)
.
Since
n\geq qt+q-1,

the number of vertices outside this cycle is at least

n-q(t-1)\geq(qt+q-1)-q(t-1)=2q-1.

Because
q(t-1)\geq 2q-1
for every
t\geq 3
, by Lemma
2.2
, we obtain a zero-sum cycle of length
q(t-1)+q=qt
.
Thus, we have
R(C_{qt},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leq\max\{R(C_{q(t-1)},\mathbb{Z}_{q}),qt+q-1\}
for every
t\geq 3
.

Iterating this process from
t=3
up to
t=k
yields

R(C_{qk},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leq\max\{R(C_{2q},\mathbb{Z}_{q}),4q-1,\dots,qk+q-1\}=\max\{R(C_{2q},\mathbb{Z}_{q}),qk+q-1\},

which completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

We denote by
\operatorname{ex}(n,F)
the
Turán number
, given by

\operatorname{ex}(n,F)=\max\{|E(G)|\colon|V(G)|=n,\,G\text{ contains no copies of }F\}.

The key ingredients for our proof of Theorem
1.2
are the following two theorems.

Theorem 2.3

(Pikhurko
[
19
]
)
.

For every integer
r\geq 2
and every integer

,

\operatorname{ex}(n,C_{2r})\leq(r-1)n^{1+1/r}+16(r-1)n.

Theorem 2.4

(Katz, Lian, Malekshahian, and Shapiro
[
17
]
)

Let
\Delta
be a positive integer.
Then there exists a constant
C=C(\Delta)
such that for any graph
G
with maximum degree
\Delta

\Gamma_{0}
such that
|\Gamma_{0}|

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

It suffices to prove the two bounds

R(C_{2q},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leq D_{0}q\qquad\text{and}\qquad R(C_{2q},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leq 35q^{2}

for every
q\geq 3
.

To see that
R(C_{2q},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leq D_{0}q,
let
G=C_{2q}
and
\Gamma_{0}=\mathbb{Z}_{q}.

Then, by Theorem
2.4
, there exists a constant
C=C(2)
such that
R(C_{2q},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leq 2Cq.

Setting
D_{0}=\lceil 2C\rceil,
we obtain
R(C_{2q},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leq D_{0}q
for every
q\geq 3.

It remains to prove that
R(C_{2q},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leq 35q^{2}
for every
q\geq 3.

Let
q\geq 3
and
N=35q^{2}
.
Consider an arbitrary labeling
w\colon E(K_{N})\to\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.
Regard the labels as
q

By the Pigeonhole Principle, there exists a color containing at least
\frac{1}{q}\binom{N}{2}
edges.
If this color contains a
C_{2q}
, then that cycle is zero-sum, since all its edges have the same label
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
and

2q\cdot a=0
in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.
Thus it is enough to prove that
\operatorname{ex}(N,C_{2q})<\binom{N}{2}/q
.
By Theorem
2.3
, it suffices to show that

(q-1)N^{1+1/q}+16(q-1)N<\frac{1}{q}\binom{N}{2}.

Dividing both sides by
(q-1)N
, this becomes

N^{1/q}<\frac{N-1}{2q(q-1)}-16.

Substituting
N=35q^{2}
, the right-hand side equals

F_{q}\coloneqq\frac{35q^{2}-1}{2q(q-1)}-16=\frac{3}{2}+\frac{35}{2(q-1)}-\frac{1}{2q(q-1)}.

One can verify that
(35q^{2})^{1/q}<F_{q}
holds for all
q\geq 3
(see Appendix A for details).
Hence
R(C_{2q},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leq 35q^{2}
for every
q\geq 3
.
Combining this with
R(C_{2q},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leq D_{0}q
gives

R(C_{2q},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\leq\min\{35q^{2},D_{0}q\}
.
The stated bound for
R(C_{qk},\mathbb{Z}_{q})
now follows from Theorem
1.1
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
∎

Lower Bounds: Proof of Theorem
1.3

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
q\geq 2
,
k\geq 1
, and let
d\mid q
.
Define

h=\begin{cases}d,&d\text{ is odd},\\
d/2,&d\text{ is even}.\end{cases}

If
h\geq 2
, then

R(C_{qk},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\geq qk+h-1.

Proof.

Let
m=qk
.
We construct a
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
-labeling of the edges of
K_{m+h-2}
that contains no zero-sum
C_{m}
.
Partition the vertex set as
V=A\cup B
with
|A|=h-1
and
|B|=m-1
.
Assign the edges weights
w(e)
as follows:

w(e)=\begin{cases}q/d,&\text{if }e\text{ crosses the cut }(A,B),\\
0,&\text{if }e\text{ lies entirely within }A\text{ or }B.\end{cases}

Note that the element
q/d\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
has additive order
d
.

Let
C
be an arbitrary
m
-cycle.
Since
|B|=m-1<m
, the cycle must use at least one vertex of
A
.
Similarly, since
|A|=h-1<m
, it also uses at least one vertex of
B
.
Therefore,
C
must cross the cut
(A,B)
a positive even number of times, say
2s
.
Hence, the total weight of
C
is

w(C)=2s\cdot\frac{q}{d}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{Z}_{q}.

Suppose that
C
uses
t
vertices of
A
, then each connected component of
C\cap A
contributes two crossings, and hence
1\leq s\leq t\leq|A|=h-1
.
If
C
is a zero-sum cycle in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, then
d\mid 2s
.
We now consider the parity of
d
.
If
d
is odd, then
d\mid 2s
implies
d\mid s
.
However, we know that
1\leq s\leq h-1=d-1
, which yields a contradiction.
If
d
is even, by the assumption that
d=2h
,

d\mid 2s
is equivalent to
h\mid s
, which again is a contradiction to
1\leq s\leq h-1
.
Thus, the graph contains no zero-sum
m
-cycle, which implies that
R(C_{qk},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\geq m+h-1=qk+h-1
.
∎

Now, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

If
q
is odd, we apply Proposition
3.1
with
d=q
.
Then,
h=q
, and thus

R(C_{qk},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\geq qk+q-1.

If
q
is even and
q\geq 4
, we apply Proposition
3.1
again with
d=q
.
Then,
h=\frac{q}{2}
, and thus

R(C_{qk},\mathbb{Z}_{q})\geq qk+\frac{q}{2}-1,

completing the proof of the lower bounds.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.6

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.6
. We assume throughout that all labels are taken from the field
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, where
p
is an odd prime, and that
s\geq 1
is a fixed integer satisfying the condition

s+2\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

(1)

For
k\geq 1
, recall that
W_{pk}(s)=C_{pk}+\overline{K}_{s}
.
The
pk

rim vertices
, while the
s
vertices of
\overline{K}_{s}
are called
cone vertices
.
In
W_{pk}(s)
, every rim vertex has degree
s+2
, every cone vertex has degree
pk
, and the total number of edges is
|E(W_{pk}(s))|=(s+1)pk
.

As usual, we will use the sum-set notation:

A_{1}+\cdots+A_{n}=\{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}\ |\ (a_{1},...,a_{n})\in A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{n}\}.

We shall use the following standard form of the Cauchy-Davenport theorem.

Theorem 4.1

(Cauchy-Davenport
[
12
,
15
]
)
.

If
A,B

, every rim vertex has degree
3
, the center has degree
n\equiv 0\pmod{3}
, and
|E(W_{n})|=2n\equiv 0\pmod{3}
.
Hence every copy of
W_{n}
has total weight zero, which is a contradiction to (
14
).
Therefore, there must exist a singular pair.

Let
xy
be a singular pair, let
z
be its exceptional vertex, and put
A=V\setminus\{x,y,z\}
.
Then
|A|=n-2\geq 4
.
By the definition of a singular pair, there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
with
a\neq b
, such that

w(xu)-w(yu)=a\quad(\text{for all }u\in A),\qquad w(xz)-w(yz)=b.

(15)

Let
\delta=a-b\neq 0
and
\ell_{u}=w(yu)
for
u\in A
. For
h\in A
, define

D_{h}=\sum_{v\in V\setminus\{h\}}w(hv).

Take distinct
u,h\in A
, and let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{s}
be an ordering of
A\setminus\{u,h\}
, where
s=n-4
. Consider the wheel with center
h
and rim

xzuyv_{1}\cdots v_{s}x.

Interchanging
z
and
u
changes the wheel’s weight by
a-b=\delta
.
Since both resulting wheels have non-zero sums, the original wheel must have weight
\delta
.
Therefore, we obtain that

w(xz)+w(zu)+w(uy)+w(yv_{1})+\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}w(v_{i}v_{i+1})+w(v_{s}x)+D_{h}=\delta.

(16)

We define

F(u)=w(zu)+\ell_{u}\quad\text{and}\quad H(v_{1},\ldots,v_{s})=\ell_{v_{1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}w(v_{i}v_{i+1})+\ell_{v_{s}}.

(17)

Substituting the above equalities into (
16
), and by (
15
), there is a constant
C_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
independent of
u,h,v_{1},\ldots,v_{s}
such that

F(u)+D_{h}+H(v_{1},\ldots,v_{s})=C_{0}.

(18)

Interchanging the roles of
u
and
h
in (
18
) yields
D_{h}-F(h)=D_{u}-F(u)
.
Thus, we may assume that there is a constant
c_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
such that
D_{i}-F(i)=c_{1}
for every
i\in A
.
Therefore, (
18
) implies that there is a constant
C_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
such that

H(A\setminus\{i,j\})+F(i)+F(j)=C_{1}

(19)

for every pair
i,j\in A
, and the value of
H
is independent of the ordering of its arguments.

First, assume that
k\geq 3
.
Then
|A|\geq 7
.
Since
|A|\geq 7
, for any distinct
r,p,t\in A
we may choose
i,j\in A\setminus\{r,p,t\}
. Applying (
19
) to the same pair
i,j
and to two orderings of
A\setminus\{i,j\}
beginning with
r,p,t,\ldots
and
p,r,t,\ldots
, respectively, using (
17
), we have

\ell_{r}+w(rp)+w(pt)=\ell_{p}+w(pr)+w(rt),

and hence, for a fixed
t
, the value
w(rt)-\ell_{r}
is independent of
r\neq t
.
By symmetry,
w(rt)-\ell_{t}
is independent of
t\neq r
.
Since
r
and
t
are arbitrarily chosen from
A
, there is a constant
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
such that
w(ij)=\ell_{i}+\ell_{j}+c
with
i,j\in A
and
i\neq j
.
Substituting this into (
19
) gives

2\sum_{v\in A\setminus\{i,j\}}\ell_{v}+c(s-1)+w(zi)+\ell_{i}+w(zj)+\ell_{j}=C_{1},

which implies that

2\sum_{v\in A}\ell_{v}+c(s-1)+\bigl(w(zi)-\ell_{i}\bigr)+\bigl(w(zj)-\ell_{j}\bigr)=C_{1}.

Thus, there exists a constant
C_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, independent of the pair
i,j
, such that

\bigl(w(zi)-\ell_{i}\bigr)+\bigl(w(zj)-\ell_{j}\bigr)=C_{2}\qquad(\text{for all }i\neq j\in A),

and therefore, for some constant
g\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, we have

w(zi)=\ell_{i}+g\qquad(\text{for all }i\in A).

Now assume that
k=2
.
Then,
|A|=4
.
Equation (
19
) states that, for every partition
A=\{r,s\}\cup\{u,h\}
, there exists a constant
C_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, independent of the partition, such that
w(rs)+w(zu)+w(zh)=C_{3}
.
Let
m_{u}=w(zu)
, this gives
w(rs)=m_{r}+m_{s}+c^{\prime}
for some constant
c^{\prime}
.
For
u,v\in A
, compare the five differences from
u
and
v
to the other vertices.
By (
15
), the two differences to
x,y
are
\ell_{u}-\ell_{v}
, while the three differences to
z
and to the other two vertices of
A
are
m_{u}-m_{v}
.
Since at least four of the five differences are equal,
m_{u}-m_{v}=\ell_{u}-\ell_{v}
.
Hence, for suitable constants
c,g\in\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, for all
u\in A
,
m_{u}=\ell_{u}+g
and again

w(uv)=\ell_{u}+\ell_{v}+c,\qquad w(zu)=\ell_{u}+g\qquad(\text{for all }u,v\in A,\ u\neq v)

In all cases, for
u,v\in A
,
u\neq v
, we have

w(yu)=\ell_{u},\qquad w(xu)=\ell_{u}+a,\qquad w(zu)=\ell_{u}+g,\qquad w(uv)=\ell_{u}+\ell_{v}+c.

(20)

Define a weight
q_{0}(uv)=\lambda_{u}+\lambda_{v}+c
by

\lambda_{u}=\ell_{u}\ (\text{for all }u\in A),\qquad\lambda_{y}=-c,\qquad\lambda_{x}=a-c,\qquad\lambda_{z}=g-c.

Then,
q_{0}
agrees with
w
on every edge incident with at least one vertex of
A
. Replacing
w
with
w-q_{0}
preserves the weight of every
W_{n}
, because every vertex degree in
W_{n}
is divisible by
3
and
|E(W_{n})|=2n\equiv 0\pmod{3}
.
Hence, we may assume that all edges incident with
A
have weight
0
.
Only the three edges
xy,xz,yz
may be non-zero.

We choose a center
h\in A
.
Since
|A\setminus\{h\}|=n-3\geq 3
, we can order the rim vertices so that
x,y,z
are pairwise non-adjacent.
For instance, we may choose
xa_{1}ya_{2}za_{3}\cdots x

with
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in A\setminus\{h\}
distinct and all remaining vertices of
A\setminus\{h,a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}
inserted in the final path.
This wheel uses no edge among
x,y,z
, and all its other edges are incident with
A
.
Hence, its total weight is zero, contradicting the assumption (
14
).

Therefore, every labeling of
K_{3k+1}
contains a zero-sum
W_{3k}
, and

R(W_{3k},\mathbb{Z}_{3})=3k+1,

which completes the proof of Theorem
1.8
.
∎

Appendix A: Justification of
(35q^{2})^{1/q}<F_{q}
for
q\geq 3

Recall that
F_{q}=\frac{3}{2}+\frac{35}{2(q-1)}-\frac{1}{2q(q-1)}
.
For
3\leq q\leq 6
, the following elementary bounds suffice:

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}q&(35q^{2})^{1/q}\text{ is less than}&F_{q}\text{ is greater than}\\
\hline\cr 3&7&10\\
4&5&7\\
5&4&5\\
6&4&4\end{array}

For example, the left inequalities follow from

315<7^{3}
,
560<5^{4}
,
875<4^{5}
, and
1260<4^{6}
.

For
7\leq q\leq 12
, we have
35q^{2}<3^{q}
since it holds at
q=7
and the ratio
3^{q}/q^{2}
is increasing for
q\geq 2
.
Thus,
(35q^{2})^{1/q}<3
.
Additionally, we have
F_{q}>3

for
7\leq q\leq 12
.
Hence, the desired inequality holds in this range.

For
13\leq q\leq 35
, we have
35q^{2}<2^{q}
, since it holds at
q=13
and the ratio
2^{q}/q^{2}
is increasing for
q\geq 4
.
Thus,
(35q^{2})^{1/q}<2
.
Moreover
F_{q}>2
for
q\leq 35
, since
F_{q}>2
is equivalent to
36-\frac{1}{q}>q
, which is true for
13\leq q\leq 35
.

Finally, for
q\geq 36
we have

35q^{2}<\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{q}.

Indeed, this holds at
q=36
, since

\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{36}=\left(\frac{9}{4}\right)^{18}>2^{18}>35\cdot 36^{2},

and the ratio
(3/2)^{q}/q^{2}
is increasing for
q\geq 5
.
Thus,
(35q^{2})^{1/q}<3/2
. Since
F_{q}>3/2
for every
q\geq 3
, the desired inequality follows.
∎
