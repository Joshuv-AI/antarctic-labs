How Contrastive Decoding Enhances Large Audio Language Models?
Tzu-Quan, Wei-Ping, Yi-Cheng, Hung-yi

Abstract
While Contrastive Decoding (CD) has proven effective at enhancing Large Audio Language Models (LALMs), the underlying mechanisms driving its success and the comparative efficacy of different strategies remain unclear. This study systematically evaluates four distinct CD strategies across diverse LALM architectures. We identify Audio-Aware Decoding and Audio Contrastive Decoding as the most effective methods. However, their impact varies significantly by model. To explain this variability, we introduce a Transition Matrix framework to map error pattern shifts during inference. Our analysis demonstrates that CD reliably rectifies errors in which models falsely claim an absence of audio or resort to uncertainty-driven guessing. Conversely, it fails to correct flawed reasoning or confident misassertions. Ultimately, these findings provide a clear guideline for determining which LALM architectures are most suitable for CD enhancement based on their baseline error profiles. Code and additional analysis results are provided in our repository: https://github.com/nervjack2/LALM-Contrastive-Decoding-Error-Profiles .

2.2
Contrastive Decoding Methods

Contrastive decoding methods enhance model generation by amplifying the difference between an expert distribution and a flawed amateur distribution
[
21
]
. In the context of Large Audio-Language Models (LALMs), given audio input
\mathbf{a}
, text instruction
\mathbf{p}
, and previous decoded token
\mathbf{x}_{1},..,\mathbf{x}_{t}
, we denote the standard LALM logits as the expert
z_{t+1}=\text{LALM}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{x_{\leq t}})
. We subtract the amateur logits
\hat{z}_{t+1}
from the expert logits. The final manipulated logits
\tilde{z}_{t+1}
for generating the next token
x_{t+1}
are computed as:

\tilde{z}_{t+1}=\alpha\cdot z_{t+1}-\beta\cdot\hat{z}_{t+1}

(1)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are hyperparameters controlling the strength of the contrast.
We investigate four distinct strategies to construct the amateur logits
\hat{z}_{t+1}
.

Audio-Aware Decoding (AAD).

Originally proposed by Hsu et al.
[
14
]
to mitigate object hallucinations in LALMs, AAD operates on the premise that hallucinations often stem from the language model’s prior rather than the audio content. As shown in the left panel of Figure
1
, AAD constructs the amateur by removing the audio modality (setting
\mathbf{a}=\emptyset
), forcing the model to rely solely on the text prompt.

\hat{z}_{t+1}=\text{LALM}(\emptyset,\mathbf{p},\mathbf{x_{\leq t}})

(2)

By contrasting this text-only prior with the full multimodal output, AAD highlights audio-grounded information.

Audio Contrastive Decoding (ACD).

Originally proposed as Visual Contrastive Decoding (VCD)
[
20
]
to mitigate visual hallucinations, this method posits that statistical biases are robust to input noise while correct perception is sensitive to it. As shown in the left panel of Figure
1
, we extend this principle to the audio domain (ACD) by contrasting the expert output with an amateur distribution derived from distorted audio inputs
\mathbf{a}_{dist}
. Specifically, the distorted audio is generated by injecting Gaussian noise into the original signal.

\hat{z}_{t+1}=\text{LALM}(\mathbf{a}_{dist},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{x_{\leq t}})

(3)

This method penalizes tokens that remain unchanged despite significant degradation in the audio signal.

Audio Minimal Test-Time Intervention (AMTI).

Derived from the MTI framework
[
31
]
for LLM reasoning, AMTI applies intervention only when necessary, achieving better efficiency.
It monitors the entropy
\mathbf{H_{t+1}}
of the expert distribution. Contrastive decoding is triggered only when the model is uncertain (i.e.,
\mathbf{H_{t+1}}>\tau
).
Notably, the amateur logits are computed by reusing the KV-cache to ensure efficiency.
As shown in the middle panel of Figure
1
, we adapt the negative prompt concept from MTI by using an instruction
\mathbf{p}^{-}
(e.g., ”Ignore Audio”) to induce an amateur that disregards acoustic evidence.

\hat{z}_{t+1}=\begin{cases}\text{LALM}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{x_{\leq t}},\mathbf{p}^{-})&\text{if }\mathbf{H_{t+1}}>\tau\\
\phi&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(4)

This selective application improves reasoning efficiency by focusing computational resources on high-uncertainty tokens.

Decoding by Contrasting Layers (DoLa).

Originally proposed to enhance LLM factuality
[
6
]
, DoLa exploits the localization of factual knowledge in later transformer layers. Unlike static amateurs, DoLa dynamically selects an intermediate layer to serve as the amateur. Specifically, we obtain the amateur logits
\hat{z}^{k}_{t+1}
from the
k
-th layer by projecting its hidden state
\mathbf{h}^{k}_{t+1}
into the vocabulary space using the pre-trained language modeling head
\mathbf{W}_{\text{head}}
of the final layer:

\hat{z}^{k}_{t+1}=\mathbf{W}_{\text{head}}\cdot\text{Norm}(\mathbf{h}^{k}_{t+1})

(5)

where
\text{Norm}(\cdot)
corresponds to the final layer normalization. We then determine the optimal amateur layer
k\in\mathcal{K}
at each step by maximizing the Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) between the expert and candidate distributions:

k=\operatorname*{argmax}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\text{JSD}(z_{t+1}||\hat{z}^{k}_{t+1}),\quad\hat{z}_{t+1}=\hat{z}^{k}_{t+1}

(6)

where
\mathcal{K}=\{N/2,N/2+2,\dots,N-1\}
is the candidate set for an
N
-layer model.

This amplifies information fully processed by deep layer while suppressing superficial patterns found in early layers. The whole process is shown in the right panel of Figure

Analysis Methods

Evaluating the internal processing of contrastive decoding is inherently challenging, as standard accuracy metrics quantify performance but fail to elucidate the underlying mechanisms behind error correction. To identify which failure modes, such as audio blindness or flawed reasoning, are more easily corrected into accurate responses, we introduce an analysis framework utilizing Transition Matrices derived from LLM-as-a-Judge evaluations.

3.1
Categorization of Response States

To analyze the impact of contrastive decoding on model behavior, we categorize responses into distinct states based on error type. We enforce a strict priority order (
1\rightarrow 4
) to ensure mutually exclusive classification:

1.

Hallucinated No Audio (
W_{NoAudio}
): The model falsely claims no audio was provided or asks to play the sound. Crucially, this excludes cases where the model acknowledges the audio but finds it ”unclear”.

2.

Reasoning but Wrong (
W_{Reason}
): The model provides specific evidence to support a wrong answer. This state strictly excludes ”circular reasoning” (e.g., ”It is A, so the answer is A”) or ”simple intuition” (e.g., ”It sounds like A”).

3.

Direct Assertive Wrong (
W_{Direct}
): The model asserts a wrong answer without specific evidence. This includes short assertions, circular reasoning, and simple intuition.

4.

Guessing or Refusal (
W_{Guess}
): The model explicitly states it is ”not sure,” ”guessing,” or refuses to answer. This also includes cases where the model attempts to reason but concludes by refusing to answer due to insufficient information.

5.

Correct: This state encompasses all responses where the model successfully answers the question.

Ultimately, this 1-to-4 priority order ensures a collectively exhaustive classification of all incorrect responses. It divides errors into four logical buckets: (1) unperceived audio, (2) perceived audio with a reasoned incorrect assertion, (3) perceived audio with an unreasoned incorrect assertion, and (4) perceived audio with an inability to provide a definitive answer.

3.2
Automated Evaluation via LLM-as-a-Judge

Manually classifying thousands of responses is infeasible. We employ gpt-4o-2024-11-20 with greedy decoding as an automated judge
[
3
,
2
]
.
The judge is provided with the LALM’s response, the question, the correctness status (Correct/Wrong), and strict definitions for the states described above.

The judge selects the single most suitable category based on the correctness status and specific linguistic markers. For instance, it distinguishes between
W_{Reason}
and
W_{Guess}
by checking for the presence of logical justification versus simple expressions of uncertainty.

3.3
The Transition Matrix Framework

The core of our analysis is the Transition Matrix. This tool visualizes how the application of a contrastive decoding method changes the distribution of response states compared to the baseline greedy decoding.

We define a matrix
M
where the rows (
i
) represent the state under greedy decoding and the columns (
j
) represent the state under Contrastive Decoding (e.g., AAD). Let
N
be the total number of samples. The value of cell
M_{i,j}
represents the percentage of samples that start in state
i
and end in state
j
:

M_{i,j}=\frac{100}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}(S_{base}^{(k)}=i\text{ AND }S_{contrast}^{(k)}=j),

(7)

where
S_{base}^{(k)}
and
S_{contrast}^{(k)}
denote the response states under greedy decoding and contrastive decoding for the
k
-th sample, respectively. This framework allows us to identify the mechanism by which a method improves or degrades performance.

Experimental Setup

4.1
Benchmarks

We utilize three benchmarks ranging from perception to reasoning: SAKURA (Single Hop)
[
30
]
evaluates fundamental perceptual capability across the Animal, Emotion, Gender, and Language tracks. MMAU
[
25
]
tests advanced cognitive abilities requiring complex information extraction and reasoning, while MMAR
[
23
]
challenges deep reasoning in mixed-modality scenarios (e.g., speech overlapping with music). For evaluation, the LALM generates a response for each sample. This response, alongside the original question and ground truth, is fed to gpt-4o-2024-11-20 to determine binary correctness.

4.2
Implementation Details

We implemented contrastive decoding methods on Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, DeSTA2.5-Audio, and Audio Flamingo 3 by adhering closely to their original frameworks.
The hyperparameters
\alpha
and
\beta
for each method were empirically tuned to achieve optimal performance. Consequently, we set
\alpha=2.0
and
\beta=1.0
for AAD, ACD, and AMTI, while
\alpha=1.0
and
\beta=1.0
were used for DoLA.
For AMTI, the entropy threshold
\tau
was set to 1.0.
Adhering to the original configurations of VCD and DoLA, we incorporate the Adaptive Plausibility Constraint
[
21
]
into both ACD and our audio implementation of DoLA to filter out implausible tokens.

Findings

5.1
Performance

We find that AAD and ACD consistently provide the most significant improvements across tasks. This indicates that contrastive methods manipulating the audio input directly are superior for LALMs compared to strategies such as AMTI, which uses negative prompts, or DoLa, which contrasts internal layers. However, the effectiveness of AAD and ACD depends heavily on the model architecture. While Qwen2.5-Omni achieves substantial gains, DeSTA2.5-Audio and Audio Flamingo 3 show limited responsiveness. This disparity prompts our second question:

We investigate the underlying causes of this variability in the following section.

5.2
Transition Matrix Analysis

To address our second research question regarding what characteristics make an LALM susceptible to improvement via contrastive decoding, we analyze the shift in error patterns.
We visualize this using the Transition Matrix framework, focusing specifically on samples where the baseline greedy decoding produces wrong answers.
We intentionally exclude initially correct samples to highlight the error correction mechanism and prevent the predominant ”Correct
\rightarrow
Correct” transitions from dominating the visual scale.
Figure
2
displays these results for different models, averaged across all tasks and the two consistently effective methods, AAD and ACD. For clarity, we do not include the transition matrices for every individual task. We encourage readers to explore the complete, task-specific transition matrices provided in
our repository
.

Our analysis reveals distinct error profiles across architectures: Qwen2.5-Omni exhibits a highly distributed error pattern. While it suffers from all error types,
W_{NoAudio}
and
W_{Guess}
are the most frequently corrected states, transitioning to Correct at high rates. Conversely,
W_{Reason}
and
W_{Direct}
prove more resistant to correction. DeSTA2.5-Audio errors are heavily concentrated in the
W_{Reason}
state. Audio Flamingo 3 primarily fails through
W_{Direct}
assertions. Both
W_{Reason}
and
W_{Direct}
are difficult to rectify using contrastive decoding, explaining the limited responsiveness of these two models compared to Qwen.

Based on these observations, we conclude that AAD and ACD are highly effective at correcting
W_{NoAudio}
and
W_{Guess}
errors but struggle with
W_{Reason}
and
W_{Direct}
. Consequently, an LALM is most susceptible to improvement via contrastive decoding if its baseline error profile is characterized by high proportions of audio blindness or uncertainty-driven guessing.

6
Conclusion

This work presents a comprehensive evaluation of Contrastive Decoding strategies for enhancing Large Audio Language Models. Through a comparative analysis of four decoding methods across three diverse architectures, we determine that Audio-Aware Decoding and Audio Contrastive Decoding yield the most significant performance gains. However, our Transition Matrix analysis reveals that these improvements are not uniform across all error types. We find that Contrastive Decoding primarily serves as a corrective mechanism for audio blindness and uncertainty, making it highly effective for models like Qwen2.5-Omni that exhibit these specific failure modes. In contrast, models prone to flawed reasoning or confident misassertions, such as DeSTA2.5-Audio and Audio Flamingo 3, show limited responsiveness to these interventions. Our findings underscore the necessity of evaluating a model’s baseline error profile when deploying contrastive strategies. Future research should focus on mitigating reasoning errors, where current contrastive strategies fall short.
