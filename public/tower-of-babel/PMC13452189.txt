Implementation of a Multidisciplinary Eye Clinic within a Cancer Center for Patients at Risk of Ocular Graft versus Host Disease
Authors: Reshmi Talwar, Jihyun J Min, Desai Oula, Alex Wolfson, Katie Lowe, Cassidy Beck, Cristina Pontaza, Aaron P Rapoport, Katharine Toth, Susan Hodges, Ariel Fromowitz, Jean Yared, Nancy M Hardy, Larry Magder, Bennie H Jeng, Sarah B Sunshine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOcular graft-versus-host disease (oGVHD) affects approximately 50% of patients undergoing hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). Guidelines for monitoring patients for oGVHD development are not clearly established. An oGVHD clinic at the University of Maryland Greenebaum Comprehensive Cancer Center (UMGCCC) was established to evaluate HSCT patients at risk of developing oGVHD. The objective of this study was to examine whether an integrated eye clinic corresponded with lower odds of developing oGVHD within 2 years post-HSCT.DesignA single-center retrospective longitudinal cohort study.ParticipantsOne hundred eighty-three patients post-HSCT were included and compared patients treated before (n = 107) and after (n = 76) the UMGCCC Clinic opening in 2021.MethodsOcular graft-versus-host disease odds within 2 years post-HSCT were compared between pre-UMGCCC and post-UMGCCC groups using multivariate logistic regression. A secondary analysis evaluated ophthalmic treatments initiated at the first post-transplant visit and their associations with oGVHD incidence. Bayesian logistic regression estimated posterior probability.Main Outcome MeasuresWe assessed the odds of oGVHD 2 years post-HSCT in pre-UMGCCC and post-UMGCCC groups.ResultsThe incidence of oGVHD was 19.6% pre-UMGCCC and 11.8% post-UMGCCC, corresponding to a nominal 45% lower odds of oGVHD in the post-UMGCCC cohort (odds ratio [OR]= 0.55, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.24–1.28, P = 0.16). After controlling for sex, conditioning regimen, graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis, and systemic GVHD, the post-UMGCCC cohort demonstrated 39% lower odds of oGVHD (OR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.25–1.49, P = 0.28). Mean corneal and conjunctival staining were lower in the post-UMGCCC versus pre-UMGCCC cohort (0.43 vs. 0.54; 95% CI: 0.12‑0.34; P = 0.36 and 0.37 vs. 0.42; 95% CI: 0.14‑0.26; P = 0.57, respectively). Bayesian logistic regression showed 86.5% posterior probability of reduced oGVHD odds. A higher proportion of post-UMGCCC patients received erythromycin, warm compresses, and eyelid scrubs (P < 0.0001). Eyelid hygiene (warm compresses and eyelid scrubs) was associated with a significant decrease in oGVHD odds (OR: 0.13, 95% CI: 0.03–0.57, P = 0.01).ConclusionsThe opening of the UMGCCC Eye Clinic was associated with a nonsignificant reduction in oGVHD odds, corneal, and conjunctival staining in patients 2 years after HSCT. Our results support the importance of integrated eye care in reducing morbidity in patients undergoing HSCT.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Ocular graft-versus-host disease, Stem cell transplant, Eye clinic, Eyelid hygiene

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the only definitive therapy for many hematologic, autoimmune, and hereditary disorders.1 Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) is the leading cause of morbidity after HSCT and occurs when the donor immune system attacks the host organs including the eyes, the skin, and the gut.2 Ocular GVHD (oGVHD) is an inflammatory dry eye disease and is a major cause of morbidity associated with systemic GVHD, which affects 65% of patients who undergo HSCT.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 The incidence of oGVHD is highest within the first 2 years post-transplant, occurring in approximately 30% of patients who undergo HSCT.8,9 Ocular GVHD symptoms range from severe inflammatory dry eye and vision loss to chronic pain, corneal epithelial breakdown, fibrosis, and, in severe cases, corneal perforation.5, 6, 7,10,11 Ocular GVHD is challenging to treat, as the inflammatory response is intense, not directly correlated with systemic disease activity, and results in lifelong devastating outcomes for their vision, quality of life, and risk of ocular infection.7 These lifelong consequences are a result of fibrosis of the ocular surface and eyelid margin and result in severe dry eye disease.7

However, what is less well studied is the optimal approach to monitoring patients post-HSCT for ocular complications to identify patients with oGvHD earlier. The 2020 National Institutes of Health Chronic GVHD Consensus Project identified the need for further research specifically focused on early identification of patients with oGVHD and recommended a pretransplant eye examination and a subsequent eye examination between 3 and 12 months post-HSCT.7,8,12, 13, 14 The goal of these early scheduled eye examinations is to identify patients at high risk for developing oGVHD, as the critical changes from oGVHD occur earlier in the disease than patients’ symptoms. Once the symptoms are present, it is often too late to reverse the devastating and long-term consequences; however, these evaluations are not yet considered standard of care. Currently, there are no official recommendations for standard eye examinations for patients with HSCT. Pretransplant and 3-month post-transplant eye examinations are not always covered by health insurance companies, often resulting in reduced patient follow-up. Therefore, further evidence is necessary to support the need for a comprehensive approach to eye examinations for patients undergoing HSCT.

The University of Maryland opened an eye clinic dedicated to oGVHD integrated within the University of Maryland Greenebaum Comprehensive Cancer Center (UMGCCC). This multidisciplinary clinic opened in January 2021 and resulted in: (1) ease of access for patients; (2) increased awareness by the bone marrow transplant service of oGVHD; (3) increased patient education for ocular complications from HSCT; and (4) more aggressive treatment of preclinical signs of oGVHD. Furthermore, the localization of the eye clinic within the UMGCCC resulted in close collaboration between ophthalmology and oncology. The integrated eye clinic has 2 full eye lanes, including a slit lamp camera to document ocular surface changes. Further required imaging studies are performed at the standard University of Maryland eye clinic. The goal of our study is to evaluate whether the close collaboration between ophthalmology and oncology through an integrated ophthalmology clinic within the cancer center is linked to a reduced incidence of oGVHD 2 years post-HSCT.

Methods

Study Population and Exposure

A single-center retrospective longitudinal cohort study was performed at the University of Maryland School of Medicine. Patients included under a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board provided informed consent, and the study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients who underwent an allogenic HSCT at the University of Maryland Medical Center from 2017 through 2022 were included. In 2021, the UMGCCC Eye Clinic was opened. Patients were categorized into 2 groups: those in the unexposed group, who were evaluated by an ophthalmologist from 2017 through 2020 (pre-UMGCCC Eye Clinic), and those in the exposed group, who were evaluated by an ophthalmologist from 2021 through 2022 (post-UMGCCC Eye Clinic). The goal of the UMGCCC Eye Clinic is to evaluate whether patients have reduced ocular complications from cancer therapy, specifically by increasing the availability and convenience of regular eye examinations after having undergone HSCT. The outcome is measured as the presence of oGVHD within 2 years post-HSCT to account for the fact that oGVHD incidence is highest within 2 years post-HSCT.

The covariates of interest include biological sex, age, race, conditioning regimen, GVHD prophylaxis, and presence of systemic GVHD diagnosis. The conditioning regimen distinguishes between myeloablative and reduced intensity conditioning. Myeloablative conditioning ablates the host marrow and immune system, whereas reduced intensity conditioning yields less complete myeloablation/immunoablation and results in a higher risk of developing GVHD.12 The GVHD prophylaxis regimen stratifies patients according to receipt of post-transplant cyclophosphamide, antithymocyte globulin (ATG), or no ATG. Post-transplant cyclophosphamide is highly effective in preventing rejection and GVHD, even permitting HLA-mismatched allografting. Antithymocyte globulin depletes both donor and recipient T cells in vivo; compared to prophylaxis without ATG, ATG reduces the risk of GVHD but carries an increased risk of relapse.13 Systemic GVHD is also included as a covariate, as post-HSCT patients with systemic GVHD are at a higher risk for oGVHD.

Secondary Analysis of Medication Use

Ophthalmic medications and prescribed treatments were collected from patient charts. The frequency of specific therapies initiated at the first ophthalmic visit post-HSCT was collated, and medications that were prescribed to >10 patients were included in the analysis. The most frequently recommended ophthalmic treatments were analyzed in our patient cohort: artificial tears, topical ophthalmic steroids (e.g., loteprednol etabonate 0.5%, prednisolone acetate 1%, and fluorometholone 0.1%), topical erythromycin ointment 5 mg/g, warm compresses, and eyelid scrubs. The medications included in the analysis were prescribed at the initial eye examination post-HSCT. These medications were prescribed when the ophthalmologist noted early indications of ocular surface or eyelid inflammation (even without patient-reported symptoms) or as treatments for diagnosed oGVHD and meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD). The presence of an MGD diagnosis at the first post-transplant eye examination was also evaluated in both the pre-UMGCCC and post-UMGCCC cohorts.

UMGCCC Eye Clinic

The clinic is a dedicated eye clinic integrated into the UMGCCC ambulatory care center, which allows for easy and efficient collaboration between ophthalmology and oncology. Examination in the UMGCCC Eye Clinic includes vision, intraocular pressure, slit lamp examination, slit lamp photography of the ocular surface, fluorescein staining of the ocular surface, tear break-up time, Schirmer testing, and ocular surface disease index scoring. The ocular surface disease index score is a validated patient questionnaire for symptoms of dry eye disease.14 It is important to note that during the initiation of the clinic, specific challenges were identified (Table 1); however, many of these were progressively addressed as the clinic matured and workflows became more standardized among team members. (1) Scheduling: historically, eye visits were only able to be scheduled by ophthalmology, but opening of the UMGCCC clinic allowed both ophthalmology and oncology to schedule. (2) Early visits: oncology required the diagnosis code for billing at the time of patient scheduling. If the patient did not have a codable eye complaint, the early/baseline examination could not be scheduled. This was resolved after education of the schedulers and cancer center team. (3) Patient participation: patients are followed closely by oncology for the first year after HSCT, initially up to 3 times per week. If patients’ eye visits are scheduled on different days or off-site location from their oncology visits, their burden of care would significantly increase. Therefore, integration and coordination of the eye clinic within the cancer center minimizes the patient burden required for optimal care. Allowing the oncology team to directly schedule patient eye clinic visits permits coordinated appointments with oncology follow-ups, thus requiring fewer trips to the clinic for patients. (4) Collaboration and education between oncology and ophthalmology: ophthalmologist S.B.S. joined the bone marrow transplant clinical meetings and instituted educational and research collaborations with the goal of improving patient outcomes.

Ophthalmic Examinations for oGVHD

Examination in the UMGCCC Eye Clinic was performed as described in the “UMGCCC Eye Clinic” section. Before the implementation of the UMGCCC Eye Clinic, post-transplant eye evaluations were typically performed on an as-needed basis in response to patient-reported ocular symptoms. The UMGCCC Eye Clinic enabled standardized referrals from the patient’s oncologist for routine 100-day post-transplant ocular examinations. This meant that patients with or without ocular symptoms were evaluated, allowing earlier intervention in at-risk or subclinical oGVHD, prior to the onset of symptoms.

In the “post-UMGCCC” group, oGVHD diagnosis was based on the International Chronic Ocular Graft-versus-Host-Disease Consensus Group diagnostic criteria. Prior to the UMGCCC eye clinic, the standard diagnosis was established through a clinical eye examination, including slit lamp examination and corneal fluorescein staining, and observed conjunctival injection and/or scarring. Corneal and conjunctival staining were graded on a 0 to 4 severity scale, consistent with the scoring used in the UMGCCC Eye Clinic. The evaluating ophthalmologist for patients at risk for oGVHD differs pre-UMGCCC and post-UMGCCC Eye Clinic establishment. B.H.J. was the primary ophthalmologist pre-UMGCCC, while S.B.S. was the primary ophthalmologist post-UMGCCC. Both physicians have advanced training in cornea and external disease and specialized in treating ocular surface diseases and oGVHD, specifically. Other eye care providers evaluated these patients for specialty-specific (i.e., glaucoma or retina) or acute concerns, although management continued with S.B.S. or B.H.J. for oGVHD-related issues.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute) and R Core Team (2021, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Analysis was performed using parametric tests, including t tests, analyses of variance, and logistic regression. The Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel χ2 test was used to assess associations and the Breslow-Day test for homogeneity to evaluate potential interactions. Ocular GVHD incidence was analyzed using a logistic regression model, adjusting for sex, conditioning regimen, GVHD prophylaxis, and systemic GVHD. Power calculations and minimum detectable effect estimates were performed to assess the adequacy of the sample size. Bayesian logistic regression with weakly informative priors was applied to estimate posterior probabilities of reduced odds of oGVHD. All P values were 2-sided, except for the 1-sided posterior probabilities in the Bayesian model.

Results

A total of 183 patients were divided into 2 cohorts: the pre-UMGCCC Clinic (n = 107) and post-UMGCCC Clinic (n = 76). The demographic data and variables of interest in each cohort are summarized in Table 2. There was no significant difference in the age, race, sex, conditioning regimen, GVHD prophylaxis, or presence of systemic GVHD between the pre-UMGCCC and post-UMGCCC cohorts. A majority of the pre-UMGCCC patients were first evaluated by B.H.J. (63.5%), while S.B.S. evaluated 89.3% of post-UMGCCC patients. The average time from the date of HSCT to the date of the first eye examination averaged 171 ± 113.9 days (median: 148 days) for the pre-UMGCCC group and 191 ± 112.6 days (median: 151 days) for the post-UMGCCC group; there was no significant difference in the timing of the eye examinations post-transplant (P = 0.29). Eight of 21 (38.1%) pre-UMGCCC patients and 4/9 (44.4%) post-UMGCCC were diagnosed with oGVHD at the initial visit. Overall, there were 30 patients with oGVHD in the entire patient cohort. Prior to the UMGCCC Eye Clinic, 21/107 (19.6%) of patients developed oGVHD at 2 years post-HSCT, whereas 9/76 (11.8%) of patients transplanted after the Eye Clinic opened developed oGVHD at 2 years (P = 0.16) (Table 3). Thus, the odds of developing oGVHD within 2 years post-HSCT are 45% lower in the post-UMGCCC Clinic Group (odds ratio [OR] = 0.55, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.24‑1.28, P = 0.16). The power to detect the observed reduction in oGVHD (OR = 0.55, 45% reduction) at a significance level of α = 0.05 was 0.42 (β = 0.20). Based on the sample size and study design, the minimum detectable effect with 80% power at α = 0.05 was a 68% reduction in the odds of oGVHD (OR ≤0.32). Mean corneal and conjunctival staining were lower in the post-UMGCCC cohort than in the pre-UMGCCC cohort (corneal: 0.43 vs. 0.54; 95% CI: 0.12‑0.34; P = 0.36; conjunctival: 0.37 vs. 0.42; 95% CI: 0.14‑0.26; P = 0.57) (Table 4).

Therapeutic Intervention Analysis

A total of 152 patients received an eye examination within 2 years post-HSCT, divided into the pre-UMGCCC Clinic cohort (n = 96) and post-UMGCCC Clinic cohort (n = 56). The most prescribed medications were artificial tears, topical erythromycin ointment 5 mg/g, topical steroids (loteprednal etabonate 0.5%, prednisolone acetate 1%, and fluorometholone 0.1%), warm compress, and eyelid scrubs (Table 9). More patients were prescribed topical erythromycin (32.1% vs. 5.2%, P < 0.0001), warm compresses (76.8% vs. 26.0%, P < 0.0001), and eyelid scrubs (44.6% vs. 14.6%, P < 0.0001) in the post-UMGCCC cohort compared with the pre-UMGCCC cohort. There was no significant difference in the proportion of pre-UMGCCC and post-UMGCCC patients who were given artificial tears and topical steroids at their first eye examination post-HSCT (P = 0.81 and P = 0.48, respectively).

Compared with those not prescribed warm compresses or eyelid scrubs, patients prescribed warm compresses and eyelid scrubs demonstrated lower incidences of oGVHD (27.4% vs. 8.8%, P = 0.004 and 25.7% vs. 0%, P = 0.0004, respectively), as shown in Table 10. Discussion

Ocular GVHD remains a common complication after allogeneic HSCT, often leading to devastating consequences such as irreversible scarring and fibrosis.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 The UMGCCC Eye Clinic was opened in 2021, aiming to increase collaboration with oncology and allow patients to have an integrated, multidisciplinary experience with post-transplant follow-up. This study evaluates the impact of a dedicated ophthalmology clinic within an oncology ambulatory center on the odds of oGVHD within those critical 2 years post-HSCT.

Among both the pre-UMGCCC and post-UMGCCC Clinic groups, the incidence of oGVHD is lower than the published 2-year incidence of 29% to 32%.8 We believe that this is due, at least in part, to long-standing excellent care and close collaboration between the ophthalmologists and oncologists at the University of Maryland, which was enhanced further with the addition of the UMGCCC Eye Clinic. The integrated eye clinic model increased oncologists’ awareness of oGVHD, facilitating earlier recognition and referral after symptom onset.

The post-UMGCCC Clinic group demonstrated 45% lower estimated odds of developing oGVHD. The power being below the conventional 80% threshold shows that the sample size may have contributed to a nonsignificant result. Minimum detectable effect analysis indicated that, given the sample size, a reduction in the odds of developing oGVHD of ≥68% could have been reliably detected, which is greater than the observed 45% reduction. After adjusting for sex, conditioning regimen, GVHD prophylaxis, and the presence of systemic GVHD, based on the data and the literature, the odds of developing oGVHD were 39% lower post-UMGCCC. Although the observed odds did not reach statistical significance, the Bayesian analysis suggests an 86.5% probability that post-UMGCCC patients had lower odds of developing oGVHD. The power and minimum detectable effect calculations, together with the Bayesian posterior probability, provide complementary support that the lack of statistical significance is likely attributable to insufficient sample size rather than the absence of a clinically meaningful effect of the UMGCCC Eye Clinic. This observed association of lower oGVHD odds is further supported by a similar pattern of lower corneal and conjunctival staining after implementation of the eye clinic.

While it was initially hypothesized that there would also be a decrease in time-to-first eye examination in the pre-UMGCCC Eye Clinic patients, there was no statistically significant difference. Therefore, we sought to determine if there was a difference in therapeutic interventions in the UMGCCC Eye Clinic to understand the trend of decreased oGVHD odds. A secondary analysis examining treatments used at the initial visit revealed a significantly higher proportion of post-UMGCCC patients who were prescribed topical erythromycin, warm compresses, and eyelid scrubs—common MGD treatments—at the initial eye examination visit post-HSCT. The increased prescription of these medications in patients who did not initially present with an oGVHD diagnosis corroborates the clinic’s goal of early treatment of ocular surface disease signs or symptoms in post-transplant patients. The use of warm compresses and eyelid scrubs was associated with a decreased incidence of oGVHD regardless of an MGD diagnosis, indicating that the structured practice in this clinic could potentially lead to better outcomes for post-transplant patients. Because topical steroids are a standard treatment for oGVHD, their use was correlated with an expected increased incidence of oGVHD.

Meibomian gland dysfunction has been identified as an important new area of investigation in oGVHD. Previous studies have shown an increased prevalence of MGD in patients who undergo HSCT, even prior to their transplant, with one study finding approximately 85% of post-HSCT patients having MGD.15, 16, 17 Our results show that future prospective studies are warranted to evaluate the effectiveness of MGD treatments in preventing or delaying oGVHD development in high-risk post-transplant patients. We hypothesize that early treatment of MGD, and any other form of ocular surface inflammation in patients at risk for oGVHD, will prevent localization of immune effector cells to the ocular surface, therefore decreasing the incidence of oGVHD.

The strength of this study is the unique setting of the UMGCCC Eye Clinic within a cancer center, allowing access to a cohort of post-HSCT patients and the evaluation of oGVHD development in a single center tertiary care center. Close collaboration with oncology provided a comprehensive understanding of conditioning and GVHD prophylaxis regimens, enabling thorough consideration of these variables and their impact on oGVHD status in a multidisciplinary setting.

The primary limitation is the modest sample size of post-UMGCCC Eye Clinic patients due to its recent establishment in 2021 (n = 76). Another limitation is the implementation of the International Chronic Ocular Graft-versus-Host-Disease diagnostic criteria in the UMGCCC Eye Clinic, resulting in increased specificity for oGVHD diagnosis by inclusion of Schirmer tests and ocular surface disease index evaluation. This would lead to potential bias in the analysis through different diagnostic criteria used by the pre-UMGCCC and post-UMGCCC Eye Clinic physicians. However, the systematic approach with the International Chronic Ocular Graft-versus-Host-Disease diagnostic criteria is similar to that used in the pre-UMGCCC clinic; fluorescein staining and conjunctival injection, both markers with high sensitivity, were incorporated in the evaluation for both groups. These measures are validated screening markers for assessing corneal epithelial damage in oGVHD.7,18 Additionally, there may have been gradual, unmeasured changes in medical practices over time that could have independently lowered the risk of oGVHD; however, the guidelines for HSCT were unchanged throughout this period. Further prospective study on the benefits of an integrated eye clinic for post-transplant patients and continued longitudinal follow-up to evaluate these practice recommendations will further clarify potential limitations in this study.

Taken together, this study suggests that the implementation of the UMGCCC Eye Clinic was associated with a trend of reduced oGVHD odds, potentially mediated by early proactive treatment of ocular inflammation and MGD. The increased use of MGD therapies, such as warm compresses and eyelid scrubs, and their association with lower oGVHD incidence support the clinical importance of early intervention in post-HSCT patients to potentially improve long-term outcomes. Although the observed reduction in oGVHD odds was not statistically significant, we expect that a larger sample size and longer establishment of the UMGCCC Eye Clinic may better illuminate the benefits of integrated ophthalmic care on post-transplant morbidity, ocular health, and standardized post-HSCT evaluations that enable earlier intervention. This study highlights the value of integrated eye care and suggests the need to evaluate and potentially change the way that patients are followed and treated post-HSCT to reduce the devastating and long-term consequences of oGVHD, ultimately improving patients’ quality of life.
