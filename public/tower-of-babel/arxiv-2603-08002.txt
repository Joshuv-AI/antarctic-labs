Post-Hoc Large-Sample Statistical Inference
Ben Chugg, Etienne Gauthier, Michael I. Jordan, Aaditya Ramdas, Ian Waudby-Smith

Abstract
We derive inferential procedures for large sample sizes that remain valid under data-dependent significance levels (so-called “post-hoc valid inference”). Classical statistical tools require that the significance level—the “type-I error”—is selected prior to seeing or analyzing any data. This restriction leads to some drawbacks. For instance, if an analyst generates an inconclusive confidence interval, repeating the process with a larger significance level is not an option—the result is final. Recently, e-values have emerged as the solution to this problem, being both necessary and sufficient tools for performing various forms of post-hoc inference. All such results, however, have thus far been nonasymptotic. As a result, they inherit familiar limitations of nonasymptotic inferential procedures such as requiring strong moment assumptions and being conservative in general. This paper develops a theory of post-hoc inference in the asymptotic setting, yielding asymptotic post-hoc confidence sets and asymptotic post-hoc p-values that make weaker assumptions and are sharper than their nonasymptotic counterparts.



1
Introduction

The majority of applied statistical methods are asymptotic in the sense that their validity holds in the limit as number of samples tends to infinity. Asymptotic inference is widely deployed in practice because it typically requires weak moment assumptions. This is in contrast to nonasymptotic inference, which necessarily relies on knowledge of strong moment assumptions; see
Bahadur and Savage 1956
for a formal description of how nonasymptotic inference fails without those assumptions.

Despite its wide applicability, asymptotic inference as it is used in practice still suffers the following drawback:
the significance level—often denoted by
\alpha\in(0,1)
—must be set before any data analysis takes place. Take confidence intervals (CIs) for instance. Once such an interval is computed, recomputing it on the same data with a different significance parameter rids it of the statistical guarantees it would have had under data-independent significance values.

In this work we show how asymptotic e-values offer a solution—and as we will show, the only solution—to this problem. We provide a flexible framework for constructing asymptotic post-hoc confidence intervals, p-values, and hypothesis tests that allow
\alpha
to be chosen after seeing the data. The post-hoc potential of e-values has been investigated in nonasymptotic hypothesis testing
[
26
,
35
,
34
,
8
]
as well as other areas of nonasymptotic inference; see, e.g.,
Gauthier et al. 2025a
,
Gauthier et al. 2025b
,
Gauthier et al. 2026
for applications in conformal prediction. In this work we extend this potential to the asymptotic setting. We are motivated by the wide applicability of asymptotic procedures, and seek to provide an alternative that is just as flexible while also providing post-hoc guarantees.

To set the stage more formally, let us focus on confidence intervals. Suppose that data
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are drawn from some unknown distribution
P
and there is a real-valued parameter
\theta\equiv\theta(P)
that we wish to estimate. Given a significance level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, an asymptotic confidence interval for
\theta
is a random set
\mathcal{C}_{n}(\alpha)
which covers
\theta
with probability at most
\alpha
in the limit. That is:

\text{For all }\alpha\in(0,1),\quad\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(\theta\notin\mathcal{C}_{n}(\alpha))\leq\alpha.

(1.1)

Note that
\alpha
is assumed to be a constant, and it is for this reason that it must be chosen prior to doing any analysis. The following example illustrates how choosing
\alpha
prior to analysis can lead to data being wasted on inconclusive results.

In epidemiology and immunology, confidence intervals are often built for the
rate ratio
of a disease or a vaccine, the ratio of adverse cases to some comparator class within an appropriate time window
[
48
,
53
]
. The estimate of the rate ratio may suggest different actions; if too high for a vaccine then we might stop roll out, for example.
Suppose a practitioner produces the confidence interval
\mathcal{C}_{n}(0.01)
, designed to contain the true rate ratio with 99% probability.
If the interval is too wide for easy interpretation, it is tempting to compute
\mathcal{C}_{n}(0.05)
,
\mathcal{C}_{n}(0.1)
, and so on, in order to be able to provide some sort of public guidance.
Unfortunately, most current approaches to constructing confidence intervals do not allow for such data-driven decisions and recalculating
\mathcal{C}_{n}
in this way will result in violations of the guarantee in (
1.1
).

The inability of classical confidence intervals to handle post-hoc significance levels is well-known.
Selecting
\alpha
as a function of the data has been dubbed the “problem of roving alphas”
[
22
]
and has been observed to be a problem in various disciplines, including medicine, psychology, business, and marketing (see
29
,
10
,
30
,
23
).

Among analysts who are conscious of the problem of roving alphas, a common solution is “
\alpha
-spending”
[
14
]
, part of a broader class of what are often called
group sequential
methods. Covid-19 monitoring was carried out using
\alpha
spending, for instance
[
15
]
.
The idea is to fix some global
\alpha
and perform the initial analysis with some
\alpha_{1}<\alpha
. If needed, one does a second analysis with
\alpha_{2}<\alpha-\alpha_{1}
, then a third with
\alpha_{3}<\alpha-(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})
, and so on. (Here
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots
and indeed
\alpha
must be predetermined.) Budgeting this way has clear drawbacks, however. Each analysis has significantly lower power than it would if using the original
\alpha
, and we are still limited to only a handful preplanned of downstream analyses. Note that
\alpha
-spending can be viewed as a form of Bonferroni correction on a collection of independent statistical analyses.

In this work we investigate and extend an alternative approach to
\alpha
spending. The methodology to be presented allows for an arbitrary number of downstream analyses to be run—no partitioning of
\alpha
is needed. The main difference between this approach and classical approaches is that the kind of guarantee we seek differs from (
1.1
). Instead of bounding probability of error, we bound a particular
risk
(to be defined) for many values of
\alpha
simultaneously. This follows recent trends in post-hoc hypothesis testing
[
26
,
35
,
34
,
8
]
and conformal risk control
[
1
]
.

There are both benefits and drawbacks to considering risk control instead of error probability. Our goal here is not to advocate for either side, but instead to develop the theoretical foundations of post-hoc asymptotic inference based on risk control as a complementary tool in the statistical toolbox.

This paper will study asymptotics both in the pointwise setting and the distribution-uniform setting
[
37
]
, the latter requiring the asymptotics to be uniformly valid over a class of distributions. We will introduce distribution-uniformity more formally in Section
2.3
, but let us briefly make use of the guarantee in (
1.1
)
to discuss the concept informally. In a distribution-pointwise statistical guarantee, no matter how large
n
is, there may be
some
distribution
P_{n}
such that
P_{n}(\theta\notin\mathcal{C}_{n}(\alpha))
is still far away from
\alpha
(and in some cases, arbitrarily close to 1). In a distribution-uniform guarantee, by contrast, it is required that asymptotic statements hold simultaneously for all
P
in some class
\mathcal{P}
(mathematically, a supremum is taken over
\mathcal{P}
inside the the limit superior; see Definition
2.5
). Such uniformity prohibits the aforementioned anomalous behavior.

1.2
Related work

E-values, which are derived by analyzing expectations under the null instead of tail probabilities, provide an alternative approach to obtaining valid inference in repeated analyses; see
Ramdas and Wang 2025
and
Ramdas et al. 2023
for broad introductions, and
[
49
,
24
,
60
]
for discussion papers that elaborate on connections to betting. Our particular interest in e-values is their post-hoc validity, which as we will show allows the use of data-dependent error thresholds.

The link between e-values and data-dependent thresholds was forged by
Katsevich and Ramdas 2020
in the context of false discovery rate (FDR) control. These authors worked with nonnegative supermartingales; note that nonnegative supermartingales yield e-values at stopping times, a fact that follows from Doob’s optional stopping theorem. The formal connection between e-values and FDR control for data-dependent thresholds was presented by
Wang and Ramdas 2022
. Setting the number of hypotheses to one specializes multiple testing to just testing a single hypothesis, and reduces FDR to the standard Type-I error. As such their Lemma 1 provides the first proof of the post-hoc error control delivered by thresholding e-values.

Analogous results to those of FDR control were presented in the setting of confidence intervals by
[
65
]
. This work focused on controlling the false coverage rate (FCR) using
e-confidence intervals
.
1
1

1

Note that their results for the post-hoc validity of FCR also apply to the standard miscoverage rate if there is only one interval in question, and also that similar comments apply when FDR is when specialized to a single hypothesis, which yields classical Type-I error control.

In a similar vein,
Grünwald 2024
studied post-hoc hypothesis testing with the goal of generalizing the Neyman-Pearson paradigm to allow post-hoc significance levels. This work establishes in particular that e-CIs are post-hoc valid (as we will formally describe below).

Since then, a number of authors have noted that e-values catalyze a variety of kinds of data-adaptive decision-making.
For example,
Xu et al. 2025
and
Fischer and Sechidis 2025
employ e-values for post-selection inference and FDR control in multiple testing, allowing the nominal FDR level to be data-dependent. While these past works have emphasized that e-values yield “post-hoc p-values,”
Koning 2025
,
Koning 2024
proves the converse that every “post-hoc p-value” is the inverse of an e-value.

Grünwald 2023
develops the “e-posterior” which serves as a frequentist alternative to the Bayesian posterior using e-values while also allowing for post-hoc loss functions.

Gauthier et al. 2025b
consider post-hoc confidence intervals in the setting of conformal inference, developing adaptive coverage policies which strategically change the significance level to obtain conformal sets that are optimized under constraints on their size
[
20
,
18
]
.
Chugg et al. 2026
build on the research program of
Grünwald 2024
and investigate admissibility in post-hoc hypothesis testing.

For a general overview of the post-hoc power of e-values, see
Ramdas and Wang 2025
.

1.3
Notation and background

Fix a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
.
Throughout, we let
\mathcal{P}
denote a set of distributions over real-valued random variables so that
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
is a probability space for each
{P\in\mathcal{P}}
. For a random variable
X_{1}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
and some real
q\geq 1
, we let
\mathcal{M}_{q}(\theta)
be the set of distributions on
\mathbb{R}
with mean
\mathbb{E}_{P}[X_{1}]=\theta
and a finite
q
-th moment (i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{P}|X_{1}-\theta|^{q}<\infty
). Let
\mathcal{M}_{q}=\cup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{M}_{q}(\theta)
be the set of all distributions with finite
q
-th moment.

Recall that a sequence of random variables
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
are said to be uniformly integrable with respect to
\mathcal{P}
if

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[X_{n}\mathbf{1}\left\{X_{n}\geq K\right\}]=0.

(1.2)

Note that this is a pointwise (in
\mathcal{P}
) notion of uniform integrability. We will also make use of a distribution-uniform version. In particular,
we call
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}

\mathcal{P}
-uniformly integrable if

\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[X_{n}\mathbf{1}\left\{X_{n}\geq K\right\}]=0.

(1.3)

Also important in the distribution-uniform setting will be the notion of uniform moment control. We say that a random variable
X
has a
\mathcal{P}
-uniformly bounded
q
-th moment if

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}|X|^{q}<\infty.

(1.4)

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\sigma(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
be the
\sigma
-field generated by the first
n
observations.
We will often abbreviate a sequence
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
as
(A_{n})
, and shorthand the first
n
elements
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
as
A^{n}
. The index set of a sequence
(A_{n})
should always be assumed to be the natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
.

Upon observing
X^{n}\sim P
for some unknown
{P\in\mathcal{P}}
, we are interested in estimating
\theta=\theta(P)
for some real-valued functional
\theta:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}
(e.g., the mean
\theta(P)=\int X\textnormal{d}P
). Define the parameter space
\Theta=\{\theta(P):P\in\mathcal{P}\}
.

An e-variable for
\mathcal{P}
is a nonnegative random variable
E
such that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E]\leq 1
. An e-value is the realization of an e-variable, though in this work will use the two terms interchangeably (just as p-variables and p-values are typically used interchangeably). The majority of our results will rely on
asymptotic
e-values: sequences of nonnegative random variables which are an e-value in the limit. These will be introduced more formally in Section
2.2
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Post-hoc confidence intervals and p-values

Before introducing asymptotic post-hoc inference, let us first describe nonasymptotic post-hoc inference procedures to help motivate the definitions of their asymptotic analogues to come.

Given a set of distributions
\mathcal{P}
and a desired type-I error
\alpha\in(0,1)
, an interval
\mathcal{C}(\alpha)
is said to be a
(1-\alpha)
-confidence interval for the functional
\theta
if it satisfies

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P(\theta(P)\notin\mathcal{C}(\alpha))\leq\alpha,

(2.1)

whereas a p-value
Q
satisfies

\text{for all }\alpha\in(0,1),\quad\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P(Q\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha.

(2.2)

Moving towards the definition of post-hoc confidence intervals and p-values, notice that the conditions in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) are equivalent to writing

\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}\left\{\theta(P)\notin\mathcal{C}_{n}(\alpha)\right\}}{\alpha}\right]\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}\left\{Q\leq\alpha\right\}}{\alpha}\right]\leq 1,

(2.3)

respectively.
Importantly, in (
2.3
),
\alpha
is fixed before the expectation—it is data-independent.
With (
2.3
) in mind, the definitions of post-hoc confidence intervals and p-values with data-dependent
\alpha
values can be intuited. These definitions have appeared in the literature; see
Xu et al. 2024
,
Grünwald 2024
and
Koning 2025
.

Definition 2.1

(Post-hoc confidence intervals and p-values)
.

We say that a family of random sets
\mathcal{H}\equiv\{\mathcal{H}(\alpha)\}_{\alpha>0}
is a
post-hoc confidence interval
for
\theta
and
\mathcal{P}
if

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{H};\theta,P)\leq 1\text{~ where ~}\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{H};\theta,P):=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\sup_{\alpha>0}\frac{\mathbf{1}\left\{\theta(P)\notin\mathcal{H}(\alpha)\right\}}{\alpha}\right].

(2.4)

We say that a nonnegative random variable
Q
is a
post-hoc p-value
for
\mathcal{P}
if

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{S}(Q;P)\leq 1\text{~ where ~}\mathbf{S}(Q;P):=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\sup_{\alpha>0}\frac{\mathbf{1}\left\{Q\leq\alpha\right\}}{\alpha}\right]\leq 1.

(2.5)

In the above definition and the text preceding it, we have used the symbols “
\mathcal{H}
” and “
\mathcal{C}
” to refer to post-hoc and non-post-hoc confidence sets, respectively, and we will maintain this convention throughout the paper. We refer to both
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{S}
as
risk
and refer to the guarantees (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) as
risk control
.
We will typically drop the “for
\theta
and
\mathcal{P}
” phrase from the definition unless there is ambiguity. If
\mathcal{H}

is a post-hoc confidence set then we also refer to it as
post-hoc valid
, and similarly for post-hoc valid p-values.
We often write
\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{H})
in place of
\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{H};\theta,P)
if
\theta
and
P
are understood from context.

Remark 2.2

(On the range of
\alpha
extending beyond 1)
.

When moving from (
2.3
) to (
2.4
) we not only moved
\alpha
inside the expectation, but also allowed it to take values larger than one. This is only for mathematical convenience later on and does not change the guarantee in any meaningful way. Indeed, for any
\alpha>1
,
\mathbf{1}\left\{\theta(P)\notin\mathcal{H}(\alpha)\right\}/\alpha\leq 1
, hence such an
\alpha
can never break risk control.

It is worth pondering the distinction between controlling the risk versus the error probability, noting in particular that the inequalities in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) provide fundamentally different guarantees than those in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
).
Consider the case of post-hoc confidence intervals.
If we design
k
PH-CIs based on
k
independent experiments, we should not expect that
\theta
is contained in our sets at least
(1-\alpha)
% of the time as
k\to\infty
. Instead, we should expect that for any
\alpha
, the average of
\mathbf{1}\left\{\theta(P)\not\in\mathcal{H}_{n}(\alpha)\right\}/\alpha
over all trials should be at most one (as
k\to\infty
).

Remark 2.3

(From post-hoc risk to type-I error for data-independent
\alpha
values)
.

If
\alpha
is not data-dependent, then the post-hoc guarantees in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) reduce to the usual type-I error guarantees in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
). This will be the case for all post-hoc guarantees studied throughout this paper.

Given that risk control is different than type-I error control in general, it is natural to wonder what utility the former provides the practitioner. After all, practitioners are familiar with error probabilities—can we expect them to switch to using risk instead? Our view is that such a switch is natural in settings which require post-hoc decision-making
and
frequentist-style error guarantees. While Bayesian decision theory allows for post-hoc decision-making (this is well known, but see
Chugg et al. 2026
for a formal statement and proof), it notably does not provide bounds on the long-run error, while Definition
2.1
is designed to do just that. This, coupled with the fact that the theory of post-hoc risk control turns out to be quite rich, leads us to believe that fleshing out benefits and drawbacks of (asymptotic) post-hoc p-values and post-hoc CIs is worthwhile.

2.2
Asymptotic post-hoc CIs and p-values

With the definition of post-hoc p-values and post-hoc CIs in hand, let us now introduce their asymptotic counterparts. With the notation introduced in Section
1.3
, the guarantee stated in the introduction for an asymptotic confidence interval can be written more precisely as

\text{For all }\alpha\in(0,1),\quad\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(\theta(P)\notin\mathcal{C}_{n}(\alpha))\leq\alpha.

(2.6)

Following a similar argument to that of the previous section, we rewrite (
2.6
) as a bound on the expected value, which then suggests the following definitions of asymptotic post-hoc confidence intervals and p-values.

Definition 2.4

(Asymptotic post-hoc confidence intervals and p-values)
.

We say that a sequence
\mathcal{H}\equiv(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an
asymptotic, post-hoc confidence interval
(
aph-ci
) for
\theta
under
\mathcal{P}
if

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{H}_{n};\theta,P)\leq 1.

(2.7)

We say that a sequence of random variables
(Q_{n})_{n\geq 1}
define an
asymptotic post-hoc p-value
(
aph-pval
) for
\mathcal{P}
if

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{S}(Q_{n};P)\leq 1.

(2.8)

In other words, just as an asymptotic confidence interval requires that type-I error is bounded in the limit, an
aph-ci
requires that the post-hoc risk is bounded in the limit.

Note that the supremum over
\mathcal{P}
is outside the limit in both (
2.7
) and (
2.8
). This implies that the limit supremum is upper-bounded
pointwise
for each distribution.
One might instead hope for
uniform
boundedness of the limit supremum, which would correspond to moving the supremum over
P\in\mathcal{P}
inside the limit. We introduce such “distribution-uniform”
aph-ci
s and
aph-pval
s next.

2.3
Distribution-uniform asymptotic post-hoc CIs and p-values

Suppose we have developed an
aph-ci

\mathcal{H}
for estimating the mean
\mu(P)
of a Gaussian distribution
P
. Let
P_{\sigma}
be the distribution of a Gaussian random variable centered at zero with variance
\sigma^{2}>0
and consider the family of distributions
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{\sigma}:\sigma\in(0,\infty)\}
. Suppose for the sake of example that
\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{H}_{n},P_{\sigma})\asymp\sigma/n+1
. Then for each fixed
\sigma
,
\limsup_{n}\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{H}_{n},P_{\sigma})=1
, so
\mathcal{H}
is indeed an
aph-ci
. Nevertheless, for any
n
,
\sup_{\sigma>0}\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{H}_{n},P_{\sigma})=\infty
, and hence

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{H}_{n},P)=\infty.

The above possibility motivates extending the definitions of
aph-ci
s and
aph-pval
s to the
distribution-uniform
setting, in which
the limit holds simultaneously for all distributions in
\mathcal{P}
.
Distribution-uniform limit theorems have been studied since at least
Chung 1951
, but the
the study of distribution-uniform asymptotic confidence intervals in particular was initiated by
Li 1989
, who called them “honest” confidence intervals.
Since then, distribution-uniform methods have been studied in a wide array of settings, with a notable surge in momentum in the last decade
[
54
,
44
,
50
,
61
,
63
,
64
]
.

Mathematically, extending the definition of
aph-ci
s to the distribution-uniform setting is straightforward; we simply swap the limit and the supremum in Definition
2.4
. Recall the definitions of
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{S}
from Section
2.1
. We note that distribution-uniform p-values (though not post-hoc p-values) were studied recently by
Ignatiadis et al. 2024
.

Definition 2.5

(Distribution-uniform
aph-ci
s and
aph-pval
s)
.

Fix a family of distributions
\mathcal{P}
. For each
{P\in\mathcal{P}}
, let
\theta(P)\in\mathbb{R}
be a parameter to be thought of as the target estimand of the
aph-ci
under
P
. We say that a sequence
\mathcal{H}\equiv(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a
distribution-uniform
aph-ci

for
\theta\equiv(\theta(P))_{P\in\mathcal{P}}
under
\mathcal{P}
(or a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform
aph-ci
for
\theta
) if

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{H}_{n};\theta(P),P)\leq 1.

(2.9)

We say that a sequence of random variables
(Q_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a
distribution-uniform

aph-pval

for

\mathcal{P}
(or, equivalently, a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform
aph-pval
) if

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{S}(Q_{n};P)\leq 1.

(2.10)

We will often shorten “distribution-uniform” to simply “uniform.” The distribution
\mathcal{P}
will usually be understood from context. If we do not say explicitly that an
aph-ci
or
aph-pval
is uniform, it should be assumed to hold only pointwise. When we discuss
\mathcal{P}
-uniform
aph-ci
s going forward, we may simply refer to
\theta
as “the estimand”, but this should be thought of as an implicit collection of
\mathcal{P}
-indexed estimands
(\theta_{P})_{P\in\mathcal{P}}
.

Next we study how to construct uniform
aph-ci
s using uniform asymptotic e-variables.

2.4
The sufficiency and necessity of asymptotic e-values

Let us now return to the question of how to construct
aph-ci
s. Just as post-hoc confidence intervals can be designed with e-values—as shown by
Xu et al. 2024
and
Grünwald 2024
—we may design asymptotic post-hoc confidence intervals using
asymptotic e-values
.
Following Definition 3.5 and Proposition 3.6 in
Ignatiadis et al. 2024
, an asymptotic e-value for
\mathcal{P}
is a sequence of nonnegative random variables
(E_{n})_{n\geq 1}
whose components satisfy

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}]\leq 1.

(2.11)

(The authors referred to these as
strongly
asymptotic e-variables; we omit the term strongly for brevity.) If the inequality is an equality then we call
(E_{n})
a
sharp
asymptotic e-variable.

This definition of an asymptotic e-variable is again a pointwise notion vis-a-vis
\mathcal{P}
. To construct distribution-uniform
aph-ci
s and
aph-pval
s, we will need to rely on
distribution-uniform
asymptotic e-variables, defined as a sequence of nonnegative random variables
(E_{n})
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}]\leq 1.

(2.12)

We will typically call such a sequence a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform asymptotic e-variable to make the set of distributions
\mathcal{P}
explicit.
These objects were also introduced by
Ignatiadis et al. 2024
, who called them strongly uniform asymptotic e-variables. Again, we drop the “strongly.”

If either (
2.11
) or (
2.12
) hold with equality, we call the (uniform) asymptotic e-variables
sharp
.
If
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
is the set of all distributions in some class with parameter
\theta
, we will say that
(E_{n})
is an asymptotic e-variable for
\theta
if it is an asymptotic e-variable for
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
. Similarly, we call it a uniformly asymptotic e-variable for
\theta
if it is a
θ
-uniform asymptotic e-variable.

The following result shows that we can obtain an
aph-ci
and an
aph-pval
by thresholding the components of an asymptotic e-value. In fact, this is the only way to construct (asymptotic) post-hoc confidence sets, assuming standard behavior of the sets. The proof is in Appendix
C.1
and is inspired by similar results for nonasymptotic e-values and post-hoc inference; see
Grünwald 2024
and
Koning 2025
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\Theta
be a set that can be thought of as the “parameter space” and
let
(\mathcal{H}_{n}(\alpha))_{\alpha>0}
be a family of subsets of
\Theta
that is monotonic and right-continuous in
\alpha
, meaning:

•

\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{2})\subseteq\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{1})
for all
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}
(monotonicity),

•

For every
\theta\in\Theta
, the rejection set
\{\alpha>0:\theta\not\in\mathcal{H}_{n}(\alpha)\}
is a left-closed interval of the form
[\alpha_{0},\infty)
for some
\alpha_{0}>0
(right-continuity).

Then the sequence
(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a (uniform)
aph-ci
for
\theta^{\star}
if and only if there exists a sequence of random variables
(E_{n}(\theta))
for each
\theta\in\Theta

such that for all
\alpha>0
,
H_{n}(\alpha)
takes the form

\mathcal{H}_{n}(\alpha)=\left\{\theta:E_{n}(\theta)<1/\alpha\right\},

and so that
(E_{n}(\theta^{\star}))
is a (uniform) asymptotic e-variable.
Furthermore, a sequence of random variables
(Q_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a (uniform)
aph-pval
if and only if there exists some (uniform) asymptotic e-variable
(E_{n})_{n\geq 1}
so that
Q_{n}=1/E_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Monotonicity and right-continuity are natural (and often assumed) desiderata for confidence intervals. The former implies that smaller values of
\alpha
yield smaller intervals, while the latter implies nesting. That is, if
\theta
is rejected for a sequence of levels approaching
\alpha
from above, it is also rejected at
\alpha
.

The “if and only if” in Proposition
2.6
holds only if
\alpha
is allowed to take values larger than one in Definition
2.1
. Indeed, if
\alpha
is restricted to
(0,1)
then
\{\theta:E_{n}(\theta)<1/\alpha\}
is an
aph-ci
for
\theta^{\star}
if there exists some family of processes
\{(E_{n}(\theta)):\theta\in\Theta\}
such that
(E_{n}(\theta^{\star}))
is an asymptotic e-variable for
\theta^{\star}
, but the converse does not necessarily hold.

Remark 2.7
.

Proposition
2.6
implicitly assumes that the asymptotic e-value is not a function of
\alpha
. E-values with such a dependence are not rare. Indeed, the “method of predictable plug-ins” is a common way of constructing sequential e-values (i.e., e-processes), and typically relies on choosing parameters as functions of
\alpha

[
60
]
.
E-values that integrate out parameters in
(0,\alpha)
have also been proposed
[
16
]
. Even though the e-value itself may not depend on
\alpha
, the confidence interval
\mathcal{H}_{n}(\alpha)
obtained from the e-value will.

In practice, constructing an
aph-ci
using only a single e-variable often results in one-sided intervals (unless we use the method of mixtures; see Section
3.3
). To obtain two-sided intervals we will take the intersection of the sets defined by multiple e-values.
We can, however, relax the requirement that these sets be constructed by individually valid (asymptotic) e-variables and allow them instead to be (asymptotic)
compound e-variables

[
31
]
. This result is not central to our story, however, and is thereby deferred to Appendix
B.4
.

Proving that sequences of random variables are pointwise asymptotic e-variables typically involves arguments concerning convergence in distribution combined with uniform integrability. By a result of
Billingsley 1995
, this implies convergence of means (not
in
mean). In the distribution-uniform setting, we will develop and rely on a proposition found in Appendix
B.5
which can be viewed as an extension of Billingsley’s result. We mention it here because it may of be independent interest.

3
Constructing Asymptotic Post-Hoc Confidence Intervals

By Proposition
2.6
,
constructing
aph-ci
s relies on finding asymptotic e-variables. These are less well studied than their nonasymptotic counterparts. Indeed, it is not obvious whether non-trivial asymptotic e-values ought to exist.
Ignatiadis et al. 2024
lay this fear to rest and show that, for iid
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
with mean
\theta
and finite variance, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\theta;\lambda):=\exp\left(\lambda\frac{S_{n}(\theta)}{V_{n}(\theta)}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\right)

(3.1)

is an asymptotic
e
-value
where

S_{n}(\theta):=\sum_{i\leq n}(X_{i}-\theta)\text{~ and ~}V_{n}(\theta):=\bigg(\sum_{i\leq n}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

(3.2)

The superscript
iwr
refers to the names Ignatiadis, Wang, and Ramdas
[
31
]
.
In Section
3.1
we will extend this asymptotic e-variable in several ways. First, we observe that it holds not only for all distributions with finite variance, but for those distributions in the domain of attraction of a Gaussian (a weaker condition). Second, we will prove that
(E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}})
is a uniform asymptotic e-variable under a uniformly bounded third-moment assumption.
Theorem
3.2
then presents the

aph-ci
defined by
(E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\theta;\lambda))
.

Sections
3.2
and
3.3
explore different ways to choose
\lambda
to optimize the width of the
aph-ci
. This is more involved than the nonasymptotic case, both because
\lambda
cannot depend on
\alpha
(Remark
2.7
) and because of the requirement of uniform integrability. While these two sections focus on
E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}
in particular, the principles discussed therein apply more broadly—for instance, to the two additional
aph-ci
s we present in Appendix
B.1
.

Section
3.4
provides another asymptotic e-variable using entirely different arguments than are used for the
iwr
e-variable. It is based on a truncation technique coupled with a recent nonasymptotic SLLN given by
Ruf and Waudby-Smith 2025
. The resulting
aph-ci
has pros and cons compared to the
iwr

aph-ci
s; we will discuss these tradeoffs in more depth in the discussions that follow.

3.1
The IWR asymptotic e-variable and APH-CI

Throughout this section, let
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}:=(n-1)^{-1}\sum_{i\leq n}(X_{i}-\overline{X}_{n})^{2}
denote the unbiased sample variance and let
\hat{s}_{n}^{2}=n^{-1}\sum_{i\leq n}(X_{i}-\overline{X}_{n})^{2}
denote the biased sample variance where
\overline{X}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i\leq n}X_{i}
is the sample mean.

To state our results for
E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}
we need to introduce several distributional assumptions.
Recall that for
q\geq 1
, the set
\mathcal{M}_{q}(\theta)
denotes those distributions on
\mathbb{R}
with a finite
q
-th moment and mean
\theta
, and
\mathcal{M}_{q}
is the set of all distributions with finite
q
-th moment.

We say that
(X_{n})
are in the domain of attraction of a Gaussian if there exist sequences
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
such that

\frac{\sum_{i\leq n}X_{i}-b_{n}}{a_{n}}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).

(3.3)

If the distribution has finite variance
\sigma^{2}
then (
3.3
) holds with
b_{n}=n\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]
and
a_{n}=\sigma\sqrt{n}
. But (
3.3
) can also be satisfied for distributions without a finite second moment, such a Pareto distributions with shape 2. See
Mikosch 1999
for more details on domains of attraction. We let
\textsc{DAN}(\theta)
be all distributions with mean
\theta
that are in the domain of a attraction of a Gaussian.

Our distribution-uniform result for the
iwr
e-variable will require uniformly bounded moments. Specifically, we will make use of a
\mathcal{P}
-bounded “skew,” meaning that

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}|X_{1}-\mathbb{E}_{P}X_{1}|^{3}}{\sigma_{P}^{3}}<\infty,

(3.4)

where
\sigma_{P}^{2}
is the variance of
X_{1}
under
P
.
Requiring uniformly bounded moments (typically more than two) is a standard assumption in distribution-uniform inference
[
50
,
61
]
and is known to be necessary for uniform strong convergence results
[
63
]
. In our case we will require a uniformly bounded third moment/skew due to our use of the Berry-Esseen bound
[
3
]
. To elaborate, the proof relies on upper bounding
S_{n}(\theta)/V_{n}(\theta)
deterministically by
\sqrt{n}
(using Cauchy-Schwarz), which is canceled out by the
1/\sqrt{n}
rate of decay of the Berry-Esseen bound. This allows us to conclude that
\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}|S_{n}(\theta)/V_{n}(\theta)|<\infty
, which is an integral part of the proof.

With these preliminaries in hand, we can state our result for the
iwr
e-variable. In the pointwise case, the proof follows fairly easily by appealing to results of
Giné et al. 1997
, which show that
S_{n}(\theta)/V_{n}(\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)
, and that
\exp(\lambda_{n}S_{n}(\theta)/V_{n}(\theta))
is uniformly integrable.
The distribution-uniform case is significantly more involved. The details of the proof can be found in Appendix
C.2
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(X_{n})
be a sequence of iid random variables.
Let
(\lambda_{n})
be a sequence of nonnegative random variables which converge to some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
almost surely. If
\sup_{n}|\lambda_{n}|<\infty
almost surely and
(X_{n})
are drawn from some distribution in
\textsc{DAN}(\theta)
, then
the process
(E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\theta,\lambda_{n}))
is a sharp asymptotic e-variable.
Additionally, if
(\lambda_{n})
is a deterministic sequence which converges to
\lambda
and

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
have
\mathcal{P}
-uniformly bounded skew for some
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\textsc{DAN}(\theta)
, then
(E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\theta;\lambda_{n}))
is a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform asymptotic e-variable.

We have stated Theorem
3.1
as generally as possible—asymptotic e-variables are relatively new objects in the literature and new results might thus be interesting in their own right. Going forward, however, we will not require all these degrees of freedom. In particular, the
aph-ci
based on
E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}
will not choose
\lambda
as a function of
n
.

Let us now give the
aph-ci
that results from inverting the
iwr
asymptotic e-variable as implied by Proposition
2.6
. The proof can be found in Appendix
C.3
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(X_{n})
be a sequence of iid random variables drawn from some distribution in
\textsc{DAN}(\mu)
.
For any
\lambda>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, set

A_{n,\alpha}(\lambda)=\frac{\log(2/\alpha)+\lambda^{2}/2}{\lambda\sqrt{n}}.

Then for any
\lambda>0
independent of
\alpha
,
(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\alpha;\lambda))
is an
aph-ci
for
\mu
, where

\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\alpha;\lambda):=(\overline{X}_{n}\pm W_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\alpha;\lambda)),\quad W_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\alpha;\lambda):=\frac{A_{n,\alpha}(\lambda)\hat{s}_{n}}{(1-A_{n,\alpha}(\lambda)^{2})^{1/2}},

(3.5)

if
n>(\log(2/\alpha)/\lambda+\lambda/2)^{2}
, otherwise
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\lambda)=\mathbb{R}
.
Additionally, if
(X_{n})
have
\mathcal{P}
-uniformly bounded skew for some
\mathcal{P}
, then
(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\alpha;\lambda))
is a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform
aph-ci
.

How do we choose the parameter
\lambda
in
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}
? The width will approach zero for any fixed
\lambda
, since
A_{\alpha,n}(\lambda)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
. For large
n
,
W_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\lambda)\approx A_{n,\alpha}(\lambda)\hat{s}_{n}
, so the ideal choice of
\lambda
is
\lambda_{\alpha}=\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}
which minimizes
\lambda\mapsto A_{n,\alpha}(\lambda)
.
Unfortunately, as emphasized both in the theorem statement and in Remark
2.7
, such an
\alpha
-dependent choice of
\lambda
is illegal.

Sections
3.2
and
3.3
pursue two distinct strategies for choosing
\lambda
.
First, however, let us better understand the role of a fixed
\lambda
by studying how the limiting width of
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}
scales with
\sqrt{n}
. Suppose that
(X_{n})
are iid with finite variance
\sigma^{2}
. Then a single application of the SLLN combined with the continuous mapping theorem shows that

\sqrt{n}W_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\alpha;\lambda)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{a.s.}\sigma g(\lambda,\alpha)\text{~ where ~}g(\lambda,\alpha):=\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda}{2}.

(3.6)

It is fruitful to compare this width to the usual Wald CI for finite (unknown) variance based on the CLT (which we emphasize is not post-hoc valid):

\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{wald}}(\alpha):=(\overline{X}\pm W_{n}^{\textsc{wald}}(\alpha)),\text{~ where ~}W_{n}^{\textsc{wald}}(\alpha):=z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}.

(3.7)

By the SLLN,
\sqrt{n}W_{n}^{\textsc{wald}}(\alpha)\to z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma
, where
z_{1-\alpha/2}=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)
is the standard normal quantile. For small
\alpha
,
\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)
behaves as
\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha})
(indeed,
\lim_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)/\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}=1
). Thus,
\sqrt{n}W_{n}^{\textsc{wald}}(\alpha)\approx\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}\log(2/\alpha})
for large
n
and small
\alpha
.

As discussed above, the ideal choice of
\lambda
is
\lambda_{\alpha}:=\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha})
so that
g(\lambda_{\alpha},\alpha)=\log(2/\alpha)/\lambda_{\alpha}+\lambda_{\alpha}/2=\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}
. In this case
\sqrt{n}W_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\alpha;\lambda_{\alpha})\to\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}\log(2/\alpha)}
almost surely, the same as the Wald CI, approximately the same as the Wald CI for small
\alpha
.

If one uses
(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}})
simply as regular asymptotic CIs and does not change
\alpha
,
then one is of course free to choose
\lambda=\lambda_{\alpha}
. However, in a post-hoc setting (i.e., one in which
\alpha
may change), this is not allowed. The next two sections investigate two distinct data-independent methods to choose
\lambda
in Theorem
3.2
. The first simply takes
\lambda=\lambda_{\alpha_{0}}
where
\alpha_{0}
is a data-independent “guess” for
\alpha
. The second creates a new asymptotic e-variable by integrating out the parameter
\lambda
in
E_{n}^{{\textsc{iwr}}}
.

3.2
Choosing
\lambda
, Option I: Ex ante anchoring

Figure 1
:
Empirical evaluation of the ratio in (
3.8
). Here
\alpha_{0}
ranges from 0.001 to 0.2 and we plot the curves
\alpha_{0}\mapsto R(\alpha,\alpha_{0})
for select values of
\alpha
. The maximum value
R(\alpha,\alpha_{0})
across all values of
\alpha
and
\alpha_{0}
is 1.184.

Here we consider simply fixing some nominal level
\alpha_{0}
in advance of seeing the data and choosing
\lambda=\lambda_{\alpha_{0}}
. This can be viewed as our best guess of what
\alpha
will be (or perhaps our
hope
of what it will be) and take
\lambda_{0}:=\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha_{0})}
.
In this case
g(\lambda_{0},\alpha)=\log(2/\alpha)/\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha_{0})}+\sqrt{\log(2/\alpha_{0})/2}
, which will be close to
g(\lambda_{\alpha},\alpha)
for
\alpha_{0}\approx\alpha
.

This strategy, henceforth referred to as
ex ante anchoring
, has a similar flavor to the idea of “luckiness” in Bayesian theory (PAC-Bayesian theory in particular)
[
51
]
, in which bounds are tighter the closer the posterior is to the prior, but valid for any prior; see also
[
59
, Figure 3]
for an illustration of this phenomenon with Bayesian-inspired e-values. We refer to
\alpha_{0}
as the
anchor
.

As we will see in the simulations of Section
3.5
, ex ante anchoring performs surprisingly well in practice, even for
\alpha\gg\alpha_{0}
. To get a sense of why, note that the choice of
\alpha
only affects the ratio of the asymptotic widths of the
aph-ci
s by the square root of a logarithmic factor. Indeed, the ratio of
g(\lambda_{0},\alpha)
to
g(\lambda_{\alpha},\alpha)
(defined in (
3.6
)) can be written as

R(\alpha,\alpha_{0}):=\frac{g(\lambda_{0},\alpha)}{g(\lambda_{\alpha},\alpha)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\log(2/\alpha_{0})}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\alpha_{0})}{\log(2/\alpha)}}\right).

(3.8)

For reasonable values of
\alpha
and
\alpha_{0}
, this ratio is quite small. Figure
1
plots the curves
x\mapsto R(x,\alpha_{0})
for select values of
\alpha_{0}
. For
x
between 0.0001 and 0.2, the ratio never exceeds 1.19. Thus, by (
3.6
), for such values of
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha
, the asymptotic width of
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}
does not change substantially even when
|\alpha-\alpha_{0}|\gg 0
.

Nevertheless, one may be unsatisfied by asymptotic widths of
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}
scaling linearly with
\log(1/\alpha)
. The
aph-ci
presented in the next section will remedy this issue, resulting in an asymptotic width which scales as
\Theta(\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\log(1/\alpha)})
similarly to the Wald CI. However, despite this theoretical appeal, we find that ex-ante anchoring works best in practice (Section
3.5
).

3.3
Choosing
\lambda
, Option II: The method of mixtures

We can provide asymptotic e-values which are free of
\lambda
using the “method of mixtures,” a technique pioneered in its modern form by Herbert Robbins in the context of tail inequalities for supermartingales
[
45
]
. It has become a cornerstone of anytime-valid inference, deployed in both univariate (e.g.,
[
33
,
28
,
62
]
) and multivariate (e.g,
[
12
,
7
]
) settings. The following lemma gives sufficient conditions under which we may apply the method of mixtures to a family of asymptotic e-variables to obtain another asymptotic e-variable. The proof is in Appendix
C.4
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}
and let
\pi
be any (data-free) probability distribution on
\Lambda
. Suppose that for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
, the sequence
(E_{n}(\lambda))
is an asymptotic e-variable for some set of distributions
\mathcal{P}
. If
(\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}E_{n}(\lambda))
is uniformly integrable for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
, then the process
(E_{n}(\pi))
defined as

E_{n}(\pi):=\int_{\Lambda}E_{n}(\lambda)\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda),

(3.9)

is an asymptotic e-variable. Further, if
(\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}E_{n}(\lambda))
is
\mathcal{P}
-uniformly integrable, then
(E_{n}(\pi))
is a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform asymptotic e-variable.

Appendix
B.6
provides more general sufficient conditions under which the mixture in (
3.9
) can result in an asymptotic e-variable. See Proposition
B.10
. However, the result in (
3.9
) is satisfactory for our purposes in this section.

Remark 3.4
.

In the nonasymptotic setting, any mixture of e-variables is again an e-variable. This follows immediately by Tonelli’s theorem; no other conditions are needed. We might thus wonder if Proposition
3.3
truly requires any additional assumptions beyond
(E_{n})
being an asymptotic e-variable. The answer is yes. Consider
E_{n}(\lambda)=1+2^{n}\mathbf{1}\left\{\lambda\in(2^{-(n+1)},2^{-n}]\right\}
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Let
\pi
be the uniform distribution over
[0,1]
. For large enough
n
and any fixed
\lambda\in[0,1]
, we have
E_{n}(\lambda)=1
, so
\limsup_{n}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]=1
. On the other hand,
\int E_{n}(\lambda)\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda)=1+1
, so
\limsup_{n}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}^{\pi}]=2
. Therefore,
some
extra condition beyond being an asymptotic e-variable is required for the mixture to also be an asymptotic e-variable.

Using that
(E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}})
is uniformly integrable on compact sets (Lemma
C.1
), we immediately obtain the following result.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a compact set. Let
\pi
be any (data-free) probability distribution on
\Lambda
. Then, for any fixed
\eta>0
, the mixture
E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\theta;\pi)=\int_{\Lambda}E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\theta;\lambda)\textnormal{d}\pi

is an asymptotic e-variable for
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\theta)
.

In the nonasymptotic setting, when one has a family of e-variables
E_{n}(\lambda)
valid for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, a powerful approach is to use a Gaussian mixture (or some other distribution supported on all of
\mathbb{R}
). Indeed, this was Robbins’ original approach
[
46
]
. Unfortunately, Corollary
3.5
precludes such a choice. It appears that one would need to restrict the class of distributions to those with strict control of the moment-generating function to be able to apply the Gaussian mixture. This is untenable for our purposes, as we want to assume the existence of a few moments at most.

Instead, we will use a
truncated
Gaussian to mix over a finite region
[-R,R]
. Such an approach has previously been used to develop nonasymptotic confidence sequences
[
6
,
7
]
. To state the result, define the object

I_{R}(y,u):=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{u}}\exp\left\{\frac{y^{2}}{4u}\right\}(\Phi(g_{1})-\Phi(g_{2})),

(3.10)

where
\Phi
is the cdf of the standard normal and

g_{1}=R\sqrt{2u}-y/\sqrt{2u}
and
g_{2}=-(R\sqrt{2u}+y/\sqrt{2u})
. It is easy to see that
I_{R}(y,u)
is symmetric about
y=0
. Indeed,
I_{R}(y,u)
should be thought of as an upward-facing parabola with minimum at
y=0
and shape and (positive) intercept determined by
u
and
R
.

Mixing
E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}
over a truncated Gaussian gives an asymptotic e-variable stated in terms of
I_{R}(y,u)
. The result is fairly unenlightening so we relegate it to Appendix
B.7
and proceed immediately to the resulting
aph-ci
. The proof of the following theorem is provided in Appendix
C.5
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
(X_{n})
be a sequence of iid random variables drawn from some distribution in
\textsc{DAN}(\mu)
.
Fix any
R,\kappa>0
and let
Z_{R,\kappa}=\kappa\sqrt{2\pi}(\Phi(R/\kappa)-\Phi(-R/\kappa))
. Then the process
(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}(\alpha;R,\kappa))
is an
aph-ci
for
\mu
where
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}(\alpha;R,\kappa)=(\overline{X}_{n}\pm W_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}(\alpha;R,\kappa))
with

W_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}(\alpha;R,\kappa)=\frac{\hat{s}_{n}y^{\star}_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n-(y^{\star}_{\alpha})^{2}}},

(3.11)

where

y_{\alpha}^{\star}
solves
I_{R}(y_{\alpha}^{\star},(\kappa^{2}+1)/(2\kappa^{2}))=Z_{R,\kappa}/\alpha
. If the denominator in (
3.11
) is non-positive, we take the CI to be all of
\mathbb{R}
.

We note that due to the nice shape of
I_{R}
, the implicit equation
I_{R}(y_{\alpha}^{\star},(\kappa^{2}+1)/(2\kappa^{2}))=Z_{R,\kappa}/\alpha
is easy to approximate to arbitrary precision via root finding. Thus, the
aph-ci
of Theorem
3.6
is computationally tractable and easy to implement. See Section
3.5
for experiments.

Theorem
3.6
is not a distribution-uniform result. This is because it is unclear whether
(\sup_{\lambda\in[-R,R]}E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\lambda))
is
\mathcal{P}
-uniformly integrable, whereas it is pointwise uniformly integrable by Lemma
C.1
. Proving
\mathcal{P}
-uniform integrability of
(E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\lambda))
(even without the supremum) seems to necessitate extending the work of
Giné et al. 1997
on the asymptotics of the Student t-distribution to the distribution-uniform setting, an interesting problem in and of itself which we leave for future inquiry.

The asymptotic width of
W_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}
is particularly easy to analyze under a finite second-moment assumption. Indeed, if
\hat{s}_{n}^{2}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\sigma^{2}
, then

\sqrt{n}W_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}(\alpha;R,\kappa)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\sigma y_{\alpha}^{\star}.

(3.12)

Recalling the discussion in Section
3.1
, we had
W_{n}^{\textsc{wald}}\to\sigma z_{1-\alpha/2}
. Experimentally, we find that
y_{\alpha}^{\star}<2z_{1-\alpha/2}
for most reasonable values of
R
and
\kappa
, implying that the asymptotic widths of these mixture e-variables have asymptotic width at most twice that of the Wald CI. Moreover, the bounds are very stable in practice regardless of the choice of
R
and
\kappa
. Analytically, Lemma
B.13
in Appendix
B.7
shows that for
\kappa=1
,
y_{\alpha}^{\star}\to 2\sqrt{\log(\sqrt{2}/\alpha)}
as
R\to\infty
. We thus obtain the desired square root behavior on
\log(1/\alpha)
which was missing in Section
3.2
.

3.4
Event partitioning and the R-WS asymptotic e-variable

Instead of directly defining a sequence of random variables whose means are asymptotically bounded by one, an alternative method of constructing an asymptotic e-variable is to perform event partitioning as follows. We take a “nice” sequence of random variables
(G_{n})
that has good behavior on events
(A_{n})
, and truncate the sequence by the probability that it misbehaves, i.e., the probabilities of
(A_{n}^{c})
. Formally, consider the following observation.

Observation 3.7
.

Let
(G_{n})
be a sequence of nonnegative random variables, and let
(A_{n})
be a sequence of events such that
\limsup_{n}\mathbb{E}_{P}[G_{n}\mathbf{1}\left\{A_{n}\right\}]\leq 1
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Then, for any sequence of scalars
(T_{n})
, such that
T_{n}P(A_{n}^{c})\to 0
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
,

E_{n}=G_{n}\wedge T_{n}
defines an asymptotic e-variable for
\mathcal{P}
. Meanwhile, if
\limsup_{n}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[G_{n}\mathbf{1}\left\{A_{n}\right\}]\leq 1
and
T_{n}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P(A_{n}^{c})\to 0
then
(E_{n})
is a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform asymptotic e-variable.

Proof.

Decompose
\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[G_{n}\mathbf{1}\left\{A_{n}\right\}]+\mathbb{E}_{P}[T_{n}\mathbf{1}\left\{A_{n}^{c}\right\}]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[G_{n}\mathbf{1}\left\{A_{n}\right\}]+T_{n}P(A_{n}^{c})
.
By assumption, the first term goes to one as
n\to\infty
and the second goes to zero.
∎

We will apply Observation
3.7
to a sequence of random variables designed to approximate the process
(E_{n}^{\star}(\lambda))
in the limit, where for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

E_{n}^{\star}(\lambda):=\exp\left\{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}U_{n}^{\otimes}\right\},\quad U_{n}^{\otimes}:=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{n}((X_{i}-\theta)^{2}+2\sigma^{2}).

(3.13)

Howard et al. 2020
demonstrate that
(E_{n}^{\star}(\lambda))
is a supermartingale with initial value one for distributions
(X_{n})
with constant conditional mean
\theta
and finite variance
\sigma^{2}
. Therefore, they satisfy
\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{\tau}^{\star}(\lambda)]\leq 1
for all stopping times
\tau
under such distributions
P
. The following theorem is based on building a process that approximates
E_{n}^{\star}
in the limit and bounding its probability of failure. It relies on a nonasymptotic SLLN from
Ruf and Waudby-Smith 2025
and is thus eponymously named the
r-ws
asymptotic e-variable. The proof can be found in Appendix
C.6
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
(X_{n})
be a sequence of iid random variables.
Fix
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
and
\delta>0
. Let
(T_{n})\nearrow\infty
and
(\epsilon_{n})\searrow 0
be nonnegative sequences satisfying
T_{n}=o(n^{\delta\gamma/2}\epsilon_{n}^{2+\delta/2})
.
Then
(E_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\theta;\lambda,\epsilon_{n},T_{n}))
is an asymptotic e-variable for all distributions in
\mathcal{M}_{2+\delta}(\theta)
, where

E_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\theta;\epsilon,T):=\int_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(\lambda S_{n}(\theta)-n(\hat{s}_{n}^{2}+\epsilon_{n})\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\right)\textnormal{d}F(\lambda)\wedge T_{n},

(3.14)

and
F
is any distribution over
\mathbb{R}
(including a point mass).
Moreover, if the sequence
(X_{n})
has a
\mathcal{P}
-uniformly bounded
2+\delta
moment for
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2+\delta}(\theta)
then
(E_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\theta;\lambda,\epsilon_{n},T_{n}))
is a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform asymptotic e-variable.

As made evident in the statement of Theorem
3.8
, a benefit of the truncation approach is that it can elegantly handle mixtures while sidestepping issues of uniform integrability. Indeed, if
(G_{n}(\lambda)\wedge T_{n})
is an asymptotic e-variable for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
, then so too is
(\int G_{n}(\lambda)\textnormal{d}F\wedge T_{n})
for any distribution
F
over
\Lambda
.

In particular, as we were tempted to do in Section
3.3
but could not, we take can
F
to be a centered Gaussian in the above theorem. Taking
\epsilon_{n}=1/\log(n)
and
\gamma=0.49
for concreteness, we obtain the following corollary. The calculation is standard, but is presented for completeness in Appendix
C.7
.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
(X_{n})
be a sequence of iid random variables.
Fix
C,\rho>0
and let
\hat{u}_{n}=n(\hat{s}_{n}^{2}+\log^{-1}n)
. Then the sequence
(E_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\theta;v))
is an asymptotic e-variable for all distributions in
\mathcal{M}_{2+\delta}(\theta)
where

E_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\theta;\rho)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho\hat{u}_{n}+1}}\exp\left\{\frac{\rho S_{n}^{2}(\theta)}{2\rho\hat{u}_{n}+2}\right\}\wedge C\cdot n^{\delta\cdot 0.24}.

(3.15)

Moreover, if the sequence
(X_{n})
has a
\mathcal{P}
-uniformly bounded
2+\delta
moment for some
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2+\delta}(\theta)
, then
(E_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\theta;\rho))
is a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform asymptotic e-variable.

The truncation in (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) is an explicit bound on the growth of the e-variable and translates directly to a limit on the size of the resulting
aph-ci
. Indeed, for a truncation sequence
(T_{n})
, the
aph-ci
will be vacuous for all
n
such that
T_{n}<1/\alpha
. In (
3.15
) for example, if we posit the existence of a third moment
(\delta=1)
, then to have a non-vacuous confidence interval at
\alpha=0.05
we require
Cn^{0.24}\geq 1/0.05
, i.e.,
n\approx 2.64\cdot 10^{5}/C^{1/0.24}
. One can of course take
C
extremely large to compensate, but we emphasize that
C
should not be data-dependent and must be fixed in advance. Further, as is evident from examining the proof,
C
determines when the asymptotics kick in. Thus, larger values of
C
will result in more false positives for modest values of
n
. We suggest setting
C=100
as the standard. As shown by Table
1
, this choice of
C
results in an empirical risk well below one.

While the
r-ws
asymptotic e-variable has explicit truncation, we note that the
iwr
e-variable, both in Theorem
3.1
and its mixture, have an implicit form of truncation.
In particular, in the case of ex ante tuning (Section
3.2
), since
\lambda
cannot depend on
\alpha
, as
\alpha
drifts further from the anchor
\alpha_{0}
, the e-variable becomes less powerful. And in the method of mixtures (Section
3.3
), the fact that we can only mix over a finite range
[-R,R]
inherently limits the power of the e-variable. Thus, there seems to be no way to escape some form of truncation, whether implicit or explicit, for asymptotic e-variables. We posit that this is true in general, not just for the e-variables presented in this paper. We leave it as an open question whether this observation can be formalized.

Let us state the
aph-ci
that results from the asymptotic e-variable in Corollary
3.9
. The proof follows the usual formula: we find those
\theta
such that
E_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\theta;\rho)<1/\alpha
, taking into account that the CI is vacuous if the truncation level is below
1/\alpha
. The result then follows from Proposition
2.6
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
(X_{n})
be a sequence of iid random variables drawn from some distribution in
\mathcal{M}_{2+\delta}(\mu)
for some
\delta>0
.
Fix
C,\rho>0
and let
\hat{u}_{n}=n(\hat{s}_{n}^{2}+\log^{-1}n)
.
Then
(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\alpha;\rho))
is an
aph-ci
for
\mu
, where

\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\alpha;\rho)=(\overline{X}_{n}\pm W_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\alpha;\rho))\text{~ where ~}W_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\alpha;\rho):=\sqrt{\frac{2\rho\hat{u}_{n}+2}{n^{2}\rho}\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{\rho\hat{u}_{n}+1}}{\alpha}\right),}

as long as
Cn^{\delta\cdot 0.24}\geq 1/\alpha
, otherwise
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\alpha;\rho)=\mathbb{R}
. If
(X_{n})
have a
\mathcal{P}
-uniformly bounded
2+\delta

(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\rho))

\mathcal{P}

.

A reasonable value of
\rho
in Theorem

is
\rho=2
, but we show in Appendix

that reasonable values of

and scales as
\sqrt{\log(n)/n}
.
More precisely, a bit of arithmetic gives that
\sqrt{n}W_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\alpha;\rho)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{a.s.}\infty
, and

\sqrt{\frac{n}{\log n}}W_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\alpha;\rho)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{a.s.}\sigma,

just as Robbins’ original mixture (see

). Note that this rate of
\sqrt{\log(n)/n}
is distinct from the rate of

achieved by the previous

Such a rate is common in the literature on time-uniform confidence

, as opposed to confidence

. Confidence sequences cannot scale as
1/\sqrt{n}
due to the law of the iterated logarithm

, and Robbins’ mixture is a classical way to construct such objects. This provides us with a hint that

.

4
Post-hoc Sequential Inference and Asymptotic e-processes

The discussion thus far has focused on post-hoc analogues of asymptotic confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and p-values. There is a growing literature on
sequential
(but not post-hoc) analogues of statistical inference procedures, taking the form of confidence sequences, sequential hypothesis tests, and anytime p-values, respectively. The roots of these sequential procedures lie in the work of
Wald 1945
,
Wald 1947
, Robbins and colleagues
[
45
,
11
,
36
]
and recent years have seen increased activity; see Chapter 7 of
Ramdas and Wang 2025
, the review paper by
Ramdas et al. 2023
, and other related papers
[
28
,
60
,
40
]
. Furthermore, distribution-uniform asymptotic versions of these procedures have been developed by
Waudby-Smith et al. 2023
being enabled by recent advances in distribution-uniform strong asymptotics
[
63
,
64
]
and distribution-pointwise anytime-valid inference
[
46
,
62
,
4
]
.

However, none of these were shown to enjoy post-hoc validity in the sense of the present paper, and indeed, it is not clear
a priori
what should be meant by “post-hoc sequential inference.” In this section, we provide definitions for post-hoc asymptotic sequential inference and derive explicit procedures satisfying those definitions.

4.1
Definitions and desiderata

In the definitions to follow, we will frequently rely on the notion of an “upper triangular array”, an object that frequently appears in time-uniform asymptotics
[
61
]
. An upper triangular array of random variables is a doubly indexed sequence
((X_{k}^{(m)})_{k\geq m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
, so called because it can be visually depicted as

\begin{aligned} &X_{1}^{(1)},\ &X_{2}^{(1)},\ &X_{3}^{(1)},&\dots{\\
}&&X_{2}^{(2)},\ &X_{3}^{(2)},&\dots{\\
}&&&X_{3}^{(3)},\ &\dots{\\
}&&&&\ddots\end{aligned}
asymptotics
time-uniformity

where “asymptotics” are with respect to
m
tending to infinity and “time-uniformity” is with respect to those indices
k\geq m
. With this language in mind, consider the following definition.

Definition 4.1

(Asymptotic post-hoc confidence sequences and anytime p-values)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of distributions and
f:\mathbb{N}\to(0,1)
a nonincreasing function. We say that the collection of upper triangular arrays
((\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(m)}(\alpha))_{k\geq m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
\alpha\in(0,1)
, is a distribution-uniform
asymptotic post-hoc confidence sequence
(
aph-cs
) for the parameters
(\theta(P))_{P\in\mathcal{P}}
and that it has a range growth function
f
if for any stopping time
\tau
, it holds that

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\sup_{\alpha>f(m)}\frac{\mathbf{1}\left\{\theta(P)\notin\mathcal{H}_{\tau\lor m}^{(m)}(\alpha)\right\}}{\alpha}\right]\leq 1.

(4.1)

Similarly, for a subset of distributions
\mathcal{P}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
, we say that the upper triangular array
((p_{k}^{(m)})_{k\geq m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
\mathcal{P}_{0}
-uniform
asymptotic post-hoc anytime p-value
with range growth function
f
if for any stopping time
\tau
,

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\sup_{\alpha>f(m)}\frac{\mathds{1}\{p_{\tau\lor m}^{(m)}\leq\alpha\}}{\alpha}\right]\leq 1.

(4.2)

Let us briefly parse Definition
4.1
, turning our attention to the display (
4.1
). First suppose that the guarantee in (
4.1
) holds for a fixed
m
, not only in the limit. In that case, the set
\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{\tau}
is a post-hoc confidence interval at all stopping times
\tau
taking values at least
m
but only for those
\alpha
such that
\alpha>f(m)
. That is, we imagine an analyst committing to first gathering
m
observations and performing an analysis. On the basis of those results, they may continue sampling and the results will remain valid (i.e., the risk will remain bounded by one as long as
\alpha>f(m)
). In other words,
\mathcal{H}^{(m)}_{\tau}
satisfies optional continuation after an initial burn-in period
m
.
The asymptotic guarantee of an
aph-cs
is simply one that holds as the burn-in period
m
tends to
\infty
.

Given the definitions of post-hoc asymptotic sequential inference procedures above, it is natural to ask:
What is the sequential analogue of an asymptotic e-value?
Similar to how e-processes
[
41
, §7]
are the sequential analogue of e-values, the following definition of an asymptotic e-process serves as a sequential analogue of an asymptotic e-value. While asymptotic e-values and non-asymptotic e-processes have appeared and been studied in the literature, the following definition appears to be new.

Definition 4.2

(Asymptotic e-processes)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of distributions. We say that an upper triangular array
((E_{k}^{(m)})_{k\geq m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is a distribution-uniform
asymptotic
\mathcal{P}
-e-process
if for any stopping time
\tau
, it holds that

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[E_{\tau\lor m}^{(m)}\right]\leq 1.

(4.3)

Similar to how asymptotic e-values are necessary and sufficient for the construction of asymptotic post-hoc confidence intervals and p-values, asymptotic e-processes are necessary and sufficient for the construction of the procedures described in Definition
4.1
. The following proposition makes the previous statement formal and serves as a sequential analogue of Proposition
2.6
.

Proposition 4.3

(On the necessity and sufficiency of asymptotic e-processes)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(m)}(\cdot)
forms an
aph-cs
for
\theta^{\star}
. Then there exists a family of functions
(E_{k}^{(m)}(\theta))_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}
so that for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(m)}(\alpha)
can be written as

\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(m)}(\alpha)=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}:E_{k}^{(m)}(\theta)<1/\alpha\right\},

(4.4)

and
E_{k}^{(m)}(\theta^{\star})
forms a post-hoc asymptotic
\mathcal{P}
-e-process. Conversely, if
E_{k}^{(m)}(\theta^{\star})
forms an asymptotic
\mathcal{P}
-e-process, then the set defined by (
4.4
) for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
forms an
aph-cs
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(m)}(\cdot)
be an
aph-cs
for
\theta^{\star}
. For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and any
k,m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k\geq m
, define

E_{k}^{(m)}(\theta):=\sup\left\{\frac{1}{\alpha}:\alpha>0\text{ and }\theta\notin\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(m)}(\alpha)\right\}=\sup_{\alpha>0}\frac{\mathbf{1}\left\{\theta\notin\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(m)}(\alpha)\right\}}{\alpha}.

(4.5)

By the property of
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(m)}(\alpha)
forming an
aph-cs
for
\theta^{\star}
, it holds that
E_{k}^{(m)}(\theta^{\star})
is an asymptotic e-process. The remainder of the proof proceeds identically to the case-by-case analysis found in the proof of Proposition
2.6
.
∎

Note that all of the definitions stated in this section are in the distribution-uniform setting. One can of course give pointwise analogues if desired by taking the collection of distributions
\mathcal{P}
to be a singleton, but we will continue to work in the uniform setting as the
aph-cs
we present in the next section naturally satisfies this criterion.

4.2
An asymptotic post-hoc mixture confidence sequence

Here, we observe that
r-ws
asymptotic e-variable of Section
3.4
can be used to define an asymptotic e-process. The proof can be found in Appendix
C.8
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Fix
\delta>0
and suppose that that
(X_{n})
are iid with a
\mathcal{P}
-uniformly bounded
(2+\delta)
th moment for some
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2+\delta}(\theta)
.
For
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
, suppose
T_{n}=o(n^{\delta\gamma/2}/\log^{2+\delta/2}n)
.
Define the upper triangular array of processes
(E_{k}^{(m)}(\theta))_{k\geq m}
by

E_{k}^{(m)}(\theta):=\int_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\exp\left\{\lambda S_{k}(\theta)-k(\hat{s}_{k}^{2}+\log^{-1}(m))\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\right\}\textnormal{d}F(\lambda)\land T_{m},

(4.6)

where
F
is any probability measure supported on a subset of
\mathbb{R}
.
Then
((E_{k}^{(m)}(\theta))_{k\geq m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform asymptotic e-process.

We may now combine Propositions
4.3
and
4.4
to provide the following
aph-cs
. Just as in Theorem
3.10
, we take
F
to be a centered Gaussian and
\gamma=0.49
. The mixture is then computed in precisely the same way as in the proof of Corollary
3.9
.

Theorem 4.5

(Post-hoc mixture confidence sequences and anytime p-values)
.

Let
(X_{n})
be a sequence of iid random variables with a
\mathcal{P}
-uniformly bounded
(2+\delta)
-th moment for some
\delta>0
and
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{2+\delta}(\mu)
.
Fix
C,\rho>0
and let
\hat{u}_{k}^{(m)}=k(\hat{s}_{k}^{2}+\log^{-1}m)
.
Then

\mathcal{H}_{k}^{(m)}(\alpha):=\left(\overline{X}_{k}\pm\sqrt{\frac{2\rho\hat{u}_{k}^{(m)}+2}{k^{2}\rho}\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{\rho\hat{u}_{k}^{(m)}+1}}{\alpha}\right)}\right),

(4.7)

forms a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform
aph-cs
for
\mu
with range growth function

.

Observe that (
4.7
) takes essentially the same form as some asymptotic confidence sequences of
Waudby-Smith et al. 2024a
. An alternative interpretation of Theorem
4.5
is that after a minor modification to the variance estimate, the aforementioned asymptotic confidence sequences also enjoy a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform post-hoc guarantee. From this perspective, while some of the methods introduced in this paper can be viewed as bespoke constructions for the sake of satisfying post-hoc risk control, Theorem

serves to “upgrade” the guarantees of an existing result in the literature.

5
Conclusions

We have extended the paradigm of asymptotic inference to the post-hoc setting, thus allowing the significance level to be chosen as a function of the data. There are both benefits and drawbacks to our approach. While significantly more flexible than traditional techniques, we obtain guarantees on risk control instead of error probabilities, the common currency of most inferential methods.

Our goal here has not been to argue against traditional inference, but instead to lay the foundations of an alternative statistical methodology that nevertheless retains rigorous frequentist guarantees. Instead of replacing standard p-values and confidence intervals, one could report them alongside their post-hoc variants studied here.

While our main focus has been on providing concrete constructions of post-hoc confidence intervals, p-values, and hypothesis tests, our results relied on innovations in the theory of asymptotic e-variables. In particular, we provided new asymptotic e-variables, introduced general methods for constructing them, and extended the scope of existing ones to the distribution-uniform setting.

We also introduced the notion of “post-hoc asymptotic sequential inference,” extending the notions of asymptotic e-variables and asymptotic post-hoc CIs and p-values to the sequential setting. In particular, we introduced asymptotic e-processes and post-hoc asymptotic confidence sequences and gave an example of each. Given the richness of post-hoc inference on one hand and sequential inference on the other, we anticipate that there are many interesting questions to be asked at their intersection.

For the practicing scientist interested in employing asymptotic post-hoc CIs, all of the
aph-ci
s that we have discussed have their benefits. The two
aph-ci
s based on the
iwr
asymptotic e-variable have the best performance, with
(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}})
instantiated with ex ante anchoring being the tightest
aph-ci
for reasonable ranges of
\alpha
and anchor
\alpha_{0}
, whereas
(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}})
is tighter in the worst case. The
aph-ci
based on the
r-ws
e-variable is slighter looser, but as we saw in Section
4
it provides a stronger guarantee, and providing flexibility not only in terms of post-hoc
\alpha
selection, but also in terms of sample size. Given the prevalence of optional continuation, we recommend the latter as the safest
aph-ci
in practice.

Overall, we hope to have fruitfully expanded the burgeoning area of post-hoc inference to the asymptotic setting, providing practitioners with more flexible inferential tools that allow for data-driven decision-making.

Appendix A
On Alternative Definitions of Post-Hoc CIs

Proposition
2.6
shows that post-hoc CIs which are constructed via thresholding a random variable
E
are valid if and only if
E
is an e-variable. One might ask if relaxing the definition of an e-variable results in a relaxed version of post-hoc CIs.

Consider the following definitions of
approximate
e-values and p-values, given originally by
Ignatiadis et al. 2024
. We say that
Q
is a
(\epsilon,\delta)
-approximate p-value for
\mathcal{P}
if

P(Q\leq\alpha)\leq(1+\epsilon)\alpha+\delta,\quad\text{ for all }\alpha\in(0,1)\text{ and all }P\in\mathcal{P}.

(A.1)

We say that
E
is a
(\epsilon,\delta)
-approximate e-value for
\mathcal{P}
if

\mathbb{E}_{P}[E\wedge m]\leq 1+\epsilon+\delta m,\quad\text{ for all }m>0\text{ and }P\in\mathcal{P}.

(A.2)

Following
Ignatiadis et al. 2024
,
let us say that
(Q_{n})
is a
weakly asymptotic p-value
for
\mathcal{P}
if for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
Q_{n}
is an
(\epsilon_{n}(P),\delta_{n}(P))
-approximate p-value for some
\epsilon_{n}(P)\to 0
,
\delta_{n}(P)\to 0
.
Define
weakly asymptotic e-variables
analogously. Note that
Ignatiadis et al. 2024
do not use the terminology “weakly.”
We emphasize that these definitions of asymptotic p-variables and e-variables differ from those presented in the main text. Indeed,
Ignatiadis et al. 2024
shows that weakly asymptotic e-variables are weaker (hence the name) notion than the asymptotic e-variables we use in the main text.

Based on approximate p-variables and e-variables, we might introduce the idea of an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-approximate post-hoc CI for the functional
\theta
as a set
\mathcal{H}(\alpha)
such that

\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{H};\epsilon,\delta,P)\leq 1,\text{~ where ~}\mathbf{R}(\mathcal{H};\epsilon,\delta,P)=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\sup_{\alpha>0}\frac{\mathbf{1}\left\{\theta(P)\notin\mathcal{H}(\alpha)\right\}}{(1+\epsilon)\alpha+\delta}\right].

(A.3)

The motivation is the same as in Section
2.1
: we begin with the approximate guarantee
P(\theta(P)\notin\mathcal{H}(\alpha))\leq(1+\epsilon)\alpha+\delta
and convert it to a post-hoc guarantee. We might then define a
weakly asymptotic post-hoc CI
as a sequence of sets
(\mathcal{H}_{n})
such that
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is an
(\epsilon_{n}(P),\delta_{n}(P))
-approximate post-hoc CI and
\epsilon_{n}(P),\delta_{n}(P)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
.

There are several questions one can ask about whether weakly asymptotic/approximate e-variables result in post-hoc CIs. Unfortunately, we show below that the answer to most of these question is no. Before we begin, it useful to note that the risk of an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-approximate post-hoc CI can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\sup_{\alpha>0}\frac{\mathbf{1}\left\{E^{\theta}\geq 1/\alpha\right\}}{(1+\epsilon)\alpha+\delta}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\sup_{\alpha\geq 1/E^{\theta}}\frac{1}{(1+\epsilon)\alpha+\delta}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\frac{1}{(1+\epsilon)1/E^{\theta}+\delta}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\frac{E^{\theta}}{1+\epsilon+\delta E^{\theta}}\right],

when the post-CI is of the form
\mathcal{H}(\alpha)=\{\theta:E^{\theta}<1/\alpha\}
, just as in Proposition
2.6
.

1. Do approximate e-variables result in approximate post-hoc CIs?

It appears not. Consider the
(0,\delta)
-approximate e-variable
E
defined by

P(E=1)=1-\delta,\quad P(E=u)=\delta,

for some large
u>0
. It’s easy to verify that
E
is
(0,\delta)
-approximate (hence
(\epsilon,\delta)
-approximate for any
\epsilon>0
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[E\wedge m]

\displaystyle=(1\wedge m)(1-\delta)+(u\wedge m)\delta\leq 1-\delta+m\delta\leq 1+\delta m.

However,
E
results in a risk strictly greater than 1. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\frac{E}{1+\epsilon+\delta E}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\delta}{1+\epsilon+\delta}+\frac{u\delta}{1+\epsilon+u\delta}\xrightarrow{u\to\infty}\frac{1-\delta}{1+\epsilon+\delta}+1>1,

which holds as long as
\delta>0
.

2. Do weakly asymptotic e-variables result in APH-CIs?

Here by
aph-ci
s we mean Definition
2.4
, i.e., the usual notion investigated in the rest of the paper. As above, the answer appears to be no.
Consider the sequence of random variables
(E_{n})
with
P(E_{n}=1)=1-\delta_{n}
and
P(E_{n}=u_{n})=\delta_{n}
where
\delta_{n}\to 0
and
u_{n}\gg 1/\delta_{n}
. Then as above,
\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}\wedge m]\leq 1+\delta_{n}m
for each
n
so
(E_{n})
is a weakly asymptotic e-variable. But

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\sup_{\alpha>0}\frac{\mathbf{1}\left\{E_{n}\geq 1/\alpha\right\}}{\alpha}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}]=1-\delta_{n}+u_{n}\delta_{n}\to\infty,

where the first equality follows from the proof of Proposition
2.6
.

3. Do weakly asymptotic e-variables result in weakly asymptotic post-hoc CIs?

Again, the answer is no. Using the same weakly asymptotic e-variables as above, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\frac{E_{n}}{1+\epsilon_{n}+\delta_{n}E_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\delta_{n}}{1+\epsilon_{n}+\delta_{n}}+\frac{u_{n}\delta_{n}}{1+\epsilon_{n}+u_{n}\delta_{n}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}2>1.

Appendix B
Additional Results

B.1
Two alternative asymptotic e-variables

Here we present two additional asymptotic e-variables. For parameters
\lambda,\eta
, and a sequence
(\lambda_{n})
define the objects

\displaystyle E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\theta;\lambda,\eta)

\displaystyle:=\exp\left(\frac{\lambda S_{n}(\theta)}{\sqrt{n}\widehat{\sigma}_{n}+\eta V_{n}(\theta)}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\right).

(B.1)

\displaystyle E_{n}^{\textsc{sn}}(\theta;\lambda^{n})

\displaystyle:=\exp\bigg(\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}(X_{i}-\theta)-\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}^{2}[(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}+2\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}]\bigg),

(B.2)

The superscript
sn
refers to “self-normalized,” named as such because it is based on, and proved using, the self-normalized process described in
Howard et al. 2020
. Note that it is defined using a sequence of parameters
(\lambda_{n})
instead of a single value and thus depends on weighted versions of
S_{n}(\theta)
and
V_{n}(\theta)
. We present
E_{n}^{\textsc{sn}}
out of independent interest in asymptotic e-variables. It will not result in a useful
aph-ci
.

Meanwhile,
reg
refers to “regularized,” because it is helpful to view the term
\eta V_{n}(\theta)
as a regularization term. Indeed, one might expect (or hope) that one could replace
V_{n}(\theta)
with
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}
in the
iwr
e-variable. In particular, we could consider
T_{n}:=\exp(\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{n}}S_{n}(\theta)/\widehat{\sigma}_{n}-\lambda^{2}/2)
. This is not the case, however: For Gaussian
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
,
\log(T_{n})
has a t-distribution and thus no MGF. Therefore
\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]
does not exist, let alone converge. Adding the term
\eta V_{n}(\theta)
to the denominator regularizes the growth of
(T_{n})
, ensuring that the sequence is uniformly integrable and an asymptotic e-variable.

Let us now state under what conditions these objects define asymptotic e-variables. The proof is in Appendix
C.9
.

Theorem B.1
.

Let
(\lambda_{n})
be a sequence of nonnegative scalars which converge to some finite
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
almost surely. Then:

1.

If
\lambda_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable for each
n
,
\eta_{n}\to\eta
almost surely,
\eta_{n}>0
for all
n
, and
|\lambda_{n}|\lesssim\eta_{n}
, then
(E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\theta;\lambda_{n},\eta_{n}))
is an asymptotic e-variable for all distributions in
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\theta)
.

2.

If
(\lambda_{n})
is predictable (i.e.,
\lambda_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
-measurable for each
n
) such that (i)
\sup_{n}\lambda_{n}<\infty
, (ii)
\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}^{2}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}L<\infty
a.s.  and (iii)
\lambda_{i}^{2}/\sum_{j\leq n}\lambda_{j}^{2}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
a.s. Then

(E_{n}^{\textsc{sn}}(\theta;\lambda_{n}))
is an asymptotic e-variable for
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\theta)
.

The above statements remain true if
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}
is replaced by the (biased) sample variance
s_{n}^{2}=n^{-1}\sum_{i\leq n}(X_{i}-\overline{X}_{n})^{2}
, or any other statistic which converges almost surely to the true variance.

It is worth emphasizing that for
E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}
, when we say that
\eta>0
is fixed, we mean both that it cannot be data-dependent, but also that it cannot depend on the sample size
n
. This is contrast to nonasymptotic inference, where a fixed parameter can be optimized depending on the sample size (in the Chernoff method, for instance).

We can also extend
(E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}})
to the distribution-uniform setting as follows. The proof is in Appendix
C.10
.

Theorem B.2
.

Let
(X_{n})
be iid with mean
\theta
and
\mathcal{P}
-uniformly bounded skew.
Let
(\lambda_{n})
be a bounded, deterministic sequence converging to
\lambda
. Suppose
0<\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sigma_{P}
and let
(\eta_{n})
be a deterministic sequence such that
\lambda_{n}\lesssim\eta_{n}
and
\eta_{n}>0
. Then
(E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\theta;\lambda_{n},\eta))
is a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform asymptotic e-variable.

B.2
An APH-CI based on
E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}

Next we state the
aph-ci
corresponding to the asymptotic e-variable
(E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}})
. One can perform the arithmetic to write down an
aph-ci
based on
(E_{n}^{\textsc{sn}})
, but it is the union of several disjoint intervals. Moreover, while one of these intervals contains the sample mean
\overline{X}_{n}
and behaves similarly to
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}
in terms of width, the outermost intervals behave oddly and go off to
\pm\infty
, making the resulting CI vacuous.

The proof of the following result is in Appendix
C.11
.

Theorem B.3
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be iid with finite variance and mean
\mu
.
For any
\lambda>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, set

A_{n,\alpha}(\lambda)=\frac{\log(2/\alpha)+\lambda^{2}/2}{\lambda\sqrt{n}}.

For any fixed
\lambda,\eta>0
both independent of
\alpha
,
(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\alpha;\lambda,\eta))
is an
aph-ci
for
\mathbb{E}X_{1}
, where

\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\alpha;\lambda,\eta):=(\overline{X}_{n}\pm W_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\alpha;\lambda,\eta)),\quad W_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\alpha;\lambda,\eta):=\frac{A_{n,\alpha}(\lambda)\widehat{\sigma}_{n}(1+\sqrt{B_{\alpha,\eta}(\lambda)})}{1-A_{n,\alpha}^{2}(\lambda)\eta^{2}},

(B.3)

and
B_{\alpha,\eta}(\lambda)=1-(1-A_{n,\alpha}^{2}(\lambda)\eta^{2})(1-\eta^{2}\frac{n-1}{n})
.

While both
\lambda
and
\eta
are allowed to vary with
n
in Theorem
B.1
for
E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}
, they have been fixed in Theorem
B.3
. This is because we would like both terms in
A_{n,\alpha}(\lambda)
to decay at the same rate, which forces
\lambda
to be constant. This in turn forces
\eta
to be constant since Theorem
B.1
requires
\lambda_{n}\lesssim\eta_{n}
.

Notice that by a single application of the SLLN and the continuous mapping theorem,

\sqrt{n}W_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\alpha;\lambda,\eta)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{a.s.}\sigma g(\lambda,\alpha)(1+\eta),

which is strictly larger than the limiting width of
(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}})
given in Section
3.1
since
\eta
must be positive. (Recall that
g(\lambda,\alpha)=\log(2/\alpha)/\lambda+\lambda/2
.) The performance of
(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{reg}})
is explored experimentally in Appendix
D
, as is the effect of
\eta
on the width of the bounds.

In practice, we recommend fixing
\eta=10^{-3}
. While
\eta
can, theoretically, be taken as small as desired, values smaller than
10^{-3}
have no noticeable effect on the width. Further, Proposition
B.7
in the following subsection demonstrates that small values of
\eta
increase the Type-I error of
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}
. Thus, values of
\eta
that are too small risk inflating error without any corresponding improvement in the width of the CI.

B.3
Exact asymptotic type-I error of the APH-CIs

The
aph-ci
s developed in the main text are designed to bound the post-hoc risk below 1. A natural secondary question is to evaluate their classical asymptotic Type-I error, i.e., the probability of miscoverage at a fixed (data-independent) significance level
\alpha
.

Let
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
with cdf denoted by
\Phi
. By the CLT and the LLN, the self-normalized ratio converges in distribution to a standard normal under any distribution
P\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(\theta^{\star})
:

\frac{S_{n}(\theta^{\star})}{V_{n}(\theta^{\star})}=\frac{S_{n}(\theta^{\star})/\sqrt{n}}{V_{n}(\theta^{\star})/\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow{d}Z.

We begin by studying the
aph-ci
defined by the
iwr
e-variable.

Proposition B.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\alpha;\lambda)=\{\theta:E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\theta;\lambda)<2/\alpha\text{ and }E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\theta;-\lambda)<2/\alpha\}
be the APH-CI induced by the
iwr
asymptotic e-value for any
\lambda>0
. Then:

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\big(\theta^{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\alpha;\lambda)\big)=2\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\right).

(B.4)

Proof.

Taking logarithms, the miscoverage event is equivalent to:

\lambda\frac{S_{n}(\theta^{\star})}{V_{n}(\theta^{\star})}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\geq\log(2/\alpha)\quad\text{or}\quad-\lambda\frac{S_{n}(\theta^{\star})}{V_{n}(\theta^{\star})}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\geq\log(2/\alpha),

which is equivalent to

\left|\frac{S_{n}(\theta^{\star})}{V_{n}(\theta^{\star})}\right|\geq\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda}{2}.

By the continuous mapping theorem, substituting the limit
S_{n}(\theta^{\star})/V_{n}(\theta^{\star})\xrightarrow{d}Z
yields:

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\big(\theta^{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}(\alpha;\lambda)\big)=2\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\right),

completing the argument.
∎

Note that if we set
\lambda_{\alpha}=\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}
, the asymptotic Type-I error evaluates to
2(1-\Phi(\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)}))
which is smaller than
\alpha
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, reflecting the price paid for post-hoc validity.

Next we study the
aph-ci
defined by the mixture of the
iwr
e-variable over a truncated Gaussian; see Section
3.3
.

Proposition B.5
.

The asymptotic Type-I error induced by
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}
is given by:

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\big(\theta^{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}(\alpha;R,\kappa)\big)=2(1-\Phi(y_{\alpha}^{\star})),

(B.5)

where
y_{\alpha}^{\star}
is the unique positive root of the implicit equation
I_{R}(y_{\alpha}^{\star},\frac{\kappa^{2}+1}{2\kappa^{2}})=Z_{R,\kappa}/\alpha
.

Proof.

By definition,

E_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}(\theta^{\star};R,\kappa)=Z_{R,\kappa}^{-1}I_{R}\left(\frac{S_{n}(\theta^{\star})}{V_{n}(\theta^{\star})},\frac{\kappa^{2}+1}{2\kappa^{2}}\right).

Because the function
y\mapsto I_{R}(y,u)
is continuous, we may apply the continuous mapping theorem to obtain:

E_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}(\theta^{\star};R,\kappa)\xrightarrow{d}Z_{R,\kappa}^{-1}I_{R}\left(Z,\frac{\kappa^{2}+1}{2\kappa^{2}}\right).

The asymptotic Type-I error is therefore given by:

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\big(\theta^{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}(\alpha;R,\kappa)\big)=P\left(I_{R}\left(Z,\frac{\kappa^{2}+1}{2\kappa^{2}}\right)\geq Z_{R,\kappa}/\alpha\right).

The function
I_{R}(y,u)
is symmetric about
y=0
and strictly monotonically increasing in
|y|
so the threshold inequality is satisfied if and only if
|Z|\geq y_{\alpha}^{\star}
, where
y_{\alpha}^{\star}
is the unique positive root of the implicit equation
I_{R}(y_{\alpha}^{\star},\frac{\kappa^{2}+1}{2\kappa^{2}})=Z_{R,\kappa}/\alpha
. Thus, the asymptotic Type-I error is:

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\big(\theta^{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}(\alpha;R,\kappa)\big)=2(1-\Phi(y_{\alpha}^{\star})).\qed

Finally, we study the
aph-ci
given by the
r-ws
asymptotic e-variable.

Proposition B.6
.

Under the setting of Theorem
3.10
, the asymptotic Type-I error induced by
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{r-ws}}}
vanishes:

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\big(\theta^{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{r-ws}}}(\alpha;\rho)\big)=0.

(B.6)

Proof.

Note that for sufficiently large
n
, the threshold condition
Cn^{\delta\cdot 0.24}\geq 1/\alpha
is satisfied and
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\alpha;\rho)=(\overline{X}_{n}\pm W_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\rho))
. The miscoverage event is therefore equivalent to:

\left|\overline{X}_{n}-\theta^{\star}\right|>W_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\rho).

After scaling by
\sqrt{n}/\sigma
, the miscoverage event can be written equivalently as:

\frac{\sqrt{n}\left|\overline{X}_{n}-\theta^{\star}\right|}{\sigma}>\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}W_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\rho).

By the CLT and the continuous mapping theorem, the left-hand side converges in distribution to
|Z|
. However, as noted in the main text, the entire scaled threshold diverges:

\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}W_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\rho)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{a.s.}\infty.

Let
B>0
be arbitary. For sufficiently large
n
, we have
\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}W_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\rho)\geq B
almost surely, which implies:

P\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}\left|\overline{X}_{n}-\theta^{\star}\right|}{\sigma}>\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}W_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\rho)\right)\leq P\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}\left|\overline{X}_{n}-\theta^{\star}\right|}{\sigma}>B\right).

The right-hand side converges to
2(1-\Phi(B))
, so that:

\limsup_{n\to\infty}P\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}\left|\overline{X}_{n}-\theta^{\star}\right|}{\sigma}>\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}W_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\rho)\right)\leq 2(1-\Phi(B)).

Since
B>0
is arbitrary, we conclude:

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\big(\theta^{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{r-ws}}}(\alpha;\rho)\big)=0.\qed

This exact zero error highlights the fundamental structural difference between an
aph-ci
and an
aph-cs
. Because the confidence sequence must grow by a
\sqrt{\log(n)}
factor to survive an infinite horizon of stopping times, its width shrinks only at a rate
\sqrt{\log(n)/n}
, which eventually far exceeds the actual
O_{P}(1/\sqrt{n})
sampling error, driving the Type-I error to zero.

Additionally, we study the asymptotic Type-I error of the
reg

aph-ci
introduced in Subsection
B.1
. The proof is essentially similar to that of Proposition
B.4
, noting that

\frac{S_{n}(\theta^{\star})}{\sqrt{n}\widehat{\sigma}_{n}+\eta V_{n}(\theta^{\star})}\xrightarrow{d}\frac{1}{1+\eta}Z

which follows by Slutsky’s theorem, so we omit it.

Proposition B.7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\alpha;\lambda,\eta)=\{\theta:E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\theta;\lambda,\eta)<2/\alpha\text{ and }E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\theta;-\lambda,\eta)<2/\alpha\}
be the APH-CI induced by the
reg
asymptotic e-value for any
\lambda,\eta>0
. Then:

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\big(\theta^{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\alpha;\lambda,\eta)\big)=2\left(1-\Phi\left(\left(1+\eta\right)\left(\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\right)\right).

(B.7)

B.4
APH-CIs via compound asymptotic e-variables

Compound e-variables arise naturally in multiple testing; for instance in the e-BH procedure
[
58
]
and derandomizing knockoffs
[
43
]
; in fact
[
31
]
show that compound e-values underlie
all
FDR controlling procedures. They refer to a collection of
K
nonnegative random variables
E^{j}
,
j=1,\dots,K
, as
compound e-variables
for
\mathcal{P}
if
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E^{j}]\leq K
. We thus refer to
K

collections
of random variables
(E_{n}^{j})
,
j=1,\dots,K
as
asymptotic
compound e-variables for
\mathcal{P}
if

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j\leq K}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}^{j}]\leq K.

(B.8)

Likewise,
(E_{n}^{j})
are
\mathcal{P}
-uniform asymptotic compound e-variables for
\mathcal{P}
if

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{j\leq K}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}^{j}]\leq K.

(B.9)

Asymptotic compound e-variables yield
aph-ci
s as follows.

Proposition B.8
.

For
j=1,\dots,K
and each
\theta\in\Theta
,
let
(E_{n}^{j}(\theta))
be a collection of random variables. For all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
\mathcal{H}_{n}(\alpha)=\{\theta:E_{n}^{j}(\theta)<K/\alpha\text{ for all }j\in[K]\}
. Then
(\mathcal{H}_{n})
is a (uniform)
aph-ci
for
\theta^{\star}
if
(E_{n}^{j}(\theta^{\star}))
are (uniform) asymptotic compound e-variables.

Proof.

The argument is similar to Proposition
2.6
. For each
n
and
\alpha
, if
\cup_{j}\{E_{n}^{j}(\theta^{\star})\geq K/\alpha\}
occurs, then
1/\alpha\leq E_{n}^{j}(\theta^{\star})/K
for some
j
, hence

\frac{\mathbf{1}\left\{\cup_{j}\{E_{n}^{j}(\theta^{\star})\geq K/\alpha\}\right\}}{\alpha}\leq\max_{j}\frac{E_{n}^{j}(\theta^{\star})}{K}\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j}E_{n}^{j}(\theta^{\star}).

Taking expectations and limits,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\sup_{\alpha}\frac{\mathbf{1}\left\{\theta^{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}(\alpha)\right\}}{\alpha}\right]\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}^{j}(\theta^{\star})]\leq 1,

completing the argument. In the distribution-uniform setting we simply take a supremum over distribution before taking limits.
∎

In this work we typically apply this result when
(E^{1}_{n})
and
(E^{2}_{n})
are simply two e-variables (e.g., in Theorem
3.2
).

B.5
Distribution-uniform convergence in expectation

Here we present sufficient conditions under which a sequence of nonnegative random variables constitute a distribution-uniform asymptotic e-variable. In the pointwise setting, to construct asymptotic e-variables we rely on
Billingsley 1995
which gives conditions under which a sequence of random variables converges in expectation. In particular, he shows that uniform integrability together with convergence in distribution implies convergence in expectation. Since convergence in distribution of a sequence of random variables
(X_{n})
to
X
is equivalent to
\mathbb{E}[\min(X_{n},K)]\to\mathbb{E}[\min(X,K)]
for all
K>0
, the following result can be seen as an extension of Billingsley’s result to the distribution-uniform setting.

Proposition B.9
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of distributions and for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
let
(T_{n,P})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of statistics depending on
P
. Let
Z
be a random variable with law
R
such that
\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)]<\infty
. Suppose further that

1.

\lim_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n,P})\mathbf{1}\left\{g(T_{n,P})\geq K\right\}]=0
, and

2.

for all
K>0
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n,P})\wedge K]\leq\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)\wedge K].

Then

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n,P})]\leq\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)].

(B.10)

Moreover, if condition (2) is replaced by for all
K>0
,
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}|\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n,P})\wedge K]-\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)\wedge K]|=0
, then we can conclude that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}|\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n,P})]-\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)]|=0
.
The same statements hold if
\limsup_{n}
is replaced by
\sup_{n}
everywhere.

Proof.

Throughout the proof we write
T_{n}
instead of
T_{n,P}
;
P
should be understood from context. For any
x
and
K>0
, note that we can write
g(x)=g(x)\wedge K+0\wedge(g(x)-K)\leq g(x)\wedge K+g(x)\mathbf{1}\left\{g(x)\geq K\right\}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n})]\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n})\wedge K]+\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(T_{n})\mathbf{1}\left\{g(T_{n})\geq K\right\}].

Taking the supremum over all
P
and then the limit as
n\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n})]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n}\sup_{P}\left(\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n})\wedge K]+\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(T_{n})\mathbf{1}\left\{g(T_{n})\geq K\right\}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)\wedge K]+\limsup_{n}\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(T_{n})\mathbf{1}\left\{g(T_{n})\geq K\right\}]

using assumption (1). Letting
K\to\infty
the second term on the right hand side above vanishes by assumption (2) and the first converges to
\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)]
by the monotone convergence theorem. If we replace assumption (2) with
\limsup_{n}\sup_{P}|\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n})\wedge K]-\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)]|=0
for each
K>0
, a similar proof applies. By the triangle inequality, write

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n})-\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)]]

\displaystyle\leq|\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n})\wedge K]-\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)\wedge K]|

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n})\mathbf{1}\left\{g(T_{n})\geq K\right\}]+\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)\mathbf{1}\left\{g(Z)\geq K\right\}].

Hence,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}\sup_{P}|\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n})-\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)]]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n}\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n})\mathbf{1}\left\{g(T_{n})\geq K\right\}]+\mathbb{E}_{R}[g(Z)\mathbf{1}\left\{g(Z)\geq K\right\}].

Letting
K\to\infty
, both terms on the right of the inequality vanish—the first by assumption and the second by integrability of
g(Z)
. This completes the argument.
∎

B.6
Generalized sufficient conditions for the mixture process

Here we give a mild generalization of Proposition
3.3
, by weakening the sufficient conditions for a mixture process to be an asymptotic e-variable. We comment more on the scope of the generalization after stating the result.

Proposition B.10
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}
and let
\pi
be any (data-free) probability distribution on
\Lambda
. Suppose that for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
, the sequence
(E_{n}(\lambda))
is an asymptotic e-variable for some set of distributions
\mathcal{P}
. Assume that for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
, there exists a measurable function
h_{P}:\Lambda\to[0,\infty)
such that:

•

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]\leq h_{P}(\lambda)
for
\pi
-almost all
\lambda
(domination),

•

\int_{\Lambda}h_{P}(\lambda)\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda)<\infty
(integrability).

Then, the mixture process defined by

E_{n}(\pi):=\int_{\Lambda}E_{n}(\lambda)\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda),

(B.11)

is an asymptotic e-variable for
\mathcal{P}
.
Further, if
\sup_{P}h_{P}
is measurable and integrable, i.e.,
\int_{\Lambda}h_{P}(\lambda)\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda)<\infty
, then the mixture
(E_{n}(\pi))
is a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform asymptotic e-variable.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}
. By Tonelli’s Theorem (since
E_{n}(\lambda)
is non-negative), we can exchange the expectation and the mixture integral:

\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\pi)]=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\int_{\Lambda}E_{n}(\lambda)\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda)\right]=\int_{\Lambda}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda).

We can now apply Reverse Fatou’s Lemma to the sequence of functions
\lambda\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]
, which states that if such a sequence is dominated by an integrable function, then the limit superior of the mixture is less than or equal to the mixture of their limit superiors:

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Lambda}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda)\leq\int_{\Lambda}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda).

Since
\left(E_{n}(\lambda)\right)
is an asymptotic e-variable for each
\lambda
, we have
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]\leq 1
for all
\lambda
. Therefore:

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Lambda}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda)\leq\int_{\Lambda}1\cdot\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda)=1.

Since
P\in\mathcal{P}
was arbitrary, the result holds for the entire class
\mathcal{P}
.

In the distribution-uniform case, the proof is largely unchanged after noting that

\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\pi)]=\sup_{P}\int_{\Lambda}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda)\leq\int_{\Lambda}\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda).

Since
g(\lambda)=\sup_{P}h(\lambda)
is measurable by assumption, we may apply Reverse Fatou’s lemma to
\lambda\mapsto\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]\leq g(\lambda)
, which gives

\limsup_{n}\int_{\Lambda}\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda)\leq\int_{\Lambda}\limsup_{n}\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}(\lambda)]\textnormal{d}\pi(\lambda)\leq 1,

by definition of a
\mathcal{P}
-uniform asymptotic e-variable. This completes the proof.
∎

The two conditions of domination and integrability in Proposition
3.3
are strictly less prohibitive than requiring that
(\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}E_{n}(\lambda))
be uniformly integrable (which is what is required in Proposition
3.3
). Indeed, consider
X\sim N(0,1)
,
\Lambda=\mathbb{R}
,
\pi=N(0,1)
and let

E(\lambda)=\exp\left(\lambda X-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\right).

Then
\sup_{\lambda}E(\lambda)=\exp\left(X^{2}/2\right)
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{X^{2}}{2}\right)\right]=\infty
, so it is not uniformly integrable. However,
\mathbb{E}[E(\lambda)]=1
so we can simply choose
h(\lambda)=1
in Proposition
3.3
and conclude that
E_{n}(\pi)
is an e-variable.

B.7
Further details on the truncated Gaussian mixture

First let us present the asymptotic e-variable that one obtains from mixing the
iwr
e-variable according to Corollary
3.5
. We do the same for the
reg
e-variable presented in Appendix
B.1
.

Proposition B.11
.

For any fixed
R,\kappa,\eta>0
, define
Z_{R,\kappa}:=\kappa\sqrt{2\pi}(\Phi(R/\kappa)-\Phi(-R/\kappa))
. Then
(E_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}(\theta;R,\kappa))
and
(E_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{reg}}}(\theta;R,\kappa,\eta))
are asymptotic e-variables for all distributions in
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\theta)
, where

E_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}}(\theta;R,\kappa):=Z_{R,\kappa}^{-1}I_{R}\left(\frac{S_{n}(\theta)}{V_{n}(\theta)},\frac{\kappa^{2}+1}{2\kappa^{2}}\right),

(B.12)

and

E_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{reg}}}(\theta;R,\kappa,\eta):=Z_{R,\kappa}^{-1}I_{R}\left(\frac{S_{n}(\theta)}{\sqrt{n}\widehat{\sigma}_{n}+\eta V_{n}(\theta)},\frac{\kappa^{2}+1}{2\kappa^{2}}\right).

(B.13)

Proof.

Both
E_{n}^{{\textsc{iwr}}}
and
E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}
can be written as
E_{n}(\lambda)=\exp(\lambda M-\lambda^{2}/2)
for some
M
. Let
\rho
be the density of a truncated Gaussian on
[-R,R]
with mean 0 and variance
\kappa^{2}
, i.e.,

\rho(\xi)=Z_{R,\kappa}^{-1}\exp\left(\frac{-\xi^{2}}{2\kappa^{2}}\right)\text{~ where ~}Z_{R,\kappa}:=\sqrt{2\pi\kappa^{2}}\left(\Phi\left(\frac{R}{\kappa}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{-R}{\kappa}\right)\right),

(B.14)

where
\Phi
is the cdf of the standard normal.
Letting
u=1/2+1/(2\kappa^{2})
, observe that

\displaystyle E_{n}(\rho)=\int_{-R}^{R}E_{n}(\lambda)\rho(\lambda)\textnormal{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=Z_{R,\kappa}^{-1}\int_{-R}^{R}\exp\left(\lambda M-\lambda^{2}u\right)\textnormal{d}\lambda.

Completing the square, write

\exp(\lambda M-\lambda^{2}u)=\exp\left(-u\left(\lambda-\frac{M}{2u}\right)^{2}\right)\exp\left(\frac{M^{2}}{4u}\right),

and let
t=\sqrt{2u}(\lambda-M/(2u))
so that
\textnormal{d}t=\sqrt{2u}\textnormal{d}\lambda
. Set
t_{2}=\sqrt{2u}(R-M/(2u))
and
t_{1}=-\sqrt{2u}(R+M/(2u))
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{-R}^{R}\exp(\lambda M-\lambda^{2}u)\textnormal{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2u}}\exp\left(\frac{M^{2}}{4u}\right)\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\exp(-t^{2}/2)\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2u}}\exp\left(\frac{M^{2}}{4u}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\exp(-t^{2}/2)\textnormal{d}t-\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\exp(-t^{2}/2)\textnormal{d}t\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{u}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2u}}\exp\left(\frac{M^{2}}{4u}\right)\left(\Phi(t_{2})-\Phi(t_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=I_{R}(M,u),

using that
\Phi(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{z}\exp(-t^{2}/2)\textnormal{d}t
. Therefore,
E_{n}(\rho)=Z_{R,\kappa}^{-1}I_{R}(M,u)
. Substituting
M=S_{n}(\theta)/V_{n}(\theta)
for
E_{n}^{\textsc{iwr}}
and
M=S_{n}(\theta)/(\sqrt{n}\widehat{\sigma}_{n}+\eta V_{n}(\theta))
for
E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}
completes the proof.
∎

Inverting the asymptotic e-variable
(E_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{iwr}}})
above gives rise to Theorem
3.6
.
Let us state the equivalent result for
(E_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{reg}}})
.

Theorem B.12
.

Fix any
R,\kappa,\eta>0
and let
Z_{R,\kappa}=\kappa\sqrt{2\pi}(\Phi(R/\kappa)-\Phi(-R/\kappa))
.
(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{reg}}}(R,\kappa,\eta))
is an
aph-ci
where
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{reg}}}(R,\kappa,\eta)=(\overline{X}_{n}\pm W_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{reg}}}(R,\kappa,\eta))
with

\quad W_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{reg}}}(R,\kappa,\eta)=\frac{\sqrt{n}\cdot s_{n}y^{\star}_{\alpha}(1+\eta D_{\alpha,\eta})}{n-(\eta y_{\alpha}^{\star})^{2}},

(B.15)

where
D_{\alpha,\eta}=\sqrt{1+(y_{\alpha}^{\star})^{2}(1-\eta^{2})/n}
and

y_{\alpha}^{\star}
solves
I_{R}(y_{\alpha}^{\star},(\kappa^{2}+1)/(2\kappa^{2}))=Z_{R,\kappa}/\alpha
. If the denominator in (
B.15
) is non-positive, we take the CI to be all of
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Now we turn to
E_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{reg}}}
. We use a version of
E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}
with
\hat{s}_{n}
replacing
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}
in the denominator, which is valid by Theorem
B.3
.
Let
w_{n}=\sqrt{n}s_{n}
.
The equation
|S_{n}(\theta)/V_{n}(\theta)|<y^{\star}
thus becomes

n|\Delta|-y^{\star}w_{n}<y^{\star}\eta\sqrt{w_{n}^{2}+n\Delta^{2}}.

If
n|\Delta|<y^{\star}w_{n}
then
\theta\in(\overline{X}_{n}\pm y^{\star}w_{n}/n)
, which is thus part of our solution.
If
n\Delta\geq y^{\star}w_{n}
then both terms in the above display are nonnegative so we can square both sides which eventually yields the inequality

(n^{2}-(y^{\star}\eta)^{2}n)\Delta^{2}-2ny^{\star}w_{n}\Delta+(y^{\star}w_{n})^{2}-(y^{\star}\eta)^{2}w_{n}^{2}<0.

After a significant amount of arithmetic, the roots of this polynomial are given by

\Delta_{\pm}=\frac{y^{\star}w_{n}\pm y^{\star}w_{n}\eta\sqrt{1+(y^{\star})^{2}(1-\eta^{2})/n}}{n-(y^{\star}\eta)^{2}}=\frac{y^{\star}w_{n}(1\pm\eta D)}{n-(y^{\star}\eta)^{2}},

where
D=D_{\alpha,\eta}
. Conservatively taking the positive root as it defines a larger interval, we obtain

\theta\in\left(\overline{X}_{n}\pm\frac{y^{\star}w_{n}(1\pm\eta D)}{n-(y^{\star}\eta)^{2}}\right),

which is a superset of
(\overline{X}_{n}\pm y^{\star}w_{n}/n)
.
∎

The performance of
(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{reg}}})

\eta
.

Next let us analyze the limiting behavior of
y_{\alpha}^{\star}
so that we can relate the width of

and
W_{n}^{{\textsc{mix}},{\textsc{reg}}}
to that of the Wald interval.

Lemma B.13
.

For
\kappa=1
, we have that
y_{\alpha}^{\star}\xrightarrow{R\to\infty}2\sqrt{\log(\sqrt{2}/\alpha)}
where
y^{\star}
satisfies
I_{R}(y^{\star},1)=Z_{R,1}/\alpha
and
Z_{R,\kappa}=\kappa\sqrt{2\pi}(\Phi(R/\kappa)-\Phi(-R/\kappa))
.

Proof.

Let
g_{1}(y)=\sqrt{2}R-y/\sqrt{2}
and
g_{2}(y)=-\sqrt{2}R-y/\sqrt{2}
. Then

I_{R}(y,1)=\sqrt{\pi}\exp\left(\frac{y^{2}}{4}\right)(\Phi(g_{1}(y))-\Phi(g_{2}(y)).

Setting
I_{R}(y,1)=Z_{R,1}/\alpha
and taking logs, we obtain

\displaystyle y_{\alpha}^{\star}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\log(Z_{R,1}/\alpha)-\log(\sqrt{\pi})-\log(\Phi(g_{1}(y_{\alpha}^{\star})-\Phi(g_{2}(y_{\alpha}^{\star}))}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\log(\sqrt{2}/\alpha)+\log(\Phi(R)-\Phi(-R))-\log(\Phi(g_{1}(y_{\alpha}^{\star})-\Phi(g_{2}(y_{\alpha}^{\star}))}

Now, as

. Furthermore,

as

and
g_{2}(y_{\alpha}^{\star})\to 0
. To see this, note that either

goes to
\infty
, 0, or stays bounded as

grows (it cannot be negative). And in each case we get the desired behavior on

and
g_{2}
. Therefore,
-\log(\Phi(g_{1}(y_{\alpha}^{\star}))-\Phi(g_{2}(y_{\alpha}^{\star})))\to 0
and we conclude that

completing the argument.

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(CM^{2}).

Then we can apply de la Vallée Poussin’s theorem as above, which proves (iii).

\lim_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{n}\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(T_{n};\lambda)\mathbf{1}\left\{g(T_{n};\lambda)\geq K\right\}.

Therefore, in order to show that (

As we did in the pointwise case, we may apply Theorem 2.5 from

Lemma C.2

(A nonasymptotic SLLN)
.

Let

\theta

P\left[\sup_{k\geq m}\left\lvert\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}Z_{i}-\theta\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right]\leq c_{\gamma}\exp\{-m^{1-\gamma}\}+\frac{451}{\epsilon^{2}}U\left(\frac{\epsilon m^{\gamma}}{6.13}\right),

(C.16)

where
c_{\gamma}=(3-4\gamma)/(2-4\gamma)

Now let us proceed to the proof. Recall the definition of
U_{n}^{\otimes}
from (
3.13
) and note that
\mathbb{E}[U_{n}^{\otimes}]=n\sigma^{2}
.
Further note that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\overline{X}_{n})^{2}+(\overline{X}_{n}-\theta)^{2},

for all

\displaystyle\hat{s}_{n}^{2}-\frac{U_{n}^{\otimes}}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}-\sigma^{2}\right)-(\overline{X}_{n}-\theta)^{2}.

Taking absolute values, a union bound gives

P\left(\bigg|\hat{s}_{n}^{2}-\frac{U_{n}^{\otimes}}{n}\bigg|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq P\left(\frac{2}{3}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}-\sigma^{2}\right|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)+P\left((\overline{X}_{n}-\theta)^{2}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right).

We may apply Lemma
C.2
to both terms on the right hand side of the above display. This gives

\displaystyle P\left(\frac{2}{3}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}-\sigma^{2}\right|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}-\sigma^{2}\right|\geq\frac{3\epsilon}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(-n^{1-2\gamma})+\frac{(4/3)^{2}\cdot 451}{\epsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[|X_{1}-\theta|^{2}\mathbf{1}\left\{|X_{1}-\theta|^{2}\geq\frac{3\epsilon n^{\gamma}}{4}\right\}\right]

where
A_{\delta}=(4/3)^{2}\cdot 451\cdot(4\cdot 38/3)^{\delta/2}
and
the final inequality uses that
\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}\mathbf{1}\left\{Z\geq u\right\}]\leq u^{-\delta}\mathbb{E}[Z^{2+\delta}]
for any nonnegative random variable
Z
(on
\{Z\geq u\}

). Note that here we’re using that

\displaystyle P\left((\overline{X}_{n}-\theta)^{2}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(|\overline{X}_{n}-\theta|\geq\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(-n^{1-2\gamma})+\frac{902}{\epsilon}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[|X_{1}-\theta|\mathbf{1}\left\{|X_{1}-\theta|\geq\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon/2}\cdot n^{\gamma}}{38}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(-n^{1-2\gamma})+\frac{902}{\epsilon}\left(\frac{38\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}n^{\gamma}}\right)^{1+\delta}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[|X_{1}-\theta|^{2+\delta}\right].

\displaystyle P\left(\bigg|\hat{s}_{n}^{2}-\frac{U_{n}^{\otimes}}{n}\bigg|\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\exp(-n^{1-2\gamma})+\left(\frac{A_{\delta}}{n^{\delta\gamma/2}\epsilon^{2+\delta/2}}+\frac{B_{\delta}}{\epsilon^{(3+\delta)/2}n^{(1+\delta)\gamma}}\right)\mathbb{E}_{P}|X_{1}-\theta|^{2+\delta},

Define the “good” event,

On

we have that

, hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}^{\textsc{r-ws}}(\theta;\epsilon_{n},T_{n})\mathbf{1}\left\{A_{n}\right\}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\left(\int E_{n}^{\star}(\lambda)\textnormal{d}F\wedge T_{n}\right)\mathbf{1}\left\{A_{n}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\int E_{n}^{\star}(\lambda)\textnormal{d}F\right]=\int\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}^{\star}(\lambda)]\textnormal{d}F\leq 1,

where the final equality follows from Tonelli’s theorem and the final inequality comes from the fact that

is a nonnegative supermartingale with initial value 1.
Therefore, letting

for brevity,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}\mathbf{1}\left\{A_{n}\right\}]+\mathbb{E}_{P}[E_{n}\mathbf{1}\left\{A_{n}^{c}\right\}]\leq 1+T_{n}P(A_{n}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq 1+\left(4\exp(-n^{1-2\gamma})+\left(\frac{A_{\delta}}{\epsilon_{n}^{2+\delta/2}n^{\delta\gamma/2}}+\frac{B_{\delta}}{\epsilon_{n}^{(3+\delta)/2}n^{(1+\delta)\gamma}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{1}-\theta|^{2+\delta}\right]\right)\cdot T_{n}.

C.9
Proof of Theorem
B.1

We take each asymptotic e-variable in turn.

C.9.1
(E_{n}^{\textsc{sn}})
is an asymptotic e-value.

This asymptotic e-value is based on the following result from
Howard et al. 2020
. See also
Chugg et al. 2025
for a statement which more closely resembles our presentation here (though in the multivariate setting), as well as a direct proof.

Lemma C.3
.

For any predictable sequence
(\lambda_{n})
taking values in
\mathbb{R}
, (i.e.,
\lambda_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}=\sigma(X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1})
-measurable),
the process
(E_{n}^{\star})_{n}
given by

E_{n}^{\star}(\lambda):=\exp\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(X_{i}-\theta)-\frac{1}{2}V_{n}^{\otimes}\right\},

(C.18)

is a test supermartingale, where
V_{n}^{\otimes}
is given by

V_{n}^{\otimes}:=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}((X_{i}-\theta)^{2}+2\sigma^{2}).

(C.19)

In particular,
E_{\tau}^{\star}
is an e-value at any stopping time
\tau
.

Recall that we’re trying to prove that

E_{n}^{\textsc{sn}}(\theta;\lambda^{n})=\mathbb{E}_{P}\exp\left\{\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}(X_{i}-\theta)-\sum_{i\leq n}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{6}[(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}+2\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}]\right\},

defines an asymptotic e-value. Write

\lambda_{i}(X_{i}-\theta)-\frac{1}{6}\lambda_{i}^{2}[(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}+2\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}]=\lambda_{i}(X_{i}-\theta)-\frac{1}{6}\lambda_{i}^{2}[(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}+2\sigma^{2}]+\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{3}(\sigma^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}),

so

\mathbb{E}_{P}\exp\left\{\lambda_{i}(X_{i}-\theta)-\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{6}[(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}+2\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}]|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right\}\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}\exp\left\{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{3}(\sigma^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2})\right\},

(C.20)

hence the law of total expectation gives

\mathbb{E}_{P}\exp\left\{\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}(X_{i}-\theta)-\sum_{i\leq n}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{6}[(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}+2\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}]\right\}\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}\exp\left\{\sum_{i\leq n}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{3}(\sigma^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2})\right\}.

It thus remains to show that the term on the right has limit at most 1. This is done via the following lemma.

Lemma C.4
.

Suppose that (i)
\sup_{n}\lambda_{n}<\infty
, (ii)
\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}^{2}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}L<\infty
a.s.  and (iii)
\lambda_{i}^{2}/\sum_{j\leq n}\lambda_{j}^{2}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
a.s. Then

\lim_{n}\mathbb{E}_{P}\exp\bigg(\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}^{2}(\sigma^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2})\bigg)=1.

(C.21)

Proof.

Let
A_{n}=\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}^{2}
.
We claim that the term
A_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}
goes to
\sigma^{2}
almost surely. If so, then by the continuous mapping theorem,

\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}^{2}(\sigma^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2})=A_{n}\left(\sigma^{2}-\frac{\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}}{A_{n}}\right)\xrightarrow{a.s.}L(\sigma^{2}-\sigma^{2})=0.

Since
\sup_{n}\sum_{i\leq n}(\sigma^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2})\leq\sup_{n}\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}^{2}\sigma^{2}<\infty
by assumption, we may apply the dominated convergence theorem to conclude that

\lim_{n}\mathbb{E}_{P}\exp\bigg(\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}^{2}(\sigma^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2})\bigg)=\mathbb{E}_{P}\exp\bigg(\frac{1}{3}\lim_{n}\sum_{i\leq n}\lambda_{i}^{2}(\sigma^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2})\bigg)=1.

Now it remains to show that
\sum_{i\leq n}w_{i,n}\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\sigma^{2}
, where
w_{i,n}=\lambda_{i}^{2}/A_{n}
. Fix
\epsilon>0
and let
\omega
be a sample path on which
\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}\to\sigma^{2}
and
w_{i,n}\to 0
. Then there exists some
N=N_{\epsilon,\omega}
such that
|\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}(\omega)-\sigma^{2}|\leq\epsilon
for all
i>N
. Therefore, noting that
\sum_{i\leq n}w_{i,n}=1
,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i\leq n}w_{i,n}\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}(\omega)-\sigma^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\leq n}w_{i,n}|\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}(\omega)-\sigma^{2}|\leq\sum_{i\leq N}w_{i,n}|\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}(\omega)-\sigma^{2}|+\epsilon\sum_{N<i\leq n}w_{i,n}.

The final sum is at most
\epsilon
since
\sum_{N<i\leq n}w_{i,n}\leq\sum_{i\leq n}w_{i,n}=1
. Letting
W_{N}=\max_{i\leq N}|\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}(\omega)-\sigma^{2}|
, the penultimate sum can be bounded as

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq N}w_{i,n}|\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}(\omega)-\sigma^{2}|

\displaystyle\leq N\cdot W_{N}\cdot\max_{i\leq N}w_{i,n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty},

since
w_{i,n}=\lambda_{i}^{2}/A_{n}\to 0
for each fixed
i
by assumption. Since
\omega
lies in a set which occurs with probability 1, we conclude that
\sum_{i\leq n}w_{i,n}\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}\to\sigma
with probability 1.
∎

|S_{n}(\theta)|\leq\epsilon_{n}n\sigma^{2}/2\leq\epsilon_{n}n\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}
.) Therefore,

\displaystyle P\left(\frac{|S_{n}(\theta)|}{n\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}}>\epsilon_{n}\right)\leq P\left(|S_{n}(\theta)|>\frac{\epsilon_{n}n\sigma_{P}^{2}}{2}\right)+P\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}<\frac{\sigma_{P}^{2}}{2}\right).

(C.23)

For the first term on the right side of (
C.23
) we apply Markov’s inequality:

\displaystyle P\left(|S_{n}(\theta)|>\frac{\epsilon_{n}n\sigma_{P}^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{4\mathbb{V}(\sum_{i}X_{i})}{n^{2}\epsilon_{n}^{2}\sigma_{P}^{4}}=\frac{4}{n\epsilon_{n}^{2}\sigma_{P}^{2}}\leq\frac{4C}{n\epsilon_{n}^{2}},

(C.24)

for some
C>0
by assumption of a uniformly lower bounded variance.
Notice that
n\epsilon_{n}^{2}\to\infty
since

n\epsilon_{n}^{2}\asymp\frac{n}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}+n\lambda_{n}+n\lambda_{n}^{2}.

At least one of these terms goes to
+\infty
: If
n\lambda_{n}^{2}\asymp c
then
\lambda\asymp 1/\sqrt{n}
so
n/\lambda_{n}^{2}\asymp n^{2}\to\infty
.
Now for the next term in (
C.23
). Let
Y_{i}=X_{i}-\theta
,
\overline{Y}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y}Y_{i}
and
\overline{Y^{2}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}Y_{i}^{2}
. Recall the standard identity

\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}=\frac{n-1}{n}\left(\overline{Y^{2}}-\overline{Y}^{2}\right).

Observe that
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\geq\sigma_{P}^{2}/2
iff
\overline{Y^{2}}-\overline{Y}^{2}\leq\frac{n-1}{2n}\sigma_{P}^{2}
which implies that either
\overline{Y}^{2}>\sigma_{P}^{2}/4
or
\overline{Y^{2}}\leq\frac{n-1}{2n}\sigma_{P}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sigma_{P}^{2}\leq\frac{3}{4}\sigma_{P}^{2}
. A union bound gives

P\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\geq\frac{\sigma_{P}^{2}}{2}\right)\leq P\left(\overline{Y}^{2}>\frac{\sigma_{P}^{2}}{4}\right)+P\left(\overline{Y^{2}}\leq\frac{3}{4}\sigma_{P}^{2}\right).

(C.25)

Under
P
,
\overline{Y^{2}}
is an average of
n
iid random variables with mean
\sigma_{P}^{2}
. Hence, by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle P\left(\overline{Y^{2}}\leq\frac{3}{4}\sigma_{P}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(\sigma^{2}-\overline{Y^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{4}\sigma_{P}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq P\left(|\sigma^{2}-\overline{Y^{2}}|\geq\frac{1}{4}\sigma_{P}^{2}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}|n\sigma_{P}^{2}-\sum_{i}Y_{i}^{2}|^{3/2}}{(n\sigma_{P}^{2}/4)^{3/2}}.

From here we appeal to an inequality of
von Bahr and Esseen 1965
, which states that for
Z_{i}
iid mean zero and any
1\leq p\leq 2
with
\mathbb{E}|Z_{1}|^{p}<\infty
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{i\leq n}Z_{i}\bigg|^{p}\leq C_{p}\sum_{i\leq n}\mathbb{E}|Z_{i}|^{p},

where
C_{p}
is some (distribution-independent) constant depending on
p
. Using this above with
p=3/2
gives

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}|n\sigma_{P}^{2}-\sum_{i}Y_{i}^{2}|^{3/2}}{(n\sigma_{P}^{2}/4)^{3/2}}\leq\frac{C_{p}n\mathbb{E}_{P}|\sigma_{P}^{2}-Y_{1}^{2}|^{3/2}}{(n\sigma_{P}^{2}/4)^{3/2}}\leq\frac{D_{p}(\sigma_{P}^{3}+\mathbb{E}_{P}|Y_{1}|^{3}}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{P}^{3}},

where
D_{p}=\sqrt{2}\cdot 4^{3/2}C_{p}
. Here we’ve used the inequality
(a+b)^{p}\leq 2^{p-1}(a^{p}+b^{p})
. Using the assumption of a uniform third moment, we have

\sup_{P}P\left(\overline{Y^{2}}\leq\frac{3}{4}\sigma_{P}^{2}\right)\leq\frac{D_{p}}{\sqrt{n}}\left(1+\sup_{P}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}|Y_{1}|^{3}}{\sigma_{P}^{3}}\right)\leq\frac{D_{p}}{\sqrt{n}}\left(1+M\right).

(C.26)

Next we need to handle the term
P(\overline{Y}^{2}>\sigma_{P}^{4}/4)
in (
C.25
), which is done via Chebyshev’s inequality. Write

\displaystyle P\left(\overline{Y}^{2}>\frac{\sigma_{P}^{2}}{4}\right)=P\bigg(\bigg|\sum_{i\leq n}(X_{i}-\theta)\bigg|>\frac{n\sigma_{P}}{2}\bigg)\leq\frac{4\mathbb{V}(\sum_{i}X_{i})}{n^{2}\sigma_{P}^{2}}=\frac{4}{n}.

Returning to (
C.25
), we have shown that

P\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\geq\frac{\sigma_{P}^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{D_{p}}{\sqrt{n}}(1+M)+\frac{4}{n},

which when combined with (
C.24
), (
C.23
), and (
C.22
) gives

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(A_{n})\wedge K]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{K}\left(\frac{4C}{n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}+\frac{D_{p}}{\sqrt{n}}(1+M)+\frac{4}{n}\right)\textnormal{d}u

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{K}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{4C}{n\epsilon_{n}^{2}}+\frac{D_{p}}{\sqrt{n}}(1+M)+\frac{4}{n}\right)\textnormal{d}u=0,

where we’ve used reverse Fatou’s lemma to switch the limit superior and the integral, in addition to the fact that
n\epsilon_{n}^{2}\to\infty
as argued above.

We have thus demonstrated that
g(A_{n})
satisfies the two conditions of Proposition
B.9
, which completes the proof.

C.11
Proof of Theorem
B.3

We now turn to
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}
. Proceeding as usual, rewrite
E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\theta;\lambda,\eta)<2/\alpha
as

\displaystyle y-\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}<\eta\frac{V_{n}(\theta)}{\sqrt{n}}A_{n},

where again,
y=\overline{X}_{n}-\theta=S_{n}(\theta)/n
. Since
\eta>0
and
V_{n}(\theta)\geq 0
, this inequality is trivially satisfied for
y<\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}
, i.e.,
\theta>\overline{X}_{n}-\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}
.
Thus
R_{1}=(\overline{X}_{n}-\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n},\infty)\subset\{\theta:E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\theta;\lambda,\eta)<2/\alpha\}
. To find the rest of the set, assume that
y\geq\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}
. Then both sides of the above display are positive and we may square them to obtain the inequality

y^{2}-2y\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}+\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}A_{n}^{2}<\frac{\eta^{2}}{n}(\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}(n-1)+ny^{2})A_{n}^{2}.

(C.27)

Gathering terms, rewrite this as the polynomial equation

Q(y):=y^{2}(1-\eta^{2}A_{n}^{2})-2y\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}+\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}A_{n}^{2}\left(1-\eta^{2}\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)\right)<0.

(C.28)

Performing the relevant arithmetic on the quadratic equation,
Q(y)
has roots

y_{\pm}=\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-(1-\eta^{2}A_{n}^{2})(1-\eta^{2}\frac{n-1}{n})}\right)}{1-\eta^{2}A_{n}^{2}}

(C.29)

For
1-\eta^{2}A_{n}^{2}>0
, we claim that
y_{-}<\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}
, thus disqualifying it from consideration (since we are considering
y
such that
y\geq\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}
). Indeed, let
a=\eta^{2}A_{n}^{2}
and
b=\eta^{2}(n-1)/n
and notice that

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-(1-a)(1-b)}

\displaystyle\geq 1-(1-a)(1-b)=b-ab+a>a,

since
a,b<1
. Therefore,

y_{-}=\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}(1-\sqrt{1-(1-a)(1-b)}}{1-a}>\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}(1-a)}{1-a}=\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}.

Consequently, the solution we are considering is
y_{+}
, which gives the half interval
\theta>\overline{X}_{n}-y_{+}
, which subsumes the previous half interval since
y_{+}\geq\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}
(this is easy to see:
y_{+}
multiples
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}A_{n}
by something
>1
in the numerator and divides it by something
<1
in the denominator). Solving
E_{n}^{\textsc{reg}}(\theta;-\lambda,\eta)<2/\alpha
gives the other half interval
\theta<\overline{X}_{n}-y_{+}
, completing the proof.
