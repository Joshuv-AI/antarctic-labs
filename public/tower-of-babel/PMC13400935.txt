The association between lactate trajectories and prognosis in ICU patients with pneumonia: A retrospective group-based trajectory model analysis
Authors: Qiancai Lin, Xueman Lin, Xingyi Yang, Meizhen Wang, Qingqing Zhang, Guo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify pneumonia-specific lactate trajectory phenotypes and evaluate their association with 28-day mortality in ICU patients with pneumonia.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed using the MIMIC-IV database. Adult ICU patients with pneumonia were identified via ICD-9/10 codes. Exclusions were non-index ICU admissions, age <18 or >100 years, ICU stay <3 days, and incomplete lactate data in the first 72 hours. Demographics, vital signs, laboratory results, comorbidities, severity scores, and treatments were extracted. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was applied to lactate values over 72 hours. Model selection considered BIC, AIC, average posterior probability (AvePP), odds of correct classification (OCC), class size, and clinical interpretability. Outcomes included 28-day all-cause mortality (primary) and secondary endpoints. Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox regression were used to examine associations, with subgroup analyses across demographic and clinical strata.ResultsA total of 2,091 patients were included. Four distinct lactate trajectories were identified: low-stable (n=1161), moderate with gradual decline (n=721), initial rise then decline (n=89), and very high with rapid decline (n=120). Unfavorable trajectory classes were associated with greater illness severity, metabolic derangements, resource use, and higher mortality. As a continuous variable, lactate showed a borderline association with 28-day mortality in unadjusted analysis (HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.00-1.05, p=0.082), but lost significance after adjustment. In contrast, trajectory-based groups remained independently associated with outcomes: fully adjusted HRs were 1.30 (95% CI 1.08-1.56, p=0.004) for class 2, 1.49 (95% CI 1.03-2.15, p=0.034) for class 3, and 1.71 (95% CI 1.20-2.45, p=0.003) for class 4. Subgroup analyses showed consistent associations, with a stronger effect in patients <65 years (p for interaction=0.033).ConclusionsIn ICU patients with pneumonia, 72-hour lactate trajectory phenotypes were independently associated with 28-day mortality and may help characterize prognostic heterogeneity, including delayed lactate elevation patterns not captured by admission lactate alone.

1. Introduction

Pneumonia remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and critically ill patients requiring intensive care are at particularly high risk of adverse outcomes. 1 Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy and supportive care, mortality among ICU patients with pneumonia remains substantial, especially in those who develop shock, multi-organ dysfunction, or sepsis.2,3 Precise risk stratification is essential in these patients to guide early interventions, allocate resources, and tailor individualized management.

Serum lactate is widely used as a biomarker reflecting tissue hypoperfusion, impaired oxygen delivery, or metabolic derangements, and elevated lactate levels have been strongly associated with worse outcomes in critically ill patients, including those with pneumonia or sepsis.4,5 Single‐point lactate measurements, however, may not fully capture the temporal dynamics of metabolic stress, and their prognostic power is limited by timing-related variability and measurement noise. To overcome these limitations, various dynamic lactate metrics, such as lactate eliminationand serial measurements, have been explored. For example, early lactate decline is associated with improved survival in sepsis and critical illness, 6 these approaches still treat lactate as a unidimensional variable rather than modeling its trajectory over time.

Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) is a statistical method that identifies latent subgroups following distinct longitudinal patterns. 7 It has been increasingly applied in critical care settings to uncover dynamic phenotypes (e.g. SOFA trajectories) associated with outcomes.8,9 In sepsis cohorts, lactate trajectories constructed via GBTM have revealed meaningful prognostic subclusters, with “hyperlactatemia with gradual resolution”, “hyperlactatemia with rapid resolution”, and “persistent high lactate” patterns showing higher mortality risks than “low-stable” pattern.10,11 Such trajectory-based phenotyping may provide superior discrimination compared to static lactate alone. Pneumonia is frequently complicated by hypoxemia, systemic inflammation, sepsis, respiratory failure, and organ dysfunction. In this setting, lactate concentration may reflect not only circulatory hypoperfusion but also respiratory and inflammatory stress, treatment-related hemodynamic changes, and altered metabolic handling. Therefore, characterizing lactate trajectories in ICU patients with pneumonia may provide clinically relevant information on dynamic metabolic phenotypes and short-term prognosis.

However, early lactate trajectory phenotypes and their prognostic significance have not been fully investigated in ICU patients with pneumonia. We hypothesized that distinct lactate trajectories during the first 72 hours of ICU admission would be associated with different risks of short-term mortality. Therefore, this study aimed to identify early lactate trajectory patterns using group-based trajectory modeling and to evaluate their association with 28-day mortality in ICU patients with pneumonia.

2. Methods

2.1. Data source

This study used data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, a publicly available critical care resource developed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. MIMIC-IV includes de-identified clinical data from over 90,000 ICU admissions at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022, covering demographics, vital signs, laboratory tests, diagnoses, medications, procedures, and outcomes. Access to the database requires completion of the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative and PhysioNet credentialing; the first author (Certification ID: 71755321) has obtained access. The database was approved by the institutional review boards of MIT and BIDMC. As all data are de-identified, no additional ethical approval or informed consent was required. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki Declaration of 1975 as revised in 2024. The reporting of this study conforms to STROBE guidelines. 12

2.2. Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC-IV database. Adult patients admitted to the ICU with pneumonia were identified by International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes through structured query language (SQL). To ensure data quality and comparability, we excluded patients with non-index ICU admissions, those younger than 18 or older than 100 years, ICU stays shorter than 3 days, and cases without complete lactate measurements during the first 72 hours of ICU admission. The final cohort was categorized into four groups based on distinct lactate trajectories (Supplementary Figure 1). The ICD code list was shown in Supplementary Table 1.

2.3. Data extraction

Patient data at admission were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database using Navicat Premium (version 16.3.2) with SQL. The extracted variables were grouped into the following categories: Demographics: age, gender, race, and marital status. Vital signs and anthropometrics: heart rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), respiratory rate, temperature, weight, height, and urine output. Laboratory tests: complete blood count (e.g., white blood cells (WBC), neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, eosinophils, basophils, red blood cells (RBC), hemoglobin, hematocrit, platelets), inflammatory and biochemical markers (e.g., C-reactive protein (CRP), γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin, albumin, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), electrolytes, glucose, coagulation parameters (INR, PT, PTT, D-dimer, fibrinogen), and arterial blood gas indices (pH, base excess, SO2, bicarbonate, PO2, PCO2, PaO2/FiO2 ratio, lactate)). Comorbidities: myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, liver disease, renal disease, hypertension, diabetes, malignant cancer, and acute kidney injury (AKI). Severity and comorbidity scores: Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), Logistic Organ Dysfunction Score (LODS), Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPSII), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Charlson comorbidity index, and CURB-65. Therapies and medications: invasive or noninvasive ventilation, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), systemic glucocorticoids, inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and statins. Outcomes: the primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality, defined as death occurring within 28 days after ICU admission. Survival time was calculated from the date of ICU admission to the date of death or the end of the 28-day observation window, whichever came first. Patients who were alive at 28 days were right-censored at day 28. For patients discharged from the hospital before 28 days, vital status was ascertained using the date of death recorded in the MIMIC-IV database, which links to the Social Security Death Index and hospital records. Patients who were discharged alive and had no recorded date of death within 28 days were considered survivors and censored at day 28. Secondary outcomes included 14-day mortality, ICU mortality, hospital mortality, hospital length of stay, ICU length of stay, and survival time at 14 and 28 days. Variables with more than 25% missing values were excluded from further analysis. For variables with ≤25% missingness, missing data were imputed using the missForest algorithm. The detailed missing data was displayed in Supplementary Table 2.

2.4. Group-based trajectory modeling

To identify heterogeneous patterns of lactate changes within the first 72 hours of ICU admission, group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was applied. Lactate values were extracted for each of the first three days following ICU admission. When multiple lactate measurements were available on the same day, the daily mean value was calculated and used as the representative lactate level for that day. If lactate was missing for an entire day, that observation was excluded from trajectory modeling. Models with two to seven latent classes were estimated. The optimal model was determined through a comprehensive assessment of the following indicators 13 : (1) information criteria including Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), with lower values indicating better goodness-of-fit; (2) the average posterior probability (AvePP) of class assignment, with a threshold of >0.7 considered acceptable; (3) the proportion of patients within each trajectory group, requiring at least 5% per class; (4) odds of correct classification (OCC), with a minimum value >5.0, where higher values denote stronger discrimination; and (5) the overall balance between statistical adequacy, model simplicity, and clinical interpretability.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) according to distribution. For comparisons across the four trajectory classes, one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for continuous variables, as appropriate. For comparisons between binary outcome groups, unpaired Student’s t test or the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. The relationship between lactate trajectories and 28-day mortality was assessed using univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression, and results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Covariates were selected from baseline variables that differed significantly between survivors and non-survivors (Supplementary Table 3), with multicollinearity and high correlation addressed by correlation analysis: Spearman correlation for continuous variables 14 (excluding one variable when r >0.6) and Cramér’s V for categorical variables 15 (excluding one variable when V >0.5), shown in Supplementary Figure 2 and Supplementary Figure 3. Subgroup analyses were performed to examine the robustness of associations across age, gender, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, liver disease, renal disease, and malignant cancer.

All analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.4.2), and a two-sided p <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Lactate trajectory modeling

A total of 2,091 pneumonia patients admitted to the ICU were included in the analysis (Supplementary Figure 1). Lactate trajectories during the first 72 hours of ICU stay were estimated using GBTM. Models with 2 to 7 latent classes were compared, and their fit indices and classification performance were presented in Supplementary Table 4.

With increasing class number, BIC and AIC values decreased, suggesting improved statistical fit. However, models with ≥5 classes produced multiple small subgroups (<5% of patients) and showed less satisfactory diagnostic indices (e.g., lower AvePP and reduced OCC), which compromised robustness and interpretability. The four-class model provided an appropriate balance: it achieved acceptable statistical performance (AvePP >0.90 and OCC >5), while also yielding clinically distinguishable lactate patterns that were consistent with plausible disease trajectories in critically ill patients. Although one subgroup contained slightly fewer than 5% of patients, this trajectory reflected a distinct high-risk metabolic phenotype and was therefore retained. Based on these considerations, the four-class model was chosen as the final solution for subsequent analyses.

In the final four-class model, patients were classified into distinct lactate trajectories (Figure 1): class 1 (low-stable, n=1161), class 2 (moderate with gradual decline, n=721), class 3 (initial rise then decline, n=89), and class 4 (very high with rapid decline, n=120).The smooth curves in Figure 1 represent model-fitted trajectories based on these daily averaged values rather than observed lactate measurements at intermediate time points. 3.2. Baseline characteristics of pneumonia patients in ICU grouped by lactate trajectories

The baseline characteristics of 2,091 ICU patients with pneumonia stratified by lactate trajectories are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 63.5 years, and approximately 60.6% were male. Age, sex, race, and marital status were generally comparable among the four trajectory groups, with no statistically significant differences observed. Inflammatory and metabolic derangements were more pronounced in higher-lactate groups. Compared with class 1, patients in classes 3 and 4 had elevated neutrophil counts, lower platelet levels, reduced hemoglobin and hematocrit, and higher total bilirubin. Class 4 patients in particular showed markedly increased ALT, AST, LDH, and anion gap values, together with higher glucose and coagulation abnormalities (prolonged PT, INR, and PTT). Baseline lactate was lowest in class 1 (1.75 mmol/L) and highest in class 4 (8.53 mmol/L, p <0.001). The prevalence of comorbid conditions varied across groups. Chronic pulmonary disease was most frequent in class 1, whereas liver disease was more common in classes 3 and 4 (p <0.001). The incidence of AKI was also higher in class 1 compared to other groups. Other comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, and malignancy, were similarly distributed. Patients in class 3 and class 4 were more likely to receive CRRT, and systemic glucocorticoid use was highest in class 3. Illness severity increased in parallel with higher lactate trajectories. Mean APSIII, LODS, OASIS, SOFA, and SAPSII scores were lowest in class 1 and progressively higher across classes 2-4 (all p <0.001). Mortality and resource use were strongly associated with lactate patterns. Length of ICU and hospital stay was longest in class 3, while survival time was significantly shorter in classes 3 and 4 compared with classes 1 and 2.

To assess potential selection bias, we compared the main analytic cohort with patients excluded because of ICU length of stay between 0-3 days or incomplete lactate data. Patients were stratified into three groups: Group 1, the main analytic cohort, including patients with ICU length of stay ≥3 days and complete lactate measurements within the first three ICU days; Group 2, including patients with ICU length of stay between 0-3 days; and Group 3, including patients with ICU length of stay ≥3 days but incomplete lactate data during the first three ICU days. The results are shown in Supplementary Table 5. The three cohorts differed significantly in demographics, laboratory indices, comorbidities, treatments, severity scores, and outcomes. Compared with Groups 2 and 3, the main analytic cohort had higher global severity scores, including APSIII, LODS, OASIS, SOFA, and SAPSII. The main cohort also showed more pronounced metabolic and coagulation abnormalities, including higher BUN, creatinine, anion gap, glucose, INR, PT, and PTT. Mortality outcomes also differed across cohorts. The 28-day mortality was 31.5% in Group 1, compared with 23.8% in Group 2 and 25.5% in Group 3. ICU mortality was 26.2%, 8.0%, and 15.2%, respectively. However, AKI and ventilation were more frequent in the excluded cohorts, indicating that the selection process was complex. These findings suggest that the final analytic cohort was not fully representative of all ICU pneumonia patients and may reflect a subgroup with longer ICU observation and complete serial lactate monitoring.

When lactate was analyzed as a continuous variable, a borderline association with 28-day mortality was observed in the unadjusted model (HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.00-1.05, p = 0.082), but this relationship was attenuated and no statistical significance after multivariable adjustment (Models 2-4). In contrast, trajectory-based classifications demonstrated stronger and more consistent prognostic value. In unadjusted analysis (Model 1), class 2 (HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.08-1.51, p=0.004), class 3 (HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.11-2.22, p=0.011), and class 4 (HR 1.73, 95% CI 1.29-2.33, p<0.001) were all significantly associated with higher 28-day mortality compared with class 1. After progressive adjustment for demographics, vital signs, laboratory indicators, and comorbidities (Models 2-4), these associations remained robust. In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), the risk of 28-day mortality was 1.30 (95% CI 1.08-1.56, p=0.004) for class 2, 1.49 (95% CI 1.03-2.15, p=0.034) for class 3, and 1.71 (95% CI 1.20-2.45, p=0.003) for class 4. Together, these findings indicated that early lactate trajectories were independently associated with short-term mortality in ICU patients with pneumonia, and that patients with persistently moderate, rising, or very high lactate patterns represent higher-risk populations compared with those maintaining low-stable levels.

4. Discussion

In this large cohort of ICU patients with pneumonia, we identified four distinct lactate trajectories within the first 72 hours in ICU using GBTM. Single-point serum lactate was not associated with 28-day mortality significantly, while trajectory-based classification provided robust and consistent prognostic value. After full adjustment, patients in the moderate-decline, rise-decline, and very high-rapid decline groups had significantly increased mortality risks relative to the low-stable group. Subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness of these associations across most clinical strata, with a particularly pronounced effect among patients younger than 65 years. These findings suggest that serial lactate evolution during the early ICU course captures prognostically relevant metabolic heterogeneity.

Although the prognostic importance of abnormal lactate evolution has been recognized in sepsis, shock, and lactate-guided resuscitation studies, 16 the present study adds pneumonia-specific trajectory-based evidence in ICU patients, a common subgroup in whom lactate dynamics may be influenced by hypoxemia, infection-related inflammation, circulatory dysfunction, adrenergic stress, and organ dysfunction. Most previous pneumonia studies evaluated lactate using admission values, predefined thresholds, or simple early changes. For example, in the emergency department, lactate as a continuous variable was found to predict in-hospital mortality, with a threshold of 3.35 mmol/L identified as optimal for risk stratification. 17 Similarly, when lactate was categorized using commonly applied cutoffs (<2.0, 2.0-4.0, >4.0 mmol/L), it outperformed the CURB-65 score in predicting 28-day mortality, hospitalization, and ICU admission among pneumonia patients presenting to the emergency department (p <0.01). 18 In critically ill patients with community-acquired pneumonia admitted to the ICU, admission lactate levels, both as a continuous measure and when stratified into quartiles, were independently associated with hospital mortality, with the most prominent effect observed within the range of 1.5-4 mmol/L. 19 Consistently, another study reported that initial lactate levels, analyzed both continuously and as tertiles, were significantly associated with in-hospital mortality among hospitalized CAP patients, independent of traditional risk scores and laboratory variables. 20 More recently, Ağ açkıran et al. reported that lactate level was significantly associated with 30-day mortality, and suggested that lactate, shock index, age-adjusted shock index, and modified shock index may serve as supportive tools for early risk stratification rather than stand-alone prognostic markers. 21 The contribution of our study is the identification of pneumonia-specific lactate trajectory phenotypes in ICU patients. Using group-based trajectory modeling, we identified four distinct patterns, including low-stable, moderate-decline, rise-decline, and very high-decline trajectories. Notably, the rise-decline subgroup may be clinically relevant because these patients did not belong to the very-high initial lactate group but developed subsequent lactate elevation, suggesting that trajectory-based assessment may capture delayed metabolic deterioration that could be missed by admission lactate alone.

Across critical illnesses, GBTM has repeatedly uncovered mortality‐relevant lactate patterns. In sepsis, five classes were reported, including low-stable, low-slowly declining, high-rapidly decline, moderate-slow decline, and high-slow decline, with higher or more slowly resolving levels linked to greater 28-day mortality. 11 Another sepsis study identified four clusters (near-normal, gradual resolution, extreme high with prompt decline, and consistent hyperlactatemia) with the latter two conferring the highest risk. 10 In ischemic stroke, two trajectories were observed, with a steeper reduction versus a more gradual, sustained decrease, and the slower-decline class had significantly higher short- and long-term mortality. 22 In ARDS with sepsis, three trajectories were delineated over the first 48 h, and patients starting from higher baselines fared worst. 23 Consistent with this literature, our pneumonia cohort contained low-stable, moderate-decline, and high-decline trajectories, each associated with graded increases in 28-day mortality relative to the low-stable group. Notably, we also identified a small subgroup with a low baseline followed by an early rise in lactate, which was linked to high short-term mortality. Clinically, this rising-from-low pattern may flag evolving hypoperfusion or metabolic deterioration that warranted intensified monitoring and early optimization of resuscitative and organ-support measures.

Distinct lactate trajectories likely reflect different metabolic and hemodynamic states. Blood lactate concentration represents the net balance between lactate production and elimination. A low-stable trajectory is consistent with preserved tissue perfusion and intact metabolic handling of lactate, which accords with contemporary views that lactate is continuously produced, even under aerobic conditions, and shuttled as an oxidative substrate and signaling molecule.24,25 A moderate-decline pattern may represent early stress with prompt reversal of hypoperfusion and improved microcirculatory flow under effective resuscitation, leading to falling lactate as oxygen delivery/uptake and organ function recover. 26 Serial lactate declines during resuscitation are mechanistically plausible and clinically meaningful, and lactate-guided therapy has been associated with improved outcomes in hyperlactatemic ICU patients. 27 A rise-decline trajectory could indicate delayed resolution of tissue dysoxia or sustained metabolic stress, like ongoing microcirculatory dysfunction, inflammation-driven glycolysis, or catecholamine-mediated lactate generation, followed by partial recovery. Adrenergic stimulation (notably β-adrenergic signaling) can accelerate aerobic glycolysis and raise lactate independent of anaerobiosis, a phenomenon observed in shock states and with β-agonist exposure. 28 “Very high but declining” lactate trajectory, despite trending downward, may capture early, substantial tissue injury and bioenergetic failure with incomplete reversibility, consistent with evidence that sepsis can impair mitochondrial function and cellular oxygen utilization (tissue dysoxia) even when macro-hemodynamics appear corrected. 29 Across patterns, lactate levels integrated both production (accelerated glycolysis from hypoperfusion, inflammation, or adrenergic drive) and lactate elimination, which was dominated by the liver and kidneys. Hepatic or renal dysfunction therefore amplified or prolonged hyperlactatemia. 30 Consistent with this pathophysiological rationale, in our baseline analysis we observed a higher prevalence of liver disease among patients in classes 2-4, suggesting impaired lactate elimination may have contributed to the elevated trajectories in these groups.

This study has several strengths. First, it leveraged the large-scale, granular data of MIMIC-IV, ensuring statistical power and broad generalizability. Second, it represents one of the first systematic applications of lactate trajectory modeling specifically in pneumonia patients. Third, robust adjustment for demographics, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, and illness severity scores supports the independence of our findings. Finally, subgroup analyses provided additional evidence for the stability of associations. The clinical implications of these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Because trajectory classification in this study required serial lactate measurements over the first 72 ICU hours, these patterns are most relevant for dynamic risk reassessment after repeated measurements have accumulated. Patients with moderate persistent elevation, an early rise followed by decline, or very high lactate followed by rapid decline had higher short-term mortality risks, suggesting that these trajectory-defined subgroups may reflect different degrees of metabolic vulnerability. Recognition of these patterns may support closer reassessment of hemodynamic status, oxygenation, infection control, and organ dysfunction during the ICU course. Future studies are needed to determine whether shorter-window or continuously updated lactate models can identify high-risk phenotypes earlier and whether such models add value to prospective clinical risk assessment.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, and external validation is required. Second, lactate measurement frequency and timing varied in practice, which may have introduced misclassification. More severely ill patients were likely to undergo more frequent testing, and this informative measurement process may have influenced daily average lactate values and trajectory assignment. Although daily averaging reduced the influence of repeated measurements within a single day, it may also obscure rapid fluctuations and does not fully account for the fact that sampling intensity itself reflects clinical severity. Additionally, trajectory classification required lactate data over the first 72 ICU hours, which limits its use for real-time risk assessment at the time of ICU admission. Selection bias may have been introduced by requiring ICU length of stay ≥3 days and complete lactate measurements during the first 72 hours. Patients who died early, improved rapidly, were transferred from the ICU within 3 days, or had less frequent lactate monitoring were not included in the trajectory analysis. Because the primary exposure was the 72-hour lactate trajectory, these patients could not provide sufficient longitudinal lactate information for the same GBTM analysis. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as applicable mainly to ICU pneumonia patients who remained in the ICU for at least 72 hours and had complete serial lactate data. Third, the observational design precludes causal inference, and unmeasured confounders such as fluid resuscitation strategies and infection source control could not be accounted for. Furthermore, some subgroups, particularly the rise-decline and very high-rapid decline classes, had relatively small sample sizes, which may affect precision of estimates. Future research should focus on validating these lactate trajectories in multicenter prospective cohorts, integrating them into real-time clinical decision support tools, and testing whether trajectory-guided management strategies, such as individualized resuscitation intensity or organ support timing, can improve outcomes in critically ill pneumonia patients.

5. Conclusions

In summary, distinct lactate trajectories over the first 72 hours of ICU stay were independently associated with 28-day mortality in patients with pneumonia. Low-stable patterns were associated with the most favorable outcomes, whereas moderate-decline, rise-decline, and very high-rapid decline trajectories were associated with progressively higher mortality risks. These findings suggest that the dynamic pattern of lactate evolution provides prognostic information beyond isolated lactate values and may support risk reassessment during the ICU course after serial lactate measurements have accumulated.
