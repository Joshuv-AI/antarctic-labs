Completeness of Relational Algebra via Cylindric Algebra
Jan Laštovička ORCID:0000-0002-0403-9709

Abstract
. An alternative proof of the completeness of relational algebra with respect to allowed formulas of first-order logic is presented. The proof relies on the well-known embedding of relational algebra into cylindric algebra, which makes it possible to establish completeness in a more algebraic way. Building on this proof, we present an alternative algorithm that produces a relational expression equivalent to a given allowed formula. The main motivation for the present work is to establish a proof of completeness suitable for generalisation to relational models handling incomplete or vague information.



1.
Introduction

Expressions of relational algebra
[
3
]
form a procedural language suitable for querying relational databases. In the search for a declarative querying language, a natural approach is to consider all first-order formulas. For practical purposes, however, a declarative query must be translated into an equivalent procedural query that can be efficiently executed by a computer. This is not possible for every formula; for instance, the formula
\neg r(x)
has no equivalent relational expression. In the following, we restrict our attention to allowed formulas
[
8
,
11
]
, which possess the desired property and are defined by a simple, easily verifiable structural condition.

Here, by the completeness of relational algebra, we refer to the theorem asserting that each allowed formula has a semantically equivalent relational expression, and conversely, each relational expression has a semantically equivalent allowed formula. As explained, we further require that for each allowed formula, a semantically equivalent relational expression can be constructed, which can then be efficiently evaluated. In
[
11
]
, the completeness theorem was proved, and an algorithm for converting allowed formulas into relational expressions was presented.

Cylindric algebras
[
4
,
5
]
were introduced by Tarski for the algebraization of first-order logic. Imieliński and Lipski
[
6
]
showed how a relational algebra can be naturally embedded into a cylindric algebra. By leveraging this embedding and partially following the approach of the original proof of completeness, we derive an alternative proof of the completeness theorem.

The primary advantage of our alternative proof is that it is carried out within an appropriate cylindric algebra. This makes the proof technique applicable to the generalized relational models as well. For example, it is desirable to establish analogous completeness results for the relational model with incomplete information
[
7
]
or for models incorporating similarity of values in domains
[
2
]
. To this end, cylindric algebras can be generalized to accommodate these cases, and corresponding lemmas and theorems can then be established, as shown in the following sections.

Compared with
[
11
]
, our approach benefits from treating negation as a full-fledged logical connective, which makes it possible to define allowed formulas more clearly. Another advantage is that we permit equality in formulas, so there is no need to eliminate it before processing. Our algorithm for converting an allowed formula into an equivalent relational expression works in two steps. First, the allowed formula is transformed into a normalized formula, after which it is straightforwardly converted into a relational expression. The first normalization step preserves the structure of the input formula, a feature that can provide practical benefits.

The article has the following structure. In the second and third sections, the necessary notions of first-order logic and relational algebra are presented. Semantic equivalence of relational expressions with formulas is studied in the fourth section. Here also normalized formulas and they translation to relational expressions are defined. The section ends with remarks on domain-independent formulas. The fifth section is dedicated to allowed formulas. Finally, the algorithm for normalization of allowed formulas and its correctness is given in the last sixth section.

2.
First-order logic

We begin by defining the syntax and semantic of first-order logic, selecting from the available definitions those most appropriate for our purposes.

Syntax.

(
,
)
(left and right parenthesis). Note that, contrary to common practice, we do not consider equality as a relation symbol. Another unusual feature is that the set of variables is finite. Each language is determined by

,
\delta

\mathcal{X}
. The triple
(\mathcal{R},\delta,\mathcal{X})
is called a

A

of a language of type

is inductively defined as follows:

(1)

is a formula;

(2)

if

is a relation symbol of arity

, and

(3)

if

and

are variables, then

(4)

if
\varphi
is a formula, then
\neg\varphi
is a formula;

(5)

\varphi

(6)

Formulas given by the first three rules are called

, while those given by the remaining rules are called

. Formulas constructed by the second rule are called

, and those constructed by the third rule are called

An expression

is an abbreviation for
(\neg\varphi\vee\psi)
,
(\varphi\Leftrightarrow\psi)
is an abbreviation for
((\varphi\Rightarrow\psi)\wedge(\psi\Rightarrow\varphi))
,
(\forall x)\varphi
is an abbreviation for
\neg(\exists x)\neg\varphi
,
(\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\varphi_{n})
is an abbreviation for
((\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2})\wedge\cdots\wedge\varphi_{n})
, similarly
(\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2}\vee\cdots\vee\varphi_{n})
is an abbreviation for
((\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2})\vee\cdots\vee\varphi_{n})
. We usually do not include the outermost parenthesis in formulas.

We assume that variables in
\mathcal{X}
are linearly oredered. This order is used — as the next definition demonstrates — whenever we need to consider variables in a specific order. For
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}=X\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,
(\exists X)\varphi
is an abbreviation for
(\exists x_{1})\cdots(\exists x_{n})\varphi
and
(\forall X)\varphi
is an abbreviation for
(\forall x_{1})\cdots(\forall x_{n})\varphi
.

The set
\textrm{FV}(\varphi)
of

of a formula

is defined as follows:
\textrm{FV}(1)=\emptyset
;
\textrm{FV}(r(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
;
\textrm{FV}(x_{1}\approx x_{2})=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}

\textrm{FV}(\neg\varphi)=\textrm{FV}(\varphi)

\textrm{FV}(\varphi\vee\psi)=\textrm{FV}(\varphi\wedge\psi)=\textrm{FV}(\varphi)\cup\textrm{FV}(\psi)

A

Let

We do not define when a formula is true in a structure with respect to a valuation. Rather, for a given formula and structure, we define the set of all valuations for which the formula is true. We begin with a few operations that will be useful for this purpose.

When
M

For a variable

\displaystyle\textrm{C}_{x}(V)=\{v\in\mathcal{V}\;|\;\text{there is }u\in V\text{ such that }u=_{x}v\}.

(1)

Clearly, cilindrifications are closure operators.

Lemma 1
.

The following holds.

(1)

\overline{\textrm{C}_{x}(V)}\subseteq\overline{V}
,

(2)

\textrm{C}_{x}(\overline{\textrm{C}_{x}(V)})=\overline{\textrm{C}_{x}(V)}
,

(3)

\overline{\textrm{C}_{x}(V)}\subseteq\textrm{C}_{x}(\overline{V})

.

(1)

This follows from

.

(2)

Clearly,
\textrm{C}_{x}(\overline{\textrm{C}_{x}(V)})\supseteq\overline{\textrm{C}_{x}(V)}

and
v^{\prime}=_{x}v
. For each
v^{\prime\prime}\in V
, we have
v^{\prime\prime}\not=_{x}v^{\prime}
. Let
v^{\prime\prime}\in V
be such that
v^{\prime\prime}\not=_{x}v^{\prime}
. Then there is a variable

distinct from

with

. But since

, we have

. Hence,
v\in\overline{\textrm{C}_{x}(V)}
.

(3)

From (1) and (2), it follows that
\overline{\textrm{C}_{x}(V)}=\textrm{C}_{x}(\overline{\textrm{C}_{x}(V)})\subseteq\textrm{C}_{x}(\overline{V})
.

For variables

, we define set

\displaystyle\textrm{D}_{xy}=\{v\in\mathcal{V}\;|\;v(x)=v(y)\}.

(2)

The relation

in

(1)

;

(2)

||r(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})||_{M}=\{v\in\mathcal{V}\;|\;(v(x_{1}),\ldots,v(x_{n}))\in r^{M}\}
;

(3)

||x\approx y||_{M}=\textrm{D}_{xy}
;

(4)

if

is a formula, then
||\neg\varphi||_{M}=\overline{||\varphi||_{M}}
;

(5)

if

\displaystyle||\varphi\wedge\psi||_{M}

\displaystyle=||\varphi||_{M}\cap||\psi||_{M},

\displaystyle||\varphi\vee\psi||_{M}

\displaystyle=||\varphi||_{M}\cup||\psi||_{M};

(6)

if

\displaystyle||(\exists x)\varphi||_{M}=C_{x}(||\varphi||_{M}).

If

For a set

\displaystyle\textrm{C}_{S}(V)=\textrm{C}_{x_{1}}(\cdots\textrm{C}_{x_{n}}(V)\cdots).

(3)

Clearly, it holds

Formulas

\displaystyle\epsilon(||E||_{M})=||\varphi||_{M}

Theorem 3

Every relational expression is semantically equivalent to a formula.

Proof 4.1

Follows directly from Theorem

Normalization

This section addresses the construction of a normalized formula semantically equivalent to a given allowed formula. We start by introducing a derived logical connective. We denote by

We conclude our study by presenting two main results concerning allowed formulas.

Theorem 22
.

Every allowed formula is semantically equivalent to its normalisation.

Proof 6.8
.

Let
\varphi
be an allowed formula. Since
\varphi
is allowed, we have
\textrm{FV}(\varphi)=\textrm{FV}(gen(\varphi))
. By Theorem
16
, it follows that
||\varphi||\subseteq||gen(\varphi)||
, and, by Lemma
21
,
||norm(\varphi,gen(\varphi))||\cap||gen(\varphi)||=||\varphi||\cap||gen(\varphi)||=||\varphi||
.

Theorem 23
.

Allowed formulas are domain independent.

Proof 6.9
.

Let
\varphi
be an allowed formula. Then the normalization
norm(\varphi,gen(\varphi))
is semantically equivalent to
\varphi
(Theorem
22
) and is in normalized form. By Lemma
4
, the normalization
norm(\varphi,gen(\varphi))
is semantically equivalent to the relational expression
expr(norm(\varphi,gen(\varphi)))
. By Theorem
7
, the normalization is domain independent, and thus
\varphi
is also domain independent.
