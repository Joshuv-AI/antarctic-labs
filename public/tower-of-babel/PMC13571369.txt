Transcatheter Closure of Aortic Paravalvular Leak Following the Ozaki Procedure

A 62-year-old man with previous Ozaki aortic valve reconstruction presented with NYHA functional class III dyspnea due to paravalvular (periannular) regurgitation. Multimodality imaging, including transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and fluoroscopy, identified the regurgitant defect between the right coronary cusp (RCC) and noncoronary cusp (NCC) and guided procedural planning. The defect was successfully crossed under TEE guidance, followed by transfemoral implantation of a 5 × 4-mm occluder device. Postprocedural TEE demonstrated only mild residual regurgitation with a stable device position. At 6-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically improved with trivial residual regurgitation.

History of Presentation

We present the case of a 62-year-old man who attended our outpatient clinic with complaints of progressive dyspnea over the preceding 2 months and orthopnea, consistent with NYHA functional class III. On admission, his blood pressure was 130/80 mm Hg and electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm. On physical examination of the patient, a 3/6 diastolic murmur was heard in the apex and mesocardiac area.

Past Medical History

His past medical history included hypothyroidism, coronary stent implantation in 2019, and in June 2024 the patient underwent Ozaki aortic valve reconstruction for severe aortic stenosis at an external center. His medications included aspirin, carvedilol, a statin, and levothyroxine sodium.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included prosthetic valve dysfunction (paravalvular leak [PVL] or structural degeneration), infective endocarditis, and heart failure due to other causes.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal left ventricular ejection fraction, aortic valve reconstruction via the Ozaki procedure, and aortic regurgitation (Figure 1).

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed grade 2 to 3 (moderate to severe) periannular aortic regurgitation (Figure 2).

Management

Considering the patient's high surgical risk (Society of Thoracic Surgeons score 9.1%), transcatheter closure was selected. Under general anesthesia and TEE guidance, transcatheter PVL closure was performed. Access was obtained via the right femoral artery, and a 6-F EBU 4 guiding catheter was advanced to the leak site. The defect was crossed using a 0.035-inch hydrophilic guidewire, which was then exchanged for a stiffer Safari wire to ensure stability. The defect was measured with TEE and a 5 × 4-mm Amplatzer Duct Occluder II device was deployed. Imaging confirmed appropriate deployment and positioning of the Amplatzer Duct Occluder II device across the periannular regurgitant defect (Figures 4A and 4B).

Postprocedural TEE confirmed correct device positioning, reduction of paravalvular regurgitation to grade 1 (mild grade) with a small residual leak through the device and no evidence of valve dysfunction or increased gradient (Figure 5).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative course was uneventful and the patient was discharged in stable condition after 48 hours. At the 6-month follow-up, transthoracic echocardiography showed the device to be endothelialized with trivial central regurgitation. No valve dysfunction was observed. At 6-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated symptomatic improvement and improved functional capacity, remaining independent in daily activities.

Discussion

The Ozaki procedure is a surgical aortic valve reconstruction technique using glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium to create new aortic valve cusps. The main advantage of that procedure is preservation of the native aortic root, allowing natural motion of the aortic annulus throughout the cardiac cycle, supported by continued annular growth. Given that the Ozaki procedure reconstructs the native aortic valve, the pericardial leaflets were individually sutured to the aortic annulus. The procedure has gained increasing attention because of its favorable hemodynamic profile, avoidance of prosthetic material, and reduced need for long-term anticoagulation. Early and mid-term outcomes have been encouraging; however, data regarding complications and their management remain limited.1

Although the Ozaki procedure does not involve a prosthetic sewing ring, the regurgitant jet in our patient originated from the periannular region between the reconstructed cusps and was managed using a transcatheter strategy similar to that used for paravalvular defects. Therefore, the term “paravalvular leak” was used in the context of periannular regurgitation following valve reconstruction (Figure 6).

The patient was evaluated by the multidisciplinary heart team. Although redo surgery remained a potential bailout strategy in the event of procedural failure or complications, a minimally invasive transcatheter approach was initially preferred considering the elevated operative risk, prior sternotomy, and the patient's preference for a less invasive strategy.

There is currently insufficient literature on PVL closure following the Ozaki procedure; however, reports exist of transcatheter PVL closure after both mechanical and bioprosthetic valve implantation. One study demonstrated that reducing PVL severity to less than mild via percutaneous intervention significantly decreased long-term mortality in patients with surgically implanted prosthetic valves.2 Another study on PVL closure after transcatheter aortic valve replacement showed sustained reduction in aortic regurgitation, persistent improvement in NYHA functional class, and a high 5-year survival rate of 85%.3 Achieving complete or near-complete PVL elimination remains challenging; in one study, although the defect was successfully crossed and a closure device deployed in 90% of patients, PVL was reduced to mild or less in only 62%, depending largely on the etiology.4

Smaller case series, including those using percutaneous transapical and surgical approaches, have reported similarly favorable outcomes.5,6 Devices originally designed for other lesions—such as ventricular septal defects, atrial septal defects, or patent ductus arteriosus (eg, Amplatzer Septal Occluder, Amplatzer Duct Occluder, Amplatzer Vascular Plug II, Amplatzer Muscular VSD Occluder; Abbott)—have been adapted for PVL closure. Limitations include suboptimal shapes for irregular PVL anatomy and the need for large delivery systems, potentially reducing procedural success rates. Despite inherent procedural challenges, this case suggests that percutaneous transcatheter closure, when performed by experienced operators, may represent a feasible and safe therapeutic option associated with reduced hospitalization duration and lower perioperative morbidity.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case demonstrating that transcatheter PVL closure can be safely considered not only for mechanical or bioprosthetic valves but also for patients who had undergone the Ozaki procedure. Furthermore, the relatively short procedural and hospital stay duration support its role as a preferred treatment option in selected high surgical risk cases.

Although sufficient data are lacking regarding the optimal timing and safety of PVL closure after the Ozaki procedure, in our patient who underwent PVL closure 1 year after surgery, the procedure was performed safely without complications and yielded favorable outcomes.
