Relationship between health literacy levels and breastfeeding self-efficacy perceptions of pregnant women
Authors: Büşra Korkmaz, Ayse Aydın
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth literacy during pregnancy plays a critical role in maternal and infant health outcomes. Adequate health literacy may influence women’s ability to access, understand, evaluate, and apply health information related to breastfeeding. This study aimed to describe pregnant women’s levels of health literacy and prenatal breastfeeding self-efficacy, to evaluate differences according to descriptive and obstetric characteristics, and to examine the relationship between these two variables.MethodsA descriptive-correlational study was conducted using convenience sampling. The study included 292 third-trimester pregnant women who were admitted to the Recep Tayyip Erdogan Training and Research Hospital Obstetrics and Gynecology Clinic for prenatal control between July and September 2020, met the research criteria, and agreed to participate. The Personal Information Form, the Turkish Health Literacy Scale-32 (THLS-32), and the Prenatal Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale (PBSES) were administered through face-to-face structured interviews.ResultsThe mean total score of the THLS-32 was 32.81 ± 9.93, and the mean PBSES score was 79.29 ± 14.65. It was determined that 33.2% of the pregnant women had a problematic-limited level of health literacy. A positive and statistically significant relationship was found between the mean THLS-32 total and subscale scores and the mean PBSES score of pregnant women (p < 0.05). ConclusionThe findings indicate that pregnant women had a problematic-limited level of health literacy and relatively high levels of perceived breastfeeding self-efficacy. A positive and statistically significant association was found between health literacy and breastfeeding self-efficacy. These findings suggest that integrating health literacy–focused education into antenatal care may be beneficial.Keywords: Breastfeeding, Pregnant women, Perception of self-efficacy, Health literacy

Background

Individuals should have adequate levels of health literacy to make appropriate health-related decisions and effectively utilize healthcare services [1–4]. Health literacy is defined as the knowledge, motivation, and competencies of individuals to access, understand, evaluate, and apply health information in order to make appropriate health decisions in everyday life and to maintain or improve quality of life [5]. Worldwide, health literacy levels are reported to range from low to moderate. In European countries, 12.4% of individuals have insufficient health literacy and 47.6% have limited-problematic levels [5], while similar patterns have been reported in Asian countries [6] and Turkey, where 38% of individuals fall within the limited-problematic category [7].

Pregnancy is associated with significant physiological and anatomical changes that may increase susceptibility to health complications [8]. Maternal health behaviors during this period directly influence both maternal and infant outcomes [3, 9]. However, previous studies indicate that pregnant women frequently demonstrate limited or problematic health literacy levels [10–12]. Strengthening women’s health literacy may be important for improving maternal and child health outcomes [5].

Breastfeeding is a fundamental maternal behavior that is largely determined during the prenatal period, since most women make decisions about the duration of breastfeeding during pregnancy [13]. Despite global efforts, recommended breastfeeding targets have not yet been fully achieved worldwide [14, 15], although early initiation of breastfeeding rates in Turkey remain relatively high, with approximately 71% of infants being breastfed within the first hour after birth [16]. Breastfeeding self-efficacy plays a central role in mothers’ readiness to initiate and maintain breastfeeding, and higher self-efficacy has been associated with improved breastfeeding initiation and continuation [17, 18]. Although breastfeeding self-efficacy has been widely studied in the postpartum period in Turkey, research focusing on the prenatal period remains limited [19, 20].

Health literacy may contribute to breastfeeding self-efficacy by enhancing women’s ability to access, understand, evaluate, and apply breastfeeding-related information. Pregnancy represents a critical opportunity for strengthening these competencies through antenatal education and healthcare interactions [21, 22]. Therefore, examining pregnant women’s levels of health literacy and their relationship with prenatal breastfeeding self-efficacy is of particular importance. The aim of this study was to examine the relationship between pregnant women’s levels of health literacy and their perceptions of breastfeeding self-efficacy.

Methods

Aim, design and setting

This study aimed to describe pregnant women’s levels of health literacy and breastfeeding self-efficacy, to evaluate differences according to descriptive and obstetric characteristics, and to examine the relationship between these two variables. A descriptive-correlational study was conducted at the Gynecology Outpatient Clinic of Recep Tayyip Erdoğan Training and Research Hospital in Rize, Turkey, between July and September 2020.

Participants

The study population consisted of third-trimester pregnant women who attended the Gynecology Outpatient Clinic for prenatal follow-up during the study period. The inclusion criteria were: being 18 years of age or older, being literate, being in the third trimester of pregnancy, not being employed in any health institution, having no diagnosed psychiatric disorder, and providing voluntary consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were: having a condition that prevented completing the questionnaires or participating in the interview (e.g., communication or cognitive difficulties), having an acute obstetric condition requiring urgent clinical management at the time of data collection, or returning incomplete questionnaires. A convenience sampling approach was used, and all eligible pregnant women who met these criteria and agreed to participate during the study period were included in the sample. A total of 292 pregnant women participated in the study.

Data collection

The data were collected by the researcher through face-to-face structured interviews using the Personal Information Form, the Turkish Health Literacy Scale-32 (THLS-32), and the Prenatal Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale (PBSES), in accordance with pandemic precautions. Each interview lasted approximately 15–20 min.

Instruments

Personal information form

This form, prepared by the researcher in line with the literature [23–25], comprises 16 questions designed to determine pregnant women's descriptive characteristics (age, education level, occupation, spouse's education and occupation, economic status, family type, health-related source found to be the most reliable, frequency of reading books) and obstetric characteristics (number of pregnancies, number of living children, status of desiring pregnancy, gestational week, sex of the fetus, breastfeeding experience, and the method planned for infant feeding in the postpartum period). The breastfeeding experience variable was assessed as a dichotomous item (Yes/No), and the quality of prior breastfeeding experience (positive or negative) was not evaluated. In the item assessing the most reliable health-related source, the “brochure” category referred specifically to printed educational materials prepared by the Ministry of Health and distributed in public health institutions.

Turkish Health Literacy Scale-32 (THLS-32)

On the basis of the conceptual framework developed by the European Health Literacy Research Consortium, the THLS-32 was developed to evaluate the health literacy of literate people above the age of 15. Turkish validity and reliability studies were performed by Okyay and Abacıgil in 2016 [26]. The THLS-32 has two horizontal subscales (Treatment and Service, Disease Prevention and Health Promotion) and 4 vertical subscales (Accessing Health-Related Information, Understanding Health-Related Information, Evaluating Health-Related Information, and Using/Applying Health-Related Information) (Table 1). In the evaluation of both the horizontal and vertical subscales, the total number of items was 32 in every case. The score value that can be obtained from each subscale is a minimum of 0 and a maximum of 50. The Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale is 0.93. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.94.

Subscales and items of the THLS-32

Each item in the scale is graded as “1 = Very easy,” “2 = Easy,” “3 = Difficult,” “4 = Very difficult,” or “5 = No idea” on a 5-point Likert-type scale. In the calculation, the total score is formulated with values between 0 and 50, as stated below. A score of 0 indicates the lowest level of health literacy, and a score of 50 indicates the highest level of health literacy. The level of health literacy was assessed in four categories according to the score obtained [26]:

(0–25): insufficient health literacy.

(> 25–33): limited-problematic health literacy.

(> 33–42): sufficient health literacy.

(> 42–50): excellent health literacy.

Prenatal Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale (PBSES)

This scale was developed by Wells et al. [27] in 2006 to determine the breastfeeding self-efficacy perceptions of pregnant women in the prenatal period. Turkish validity and reliability studies were conducted by Aydın and Pasinlioğlu [19] in 2016. The scale consists of 20 items in total. Each item of the scale is graded as “1 = Not at all sure,” “2 = Slightly sure,” “3 = Fairly sure,” “4 = Very sure,” or “5 = Completely sure” on a 5-point Likert-type scale. It has no subscales. It is assessed over the total score. The lowest score that can be obtained from the overall scale is 20, and the highest score is 100. An increasing score indicates increased perceptions of breastfeeding self-efficacy. The Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale is 0.86. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.92.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26 package software. After confirming a non-normal distribution, data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, and mean), Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis H, and Spearman correlation tests. Post hoc analyses with Bonferroni correction were conducted following significant Kruskal-Wallis results to determine group differences. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval.

Ethical considerations

Written informed consent was obtained from all pregnant women who participated in the study. Participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study and were assured that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without any consequences. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the research process. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Discussion

Women’s and children’s health plays a critical role in shaping healthy families, communities, and future generations. Children’s health depends on having a healthy intrauterine period starting from the prenatal period and having healthy growth and development processes [28]. Therefore, health literacy in pregnant women is crucial not only in terms of women’s health but also in terms of protecting and improving child health and preventing diseases [29]. One of the issues that should be addressed within the scope of protecting and improving women’s health is breastfeeding. Accordingly, the findings of this study, which were carried out to examine the correlation between health literacy and the self-efficacy of breastfeeding among pregnant women, are discussed in light of the literature.

The distribution of health literacy levels observed in this study aligns with national and international research [7, 11, 12, 30, 31], indicating that a considerable proportion of pregnant women demonstrate limited or problematic health literacy. Studies conducted in Turkey and other countries consistently report that approximately one-third to nearly half of pregnant women fall within insufficient or limited health literacy categories [7, 11, 12, 30, 31].

These similarities may reflect common structural and socioeconomic determinants, such as educational attainment and access to reliable health information [5]. Differences across countries may also be influenced by variations in healthcare systems, antenatal education programs, and cultural contexts related to maternal health.

National data from Turkey indicate that a substantial proportion of the population has insufficient or limited health literacy levels, which supports the present findings [7].

The subdimension analysis revealed that pregnant women demonstrated relatively stronger skills in understanding, accessing, and applying health-related information, whereas evaluating health-related information emerged as the most challenging domain. This pattern suggests that while women may be able to obtain and comprehend health information, critically assessing its reliability and accuracy may require more advanced cognitive and analytical skills.

A similar distribution across subdimensions has been reported by Biçer and Malatyalı [32], although the lower score observed in the evaluative domain in the present study may reflect contextual differences such as regional characteristics, educational background, or the composition of the study population.

Limited evaluative health literacy is particularly important in pregnancy, a period during which women are frequently exposed to diverse and sometimes conflicting information sources [22]. As described by Vamos et al., pregnant women often compare multiple sources—such as online information and healthcare professionals—before making decisions. This process requires not only understanding but also the ability to critically appraise and synthesize information [22].

Adequate health literacy, especially in evaluative and applied domains, is essential for recognizing warning signs, interpreting health advice appropriately, and engaging in safe maternal behaviors [29]. Therefore, strengthening higher-order health literacy skills during prenatal care may be crucial for supporting informed decision-making.

The relatively higher scores in understanding health-related information may be related to increased information-seeking behaviors during pregnancy [22]. However, limited prior experience may partly explain the lower performance in evaluating information, as critical appraisal skills generally develop over time.

The horizontal subscale findings suggest that pregnant women demonstrated relatively stronger competencies in the “treatment and service” domain compared to “disease prevention and health promotion.” This pattern may reflect the persistence of treatment-oriented health perceptions in many societies, where healthcare utilization is often prioritized over preventive behaviors. As indicated by previous studies [5, 9, 24], preventive health literacy is closely associated with educational attainment, socioeconomic status, and exposure to structured health education programs, which may not be equally distributed across populations.

A comparable pattern has been reported by Okur et al. [33], although differences in the “disease prevention and health promotion” domain were observed. These variations may be attributable to differences in educational level, occupational status, access to preventive healthcare services, or regional disparities between study populations. In particular, higher education and regular engagement with healthcare services have been shown to positively influence preventive health literacy competencies.

The findings indicate that pregnant women in this study demonstrated relatively high levels of prenatal breastfeeding self-efficacy. Similar mean scores have been reported in studies conducted in different populations [19, 27, 34–36], suggesting a consistent pattern across contexts.

The comparatively elevated self-efficacy levels observed may be related to positive breastfeeding intentions and increased motivation during pregnancy. In particular, for many women experiencing their first pregnancy, heightened attentiveness to maternal and infant health may be associated with stronger confidence in anticipated breastfeeding behaviors.

Sociodemographic characteristics were consistently associated with both health literacy and prenatal breastfeeding self-efficacy. Higher levels of health literacy were observed among women aged 23–27 years, those with university education, and those employed in professional or public-sector positions, findings consistent with studies demonstrating the influence of age, education, and occupational status on health literacy competencies [10, 11, 37–40].

Similarly, spousal education and occupation were associated with maternal health literacy, supporting evidence that the broader educational environment and socioeconomic context within the household contribute to women’s ability to access and utilize health information [41, 42]. Economic status also played a significant role, in line with literature identifying socioeconomic conditions as key determinants of health literacy [43, 44].

Reading habits were also associated with both health literacy and prenatal breastfeeding self-efficacy. Engagement in reading activities may reflect broader cognitive and informational competencies that support women’s capacity to interpret health information and develop confidence in breastfeeding [45–52]. Taken together, these findings suggest that health literacy and prenatal breastfeeding self-efficacy are embedded within a broader sociodemographic and socioeconomic framework.

Parity also appeared to be associated with health literacy levels. Pregnant women experiencing their first pregnancy demonstrated significantly higher THLS-32 scores compared to those with multiple pregnancies. Similar associations between the number of pregnancies and health literacy have been reported in the studies [53].

Likewise, women with no living children had higher health literacy scores than those with one or more children. Aktan and Özdemir [54] also identified a relationship between the number of living children and health literacy levels. This pattern may reflect the increased caregiving responsibilities and time constraints experienced by multiparous women, which could limit opportunities to seek, access, or critically engage with health-related information. In addition, first-time mothers may be more actively engaged in information-seeking behaviors due to the novelty of the pregnancy experience.

Parity was also associated with breastfeeding self-efficacy levels. Women experiencing their first pregnancy demonstrated higher PBSES scores compared to those with multiple pregnancies. Similar findings have been reported by Shiraishi et al. [55], indicating that parity may influence perceptions of breastfeeding self-efficacy.

In addition, pregnant women without living children had higher PBSES scores than those with one or more children. Aydın and Pasinlioğlu [19] likewise reported higher breastfeeding self-efficacy levels among women without prior breastfeeding experience. This finding may reflect the absence of previous negative breastfeeding experiences, which can influence maternal confidence. Conversely, multiparous women who have encountered breastfeeding difficulties in the past may report lower perceived self-efficacy [56]. However, since the present study did not evaluate the quality of prior breastfeeding experiences, this interpretation should be considered with caution.

A positive correlation was observed between the total and subscale scores of the THLS-32 and the mean PBSES scores. This finding suggests that higher health literacy levels are associated with stronger perceptions of breastfeeding self-efficacy. Similar associations have been reported in studies, indicating that maternal health literacy is positively related to breastfeeding self-efficacy [25, 57].

From a conceptual perspective, health literacy may enhance women’s ability to access, understand, evaluate, and apply breastfeeding-related information, which in turn can strengthen confidence in their capacity to breastfeed [23]. Women who are more competent in navigating health information may feel better prepared to manage potential breastfeeding challenges [58–59]. However, given the cross-sectional design of the study, the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

From a clinical and public health perspective, these findings highlight the importance of strengthening health literacy during pregnancy in relation to breastfeeding self-efficacy. Integrating health literacy–focused education into prenatal care and childbirth preparation classes may help support breastfeeding self-efficacy and maternal confidence.

Given that higher breastfeeding self-efficacy is associated with improved breastfeeding initiation, exclusivity, and duration, strengthening health literacy during pregnancy may be associated with more sustained and effective breastfeeding practices.

In addition to individual-level factors such as health literacy, breastfeeding self-efficacy may also be influenced by broader contextual and structural determinants, including access to breastfeeding support, healthcare system practices, and the social environment surrounding infant feeding. Therefore, breastfeeding behaviors should be considered within a multidimensional framework that includes both individual competencies and external influences [60].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it examined both health literacy and prenatal breastfeeding self-efficacy simultaneously in a relatively large sample of third-trimester pregnant women. In addition, validated and reliable measurement tools were used.

However, some limitations should be considered. The study was conducted in a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences. In addition, previous breastfeeding experience was assessed only as a dichotomous variable (Yes/No), and the quality of prior experiences was not evaluated.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that pregnant women’s perceptions of breastfeeding self-efficacy were above the midpoint, while their health literacy levels were predominantly at a limited–problematic level. Health literacy was positively associated with prenatal breastfeeding self-efficacy, indicating that higher health literacy levels are accompanied by stronger perceptions of breastfeeding self-efficacy.

Based on these findings, integrating health literacy–focused education into prenatal care services and childbirth preparation classes may help support improvements in both health literacy and breastfeeding self-efficacy during pregnancy. Providing structured guidance on how to access, evaluate, and apply reliable health information during the prenatal period may contribute to improved maternal confidence and breastfeeding outcomes.

Future studies using longitudinal or interventional designs are recommended to better clarify the causal relationship between health literacy and breastfeeding self-efficacy. In addition, multicenter studies including more diverse populations would enhance the generalizability of the findings. Further research exploring qualitative dimensions of maternal experiences may also provide deeper insight into how health literacy influences breastfeeding behaviors.
