Lower cardiac output is a risk factor for faster cerebral atrophy over a 11‐year follow‐up period in APOE‐ε4 carriers
Authors: Elizabeth E Moore, Panpan Zhang, Omair A Khan, Dandan Liu, Deepak K Gupta, Kimberly R Pechman, Katherine A Gifford, Bennett A Landman, Timothy J Hohman, Angela L Jefferson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONSubclinical lower cardiac output (volume of blood pumped per minute) cross‐sectionally relates to smaller cerebral gray matter volumes in older adults. This study relates cardiac output to longitudinal gray matter volumes among middle‐aged and older adults over an 11‐year period.METHODSLinear regression (cross‐sectional) and mixed effects (longitudinal) models related baseline cardiac output to gray matter volume trajectory, adjusting for demographic factors. Secondary models tested cardiac output × apolipoprotein E (APOE) ‐ε4 status interactions.RESULTSLower cardiac output related to smaller brain volumes (p‐values < 0.04) in APOE‐ε4 positive participants only. In longitudinal models, baseline cardiac output interacted with APOE‐ε4 status (p = 0.006). Lower baseline cardiac output related to greater increase in inferior lateral ventricle volume over time in APOE‐ε4 carriers (p = 0.01) only.DISCUSSIONResults suggest among APOE‐ε4 carriers, subclinical cardiac dysfunction relates to greater neurodegeneration cross‐sectionally and longitudinally. However, results must be interpreted with caution and replicated.Keywords: APOE, brain MRI, cardiac output, gray matter, neurodegeneration

BACKGROUND

While the effects of overt cardiac dysfunction and heart failure on brain health are well‐established, 1 , 2 a growing body of evidence now suggests subtle reductions in cardiac function can lead to adverse brain health outcomes even in the absence of cardiovascular disease (CVD). 3 , 4 Prior work has shown that lower cardiac output (representing the amount of blood pumped out the heart per minute), even in the normal range, is cross‐sectionally associated with decreased cerebral blood flow (CBF), 5 , 6 global structural brain changes, 2 , 3 , 4 , 7 and worse cognitive performance, 8 , 9 , 10 among middle‐aged and older adults. Specifically, lower cardiac output is cross‐sectionally associated with smaller total, frontal lobe, and occipital lobe gray matter volumes, 11 but not white matter changes. 3 , 4 Thus, it is likely that gray matter damage is a primary pathway through which subclinical cardiac dysfunction affects brain health over the lifespan. Given increasing evidence that age‐related changes in systemic vascular health contribute to cognitive decline and Alzheimer's disease (AD), 12 , 13 , 14 longitudinal studies are needed to better understand how subtle reductions in cardiac output may precipitate or exacerbate abnormal structural brain changes.

The current study examines baseline cardiac output in relation to the cross‐sectional and longitudinal trajectory of gray matter volumes assessed on T1‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) among middle‐aged and older adults free of clinical stroke, dementia, and heart failure at study entry. Based on prior cross‐sectional associations, 3 , 4 we hypothesize that lower cardiac output at baseline is associated with faster atrophy in gray matter regions over the follow‐up period. Additionally, we hypothesize that associations are modified by the presence of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ‐ε4 allele, a genetic risk factor for AD, 15 and a moderator of vascular damage 16 and cognitive status. 17 Past cross‐sectional studies have shown that associations between cardiac output and brain health are driven by APOE‐ε4 carriers, 10 likely due to the effect of APOE‐ε4 on cerebrovascular autoregulation. 18 Prior cross‐sectional work has shown that associations between cardiac output and gray matter volumes are driven by cognitively unimpaired (CU) participants, 11 likely due to subtle changes in blood flow that occur with age, 19 prior to the onset of cognitive impairment. Based on these prior studies, we hypothesize that associations between baseline cardiac output and gray matter atrophy will be strongest among CU participants and APOE‐ε4 carriers.

METHODS

Study cohort

The Vanderbilt Memory and Aging Project (VMAP), 20 is a longitudinal study investigating vascular health and brain aging. Between 2012 and 2014, participants were recruited into the Legacy Cohort who were age 60 years or older and met cognitive status criteria for CU, early mild cognitive impairment (eMCI), 21 or MCI. 22 In 2021, the study enrolled additional CU participants into the Expansion Cohort who were age 50 years and older and met cognitive status criteria for CU. Inclusion criteria required participants to be proficient in English, have adequate auditory and visual acuity, and have a study partner. At eligibility, participants underwent a medical history and record review, clinical interview, and neuropsychological assessment for cognitive status by consensus. CU was defined as (a) Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) = 0 (no dementia), (b) no deficits in activities of daily living directly attributable to cognitive impairment, and (c) no evidence of neuropsychological impairment defined as standard scores falling within 1.5 standard deviations of the age‐adjusted normative mean. 21 eMCI was defined as (a) CDR = 0.5 (reflecting mild severity of impairment), (b) no deficits in activities of daily living attributable to cognitive issues, and (c) no neuropsychological impairment defined as standard scores falling within 1.5 standard deviations of the age‐adjusted normative mean. 21 Finally, MCI was defined as (a) CDR = 0 or 0.5 (reflecting mild severity of impairment), (b) relatively spared activities of daily living, (c) neuropsychological impairment within at least one cognitive domain (i.e., performances falling greater than 1.5 standard deviations outside the age‐adjusted normative mean or pre‐morbid level of functioning), (d) concern of a cognitive change by the participant, informant, or clinician, and (e) absence of a dementing syndrome. 22

Participants were excluded from either cohort for MRI contraindication, history of neurological disease (e.g., stroke, dementia, head injury with loss of consciousness > 5 minutes), prevalent heart failure, major psychiatric illness, and systemic or terminal illness that could impact follow‐up participation. At enrollment, participants completed a comprehensive evaluation, including but not limited to fasting blood draw, physical examination, clinical interview with medication review, echocardiogram, and brain MRI. Assessments were repeated at 18‐month, 3‐year, 5‐year, 7‐year, and 9‐year follow‐up visits. Participants were excluded from the current study for missing cardiac output, brain MRI, or covariate data (Figure 1). The Vanderbilt University Medical Center Institutional Review Board approved the protocol. Written informed consent was obtained from participants prior to the commencement of data collection. Data, analytic methods, and study materials can be obtained by contacting the corresponding author.

Echocardiogram

As previously reported, 6 standard two‐dimensional, M‐mode, and Doppler transthoracic echocardiography was performed by a single research sonographer on a Philips IE33 cardiac ultrasound machine (Philips Medical, Andover, MD) at baseline. Digital images with measurements were confirmed by a board‐certified cardiologist (D.K.G.) blinded to clinical information. Stroke volume was calculated from the left ventricular outflow tract velocity‐time integral and diameter. Cardiac output was calculated as stroke volume times heart rate. Final measurements were from a single cardiac cycle for participants in normal sinus rhythm or the average of three cardiac cycles for participants in atrial fibrillation.

Brain MRI

Participants were scanned at the Vanderbilt University Institute of Imaging Science on a 3T Philips Achieva system (Best, the Netherlands) using an eight‐channel SENSE reception coil array between 2012 and 2017. The system was upgraded to a 32‐channel coil in 2017. In the Legacy Cohort, T1‐weighted images were acquired with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 8.9 ms, echo time (TE) = 4.6 ms, spatial resolution = 1 × 1 × 1mm3. In the Expansion Cohort and Legacy Cohort for 9‐year follow‐up, T1‐weighted images were acquired with the following parameters: TR = 6.5 ms, TE = 2.9 ms, spatial resolution = 1 × 1 × 1mm3. Images were post‐processed with a Multi‐Atlas Segmentation pipeline. 23 Images were registered to Montreal Neurological Institute space, appropriate atlases were registered to T1‐weighted images, and labels were statistically fused. Gray matter volume regions of interest (ROIs) were calculated for total, frontal lobe, temporal lobe, parietal lobe, occipital lobe, hippocampal, and inferior lateral ventricle volumes. 24

To evaluate gray matter changes associated with neurodegeneration due to AD pathology, 25 an AD signature imaging variable was calculated. As previously described, 26 T1‐weighted images were also post‐processed using FreeSurfer (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/). 27 The AD signature imaging marker was calculated by summing bilateral cortical thickness measurements from regions shown to distinguish individuals with clinical AD from CU, including the entorhinal cortex, temporal lobe, parietal lobe, fusiform gyrus, and precuneus. 11 , 25 , 26 , 28

Because T1‐weighted image sequence parameters differed between the VMAP Legacy and Expansion Cohorts, we used longitudinal ComBat, 29 to harmonize T1‐weighted image outputs across both cohorts. Longitudinal ComBat inputs included the features to be harmonized, the specification of batch variables, and the specification of a linear mixed‐effects model.

Analytical plan

As previously described, 6 covariates were collected at enrollment and selected a priori for their potential to confound analytical models. Covariates included age, sex, education, race/ethnicity, baseline body surface area, baseline Framingham Stroke Risk Profile (FSRP) score, 30 (excluding points assigned for age), cognitive status, intracranial volume, and APOE‐ε4 status. Body surface area (m2) was calculated as weight0.425 * height0.725 * 0.007184. APOE‐ε4 status was defined as positive (ε2/ε4, ε3/ε4, ε4/ε4) or negative (ε2/ε2, ε2/ε3, ε3/ε3).

Linear regression models with ordinary least square estimates cross‐sectionally related baseline cardiac output and regional gray matter volumes, adjusting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, body surface area, cognitive status, FSRP (minus points assigned for age), APOE‐ε4 status, and intracranial volume (for example, frontal gray matter volume ∼ cardiac output + age + sex + race/ethnicity + education + body surface area + cognitive status + FSRP (minus age points + APOE‐ε4 status + intracranial volume). Models examining the AD signature did not include intracranial volume because the AD signature imaging marker is calculated from cortical thickness measurements, which are not correlated with intracranial volume. 31 Models also tested cardiac output × APOE‐ε4 status and cardiac output × cognitive status interactions on gray matter volumes, with follow‐up models stratified by APOE‐ε4 status (positive, negative) and cognitive status (CU, MCI), excluding the small subset of participants with eMCI.

Linear mixed effects regression with random intercepts and slopes and a follow‐up time interaction term related baseline cardiac output to longitudinal gray matter volumes (one ROI per model). Models adjusted for baseline age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, baseline body surface area, baseline FSRP (excluding points assigned for age as age was included as its own covariate), follow‐up time, baseline cognitive status, APOE‐ε4 status, and baseline intracranial volume (for example, frontal gray matter volume ∼ baseline cardiac output*follow‐up time + baseline age + sex + race/ethnicity + education + baseline body surface area + baseline cognitive status + baseline FSRP (minus age points + APOE‐ε4 status + baseline intracranial volume). Models examining the AD signature imaging marker omitted intracranial volume as a covariate. 31 Models also tested cardiac output × APOE‐ε4 status and cardiac output × cognitive status interactions with follow‐up time on longitudinal gray matter volumes, with follow‐up models stratified by APOE‐ε4 status (positive, negative) and cognitive status (CU, MCI). Cognitive status interaction models excluded the subset of participants with eMCI (n = 27) as the sample size is too low to be powered to detect a three‐way interaction with CU, eMCI, and MCI.

To determine if outliers were driving the results, additional models were calculated excluding predictor or outcome values > 4 standard deviations from the group mean. 24 total outliers were identified across 1840 observations (1.3%) and they were excluded in a case wise fashion. Multiple comparison correction was performed across outcomes in each model using a false discovery rate (FDR) based on the Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure. 32 Significance was set a priori at p < 0.05, and analyses were conducted using R 4.2.2 (www.r‐project.org).

Cardiac output × cognitive status interactions and gray matter

In cross‐sectional models, cardiac output did not interact with cognitive status on gray matter volumes (p‐values > 0.08, Table S2). In stratified models, lower cardiac output was not cross‐sectionally related to gray matter volumes after FDR correction. In CU participants only, associations with lower total brain (β = 3167, p = 0.05, pFDR = 0.17) and frontal lobe volume (β = 1438, p = 0.03, pFDR = 0.17) approached significance, consistent with prior published work. 26 In post‐hoc analyses examining whether heart rate or stroke volume were driving associations, neither heart rate nor stroke volume was associated with brain volumes (p‐values > 0.06).

In longitudinal models, baseline cardiac output did not interact with cognitive status on gray matter volumes (p‐values > 0.07). In stratified models, baseline cardiac output was not associated with a change in gray matter volume in any region in CU or MCI participants after FDR correction (pFDR > 0.07). See Table S3 for details. Results were similar when excluding outliers (data not shown). In post‐hoc analyses examining whether heart rate or stroke volume were driving associations, heart rate was associated with smaller occipital lobe volume over time (β = 17, p = 0.04) over time, but stroke volume was not (β = 6.5, p = 0.17).

DISCUSSION

This study is among the first to examine the impact of subclinical cardiac dysfunction on neurodegeneration over time, building on previous cross‐sectional work, 11 and among the first to comprehensively examine the impact of APOE‐ε4 on these associations. Among community‐dwelling older adults, this study found for the first time that subclinical cardiac dysfunction at baseline was associated with greater cross‐sectional and longitudinal rates of atrophy in individuals who are APOE‐ε4 carriers. In longitudinal models, associations are strongest in the medial temporal lobe. Importantly, this study includes the largest sample size to date (n = 756), examining how baseline cardiac output relates to neurodegeneration over time. Further, this study has a robust mean follow‐up time of 4.7 years, ranging up to 11 years, allowing for novel observations of how subtle changes in cardiac health in middle and older age impact brain health over time. Collectively, findings suggest that subclinical cardiac dysfunction, even prior to overt cardiac disease, is a key risk factor for exacerbating age and AD‐related neurodegeneration among individuals with a genetic predisposition for AD.

First, we found that among APOE‐ε4 carriers, lower cardiac output was cross‐sectionally associated with smaller volumes in the whole brain, temporal lobe, and occipital lobe. Associations were not present among APOE‐ε4 non‐carriers, suggesting that APOE‐ε4 carriers are more vulnerable to small, sustained reductions in cardiac function. Cardiac output likely impacts neurodegeneration through reduced CBF, as previously reported. 6 Subtle reductions in CBF lead to impaired neuronal regulation, 33 and hyperexcitability, 34 resulting in neurodegeneration. APOE‐ε4 is expressed in astrocytes, 35 and causes greater blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability through initiating an inflammatory cascade in pericytes. 36 Therefore, in APOE‐ε4 carriers, the BBB response to subtle changes in CBF is likely less effective, making them more vulnerable to subtle changes in cardiac output and leading to greater atrophy. Cross‐sectional results largely implicate the entire brain, suggesting there is no regional specificity. However, it should be noted that there was not a significant APOE‐ε4 interaction in cross‐sectional models, so these findings must be replicated in other cohorts.

Second, in longitudinal models, we found that cardiac output interacted APOE‐ε4 carrier status on inferior lateral ventricle volume. Though stratified results were not significant, they suggest that lower cardiac output is associated with expansion of the inferior lateral ventricle in APOE‐ε4 carriers but not among APOE‐ε4 non‐carriers. Expansion of the inferior lateral ventricle is attributable to atrophy in adjacent brain structures, namely the medial temporal lobe. The medial temporal lobe is where AD pathology first develops. 37 It is possible that, as AD pathology builds up over time, this region becomes more susceptible to damage from subclinical cardiovascular changes, leading to greater atrophy. Another possibility is that this region is uniquely susceptible to vascular dysfunction due to a lower density of capillaries compared to other parts of the brain, 38 which is consistent with recent work showing that the medial temporal lobe specifically is susceptible to small fluctuations in blood pressure, 39 and hypoperfusion. 40 More work is needed to understand the selective vulnerability of this region over other portions of the temporal lobe. Taken together, our results are among the first to suggest that the association between subclinical cardiac dysfunction and inferior lateral ventricular volume is modified by APOE‐ε4 status. Results highlight the need to take genetic information into account for precise prevention and treatment approaches for AD. Importantly stratified results did not meet the significance threshold after FDR correction, so findings must be interpreted with caution, highlighting that future studies are needed with larger APOE‐ε4 sample sizes.

Results also show that diagnostic interactions and stratification by cognitive status were largely null after correction for multiple comparisons. This is contrary to prior work, as we previously published cross‐sectional associations between cardiac output and lower gray matter volumes in CU participants. 11 This difference may be due to changes in the sample size. Longitudinal results were also null, suggesting that the association between cardiac output and gray matter volume trajectory overtime is not modified by cognitive status. There was a mild association between lower baseline cardiac output and faster rates of atrophy in the occipital lobe in MCI participants only, but this did not survive correction for multiple comparison. More studies are needed to explore this trend, but this region may be more susceptible to subclinical cardiac dysfunction in the context of cognitive impairment. The occipital lobe is thought to be preserved until the later stages of AD, 41 so it is possible that the occipital lobe is most susceptible to changes to cardiac output compared to other regions of the brain with evolving AD pathology. This finding also aligns with other evidence suggesting the occipital lobe is more vulnerable to vascular risk in aging, 42 , 43 but more work is needed to understand the susceptibility of the occipital lobe in normal aging and AD.

Our study has several strengths, including a clinically well‐characterized cohort, comprehensive ascertainment of potential confounders, and the use of core laboratories to analyze all echocardiography and brain MRI measurements in batches with technicians blinded to clinical information. Additionally, this study is among the largest to examine associations between cardiac output and brain health with a very robust follow‐up time of up to 11 years, greatly expanding our knowledge on how subtle alterations in cardiovascular health impact brain aging. Limitations include the predominantly White, well‐educated, and relatively healthy cohort, limiting generalizability to other races, ethnicities, and medical conditions. However, we might expect associations to be stronger among individuals with a greater burden of vascular risk or CVD. A single cohort was also used, so replication is needed. Additionally, cardiac output was measured during a single assessment, which may be influenced by acute fluctuations in metabolism not fully reflecting resting cardiac function. Finally, it should be noted that many findings did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, significant interaction results did not necessarily yield significant stratified results, and significant stratified results were not couple with a significant interaction, so results must be interpreted with caution.

Among older adults who are APOE‐ε4 carriers, lower baseline cardiac output is associated with smaller brain volumes cross‐sectionally and larger inferior lateral ventricle volumes over time. In individuals with a genetic predisposition to AD, subclinical cardiac dysfunction may be one pathway through which individuals have worse brain health outcomes. Reduced cardiac output may lead to oligemia, placing vulnerable neurons at risk for dysfunction and eventual neurodegeneration. Results suggest that cardiac screening may be an important component of future screening and prevention efforts in this population.
