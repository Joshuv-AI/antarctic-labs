An integrative redescription of the copepod Tracheliastes polycolpus (Lernaeopodidae) based on ultrastructure, COI barcoding, and Palearctic distribution
Authors: Wojciech Piasecki, Eglantine Mathieu-Bégné, Geoffrey A. Boxshall, Remigiusz Panicz, Piotr Eljasik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tracheliastes polycolpus is one of the most widely reported parasitic copepods of Palearctic freshwater fishes. Despite nearly two centuries of citations in fish-disease literature, its morphology, taxonomy, and genetic identity have remained insufficiently resolved and summarized. We provide an integrative redescription based on scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) barcoding of specimens collected from the type host Leuciscus idus in Lake Dąbie, Poland. Molecular identity was confirmed by PCR using universal COI primers, with phylogenetic reconstruction under the GTR + I + G4 model. SEM examination revealed fine-scale features of all cephalothoracic appendages and genital processes. Secondary denticles on the antennal endopod and exopod are demonstrated to constitute reliable diagnostic characters within the genus Tracheliastes. Vestigial thoracic legs, each surrounded by concentric cuticular patches of distinct ultrastructure, are documented for the first time in this species. This study presents the first COI sequences for T. polycolpus (GenBank: PQ520502–PQ520503), only the second for the genus, confirming its distinct genetic identity and close relationship to T. maculatus. A maximum-likelihood phylogeny places T. polycolpus firmly within the Lernaeopodidae. Based on morphological reassessment and historical evidence, T. chondrostomi and T. mourkii are synonymized with T. polycolpus, reducing the number of valid species in the genus. Introduction

Tracheliastes polycolpus is one of the best known Palearctic parasitic copepods infecting freshwater fishes. It can be found in areas of the temperate climate zone from western Europe through to eastern Asia. It belongs to the copepod family Lernaeopodidae, known for its enlarged, metamorphic adult females and narrow host specificity although in this case, the specificity is wider as the parasite has been recorded on many different teleost fish species, mostly cyprinids. After almost two centuries since its original description, Tracheliastes polycolpus has been mentioned in the majority of textbooks on fish diseases, in countless original and review papers, as well as in many theses. The number of those sources exceeds three hundred (Supplementary file S1). Despite this, real advances in our knowledge of its morphology, biology, physiology, ecology, and pathogenicity have been slow. This paper summarizes existing data on this parasite, as well as providing new information on its morphology and genetic aspects.

Taxonomic background and nomenclatural stability

Piasecki and Boxshall1 recently discovered that the parasite commonly known as Tracheliastes polycolpus and attributed to von Nordman2 was, in fact, discovered, properly described, and illustrated 49 years earlier. In 1783, the Alsatian naturalist, Johann (Jean-Frederic) Hermann3 from Strasbourg, reported a new parasite species of common dace, Leuciscus leuciscus (Linnaeus, 1758) [“Cyprinus Leuciscus”], captured by local fishermen in the Ill River. Hermann named the new species Lernaea squamicola. The description and the figures permitted Piasecki and Boxshall1 to conclude without a doubt that the dace parasite from the Ill River represents the species described almost half a century later by von Nordmann2 as Tracheliastes polycolpus. According to the Principle of Priority of the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature (ICZN), Lernaea squamicola should be treated as a senior synonym, while Tracheliastes polycolpus should lose its valid status, becoming a junior synonym. Such a decision, however, could disrupt intergenerational continuity of parasitological nomenclature and might have a profound effect on parasitology, zoology, and the study of fish diseases. For the sake of nomenclatural stability, Piasecki and Boxshall1, using available provisions of the CODE (Article 23.9), proposed a reversal of the Principle of Priority in this particular case, thus retaining the name Tracheliastes polycolpus as valid. The type specimens of T. polycolpus are kept in the collections of the Museum für Naturkunde, Berlin, Germany4.

Host specificity

In the sources listed in Supplementary file S1, we identified 110 literature host records spanning nearly 250 years of sampling fishes that host T. polycolpus. The majority of those records were from species representing the subfamily Leuciscinae (family Cyprinidae) [69 records], followed by cyprinids with no distinguished subfamily [14 records], cyprinids representing the subfamily Barbinae5, and two other less represented cyprinid subfamilies (Acheilognathinae and Xenocypridinae) [3 records]. Only 13 records were from fishes representing non-cyprinid families (Gobionidae, Esocidae, and Percidae).

Tracheliastes polycolpus has been recorded from at least 37 teleost freshwater host fish species representing five families: Rhodeus sericeus (Pallas, 1776) [Cyprinidae: Acheilognathinae]; Barbus barbus (Linnaeus, 1758); Barbus petenyi Heckel, 1852; Luciobarbus barbulus (Heckel, 1847); Luciobarbus kersin (Heckel, 1843); Schizothorax labrosus Wang, Zhuang et Gao, 1981; Schizothorax microstomus Hwang, 1982; Schizothorax ninglangensis Wang, Zhang et Zhuang, 1981 [Cyprinidae: Barbinae]; Abramis brama (Linnaeus, 1758); Chondrostoma nasus (Linnaeus, 1758); Leuciscus aspius (Linnaeus, 1758); Leuciscus baicalensis (Dybowski, 1874); Leuciscus burdigalensis Valenciennes, 1844; Leuciscus idus (Linnaeus, 1758); Leuciscus leuciscus (Linnaeus, 1758); Leuciscus waleckii (Dybowski, 1869); Parachondrostoma toxostoma (Vallot, 1837); Phoxinus phoxinus (Linnaeus, 1758); Rhynchocypris lagowskii (Dybowski, 1869); Rhynchocypris percnurus (Pallas, 1814); Rutilus rutilus (Linnaeus, 1758); Squalius cephalus (Linnaeus, 1758); [Cyprinidae: Leuciscinae]; Capoeta capoeta (Güldenstädt, 1773); Capoeta damascina (Valenciennes, 1842); Capoeta gracilis (Keyserling, 1861); Capoeta sevangi De Filippi, 1865; Capoeta trutta (Heckel, 1843); Pseudaspius sp.; Ptychobarbus chungtienensis (Tsao, 1964); Schizopygopsis stoliczkai Steindachner, 1866 [Cyprinidae: subfamily not distinguished]; Hemiculter leucisculus (Basilewsky, 1855) [Cyprinidae: Xenocypridinae]; Esox lucius Linnaeus, 1758 [Esocidae: Esocinae]; Gobio gobio (Linnaeus, 1758); Gobio soldatovi Berg, 1914; Pseudorasbora parva (Temminck et Schlegel, 1846); Sander volgensis (Gmelin, 1789) [Percidae: Luciopercinae]; Stenodus nelma (Pallas, 1773) [Salmonidae: Coregoninae].

In the majority of the 110 records mentioned above, the findings represented random discoveries. This existing knowledge was recently supplemented with an additional 95 records from France published by Mathieu-Bégné et al.6. The majority of those records (73) were findings of the parasite on Leuciscus burdigalensis, followed by records from Leuciscus leuciscus (22). A direct merging those new records with the old ones would, however, distort the overall picture, because the new records were not random, but were the result of targeted sampling, specifically focused on finding Tracheliastes polycolpus.

Mathieu-Bégné et al.6 concluded that, throughout its colonization history, T. polycolpus demonstrated the ability to infect local host species in French watersheds. There, its main host species are two Leuciscus species: in the northeastern half of France, it is Leuciscus leuciscus, while in the southwestern half of France it is Leuciscus burdigalensis. This preference for one species over the other is simply because the ranges of L. leuciscus and L. burdigalensis do not overlap, although the two species are closely related phylogenetically (see6, for a map of the ranges of both species in France).

Tracheliastes polycolpus can also infect other cyprinid species in France. Lootvoet et al.7 investigated the host range of T. polycolpus in two rivers in southwestern France. They revealed that while T. polycolpus is most prevalent on Leuciscus burdigalensis it was also occasionally found infecting five alternative host species: the toxostome (Chondrostoma toxostoma), gudgeon (Gobio occitaniae), minnow (Phoxinus phoxinus), roach (Rutilus rutilus), and chub (Squalius cephalus). Using causal analysis, the authors showed that the primary factor explaining host range in T. polycolpus was their ecological proximity (i.e., a close ecological niche relative to Leuciscus species, the main hosts of T. polycolpus) rather than phylogenetic proximity. In follow up studies, Mathieu-Bégné et al.8 showed that this ability of T. polycolpus to exploit different host species was associated with molecular plasticity measured as changes of gene expression in T. polycolpus depending on the host species infected. They reveal the existence a common molecular strategy used to exploit alternative host species such as gudgeon or minnow, that differs from that of Leuciscus burdigalensis. Specifically, T. polycolpus expressed biological processes related to energy acquisition on alternative host species, rather than investing in cellular machinery on its main host. This plasticity may have played an important role in the dynamics of T. polycolpus invasion in France, potentially allowing the maintenance of parasitic populations on alternative host species.

Geographic range

Tracheliastes polycolpus has a broad distribution across the Palearctic, having been recorded from Finistère, Brittany, France [48°31′46.86′′N, 4°25′28.34′′W] in the West to Sakhalin [49°13′N, 143°07′E] in the East and from Lapland [68°05′N, 27°25′E] in the North to central Iraq [34°36′N, 43°40′E] and Sihuan, Yunnan, China [30°39′N, 104°03′E] in the south (Supplementary file S2).

More focused zoogeographic studies have recently been conducted in France, where T. polycolpus is considered an emerging pathogen6,9. Rey et al.9 attempted to reconstruct the invasive history of T. polycolpus in French watersheds. Based on ABC models and genetic data, Rey et al.9 demonstrated that T. polycolpus was introduced into France from a single genetic pool, most likely following the 1920s introduction of its original host (the common ide) via aquaculture in central France. Afterwards, peripheral watersheds were rapidly colonized by T. polycolpus in three waves over a period of as little as two decades. After remaining undetected for the first 10 years, T. polycolpus underwent a major demographic expansion. However, it is worth noting that the range expansion of T. polycolpus in France, despite its high invasive potential, has not been recorded in the northeastern part of France6. This range limitation was subsequently shown to be due to the host’s local genetic background rather than environmental conditions. This spatial limitation suggests that host–parasite coevolutionary dynamics can constrain invasion of emerging parasites such as T. polycolpus.

Location on host

In most cases, the parasite has been reported from the caudal, dorsal, pelvic, pectoral, and anal fins, and occasionally from the skin. Interestingly, T. polycolpus exhibits micro-habitat preferences within its hosts10. Hence, T. polycolpus tends to be more often recorded on the anal fin, pelvic fin, and the external side of the pectoral fin, while it is very rarely found on the host body, for instance10. Only exceptionally, T. polycolpus was reported from the gill arch of common bream by Monod and Vladykov11 and from pike and Siberian dace according to two secondary Russian records cited by Pugachev5. A single record from a scale was mentioned by Hirvenoja12.

Pathogenicity

Only female T. polycolpus are parasitic. They attach to the fins of their host and cause fin erosion through their feeding activities. Tracheliastes polycolpus can also induce inflammatory changes in the damaged sites on its host, which may then favor secondary viral, fungal, or bacterial infection10. In addition to this direct effect, T. polycolpus can also have some indirect pathogenic effects on its host, such as reducing growth13, altering locomotion and feeding abilities (14, annexe 1), or impacting host genetic diversity and demography15,16.

Pathogenicity of T. polycolpus is influenced by both internal and external factors17. For instance, the number of parasites on fins is positively associated with individual parasite fitness whereas parasites tend to be larger and more fecund on larger hosts17. In a later study, it was further elucidated that pathogenicity varies non-linearly and inversely with host body size, although higher burdens are still observed on larger hosts18.

Similarly, the ability of T. polycolpus to infect its hosts is influenced by both abiotic and biotic factors. Local environmental conditions have been shown to affect the prevalence level of T. polycolpus at the micro-geographic scale (see19). Such micro-habitats are characterized by deeper zones with low stream flow, where the author suggested that the conditions for T. polycolpus attachment to its host are better, or shallow zones with whirlpools in which a “washing machine” effect tends to increase the encounter rate between T. polycolpus and its host. Similarly, in Mathieu‐Bégné et al.20, the authors investigated the impacts of host, parasite, and environmental microbiota factors in the prevalence of T. polycolpus. Using a semi-experimental set up conducted in a river with caged hosts, the authors found that the host fin microbiota changes after infection. Furthermore, they were able to show that a particular family of bacteria, the Burkholderiaceae, was associated with infected fins in T. polycolpus adults and larvae, suggesting that these bacteria could be involved in the infection process of T. polycolpus. Hence, the pathogenic effects of T. polycolpus have been studied in an integrative perspective, revealing both direct tissue damage and indirect metabolic or behavioral consequences, as well as complex interaction with its biotic and abiotic environment, making it a useful system for studying host–parasite interactions in freshwater ecosystems.

Morphology

Old-style descriptions and visualizations of T. polycolpus were published by Hermann3, von Nordmann2, and Vejdovsky21. More recent morphological information was provided by Gurney22 and Kabata23. The latter data and images were too general and lacked the potential to discriminate Tracheliastes at the species level. A few authors published drawings11,24–28 or photographs of the female habitus12,29–32. Aubrook and Fryer27, Blanchet et al.15, and Prunier et al.33 illustrated attachment of the parasite to fish fins. The ultrastructure of the bulla was studied by Benkirane et al.34, while some SEM micrographs were published by Kuang and Xie35. Markevich36 and Yamaguti37 described the male, but the descriptions lacked morphological detail.

None of the sources mentioned above is sufficient to define T. polycolpus as a species and conclusively differentiate it from congeners.

Biology

Von Nordmann2 described and illustrated the ovaries inside the genital trunk, the egg sacs, the nauplius, and copepodid stages, while Vejdovsky21 also focused on reproductive aspects, including the anatomy of reproductive organs, embryogenesis, and early developmental stages such as nauplius and copepodid. The life cycle of a congener (T. maculatus) was described in detail by Piasecki38. Despite the above, some French authors disseminated simplified and potentially deceptive information about the life cycle of T. polycolpus (see7).

Genetic aspects

Genetic resources of T. polycolpus have been primarily focused on microsatellite data for population genetic studies. In France, such population genetic studies have shown that T. polycolpus exhibits a weak to moderate spatial genetic structure with a clear pattern of isolation by distance along river networks33. Fine-scale analyses using microsatellite markers and sibship reconstruction further reveal substantial gene flow, characterized by frequent downstream dispersal over several tens of kilometers and more limited upstream movements likely mediated by infected hosts rather than by passive larval drift alone33. Comparisons with fish hosts indicate that parasite populations are often less genetically structured than host populations, from which we infer that T. polycolpus disperses over larger spatial scales than its hosts39. This pattern has been found in several host-parasite systems39.

In addition to the microsatellite data, a high-quality reference transcriptome assembly for T. polycolpus was recently produced. This resource was constructed from 15 adult female parasites sampled from three different host species (Leuciscus burdigalensis and two alternative hosts) in a single southwestern French river19. This transcriptome represented an important milestone in the characterization of Tracheliastes polycolpus, as it provided the first high-throughput sequencing resource available for this species. It enabled the identification of species-specific genes and molecular-based functional characterization of the molecular mechanisms involved in host switching in this emerging parasite. Precisely, this assembly yielded 17 157 non-redundant protein-coding genes (76% successfully annotated) and included 7979 T. polycolpus-specific genes compared with the two closest referenced species (Lepeophtheirus salmonis and Caligus rogercresseyi). Such genomic resources are essential for moving beyond descriptive studies toward a mechanistic understanding of the adaptive potential of T. polycolpus. However, no mitochondrial barcodes, which are essential for phylogenetic studies, have been reported for the species to date. Only Piasecki et al.40 recently published the COI gene sequence of T. maculatus, representing the first COI sequence in the genus.

Materials and methods

Fish samples for the presently reported study were collected in the northern part of Lake Dąbie, within the city limits of Szczecin, Poland, by a professional fisherman. At our request, specimens of ide, Leuciscus idus, were delivered to the landing site at the Stołczyn Base and examined promptly by the senior author. The fish were already dead before reaching the landing site. Ide is not considered a “fish suitable for consumption,” therefore, it is normally discarded or released at the site. The fishing gear consisted of stationary fyke-nets attached to long guiding wings, spread on long poles, stuck to the bottom. The fish were examined on seven occasions between 8 and 24 May 2024. An estimated 390 ide were examined and a total of 15 specimens of Tracheliastes polycolpus were collected. Some of them were used for scanning electron microscope (SEM) studies, while others were utilized for genetic barcoding.

For SEM examination, the parasitic copepods were first rinsed in 75% ethanol and then dehydrated through a stepwise acetone series (30%, 50%, 75%, 90%, and 100%). Specimens were subsequently dried using a Polaron E3000 CO₂ critical point dryer (Quorum Technologies, Laughton, East Sussex, UK). After drying, they were sputter-coated with an Au–Pd alloy in a JEOL JEE-4X thermal evaporator (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). Scanning Electron Microscopy was conducted with a Hitachi SU-70 field-emission instrument (Hitachi, Naka, Japan) operated at 1.5 kV. All preparation and SEM imaging were completed at the Faculty of Mechanical Engineering and Mechatronics, West Pomeranian University of Technology (ZUT), Szczecin, Poland.

Morphological terminology used in the description and the discussion follows Huys and Boxshall41 and Kabata23. The dental formula of the mandible follows the standards introduced by Kabata23 and widely used by subsequent authors.

Specimens selected for DNA extraction were processed using the High Pure PCR Template Preparation Kit (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quantity and purity were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel. A fragment of the mitochondrial COI gene (cytochrome c oxidase subunit I) was amplified by end-point PCR using the universal primers LCO1490 (GGTCAACAAATCATAAAGATATTGG) and HCO2198 (TAAACTTCAGGGTGACCAAAAAATCA) described by Folmer et al.42. PCR cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 60 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min, followed by cooling to 4 °C. Reactions were performed on a T100™ Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad) using the GoTaq PCR system (Promega), with each 25 μL reaction containing 5 μL Green GoTaq® Flexi Buffer, 2.5 μL MgCl2 (25 mM), 0.5 μL PCR Nucleotide Mix (10 mM), 0.125 μL GoTaq® DNA Polymerase (5 U/μL), 0.5 μM of each primer, and 2 μL of template DNA. PCR products were checked on a 2% agarose gel and subsequently submitted for bidirectional Sanger sequencing to Genomed S.A. (Poland). Chromatograms were assembled and edited in Geneious 8.043, and sequences were compared to GenBank entries using BLAST44. Translation into amino acids was carried out to verify sequence integrity and to screen for potential nuclear pseudogenes, insertions, deletions, or premature stop codons. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogeny was reconstructed in raxmlGUI 2.045. Prior to tree inference, the best-fitting nucleotide substitution model was identified using ModelTest-NG v0.1.746, with GTR + I + G4 selected as the optimal model under both the Akaike (AIC) and Bayesian (BIC) Information Criteria. The best-scoring ML tree with bootstrap support values mapped onto its branches was visualized in MEGA 1147.

Results

Redescription of Tracheliastes polycolpus from the type host

Morphological study

The focus is on morphological details of the female that were hitherto not covered or inadequately presented in available descriptions. The overall body shape presented here is based on SEM composite photos. A short video is available as Supplementary file S4. Males were not seen (studied) because of their extreme scarcity.

Overall structure

The habitus is typical for representatives of the genus Tracheliastes, i.e., slim and elongated body consisting of three main tagmata: “neck”, “arms”, and “genital trunk”. The “neck” represents the somites of the cephalon plus the maxillipedal somite. The “arms” are the modified maxillae which are displaced posteriorly relative to the maxillipeds. Distally they are attached to the bulla. The “genital trunk” represents the posterior thoracic somites and abdomen. A region of the body referred to as the “torso” is sometimes recognized as a posterior part of the cephalosome constituting the bases for the maxillulae and maxillae. One of the studied females (Fig. 2) was ovigerous (OV) while the other one (Fig. 3) was not (NO). None of the specimens had a bulla, which was accidentally detached. In live females, the neck and arms were very contractile and capable of changing length, while the genital trunk was more stable. The respective measurements were as follows in fixed SEM specimens: arms = 2.54 mm (OV) and 3.46 mm (NO); neck = 2.39 mm (OV) and 2.90 mm (NO); genital trunk = 4.33 mm, and (OV) and 4.04 mm (NO). The egg sacs were 4.24 mm long. The arms are longer than the neck, therefore for practical reasons the animal should be measured not from the tip of the cephalon but from the bulla to posterior tip of the genital trunk. This is how it aligns with the flow of the water while attached to a fish fin. The total length of the body was 7.54 mm (OV) and 8.19 mm (NO). The length including the egg sacs was 11.78 mm (Fig. 2). Discussion

Morphological remarks

While measuring the lengths of the three major body regions (neck, arms, genital trunk), we observed that only the genital trunk length is relatively stable, and the neck and arms varied substantially due to contraction and extension. Therefore, studies considering body proportions should be based on a large number of specimens and extreme results are likely to be misleading. Of the seven Tracheliastes species hitherto regarded as valid, only two (T. maculatus and T. polycolpus) have been adequately studied according to modern standards (38,40, and this study). The remaining five species, T. tibetanus Kuang, 1964, T. chondrostomi Hanek, 1969, T. sachalinensis Markevich, 1936, T. longicollis Markevich, 1940, and T. brevicorpus Kuang, 1980, can only be distinguished based on the body proportions40.

Tracheliastes maculatus, as determined by Piasecki et al.40, differs from T. polycolpus in the shape of the antennule, mandibular formula, number of secondary denticles on Claw 1 of the antennal endopod, shape of denticles on the first antennal endopod segment, the relative size of the lateral process of the antennal exopod, and the apical denticulation of the antennal exopod (Table 1).Table 1Comparison of differentiating morphological traits of Tracheliastes maculatus, T. polycolpus, T. chondrostomi, and T. sachalinensis sensu Nagasawa and Urawa (2017)48.Morphological featureTracheliastes maculatusTracheliastes polycolpusTracheliastes chondrostomiTracheliastes sachalinensissensu Nagasawa and Urawa 2017AntennuleCylindricalClaviformClaviform?Secondary denticles on Claw 1 of antennal endopod10 + 61–2 + 2–?Denticles on first antennal endopod segmentBifidSingle–?Lateral process of antennal exopodSmallProminentProminent?Apical denticulation of antennal exopodMultirow patch of densely packed strong denticlesSingle row of delicate denticlesDelicate denticles?Mandibular formulaPP_bbbbbbpPsbbbbbbpPsbbbbbbpPsbbbbbbCaudal rami setae0?1 + 1–?P, primary teeth; p, smaller primary teeth; s, secondary teeth; b, basal teeth (according to Kabata23).

Piasecki et al.40 critically reviewed and discussed T. chondrostomi, without, however, reaching a conclusion concerning its status. Tracheliastes infecting the nase, Chondrostoma nasus, were first reported by Kollar49, who collected the fish at the fish market in Vienna, Austria. Kollar mentioned that at that time, nase was the most commonly traded fish there. Initially, he was uncertain about the parasite’s identity and emphasized the differences between the newly found organisms and those depicted by von Nordmann. However, after examining more specimens, he concluded that those copepods represented T. polycolpus. Also, Marcu50 identified the nase parasites as T. polycolpus. All findings of Tracheliastes on the nase are from the Danube River basin. Hanek51 found T. chondrostomi in the Latorica River, Kráľovský Chlmec, Slovakia, some 500 km from Vienna, Austria. After providing new details in the presently reported study and further analysis and comparisons with published information (Table 1) we now conclude that T. chondrostomi is a junior subjective synonym of T. polycolpus. This reduces the number of valid Tracheliastes species to six.

Tracheliastes sachalinensis, infecting Leuciscinae fishes in Japan, as illustrated by Nagasawa and Urawa48, has the same mandibular formula as T. polycolpus. The body shape and proportions of the Japanese specimens are more similar to those of T. polycolpus, but the maxillule is more similar to that of Tracheliastes sachalinensis from the original description. Consequently, the specific identity of those Japanese findings must be confirmed.

Tracheliastes mourkii Hoffmann, 1881 – we have just determined that the specimens described by Hoffmann52 came from “a small fish” donated by Mr. Theodor Mourek, a bookseller in Prague, and were known to Vejdovsky21, who commented on them, treated them as Tracheliastes polycolpus, and even provided the host name: “Phoxinus laevis” = Phoxinus phoxinus (Linnaeus, 1758). It is evident that Vejdovsky21 worked on the same specimens from Theodor Mourek that four years later were used by Hoffmann52 to establish his new species, Tracheliastes mourkii. Vejdovsky21, noticing some differences from the von Nordmann’s specimens proposed to establish a new variety: Tracheliastes polycolpus var. phoxini Vejdovsky, 1877. We now consider T. mourkii to be a junior subjective synonym of T. polycolpus.

One of the specific features developed by lernaeopodid copepods is the frontal organ. It is used by copepodid (chalimus) stages to secrete the frontal filament and its extension lobes and by adult females to produce bulla53. The frontal organ was first observed by Gurney22 in T. polycolpus but interpreted as the “ ‘nuchal organ’ of other copepods”. The true nature of the frontal organ was explained and well-illustrated in a series of drawings by Friend54 for Salmincola salmoneus (Linnaeus, 1758). The frontal organ is responsible for secreting the filament and its extension lobes. An exposed frontal organ showing its fine structure was documented and described by Piasecki55 for Achtheres percarum. In T. maculatus, it was described and illustrated by Piasecki38.

During the presently reported study, we noted the importance of the overall shape and proportions of the antennule, which are compared between species in Table 1. The antenna also expresses characters that are potentially useful in discriminating species. Piasecki et al.40 suggested that the secondary denticles on Claw 1 of the antennal endopod might help to differentiate species in some lernaeopodid genera. The feature can be seen in illustrations of Salmincola56 and Lernaeopoda57, but without an explicit acknowledgement by those authors. Confirmation of their presence in T. polycolpus has made them a potential discrimination trait within the genus. We have now demonstrated this for T. polycolpus. In the presently reported study, we also observed the taxonomic value of the denticles on the first antennal endopod segment, the proportions of the two processes/setae of the antennal exopod, and the form of the apical denticulation of the antennal exopod.

Thoracic legs have long been considered absent in adult Lernaeopodidae females. Piasecki58, for the first time, reported two pairs of vestigial legs in T. maculatus, Achtheres percarum von Nordmann, 1832, and Salmincola extensus (Kessler, 1868). They were described in detail and illustrated by Piasecki38 for T. maculatus. In the presently reported study, we also noted a presumed third vestigial leg. For the first time, we observed that the vestigial legs are surrounded by two concentric patches of cuticle that differ in fine structure from the surrounding areas. A canal associated with those sites38 suggests a secretory role, but this assumption requires further biochemical/physiological studies.

The vestiges of legs 1–3 are followed by four paired similar sites on the ventral surface of the genital trunk. These are also marked by two concentric patches of cuticle but lack the setae, and the inner patch is distinctly darker. Very similar structures can also be observed in the dorsal part of the genital trunk (Fig. 2).

The genital process of T. polycolpus was illustrated by Vejdovsky21, who also noted the presence of two lateral setae, rediscovered here. We tentatively infer that these setae may represent paired vestiges of the caudal rami.

Molecular study

Despite the long history of research on Tracheliastes polycolpus, molecular data for this and other species in the family Lernaeopodidae remain extremely limited. The COI sequences generated herein represent the first mitochondrial barcode data for T. polycolpus and it is only the second species of the genus Tracheliastes for which COI data are available40. In the maximum-likelihood phylogeny, the newly generated sequences cluster consistently with T. maculatus, confirming their close genetic relationship and supporting the monophyly of the genus as currently defined. Although this placement is well supported, the broader phylogenetic context of Tracheliastes within the Lernaeopodidae remains poorly resolved due to the scarcity and uneven taxonomic coverage of available COI sequences in public databases. Most lernaeopodid taxa are either completely unrepresented or represented by multiple identical or near-identical sequences derived from a limited number of studies, which restricts robust intergeneric and intrafamilial comparisons.

In the case of the COI, this molecular marker has been widely applied across siphonostomatoid copepods, most extensively in the lernaeopodid genus Salmincola59,60, and performs reliably at the species and sister-species level, where independent studies recover congruent topologies and clear barcoding gaps. The recent and most taxonomically comprehensive COI-based phylogeny of Lernaeopodidae to date59 resolved many intra- and intergeneric relationships within the family with moderate to high support, but emphasised the value of combining COI with nuclear markers (28S) and acknowledged that resolution remains constrained by uneven taxon coverage. Our tree (Fig. 10) is consistent with these conclusions: the clustering of the two T. polycolpus sequences with T. maculatus, supported by the smallest pairwise distances (Supplementary file S5) and high bootstrap support, is precisely the kind of shallow-node relationship that COI recovers reliably. The relative arrangement of Tracheliastes, Salmincola, Achtheres, and other lernaeopodid genera in our tree should nonetheless be regarded as preliminary, given the small number of available COI sequences for the family relative to59 and would benefit from the addition of genomic-scale data. Within these caveats, the present analysis fulfils its intended purpose: confirming the genetic identity of T. polycolpus, providing the first COI reference sequences for the species, and anchoring it phylogenetically against its only congener for which molecular data exist.

The addition of COI data for T. polycolpus, therefore, constitutes an important reference point for future molecular identification, comparative taxonomy, and phylogenetic analyses of Lernaeopodidae copepods, ensuring reproducibility of the approach applied in the present work. When interpreted together with the detailed ultrastructural data presented here, the molecular results reinforce the taxonomic conclusions drawn from morphology, including the close affinity between T. polycolpus and T. maculatus and the distinctiveness of T. polycolpus relative to other nominal congeners. Further expanding molecular sampling across the family, particularly for freshwater taxa and type species of related genera, will be essential for testing hypotheses on host associations, habitat transitions, and evolutionary relationships within Lernaeopodidae. In this context, broader taxon coverage, combined with next-generation sequencing approaches, including multilocus and genomic-scale datasets, will be necessary to achieve high phylogenetic resolution and to reveal deep and recent divergences within the family.

Taken together, our results provide a modern taxonomic framework for Tracheliastes polycolpus and serve as a reference point for integrative morphological, molecular, and ecological studies of lernaeopodid copepods.

Concluding remarks

Future studies on representatives of the genus Tracheliastes should include the mandibular formula, ultrastructural data on the antenna (secondary denticles of endopodal Claw 1 and the number and distribution of endopodal and exopodal side denticles), and vestigial caudal rami. The latter features might also be useful for the closely related genera, such as Achtheres, Salmincola, Parabrachiella (possibly also Basanistes and Thysanote). Newly discovered and very puzzling concentric patches of cuticle, resembling those associated with the vestigial legs, should also be studied in all copepods mentioned above. Tracheliastes sachalinensis (sensu Nagasawa and Urawa48) should be redescribed in detail, including genetic barcoding to verify its potential similarity with T. polycolpus.
