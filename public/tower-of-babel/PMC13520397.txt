Digital personalized trials of a multicomponent behavioral change intervention for increasing habitual low-intensity physical activity in middle-aged and older healthcare workers: results of a feasibility study
Authors: Mark J. Butler, Jerry Suls, Ciaran P. Friel, Joan Duer-Hefele, Patrick L. Robles, Frank Vicari, Thevaa Chandereng, Ying Kuen Cheung, Karina W. Davidson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrior research has shown that multi-component behavior change technique (BCT) interventions can potentially increase physical activity (PA) in middle-aged and older adults and thereby reduce risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality. However, habit formation theory suggests that the PA is most likely to be sustained when the behavior involves high levels of automaticity for walking. Therefore, PA behavior such as walking must be automatic to be sustainable. The current National Institutes of Health (NIH) Stage Model of Behavioral Intervention Stage 1B trial examines the feasibility of a digital, personalized, multi-component BCT intervention to increase the automaticity with which participants execute a walking routine to ultimately reduce likelihood of future CVD and mortality.MethodsA personalized, single-arm, in 44 participants involving digital recruitment and intervention delivery was delivered to adults ages 45 to 75 who worked in Northwell Health system. Participants continuously wore a Fitbit activity tracker during a 2-week baseline and a 10-week intervention. During intervention, participants received five daily BCT associated with habit formation (i.e., goal-setting, action-planning, self-monitoring of behavior, behavioral practice/rehearsal, and behavioral repetition) to follow to execute a walking plan. Participants rated their satisfaction with personalized trial components at the conclusion of the intervention and made ratings of the walking plan’s hypothesized mechanism of action—automaticity—over the course of the intervention phase. Step counts, adherence to the walking plan, adherence to step-count monitoring of step count, and attitudes about personalized trial implementation were also collected.ResultsHalf of the participants acquired automaticity of their daily walking routine in this virtual trial, but there was considerable heterogeneity across participants. Satisfaction with personalized trial elements was consistently high (rated as “satisfied” to “very satisfied” for most items). Adherence to the walking routine and self-monitoring of steps was moderate to good, with participants adhering to their walking plan for 55% of days. Eight of 34 participants achieved an increase of 2000 steps from baseline.ConclusionsThis stage 1b study found that patients were satisfied with personalized trial components and implementation. Furthermore, half of the participants in the trial reached the hypothesized automaticity criterion over the course of intervention (albeit at different rates). This preliminary evidence suggests that personalized BCT interventions to initiate a walking habit among middle-aged and older adults are both feasible and acceptable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40814-026-01865-z.

Introduction

Sedentary behavior is a risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1, 2] and other health conditions [3]. This may be particularly problematic for middle-aged and older adults who rarely engage in even low-intensity activity (e.g., walking) [4–6], although observational studies show that older individuals who increase their steps by 1000 to 2000 per day have a lower mortality rate [7, 8], reduced inflammation [9], and increased life satisfaction [10]. Experimental evidence from between-subject randomized clinical trials (RCTs) also indicates that physical activity (PA) interventions designed to increase walking can reduce deaths [11, 12]. Although conventional between-subject RCTs delivering behavioral change techniques (BCTs), such as goal-setting, self-monitoring, and behavioral repetition [13, 14], have had success increasing PA on average, not all participants show gains in exercise, while some may instead show modest or no effects [8, 15, 16]. This heterogeneity of effects (HTE) [15, 16] reflects the fact that “habit typically develops asymptotically and idiosyncratically, differing in rate across people, cues and behaviours” [17]. An N-of 1 focus on individual-level habit formation monitored frequently over an extended period, however, can identify each person’s rate and intensity of habit change and benefits (if any) of an intervention [17, 18].

To determine which participants may benefit from interventions with HTE such as PA interventions, personalized (N-of-1) trial designs are ideal. This patient-centered approach using single-case experimental research design can provide clinical information for selecting the best treatments for individual patients [13]. Personalized trials are designed to assist patients in making treatment decisions that are informed by high-integrity, evidence-based information examining how interventions influence outcomes of interest [14]. Prior N-of-1 trials have been successfully applied to such conditions as fatigue, depression, and preventive behaviors [18, 19]. Two types of designs can be differentiated. Observational (also referred to as “naturalistic”) N-of-1 trials assess study participant responses to a single intervention over time. Experimental N-of-1 trials are multiple crossover trials with data collected continuously throughout the trial with alternative time periods of treatment, alternative treatment, or placebo therapies in randomized blocks.

The present study used an observational N-of-1 design as a first step toward the goal of developing a single-patient methodological approach that is automated and can be used at point of care. Specifically, this paper describes the results of a study testing the feasibility, acceptability, adherence, and preliminary efficacy of an innovative, virtual personalized trial approach for increasing habitual PA in middle aged and older adults. Support for the feasibility and acceptability of virtual trial elements will serve as a foundation for an experimental N-of-1 trial comparing the effects of an intervention versus placebo or an alternative treatment.

Uptake of within-subject (N-of-1) trial designs has been limited because they required effortful (often manual) engagement of clinicians and patients over extensive time periods. An automated, sustainable, user-friendly technology platform was unavailable to make conduct of such a trial facile for researchers, clinicians, or participants [20]. Recent advances in computing and technology, however, make available automated, sustainable, user-friendly platforms [20], which integrate smartphones, texting, e-mail, activity trackers, and remote sensors capable of recruitment, delivering instructions and behavioral interventions, and obtaining real-world data at home or work without personal contact.

The integration of these virtual technologies is welcome, but questions remain whether automated personalized instructions, daily interventions, and data collection involving no direct personal contact are feasible and acceptable to participants. This study tested the feasibility and acceptability to older patients of a virtual personalized trial delivering a set of behavioral change prompts (BCTs) to make a walking habit an automatic response pattern. Five BCTs were selected for a multi-component intervention on the basis of habit formation theory [17] and empirical reviews identifying strategies associated with improving PA [21]: goal setting, action planning, self-monitoring, behavioral practice/rehearsal, and behavioral repetition. This multi-strategy intervention should prompt repetition of a walking plan in the presence of contextual cues to strengthen a mental cue–behavioral association [17, 22] to the point that the cue triggers a mental representation that elicits the action with minimal conscious oversight.

One primary aim of this study was to determine the feasibility that a person can reach automaticity of a daily walking routine, involving an increase of 2000 steps more than their baseline, by virtual (i.e., with no personal contact) BCT delivery. The automaticity of a behavior is defined as engaging in that particular behavior without intentional thought [23]. Automaticity is considered a mechanism of action (MoA) by which these BCTs increase low-intensity walking [17, 24–26] and is an essential component for the development of habitual walking [26]. Automaticity of walking habit was assessed with a reliable and valid self-report questionnaire answered each day a walk had been scheduled [25]. If a participant’s daily score on the questionnaire leveled out and reached a set criterion on more than seven consecutive days (referred to as asymptote), automaticity was deemed to be achieved [17]. A primary outcome was the proportion of participants who achieve an asymptote for a daily walking routine during a multi-week intervention phase and whether variation in reaching automaticity across participants justifies the rationale for a personalized trial approach.

A second primary aim was to assess (via questionnaire) how satisfied and accepting participants were about the overall personalized trial protocol and its components (i.e., the BCTs, video explanations, and demonstrations of study devices and procedures—all delivered virtually). Level of satisfaction with the virtual, automated protocol should indicate whether scaling up the intervention for a larger study is feasible.

Secondary outcomes (delivered by text messaging) included self-reported daily adherence to the walking plan and daily adherence to self-monitoring and recording of the daily step count over the course of the 10-week intervention. Step counts were available to the participant (and researchers) from the Fitbit activity tracker that participants wore throughout the study. Degree of acceptance of several elements of protocol implementation was assessed with a questionnaire sent to participants at the conclusion of the study.

Methods

Design

A total of 44 participants were enrolled in virtual personalized interventions designed to increase the automaticity of walking behavior. Potential participants engaged in a 2-week baseline phase to gauge both their adherence to wearing an activity tracker for 10 h or more per day and responding to automated text messages. Then, those who were deemed adherent were assigned to the 10-week intervention phase and continued to wear the activity tracker for 10 h or more each day. By providing step counts, the 2-week baseline afforded the ability to compare baseline steps with step counts achieved during intervention. Upon starting the intervention phase, each participant received text message prompts to execute five BCTs on five successive days of each week. Prior to baseline, each participant had selected the 5 days each week they wanted to receive the text prompts. A primary outcome measure involved daily self-reports about the automaticity with which the participant executed the walking plan. Another primary outcome important for assessing protocol feasibility was participants’ levels of satisfaction (completed via questionnaire at study completion) with components of the personalized trial, such as instructions delivered via e-mail, text, or videos; wearing the Fitbit; and answering survey questions. Secondary outcomes consisted of daily self-reports about adherence to the daily walking plan and to daily self-monitoring of step count. In addition, Fitbit-assessed step counts collected during baseline were compared with step counts during intervention. Also, ratings of acceptability of personalized trial implementation (e.g., instructions were easy to follow, receiving daily text message prompts and surveys via cell phone was enjoyed) were collected at study completion. The project (20–1182) received institutional review board (IRB) approval from Northwell Health on April 16, 2021. The protocol was registered at https://clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04869644) on May 3, 2021, and last updated on June 13, 2022. Additional details regarding the study design can be found in the study protocol previously published [27].

Participants, recruitment, and screening

All participants were volunteers employed by Northwell Health, a large, academic medical system. Potential participants were recruited using e-mail newsletters and lists of those who have previously expressed an interest in participation in a personalized trial with the Institute of Health System Science. Flyers were also shared via Northwell Health social network platforms and Northwell Health employee social media site (i.e., Facebook).

Those who expressed interest were directed to a series of webpages and short videos that described study aims and procedures. If they indicated continued interest, they were asked to complete an initial online screening measure containing questions regarding study inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants who did not meet inclusion criteria (Table 1) were found to be ineligible and were notified within two business days. All eligible individuals proceeded with the digital consent process. Study eligibility criteria

Consent process

Those persons who were eligible to participate after the eligibility screening received an email message from study staff with a link to access a short video explaining key details of the study protocol and an electronic copy of the informed consent form. A four-question screening measure was also delivered to assess participant understanding of the protocol and consent process. In accordance with all regulatory guidelines, consent was then obtained electronically, and a copy of the signed consent form was mailed to the participant. Signed consent forms were stored electronically on a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)–compliant, Northwell Health–approved shared drive accessible only to the IRB-approved study staff.

Participation compensation

After completing all aspects of the study, participants received a $100 payment card as compensation. Participants also were allowed to keep the activity monitor (Fitbit Charge 4™, a value of $150), at no charge.

Procedures

Prior to the start of baseline, participants completed a demographic questionnaire and were shipped an initial study kit, including a Fitbit Charge 4™ activity tracker and printed materials to guide the participant through the study. Written instructions directed participants to download a Fitbit app to their personal phone and to charge the Fitbit and sync it to their phone. Anonymous study accounts were created to protect privacy of the participant. Participants who required support in preparing their Fitbit activity tracker for use in the study were assisted by the study team over the phone.

Baseline phase of the trial

During the 2-week baseline phase, participants were asked to wear the Fitbit for a minimum of 10 h per day and at night (even during sleep). To assess potential adherence to the trial protocol, participants were asked to respond to a daily acknowledgement survey (e.g., “Today is a baseline day? Yes/No”). Minute-level heart rate data from the Fitbit was collected as an indication of adherence to wearing the device. Additional text messages were sent to remind participants to sync their Fitbit or respond to the acknowledgement surveys if staff noticed non-adherence on the platform’s dashboard. Those persons who adhered to wearing the device and responded to text messages at a rate of 80% or more over the first 10 days of baseline were then contacted about when the 10-week intervention phase would begin. An 80% adherence criterion was adopted to help ensure that those moving to the intervention phase would continue to follow the protocol and hence provide quality data about protocol feasibility. There is no universal agreement about what constitutes high adherence, though an 80% adherence rate represents a commonly used standard for medication adherence [28]. To assess whether adherent versus nonadherent participants differed in demographic characteristics (i.e., age, sex, race, ethnicity), chi square analyses were conducted.

Preparation for intervention phase of the trial

Following day 10 of baseline, participants declared eligible to continue received an email message asking them to form a plan (i.e., time and location) for walking 2000 steps more than their 2-week baseline: “You are about to begin the 10-week intervention portion of your study. During the intervention portion, you will receive BCT text messages tailored to the specifics of your walking plan for 10 weeks. To tailor your walking plan to your daily schedule, please complete the following form (link to URL).” Participants had the final 4 days of the baseline period to formulate their walking plan.

Participants indicated on what days of the week they could walk, the general time of day (i.e., morning, afternoon, evening), followed by specific 1-h blocks. A final question inquired about where they can walk consistently with a parenthetical note that “The average person needs to walk 1 mile, or for about 20 min, to achieve 2,000 steps.” The completed walking plan form was used by research staff to program the timing and tailored content of messages sent to individual participants during the 10-week intervention.

Intervention phase

During the intervention phase, participants received five BCTs that were chosen on the basis of habit formation theory and followed a review by the research team of 40 strategies that increased PA in previous studies [14]. These BCTs were selected on the basis of the following considerations: (a) hypothesized to change automaticity [21]; (b) associated with large effect sizes from the extant multi-BCT interventions; (c) relevant to walking; (d) required only low-resource intensity; (e) implemented easily via text; and (f) could be delivered repeatedly. The following five BCTs comprised the multi-component intervention: goal-setting (i.e., the behavior to be achieved); action-planning (i.e., detailed planning of the behavior); behavioral practice/rehearsal (i.e., increasing habit and skill at a time different from the scheduled walk when performance may not be necessary); self-monitoring (i.e., using a method to monitor the behavior); and behavioral repetition/habit formation (i.e., prompt rehearsal and repetition of behavior in the same context so the context elicits the behavior) [24]. The BCTs were administered using two text messages per day on five successive days for 10 weeks. An example of a self-monitoring text message would be as follows: “How many steps did your Fitbit say you took during your planned walk?” The order and timing of BCT messages were tailored based on the participant’s walking plan and the goals were tailored based on the participant’s walking goal [27]. For example, a participant may plan to walk at 8:00 am and walk 8000 steps (2000 steps more than their 6000 steps during baseline). The timing and content of text messages would be tailored based on this. Participants completed a daily measure of automaticity after the time of their planned walk via text message with a link to an online survey.

Post-intervention phase of the trial

Following the intervention, participants received a survey comprised of nine items to assess their level of satisfaction with the overall trial and its components. In addition, a report of each participant’s trial results was sent alongside an email offering congratulations on their completion of the study. This report summarized how the individual’s data (i.e., automaticity and step count) changed throughout the study and presented the rate of adherence to wearing the Fitbit, adhering to BCTs, and responding to texts/surveys. All feedback was presented in both narrative and numerical form (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Finally, participants were sent a completion survey text with a link to the “Participant Attitudes and Opinions Toward Personalized Trials Survey,” consisting of Likert-style items about personalized trial implementation (e.g., “I felt like I knew what was coming next in my personalized trial”) (For all item content, see the “Outcomes” section.)

Outcomes and statistical analysis

Supplemental Table 1 lists the relationship between each aim and the outcome assessments, including how each is measured.

Primary outcomes

Within-subject habit automaticity

An average daily automaticity score was computed for each participant’s ratings of the four-item Self-Report Behavioural Automaticity Index (SRBAI) [25] administered daily during the intervention period. Each item started with the phrase “Today, going on my (planned) walk was something…” followed by “I did automatically,” “I did without having to consciously remember,” “I did without thinking,” and “I started doing before I realized I was doing it.” The participant’s ratings of agreement with each statement on a 4-point scale were summed together to form a total score from 0 to 12 for each week of the intervention.

Changes in the automaticity levels of habits over the course of the intervention period were tracked to identify whether scores leveled out and reached a score of 8 (i.e., two-thirds of the maximum possible) or higher on more than seven consecutive days (which were defined as days during which the participant had agreed to follow their walking plan) [25, 29]. In accordance with this criterion, each participant was judged to have reached (or not reached) asymptote at this point in time. Time-to-event analyses were conducted to examine participant differences in reaching asymptote for automaticity. Overall time-to-event was represented using Kaplan–Meier curves [25, 30]. Prior research has shown that habits can potentially be formed over 66 days if behavior is practiced within a consistent context [26]. As the intervention is 70 days (10 weeks) in length, we were hopeful that 50% or more of the sample would demonstrate automaticity for walking.

Satisfaction with the personalized trial

Participants rated several statements on 4-point scales, with “Not at all satisfied” and “very satisfied” as endpoints. The items were as follows: “overall satisfaction with the BCTs”; “overall ease of using the BCTs”; “overall effectiveness of the BCTs”; “video explanations and demonstrations of study devices and procedures”; “text messaging for reminders (e.g., sync your Fitbit)”; “text messaging for data collection (i.e., surveys)”; “use of the Fitbit Charge 4™ to track activity and sleep”; “study communications”; “presentation of your results.” Means and standard deviation (SD) aggregated across participants for each item were computed. Our goal was to demonstrate that participants reported they were “satisfied” or “very satisfied” with all elements of the trial.

Secondary outcomes

Proportion of days participants adhered to walking plan

The proportion of days during the intervention phase that each participant responded “Yes” to following the walking plan were aggregated across participants.

Proportion of days participants adhered to self-monitoring

The proportion of days during the intervention phase that each participant reported checking the Fitbit and recording the step count was aggregated across participants.

Attitudes and opinions about the personalized trial implementation

This survey consisted of 9-item, 5-point Likert scales with responses ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” The items were “I found the onboarding process (from the initial survey to getting my materials) for my personalized trial unnecessarily complex,” “I think my Fitbit device was easy to use,” “I think that I needed the support of a technical person to be able to successfully complete my personalized trial,” “the informational videos helped me understand how to participate in this study,” “the materials I received in the mail were clear and easy to follow,” “I enjoyed receiving daily text message prompts and surveys on my cell phone,” “I would imagine that most people would learn how to get started with their personalized trial very quickly,” “I have found my personalized trial to be very burdensome,” “I felt very confident starting my personalized trial,” “I felt like I knew what was coming next in my personalized trial,” and “I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going with my personalized trial.”

Within-subject change in daily steps

Fitbit-assessed daily steps were computed for the baseline assessment period (2 weeks) and intervention (10 weeks). Prior studies and meta-analyses have found high reliability and validity for Fitbit wearable activity trackers used to estimate participant step counts [31–34]. For individuals missing greater than 10% of days of their minute-by-minute daily step count data during the total trial duration (i.e., less than 1296 min of wear time), imputation was utilized to account for missingness.

Exploratory analyses

Though all analyses of primary and secondary outcomes were planned a priori, we did conduct additional exploratory analyses. These exploratory analyses examined whether automaticity levels mediated the association between the intervention and daily step counts, whether walking plan adherence differed based on participants’ achievement of (or failure to achieve) automaticity, and whether adherence to the self-monitoring BCTs was associated with participant automaticity.

We also examined the heterogeneity in the treatment effect (HTE) of the intervention on participants steps in exploratory analyses. The HTE indicates how greatly the effects of a particular treatment vary between individual participants in a population [35]. The larger the HTE, the greater the indication that an intervention may benefit from using a N-of-1 trial design [36]. Additionally, the authors have calculated an index of heterogeneity to quantify the HTE for interventions such as the current trial [13, 37].

Statistical power and sample size

Sample size was based on the primary outcome of automaticity. Results from previous research show that levels of automaticity prior to intervention are generally low (with automaticity of health behaviors ranging from 0 to 20% for participants) [26]. Assuming that execution of a walking routine will not be automatic for most participants entering the trial, we predict that only 15% of the sample will display automaticity of walking behavior prior to the intervention. Following physical activity interventions, automaticity has ranged between 62 and 81% in intervention samples [26]. Assuming a conservative intervention effect, we felt that it was reasonable that 66% of the sample would display automaticity for walking upon trial completion. With a sample size of 60 and an alpha level of 0.05 (one-sided), we expected 83% power to detect intervention effects using a one-sample z-test of proportions.

Results

Of the 114 Northwell employees who were assessed for study eligibility, 49 participants were enrolled (see Fig. 1). Those deemed ineligible either did not meet or complete the inclusion criteria form (n = 35) or did not provide study consent (n = 30). Of the 49 who enrolled, five participants withdrew prior to the baseline phase, leaving a sample of 44 prior to the intervention phase. The majority (59.1%) of the 44 participants who began the 2-week baseline were between the ages of 45 and 65 (see Table 2); 84.1% were female (n = 37), 77.3% were White, 6.8% were Black, 6.8% were Asian, and 2.3% identified as “Other.” Another 6.8% declined to respond about their race. In terms of ethnicity, 86.4% were non-Hispanic/Latino and 13.6% were Hispanic/Latino. Participant demographics by enrollment status

Two participants withdrew from the study during the baseline phase, and eight participants were deemed ineligible after the baseline because they failed to achieve at least 80% adherence (a requirement for beginning the 10-week intervention phase). Analyses of participant characteristics showed no difference in important demographic characteristics (including age, gender, race, and ethnicity) between those who moved to the interventions phase and those who withdrew or were non-adherent during baseline (Supplemental Table 2). Thirty-four participants began the intervention phase, but two discontinued the intervention—one because of an adverse effect and a second for unknown reasons. Thus, 32 participants completed the intervention trial.

Participant satisfaction with personalized trial elements

Table 4 shows the means and SD for each of the nine items aggregated across participants (e.g., “Please rate your satisfaction with the BCT text messages”). All means exceeded the midpoint (i.e., 1.00) and ranged between 2.38 and 2.97 on the satisfaction scale ranging from 0 (“Very Dissatisfied”) to 3 (“Very Satisfied”). Participant responses indicated high levels of satisfaction with all trial elements. Within-subject change in daily steps

To assess whether each participant’s step count during intervention phase increased from their 2-week baseline, average step count during baseline and intervention were analyzed with generalized linear mixed model analyses. For individuals missing greater than 10% of days of minute-by-minute daily step count throughout the trial, imputation was used to account for missingness. The results reported represent the average values obtained after 100 imputations.

Figure 3 shows the forest plots of changes from baseline to intervention for each participant. Eight participants showed positive and statistically significant step improvements (p < 0.05). Thus, of those who completed the intervention phase, eight (23.5%) significantly increased their step counts. Exploratory analyses

Does automaticity mediate the association between the intervention and daily steps?

A regression was conducted to assess the association between participants’ (average) automaticity score and within-subject change in step count. The multiple R-squared was 0.024 (residual standard error [SE] = 3.87 with 32 degrees of freedom). No support was found for automaticity as a mediator of step count increases.

Is walking plan adherence associated with automaticity?

To determine whether adherence to the walking plan was associated with automaticity, a Welch two-sample t-test was conducted to compare adherence rates to the walking plan between the group that achieved automaticity (n = 17) and the group that did not (n = 17). Rates of adherence to the walking plan were comparable between the group with automaticity (77.8% adherence) versus those without (72.7% adherence, p = 0.554).

Is adherence to the self-monitoring BCT associated with automaticity?

Rates of adherence to the self-monitoring BCT were also compared between the sample of participants who achieved automaticity and the sample that did not using another Welch two-sample test. Adherence to this BCT did not significantly differ between the participants who achieved automaticity (70.6% adherence) and the participants who did not (61.2% adherence, p = 0.310).

Was there HTE for the intervention between participants?

Additionally, the current trial examined heterogeneity in effects of the intervention on daily step counts using a method previously established for personalized trials [37]. First, the effect of the intervention on steps was modeled using linear mixed models with first-order autocorrelation (AR [1]). We then compared two models of the effect of the intervention on step counts (one with a random intercept and one with a random slope) using a likelihood ratio test. The random slope model accounts for HTE, whereas the random intercept model does not. Comparisons of these two models showed that a random slope model did not fit better (p > 0.05), indicating that the intervention effectiveness did not vary significantly between participants (Supplemental Table 3). However, these results also showed that the outcome was highly variable between subjects (SD = 3040 steps) and within subjects (SD = 4200 steps). These findings do suggest that daily steps may vary more within an individual than between individuals, though not at a level that achieves statistical significance in the current sample. Additionally, we calculated an index of heterogeneity to quantify the HTE for the intervention on daily steps [13, 37]. Briefly, this index measures the extent of HTE by incorporating the within-participant variation, between-participant variation, and average treatment effects into the SD of the random slope. This index has values ranging from 0 to 1, with higher scores indicating higher levels of heterogeneity. These results supported the previous results that the intervention did not have HTE (Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

This National Institutes of Health (NIH) Stage Model 1B trial assessed the feasibility and acceptability of a personalized behavioral intervention delivered entirely by virtual means. Prior between-subject RCT tests of behavioral interventions to increase physical activity have used some aspects of virtual delivery. However, participants may be wary, challenged, or lack interest in a trial that administered all trial elements remotely. In fact, the present study delivered all personalized trial elements—including recruitment, screening, informed consent, study orientation, run-in, activity and sleep monitoring, subjective reporting about trial satisfaction/acceptability, intervention, and data collection—with no direct personal contact. Participant reports expressed satisfaction with the virtual trial features, such as the demonstrations of study devices and procedures, text messaging for data collection, and Fitbit activity/sleep tracking. Elements of trial implementation were also evaluated positively. For example, participants reacted positively to aspects of the trial including study onboarding and the clarity of trial materials. In sum, although several trial features were unfamiliar to participants, they appeared to find them acceptable.

Another unique feature of the trial was its use of a within-subject design to assess the preliminary efficacy of habit formation theory. The theory posits that repetition of a specific plan in the same context prompted by certain BCTs can increase the automaticity of a walking plan and increase step count. Although previous between-subject intervention studies have tested the effects of some of these BCTs on physical activity [22], none has assessed their effects on a daily basis over a period of several weeks. The within-subject observational N-of-1 design provided estimates of each participant’s performance, in contrast to conventional between-subject trials that can only provide an estimate for the hypothetical average subject. The present design and methodology allowed us to track the rate at which each participant did or did not achieve automaticity for a walking routine and whether each participant significantly increased their step count as a function of the intervention. Our preliminary findings show that some participants (50%) did acquire automaticity, although at different rates. Though this is lower than the anticipated rate of 66% we estimated in our sample size calculations, our sample size did fall significantly short of our goal of 60 participants. It is possible that with a larger sample, we may have identified a greater proportion of participants who achieved automaticity. In addition, some participants exhibited a significant increase in their step count. Though formal analyses of HTE did not identify significant HTE for daily steps, the current findings do provide some support for the idea that not all participants benefit equally from BCT interventions to increase habitual walking behavior.

Variation was also found in adherence to daily self-monitoring of step count and adherence to a daily walking plan. On average, the plan was followed on 50% of days. Step count was self-monitored on 66% of days. Hence, although participants reported favorable reactions to the personalized trial elements and implementation, their adherence to the self-monitoring BCT prompt was inconsistent. However, as with step counts and automaticity, variation across subjects was substantial with some individuals showing full adherence and others non-adherent on a majority of study days.

Limitations

Sample size calculations indicated that a sample of 60 would have sufficient power to detect intervention effects. However, only 44 participants entered baseline and 34 were randomized to the intervention. Hence, this stage 1B trial failed to accrue the number of participants needed based on initial statistical power estimates. A substantial number of persons failed to meet or complete the inclusion criteria (n = 35) or did not provide informed consent (n = 30). These matters may prompt concern that the virtual trial lacked feasibility because of the demands or unfamiliarity it presented. However, the trial was conducted during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, which caused many disruptions to work, social life, and health for an extended period. These challenges may have contributed to the number of persons who did not provide consent or complete inclusion criteria. We favor the second explanation, as those participants who enrolled and actually engaged in the intervention uniformly reported positive attitudes about trial implementation and satisfaction with personalized trial elements. It is possible that being provided with compensation for participation and a free Fitbit activity tracker may have resulted in higher satisfaction ratings. Future studies should examine the feasibility of implementing this intervention without compensation among individuals already using Fitbit devices. We should note that despite these limitations, some participants did attain automaticity and significantly increased their step count.

A brief 2-week baseline was used to identify participants who would be more likely to be adherent to the 10-week phase and to collect baseline step counts. Seventy-seven percent of the enrolled participants met the 80%-or-more adherence threshold. However, a longer baseline might increase the precision of usual step-count estimates. Similarly, a longer intervention phase and the addition of a post-intervention (no-BCT-prompts) phase would provide a more comprehensive assessment of longer-term outcomes. For the present study, costs and concerns about attrition were perceived to be too high to consider these extensions. In addition, we did not measure participant levels of automaticity during the baseline period. Repetition in a consistent context is essential for the development of automaticity [23], suggesting that individuals who do not walk consistently would have low levels of automaticity. However, measuring automaticity during the baseline period would confirm this. In addition, participants in the baseline period were not blinded to their daily step counts. Seeing these daily step counts on their Fitbit activity tracker prior to the intervention may have influenced participant’s walking. Furthermore, no systematic record-keeping was conducted to identify reasons for missing data. This was thought to be both intrusive and likely to potentially bias our ability to assess feasibility. The two questionnaires completed at the close of the study, however, inquired about any difficulties or burden participating in the study protocol. Another limitation is the fact that participant recruitment was limited to employees of a large healthcare system and the majority of the sample identified as white and female. This means that the degree to which our results generalize to more representative populations is unclear. It is possible that individuals who work within a healthcare system may be more motivated to walk and improve their physical activity. It is also possible that individuals employed in a healthcare system may have higher levels of educational attainment which may influence how the participants in this trial engaged with the intervention and answered outcome measures. Additional research in other samples will be required to confirm this.

Future directions

Several elements of this virtual personalized trial were found to be feasible, especially satisfaction and acceptability of trial components and implementation. Half of the participants demonstrated the feasibility of producing automaticity of a walking plan via virtual delivery of a multicomponent BCT intervention. One point of concern is that a substantial proportion of eligible persons declined to give consent; greater efforts need to be mounted to attract willing participants. Furthermore, additional research may be required to identify what participant characteristics (e.g., demographics, environment, social networks, medical comorbidity) may be associated with participation in this type of physical activity trial. Additional refinement may be required to refine the protocol to make it appealing to a broad, generalizable population.

Both the demonstrated feasibility for eliciting positive responses to trial elements and instilling an automatic walking routine in half of those enrolled suggest that scaling up the project may be appropriate. The logical next step would involve a series of virtual multi-crossover, blinded, randomized, single-patient N-of-1 trials after habit initiation to maintain the behavioral habit. This next stage of trials could have an individual try two interventions (e.g., BCT habit maintenance intervention versus no BCT intervention) multiple times, to determine if the BCT maintenance intervention maintains automaticity, and the absence of the BCT intervention does not maintain automaticity. This type of trial design would also aid in identifying heterogeneity of habit maintenance or harms across participants. Consideration of future trial designs is informed and consistent with the NIH Stage Model of Intervention [14]. Continued research will serve our long-range goal to develop a single-patient, N-of-1 approach that is automated, can be used at point-of-care, and can ultimately revolutionize how we interact with patients and set the stage for a truly transformative approach to precision therapeutics.
