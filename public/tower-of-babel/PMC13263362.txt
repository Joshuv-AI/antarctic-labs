Exploring the bioactive potential of green synthesized silver nanoparticles coated serratiopeptidase and chalcone derivatives
Authors: Aswin Viswan, Anice Jossia Hermon, Anjana Hemabindu Madhusoodanan, Prannavraj Geetha Amutharaj, Sreelakshmi R. Nair, Sanjeev K. Ganesh, Samyuktha Arimalai Dinakararaja, Ethiraj Kannatt Radhakrishnan, Mohanasrinivasan Vaithilingam, Subathra Devi Chandrasekaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSerratiopeptidase is a proteolytic enzyme that has gained considerable interest in the medical and pharmaceutical industries due to its analgesic, fibrinolytic, and anti-inflammatory effects. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were coated on Serratiopeptidase to analyze and compare its bioactivity along with the bioactivity of newly synthesized chalcone derivatives.ResultsIn the present study, two novel chalcone derivatives were successfully synthesized and evaluated for their biological activities. Among the synthesized compounds, VIT-A3S exhibited the highest proteolytic activity (231.63 ± 0.43 U/mL) and anti-inflammatory activities (92.67 ± 0.48%), indicating its therapeutic potential. The structural elucidation of VIT-A3S was carried out using spectroscopic techniques, including Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), and Ultraviolet–Visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy, confirming its molecular architecture. Furthermore, silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) were synthesized and characterized, with their colloidal stability assessed using zeta potential analysis. The integrated approach combining synthetic chemistry, biological evaluation and nanotechnology highlighted the potential of VIT-A3S for further pharmaceutical development. Overall, the study highlighted that the chalcone derivatives (VIT-A3S and VIT-PAGS) possessed a broad spectrum of biological activities including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and protease functions, with significantly higher potency compared to serratiopeptidase based formulations.ConclusionThese findings suggest that chalcone derivatives represent a promising class of therapeutic agents, with potential application in the management of inflammation-related disorders and warrant further in-depth pharmacological and clinical investigations.

Introduction

Serratiopeptidase is a zinc-containing metalloprotease with a molecular weight between 45 and 60 kDa [23]. It possesses an extensive background in medicine and is commonly utilized to address various inflammatory conditions and inflammation. This enzyme has employed across various fields, including gynecology, orthopedics, surgery, dentistry and otorhinolaryngology due to its analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antiedema characteristics [51]. It is also reported for its proteolytic and fibrinolytic effects which are achieved by dissolving complement and enhancing plasmin activity through the inhibition of plasmin in-activators [7]. Organisms such as Serratia liquifaciens, Bacillus licheniformis, and Bacillus subtilis have also been documented for their synthesis of the enzyme serrapeptase. The advancement in nanotechnology and several studies demonstrated the efficacy and potential ability of silver nanoparticles in having anti-inflammatory properties [56]. There are several other nanoparticles reported to have anti-inflammatory property viz, Gold (Au), Zinc oxide (ZnO), Selenium (Se) etc. and the widely studied are the silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) [1]. Various species of Phyllanthus have been documented for the manufacture of silver nanoparticles. Phyllanthus emblica, Phyllanthus amarus, Phyllanthus pinnatus, and Phyllanthus urinaria L. are extensively utilized for the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles, which possess notable antibacterial, catalytic, and mosquitocidal capabilities Ajitha et al. [2, 11, 40, 48, 50]. Phyllanthus niruri, a member of the Phyllanthus family, has been used for the synthesis of silver nanoparticles that demonstrated antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and mosquitocidal characteristics [4, 18]. A recent work by Kotaru et al. [28] found that silver nanoparticles synthesised from Phyllanthus niruri had anti-diabetic properties as well [28].

Silver nitrate predominantly exerted its anti-inflammatory effect via physical and chemical mechanism, whereas chalcones a category of natural chemicals, function through a variety of metabolic routes. Chalcones, or 1,3-diphenyl-2-propen-1-ones, constitute a significant class of flavonoids within the plant kingdom [21, 33]. Chalcones have several interesting biological characteristics other than anti-inflammatory function such as anti-cancer, antimicrobial, antiprotozoal, anti-ulcer, antihistaminic, and antioxidant effect [26]. The exhibition of these features were the results from their diminutive structures and Michael acceptor characteristics, which facilitate rapid or reactive interactions with biological molecules, rendering them tolerant to various biological compounds [57]. There are various types of reported chalcones, such as simple or classical chalcones and hybrid chalcones, which are synthesized or modified artificially. Bichalcones, which have two chalcone moieties in a single structure, and dihydrochalcones, which have a decreased α-β unsaturated double bond, were the two further classifications for them [41]. It has been discovered that chalcones that scavenge free radicals also have anti-inflammatory properties. Flavokawain B, isoliquiritigenin, licoagrochalcone A, licochalcone B, licochalcone C, and mallotophilippens C-E are a few examples. Flavokawain B can limit the levels of NF-B protein, reduced TNF-R release, and prevent the formation of prostaglandin E2 and nitric oxide in murine leukaemia macrophages [17]. Many research have advanced chalcone-based drug discovery and offered useful information for advancing in vivo chalcone studies [57]. In this study, two novel chalcone derivatives were synthesized and their biological activities were assessed. Among these, VIT-A3S showed the most potent protease activity and anti-inflammatory effects, indicating its therapeutic potential. The structural confirmation of VIT-A3S was done by Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), and Ultraviolet–Visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy. Furthermore, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were prepared and characterized, with their colloidal stability evaluated by zeta potential analysis. Ag coating of serratiopeptidase enhanced its proteolytic and anti-inflammatory activities. This also imparted antioxidant potential to the nano enzyme composite compared to the native enzyme. This demonstrated the enhanced therapeutic potential and stability of serratiopeptidase coated silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs). In comparative evaluation, chalcones exhibited superior bioactivity relative to the nano formulated enzyme.

Materials and methodology

Sample collection

Phyllanthus niruri plants were gathered from Kottooli, Calicut, Kerala, India, which is located at 11.261233° N, 75.799849° E. Phyllanthus niruri is a commonly seen wild medicinal plant in this region. The collected plant sample was identified and confirmed as Phyllanthus niruri L. by Dr. Rajasekaran C., Professor and Taxonomist, Department of Biotechnology, Vellore Institute of Technology, Vellore, India. The herbarium of Phyllanthus niruri was submitted with Specimen No. VITMN029-1 The plant was first rinsed with tap water and then thoroughly cleaned using double distilled water. The leaves were separated, blanched and washed followed by shade drying of the sample in sunlight for 1 week. After being ground with a grinder, the dried leaves were stored in an airtight container [30].

Synthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) from Phyllanthus niruri

Leaf powder (5 g) was measured, mixed with 80 mL of Milli-Q water, and placed in a boiling water bath for 1 h, following which the mixture was filtered using Whatman filter paper (no.1). To the filtrate (70 mL), 0.118 g of freshly prepared 10 mM silver nitrate (AgNO3) was added. The mixture was thereafter maintained on a magnetic stirrer with constant stirring at 120 rpm for 2 h. The synthesized product was subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min and consecutively rinsed with ethanol and distilled water. The resultant residue was dried at 80 °C for 24 h [45].

Characterization of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs)

The synthesized silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) was characterized through various techniques, including UV–Vis Spectroscopy, Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and X-Ray Diffraction (XRD), to confirm and analyze their functionality, functional groups, and morphology.

UV–Visible spectroscopy analysis of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs)

Initial characterization of the silver nanoparticles was conducted using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer. The transition of silver ions into nanoparticle form was detected through the analysis of UV–Visible spectroscopy of the solutions following the dilution of the sample with 70% ethanol. The spectroscopy of the silver nanoparticles was performed from 300 to 600 nm, utilizing ethanol as the blank [8].

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) analysis of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs)

FTIR analysis was done using Agilent Cary 630, FTIR Benchtop Systems for the silver nanoparticles to find biomolecules and the functional groups that are responsible for reducing and stabilizing them. The recorded spectra were drawn as a graph of transmittance (%) vs. wavenumber (cm–1) [46].

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs)

The synthesized nanoparticles were examined using X-Ray Diffraction with a D8 Advance X-ray Diffractometer (Bruker, Karlsruhe, Baden-Wurttemberg, Germany), a secondary technique employed to ascertain particle size by the analysis of diffraction peaks. The Scherer equation was employed to determine the size of silver nanoparticles [3] ie; D = K × λ ÷ β × Cosθ.

Zeta potential and dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs)

The surface electric charge of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were evaluated by examining the most stable particles during instances of electrostatic repulsion among them. The zeta potential of the nanoparticle was assessed using the Particle Metrix Zetaview-PMX 130-Mono laser, while the hydrodynamic diameter of silver nanoparticles in solution was determined using dynamic light scattering (Horiba Scientific, Model: SZ-100) [3].

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs)

A thin film of the sample was produced on a carbon-coated copper grid by applying a minimal quantity of the sample onto the grid. The moulded specimen was subsequently utilised for SEM analysis (CARL ZEISS CARL EVO 18) [24].

Synthesis of chalcone derivative

The chalcone derivatives VIT-A3S and VIT-PAGS were prepared by using different methods. Usually, chalcone compound synthesis follows a condensation reaction. VIT-A3S 6 was prepared using 5-methyl-2-hydroxyacetophenone 1 and 3,4-dimethyoxy benzaldehyde 2 as reactants along with ethanol and NaOH. 5-methyl-2-hydroxyacetophenone 1 was fixed as the starting compound. The reaction order followed by initial addition of 0.1 g acetophenone 1 followed by 5 mL ethanol, 0.052 g NaOH and finally 0.11 g 3,4-dimethyoxy benzaldehyde 2. The contents in the round bottom flask were kept in a magnetic stirrer for 24 h at room temperature with constant stirring. VIT-PAGS 7 was synthesized using two steps: the hydroxyl groups were protected first and the Claisen Schmidt condensation followed. Salicylaldehyde 3 (0.610 g) was first methylated using 2.12 g of methyl iodide 4, with potassium carbonate (K₂CO₃) serving as a catalyst and 2 ml of dimethylformamide (DMF) as the solvent. The reaction was conducted at ambient temperature for 1 h, yielding 2-methoxybenzaldehyde 5. In the next phase, 5-methyl-2-hydroxyacetophenone 1 (200 mg) was combined with 2-methoxybenzaldehyde 5 (0.180 g) in an Claisen Schmidt condensation reaction that used 0.1598 g of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) as the base and 6-7 mL of ethanol as the solvent. The reaction mixture was stirred continuously for 18 h at ambient temperature. Thin layer chromatography (TLC) was conducted at 2–3 h intervals to verify product formation. After the formation of product, the mixture was worked up using equal amount of ethyl acetate, dil. HCl and water in separating funnel for about 3 times and the organic phase was then dried and further purified [16].

Synthesis of VIT-A3S 6

Claisen Schmidt condensation was carried out between 0.665 mmol 5-methyl-2-hydroxyacetophenone 1 (0.1 g) and 0.665 mmol 2-methoxybenzaldehyde 5 (0.11 g) in the presence of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) in ethanol as the solvent. The quantities and equivalence indicated that a 1:1 molar ratio of the two organic compounds was used, with NaOH serving as the base.

Synthesis of VIT-PAGS 7

A reaction was performed with salicylaldehyde 3 treated with methyl iodide 4 in the presence of potassium carbonate (K2CO3). Salicylaldehyde 3 (5 mmol, 0.532 mL) was used as the starting material, while methyl iodide 4 (15 mmol, 0.93 mL) and potassium carbonate (15 mmol, 2 g) were used in three equivalents each. The reaction was carried out in 5-7 mL. Under these conditions, the phenolic OH group of salicylaldehyde 3 was methylated, yielding 2-methoxybenzaldehyde 5 as the product. Then, Claisen Schmidt condensation was performed for 1.3318 mmol 5-methyl-2-hydroxyacetophenone 1 (0.2 g) and 1.3318 mmol 2-methoxybenzaldehyde 5 (0.16 mL) in the presence of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) in ethanol as the solvent.

Purification of the synthesized chalcones

Column chromatography was performed for purification. The elution started with 1% hexane as mobile phase to 10% ethyl acetate. The elution of pure product was confirmed by performing TLC. The fractions having single spot in TLC plate was collected and dried for characterization and analysis (Vidja et al., 2020).

Purified chalcone (15–20 mg) is combined with 400–450 µL of CDCl3 and transferred to cleaned 5 mm NMR tubes. A Bruker 400 MHz NMR spectrometer was chosen to obtain NMR spectra of the purified sample, operating at 400 MHz for 1H NMR and 101 MHz for 13C NMR [34, 55]. HPLC analysis of chalcones

The synthesized chalcones were analysed by HPLC using acetonitrile as mobile phase at 365 nm with run time of 10 min.

Screening for proteolytic activity of chalcones

Skim-Milk Agar (10%) was prepared, and 4 wells of 5 mm were punched using cork borer. Different concentrations of chalcones were prepared from 5 mg stock viz: 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 mg in Dimethyl sulfoxide (1% DMSO). To each wells respective concentration of chalcones were added and incubated for 24 h [6].

Secondary screening for proteolytic activity by azo-casein assay

The modified protocol of Oda & Murao [35] was followed for determining the protease activity of enzyme as well chalcone. To 200µL of chalcone and enzyme respectively, 0.05 mL of azo casein was added followed by incubation at 45 °C for 20 min. The subsequent step involved the addition of 0.06 mL Trichloro acetic acid (TCA) to terminate the reaction, followed by incubation of the contents on ice for 10 min. The contents were subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min. To the supernatant, 0.3 mL of FC reagent and 0.45 mL of Na2CO3 were added, followed by incubation in the dark for 30 min. The optical density was then measured at 660 nm [35].

Purification of serratiopptidase from Bacillus siamensis by triple phase partitioning method

Fresh seed culture of Bacillus siamensis (PP077310). (3%) was inoculated into tryptic soy broth and incubated for 28 h followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 20 min. To the supernatant obtained, ammonium sulphate of 30% and equal volume of t-butanol was added. The contents were then kept in shaking incubator for 28 h at 37 °C [10].

Coating of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) to serratiopeptidase

Synthesised Ag NPs (10 g) along with 10 mL of purified enzyme is mixed with 10 mL of methanol and the contents were kept in magnetic stirrer for 4 h with constant stirring to evaporate the solvent. The remaining contents was collected and dried at 60 °C to obtain the nanoparticles coated enzyme. The silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) coated serratiopeptidase was then characterised by zeta potential and DLS [52].

Anti-inflammatory activity

Anti-inflammatory assay was performed according to the protocol described in Joseph et al. [25]. Freshly obtained human blood (5 mL) from a volunteer who had not taken NSAIDs for two weeks prior to the experiment was combined with an equal volume of sterilized Alsever’s solution. After centrifugation of the blood at 3000 rpm for 10 min, the packed cells were rinsed three times with an iso saline solution. The blood volume was quantified and reconstituted with a 10% v/v iso saline solution. Enzymes coated with silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) and different quantities of chalcones were subsequently added to each tube. Subsequently, 1 mL of phosphate buffer (0.15 M, pH 7.4), 0.5 mL of a 10% red blood cell solution, and 2 mL of hyposaline were added. In a separate tube, 1 mL distilled water was introduced, and remaining contents, excluding test sample (supernatant), was incorporated. The tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 20 min. The concentration of haemoglobin in the supernatant was spectrophotometrically quantified at 560 nm. Diclofenac (100 µg/mL) was employed as a standard.

Anti-oxidant assay

The methodology described in Sharma et al. [44] was followed for performing anti-oxidant assay. 2 mg of 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) was added to 50 mL of methanol and for the standard, 10 mg of ascorbic acid was weighed and mixed with 10 mL of distilled water. Different chalcone concentrations have been made and added to 2.5 mL of DPPH, distilled water has been used to bring the final volume down to 3 mL. The exact methodology was employed to assess the antioxidant activity of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) coated enzyme. The tubes underwent incubation at 37 °C for 20 min in darkness, and the absorbance was spectroscopically assessed at 515 nm.

Statistical analyses

Data are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test for anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidant assays, comparing all treatment groups with positive control. For secondary screening for proteolytic activity by azo-casein assay, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was done. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the analysis was done using GraphPad Prism 8.4.3.

Discussion

Nanoparticles generated from Hevea brasiliensis displayed a distinct absorption peak at 435 nm, signifying surface plasmon resonance, whereas a reduction in AgNO3 concentration led to decreased particle sizes. Correspondingly, nanoparticles derived from Mangosteen leaves exhibited a peak absorption at 438 nm, characterised by good resolution and rapid scanning.

The extract of Jatropha curcas seeds was utilised to synthesise silver nanoparticles, exhibiting a notable absorbance peak at 425 nm [20]. Rajendrachari et al. [39] demonstrated that the absorption peak at 435 nm is associated with electronic transitions between valence and conduction bands, hence affirming the production of silver nanoparticles.

In FTIR analysis, Haris et al. [22] observed absorption peaks at 2924, 3417, 1411, 1642, 1031, and 774 cm − 1 corresponds to similar functional groups and confirmed the presence of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs). According to Kumar et al. [29] silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) synthesized from leaf extract of Phyllanthus niruri showed functional groups like C=O, –OH and C=N which validateed and confirmed the synthesised nanoparticles were silver. David et al. [14] revealed in his study that the XRD spectrum displayed four distinct Bragg's peaks at 2θ values of approximately 38.2°, 44.3°, 62.2°, and 77.9°. These peaks corresponded to the face-centered cubic (FCC) structure of silver: {111}, {200}, {220}, and {311}, respectively. The Ag NPs-serratiopeptidase formulation’s zeta potential investigation showed a negative surface charge of around − 32.28 mV, which is within the reported stability range for silver nanoparticles. Anandalakshmi et al. [5] similarly linked negative zeta potential values to increased colloidal stability of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs), whereas David et al. [14] reported a zeta potential value of − 20.9 mV for green synthesised silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs), attributing the negative charge of the capping by plant-derived polyphenols. Also according to Azouri et al. [9], zeta potential values for silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) typically range from + 8 to -44 mV. The change in surface charge that is seen following engagement with serratiopeptidase indicates that the surface characteristics of the nanoparticles have been altered, suggesting a link between the enzyme and the nanoparticles. To definitively demonstrate enzyme coating, binding mechanism, and activity retention, more spectroscopic and enzymatic investigations are needed. However, zeta potential and particle size examinations only offer indirect evidence of such interaction.

The synthesis of silver nanoparticles from Phyllanthus niruri by Suresh et al. [50], showed that nanoparticle’s shape was obtained as round and aggregated, with an average size of range between 30–60 nm. Nano particles synthesized using plant extracts can be observed as larger particles because of the aggregation of nano particles during synthesis and preparation. And the morphology changes according to the preparation followed and the source. Ajitha et al. [2] also demonstrated that the size range of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) synthesized using Lantana camara leaf extract was between 10–100 nm. Another notable study by Sivakumar et al. [48] reported that silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) from the leaf extract of Phyllanthus urinaria L. had a size range of 30–60 nm, with a round shape and less agglomeration. Devi et al., [15] reported that, silver nanoparticles synthesized from endophytic fungi exhibited a peak at 3 keV, validating the presence of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs). The EDS mapping in Banerji et al. [12] green synthesized silver nanoparticles also showed peak near the region of Ag which is 3 keV thereby validating our study. Vidja et al. [54] synthesized chalcone compounds via aldol condensation and purified them using column chromatography and recrystallization. The compounds were characterized by physical properties and spectroscopic analysis viz, IR and NMR. Setyaningrum et al. [42] synthesized chalcone via Claisen-Schmidt condensation, reacting acetophenone and benzaldehyde with KOH catalyst using reflux and grinding methods. Ustabas et al. [53] analyzed the NMR spectra of the synthesized chalcone compound. The 1H NMR spectrum confirmed the conversion of amine to imine, with a new signal at 8.68 ppm, and the 13C -NMR spectrum showed the corresponding carbon signal at 163.04 ppm. These values were close to the calculated values of 8.83 and 170.07 ppm. The 1H -NMR spectrum also displayed proton signals from phenyl and furan rings (6.72–8.16 ppm), and the 13C -NMR spectrum showed carbon signals (113.65–163.04 ppm). Maidur et al. (2018) utilized 1H and 13C NMR analysis to confirm the molecular structures of the two synthesized compounds. Each molecule consisted of 15 protons and 23 carbon atoms. Theoretical 1H NMR spectra showed distinct proton peaks, while experimental spectra exhibited multiple peaks due to magnetic field-induced degeneracy and proton-proton interactions, validating the molecular structures of the synthesized compounds. The FTIR spectrum of the chalcone compound synthesized by Ustabas et al. [53] revealed significant structural features. The disappearance of the NH signal and the appearance of the N=CH imine group at 1630 cm1 confirmed the compound formation. The stretching vibration of aromatic C-H was detected at 3104 cm1, whereas the C=O stretching appeared at 1659 cm1, closely matching the calculated value of 1655 cm−1. Stretching vibrations corresponding to C=C and C–O–C groups were also detected at 1599 and 1222 cm−1, respectively. Maidur et al. [31] conducted a similar FTIR analysis on chalcone derivatives, observing 40 atoms and 114 normal vibrational modes in each molecule. Stretching vibrations corresponding to C-H were detected in 3270–3000 cm−1 region. Aromatic C–H stretching modes appeared in the 3206–3157 cm−1 range theoretically, and between 3263–3023 cm−1 in experimental measurements. A strong carbonyl stretching vibration appeared in the 1700–1600 cm−1 region, with experimental peaks at 1640 and 1641 cm−1, and theoretical ones in 1703 and 1670 cm−1. Stretching vibrations of C=C and C–C bonds, including those from aromatic rings, were found in the 1670–1200 cm−1 region.

Ultrasound-Assisted Three Phase Partitioning (UATPP) was effectively utilised by Pakhale et al. [36] for the enrichment and isolation of serratiopeptidase from Serratia marcescens. The formation of an interfacial precipitate layer, which separated and concentrates the target enzyme from the mixture is a crucial step in this assay. TPP offers numerous advantages over conventional concentration and separation techniques, including simplicity, single-step processing, cost-effectiveness, scalability, and enhanced purity. UATPP significantly reduced processing time while achieving higher recovery rates of serratiopeptidase compared to traditional Three Phase Partitioning. Sharma et al. [43] effectively purified cellulase and pectinase using the Three Phase Partitioning (TPP) method. The resulting three phases were separated, and the enzyme activity was measured after dissolving the interfacial precipitate in a pH 4.8 acetate buffer solution. The proteolytic activity exhibited by the chalcone compounds in skim milk agar plate as well as azo casein digestion assays signifies the newly synthesized chalcone derivatives possess proteolytic activity. Eventhough the literature limits the proteolytic explanation of chalcones, the newly synthesized chalcone derivatives shown notable activity and demonstrates its capability in cleaving proteins. [37] extracted chalcone compounds from A. keiskei and tested their ability to inhibit cysteine proteases, including SARS-CoV proteases. According to Maria et al. [32], the in vivo anti-inflammatory potential of the synthesized compounds was associated with their antioxidant and reducing capabilities observed in vitro, with three compounds showing promising anti-inflammatory effects and two exhibiting strong LO inhibition. Bano et al. [13] investigated chalcones, including 20-hydroxychalcones, and found that they exhibited mild to anti-inflammatory effects. However, substituting Cl, NO2, or OCH3 at either the ortho (C-2) or para (C-4) positions reduced their activity. But an oxy group introduction at meta positions (C-3 or C-5) enhanced their activity. One of the chalcones showed most potent activity, achieving approximately 90% inhibition of edema. David et al. [14] found that silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) cream synthesized from European black elderberry fruits extract reduced skin thickness by 50.78% in an 8-patient study, highlighting silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) potential as a potent anti-inflammatory agent. Chalcones were known to exhibit diverse biological effects, such as anti-fungal, antimalarial, and antimicrobial properties [47]. Singha et al. [47] screened various chalcone derivatives for antioxidant activity and they exhibited significant radical scavenging potential. Specifically, substitution with 2,4-dimethoxy, 2-methoxy, 4-methoxy, 2,4-dichloro, and 4-nitrobenzaldehyde moieties showed enhanced antioxidant activity. The chalcone derivative substituted with 2,4-dimethoxybenzaldehyde emerged as the most effective antioxidant agent. In contrast, derivatives bearing 2,4-dichloro, 2-methoxy, 4-methoxy, and 4-nitro substitutions displayed moderate to mild antioxidant activity. The free radical scavenging capacity of chalcones in the study by Sokmen et al. [49] was observed to increase with concentration. The observed antioxidant activity was associated with phenolic substitutions at the ortho and/or para positions of the aromatic rings, consistent with earlier studies. Chalcones featuring a 4-hydroxyphenyl moiety, demonstrated exceptional antioxidant potency, surpassing that of butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT). Keshari et al. [27] reported that silver nanoparticles derived from Cestrum nocturnum exhibited greater antioxidant activity (29.55%) than vitamin C (24.28%), highlighting the potential of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) as a powerful antioxidant.

Conclusion

Serratiopeptidase has wide applications in pharmaceutical and medical sectors because of it's anti-inflammatory, antibacterial and protease activities. The coating of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) extracted from Phyllanthus niruri with enzyme enhanced the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity. The chemically synthesized chalcone derivative has also proven to be a significant compound having anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant as well as proteolytic activity. However, the chalcone derivative exhibited notably higher therapeutic potential. The antioxidant assay revealed significant free radical scavenging activity at different concentrations of chalcones, with 2% and at 5%. These results highlighted the antioxidant potential of chalcones compared to the enzyme-based formulations. Furthermore, the percentage protection clearly demonstrated the effectiveness of chalcones, with the reference standard exhibiting 94.6% activity. Overall, the study highlighted that the chalcone derivatives (VIT-A3S and VIT-PAGS) possessed a broad spectrum of biological activities including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and proteolytic functions, with significantly higher potency compared to serratiopeptidase based formulations. These findings suggest that chalcone derivatives represent a promising class of therapeutic agents, with potential application in the management of inflammation-related disorders and warrant further in-depth pharmacological and clinical investigations.
