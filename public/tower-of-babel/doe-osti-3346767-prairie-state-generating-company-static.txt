EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The objective of this study is to evaluate the feasibility of storing 162.5 million tonnes of
industrially sourced carbon dioxide (CO2) over 20 years at the Prairie State Generating Company’s
(PSGC) facility near Marissa, Washington County, Illinois. The targeted CO2 storage formations
are the St. Peter Sandstone, Everton Formation and Knox Group, with the Maquoketa Shale
serving as the primary confining unit. Data from the Lively Grove #1 stratigraphic test well were
utilized to characterize the St. Peter-Everton and Knox storage complexes, construct static and
dynamic models, estimate prospective storage resources, and propose development strategies for
storing 8.125 million tonnes of CO2 annually at the PSGC site.

Static modeling
Rigorous 3-D static models of the St. Peter – Everton and Knox storage complexes were developed
to assess their prospective CO2 storage resources. Separate models were developed for each
complex, detailing injection storage units and overburden formations to capture vertical and lateral
heterogeneity. The process involved six main steps: compiling various data sources, creating 3D
structural frameworks, defining injection zones and confining units, scaling up porosity and
permeability data, analyzing well data, constructing trend maps of porosity, and populating grids
with data using geostatistical algorithms.
The static reservoir models for the Knox and St. Peter – Everton storage complexes were developed
using PetrelTM software, integrating various data including logs, structure surfaces, thickness maps,
well test data, and permeability data. Structural frameworks were constructed based on well log
correlations and regional surfaces refined with depth information from 2-D seismic data. For the
Knox storage complex, permeability estimations for different formations were conducted using
equations and methods tailored to their characteristics, with final models built as layer cake
structures. The St. Peter – Everton model incorporated similar methodology but with different
included surfaces, utilizing petrophysical log data for calibration and permeability estimations
based on the Illinois State Geological Survey transforms.
Knox storage complex: The structural framework of the Knox Group carbonates was constructed
using regional structural surfaces and thickness maps. Despite limited data availability, a layer
cake model was developed, integrating information from the LG #1 well to depict geological
characteristics and subsurface formations comprehensively. A project boundary centered around
the LG #1 well was established to ensure adequate spatial coverage for analysis. The model boasts
high resolution with 155 cells in both X and Y directions, allowing accurate representation of
subsurface structures. Additionally, a static reservoir model with a thickness of approximately
2,700 ft provides a complete picture of subsurface geology, enabling in-depth exploration.
Petrophysical logs reveal dense dolomite intervals alongside thinner dolomite streaks exhibiting
high porosity. These streaks, confirmed by image logs, are likely continuous, facilitating their
designation as distinct zones in the reservoir model. Furthermore, data scale-up techniques were
employed to accurately depict vertical heterogeneity, resulting in a detailed 3-D static reservoir
model covering a surface area of 45-miles by 45-miles with 179 layers.
St Peter-Everton storage complex: The structural framework for the St. Peter – Everton storage
complex incorporated regional surfaces from the Maquoketa Shale to the Eminence Dolomite,
covering various formations. The model aimed for a comprehensive porosity assessment and
identified trends in porosity and permeability across the storage units over a large area of 120 x 80

 ii

miles (193-km by 129-km). To balance computational efficiency and accuracy, a grid spacing of
1,320 ft x 1,320 ft (402 x 402 m) was chosen, covering an area of 45 by 45 mile (72.4 by 72.4
km) around the Lively Grove #1 well location and comprising a total of 5,962,502 cells.
The model focuses on two main storage units: the St. Peter Sandstone and the sandstone interval
of the Everton Formation (Everton sandstone), with the former exhibiting excellent reservoir
characteristics for natural gas storage based on past investigations. The Everton Dolomite acts as
a baffle with low reservoir quality, and variations in reservoir quality within formations like the
Joachim Dolomite prompted subdivision into distinct zones. Overburden formations like the
Platteville, Trenton, and Maquoketa exhibit very low porosity, while the Knox Group at the base
has intervals with vuggy porosity and intense fracturing, subdivided into four main zones.
The St. Peter Sandstone and Everton sandstone were subdivided into fine grids ranging from 0.5
to 6.2 ft thick to accurately model their reservoir quality. Variogram analysis was used to determine
vertical variogram ranges and layer thicknesses. Additionally, the intervals beneath the Everton
Formation, including the Shakopee and Oneota Dolomites, were divided into grids for further
study of their properties. Overburden formations such as the Joachim Dolomite, Platteville,
Trenton, and Maquoketa Shale were also subdivided into layers, resulting in a 3-D static reservoir
model comprising 5,962,502 grid cells across 182 layers within a 45 by 45 mile (72.4 by 72.4 km)
area.
The analysis of log and core data reveals variations in average porosity across different parts of
the model, with the St. Peter Sandstone exhibiting higher porosity compared to the Everton
sandstone, particularly in western regions. Trend maps were developed to guide static reservoir
modeling and co-kriging processes for more accurate porosity estimates. Variogram data analysis
was employed to understand the spatial relationship of data points and determine ranges of spatial
continuity, aiding in the distribution of porosity and permeability data using geostatistical methods
like sequential Gaussian simulation. The model also considered variations in permeability using
methods like the Illinois State Geological Survey (ISGS) and Lucia methods, with adjustments
made for dolomitic intervals. Finally, the model was trimmed using the project boundary to
perform dynamic reservoir modeling.

Dynamic reservoir modeling
Dynamic reservoir models were constructed to evaluate the feasibility of storing industrially-
sourced CO2 at the PSGC site near Marissa, Illinois, over a period of up to 20 years. These models,
developed using Landmark's Nexus® reservoir simulation software, were used to assess CO2
injectivity, well requirements, CO2 plume size, and pressure distribution over time. Separate
models were created for the St. Peter – Everton and Knox storage complexes, with simulation
results informing Class VI permits and providing insights into the area of review (AoR).
Knox storage complex: The static reservoir model of the Knox Storage Complex including fluid
PVT data and rock properties were exported to Nexus® to perform dynamic reservoir simulations.
The fluid PVT data, based on a compositional CO2-water model using the Peng-Robinson
equation-of-state, and rock properties were established, with brine properties derived from
correlations and relative permeability data based on published sources. The Knox Storage
Complex model covers 45 miles by 45 miles area, including various formations and vuggy
intervals, represented in a layer cake model with constant properties within each layer. Injectivity
assessment simulations were conducted, with two scenarios examining multi-well injection into

 iii

the entire Knox Group and specifically into the Potosi formation. The simulation results revealed
significant CO2 plume size, leading to a recommendation for injection into the St. Peter Sandstone
and Everton Formation due to the large plumes resulting from injection into the Knox Group.
St. Peter-Everton storage complex: Similar to the Knox Storage Complex model, the St. Peter –
Everton storage complex static model including fluid PVT data and rock properties was exported
to Nexus® to perform dynamic reservoir simulations. The static model of the St. Peter – Everton
storage complex was iteratively refined to incorporate additional information obtained during the
project, encompassing analyses of seismic data, core data, and well-testing data. Fluid PVT data
were utilized to establish a compositional CO2-water model using the Peng-Robinson equation of
state, with brine properties derived from water property correlations based on measured salinity.
Rock properties, such as compressibility and relative permeability, were inferred from published
correlations due to the lack of site-specific laboratory measurements. The injection well maximum
bottomhole pressure was constrained to 90% of the fracture gradient to prevent rock fracturing
during CO2 injection, with preliminary simulations initially assuming a fracture pressure gradient
before well test results were available. Additionally, historical wastewater injection data in the St.
Peter Sandstone were provided by PSGC and integrated into the simulation model for forecasting
purposes.
Preliminary Injectivity Assessment: Preliminary injectivity assessment of the St. Peter and
Everton sandstones at the Lively Grove #1 well was performed through dynamic simulations.
These simulations were performed before all relevant core and well test data from the Lively Grove
#1 well became available. The simulation model, with refined grid dimensions around Lively
Grove #1, incorporates porosity and permeability variations and accounts for wastewater disposal
via the nearby WWDW #1 well (API# 121892488600). The model covers a 35 by 35 mile area,
encompassing the St. Peter, Everton Dolomite, and Everton sandstone, with a total of 6 million
cells. Porosity and permeability distributions were analyzed, with St. Peter and Everton sandstones
exhibiting significantly higher permeability compared to adjacent formations. Injection of CO2 for
30 years via a vertical well at Lively Grove #1 perforated from St. Peter Sandstone to Everton
sandstone was simulated.
Simulation results indicate that an average of 2.04 million metric tons of CO2 can be injected, with
the majority directed to the St. Peter Sandstone. After 30 years, a total of 61 million tonnes (MMT)
of CO2 is injected, with the St. Peter receiving 58% (35.6 MMT) and the Everton sandstone
receiving 42% (25.4 MMT) of the total injected volume. The CO2 plume is 6.6 miles wide in the
east-west direction and 6.25 miles wide in the north-south direction after 30 years of injection. The
CO2 plume extends 0.3 miles and 0.75 miles in the east-west and north-south directions,
respectively, after a 50-year post-injection period. The CO2 plume migrates both laterally and
vertically during both the injection and post-injection periods, extending into the Joachim and
upper Shakopee formations but not shallower or deeper. The low permeability in the overlying
Platteville and underlying Shakopee inhibits propagation of pressure in the vertical direction.
Multi-Well Simulations: The dynamic reservoir simulations were conducted to determine the
optimal well count and spacing for injecting 8.125 MMTA of CO2 over 20 years into the St. Peter
and Everton storage complex at the PSGC site. Various scenarios, including vertical, slant, and
line injection, were evaluated by constraining the well maximum BHP to 90% of the fracture
pressure. Updates to the model involved incorporating newly acquired data and expanding the
areal size from 35-miles by 35-miles to 45-miles by 45-miles. Historical and forecasted water
injection data from WWDW #1 were integrated into the model. Simulation results indicate that

 iv

both regular and line injection patterns can meet injection targets. However, the line injection
pattern requires fewer wells (8 or 9 wells) than regular patterns (18 to 21 wells) and exhibits less
pressure interference between wells. Additionally, injection rates are influenced by permeability-
thickness variations, with higher injectivities observed in regions north of Lively Grove #1.
Additional injection scenarios were simulated to optimize well count, orientation, and the impact
of wastewater injection via the WWDW #1 well into the St. Peter Sandstone at PSGC site. The
number of CO2 injection wells simulated ranged from 6 to 10 vertical or slanted wells placed about
5 miles apart. Simulation results indicated that none of the scenarios could achieve the target
injection rate, with the highest annual CO2 rate reaching 7.7 MMTA for a scenario where ten
slanted wells are perforated in the St. Peter Sandstone and Everton Sandstone, with no wastewater
injection via WWDW #1 well. Vertical pressure interference between wells perforated in different
formations contributed to lower injection rates in the St. Peter Sandstone. As a result, we explored
brine extraction as technique for managing pressure buildup and guide the CO2 plume movement
during injection.
Brine Extraction: Simulation scenarios involving brine extraction alongside CO2 injection were
performed to achieve the target rate of 8.125 MMTA of CO2 over 20 years. Simulation results
indicate that two or three brine extraction wells, perforated in both the St. Peter and Everton
sandstones, are required, but extracting such large volumes poses logistical challenges. Scenarios
in which the producing bottomhole pressure of the extraction wells are adjusted to minimize brine
production while meeting the CO2 injection targets were simulated. Simulation results indicate
that at least 31,918 bbl/day (233 million barrels of brine in 20 years) must be produced via three
wells (10,639 bbl/d/well) to achieve the desired CO2 injection rate. Increasing the producing BHP
significantly reduced brine volume, mitigating handling challenges, and shrinking pressure fronts.

Summary
Modeling results suggest that injecting 8.125 MTA of CO2 for 20 years into the St. Peter and
Everton Sandstones is challenging due to pressure interference. Injecting the same amount of CO2
into the Knox Group or Potosi Dolomite is less problematic except that the CO2 plume, within
their thin vuggy intervals, can be excessively large. For example, CO2 injected into the Knox
Group reaches the boundaries of a 45 mi x 45 mi model after 2 years. As a result, the Knox Group
or Potosi Dolomite are not recommended for usage as a primary storage unit. Proposed
development strategies for PSGC include:
 • a multi-reservoir approach, maximizing CO2 injection into St. Peter and Everton and
 utilizing Knox Group for remaining amounts, and
 • a pressure management approach involving brine extraction, optimized well spacings,
 sequential injection-extraction scenarios, including an "inverted pump" technique that
 transfers brine from the St. Peter and Everton sandstones to the Knox Group during CO2
 injection to eliminate brine handling challenges at the surface.

1. INTRODUCTION
The objective of the Illinois Storage Corridor (ISC) project’s static and dynamic modeling task is
to assess the feasibility of storing 162.5 million tonnes (8.125 million tonnes annually; MMTA)
of industrially sourced carbon dioxide (CO2) in the St. Peter – Everton and Knox storage
complexes at Prairie State Generating Company’s (PSGC) facility near Marissa, Washington
County, Illinois (PSGC site) over a period of 20 years. The targeted formations for storing CO2 are
(1) St. Peter Sandstone and the sandstone interval of Everton Formation (Everton sandstone) and
(2) Knox Group carbonates. The Maquoketa Shale is the primary confining unit for both storage
complexes.
Data from the recently drilled Lively Grove #1 (LG #1) stratigraphic test well (now plugged and
abandoned) were used to construct both the static and dynamic models. The proceeding sections
of this report describe; (1) the geologic models that were constructed from characterizing both St.
Peter - Everton and Knox storage complexes; (2) the dynamic reservoir models that were
constructed to estimate the prospective storage resources of the St. Peter and Everton and Knox
formations; and (3) propose development strategies for storing 8.125 MMTA CO2 at PSGC site.

2. STATIC RESERVOIR MODELING
The goal of the static reservoir modeling was to construct rigorous 3-D models of the St. Peter –
Everton and Knox storage complexes to assess their prospective CO2 storage resources. Two
separate models were constructed, one for the St. Peter – Everton complex and the other for the
Knox storage complex. The models include the injection storage units and overburden formations
with sufficient details to represent the vertical and lateral heterogeneity at the injection site and be
useful for simulation and management purposes.
The process of developing the static reservoir models of the storage complexes consists of six main
steps: (1) gathering existing structure surfaces, formation tops, seismic and petrophysical data,
core, and well test data; (2) creating 3D structural frameworks for storage units and their overlying
formations; (3) defining zones and layers for injection zones and confining units; (4) scaling up
the porosity and permeability data into the zones and layers using mathematical averaging
methods; (5) analyzing well data and defining the variograms for the porosity and permeability
data; (6) constructing 2-D trend maps of porosity for St. Peter and Everton sandstones; and (7)
populating grids with porosity and permeability data using geostatistical algorithms and trend
maps.

2.1 Data Collection and Methods
The static reservoir models for the Knox and St. Peter – Everton storage complexes were developed
using PetrelTM, a reservoir modeling software from Schlumberger. The input data for the Knox
storage complex model included half-foot interval logs (e.g., gamma ray, resistivity, porosity,
photoelectric, and sonic) and structure surfaces from the Lively Grove #1 well, as well as thickness
maps, well test data, and permeability data. The structure surfaces were created by importing log
data from 36 wells, correlating the logs via cross sections, and constructing regional surfaces based
on the correlations and well entry points. The regional surfaces were further refined by
incorporating depth information from 2-D seismic data covering part of Washington County and
adjacent areas. The structural framework of the Knox storage complex model was constructed by

 7

incorporating the surfaces from the top of the Maquoketa to the top of the Eau Claire Shale into
the model. The structural framework of the Knox storage complex model was established by
including surfaces from the top of the Maquoketa to the top of the Eau Claire Shale. The
permeability of the Knox Group's vuggy interval was estimated using well test data, while the
permeability of the overburden formation (carbonate without vuggy porosity) was estimated using
equations from Lucia (1983, 1995). The permeability of the sandstone intervals was estimated
using a combination of the ISGS method and well test data and calibrated with core analysis data.
The final step involved building a layer cake model for the Knox storage complex.
The structural framework of the St. Peter – Everton storage complex model was constructed
similarly to the Knox storage complex model, with the difference being the surfaces included. The
St. Peter – Everton storage complex model incorporates surfaces from the top of the Maquoketa
Shale to the top of the Eminence Formation. The static reservoir model of the St. Peter – Everton
storage complex was built using petrophysical log data from 36 wells, 12 of which had porosity
data. Porosity logs were run with a limestone matrix (2.71 g/cc) and subsequently calibrated using
a sandstone matrix (2.65 g/cc). Cross-plots of neutron-density porosity logs were calculated and
incorporated into the model for wells with both density-porosity and neutron-porosity data. In the
absence of porosity logs, porosity values were derived from bulk density logs. The permeabilities
of the St. Peter Sandstone and Everton sandstone were estimated based on permeability transforms
proposed by the Illinois State Geological Survey (Frailey, unpublished work).

2.2 Knox Group Carbonates
2.2.1 Structural framework
The Knox Group model's structural framework presents a comprehensive and detailed
representation of the geological characteristics and subsurface formations within the study area.
This framework was established by utilizing regional structural surfaces and thickness maps,
resulting in a robust and precise model that facilitates the dynamic modeling of CO2 for storage.
Initially, as described in a previous section, the regional boundary was established, and surfaces
from the top of the Maquoketa Shale to the top of the Eau Claire Formation were imported.
However, due to the limited data availability, with only a few wells penetrating the Knox Group
and most lacking relevant petrophysical data, a layer cake model was created for the Knox Group.
This model incorporated data from the LG #1 well and provided a comprehensive representation
of the geological characteristics and subsurface formations within the study area.
Subsequently, a project boundary centered on the LG #1 well was defined, covering an area of
approximately 45 x 45 miles (72.4 x 72.4 km, Figure 1). This boundary's size was carefully
selected to provide adequate spatial coverage for analyzing subsurface formations and geological
features within the study area. The model includes a high density of cells, with 155 cells in both
the X and Y directions, allowing for a high level of resolution and detail. A grid spacing of 1,200
x 1,200 ft (365.7 x 365.7 m) is utilized, enabling the accurate representation of subsurface
structures. The ground elevation of approximately 477 ft (145.3 m) above mean sea level serves
as a reference point for the analysis of subsurface formations.
Additionally, the static reservoir model's overall thickness, approximately 2,700 ft (823m),
comprehensively represents the subsurface formations' full extent within the study area, from the
top of the Maquoketa Shale to the top of the Eau Claire Formation. This comprehensive thickness

provides a complete picture of the subsurface geology, making it possible to explore the geological
features and formations within the area in-depth.
2.2.2 Model Zonation
The petrophysical logs, particularly the porosity and image logs of the well, provide clear evidence
that the Knox Group contains dense and thick intervals of dolomite, as well as thinner dolomite
streaks with high porosity ranging from 12% to over 42% (Figure 2). The image logs show that
these streaks exhibit large vuggy porosities, but the extent of these high-porosity streaks is
unknown. Nonetheless, data on mud circulation losses and core recovery from equivalent intervals
suggest that these intervals are likely continuous.
From the lower part of the Shakopee Dolomite to the top of the Eau Claire Formation, a total of
ten high-porosity streaks were identified, each exhibiting large vugs as confirmed by analyzing the
image logs. Based on well test data, each of these streaks was assigned a permeability value of
10,000 md. Meanwhile, the tight zones between high-porosity streaks only exhibit matrix porosity,
with a thickness ranging from 17 to over 180 feet (5.1 to 54.8 meters) and a porosity of less than
10%. To differentiate the high-porosity streaks from the tight zones, the top and base of each streak
were treated as separate zones in the static reservoir model. As a result, the Shakopee Dolomite
was divided into five zones, Oneota into seven zones, Eminence into five zones, Potosi into five
zones, and Franconia into three zones. Additionally, each overburden formation from Maquoketa

2.3.4 3D Static Reservoir Model
The consistency of the permeability and thickness of the streaks in the Knox Group within the
project boundary remains uncertain. Nevertheless, evidence of vuggy intervals and lost circulation
zones in almost all wells encountering the Knox Groups, particularly Eminence and Potosi

throat systems of vuggy intervals. Consequently, the Knox Group static model assumes the spatial
connectivity of vugs in the horizontal direction and discontinuity of pore systems in the vertical
direction.
The 3-D static reservoir model constructed for the Knox Group is a layer cake model, illustrating
that the streaks exhibit connectivity in the horizontal direction and discontinuity in the vertical
direction. Based on well test results, a permeability value of 10,000 md was assigned to the streaks
with a porosity cutoff of 19%. The cutoff was chosen based on an analysis of the Knox interval
image logs, which confirmed that the vugs were large enough and connected. The permeability of
the carbonate interval between the streaks was estimated using the Lucia equations (Lucia 1983,
Lucia 1995). Kriging was used to distribute porosity and permeability to ensure a smooth
distribution of properties.
Porosity and permeability models, presented in Figure 3 and Figure 4, respectively, offer valuable
insights into the geological characteristics of the Knox Group within the project boundary.
Accompanying east-west cross-sections provides a higher resolution of the porosity and
permeability ranges of the Knox Group, while warmer colors depict the vuggy intervals with high
porosity and high permeability. The model identifies at least 10 zones with high porosity and
permeability values (Figure 5), which could be considered for CO2 injection.
Moreover, the model indicates that the overburden exhibits low porosity and permeability, except
for the St. Peter Sandstone and Everton sandstone described in the previous section. These findings
have significant implications for the feasibility and effectiveness of CO2 sequestration in the Potosi
Dolomite. Selecting zones with high porosity and permeability for CO2 injection is crucial to
achieve efficient and effective storage. Additionally, the low porosity and permeability of the
overburden could prevent the upward migration of CO2, ensuring its long-term storage and
minimizing environmental risks.

2.3 St. Peter - Everton Storage Complex Model
2.3.1 Structural framework
The structural framework of the St. Peter–Everton storage complex was established by
incorporating regional structural surfaces from the top of the Maquoketa Shale to the top of the
Eminence Dolomite. The resulting model included surfaces, in increasing depth, such as the
Maquoketa Shale, Trenton, Platteville, Joachim, St. Peter Sandstone, Everton Dolomite, Everton
sandstone, Shakopee, Oneota, and Eminence, as depicted in Figure 6. The model covered an area
of 120 x 80 miles (193 x 129 km) and aimed to incorporate more wells and petrophysical logs for
a more comprehensive porosity assessment and to identify porosity and permeability trends in the
storage units.
A challenge in constructing the static reservoir model for St. Peter Sandstone and Everton
sandstone was determining the optimal number of grids for the model. The large area covered by
the model posed a trade-off: using a high number of small grid cells would require significant

 13

computational resources and time, while using larger grid cells could result in a lack of small-scale
features and variations in reservoir quality, leading to less accurate results. To balance
computational efficiency and accuracy, the regional model was created using a grid spacing of
1,320 ft x 1,320 ft (402 x 402 m). A project boundary was also defined, covering an area of 45 x
45 miles (72.4 x 72.4 km) around the Lively Grove #1 well location (Figure 7). The project model
(represented by the red boundary in Figure 7) consists of 181 x 181 grid cells with a spacing of
1,320 ft x 1,320 ft (402 x 402 m) and has an overall thickness of approximately 6,976 ft (2,126 m),
comprising a total of 5,962,502 cells.

2.3.2 Model Zonation
The storage units in the model are the St. Peter Sandstone and the Everton sandstone. The
Ordovician St. Peter Sandstone has a widespread distribution across Illinois and is composed
mainly of fine to medium-grained, well-sorted pure quartz sandstone that is free of clay minerals.
The formation is believed to have been deposited by a sea advancing northward across Illinois
(Collinson and Atherton, 1975). The St. Peter Sandstone has been investigated for its use as a
natural gas storage site in Illinois, as reported by Buschbach and Bond in 1974. These storage
fields have been determined to have excellent reservoir characteristics, with an average porosity
of 14% to 16% and an average permeability of 150 to 400 md. The Everton Dolomite occurs
primarily in western and southwestern Illinois and is composed of a mixture of pure quartz
sandstone and dolomitic sandstone, overlain by intervals of dolomite. Shaw and Schreiber (1991)
suggest that these dolomite intervals were likely deposited in a lagoonal environment, consisting
mainly of mottled dolomitic wackestone lithofacies, predominantly limy micrite and anhedral to
subhedral, finely crystalline dolomite with floating fine- to medium-grained, well-rounded to
subrounded quartz grains. Despite the name of the formation, a thick interval of sandstone is
present beneath the dolomite intervals at Well 2 (Lively Grove#1) and Well 3 in Washington
County, as shown in Figure 8. The sandstone in Lively Grove well is distinguished by its pure and
fine to medium-grained grains, with porosity reaching above 15%. In the far west part of Illinois,
where Well 1 is located, the sandstone intervals transition laterally to dolomite intervals.
Petrophysical analysis of the logs reveals that the Everton sandstone intervals are thick and
intermingled with the St. Peter Sandstone, where the dolomite interval is pinched out in central
Illinois (Figure 8). The Everton is underlain by the Shakopee Dolomite, with a major unconformity
separating the two formations. Additionally, there is a major unconformity between the Everton
Dolomite and the St. Peter Sandstone (Figure 8).
The overburden formations in the study area are, from base to top, the Joachim Dolomite,
Platteville Limestone, Trenton Formation, and Maquoketa Shale (Figure 9). The Joachim
Dolomite is of the Middle Ordovician age and is the uppermost formation of the Ancell Group.
The petrophysical log interpretation of the Lively Grove#1 well suggests that the Joachim
primarily comprises dolomite, interbedded with shaly intervals. Further analysis indicates that the
upper portion of the Joachim Dolomite exhibits the presence of anhydrite layers, with an estimated
thickness of approximately 18 feet (5.5 meters, Figure 9). These layers of anhydrite appear to have
undergone dissolution in the outcrop area, resulting in the formation of breccia in the upper section
of the formation, as per the Illinois stratigraphy. The Joachim Dolomite is overlain by thick, dense
limestone intervals of the Platteville and Trenton. These formations primarily comprise pure
limestone with minor shale beds and exhibit less than 5% porosity. The Trenton Formation, also
of Ordovician age, is composed mainly of dolomite, limestone, and shale, but in southwestern and
south-central Illinois, it is primarily composed of fine-grained limestone. The Trenton is covered
by thick shale intervals of the Maquoketa in western and southwestern Illinois, with a thickness of
about 160 ft (48.8 meters), acting as the primary caprock for storage. The model’s base was
extended to the top of the Eminence Dolomite, a part of the Knox Group. The Knox Group is
primarily composed of dolomite. The analysis of FMI data of the Knox Group revealed the
presence of intervals with vuggy porosity and intense fracturing within the formations of the Knox
Group. Based on the reservoir characteristics of the formations and their respective lithology, the

 15

storage units have been subdivided into two distinct zones: the St. Peter Sandstone and the Everton
sandstone. The Dolomite intervals of the Everton act as a baffle with a very low reservoir quality.
Furthermore, due to variations in the reservoir quality of the lower and upper parts of the Joachim
Dolomite, it has been divided into two main zones. The entire intervals of the Platteville, Trenton,
and Maquoketa formations exhibit very low porosity, and as such, each formation has been
considered a separate zone. The Knox Group at the base of the model has been separated into four
main zones, including Shakopee, Middle Shakopee, Lower Shakopee, and Oneota.

To model the porosity of the St. Peter and Everton formations, two trend maps were constructed
using data-based porosity ranges and trends (Figure 11). These trend maps were then utilized as a
guide for both the static reservoir modeling and co-kriging processes, with the objective of
enhancing the accuracy of porosity estimates for St. Peter and sandstone intervals of the Everton.
As depicted in Figure 11 and Figure 12, the trend of porosity for both formations exhibits a general
pattern of decreasing porosity from northwest to southeast in relation to the project boundaries.
However, in a small region of southern Washington County and northern Perry County,
petrophysical log analysis has revealed that the dolomite intervals of the Everton formation extend,
and no sandstone is present. Consequently, specialized maps were developed specifically for this
area, excluding the sandstone interval.
To model the porosity, variogram data analysis was used to determine the spatial relationship of
the data points. The vertical and horizontal variations of the data points were extracted through the
normalization of data using the normal score distribution. In order to understand the spatial
distribution of porosity values within the St. Peter and Sandstone intervals of the Everton
formation, geostatistical methods were employed to calculate the major and minor variograms
ranges. Using a sample of data points, variograms were constructed and analyzed to estimate the
ranges of spatial continuity in the dataset. The major range, also known as the long-range
continuity, was found to be 260,000 ft and the minor range, also referred to as the short-range
continuity, was calculated to be 160,000 ft. Additionally, the vertical range, which pertains to the

 19

continuity of the data in the vertical direction, was determined to be 20 ft. Subsequently, the model
co-kriged with associated porosity trends to increase the accuracy of distribution of porosity data
within the model.

The permeability of the intervals in the St. Peter Sandstone and Everton sandstone was estimated
using the ISGS method, while the Lucia method was used for carbonate intervals. The vertical
permeability was calculated by utilizing the relationship between vertical (kv) and horizontal (kh)
permeability determined from core data. For dolomitic intervals above and below the sandstone
intervals, the average kv/kh ratio was approximately 0.47. This ratio was then multiplied with the
kh model to estimate the vertical permeability. In the sandstone intervals, the zones were divided
into cells with a thickness of 0.5 feet to obtain the log values. Since the range of kh and kv of core
samples were similar, a multiplier of 0.85 was applied to calculate kv. The resulting kv values were
then upscaled into larger grid cells (approximately 3 feet) using the harmonic averaging method.

permeability value of 0.0001 md. After distributing the porosity and permeability values for each

3. DYNAMIC RESERVOIR MODELING
Dynamic reservoir simulation models were built and used to assess several scenarios for the
feasibility of storing 162.5 million tonnes (8.125 million tonnes per year), over a period of up to
20 years, of industrially-sourced carbon dioxide (CO2) in a commercial-scale geological storage
complex at Prairie State Generating Company’s (PSGC) facility near Marissa, Illinois.
Simulation results include CO2 injectivity, number of wells, and spacing required to inject 8.125
MMTA of CO2 for 20 years, CO2 plume size, and pressure distribution as a function of time. The
pressure front and CO2 plume sizes from the dynamic simulation results are also used to estimate
the area of review (AoR). Simulation results were also used to provide input to Class VI permits.
The dynamic reservoir simulations were run using Landmark’s Nexus® reservoir simulation
software. Separate simulation models were created for St. Peter – Everton storage complex and

3.1.3 Injectivity Assessment of the Knox Group
Screening-level simulations were performed to assess the injectivity of the Knox Group. Two

3.2.2 AoR Delineation
Area-of-Review (AoR) is the greater of either the maximum areal extent of the free-phase CO2
plume or a pressure-defined front during the lifetime of a storage project (White et al. 2019) or a
combination of both. The CO2 plume extent is estimated based on CO2 saturation within the
injection reservoir using a predefined saturation cutoff. The pressure front is defined by the extent
of the minimum pressure required to initiate fluid flow from the injection zone into the deepest
underground source of drinking water (USDW) through a notional conduit (White et al. 2019,
Nicot et al., 2009).
Two approaches for estimating the minimum pressure are adopted; (1) a method that assumes fluid
density in the wellbore to be uniform and equal to the fluid density in the injection zone (Birkholzer
et al., 2011) and (2) a slightly more complex method that accounts for changes in fluid density
between the USDW and the injection zone (Nicot et al., 2009).
In the first method, pressure buildup (Δpi,f) equivalent to the hydraulic head between the storage
formation and the USDW is calculated using the following equation (Birkholzer et al., 2011):

If Δpi,f is positive, the injection zone is under-pressured relative to the USDW and formation
pressure buildup equivalent to Δpi,f is required to initiate the flow of injection zone brine into the

3.2.4 Multi-Well Simulations to Inject 8.125 MMTA
Numerous CO2 injection scenarios were run on an updated St. Peter and Everton model to assess
the number of wells and well spacing required to inject 8.125 MMTA of CO2 for 20 years, a total
of 162.5 million tonnes of CO2. All simulations were run by constraining the well maximum BHP
to 90% of the fracture pressure.
The St. Peter and Everton Nexus® model was updated to incorporate newly acquired data and
expand the areal size. The updates include:
 • Updated property arrays from a revised static reservoir model with porosity and permeability
 distributions calibrated to St. Peter Sandstone and Everton sandstone whole core, rotary
 sidewall core, and well-test data from Lively Grove #1.
 • Increased the area size of the model from 35-mile x 35-mile to 45-mile x 45-mile; the model
 extended further north than south, so the model is no longer centered around Lively Grove
 #1.
 • Updated the fracture gradient to 0.62 psi/ft as interpreted from step-rate tests performed on
 Lively Grove #1 within St. Peter.
 • Added historical and forecasted water injection into the St. Peter from WWDW #1.
The injectivity of WWDW #1 was adjusted in the simulation model to match historical rates and
pressures. Historical BHP data was not available, so the well injectivity was adjusted to match
historical THP data. An injectivity multiplier of 1.05 was applied to WWDW #1 in the simulation
model to match historical THP data. Figure 38 shows the match between historical THP data and
model predictions.
The updated simulation model is 45 x 45 miles areally and includes St. Peter, Everton Dolomite,
Everton sandstone, and the overlying: Joachim, Platteville, Trenton, and Maquoketa, as well as the
underlying Shakopee and Oneota. The reservoir properties are heterogeneous in both the lateral
and vertical directions. Figure 39 shows a 3-D image of the updated St. Peter and Everton Nexus®
model, the blocks are colored by geologic formation. The thickness of each formation is denoted
in the figure. The model grid block dimensions are 1,320 x 1,320 ft areally. The cell thickness is
variable and ranges from 1 to 35 ft, but averages 2.5 ft within the St. Peter and Everton. There are
182 layers in the model, of which 113 are within the St. Peter and Everton. There is a total of 6
million cells in the simulation model. Figure 31 also shows the location of the wastewater disposal
well (WWDW), which is 5.5 miles southwest of Lively Grove #1.
Porosity and permeability were populated within the PetrelTM model and exported to Nexus®.
Figure 40 shows a map of permeability-thickness (k-H) summed through St. Peter and Everton
formations; displayed on a log scale. Well injectivity is proportional to k-H, so this map represents
well injectivity and is used to help locate wells to maximize injection. The maps shows an area of
high k-H just north of Lively Grove #1, which is why the model was extended further to the north
than the south.
The model’s top and bottom surfaces are sealed with no flow boundaries. An infinite-acting aquifer
is attached to the edges (lateral) of the model. The infinite-acting aquifer was calibrated prior to
running CO2 injection scenarios. In the simulation model, the maximum BHP is constrained to
90% of the fracture pressure to prevent fracturing the rock during CO2 injection. The maximum
BHP constraint is applied at the top of the perforated interval. The fracture pressure gradient is
0.62 psi/ft, which was measured during the testing of Lively Grove #1.

3.2.5 Additional Injection Scenarios
The St. Peter and Everton Nexus® model was updated to incorporate newly acquired data to update
the static reservoir model. The updates to the static reservoir model include:
 • Updates to the porosity and permeability arrays from a revised static reservoir model with porosity
 and permeability distributions updated in St. Peter, Everton Dolomite, and Everton sandstone to
 reflect the general trend of decreasing porosity and permeability from northwest to southeast.
 • The fracture pressure gradient was updated to 0.58 psi/ft and 0.66 psi/ft in St. Peter and Everton,
 respectively. Fracture pressure gradients, as interpreted from step-rate tests performed on Lively
 Grove #1 within St. Peter (Frailey, unpublished work), were used to calculate the fracture pressures
 of both formations.
All other model properties and input are the same as described in the previous section (section
3.1.3).
Simulations were performed to assess the feasibility of injecting 8.125 MMTA of CO2 into St.
Peter and Everton for 20 years. Multiple injection scenarios were simulated to determine well
count and spacing, well patterns and orientation, and assess CO2 injection with and without
wastewater injection via WWDW #1. The number of CO2 injection wells simulated ranged from
6 to 10 vertical or slanted wells placed about 5 miles apart. Figure 55 shows the well locations of
a scenario where CO2 is injected via 10 1-mile-long slanted wells consisting of five pads placed 5
miles apart, including wastewater injection via WWDW #1. Each blue circle in Figure 55
represents a pad consisting of two slanted wells; one perforated in the St. Peter Sandstone and the
other perforated in the Everton sandstone.
Simulation results show that none of the scenarios can inject 8.125 MMTA of CO2 for 20 years,
see Table 13. The scenario where ten slanted wells are perforated in the St. Peter Sandstone and
Everton sandstone, with no wastewater injection via WWDW #1 well, has the highest annual CO2
rate of 7.7 MMTA. A close look at the BHP and injection rate of two nearby wells, one perforated
in the St. Peter and the other perforated in the Everton sandstone, shows a significant difference in
the injection rate due to vertical pressure interference. The injection rate of the well perforated in
St. Peter is significantly low due to pressure buildup in the underlying injector perforated in the
Everton sandstone.

3.2.6 Brine extraction during CO2 injection
Three scenarios (7 cases) involving one or more brine extraction wells and ten slanted injection
wells were simulated to determine the number of brine extraction wells needed to mitigate pressure
and inject 8.125 MMTA of CO2 for 20 years. The scenarios simulated included brine extraction
from either (1) St. Peter or (2) Everton or (3) simultaneously from both St. Peter and Everton using

 57

one or more brine extraction wells with producing BHP ranging from 100 psia to 1,565 psia at
well datum. All simulated scenarios involving brine extraction assumed that the extracted brine
and WWDW #1 wastewater are injected into the vuggy intervals of Knox formations.
Simulation results indicate that two or three brine extraction wells perforated both in St. Peter
Snadstone and Everton sandstone are required to inject 8.125 MTA of CO2 for 20 years (Table
15). However, about 72,000 barrels per day (~525 million barrels of brine in 20 years) must be
extracted, which is a very large volume of water to produce using two wells and to handle at the
surface.
As a result, twelve cases involving changes in producing BHP were simulated to determine the
minimum volume of extracted brine required to meet a target injection rate of 8.125 MTA for 20
years, using three brine extraction wells, i.e., brine extraction volume sensitivity. The objective of
this exercise was to increase producing BHP to reduce brine production and determine the
minimum volume of brine required to inject 8.125 MTA of CO2 for 20 years. In the simulations,
the producing BHP was progressively increased from 100 psia to 1,565 psia, which is the
hydrostatic pressure of the well at datum. In other words, cases in which the producing BHP is less
than 1565 psia will use pumps to extract brine. Simulation results indicate that at least 233 million
barrels (MMstb) of brine in 20 years (32,000 stb/day) must be produced via three wells to inject
8.125 MTA for 20 years (Figure 57). The volume of brine extracted was reduced by 55% by
increasing the producing BHP from 100 psia to 1300 psia, which will mitigate brine handling
challenges, especially if the brine is extracted to the surface. Figure 58 shows the CO2 plume and
pressure front distribution for the case involving brine extraction from three wells at a producing
BHP of 1300 psia at the depth of the top perforation (datum). A comparison of the pressure front
in Figure 56 (right) and Figure 58 (right) shows the shrinking of the pressure front and reduced
pressure buildup in the latter, especially at the extraction wells, due to brine extraction.

4. DEVELOPMENT STRATEGY(IES)
Lessons learned from the dynamic modeling section (section 3) indicate that it is challenging to
inject 8.125 MTA of CO2 for 20 years into St. Peter and Everton sandstones due to pressure
interference. On the other hand, the same quantity of CO2 can be easily injected into either the
Knox Group formations or Potosi Dolomite with minimal pressure interference. However, the CO2
injected into the thin vuggy intervals of the Knox Group extends further from injection wells,
creating very large plumes. For example, CO2 injected into the Knox Group reaches the boundaries
of a 45 mi x 45 mi model after 2 years. As a result, using the Knox Group as the primary storage
unit (target) is not recommended. However, it should be noted that 8.125 MTA for 20 years (162.5
MT) of CO2 is very large quantity to store compared to existing commercial-scale storage projects
such as the Sleipner (Cavanagh, 2013; Eiken et al., 2011), SnØhvit (Hansen et al., 2013; Eiken et
al., 2011), and In-Salah (Eiken et al., 2011) CO2 storage projects.
A list of proposed strategies for developing the PSGC site includes the following:
 1. Multi-reservoir approach:
 a. Inject the maximum allowable quantity of CO2 into St. Peter and Everton (.i.e., BHP-
 constrained)
 b. Inject the remaining portion into the Knox Group to meet the injection target (i.e.
 injection rate constrained)

 60

 2. Pressure management approach:
 a. Use brine extraction wells to mitigate pressure buildup within the St Peter and Everton
 sandstones and increase injectivity.
 b. Optimize well spacings to minimize CO2 plume size.
 c. Develop sequential injection-extraction well scenarios to minimize the number of wells
 to construct and the CO2 plume size.
 d. Employ the “inverted pump” technique to extract brine from the St Peter and Everton
 sandstones and inject it in the Potosi and/or other Knox Group formations. The “inverted
 pump” technology eliminates the need to handle and treat brine extracted to the surface,
 which is very challenging for large volumes (Okwen and Frailey, 2021)

SUMMARY
A reservoir modeling study was conducted to assess the feasibility of storing 162.5 million tonnes
(8.125 million tonnes annually) of industrially sourced carbon dioxide (CO2) in the St. Peter –
Everton and Knox storage complexes at the Prairie State Generating Company’s (PSGC) site near
Marissa, Washington County, Illinois (PSGC site). Two separate models were constructed for the
St. Peter – Everton and the Knox storage complexes. The St Peter and Everton sandstones are the
target storage units for the St. Peter – Everton storage complex. The Knox Group formations are
the target storage units for the Knox storage complex. The Maquoketa Shale is the primary
confining unit for both storage complexes.
The static reservoir models used as input to the dynamic reservoir models of the storage complexes
were developed from interpretations of logs (e.g., gamma ray, resistivity, porosity, photoelectric,
and sonic), structure surfaces using data from 36 wells, thickness maps, well test data, seismic
data, and permeability data in PetrelTM. Ten high-porosity streaks exhibiting large vugs between
dense and thick tight zones, with porosity ranging from 12% to over 42%, were identified in the
Knox Group from image log analyses. The permeability of the Knox Group's vuggy interval was
estimated using well test data, while the permeability of the overburden formation (carbonate
without vuggy porosity) was estimated using equations from Lucia (1983, 1995). The 3-D static
reservoir model of the Knox storage complex was discretized into 6,800,000 grid cells to represent
an area of 45 x 45 miles (72.4 x 72.4 km) with 179 layers from the top of the Maquoketa Shale to
the top of the Eau Claire Formation. The permeabilities of St. Peter and Everton sandstones were
estimated based on permeability transforms proposed by the Illinois State Geological Survey. The
3-D static reservoir model of the St. Peter – Everton storage complex is 45 mi long, 45 mi wide,
and 6,976 ft (2,126 m) on average, and discretized into 5,962,502 grid cells with 182 layers from
the top of the Maquoketa Shale to the top of the Eminence Dolomite.
Two dynamic reservoir models, one for the Knox storage complex and the other for the St Peter –
Everton storage complex, were built to assess the feasibility of storing 8.125 million tonnes per
year (MMTA) of industrially sourced CO2 for 20 years at the Prairie State Generating Company
(PSGC) site near Marissa, Illinois. Landmark’s Nexus® reservoir simulation software was used to
run dynamic simulations. Dynamic reservoir simulation results indicate that 8.125 MMTA of CO2
can be injected into the Knox Group formations; however, the CO2 plume reaches the model’s
lateral boundaries within 4 years because the injected CO2 preferentially flows into the thin vuggy
intervals within the Knox Group.
