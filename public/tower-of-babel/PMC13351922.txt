A single-arm retrospective feasibility study of biphasic calcium phosphate bone graft with submicron surface topography in anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) procedures 6 months postoperative
Authors: Casey Hatfield, Katherine Sage, Gregory Boys, Adam Bruggeman, Om Neeley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal fusion surgeries are a common procedure for cervical trauma, degenerative spondylosis, and radiculopathy and/or myelopathy. Though this surgical approach has excellent patient clinical outcomes, there is a paucity of rigorous data quantifying fusion rates at early time points. This retrospective safety and feasibility study evaluates a biphasic calcium phosphate bone graft with submicron surface topography (BCP<µm putty) with or without autograft in anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) procedures.MethodsAn independent neuroradiologist blinded to the clinical status evaluated computed tomography (CT) images obtained at 6 months and graded each treated level based on the extent of bridging trabecular bone. Twenty subjects were enrolled in the study. A total of 43 spinal levels were treated with an average of 2.15 levels per subject (C3-C4 to C6-C7).ResultsThe primary endpoint of CT-based fusion was 97.7% (42/43 levels) based on the presence of bridging bone between the endplates at 6 months. This cohort included 35% of patients treated with BCP<µm putty with ICBG and 65% of patients fused with BCP<µm putty standalone. The observed fusion rates were accompanied by an improvement in pain scores. No graft-related adverse events (AEs) were reported. Limitations of this study include a small sample size and a heterogeneous surgical population.ConclusionsThe high fusion rate observed at 6 months postoperative and favorable safety profile support the performance of BCP<µm putty for use with or without autograft in cervical interbody fusion procedures.

Introduction

Autograft is considered the gold standard bone graft for spinal fusion. However, limitations in the availability of autologous bone and pain and morbidity associated with second site harvests have led to the development of alternative bone grafts (1). Alternative grafts can be derived from donor bone or manufactured from synthetic compounds. While allograft, cell-based allografts (CBAs), and demineralized bone matrices (DBMs) are processed from donor-derived bone, calcium phosphate bone grafts eliminate the need for cadaveric tissue. Unlike CBAs and DBMs, calcium phosphates provide consistency between batches and do not rely on the health of a tissue donor (2,3). In contrast to bone grafts derived from donor tissue, calcium phosphates do not have an inherent risk of disease transmission (4).

Calcium phosphate bone grafts are manufactured with a range of porosities, resorption profiles, and surface topographies (5,6). A novel biphasic calcium phosphate with submicron surface topography (BCP<µm) was developed with an optimal resorption profile, porosity, and surface architecture to facilitate bone growth through the core of the bone graft (6,7). BCP<µm is composed of 65–75% beta tricalcium phosphate and 25–35% hydroxyapatite for an intermediate resorption rate. BCP<µm also contains macro- and micro-porosity to facilitate bony ingrowth and provide a structural scaffold for angiogenesis (8). The hallmark feature of BCP<µm is the surface topography composed of submicron needle-shaped structures, which promotes polarization of pro-healing M2 macrophages (9). In response, mesenchymal stem cells are recruited to the site of the bone graft and differentiate into bone-building osteoblasts (8,9). Different surface topographies on calcium phosphates lead to different cellular responses, which influence downstream bone formation (6).

While it is often impossible to biopsy clinical fusions at multiple time points, animal models provide an opportunity to study how bone grafts mature over time, both radiographically and histologically. Ovine and canine pre-clinical animal models are used to assess the effects of synthetic bone graft chemical composition and surface topography on bone formation. In a canine paraspinal muscle pouch study, BCP<µm formed more bone throughout the core of the bone graft compared to other calcium phosphate bone grafts without a submicron surface topography in the absence of decorticated bone (6). In ovine instrumented posterolateral fusion studies, BCP<µm repeatedly demonstrated the ability to form bridging bone by 12 weeks postoperative (7,10). In comparison, a calcium phosphate bone graft without a submicron topography led to scar tissue formation, characteristic of a pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage response (10). This ovine study revealed the ability of BCP<µm to form bone through the core of the bone graft as early as 6 weeks postoperative (7). Evaluation of the tissue at a higher magnification revealed integration of BCP<µm granules in lamellar and woven bone, with osteoclasts resorbing residual granules, and osteoblasts forming osteoid at the bone-graft interface. Furthermore, BCP<µm led to bridging bone in all treated levels by 12 weeks postoperative in this ovine model (7). These histology studies demonstrate rapid time to fusion and a healthy, mature bone tissue composition within the fusion mass. Animal studies demonstrate both radiographic and histological evidence of bone formation with BCP<µm in vivo (6,7,10).

In addition to animal studies, human clinical studies are essential to evaluate the effectiveness of bone grafts at promoting spinal fusion in patients. A prospective, randomized controlled trial for posterolateral fusion provided level 1 data comparing BCP<µm granules with autograft in an intrapatient controlled design. In this study, BCP<µm obtained a fusion rate of 79% compared to a 47% fusion rate with autograft (at least 50% iliac crest bone graft, ICBG) (11). BCP<µm was also the subject of three retrospective interbody studies, which evaluated fusion in anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF), transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF), and lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF) surgeries at 12 months postoperative with computed tomography (CT) (12-14). One benefit of a retrospective study design is that the patient demographics represent a real-life patient population with multiple comorbidities known to lower fusion rates and lead to worse clinical outcomes (15). The clinical fusion data with BCP<µm putty are further supported by histological observations. A prospective clinical biopsy study revealed healthy lamellar bone and few residual BCP<µm granules well-incorporated in a patient who underwent a posterolateral fusion revision for hardware removal at 22 months postoperative (16). The histological findings in tissue adjacent to the rods in the thoracic and lumbar regions provide evidence of bone formation in the grafted region. These observations were corroborated by consolidated bone formation on CTs at the time of revision surgery (16).

A variety of surgical indications and a combination of prospective randomized control studies and retrospective single-arm studies have revealed high fusion rates with BCP<µm at 12 months postoperative (11-14). However, there is a paucity of data evaluating earlier fusion time points and focusing on the cervical spine region. This retrospective review aims to report anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) fusion with BCP<µm with high-quality CTs at 6 months postoperative assessed by a blinded, independent reviewer. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0078/rc).

Methods

This retrospective safety and feasibility data review was conducted at two centers, in accordance with the protocol, the ethical principles that have their origin in the Good Clinical Practice, and the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by Western Copernicus Group (WCG) IRB (No. 20244370). Patient consent was waived due to the retrospective, deidentified study design. This retrospective review was a single-arm, non-comparative evaluation of the first 20 patients who received BCP<µm putty [MagnetOs Easypack PuttyTM, Kuros Biosciences, Bilthoven, The Netherlands] standalone (without other bone graft) by one investigator or mixed with ICBG autograft by a second investigator in ACDF surgeries performed between April 2024 through April 2025. A cage, plate, and screws accompanied the biologic. Electronic medical records were reviewed to collect information regarding surgical approach, graft volume, and availability of follow-up CTs. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, subjects who had prior physical exams, patient-reported outcome questionnaires, X-rays, and CT scans at six months postoperative were included in the analysis. All subjects who met the inclusion criteria were included in the study (Table 1). The retrospective analysis was designed to enroll 20 subjects.

The study consisted of a retrospective review of subjects’ medical records, collection of available imaging (CTs and X-rays), and patient-reported outcomes questionnaires. Patient charts were reviewed for visual analog scale (VAS) scores for pain, radiographs, CT scans, demographics, medical history, surgical history, and post-surgical care. These data were analyzed to assess complications, determine factors that may affect the performance of BCP<µm putty (with or without ICBG), and quantify fusion rates and improvements in pain scores. Collected X-rays and CT scans were interpreted by an independent neuroradiologist who was blinded to the clinical status of the patients.

The primary endpoint of this study was radiographic fusion by level at 6 months post-surgery. The investigators in this study routinely assess fusion at 6 months in patients who underwent ACDFs. The patients were selected in series without any exclusion of ACDFs outside the range of included patients. Each level was graded based on the CT scans by an independent, blinded neuroradiologist. Serial fine-cut CT scans were used to assess the presence of bridging trabecular bone. Levels were deemed fused if there was at least 50% bridging trabecular bone between endplates with no evidence of a radiolucent gap between the graft and endplates (score of 1) or if there was evidence of trabecular bone bridging with some radiolucency between the graft and endplate (score of 2) due to the stability provided by the bridging bone (12). In cases with titanium implants, fusion was also assessed based on lack of implant loosening and endplate cortication and preservation. The levels were not fused in the presence of a pseudoarthrosis/non-union (score of 3) (17).

Secondary outcomes assessed included baseline to postoperative change in the VAS scores for pain. The number of patients with complications considered to be related to the subject device within 6 months post-surgery and the number of patients that underwent revision/reoperation within 6 months of surgery were assessed.

Adverse events (AEs) were collected and documented. An AE was defined as any undesirable deviation from the subject’s baseline condition to include all new conditions and/or worsening of the pre-existing symptoms regardless of the cause. The intensity of the AE was determined by the investigator using the following definitions: mild AEs caused minimal symptoms and did not require medical or surgical intervention; moderate AEs caused discomfort and required medical or surgical intervention but did not pose any significant or permanent risk to the subject; severe AEs were incapacitating events that left the subject unable to perform usual activities and necessitating medical or surgical intervention to prevent permanent disability.

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics and comorbidities were descriptively analyzed and reported as a mean, standard deviation, and/or range of values. Percent levels fused were quantified and change in VAS was calculated as a percentage from the initial value. Due to the single-arm study design, statistical analyses will not be performed.

Results

Twenty subjects met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in this study. Of the 20 subjects, 10 were females (50%) and 10 were males (50%). The average age at the time of surgery was 63.4 years (range, 51–87 years), and the average body mass index (BMI) was 32 kg/m2 (range, 21–45 kg/m2). Of the patients with BMIs recorded, 10/19 (52.6%) had a BMI greater than or equal to 30. Nineteen of the 20 subjects had comorbidities listed in their medical records, with an average of 2.84±1.34 comorbidities per patient. Three or more comorbidities were documented in 12/19 of the subjects (63.16%). The most common comorbidities observed were obesity, previous orthopedic surgeries, previous lumbar fusion surgery, diabetes, and arthritis (Table 2, Appendix 1).

All subjects in the retrospective review had an ACDF procedure. Twenty subjects had 1–4 level ACDF surgery, with 43 levels treated in the study (average of 2.15 levels per patient; Table 3). Patients 1–13 were fused at one site, and patients 14–20 were treated at the second site. Ten patients had single level ACDFs, 7 had three level ACDFs, and 3 had four level ACDFs (Appendix 2). An average of 1.19 cc of BCP<µm putty was used per level (range, 0.625–2.5 cc). ICBG was used in combination with BCP<µm putty in 7/20 subjects (35%), and BCP<µm putty was used standalone without the addition of another bone graft in 13/20 subjects (65%). On a per-level basis, 7/43 were treated with BCP<µm putty and ICBG (16.3%), and 35/43 levels were treated with BCP<µm putty standalone (81.4%).

The primary endpoint for this study was 6-month fusion assessed with CT by a blinded, independent neuroradiologist. Levels were scored 1–3 based on the extent of bony trabecular bone bridging (Figure 1). Of the 43 treated levels, 42 (97.7%) were considered fused (score of 1 or 2) at 6 months postoperative (Table 4). Of the 43 levels examined, 37 were scored 1 (86.1%) with at least 50% bridging bone in the intervertebral space. One level was not fused (score of 3), although the patient’s 2 adjacent levels were identified as fused. In patients who had single-level, three-level, and four-level surgeries, the fusion rate was 100% (10/10 levels), 95.2% (20/21 levels), and 100% (12/12 levels), respectively (Appendix 3).

Fusion results

Pain was assessed pre- and postoperative with VAS and binary presence/absence of pain. Pre- and postoperative VAS scores were collected for 16/20 patients. Patients at one site had a composite VAS recorded, while the second site collected a left and right arm VAS score. The latter scores were averaged to create a composite score. The average pre-operative VAS score was 3.75/10, and the average VAS score at 6 months postoperative was 1.7/10. The average reduction in VAS was therefore 2.1/10 or a 55% reduction in pain. All patients had pre-operative pain/myelopathy. Of the patients evaluated for pre- and postoperative pain, 14/16 had improved pain scores at 6 months postoperative.

One AE was reported in this study. A single patient had an intraoperative cerebral spinal fluid leak associated with a calcified disc osteophyte with primary patching and no further consequence for the patient or outcome. This AE was not attributed to the bone graft.

Discussion

This retrospective analysis was the first to evaluate fusion of BCP<µm putty with CT analysis at 6 months postoperative. This study reveals fusion of 97.7% of ACDF levels with BCP<µm putty with ICBG or BCP<µm putty used standalone. One of 43 levels exhibited a non-union. This patient had a 3 level ACDF with BCP<µm putty used standalone, and the adjacent levels were scored 1 and 2. The patient had 4 notable comorbidities—arthritis, thyroid disease, prior lumbar surgery, and prior orthopedic surgery. Arthritis and thyroid disease can both negatively impact spinal fusion outcomes (18,19). The fusion outcomes in this study were accompanied by a decrease in overall pain, with a 55% reduction in VAS scores, although there is heterogeneity in the systematic scoring. This study is also the first to report fusion BCP<µm fusion rates in the cervical spine, although multiple studies have demonstrated high fusion rates with BCP<µm in lumbar interbody fusion (12-14).

This study presents some limitations. The single-arm study design provides insight into BCP<µm putty fusion rates but does not offer a comparative control group. Additionally, the study is limited by the sample size of 20 patients and 43 levels from two surgeons at two sites. The limited sample size prevents subgroup analysis between patients fused with BCP<µm standalone by one investigator and BCP<µm mixed with ICBG by a second investigator. Combining these two groups was justified by a prior publication that found no difference in fusion outcomes between patients fused with BCP<µm standalone and BCP<µm mixed with ICBG for interbody fusions, although the heterogeneity provides an additional uncontrolled variable in the assessment (14). The patient surgical population is heterogeneous, including single- to four-level ACDFs and variability in the volume of BCP<µm used per level (0.625–2.5 cc per level). Additional clinical trials powered for comparisons are necessary to assess fusion in for single versus multi-level surgeries. The CT-based assessment may be influenced by the radiodensity of the bone graft and the radiodensity of titanium implants. Future studies are necessary to validate these retrospective data collected with additional measures such as interspinous motion (20). Future studies will also employ a secondary blinded radiographic review to ensure concordance with radiographic interpretation and allow for an assessment of the interobserver agreement. An additional limitation due to heterogeneity is the difference in VAS scoring between the two sites and the absence of validated neck-specific outcome measures. Four patients did not have VAS scores reported in their electronic medical records and could not be included in the patient reported outcome assessment due to the retrospective nature of this feasibility study. Additional prospective studies are necessary to quantify changes in neck disability index (NDI) and collect homogenous patient reported outcomes. Future studies aim to evaluate fusion rate of BCP<µm compared to various bone grafts including growth factors, CBAs, and DBMs for posterolateral and interbody fusion.

Despite limitations in the study design, the results from this feasibility and safety report are corroborated by other BCP<µm studies. A TLIF study of 20 patients and 36 treated levels revealed a 94.4% fusion rate with BCP<µm putty at 12 months post-operative (12). An LLIF and ALIF study of 63 patients and 101 treated levels reported that 90% of the levels had complete bridging trabecular bone fusion and no evidence of supplemental fixation failure at 12 months post-operative (13). Finally, a retrospective review of 55 patients (93 treated levels) with ALIF, LLIF, or TLIF surgeries with BCP<µm putty examined fusion at 12 months post-operative and revealed a 95.70% fusion rate (14). The results in these studies are also supported by a multi-center, prospective, intrapatient controlled study that revealed non-inferiority of BCP<µm compared to autograft for posterolateral fusion and even indicates superiority at 1 year postoperative (11). Taken together, these studies reveal the ability of BCP<µm to form bone in various spinal surgical indications, at early time points, and in both highly controlled and retrospective real-life patient cohorts. CT-based fusion data, analysis of fusion at multiple time points, and the representation of various indications support the use of BCP<µm for spinal fusion surgery.

Few prior studies have examined ACDF fusion with different bone grafts at 6 months postoperative with CTs. One study with a CBA (Trinity Evolution) examined ACDF fusion at 6 months postoperative with CTs. This study evaluated fusion based on flexion-extension X-rays as well as the presence/absence of bridging bone on CT and revealed at 78.6% fusion rate with Trinity Evolution at 6 months postoperative (21). Another study observed an 82.9% ACDF fusion rate with CTs at 6 months postoperative with bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2) adsorbed on beta tricalcium phosphate particles (22). One study assessed spinal fusion with local bone dust and morselized bone as grafting material at 6 months post-operative with less rigorous X-ray analysis (23).

Many spinal fusion studies evaluate fusion with CTs at 12 months postoperative. However, few studies examine fusion at earlier time points with high-quality imaging in diverse patient populations. This preliminary feasibility report contributes to a largely uninvestigated field of clinical research, revealing the potential of BCP<µm putty with or without ICGB to form bone in ACDFs in a diverse patient population by 6 months postoperative.

Conclusions

This retrospective safety and feasibility review suggests that BCP<µm putty with or without ICBG may achieve early fusion in ACDFs at 6 months postoperative. The use of BCP<µm putty also led to improved patient outcomes in a diverse patient population with multiple comorbidities, although additional time points are required to quantify complications beyond 6 months postoperative. The evaluation of fusion and patient reported outcomes at 6 months postoperative demonstrates potential early fusion, although the limited sample size and heterogenous population warrants further investigation. Future prospective, controlled studies are necessary to confirm these findings and evaluate whether BCP<µm putty is well suited to serve as a standalone bone graft in multi-level ACDF surgeries.
