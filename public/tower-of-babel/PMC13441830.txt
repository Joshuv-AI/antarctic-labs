Case Report: Acute monocytic leukemia mimicking large-vessel vasculitis on 18F-FDG PET/CT
Authors: Liuyan Nie, Hongli Wang, Ning Shen, Yongmei Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSystemic inflammatory manifestations caused by hematologic malignancies may be very similar to autoimmune diseases, making diagnosis difficult. Acute monocytic leukemia (AML-M5) is characterized by significant cytokine production and, in rare cases, can mimic large vessel vasculitis (LVV).Case presentationA 56-year-old female with a two-week history of recurrent fever accompanied by significantly elevated inflammatory markers. Laboratory tests showed elevated levels of erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and ferritin. The infection and autoimmune assessment are not significant. 18F-FDG PET/CT showed increased metabolic activity along the aortic arch, descending aorta, and bilateral iliac arteries, which raised suspicion of LVV. Glucocorticoid treatment quickly alleviated fever and partially reduced inflammation index. However, follow-up showed marked leukocytosis, monocytosis, anemia and thrombocytopenia. Bone marrow examination confirmed acute monocytic leukemia. Cytogenetic analysis confirmed that t (11; 19) (q23; p13.1) is consistent with KMT2A rearrangement. Understanding this presentation is crucial. For patients with atypical inflammatory features, early hematological assessment should be considered to avoid delayed diagnosis.

Introduction

Large vessel vasculitis (LVV) is a chronic inflammatory disease that primarily affects the aorta and its major branches, often accompanied by systemic symptoms and elevated inflammatory markers (1). 18F-FDG PET/CT has become an important imaging method for detecting vascular inflammation because it can visualize metabolic activity within the arterial wall (2).

However, increased FDG uptake in blood vessels is not specific to vasculitis. Many non-vasculitic diseases, including infections, atherosclerosis, and malignancies, can produce similar imaging findings (3). Hematologic malignancies are particularly challenging because they can trigger a strong systemic inflammatory response (4).

Acute monocytic leukemia (AML-M5) is a unique subtype of acute myeloid leukemia. It exhibits significant monocyte differentiation and strong cytokine secretion (5). These features may blur the boundary between malignant and inflammatory diseases.

Here, we report a case of acute myeloid leukemia with KMT2A rearrangement, initially presenting as suspected vasculitis, illustrating the diagnostic pitfalls in rheumatologic practice.

Case presentation

A 56-year-old woman was admitted to the hospital due to intermittent fever for over two weeks. She also reported muscle pain in her groin, knee, and ankle, accompanied by a headache on the right side. These symptoms temporarily subside but quickly recur every day. Fever usually starts at night, peaks around midnight (reaching 39.5 °C), and subsides on its own in the morning.

She was initially evaluated at two local hospitals. Initial laboratory tests showed a significant increase in inflammatory markers. C-reactive protein (CRP) reached 159 mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) increased to 133 mm/h, and serum amyloid A(SAA) exceeded 350 mg/L. The ferritin level is higher than 1500 ng/mL. A chest CT scan showed no obvious abnormalities. She subsequently received empirical antibiotic treatment including cefazolin, ceftriaxone, oseltamivir, levofloxacin and doxycycline, but her clinical symptoms did not improve. Extensive screening for infectious diseases, including procalcitonin, cytomegalovirus DNA, EBV DNA, blood cultures, and other microbiological examinations, failed to identify the source of infection. Despite receiving multiple cycles of antibiotic treatment, she continued to have a fever and elevated inflammatory markers, and was therefore referred to our hospital for further evaluation.

On admission, her WBC was 9.3 ×109/L, hemoglobin 90 g/L, and platelets 185 ×109/L. It is noteworthy that monocytes accounted for 20.4% of her blood (absolute count 1.89×109/L), but no morphologically abnormal monocytes or circulating blasts were identified at that time. Inflammatory markers remained significantly elevated, with ESR of 137 mm/h and CRP of 222.8 mg/L (Supplementary Table 1). Autoimmune screening, including anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA) and anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), did not detect any abnormalities. Empirical antibiotic treatment (moxifloxacin, 0.4 g once daily) failed to control her fever. Since the fever persisted despite anti-infective and antipyretic treatment, a single dose of 3 mg dexamethasone was administered to control the symptomatic fever, resulting in a temporary improvement in clinical symptoms.

To further clarify the three days after admission, 18F-FDG PET/CT was performed to further clarify the diagnosis. The scan showed an increase in FDG uptake in the aortic arch, descending aorta, and bilateral common iliac arteries, with a maximum standard uptake value (SUVmax) of 6.49. These results suggest LVV. In addition, diffuse high metabolic activity was observed in the bone marrow, indicating enhanced hematopoietic activity (Figures 1A, B; Supplementary Figure 1). Based on the patient’s age and the vascular distribution pattern shown on PET/CT (including involvement of the aortic arch, descending aorta, and bilateral common iliac arteries), which were compatible with features included in the 2022 ACR/EULAR Takayasu arteritis (TAK) classification criteria, TAK was initially considered. Subsequently, the patient received treatment with methylprednisolone (40 mg/day), with stable body temperature and reduced inflammatory markers.

However, the clinical course of the disease quickly deviated from typical vasculitis. During the gradual decrease of hormones, patients experience palpitations and new hematological abnormalities. Approximately three weeks after admission, laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 68.2 × 10 9/L, accompanied by anemia and thrombocytopenia. The peripheral blood smear showed a significant increase in monocytes (55%) and circulating monoblasts (5%). Bone marrow examination showed significant proliferation of bone marrow, with approximately 60% of cells being immature. The number of megakaryocytes increases and shows developmental abnormalities, including reduced size and decreased nuclear lobulation. Reticulin staining showed no fibrosis (MF-0). Immunohistochemistry was partially positive for CD117, with occasional CD34 expression and myeloid peroxidase positivity. PAS was positive; granulocyte oxidase was negative (Figure 2). Flow cytometry identified abnormal myeloid progenitor populations with monocytic differentiation. Cytogenetics showed 46, XX, t(11;19)(q23; p13.1), indicating KMT2A rearrangement. NGS detected KRAS p.G13D (VAF 32.16%, Tier I) and ETV6 p.L341F (VAF 40.9%, Tier III). Together, these findings established the diagnosis of AML-M5. Approximately four weeks after admission, she began receiving cytarabine 160mg for days 1–7 and idarubicin 20mg for day 1 and 10mg for days 2–3 for induction chemotherapy. She also gradually discontinued glucocorticoids. A follow-up bone marrow aspiration after six weeks showed no morphologically identifiable leukemic blasts, and flow cytometry showed no detectable residual abnormal myeloid progenitor population (minimal residual disease (MRD) negative). Peripheral blood cell counts returned to normal, as did neutrophil and platelet counts. No hepatosplenomegaly or lymphadenopathy was observed. These results are consistent with complete remission following induction therapy. In addition, a follow-up ultrasound of the abdominal aorta, bilateral iliac arteries, and aortic arch showed no significant abnormalities (Figures 1C, D).

Discussion

This case illustrates how acute monocytic leukemia can initially mimic LVV—both clinically and on PET/CT imaging. The patient had persistent fever, sky-high inflammatory markers, and vascular FDG uptake in the aorta and its branches— features that overlap substantially with LVV. The temporary improvement in fever following glucocorticoid administration increased clinical suspicion of an inflammatory disorder. Only the subsequent development of profound leukocytosis, monocytosis, and circulating monoblasts led to the correct diagnosis of AML. Transient symptom improvement after glucocorticoid treatment cannot distinguish between vasculitis and malignant tumor-related inflammatory syndromes, as paraneoplastic inflammatory manifestations may also produce a transient response to glucocorticoid treatment. This pattern—where malignancy presents as apparent autoimmune disease— underscores the importance of reconsidering the diagnosis when clinical evolution becomes atypical.

In this case, several findings support the explanation of paraneoplastic inflammatory syndrome rather than true immune-mediated vasculitis. First, the diffuse bone marrow hypermetabolism shown on PET/CT, which ultimately proved more informative than the vascular findings themselves, the rapid progression of hematological parameters, and the absence of detectable vascular abnormalities on follow-up imaging after anti-leukemia treatment are not typical features of primary vasculitis. Vascular ultrasound performed after remission showed no obvious abnormalities, but unfortunately, baseline ultrasound assessment prior to glucocorticoid therapy was lacking. While these results should not be considered definitive evidence of vasculitis remission, the overall clinical course is more indicative of leukemia-related inflammatory disease than true immune-mediated LVV.

Secondly, increasing evidence suggests that hematologic malignancies, especially those differentiated from mononuclear cells, can induce a strong systemic inflammatory response through cytokine release. Although a comprehensive cytokine profile analysis was not performed on this patient, her serum interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels were significantly elevated. Previous studies have shown that leukemic mononuclear cells can secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), thereby driving an acute-phase response and leading to significant increases in CRP, ESR, ferritin, and SAA levels (6, 7). Similar cytokine-driven inflammatory syndromes have been increasingly recognized as part of the paraneoplastic syndromes of AML (8).

At the molecular level, the presence of the KMT2A rearrangement may further explain the patient’s prominent inflammatory phenotype. KMT2A rearrangement leukemia is closely associated with monocyte differentiation and aggressive disease behavior (9). Experimental studies have shown that KMT2A fusion proteins can disrupt HOXA-related transcriptional programs and inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby enhancing cytokine production (10). In addition, the coexisting KRAS p.G13D mutation may further amplify inflammatory signals by activating the RAS/MAPK pathway (11). These molecular changes may be related to the excessive inflammatory response and vasculitis-like manifestations observed in this patient. However, the potential relationship between KMT2A rearrangements, KRAS mutations, and vasculitis-like manifestations remains speculative, and a direct causal relationship cannot be established based on a single case.

Another important diagnostic challenge is the interpretation of FDG uptake in PET/CT vessels. While increased FDG uptake in large vessels is considered a hallmark of active vasculitis, it is not a disease-specific finding. FDG uptake reflects glucose metabolism in activated inflammatory or malignant cells, rather than a specific histopathological process (12). In leukemia, increased vascular uptake may be due to cytokine-mediated endothelial cell activation, transient leukemic cell-endothelial cell interactions, and a systemic hypermetabolic state (13). Importantly, diffuse myelometabolism observed in this patient may suggest an underlying hematologic malignancy rather than primary vasculitis (14). Suenaga et al. reported similar findings, demonstrating that acute leukemia can present with diffuse and intense bone marrow FDG uptake, which differs from the patterns typically observed in inflammatory diseases. Therefore, when diffuse myelometabolism is accompanied by vascular FDG uptake, clinicians should consider the possibility of an underlying hematologic malignancy and perform appropriate hematological evaluations (15).

Another issue to consider is whether this case truly represents the coexistence of vasculitis and leukemia. While there are occasional reports of vasculitis and malignancy occurring simultaneously (16), several findings in this case are more indicative of a leukemia-related inflammatory mimicry rather than true vasculitis, including diffuse bone marrow metabolism, rapid changes in hematological parameters, and regression of vascular lesions after chemotherapy rather than immunosuppressive therapy. Although there have been reports of leukemic vasculitis, Kawasaki disease-like disease, and polyarteritis nodosa associated with monocytic leukemia, AML-M5 characterized by LVV-like vascular FDG uptake on PET/CT scans remains extremely rare (17–19).

From a clinical perspective, this case highlights several warning signals. Extreme elevation of inflammatory markers, especially ferritin and IL-6, should be a cause for concern. Transient response to glucocorticoids does not rule out malignant tumors. The PET/CT examination results must be interpreted in conjunction with specific circumstances. Diffuse bone marrow hypermetabolic activity may be the earliest clue to hematologic disorders and should not be overlooked when interpreting PET/CT scan results in patients suspected of having vasculitis. Finally, once hematological abnormalities occur, immediate reassessment should be conducted.

Conclusion

Acute monocytic leukemia may present with significant systemic inflammation and vasculitis-like imaging features, which may lead to misdiagnosis in rheumatology. Clinical doctors should recognize that vascular FDG uptake is not unique to vasculitis. When systemic inflammation coexists with high bone marrow metabolism or progressive blood abnormalities, the possibility of malignant tumors should be considered. In these cases, early bone marrow assessment remains crucial as timely diagnosis directly affects treatment decisions and patient prognosis.
