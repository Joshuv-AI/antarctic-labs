Introduction

Young adulthood (ages 15–24) is a formative life stage characterized by rapid physical changes, cognitive development, and evolving social roles [1]. During this period, individuals begin to make sexual and reproductive health (SRH) decisions that can profoundly influence lifelong well-being [2]. Access to accurate SRH information is therefore essential for preventing unintended pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections, and adverse reproductive outcomes. Yet, despite this importance, many youths continue to report unmet SRH information needs [2–4].

These gaps become even more pronounced among youth living with chronic health conditions, whose reproductive health is shaped not only by normal developmental processes but also by the complexities of disease trajectories and treatment regimens [5, 6]. Epilepsy, congenital heart disease (CHD), and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) vividly illustrate these challenges [7–9]. These three conditions were selected because they represent distinct chronic disease trajectories (neurologic, cardiac, and autoimmune) with clinically important reproductive implications, require long-term specialty care beginning in adolescence, and involve medication- or physiology-related reproductive risks that necessitate tailored SRH information. Specifically, enzyme-inducing antiseizure medications in epilepsy can reduce hormonal contraceptive efficacy; structural cardiac disease in CHD is linked to serious pregnancy-related cardiovascular complications; and teratogenic immunosuppressive therapies in SLE can severely limit contraceptive options [7, 8]. These condition-specific reproductive risks are insufficiently addressed in the broader SRH literature, which supports their selection as a focused, clinically coherent group [7, 8]. Examining these conditions together enables cross-condition comparison of shared and condition-specific barriers and facilitators influencing access to SRH information and services.

Globally, more than 8 million young adults live with epilepsy, and in the USA alone, approximately 456,000 young adults have active epilepsy [8, 9]. Despite this prevalence, evidence shows that SRH counseling is often inadequate [10]. In a USA cohort of nearly 1000 adolescent and young women with epilepsy, 38% had no documented reproductive health counseling, and 49% of patient-years included no SRH counseling at all [10, 11]. These gaps are concerning because enzyme-inducing antiseizure medications used for epilepsy reduce the effectiveness of combined hormonal contraceptives, with pharmacologic evidence suggesting that this interaction contributes to hundreds of unintended pregnancies each year in the USA [12]. Moreover, treating physicians provide SRH counseling to patients with epilepsy less frequently than recommended by the American Academy of Neurology (AAN) and the Child Neurology Foundation (CNF), based on documentation [13].

Similarly, young adults with CHD face equally significant reproductive risks. Among women with severe CHD (modified WHO class III), cardiac complications occur in 19–27% of pregnancies [14], and long-term follow-up studies reveal that nearly one in three experience major adverse cardiovascular events within two decades after pregnancy [15]. Despite these elevated risks, contraceptive and fertility counseling for youth with CHD is frequently inadequate or delayed [16, 17], leaving many unprepared for safe reproductive decision-making.

Young adults with SLE encounter overlapping and compounding reproductive challenges. Active disease at conception is associated with a nearly threefold increased risk of preterm birth and a more than twofold increased risk of pre-eclampsia [18–20]. For young adults with these chronic conditions, reproductive decision-making is further complicated by immunosuppressive or teratogenic therapies that severely limit contraceptive options [21].

This evidence shows both the magnitude of the population affected and the preventable harm that results from inconsistent SRH information and lack of personalized care. Although clinical guidelines exist for epilepsy and CHD in pregnancy [12, 14, 21] and expert recommendations are available in lupus [18–21], their implementation for adolescents and young adults with chronic conditions remains fragmented. Existing research is scattered across specialties and focuses mainly on single conditions or maternal outcomes, with limited attention to young adults’ SRH needs. Previous studies have examined related areas, including sexual health communication between healthcare professionals and young adults with chronic conditions in Western countries [12, 21, 22]. A preliminary search of MEDLINE and CINAHL was undertaken to determine whether existing reviews had synthesized evidence on SRH information needs and access-related barriers and facilitators among young adults aged 15–24 years living with epilepsy, CHD, and SLE in the USA and Canada. The search indicated limited synthesis addressing this combined, young adult-centered focus. Given the heterogeneity of populations, outcomes, and study designs within this literature, a scoping review is methodologically appropriate to systematically map the extent and characteristics of available evidence and to identify gaps to inform future research, policy, and clinical practice.

Review objectives and questions

Objective

The objective of this scoping review is to identify, map, and synthesize existing evidence on the SRH information needs of young adults living with epilepsy, CHD, or SLE in the USA and Canada, and to examine the barriers and facilitators that influence their access to SRH information and services.

Inclusion criteria

This review will apply the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework [23].

Participants

The population is young adults 15–24 years (of any sex, gender identity or sexual orientation), living with epilepsy, CHD, or SLE. This age range aligns with the World Health Organization definition of “young adults” (15–24 years), encompassing late adolescence and young adulthood. Studies with broader age ranges will be included only if young adult-specific data (15–24 years) are extractable or if at least 70% of participants fall within the 15–24 range; studies where age distribution cannot be verified, or young adult data cannot be disaggregated, will be excluded to reduce contamination by older adult populations. Perspectives from caregivers and healthcare providers will be considered when they directly address young adults’ SRH needs or experiences. Caregiver/provider perspectives will be charted separately from young adult-reported data to avoid conflating perceived needs with expressed young adults’ information needs.

Concept

The concept is SRH information needs and the barriers and facilitators to accessing SRH information and services among young adults with chronic conditions. Information needs include information/counseling related to puberty, contraception, fertility, sexual activity, pregnancy planning, preconception counseling, and medication teratogenicity. For this review, “SRH information needs” are defined as young adult-reported knowledge gaps, expressed desire for information, or documented unmet informational needs related to SRH. When studies do not explicitly measure information needs, needs may be identified through clearly described counseling gaps or young adult-reported uncertainty regarding SRH topics; these will be charted as “inferred” needs and distinguished from explicitly reported needs. Barriers and facilitators may occur at individual, interpersonal, organizational, or system levels, including stigma, lack of knowledge, confidentiality concerns, condition-related risks, provider training and role clarity, care coordination, affordability, insurance, and availability of young adult-friendly services.

Context

The context includes Canada and the USA, encompassing all settings such as primary care, specialty clinics, schools, community services, and digital platforms. This geographic scope was selected to focus on North American contexts with comparable clinical practice structures and to reflect the team’s capacity to screen and interpret English-language evidence. Both countries share comparable pediatric specialty care systems, transition-to-adult care challenges, and health financing structures that shape the delivery of SRH services for young adults with chronic conditions. While evidence from the UK, Australia, and Western Europe may provide relevant complementary insights given similar health system structures, the inclusion of these settings was beyond the scope of this review and is recommended for future syntheses. Differences between healthcare financing and service delivery in the USA and Canada will be considered in interpretation and will be acknowledged as a limitation.

Types of sources

Eligible sources will include peer-reviewed primary qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-methods studies; observational or interventional designs; program and policy evaluations; and empirical grey literature (e.g., theses, dissertations, reports, conference abstracts). Empirical grey literature is operationalized as documents that present original data collection or analysis (e.g., theses/dissertations, government or organizational reports with findings, and conference abstracts reporting empirical results). Editorials, opinion pieces, commentaries, and policy briefs without original data will be excluded. Publications without empirical data, studies on unrelated conditions, or research conducted outside the USA and Canada will be excluded. No date limits will be applied, and studies in English will be included. The language restriction will be acknowledged as a limitation and may reduce the capture of evidence from other linguistic contexts. Studies involving overlapping conditions (e.g., multiple cardiac or chronic diseases) will be included if they provide disaggregated data for the population of interest. When disaggregated data are unavailable, overlaps will be documented, and findings interpreted cautiously, acknowledging potential confounding and limitations.

Methods

This scoping review will be conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) scoping review methodology [23] and reported using the PRISMA-ScR checklist [24]. The review protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework on September 18, 2025, at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/5SWTY.

Search strategy

A three-step search strategy will be employed. First, an initial limited search of MEDLINE and CINAHL will be undertaken to identify relevant articles and analyze the text words in the titles and abstracts, as well as the index terms used to describe the articles. This preliminary search was conducted in August 2025 and informed refinement of keywords, indexing terms, and eligibility criteria. Second, a comprehensive search using all identified keywords and index terms will be adapted for each included information source. Third, the reference lists of all included studies and relevant reviews will be screened, and any additional eligible studies identified through this process will be included.

The databases to be searched include MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science, and CENTRAL. Sources of unpublished studies and grey literature will include ProQuest Dissertations and Theses, Open Grey, and the websites of key organizations such as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), National Institutes of Health (NIH), American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), Canadian Paediatric Society, Epilepsy Foundation, Lupus Foundation of America, and the Heart and Stroke Foundation. No date limits will be applied, and studies published in English and French will be considered. A draft MEDLINE search strategy is provided in Supplementary material 1. Database-specific search strategies (e.g., Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO) will be included as supplementary files to enhance reproducibility.

Study selection

Following the search, all identified citations will be imported into Covidence (an online software platform for screening and data management). Study selection will occur in two stages. First, titles and abstracts will be independently screened by two reviewers (YA and SN) against the inclusion criteria. Before formal screening, a pilot test will be conducted on a sample of titles and abstracts (50–100 records) to ensure consistent application of the criteria. Inter-rater agreement will be assessed using Cohen’s kappa, with a priori threshold of ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable before proceeding to full screening. Discrepancies will be discussed and criteria refined as needed. Second, full texts of potentially relevant articles will be retrieved and assessed in detail by the same reviewers, with a similar pilot test conducted to confirm consistent interpretation of eligibility. Reasons for excluding full-text studies that do not meet the inclusion criteria will be documented. Any disagreements at either stage will be resolved through discussion or, if unresolved, through consultation with a senior reviewer (SM). The entire selection process will be recorded in a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram and presented in the final review. Non-English records identified through searching will be documented at the full-text stage when feasible, and exclusion due to language will be reported as part of the PRISMA-ScR flow.

Data extraction

Data will be extracted from the studies included by two independent reviewers (YA and SN) using a data extraction tool developed by the reviewers and adapted from the JBI methodology guidance for scoping reviews [24]. Extracted data will include information on the study population, concept, context, study methods, and key findings relevant to the review questions. A draft version of the extraction tool is provided in Supplementary material 2. Before full data extraction begins, a pilot test will be conducted in which both reviewers independently extract data from a small sample of included studies. The results will be compared, and any discrepancies or ambiguities will be discussed to refine the tool and clarify instructions. This ensures consistency and accuracy before proceeding with extraction from all included studies. The extraction tool may be further refined as needed during the process, and any modifications will be fully documented and reported in the final scoping review. The extraction tool will also capture whether sexual orientation and gender identity (SOGI) data were collected or reported, and whether findings are reported for key demographic subgroups (e.g., sexual and gender minorities, racial and ethnic groups, socioeconomic indicators), to support mapping of equity-related evidence gaps. Where studies include caregiver or provider perspectives, the extraction tool will capture whether data reflect young adult-reported needs (quoted or explicitly attributed to the young adult) versus proxy perceptions, and these will be charted separately.

Data analysis and presentation

Findings will be synthesized descriptively and through content analysis. Descriptive synthesis will summarize study characteristics, populations, contexts, and key outcomes in tables or diagrams. Content analysis will involve coding and grouping recurring concepts across studies to identify key patterns relevant to the review questions. Consistent with scoping review methodology, content analysis will be conducted at a manifest, descriptive level to categorize concepts (rather than interpretive thematic synthesis). The coding approach will be primarily inductive, with an initial codebook developed from approximately 10% of included sources and refined through team discussion; a hybrid approach will be used if pre-specified categories (e.g., socio-ecological levels) are applied as organizing frameworks. Coding will explicitly distinguish SRH information needs from barriers and facilitators and will differentiate young adult-reported needs from caregiver/provider perspectives. Findings will be mapped across socio-ecological levels: individual, interpersonal, community, organizational, and policy [25], and by developmental stages relevant to young adults with chronic conditions: adolescence (15–19 years) and young adulthood (20–24 years). These stages align with the protocol’s age-inclusion range and are intended to support age-specific mapping of SRH information needs and access experiences. Two reviewers will conduct the analysis independently, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or consultation with a senior reviewer. Frequency counts (e.g., number of studies addressing specific SRH topics, barriers/facilitators, settings, and populations) will be reported as descriptive mapping to characterize the evidence landscape.

Discussion

Understanding the SRH information needs of vulnerable young adults and the barriers and facilitators to accessing care is essential for preventing adverse health outcomes and promoting lifelong well-being. Young adults with chronic health conditions such as epilepsy, congenital heart disease, and systemic lupus erythematosus often experience unique challenges related to their SRH needs, which are further complicated by fragmented care pathways, limited provider knowledge, stigma, and a lack of young adult-friendly resources [26–31].

By synthesizing the current body of evidence, this review aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the factors that shape access to SRH services for this population. This includes identifying knowledge gaps, systemic barriers to care, and potential entry points for supportive interventions. Recognizing the role of caregivers and healthcare providers, as well as the broader healthcare and educational systems, will be critical in framing a holistic approach to care.

The findings will offer valuable insights for healthcare providers, educators, and policymakers by highlighting where existing services fall short and where targeted, young adult-centered interventions can make a meaningful difference. Specifically, the review will inform the design and implementation of developmentally appropriate, accessible, and culturally responsive SRH resources and services. It will also underscore the importance of integrating SRH education into routine care for young adults with chronic conditions, and the need for provider training to ensure consistent, sensitive, and informed communication. Ultimately, this review will help identify gaps in SRH information and service delivery for young people with chronic conditions and may inform future research, policy, and service development to improve access to timely, relevant, and developmentally appropriate SRH information and services. The review will also explicitly map areas where evidence is limited for subgroups such as LGBTQ + young adults, and racialized or socioeconomically marginalized young adults to support equity-oriented research and service development. The findings will characterize the available evidence and identify knowledge gaps rather than evaluate intervention effectiveness, service quality, or outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several strengths. Focusing on literature from the USA and Canada makes the findings more relevant to North American healthcare and policy contexts. The use of clear inclusion and exclusion criteria ensured a systematic and transparent review process. However, a few limitations should be acknowledged. Restricting the review to studies conducted in the USA and Canada and published in English may limit the scope of the evidence captured and exclude relevant evidence from other settings or languages. In addition, the focus on epilepsy, CHD, and SLE means that the findings may not reflect the experiences of young adults living with other chronic conditions. Variability in how SRH information needs, barriers, and facilitators are defined and measured across studies may also affect comparisons across the evidence base. These limitations will be transparently reported in the final review.

Ethics and dissemination

As this review will synthesize data from publicly available sources, ethics approval is not required. Results will be disseminated through publication in JBI Evidence Synthesis, conference presentations, and knowledge translation materials for clinicians, policymakers, and young adult health organizations. The summaries of findings will be developed in collaboration with young adult advocacy groups, patient partners, and healthcare providers working with young people living with epilepsy, CHD, and SLE. Engaging these partners is important to ensure that study findings are communicated in language that is accessible, relevant, culturally sensitive, and meaningful to young adults and their families. This collaborative approach also supports equitable knowledge dissemination by incorporating the lived experiences and perspectives of young adults with chronic conditions into the co-creation of knowledge translation resources.
