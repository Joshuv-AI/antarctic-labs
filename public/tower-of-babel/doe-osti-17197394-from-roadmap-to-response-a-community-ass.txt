Executive Summary
 HE SECOND Annual Autonomous Workflows Workshop was convened to write a community roadmap

T for autonomous science. The roadmap was published in the same month, so the workshop answered
 one instead. Over two days, participants from national laboratories, universities, instrument and
platform vendors, applied research institutes, and federal program offices tested its eighteen milestones
against what their organizations actually run. The roadmap’s own reading holds, in that the milestones closest
to model capability have advanced and those requiring someone else’s cooperation have not. Our assessment
sharpens that diagnosis in one respect.

 The community does not lack a plan. It lacks anyone whose job is to execute one.
 Implementable proposals surfaced throughout, and each stalled on the same absence of a mandate, a
 budget, and staff.

• The scorecard omits the deciding actors. Rating milestones against laboratories, academia, and industry
  leaves out instrument manufacturers, whose incentives determine whether the interface milestones can
  close at all, and the federal programs that scaffold national-scale ones.
• Milestone status is not one number. A milestone closed two years ago for some applications is five years
  away for others, and some are pulled by blocked researchers while others are pushed by technologists
  without a confirmed need.
• Decision-relevant state does not cross the laboratory door. Automation already maintains instru-
  ment and workflow state, but no laboratory passes decision-relevant state beyond its own walls, so the
  requirement is portable state with shared event semantics, ordering, and reconciliation rules.
• Verification infrastructure has already earned its keep. Cross-framework provenance applied to three
  deployed agentic workflows exposed a model ignoring its instructions, a pipeline discarding sensor data,
  and a design loop returning results matched to the wrong molecules. All three reported clean runs.
• Metadata is being solved by agents, not by forms. Facility users have begun supplying metadata through
  programmatic agent access, and the measured result is more metadata, so the requirement is machine
  accessibility.
• Failure records need shared scientific semantics. Building on existing domain schemas, the cross-
  domain task is to distinguish execution disposition, measurement validity, and scientific interpretation,
  since a valid low-yield result, a failed reagent delivery, and a missing readout warrant different conclusions.
• Mathematics belongs between the agent and the actuator. Agents should generate and update the
  mathematical problem while established methods solve it and issue certificates, because the dangerous
  hallucinations are the feasible ones, not the absurd ones.
 We propose ten candidate milestones for gaps the roadmap does not cover, among them shared semantics
for failure records, a vendor engagement mechanism for instrument interfaces, data ingress integrity, and
model qualification. The investment agenda that follows is anchored by a common instrument automation
layer, in software for instruments that expose interfaces and in robotics for those that do not. The most
persuasive precedents came from industrial robotics and automated vehicles, whose communities built an
institution to solve the same vendor-incentive and regulator-acceptance problems.

1 Introduction
 Autonomous science couples instruments, robots, data systems, and AI agents into workflows that plan,
execute, and interpret experiments with progressively less human intervention. The past year moved the
field faster than the community anticipated, with multi-agent systems producing experimentally validated
hypotheses [1, 2, 3], agent interface protocols consolidating around a recognizable stack [4, 5], and a national
mobilization placing robotic laboratories at the center of U.S. federal science strategy [6]. It also produced a
counter-current. A published correction to a flagship autonomous-discovery result walked back its novelty
claims and removed a compound that had leaked from the training data [7]. Benchmarks show that agents
approaching expert performance on closed-ended questions complete only a small fraction of open-ended
research tasks [8, 9], and fabricated citations have appeared in papers accepted at leading venues [10].
 A two-year community roadmap published in July 2026 read this asymmetry as the defining tension of the
field, revisiting five dimensions from an earlier grassroots roadmap [11] and elevating trust and verification,
together with safety, security, and governance, to first-class dimensions [12]. That roadmap carries eighteen
milestones, M1 through M18, each with a status and a target year, which Table 1 reproduces alongside the
section that assesses it, and its own scorecard records that after one year none had been fully achieved, six
were partially achieved, one was reframed, seven remained open, and four were newly proposed.
 The second Annual Autonomous Workflows Workshop (AWW26) was convened in Arlington, Virginia,
on July 29 and 30, 2026, to write such a roadmap, and the roadmap was published in the same month, which
changed the workshop’s purpose before it began. Instead of drafting an agenda, participants were asked to
answer one, testing each milestone against what their own organizations run today. We organized the two
days around four questions, which are also the contributions of this report.
 1. We report where deployment practice stands against M1 through M18, and identify where the milestone
 framing itself did not survive contact with that evidence (Sections 2 to 6).
 2. We catalog the systems, standards, and precedents already in production that address roadmap compo-
 nents, together with the barriers that stop others from adopting them.
 3. We identify gaps the roadmap does not cover, stated precisely enough to carry as candidate milestones
 with target years (Section 7).
 4. We set out an investment agenda, including the applied mathematics that autonomous laboratories need
 and that is not currently funded (Section 8).
 This report is meant to be read alongside the roadmap, not in place of it, and where the room split we
record both positions instead of resolving them. Two findings recur across all four topic areas. The first is that
the milestones closest to raw model capability have advanced fastest while those requiring cross-institutional
coordination, verification infrastructure, or vendor cooperation have barely moved, which confirms the
roadmap’s own reading. The second is that the community has no institution capable of carrying a milestone
forward once a roadmap names it, and several of the gaps in Section 7 follow from that absence. The
remainder of this report is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the method and what the assessment
revealed about the scorecard, Sections 3 to 6 report the four milestone clusters, Section 7 collects the
cross-cutting gaps, Section 8 presents the investment agenda, and Section 9 concludes.

2 Assessing the Roadmap
Method. The assessment was designed around four staffed working groups, each owning one of the milestone
clusters reported in Sections 3 to 6. Each group was to rate every milestone by sector, then produce solution
cards, gap cards, and investment pitches answering what they would fund given $10M immediately and
$100M over five years.
 We record what actually happened, because it bears on how the findings below should be read. The
sessions ran as a single plenary instead of as breakouts, with each workbook’s milestone list projected in turn
and the discussion moving on whenever a topic stalled. The workbooks were distributed for participants to
complete individually and deposit in a shared directory, during the workshop or in the weeks after it, and
most scorecard cells were never filled. This assessment therefore rests primarily on the recorded plenary
discussion, which we treat as the community position throughout. Two channels supplement it, namely
written workbook contributions submitted afterwards by individual organizations, which we treat as input
from one participant and not as consensus, and presentation material, which we treat as claims about the
presenter’s own systems. The empty scorecards are themselves the first result.
The scorecard did not survive contact with the room. Rating each milestone against DOE laboratories,
academia, and industry leaves out the two actors whose behavior determines whether several milestones can
close at all. The first is the instrument vendor, because for the interface milestones the relevant question is
not how well laboratories perform but whether a manufacturer has any incentive to expose a programmatic
interface, and vendors were largely absent from the room. The second is the federal program itself, since

 6

a milestone such as a scalable national instrument framework is scaffolded by agency action, not by any
individual laboratory.
 The scorecard also assumes that a milestone is either met or not met at a point in time, and practice
does not behave that way. For some applications a milestone was effectively closed two years ago while
for others it remains five years out, and the difference is a property of the application, not of the milestone,
so participants proposed assembling case studies of specific applications and mapping each against the
milestones. A distinction the roadmap does not draw also emerged, between milestones pulled by researchers
who are blocked without them and milestones pushed by technologists whose value proposition no application
group has confirmed, and participants argued that perhaps four of the original fourteen matter substantially
more than the rest.
 The most consistent observation across all four topic areas concerned execution and not content. Imple-
mentable proposals surfaced repeatedly, including a certification mark for instruments, a shared benchmark
platform, and a cross-sector consortium, and each stalled because no organization has the mandate and the
resources to carry it forward. A roadmap does not act, and we return to this in Sections 7 and 8 as the binding
constraint on the roadmap as a whole.

3 Instruments and Interfaces
 This cluster covers M1 through M4 and M10 through M12, defined in Table 1, which span instrument
interfaces, cross-institution workflows, the compute fabric beneath them, and the agent interface layer that
the roadmap reframed around MCP and A2A.
M1 and M10 ask for different things. M10 is the logical layer at which agents reach tools, reframed by the
roadmap around MCP and A2A [4, 5, 13], and M1 covers everything beneath it, including the physical and
on-the-wire interfaces a manufacturer ships and the vendor-supplied API where one exists. Participants were
clear that MCP does not belong to M1, because no instrument vendor ships an MCP server and every such
server in use has been written by the community on top of whatever API the vendor provides. The room
then split on how much M1 matters, and we record both positions. One holds that agreement on common
hardware interfaces is close to a lost cause because no two facilities converge on anything, so M1 is better
understood as a collateral artifact that materializes if the other milestones succeed than as a prerequisite to
attack directly. The other holds that instrument automation is roughly eighty percent of the problem, that
nothing else in the roadmap can be built without programmatic access to every element of a workflow, and
that solving it releases the resources needed everywhere else.
 One development after the workshop bears directly on this layering. The Model Hardware Standard
(MHS), released as a research preview in August 2026, proposes a standardized driver layer that exposes
devices through read and write primitives and makes them discoverable in a common format, reachable by
any agent harness through protocols such as MCP [14]. It sits where this cluster locates M1, beneath the
agent interfaces of M10, and whether it closes the milestone turns on the vendor adoption that the rest of this
section identifies as the binding constraint.
Vendor incentives, not facility capability, are the binding constraint. Manufacturers design the graphical
interface first, because it is a substantial part of what they sell, and where an API exists, it rarely exposes
everything the interface does. Participants reported the full spectrum, from one electron microscope vendor
supplying a capable API and a full digital twin of the instrument to another refusing API access entirely.
The economics are not irrational, since instruments are high cost, long-lived, and sold in low volume, and
proprietary interface software carries good unit economics, so pressure from any single laboratory has no

 7

effect. Lifetimes measured in decades compound this, because laboratories run equipment fifteen to thirty-five
years old that they cannot replace and must nonetheless drive under program control. Beneath the agent
layer the picture is better than the scorecard suggests. Typed device-control standards are in production
use, including SiLA 2 [15], OPC UA with its laboratory device companion specification [16], and EPICS at
beamline facilities [17]. At least one deployed autonomous laboratory runs its robots on OPC UA specifically
because it supplies a reliable state machine over the whole cell.
The interface question that matters is whether it streams. The dominant model at user facilities remains a
scientist arriving for a multi-day run, leaving with files, and analyzing them over the following six months,
only to find that what they needed is not there and that another proposal cycle stands between them and a
second attempt. Participants were emphatic that an API delivering a complete data set after the run is not a
useful target. The capability to pursue is adaptive acquisition, in which the experiment is modified while it
runs. Current agents are poorly suited to it, because interrupting one typically halts processing instead of
redirecting it. As data rates outpace centralized pipelines [18, 19], inference has moved to the instrument
edge, with real-time reconstruction and microsecond triggering demonstrated [20, 21] and software-defined
acquisition removing the store-first step from the critical path [22]. Deployed inference is often light enough
for a small accelerator or an FPGA behind the detector. The persistent difficulty is the round trip. Moving a
trained model to an edge device remains substantially manual, and the scheduling model of submitting a job
and returning later is mismatched with inference that must answer on demand.
Collective action has worked elsewhere. The strongest solutions offered were precedents, not technologies.
In 2011, driving an industrial robot required the manufacturer’s own tooling, and moving to a different
manufacturer meant rewriting everything. Open drivers now exist for all major manufacturers [23]. A
consortium achieved this by assembling a user base large enough to matter, contributing the engineering so
that manufacturers spent money instead of scarce staff, and using one early adopter as an existence proof once
buyers began preferring the robot with open interfaces. In automated vehicles, an organization purchased an
exemplar of every drive-by-wire interface on the market and wrote shims from one common logical API to
each, so any autonomy stack could drive any vehicle, after which later vendors implemented that interface
natively. The technical target that follows is functional interoperability in place of standardization. Each
beamline is genuinely a unique instrument, and every facility is incentivized to advertise how distinctive it is.
The community should therefore build enough functional similarity that one experiment can span several
facilities and yield comparable data, instead of trying to make them identical. The proposed mechanism
is to publish behaviors and machine-readable capabilities instead of prescribing implementations, so that
an instrument advertises what it can do and where its limits are. The syntax is secondary. Event-driven
architecture was reported as what makes this work in practice. In one materials characterization facility, robots,
instruments, and conveyance are coordinated through an industrial control protocol for state and a streaming
backbone for data, so that provenance, live visualization, and portal linkage are produced continuously instead
of being reconstructed afterwards. Throughput there moved from roughly six measurements a day for a
strong graduate student to on the order of a thousand.
What is missing. Vendors frequently lack the engineering skill set to build the interfaces the community wants,
and the likely alternative is worse, since manufacturers will increasingly generate them with coding agents
and each will arrive at a different design. No certification or rating scheme exists, and a mark indicating that
an instrument meets a defined level of programmatic accessibility, analogous to a safety certification on an
appliance, was endorsed and immediately recognized as unowned.

 Insights/Recommendations: Add instrument vendors as a sector on the scorecard, reframe M1
 around vendor incentive instead of facility capability, and adopt functional interoperability, in which
 instruments publish machine-readable capabilities and limits, as its target in place of interface standard-
 ization. Treat streaming and mid-run modification, not programmatic access alone, as the acceptance
 criterion for M1 and M2, and fund a distributed instrument and robotics testbed together with a shared
 engineering capability that writes open interfaces on manufacturers’ behalf.

4 Data and Orchestration
 This cluster covers M5 through M9, defined in Table 1, which span metadata, federated data governance,
provenance, orchestration, and cross-facility knowledge integration.
Portable state is the primitive the roadmap does not name. Participants framed the distinction as one between
automation and autonomy. Automation already maintains instrument and workflow state, as the OPC UA
state machine of Section 3 shows, but that state is local and operational. Autonomy requires knowing what
has happened, in what order, and how recently, because an experiment is designed from what is already
known and replanned halfway through from what has just been measured. Participants argued that very few
autonomous laboratories model this decision-relevant state explicitly and that none pass it beyond their own
door. The barrier to M2 and M9 therefore sits at the laboratory boundary and not in the network between
laboratories, and the cross-laboratory requirement is portable, decision-relevant state with shared event
semantics, ordering, and reconciliation rules. The corresponding proposal is that events are the currency of
autonomy, so that a laboratory built around an event stream is stateful by construction.
Provenance has already paid for itself. A cross-framework provenance layer built on the W3C PROV
vocabulary [24, 25] was applied to three deployed agentic workflows, and in each case exposed an error
that the workflow itself reported as a clean run [26]. In an agent-driven reasoning system, a substantial
fraction of the instructions given to the agent were ignored by the underlying model with no warning in the
downstream data. In a cross-facility sensing pipeline running between a plant phenotyping facility and a
leadership computing facility, a large portion of the captured data was silently dropped. In a molecular design
loop, simulated energies were mismatched against the molecules they belonged to, so the campaign returned
a ranked report built on scrambled results and completed without raising a single error. All three failures
occurred between components instead of inside any one of them, so no component’s local logs could have
revealed them, and language-model agents do not crash, because they always produce an answer, which thus
makes their errors silent by default. Participants took this as the strongest argument that M7 is a prerequisite
for M8 and not a parallel activity.
Agents, not forms, are what is fixing metadata. The assessment of M5 was the most contested in the
workshop and turned on incentives. The position argued from long experience with community repositories
is that metadata standardization has been attempted for decades and largely failed, so practice has moved to
crosswalking heterogeneous schemas. The failure mode was described plainly in the observation that metadata
entry forms are where science goes to die. Against this, a new development was reported. Over the preceding
eighteen months, facility users have begun supplying metadata through MCP-mediated programmatic access
instead of through forms, and the observed effect has been more metadata rather than less, because the barrier
now falls on the agent instead of on the scientist. We regard this as the most encouraging finding for M5,
and it argues that the requirement on metadata systems is programmatic accessibility, not a better human

 9

interface. Participants also noted that autonomous laboratories learning from the humans who operate them,
for instance by eliciting metadata through their corrections, may constitute human subjects research more
often than the community assumes.
Two structural problems the roadmap treats separately. The community sends two contradictory messages
about data, one that instruments produce more than can be stored and another that there is not enough credible
data to train on, which motivates synthetic generation. Generated data and physics-based simulation belong
on opposite sides of this argument, because they fail in different ways. Data drawn from a trained generative
model reinforces the existing distribution instead of exploring beyond it, and recursive training on it erodes
the distributional tails where new science lives [27]. Simulation output is not sampled from the experimental
distribution at all, so it carries independent physical information, and its limit is the fidelity of the model
and the regimes in which that model holds. The formulation the room converged on is that data are useful
only for the decisions they can drive. The second problem concerns what raw data means, because nobody
receives raw data and every measurement is calibrated and processed in place before a human sees it, so
the honest requirement is recording what happened between signal acquisition and the quantity a scientist
believes they measured. Participants noted that this understanding is eroding, because students load an array
from an HDF5 file with a general-purpose imaging library, obtain a picture, and believe they have the data. At
the instrument edge, reduction discards signal permanently, so what was removed and why must be recorded.
What already works. An interface that fits on a mobile phone allowed the team to steer a running experiment
from outside the facility, which sounds trivial and had not previously been done. Participants framed the
underlying problem as fatigue and not convenience. A nine-day beamtime leaves the experimenter exhausted
exactly where the decisions matter most, and fatigue is a first-order source of error that automation removes.
 The most portable practice concerns how agents are built, not how they run. Because the frontier model
layer moves faster than any scientific team can track, the durable asset is the scientist-authored evaluation
contract against which an automated builder iterates a coding agent [28]. Rebuilding against that contract
when the next model arrives produces a candidate, not an assumed equivalent agent, and a reused rubric
alone does not establish equivalent scientific performance. Qualification should include intended-use tests
and reserved cases held outside the builder’s iterative feedback, as the demonstrated system already does. It
should also record the model, tool, judge, and contract versions, determine which prior evidence remains
applicable, and rerun the tests the change affects, which gives M23 in Section 7 an actionable form.
What is missing. Trusted traceability is distinct from provenance. Materials qualification rests on physical
testimony that a specimen is what it is claimed to be, and the digital equivalent needs provenance, full
traceability, and a trust authority that attests to them. Participants could describe the first two and could not
name a candidate for the third, a point we return to in Section 7 together with the metrology foundations
that already exist. Data ingress integrity is the second gap. The community’s anxiety has historically been
about giving data away, and participants argued it should now be about what comes in, whether from a faulty
system generating incorrect metadata or a deliberate attempt to shift a field’s consensus by poisoning its
corpus. Instructions hidden as white text in a proposal to bias an automated reviewer were offered as evidence
that the attack surface is live. The third is that foundation models are unqualified components, since open
weights do not make a model reproducible because the training cannot be recreated, and releasing the data
does not establish what the model learned.

 10

 Insights/Recommendations: Treat portable, decision-relevant laboratory state, with shared event
 semantics, ordering, and reconciliation rules, as a first-class requirement. Make cross-framework
 provenance a precondition for M8, on the evidence that silent inter-component errors are the dominant
 failure mode in deployed agentic workflows. Restate M5 as programmatic, agent-accessible metadata
 capture instead of schema standardization, add data ingress integrity, and treat model qualification and
 the trust authority for traceability as open requirements.

5 Trust, Safety, and Governance
 This cluster covers M15 through M18, defined in Table 1, which carry the two dimensions the roadmap
elevated to first class.
Reproducibility needs a different definition. Participants rejected the reading the milestone language
invites, since bitwise reproducibility is neither achievable nor desirable for a workflow containing a physical
experiment and a non-deterministic orchestrator. Two replacements were offered and both endorsed. The first
defines reproducibility through provenance and annotation. What must be recoverable is the account of what
happened between the start and the end of a workflow, including which intermediate data existed, where it
was written, how it was extracted without disrupting a running experiment, and how model architectures and
weights changed over a campaign. The second concerns adoption, on the view that a one-off demonstration
nobody can build on is marketing and that the reproducibility which matters is whether an outside group,
including a company with no stake in the original result, can take the work as a foundation and proceed.
M15 and M16 are confused in practice, at a cost. A project lead asks a statistician to benchmark a model
by the following week; the lead is asking for M16, and the statistician hears M15. Participants reported the
same miscommunication repeatedly. The definitions the room settled on are that verification asks whether
the system does what it was specified to do and validation asks whether it does what the user needs. The
prescription that followed is systems-engineering discipline applied to requirements before metrics, on the
argument that a metric is only an observation and carries no verdict, so a measurement becomes a success or
a failure only against a stated requirement or hypothesis.
 For M16, the assessment was that the community has no metrics worth the name, since floating-point
rate, bandwidth, and latency carry no scientific meaning for an autonomous campaign and machine learning
benchmarks are narrow and saturating quickly. The attached warning is that if the community does not define
these metrics while it still can, someone else will. The proposal that followed was a shared grand challenge
on a common platform, which we take up in Section 8.
Physical consequence changes what M17 must deliver. The taxonomy proposed distinguishes a performance
failure, in which the goal is met at excess cost in measurement time or energy, a result failure, in which the
intended outcome is not obtained, and a harm failure, in which a person is injured or a facility is damaged.
The most useful precedent came from autonomous reactor operations, where operations and safety are strictly
separate systems, so that engaging the safety path cuts off the control path and the control path cannot override
it. The corresponding design rule is that the adaptive controller may be untrusted provided a deterministic,
independently implemented safety layer and native interlocks sit beneath it and are unreachable from the
controller. The open question participants could not answer is how to test such a system. The analogy raised
was the flight test regime in which a pilot flies at the ground to demonstrate that terrain avoidance engages.
Nobody wishes to replicate that with a high-power laser or a reactor.

 11

Testbeds are the mechanism, and evaluators belong inside them. Testbeds resolve the circularity of a system
that cannot be deployed until its safety is demonstrated and cannot demonstrate safety until it is deployed. The
reported practice is to carve a diagnostic line out of an operating facility, or to use a licensed research reactor
where a commercial one is unavailable, so the demonstration happens on hardware that is real but expendable.
The second practice comes from automated vehicles. An early attempt to demonstrate an automated ground
vehicle to an army test organization failed, because the existing evaluation regime had no way to assess a
robotic vehicle. The successful approach on the following project included the test and evaluation command
from the start, so that evaluators helped design the instrumentation, the safety systems, and the teleoperation
capability. Participants proposed inviting regulators and security organizations into these testbeds specifically
to red team them. A live policy obstacle was identified that no milestone addresses. The security posture that
protects a network by isolating it also prevents the instrument integration autonomous workflows require, and
air gapping is the default answer. Participants described receiving simultaneous instructions to secure the
network and to build autonomous capability, with the security posture currently prevailing.
What is missing. No portable profile connects an authorized action to independent evidence that it physically
occurred, and to a separate judgment about whether the resulting claim is valid. Identity, access tokens,
provenance, signatures, and attestation each establish a different digital property, and none establishes that
an instrument acted or that a finding holds, so these must remain separate verdicts. Accountability across
institutions is undefined. When an agent acting across three institutions causes a costly failure, no existing
agreement determines whether the operator, the model vendor, the facility, or the principal investigator is
answerable. Participants treated that absence as a nearer barrier than any technical component of M18.

 Insights/Recommendations: Separate M15 and M16 explicitly, restate M16 so reproducibility is
 measured by whether an independent group can build on a result, and define scientific metrics for
 end-to-end campaigns before external definitions are imposed. Adopt the separation of control from
 safety systems as an architectural requirement for M17, and invest in testbeds as the primary route
 to safety evidence, with evaluators and regulators inside them from the start. Add cross-institution
 accountability agreements to M18 alongside the technical identity work.

6 Mathematical Foundations and Workforce
 This cluster covers M13 and M14, together with the two cross-cutting items the workshop added to the
scorecard, X1 and X2, all defined in Table 1. The mathematics discussion was the clearest single addition the
workshop made to the roadmap, and it produced a thesis instead of a list.
Agents should pose the mathematical problem, not replace it. Under the arrangement participants endorsed,
an agent expresses a mission description, decomposes it into a task graph, and replans when something
fails, while game theory, dynamic programming, integer optimization, and control theory solve the resulting
problems and issue certificates that a verifier checks before anything actuates. Current practice couples a
conventional multi-robot control stack, in which decomposition is handcrafted and any substantial mission
change requires human redesign, to AI components used inside that stack. The alternative places many
interacting agents above an operations-research layer, so that they negotiate, compete, or learn from one
another through an explicit mathematical framework while the optimization, control, and verification layers
beneath remain classical. The architectural argument is specific, since uncertainty is symbolic and loosely
constrained at the mission layer and becomes progressively harder as it descends until physical constraints

 12

act as hard boundaries, so the architecture can reject constraint violations before they reach actuation. A
certificate, however, establishes only the properties checked under stated models and assumptions, not that
the agent formalized the intended scientific question correctly [29]. This limit matters because the dangerous
hallucinations are not the ridiculous ones, which are self-flagging, but the feasible ones, which pass casual
inspection and execute.
 The mathematics named is broad and mostly not new, spanning game formulations, dynamic programming
over task graphs, and auctions for scheduling under hard constraints. Filtering methods were offered as
the route to digital twins that reproduce an instrument’s imperfections instead of an idealization of it.
Participants argued that such twins serve campaign rehearsal and hands-on training at once. Four open
problems were stated in fundable form, asking whether (i) an agent-generated task decomposition preserves
optimal substructure and exposes subproblems that dynamic programming can admit; (ii) equilibrium-seeking
symbolic agents can be remapped onto distributed optimization problems returning feasibility certificates;
(iii) these methods extend to the non-equilibrium problems where participants located the scientific frontier;
and (iv) temporal logic constraints and control barrier functions can certify a language-generated plan before
it executes.
What is under-invested. High-performance ensemble methods were named first and most often, because
verifying an AI-produced result requires large-scale statistics that cannot be assembled without them and
because many established verification methods are dimensionally challenged at the scales autonomous
campaigns now reach. Optimization capability at the national laboratories was described as strong but
classical and continuous, with integer, mixed-integer, and stochastic formulations identified as the gap,
and statistical physics, including non-Gaussian and nonlinear statistics, was named as the route into the
non-equilibrium problems where participants located the scientific frontier. Data reduction is not free in an
information-theoretic sense, because recovering a quantity of a given order requires transmitting information
of the corresponding order, so every reduction scheme trades something away and the trade should be stated
instead of assumed. Statistical problems also resist edge decomposition, because a statistical statement
depends on the totality of the sample and the computation cannot be cleanly isolated to one node, which
participants offered as a well-posed and currently unaddressed opportunity.
 One failure mode was described in enough detail to serve as a requirement for X2. Participants warned
that federated learning across laboratories, which the roadmap treats as an enabler of M6 and M9, may
contribute nothing when the datasets held at different sites do not overlap. The more precise statement
is that heterogeneity can make naive aggregation harmful, and that the value of collaboration depends on
shared structure, measurement comparability, and method, not on overlap alone [30, 31]. Evaluations should
therefore compare against site-local baselines, test each participating site and any claimed use at a new site,
and report negative transfer. Participants also observed that agentic systems are bypassing the long-established
discipline of optimal experimental design. That discipline carries assumptions of its own, since Bayesian
optimization presumes a degree of smoothness that many scientific landscapes lack [32], and composition
space in materials is punctuated by line compounds that are discontinuous by construction. The requirement
is therefore to state what a method assumes and to test that assumption against the landscape before either an
agent or an established method is trusted to choose the next experiment.
Workforce. The workforce findings concern judgment, not tooling. Students now take machine learning
courses and not experimental design or statistics. Participants reported that statisticians feel displaced by
that shift, and that the researchers who function best in autonomous science are those who have worked on
both sides. User facilities hold the largest concentration of people who pay careful attention to whether an

 13

experiment is well designed, so losing that expertise would be difficult to reverse. Human factors engineers
should join projects well before autonomy is the goal, on the reported practice from autonomous reactor
operations of bringing them in two projects ahead, so that concepts of operations are designed with human
constraints in view. The empirical backdrop is that AI-engaged researchers publish more and are cited more
even as the range of topics the community studies contracts [33], so a workforce agenda producing fluency
without judgment would accelerate that contraction.

 Insights/Recommendations: Adopt as a design principle that agents generate and update the
 mathematical problem while established methods solve it and certify the solution, with a verification
 layer between the agent and any actuator. Fund high-performance ensemble methods, integer and
 stochastic optimization, and non-equilibrium statistics as prerequisites for verifying autonomous
 results at scale, and fund instrument-aware digital twins, which address X1 and M14 with one artifact.
 Require that established optimal experimental design be tried first, with its smoothness and continuity
 assumptions checked against the landscape, before an agent is used to select experiments. Restate
 M13 around team composition, statistical judgment, and honest reporting of failure.

7 Cross-Cutting Gaps and Candidate Milestones
 Four concerns surfaced independently under more than one topic area and map onto no existing milestone,
and Table 2 collects the full set of candidate milestones.
Failure records need shared scientific semantics. The community routinely says that unsuccessful ex-
periments are valuable training data, and participants pointed out that failure is rarely recorded in a form
a machine can learn from. Existing domain schemas and failed-experiment datasets provide a starting
point, since reaction databases already represent experimental conditions, analyses, and outcomes [34],
and published work has used failed syntheses to train a model that predicted reaction success better than
experienced chemists [35], although participants noted that it drew on an unusually controlled experimental
series. The cross-domain task is to distinguish execution disposition, measurement validity, and scientific
interpretation. A valid low-yield result, a failed reagent delivery, and a missing analytical readout warrant
different conclusions, and a negative finding can be informative without being an operational failure. A useful
record therefore preserves the procedure, deviations, quality controls, detection or censoring limits, and the
basis on which an outcome was used in learning or decisions.
Traceability stops short of the scientific claim. Provenance establishes what happened and traceability
establishes an unbroken chain back to a source, and neither establishes that anyone stands behind the chain.
The analogy used comes from materials qualification, where a certified alloy carries physical testimony
that it is what it is claimed to be. The answer offered when the digital equivalent was proposed is that
provenance and traceability are achievable now and that the missing element is a trust authority, with the
speaker explicitly declining to be one. No candidate institution was identified in the room. Measurement
traceability nonetheless has existing foundations, namely documented calibration chains to stated references
with uncertainty [36], supported where applicable by national metrology institutes and accredited laboratories,
and digital calibration certificates provide a machine-readable starting point [37]. The task is to link these
scoped measurement claims to sample identity, processing provenance, and the result being used. Neither
calibration nor a digital signature establishes fitness for a particular decision or validates a scientific inference,

 14

so those claims need their own evidence and accountable assessment, and it is there that the vacancy the room
identified remains.
Real-time control and agent governance run on incompatible clocks. Acquisition and control decisions at
the instrument occur on microsecond to millisecond timescales, and no agent-mediated authorization can
participate at that rate. Policy arriving later must nonetheless constrain what happens next and reconcile
actions already admitted under an authority since revoked, and the roadmap treats edge processing, agent
protocols, and zero-trust coordination as separate concerns without defining the contract between them.
Nobody carries a milestone forward. The pattern noted in Section 2 is a gap in its own right, since the
community has no entity with a mandate, a budget, and staff to hold a standard, run a plugfest, or maintain a
benchmark, and the industrial robotics consortium participants cited repeatedly succeeded precisely because
it had those things [23].
 One item is recorded without a proposed milestone, because it requires a decision by bodies outside this
community. Institutional information technology policy and operational technology practice are in direct
conflict for cyber-physical research, as described in Section 5, and the resolution is a policy question, not a
technical one.

8 Investment Agenda
 Participants were asked what they would fund with $10M immediately and $100M over five years, which
milestone it would close, and what applied mathematics sits inside it. The proposals cluster into four groups,
ordered here by how directly each unblocks other work.
An instrument automation layer. The largest single proposal addresses M1 in two parts. The software
part builds shims from one common logical interface to every instrument interface in scope, following the
automated-vehicle precedent of Section 3. A proof of concept scoped to one laboratory or instrument class
was estimated at roughly $1M, with a full effort in the range of $10M to $25M once safety analysis, integration
of new instruments, and ongoing maintenance are included. The physical part addresses instruments that
expose no logical interface and can only be driven by pressing buttons, opening drawers, and reading screens,
and proposes mobile manipulators on wheeled platforms, gantries, or rails that mimic human input. A proof
of concept here was estimated at roughly $3M with a comparable full scale. Participants placed it in the
second year, on the reasoning that the software route reaches further, faster, and more cheaply and should
start first, while acknowledging that it has hard limits the physical route does not.
Testbeds and the machinery of collective action. Three proposals are institutional and not technical, and
Section 7 records why the community has been unable to execute them. The first is a distributed instrument
and robotics testbed holding representative equipment for continuous interoperability testing, on the plugfest
model from connected-vehicle standards work, adapted to instruments that cannot be moved. The second is a
consortium for pre-competitive work on autonomous laboratory interfaces, which participants noted may not
require a new organization at all, since broad participation in the existing industrial robotics consortium could
serve. The third is safety and regulatory testbed work funded so that evaluators and regulators participate
from the start, on the precedent of Section 5.
Verification and benchmarking infrastructure. Two proposals follow from the assessment of M15 and M16.
The first is a shared grand challenge built on a common accessible experimental platform, on the model of
the long-running robotics competitions, so that results compare directly across groups instead of each group
benchmarking against its own apparatus. The second is cross-framework provenance capture deployed as
facility infrastructure instead of as a research prototype, justified by the three silent failures of Section 4.
The applied mathematics program. The proposals from Section 6 constitute a coherent program that no
current milestone funds. High-performance ensemble methods are the enabling investment, because verifying
autonomous results at scale requires statistics that cannot otherwise be assembled. Instrument-aware digital
twins were the highest-leverage item, because one artifact serves campaign rehearsal and hands-on training
and therefore addresses X1 and M14 together. Participants noted that an offer to build such a twin for a large
facility had previously found no route to adoption despite interest, which is a coordination failure and not a
technical one.
What participants asked of funding agencies. Three inexpensive requests were directed at program offices.
The first is visibility, since awards under a major national initiative are published as a document listing
an investigator and a title, and participants asked instead for a searchable catalog of funded work with
demonstrations. The second is model and data sharing, since laboratories are independently building models
for similar tasks and few-shot adaptation of a shared foundation model is far cheaper than retraining, picking
up a glass and picking up a beaker not being different in kind. The third is speed of engagement with industry,
because arrangements between national laboratories and commercial partners currently take months, which
participants described as a long time in this field.

9 Conclusion
 In this report, we have presented the community’s assessment of the two-year autonomous science
roadmap [12], produced by a workshop convened to write such a roadmap that instead found itself answering
one. We have reported where practice stands against M1 through M18, proposed ten candidate milestones,
and set out an investment agenda.
 The pattern across the four clusters is consistent with the roadmap’s own reading and sharpens it in
one respect. The milestones that depend on model capability have advanced quickly, and those that depend
on someone else’s cooperation have not, whether that someone is an instrument manufacturer with no
commercial reason to expose an interface or a regulator with no framework for evaluating an autonomous
system. The roadmap describes this as a coordination problem, and our assessment is that it is an ownership
problem, since no organization holds the mandate, the budget, and the staff to carry the proposals forward.
 Three findings are worth carrying forward independently of the milestone structure. Data and interfaces
cross the laboratory boundary while decision-relevant state does not, so the federation problem sits in the
laboratory itself and not in the network. Cross-framework provenance has already found silent errors in three
deployed agentic workflows, which makes verification infrastructure a precondition for orchestration and not
a companion to it. Agents should generate and update the mathematical problem while established methods
solve it and certify the solution, giving the capability-reliability gap an architectural response and not only a
research agenda.
 In future work, we plan to carry the candidate milestones of Table 2 into the next revision of the roadmap,
to assemble the application case studies that would replace single-valued milestone status, and to determine
who can hold the interoperability, benchmarking, and certification functions this assessment found unowned.
