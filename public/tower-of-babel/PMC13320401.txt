A dataset of Vietnamese engineering students’ perceptions of career readiness
Authors: Hoc Le-Hieu, Hai Pham-Thi-Thanh, Hoai Le-Thu, Dinh-Hai Luong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe engineering workforce plays a crucial role in the context of digital transformation and Industry 4.0. The career readiness of engineering students impacts the quality of the future high-skilled workforce. The dataset was collected to depict the current status of career readiness and related factors among engineering students in Vietnam. Data were gathered through an online survey from December 23, 2025, to January 12, 2026. The participants are engineering students enrolled in higher education institutions in Vietnam. The selection of students was based on a convenience sampling method, and participation was voluntary. A total of 439 valid records were collected and stored in the Mendeley Data repository [1]. The dataset is composed of 49 items covering demographic information (6 items), active learning strategies (8 items), student engagement (12 items), self-efficacy (5 items), skill application (8 items), and career readiness (11 items). This dataset can help higher education institutions fit their engineering curricula to improve career readiness and related factors among students, addressing labor market demands, while also establishing an active learning environment to encourage and motivate learners to enhance their career readiness and related factors.Keywords: Vietnam, Self-efficacy, Transformational skills, 21st century skills, Career competence

Background

In the era of the Fourth Industrial Revolution and global integration, the labor market increasingly demands a workforce equipped with both strong technical expertise and a broad set of transferable skills, including communication, teamwork, critical thinking, problem-solving, and adaptability [2,3]. This reality places growing pressure on higher education institutions - particularly in engineering disciplines - to reform instructional methods in ways that better prepare students for the complexities of modern workplaces.

In this context, traditional lecture-driven methods have been increasingly critiqued as insufficient to meet the complex learning needs of engineering students [4]. Conversely, active learning methods encourage deeper engagement, promote critical thinking, simulate real engineering practices, and develop career professionalism [5]. Meanwhile, career readiness encompasses soft skills, adaptability, and the ability to navigate complex, multidisciplinary environments. This is a critical outcome for engineering education, economic competitiveness, and individual student success [6].

However, numerous factors continue to influence the career readiness of engineering students, including barriers specific to certain engineering fields [7], attitudes toward engineering careers [8], and self-efficacy and a sense of belonging [9]. This not only suggests the need for research on career readiness and the factors shaping it among engineering students, but also underscores the importance of establishing an active learning environment that meets students' learning needs.

Data Description

This paper describes a dataset that assesses career readiness and related factors based on students’ perceptions from higher education institutions in Vietnam. The dataset is stored in the Mendeley Data Repository in Microsoft Excel format (see [1]).

In the file named “dataset.xlsx,” there are two sheets: the “data” sheet contains the survey data, and the “items” sheet provides descriptions of the variables. The “items” sheet lists 49 variables in three columns: “Variable” stores the variable name, “Description” describes the variable information, and “Coding” presents the variable values.

The “data” sheet stores 439 valid records, with the header row listing 49 variable names, corresponding to 49 columns. A1-A6 variables describe the demographic characteristics of engineering students in Vietnam, including Gender (with values Male/Female), Year of Study (1/2/3/4), GPA (categorized as: 0 - 1.49/1.5 - 1.99/2.0 - 2.49/2.5 - 3.19/3.2 - 3.49/3.5 - 4.0), Participation in internship or project-based work (No/Yes); Discipline (list of engineering fields: Biological Sciences - Food Science and Technology/Chemical Engineering - Metallurgy - Environmental Engineering/Civil Engineering - Transportation Engineering - Logistics/Computer Science and Information Technology/Economics and Business/Electrical Engineering - Electronics - Automation - Telecommunications - Biomedical Engineering/Languages and Foreign Languages/Mechanical Engineering - Mechatronics - Manufacturing Engineering - Naval Architecture and Marine Engineering - Automotive Engineering - Thermal Engineering/Pharmacy/Physics and Materials Science/Textile and Garment - Leather Technology/Others). Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The dataset was collected through an online survey conducted with engineering students at universities in Vietnam. The dataset is structured into three parts. Part 1 introduces the research title, the purpose of the study, survey instructions, a statement regarding the confidentiality of personal information, and the contact information for the research team. Part 2 includes 6 questions to gather demographic information from the participants (see Table 1). Part 3 is designed to collect opinions on career readiness and related factors for engineering students (see Table 2). The survey was designed using Google Forms to collect participants' evaluations.

The demographic information of the participants includes (i) gender (options: Male/Female/I do not wish to share this information, coded 1/2/3), (ii) year of study (1st year/2nd year/3rd year/4th year, coded 1/2/3/4), (iii) field of study (Biological Sciences - Food Science and Technology/Chemical Engineering - Metallurgy - Environmental Engineering/Civil Engineering - Transp ortation Engineering - Logistics/Computer Science and Information Technology/Economics and Business/Electrical Engineering - Electronics - Automation - Telecommunications - Biomedical Engineering/Languages and Foreign Languages/Mechanical Engineering - Mechatronics - Manufacturing Engineering - Naval Architecture and Marine Engineering - Automotive Engineering - Thermal Engineering/Pharmacy/Physics and Materials Science/Textile and Garment - Leather Technology/Others), (iv) current university attended, (v) GPA (0 −1.5; 1.5–1.99; 2.00–2.49; 2.50–3.19; 3.20–3.49; 3.50–4.0), and (vi) participation in internships or practical projects (Yes/No, coded 1/0).

The perceptions of engineering students on career readiness and related factors were collected using a 5-point Likert scale (1: Strongly Disagree; 2: Disagree; 3: Neutral; 4: Agree; 5: Strongly Agree, coded 1;2;3;4;5). This section includes 49 items adapted from various sources (see Table 3).

The Active Learning Strategy scale (8 items, ALS1-ALS8) was adapted from [10], used to assess students' opinions on the active learning strategies employed during their studies. Students sponsed each items, including (i) the lecturers frequently use problem- or project-based learning activities (ASL1 item), (ii) I regularly work in pairs or small groups to solve real-world problems (ALS2), (iii) Class sessions encourage students to discuss and share ideas (ALS3), (iv) My lecturers use digital technologies to support active learning (ALS4), (v) I am encouraged to reflect on what I learn and how I learn (ALS5), (vi) I receive constructive feedback that helps improve my learning outcomes (ALS6), (vii) My lecturers design tasks that connect theory to real-world or industry contexts (ALS7), and (viii) My lecturers give me autonomy in my learning (ALS8).

The Student Engagement scale (12 items) was adapted from [6], used to gather feedback on three subscales: Social and Emotional Engagement (4 items, SEA1-SEA4), Behavioral Engagement (4 items, SEB1 – SEB4), and Cognitive Engagement (4 items, SEC1 – SEC4). Social and Emotional Engagement subscale has 4 items, including (i) I enjoy participating in active-learning classes (SEA1), (ii) I feel motivated and interested in learning new things (SEA2), (iii) I feel a sense of belonging and connection to my class (SEA3), and (iv) I feel confident expressing my opinions in class (SEA4). Behavioral Engagement subscale has 4 items, including (i) I attend class regularly and actively participate in class activities (SEB1), (ii) I always complete assignments and projects on time (SEB2), (iii) I regularly take part in group discussions and teamwork activities (SEB3), and (iv) I ask questions and seek clarification during or after class (SEB4).

Cognitive Engagement subscale has 4 items, including (i) I try to connect what I learn in class with prior knowledge (SEC1), (ii) I take time to understand complex ideas or problems (SEC2), (iii) I use critical thinking to analyze and evaluate information (SEC3), and (iv) I seek additional resources to deepen my understanding (SEC4).

The Self-Efficacy subscale (5 items, D1-D5) was adapted from [11], used to assess students' confidence in their learning process. This scale has 5 statements, including (i) I can learn and understand difficult engineering concepts (D1), (ii) I am confident in solving unfamiliar problems independently (D2), (iii) I feel ready to take on professional engineering tasks (D3), (iv) I believe I can adapt to different work or learning situations (D4), and (v) I trust in my ability to perform well in job interviews or internships (D5).

The Skill Application scale (8 items, E1-E8) was adapted from [12], collected feedback on the application of knowledge and skills in both academic and practical contexts. The scale consists of 8 statements: (i) I can apply theoretical knowledge to real-world problems (E1), (ii) I have integrated various knowledge areas to complete assignments (E2), (iii) I have used classroom knowledge in real or simulated projects (E3), (iv) I confidently apply problem-solving strategies in new situations (E4), (v) I am able to justify decisions based on analysis and evidence (E5), (vi) I understand how to use my technical skills in workplace settings (E6), (vii) I can transfer skills learned in class to different contexts (E7), and (viii) I can connect what I’ve learned to current trends in engineering (E8).

The Career Readiness scale (11 items, CR1-CR11) was adapted from [6,13], gatherred feedback on students' readiness for the workforce. The scale has 11 statements, including (i) I can communicate my ideas clearly in writing and speech (CR1), (ii) I can work effectively in teams and respect different perspectives (CR2), (iii) I can analyze problems and propose creative, practical solutions (CR3), (iv) I can apply disciplinary knowledge to real workplace situations (CR4), (v) I can manage my time and prioritize tasks effectively (CR5), (vi) I am confident in using digital tools and technologies for learning or work (CR6), (vii) I have a clear understanding of career options in my field (CR7), (viii) I can present myself professionally (e.g., CV, interviews, networking) (CR8), (ix) I demonstrate creative thinking and openness to innovation and new opportunities (CR9), (x) I can apply what I have learned to solve real-world or community problems (CR10), and (xi) I believe my current learning experiences are preparing me for future work (CR11).

Convenience sampling was used in this study. The authors contacted coordinators at engineering universities in Vietnam, who then distributed the online questionnaire. Prior to participating in the survey, students were provided with information about the topic, the purpose of the survey, and the voluntariness of their participation. The data collection process was conducted from December 23, 2025, to January 12, 2026. A total of 463 survey responses were collected.

The dataset was reviewed to assess its validity. The authors calculated the number of missing values for each record and variable. Twenty-four records with >15% missing values were removed from the dataset, following the guidelines of [14]. The same process was applied to each variable, with no variable being excluded for this reason. Finally, the remaining dataset consists of 439 valid records. The dataset is stored in the Mendeley Data Repository (referred to [1]).

Limitations

Data were collected using a convenient sampling method; therefore, the dataset does not fully represent the perceptions of engineering students according to their demographic characteristics.
