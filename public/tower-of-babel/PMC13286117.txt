Tranexamic Acid-Induced Seizure After Cardiopulmonary Bypass: A Case Report
Authors: Kaiki Oya, Jun Maki, Midoriko Higashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tranexamic acid (TXA) is a risk factor for perioperative seizures following cardiac surgery. However, its clinical diagnosis is difficult because these signs are masked by anesthetics and muscle relaxants. In this case, a direct temporal association between high-dose TXA administration and seizure onset was confirmed using intraoperative electroencephalography (EEG) and central venous oxygen saturation (ScvO2) changes.This case demonstrates that seizures can be triggered immediately after high-dose TXA administration and highlights the importance of integrated assessment of continuous EEG and ScvO2 for the early detection of subclinical seizures. This allows for prompt intervention and may lead to better neurological outcomes.

Tranexamic acid (TXA), an antifibrinolytic agent with a lysine-like structure that binds to the lysine-binding sites of plasminogen,1 is widely used in cardiovascular surgery to reduce bleeding and transfusion requirements.2 However, TXA is a known risk factor for perioperative seizures, and this risk is strongly dose-dependent, particularly in cardiac surgery.3,4 Other independent risk factors include advanced age, female sex, preoperative renal dysfunction, severe aortic calcification or atherosclerosis, poor preoperative systemic conditions, open-chamber surgery, prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time, and the use of deep hypothermic circulatory arrest.4–6

The in-hospital mortality rate for patients experiencing seizures is approximately five times higher.5 However, clinical diagnosis during the intraoperative or early postoperative period is often challenging, as general anesthetics, sedatives, and muscle relaxants mask typical manifestations. Consequently, perioperative seizures, which occur in approximately 1% of cardiac surgeries, are generally recognized as a postoperative complication becoming apparent in the intensive care unit when sedation is tapered, with a median onset time of 7 hours post-surgery.4 This time lag in clinical recognition may worsen prognosis and complicate determining the exact onset time and direct causality to TXA.

We present a case in which clinical convulsions occurred immediately after post-CPB TXA administration. This event was continuously documented via intraoperative electroencephalogram (EEG) alongside characteristic hemodynamic and oxygenation changes. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication. This manuscript adheres to the applicable EQUATOR guideline (CARE).

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 44-year-old man (173 cm, 65 kg) underwent mitral valve repair via a minimally invasive cardiac surgical approach for severe mitral regurgitation. He was classified as New York Heart Association functional class I. Electrocardiography was unremarkable. Echocardiography revealed posterior mitral leaflet prolapse, with a left atrial diameter of 43 mm. He had no notable comorbidities or current medications, and routine blood tests were normal. He had a single episode of febrile seizure in childhood with no history of treatment, and neurological and head CT examinations were normal.

General anesthesia was induced with propofol (120 mg), fentanyl (200 µg), and rocuronium (70 mg), and maintained with fentanyl (total 800 µg), remifentanil (0.1–0.3 µg·kg−1·min−1), propofol (3 mg·kg−1·h−1), and/or sevoflurane (1–3%). Alongside standard monitoring, we used an invasive arterial pressure monitoring system (FloTrac/Vigileo System, Edwards Lifesciences), central venous pressure, central venous oxygen saturation (ScvO2) with an Oximetry CV Catheter (Edwards Lifesciences), regional oxygen saturation (rSO2) with O3 (Masimo Corporation), and brain function monitoring (Patient State Index; PSI) with SedLine (Masimo Corporation).

Mitral valve repair was performed under moderate hypothermia (33°C). Anesthesia during CPB was maintained with remifentanil (0.3 µg·kg−1·min−1) and propofol (3 mg·kg−1·h−1). This continuous administration of remifentanil during CPB is part of our standard institutional protocol. The aortic cross-clamp and CPB times were 80 and 129 minutes, respectively. TXA (3000 mg) was administered immediately before CPB initiation and another 3000 mg four minutes after successful CPB weaning. Thirteen minutes after CPB termination, generalized convulsions with prominent muscle rigidity occurred. The train-of-four (TOF) count was 1. Convulsions (duration: 1 minute) were promptly terminated with midazolam (5 mg). Subsequently, propofol infusion (increased to 5 mg·kg−1·h−1) and levetiracetam (1000 mg) were administered, preventing further convulsions.

During the convulsive episode, heart rate (88–116 bpm), blood pressure (76/40–100/54 mm Hg), cardiac output (5.8–7.9 L/min), and bilateral rSO2 (left: 72%–77%; right: 55%–59%) increased, while ScvO2 (82%–65%) decreased. Cardiac function on transesophageal echocardiography remained unchanged. The neuromonitoring data before and after the onset of convulsions are shown in Figure 1. PSI became undetectable 7 minutes before the convulsions (2 minutes post-TXA), with the last measurable value of 26. After termination of the convulsions, PSI recovered to 92.

The patient was transferred to the ICU under propofol sedation. Post-admission head CT and 10 to 20 system EEG showed no abnormalities. Sedatives were tapered on postoperative day 1, and continuous 10-20 EEG confirmed the absence of abnormal brain waves. He was extubated upon awakening and exhibited no neurological deficits. Levetiracetam (1000 mg/day) was continued until day 2. Awake EEG (day 12) and head CT (day 13) remained normal. He was discharged on day 20.

DISCUSSION

TXA induces seizures by inhibiting inhibitory neurotransmission via its antagonistic effect on the γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptor and the inhibition of glycine receptors.6 High cumulative TXA doses, particularly exceeding 80 mg/kg, are strong independent predictors of postoperative convulsive seizures.4 Additionally, altered blood-brain barrier permeability during CPB facilitates higher cerebrospinal fluid drug concentrations, exacerbating the risk with high cumulative dosing.6 Consequently, contemporary cardiac anesthesia protocols favor lower dosing strategies (eg, 10–30 mg/kg loading dose followed by low-dose infusion) to balance antifibrinolytic efficacy with neurotoxicity risk.7,8 However, universally established standardized regimens remain elusive, with various regimens proposed based on clinical trials, dose adjustments using point-of-care fibrinolysis testing,9 and pharmacokinetic model-based approaches.10

The rationale for the high dosing in our case stems from a historical institutional protocol, driven by surgical requests to maximize hemostasis. This protocol involved administering approximately 50 mg/kg of TXA pre-CPB, followed by another 50 mg/kg immediately post-CPB to rapidly counteract CPB-induced hemodilution and fibrinolysis. Consequently, the patient received 3000 mg prior to CPB and 3000 mg immediately after (total 6000 mg, approximately 92 mg/kg), exceeding the recognized >80 mg/kg seizure risk threshold. Owing to reduced metabolic rates during hypothermic CPB, pre-CPB TXA administration may have persisted longer than anticipated. Furthermore, the short CPB duration in this case likely contributed to the accumulation of the drug. Therefore, the intraoperative seizure onset observed in our case is highly likely the result of the second bolus administration further accelerating the accumulation of TXA in the central nervous system, pushing it beyond the seizure-inducing threshold, in a state where blood and tissue concentrations of TXA were already high due to the pre-CPB high-dose administration.

However, attributing the seizure solely to TXA requires caution, as perioperative seizure etiology is multifactorial. The patient’s history of a single childhood febrile seizure may have inherently lowered his seizure threshold. Additionally, certain anesthetics (eg, sevoflurane, propofol) can paradoxically modulate cortical excitability, and lower the seizure threshold in specific clinical contexts.11,12 Finally, transient cerebral microembolism, such as air emboli during the deairing process and CPB weaning, cannot be definitively excluded as a contributing factor,13 as the day 13 head CT may not detect early transient emboli.

Early detection and treatment of seizures during the intraoperative period are important for the outcome. However, clinical diagnosis is challenging because general anesthetics, sedatives, and muscle relaxants mask typical manifestations, making neuromonitoring essential. A review of the raw EEG waveforms from the SedLine monitor revealed a normal pattern upon weaning from CPB (Figure 2A). However, 1 minute after the second TXA administration, the first spike wave appeared (Figure 2B), increasing in frequency after four minutes and transitioning into a sustained rhythmic discharge (status epilepticus pattern) (Figure 2C). Coinciding with this electrographic seizure activity, a prominent generalized tonic convulsion occurred despite the administration of a muscle relaxant (TOF = 1). The EEG pattern normalized after the continuous intraoperative administration of levetiracetam; however, sporadic spike waves remained (Figure 2D). The PSI became unmeasurable for approximately 8 minutes, from 2 minutes after TXA administration, until the seizure was terminated with midazolam. This was likely due to the high-amplitude, rhythmic brain activity associated with the seizure interfering with PSI analysis, suggesting the importance of checking raw EEG waveforms when PSI is unmeasurable.

In addition to EEG monitoring, continuous hemodynamic and oxygenation monitoring provided critical insights. Reflecting the activation of the sympathetic nervous system associated with seizure onset, we observed an increase in heart rate (88 to 116 bpm), arterial pressure (76/40 to 100/54 mm Hg), and cardiac output (5.8 to 7.9 L/min). Simultaneously, reflecting increased regional blood flow due to abnormal cerebral hyperactivity, rSO2 also increased by approximately 5% bilaterally. Interestingly, despite the increase in systemic oxygen delivery, ScvO2 decreased sharply from 82% to 65%. During this event, the patient exhibited prominent generalized muscle rigidity, which significantly increased systemic oxygen demand. Therefore, this rapid decrease in ScvO2 likely reflects a global mismatch between oxygen delivery and a sudden surge in whole-body metabolic consumption, including both intense muscular activity and cerebral hyperactivity. Consequently, these abrupt changes in global oxygenation and hemodynamics served as an early indicator of seizure onset.

In recent years, the understanding of the pathophysiology of non-convulsive status epilepticus has advanced, with a reported incidence of 0.8% after cardiac surgery.14 Early postoperative EEG monitoring has been reported to detect abnormal patterns and electrical seizures at a high frequency,15 suggesting that early detection and prompt intervention through EEG monitoring may be crucial for preventing secondary brain injuries. To the best of our knowledge, there are few reports that have captured detailed EEG changes from TXA administration to seizure onset, along with corresponding clinical symptoms and vital sign changes, in a continuous manner. This case provides valuable information for understanding the mechanisms and early detection of TXA-induced seizures. The integrated evaluation of perioperative EEG monitoring with circulatory and oxygenation dynamics may enable early detection and rapid intervention for perioperative seizures, including those induced by TXA.
