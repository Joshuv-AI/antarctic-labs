Filtered Posterior Mean Collections: A Unified Framework for Analytical Models of Diffusion Generalization
Matthew Niedoba mniedoba@cs.ubc.ca, Berend Zwartsenberg berend.zwartsenberg@inverted.ai, Frank Wood fwood@cs.ubc.ca, [1em] \AFFS University of British Columbia, Inverted AI, Alberta Machine Intelligence Institute

Abstract
The neural-network denoising functions which form the backbone of image diffusion models are remarkably consistent in their generalization behaviour across a wide variety of network architectures and training procedure hyperparameters. A recent line of research has sought to model the outputs of these networks by aggregating posterior weighted averages of training dataset patches. In this work, we consolidate these approaches into a unified model class which we call Filtered Posterior Mean Collections (FPMCs). We define this model class using query precision vectors, response weights, and source distributions, and illustrate that existing methods are recoverable with specific choices of these design axes. Investigating each axis in turn, we find that FPMC performance can be improved with soft relaxations of prior patch-based methods, and through augmentations of source distributions. Applying these findings to an existing FPMC, we demonstrate consistent sample improvement across three natural image datasets.

Introduction

Diffusion models
[
24
,
8
,
26
]
are a powerful class of generative models, especially in the domain of visual data where they have been widely adopted for image
[

[

[

[

The generalization ability of diffusion models therefore implies that network denoisers learn biased approximations of the optimal denoiser
[
32
,
25
]
. In the image domain, this bias is remarkably consistent across a wide variety of hyperparameters
[
34
]
. This observation has led to two complementary lines of research. The first of these seeks to understand the inductive biases responsible for denoiser convergence
[
9
,
20
,
10
,
1

27

[
23
,
30
,
19
,
16
,
10
,
18
]

Among analytical approaches, several methods have sought to approximate denoiser bias through posterior weighted averages of training dataset patches. These include
20
who propose patches based on average network gradients,
10
who utilize square patches and equivariant groups based on the inductive biases of convolutional networks, and
18
who derive patches from the eigendecomposition of the data covariance. Together, these methods represent the most accurate analytical approximations of network denoiser behaviour currently available. However, the design space of these methods lacks clarity, providing no clear directions for model improvement.

In this work, we directly address this limitation by consolidating prior approaches into a unified model class which we call filtered posterior mean collections (FPMCs). Our framework defines FPMCs using sequences of query precisions
\mathcal{Q}
which filter the posterior means, response weights
\mathcal{R}
which control aggregation, and source distributions
\mathcal{V}
which vary the support of the averaging. Together, these three principal axes differentiate prior methodologies, and form the foundation for systematic improvement of model performance.

We investigate the FPMC design axes in turn, in each case identifying and relaxing assumptions made by prior methodologies. Specifically, we improve FPMC denoisers by learning soft relaxations of prior patch-based methodologies, and through data augmentation strategies of their source distributions. When applied together, these modifications consistently improve the FPMC sample similarity versus diffusion model outputs on three natural image datasets. Our contributions are summarized as follows:

Background

The basis of diffusion models as well as many flow-based generative models is a stochastic forward process which gradually perturbs a base density
p(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with additive Gaussian noise. In this work, we consider
C
-channel image data of width
W
and height
H
, corresponding to
d=W\cdot H\cdot C
. Diffusion processes can be defined by a stochastic differential equation (SDE) using drift function
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},t)
and diffusion coefficient
g(t)
of the form

d\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},t)dt+g(t)d\mathbf{w}.

(1)

Integration of
eq.

1
from initial values
(\mathbf{z},t)=(\mathbf{x},0)
to any
t\in(0,T]
induces a conditional Gaussian distribution
p_{t}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}\right)
. Through marginalization, one can obtain time-dependent distributions
p_{t}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)=\int p_{t}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}\right)p(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}
with
p_{0}(\mathbf{z})=p(\mathbf{x})
. Although,
\mathbf{f}
can be an arbitrary function, it is convenient to select affine
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},t)=f(t)\mathbf{z}
, which results in
p_{t}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}\right)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z};\alpha(t),\sigma(t)^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
with closed form
\alpha(t)
and
\sigma(t)

[
15
]
. Typically
f(t)
and
g(t)
are chosen such that
p_{T}(\mathbf{z})\approx\pi\left(\mathbf{z}\right)
, a tractable Gaussian prior distribution
1
1

1

Unless otherwise noted, we adopt the choices of
[
11
]
selecting
f(t)=0
,
g(t)=\sqrt{2t}
, resulting in
\alpha(t)=1,\sigma(t)=t
.
.

Diffusion models aim to learn a process which reverses
eq.

1
. While this can be achieved through a corresponding reverse-time SDE, we will instead focus on the unique deterministic process

\frac{d\mathbf{z}}{dt}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},t)-\frac{1}{2}g(t)^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{z})

(2)

whose marginal distributions also match those of
eq.

1
for all
t\in(0,T]
. By leveraging this deterministic probability-flow differential equation (PF-ODE), diffusion models can generate samples by solving the initial value problem for
t=0
with initial conditions
\mathbf{z}\sim\pi(\mathbf{z})
and
t=T
via numerical integration techniques.

Critically, integration of
eq.

2
relies on the score function
\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{z})
. Diffusion models approximate this function using deep neural networks. One common method of approximating the score is through a denoising network
D_{\theta}
, optimized on the objective

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\sim p_{t}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}\right)p(\mathbf{x})\\
t\sim p(t)\end{subarray}}\left[\lambda(t)\left\lVert D_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},t)-\mathbf{x}\right\lVert_{2}^{2}\right].

(3)

The denoiser is trained jointly over
t\in(0,T]
distributed according to
p(t)
and weighted via hyperparameter
\lambda(t)
. From this denoising model, the score can be estimated using Tweedie’s formula

\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\log p_{t}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)=\frac{\alpha(t)\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z},t\right]-\mathbf{z}}{\sigma(t)^{2}}\approx\frac{\alpha(t)D_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},t)-\mathbf{z}}{\sigma(t)^{2}}

(4)

Remarkably, independent image diffusion models, when trained on the same data distribution, will often produce near-identical samples via
eq.

2

. This behaviour is robust across model architectures, training procedures, and numerical samplers

parameterized by the generalization behaviour of denoiser neural-networks.

To gain insight into this generalization behaviour, analytical denoisers have been proposed to approximate black-box neural-network denoisers. One such analytical denoiser can be derived from

4
which shows that the score is related to the posterior mean
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z},t\right]

\mathcal{D}=\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(N)}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\sim p(\mathbf{x})\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z},t\right]

\displaystyle=\int p_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\ \mathbf{x}\ d\mathbf{x}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{z})\ \mathbf{x}^{(i)},

(5)

\displaystyle p_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{t}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}\right)p_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x})}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}}p_{t}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\right)p_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})}.

(6)

Although this
optimal
denoiser described by
eq.

5
is the minimizer of
eq.

3

[
29
,
11
]
, it is a poor approximator of the behaviour of diffusion models for two reasons. First, its estimates do not match those of neural-network denoisers
[
20
]
, and secondly, because the optimal denoiser is incapable of producing samples outside of the training dataset
[
6
]
. To address these shortcomings, a wide variety of methods propose modifications to the optimal denoiser to better approximate network behaviour
[
23
,
20
,
10
,
18
]
.

Motivated by the diversity of prior methods which leverage posterior mean denoisers, we propose a framework which encompasses these methods and clarifies the common factors among their approaches. In the following section, we define the class of models which we refer to as filtered posterior mean collections (FPMCs).

The foundation of FPMCs are a family of denoiser functions
\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{z},t;q,\nu):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
based on filtered posterior expectations. Functions in this family calculate posterior expectations of the form

\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{z},t;q,\nu)=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\text{supp}(\nu)}\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z};q,\nu)\mathbf{x},

(7)

where the weights of sum are based upon a Gaussian likelihood

\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x};q)\propto\exp\left(\frac{(\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})^{\top}\text{diag}(q)(\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})}{-2\sigma(t)^{2}}\right),

(8)

which, through Bayes’ Rule, induces posterior probabilities

\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z};q,\nu)=\frac{\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x};q)\nu(\mathbf{x})}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\text{supp}(\nu)}\tilde{p_{t}}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}^{\prime};q)\nu(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}.

(9)

FPMC denoiser functions are parameterized by a query vector
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a discrete source probability measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite support
\text{supp}(\nu)
. Intuitively, the query vector
filters

\hat{\mu}
by adjusting the per-dimension importance of differences between
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{x}
. A
q
vector with localized structure attends to local regions in each
\mathbf{x}
, upweighting images which are most similar to
\mathbf{z}
within that region. By contrast, setting
q=\mathbf{I_{d}}
recovers
\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x};q)=p_{t}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}\right)
, which uniformly weights differences between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}
across dimensions. The source measure
\nu
controls the distribution which is filtered. Selecting
\nu=p_{\mathcal{D}}
, along with
q=\mathbf{I}_{d}
results in
\hat{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathcal{D}}}[\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z},t]
.

In practice, FPMCs typically employ a collection of
L
filtered denoisers, each specializing to specific spatial regions of
\mathbf{z}
. We define the contribution of each specialist function to the final output through a response vector
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
which weights the dimensions of
\hat{\mu}
. A filtered posterior mean collection of
L
denoiser functions is characterized by the choice of
q,r,
and
\nu
for each of its component estimators. Summarizing these paramaters into sequences
\mathcal{Q}=(q_{\ell})_{\ell=1}^{L}
,
\mathcal{V}=(\nu_{\ell})_{\ell=1}^{L}
, and
\mathcal{R}=(r_{\ell})_{\ell=1}^{L}
, the output of an FPMC denoiser is given by

D_{\text{FPMC}}(\mathbf{z},t;\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{R})=\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}r_{\ell}\odot\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{z},t;q_{\ell},\nu_{\ell})\right)\oslash\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}r_{\ell}\right),

(10)

where
\odot
and
\oslash
denote element-wise multiplication and division respectively. Prior FPMC methods
[
20
,
10
,
18
]
are defined by their selections of
\mathcal{Q}
,
\mathcal{V}
, and
\mathcal{R}
, which we summarize in

In this section, we investigate the principal design axes of FPMCs, identifying the limitations of prior choices and proposing improvements along each axis.

4.1
Learning Soft
\mathcal{Q}

The choice of
\mathcal{Q}
is the central decision of FPMC design. Prior art has primarily used
\mathcal{Q}
to imbue their FPMCs with a
local
inductive bias – supposing that model outputs for a given pixel should be correlated more strongly to spatially nearby input pixels. While motivations for this inductive bias are grounded in network gradients
[
20
]
, model architectures
[
10
]
, and covariance eigendecomposition
[
18
]
, all choices of
\mathcal{Q}
in prior methods share two key characteristics.

The first shared characteristic is a restriction of
q
to
\{0,1\}^{d}
. This choice corresponds to
\hat{\mu}
which are averaged over
patches
of the dimensions of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As illustrated in
fig.

1
, we argue that this restriction is inconsistent with the locality motivation of prior art as it requires every dimension within a patch to provide an equally weighted contribution to the posterior weights, regardless of spatial proximity.

Secondly, prior art uses manually tuned heuristics to derive the patches in
\mathcal{Q}
. Both
20
and
10
draw inspiration from convolutional receptive fields, selecting
q
corresponding to square image patches. However, hyperparameter tuning is needed to select the patch size
s_{t}
. Even when
\mathcal{Q}
is derived from data as in PSPC-Flex
[
20
]
and
18
, binary
q
vectors require a clipping function and corresponding threshold hyperparameter
\tau_{t}
.

We address the limitations of both characteristics. As presented in
table

1
, we relax the space of
q
from
\{0,1\}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
. Furthermore, we hypothesize that directly optimizing
\mathcal{Q}
against network denoiser functions

D_{\theta}
will lead to better FPMCs than selecting
\mathcal{Q}
through intuition and heuristics. We propose the straightforward optimization objective which mirrors the denoising score matching objective of
eq.

3

\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{R}\right)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\sim p(t)\\
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\sim\sim p_{t}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}\right)p_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}),\end{subarray}}\left[\lambda(t)\left\lVert D_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{z},t\right)-D_{FPMC}\left(\mathbf{z},t;\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{R}\right)\right\lVert_{2}^{2}\right].

(11)

To minimize
eq.

11
with respect to
\mathcal{Q}
the other FPMC design parameters must be selected. Specifically, we must choose response weights
\mathcal{R}
and source distributions
\mathcal{V}
. To test the efficacy of optimizing
\mathcal{Q}
across a variety of
\mathcal{R},
and
\mathcal{V}
choices, we apply our optimization procedure to three prior FPMCs on the CIFAR-10 dataset
[
14
]
. In each case, we adopt
\mathcal{R}
from the base method as specified in
table

1
, and learn independent
\mathcal{Q}
per-

table

1

\mathcal{V}
during training per-batch such that
\nu(\mathbf{x})=0
for the source training batch. We make this change to encourage generalization and avoid the trivial solution in which arbitrarily large precision vectors will collapse
\hat{\mu}
to the source training batch
\mathbf{x}
in the small

4.2
Learning Soft
\mathcal{R}

The purpose of
\mathcal{R}
is to define how the
L
posterior mean estimators of an FPMC are aggregated to form a single output. As with the query precisions, all prior methods constrain their response weights to
r_{\ell}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
. Considering the methodologies of
10
and
18
, this is a natural restriction as both works propose
\mathcal{R}
such that
\sum_{\ell}r_{l}=\mathbf{1}_{d}
. That is, each
\hat{\mu}_{\ell}
estimator directly estimates a disjoint subset of the output dimensions. By contrast,
20
set
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{Q}
, and propose a simple uniform average over all overlapping output means.

We suggest that neither approach is ideal. Partitioning
\mathbb{R}^{d}
through
\mathcal{R}
ignores that estimators with similar query precisions
q_{\ell}
induce similar likelihoods
\tilde{p}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x};q_{\ell})
, and therefore correlated
\hat{\mu}_{\ell}
. Aggregation of such correlated estimators may improve the final estimate. However, uniform aggregation ignores the degree of correlation between estimators, treating all overlapping estimators as equally informative.

We argue that better values of
\mathcal{R}
lie between these choices. As with
\mathcal{Q}
, we propose a relaxation of
r_{\ell}
from
\{0,1\}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}
, and a data-driven selection of
\mathcal{R}
through minimization of
eq.

11
with respect to
\mathcal{R}
. We consider the effect of fine-tuning
\mathcal{R}
from the baseline values described in
table

1
for the same FPMCs considered in
section

4.1
. Mirroring our optimization of
\mathcal{Q}
, we freeze the other FPMC parameters to their baseline
\mathcal{Q}
values and use the modified
\mathcal{V}
discussed in
section

4.1

The effect of fine-tuning soft
\mathcal{R}
, is shown in the bottom row of
fig.

2
. Across all three FPMCs, fine-tuning
\mathcal{R}
reduces FPMC error vs the DDPM++ denoiser. Among FPMCs, the most pronounced improvement is observed for
18
, whose
\mathcal{R}
is comprised of one-hot
r
vectors which prevent any mixing between posterior mean estimates. The observed improvement of
18
supports our claim that averaging related estimators can lead to improved FPMC performance.

4.3
\mathcal{V}
Augmentations

The choice of source image distributions
\mathcal{V}
is the least explored axis of FPMC design. Among prior methods, only the ELS model
[
10
]
does not exclusively use
\nu=p_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x})
. Motivated by the equivariant inductive bias of convolutions, ELS differs from the related LS model solely through the use of per-estimator translation-augmented
\nu
. However, diffusion generalization consistency is not limited to purely convolutional architectures. Similar outputs are also produced by attention-enabled U-Nets and DiTs
[
20
]
, neither of which are translation equivariant due to their use of attention layers
[
28
]
.

Nevertheless, translational transformations are commonly employed in generative model training as a part of a broader class of data augmentation strategies. Such augmentations improve model training by expanding the empirical training distribution
p_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x})
with plausible synthetic images to better approximate the true data generating distribution
p(\mathbf{x})
. We hypothesize that applying similar data augmentation to the source distributions of FPMC will also improve model performance. We therefore investigate augmenting
\mathcal{V}
with other classical augmentation strategies, testing augmentation via translation, scaling, rotations, and reflections across the horizontal and vertical axes. Beyond classical augmentations, we additionally evaluate the impact of augmentation with additional diffusion model samples. Unlike classical augmentation strategies, diffusion samples represent the closest practical approximation to drawing additional i.i.d. samples from
p(\mathbf{x})
.

For our investigation, we generate augmented CIFAR-10
[
14
]
source distributions through the union
\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, where
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is comprised of images produced by a single augmentation strategy. We vary
|\mathcal{D}^{\prime}|
from
20\%
to
2000\%
of
|\mathcal{D}|
, corresponding to between 10,000 and 1,000,000 additional images. For each
|\mathcal{D}^{\prime}|
and corresponding
\nu=p_{\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}
, we evaluate a PSPC-Flex denoiser using 1000 test set images, and
\mathbf{z}\sim p_{t}\left(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}\right)
at three
\sigma(t)
. For each noise level, we report the relative change in FPMC approximation error versus DDPM++, expressed as a percentage of the unaugmented FPMC error.

Figure

3
presents the effect of each augmentation strategy at each noise level. For low
t
, every augmentation method improves FPMC performance. However, as
t
increases, some strategies become less effective, and even detrimental to FPMC performance. We find only horizontal reflection and synthetic augmentations are consistently effective at reducing FPMC error. We speculate that this is related to the growth of PSPC-Flex
\mathcal{Q}
patch size with
t

[
20
]
. At low
t
, the denoising task collapses to estimation of individual training images
[
2
]
. At this scale, small patches may capture plausible local texture, even if the global structure is inconsistent with the target image. However, as patch sizes increase with
t
, globally implausible augmentations become harmful. Horizontal reflections and synthetic samples remain plausible at all scales, explaining their consistent benefit.

Related Work

Diffusion Model Generalization

The denoising objective of
eq.

3
admits the optimal denoiser as its closed-form minimizer
[
11
]
, but this denoiser is incapable of generalization
[
6
]
.
33
demonstrate generalization occurs only when networks fail to memorize, a phenomenon which is driven by model capacity and dataset size
[
33
,
6
]
.
32
demonstrate that generalization emerges from biased estimators of the optimal score, which they attribute to optimization bias.
34
demonstrate that this bias is remarkably consistent across a variety of hyperparameters. A line of research has sought to investigate sources of this bias, such as model architecture
[
10
,
9
]
, training distribution
[
20
,
25
]
, or loss stochasticity
[
27
,
19
,
1
]
.

Analytic Denoisers

Many classical methods aim to recover images corrupted by moderate amounts of Gaussian noise
[
22
,
5
,
3
]
. Of these, the Wiener filter is particularly relevant in the diffusion context. Research has found that these optimal linear denoisers are effective approximators of diffusion models
[
30
,
16
]
, echoing findings that show diffusion generalization can be approximated using shrinkage operations on the eigenspace of network jacobians instead of data covariance matrices
[
9
]
.
18
bridges these findings, connecting average network denoiser gradients and Wiener filters.

A distinct line of work proposes analytical denoisers which directly approximate diffusion model generalization through modifications to the optimal denoiser. One approach smooths the optimal score function to produce a generalizing denoiser
[
23
]
. The focus of this work are methods based on the aggregation of multiple modified posterior means
[
20
,
10
,
18
]
. Our work, unifies these denoisers and elucidates their central design axes.

7
Conclusions and Future Work

The consistent generalization of neural-network image denoiser functions has motivated a growing line of research which aims to analytically approximate network denoisers. Among these, methods which aggregate posterior means over patches of the data have been particularly successful. In this work, we propose a unified filtered posterior mean collection framework which encompasses these fully network-free diffusion approximators. We investigate the design space of the model class, relaxing prior binary choices of
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{R}
through optimization, and identifying beneficial strategies to augment
\mathcal{V}
. When applied jointly, these changes consistently improve FPMC sample similarity across three datasets. More broadly, we argue the systematic strategy employed by this work demonstrates the value of a unified FPMC framework which clearly delineates directions for future research.

Despite the improvements proposed in this work, there remain substantial limitations. Chief among these, there remains a substantial gap between FPMCs and network denoisers, at the level of both denoiser and sample similarity as demonstrated by
fig.

2
and
table

2
respectively. Additionally, FPMCs are substantially less computationally efficient than their network counterparts. Regarding our contributions, although our method improves upon a PSPC-Flex baseline, in most cases, it does not improve MSE on FFHQ-
64\times 64
, and falls short of state of the art for AFHQ
r^{2}
. Moreover, evaluation in this work is performed solely on datasets which generally have a single subject. More complex image content may require further advancements beyond what we have proposed.

The limitations discussed motivate several promising directions for FPMC development. The number of estimators
L
in the FPMC is an implicit, unexplored design axis. Prior methods make widely varying choices of
L
, suggesting that systematic investigation of the optimal collection size may be warranted. In addition, relaxing the structure of the FPMC framework may produce further benefit. From
eq.

8
, current FPMCs have only investigated diagonal query precision matrices. More expressive precision matrices which incorporate important correlations between dimensions may lead to greater improvements. Going further, moving from Gaussian likelihoods to non-linear kernels may better approximate the non-linear behaviour of neural-network parameterized denoisers. Finally, while this work adapts the query precisions and response weights based on
t
, adapting FPMCs based on the input
\mathbf{z}
represents a natural extension which is likely to be especially useful for images which have discrete latent structure.

A.1
Patch Set Posterior Composites

We will first derive a generic form for PSPC denoisers under our FPMC framework, before discussing specific the specific PSPC-Square and PSPC-Flex denoisers in
sections

A.1.1
and
A.1.2
respectively.

20
express their patch set posterior composite denoiser using posterior means over patch datasets. They express a patch using cropping matrices
\mathbf{C}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times d}
where
n\leq d
is the dimension of the cropped patch. For any patch
\mathbf{C}
, they define patch likelihoods with
\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}
as

p_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}})=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}};\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}},t^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right).

(12)

In their work, they also adopt the parameterization of
11
with
\alpha(t)=1
and
\sigma(t)=t
. We can express their patch likelihoods more generally as

p_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}})=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}};\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}},\sigma(t)^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)

(13)

The log likelihood of this distribution satisfies

\displaystyle p_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\frac{\lVert\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}-\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}\lVert_{2}^{2}}{-2\sigma(t)^{2}}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle p_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\frac{\lVert\alpha(t)\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{z}\lVert_{2}^{2}}{-2\sigma(t)^{2}}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle p_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\frac{\lVert\mathbf{C}\left(\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right)\lVert_{2}^{2}}{-2\sigma(t)^{2}}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle p_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\frac{\left(\mathbf{C}\left(\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right)\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{C}\left(\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right)\right)}{-2\sigma(t)^{2}}\right)

(17)

\displaystyle p_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\frac{\left(\left(\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right)\right)^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\left(\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right)}{-2\sigma(t)^{2}}\right)

(18)

\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}
is a
d\times d
matrix for which
(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})_{i,j}=\mathbf{c}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{c_{j}}
where
\mathbf{c}_{i}
is the i-th column of
\mathbf{C}
. Since
\mathbf{C}
is a cropping matrix its columns are orthonormal, which results in a diagonal
\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{C}
matrix. We can define
\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{C}=\text{diag}(q_{\mathbf{C}})
with
q_{i}=\mathbf{c}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{c}_{i}
and
\mathbf{c}_{i}
is the
i
-th column of
\mathbf{C}
. We have
q_{\mathbf{C}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, with non-zero entries for dimensions which are part of the cropped region. Together, the PSPC patch likelihood can be expressed as

p_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}})\propto\exp\left(\frac{\left(\left(\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right)\right)^{\top}\text{diag}\left(q_{\mathbf{C}}\right)\left(\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right)}{-2\sigma(t)^{2}}\right),

(19)

which is equivalent to the modified likelihood of our framework given by
eq.

8
, with
q=q_{\mathbf{C}}
.

Using their patch posterior likelihood,
20
define a patch posterior mean as

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}},t\right]=\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}})\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}^{(i)},

(20)

with

p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}})=\frac{p_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{\mathbf{C}})}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{D}}p_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}^{(j)})}

(21)

where
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}
is assumed. Substituting
p_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{\mathbf{C}})=\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(i)};q_{\mathbf{C}})
, and defining
\nu(\mathbf{x})=p_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}
, with
\text{supp}(\nu)=\mathcal{D}
, we have

\displaystyle p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(i)})}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{D}}\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(j)})}

(22)

\displaystyle p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\cdot\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{D}}\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(j)})\cdot\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}}

(23)

\displaystyle p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\nu(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{D}}\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(j)})\nu(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})}

(24)

\displaystyle p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\nu(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\text{supp}(\nu)}\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\nu(\mathbf{x})}

(25)

Therefore, with choices
q=q_{\mathbf{C}}
and
\nu=p_{\mathcal{D}}
, we recover the modified posterior of
eq.

9
. Substituting this posterior into
eq.

20
yields

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}},t\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}}\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{z};q_{\mathbf{C}},p_{\mathcal{D}})\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}^{(i)}

(26)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}},t\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}}\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{z};q_{\mathbf{C}},p_{\mathcal{D}})\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}

(27)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}},t\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}}\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{z};q_{\mathbf{C}},p_{\mathcal{D}})\mathbf{x}^{(i)}

(28)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}},t\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\text{supp}(p_{\mathcal{D}})}\tilde{p}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{z};q_{\mathbf{C}},p_{\mathcal{D}})\mathbf{x}^{(i)}

(29)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{p_{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}},t\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{z},t;q_{\mathbf{C}},p_{\mathcal{D}}).

(30)

Therefore, we can recover the patch posterior means of
20
using our modified posterior mean estimator defined in
eq.

7
with choices
q=q_{\mathbf{C}}
and
\nu=p_{\mathcal{D}}
.

The final PSPC denoiser of
20
is an aggregation of a set of patch posterior means. they define their PSPC denoiser as

D\left(\mathbf{z},t,\mathcal{C}\right)=\bigg(\sum_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\bigg)^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{C}},t\right].

(31)

As previously stated, for a cropping matrix with orthonormal rows,
\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}=\text{diag}(q_{\mathbf{C}})
. With this simplification, substituting
eq.

30
into
eq.

31
produces

\displaystyle D\left(\mathbf{z},t,\mathcal{C}\right)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\bigg)^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{z},t;q_{\mathbf{C}},p_{\mathcal{D}})

(32)

\displaystyle D\left(\mathbf{z},t,\mathcal{C}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C}}\text{diag}(q_{\mathbf{C}})\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{z},t;q_{\mathbf{C}},p_{\mathcal{D}})\bigg(\sum_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C}}\text{diag}(q_{\mathbf{C}})\bigg)^{-1}

(33)

Noting that multiplication by a diagonal matrix is equivalent to elementwise multiplication, and defining
r_{\mathbf{C}}=q_{\mathbf{C}}
, we have

D\left(\mathbf{z},t,\mathcal{C}\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C}}r_{\mathbf{C}}\odot\hat{\mu}(\mathbf{z},t;q_{\mathbf{C}},p_{\mathcal{D}})\oslash\bigg(\sum_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C}}r_{\mathbf{C}}\bigg),

(34)

recovering the generic FPMC denoiser defined by
eq.

10
with
\mathcal{Q}=(q_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C})
,
\mathcal{R}=(q_{\mathbf{C}}\mid\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C})
, and
\mathcal{V}=(\nu_{\ell}=p_{\mathcal{D}})_{\ell=1}^{|\mathcal{C}|}
. Next, we will discuss the two specific PSPC denoisers proposed by
20
, and define their
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{R}
.

A.1.1
PSPC-Square

PSPC-Square defines a square patch function
\mathbf{C}(x,y,s)
which produces a cropping matrix corresponding to the square patch of an image with upper left corner at pixel
(x,y)
. They define the set of all overlapping patches with size
s
as

\mathcal{C}_{s}=\left\{\mathbf{C}(x,y,s)\mid x,y\in\{0,\ldots,W-s\}\times\{0,\ldots,H-s\}\right\}.

(35)

The size of patches in PSPC-Square is controlled by a patch schedule function
s(t):\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
which they manually tune through hyperparameter search.

For simplicity, we define
q_{\mathbf{C}}
directly using an indicator function. Let
\mathbf{p}(x,y,s):\{0,\ldots,W-1\}\times\{0,\ldots,H-1\}\times\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{d}
be the indicator function which assigns a value of
1
to pixels contained in the
s\times s
square patch centred at
(x,y)
. That is
\forall\ i,j\in\{0,\ldots,W-1\}\times\{0,\ldots,H-1\}
we have

PSPC-Flex applies a data-informed process to select their patch sets. Patch sets for a given

Appendix B
Augmentation Strategies

In this section, we detail the augmentation utilized in
section

4.3
. We first give an overview of how we generate our agumented datasets, and then detail each agumentation strategy. For the classical augmentation strategies, we use the augmentation pipeline of
11
, which was adapted from
12
. The augmentation pipeline is a stochastic generator which produces tuples of augmented images and labels which describe the parameters used to generate the augmentation transformation. Although the augmentation pipeline can be configured produce a composition of multiple geometric augmentations, we restrict our investigation to generating images with one augmentation strategy at a time.

For each augmentation strategy, excluding the reflection transforms where only one augmentation per source image is possible, we first generate 1 million augmented images by sampling 20 augmentations per original training set image. When sampling augmented images using classical augmentation strategies, we keep a hash of the generated augmentation labels and discard any duplicate augmentations. From the original augmented dataset of 1 million images, we produce smaller augmentation sets through random sub sampling. For dataset sizes which are integer multiples of
|\mathcal{D}|
, we randomly select an equal amount of augmented images per source training image. For augmentation sets which are smaller than the original dataset size, we select one augmented image each for a subset of the original training images. The following augmentation strategies were explored

Horizontal Reflection

For this reflection, we simply flip the image horizontally.

Vertical Reflection

For this reflection, we simply flip the image vertically.

Translation

We translate images according to random offsets sampled as

\displaystyle\delta_{x}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{x}\cdot\frac{W}{8},\quad\epsilon_{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

(92)

\displaystyle\delta_{y}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{y}\cdot\frac{H}{8},\quad\epsilon_{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(93)

Translated images are padded with reflection padding post-transformation.

Rotation

The amount of rotation expressed in radians is randomly sampled from the uniform distribution

\theta\sim\mathcal{U}(-\pi,\pi)

(94)

Like translations, images are padded with reflection padding to retain the original shape of the image post transformation.

Scaling

Scaling up or downsamples the image to resolution
sH\times sW
. The amount of scaling is sampled from the log normal distribution

\ln(s)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.2)

(95)

and reflection padding is used when
s<1
.

Synthetic

C.3
Fine-tuning Details

In this section, we detail the hyperparameters used to fine tune
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{R}
both independently as presented in
fig.

2
, as well as jointly for our methodology presented in
table

2
.

We paramaterize our soft
\mathcal{Q}_{\theta}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\phi}
as
q_{\ell}=\exp(\theta_{\ell})
and
r_{\ell}=\exp(\phi_{\ell})
for
l=1,\ldots,L
. To intialize training from binary
q
or
r
vectors, for all
\ell
, we set
\theta_{\ell}=\max(\log(q_{\ell}),10^{-3})
and
\phi_{\ell}=\max(\log(r_{\ell}),10^{-3})
.

We minimize
eq.

11
with respect to
\theta
,
\phi
or both, depending on the experiment, with fixed
t
, and loss weight
\lambda(t)=1
. We use the AdamW optimizer
[
17
]
, and use a constant learning rate of
0.05
for all experiments with
\beta_{1},\beta 2=0.9,0.999
, and
\epsilon=1E-8
. We found weight decay of
\lambda=0.01
was beneficial for
t\geq 1.92
on CIFAR-10,
t\geq 4.37
on FFHQ and
t\geq 8.03
(steps 9,17, and 14 respectively), but use
\lambda=0
otherwise. All models are trained with a batch size of 256, for 2000 steps or until convergence. We evaluate model performance on a validation set against the DDPM++ denoiser after each epoch, and select the best performing checkpoint across validation epochs. We use the test set of CIFAR-10, and a set of 10,000 synthetic diffusion model samples for FFHQ and AFHQ as a held-out validation set is not available for those datasets.

We run one optimization procedure per sampling timestep for the EDM sampling schedules of each dataset (values provided in
tables

5
,
6
and
7
). We use one Nvidia L40 48Gb GPU per training run. Total training time varies depending on dataset resolution, and the number of estimators in the FPMC, but a typical fine-tuning of PSPC-Flex on CIFAR-10 is completed in approximately 14 hours.

Due to VRAM limitations, when training FFHQ we leverage Monte-Carlo score estimation techniques
[
31
,
19
]
, estimating the
eq.

10
using a random sample of
10,000

\mathbf{x}\sim\nu(\mathbf{x})
instead of iterating over the full dataset. We make this modification in the training step only, using the full average over
\text{supp}(\nu)
for sampling and validation.

C.4
Sampling Procedure

When generating images, we follow the deterministic sampling procedure of
[
11
]
, with diffusion parameters
\alpha(t)=1
,
\sigma(t)=t
. This corresponds to the PF-ODE

d\mathbf{z}=-t\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{z})dt

(96)

and a prior distribution
p_{T}(\mathbf{z})=\pi(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(0,T^{2}\mathbf{I})
, where
T=80
. We use the Heun second-order ODE solver, over
18
values of
t
for CIFAR-10 and
40
values of
t
for FFHQ
64\times 64
, and AFHQ
64\times 64
, for a total of 35 and 79 denoiser evaluations per sample respectively. We use a fixed set of initial 1000
\mathbf{z}
for each dataset, shared among all denoisers.
