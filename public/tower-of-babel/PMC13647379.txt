C‐20 Diterpenoid
Alkaloids with PD-L1 Inhibitory
Activity from the Roots of Aconitum coreanum

Kei-Fong Ng, Xiao-Qian Li, Hao-Ming Xiong, Lin Chen, Xi Chen, Li-Ping Bai, Wei Zhang, Ancheng C Huang, Zhi-Hong Jiang, Guo-Yuan Zhu

Abstract

Fifteen previously
unreported diterpenoid alkaloids, named aconicorines
A-O (1–15), and 13 known diterpenoid
alkaloids (16–28) were isolated from
the roots of Aconitum coreanum. Their
structures and configurations were determined by comprehensive spectroscopic
analysis including HRMS, NMR, and single-crystal X-ray diffraction.
Structurally, all new compounds are classic C20-hetisine-type diterpenoid
alkaloids with various oxidation sites. Among them, aconicorines A-D
(1–4) are unique C20-diterpenoid
alkaloids containing a rare sulfonate group. Bioassay results showed
that 1, 7, 13–17, and 20 dose-dependently inhibited the PD-L1
protein expression at 2.5–20 μM in MDA-MB-231 cells.
Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining confirmed the inhibitory
effect of 7, 14, 15, and 17 on PD-L1 expression. These findings first demonstrated
the effects of C20-diterpenoid alkaloids on PD-L1 expression and suggested
that C20-diterpenoid alkaloids are a new type of PD-L1 modulators.

Introduction

Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), an
immunomodulatory transmembrane
protein, interacts with its receptor PD-1 on T cells to help control
immune activity and maintain immune tolerance. In the tumor microenvironment, PD-L1 is highly expressed in cancer
cells and achieves immune escape after binding to PD-1. Blocking the
binding of PD-L1 to PD-1 can reactivate T cells to kill tumor cells. To date, a large amount of clinical data have
demonstrated the good efficacy of PD-L1 inhibitors, specifically PD-L1
monoclonal antibodies, as immunotherapies in various cancers. However, PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies still face
limitations in cancer treatment, including adverse immune responses,
poor oral bioavailability, and high production costs. In recent years, the development of small-molecule PD-L1
inhibitors has attracted widespread attention and has become a promising
field of cancer immunotherapy. Natural
products, as an important source of small-molecule drugs, offer various
compounds with PD-L1 inhibitory activities including diterpenoids,
triterpenoids, flavonoids, and alkaloids. In our recent work, oridonin, a natural diterpenoid, was identified
as a potential PD-L1 inhibitor capable of boosting antitumor immunity.

The genus Aconitum (Ranunculaceae),
comprising over 300 species globally, represents one of the most prominent
resources for bioactive diterpenoid alkaloids. In last 5 years, various C19- and C20-diterpenoid alkaloids with
aconitine-, hetisine-, denudatine-, napelline-, and lycoctine-type
skeletons were reported from A. heterophyllum,
A. carmichaeli
i,
A.
gymnandrum,
A. nagarum,
A. pendulum, and A. refractum. These
metabolites have been associated with multiple bioactivities, including
anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antiplasmodial, insecticidal activities, NLRP3 inflammasome-inhibitory, and autophagy-inducing effects. Among Aconitum species, A. coreanum is an important medicinal plant due to
the long-standing traditional medicinal use and its diverse diterpenoid
alkaloids. A. coreanum is widely distributed
in northeast China, Mongolia, Korea, and the Russian Far East. The roots of A. coreanum are processed and used as an herbal medicine, called Guanbaifu in
China or Korean monkshood in Korea, to treat stroke, facial paralysis,
epilepsy, and migraine. A variety of
chemical constituents have been isolated from A. coreanum, including diterpenoid alkaloids,

extract of A. coreanum roots (10 kg)
was partitioned sequentially with petroleum ether
(PE), ethyl acetate (EA), and n-butanol. These extracts

base A1 (26), guanfu base P (27), and
coreanine A (28).

Based on preliminary
experiments, the effects of isolated diterpenoid
alkaloids (1–25) on PD-L1 expression
were screened at a noncytotoxic concentration (Figures S151–S152) in human breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231
cells). Western blot analysis showed that compounds 1, 7, 13–17, and 20 inhibited PD-L1 expression at a concentration of 10 μM
(). Subsequently,
the effects of different concentrations of 7 (2.5, 5,
and 10 μM), 1, 13–17, and 20 (5, 10, and 20 μM) were evaluated. Results
demonstrated that 7, 14, 15, and 17 significantly inhibited PD-L1 expression in
a dose-dependent manner, while other tested compounds (1, 13, 16, and 20) exhibited
varying degrees of inhibitory effects (A and B). Additionally, the PD-L1 inhibitory
effects of 7, 14, 15, and 17 were verified by immunofluorescence staining (C). Compared with
the blank control group, the bright spots of PD-L1 in the field of
view were reduced after the treatment with 10 μM of 7, 14, 15, and 17, respectively,
in MDA-MB-231 cells (C). According to the above-mentioned first discovery of the
effect of diterpenoid alkaloids on PD-L1, it was found that the type
of oxidizing group and the substitution position on the hetisine skeleton
have different effects on PD-L1. Although compound 1 showed
potential PD-L1 downregulation activity, other sulfonate-containing
diterpenoid alkaloids (2–4) did not
exhibit significant activity. Furthermore, compared to 8 and 9, compound 7 exhibited better PD-L1
inhibition due to simultaneous oxidative substitution at C-6 and C-7.
Compounds 16 and 17 exhibited better PD-L1
inhibition compared to 18 and 19, suggesting
that benzoylation at C-13 could be important for this activity. To
refine more effective structure–activity relationships, more
bioassay of hetisine-type alkaloids will be investigated in future.

In summary, phytochemical
studies of A. coreanum identified 15
new C20 hetisine-type diterpenoid alkaloids (1–15) and 13 known diterpenoid alkaloids
(16–28). All new compounds are highly
oxygenated hetisine-type diterpenoid alkaloids with diverse oxidation
sites and substituents at C-1–3, C-6, C-7, C-11, C-13–15,
or C-19 of hetisine skeleton. Compounds 1–4 are abnormal C20 diterpenoid alkaloids possessing a rare
sulfonate group. Eight alkaloids (1, 7, 13–17, and 20) exhibited
PD-L1 inhibitory activity at concentrations ranging from 2.5 to 20
μM in MDA-MB-231 cells. This study first reported the effects
of C20 diterpenoid alkaloids on PD-L1 expression and suggested that
C20 diterpenoid alkaloids could be a new type of PD-L1 modulator.

Experimental Section

General Experimental Procedures

Optical rotation was
measured at room temperature using an Autopol I automatic polarimeter.
UV and circular dichroism spectra were measured using a JASCO J-1500
circular dichroism spectrophotometer. HRESIMS spectra were measured
using an Agilent 6230 TOF mass spectrometer. Medium-pressure liquid
chromatography (MPLC) was performed using a Buchi C-620 system (Buchi,
Switzerland) equipped with a C-635 UV detector and a SiliaBond C18
column (ODSGel, 40–63 μm, 26 × 460 mm). Semipreparative
HPLC was performed on an Agilent Technologies 1200 series and ThermoFisher
Ultimate 3000 system, using a variety of chromatographic columns,
including an Atlantis T3 OBD column (5 μm, 10 × 250 mm,
Waters), a SunFire C18 OBD column (5 μm, 10 × 250 mm, Waters),
a ChromCore Phenyl-Ether column (5 μm, 10 × 250 mm, NanoChrom),
and a Symmetry C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm, Waters).
The flow rate was 3 mL/min, and UV detection was performed at 210
and 254 nm. 1H, 13C, DEPT, HSQC, HMBC, 1H-1H COSY, and NOESY NMR spectra were acquired
on a Bruker Avance 600 MHz spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) using TMS
as the internal standard. Thin-layer chromatography was performed
using Precoated silica gel GF254 plates (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany),
and the diterpenoid alkaloid-containing fractions were visualized
with potassium bismuth iodide. Automated CO2 Incubator
at 37 °C and 5% CO2, Thermo Scientific Heraeus X1R
Refrigerated Centrifuge, Olympus CKX53 IPC Inverted Microscope, Tecan
Infinite M200 PRO, and Agilent BioTek Cytation 5 were used.

Absolute Configuration Analysis of Sugar

The absolute
configuration of sugars in 1 was determined using methods
previously reported in the literature. Compound 1 (1 mg) underwent acid hydrolysis in 1 N
HCl (0.1 mL) at 80 °C for 4 h, followed by solvent removal. The
residue was dissolved in a pyridine solution of L-cysteine methyl
ester hydrochloride (0.5 mg in 0.1 mL) at 60 °C for 1 h, followed
by a secondary derivatization reaction with phenyl isothiocyanate
(0.1 mL, 0.5 mg/mL in pyridine) at 60 °C for 1 h. The resulting
reaction product (5 μL) was determined under the following chromatographic
conditions: Symmetry C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm, Waters),
DAD detection wavelength 250 nm, column temperature 35 °C, mobile
phase 25% MeCN 0.1% phosphoric acid solution, and flow rate 0.8 mL/min.
The presence of d-glucose in 1 was confirmed
by comparing the retention time (16.98 min) of its derivative to those
of standard d-glucose (17.09 min, TCI) and l-glucose
(16.02 min, MACKLIN).

Cell Lines and Culture Conditions

Human breast cancer
cell line (MDA-MB-231) was sourced from the American Type Culture
Collection (ATCC, Rockville, Maryland, USA). Cells were incubated
in DMEM high-glucose medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum
(FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin antibiotics under standard
humidified conditions at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Upon achieving
80–90% confluence, the cells were subcultured at intervals
of 2–3 days.

In Vitro Cytotoxicity Assay

MDA-MB-231
cells were initially
plated in 96-well culture plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well and cultured overnight. Following a 48 h incubation with
a series of concentrations (10, 20, and 40 μM) of compounds 1–25, cell viability was evaluated using
the MTT assay as previously reported.

Western Blot Analysis

For protein analysis, MDA-MB-231
cells were plated in 6-well plates (5 × 105 cells/well)
and treated with 10 μM compounds 1–25 and 40 μM berberine for 48 h. Following treatment,
cells were harvested and lysed using RIPA buffer. Equal amounts of
proteins were electrophoresed on an 8% SDS-PAGE gel and then transferred
to a PVDF membrane. The membranes were blocked and probed overnight
at 4 °C with an anti-PD-L1 primary antibody (ABclone, USA). After
washing three times with TBST, the blots were incubated with a secondary
antibody (rabbit monoclonal) at ambient temperature for 1 h. Ultimately,
the protein blots were analyzed using a LI-COR Odyssey imaging system
(Lincoln, Nevada, USA).

Immunofluorescence Staining

MDA-MB-231
cells were treated
with compounds 7, 14, 15, and 17 (with blank solvent and berberine as controls), followed
by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde. Following cell permeabilization
and blocking, samples were probed overnight at 4 °C with anti-PD-L1
primary antibody. On the following day, the cells were incubated for
2 h with FITC-labeled goat antirabbit IgG (H+L) secondary antibody.
Nuclear counterstaining was subsequently carried out using DAPI. Fluorescence
imaging was conducted on an ImageXpress Micro confocal system to observe
DAPI (nucleus) and FITC (target protein) channels, respectively.
