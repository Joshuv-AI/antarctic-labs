Neural synchrony and cognitive map formulation in collaborative navigation: leader-follower couplings revealed by EEG hyperscanning
Authors: Chun-Hsiang Chuang, Po-Hsun Peng, Yi-Chieh Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to address a theoretical gap in how people formulate cognitive maps in a social context. The concept of a cognitive map, a mental representation of spatial relations, has been widely used to explain individual navigation. When navigating with others, the exchange of spatial knowledge and the formation of cognitive maps in leaders and followers are natural and ubiquitous, yet their neural underpinnings remain insufficiently understood. We investigated these mechanisms by recording hyperscanning electroencephalography (EEG) from 70 participants engaged in dyadic route-planning and navigation tasks within a virtual reality environment. Intrabrain and interbrain couplings were analyzed across frequency bands using connectivity measures. We observed robust neural synchronization patterns associated with collaborative navigation performance and role. Intrabrain connectivity analyses showed increased delta coupling in both leaders and followers, whereas theta connectivity was particularly enhanced in followers. Alpha-band connectivity displayed divergent patterns between roles, suggesting distinct neural strategies for spatial processing. Interbrain analyses revealed increased delta causality between partners but decreased theta and gamma couplings from followers to leaders. Faster-performing dyads exhibited overall reduced interbrain coupling, especially in theta bands, indicating more efficient neural coordination. Collaborative navigation relies on frequency-specific, role-dependent neural coordination both within and between brains, and more efficient dyads may require less sustained interbrain coupling. These findings can inform the design of navigation aids, training protocols, and collaborative interfaces that better support leader–follower coordination and improve joint spatial decision making in real-world settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11571-026-10521-4.

Introduction

Spatial navigation, essential for human functionality, involves accurately tracking changes in position and orientation, which are influenced by the surrounding environment and cognitive strategies (Baumann and Mattingley 2021; Ehinger et al. 2014; Epstein 2008; Moffat 2009; Singh et al. 2021). This capability involves the use of various strategic choices. Some individuals anchor their navigation to prominent environmental features, such as landmarks, using them as cognitive aids (Chan et al. 2012; Ekstrom et al. 2014; Walter et al. 2022). Others rely on the formation of mental maps, which are detailed internal representations of space that facilitate orientation and movement (Epstein et al. 2017; van der Ham and Claessen 2020). Although both approaches effectively enhance spatial understanding and help individuals reach their destinations, a critical question emerges: How do individuals synchronize and harmonize their cognitive maps (Epstein et al. 2017) with others during collaborative route planning or navigation? This question is central to understanding the mechanisms underlying collaborative spatial tasks and represents a compelling area for further research.

A frequent scenario in daily collaborative spatial tasks (Galati and Avraamides 2013) involves guiding individuals who are unfamiliar with their surroundings to a specific destination. When such guidance is provided remotely, such as over the phone, it often relies on shared spatial understanding, which is mediated through descriptions of visual cues (Chan et al. 2012; Delaux et al. 2021) to convey their position and orientation to the guide. Moreover, this type of guidance tests the ability of the guide, who is assumed to be familiar with the setting, to recall the layout and plan routes effectively. Previous studies have indicated that an individual’s familiarity with a given setting can shape how that setting is spatially conceptualized. In general, spatial navigation primarily operates within egocentric and allocentric reference frames (Klatzky 1998). The egocentric frame is viewer-dependent, encoding spatial relationships relative to one’s own body, whereas the allocentric frame captures spatial relationships among environmental features, irrespective of the viewer’s location. Existing studies (Burgess 2006; Ruggiero and Iachini 2006) have argued that familiar environments typically activate allocentric representations, whereas unfamiliar ones prompt an egocentric approach. Furthermore, increased familiarity with a specific location may facilitate the extraction of spatial information from both egocentric and allocentric perspectives (Hatzipanayioti et al. 2021). Thus, in collaborative spatial tasks, the information provider and receiver are speculated to use different spatial reference frames during their communication. Additionally, both interacting members are likely to demonstrate flexibility, seamlessly switching between these frames to navigate effectively.

Spatial navigation involves several brain regions, including the retrosplenial cortex, posterior parietal cortex, prefrontal region, and hippocampus (Gomez et al. 2014; Gramann et al. 2010; Leplaideur et al. 2021; Moraresku et al. 2023). Using electroencephalography (EEG), a widely used neural imaging tool in spatial navigation research, studies have shown increased activity in the delta and theta bands within the parietal and occipital lobes during the development of mental maps and spatial navigation (Chrastil et al. 2022; Grön et al. 2000; Pu et al. 2017; Watrous et al. 2013). Moreover, recent neuroscientific research (Boccia et al. 2017; Clark et al. 2018; Ekstrom et al. 2017; Whitlock et al. 2008) has highlighted how the brain uses egocentric and allocentric reference frames for spatial navigation. For individuals who predominantly use an egocentric frame, enhanced neural activity is primarily found in the visual cortex and parieto-occipital network. Conversely, those using an allocentric perspective exhibit notable EEG activity in the occipitotemporal, bilateral inferior parietal, and retrosplenial cortical areas, which help transition between spatial reference frames (Delaux et al. 2021; Gramann et al. 2010). Based on these findings, we hypothesize that these brain regions are pivotal in collaborative spatial navigation, evident not only in individual brain activities but also in interbrain interactions during such tasks. We also speculate the involvement of additional brain regions pivotal for social interaction tasks, such as the exchange of spatial information and coordination of mental maps, in contributing to neural synchrony. Specifically, we expect to observe increased neural synchronization in areas such as the right temporoparietal junction during tasks that require social navigation, indicating its significance in collaborative processes (Zhou et al. 2023).

This study used EEG hyperscanning (Babiloni and Astolfi 2014; Czeszumski et al. 2020) to investigate how dyads cooperate during spatial navigation. Hyperscanning enabled simultaneous recording of brain activity from both partners while they engaged in collaborative route planning in a virtual reality town presented with an omnidirectional treadmill, with one participant assigned as leader and the other as follower. To examine neural synchronization and coordination and relate them to dyadic performance, we analyzed intra- and interbrain connectivity using functional and effective connectivity metrics. For functional connectivity, we focused on phase-based measures. To characterize how brain regions within and across individuals synchronize their phase dynamics during collaboration, we computed the corrected imaginary phase locking value (ciPLV)(Bruña et al. 2018), which effectively addresses sensitivity to volume conduction and thereby reduces spurious zero-lag coupling. To capture the directionality of neural interactions, particularly within leader and follower dynamics, we examined effective connectivity using the directed transfer function (dDTF) (Korzeniewska et al. 2003). dDTF, derived from multivariate autoregressive modeling, estimates frequency-specific information flow while attenuating indirect influences, making it suitable for detailed connectivity analyses. Together, ciPLV and dDTF provide complementary views of neural coordination during collaborative navigation and were selected over alternatives such as coherence or mutual information because they jointly characterize both the strength and direction of interbrain coupling.

This study addressed three key research questions: (1) Do the navigation leader and follower demonstrate distinct role-specific intrabrain connectivity patterns for processes such as spatial memory and trajectory planning? (2) Do interacting dyads exhibit the interbrain couplings crucial for managing shared attention and conflicting information during collaborative route planning? (3) Is interbrain connectivity associated with effective task performance? These questions explore the neural dynamics underlying collaborative navigation tasks.

Methods and materials

Ethics statement

This research complied with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board at National Tsing Hua University in Taiwan (Number: 11001HT006). Informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Participants

A group of 70 volunteers, including 26 men and 44 women with an average age of 21.9 ± 2.8 years, participated in this study. These participants were in good health, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and exhibited normal hearing. The female participants were not pregnant. None of the participants had a history of chronic smoking, drug or alcohol abuse, physical impairment, psychiatric disorders, or cardiovascular issues. The participants were grouped into pairs randomly. They alternated roles as leader and follower to collaboratively complete navigation tasks.

VR experimental environment and treadmill

In this study on dyadic route planning, participants in the follower role were deeply immersed in a VR environment (Fig. 1A) that facilitated intense engagement with experimental tasks. The VR setting displayed three-dimensional street models and was developed using the Unity engine (Unity Software Inc.). Participants navigated this virtual streetscape using a VIVE Cosmos Elite headset, enhanced by a VIVE Wireless Adapter (HTC Co., Ltd.). The inclusion of an omnidirectional treadmill (KAT Walk mini, Hangzhou Virtual & Reality Technology Co., Ltd.) and a VIVE controller not only increased participants’ freedom of movement but also allowed for a realistic simulation of walking within the virtual world (C. H. Chuang et al. 2024a, b). The participant assigned the leader role was positioned in front of a screen throughout the experiment. Consistent with the configurations in our previous study (Chuang and Hsu 2023), we used Lab Streaming Layer (LSL)(Kothe et al. 2019), integrating LSL-LiveAmp, LSL4Unity, BrainVision LSL Viewer, and LSL-LabRecorder, to construct a data acquisition platform (Fig. 1B).

Dyadic navigation task

The whole experimental procedure is shown in Fig. 2A.

Given that VR sickness presented challenges to the experiment’s integrity and raised safety concerns, we administered a VR sickness assessment to participants one month prior to the formal experiment to mitigate these issues. During this assessment, participants wore a VR headset and entered a virtual town that closely resembled the experimental setting. Their task was to locate a specific target while exploring the virtual space for 15 min. Only participants who experienced no discomfort during this preliminary test were considered suitable for the main experiment and recruited.

Two weeks before the experiment, participants were randomly assigned one of the designated maps (Fig. 2B) and instructed to familiarize themselves with it. The two street maps (Map A and Map B) designed for this study measured 4 × 4 and featured 15 building models. Although both maps featured identical buildings, their layouts were different. During these two weeks, we used a video game, which included three training tasks, to aid this memorization. The first task was a jigsaw puzzle game where participants placed landmarks in their correct locations on a map provided for reference. The second task was a coin-searching game within the VR town, where participants navigated using cues displayed in the upper right corner of their view. The final task was similar to the first task but added a twist. In this task, participants placed landmarks based solely on their recall without the aid of the guiding map. Their gameplay was logged, and only those who engaged in this training activity at least three times and achieved perfect accuracy were selected for the main experiment. On the day of the experiment, the participants completed another round of the third task (pre-session assessment) to ensure that they could flawlessly recall their maps.

Participants completed the formal dyadic navigation task in pairs, with one member familiar only with Map A and the other only with Map B. One participant assumed the leader role and guided the partner, who took the follower role, to a predetermined destination. The follower navigated a VR town configured according to a street map known only to the leader and, starting from an arbitrary point, relied entirely on the leader’s cues to reach the specified endpoint.

Each trial (Fig. 2C) consisted of a 5-second cuing phase, a 15-second discussion phase, a navigation phase of up to 120 s, and a 3-second homing direction test. During cuing, the follower viewed a fixation cross while the leader saw the target box location; once the cues disappeared, the follower was placed at a random position in the virtual town. In the discussion phase, the follower described nearby buildings and the leader indicated the target box, allowing them to plan a route. The follower then navigated toward the box while they continued to communicate, with roadblocks in half of the trials and decoy boxes placed throughout the town; trials exceeding 120 s were terminated. After 30 trials, the follower completed a jigsaw puzzle of the town to assess map learning, and the score was recorded. The maps were then swapped, the leader and follower roles were reversed, and another 30 trials were performed with the alternate map.

Hyperscanning EEG

In this hyperscanning EEG study, we simultaneously captured EEG signals from each dyad using two identical LiveAmp wireless amplifier systems, each equipped with 32-channel actiCAP slim active electrodes from Brain Products GmbH, Germany. To ensure optimal signal acquisition, conductive gel (SuperVisc, EASYCAP Gmbh) was used to fill the space between the electrodes and the scalp. The impedance for every electrode was ensured to be under 10 kΩ. EEG data were captured at a sampling rate of 1,000 Hz, with an analog-to-digital convertor resolution of 16 bits. Real-time monitoring of all data streams was conducted, safeguarding against potential equipment malfunction. The collected data were saved in an Extensible Data Format file. Subsequently, we used the MoBILAB EEGLAB plugin to transform the data into a format compatible with EEGLAB (Delorme and Makeig 2004).

Data processing and brain connectivity measures

From the behavioral data stream and associated event markers, we recorded the walking trajectory and completion time (i.e., latency for each target search trial). To compare the complexity of the two maps, we used two metrics: the number of turns and the taxicab distance required for an optimal route in each trial.

EEG signals were mainly processed using native and plugin functions from EEGLAB version v.14.1.2 (Delorme and Makeig 2004) and Matlab R2020a, adhering to Makoto’s preprocessing guidelines (Makoto, n.d.). As illustrated in Fig. 3A, raw EEG data were initially downsampled to 250 Hz and then band-pass filtered between 1 and 50 Hz to eliminate a substantial amount of high-frequency noise typically associated with muscle activity during speech. Line noises were mitigated using the cleanline EEGLAB plugin (Bokil et al. 2010). The data were further refined through Artifact Subspace Reconstruction (ASR)(Kothe and Makeig 2013) to handle high-amplitude transient noise. Subsequently, Independent Component Analysis combined with ICLabel (Pion-Tonachini et al. 2019) and the Artifact Removal Transformer (ART)(Chuang et al. 2025) were applied to identify and eliminate prominent non-neural artifacts, such as eye movements, blinks, facial muscle activity, and conversation-induced motions. Finally, the data were processed using common average referencing.

This study argued that the follower’s cognitive map, which is essential to navigation, would be formulated during the 15-second discussion phase of the navigation task. Thus, we only evaluated the EEG data during the 15-second discussion phase and the 3-second resting baseline before it. In this phase, the follower identified nearby landmarks to communicate their location, whereas the leader formulated the most effective path to reach the target location. As shown in Fig. 3B, EEG epochs within the 15-second route planning phase were extracted for subsequent analysis. Corresponding baseline segments were drawn from the initial resting-state blocks for comparison.

Regarding functional connectivity, ciPLV (Bruña et al. 2018) was used to examine intrabrain and interbrain connections. In addition, dDTF (Korzeniewska et al. 2003) was used to assess causality within and between brains, providing a comprehensive view of functional and effective connectivity (Fig. 3C). For these computations, ciPLV utilized all scalp channels, while dDTF was computed using a representative subset of 12 channels (Fp1, Fp2, Fz, Cz, T7, T8, P7, P3, Pz, P4, P8, Oz) to maintain computational stability during multivariate autoregressive modeling. These connectivity analyses were conducted across a spectrum of EEG frequency bands, including delta (1–3 Hz), theta (4–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (31–50 Hz). Baseline correction was applied to both connectivity metrics via absolute subtraction. Specifically, the mean connectivity magnitude of the 3-second resting baseline was subtracted from the continuous connectivity values obtained during the 15-second discussion epoch. Moreover, we determined the relationship between intrabrain/interbrain couplings and task performance metrics, including completion time and rate. The completion rate was calculated as the percentage of the 30 total trials where the dyad successfully navigated to the target box within the 120-second limit.

Statistics

We used independent-samples t-tests and chi-square tests to examine whether Maps A and B differed in difficulty and whether the fast and slow groups differed in age, gender, and task performance.

For EEG analyses, we compared data by navigation role (leader vs. follower) and by task performance (fast vs. slow). For each role, ciPLV and dDTF values during the discussion phase were compared with the 3-second baseline across frequency bands using paired-samples t-tests, revealing neural dynamic differences between leaders and followers. To examine performance effects, participants were divided into fast and slow groups based on time spent in the dyadic navigation task, and theta-band dDTF during the discussion phase was compared between groups using independent-samples t-tests.

EEG data from all trials within a dyad were pooled and treated as one sample so that trial-wise learning effects did not confound the analyses. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to all EEG analyses to control for multiple comparisons. Because electrode-based analyses yield many t-test results simultaneously, the figures do not list all statistics; instead, test values are represented by color in the legends, and only results with FDR-corrected p values < 0.05 are shown.

Demographic analysis revealed an equal distribution of sex across both map groups, with 21 women and 14 men for Map A and 23 women and 12 men for Map B. This finding indicated no significant difference in map learning between the two conditions.

The average completion times for navigating Map A and Map B were 52.8 (SD = 10.9) seconds and 50.8 (SD = 10.2) seconds, respectively. Statistical analysis indicated no significant difference in navigation times between the two maps (t[68] = − 0.80, p=.425).

Functional intrabrain and interbrain connectivity

Figure 5 shows functional intrabrain connectivity for leaders and followers, as well as interbrain connectivity between dyads, across five frequency bands.

In the delta band, ciPLV revealed significantly increased intrabrain connectivity across most regions in both leaders and followers (FDR-adjusted p <.05), suggesting enhanced within-brain coordination during the task. In the theta band, followers showed increased intrabrain connectivity, particularly between parietal and occipital regions, indicating greater engagement of these areas. In the alpha band, followers exhibited reduced intrabrain connectivity, primarily between occipital and parietal regions, whereas leaders showed significant increases, highlighting divergent role-dependent neural engagement. In the beta band, only weak and sparse significant connectivity was observed in both the leaders and the followers. In the gamma band, leaders exhibited rare significant connectivity, while followers demonstrated a marked increase in connectivity, particularly localized to the frontal regions. Overall, ciPLV pointed to distinct neural strategies between roles.

Regarding interbrain functional connectivity, the ciPLV analysis revealed only sparse and weak significant connections between the dyads across all frequency bands. This suggests that sustained phase-locked interbrain synchronization was not a prominent neural feature during the route-planning phase.

In the delta band, we noted a significant enhancement of intrabrain causality in most regions in both leaders and followers. This finding indicates an association of increased connectivity with route planning. In addition, interbrain causality was markedly enhanced from leaders to followers, spanning across multiple brain regions in both individuals. Similar modest enhancements in both intrabrain and interbrain causality were observed within the alpha and beta frequency bands, with the beta band showing a particularly rare occurrence of follower-to-leader interbrain causality.

In the theta band, we noted a marked increase in intrabrain causality in the leaders, predominantly in the parietal regions. Conversely, the follower exhibited decreased intrabrain causality. This reduction pattern was particularly evident in the gamma band in both the leader’s and follower’s brain activity as well as in the connectivity from the follower to the leader.

To systematically examine the quantitative association between behavioral performance and neural indices, Fig. 7 illustrates the contrast in effective connectivity between dyads based on their task completion times (fast vs. slow). Given the role-divergent connectivity patterns observed in Fig. 6, our inter-group analysis specifically focused on differences in theta-band connectivity between the fast and slow task completion groups. This empirical decision is grounded in extensive evidence linking theta oscillations to spatial memory processing, active trajectory planning, and cognitive map formulation (Belchior et al. 2014; Chrastil et al. 2022; Valencia and Froese 2020). This result shows changes in neural connections both within individual brains and between the dyad. The results indicate that the intrabrain connectivity of followers may be a crucial factor in task completion. Moreover, we observed that decreased connectivity in specific brain regions was associated with more efficient navigation performance. The findings on interbrain connectivity suggest that the fast group exhibited decreased neural coupling between the dyad. This reduction in neural communication, particularly from the leader’s to the follower’s parietal regions and vice versa, may be correlated with more efficient task completion in the fast group. For a comprehensive overview, the full effective connectivity differences between the fast and slow groups across all five frequency bands are provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Discussion

Cognitive map formulation and route planning

In this study, unique map layouts were designed to create asymmetrical knowledge among participants in a dyad, assigning specific roles of leader and follower. In the collaborative navigation task, interactions between leaders and followers relied on critical elements, such as a sense of agency, perspective-taking, shared attention, and the analysis of spatial memory processing over time, to actively bridge this asymmetry and form a shared cognitive map. This setup enabled the exploration of how varying levels of spatial information affect collaborative interactions and their associated neural connectivity, both inter- and intrabrain, during navigation tasks. Ultimately, the high task completion rates (Table 1) serve as behavioral evidence that these dyads successfully formulated this shared map and collaborated effectively.

Multiple neuroimaging studies have found a series of cell representations related to cognitive map in the hippocampal–entorhinal system when people were navigating (Epstein et al. 2017; Whittington et al. 2022). Due to the research design, our EEG evidence might not make a direct comparison to the corresponding findings. Yet, this study found a remarkable theta intrabrain effective connectivity increase in the parietal region of the leaders, echoed to the evidence of the activated parietal region (retrosplenial complex, at the parietal-occipital sulcus) when people were watching landmark images and outperformed in a navigation task (White et al. 2012). Indeed, the posterior parietal region was related to short-term egocentric spatial memory (Byrne et al. 2007), and the medial parietal region responded strongly to building and landscape images, but not human faces (Epstein and Kanwisher 1998). The parietal region is believed to be a specialist in navigation and environmental recognition, and it should also be essential to cognitive map formulation.

We found relatively robust results in the delta, theta, and gamma bands, as shown in Figs. 5 and 6. Because of the absence of EEG evidence in cognitive map research, we refrained from making premature statements on the relationship between frequency band and cognitive map formulation. Yet, the previous studies on navigation showed that delta and theta signals had strong ties to attention and navigation performance (Drijvers and Holler 2022; White et al. 2012). Delta oscillations, which are linked to attention-demanding encoding, semantic processing, and speech perception, play a significant role in these activities (Drijvers and Holler 2022; Tóth et al. 2019). The findings of this study regarding both functional and effective connectivity are consistent with those of previous studies on conversational interactions and speech content tracking, highlighting the cognitive processes involved in navigation tasks.

Although previous studies (White et al. 2012) have shown that increased theta activity in medial–temporal–parietal regions correlates with better navigation performance, our findings revealed a decrease in both intrabrain connectivity within followers and interbrain connectivity between leaders and followers in groups that completed tasks quickly. This result contrasts with that of a previous study (Barraza et al. 2020) that measured task performance using millisecond-scale reaction times, suggesting that the need for attention resources is brief and transient in nature. The findings of this study stem from route planning sessions where dyadic participants discussed their spatial strategies. Notably, increased theta connectivity was observed in leaders, aligning with their involvement in trajectory planning (Belchior et al. 2014) and place memory (Valencia and Froese 2020), whereas a decrease was noted in followers. This divergence in brain activity, tailored to each role’s demands, led to decreased interbrain theta couplings. These results suggest that distinct cognitive processes are at play within the dyad during collaboration, highlighting role-specific neural dynamics.

We also found significant alpha connectivity differences in our results, although they were not the brightest findings. Previous studies have observed significant alpha suppression in the temporal and parietal areas during spatial navigation tasks, which can be attributed to the need for increased cortical excitability essential for spatial updating (Ehinger et al. 2014). Regardless of specific brain regions involved in encoding and retrieving spatial data, the alpha neural network plays a crucial role in processing spatial reference frames (Chiu et al. 2012). This study builds on these insights by showing decreased alpha couplings among brain regions, particularly in followers. This role-specific alpha decoupling likely reflects the followers’ need to integrate their location with guidance from leaders, manage conflicting information, and facilitate the transformation of spatial information.

The role-specific neural features in collaborative navigation

The current experimental setup clearly designated participants as leaders or followers, creating an asymmetry in spatial information that naturally established leadership roles. This structure aligns with the findings of previous research (Luft et al. 2022), which observed increased alpha interbrain effective connectivity from followers to leaders but not vice versa, supporting the notion that leaders and followers engage differently in collaborative tasks. However, upon deeper analysis, the designated roles of leader and follower in the experiment might not accurately reflect actual leadership dynamics. The observed alpha suppression in followers suggests that they play a more critical role in driving the interaction (Konvalinka et al. 2014). This pattern indicates that followers could be the ones leading the navigational decision-making process, challenging the traditional perception of their role.

Previous studies (Decety and Lamm 2007; Santiesteban et al. 2012) have demonstrated that the right temporoparietal junction is crucial in social interactions. This association is indicated by strong interbrain synchronization observed in the interacting dyad, as noted in functional near-infrared spectroscopy signals during tasks involving social navigation (Zhou et al. 2023). The findings of this study highlight that the temporal and parietal areas are crucial functional hubs in collaborative route planning. Moreover, the results revealed an inverse relationship where decreased interbrain synchronization in these regions is correlated with more efficient task execution, suggesting that less neural coupling enhances performance. This phenomenon may be explained by irruption theory (Froese et al. 2024), which posits that higher subjective engagement and motivation in social interactions lead to increased neural entropy, resulting in lower interbrain synchronization in the fast task completion group.

An alternative explanation for the reduced interbrain synchrony lies in the interplay between subjective task difficulty and varying communication demands. Previous research (Park et al. 2022) demonstrated decreased interbrain synchrony among participants engaged in easier tasks compared with those involved in more challenging tasks. In this study, the fast task completion group appeared to have established efficient communication, which facilitated seamless sharing of spatial information and intentions. This efficiency often stems from a tacit understanding, or a robust shared mental model (Mathieu et al. 2000), between team members. In team cognition literature (MacMillan et al. 2004), the formation of an accurate shared mental model enables implicit coordination, allowing highly coordinated teams to anticipate each other’s needs and dramatically reduce the necessity for overt verbal communication. Consequently, the concise nature of their verbal interactions during the planning phases suggests not only that these members perceived the task as subjectively easier, but also that they experienced a significantly reduced communication demand. Therefore, highly coordinated dyads may require fewer, but more precise, verbal exchanges to formulate a shared spatial strategy, naturally resulting in reduced sustained interbrain coupling.

The possibility of egocentric and allocentric spatial reference frames

Although the brain’s navigation networks can distinguish between different spatial reference frames (egocentric and allocentric) used during navigation (Lin et al. 2018), this study did not investigate how these frames affect intrabrain or interbrain connectivity. This omission was due to the variability in the use of spatial reference frames, which can significantly change based on task demands. Factors such as cultural background (Goeke et al. 2015), the nature of verbal descriptions (Coventry et al. 2018), the dimensionality and spatial scale of the environment (Ekstrom and Isham 2017), and specific characteristics of navigation tasks (Do et al. 2021) all influence the choice of spatial strategies employed. This variability suggests that spatial navigation strategies are not one-size-fits-all but are instead adapted to meet the unique requirements of different contexts and challenges.

Limitations

Several limitations of the current study warrant consideration. First, while controlled tasks can isolate specific spatial reference frames, real-world collaborative navigation requires participants to dynamically switch between egocentric and allocentric perspectives. To quantitatively associate our observed neural indicators with these specific transformations, it’s necessary to time-lock EEG epochs to the semantic context of the dyads’ verbal communication. Because we did not record audio logs during the experiment, a direct mapping of these continuous frame transformations to neural connectivity was not feasible. Future studies incorporating synchronized verbal transcriptions are needed to map these rapid cognitive shifts. Second, a methodological limitation of the current study lies in the selection and consistency of functional connectivity metrics. While we primarily reported ciPLV to mitigate volume conduction, exploratory analyses using standard phase locking value (PLV) (Lachaux et al. 1999) and weighted phase lag index (wPLI) (Vinck et al. 2011) yielded inconsistent patterns, particularly in beta and gamma bands (see Supplementary Figure S2). These discrepancies underscore the inherent complexity of hyperscanning EEG data and the differential sensitivity of these metrics to zero-lag versus phase-lagged synchronization. Consequently, we encourage future studies to systematically evaluate a broader range of connectivity measures on dynamic collaborative data. Incorporating advanced effective connectivity approaches, such as adaptive directed transfer function (Wilke et al. 2008) or deep learning-based approaches (Chuang et al. 2024a, b), will help establish robust methodological standards and clarify the nuanced mechanisms of joint neural coordination. Third, the relationship between neural synchronization and cognitive map formulation should be interpreted with caution. The observed patterns likely represent a composite manifestation of collaborative states, encompassing simultaneous cognitive activities like shared attention, working memory, and verbal communication, rather than spatial mapping in isolation. Future research must disentangle these intertwined processes.

Conclusions

This study used hyperscanning EEG to examine intrabrain and interbrain connectivity during collaborative spatial navigation, providing evidence that can inform neuroergonomic design rather than only advancing theory. We identified distinct, role-specific neural strategies, with leaders showing stronger frontal–parietal connectivity consistent with planning and guidance, and followers exhibiting patterns aligned with monitoring, updating, and executing route instructions. These findings suggest that leader–follower coordination in real-world settings such as joint driving, aviation, security patrol, or emergency response may benefit from interfaces and training protocols that differentially support these roles. Interbrain analyses further indicate that more efficient dyads, who completed the task more quickly, exhibited connectivity patterns consistent with more optimized neural communication, particularly in the theta band. Theta-band connectivity may therefore serve as a candidate neural marker for effective collaboration that could be leveraged in adaptive systems, for example to monitor team coordination in real time and trigger supportive feedback when synchrony degrades.
