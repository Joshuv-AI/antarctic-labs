Introduction

Infections of prosthetic vascular grafts are rare but serious complications. They are associated with high rates of morbidity and mortality, particularly when diagnosis is delayed or surgical management is not feasible. Vascular graft infections with Listeria are uncommon and usually result from hematogenous seeding. The gold standard for treatment of infected vascular prostheses remains a complete surgical excision of the graft, reconstruction of an extra-anatomic bypass, combined with targeted antibiotic treatment.

This is a very rare case of an axillo-femoral graft infection caused by Listeria monocytogenes. Its presence poses diagnostic and therapeutic dilemmas, particularly in patients with a significant surgical history and high operative risk.

A written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Case report

A 59-year-old male with a history of advanced peripheral artery disease, ischemic heart disease, chronic alcohol use and multiple cardiovascular risk factors presented with one week of generalized weakness, morning nausea, and intermittent vomiting. He reported fever up to 39°C and anorexia. He also presented with intermittent diarrhea. There was no history of recent travel, illness contacts, or localized infectious symptoms. He was admitted to our hospital early July 2025.

Initial clinical examination was within normal limits. The patient was afebrile and had good parameters except low blood pressure at 94/64 mmHg at intake, but this was not confirmed in the hours after admission, where he had figures of a normal tension of 107/63 mmHg without any changes in medical treatment for this matter. The abdominal and cardiopulmonary exams were unremarkable except a systolic aortic murmur (4/6). Vascular exam revealed a bilateral carotid murmur. There was a notion of a collection surrounding the abdominal part of the left axillo-femoral bypass and discrete redness of the skin.

Laboratory testing showed high CRP-levels up to 226 mg/l, without hyperleukocytosis. Hemoglobin was 12 g/dl and other tests were normal, in particular liver, kidney and coagulation tests.

The patient was not considered being septic.

Past medical and surgical vascular history included: left axillo-femoral prosthetic bypass with a knitted reinforced Dacron graft, a femoral-femoral left-right crossover bypass with a biosynthetic graft, left subclavian stenting, bilateral carotid disease with right internal carotid artery stenting, complete thrombosis of the left carotid arteries and a trans-tibial amputation of the left leg. The left axillo-femoral and femoral-femoral bypasses were done in November 2021.

A computerized tomographic (CT) scan revealed a well-encapsulated collection surrounding the left axillofemoral bypass graft, suggesting infection. No intra-abdominal pathology was noted (Fig. 1).

CT-scan revealed a well-encapsulated collection surrounding the left axillofemoral bypass graft.

A leucocytes SPECT-CT scan showed no clear image for infection, but signs of inflammation on the axillo-femoral bypass and the collection described on CT scan before (Fig. 2).

SPECT-CT scan showed signs of inflammation on the axillo-femoral bypass (middle arrow) and the well-encapsulated collection (outher arrows) surrounding the graft.

Trans-thoracic echo of the heart showed no signs of endocarditis and a slightly altered cardiac output (45–50%).

Puncture and needle aspiration of the peri-prosthetic collection was performed and the cultures yielded growth of Listeria monocytogenes, whereas blood cultures remained negative, except one sample positive on Micrococcus luteus.

An additional noteworthy finding was the isolated elevation in lipase levels (440 U/L) without clinical or radiological features of pancreatitis. This may represent subclinical pancreatic enzyme release in the context of chronic alcohol consumption and metabolic stress, rather than true acute pancreatitis. Furthermore, the patient displayed a macrocytic anemia with low folate levels, probably compatible with chronic alcohol use and nutritional deficiency. The patient was not immunosuppressed with any other known medical condition. No other clear risk factors were identified for the Listeria infection.

Despite recommendation for surgical graft explantation, reconstruction by another extra-anatomic bypass, combined with targeted antibiotic treatment, the patient refused. The mean reason for his refusal was the complexity and extension of this intervention. He also refused surgical drainage of this peri-prosthetic collection for bacteriological sampling. He accepted long term medical therapy.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test showed resistance for Penicillin G, but the Listeria was sensitive for Ampicillin, Meropenem and Erythromycin. Ampicillin was an obvious choice as a medical treatment because of better tissue penetration than Erythromycin and narrower spectrum than Meronem. He was treated conservatively with high-dose (6 times 2000 mg per day) amoxicillin by intravenous administration initially for 2 weeks, followed by long-term oral administration of 1000 mg, three times a day, which is very well tolerated. Under this antibiotic treatment, the CRP-level dropped from 161.8 mg/l to 6.2 mg/l. No drainage of the collection by puncture nor surgical removal was performed. The intravenous administration was stopped after two weeks at patient’s request, so oral administration was started.

The patient is doing well after an eleven-months follow-up period, with a significant regression of the peri-prosthetic collection surrounding the knitted Dacron prosthesis on CT scan (Fig. 3). CRP-level is low at 1.3 mg/l. Clinical examination was normal, with disappearance of the redness of the skin and no collection could be identified. The antibiotic treatment is still ongoing. We have planned a new CT scan and SPECT-CT scan at one year of follow-up, before a multidisciplinary decision will be taken, whether or not to continue antibiotic therapy.

CT-scan at six months interval, revealed a significant regression of the peri-prosthetic collection surrounding the knitted Dacron prosthesis after a six-months treatment.

Discussion

Infections of prosthetic vascular grafts are relatively rare but serious complications, with an incidence estimated between 0.5% and 6% depending on graft location and patient risk factors. They are associated with high rates of morbidity and mortality, particularly when diagnosis is delayed or surgical management is not feasible [1], [2]. The most common pathogens involved are Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and gram-negative bacilli. In contrast, Listeria monocytogenes is a very rare but increasingly recognized cause of vascular graft infection, especially in immunocompromised hosts or patients with chronic comorbidities [3], [4], [5].

Listeria monocytogenes is a facultative anaerobic intracellular Gram-positive bacillus primarily associated with foodborne infections, often causing meningitis or bacteremia in at-risk patients, such as the elderly, pregnant women, and those with impaired cellular immunity [5], [6]. Vascular graft infections with Listeria are uncommon and most likely result from hematogenous seeding in the setting of a bacteremia, but this remains a hypothesized mechanism. Chronic alcohol use, as seen in this patient, is known to impair immune function and may have contributed to an increased susceptibility [4].

In this case, the patient presented with systemic signs of infection and an inflammatory syndrome without a clear source. The diagnosis of vascular graft infection was suspected following abdominal CT imaging, which revealed a well-encapsulated collection along the axillofemoral graft [7]. Notably, the patient had no signs of sepsis, localized pain, or graft instability, highlighting the often insidious and nonspecific presentation of such infections [8]. Due to the patient’s refusal for surgical drainage or drainage by aspiration of the collection, only percutaneous needle aspiration could be performed which could be a limiting factor for diagnostic certainty. The patient was not considered being septic at admission, because he had only one criterium (high CRP-levels), following the international guidelines for the management of sepsis [9].

The most common microbiological diagnosis in the literature was through positive blood cultures; nevertheless, more than half of the patients (55%) had negative blood cultures like in our case [5]. Microbiologic diagnosis was obtained via culturing percutaneous drained aspirations or biopsy taken during surgery [5].

Lupia T et al., did a comprehensive review of the literature on vascular graft infections due to Listeria spp in 2025. They found that the majority of infections of the reported cases in the literature were associated with abdominal aortic grafts (50%), followed by femoro-popliteal grafts (20%) and thoracic aortic grafts (10%). Other types of less common vascular prostheses involvement are iliac grafts and arterio-venous grafts. There was no mention for involvement of axillo-femoral grafts [5]. A search we performed on PubMed, did not reveal any report describing an axillofemoral graft infection with Listeria monocytogenes. To our knowledge, this is the first published case of an axillofemoral graft infection with Listeria monocytogenes at the time of our paper submission.

The gold standard for treatment of infected vascular prostheses remains a complete surgical excision of the graft, reconstruction of an extra-anatomic bypass, combined with targeted antibiotic treatment. However, surgical removal carries substantial risk, particularly in patients with diffuse atherosclerosis, multiple prior vascular surgeries, and compromised cardiopulmonary function. In this case, the patient declined surgical intervention, leading to the decision for long-term suppressive antibiotic therapy with amoxicillin, given Listeria’s susceptibility profile [10]. Most of the patients in the literature were treated with a combination of ampicillin and a aminoglycoside [5]. However, evidence supporting the efficacy of a combination therapy in Listeria monocytogenes infections is lacking in the literature [11].

Few cases of Listeria-related vascular prosthetic infections have been reported in the literature, and outcomes are variable. Some authors advocate for aggressive surgical management given the potential for graft-related sepsis or rupture, while others report success with conservative therapy in selected cases. This case contributes to the growing body of evidence that non-operative management may be a viable option in highly selected, non-toxic patients who are poor surgical candidates or who decline surgery [3], [5], [8], [10]. In there review of the literature, Lupia et al., found half of the patients were treated with medical treatment only [5].

An additional noteworthy finding was the isolated elevation in lipase levels (440 U/L) without clinical or radiological features of pancreatitis. This may represent subclinical pancreatic enzyme release in the context of chronic alcohol consumption and metabolic stress, rather than true acute pancreatitis. Furthermore, the patient displayed a macrocytic anemia with low folate levels, again compatible with chronic alcohol use and nutritional deficiency [12].

Conclusion

Overall, this is an exceedingly rare case of Listeria monocytogenes vascular graft infection in a patient with diffuse atherosclerosis and multiple comorbidities, illustrating the complexity of managing vascular graft infections in multimorbid patients and underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach, weighing surgical risk, pathogen virulence, and patient preferences.
