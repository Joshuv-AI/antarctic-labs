Near-Optimal Regret in Adversarial Kernel Bandits
Yu-Jie Zhang, Hao Qiu, Jonathan Scarlett, Kevin Jamieson

Abstract
We study the adversarial kernel bandit problem, in which the loss at each round is induced by an arbitrary bounded element of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). We propose an exponential-weights algorithm built on a regularized importance-weighted loss estimator, together with an explicit correction term that cancels the bias introduced by the regularization. Our main result bounds the regret by 𝒪 ~ ​ ( T ​ d ∗ ​ ( λ ) ​ log ⁡ | 𝒳 | ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\sqrt{T\,d_{*}(\lambda)\,\log|\mathcal{X}|}\big) , where d ∗ ​ ( λ ) d_{*}(\lambda) is a widely-adopted notion of effective dimension that captures the complexity of the kernel. Up to logarithmic factors, this matches the known rate achieved in the related stochastic kernel bandit problem. A notable application is the Matérn ( ν , d ) (\nu,d) kernel with smoothness parameter ν \nu on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , for which our bound specializes to 𝒪 ~ ​ ( T ( ν + d ) / ( 2 ​ ν + d ) ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(T^{(\nu+d)/(2\nu+d)}\big) , improving over the best-known prior rate of 11 while simultaneously removing the rank-one adversary assumption required by their analysis. Moreover, this rate is the same as the known optimal rate for stochastic kernel bandits, and also matches a lower bound from concurrent work up to a log ⁡ T \log T factor.

Introduction

Online decision-making under bandit feedback (e.g., see
9
;
17
;
24
) is a fundamental problem in machine learning, with applications ranging from recommendation systems
(
25
)
to experimental design
(
33
)
. In the classical adversarial bandit setting, each arm has an arbitrary loss sequence, a learner repeatedly selects actions and observes only the loss incurred by the chosen action, with the goal of competing against the best fixed action in hindsight. A well-studied extension of this framework is adversarial linear bandits
(
3
;
14
;
4
)
, where the loss of an action at each time step is assumed to be a linear function of its features. While this linear structure enables efficient learning and strong regret guarantees, it is often too restrictive to capture complex, nonlinear relationships present in modern applications.

To address this limitation, we study the
adversarial kernel bandits
problem
(
11
;
35
)
, in which losses are modeled as functions in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). This framework strictly generalizes linear bandits by allowing nonlinear losses with a potentially infinite-dimensional feature representation. It serves as an adversarially time-varying counterpart to the well-studied stochastic kernel bandit problem, which is typically viewed through the lens of Bayesian optimization via Gaussian process methods
(
16
)
.
The added expressivity of RKHS functions (compared to linear functions) can be highly valuable in diverse applications. This is evidenced by the extensive applications of the stochastic formulation, such as hyperparameter tuning
12
, molecule design
23
, environmental monitoring
29
, and many more. The adversarial formulation is of interest in scenarios where the underlying function may be subject to unknown dynamic behavior, changepoints, and so on, and also has a strong theoretical precedent based on the extensive work on adversarial (non-kernel) bandits.

The additional expressiveness of this setup introduces significant challenges: under bandit feedback, the learner observes only a single scalar loss per round, making it difficult to estimate loss functions whose underlying structure may be infinite-dimensional. Despite its practical and theoretical importance, existing regret guarantees for adversarial kernel bandits remain incomplete for some of the most commonly used kernels. The closest prior work,
11
, considers kernels whose Mercer eigenvalues exhibit either exponential or polynomial decay. In the former case, which includes the squared-exponential (SE) kernel, they obtain a near-optimal
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
rate. Under polynomial eigendecay, however, their bound is no longer tight. This looseness is particularly evident for the Matérn
(\nu,d)
kernel, a standard choice in Gaussian process modeling and Bayesian optimization. For this kernel, their rate becomes
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{(2\nu+d)/(4\nu)})
, which is far from the
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{(\nu+d)/(2\nu+d)})
rate known to be optimal in the analogous stochastic kernel bandit problem. In fact, it fails to be sublinear in
T
when the smoothness
\nu
is small relative to the ambient dimension
d
, namely
\nu<d/2
. This gap motivates the central question of this paper:

Can one design an adversarial kernel bandit algorithm whose regret

matches the optimal stochastic kernel bandit rate (within log factors)?

Contributions.

In this paper, we make substantial progress on this question, including an affirmative answer for the Matérn kernel. We propose a simple and principled algorithm for adversarial kernel bandits that combines exponential weights with a regularized importance-weighted loss estimator in the RKHS. The regularization prevents the estimator from becoming unbounded in infinite-dimensional feature spaces, while an explicit correction term compensates for the bias introduced by regularization. This correction is central to the analysis: it cancels the comparator-side bias and leaves only a learner-side residual that can be controlled through a well-known notion of
effective dimension
of the kernel.

Our main result is a regret bound of the form
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T\,d_{*}(\lambda)\,\log|\mathcal{X}|})
, where
d_{*}(\lambda)
is the effective dimension of the kernel and
|\mathcal{X}|
is the size of the action set. Up to logarithmic factors, this matches the rate achievable in the analogous stochastic kernel bandit problem when expressed via the maximum information gain (as defined in
(
33
)
among many others). In particular, when specialized to the Matérn
(\nu,d)
kernel, our bound yields the regret
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{(\nu+d)/(2\nu+d)})
, improving over the best-known prior rate
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{(2\nu+d)/(4\nu)})
of
11
while simultaneously removing their restrictive rank-one adversary assumption. This rate further matches the lower bound appearing in a concurrent work
(
20
)
up to logarithmic factors in
T
. For the SE kernel, our bound recovers the near-optimal
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
rate already established by
11
, while again removing the rank-one assumption. Our bound also yields corollaries under suitably-defined polynomial and exponential eigendecay conditions on the kernel spectrum: Under polynomial eigenvalue decay with exponent
\beta>1
, our algorithm achieves
regret

\widetilde{O}\big(T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta)}\big),

and under exponential eigenvalue decay, the regret improves to

\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T}).

Although the polynomial-decay rate has the same expression in the exponent as a lower bound from
11
, the two results are proved under different settings and are not directly comparable; see
Appendix
B.1

Concurrent work.

In independent concurrent work,
20
closes the same gap through an approach that is algorithmically and analytically close to ours. Both works use exponential weights with a regularized importance-weighted loss estimator, together with an additive correction term that cancels the bias introduced by the regularization. There are some minor differences in the problem setup and complexity measures used, but these are essentially inconsequential; see
Appendix
B.2

Beyond this shared core,

Related Works

Stochastic kernel bandits.

There have been extensive studies on the stochastic kernel bandit problem, in which the underlying function is fixed and the observations are corrupted by noise. Here we focus only on the most fundamental and/or relevant works. Prominent early works studied UCB-style algorithms
33
;
13

\widetilde{O}(\gamma_{T}\sqrt{T})
, where
\gamma_{T}
is an
information gain

effective dimension
(e.g., see
(
7
, Prop. 5)

\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T\gamma_{T}})
via the SupKernelUCB algorithm
37

26
;
8

31

\gamma_{T}
were derived in
36

\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T\gamma_{T}})

32

5

\beta>1
, the information gain scales as
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{1/\beta})
, yielding a regret bound of order
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\frac{\beta+1}{2\beta}})
, whereas for exponential eigenvalue decay the regret becomes
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})

Various works have also studied non-stationary stochastic kernel bandits (see
18
;
6
;
19
and the references therein), but their setup is fundamentally different from adversarial kernel bandits, with results from the two settings being incomparable. Specifically, they seek to minimize
dynamic regret
(i.e., track the maximum at every time step) and they crucially place explicit bounds on how much the function can vary.

Adversarial linear bandits.

The adversarial linear bandit problem was introduced to the online learning literature by
3
. In this setting, a learner repeatedly selects an action from a decision set and incurs a loss that is linear in the chosen action, while the loss vectors may be chosen adversarially. A wide collection of subsequent work has developed efficient algorithms for this problem. In particular, early results established polynomial-time algorithms for compact convex action sets with regret scaling as
\widetilde{O}(d\sqrt{T})
, where
d
denotes the dimension of the linear bandit problem, using variants of exponential weights combined with additional exploration

14

10
;
4
)

\widetilde{O}(d^{3/2}\sqrt{T})

Adversarial kernel bandits.

The closest prior work on adversarial kernel bandits is
11

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})

3
Problem Setup

This section introduces the problem setup for adversarial kernel bandits, a generalization of adversarial linear bandits in which the loss functions belong to a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). We begin by stating the kernel loss model and assumptions, and then present the learning protocol and define the notion of regret.

Kernels and RKHS.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an action space. We will focus on the case that
\mathcal{X}
is finite (possibly very large), but our main result extends easily to continuous domains via a standard covering argument, e.g., for the domain
[0,1]^{d}
, taking a grid of size
T^{d/2}
leads to negligible discretization error under mild assumptions on the kernel
21
;
26
.

We are given a symmetric positive definite kernel
\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, and denote by
\mathcal{H}
its associated reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). The RKHS
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space of functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, equipped with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
and norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}
. It satisfies the reproducing property: for every
f\in\mathcal{H}
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,
f(\mathbf{x})=\langle f,\mathcal{K}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.
The kernel also induces a feature map
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),\Phi(\mathbf{y})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{X}
. The feature vector
\Phi(\mathbf{x})
may be infinite-dimensional. When
\mathcal{K}
admits a Mercer decomposition under a reference measure, an explicit feature map can be constructed by setting
\Phi_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{\mu_{j}}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
for each
j\geq 1
, where
\Phi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the
j
-th coordinate of
\Phi(\mathbf{x})
, and
\{\mu_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
and
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
are the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the associated kernel integral operator.
We refer the reader to
(
11
, Section 2.1)
and
22
for further background on RKHS functions.

We use the following operator notation on
\mathcal{H}
. For
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{H}
, we use the matrix-style notation
\mathbf{v}\mathbf{w}^{\top}
to denote the rank-one linear operator on
\mathcal{H}
defined by
(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{w}^{\top})\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}\,\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
for all
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{H}
. The identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
I_{\mathcal{H}}
. For a bounded, self-adjoint, positive semidefinite operator
A:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and any
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{H}
, we define
\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_{A}:=\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{w},A\mathbf{w}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}
,
which is a seminorm in general and a norm when
A
is positive definite. We write
A\preceq B
if
B-A
is positive semidefinite.

Learning protocol.

Throughout the paper, we use
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
to denote the set of positive integers and
[m]
to denote the set
\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
for
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. At each round
t\in[T]
, the learner selects an action
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
while the environment simultaneously selects a vector
\mathbf{w}_{t}\in\mathcal{H}
. The learner then incurs the loss of its decision
\ell_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.
Afterward, the learner observes only this scalar loss
\ell_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
and then proceeds to the next round. We consider an oblivious adversary, in which the sequence
\{\mathbf{w}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
is fixed before the interaction begins and does not depend on the learner’s realized actions or internal randomness. The goal of the learner is to minimize the expected regret:

\mathcal{R}_{T}\;:=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\;-\;\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right],

where
\mathbf{x}_{*}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the best fixed action in hindsight and the expectation is over the randomness in the algorithm. We also impose the following standard regularity assumptions, which are common in the literature
(
11
;
26
)
.

Assumption 1

(Bounded Kernel and Adversary)
.

There exists a constant
B>0
such that
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq B
for all
t\in[T]
. The kernel is bounded by
\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})\leq 1
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
.
2
2

2

This assumption easily generalizes to any upper bound of the form
\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})\leq G^{2}
for a fixed constant
G
, but we focus on
\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})\leq 1
to reduce notation and since this normalization is very standard in the stochastic kernel bandit literature (e.g., see
33
).

Remark 1
.

We note that the adversarial kernel bandit model of
11
imposes a
rank-one
restriction on the adversary, which requires
\mathbf{w}_{t}=\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})
for some
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
. This is quite restrictive, and to our knowledge it has not been considered in any works on stochastic kernel bandits. Our framework removes this restriction, allowing
\mathbf{w}_{t}
to be an arbitrary element of the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
subject only to a bounded norm condition. See Figure
1
for an illustration. The rank-one restriction in
11
is tied to their projection-based loss estimator, whereas our use of a regularized loss estimator frees us from this requirement.

4
Exponential Weights with Regularized Estimator

In this section, we develop a regularized variant of Exp2 for adversarial kernel bandits and derive its regret in terms of the effective dimension. Up to logarithmic factors, this matches the rate achievable in the analogous stochastic kernel bandit problem, and specializes to near-optimal rates for commonly used kernels such as the SE and Matérn kernels. We first step through the challenges that arise when extending Exp2 from linear to kernel losses and explain why existing approaches fall short, then present the algorithm and its regret guarantee.

4.1
Exp2 and the Kernel Obstacle

A central challenge in moving from adversarial linear bandits to kernel bandits is that the underlying parameter
\mathbf{w}_{t}
may be infinite-dimensional. This added expressiveness brings a fundamental algorithmic difficulty: The standard importance-weighted loss estimator from linear bandits can take values of unbounded magnitude when the feature map lies in an infinite-dimensional RKHS, which rules out a direct application of linear-bandit techniques.

To make this concrete, we briefly recall the Exp2 algorithm developed for linear bandits. At each round
t
, Exp2 maintains a distribution
p_{t}
over the action set, samples
\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p_{t}
, and observes the scalar loss
y_{t}=\langle\mathbf{w}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t}\rangle
. From this single observation, it constructs the importance-weighted estimator

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\;=\;y_{t}\,\Sigma_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{t},\qquad\Sigma_{t}\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{t}}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}].

Exp2 then updates
p_{t}
by exponential weights on the loss proxy
\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x}):=\langle\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t},\mathbf{x}\rangle
. The Exp2 algorithm requires a positive lower bound on the minimum eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{t}
, which is ensured by mixing
p_{t}
with a suitable exploration distribution (e.g., John’s distribution), and yields the optimal regret
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{d\,T\log|\mathcal{X}|})

(
14
;
4
)
. Translating this construction to the kernel setting amounts to replacing
\mathbf{x}
by
\Phi(\mathbf{x})
throughout, with
\Sigma_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{t}}[\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}]
now acting on the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
. However, in an infinite-dimensional RKHS, the covariance operator
\Sigma_{t}
may have eigenvalues arbitrarily close to zero, which can make the loss estimator unbounded.

Existing biased estimators and limitations.

To circumvent this issue, existing work
(
11
;
30
)
resorts to biased estimators that trade some bias for variance control.
11
construct a finite-dimensional proxy kernel by projecting the Mercer expansion of
\mathcal{K}
to its top-
m
eigenfunctions, and run Exp2 in this
m
-dimensional space. While the truncation restores invertibility of the covariance and makes Exp2 applicable, the bias it introduces is hard to balance tightly against the estimator variance, leaving room for improvement in the resulting bound. When the kernel satisfies a polynomial eigenvalue decay condition with exponent
\beta>1
, their analysis yields a regret bound of order
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{\beta/(2(\beta-1))})
. This gives a suboptimal rate for Matérn kernels and leaves a gap to the lower bound
\Omega(T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta)})
established in the same paper. Moreover, to control the bias introduced by their projection-based estimator,
11
restrict the adversary to rank-one losses
\mathbf{w}_{t}=\Phi(\mathbf{z}_{t})
, so that the loss can be written as
\ell_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{t})
. Our approach removes this restriction and handles arbitrary bounded RKHS losses
\mathbf{w}_{t}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{H}}
with
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq B
.

As outlined in the introduction,
30
consider the kernelized contextual bandit setting and design a different biased estimator via Kernel Geometric Resampling, a finite-step truncation of a Neumann-series approximation to
\Sigma_{t}^{-1}
. Even with this approximation, the small eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{t}
still leave the loss estimator with potentially large magnitude, so they replace exponential weights with a FTRL update under log-barrier regularization, which is more robust to unbounded loss estimators. Their bound matches the optimal
T
-exponent
T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta)}
under polynomial eigenvalue decay. Although their setting is not the same as ours, their estimator can also be applied to our setting, but the use of log-barrier inherently incurs at least a
\sqrt{|\mathcal{X}|}
dependence on the action-set cardinality, which is highly unfavorable in the kernel bandit setting as we discussed at the end of Section
2
.

4.2
Our Algorithm

Our method follows the Exp2 template with two key modifications targeting the obstacles identified above: a
regularized estimator
that keeps the loss estimator bounded despite the vanishing eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{t}
, and a
correction term
that compensates for the bias introduced by regularization.

Regularized estimator.

We replace the design covariance
\Sigma_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{t}}[\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}]
with its ridge-regularized version
\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda}:=\Sigma_{t}+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}}
and form the importance-weighted estimator

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\;=\;y_{t}\cdot(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\qquad y_{t}=\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(1)

The added
\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}}
term enforces a positive lower bound on the spectrum of
\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda}
, keeping
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
bounded even when
\Sigma_{t}
has eigenvalues close to zero; the price paid for this is bias. Letting
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
denote the filtration of the history before round
t
, a direct calculation (deferred to
Lemma
4
in the appendix) shows that the conditional bias of
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
takes the explicit form

\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\;=\;\lambda\,(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\mathbf{w}_{t}.

(2)

This explicit form is what the correction term introduced next will be designed to cancel.

Correction term.

To compensate for this regularization bias, we define the loss proxy

\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\langle\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t},\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-c_{t}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad c_{t}(\mathbf{x})\;=\;B\sqrt{\lambda}\,\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}.

(3)

c_{t}
offsets the comparator-side bias

B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}),\mathbf{w}_{t}-\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\bigr]

and makes the resulting residual non-positive, leaving only the learner-side bias, which is more tractable to bound.

We note that the use of a correction term arises in various online-learning settings
(
34
;
38
)
, where it serves to translate a bias (or error) term from the comparator side to the learner side of the regret decomposition. In the context of adversarial linear/kernel bandits and policy optimization, different forms of correction have been designed to compensate for the bias of different loss estimators
(
28
;
27
;
30
)
. Our correction arises naturally from the closed-form bias of the regularized loss estimator and integrates seamlessly into the exponential-weights update without inflating the order of the loss-estimator range, which is essential for the regret analysis.

Overall algorithm.

Combining the two ingredients above with a mixing of exploration distribution and exponential-weights update yields
Algorithm
1
. At each round
t
, the learner samples
\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p_{t}=(1-\gamma)q_{t}+\gamma\nu_{G}
, a mixture of the current distribution
q_{t}
and a fixed exploration distribution
\nu_{G}
given by a regularized G-optimal design (e.g., see
(
8
)
). Upon observing
y_{t}
, it forms the corrected loss proxy
\widehat{\ell}_{t}
via (
1
) and (
3
) and updates
q_{t+1}
by exponential weights on
\widehat{\ell}_{t}
. Although defined through
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\in\mathcal{H}
, the proxy
\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})
admits a closed-form expression in terms of kernel evaluations on
\mathcal{X}
, so
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
is never instantiated explicitly. We defer the computational details to the end of this section.

Remark 2

(Computational Cost)
.

The computational cost of
Algorithm
1
mainly comes from two parts: evaluating the loss proxy
\widehat{\ell}_{t}
at each round, and computing the exploration distribution
\nu_{G}
once at initialization. Both can be implemented entirely via kernel evaluations
\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),\Phi(\mathbf{y})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
, without ever instantiating the feature map
\Phi(\cdot)
explicitly. The two terms in the loss proxy
\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\langle\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t},\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-c_{t}(\mathbf{x})
are both bilinear forms
\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Phi(\mathbf{y})
in the regularized covariance. By
8
, any such form admits the closed-form expression

\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Phi(\mathbf{y})\;=\;\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\bigl(k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-k_{t}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}(K_{t}+\lambda I_{|\mathcal{X}|})^{-1}\,k_{t}(\mathbf{y})\bigr),

where
[K_{t}]_{i,j}=\sqrt{p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\,p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j})}\,k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
and
[k_{t}(\mathbf{x})]_{i}=\sqrt{p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{i})}\,k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x})
are both expressed only through kernel evaluations on
\mathcal{X}
. The dominant per-round cost is the inversion of
K_{t}+\lambda I_{|\mathcal{X}|}
at
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{X}|^{3})
. Once this is computed, each evaluation of
\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})
takes
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{X}|^{2})
. This complexity might ideally be brought down to at most linear in
|\mathcal{X}|
(the cost of iterating over the arms), but we leave this for future work. In practice, since our estimator follows the same structure as kernel ridge regression (KRR), acceleration techniques, such as Nyström
(
15
)
or random Fourier feature
(
2
)
approximations, can also be applied.

The exploration distribution
\nu_{G}\in\arg\min_{\nu\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert^{2}_{(\Sigma(\nu)+(\lambda/\gamma)I_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}}
depends only on the fixed parameters
\lambda
and
\gamma
, so it is computed once at initialization. The objective is convex in
\nu
and its gradients are again bilinear forms of the same kind, so
\nu_{G}
can be obtained by first-order methods using only kernel evaluations
(
8
)
.

Bounding

The effective dimension

at noise level
\sigma^{2}=1

in

shows that

the regret bound of

\mathcal{R}_{T}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(B\sqrt{\bar{\gamma}_{T}\,T\,\log|\mathcal{X}|}\right).

In particular, when
B
is constant and
\log|\mathcal{X}|=O(\log T)

Remark 3
.

While the adversarial kernel bandit problem intuitively seems to be more difficult than the stochastic counterpart, we highlight that neither is a special case of the other. In particular, the additive noise in the stochastic problem can create bottlenecks that are not present in the adversarial problem, and for linear rewards these can actually lead to strictly higher regret; see for example

Bounding
d_{*}
via eigenvalue decay conditions.

While relating the effective dimension to the maximum information gain (which has been studied extensively previously) is a natural approach to obtaining results for specific kernels, another route is to place assumptions directly on the kernel eigenvalue decay and upper bound
d_{*}(\lambda)
directly based on that decay, with suitable tuning of

\eta
. Corollaries 1 and 2 below carry this out for polynomial and exponential eigenvalue decay, respectively, defined as follows.

Definition 1

(Eigenvalue Decay)
.

Let
p
be any probability measure over
\mathcal{X}
, and let

\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0
denote the eigenvalues of the covariance operator

\Sigma=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p}\!\left[\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\right].

1.

\mathcal{K}
is said to have
(C,\beta)
-polynomial eigenvalue decay, with
\beta>1
, if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\mu_{j}\leq Cj^{-\beta}

for all integers
j\geq 1
, regardless of
p
.

2.

\mathcal{K}
is said to have
(C,\beta)
-exponential eigenvalue decay if there exist constants
C>0
and
\beta>0
such that

\mu_{j}\leq Ce^{-\beta j}

for all integers
j\geq 1
, regardless of
p
.

It is worth noting that the uniformity over
p
makes this definition more restrictive than the assumption of similar decay for a fixed measure (e.g., see
(
11
, Definition 11)
). Nevertheless, under these conditions, we have the following corollaries, whose proofs are deferred to
Appendix
A.6
.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{K}
has
(C,\beta)
-polynomial eigenvalue decay defined in
Definition
1
. Under
Assumption
1
, Algorithm
1
initialized with the uniform distribution

q_{1}
over
\mathcal{X}
and run with

m=\left\lceil T^{1/\beta}\right\rceil,\quad\lambda=m^{-\beta},\quad\gamma=m^{-\frac{\beta-1}{2}},\quad\eta=\frac{1}{2B(1+C/(\beta-1))}\,m^{-\frac{\beta+1}{2}}

guarantees

\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(B\left(1+\left(1+\frac{C}{\beta-1}\right)\log|\mathcal{X}|+\sqrt{1+\frac{C}{\beta-1}}\right)T^{\frac{\beta+1}{2\beta}}\right).

Thus, when
(B,\beta,C)
are constant and
\log|\mathcal{X}|=O(\log T)
, we have
\mathcal{R}_{T}=O\big(T^{\frac{\beta+1}{2\beta}}\log T\big)
.

Corollary 3
.

If
\mathcal{K}
has
(C,\beta)
-exponential eigenvalue decay defined in

Definition
1
. Under
Assumption
1
, Algorithm
1

initialized with the uniform distribution
q_{1}
over
\mathcal{X}
and run with

m=\left\lceil\frac{\log T}{\beta}\right\rceil,\quad\lambda=e^{-\beta m},\quad\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}{T}},\quad\eta=\frac{\gamma}{2B\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)},

\gamma\leq 1

\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(B\sqrt{T\left(\frac{\log T}{\beta}+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}\right).

Thus, when
(B,\beta,C)
are constant and

\log|\mathcal{X}|=O(\log T)

\mathcal{R}_{T}=O\!\big(\sqrt{T\log T}\big).

Remark 4
.

For the exponential decay case, the regret bound is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
, which matches the tight rate in
11
. For the polynomial decay case, our regret has the key dependence
\widetilde{O}\big(T^{\frac{\beta+1}{2\beta}}\big)
, which improves over the previous
\widetilde{O}\big(T^{\frac{\beta}{2(\beta-1)}}\big)
upper bound from

T

\log|\mathcal{X}|

Proof of Theorem

Let

\mathcal{F}_{t-1}=\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{w}_{t-1})
as the history available before round

and the learner’s prediction distribution

are
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}

\mathbf{x}_{t}

applying the tower property of conditional expectation yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right]

(4)

6
Conclusion

For the adversarial kernel bandit problem, we proposed an exponential-weights algorithm with a regularized importance-weighted loss estimator and a correction term that mitigates the regularization bias. We established a regret bound of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(B\sqrt{T\,d_{*}(\lambda)\,\log|\mathcal{X}|}\big)
, which matches the optimal rate of the analogous stochastic kernel bandit problem up to logarithmic factors. As a direct consequence, our result yields a near-optimal
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(T^{(\nu+d)/(2\nu+d)}\big)
regret bound for the Matérn
(\nu,d)
kernel, improving over the best-known prior rate of
11
while removing their rank-one adversary assumption. We also derived corollaries under structural assumptions on the kernel spectrum, obtaining
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta)}\big)
regret under polynomial eigenvalue decay with exponent
\beta>1
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
regret under exponential eigenvalue decay. Possible future directions include obtaining high-probability regret bounds and considering the contextual kernel bandit setting.

A.1
Useful Lemmas

The following lemma characterizes the conditional expectation of the regularized
importance-weighted estimator. It shows that the estimator is biased due to
regularization: instead of recovering
\mathbf{w}_{t}
in expectation, it returns a
shrunk version
(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}=(I_{\mathcal{H}}-\lambda(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1})w_{t}
.
This explicit form of the bias will be useful in controlling the effect of
regularization in the regret analysis.

Lemma 4

(Conditional Mean of Loss Estimator)
.

Fix any round
t\in[T]
, and let

\Sigma_{t}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{t}}[\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}]
be the unregularized covariance operator. Then for any
\lambda>0
,
\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda}=\Sigma_{t}+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}}.

Conditioning on the history
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, the estimator

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}=\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\quad(\mathbf{x}_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\sim p_{t},

satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\,\mathbf{w}_{t}=\bigl(I_{\mathcal{H}}-\lambda(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\bigr)\mathbf{w}_{t}.

(10)

Proof.

We condition on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, meaning that
p_{t}
,
\mathbf{w}_{t}
,
\Sigma_{t}
, and

(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}
are fixed. Thus,

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{t}}\!\left[\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,\Phi(\mathbf{x})\right].

By the definition of
\Sigma_{t}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{t}}\!\left[\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,\Phi(\mathbf{x})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{t}}\!\left[\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\right]\mathbf{w}_{t}=\Sigma_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t},

which implies

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}.

Finally, since
\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda}=\Sigma_{t}+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}}
, we have

(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}=(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}})=I_{\mathcal{H}}-\lambda(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}.

Substituting this identity gives
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\bigl(I_{\mathcal{H}}-\lambda(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\bigr)\mathbf{w}_{t}
as claimed.
∎

The following lemma establishes a uniform bound on the corrected proxy loss. This boundedness condition allows us to invoke the standard exponential-weights regret guarantee despite the estimator being defined in a potentially infinite-dimensional RKHS.

Lemma 5

(Bounded Loss Estimator)
.

Under Assumption
1
and condition (
8
), and assuming
\lambda\leq 1/(2\eta B)
, the loss proxy
\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})

satisfies
|\eta\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})|\leq 1.

Proof.

By the definition of the loss proxy, for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
we have

\displaystyle|\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})|

\displaystyle\leq|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t},\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}|+B\sqrt{\lambda}\,\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\,\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}+B\sqrt{\lambda}\,\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq B\,\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}\,\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}+B\sqrt{\lambda}\,\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}},

(11)

where the second inequality holds using the definition
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}=\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})
, and applying
|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t},\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle|=|\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle\cdot\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})\rangle|\leq\lVert{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\,\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}
by Assumption
1
and two applications of Cauchy-Schwarz. The last inequality uses
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq B
, again by Assumption
1
.

Next, we derive a uniform upper bound on

\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}
. Recall that Algorithm
1

uses the exploration distribution

\nu_{G}\in\argmin_{\nu\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})}M_{\lambda/\gamma}(\nu),

where
M_{\lambda/\gamma}(\nu):=\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert^{2}_{\left(\Sigma(\nu)+\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}I_{\mathcal{H}}\right)^{-1}}
and
\Sigma(\nu):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\nu}\!\left[\Phi(\mathbf{z})\Phi(\mathbf{z})^{\top}\right].

By
8
, we can relate
M_{\lambda/\gamma}
to the effective dimension as follows:

M_{\lambda/\gamma}(\nu_{G})\leq\mathrm{Tr}\Big((\Sigma(\nu_{D})+\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}I_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}\Sigma(\nu_{D})\Big)=d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(\nu_{D}),\lambda/\gamma),

(12)

where
\nu_{D}\in\argmax_{\nu\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})}\log\det\!\big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\nu}[\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}]+\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}I_{\mathcal{H}}\big)
.
Furthermore, since
p_{t}=(1-\gamma)q_{t}+\gamma\nu_{G}
, we have

\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda}=\Sigma_{t}+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}}\succeq\gamma\Sigma(\nu_{G})+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}}=\gamma\Big(\Sigma(\nu_{G})+\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}I_{\mathcal{H}}\Big).

By monotonicity of matrix inversion, we have

(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\preceq\frac{1}{\gamma}\big(\Sigma(\nu_{G})+\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}I_{\mathcal{H}}\big)^{-1},

which implies for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
that

\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert^{2}_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}M_{\lambda/\gamma}(\nu_{G})\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(\nu_{D}),\lambda/\gamma).

(13)

Finally, substituting (
13
) into (
11
), we obtain

|\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})|\leq\frac{B}{\gamma}d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(\nu_{D}),\lambda/\gamma)+B\sqrt{\frac{\lambda\,d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(\nu_{D}),\lambda/\gamma)}{\gamma}}\leq\frac{1}{2\eta}+\sqrt{\frac{B\lambda}{2\eta}},

where the last inequality follows from condition (
8
).
Using
\lambda\leq 1/(2\eta B)
, it then follows that
|\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})|\leq\frac{1}{\eta}
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, and hence

|\eta\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})|\leq 1.

This completes the proof.
∎

The next lemma translates the eigenvalue-decay condition into explicit upper bounds on the effective dimension under both polynomial and exponential decay regimes. These bounds are then used to specialize the general effective-dimension regret bound to each decay setting.

Lemma 6
.

Define the effective dimension

d_{*}(\xi):=\max_{p\in\Delta}d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(p),\xi),

where

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(p),\xi):=\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl((\Sigma^{\xi}(p))^{-1}\Sigma(p)\bigr),

\Sigma(p)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p}[\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}],

and

\Sigma^{\xi}(p)=\Sigma(p)+\xi I_{\mathcal{H}}.

Then, under the eigenvalue decay conditions of
Definition
1
, the following hold.

1.

If
\mathcal{K}
has
(C,\beta)
-polynomial eigenvalue decay with
\beta>1
, then for any integer
m\geq 1
,

d_{*}(\xi)\leq m+\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{poly}}}{\xi},

where

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{poly}}:=\frac{C}{\beta-1}m^{1-\beta}.

2.

If
\mathcal{K}
has
(C,\beta)
-exponential eigenvalue decay with

\beta>0
, then for any integer
m\geq 1
,

d_{*}(\xi)\leq m+\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}}{\xi},

where

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}:=\frac{Ce^{-\beta(m+1)}}{1-e^{-\beta}}=\frac{Ce^{-\beta m}}{e^{\beta}-1}.

Proof.

Fix any
p\in\Delta
and let
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0
denote the eigenvalues of

\Sigma(p)
. Since
\Sigma^{\xi}(p)=\Sigma(p)+\xi I_{\mathcal{H}}
, the operator

(\Sigma^{\xi}(p))^{-1}\Sigma(p)
has eigenvalues

\frac{\mu_{i}}{\mu_{i}+\xi},\qquad i\geq 1.

Therefore, for any integer
m\geq 1
,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(p),\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl((\Sigma^{\xi}(p))^{-1}\Sigma(p)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{i}}{\mu_{i}+\xi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\mu_{i}}{\mu_{i}+\xi}+\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{i}}{\mu_{i}+\xi}

\displaystyle\leq m+\frac{1}{\xi}\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i},

(14)

where we used

\frac{\mu_{i}}{\mu_{i}+\xi}\leq 1,\qquad\frac{\mu_{i}}{\mu_{i}+\xi}\leq\frac{\mu_{i}}{\xi}.

Suppose
\mathcal{K}
has
(C,\beta)
-polynomial eigenvalue decay with

\beta>1
. By
Definition
1
, the bound
\mu_{i}\leq Ci^{-\beta}
holds regardless of
p
, hence

\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}\leq\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}Ci^{-\beta}\leq\int_{m}^{\infty}Cx^{-\beta}\,dx=\frac{C}{\beta-1}m^{1-\beta}=:\varepsilon_{\mathrm{poly}}.

Substituting this
p
-independent tail bound into (
14
) gives

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(p),\xi)\leq m+\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{poly}}}{\xi}=m+\frac{C}{(\beta-1)\xi}m^{1-\beta},

for every
p\in\Delta
. Taking the maximum over
p\in\Delta
on the left-hand side yields

d_{*}(\xi)=\max_{p\in\Delta}d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(p),\xi)\leq m+\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{poly}}}{\xi}.

Suppose instead
\mathcal{K}
has
(C,\beta)
-exponential eigenvalue decay with

\beta>0
. By
Definition
1
,
\mu_{i}\leq Ce^{-\beta i}
regardless of
p
, hence

\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}\leq\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}Ce^{-\beta i}=Ce^{-\beta(m+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta k}=\frac{Ce^{-\beta(m+1)}}{1-e^{-\beta}}=:\varepsilon_{\mathrm{exp}},

or equivalently,

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}=\frac{Ce^{-\beta m}}{e^{\beta}-1}.

Substituting this
p
-independent tail bound into (
14
) and taking the maximum over
p\in\Delta
, we obtain

d_{*}(\xi)\leq m+\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}}{\xi}=m+\frac{Ce^{-\beta(m+1)}}{(1-e^{-\beta})\xi}.

This completes the proof.
∎

The next lemma bounds the second moment of the regularized kernel features appearing in the proxy-loss variance term. This provides the key variance control needed in the exponential-weights regret analysis.

Lemma 7
.

Fix any round
t\in[T]
and condition on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
. Let

\Sigma_{t}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{t}}\!\left[\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\right],\qquad\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda}:=\Sigma_{t}+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}},

and let
A_{t}:=(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p_{t}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\left\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),A_{t}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\;\bigg|\;\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\leq d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma_{t},\lambda),

where
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma_{t},\lambda):=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left((\Sigma_{t}+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\right).

Proof.

We condition on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, meaning that
p_{t}
,
\Sigma_{t}
, and

A_{t}=(\Sigma_{t}+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}
are fixed, and the only
remaining randomness is over
\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p_{t}
. For any fixed
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{X}
, by the definition of
\Sigma_{t}
, we have

\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\left\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),A_{t}\Phi(\mathbf{z})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\left\langle A_{t}\Phi(\mathbf{z}),\Sigma_{t}A_{t}\Phi(\mathbf{z})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

Taking expectation over
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p_{t}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p_{t}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\left\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),A_{t}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_{t}}\left[\left\langle A_{t}\Phi(\mathbf{z}),\Sigma_{t}A_{t}\Phi(\mathbf{z})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{t}A_{t}\Sigma_{t}A_{t}\right).

Since
A_{t}=(\Sigma_{t}+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}
is a spectral function
of
\Sigma_{t}
, the two operators commute. Hence

\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{t}A_{t}\Sigma_{t}A_{t}\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left((A_{t}\Sigma_{t})^{2}\right).

Let
\{\mu_{t,i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be the eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{t}
. Then the
eigenvalues of
A_{t}\Sigma_{t}
are

\frac{\mu_{t,i}}{\mu_{t,i}+\lambda},\qquad i\geq 1.

Therefore,

\operatorname{Tr}\!\left((A_{t}\Sigma_{t})^{2}\right)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(\frac{\mu_{t,i}}{\mu_{t,i}+\lambda}\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{\mu_{t,i}}{\mu_{t,i}+\lambda}=d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma_{t},\lambda),

where the inequality follows because

\mu_{t,i}/(\mu_{t,i}+\lambda)\in[0,1]
for every
i
.
This proves the claim.
∎

The final lemma of this subsection bounds the effective dimension
d_{*}(\lambda)
directly by the standard
T
-point maximum information gain used in the stochastic kernel bandit literature
[
33
,
37
,
36
]
. This is the technical bridge that lets us reformulate our effective-dimension based regret bound in the conventional information gain form.

Lemma 8

(Effective dimension vs. maximum information gain)
.

Assume
\mathcal{X}
is finite, let
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}
be a feature map with
\|\Phi(\mathbf{x})\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq 1
for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, and define

\Sigma(\nu):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\nu}[\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}],\qquad d_{*}(\lambda):=\max_{\nu\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})}\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl((\Sigma(\nu)+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}\Sigma(\nu)\bigr).

Then, for any
T\geq 1
and any
\lambda\geq 1/T
, we have

d_{*}(\lambda)\;\leq\;2\,\max_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T}\in\mathcal{X}}\log\det\!\left(I_{\mathcal{H}}+\frac{1}{T\lambda}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})^{\top}\right).

Proof.

By
7
, the effective dimension is bounded by an operator-level log-determinant quantity:

d_{*}(\lambda)\;\leq\;\max_{\nu\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})}\log\det\!\left(I_{\mathcal{H}}+\frac{\Sigma(\nu)}{\lambda}\right).

(15)

It thus suffices to bound the right-hand side by the
T
-point information gain in the lemma. To this end, set
\alpha:=T\lambda
, so that
\alpha\geq 1
by the assumption
\lambda\geq 1/T
, and
\Sigma(\nu)/\lambda=T\Sigma(\nu)/\alpha
. Fix any
\nu\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
, write
M_{\nu}:=T\Sigma(\nu)
, and define
F(A):=\log\det(I_{\mathcal{H}}+A/\alpha)
on the positive semidefinite cone. We will construct
T
points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T}\in\mathcal{X}
such that

F\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})^{\top}\right)\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,F(M_{\nu}),

(16)

which, combined with (
15
) and taking the maximum over
\nu\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
, yields the lemma.

We construct the points greedily: set
V_{0}:=0
and, for
t=0,1,\ldots,T-1
, choose

\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\;\in\;\argmax_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\|\Phi(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}_{(\alpha I_{\mathcal{H}}+V_{t})^{-1}},\qquad V_{t+1}:=V_{t}+\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})^{\top},

(17)

where the maximizer exists since
\mathcal{X}
is finite. With the trace inner product
\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathrm{tr}}=\mathrm{Tr}(AB)
, we have
\nabla F(A)=(\alpha I_{\mathcal{H}}+A)^{-1}
, and by concavity of
F
,

\displaystyle F(M_{\nu})-F(V_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl((\alpha I_{\mathcal{H}}+V_{t})^{-1}(M_{\nu}-V_{t})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl((\alpha I_{\mathcal{H}}+V_{t})^{-1}M_{\nu}\bigr)

\displaystyle=T\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\nu}\!\bigl[\,\|\Phi(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}_{(\alpha I_{\mathcal{H}}+V_{t})^{-1}}\,\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq T\,\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})\|^{2}_{(\alpha I_{\mathcal{H}}+V_{t})^{-1}},

where the second inequality drops the non-positive term
-\mathrm{Tr}((\alpha I_{\mathcal{H}}+V_{t})^{-1}V_{t})
(note that
V_{t}\succeq 0
and
(\alpha I_{\mathcal{H}}+V_{t})^{-1}\succeq 0
), the equality uses
M_{\nu}=T\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\nu}[\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}]
, and the last step is the defining property of
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}
in (
17
). On the other hand, the matrix determinant lemma gives

F(V_{t+1})-F(V_{t})\;=\;\log\!\bigl(1+\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})\|^{2}_{(\alpha I_{\mathcal{H}}+V_{t})^{-1}}\bigr).

(18)

Let
z_{t}:=\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})\|^{2}_{(\alpha I_{\mathcal{H}}+V_{t})^{-1}}
. Since
V_{t}\succeq 0
,
\alpha\geq 1
, and
\|\Phi(\mathbf{x})\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq 1
, we have
z_{t}\leq\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq 1
. Applying
\log(1+z)\geq(\log 2)\,z
for
z\in[0,1]
and combining with (
18
) gives

F(M_{\nu})-F(V_{t})\;\leq\;\frac{T}{\log 2}\,\bigl(F(V_{t+1})-F(V_{t})\bigr).

(19)

We conclude the proof using a telescoping argument. Let
\Delta_{t}:=F(M_{\nu})-F(V_{t})
. If
\Delta_{t}\leq 0
for some
t\leq T
, then since
F(V_{s})
is non-decreasing in
s
we have
F(V_{T})\geq F(V_{t})\geq F(M_{\nu})
, and (
16
) follows. Otherwise
\Delta_{t}>0
for all
t<T
, and (
19
) rearranges to
\Delta_{t+1}=\Delta_{t}-(F(V_{t+1})-F(V_{t}))\leq(1-\tfrac{\log 2}{T})\Delta_{t}
. Since
V_{0}=0
, we have
\Delta_{0}=F(M_{\nu})
, and iterating
T
times yields

\Delta_{T}\;\leq\;\Bigl(1-\tfrac{\log 2}{T}\Bigr)^{T}F(M_{\nu})\;\leq\;e^{-\log 2}\,F(M_{\nu})\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,F(M_{\nu}),

where the second inequality uses
(1-x)^{T}\leq e^{-xT}
. Therefore
F(V_{T})\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\,F(M_{\nu})
, which establishes (
16
) and completes the proof.
∎

A.2
Proof of
Lemma
1
(Learner-side Residual)

The following lemma bounds the learner-side residual terms introduced by the
regularized estimator. It shows that these terms can be controlled by the effective dimension induced by the covariance operator

\Sigma_{t}
.

See
1

Proof.

Define the regularization bias operator

E_{t}:=I_{\mathcal{H}}-(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}=\lambda(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}.

Since
\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda}=\Sigma_{t}+\lambda I_{\mathcal{H}}
, we have

0\preceq E_{t}\preceq I_{\mathcal{H}}
. Moreover, recalling that

c_{t}(\mathbf{x})=B\sqrt{\lambda}\,\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}=B\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}},

we can express both residual terms
B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}
and
C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}
in terms of the same operator
E_{t}
.

As for the term
B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}
, by definition we have

B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{w}_{t}-\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right].

Conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, the only remaining randomness is in

\mathbf{x}_{t}
. Hence, by
Lemma
4
,

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}.

Applying the tower property gives

\displaystyle B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),\bigl(I_{\mathcal{H}}-(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\bigr)\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),E_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right].

Since
E_{t}\succeq 0
, Cauchy–Schwarz in the
E_{t}
-seminorm yields

\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),E_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle E_{t}^{1/2}\Phi(\mathbf{x}),E_{t}^{1/2}\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}\rVert_{E_{t}}.

Using
0\preceq E_{t}\preceq I_{\mathcal{H}}
and Assumption
1
, we have
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}\rVert_{E_{t}}\leq\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq B.

Therefore, one can further bound the bias term as

B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\leq B\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}\right].

On the other hand, by the definition of
C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}
, we have

C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}=B\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}\right].

Combining the two displayed equations, we obtain

B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\leq 2B\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}\right].

(20)

It remains to relate the right-hand side to the effective dimension. For
any
\alpha>0
, Young’s inequality gives

B\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}\leq\alpha\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}^{2}+\frac{B^{2}}{\alpha}.

Multiplying by
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
and summing over
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, we get

\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,B\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}\leq\alpha\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}^{2}+\frac{B^{2}}{\alpha},

where we used
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})=1
. Substituting this into
(
20
) gives

\displaystyle B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\alpha\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}^{2}+\frac{B^{2}}{\alpha}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=2\alpha\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}^{2}\right]+\frac{2B^{2}T}{\alpha}.

We now handle the quadratic term. By the definition
\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}^{2}=\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),E_{t}\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),E_{t}\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(E_{t}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})\Phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(E_{t}\Sigma_{t}).

Using
E_{t}=\lambda(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}
, we obtain

\mathrm{Tr}(E_{t}\Sigma_{t})=\lambda\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl((\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\bigr).

Therefore,

\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{E_{t}}^{2}=\lambda\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl((\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\bigr).

Substituting this identity into the previous bound yields

B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}+C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\leq 2\alpha\lambda\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{Tr}\bigl((\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\bigr)\right]+\frac{2B^{2}T}{\alpha}=2\alpha\lambda\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma_{t},\lambda)\right]+\frac{2B^{2}T}{\alpha},

which proves the claim by choosing
\alpha=B\sqrt{\frac{T}{\lambda\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma_{t},\lambda)\right]}}.
Note that If
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t}d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma_{t},\lambda)\right]=0
, the claim is trivial.
∎

A.3
Proof of
Lemma

(Comparator-side Residual)

The following lemma shows that the comparator-side residual is non-positive.
For any fixed comparator
\mathbf{x}_{*}
, the correction term dominates the bias induced
by regularization in the comparator direction. Consequently, this residual does
not contribute positively to the regret. This is a key structural property of
the analysis, as it eliminates the need to further bound the comparator-side
bias and leaves only the learner-side residual to be controlled.

See
2

Proof.

Define the regularization bias operator

E_{t}:=I_{\mathcal{H}}-(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}=\lambda(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}.

As in the proof of Lemma

, we have
0\preceq E_{t}\preceq I_{\mathcal{H}}
. By
Lemma
4
, conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t},

and hence

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}_{t}-\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=E_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}.

Since the comparator
\mathbf{x}_{*}
is fixed,
\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable. Applying the tower property,

\displaystyle B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}),\mathbf{w}_{t}-\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}),E_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right].

Recall that

C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}=B\sqrt{\lambda}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})\rVert_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}\right]=B\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})\|_{E_{t}}\right].

We now combine
B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}
and
C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}
:

\displaystyle-B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}-C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}),E_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right]-B\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})\|_{E_{t}}\right].

Since
E_{t}\succeq 0
, Cauchy–Schwarz in the
E_{t}
-seminorm yields

|\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}),E_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}|=|\langle E_{t}^{1/2}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}),E_{t}^{1/2}\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}|\leq\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})\|_{E_{t}}\,\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{E_{t}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle-B_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}-C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})\|_{E_{t}}\,\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{E_{t}}-B\,\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})\|_{E_{t}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})\|_{E_{t}}\bigl(\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{E_{t}}-B\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 0,

where the last inequality holds due to
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{E_{t}}\leq\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq B
by Assumption
1
.

∎

A.4
Proof of
Lemma
3
(Regret Bound via Exponential Weights)

The following lemma bounds the estimated regret term arising from the
exponential-weights update. Under a suitable exploration condition, the proxy
losses are uniformly bounded, allowing a standard exponential-weights analysis.
The resulting bound separates the contribution of the effective dimension term, the entropy term
\log|\mathcal{X}|/\eta
, and the
cost of explicit exploration.

See
3

Proof.

We can decompose
\mathcal{R}_{T}^{\widehat{\ell}}
into two terms using
p_{t}=(1-\gamma)q_{t}+\gamma\nu_{G}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}^{\widehat{\ell}}\;=\;

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\bigl(q_{t}(\mathbf{x})-p_{*}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]}_{\texttt{Term~(A)}}

\displaystyle\quad+\;\gamma\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\bigl({\nu}_{G}(\mathbf{x})-p_{*}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]}_{\texttt{Term~(B)}}.

Bounding Term (A).

When
\gamma=1
, the coefficient
1-\gamma
makes Term (A) vanish and the regret reduces to
\gamma\cdot\texttt{Term~(B)}
. It therefore suffices to consider
\gamma\in(0,1)
in the following. Term (A) is the regret of the distribution
q_{t}
generated by the exponential weights algorithm. According to Lemma
5
, we have
|\eta\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})|\leq 1
. Then, the standard analysis for the exponential weights algorithm (e.g., see
[
4
, Theorem 1]
) yields

\displaystyle\texttt{Term~(A)}\;\leq\;

\displaystyle\eta\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}q_{t}(\mathbf{x})\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})^{2}\right]+\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}|}{\eta}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{1-\gamma}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})^{2}\right]+\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}|}{\eta}

\displaystyle\;=\;

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p_{t}}\Big[\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})^{2}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\Big]+\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}|}{\eta}\right]

(21)

where the second inequality uses
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})=(1-\gamma)q_{t}(\mathbf{x})+\gamma\nu_{G}(\mathbf{x})\geq(1-\gamma)q_{t}(\mathbf{x})
.

We can further upper bound the quadratic term on the right-hand side by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p_{t}}\!\left[\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\;2\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p_{t}}\!\left[\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,|\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}|^{2}\cdot\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]+2\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})c_{t}(\mathbf{x})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\;2B^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p_{t}}\!\left[\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]+2\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})c_{t}(\mathbf{x})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\;2B^{2}d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma_{t},\lambda)+2\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})c_{t}(\mathbf{x})^{2},

(22)

where:

•

the first inequality follows from the definition

\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-c_{t}(\mathbf{x})

and the fact that
(u-v)^{2}\leq 2u^{2}+2v^{2}
with
u=\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
and
v=c_{t}(\mathbf{x})
;

•

the second inequality uses
Assumption
1
;

•

the last step applies
Lemma
7
.

For the second term in (
22
), using the definition of
c_{t}(\mathbf{x})
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{t}(\mathbf{x})c_{t}(\mathbf{x})^{2}\;=\;

\displaystyle B^{2}\lambda\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left((\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\right)=B^{2}\lambda d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma_{t},\lambda).

(23)

Substituting (
22
) and (
23
) into (
21
) gives

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\cdot\texttt{Term~(A)}\;\leq\;

\displaystyle 2(1+\lambda)B^{2}\eta\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma_{t},\lambda)\right]+\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}|}{\eta}.

Bounding Term (B).

Using the tower property together with
Lemma
4
, we have

\texttt{Term~(B)}=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}(\nu_{G}(\mathbf{x})-p_{*}(\mathbf{x}))\Bigl(\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\,\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-c_{t}(\mathbf{x})\Bigr)\Bigg].

We bound the two contributions separately. For the first part, since
(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\preceq I_{\mathcal{H}}
and
\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq B
, we have

\left|\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\,\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right|\leq\|\Phi(\mathbf{x})\|_{\mathcal{H}}\,\|(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\,\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq B\quad\text{for all $\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}$}.

Moreover, using
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}|\nu_{G}(\mathbf{x})-p_{*}(\mathbf{x})|\leq 2
gives

\left|\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}(\nu_{G}(\mathbf{x})-p_{*}(\mathbf{x}))\langle\Phi(\mathbf{x}),(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\Sigma_{t}\,\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right|\leq 2B.

For the correction term,

-\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}(\nu_{G}(\mathbf{x})-p_{*}(\mathbf{x}))c_{t}(\mathbf{x})=c_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{*})-\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\nu_{G}(\mathbf{x})\,c_{t}(\mathbf{x})\leq c_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{*}),

since
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\nu_{G}(\mathbf{x})\,c_{t}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
.

Combining both contributions and summing over
t=1,\dots,T
and taking expectation yields

\texttt{Term~(B)}\leq 2BT+C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}.

We can further upper bound the term
C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}
by

\displaystyle C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}=B\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})\|_{E_{t}}\Big]\leq B\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})\rVert^{2}_{E_{t}}}\right],

where
E_{t}=\lambda(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}
and the last step is due to the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

Now, writing
\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})\|_{E_{t}}^{2}=\lambda\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})\|_{(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}}^{2}
, and using
\Sigma_{t}\succeq\gamma\Sigma(\nu_{G})
, we have

(\Sigma_{t}^{\lambda})^{-1}\preceq\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big(\Sigma(\nu_{G})+\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}I_{\mathcal{H}}\Big)^{-1}.

Hence,

\|\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})\|_{E_{t}}^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\big(\Sigma(\nu_{D})+\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}I_{\mathcal{H}}\big)^{-1}\Sigma(\nu_{D})\Big]=\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(\nu_{D}),\lambda/\gamma),

where the inequality holds following the same arguments in deriving (
12
) in the proof of Lemma
5
.

Substituting back, we obtain

C_{T}^{\mathbf{x}_{*}}\leq B\sqrt{\frac{\lambda T}{\gamma}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(\nu_{D}),\lambda/\gamma)}

Conclusion.

Combining the bounds for
(1-\gamma)\cdot\texttt{Term~(A)}
and
\gamma\cdot\texttt{Term~(B)}
gives (
9
).
∎

Proof of
Corollary
1
and Applications to Specific Kernels

Here we provide the omitted proof details for
Corollary
1
and the subsequent applications to specific kernels.

Proof of Corollary
1
.

Recall that
Theorem
1
bounds the regret in terms of

d_{*}(\rho)=\max_{\nu\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})}\operatorname{Tr}\left((\Sigma(\nu)+\rho I_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}\Sigma(\nu)\right)~,

where

\Sigma(\nu)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\nu}\left[\Phi(x)\Phi(x)^{\top}\right]~.
Fixing
\alpha=1
, we have

\lambda=1/T
and
Lemma
8
gives

d_{*}(\lambda)\leq 2\max_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T}\in\mathcal{X}}\log\det\left(I+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Phi(x_{t})\Phi(x_{t})^{\top}\right)=:4\bar{\gamma}_{T}.

We will also need to control
d_{*}(\lambda/\gamma)
, where

\gamma
denotes the mixing coefficient in
Algorithm
1
. Since

\gamma\in(0,1]
, we have

\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\gamma T}\geq\frac{1}{T}.

Moreover, since
d_{*}(\rho)
is non-increasing in
\rho
, we have

d_{*}(\lambda/\gamma)\leq d_{*}(\lambda)\leq 4\bar{\gamma}_{T}.

(24)

We choose

\eta=\frac{1}{2B}\sqrt{\frac{\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}{2(1+\lambda)d_{*}(\lambda)T}},\qquad\gamma=\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{2d_{*}(\lambda)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}{(1+\lambda)T}},1\right\}.

(25)

We claim that it suffices to assume that
T\geq 2d_{*}(\lambda)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)
; this is due to the fact that when
T\leq 2d_{*}(\lambda)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)
, we immediately have
\mathcal{R}_{T}\leq 2BT\leq 2B\sqrt{2Td_{*}(\lambda)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}
.

Under the condition
T\geq 2d_{*}(\lambda)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)
we have
\gamma<1
, and recalling that
\lambda=1/T
, a direct substitution readily verifies that
\lambda\leq 1/(2\eta B)
.
Moreover, we observe from (
24
) that

d_{*}(\lambda/\gamma)\leq 2d_{*}(\lambda)=\frac{\gamma}{2\eta B}.

Hence, both conditions of
Theorem
1
are satisfied.
Applying
Theorem
1
(with

\lambda=1/T
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}|}{\eta}+2\Big(1+\frac{1}{T}\Big)B^{2}\eta d_{*}(\lambda)T+4B\sqrt{Td_{*}(\lambda)}+B\sqrt{\gamma Td_{*}(\lambda)}+2\gamma BT.

The first two terms satisfy

\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}|}{\eta}+2\Big(1+\frac{1}{T}\Big)B^{2}\eta d_{*}(\lambda)T\leq 4B\sqrt{(1+\lambda)d_{*}(\lambda)T\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)},

and the fourth term satisfies the following since
\gamma\leq 1
:

B\sqrt{\gamma Td_{*}(\lambda)}\leq B\sqrt{Td_{*}(\lambda)}.

For the final term, we have

2\gamma BT=2BT\sqrt{\frac{2d_{*}(\lambda)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}{(1+\lambda)T}}\leq 2B\sqrt{2d_{*}(\lambda)T\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}~.

Combining these bounds and using (
24
) gives

\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{O}\left(B\sqrt{d_{*}(\lambda)T\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(B\sqrt{\bar{\gamma}_{T}T\log(|\mathcal{X}|)}\right).

(26)

∎

Application to specific kernels.

For the Matérn
(\nu,d)

[
36
]

\bar{\gamma}_{T}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(T^{d/(2\nu+d)}\right).

Substituting this into (
26
), we obtain

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(B\sqrt{T\cdot T^{d/(2\nu+d)}\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(BT^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{d}{2(2\nu+d)}}\sqrt{\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(BT^{(\nu+d)/(2\nu+d)}\sqrt{\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}\right).

In particular, if
\log|\mathcal{X}|=\mathcal{O}(\log T)
and

is treated as a constant, then

\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{O}\left(T^{(\nu+d)/(2\nu+d)}\right),

which proves the desired claim.

For the Squared Exponential kernel, the standard

[
36
]

\bar{\gamma}_{T}=\mathcal{O}\big((\log T)^{d+1}\big)~.

Substituting this into (
26
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(B\sqrt{T\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)(\log T)^{d+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(B\sqrt{T\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}(\log T)^{(d+1)/2}\right).

In particular, if
\log|\mathcal{X}|=\mathcal{O}(\log T)
and

is treated as a constant, then

\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{T}(\log T)^{(d+2)/2}\right)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T}),

A.6
Proofs of Corollary
2
and Corollary
3
(Polynomial and Exponential Eigendecay)

Proof of Corollary
2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{K}
has
(C,\beta)
-polynomial eigenvalue decay defined in
Definition
1
,
Lemma
6
shows that
the effective dimension can be upper bounded by

d_{*}(\lambda)\leq m+\frac{Cm^{1-\beta}}{(\beta-1)\lambda}

and

d_{*}(\lambda/\gamma)\leq m+\frac{\gamma Cm^{1-\beta}}{(\beta-1)\lambda}.

Then, Theorem
1
gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}\leq{}

\displaystyle 2\gamma BT+\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}|}{\eta}+2(1+\lambda)B^{2}\eta T\left(m+\frac{Cm^{1-\beta}}{(\beta-1)\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+BT\sqrt{\lambda\gamma m+\frac{\gamma^{2}Cm^{1-\beta}}{\beta-1}}+4BT\sqrt{\lambda m+\frac{Cm^{1-\beta}}{\beta-1}}.

We now choose

m:=\left\lceil T^{1/\beta}\right\rceil,\qquad\lambda:=m^{-\beta},\qquad\gamma:=m^{-\frac{\beta-1}{2}},\qquad\eta:=\frac{1}{2B(1+C/(\beta-1))}m^{-\frac{\beta+1}{2}}.

(27)

By this choice,

m+\frac{Cm^{1-\beta}}{(\beta-1)\lambda}=\left(1+\frac{C}{\beta-1}\right)m

and

m+\frac{\gamma Cm^{1-\beta}}{(\beta-1)\lambda}=\left(1+\frac{C\gamma}{\beta-1}\right)m\leq\left(1+\frac{C}{\beta-1}\right)m,

where we used
\gamma\leq 1
. Substituting these bounds into the regret bound yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}\leq{}

\displaystyle 2\gamma BT+\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}|}{\eta}+2(1+\lambda)B^{2}\eta T\left(1+\frac{C}{\beta-1}\right)m

\displaystyle\quad+BT\sqrt{\lambda\gamma m\left(1+\frac{C\gamma}{\beta-1}\right)}+4BT\sqrt{\lambda m\left(1+\frac{C}{\beta-1}\right)}.

Since
m=\lceil T^{1/\beta}\rceil
, we have

m\leq T^{1/\beta}+1
and
m^{-a}\leq T^{-a/\beta}
for any
a>0
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}\leq{}

\displaystyle 2B\left(1+\frac{C}{\beta-1}\right)\log|\mathcal{X}|\,\left(T^{1/\beta}+1\right)^{\frac{\beta+1}{2}}

\displaystyle+BT^{\frac{\beta+1}{2\beta}}\left(3+T^{-1}+4\sqrt{1+\frac{C}{\beta-1}}\right)

\displaystyle+BT^{\frac{\beta+3}{4\beta}}\sqrt{1+\frac{CT^{-\frac{\beta-1}{2\beta}}}{\beta-1}}.

Since the last term is lower order for
\beta>1
, we obtain

\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(B\left(1+\left(1+\frac{C}{\beta-1}\right)\log|\mathcal{X}|+\sqrt{1+\frac{C}{\beta-1}}\right)T^{\frac{\beta+1}{2\beta}}\right).

It remains to verify the conditions of Theorem
1
. By the parameter choice,

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(\nu_{D}),\lambda/\gamma)\leq m+\frac{\gamma Cm^{1-\beta}}{(\beta-1)\lambda}=\left(1+\frac{C\gamma}{\beta-1}\right)m\leq\left(1+\frac{C}{\beta-1}\right)m=\frac{\gamma}{2\eta B}.

Moreover,

\lambda=m^{-\beta}\leq 1\leq\left(1+\frac{C}{\beta-1}\right)m^{\frac{\beta+1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2\eta B}.

Thus both conditions of Theorem
1
are satisfied.
∎

Proof of Corollary
3
.

Suppose
\mathcal{K}
has
(C,\beta)
-exponential eigenvalue decay defined in
Definition
1
,
Lemma
6
shows that
the effective dimension can be upper bounded by

d_{*}(\lambda)\leq m+\frac{Ce^{-\beta m}}{(e^{\beta}-1)\lambda},\qquad d_{*}(\lambda/\gamma)\leq m+\frac{\gamma Ce^{-\beta m}}{(e^{\beta}-1)\lambda}.

Then, Theorem
1
gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}\leq{}

\displaystyle 2\gamma BT+\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}|}{\eta}+2(1+\lambda)B^{2}\eta T\left(m+\frac{Ce^{-\beta m}}{(e^{\beta}-1)\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+BT\sqrt{\lambda\gamma m+\frac{\gamma^{2}Ce^{-\beta m}}{e^{\beta}-1}}+4BT\sqrt{\lambda m+\frac{Ce^{-\beta m}}{e^{\beta}-1}}.

We now choose

m:=\left\lceil\frac{\log T}{\beta}\right\rceil,\qquad\lambda:=e^{-\beta m},

(28)

and

\gamma:=\sqrt{\frac{\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}{T}},\qquad\eta:=\frac{\gamma}{2B\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)}.

Since
m=\lceil(\log T)/\beta\rceil
, we have

\lambda=e^{-\beta m}\leq\frac{1}{T}.

Moreover,

m+\frac{Ce^{-\beta m}}{(e^{\beta}-1)\lambda}=m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1},

and

m+\frac{\gamma Ce^{-\beta m}}{(e^{\beta}-1)\lambda}=m+\frac{\gamma C}{e^{\beta}-1}\leq m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1},

where the last inequality uses
\gamma\leq 1
.

Substituting these bounds into the regret bound yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}\leq{}

\displaystyle 2\gamma BT+\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}|}{\eta}+2(1+\lambda)B^{2}\eta T\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+BT\sqrt{\lambda\gamma\left(m+\frac{\gamma C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)}+4BT\sqrt{\lambda\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)}.

We estimate the terms separately. First, by the choice of
\gamma
,

2\gamma BT=2B\sqrt{T\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}.

For the entropy term, since
\log|\mathcal{X}|\leq\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)
,

\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}|}{\eta}=\frac{2B\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)\log|\mathcal{X}|}{\gamma}\leq 2B\sqrt{T\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}.

For the variance term, using
\lambda\leq 1
,

\displaystyle 2(1+\lambda)B^{2}\eta T\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4B^{2}\eta T\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)

\displaystyle=2B\gamma T

\displaystyle=2B\sqrt{T\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}.

For the first square-root term, using
\lambda\leq 1/T
and
\gamma\leq 1
,

\displaystyle BT\sqrt{\lambda\gamma\left(m+\frac{\gamma C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq BT\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{T}\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)}

\displaystyle=B\sqrt{T\gamma\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq B\sqrt{T\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)},

where the last inequality uses
\gamma\leq 1\leq\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)
. Similarly,

\displaystyle 4BT\sqrt{\lambda\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 4BT\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)}

\displaystyle=4B\sqrt{T\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 4B\sqrt{T\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}.

Combining the above estimates gives

\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(B\sqrt{T\left(m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}\right).

Since

m=\left\lceil\frac{\log T}{\beta}\right\rceil,

we obtain

\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(B\sqrt{T\left(\frac{\log T}{\beta}+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}\right)\log(e|\mathcal{X}|)}\right).

In particular, when
(B,\beta,C)
are constant and
\log|\mathcal{X}|=O(\log T)
,
we have

\mathcal{R}_{T}=O\!\left(\sqrt{T}\log T\right).

It remains to verify the conditions of Theorem
1
. By the parameter choice,

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma(\nu_{D}),\lambda/\gamma)\leq m+\frac{\gamma C}{e^{\beta}-1}\leq m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}=\frac{\gamma}{2\eta B}.

Moreover,

\lambda=e^{-\beta m}\leq 1\leq\frac{m+\frac{C}{e^{\beta}-1}}{\gamma}=\frac{1}{2\eta B},

where we used
\gamma\leq 1
. Thus both conditions of Theorem
1

are satisfied.
∎

. While our upper bound also exhibits
T^{\frac{\beta+1}{2\beta}}
dependence, there are important differences in the underlying assumptions that makes these bounds incomparable and/or introduces a gap between them.

Perhaps most importantly, the kernel used in
[
11
, Theorem 43]
is an infinite-dimensional linear kernel, as opposed to a kernel with low-dimensional input such as
[0,1]^{d}
. Their proof can be adapted to a finite action set of the form
\{-1,1\}^{M}
for suitably-chosen
M
(with all values beyond
M
implicitly set to zero), but this requires
M
to grow at rate
T^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}
, thus inducing an action set
|\mathcal{X}|
satisfying
\log|\mathcal{X}|=\Omega(T^{\frac{1}{2\beta}})
, rather than the usual
\log|\mathcal{X}|=O(\log T)
. Accordingly, the
\log|\mathcal{X}|
dependence in our upper bound (and in related upper bounds, including that of
11
) would be significant due to being polynomial in
T
, and the overall regret scaling would be strictly higher than
T^{\frac{\beta+1}{2\beta}}
.

Two other notable differences are (i) our Definition
1
considers uniform bounds with respect to the measure
p
, whereas the decay in
[
11
, Theorem 43]
is with respect to a fixed measure, and (ii) we assume an oblivious adversary, whereas the adversary in
[
11
, Theorem 43]
is non-oblivious. These differences may potentially be less of a hurdle to overcome compared to the previous paragraph, but they are nevertheless also important.

B.2
Algorithm and Upper Bound from
20

Here we highlight a few differences between our main results and the algorithm and its upper bound from the current work
20
, but note that all of these differences are ultimately minor:

1.

We study the expected regret under an oblivious adversary, whereas
20
studies the pseudoregret under an adaptive adversary. However, this distinction ultimately has almost no impact on the analysis, and it is straightforward to adapt either analysis to the other setting.

2.

We focus more on the effective dimension
d_{*}
, whereas
20
focuses more on the maximum information gain
\gamma_{T}
, but the two are well known to be closely related, e.g., see
7
as well as our
Lemma
8
.

3.

We use a G-optimal design for our exploration term in
Algorithm
1
, whereas
20
uses maximum variance reduction (MVR). These are essentially equivalent for statistical/regret purposes (though possibly different in terms of computation), since MVR is a greedy Frank-Wolfe like procedure for the log-det (D-optimal design) objective, whose optimality conditions are equivalent to G-optimal design. In fact, it is explicitly stated in
[
20
, Appendix I]
that exploration via a G-optimal design yields the same regret guarantee.
