Comprehensive Analysis of Physicochemical Properties, Structure, Bioactive Potential, and Flavor Profiles of Tangerine Pericarp Powder from Different Varieties
Authors: Karthikeyan Venkatachalam, Narin Charoenphun, Paramee Noonim, Jaraslak Pechwang, Pattama Senthong, Somwang Lekjing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tangerine pericarp (TP), a citrus byproduct rich in bioactive compounds, was investigated to assess the physicochemical characteristics and functional properties of powders derived from three Thai cultivars: Cho Kun (TP-C), Sai Nam Phueng (TP-S), and Bang Mod (TP-B). Dry matter recovery ranged from 23.17% to 27.05%, with moisture content between 4.93% and 6.00%, and water activity values below 0.30 in all samples, showing good stability. Analysis of bioactive compounds revealed significant varietal differences. TP-C exhibited the highest levels of total phenolics (49.98 ± 0.24 mg GAE/g DW), total flavonoids (36.11 ± 0.02 mg QE/g DW), and total carotenoids (46.78 ± 0.27 mg β-carotene/kg DW), while with TP-B intermediate and TP-S the lowest. Antioxidant activities were also highest in TP-C, with DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging reaching up to 74.18% and 70.12% and FRAP values at 24.79 ± 0.40 μmol TE/100 mg DW. Ascorbic acid content varied among samples, with TP-C recording the highest value at 76.78 ± 1.27 mg/100 g DW. All samples exhibited strong antimicrobial activity against Bacillus cereus ATCC 11778, with inhibition zones ranging from 20.91 to 29.33 mm. Fatty acid profiles were dominated by linoleic acid (C18:2) and palmitic acid (C16:0), while D-limonene was the major volatile compound across all varieties. Overall, while all TP powder (TPP) samples showed functional potential, TP-C exhibited superior bioactive content, antioxidant capacity, and antimicrobial properties, showing its suitability as a natural functional ingredient for food and nutraceutical applications.

Introduction

Citrus fruits, particularly tangerines (Citrus reticulata Blanco), occupy a prominent position in global fruit production due to their vibrant flavor, appealing aroma, and exceptional nutritional properties. Among the ASEAN countries, Thailand is recognized as one of the leading producers of tangerines, with cultivation concentrated in northern and central regions where favorable climates support year-round harvests. Among the domestically grown varieties, Cho Kun, Sai Nam Phueng, and Bang Mod are especially valued for their distinctive taste profiles, commercial importance, and widespread consumption in fresh and processed forms. Structurally, citrus fruits are characterized by a distinctive morphology, consisting of an outer colored pericarp (comprising the flavedo or exocarp), a white spongy mesocarp (albedo), and an inner edible portion (endocarp) containing juice vesicles arranged in segments. The flavedo is rich in essential oils and carotenoid pigments responsible for the fruit’s characteristic aroma and color, while the albedo primarily consists of cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, pectin, and flavonoids. This complex structural and chemical composition supports the unique functional properties of citrus fruits particularly and their byproducts. Notably, tangerine peels are particularly rich in dietary fibers, carotenoids, and polyphenols, which contribute to their antioxidant potential and suitability as promising ingredients for functional food applications. Among citrus species, tangerines are widely consumed both fresh and processed forms, particularly juices, jams, and candied products, generating substantial quantities of byproducts. Notably, the pericarp (peel) represents approximately 30–40% of the total fruit weight and has traditionally been treated as an industrial waste. However, increasing interest in sustainable resource utilization has led to recognizing tangerine pericarp (TP) as a valuable source of bioactive compounds with significant potential across the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries. Globally, the growing emphasis on reducing food processing waste and promoting circular economy strategies has intensified interest in the valorization of fruit byproducts. With consumers increasingly seeking natural, health-promoting food additives, TP offers an environmentally friendly, functional ingredient that aligns with sustainable food system objectives. Recent investigations have demonstrated the remarkable chemodiversity within citrus peels, revealing substantial variability in flavonoid, carotenoid, and essential oil content across genotypes, which supports their exploitation in diverse high-value applications. TP is particularly abundant in phytochemicals, including polymethoxylated flavones, flavanones, phenolic acids, carotenoids, dietary fibers, and essential oils. These compounds contribute to the peel’s well-documented antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and lipid-regulating activities. In a broader nutraceutical context, recent investigations on fruit- and byproduct-derived extracts have also demonstrated strong anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and disease-modulating properties, such as the anti-inflammatory activity of Juglans regia fresh fruit extract observed in both acute and chronic inflammatory models. Similarly, the development of green extraction approaches, such as ultrasound-assisted extraction combined with volatile natural deep eutectic solvents for the recovery of lycopene and other bioactive carotenoids from tomato skin waste, further highlights the growing potential of fruit byproducts as sustainable sources for functional food and nutraceutical ingredients. These recent advancements reinforce the relevance of systematically characterizing TP, a valuable citrus byproduct, for its incorporation into the emerging nutraceutical and functional food landscape.

In traditional medicine systems, especially in East Asia, dried tangerine peel has been used for centuries as both a functional food ingredient and a natural remedy. Recent studies have validated these traditional uses, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of TP bioactives in promoting cardiovascular health, exhibiting anticancer effects, and supporting metabolic regulation. Furthermore, the distinctive aromatic profile of tangerine peel characterized by volatile compounds such as limonene, γ-terpinene, and β-myrcene has opened opportunities for its application as a natural flavor enhancer in food systems. In the context of a circular economy and sustainable agriculture, the valorization of fruit byproducts has become an important area of research. Converting TP into powder form offers numerous advantages, including extended shelf life, simplified transportation, and enhanced incorporation into diverse formulations, while preserving its bioactive integrity. Nevertheless, the effective utilization of tangerine pericarp powder (TPP) in high-value applications depends on a comprehensive understanding of its physicochemical attributes, structural features, bioactive potential, and sensory properties. Critically, the physicochemical properties, structural characteristics, bioactive potential, and sensory attributes of TPP can vary significantly among cultivars, influenced by genetic factors, environmental conditions, maturation stages, and postharvest practices. Variations in flavonoid concentration, essential oil composition, fiber content, and microstructural integrity directly impact the functional, nutritional, and sensory qualities of the powder, shaping its suitability for specific industrial applications. In particular, regional tangerine cultivars such as Cho Kun (TP-C), Sai Nam Phueng (TP-S), and Bang Mod (TP-B) are widely grown and consumed in Thailand, each exhibiting unique morphological characteristics, cultivation conditions, and flavor profiles. However, limited information exists regarding how these specific varieties differ in their physicochemical properties, bioactive composition, and structural features. Recognizing these varietal distinctions is fundamental for optimizing their application in functional foods and for developing standardized ingredients. Recent metabolomic studies have identified key chemical markers like hesperidin, nobiletin, and tangeretin as significant contributors to both health benefits and quality differentiation of tangerine peel products across various cultivars. Despite increasing interest in the valorization of citrus byproducts and the acknowledged potential of TP, a significant gap persists in systematic comparative studies assessing how varietal differences affect the comprehensive quality attributes of TPP. While prior studies have explored the phytochemical profiles or essential oil compositions of individual tangerine varieties, comprehensive analyses that simultaneously evaluate physicochemical, structural, and bioactive properties across cultivars are limited.

This study aimed to address the existing knowledge gap by conducting a comprehensive analysis of the physicochemical properties, structural characteristics, antioxidant capacity, antimicrobial activity, fatty acid composition, and flavor profiles of pericarp powder derived from three tangerine cultivars. Based on known variations in phytochemical and structural profiles among citrus cultivars, we hypothesized that TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B would exhibit significant differences in these parameters. These differences were expected to arise from genetic, morphological, and compositional variability characteristics of each cultivar. This hypothesis provided a framework for comparative evaluation and guided the interpretation of the results.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals, Reagents and Media

All chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Bangkok, Thailand), unless otherwise specified. These included Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (≥99.9%), sodium carbonate (≥99.5%), gallic acid (≥98.0%), sodium nitrite (≥97.0%), aluminum chloride (≥99.99%), sodium hydroxide (≥98.0%), quercetin (≥95.0%), trichloroacetic acid (≥99.0%), 2,6-dichlorophenol indophenol (≥99.0%), DPPH (≥97.0%), ABTS (≥98.0%), tripyridyltriazine (≥98.0%), ferric chloride (≥98.0%), and Trolox (≥98.0%). Solvents used included n-hexane (≥99.0%), acetone (≥99.5%), ethanol (≥99.8%), chloroform (≥99.5%), and methanol (≥99.9%) (all HPLC grade), and anhydrous sodium sulfate (≥99.0%). Gentamicin sulfate (≥98.0%) and dimethyl sulfoxide (≥99.9%) were also used. Fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) standards (purity ≥ 99.0%) were obtained from Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, CA). Mueller-Hinton agar for antimicrobial testing was purchased from HiMedia Laboratories (Mumbai, India).

Preparation of Tangerine Pericarp Powders (TPP)

Fresh, fully ripened tangerine fruits (C. reticulata Blanco) from three distinct varieties including TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B were sourced from a local fruit market in Surat Thani Province, Thailand. For each variety, three independent batches were prepared from separate lots of fresh fruits, with each batch consisting of approximately 30 fruits of uniform size, maturity, and freshness, corresponding to a total of approximately 90 fruits per variety used in the study. The three independent batches served as the biological replicates (n = 3) for all subsequent physicochemical, structural, bioactive, antimicrobial, fatty acid, and volatile analyses. Collected fruits were carefully selected based on uniform size and freshness. Following selection, the tangerines were thoroughly washed with tap water and drained for approximately 10 min. The fruits were then manually peeled using a knife and cut into small segments measuring 10 to 20 mm. Subsequently, the pericarp pieces were dried in a hot air oven (Binder, model FD 115, Tuttlingen, Germany) at 60 °C for 8 h. Once dried, the samples were ground using a high-speed blade grinder (Thai grinder, WF-20B, Bangkok, Thailand) operating at 25,000 rpm for 2 min. The resulting powder was sieved through a 100-mesh screen to achieve fine consistency. Finally, the TPP (TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B) were sealed in zip-lock foil bags and stored at 4 ± 2 °C in a refrigerator until further analysis. Determination of Visual, Structural and Morphological Characteristics

Visual Characteristics

The visual appearance of the fresh peel, dried peel, and powdered samples was recorded using a hand-held digital camera (Coolpix B500, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Representative images were captured to document the morphological changes during each processing stage.

X-ray Diffractometry (XRD) Analysis

The crystalline structure of the TPP was examined using an X-ray diffractometer (D2 Phaser, Bruker, Germany), following a modified protocol based on Ituen et al. The analysis was performed with a copper radiation source operated at 30 kV and 10 mA, producing X-rays with a wavelength of λ = 1.5406 Å. Diffraction patterns were recorded across a 2θ range of 1° to 60° at a scanning rate of 2° per minute. The diffraction intensity was reported in arbitrary units (a.u.).

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) Spectroscopy

The FTIR spectroscopy was performed using a Spectrum Two spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Bucks, U.K.), following the method described by Ituen et al. Each sample was scanned 16 times within the wavelength range of 400–4000 cm–1, with a spectral resolution of 8 cm–1. The obtained spectra were recorded and presented as transmittance (%).

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

TGA was conducted to assess the thermal stability and weight loss of the samples using a thermogravimetric analyzer (SDT Q-600, TA Instruments, Artisan Technology Group, Champaign, IL), following a modified method by Güzel and Akpınar. Approximately 5–10 mg of each sample was heated from 25 to 500 °C at a constant rate of 10 °C per minute. The weight loss was recorded and expressed as a percentage (%).

Microstructural Characteristics

The microstructural characteristics of the TPP were observed using a scanning electron microscope (SU3900, Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), by following the method based on Hitayezu and Kang. For surface analysis, the samples were cryo-fractured in liquid nitrogen and coated with a thin layer of gold for 5 min using a sputter coater. The coated specimens were then examined under the scanning electron microscope (SEM) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, with images captured at magnifications of 50×, 100×, and 1000×.

Determination of Physicochemical and Functional Properties

Moisture Content

The moisture content of both fresh and dried TP samples (2 g) was determined using an infrared moisture analyzer (Sartorius, MA160, Göttingen, Germany) operating at 105 °C. The results were calculated and reported as a percentage (%).

Water Activity

The water activity (aw) of both fresh and dried TP samples was measured at 25 °C using a dew point water activity analyzer (Series 4TEV, Aqua Lab, WA).

Color Characteristics

The color attributes of the TPP samples, including lightness (L), redness (a), yellowness (b), hue angle (H°), and chroma (C), were evaluated using a Hunter Lab colorimeter (model WR18, Jedto, Pathum Thani, Thailand).

Dry Matter Recovery

The dry matter recovery of the TPP samples was calculated and expressed as a percentage (%) according to the following equation: drymatterre⁢covery(%)=((weight of driedTPPpowder)/(weight ofdrymatterinfresh peel))×100

Determination of Phytochemicals and Antioxidant Activities

Extraction Procedure

Prior to analysis, TPP samples were extracted following a modified method from Sharma et al. Briefly, 30 g of TPP was soaked in 60 mL of 80% ethanol and agitated on an orbital shaker (Miulab GS-20, Zhejiang, China) at 200 rpm for 48 h at room temperature (30 ± 2 °C). The mixture was filtered and centrifuged at 10,000×g for 20 min at room temperature. The supernatant was collected, and the solvent was evaporated using a rotary evaporator (Buchi R-300, Flawil, Switzerland). The obtained extract was used for determining total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, total ascorbic acid content and antioxidant activities.

Determination of Phytochemicals

2.4.5.2.1. Total Phenolic Content (TPC)

TPC was determined based on the Folin–Ciocalteu (FC) method, following the procedure of Costanzo et al. with modifications. An aliquot (274 μL) of the extract was mixed with 274 μL of FC reagent. After 3 min, 1.452 mL of 700 mM sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) was added. The mixture was incubated in the dark for 2 h, and absorbance was measured at 765 nm using an ultraviolet-visible (UV–vis) spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV1800, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per gram dry weight (mg GAE/g DW) using a gallic acid standard curve.

2.4.5.2.2. Total Flavonoid Content (TFC)

TFC was determined using the aluminum chloride (AlCl3) colorimetric method by following the method of Costanzo et al. Briefly, 250 μL of diluted extract was mixed with 75 μL of 5% sodium nitrite (NaNO2). After 6 min, 150 μL of 10% AlCl3 and 500 μL of 1 M NaOH were added. The mixture was adjusted to a final volume of 1.525 mL with distilled water, and absorbance was recorded at 510 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Results were calculated using a quercetin (QE) standard curve and expressed as mg QE equivalents per gram dry weight (mg QE/g DW).

2.4.5.2.3. Total Ascorbic Acid (TAA) Content

TAA content was determined according to Ywassaki and Canniatti-Brazaca with slight modifications. A 2 mL aliquot of the extract was mixed with 2 mL of 5% trichloroacetic acid. The mixture was titrated with 2,6-dichlorophenol indophenol until a stable purple end point was reached. Results were calculated and expressed as mg per 100 g dry weight (mg/100 g DW).

Determination of Antioxidant Activities

2.4.5.3.1. DPPH Radical Scavenging Activity

The DPPH radical scavenging activity in the TPP samples was measured by following the method of Keawpeng et al. with modifications. A 0.1 mL aliquot of the extract was mixed with 3.9 mL of 60 μM DPPH solution. The mixture was incubated in the dark for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured at 515 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Results were reported as the percentage of DPPH radical scavenging activity.

2.4.5.3.2. ABTS Radical Scavenging Activity

The ABTS radical scavenging activity in the TPP samples was measured by following the method of Keawpeng et al. with modifications. One mL of the extract was combined with 1 mL of ABTS reagent and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 734 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Results were expressed as the percentage of ABTS radical scavenging activity.

2.4.5.3.3. Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP)

The FRAP activity in the TPP samples was measured by following the method of Lim et al. with modifications. A 150 μL extract was mixed with 2.5 mL of 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 250 μL of 10 mM tripyridyltriazine (TPTZ), and 250 μL of 12 mM FeCl3. The mixture was incubated in the dark for 1 h, and absorbance was measured at 593 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Antioxidant capacity was calculated using a Trolox standard curve and expressed as μmol Trolox equivalents per 100 mg dry weight (μmol TE/100 mg DW).

Total Carotenoid Content (TCC)

TCC of the TPP samples was determined following the method of Wang et al. with minor modifications. Briefly, 5 g of TPP was mixed with 50 mL of an extraction solvent composed of n-hexane, acetone, and ethanol (50:25:25, v/v/v). The mixture was shaken at 200 rpm for 10 min at room temperature and then centrifuged at 6,500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and adjusted to a final volume of 50 mL with the extraction solvent. The absorbance of the extract was measured at 450 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). TCC was calculated and expressed as mg of β-carotene equivalent per kg of dry weight (mg β-carotene/kg DW).

Volatile Profile Analysis

The volatile compounds of the TPP samples were analyzed using headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS), following the method described by Figueira et al. with slight modifications. An 80 μm divinylbenzene/carbon wide range/polydimethylsiloxane (DVB/C-WR/PDMS) fiber (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) was used for extraction and conditioned prior to use, according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. For analysis, 1 g of each TPP sample was placed into a 20 mL SPME vial, sealed with a silicone septum, and preconditioned at 60 °C for 5 min under continuous agitation at 250 rpm to ensure homogeneous volatilization. The fiber was then exposed to the headspace of the vial for 30 min under the same conditions. After extraction, the fiber was inserted immediately into the GC injector in splitless mode for 2 min at 280 °C for desorption. The GC-MS system (7890B GC coupled to 7000D MS, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) was equipped with an HP-5MS quartz capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm inner diameter × 0.25 μm film thickness; Agilent 19091S-4331). Chromatographic separation was performed under the following oven temperature program: initial hold at 40 °C for 1 min, ramped to 150 °C at 3 °C/min, then increased to 280 °C at 20 °C/min, and held at 280 °C for 5 min. Helium (99.999% purity) was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0–1.3 mL/min. The temperatures of the transfer line, ion source, and quadrupole were set at 280 °C, 230 °C, and 150 °C, respectively. Mass spectra were acquired in electron impact (EI) mode at 70 eV with full scan mode ranging from m/z 35 to 450. Compound identification was based on linear retention indices and mass spectral comparison with the NIST 23 and Wiley 2023 library databases (match score >90%). The relative concentration of each volatile compound was calculated based on its peak area relative to the total peak area of all detected volatiles and expressed as a percentage (%).

Determination of Fatty Acid Composition

The fatty acid composition of the TPP was analyzed following lipid extraction based on the method of Bligh and Dyer with minor modifications. Briefly, 30 g of TPP was homogenized with 210 mL of a solvent mixture comprising chloroform, methanol, and distilled water (60:120:30, v/v/v) at 10,000 rpm for 1 min at 4 °C using a homogenizer (IKA T25 digital ULTRA TURRAX, Germany). Subsequently, 60 mL of chloroform was added, followed by additional homogenization at 10,000 rpm for 30 s. Next, 60 mL of distilled water was incorporated, and homogenization continued for another 30 s. Then, the mixture was centrifuged at 5,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C (Sorvall RC 55 Plus, Thermo-Scientific, Waltham, MA). The supernatant was collected in a separating flask, and the chloroform layer was transferred to an Erlenmeyer flask containing 2–5 g of anhydrous sodium sulfate. After thorough mixing, the solution was filtered through Whatman No. 4 filter paper into a round-bottom flask. The solvent was then evaporated at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator (Eyela N-100, Tokyo, Japan), and any residual solvent was removed by nitrogen flushing. The extracted lipids were subsequently used for fatty acid analysis. Fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) were prepared using methanolic potassium hydroxide (KOH) as the methylation catalyst. Briefly, 100 mg of extracted lipid was mixed with 2 mL of 0.5 M KOH in methanol in a screw-cap glass tube. The mixture was heated in a water bath at 60 °C for 20 min to allow transesterification. After cooling to room temperature, 2 mL of hexane and 1 mL of saturated sodium chloride (NaCl) solution were added. The upper organic layer containing the FAMEs was separated and used for GC-FID analysis. FAMEs prepared following the method of Jahromi et al. A 1 μL aliquot of FAMEs was injected into a gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, GC Agro, Santa Clara, CA) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) operating at a split ratio of 50:1. Separation was performed using an Agilent CP 9080 fused silica capillary column (0.32 mm inner diameter × 30 m, 0.25 μm film thickness). The injection port and detector temperatures were set at 250 °C. The oven program was as follows: initial temperature at 140 °C held for 5 min, ramped to 210 °C at 10 °C/min and held for 5 min, then increased to 250 °C at 20 °C/min and held for 8 min. Helium (99.999%) was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Fatty acids were identified by comparing retention times with a FAME standard mixture (C8:0 to C24:1, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). The results were quantified based on peak area ratios and expressed as relative percentages (%). Reporting was limited to fatty acids with relative concentrations of 0.1% or more of the total peak area. Values were determined from unnormalized gas chromatography-flame ionization detection (GC-FID) peak areas and reported as relative percentages. To minimize analytical inconsistencies and ensure focus on consistently measurable components, fatty acids present in trace amounts (<0.1%) were excluded from the analysis. As a result, the total reported percentages do not sum to 100%.

Antimicrobial Activity

The antibacterial activity of the TPP extract was evaluated against two Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 and B. cereus ATCC 11778) and two Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853) using a modified agar well diffusion method based on Kamel et al. Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to a turbidity equivalent to the 0.5 McFarland standard, corresponding to approximately 108 CFU/mL. Each suspension was evenly spread onto Mueller-Hinton agar plates using sterile swabs. Wells with a diameter of 6 mm were then punched into the agar, and 50 μL of the test extract (dissolved in 10% DMSO at 50 mg/mL) were introduced into each well. The plates were left at room temperature for 1 h to allow prediffusion and subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Gentamicin (0.1 mg/mL) served as the positive control, while 10% DMSO was used as the negative control. Following incubation, the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured in millimeters (mm).

Statistical Analysis

All results are presented as mean values ± standard deviation from three independent replicates (n = 3). Statistical significance was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test at P < 0.05. Data analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 6, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results and Discussion

Visual, Structural and Morphological Characteristics

Visual Characteristics

The visual characteristics of three TP varieties TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B were systematically assessed from fresh peel to dried peel and powder form (Figure ). Distinct visual differences were maintained throughout processing, reflecting varietal compositional differences and the influence of processing conditions on final product quality. In the fresh state, TP-C exhibited a bright orange flavedo with a pale yellow-white albedo, while TP-S showed a similar flavedo but with a noticeably thicker albedo, potentially indicating higher fiber content. TP-B presented a more yellow-orange hue, likely due to variations in carotenoid composition, particularly β-cryptoxanthin and β-carotene. After drying, all samples experienced moisture loss, structural shrinkage, and intensified coloration, with the flavedo retaining pigmentation and the albedo appearing pale yellow. TP-S maintained particularly vivid coloration, suggesting higher pigment concentration or thermal stability, despite expected degradation of heat-sensitive compounds. In powder form, visual distinctions persisted. TP-C appeared medium yellow-orange and uniform, TP-S displayed a bright golden-orange hue with fine, consistent particles, and TP-B exhibited a deeper yellow-orange tone with noticeable heterogeneity. These observations correspond to microstructural results as shown in Figure , where TP-B showed larger, more aggregated particles. These visual differences likely reflect variations in phytochemical composition, particularly flavonoids (hesperidin, narirutin) and carotenoids, with TP-S’s intense coloration potentially indicating higher bioactive content. Moreover, the homogeneity of TP-S powder and the coarser appearance of TP-B align with their respective microstructural properties. Overall, the retention of distinctive visual traits through processing underscores the potential of these powders for applications where color, uniformity, and bioactive content are critical quality indicators, particularly in functional food, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical products. ,

Structural Characteristics

The predominance of the amorphous structure of the tested TPP samples offers significant advantages for food and nutraceutical applications, as confirmed by XRD (Figure A). XRD patterns of the TPP showed broad diffraction peaks within the range of 2θ = 15°–25°, indicative of a largely amorphous polysaccharide matrix. This pattern is characteristic of plant-derived biopolymers such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin, which typically lack long-range crystalline order. Similar XRD profiles have been reported in citrus peel-derived fibers, where broad peaks reflect the disordered molecular arrangement of amorphous cellulose and pectin components. These amorphous structures are known to enhance water absorption, solubility, and the bioavailability of embedded bioactives. Among the samples, TP-B exhibited a slightly more pronounced peak at approximately 2θ = 20.2°, which corresponds to the (110) or (020) planes of cellulose II, a form commonly observed in regenerated or structurally realigned cellulose fibers. This peak may also arise from conformationally aggregated low-methoxyl pectin, which has been shown to form semicrystalline structures under drying or alignment conditions. , The presence of this localized semicrystalline signal suggests a partially preserved or regenerated polysaccharide matrix in TP-B. This interpretation aligns with prior work showing increased crystallinity and peak sharpness in citrus peel-based materials following structural reordering or partial fiber recovery. , TP-S, in contrast, displayed the broadest and least intense diffraction pattern, consistent with a highly amorphous structure likely influenced by finer particle size and greater mechanical disruption of residual crystalline regions, as also observed in finely ground citrus biofibers. The combined FTIR and XRD results highlight distinct molecular and structural variations among the TPP samples. Differences in peak intensities and diffraction patterns across TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B appear to reflect variations in cultivar type, fruit maturity, and processing conditions. For instance, the more intense FTIR signals in TP-S and TP-B indicate higher concentrations of bioactive compounds and polysaccharides, which may contribute to their enhanced functional properties. Likewise, the XRD analysis suggests that TP-B possesses a slightly more ordered structure, likely influenced by increased fiber content, while TP-S exhibits a highly amorphous structure, likely due to finer particle size and more uniform morphology.

XRD pattern (A), FTIR spectra (B) and TGA pattern (C) of three different varieties of TPP (TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B).

FTIR analysis was conducted to identify potential interactions among the components present in the TPP. Overall, the FTIR spectra of the TPP (TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B) exhibited characteristic absorption bands corresponding to functional groups commonly associated with citrus peel components (Figure B). All samples displayed broad absorption peaks in the range of 3300–3400 cm–1, attributed to O–H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups. These features suggest the presence of polysaccharides such as pectin and cellulose, along with phenolic compounds. The notable intensity and breadth of these peaks imply extensive hydrogen bonding and strong hydrophilic interactions, factors considered critical for moisture retention and antioxidant activity, as reported in previous studies on citrus peel-based materials. In the region of 2920–2950 cm–1, distinct peaks were observed corresponding to C–H stretching vibrations of aliphatic chains. These are typically associated with cellulose, hemicellulose, and essential oils such as limonene, which are prevalent in citrus matrices. Additionally, pronounced peaks near 1730–1750 cm–1 were assigned to CO stretching vibrations of esterified carboxyl groups, primarily originating from pectin and flavonoid glycosides, consistent with findings from studies on tangerine peel-based biocomposites. These observations reinforce the structural significance of pectic substances and the presence of bioactive flavonoids, which contribute to both the gelling properties and functional health benefits of citrus-derived materials. Further peaks detected within 1600–1650 cm–1 were attributed to CC stretching vibrations of aromatic rings, indicative of flavonoid structures, such as hesperidin and naringin. Signals observed in the 1000–1200 cm–1 region corresponded to C–O stretching vibrations, commonly linked to polysaccharides and glycosidic linkages. Similar functional group assignments have been reported in citrus peel formulations with demonstrated antioxidant potential. Notably, TP-S and TP-B exhibited slightly higher peak intensities in these regions compared to TP-C, suggesting greater concentrations of bioactive compounds and polysaccharides. This observation is consistent with their brighter coloration and enhanced functional properties, as polysaccharide-rich fractions from citrus peels have been correlated with elevated bioactivity levels.

TGA of the TPP (TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B) was conducted to evaluate their thermal stability and compositional characteristics (Figure C). The TGA curves revealed a multistage decomposition process, consistent with the complex composition of citrus derived materials. In the initial stage, a gradual weight loss was observed from room temperature to approximately 150 °C, attributed to the evaporation of free and bound moisture. TP-S and TP-B exhibited slightly higher moisture loss compared to TP-C, aligning with FTIR results that indicated extensive hydrogen bonding and hydrophilic interactions in these samples, likely due to their higher polysaccharide content and finer particle size. This observation is consistent with the study of Teng et al. who found that citrus peel hydrogels rich in hydroxyl-containing polysaccharides such as pectin and cellulose demonstrated enhanced water retention through hydrogen bonding. Additionally, smaller particle sizes increase surface area, facilitating moisture loss and thermal sensitivity. The major decomposition stage, occurring between 150 and 400 °C, corresponds to the thermal degradation of polysaccharides, particularly pectin, cellulose, and hemicellulose, along with phenolic compounds and flavonoids. The more pronounced weight loss observed in TP-S and TP-B during this stage is consistent with their higher concentrations of bioactive compounds, as indicated by stronger FTIR peaks. This behavior aligns with findings that pectin begins to degrade around 180 °C, followed by cellulose and hemicellulose near 270–370 °C, while thermally labile phenolics and flavonoids contribute additional weight loss due to their aromatic and esterified structures. Similarly, Xu et al. reported that pectin decomposition begins at lower temperatures compared to cellulose and significantly influences the thermal behavior of plant biomass. Moreover, the presence of esterified carboxyl groups (CO stretching) and aromatic rings (CC stretching) detected in FTIR further contributed to the decomposition profile. Zannini et al. reported that functional groups such as CO and CC are prone to thermal cleavage, intensifying weight loss in samples with richer bioactive content. At temperatures above 400 °C, the residual weight loss reflects the decomposition of more thermally stable components, such as lignin or char. Differences in residual ash content among the samples potentially relate to variations in inorganic content or fiber composition. Lignin’s complex aromatic structure is known for its slow thermal degradation, contributing significantly to residual mass. Notably, TP-B displayed a slightly different thermal profile due to its higher fiber content, which exhibits semicrystalline order. This semicrystalline nature enhances thermal resistance, delaying decomposition. In contrast, TP-S, with its highly amorphous structure and finer particle size, likely underwent more uniform thermal degradation due to the absence of such structural order. Overall, the TGA behavior of the TPP reflects their compositional differences, where moisture content, polysaccharide structure, bioactive compounds, and fiber crystallinity influence thermal stability and degradation patterns.

Morphological Characteristics

The morphological characteristics of the three TPP samples (TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B) were systematically analyzed using SEM at varying magnifications (Figure ). Distinct differences in particle size, shape, and surface texture were observed, indicating the influence of both the raw material properties and processing conditions on the final powder structure. At lower magnifications (×50 and ×100), TP-C exhibited irregular, flake-like particles with a broad size distribution and jagged edges. These features are indicative of a higher fibrous content and suggest that the milling process induced a brittle fracture, resulting in heterogeneous particle morphologies. This is consistent with findings that grinding fibrous plant materials leads to irregular particle shapes and rough surface textures. In contrast, TP-S displayed a more uniform particle distribution, characterized by smaller, rounded particles with comparatively smoother surfaces. This regularity implies a controlled milling process that reduced particle size effectively, potentially increasing the surface area available for mass transfer during extraction processes. Similar observations have been made where superfine grinding increased surface area and improved the bioactive compound yield in plant-based powders, as well as in citrus fiber powders where reduced particle size modified physicochemical and functional properties. TP-B, on the other hand, showed larger, clustered particle aggregates with visibly porous surfaces and embedded fibrous structures. The presence of these porous, interconnected networks is likely to enhance water retention and promote sustained release of bioactive compounds, as has been noted in porous powders with larger particle structures used for controlled release applications.

At higher magnifications (×1000), additional morphological distinctions were evident. TP-C maintained a flaky, sheet-like microstructure, consistent with a high proportion of fibrous components. TP-S exhibited compact, plate-like particles arranged in layered structures, which may facilitate close packing and improved flow-ability in powder formulations. TP-B was characterized by dense, cohesive aggregates with rough, porous surfaces, suggesting stronger interparticle adhesion and a higher tendency toward agglomeration. Such morphological differences have been shown to influence the flow properties and bulk behavior of powders directly, with surface roughness and particle shape playing crucial roles. Furthermore, the degree of particle agglomeration varied significantly among the samples. TP-C showed moderate agglomeration, while TP-S demonstrated higher levels of particle clustering, potentially due to increased surface energy from finer particles. TP-B exhibited the most pronounced agglomeration, likely attributed to its larger, porous structures that favor mechanical interlocking and moisture adsorption. These differences in agglomeration behavior are expected to influence key functional properties, including powder flow-ability, hygroscopicity, and oxidative stability during storage. From a functional perspective, the observed morphological variations have important implications for the application of TPP. TP-S, with its finer and more uniform particles, is potentially advantageous for formulations requiring rapid dispersion, such as instant beverages or bioactive-enriched drinks, due to its increased surface area and reduced sedimentation tendency. In contrast, the larger, porous aggregates of TP-B suggest suitability for controlled-release applications, such as nutraceutical formulations and functional food products aimed at prolonged bioactive delivery. TP-C, with its intermediate characteristics, may provide a balance of dispersibility and sustained release, depending on formulation requirements. Overall, these findings underscore the critical role of morphological attributes in determining the functional performance of the TPP. A clear understanding of these structural differences is essential for optimizing processing parameters and tailoring powders to specific applications in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries.

Physicochemical and Functional Properties

The physicochemical properties, bioactive compound contents, and antioxidant activities of TPP varied significantly among the three studied cultivars (Table ). These findings highlight the impact of cultivar type on the functional quality of citrus peel powders and provide valuable insights into their potential use in functional foods, nutraceuticals, and natural antioxidant formulations. , Fresh tangerine peels from all cultivars exhibited high moisture content (82.44–83.59%). After drying, the moisture content significantly decreased, with TP-B showing the lowest value (4.93%), indicating superior stability during storage. However, the water activity (aw) of the dried peels varied slightly, with TP-C showing the lowest value (0.19), followed by TP-S (0.20), and TP-B (0.21). This indicates that, while TP-B had the lowest moisture content, TP-C may possess slightly better microbial stability due to its lower aw value. Similar observations have been reported in studies on citrus peels, where both low moisture content and aw are critical for ensuring storage stability and microbial safety. , Color analysis revealed noticeable visual differences among the cultivars. TP-S exhib-ited the highest lightness (L = 67.70) and yellowness (b = 135.25), resulting in a brighter and more vibrant appearance, which is advantageous in food products, where visual appeal is important. Moreover, TP-B showed the highest redness (a = 4.36), adding to its distinct color profile. Hue angle (H°) and chroma (C) values were relatively consistent across the samples, with TP-S again displaying slightly higher chroma (135.27), enhancing its color intensity. In contrast, TP-C and TP-B exhibited darker and less vibrant color profiles, which can still be valuable in applications prioritizing functionality over appearance. In terms of bioactive compounds, TP-C demonstrated the most robust nutritional profile, with the highest TPC (49.98 mg GAE g–1 DW) and TFC (36.11 mg QE g–1 DW). These elevated levels directly contributed to its superior antioxidant activities, as evidenced by its leading performance in DPPH radical scavenging activity (74.18%), ABTS radical scavenging activity (70.12%), and FRAP (24.79 μmol TE/100 mg DW). Such findings align with prior research confirming that citrus peels are rich in phenolic compounds that significantly enhance antioxidant capacity. Furthermore, TP-C contained the highest levels of TAA (76.78 mg/100 g DW) and TCC (46.78 mg β-carotene/kg DW), supporting its antioxidant potency. Carotenoids contribute to both the coloration and health benefits of citrus peels, including anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. The phenolic-flavonoid profile recorded in the TPP samples, particularly in TP-C, is consistent with the classes of compounds recently associated with potent in vivo anti-inflammatory and analgesic activities, such as those demonstrated for quercetin, ellagic acid, chlorogenic acid, and neochlorogenic acid in a phenolic-rich fruit extract of J. regia. This supports the potential of TPP, and particularly TP-C, as a multifunctional nutraceutical matrix beyond its antioxidant role. While TP-C excelled in bioactive compound content and antioxidant activities, TP-S consistently exhibited lower values across these parameters, limiting its potential for health-promoting formulations despite its appealing color attributes. Meanwhile, TP-B presented a balanced profile with moderate levels of bioactive compounds and antioxidant activities, along with the highest dry matter recovery (27.05%). This suggests TP-B is particularly practical for applications prioritizing production efficiency while still maintaining acceptable functional properties. Beyond compositional characterization, the relatively high phenolic, flavonoid, and carotenoid levels recorded in the TPP samples, particularly in TP-C, also support the feasibility of adopting emerging green extraction strategies for their large-scale recovery. Recent studies have demonstrated that bioactive carotenoids can be efficiently recovered from fruit-processing byproducts using ultrasound-assisted extraction combined with volatile natural deep eutectic solvents such as menthol:thymol, achieving yields comparable to those obtained with conventional organic solvents. The application of such eco-friendly extraction approaches to TP could further strengthen its valorization as a sustainable, high-value nutraceutical ingredient.

When compared with previously published reports on other tangerine (C. reticulata) cultivars and closely related citrus species, the three Thai cultivars examined in the present study, particularly TP-C, exhibited notably superior phytochemical and antioxidant profiles. For example, Chen et al. evaluated 27 local citrus cultivars from China and reported that the total phenolic content of the peels ranged between 3.31 and 23.46 mg GAE/g DW, with the highest value recorded in the mandarin cultivar Guihuadinanfeng (C. reticulata) at 23.46 mg GAE/g DW, accompanied by a total flavonoid content of 21.37 mg rutin equivalent/g DW. Similarly, Wang et al. analyzed 35 C. reticulata varieties belonging to pummelos, oranges, tangerines, mandarins, and hybrids, and reported widely varying antioxidant capacities across cultivars, yet the highest values recorded in that study were still below the levels observed in the present work. In comparison, the TPC of TP-C (49.98 mg GAE/g DW) was more than 2-fold higher than the highest value reported by Chen et al., and even the lowest among the three Thai cultivars tested here (TP-S, 32.41 mg GAE/g DW) exceeded the upper end of that range. On the other hand, comparable or lower values have also been reported for other tangerine-based matrices, including the Sai Nam Phueng tangerine from Northern Thailand, tangerines cultivated on Madeira Island, and aged and fresh dried tangerine peel. Furthermore, when the comparison is extended to closely related citrus fruit species, previous reports on mandarin, orange, lemon, grapefruit, and kumquat peels cultivated in Vietnam, Phlegrean mandarin (C. reticulata), and several citrus peel extracts summarized by Sharma et al. also showed phenolic, flavonoid, ascorbic acid, and antioxidant values that were generally in the lower range of those presented in this study. Similar trends were observed for DPPH, ABTS, FRAP, TAA, and total carotenoid contents, where TP-C consistently outperformed the majority of tangerine cultivars and citrus species reported in the recent literature, and TP-B and TP-S remained comparable to or higher than most of those reported values. These differences could possibly be attributed to cultivar-specific genotypic features, cultivation environment, maturity at harvest, and postharvest handling, which together govern the accumulation of bioactive metabolites in the pericarp tissue. Overall, the results showed that the three Thai tangerine cultivars evaluated in this study, particularly TP-C, could possibly be positioned among the most bioactive-rich tangerine pericarp sources reported in the recent literature, thereby strengthening their candidacy for high-value applications in the functional food and nutraceutical sectors.

Volatile Profile

A total of 73 volatile compounds were identified across three TPP varieties (TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B), and their volatile profiles are detailed in Table . Analysis revealed the presence of monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, aldehydes, alcohols, and esters, with notable intervarietal differences in composition and relative abundance. Monoterpene hydrocarbons were the predominant class of volatiles in all samples, with D-limonene being the most abundant compound, accounting for 79.81%, 79.67%, and 81.89% of the total volatile fraction in TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B, respectively. This finding is consistent with previous studies highlighting its major role in tangerine peel aroma and its high concentration in citrus peels. Its high relative abundance across all varieties underscores its significance in providing the characteristic fresh and citrus-like fragrance of tangerine peel. Following D-limonene, β-pinene was the second most abundant volatile compound, present at 8.91%, 8.83%, and 8.73% in TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B, respectively, followed by α-pinene (2.18%, 2.05%, and 2.20%). These results align with findings that these compounds are inherent to tangerine peel, albeit with some varietal differences in concentration. The three tangerine varieties exhibited notable differences in their volatile composition. TP-C had the most diverse volatile profile, with 59 identified compounds, followed by TP-S (55 compounds) and TP-B (49 compounds). These variations suggest genotypic influences on volatile biosynthesis pathways. Several compounds were found exclusively in specific varieties, further supporting this hypothesis. TP-C was distinguished by its exclusive compounds such as linalyl acetate, and α-sinensal, which contribute to floral and sweet aromatic notes. Similarly, TP-B contained distinctive compounds like methyl geranate and linalyl formate, while TP-S was marked by the presence of hashishene and α-thujene. These variations suggest that each variety may offer distinct sensory attributes, making them suitable for different applications in food and fragrance industries. Although monoterpenes were the dominant class, oxygenated volatiles (such as aldehydes, alcohols, and esters) are expected to play an important role in the overall aroma complexity due to their low odor thresholds. Among aldehydes, decanal (0.52%, 0.38%, 0.53%), perillaldehyde (0.20%, 0.15%, 0.15%), and dodecanal (0.10%, 0.06%, 0.08%) were present in varying concentrations, contributing citrus, soapy, and sweet notes. Alcohols, including linalool (0.90%, 0.71%, 0.48%), α-terpineol (0.59%, 0.41%, 0.19%), and limonene-10-ol (0.09%, 0.05%, 0.07%), added floral, sweet, and citrus-like attributes. The elevated concentration of oxygenated volatiles in TP-C suggests a more intense and multifaceted aroma profile compared to TP-B and TP-S. Since oxygenated compounds generally have a stronger olfactory impact, their presence is likely to enhance the sensory perception of TP-C.

Fatty Acid Profile

The fatty acid (FA) composition of TPP from three different varieties (TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B) showed significant variations in individual fatty acids (Table ). The most abundant fatty acids across all varieties were linoleic acid (C18:2), palmitic acid (C16:0), and linolenic acid (C18:3), consistent with previous studies on citrus pericarp lipid profiles. Recent studies have also indicated that citrus peels are rich sources of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) and can play a role in lipid metabolism and bioactive functions. The total reported fatty acid percentages ranged from approximately 42% to 48% across the three cultivars. This outcome reflects our methodological decision to report only fatty acids present at or above 0.1% relative abundance. Presenting the data in this way avoids dilution of meaningful trends by trace-level noise and improves the interpretability of cultivar-specific lipid differences. Because the values were not normalized, they represent raw relative proportions suitable for comparing major fatty acid distributions rather than total lipid profiles. However, distinct differences were observed among the varieties, likely due to genetic factors, environmental conditions, and metabolic enzyme activities affecting lipid biosynthesis. The major saturated fatty acids detected included palmitic acid (C16:0), stearic acid (C18:0), and myristic acid (C14:0). TP-S had the highest concentration of palmitic acid (11.74%) compared to TP-B (11.16%) and TP-C (10.33%) (P < 0.05). Stearic acid followed a similar trend, being significantly higher in TP-B (1.59%) than in TP-S (1.28%) and TP-C (1.00%). These differences may be attributed to varietal specificity in lipid metabolism, particularly the enzymatic regulation of fatty acid elongation and desaturation.

Similarly, palmitoleic acid (C16:1) exhibited the highest con-centration in TP-B (1.12%) compared to TP-S (0.84%) and TP-C (0.72%). The variations in MUFA content may be linked to differential expressions of stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD), an enzyme responsible for converting SFAs into MUFAs, which could be influenced by genetic and environmental factors. Linoleic acid (C18:2) and linolenic acid (C18:3) were the dominant PUFAs, with TP-S exhibiting the highest linoleic acid content (16.01%), significantly higher than TP-B (13.84%) and TP-C (13.20%) (P < 0.05). Linolenic acid followed a similar pattern, with TP-S (8.46%) having a slightly higher concentration than TP-B (8.15%) and TP-C (6.21%). This suggests that TP-S may have a more active fatty acid desaturation process, facilitated by enzymes such as fatty acid desaturase (FAD), which converts linoleic acid into linolenic acid. Conversely, caprylic acid (C8:0) and nonanoic acid (C9:0) were undetectable in TP-B, likely reflecting genetic differences that influence specific fatty acid biosynthesis. Studies suggest that these short-chain fatty acids are typically synthesized through microbial activity in citrus peels rather than plant-based enzymatic pathways, which could explain their absence in some varieties. Additionally, these fatty acids are often products of lipid degradation or secondary metabolism, indicating that differences in postharvest processing or enzymatic activity could play a role in their presence or absence. Furthermore, citrus peels are known to contain varying levels of essential oil compounds that may influence fatty acid synthesis and degradation pathways, potentially affecting their detectability in different tangerine varieties.

Antimicrobial Activity

The antimicrobial activity of tangerine pericarp extract (TPE) from different varieties was evaluated against S. aureus (ATCC 25923), B. cereus (ATCC 11778), E. coli (ATCC 25922), and P. aeruginosa (ATCC 27853). The results of inhibition zones indicate differential antimicrobial potency against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (Table ). The antimicrobial potential of citrus peels is highly dependent on their chemical composition, particularly limonene and other terpenoids. TPE exhibited significant antibacterial activity against S. aureus, with inhibition zones for TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B ranging from 27.01 ± 0.12 mm to 28.33 ± 0.23 mm, surpassing the inhibition zone of the positive control, gentamicin (26.03 ± 0.16 mm). This strong inhibition suggests that bioactive compounds in TP, such as flavonoids (hesperidin, naringin), phenolic acids, and essential oils, effectively disrupt bacterial cell membranes and enzymatic functions. Alsakhawy et al. reported that citrus peel extract, particularly essential oils (EOs), exhibit strong antimicrobial activity against S. aureus, attributed to their high limonene, flavonoid, and terpenoid content. Similarly, Ariani et al. reported that tangerine peel contains high levels of D-limonene and linalool, which possess potent antibacterial properties against S. aureus. Prevete et al. demonstrated that encapsulation of citrus peel extracts in liposomes significantly enhanced their antibacterial activity, particularly against S. aureus. The inhibition zones for B. cereus ranged from 20.91 ± 0.87 mm (TP-S) to 29.33 ± 0.31 mm (TP-C), with TP-C showing superior activity compared to the positive control (gentamicin, 27.55 ± 0.58 mm). Hasoon et al. demonstrated that tangerine peel-derived silver nanoparticles exhibited enhanced antibacterial efficacy against B. cereus. Shehata et al. evaluated the antimicrobial activity of citrus peel extracts against B. cereus and determined that this activity is attributed to phenolic acids and essential oils present in the peels, which can effectively disrupt bacterial cell membranes and inhibit cellular functions. On the other hand, the TPE exhibited no inhibition against E. coli, while gentamicin produced a clear inhibition zone of 24.47 ± 0.21 mm. Yahya and Abid demonstrated that silver nanoparticles synthesized from citrus peels showed significant inhibition of E. coli, whereas plain citrus extracts alone were ineffective. In another study, Shehata et al. found minimal inhibition of TPE against E. coli due to its ability to modify porins (outer membrane proteins) and actively pump out antimicrobial molecules, preventing intracellular accumulation. Similarly, TPE exhibited no inhibition against P. aeruginosa, while gentamicin demonstrated an inhibition zone of 24.15 ± 0.17 mm. This aligns with previous reports that P. aeruginosa is highly resistant to plant-derived antimicrobials due to its efflux pump mechanisms and reduced membrane permeability. Hassan et al. found that P. aeruginosa has high biofilm-forming capability and that biofilm contributes significantly to resistance against antimicrobial agents, including plant phenolics. Several studies have shown that Gram-negative bacteria possess an outer membrane that limits the penetration of bioactive compounds, reducing susceptibility as compared to Gram-positive bacteria. , The observed lack of antimicrobial activity against E. coli and P. aeruginosa can be attributed to their structural and physiological defenses characteristics of Gram-negative bacteria. Both species possess an outer membrane enriched with lipopolysaccharides, which limits the penetration of many plant-derived bioactive compounds. In addition, P. aeruginosa is known for its low membrane permeability, highly active efflux pumps, and strong biofilm-forming ability, all of which contribute to its intrinsic resistance to antimicrobial agents. These mechanisms explain why TPE, while effective against Gram-positive strains, showed no inhibitory effect against these Gram-negative pathogens. Furthermore, Overall, among the three TPE varieties tested, TP-C exhibited the strongest antimicrobial activity, particularly against S. aureus and B. cereus, while TP-S and TP-B demonstrated slightly lower but still significant antibacterial effects. This variation may be attributed to differences in the flavonoid and phenolic acid content among tangerine varieties, which are influenced by genetic and environmental factors.

Conclusion

This study presents a detailed comparative analysis of the physicochemical properties, structural characteristics, bioactive compounds, antioxidant capacity, and antimicrobial activities of TPP derived from three Thai cultivars: TP-C, TP-S, and TP-B. TP-C demonstrated the most advantageous functional attributes, exhibiting superior levels of total phenolic content (49.98 mg GAE/g DW), total flavonoids (36.11 mg QE/g DW), and antioxidant activity (DPPH: 74.18%). Notably, it demonstrated superior antimicrobial efficacy against S. aureus and B. cereus, exhibiting inhibition zones up to 29.33 mm. Structural analysis indicated a higher degree of semicrystallinity in TP-B, evidenced by a characteristic XRD peak at 2θ = 20.2°, which may be attributed to fiber rearrangement. Conversely, the TP-S sample exhibited the most amorphous structure, a characteristic attributed to its smaller particle size and more extensive mechanical processing. This is consistent with its superior water retention and reduced thermal stability as determined by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). TP-C exhibited greater bioactivity; however, TP-B yielded a higher dry matter recovery percentage (27.05%) and a more organized fiber structure, potentially advantageous for thermally stable applications. While exhibiting reduced bioactive components, TP-S displayed advantageous characteristics, namely a fine particle structure and vibrant coloration, which may prove beneficial in food applications prioritizing aesthetics. The methodology was limited by the use of unnormalized fatty acid data, with the reporting restricted to components ≥ 0.1%, and the employment of a single extract concentration (50 mg/mL) in antimicrobial assays, precluding a comprehensive assessment of dose–response relationships. The research concludes that tangerine varietal selection significantly influences the functional attributes of pericarp powders, thereby establishing TP-C as the most promising candidate for applications within the functional food and nutraceutical sectors.
