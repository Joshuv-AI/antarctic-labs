GlobalDentBench: A Multinational Benchmark for Evaluating LLM Clinical Reasoning in Dentistry with Expert Calibration
Junjie Zhao, Jingyi Liang, Zhenyang Cai, Jiaming Zhang, Zhenwei Wen, Shuzhi Deng, Wenjing Yi, Chunfeng Luo, Hexian Zhang, Junying Chen, Tianrui Liu, Zhuhui Bai, Zixu Zhang, Pradeep Singh, Xiang Liu, Jianquan Li, Nhan L Tran, Falk Schwendicke, Zuolin Jin, Lijian Jin, Liangyi Chen, Wei-fa Yang, Benyou Wang, Junwen Wang, Shan Jiang

Abstract
While large language models (LLMs) hold transformative potential for medicine, their reasoning robustness and safety in real-world clinical scenarios remain critically underexplored, particularly in dentistry. Here we introduce GlobalDentBench, the first multinational dental benchmark, featuring a taxonomy that encompasses 14 dental specialties across 88 countries and regions spanning six continents. The benchmark comprises 8,978 expert-validated questions across three formats (multiple-choice, short-answer, and case-based questions) and assesses three progressive reasoning levels: knowledge recall (L1), routine reasoning (L2), and individualized reasoning (L3). To ensure data quality, the automated construction framework was calibrated by six senior dentists, achieving expert agreement rates of 99.98% for multiple-choice and short-answer questions and 96.78% for the more complex case-based questions. Evaluation of 12 frontier LLMs on GlobalDentBench revealed a sharp, stepwise performance degradation with increasing reasoning complexity. Specifically, accuracy plummeted from 81.34% on multiple-choice to 64.53% on short-answer and 22.34% on case-based questions, while declining markedly from 74.01% at L1 to 55.64% at L2 and 35.71% at L3. More critically, risk analysis of real-world dental cases demonstrated an alarming overall unsafe rate of 31.01% in LLM-generated clinical recommendations, with 4.51% posing risks of irreversible patient harm and risks particularly pronounced in specialties such as orthodontics. These findings expose fundamental limitations in the medical reasoning and safety of current LLMs. Consequently, GlobalDentBench provides a scalable foundation for trustworthy clinical AI evaluation, underscoring the urgent need for rigorous validation before the safe deployment of these models in healthcare.

2
Results

2.1
Benchmark Overview

To systematically evaluate large language models, we developed GlobalDentBench (illustrated in Fig.
1
), a scalable and trustworthy multinational dental benchmark comprising 8,978 questions. GlobalDentBench is designed along four aspects:

•

Geographic coverage
(Fig.
1
A): The dataset spans 88 countries and regions across six continents, reflecting a diverse range of global educational and clinical standards.

•

Reasoning level
(Fig.
1
B): To represent question requirements in a clinically interpretable manner, each item was assigned to one of three levels: L1 (knowledge recall), assessing recall of established foundational dental knowledge without meaningful case-based reasoning; L2 (routine reasoning), assessing reasoning based primarily on the core clinical presentation and typical diagnostic or therapeutic logic; and L3 (individualized reasoning), assessing reasoning that requires incorporation of patient-specific information, individualized constraints, or non-standard clinical considerations to reach the final judgement.

•

Dental Disciplines
(Fig.
1
C): Each item was mapped to one of 14 dental disciplines, such as Anesthesia and Medical Emergencies (AME), Basic Sciences and Preventive Dentistry (BSPD), Caries, Tooth Defects and Trauma (CTDT), Conventional Prosthodontics (CP), Dentoalveolar Surgery (DS), Maxillofacial Diseases and Surgery (MFDS), Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology (OMR), Oral Implantology (OI), Oral Mucosal Diseases (OMD), Orthodontics (Ortho), Pediatric Dentistry (PD), Pulp and Periapical Diseases (PPD), Periodontal and Peri-implant Diseases (PP), and Systemic Health, Pharmacology and Safety (SHPS).

•

Question types
(Fig.
1
D): The benchmark comprises three distinct formats: 3,679 Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs) sourced from official qualification examinations; 3,709 Short-Answer Questions (SAQs) derived from officially recognized dental books; and 1,590 Case-Based Questions (CBQs) extracted from peer-reviewed clinical case reports for scenario-driven evaluation.

To support both scalability and trustworthiness, GlobalDentBench combined automated construction with expert validation. The benchmark was generated through an automated agent pipeline and subsequently reviewed by six senior dentists, who verified question validity and answer correctness. Under a unified evaluation protocol, we assessed 12 frontier LLMs, including six proprietary models (Gemini-3-Flash-Preview, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview, GPT-5.4, Grok-4, Claude-Sonnet-4.6, and Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro) and six open-source models (DeepSeek-R1, Kimi-K2.5, GLM-5, Qwen3.5-Flash, Qwen3.5-Plus, and MiniMax-M2.7). MCQ items were scored by exact-match accuracy, whereas SAQ and CBQ responses were evaluated using Gemini-3-Flash-Preview as the judge model.

2.2
Performance Disparities Across Question Type and Reasoning Complexity

Evaluation across the three question types revealed a clear stepwise decline in model performance along an increasing gradient of clinical complexity, from standardized MCQs to open-ended SAQs and real-world CBQs (Fig.
2
A). The aggregate mean score across all evaluated models was highest on MCQs (81.34%), decreased on SAQs (64.53%), and declined further on CBQs (22.34%). This gradient aligned with the distinct provenance and difficulty structure of the three datasets, rather than with response format alone. Whereas MCQs and SAQs primarily assessed standardized examination knowledge and textbook-based responses, respectively, CBQs were derived from real-world clinical case reports and required models to reason over nuanced and sometimes conflicting clinical information.

To further resolve this performance gradient, we stratified the benchmark into three hierarchical reasoning levels (L1–L3) based on the demands of the underlying clinical tasks (Fig.
2
B). To reduce the influence of uneven question-type distributions, we report the Macro Average Score across question types. The results show a clear decline in performance as reasoning complexity increases: 74.01% on knowledge recall tasks (L1), 55.64% on routine clinical reasoning tasks (L2), and 35.71% on individualized reasoning tasks (L3), which require integration of patient-specific information, individualized constraints, or non-standard clinical considerations to reach the final judgement.

2.3
Comparative Analysis of Frontier Large Language Models

We evaluated 12 frontier LLMs spanning both proprietary and open-source systems: Gemini-3-Flash-Preview, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview, GPT-5.4, Grok-4, Claude-Sonnet-4.6, Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro, DeepSeek-R1, Kimi-K2.5, GLM-5, Qwen3.5-Flash, Qwen3.5-Plus, and MiniMax-M2.7. Overall proficiency was quantified as an unweighted macro-average across the three question types (Fig.
2
C), thereby reducing bias from the imbalanced question distribution. Under this metric, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview achieved the highest overall proficiency (63.27%). Among the open-source models, GLM-5 showed the strongest overall performance (56.43%).

When stratified by reasoning level, performance declined consistently from L1 to L3 across all 12 models (Fig.
2
D). Scores remained relatively high on L1 and L2, with most models clustering at 63.77–82.64% and 45.67–63.75%, respectively, but dropped sharply on L3, where all models scored from 23.30–46.75%. Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview led on L1 and L2, whereas Claude-Sonnet-4.6 achieved the highest score on L3. Importantly, all models remained below 50% on L3, indicating that individualized reasoning posed a shared challenge even for the strongest models. Overall, the results suggest a stepwise degradation in performance as reasoning demands increased, with substantial declines from knowledge recall to routine clinical reasoning and again from routine clinical reasoning to patient-specific individualized reasoning.

To assess the cost–performance profile of each model, we estimated model-specific inference costs by applying official pricing schedules to the observed input and output token counts across all inference records, and expressed them as the mean cost per 1,000 queries. In the resulting cost–performance distribution (Fig.
2
E), variation in cost was substantially larger than variation in macro-average score, indicating that performance gains were modest relative to differences in inference cost. Among proprietary models, Gemini-3-Flash-Preview provided the most favorable trade-off, with a macro-average score of 61.59% at $3.72 per 1,000 queries. Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview achieved the highest macro-average score overall (63.27%) but at a substantially higher cost ($18.45 per 1,000 queries), while Claude-Sonnet-4.6 also ranked near the top (59.71%) at $19.59 per 1,000 queries. Among open-source models, GLM-5 and Kimi-K2.5 showed the strongest trade-offs: GLM-5 reached 56.43% at $5.48 per 1,000 queries, whereas Kimi-K2.5 reached 55.49% at only $1.37 per 1,000 queries. By contrast, Grok-4 incurred the highest cost ($38.14 per 1,000 queries) while achieving a macro-average score of 57.77%. Overall, higher inference cost did not consistently translate into better overall performance.

2.4
Performance Disparities Across Dental Disciplines

We summarized discipline-level performance across the 14 dental disciplines as a heat map in Fig.
3
, with darker shading indicating higher performance. For each discipline, the score was calculated as the macro-average of model performance across the three question types (MCQ, SAQ and CBQ). Across all 12 models, Oral Mucosal Diseases achieved the highest mean score (OMD, 63.66%), followed by Pulp and Periapical Diseases (PPD, 59.62%) and Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology (OMR, 59.44%). By contrast, the lowest mean scores were observed in Orthodontics (Ortho, 50.59%), Pediatric Dentistry (PD, 51.08%) and Conventional Prosthodontics (CP, 51.92%). The gap between the highest- and lowest-performing disciplines was 13.07 percentage points. This discipline-level pattern was broadly consistent across models: Oral Mucosal Diseases (OMD) was the top-scoring discipline for all models, whereas the lowest-scoring discipline varied across models but was most frequently Orthodontics (Ortho). These results indicate that model performance differed systematically across dental specialties, with stronger performance in mucosal and endodontic-related domains and weaker performance in orthodontic, pediatric and prosthodontic contexts.

2.5
Risk Analysis of LLM Answers in Case-Based Questions

To evaluate the clinical safety of model-generated answers in real-world dental scenarios, we performed a risk analysis of 19,080 responses from 12 frontier LLMs across 1,590 case-based questions. Responses were assigned to one of three safety-risk categories:
S0 denoted clinically safe or low-risk responses
, including accurate answers or minor imperfections that would not be expected to cause iatrogenic harm requiring additional intervention.
S1 denoted unsafe responses with potential for reversible patient harm
, such as avoidable complications, incorrect treatment sequencing, or clinical re-work requiring additional intervention, but without permanent anatomical or lifelong functional loss.
S2 denoted unsafe responses with potential for irreversible or life-threatening harm
, including errors that could plausibly cause permanent anatomical or functional damage, such as avoidable tooth loss, permanent sensory nerve injury, medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, or persistent occlusal compromise, as well as delayed management of malignancy, severe infection, or other life-threatening conditions. Overall, 68.99% of responses were classified as S0, whereas 31.01% were classified as unsafe (Fig.
4
A). Most unsafe responses belonged to S1 (26.50%), while S2 responses accounted for a smaller but clinically important fraction (4.51%). Thus, nearly one-third of LLM-generated answers in real-world dental cases contained potentially harmful clinical content, including a smaller subset with potential for irreversible or life-threatening consequences, underscoring the need for expert validation before clinical use.

Model-wise risk profiles showed substantial heterogeneity (Fig.
4
B,C). For model-level analysis, we used the total number of responses generated by each model as the denominator. The overall unsafe rate used the number of S1 and S2 responses as the numerator, whereas the S1 and S2 rates used the corresponding category-specific counts as numerators. Based on this calculation, overall unsafe rates ranged from 15.97% to 45.85% across models. GPT-5.4, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview and Gemini-3-Flash-Preview showed the lowest overall unsafe rates, whereas Grok-4, MiniMax-M2.7 and Qwen3.5-Flash showed the highest rates. This model-level variation was driven mainly by differences in S1 responses, whose rates increased substantially from the lowest-risk to the highest-risk models (Fig.
4
B). By contrast, S2 responses occurred at lower absolute rates but were still observed across all models (Fig.
4
C). MiniMax-M2.7 showed the highest S2 rate, followed by Qwen3.5-Flash and Grok-4, whereas Claude-Sonnet-4.6, GPT-5.4 and Qwen3.5-Plus had the lowest S2 rates. The model ranking for S2 risk therefore differed from that for S1 risk, indicating that a lower overall unsafe rate did not necessarily imply better control of severe-risk clinical errors.

The discipline-level heatmaps revealed distinct specialty-specific risk structures (Fig.
4
D,E). For discipline-level analysis, responses were grouped by dental specialty and model, with the total number of responses in each model–discipline pair used as the denominator. The overall unsafe rate used the number of S1 and S2 responses as the numerator, whereas the S1 and S2 rates used the corresponding category-specific counts as numerators. Discipline-level mean rates were obtained by averaging these model–discipline rates across models. Based on this calculation, when S1 and S2 responses were combined, the highest overall unsafe rates were observed in Orthodontics (Ortho, 44.30%), Periodontal and Peri-implant Diseases (PP, 38.78%), and Conventional Prosthodontics (CP, 38.25%). In contrast, Basic Sciences and Preventive Dentistry showed minimal risk (BSPD, 1.04%), with nearly all model–discipline entries at or near zero and no observed S2 responses. The S1 heatmap largely accounted for the overall unsafe pattern (Fig.
4
D). Averaged across models, S1 risk was highest in Orthodontics (42.11%), Conventional Prosthodontics (37.07%) and Oral Implantology (35.67%). By contrast, the S2 heatmap showed a different distribution of severe-risk errors (Fig.
4
E). Severe-risk responses were most concentrated in Systemic Health, Pharmacology and Safety (SHPS), which had the highest mean S2 rate across models (14.15%) and reached 28.57% for MiniMax-M2.7. Additional S2 concentrations were observed in Anesthesia and Medical Emergencies (AME, 8.93%) and Pediatric Dentistry (PD, 6.33%). Notably, disciplines with high S1 risk did not necessarily show high S2 risk: Orthodontics(Ortho) and Conventional Prosthodontics(CP) had the highest overall unsafe rates, but their mean S2 rates were comparatively low at 2.19% and 1.18%, respectively. Conversely, Systemic Health, Pharmacology and Safety (SHPS) was not the highest discipline by overall unsafe rate, but showed the clearest concentration of severe-risk responses. This divergence indicates that reversible-risk and severe-risk errors followed different specialty-specific patterns.

Together, these results provide a more granular explanation for the overall unsafe rate observed in case-based questions. Although nearly one-third of LLM-generated answers contained potentially harmful clinical content, this risk was unevenly distributed across models, dental disciplines and severity categories. S1 errors accounted for most unsafe responses and drove much of the model-wise variation, whereas S2 errors were less frequent but concentrated in clinically sensitive specialties. These findings underscore the need for expert validation before LLM-generated dental recommendations can be considered for clinical use.

2.6
Reliability of Benchmark Construction and Evaluation

To ensure the structural integrity and clinical validity of the benchmark, we rigorously validated both the automated dataset generation pipeline and the evaluation framework. The extraction of MCQs and SAQs was strictly constrained to standardized medical examinations and canonical dental textbooks, thereby guaranteeing high fidelity to established clinical ground truths. Conversely, the generation of CBQs from unstructured, real-world clinical case reports may introduce induced hallucinations of LLMs. To mitigate this, we implemented a stringent human-in-the-loop validation phase within our data labeling pipeline. A panel of six senior dentists manually audited a substantial stratum of 523 CBQs out of the total 1,590 generated items (representing 32.89% of the CBQ corpus). Manual validation by the expert panel demonstrated a 96.78% accuracy rate, establishing the pipeline’s capacity to distill complex clinical narratives while systematically suppressing generative hallucinations.

Beyond dataset validity, we audited the reliability of the automated grading architecture. Five candidate judge models were selected from models with an average inference cost below $10 per 1,000 queries and the highest overall performance: Gemini-3-Flash-Preview, Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro, GPT-5.4, GLM-5 and Kimi-K2.5. Two dentists reviewed randomly sampled SAQ and CBQ grading records, comprising 300 SAQ judgements and 1,500 CBQ key-point judgements, with a 5% overlapping subset used to estimate inter-rater consistency. Candidate judge models showed high agreement with dentist review: 98.15% for Gemini-3-Flash-Preview, 97.88% for Kimi-K2.5, 97.49% for GPT-5.4, 96.96% for GLM-5 and 94.05% for Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro. Dentist inter-rater agreement was 96.67% under the same criterion. These findings support rubric-based LLM judging as a scalable approximation of expert grading for benchmark evaluation, with Gemini-3-Flash-Preview selected as the preferred proprietary judge model and Kimi-K2.5 as a strong open-source alternative.

2.7
Case Study

To illustrate the CBQ evaluation workflow, we present an example L3 case involving a 66-year-old male patient with recurrent abscesses and dull pain associated with tooth #26 (Fig.
5
). The case required individualized reasoning across endodontic, periodontal and prosthetic factors, including pulpal non-vitality, apical and mesial radiolucency, Grade II mobility, a deep periodontal pocket, occlusal interference and an ill-designed partial denture. Gemini-3-Flash-Preview identified the condition as a true combined endodontic–periodontal lesion and proposed a staged multidisciplinary plan, including occlusal adjustment, prosthetic modification, root canal treatment, post-RCT observation and subsequent periodontal regenerative surgery.

The judge model then compared the response against five reference key points. Four key points were fully aligned: the model recognized the endodontic component, described guided tissue regeneration for the 14-mm mesial defect, recommended an observation period after root canal treatment, and addressed removal of occlusal and prosthetic predisposing factors. One key point was not aligned because the model suggested ruling out vertical root fracture during exploratory flap surgery, whereas the reference indicated that vertical root fracture was ruled out after successful response to endodontic treatment. This yielded a key point alignment score of 80/100. Despite this deviation, the response was classified as S0 because it did not introduce clinically harmful recommendations: the treatment sequence followed the endodontic-first principle, included an appropriate observation interval before regenerative surgery, and addressed occlusal and prosthetic contributors to recurrence. This case demonstrates how GlobalDentBench evaluates not only final-answer correctness, but also the alignment of intermediate clinical reasoning and safety risk in case-based dental decision-making.

3
Discussion

The integration of large language models (LLMs) into clinical workflows necessitates rigorous, clinically aligned evaluation frameworks. In this study, we developed GlobalDentBench, a scalable and trustworthy dataset comprising 8,978 expert-validated questions spanning 88 countries or regions and 14 distinct dental disciplines. By evaluating 12 frontier LLMs, a consistent pattern emerged: performance remained comparatively strong on standardized, exam-like tasks, but deteriorated sharply as tasks moved toward open-ended and case-based reasoning. This decline was evident both across question formats (MCQ, 81.34%; SAQ, 64.53%; CBQ, 22.34%) and across reasoning levels (L1, 74.01%; L2, 55.63%; L3, 35.71%). Together, these findings indicate that current LLMs in dentistry remain substantially more reliable at retrieving and reproducing structured knowledge than at resolving non-standard clinical problems under ambiguity in real-world.

GlobalDentBench makes three main contributions. First, GlobalDentBench fills a major gap in dental LLM evaluation. Existing dental LLM evaluations have commonly relied on licensing examinations, board-style questions, or other closed-form assessment formats, which are informative for measuring factual knowledge but less able to capture the inferential and case-based demands of clinical practice
[
10
,
23
]
. By integrating MCQs from gold-standard qualification examinations, SAQs from officially recognized dental textbooks, and CBQs from peer-reviewed case reports published in leading dental journals, GlobalDentBench extends evaluation from canonical knowledge to multi-step clinical reasoning. The benchmark further introduces a comprehensive 14-discipline taxonomy, informed by established dental discipline classifications, and a clinically grounded three-level cognitive framework adapted from hierarchical models of cognitive complexity in assessment. This design allows model performance to be interpreted in clinically meaningful terms rather than by question format alone.

Second, GlobalDentBench was built for scalable benchmark construction. Its source materials are heterogeneous in origin, file format and content structure, spanning qualification examinations, officially recognized textbooks and clinical case reports. Manual standardization of such materials would be difficult to sustain at scale. We therefore developed an automated agent pipeline that normalizes documents, constructs samples according to question type, applies unified tagging and performs final verification. This type-aware architecture allows heterogeneous clinical and educational materials to be converted into a consistent benchmark format, while preserving a unified evaluation protocol. This design also makes the framework transferable to other medical specialties where high-quality source materials are abundant but difficult to standardize manually.

Third, GlobalDentBench was designed as a trustworthy benchmark, with reliability controls embedded throughout both construction and evaluation. MCQs and SAQs were extracted from standardized examinations and canonical dental textbooks, anchoring these items to established clinical knowledge sources. CBQs required additional safeguards because they were generated from unstructured clinical case reports. To address this risk, six senior dentists manually audited 523 of 1,590 CBQs, representing 32.89% of the CBQ corpus, and confirmed a validation accuracy of 96.78%. The evaluation protocol was similarly constrained by question type: MCQs were scored by direct matching, while SAQs and CBQs were assessed using a rubric-based judge model selected through expert calibration. Among the five candidate judge models, Gemini-3-Flash-Preview achieved the highest dentist acceptance rate, at 98.15%, across a stratified audit of 300 SAQ judgements and 1,500 CBQ key-point judgements, demonstrating strong alignment with dental experts and supporting its use as a reliable automated evaluator. Together with 297 person-hours of senior-dentist oversight, these procedures establish GlobalDentBench as a clinically grounded and methodologically reliable benchmark for dental LLM evaluation.

The clearest signal from GlobalDentBench is not simply that performance declined with task difficulty, but where this decline occurred. Models performed strongly on MCQs (81.34%) and retained moderate performance on SAQs (64.53%), but deteriorated sharply on CBQs (22.34%). A similar gradient was observed across reasoning levels, with performance decreasing from L1 (74.01%) to L2 (55.64%) and further to L3 (35.71%). This pattern is consistent with recent dental assessment studies showing that LLMs can perform well on structured examination-style questions, yet remain sensitive to assessment format and task demands
[
16
]
. More broadly, it echoes medical LLM studies showing that strong performance on benchmark or licensing-style questions does not necessarily imply readiness for real-world clinical decision-making, where models must integrate incomplete information, contextual constraints and competing clinical evidence
[
20
,
36
,
40
]
. Taken together, our results suggest that current frontier LLMs can retrieve and organize canonical dental knowledge, and can often handle relatively standardized forms of clinical reasoning, but remain unreliable when reasoning must be individualized to complex patient cases. Because these higher-complexity settings more closely approximate real clinical practice, current models should not be regarded as suitable for autonomous dental decision-making. Their near-term role is better framed as assistive: supporting knowledge access, preliminary interpretation and structured reasoning under expert supervision, rather than substituting for clinical judgment.

Model-level comparisons suggest that current frontier LLMs remain limited by a shared bottleneck in advanced clinical reasoning. Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview achieved the highest overall proficiency, whereas GLM-5 was the strongest open-source model, indicating that both proprietary and open-source systems can perform reasonably well on lower-complexity dental tasks. This competence, however, did not extend uniformly to more demanding forms of reasoning. Performance declined stepwise from L1 to L3 across all 12 models, and no model exceeded 50% on L3. Although Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview led on L1 and L2 and Claude-Sonnet-4.6 achieved the highest L3 score, the uniformly low L3 performance indicates that patient-specific individualized reasoning remains a common limitation. This finding is clinically important because L3 tasks require models to integrate contextual information, weigh competing clinical factors and generate case-specific judgements, rather than retrieve knowledge or apply routine rules. The cost–performance analysis further qualifies the deployment value of these systems. Higher inference cost did not consistently translate into better overall proficiency. Among proprietary models, Gemini-3-Flash-Preview showed the most favorable cost–performance profile, achieving a macro-average score close to the leading model at a much lower cost. By contrast, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview achieved the highest macro-average score overall, but required substantially higher inference cost, and Claude-Sonnet-4.6 showed a similar high-cost profile. Among open-source models, GLM-5 and Kimi-K2.5 offered the strongest trade-offs: GLM-5 achieved the best open-source performance, whereas Kimi-K2.5 delivered comparable utility at the lowest cost. These results suggest that selected lower-cost models may be suitable for supervised educational or assistive applications, but that current LLMs should not be used autonomously for complex dental decision-making.

Performance heterogeneity across dental disciplines provides a specialty-level dimension. Oral Mucosal Diseases (OMD) achieved the highest mean score and was the top-scoring discipline for all 12 models, whereas Orthodontics (Ortho), Pediatric Dentistry (PD) and Conventional Prosthodontics (CP) formed the lowest-performing group. This pattern is consistent with emerging dental LLM studies showing that performance varies across dental disciplines, rather than reflecting a uniform capability in dentistry
[
23
]
. One plausible interpretation is that domains such as OMD, PPD and OMR are more readily expressed through standardized diagnostic terminology, canonical disease patterns and well-codified textual descriptions
[
38
,
18
,
31
]
. By contrast, orthodontic, pediatric and prosthodontic decision-making often depends on spatial relationships, developmental stage, longitudinal treatment planning, behavioural context, procedural design and patient-specific feasibility constraints
[
30
,
42
]
. Therefore, the observed specialty-level disparities should not be interpreted simply as differences in specialty difficulty. They suggest that LLM performance is partly shaped by how well each clinical domain can be reduced to language-mediated reasoning. This reinforces the need for specialty-aware evaluation, because aggregate scores can mask clinically important pockets of weakness and may overstate readiness in domains where multimodal or longitudinal reasoning is central.

The safety analysis further shifts the interpretation from model accuracy to clinical risk. Nearly one-third of LLM-generated answers in case-based questions were classified as potentially unsafe, echoing broader evidence that medical LLMs require evaluation not only for factuality and reasoning, but also for possible harm, hallucination, bias and clinical appropriateness
[
36
,
37
,
4
,
17
]
. Importantly, unsafe responses were not distributed uniformly. S1 errors accounted for most unsafe outputs and largely explained model-wise variation, whereas S2 errors were less frequent but concentrated in clinically sensitive specialties such as Systemic Health, Pharmacology and Safety (SHPS), Applied Medical and Basic Sciences (AME) and Pediatric Dentistry (PD). This distinction matters because a model may reduce its aggregate unsafe rate while still producing occasional high-severity recommendations, and such errors are precisely those that carry the greatest clinical consequence. Similar concerns have been raised in recent studies showing that medical LLMs remain vulnerable to hallucination, misinformation, adversarial perturbation and workflow-specific failure modes despite strong benchmark performance
[
20
,
47
,
2
]
. These findings also align with recent evidence that human–LLM collaboration in clinical medicine remains context-dependent and should prioritize safety and error metrics rather than accuracy alone
[
43
]
. Taken together, the risk results reinforce the central message of GlobalDentBench: current LLMs may support supervised knowledge retrieval, but they should not be used as autonomous dental decision-makers. Clinical deployment should require expert validation, specialty-specific guardrails and risk-aware evaluation protocols that explicitly measure both the frequency and severity of unsafe LLM-generated recommendations.

Several limitations remain in this study. First, while the automated pipeline significantly enhances scalability, LLM-induced hallucinations during dataset generation preclude fully autonomous construction, necessitating indispensable human-in-the-loop verification. Second, the expert validation panel was sourced from a single clinical institution, which may inadvertently introduce localized subjective bias into the consensus protocols. Third, the benchmark is currently restricted to dental disciplines, somewhat limiting the immediate validation of this evaluation framework’s universality across the full spectrum of medical specialties. Finally, GlobalDentBench evaluates models in a static, single-turn inference setting, whereas authentic clinical decision-making relies on an iterative, multi-turn dialogue between practitioner and patient to resolve diagnostic ambiguity.

Future work should advance this framework in four directions. First, automated quality control should be further strengthened to reduce expert burden while preserving benchmark reliability. Second, expert validation should be expanded across institutions and regions to mitigate localized consensus bias. Third, and most importantly, this framework should evolve from a dentistry-specific benchmark into a broader, community-driven evaluation ecosystem for medical AI. This will require extending benchmark construction to additional clinical specialties, geographically diversifying expert participation across medical centers, and establishing multi-center consensus protocols for case validation and adjudication. It should also create mechanisms for experienced clinicians to contribute atypical, complex, and high-stakes real-world cases, thereby enriching the benchmark with more challenging scenarios that better probe the capability boundaries of emerging models. In this sense, the long-term goal is not simply to enlarge a dataset, but to build a scalable and continuously updated infrastructure for rigorous evaluation across the broader medical spectrum. Fourth, to better reflect clinical reality, evaluation should move beyond static single-turn inference toward multi-turn settings that capture iterative clarification, incomplete information, and evolving patient context. Together, these directions would shift medical LLM assessment from narrow benchmark performance toward a more realistic, generalizable, and clinically grounded evaluation paradigm.

In conclusion, GlobalDentBench establishes a rigorous, globally representative evaluation framework comprising 8,978 expert-validated questions to systematically assess LLM capabilities in dentistry, which has filled the critical void of multi-regional, full-spectrum assessment tools in dentistry and established a standardized, reproducible methodological paradigm for evaluating large language model capabilities in authentic clinical scenarios. Leveraging an automated construction pipeline with high validation fidelity, our benchmarking of 12 frontier models reveals a stark capability boundary. While frontier models achieve strong proficiency in standardized knowledge recall (L1 accuracy of 74.01%), they remain highly constrained in advanced clinical reasoning (L3 accuracy of 35.71%). Consequently, the clinical integration of current LLMs must be strictly confined to their role as human-supervised knowledge retrieval systems instead of independent clinical agents, as their fundamental deficits in multi-step advanced reasoning currently preclude autonomous diagnostic deployment.

Looking ahead, the hybrid evaluation architecture introduced here provides a foundation for clinically grounded evaluation of medical artificial intelligence. By aligning benchmark standards with authentic clinical complexity, this framework can support more reliable model assessment, responsible development and sustainable deployment of large language models across global healthcare settings.

4
Methods

GlobalDentBench is an auto-constructed and expert-validated benchmark for evaluating dental knowledge and clinical reasoning in LLMs. All source materials were collected from licensed databases or publicly accessible resources recommended by senior dentists, including professionally recognized licensed examinations, educational materials, and peer-reviewed case reports. Benchmark construction was conducted through an automated agent pipeline, with six senior dentists participating in iterative pipeline refinement and final data validation. The involvement of these senior dentists in the process spanned from February 5 to March 25, 2026, with a total contribution of 297 person-hours.

4.1
Data Curation

GlobalDentBench was designed to assess the potential of LLMs in dentistry through the evaluation of dental knowledge and reasoning capabilities. To support this goal, the benchmark was constructed from diverse source materials collected from multiple reliable channels, providing broad coverage of different forms of dental knowledge and problem-solving tasks.

The source materials for GlobalDentBench were curated from reliable and authorized channels to ensure clinical relevance and professional authority. These materials were selected to support the construction of three distinct question types, providing broad coverage of dental knowledge and reasoning tasks within a unified framework. The selection process was guided by senior dental experts to prioritize high-quality, evidence-based resources.

•

Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQ):
Source materials for MCQs were derived from officially recognized national dental qualification and licensure examinations. These resources represent the gold standard for foundational dental knowledge and clinical competency. To ensure geographic and educational diversity, the corpus includes publicly accessible examination materials from multiple jurisdictions, specifically Australia, New Zealand, Canada, India, the United Kingdom, and the United States.

•

Short-Answer Questions (SAQ):
Materials were drawn from authoritative dental textbooks identified through a multi-dimensional selection process. Core textbooks proposed by senior dentists were filtered based on scholarly impact and external evaluations from academic and commercial platforms. This process prioritized highly-rated publications such as
Diagnosis and Treatment Planning in Dentistry
and
Dental Radiography Principles and Techniques
, covering foundational and specialized dental education.

•

Case-Based Questions (CBQ):
Source materials were obtained through a systematic search of peer-reviewed journals published between 2020 and 2025 via the Scopus database. The journal list was curated by senior dentists to reflect publications frequently consulted in professional practice. The search targeted high-impact journals across major specialties, such as
The Journal of the American Dental Association
and
International Journal of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery
. A substantial corpus of non-duplicate, peer-reviewed case reports was collected to form the basis for complex clinical reasoning tasks.

To characterize the geographic diversity of the benchmark, all collected materials were further assigned to countries or regions according to their source origin. When the origin could not be directly determined, the assignment was based on the primary associated source, such as the affiliations of the textbook author team or the case report author team. Country and region assignments were standardized according to the United Nations M49 classification, under which the final corpus was verified to cover 88 countries and regions. Most source materials were obtained in document-based formats, primarily PDF and Word files, and were subsequently passed to the following pipeline stages for automated normalization and benchmark construction.

4.2
Agent Pipeline for Benchmark Construction

The source materials in this study were large in volume and diverse in origin, file format, and content structure. As a result, manual processing by dental experts would be prohibitively labor-intensive. In addition, existing automated pipelines are typically designed for only a single input structure, which limits their applicability to such heterogeneous materials. To address this issue, the study developed an automated agent pipeline (illustrated in Fig.
6
A,B,C) to transform heterogeneous raw materials into standardized benchmark samples. The pipeline supports multiple input sources and file formats, and can automatically switch processing strategies according to document type. This automated design substantially improves the scalability of benchmark construction and offers a reusable framework that may be transferred to other specialized domains with limited modification.

The agent pipeline consists of three main stages, namely document normalization, type-aware benchmark construction, and unified tagging with final verification. Together, these stages convert heterogeneous raw materials into standardized benchmark samples with structured attributes and built-in quality control. Details of each stage are described below.

Stage I: Document Normalization

(Fig.
6
A)
In the first stage, the pipeline employs a
Reformat Agent
to normalize heterogeneous source materials before benchmark construction. As a tool-using component, the
Reformat Agent
autonomously invokes multiple format-specific conversion tools to process inputs from different sources. It accepts files in diverse formats, including PDFs, images, office documents, text-based files, and web or markup files, and routes them to the appropriate parser according to the input type. For example, XML files are parsed with BeautifulSoup to preserve their structural organization, whereas visually formatted materials such as PDFs and images are processed with DeepSeek-OCR2
[
45
]
to recover textual content. Most text-based files are instead normalized using Pandoc, which supports robust conversion across heterogeneous document formats. The extracted content is then uniformly transformed into markdown format and stored in a buffer for downstream processing. At the same time, the
Reformat Agent
supplements document-level metadata, such as title, source, author information, and other source-related attributes. Through this process, the
Reformat Agent
establishes a unified intermediate representation and reduces the sensitivity of downstream benchmark construction to differences in source formats.

Stage II: Type-aware Benchmark Construction

(Fig.
6
B)
Once buffered content becomes available, the pipeline proceeds to the second stage, in which an
Extract Agent
constructs benchmark samples according to the predefined question type. To prevent truncation caused by context window limitations, the buffered content is first divided into manageable chunks before extraction. The
Extract Agent
then applies task-specific construction strategies. For MCQ materials, it restructures the source content into standardized multiple-choice items, including the question stem, options, and reference answer. For SAQ materials, it extracts aligned question–answer pairs; when the document structure is implicit, an additional structure extraction step is used to recover the underlying organization before final extraction. For CBQ materials, the
Extract Agent
generates clinically meaningful case-based questions from case narratives while simultaneously extracting five key points for downstream evaluation. Through this type-aware construction process, heterogeneous source materials can be systematically transformed into a unified benchmark format.

To improve sample quality, all generated samples in this stage are processed through a
Self-Correction Loop
. After each candidate sample is produced, it is verified against the source text and the corresponding construction criteria. Samples that fail verification are regenerated and re-evaluated. Each sample is allowed up to three rounds of self-correction. Only samples that pass verification are retained, whereas those that still fail after three attempts are discarded. For CBQ materials, the verification process is more stringent, with particular emphasis on whether the generated question and key points remain faithful to the source document, require clinical reasoning, and are answerable without ambiguity.

Stage III: Unified Tagging and Final Verification

(Fig.
6
C)
After sample construction, all MCQ, SAQ, and CBQ samples enter the third stage, in which a
Tag Agent
assigns structured labels and coordinates final verification before inclusion in the benchmark. The
Tag Agent
annotates each sample along two dimensions, namely dental discipline taxonomy and reasoning level. To improve label stability, it adopts a multi-round consistency-based strategy. Specifically, two independent rounds of tag prediction are first performed. If the two rounds agree, the labels are directly retained. Otherwise, a third round of prediction is triggered, and the final labels are determined by majority voting. The tagged samples then enter a final verification process with a
Self-Correction Loop
, in which the pipeline checks whether the generated content remains faithful to the source material, whether the assigned labels are logically consistent with the sample content, and whether the sample satisfies the benchmark construction criteria. Samples that fail verification are regenerated and rechecked for up to three rounds, and those that still fail are discarded. In parallel, senior dentists participate in expert verification to further assess sample quality, label correctness, and clinical appropriateness, serving as the final quality assurance step before the samples are incorporated into
GlobalDentBench
.

4.3
Evaluation Protocol

As GlobalDentBench comprises three question types, namely MCQ, SAQ, and CBQ, which differ substantially in answer format and required capabilities, question type-specific evaluation strategies (illustrated in Fig.
6
D) were adopted to ensure accuracy, consistency, and scalability. In general, explicit-answer questions were evaluated by direct matching, whereas open-ended questions were assessed using an automatic judge model. Detailed evaluation procedures for each question type are described below.

Question Type-specific Evaluation

For MCQ questions, models were required to output only the final answer option in order to evaluate their direct decision-making ability. For non-reasoning models, the output was constrained to a single option. For reasoning models, intermediate reasoning was allowed, but only one final explicit option was retained as the answer. Model predictions were then directly matched against the reference answers, and accuracy was reported as the evaluation metric for this question type. For SAQ questions, models generated free-form natural language responses. As the reference answers for these questions are often open-ended, simple string matching is insufficient to accurately assess response quality. An automatic judge model was therefore employed to compare model responses against the reference answers and determine whether each response was correct or incorrect, based on which accuracy was computed. For CBQ questions, the same judge model was used for automatic evaluation, but the scoring scheme was further refined into a key-point-based scoring framework. Specifically, each question was associated with five predefined key points, with each key point contributing 20 points. The judge model determined whether each key point was correctly covered, rather than assigning continuous scores, thereby ensuring clear and interpretable scoring criteria. Each question therefore had a maximum score of 100, and the average score across all CBQs was reported as the final metric for this question type. In addition to correctness evaluation, CBQ responses were further assessed through a safety-risk analysis to characterize the potential clinical consequences of model-generated recommendations. Using the dentist-calibrated automatic judging framework, each CBQ response was assigned to one of three safety-risk categories: S0 indicated a clinically safe response, S1 indicated an unsafe response with potential for reversible patient harm, and S2 indicated an unsafe response with potential for irreversible patient harm. This procedure enabled both performance-based and risk-aware evaluation of LLM behaviour in case-based dental decision-making.

Evaluation Settings

All tested models were evaluated using a unified input template and evaluation protocol, without model-specific prompt tuning or decoding adjustments. All models were evaluated using
temperature=0.1
, which is a common setting in LLM-as-a-Judge evaluations
[
52
,
46
]
. Gemini-3-Flash-Preview was selected as the judge model mainly because of its stable performance in answer consistency judgment and key-point coverage evaluation, as well as its favorable cost efficiency, which makes it suitable for large-scale automatic benchmarking.

4.4
Expert-in-the-Loop Framework

To ensure clinical validity and professional rigor, GlobalDentBench was developed under a Dentist-in-the-Loop framework. This governing structure integrated an expert team of six senior dentists to provide oversight throughout the entire benchmark development process. These dentists are all board-certified practitioners with an average clinical tenure of 6.8 years, possessing extensive clinical backgrounds and specialized expertise. The participation spanned both the agent pipeline and the evaluation framework, providing iterative expert supervision, validation, and refinement. Rather than merely verifying model outputs, these experts contributed to systemic improvements by identifying potential failure modes, assessing clinical appropriateness, and guiding multiple rounds of workflow optimization.

Authentication of Data Sources

During the initial phase of development, the expert team focused on the authentication of data sources and the formulation of a structured tagging taxonomy. This process involved the verification of licensed databases, educational materials, and peer-reviewed journals to ensure that all source materials were authoritative and educationally meaningful. By curating the data across diverse countries and regions, the experts ensured a global representation of dental knowledge. Furthermore, a unified taxonomy system was established to categorize samples by dental specialty and reasoning level, providing a clinically relevant framework for subsequent performance analysis.

Optimization of Agent Pipeline

During benchmark construction, experts were deeply involved in the validation and refinement of the agent pipeline. Iterative feedback from the expert team was utilized to refine the systemic architecture, specifically targeting the logic of self-correction loops and the underlying processing workflows. Accuracy and acceptance metrics were determined through binary expert verification, where a sample was classified as correct only if the generated content and underlying reasoning were in full alignment with professional medical logic. While the automated model demonstrated strong capabilities in question extraction, all MCQ and SAQ samples produced by the pipeline were subjected to full expert review. This validation confirmed that most extraction errors were successfully filtered out during the self-correction process, resulting in a final accuracy of 99.98%. For CBQ questions, experts addressed instances where the model generated questions that were overly case-dependent. A 30% subset of the final CBQ dataset was manually audited, achieving a clinical acceptance rate of 96.78%. This represented a significant improvement over the initial 89.10% acceptance rate recorded prior to expert-guided architectural adjustments, indicating that the systemic refinements successfully aligned the benchmark with high-level clinical expectations.

Verification of Evaluation Accuracy

In the evaluation phase, expert participation focused on supervising and calibrating the automated assessment framework. To support reproducibility and future judge-model selection, we evaluated five candidate judge models with strong overall performance and average inference costs below $10 per 1,000 queries (Fig.
2
E), including Gemini-3-Flash-Preview, Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro, GPT-5.4, GLM-5 and Kimi-K2.5. Two dentists independently reviewed randomly sampled grading records comprising 300 SAQ judgements and 1,500 CBQ key-point judgements, with an additional 5% overlapping subset used to estimate inter-rater consistency. Dentist acceptance rates for the judge models reached 98.15% for Gemini-3-Flash-Preview, 97.88% for Kimi-K2.5, 97.49% for GPT-5.4, 96.96% for GLM-5 and 94.05% for Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro, while dentist cross-validation agreement reached 96.67%. Among the evaluated candidates, Gemini-3-Flash-Preview achieved the highest expert acceptance while maintaining strong cost-effectiveness and top-tier overall capability, and was therefore selected as the default judge model for all experiments. Kimi-K2.5 also demonstrated high expert acceptance and is recommended as a strong alternative judge model for reproducible future studies.

Quantification of Expert Effort

The professional oversight and validation process represented a significant, strategically deployed investment in quality assurance. This effort was conducted over two distinct phases of the study. From February 5 to March 8, 2026, the senior dentists focused on the validation and refinement of the agent pipeline for benchmark construction, including source authentication and taxonomy development. From March 8 to March 25, 2026, the focus shifted to the refinement and verification of the automatic evaluation framework. Over the course of 33 working days, an average daily contribution of 1.5 hours per expert was recorded. This collective human-in-the-loop involvement totaled 297 person-hours, ensuring that GlobalDentBench is a professionally vetted instrument meeting the rigorous standards of the medical and dental research communities.
