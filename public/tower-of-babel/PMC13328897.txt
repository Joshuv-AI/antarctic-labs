Serving beyond glory: lessons from Chipyong-ni and military medicine
Authors: Kun Hwang
Published: 2026

CHIPYONG-NI AND MONCLAR

A few days ago, I visited the Chipyong-ni Battle Memorial, a site commemorating one of the most decisive engagements of the Korean War (Fig. 1). The Battle of Chipyong-ni has been described as a turning point that shifted momentum in favor of the United Nations forces [1]. Among the exhibits, the stories of three officers stood out: Colonel Paul Freeman, Lieutenant Colonel Ralph Monclar, and Colonel Crombez. Through their leadership and personal bravery, these men played pivotal roles in resisting the invading Chinese forces, leaving legacies that continue to inspire (Table 1).

Of these, the story of Lieutenant Colonel Monclar resonated most deeply. A veteran of both World War I and World War II, Monclar had attained the rank of three-star general in the French Army [2]. Yet, when he joined the Korean War, he willingly accepted the rank of lieutenant colonel, setting aside his former prestige to serve on the front lines. At 58 years old, Monclar led his troops under harsh conditions, personally participating in combat and exemplifying both courage and humility. His devotion to his soldiers and his willingness to forgo rank and privilege left a lasting impression on all who came to know his story [3,4].

RETURNING TO MILITARY SERVICE

This narrative resonated profoundly with my own experiences. From February 1983 to April 1986, I served 38 months in the Korean military as a first lieutenant, fulfilling my duties as a junior officer. Following this service, I trained in plastic surgery, became a professor, and dedicated 30 years to academic medicine and clinical practice. Upon retirement, I was honored with the Okjo Geunjeong Medal (Order of Service), a recognition of professional achievement and contribution.

Despite a long civilian career, I felt drawn back to military medicine. At the age of 65, I returned to the Armed Forces Capital Hospital as a grade-A fixed-term military physician, a specialist appointment roughly equivalent in status to the rank of major or lieutenant colonel.

During my interview, I was asked, “Why do you want to work here?” I replied, “During my university hospital years, active-duty soldiers with facial fractures or hand injuries often came to me for surgery. They probably believed the university hospital would provide superior care. But at a military hospital, I can treat them directly and ensure they return safely to their units. That is why I am here.”

DAILY FULFILLMENT IN MILITARY MEDICINE

It has now been more than 2 years and 7 months since I returned to AFCH. Each day, I find profound satisfaction in treating servicemen, performing surgeries for injuries sustained during training or operational duties, and seeing them return to their units healthy and ready to serve. There is a unique fulfillment in contributing directly to the operational readiness and well-being of soldiers who willingly place themselves in harm’s way.

Among the civilian physicians at our hospital, I am the seventh oldest. Six of my colleagues are senior to me in age, and two previously served as hospital directors at major universities. Despite these prior positions of prestige, we serve with humility and dedication, fully embracing the hospital’s central mission: safeguarding the health and readiness of our soldiers. In many ways, our service mirrors Monclar’s ethos—relinquishing personal accolades and rank to stand beside those in need [2–4].

LESSONS FROM MONCLAR FOR MILITARY PHYSICIANS

Reflecting on Monclar’s service offers a profound lesson for all military medical professionals. True service is not measured by titles, accolades, or prestige, but by steadfast commitment to duty and devotion to those entrusted to our care. In military medicine, this means prioritizing the welfare of soldiers above personal recognition or administrative convenience.

The parallels between Monclar’s experience and my own are striking. Both in combat and in the frontline hospital, the focus remains unchanged: direct engagement with those who require care, irrespective of one’s previous status or accomplishments. This perspective transforms service from a mere professional obligation into a deeply meaningful vocation. Restoring a soldier’s function and returning them safely to duty embodies the same courage and commitment that Monclar demonstrated decades ago on the battlefield [5].

CONTINUING SERVICE UNTIL THE END

As I contemplate the remainder of my tenure at AFCH, I am aware that I, too, will eventually retire. Yet until that day comes, I remain committed to operating on injured soldiers with the same devotion Monclar displayed, ever mindful of the heroism of “Operation Thunderbolt” and the sacrifices made at Chipyong-ni. Military medicine, much like combat, demands humility, courage, and dedication; age and prior prestige are secondary to the immediate mission of healing and preserving life.

Serving as a military physician is not about rank, medals, or recognition. It is about the lives we touch, the readiness we uphold, and the quiet legacy of care we leave behind. Whether young lieutenants or retired professors returning to duty, our fulfillment arises from contributing meaningfully to the lives of soldiers. By embracing this ethos, we honor both those we serve today and veterans like Lieutenant Colonel Monclar, whose courage and humility continue to inspire generations of military medical professionals.

Ultimately, true glory is found not in personal achievement but in selfless service that restores health, strengthens resilience, and enables soldiers to continue their mission. As a physician on the front lines of military medicine, I am privileged to experience this fulfillment each day—and to walk, in my own small way, in the footsteps of heroes who chose service over rank, mission over accolades, and courage over comfort.
