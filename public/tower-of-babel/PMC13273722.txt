Patient-specific timing acquisition for coronary CT angiography: A retrospective patient validation study
Authors: Sabee Molloi, Shiva Mostafavi, Alireza Shojazadeh, Kyrillos Grace, Connor Edwards Miles All Leska-Kent, Justin Truong, Katherine Hyde, Liqiang Ren, Cecelia Brewingtown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo retrospectively validate a patient-specific post-trigger delay technique for coronary CT angiography (CCTA) and compare it with the conventional fixed-delay approach using true peak arterial enhancement as the reference standard.MethodsThirty-seven patients (mean age 63 ± 9 years; 35% male) who underwent dynamic CT perfusion imaging were retrospectively analyzed. Serial volumetric acquisitions were obtained during and after intravenous administration of 40–50 mL iodinated contrast. Time–attenuation curves from the descending aorta and major coronary arteries (LAD, LCx, RCA) were modeled using a gamma variate function to determine true time-to-peak enhancement (TTP), peak attenuation, and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR). Coronary enhancement was simulated using (1) a fixed 5-second post-trigger delay and (2) a patient-specific delay defined as half the injection duration plus a dispersion constant. True peak enhancement was used as the reference standard. Timing accuracy and enhancement differences were evaluated using paired statistical analyses.ResultsThe patient-specific delay predicted peak coronary enhancement with a mean deviation of −0.80 ± 0.85 s, maintaining enhancement and CNR within 10% of peak values. The fixed 5-second delay showed a larger deviation (−2.11 ± 0.82 s; p < 0.001) and reduced coronary enhancement (−37%) and CNR (−27%) relative to peak (p < 0.05). Improvements were consistent across all coronary arteries.ConclusionInjection-duration–based patient-specific timing more accurately predicts peak coronary enhancement than a fixed-delay strategy and improves CCTA contrast enhancement and CNR without increasing radiation dose or workflow complexity.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Coronary CT angiography (CCTA) is widely used for the non-invasive evaluation of coronary stenosis and plaque characterization [2]. Optimal intraluminal contrast enhancement is essential for accurate stenosis assessment, reliable visualization of distal coronary segments, and overall diagnostic confidence [3], [4].

Precise scan timing is critical to achieving maximal coronary contrast enhancement and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) [5], [6]. Historically, individualized timing was achieved using a test bolus technique; however, this approach increases radiation exposure and prolongs examination time [7], [8]. Consequently, bolus tracking with a fixed post-trigger delay—most commonly 5 s—has become the predominant clinical strategy due to its simplicity and efficiency [9].

Despite its practicality, a fixed post-trigger delay does not account for variations in contrast injection protocols [10]. The interval between threshold detection in the aorta and true peak coronary enhancement may therefore vary substantially across patients, potentially resulting in systematic mistiming and suboptimal image quality.

In prior controlled animal experiments, it was demonstrated that the time from bolus tracking trigger to peak coronary enhancement is proportional to the contrast injection duration. Specifically, peak timing was accurately estimated by one-half of the injection duration plus a constant dispersion term [11]. This model improved peak alignment in both coronary [12] and pulmonary [13] CT angiography in swine and enabled development of a low-dose CT perfusion framework for myocardial [11] and pulmonary [14] CT perfusion.

The present study translates and validates this concept in human subjects using retrospectively acquired dynamic CT datasets as the reference standard. We hypothesized that:1.The patient-specific post-trigger delay approach would more accurately predict true coronary peak contrast enhancement than a fixed 5-second delay.2.Improved timing accuracy would result in significantly higher coronary contrast enhancement and CNR.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study included 46 consecutive adult patients (≥ 18 years) identified from an institutional database who underwent dynamic CT perfusion imaging for evaluation of suspected pulmonary lesions between December 2012 and August 2014 (Table 1). The exclusion criteria included known contrast allergy, excessive motion artifacts, and inadequate contrast opacification. Among the 46 eligible patients, 9 were excluded because the coronary arteries could not be adequately evaluated due to excessive image noise (n = 2), inadequate coronary contrast opacification (n = 4), or limited field of view with incomplete visualization of the coronary arteries (n = 3). The final study cohort consisted of 37 patients (Fig. 1). Institutional review board approval was obtained from UT Southwestern Medical Center, and all subjects had provided written informed consent at the time of imaging. Enhancement curve modeling

Patient-specific CCTA timing model

The temporal width of the contrast bolus in the aorta is primarily determined by the injection duration [17], [18]. Based on prior experimental validation, post-trigger time-to-peak enhancement was estimated as:(2)PredictedTTP=Tinj2+TdWhere: Tinj= injection duration and Td= dispersion constant. The dispersion constant (2.3 s) was derived from prior experimental validation studies [12].

Standard CCTA timing simulation

To simulate the conventional clinical CCTA approach, a fixed 5-second delay following the simulated trigger was applied to the fitted aortic enhancement curve. This time point was defined as the standard post-trigger time-to-peak (Standard TTP). The 5-second fixed post-trigger delay reflects the globally established clinical standard for CCTA acquisition following bolus tracking trigger. Existing standard CCTA protocols employ a fixed delay of 4–7 s following bolus tracking trigger without considering patient-specific parameters such as contrast injection protocol or hemodynamic conditions, with 5 s representing the most widely adopted value within this range in clinical practice [19].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (SciPy.stats v1.10.1). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used, as appropriate, to compare predicted and standard TTP values against reference measurements. Agreement was evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis with 95% limits of agreement. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson or Spearman coefficients depending on data distribution. The coefficient of determination (R²) and root mean square error (RMSE) were calculated to assess predictive accuracy. Effect size was quantified using Cohen’s d for key comparisons. Three independent readers performed ROI measurements. Inter-reader agreement was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and Spearman correlation analysis. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Discussion

In this retrospective clinical validation study, we evaluated a patient-specific post-trigger delay protocol for coronary CT angiography (CCTA) in a cohort undergoing dynamic CT perfusion imaging. The proposed method predicts time-to-peak (TTP) enhancement in the descending aorta and coronary arteries based solely on contrast injection duration. Our findings demonstrate that this simple model accurately estimates peak enhancement timing, with a mean deviation of −0.80 ± 0.85 s from the true peak.

Compared with the conventional fixed 5-second post-trigger delay, the patient-specific protocol showed consistently superior performance across all coronary arteries. Strong correlations were observed between predicted and reference enhancement values (r = 0.81–0.96; R² up to 0.89), indicating accurate and stable predictive behavior. In contrast, the standard protocol demonstrated weaker correlations (r = 0.57–0.81) and poor model fit, including negative R² values, reflecting systematic mistiming and predictive performance inferior to the sample mean. Furthermore, the patient-specific approach substantially reduced root mean square error (RMSE) across all vessels, supporting its potential to improve enhancement precision in routine CCTA.

Mechanistic basis and clinical implications

The behavior of the contrast medium bolus from the injection site to the region of interest is governed by established hemodynamic and transport principles. During contrast injection, the bolus enters the venous circulation at a relatively high concentration and initially maintains a compact geometry. As the bolus propagates through the venous circulation, right cardiac chambers, pulmonary vasculature, left cardiac chambers, and arterial system, it undergoes progressive temporal broadening and dilution due to convective transport, laminar flow velocity gradients, vascular branching, and cardiopulmonary recirculation. These cumulative processes generate a dispersed time-attenuation profile that can be approximated by a gamma variate function.

The degree of bolus dispersion is influenced by the contrast injection duration. Longer injection intervals produce broader gamma variate-shaped enhancement curves with delayed peak enhancement, whereas shorter injections generate narrower enhancement profiles with earlier and sharper peaks. Importantly, the central portion of the injected bolus, corresponding approximately to the midpoint of the injection interval, is relatively less affected by temporal dispersion compared with the leading and trailing edges of the bolus. Consequently, this bolus center is expected to contain the highest iodine concentration and to correspond closely with peak vascular enhancement at the aortic root. This physiologic and hemodynamic framework provides the rationale for the proposed acquisition timing strategy. Following bolus tracking trigger detection, application of a variable post-trigger acquisition delay proportional to one-half of the contrast injection duration, together with a fixed empiric dispersion correction factor, allows image acquisition to more consistently coincide with peak or near-peak aortic root enhancement. Furthermore, because time-to-peak enhancement increases approximately linearly with increasing injection duration, an injection-duration-dependent acquisition delay may provide more reliable peak enhancement targeting compared with conventional fixed-delay approaches [9].

The proposed timing model was initially developed and validated in a controlled swine model [12], [13], where post-trigger peak timing was shown to depend primarily on contrast injection duration and vascular dispersion characteristics. The model estimates peak enhancement using one-half of the injection duration plus a fixed dispersion constant. Because contrast volume is typically adjusted according to body weight and injection rate is held constant, injection duration inherently reflects circulating blood volume and bolus width. Thus, injection duration provides a practical surrogate for individualizing scan timing.

In a previous report [14], the time-to-peak delay of the pulmonary and aortic arterial input functions were evaluated in animals with a range of body weights (25–91 kg), contrast doses (20–100 mL), injection durations (2–15 s), and cardiac outputs (1.4–5.1 L/min). The results indicated that the injection duration was the most significant injection-related parameter impacting the bolus time-to-peak, particularly in the case of short contrast injection duration (< 15 s).

Prior investigations have demonstrated that the interval between bolus trigger and peak arterial enhancement increases linearly with injection duration [20] and is modulated by cardiac output [21]. While bolus tracking accounts for variability in bolus arrival time due to variation in cardiac output, it does not address variability in post-trigger dispersion and peak timing. Consequently, the use of a fixed delay may systematically under- or overshoot peak coronary enhancement. Our results suggest that incorporating injection-duration–based timing more closely aligns acquisition with true peak enhancement, thereby improving both attenuation and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR).

Although previous studies have proposed variable post-trigger delays derived from large arterial enhancement datasets, these approaches often require extensive empirical modeling [19], [20], [22]. In contrast, the present method is analytically simple, requires no additional imaging or radiation, and can be directly integrated into existing bolus-tracking workflows for different CT scanner platforms. The simplicity of the model may facilitate clinical implementation.

Beyond CCTA, this framework may have broader implications for dynamic CT perfusion imaging. Prior experimental work has demonstrated feasibility in myocardial [11] and pulmonary [14] perfusion imaging, suggesting that injection-based temporal modeling may represent a generalizable strategy for contrast timing optimization.

It is also important to note that the IV injection site does not affect the patient-specific protocol, as the acquisition timing is governed entirely by bolus tracking rather than a fixed injection-to-scan delay. However, the IV injection site may have a minor effect on the empirically determined dispersion constant, as it can influence the degree of mixing between contrast and blood at the leading and trailing edges of the contrast bolus. Bolus tracking monitors contrast attenuation within the target vessel in real time, and when the threshold is reached, scan acquisition is triggered — thereby accounting for individual variations in contrast transit time regardless of where contrast medium enters the bloodstream. Since our protocol calculates the post-trigger acquisition delay from the moment contrast arrives at the aortic root — not from the moment of injection — variability in venous transit time introduced by injection site differences is inherently neutralized.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the temporal resolution of 2 s between early acquisitions may have introduced minor uncertainty in determining the true peak enhancement time. Second, bolus tracking was simulated retrospectively using a 150-HU threshold derived from enhancement curves, rather than implemented prospectively during acquisition. As a result, scanner-related trigger delays and workflow latency were not incorporated into timing estimates. Future prospective studies should evaluate this protocol using real-time bolus tracking across multiple CT platforms.

Third, ECG gating was not applied because the original scans were performed for lung lesion assessment. Consequently, coronary images at different time points were not acquired within identical cardiac phases. However, the descending aorta—unaffected by cardiac motion—demonstrated similar improvements, supporting the robustness of the timing model. Prospective ECG-gated validation in dedicated CCTA populations is warranted.

Fourth, the study cohort consisted primarily of older adults undergoing evaluation for pulmonary pathology, which may limit generalizability to younger or lower-risk populations. Additionally, imaging was performed using a single scanner model at one institution. Multicenter validation across different scanner platforms and injection protocols would strengthen external applicability.

Finally, radiation dose in this dynamic perfusion cohort was higher than that of routine CCTA. However, the proposed protocol itself does not require additional radiation or contrast administration; rather, it optimizes timing within standard clinical acquisition.

Clinical perspective

The patient-specific post-trigger delay technique provides a practical and individualized method for optimizing scan timing in CCTA. By relying solely on injection duration—a parameter already defined during protocol setup—the method improves coronary opacification and CNR without increasing contrast volume, radiation dose, or procedural complexity.

More accurate peak timing may improve stenosis visualization, enhance distal vessel assessment, and potentially increase diagnostic confidence in coronary artery disease evaluation. If prospectively validated, this approach may represent a straightforward modification to current bolus-tracking strategies with immediate clinical applicability.

Conclusion

In this retrospective clinical validation study, a patient-specific post-trigger delay protocol based on contrast injection duration more accurately predicted peak coronary enhancement than the conventional fixed 5-second delay. The proposed approach demonstrated significantly smaller deviations from true time-to-peak, stronger agreement with reference enhancement and CNR values, and reduced prediction error across all major coronary arteries.

By relying on a simple analytical relationship that can be integrated into existing bolus-tracking workflows, this strategy improves coronary opacification without increasing radiation exposure, contrast volume, or procedural complexity. Prospective validation in dedicated ECG-gated CCTA cohorts is warranted; however, these findings support the clinical feasibility of injection-duration–based timing as a practical method for optimizing image quality in coronary CT angiography.
