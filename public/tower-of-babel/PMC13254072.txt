Synthesis of bundle copolymers as an emergent class of copolymer architecture
Authors: Yuki Kametani, Rintaro Yamaguchi, Yusuke Ando, Yuta Kawasaki, Takashi Uemura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCopolymers play a central role in soft materials owing to their capacity for diverse molecular designs. While numerous combinations of composition and topology have been explored for the development of copolymers, only four types of basic architecture (random, sequence-controlled, block, and graft) have been synthetically and historically achievable. Herein, we introduce an emergent class of copolymers, bundle copolymers, comprising multiple different chains interlinked and aligned in a parallel configuration. To exemplify this concept, bundle copolymers are synthesised via nanoconfined polymerisation, in which radical polymerisation of vinyl monomers occurs alongside vinyl-functionalised polydimethylsiloxane within the one-dimensional channels of a metal–organic framework. Unlike conventional copolymers, bundle copolymers feature aligned lateral strands whose proximity can be modulated by the density of covalent junctions. This innovative approach for tying multiple chains opens an avenue for copolymer design, expanding the landscape of polymer chemistry.Subject terms: Polymer synthesis, Metal-organic frameworks, Polymer characterization

Introduction

Copolymers, formed by combining different monomer units, are a central class of macromolecules with widespread importance in materials science and polymer chemistry. Their capacity to introduce molecular diversity often imparts properties inaccessible to homopolymers, rendering them indispensable in both fundamental research and industrial applications1–4. In 1994, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) formally classified copolymers into four primary categories based on the arrangement of monomer units: random (statistical), sequence-controlled (alternating, periodic), block, and graft (Fig. 1a)5. These established types and their combinations have provided an outline for the rational design of functional copolymer materials. Over the past decades, advances in polymer synthesis have enabled precise control over these architectures6–11, demonstrating that both chemical composition and structural design play a key role in dictating polymer properties12–16.

a Schematic illustration of the four conventional types of copolymers categorised by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC). b Schematic illustration of bundle copolymers introduced in this work. Bundle copolymers are polymers in which different polymer chains are covalently interconnected and aligned in a parallel configuration.

Developing copolymer architectures beyond these conventional categories could expand accessible property spaces and enable a broader range of applications. Herein, we introduce a distinct class of copolymers, termed bundle copolymers (Fig. 1b), in which different polymer chains are aligned in parallel and covalently linked to form a multi-stranded architecture. This side-by-side arrangement contrasts with that in conventional copolymers, where monomer units are connected sequentially along a single backbone or at the chain termini. While each strand in a bundle copolymer preserves its intrinsic chemical identity, the bundled configuration is expected to profoundly influence chain dynamics and conformational behaviour, potentially giving rise to properties that are different from those of conventional copolymers. However, the realisation of this bundled architecture presents significant synthetic challenges. Unlike asymmetric rigid ladder motifs relying on specific monomer design17–22, the parallel alignment of distinct polymer chains remains difficult and unexplored23.

To synthesise bundle copolymers, we developed a nanoconfined polymerisation approach employing the one-dimensional (1D) nanochannels of a metal-organic framework (MOF) as a spatially confined reaction environment. MOFs provide a highly versatile platform for designing nanopores, as their size and functionality can be precisely tailored by selecting from a wide variety of metal ions and organic linkers24–26. While MOFs are widely recognised for their applications in catalysis27,28, molecular capture29,30 and separation31,32, their use as nanoreactors for polymer synthesis has recently attracted attention33–35. Figure 2a presents the synthetic scheme for bundle copolymers, which capitalises on confinement within MOF nanopores. Both a polymer bearing pendant monomers (polyA) and free monomers (B) are infiltrated into 1D nanopores with diameters comparable to the thickness of the bundled polymer chains. Within these confined spaces, polyA adopts an axially extended conformation alongside B36,37. Under this spatial constraint, copolymerisation of the pendant monomers and B occurs, effectively tethering polyA and polyB chains into a bundled arrangement. Subsequent removal of the MOF scaffold yields the bundle copolymer, polyA-bundle-polyB. Importantly, this spatially guided strategy does not rely on the specific structure of the polymer species, allowing for a broad range of copolymer designs. We demonstrate the feasibility of this approach and provide structural characterisation for bundle copolymers to establish them as a category of copolymer architecture.

Results

In this work, bundle copolymers consisting of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, polyA)38 and vinyl polymers (polyB)39,40 were synthesised (Fig. 2b). This combination of commodity polymers demonstrates the generality and practical applicability of the MOF-templated approach. Methacrylate-functionalised PDMS (PDMS-MA; Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2) was introduced into the nanochannels of [In(bdc)(OH)]n (1, bdc = 1,4-benzenedicarboxylate; Fig. 2c)41, a MOF that features 1D hexagonal pores with diameters comparable to the thickness of double-stranded copolymers composed of PDMS and typical vinyl polymers. Infiltration of PDMS-MA into 1 was carried out via a solvent-assisted method42, involving the immersion of 1 in a tetrahydrofuran (THF) solution of PDMS-MA, followed by slow solvent evaporation to afford a dry composite of 1 and PDMS-MA. The amount of PDMS-MA was empirically optimised to one-third of the weight of 1, ensuring an approximate 1:1 mass ratio between PDMS-MA and vinyl monomers in the subsequent step. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) of the composite exhibited no detectable glass transition for PDMS-MA, indicating that the polymer chains were confined within the nanochannels and no longer retained bulk-like mobility (Supplementary Fig. 3). Successful encapsulation was further supported by intensity changes in the powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns (Supplementary Fig. 4) and a marked decrease in gas adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Following the encapsulation of PDMS-MA in 1, methyl methacrylate (MMA) and a radical initiator were introduced into the nanochannels. The amount of MMA adsorbed in the composite reached 47% of the maximum loading capacity of 1, suggesting that PDMS-MA occupied approximately half of the pore volume (Supplementary Fig. 6). Radical polymerisation was then initiated by heating the composite under a nitrogen atmosphere, and the conversion of MMA reached 95% (Supplementary Fig. 6). PXRD patterns recorded before and after polymerisation confirmed that the crystallinity of 1 was retained throughout the process (Supplementary Fig. 7). The spatial arrangements and conformations of the reactants during polymerisation were evaluated by molecular dynamics simulations performed at the experimentally determined ratio of 1, PDMS-MA, and MMA (Supplementary Fig. 8). Under confined conditions within the nanochannels, PDMS-MA adopts an extended conformation aligned along the channel axis, while MMA molecules diffuse freely within the remaining pore space without phase separation. The simulations also revealed that the pores of 1 can precisely accommodate a double-stranded PDMS-bundle-PMMA structure while sterically preventing the formation of an additional third polymer strand (Fig. 3a). After removal of the MOF scaffold and subsequent purification, PDMS-bundle-PMMA was obtained as a colourless, soluble solid. Although PMMA and PDMS homopolymers are insoluble in n-hexane and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), respectively, PDMS-bundle-PMMA can be dissolved completely in both solvents, suggesting the absence of either homopolymers in the copolymer product (Supplementary Fig. 9). Notably, when polymerisation was performed without the MOF, the product was an insoluble, randomly crosslinked network polymer (Supplementary Fig. 10).

To confirm the formation of bundle copolymer, we conducted a comprehensive structural analysis of PDMS-bundle-PMMA. The 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrum of PDMS-bundle-PMMA (Fig. 3b) exhibited characteristic signals corresponding to both the PDMS and PMMA components, confirming the successful incorporation of both polymer strands. The molar ratio of PDMS to PMMA was determined from the integration ratio of these signals (PDMS/PMMA = 46/54). Considering the different repeat unit length of PDMS (0.36 nm) and PMMA (0.28 nm), the corresponding contour length ratio of the two components was estimated to be approximately PDMS/PMMA = 52/48. Broadened peaks assignable to the junction points (peaks c and e) were also observed, providing evidence for covalent linkages between the PDMS and PMMA chains. Compared with the spectrum of PDMS-MA (Supplementary Fig. 1), the olefinic proton signals almost completely disappeared, reflecting the efficient conversion of pendant methacrylate groups during radical polymerisation.

We then performed gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis to investigate the overall polymer structures. Molecular weight of the bundle copolymer was estimated relative to that of a PMMA standard, as light-scattering analysis was not applicable owing to the isorefractive property of the PDMS segment43. The observed molecular weight of PDMS-bundle-PMMA (Mn = 98,100) is significantly higher than that of the PDMS-MA precursor (Fig. 3c), indicating chain extension through radical polymerisation. To elucidate the structural features of the bundle copolymer, the molecular weight of the PMMA strand was analysed after selectively degrading the PDMS chain in PDMS-bundle-PMMA using tetrabutylammonium fluoride (Supplementary Fig. 11)44. GPC analysis of the liberated PMMA revealed a molecular weight (Mn = 25,300) lower than that of PDMS-bundle-PMMA. Considering the molecular weights of the individual components (PDMS-MA precursor and PMMA strand), PDMS-bundle-PMMA comprises multiple PDMS and PMMA chain segments interconnected through covalent crosslinking. To visualise the morphology directly, we employed atomic force microscopy (AFM). A dilute solution of PDMS-bundle-PMMA was spin-cast onto a mica substrate and imaged in tapping mode45. As shown in Fig. 3d, flexible chain-like structures with heights comparable to those of single polymer chains were observed. This result suggests that PDMS-bundle-PMMA adopts a side-by-side adsorption structure on the substrate. Both molecular weight analysis and morphological observations confirmed that multiple PDMS and PMMA chains are covalently interconnected to form a bundle copolymer, as schematically illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 12.

The hallmark of the bundle copolymer topology is the close spatial proximity of the polymer strands, arising from their parallel alignment. To experimentally validate this defining structural feature, the nuclear Overhauser effect (NOE) between the protons on PMMA and PDMS was investigated. Nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy (NOESY) enables the determination of internuclear distances through analysis of peak intensities, with an effective detection range of approximately 0.5 nm. Because of this short detection threshold, interchain NOE signals for polymers are typically observed only in highly aggregated environments, such as the cores of micelles and hairpin chains46,47. Remarkably, PDMS-bundle-PMMA exhibited clear interchain NOE signals between the two polymer segments. In the 1D NOESY spectrum, selective irradiation of PDMS methyl protons generated negative NOE peaks characteristic of macromolecular systems at chemical shifts corresponding to PMMA protons (Fig. 4a)48. This observation demonstrates that PDMS and PMMA segments are within the NOE detection range of each other. In complementary experiments, irradiation of PMMA protons yielded the corresponding NOE signals from the PDMS segment (Supplementary Fig. 13), further confirming the close spatial proximity of the two strands. In contrast, reference block and graft copolymers composed of PDMS and PMMA exhibited no detectable or minimal NOE signals between the polymer segments under the same measurement conditions because the distance between the chains exceeds the threshold for NOE detection (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 14). These results provide direct spectroscopic evidence that PDMS-bundle-PMMA adopts a tightly aligned bundled structure, distinct from those of conventional copolymers.

The synthetic strategy based on spatially confined polymerisation allows the incorporation of other monomer species without requiring significant changes to the reaction conditions. To demonstrate the generality of this method, we replaced MMA with styrene (S) and successfully synthesised PDMS-bundle-PS under reaction conditions analogous to those employed for PDMS-bundle-PMMA. Styrene monomers were introduced into the composite preloaded with PDMS-MA, and radical polymerisation was conducted within the confined environment of the size-matched MOF nanopores (Fig. 5a). Polymerisation proceeded to high conversion (88%) without disrupting the crystallinity of the host framework, and PDMS-bundle-PS was obtained as a soluble polymer. Structural analyses confirmed the successful formation of a bundled architecture with a molar ratio of repeating units (PDMS/PS = 47/53, Fig. 5b), supporting the similar contour length ratio of the strands (PDMS/PS = 53/47). This was also evidenced by the shift of the GPC profile to a higher molecular weight (Fig. 5c). These results are consistent with those obtained for PDMS-bundle-PMMA, demonstrating the successful extension of the synthetic methodology to different monomer systems.

Considering the arrangement of polymer strands in bundle copolymers, the crosslinking density is a meaningful parameter reflecting the degree of spatial proximity between the strands. To investigate this relationship, bundle copolymers with different crosslinking densities were prepared by changing the average number of PDMS repeating units between methacrylate pendants (p). While the previously discussed samples were synthesised using PDMS-MA with p = 5.1, lower-density bundle copolymers were prepared from PDMS-MA with p = 16 (Supplementary Figs. 15–17). The resulting differences in interchain proximity were assessed by analysing the intensities of the NOE signals under identical measurement conditions (Supplementary Fig. 18). Figure 5d shows the NOE signal intensity normalised by the compositional ratio, which provides an indication of spatial proximity to the irradiated protons49. The signal strength decreased with decreasing crosslinking density, suggesting an increased average interchain distance, likely due to increased conformational freedom. These results demonstrate that interstrand proximity, a key feature of bundle copolymers, can be qualitatively assessed and tuned by adjusting the crosslinking density. It should be noted that direct comparison between PDMS-bundle-PMMA and PDMS-bundle-PS is difficult because of the different chemical environments and spatial orientations of protons in the PMMA and PS units.

Finally, the thermal properties of the bundle copolymers were assessed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and DSC (Supplementary Figs. 19and 20). TGA of bundle copolymers exhibited degradation behaviours closely comparable to those of block copolymers. Consistent with previous reports that copolymer formation with thermally stable PDMS suppresses the thermal degradation of vinyl polymer segments50,51, a similar stabilisation effect was observed in the present bundle copolymers. On the other hand, in DSC measurement, block copolymers typically showed two clear glass transition temperatures (Tg), reflecting microphase separation52, where each polymer component maintains its inherent mobility within separate domains. In contrast, the covalently tethered structure of the bundle copolymers resulted in thermal behaviour different from those of traditional phase-separated systems. The PDMS segment displayed broader Tg transitions, potentially due to being constrained by the more rigid vinyl polymers in the bundled architecture, which may obscure the expression of characteristic PDMS domain mobility. For the vinyl polymer components (PS and PMMA), the Tg values of the bundle copolymers were between those of the corresponding homopolymers and block copolymers. This intermediate behaviour may reflect two competing effects: (1) reduced Tg due to restricted domain size, as the polymer chains cannot form large domains in the bundled structure, and (2) elevated Tg resulting from restricted chain mobility imposed by covalent crosslinking between the strands. The vinyl polymer components in the bundle copolymers with a higher crosslinking density (p = 5.1) exhibited a less defined or higher Tg, indicating that intense interstrand proximity may fundamentally disrupt conventional polymer dynamics. These characteristic polymer dynamics may influence the mechanical properties, as suggested by nanoindentation measurements (Supplementary Fig. 21). The bundle copolymers exhibited higher surface hardness than the corresponding block copolymers, likely due to the restricted mobility of the PDMS chains within the bundled strands. These thermal and mechanical behaviours suggest that the bundle topology fundamentally alters polymer chain dynamics through enforced interstrand proximity, establishing a structural parameter that can be systematically tuned to guide future materials design.

Discussion

We proposed bundle copolymers as a class of copolymers, in which multiple different polymer strands are aligned in parallel and covalently connected. This unique topology provides opportunities to engineer materials with properties inaccessible to existing architectures through their defining feature, tunable interstrand spatial proximity, as demonstrated by NOE interactions. The persistent close contact between the strands affects chain mobility and promotes cooperative behaviour as suggested by DSC measurements. This bundled structure, distinct from conventional copolymers, can influence not only thermal behaviour but also other polymer properties. For example, the bundle structure may facilitate energy or charge transfer between strands, microphase organisation at the chain-width scale, and enhanced interfacial stabilisation in polymer blends.

For this proof-of-concept study, we selected readily accessible commodity polymers (PDMS and PMMA/PS) to demonstrate the feasibility of the approach. Preliminary findings on the interstrand proximity and the thermal behaviour of bundle copolymers suggest potential for significant development of other properties, including viscoelastic, electrochemical and optical properties, leveraging the highly versatile MOF-templated approach. The modular nature of this approach allows for the strategic selection of polymer components based on desired target properties, opening the way to the rational design of bundle architectures, including multistrand, core-sheath and woven polymers. The synthesis of PDMS-bundle-PMMA and PDMS-bundle-PS represents the first step toward establishing bundle copolymers as a promising molecular architecture for expanding the scope of macromolecular design and properties.

Methods

Materials

All reagents were obtained from commercial sources and used without purification unless otherwise noted. Styrene (FUJIFILM Wako, >99%) and methyl methacrylate (FUJIFILM Wako, >98%) were purified by distillation under reduced pressure. [In(OH)bdc]n (1, bdc = 1,4-benzene dicarboxylate) was prepared according to the literature41.

Measurements

1H NMR spectra were recorded using a Bruker Avance III HD (500 MHz) spectrometer equipped with a PABBO probe or a JEOL JMN-ECZL500R (500 MHz) spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm FG/RO digital auto tune probe. Chemical shifts were referenced to residual solvent signals (CHCl3 = 7.26 ppm, dimethylsulfoxide = 2.50 ppm). For 1D NOESY experiments, samples (2.5 wt% in CDCl3) were irradiated at the methyl protons of the PDMS segment with a mixing time of 300 ms.

PXRD data were collected using a Rigaku SmartLab X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) operated at 40 kV and 30 mA. Data were acquired over a 2θ range of 5–40° with a step size of 0.02°.

Nitrogen and CO2 adsorption isotherms at 77 and 195 K, respectively, were measured using a Microtrac BELSORP-mini surface area and pore size distribution analyser. Before the adsorption measurements, samples were treated under reduced pressure (<10−2 Pa) at 393 K overnight.

GPC was performed at 40 °C using a Shimadzu LC-2050 liquid chromatograph equipped with two PS gel columns in series (Shodex LF-804) and refractive index detectors. The mobile phase was tetrahydrofuran (THF) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL min−1. The number-average molecular weight (Mn) and molecular weight distribution (Mw/Mn) were determined relative to those of a PS (SM-105, Shodex) or PMMA standard (Polymer Laboratories), as appropriate. The calibration standards used are indicated on the respective chromatogram axes.

AFM was performed in noncontact tapping mode using an Oxford Instruments Asylum Research MFP-3D Origin atomic force microscope. Aluminium-coated silicon cantilevers (OMCL-AC240TS-R3, Olympus) with spring constants of 0.6–3.5 pN nm−1 (resonant frequency: 70 kHz), which were calibrated using the thermal fluctuation method, were used. Samples were prepared by dissolving the polymers in CHCl3 (1 μg mL−1), spin-casting (2000 rpm, 5 s) them onto a freshly cleaved mica substrate, and drying at 45 °C overnight.

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the Materials Studio v4.4 software package (Accelrys Inc.). The Universal Force Field, as implemented in the Forcite module, was used to model the systems. Charges were accounted for using the charge equilibration method. After geometry optimisation, simulations were conducted under NVT conditions (773 K for 10 ps, then 393 K for 100 ps).

TGA was conducted at 10 °C min−1 using a Rigaku Thermo plus TG 8120 with a nitrogen at 60 mL min−1.

DSC was performed under a nitrogen atmosphere using a HITACHI High Tech DSC7020 differential scanning calorimeter. Samples (~3 mg) were sealed in aluminium pans and scanned at a rate of 10 °C min−1.

The surface hardness of the samples was measured on a ELIONIX ENT-2100 nanoindentation tester with a Berkovich diamond tip at room temperature. The test speed was constant at 1 mN/s, and the maximum depth was 1000 nm. The hardness values were determined from the load–displacement curves using the Sawa–Tanaka method. Samples were prepared by dissolving the polymer in dichloromethane (100 g L−1) and drop-casted onto silicon substrates. Before measurement, the samples were heated at 90 °C for 2 h under vacuum to remove solvent and then cooled to room temperature.

Synthesis of bundle copolymers

Synthesis of PDMS-MA53

Hydroxy-terminated oligomeric PDMS (Aldrich 481939, 5.0 g, 12 mmol), methacrylic acid 3-[diethoxy(methyl)silyl]propyl ester (3.2 g, 12 mmol) and trimethylsilyl alcohol (0.1 mL, 0.71 mmol) were dissolved in THF (10 mL). Subsequently, trifluoroacetic acid (0.1 mL) was added to the solution, and the mixture was stirred at 60 °C for 48 h. After washing with methanol, PDMS-MA (p = 5.1, 3.7 g) was obtained as a colourless oil. The 1H NMR spectrum and GPC curve of the product are shown in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2, respectively.

PDMS-MA with a lower content of methacrylate pendant (p = 16) was synthesised using a longer PDMS precursor (Aldrich 481955) following the same procedure. The 1H NMR spectrum and GPC curve of the product are shown in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2, respectively.

Synthesis of bundle copolymers

To synthesise PDMS-bundle-PMMA, activated 1 (1.77 g) was immersed in a solution of PDMS-MA (p = 5.1, 0.60 g) in THF (10 mL). The solvent was slowly evaporated at 60 °C overnight to introduce PDMS-MA into the MOF nanochannels. The composite was further dried under vacuum to thoroughly remove residual THF from the pores. The composite of PDMS-MA and 1 (2.28 g) was then added to MMA (7 mL) containing benzoyl peroxide (BPO, 21 mg), and the mixture was allowed to stand at ambient temperature for 30 min. Excess MMA was removed under reduced pressure (3 kPa). After sampling a small aliquot and purging with nitrogen, radical polymerisation was carried out at 70 °C for 24 h, followed by an additional 24 h at 120 °C, yielding a composite of 1 and PDMS-bundle-PMMA (conversion of MMA: 95%). The MOF scaffold was removed by sequential immersion in 1 M HCl in water/MeOH (1/1), saturated NaHCO3(aq), 1 M HCl in water, and 1 M EDTA·4Na in water/MeOH (1/1). The resulting product was dissolved in THF and filtered to remove MOF-derived residue. The polymer was further purified by preparative GPC to eliminate oligomeric products, affording PDMS-bundle-PMMA as a colourless solid.

PDMS-bundle-PS was synthesised following the same procedure using styrene instead of MMA. Excess styrene was removed under a pressure of 0.1 kPa, and polymerisation was performed at 120 °C for 24 h.

Degradation of PDMS44

PDMS degradation was performed via a siloxane exchange reaction using tetrabutylammonium fluoride trihydrate (TBAF) as a catalyst and excess disiloxane. PDMS-bundle-PMMA (p = 5.1, 9.9 mg) and hexamethyldisiloxane (TMS-O-TMS; 861 mg) were dissolved in THF (18 mL). Subsequently, a TBAF solution in THF (2 mL, 0.83 mg TBAF, 5 mol% relative to PDMS units) was added, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 4 h. Calcium chloride (1.0 g) was then added, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 4 h. After removal of calcium chloride by filtration, the solution was extracted with n-hexane and DMF. The DMF phase was concentrated under reduced pressure, and the polymer was characterised without further purification. The 1H NMR spectrum and GPC curve of the product are shown in Supplementary Fig. 11.

Synthesis of block and graft copolymers

Synthesis of PDMS-Br54

In a round-bottom flask, hydroxy-terminated PDMS (Aldrich 480355, 4.67 g, 1 mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous THF (50 mL), and triethylamine (1.53 mL, 11 mmol) was then added under a nitrogen atmosphere. After cooling the solution in an ice bath (0 °C), 2-bromo-2-methylpropionyl bromide (1.23 mL, 10 mmol) was slowly added with stirring. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h. Upon reaction completion, the mixture was poured into a biphasic mixture of n-hexane (~50 mL) and water (~50 mL), and the organic layer was extracted and washed three times with water. The organic layer was dried over Na2SO4, and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. The resulting oily product was washed with methanol, affording PDMS-Br as a methanol-insoluble viscous oil (3.24 g). The 1H NMR spectrum and GPC curve of the product are shown in Supplementary Figs. 22 and 23, respectively.

Synthesis of PDMS-block-PMMA55

In a glass vial sealed with a rubber septum, PDMS-Br (260 mg, 0.05 mmol), MMA (0.53 mL, 5 mmol), 1,1,4,7,10,10-hexamethyltriethylenetetramine (HMTETA; 53.6 µL, 0.2 mmol), and anisole (2 mL) were mixed and degassed by nitrogen bubbling for 5 min. The resulting solution was then transferred to a round-bottom flask containing CuBr2 (22.3 mg, 0.1 mmol) under a nitrogen atmosphere. Polymerisation was initiated by heating the mixture to 90 °C with stirring. After 30 min, the reaction was quenched by cooling and exposure to air. The resulting polymer was purified by reprecipitation into methanol, followed by preparative GPC, affording PDMS-block-PMMA as a white solid (241 mg). The 1H NMR spectrum and GPC curve of the product are shown in Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25, respectively.

Synthesis of PDMS-block-PS56

A degassed mixture of tris[2-(dimethylamino)ethyl]amine (Me6TREN; 27 µL, 0.1 mmol), tin(II) 2-ethylhexanoate (Sn(EH)2; 40.9 µL, 0.1 mmol) and anisole (1 mL) was added to a round-bottom flask containing CuCl2 (1.3 mg, 0.01 mmol) under a nitrogen atmosphere. In a separate round-bottom flask, PDMS-Br (521 mg, 0.1 mmol) was dissolved in styrene (1.15 mL, 10 mmol) and degassed by nitrogen bubbling for 5 min. The resulting PDMS-styrene solution was transferred to the Cu catalyst solution using a syringe. Polymerisation was carried out at 110 °C with stirring. After 8 h, the reaction was quenched by cooling and exposure to air. The resulting polymer was purified by reprecipitation into methanol, followed by preparative GPC, affording PDMS-block-PS as a white solid (350 mg). The 1H NMR spectrum and GPC curve of the product are shown in Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27, respectively.

Synthesis of methacrylate-terminated PDMS

In a round-bottom flask, hydroxy-terminated PDMS (Aldrich 480355, 2.34 g, 0.5 mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous THF (25 mL), and triethylamine (0.77 mL, 6 mmol) was then added under a nitrogen atmosphere. After cooling the solution in an ice bath (0 °C), methacryloyl chloride (0.62 mL, 5 mmol) was slowly added with stirring. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h. Upon reaction completion, the mixture was poured into a biphasic mixture of n-hexane (~50 mL) and water (~50 mL), and the organic layer was extracted and washed three times with water. The organic layer was dried over Na2SO4, and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. The resulting oily product was purified by preparative GPC, affording methacrylate-terminated PDMS (1.76 g). The 1H NMR spectrum and GPC curve of the product are shown in Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29, respectively.

Synthesis of PMMA-graft-PDMS

In a round-bottom flask sealed with a rubber septum, methacrylate-terminated PDMS (582 mg, 0.102 mmol), MMA (0.8 mL 7.5 mmol), 2,2′-azobis(isobutyronitrile) (AIBN, 8.2 mg 0.05 mmol) and toluene (12 mL) were mixed and degassed by nitrogen bubbling for 5 min. The resulting solution was heated at 70 °C with stirring. After 18 h, the reaction was quenched by cooling and exposure to air. The obtained polymer was purified by preparative GPC, affording PMMA-graft-PDMS as a colourless film (546 mg). The 1H NMR spectrum and GPC curve of the product are shown in Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31, respectively.

Synthesis of PDMS-graft-PMMA

In a round-bottom flask, PDMS-MA (p = 5.1, 56 mg, 0.1 mmol of pendant methacrylate), 2-cyano-2-propyl dodecyl trithiocarbonate (35 mg, 0.1 mmol), AIBN (3 mg, 0.02 mmol) and MMA (5.33 mL, 50 mmol) were mixed and degassed by nitrogen bubbling for 15 min. The resulting solution was heated for 1.5 h at 70 °C with stirring (MMA conversion 12%). After cooling, the residual monomer was removed under reduced pressure. The product was purified by preparative GPC, correcting the high molecular weight fraction, affording PDMS-graft-PMMA as a pale yellow solid (71 mg). The 1H NMR spectrum and GPC curve of the product are shown in Supplementary Figs. 32 and 33, respectively.
