Deep residual networks for short-term load forecasting: an empirical study on the impact of network depth
Authors: Junchen Liu, Faisul Arif Ahmad, Khairulmizam Samsudin, Fazirulhisyam Hashim, Mohd Zainal Abidin Ab Kadir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe impact of network depth on the performance of deep residual networks (DRNs) for short-term load forecasting (STLF) remains insufficiently understood. This study conducts a systematic empirical investigation of network depth within DRN-based frameworks, with a particular focus on its relationship with forecasting performance, model behavior, and dataset characteristics. Two representative models, namely the original DRN and the Principal Component Analysis–Deep Residual Network (PCA–DRN), are evaluated under a wide range of depth configurations using two real-world datasets with different load and meteorological characteristics: ISO-NE and MyPJ. The results suggest that the influence of network depth on forecasting performance is inherently nonlinear and highly dataset-dependent. For the ISO-NE dataset, medium-depth configurations achieve favorable performance by effectively modeling more pronounced seasonal variability and long-term temporal patterns. In contrast, relatively shallow configurations generally exhibit improved performance on the MyPJ dataset under the current experimental setting, suggesting that deeper architectures may introduce additional model complexity without consistent performance gains. Furthermore, within the MyPJ dataset containing multiple meteorological variables, the incorporation of PCA improves feature representation, enhances model robustness, and is associated with reduced sensitivity to depth variations under the current experimental setting. Comparative evaluations against mainstream deep learning (DL) models indicate that DRN-based frameworks achieve competitive forecasting performance with relatively efficient structural designs. In addition, bootstrap-based statistical analysis suggests that the observed performance differences remain distinguishable under sample-level variability within the current experimental setting. These findings suggest that appropriate network depth selection should be considered together with dataset characteristics and feature representation, providing practical insights for the design of efficient and robust STLF models.Keywords: Deep residual networks, Empirical analysis, Network depth, Principal component analysis, Short-term load forecastingSubject terms: Climate sciences, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

For dependable and efficient operation, modern power systems rely on precise Short-Term Load Forecasting (STLF)1. Utilities may optimize crucial decision-making processes, such as generation scheduling, unit commitment, and energy trading strategies, by using STLF to provide accurate demand estimates over forecasting timeframes ranging from 24 h to one week ahead2. Enhanced forecasting accuracy contributes directly to improved grid planning, operation, and management, thereby strengthening supply stability, lowering operational costs, and promoting overall energy efficiency3. However, with the ongoing growth in electricity demand and the increasing diversity of consumption behaviors, the complexity and importance of STLF have escalated considerably4. From an economic perspective, even marginal improvements in forecasting performance can yield substantial financial benefits. For large-scale utility providers, an accuracy enhancement of just 1% has been reported to translate into annual savings of several million dollars, primarily through more efficient resource allocation and reduced reserve requirements5,6. Therefore, continuous advancements in forecasting methodologies, regardless of their magnitude, can generate significant economic and operational gains.

Approaches to STLF can generally be categorized into two main groups: early methods and artificial intelligence (AI) techniques. Early studies primarily relied on statistical models and traditional machine learning algorithms7, which established the foundation for load forecasting research and provided valuable insights into electricity consumption patterns. Despite their important contributions, early methods exhibit inherent limitations in handling complex and highly nonlinear load behaviors. These methods often depend on simplified assumptions regarding the underlying dynamics of electricity demand, which restricts their ability to adapt to real-world variability. In addition, as the dimensionality of input features increases, their generalization capability tends to degrade, leading to a higher susceptibility to overfitting8,9.

To overcome these challenges, increasing attention has been directed toward artificial intelligence (AI) techniques in STLF, with artificial neural networks (ANNs) emerging as a prominent solution. Leveraging deep learning (DL) principles, ANNs are capable of modeling complex nonlinear relationships in load data and have demonstrated improved forecasting performance compared to traditional approaches10. Nevertheless, the introduction of additional input variables, hidden units, or network layers may still lead to overfitting, thereby compromising model generalization. To mitigate this issue, a variety of enhanced ANN architectures have been explored within the context of STLF11–13. Despite these advancements, early ANNs and their variants, which are typically characterized by relatively shallow structures, remain constrained in their ability to effectively capture highly intricate load patterns. Because of their better representational capacity and potential to learn hierarchical features, deep neural networks (DNNs) with several hidden layers have become more popular. Such architectures enable more effective modeling of complex temporal dependencies and spatial correlations present in diverse data sources. Consequently, STLF research has gradually transitioned from traditional ANN-based models toward more sophisticated DL frameworks that offer enhanced flexibility and predictive capability14.

In recent years, STLF research has increasingly shifted from shallow model architectures toward deeper neural network frameworks with more diverse input features. Because they can record localized temporal patterns through convolutional operations, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have been frequently used among them. However, their effectiveness in modeling long-range temporal dependencies remains limited, and increasing network depth often introduces additional training difficulties15,16. To address these limitations, Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) have been proposed as an extension of conventional CNNs. By employing dilated and causal convolutions, TCNs expand the receptive field and enable parallel modeling of longer temporal sequences. Nevertheless, their performance is highly dependent on architectural design choices, and accurately capturing very long-term dependencies may still require increasingly deep or complex configurations17,18. In order to explicitly express temporal dependencies and address the vanishing gradient issue, recurrent neural networks (RNNs), in particular Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, incorporate memory cells and gating mechanisms. Despite these advantages, their inherently sequential processing nature results in reduced computational efficiency when handling long input sequences19,20. More recently, Transformer-based architectures have gained significant attention due to their self-attention mechanisms, which allow for fully parallel modeling of long-range dependencies21–23. However, these models are often associated with high computational costs and may exhibit unstable training behavior when scaled to deeper configurations.

Hybrid architectures that combine convolutional, recurrent, and attention-based methods have been increasingly investigated in an effort to improve forecasting accuracy and generalization capacity. Representative examples include Transformer–LSTM24 and CNN-LSTM with multi-modal attention (CNN–LSTM–MMA)25, both of which aim to leverage the complementary strengths of different modeling paradigms. Although these hybrid techniques have significantly improved predictive accuracy, they frequently face issues with computing efficiency and training stability. These drawbacks emphasize the need to create DL frameworks for STLF that are more reliable, effective, and scalable.

Residual learning, supported by identity shortcut connections, was first introduced in the Residual Network (ResNet) architecture26 to address the vanishing gradient issue and improve training stability in DNNs. Building upon this principle, Chen et al.27 extended the concept to the field of STLF by proposing the Deep Residual Network (DRN), demonstrating that a well-balanced design in terms of network depth, optimization stability, and representational capability can achieve reliable and high-accuracy forecasting performance. Many DRN-based improvements have been created to further enhance forecasting accuracy and model resilience in the wake of this groundbreaking work. These include specialized residual architectures tailored for STLF tasks28, ensemble-enhanced DRN frameworks utilizing snapshot learning or residual ensemble strategies29,30, and data-driven DRN models incorporating adaptive feature selection techniques31. In addition, structural enhancements have been introduced to refine residual representations, including the incorporation of inception-inspired modules at the feature extraction stage and the redesign of residual blocks to enhance local pattern extraction32,33, while hybrid DRN architectures integrating recurrent networks or attention mechanisms have been explored to better capture temporal dependencies34–36. Furthermore, the Principal Component Analysis–Deep Residual Network (PCA–DRN) has been proposed to effectively incorporate multiple meteorological variables by applying dimensionality reduction techniques, thereby improving feature representation efficiency37.

Despite the significant progress achieved by DRN-based models in STLF, the impact of network depth on forecasting performance remains insufficiently explored. Existing studies primarily focus on architectural enhancements, feature engineering, and hybrid modeling strategies, while network depth is often treated as a predefined hyperparameter rather than a key factor for systematic investigation. As a result, the relationship between network depth, model performance, and training stability has not been thoroughly examined, particularly under different climatic conditions and load characteristics. Although several previous STLF studies have included depth-related architectural comparisons as part of broader model development and benchmarking processes, relatively few studies have treated network depth itself as the primary independent variable in a systematic empirical investigation within DRN-based STLF frameworks.

To address this gap, this study conducts a comprehensive empirical analysis of network depth in DRNs for STLF. Specifically, the original DRN and its PCA-enhanced variant (PCA-DRN) are employed as representative frameworks, and their performance is evaluated under a wide range of depth configurations. Two real-world datasets with different load characteristics and meteorological conditions are utilized to investigate the dataset-dependent behavior of network depth. In addition, statistical significance tests based on bootstrap resampling are performed to ensure the robustness and reliability of the observed results.

Related work

Because of the rapid advancement of sensor technologies, advanced metering infrastructures, and modern high-performance computing platforms, DL techniques have become more prevalent in STLF. DNNs are frequently used in modern STLF research because they are better than traditional statistical and shallow learning techniques in approximating nonlinear interactions and learning intricate temporal relationships. Convolution-based, recurrent-based, attention-driven, and hybrid modeling frameworks are commonly used to classify contemporary DL-based forecasting techniques, each of which emphasizes certain aspects of load time-series data. To provide a clearer overview of the representative studies discussed in this section, Fig. 1 summarizes the distribution of the selected DL-based STLF studies across several major modeling paradigms.

Distribution of reviewed studies across DL paradigms in STLF.

Due to their ability to capture localized temporal patterns and describe short-term relationships, CNNs have been thoroughly investigated in STLF. By converting load sequences into image-like representations, for example, Li et al.15 improved forecasting performance over a variety of prediction horizons by allowing convolutional kernels to take advantage of spatial correlations. However, the required data transformation procedures and dual-branch architecture introduced additional structural complexity, which may hinder real-time implementation. Jurado et al.16 created an encoder-decoder CNN system with Monte Carlo Dropout and probabilistic density estimation to further improve prediction performance and quantify uncertainty. Although this approach outperformed conventional recurrent models, its reduced accuracy during peak load periods revealed limited robustness under abrupt demand fluctuations.

TCNs are an extension of conventional convolutional models that employ causal and dilated convolutions to better capture long-range temporal correlations. Tang et al.17 incorporated both channel-wise and temporal attention mechanisms into a TCN framework to account for cumulative weather effects and varying feature importance, leading to improved forecasting accuracy. Nevertheless, the inclusion of attention modules further increased model complexity. To address computational efficiency, Liu et al.18 proposed a temporal depthwise convolutional network based on TCN, which utilizes depthwise separable convolutions to reduce parameter redundancy while maintaining temporal modeling capability. Despite these improvements, the model remains predominantly convolution-driven, which may limit its adaptability when dealing with highly complex temporal dynamics.

In STLF, sequence modeling continues to be a major difficulty, especially when dealing with complicated seasonal dynamics. When enough historical data is available, models built on recurrent architectures, such as LSTM variations, have proven to be highly predictive19. However, if important exogenous variables, including weather-related aspects, are not specifically taken into account, forecasting accuracy may decrease. By using metaheuristic-driven hyperparameter optimization, Bento et al.20 tried to overcome this restriction, but the ensuing rise in computing demand poses scalability issues.

However, the increasing computational complexity associated with recurrent and hybrid forecasting architectures has also motivated the development of more computationally efficient forecasting paradigms. In addition to conventional recurrent architectures, lightweight forecasting approaches designed for efficiency have attracted growing attention in time-series forecasting research. Reservoir Computing approaches, particularly Echo State Networks (ESNs), have demonstrated competitive forecasting capability while significantly reducing training complexity by fixing recurrent connections and optimizing only the output layer38. Such architectures provide an efficient alternative for modeling temporal dynamics under limited computational resources. Furthermore, uncertainty-aware forecasting strategies, including Monte Carlo Dropout and probabilistic residual-based formulations, have also been explored to improve forecasting robustness and reliability under uncertain operating conditions. Recent studies have additionally investigated residual-based formulations in time-series modeling to improve optimization stability and feature representation efficiency39. These developments further highlight the importance of understanding the relationship between model complexity, computational efficiency, and forecasting robustness in DL-based forecasting systems.

Attention mechanisms and Transformer-based models, in contrast to recurrent architectures, have garnered a lot of attention lately because of their capacity to capture long-range temporal relationships through global self-attention. For instance, to improve temporal feature extraction, Ran et al.21 integrated Transformer networks with empirical mode decomposition. However, the model’s capacity to adapt to new datasets is limited by its high training time and dependence on predetermined breakdown parameters. Jiang et al.22 improved prediction accuracy by enlarging the attention receptive field, but this enhancement came at the cost of substantially increased memory consumption. To further improve temporal dependency modeling, Li et al.23 proposed TS2ARCformer, which combines contextual encoding, cross-dimensional attention, and autoregressive components within a unified framework. Although this model achieved improved performance better on benchmark datasets, the addition of autoregressive processes and hierarchical attention structures greatly increased architectural complexity, making practical deployment in STLF applications difficult.

Recent research has concentrated increasingly on hybrid DL frameworks that use complementary modeling approaches in an attempt to lessen the inherent drawbacks of single-paradigm systems. For industrial STLF, Chen et al.24 presented a Transformer–LSTM hybrid architecture that used LSTM layers to simulate sequential dependencies and self-attention to capture global temporal interactions. The cascaded structure required careful hyperparameter tweaking and higher processing costs despite ongoing performance improvements. In a similar vein, Guo et al.25 created an improved CNN–LSTM framework with multi-modal attention to adaptively fuse a range of inputs, such as meteorological data and load demand. The attention-based fusion strategy raised computing complexity and placed more stringent constraints on data availability and quality, although improving prediction accuracy.

Even when convolution-based, recurrent-based, attention-based, and hybrid DL models produce significant performance advances, deeper and more sophisticated network designs might worsen training instability, gradient deterioration, and performance saturation. The necessity for learning paradigms that can sustain deep structures while preserving consistent optimization behavior is highlighted by these difficulties. By adding residual connections that allow for scalable depth extension and gradient propagation, DRNs have become a viable option in this situation.

DRN-based models have shown significant benefits in defining complex and highly nonlinear load patterns in STLF applications. Early studies mostly concentrated on comparative analysis with traditional DL methods and feasibility assessment. In order to improve resilience and generalization, Chen et al.27 combined residual learning with ensemble techniques, although at a higher computing cost. A task-oriented DRN architecture was presented by Kondaiah et al.28, who demonstrated that tailored residual designs successfully anticipate nonlinear load patterns across several datasets. However, highly variable or high-frequency load dynamics received little attention in these research, which primarily focused on aggregate forecasting accuracy.

Ensemble-enhanced DRN frameworks were investigated to further enhance stability and generalization. An Ensemble Residual Network (ERN) that uses snapshot ensemble learning to boost robustness without training several separate models was introduced by Xu et al.29. Chen et al.30 expanded on this idea by including snapshot ensembles and multi-scale feature extraction into a ResNet-based system. Ensemble-based DRN techniques are better at generalization, but because they usually require more processing power, longer training schedules, and precise snapshot interval design, their real-time applicability may be limited.

Meanwhile, data-driven augmentation strategies have improved learning stability in DRN-based models. Kondaiah et al.31 created a Deep-ResNet architecture that prioritizes adaptive feature selection in order to capture significant load characteristics throughout a range of operating conditions. Although forecasting performance has improved considerably, many models still exhibit limited generalization capability when applied to power systems with different load characteristics and data distributions. To enhance feature representation, structural modifications have also been put into place. Ding et al.32 incorporated inception-inspired modules into the feature extraction stage of residual architectures to facilitate multi-scale representation learning, whereas Sheng et al.33 proposed a convolutional residual network (CRN) by redesigning convolutional residual blocks (ResBlocks) to enhance local pattern extraction for high-resolution load data. Despite the improved representational capability, these enhancements increase architectural complexity and complicate hyperparameter tuning.

Some studies have combined DRNs with recurrent and attention-based mechanisms to improve temporal dependency modeling. Tian et al.34 integrated sequential modeling and deep residual feature extraction in a ResNetPlus–LSTM framework, whereas Li et al.35 used attention approaches to emphasize important time steps. A Residual LSTM Plus design that closely links ResBlocks with recurrent layers was recently presented by Sheng et al.36. Even while these hybrid frameworks frequently boost forecasting reliability, the simultaneous stacking of residual, recurrent, and attention components greatly increases model depth and computational cost, restricting scalability in large-scale or real-time STLF systems.

Previous DRN-based research have investigated expansions at the input level to accommodate better meteorological data, in addition to architectural and ensemble-based improvements. In order to expand temperature-based DRN models, Liu et al.37 introduced the PCA-DRN framework, which uses principal component analysis (PCA) to integrate many meteorological variables. The physical interpretability of meteorological variables is necessarily weakened by PCA-DRN’s reliance on linear dimensionality reduction, even while it successfully decreases input dimensionality and feature redundancy while maintaining the residual learning structure.

Despite the rapid advancement of DL techniques in STLF, the impact of network depth on forecasting performance remains an important yet insufficiently explored issue. In general, shallow neural networks are often limited in their representational capacity, which restricts their ability to capture complex and highly nonlinear load patterns. From a theoretical perspective, neural networks can approximate complex functions through hierarchical representations, and increasing network depth enhances this capability40. However, excessively deep architectures may introduce optimization challenges, such as gradient degradation and training instability, which can hinder effective model learning41.

Residual learning provides an effective solution to these challenges by introducing identity shortcut connections that facilitate gradient propagation and stabilize the training process. This capability allows DRNs to scale to greater depths while maintaining robust optimization behavior42. Furthermore, residual architectures have been shown to alleviate the degradation problem and significantly improve training performance in deep models43. Beyond conventional residual learning, several generalized residual connection mechanisms have also been proposed to further improve optimization stability and information propagation in deep neural architectures. For example, highway-based architectures introduce gated shortcut pathways to facilitate effective gradient flow across deep structures44, while recent studies have explored orthogonal additive filtering strategies to mitigate vanishing and exploding gradient problems in DNNs45. These developments further highlight the importance of understanding how residual connections influence optimization behavior and training stability as network depth increases.

The connection between network depth and model performance has been well studied in various fields of study, such as computer vision and voice recognition. For instance, in computer vision, very deep convolutional networks have demonstrated that increasing network depth can significantly improve representation learning and recognition accuracy46. Similarly, in speech recognition, deep architectures have been shown to benefit from increased depth in modeling hierarchical temporal features, although deeper models also introduce greater optimization complexity47. Empirical evidence further suggests that performance improvements with increasing depth are not monotonic, but instead reach a saturation point beyond which marginal gains diminish or even degrade due to optimization difficulties and increased model complexity48. Recent studies in time-series forecasting have also questioned whether increasingly deep architectures consistently improve forecasting performance. Some studies have shown that relatively simple linear forecasting models can achieve highly competitive performance compared with more complex deep architectures49. In addition, efficient temporal representation strategies have been reported to play a more important role than excessive network depth in improving forecasting accuracy50. Collectively, these findings indicate the existence of an appropriate depth configuration that balances model expressiveness and training stability.

However, in the context of STLF, existing studies on DL models have primarily focused on improving forecasting performance through architectural design, feature engineering, and hybrid modeling strategies14,51, while network depth is typically treated as a predefined hyperparameter rather than an independent factor for systematic investigation. Although increasing model complexity has been shown to enhance the capability of capturing nonlinear temporal patterns, the specific impact of network depth is rarely explicitly isolated or rigorously analyzed. In particular, most existing studies adopt fixed network configurations, where the number of layers is selected empirically as part of model design rather than being examined as a key variable. This limitation is especially evident in DRN-based STLF research27–37, where network depth has not been systematically evaluated as an independent factor. As a result, the influence of depth on forecasting accuracy, training stability, and generalization performance remains insufficiently understood.

Therefore, a comprehensive empirical evaluation of network depth within the DRN framework is necessary to better understand its role in STLF. By treating network depth as the primary variable under investigation and analyzing its behavior across datasets with different characteristics, this study aims to provide practical evidence on depth-related forecasting performance and model behavior rather than a theoretical optimization analysis of gradient dynamics. This empirical perspective can help identify suitable depth configurations and provide more practical guidance for model design in real-world STLF applications.

Methodology

Dataset description and preprocessing

Incomplete records, missing observations, noise contamination, and diverse data formats are frequently encountered in real-world power system applications, and if they are not appropriately treated, they can seriously impair forecasting performance52. Therefore, enhancing the resilience and dependability of STLF models requires thorough data pretreatment. Using two real-world load datasets, the New England Independent System Operator (ISO-NE) dataset and the Malaysia Petaling Jaya (MyPJ) dataset, this study investigates STLF performance in different operating situations and climates.

Hourly temperature and electricity demand data from March 2003 to December 2006 are included in the ISO-NE dataset, which includes six states in the New England region of the United States: Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, and Vermont. A moderate environment with significant seasonal and interannual demand fluctuation is represented by this dataset. While the temperature statistics reflect regional hourly observed temperatures rather than predicted values, the load records correlate to system-level power demand. Because of its consistent structure and comprehensiveness, the ISO-NE dataset has been widely utilized as a standard dataset in STLF research. No extra cleaning steps were needed for this investigation because the dataset has previously passed routine data quality control and preparation by the data vendor.

The MyPJ dataset, on the other hand, offers a typical example for analyzing patterns of power consumption in tropical regions. The dataset includes daily meteorological observations collected in the Petaling Jaya region by the Malaysian Meteorological Department from January 2020 to December 2022, as well as nationwide hourly electrical load data obtained from the Malaysian Grid System Operator under Tenaga Nasional Berhad. Rainfall, mean temperature, minimum temperature, maximum temperature, mean wind speed, wind direction, and maximum wind speed are included as the meteorological variables in this dataset. The MyPJ dataset reflects energy consumption behavior in a tropical environment, where short-term weather conditions have a more pronounced influence on load dynamics, while seasonal variations are less significant compared to temperate regions. The missing rate in the meteorological variables of the dataset is approximately 0.59%, while the load data are complete; linear interpolation is applied to impute missing values, as it preserves temporal continuity and minimizes distortion to the underlying load dynamics, thereby having negligible impact on forecasting performance and evaluation results.

The load variations of the two datasets exhibit distinct demand characteristics under different environmental conditions, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The ISO-NE load figures typically fall between 10,000 and 27,500 megawatts (MW) and show distinct seasonal and long-term changes, indicating the significant impact of temperate areas’ normal winter heating and summer cooling needs. The MyPJ load levels, on the other hand, show very little change throughout the year, primarily falling between 10,000 and 18,000 MW. In tropical climes, where seasonal fluctuation is less noticeable and short-term weather circumstances have a greater impact on load dynamics, this pattern represents the behavior of power consumption.

Data normalization was carried out using statistical parameters determined solely from the training set, and the same scaling factors were then applied to the matching test set in order to prevent information leaking during model training and assessment. A fair and trustworthy evaluation of predicting ability under practical operating settings is ensured by this preprocessing technique.

Proposed framework

To enable a systematic examination of how network depth affects training dynamics and forecasting accuracy, two representative residual-based forecasting models, namely the original DRN27 and PCA-DRN37, are employed in this study. Rather than introducing additional architectural modifications, the network structure is kept fixed, and only the depth is varied, thereby allowing the isolated impact of depth on empirical learning behavior and generalization performance to be assessed. By maintaining a consistent structural foundation, these models further facilitate a fair comparison across different input representations.

The DRN and PCA-DRN architectures utilized for the MyPJ dataset are shown in Figs. 4 and 5, respectively. Because the MyPJ dataset includes several meteorological variables (such temperature) that might add redundancy to the input representation, the PCA-DRN model is only used to this dataset. On the other hand, since the ISO-NE dataset only contains one meteorological variable—temperature—dimensionality reduction using PCA is not required. For the ISO-NE dataset, the basic DRN framework is thus used without PCA-based feature processing. A fundamental structure and a ResNetPlus module constitute the two primary components of the DRN and PCA-DRN architectures. Historical load, time-related variables, and weather data are among the input elements. These inputs are first processed by the fundamental structure to provide first feature representations of the demand for power. Deep residual learning is then used by the ResNetPlus module, an improved version of the classic ResNet architecture, to further hone the learnt representations and raise the accuracy of 24-hour forecasting.

The original DRN has good predicting abilities, however it only takes into account a small number of meteorological data. Electricity consumption in real-world STLF scenarios is frequently impacted by a variety of meteorological elements that may show significant correlations and redundancies. By adding more meteorological variables and using PCA during data preparation and feature creation, the PCA-DRN expands the original DRN framework in order to overcome this drawback. The two-component residual learning structure is maintained while dimensionality is decreased and unnecessary information is removed.

For day-ahead forecasting, both DRN and PCA-DRN use a number of hour-specific sub-models, each of which is in charge of estimating the load of a particular future hour. To capture short-term inter-hour relationships, the outputs of previous sub-models are repeatedly given back as inputs. The final day-ahead load profile is then created by jointly optimizing the generated 24-hour predictions using the ResNetPlus module. All layers in both models, except for the output layer, employ the Scaled Exponential Linear Unit (SELU) activation function, whereas the output layer uses a linear activation to generate continuous-valued predictions.

As the first component of both the original DRN and PCA-DRN frameworks, the fundamental structure is constructed using a sequence of fully connected (FC) layers to generate initial 24-hour-ahead load forecasts. This component incorporates historical load data from multiple time horizons to capture multi-scale temporal relationships.

To address the limitations associated with using a restricted set of meteorological inputs, the PCA-DRN framework extends the original DRN by incorporating a feature preprocessing strategy based on principal component analysis prior to model training. Within this framework, the first component, corresponding to the fundamental structure, retains the same architectural design as that of the original DRN, while the representation of weather-related information is reformulated. Rather than directly using raw meteorological variables, principal component analysis is employed to project multiple weather features into a lower dimensional feature space, thereby reducing redundancy and preserving the principal components that collectively account for 90% of the cumulative variance. The resulting features obtained from PCA are then used as weather-related inputs to the fundamental structure. As the second and core component of both the DRN and PCA-DRN frameworks, ResNetPlus functions as an enhanced residual learning module built upon the fundamental principles of conventional ResNet architectures, with additional task-specific structural refinements. It is composed of a series of stacked residual layers, each designed to progressively refine the learned feature representations. Each residual layer is constructed using four parallel residual branches, where each branch consists of a nonlinear hidden layer with twenty neurons activated by SELU, followed by a linear projection layer to maintain dimensional consistency for residual aggregation. The outputs of these branches are aggregated through an element-wise addition operation and subsequently combined with the input via a shortcut connection, forming a residual learning structure. By stacking multiple such residual layers, the network forms a deep hierarchical representation that enables iterative refinement of features and improves the modeling of complex nonlinear load patterns. Under the default configuration adopted from the original DRN architecture, 10 residual layers are stacked within the ResNetPlus module, representing the default depth setting defined in the original model. Shortcut connections are embedded throughout the network to facilitate effective gradient propagation and ensure stable optimization during deep model training.

Specifically, it penalizes overestimation of peak load values and underestimation of trough values through deviations in the daily maximum and minimum loads. The adopted composite loss formulation was originally introduced in the DRN framework proposed in27 and is consistently applied in the present study to maintain a unified optimization objective across DRN, PCA-DRN, and all baseline models.

Following the formulation of the loss function, the training process is conducted under a unified framework to ensure fair and consistent comparisons across different model configurations. In this study, the network architecture of both DRN and PCA-DRN is kept unchanged except for the depth parameter, which is treated as the primary variable of investigation. This design enables a controlled analysis of how structural depth influences the learning behavior of DRNs in STLF.

In particular, network depth is defined as the number of stacked residual layers in the second and central component, the ResNetPlus module. By varying the number of these residual layers while preserving all other architectural components, the influence of depth on feature representation capability, optimization stability, and forecasting performance can be systematically evaluated. Shallow network configurations, while limited in their representational capacity, may offer advantages in terms of faster convergence, reduced risk of overfitting, and improved training stability, particularly when the underlying load patterns are relatively simple. On the other hand, deepening the network usually improves the model’s capacity to represent hierarchical temporal dependencies and intricate nonlinear interactions. However, excessively deep structures may introduce optimization challenges, such as gradient degradation, increased computational cost, and reduced generalization performance. Therefore, a systematic exploration of depth is essential to identify a suitable balance between model expressiveness and training stability.

Furthermore, the impact of network depth may vary across datasets with different load characteristics, temporal structures, meteorological representations, and overall data complexity. In datasets such as ISO-NE, which exhibit pronounced seasonal variations and long-term demand fluctuations, pronounced seasonal variations and long-term demand fluctuations introduce more complex temporal patterns. In contrast, the MyPJ dataset exhibits relatively stable annual load profiles with stronger sensitivity to short-term weather variations exhibit relatively stable annual load profiles but stronger sensitivity to short-term weather variations. Evaluating depth under these distinct conditions provides a more comprehensive understanding of its effectiveness and generalizability in diverse real-world STLF scenarios. To comprehensively analyze this effect, multiple depth configurations ranging from shallow to relatively deep structures are considered. This experimental design allows for the observation of performance improvement trends as depth increases, as well as the identification of potential performance degradation associated with excessive model complexity. To ensure a fair assessment, all other model components and training settings are kept consistent across different configurations, such that any variation in forecasting performance can be attributed solely to differences in network depth.

Experimental design

Real-world power load datasets from ISO-NE and MyPJ are used in this research. The ISO-NE dataset produced a total of 24,888 training samples and 8760 testing samples. The model was trained using observations from March 2003 to December 2005, with data from 2006 set aside for testing. The model was trained using observations from January 2020 to December 2021 for the Malaysian MyPJ dataset, while data from 2022 was kept aside for testing. This resulted in a total of 17,544 training samples and 8760 testing samples. The datasets offer a strong basis for assessing forecasting efficacy in both temperate and tropical climates and encompass a range of load changes brought on by weather influences.

DRN and PCA-DRN frameworks are implemented based on the default architectural configurations established in previous studies, without introducing any additional structural modifications except for network depth. To systematically evaluate the sensitivity of model performance to network depth, this study focuses on depth as a key structural parameter. Network depth determines the number of stacked residual units within the model, thereby influencing feature representation capability, nonlinear relationship modeling, and the effectiveness of deep information propagation. Shallow networks may struggle to adequately capture complex load variations due to limited feature extraction capacity, whereas excessively deep networks may introduce parameter redundancy, increase training complexity, and lead to performance saturation or degradation. Based on these considerations, a set of representative depth configurations is selected to cover a wide range of modeling scales, including 2, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, and 50. For analytical convenience, these depth settings are categorized into shallow (2 and 5), medium (10, 15, and 20), and deep (30, 40, and 50) configurations. It should be noted that this categorization is intended solely to facilitate the analysis of optimization behavior and performance trends, rather than to define a universal classification of network depth. To ensure fairness and reproducibility, all other experimental settings are kept identical across different depth configurations, including input features, base network structure, loss function, optimizer, learning rate, batch size, and training epochs. Therefore, any observed differences in predictive performance can be primarily attributed to variations in network depth.

To provide a representative benchmark for comparison, this study selects several DL models widely adopted in STLF as baseline methods. These baselines include the reservoir computing-based ESN53, convolution-based models such as CNN and TCN, the recurrent-based LSTM model, and the attention-driven Transformer model, as well as two representative hybrid architectures, namely Transformer–LSTM24 and CNN–LSTM–MMA25, which reflect typical modeling paradigms in STLF tasks. In addition, a structure-comparable non-residual multilayer perceptron (MLP)54 baseline is introduced for the MyPJ dataset to further evaluate whether residual learning provides additional benefits under shallow network configurations. The inclusion of these baseline models aims to evaluate the predictive performance of the DRN and PCA-DRN frameworks under their respective optimal depth configurations, rather than to conduct a comprehensive architectural comparison. All models are trained under the same experimental protocol to ensure a fair comparison. This unified setup enables an objective assessment of the relative predictive performance between ResNet-based models and mainstream DL approaches, and facilitates a clearer understanding of the impact of depth optimization on DRN-based STLF models.

To provide consistency across different approaches, the ESN baseline is implemented with 100 reservoir neurons, a spectral radius of 0.9, and a sparsity level of 0.1, followed by a linear Dense output layer for forecasting. The CNN baseline has a one-dimensional convolution (Conv1D) architecture with 32 filters, a kernel size of 3, Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation, and He normal initialization. The default configuration is followed for all other variables. In order to capture temporal dependencies across various time scales, the TCN model is implemented using stacked Conv1D layers with dilated causal convolutions, where the dilation rates are set to {1, 2, 4, 8}. The Conv1D layers in the TCN use the same number of filters, kernel size, and activation function as the CNN baseline, with all other parameters remaining unchanged. The LSTM baseline has 64 hidden units, and all other architectural and training options are set to default. In order to provide adequate modeling capacity for self-attention mechanisms without limiting it to convolutional filter sizes, the Transformer baseline uses a standard encoder-only architecture, with the embedding dimension set to 64. A feedforward network of size 2048 and a single encoder layer with eight attention heads make up the model; all other elements, including positional encoding and dropout (set to 0.1), are configured by default. The Transformer–LSTM and CNN–LSTM–MMA hybrid baselines are implemented rigorously in compliance with the parameter settings and designs detailed in their original study. For the additional non-residual MLP baseline, the residual shortcut connections and residual aggregation operations in the ResNetPlus module are removed while retaining the same fully connected transformation structure used in each residual branch. Specifically, the MLP baseline consists of a Dense layer with 20 neurons using the SELU activation function and LeCun normal initialization, followed by a linear output layer with 24 neurons. All remaining training settings, including the optimizer, loss function, snapshot ensemble strategy, and input feature configuration, are kept consistent with those of the DRN/PCA-DRN models to ensure a fair comparison.

Based on the composite loss function formulation adopted from the original DRN framework proposed in27 and presented in Eq. (1) to (3), all models, including DRN, PCA-DRN, and the baseline models, are trained under a unified experimental protocol using a mini-batch size of 32 for a total of 700 epochs, including two short-term training phases of 50 epochs each following an initial training phase of 600 epochs. A snapshot ensemble learning approach, which saves model weights at the conclusion of each 50-epoch phase and averages the outputs of multiple snapshots to obtain the final prediction, is consistently applied across all models to reduce overfitting and improve model resilience55. Snapshot averaging not only offers a computationally economical substitute for several separate training runs, but also enhances prediction stability and generalization performance56. This strategy additionally helps mitigate stochastic variation arising during model optimization and training. The Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) optimizer57, which has been extensively used in earlier DRN-based STLF investigations, is used to train all models with its default learning rate.

The observed performance discrepancies are carefully evaluated for statistical significance using a nonparametric bootstrap resampling technique with 10,000 iterations. Compared to the paired Student’s t-test, which assumes normally distributed paired differences, the bootstrap technique provides a more dependable foundation for model comparison since it is distribution-free58. Statistical significance is defined by two complementary criteria. First, if the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the mean performance difference is entirely above zero, the improvement is considered statistically significant; if the interval contains zero, the difference is considered irrelevant. Second, a bootstrap-derived p-value of less than 0.05 indicates statistical significance at the 95% confidence level. Keep in mind that a stated value of p ≈ 0 denotes an extremely small likelihood (often < 0.0001) rather than an exact zero.

In addition to forecasting performance, computational efficiency is also examined in this study to provide a broader assessment of model practicality. For this purpose, the number of trainable parameters and floating-point operations (FLOPs) are reported. These indicators are widely employed in previous studies to reflect model complexity, scalability, and computational cost, which are important considerations for real-world deployment in power system forecasting applications18,59.

TensorFlow 2.10.0 and Keras 2.10.0 served as the DL backends for all testing, which were carried out in a Python 3.8 environment. A Lenovo laptop with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 Ti Laptop GPU (4 GB), 16 GB DDR5 4800 MHz RAM, and an AMD Ryzen 7 6800 H CPU was used for the computations.

Evaluation metrics

This study assesses forecasting performance using a variety of complementary metrics, in keeping with previous DRN-based research on STLF. Of these, MAPE is chosen as the main evaluation criterion because it is easy to understand and widely used in the DRN for STLF literature. It also acts as the foundation for model comparison and further statistical analysis. To provide a more comprehensive assessment, additional error- and correlation-based indicators are also taken into account, including Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Normalized Mean Squared Error (NMSE), the Correlation Coefficient (R), and the Coefficient of Determination (R²).

In particular, MAPE and MAE, which measure average relative and absolute deviations at the sample level, respectively, are regarded as point-forecast error measures. The squared-error measures MSE, RMSE, and NMSE show error dispersion and susceptibility to large forecast mistakes by penalizing greater deviations more severely. From a fitting-consistency standpoint, R and R² supplement error-based metrics by measuring overall agreement and goodness-of-fit between anticipated and observed load profiles.

Equation (4) through (10) use and to represent the actual and predicted values of the -th sample, respectively, while , and stand for the variance of the observed data and the mean values of the corresponding series; represents the total number of samples, and the remaining metrics are denoted similarly. Lower values of MAPE, MAE, MSE, RMSE, and NMSE frequently imply reduced prediction errors and improved generalization, but higher R and R² values indicate stronger correlation and better model fitting.

CNN and Transformer achieve relatively competitive forecasting results among the conventional baseline models, whereas TCN, LSTM, and CNN–LSTM–MMA exhibit comparatively larger forecasting errors. This behavior is likely associated with the relatively stable annual load profiles and weaker long-term seasonal variability observed under tropical climatic conditions.

The improved performance of PCA-DRN can further be attributed to the PCA-based meteorological feature transformation process. By projecting multiple correlated meteorological variables into a compact low-dimensional representation, PCA effectively reduces feature redundancy and improves input compactness. Consequently, the forecasting model can learn more stable feature representations while reducing unnecessary optimization complexity. As a result, the PCA-DRN demonstrates improved robustness and reduced sensitivity to network depth variations under the current experimental setting.

Bootstrap-based significance test

To formally evaluate whether the observed forecasting performance differences are statistically significant, the following hypotheses are considered in the bootstrap-based statistical analysis. The null hypothesis (H0) assumes that there is no statistically significant difference in forecasting performance between the compared models or depth configurations, whereas the alternative hypothesis (H1) assumes that statistically significant performance differences exist between the compared configurations.

To evaluate whether the observed differences in forecasting performance remain distinguishable under the current experimental setting, a nonparametric bootstrap resampling approach is employed. Table 9 summarizes the bootstrap-based statistical comparison results for both the ISO-NE and MyPJ datasets. Each comparison includes the mean and standard deviation (SD) of the MAPE distribution, the mean difference between paired models, the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI), and the associated p-value. In these comparisons, a positive mean difference indicates that the second model in each pair achieves lower forecasting error. The CI represents the range within which the performance difference is expected to lie with a given level of confidence under sample-level resampling, while the p-value reflects the likelihood that the observed difference arises by chance under the null hypothesis. In the present study, the bootstrap analysis is intended to evaluate performance differences under sample-level variability within the current experimental setting rather than variability arising from repeated independent random initializations.

Discussion

The experimental results across the ISO-NE and MyPJ datasets reveal that network depth plays a critical yet highly dataset-dependent role in determining the performance of DRNs for STLF. Rather than exhibiting a monotonic relationship, forecasting accuracy varies nonlinearly with depth, indicating the existence of an optimal structural configuration that balances representational capacity and optimization stability.

For the ISO-NE dataset, characterized by pronounced seasonal variations and complex long-term dependencies, medium-depth configurations generally exhibit favorable performance characteristics. The model’s capacity to represent hierarchical temporal patterns, including both short-term fluctuations and long-term seasonal influences, is improved by increasing depth. However, further increasing the depth beyond the optimal range does not yield additional benefits and may introduce marginal performance degradation. This suggests that while residual connections effectively alleviate gradient-related issues, excessively deep architectures can still lead to optimization inefficiencies and redundant feature representations.

In contrast, the MyPJ dataset exhibits fundamentally different behavior, which may be associated with its relatively stable load patterns, reduced seasonal variability, and distinct input feature characteristics. The relatively stable load patterns and reduced seasonal variability limit the need for deep hierarchical feature extraction. As a result, shallow network configurations appear sufficient to capture the dominant load patterns in the MyPJ dataset, while deeper architectures introduce unnecessary complexity and increase the risk of overfitting. This observation highlights that the effectiveness of network depth is strongly influenced by the intrinsic structure and variability of the underlying load data.

The incorporation of PCA-based feature transformation further provides important insights into the interaction between input representation and network depth. By projecting meteorological variables into a compact and orthogonal feature space, PCA reduces feature redundancy and enhances the efficiency of information representation. This transformation enables the model to extract meaningful patterns with fewer residual layers, thereby improving robustness to depth variations. Consequently, the PCA-DRN consistently outperforms the original DRN across most configurations, particularly in the MyPJ dataset where multiple correlated meteorological variables are present.

The benchmarking results against mainstream DL models further demonstrate that residual learning-based architectures can achieve superior or competitive performance without requiring excessively complex hybrid structures. While hybrid models such as Transformer–LSTM benefit from combining multiple modeling mechanisms, their increased architectural complexity does not necessarily translate into consistent performance gains. In contrast, the DRN and PCA-DRN frameworks achieve strong performance through a more structurally efficient design, highlighting the effectiveness of residual connections in enabling deep yet stable learning.

In addition to forecasting accuracy, the computational complexity analysis further reveals important trade-offs between model performance and training efficiency across different forecasting architectures. Transformer-based hybrid models achieve relatively competitive forecasting capability but require substantially larger parameter sizes, FLOPs, and training time. In contrast, the DRN and PCA-DRN frameworks maintain comparatively moderate computational complexity while consistently achieving superior overall forecasting performance. This suggests that residual learning can provide a more computationally efficient alternative to highly complex hybrid architectures for STLF tasks. Furthermore, the comparison between the MLP baseline and residual-based frameworks indicates that the observed performance improvements are not solely associated with increased model capacity, but are also closely related to the residual learning mechanism itself, which improves optimization stability and feature refinement even under shallow configurations.

From a statistical perspective, the bootstrap-based significance tests confirm that the observed performance differences are not attributable to random variation but reflect genuine improvements in model capability. The consistent statistical significance across multiple comparisons strengthens the reliability of the experimental findings and provides additional support for the conclusions regarding optimal depth selection and the benefits of PCA-based feature transformation.

Collectively, this study suggests that optimal model design in STLF should not rely solely on increasing architectural complexity or depth, but rather on achieving a balanced integration of network structure, feature representation, and dataset characteristics. The findings further highlight that ResNets provide a flexible and robust framework capable of adapting to diverse climatic conditions, while the optimal depth configuration remains inherently dependent on the complexity of load patterns and input features. It should be noted that the present study adopted a snapshot ensemble learning strategy to improve prediction stability and mitigate stochastic variation during model optimization and training. While the reported results provide consistent performance trends under the current experimental setting, future work may further investigate the robustness of the observed depth-performance relationships through repeated experiments with different random initializations, multi-seed evaluation strategies, and broader statistical comparison protocols across different datasets60,61.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the impact of network depth on the performance of DRNs for STLF under different climatic conditions. The link between network depth, forecasting accuracy, and model stability was thoroughly examined through extensive experiments on two real-world datasets: ISO-NE (temperate area) and MyPJ (tropical region).

The results reveal that network depth has a significant but highly dataset-dependent influence on forecasting performance. For the ISO-NE dataset, characterized by complex seasonal variations and long-term dependencies, medium-depth configurations provide the best balance between representational capacity and optimization stability, leading to superior forecasting accuracy. In contrast, shallow network designs are adequate to achieve optimal performance for the MyPJ dataset, where load patterns are rather stable and more impacted by short-term weather conditions, but deeper architectures add needless complexity and may reduce accuracy.

Furthermore, the incorporation of PCA-based feature transformation in the PCA-DRN framework demonstrates clear advantages in handling multiple meteorological variables. By reducing feature redundancy and enhancing input representation, PCA improves both forecasting accuracy and model robustness, particularly in the tropical dataset. The comparative results against mainstream DL models further confirm that residual learning-based architectures can achieve competitive or superior performance without relying on overly complex hybrid structures. In addition, the computational complexity analysis indicates that DRN-based frameworks can achieve strong forecasting performance while maintaining comparatively moderate parameter size and computational cost relative to Transformer-based hybrid architectures, highlighting the effectiveness of residual learning in balancing forecasting accuracy and structural efficiency.

The dependability of the experimental results is further supported by the bootstrap-based statistical analysis, which offers compelling evidence that the observed performance differences are statistically significant. This highlights the importance of combining empirical evaluation with statistical validation in assessing DL models for STLF.

The findings of this study demonstrate that optimal model design for STLF should not be based solely on increasing network depth or architectural complexity. Instead, it requires a careful balance between network structure, feature representation, and dataset characteristics. The results also suggest that ResNet-based frameworks offer strong adaptability across different climatic conditions, while the optimal depth configuration remains inherently dependent on the complexity of the underlying load patterns. It should be noted that the PCA-DRN framework was evaluated only on the MyPJ dataset due to the limited meteorological feature dimensionality of the ISO-NE dataset. Therefore, the generalizability of the observed PCA-related improvements to other multivariate STLF datasets remains an important direction for future investigation.

Future research will concentrate on expanding the suggested framework to more adaptive and realistic forecasting scenarios. This includes incorporating probabilistic forecasting to quantify uncertainty, integrating additional exogenous variables such as renewable energy sources to better reflect complex load dynamics, and developing adaptive or data-driven depth optimization strategies that enable the model to adjust its structure according to varying dataset characteristics. Future studies may also investigate the proposed framework using more recent post-2020 temperate-region datasets to further evaluate model adaptability under evolving load characteristics associated with post-COVID consumption behavior, increasing electric vehicle penetration, and renewable energy integration. In addition, future work may further investigate layer-wise gradient propagation behavior, gradient stability characteristics, and optimization dynamics in DRN models to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how residual connections influence effective gradient flow and training behavior across different depth configurations.
