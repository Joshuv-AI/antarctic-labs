Patterns of Glaucoma Treatment Use in Asian American Patients With Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma
Authors: Kyle J Chen, Pranav Vasu, Ryan Margolis, Syril K Dorairaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo characterize racial disparities in glaucoma management by evaluating treatment modality utilization patterns between Asian American and non-Asian American patients with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG).Patients and MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, a large-scale, multi-institutional electronic medical record database, with data from January 1, 2004, to June 16, 2025. Asian American and non-Asian American patients with POAG were identified via ICD-10 codes and stratified by disease severity (mild-to-moderate vs severe). Cohorts were balanced using 1:1 propensity score matching for demographic characteristics, systemic comorbidities, family history, and smoking status. The primary outcome was the relative utilization of topical medications, laser procedures, traditional surgery, and microinvasive glaucoma surgery.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, the study evaluated 12,526 patients with mild-to-moderate POAG (6263 per group) and 3410 patients with severe POAG (1705 per group). In the mild-to-moderate cohort, Asian Americans had a higher likelihood of receiving topical medications (odds ratio [OR], 1.041; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.016-1.068). Conversely, Asian Americans with severe POAG were less likely to receive topical medications (OR, 0.950; 95% CI, 0.906-0.996) or laser procedures (OR, 0.769; 95% CI, 0.630-0.939). No significant differences were observed in surgical or microinvasive glaucoma surgery utilization.ConclusionSignificant differences exist in POAG treatment utilization for Asian Americans, particularly a relative underutilization of adjunctive medical and laser therapies in severe disease. These findings suggest that current practice patterns may diverge from traditional treatment algorithms for this rapidly growing demographic, highlighting a need for increased awareness of potential systemic or provider-level barriers to equitable care.

Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, currently affecting over 76 million individuals and projected to exceed 110 million by 2040.1 Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), the most prevalent glaucoma subtype, affects over 2 million Americans and accounts for approximately 74% of all glaucoma cases in the United States.2 The pathophysiology of POAG is characterized by a progressive optic neuropathy due to elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) in the setting of an open drainage angle between the cornea and iris, resulting in visual field defects.3 As POAG often remains asymptomatic until more advanced stages, timely detection and effective long-term management are particularly important to ensure the delivery of equitable care across diverse patient populations.

Management of POAG focuses on reducing IOP, the principal and only modifiable risk factor, through 4 primary modalities.4 The most common first-line therapy is topical medications, which include prostaglandin analogs, β blockers, α agonists, and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors. While effective and non-invasive, they are associated with poor adherence, variability in application technique, ocular surface disease, and systemic side effects.5,6 Laser procedures, particularly selective laser trabeculoplasty, are another first-line or adjunctive treatment for POAG, with studies demonstrating similar outcomes to pharmacotherapy for IOP control but with a reduction in the long-term need for medications.7 Microinvasive glaucoma surgeries (MIGS) are an emerging category of intervention suitable for mild-to-moderate POAG that offer faster operation times, shorter recovery times, and relative procedural simplicity.8 Traditional surgeries are also utilized for POAG for more significant IOP reduction but are typically reserved for advanced cases due to risk of hypotony, bleb-related complications, and lengthy recovery times.8 Over the past decade, the introduction of selective laser trabeculoplasty and MIGS has expanded the treatment options for POAG and shifted practice patterns toward earlier, less invasive interventions. Despite these advancements, however, access to newer treatment modalities is not uniform, with disparities in utilization often driven by a complex interplay of socioeconomic status, insurance coverage, and racial or ethnic background.

Racial and ethnic differences in the epidemiology of glaucoma have been documented in the literature, with Asians constituting almost 50% of the total worldwide cases of all types of glaucoma and thus being the largest group affected.9 Among Americans, racial and ethnic minority groups tend to be disproportionately affected, where both African Americans and Hispanic Americans have a higher prevalence of glaucoma compared with non-Hispanic Caucasians, with the former being approximately twice as likely.10 However, among racial disparity studies for glaucoma, the specific health care needs and treatment patterns of Asian Americans remain critically understudied, despite this racial group being the fastest growing group in the United States and the considerable prevalence of glaucoma among Asians worldwide.11,12 This demographic characteristic faces unique barriers to equitable care, including language discordance, limited health literacy, immigration status, cultural beliefs, and lower rates of insurance coverage.13,14 Asian Americans are also less likely to receive routine eye care compared with other demographic characteristics, despite increased disease burden for certain ophthalmological conditions such as POAG.15 Epidemiological data also suggest that Asian Americans are at a considerably higher risk for developing POAG than non-Hispanic Caucasian individuals (hazard ratio, 1.51; 95% CI 1.42-1.60).16 This risk may be compounded by anatomic differences among Asian American ethnicities, further contributing to diagnostic complexity and under-recognition of POAG.17 Despite this increased burden, this population remains underrepresented in landmark glaucoma clinical trials, leaving a considerable gap in our understanding of real-world treatment utilization.18

Given these differences in epidemiology, anatomy, and access, understanding how POAG is managed across racial and ethnic groups is critical. It remains unknown whether the utilization of medical, laser, or surgical treatment modalities for POAG differs for Asian Americans compared with the broader US population. Utilizing a large-scale, multi-institutional electronic medical record database, this study aims to characterize and compare the use of topical medications, laser procedures, traditional surgeries, and MIGS among Asian American and non-Asian American cohorts.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

A retrospective cohort study was conducted using the TriNetX database (TriNetX), which included deidentified electronic health records of over 117 million patients in the United States from across 68 health care organizations. As patient data is deidentified, this retrospective study is exempt from requiring informed consent. The data analyzed is a secondary analysis of existing, deidentified data, without any intervention or interaction with human subjects. The deidentification complies with the standards outlined in section §164.514(a) of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule. A qualified expert, as specified in section §164.514(b)(1) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule, has formally certified the deidentification process. This certification was updated in December 2020. Because the database contains deidentified patient data, institutional review board approval was not required.

The data used for this study were collected on June 16, 2025, from the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, which included electronic health records from over 75 million patients and 68 health care organizations at the time. Patients from 55 of these organizations met the query for this study and were included in the analysis. Electronic health records used in the study ranged from January 1, 2004, to June 16, 2025.

Selection Criteria

Patients with a diagnosis of unilateral or bilateral mild, moderate, or severe stage POAG were identified by International Classification of Disease, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes (H40.1111, H40.1112, H40.1121, H40.1122, H40.1131, H40.1191, H40.1192, H40.1113, H40.1123, H40.1133, and H40.1193), as tabulated in Table 1. The date each patient first received any of these ICD-10 codes served as the index event, from which the date of the primary outcomes was compared relative to. Within mild-to-moderate and severe stage POAG groups, patients were sorted by race into either Asian American or non-Asian American groups. Basic demographic information, including age at index and sex were collected.

Medical treatments were defined as pharmacologic therapies, typically topical eyedrops, administered to manage IOP in POAG. These treatments were identified using RxNorm codes and included prostaglandin analogs (RxNorm codes 43611, 283810, 283809, 1244607, 1546408, and 1988390), β blockers (RxNorm codes 10600, 1520, 1813, and 2116), α agonists (RxNorm codes 134615 and 14845), and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (RxNorm codes 60207, 194881, and 6826). Current Procedural Terminology codes were utilized to identify laser procedures (65855, 66762, 66710, and 66711), traditional surgical procedures (66170, 66172, 66174, 66175, 66179, 66180, 66183, 66984, and 66982), and MIGS (65820, 0191T, 0376T, 0449T, 0450T, 0474T, 66989, 66991, and 0671T). Patients who received concurrent treatment modalities were classified based on the presence of all indexed treatments documented in their electronic medical records.

Univariate analyses were performed to study the associations among mild, moderate, and severe patients with POAG with self-identified race and frequency of utilization for each treatment category. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed, controlling for age at index, sex, weight, body mass index, family history of glaucoma, smoking status, hypertension status, and diabetes status. Race, ethnicity, and sex were determined by their designation within the database.

Statistical Analyses and PSM

All statistical analyses were conducted natively within the TriNetX platform in a similar fashion to that in existing publications using the TriNetX database.19,20 Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± SD or number (%). To minimize selection bias and confounding, PSM was performed using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor greedy matching algorithm with a caliper of 0.25 SD. The cohorts were matched for baseline demographical information, including age at index, sex, and tobacco smoking status, as well as diagnoses identified by ICD-10 codes at the time of POAG diagnosis, including overweight and obesity status (E66), diabetes mellitus (E08-E13), essential hypertension (H40.0), glaucoma suspects (H40.0), and family history of eye and ear disorders (I10). The ICD-10 code for glaucoma suspect includes subcodes (H40.00-H40.06) based on anatomical condition and risk level for glaucoma. Risk factors used to stratify risk for glaucoma suspect include African American or Hispanic race, family history of glaucoma in a first-degree relative, thin central corneal thickness, high IOP, and pseudo-exfoliation or pigment dispersion syndrome. All ICD-10 codes used for PSM are documented in Supplemental Table 1, available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org. Matching quality was assessed with standardized mean differences (SMDs), a common metric in observational studies used to quantify differences between treatment and control groups. Values less than 0.1 indicated an adequate balance between cohorts.

Primary outcomes were initiation of 1 or more treatment types within 1 year of mild, moderate, or severe POAG diagnosis. Inter-cohort comparisons of primary outcomes after PSM were made using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical tests were two-sided, and P<.05 was considered statistically significant.

Conversely, in severe POAG, our analysis reported that Asian Americans were less likely to receive topical eyedrops than non-Asian Americans, with 1111 of 1,705 patients in the Asian American cohort receiving topical eyedrops compared with 1169 of 1705 patients in the non-Asian American cohort (OR 0.950; 95% CI, 0.906-0.996). The Asian American cohort was also found to receive laser procedures less often, with 153 of 1705 patients in the Asian American cohort receiving laser procedures compared with 199 of 1705 patients in the non-Asian American cohort (OR 0.769; 95% CI 0.630-0.939). There were no differences in utilization of traditional surgery and MIGS between Asian American and non-Asian American patients for both mild-to-moderate and severe POAG.

Discussion

Racial and ethnic minorities in the United States consistently experience a greater incidence of visual impairment and blindness.21, 22, 23 However, the vast majority of these epidemiological studies focus on Hispanic and African American populations, leaving a significant data gap regarding Asian Americans—the nation’s fastest growing racial group.11 Prior studies, such as the Chinese American Eye Study, provided foundational insights but were geographically limited to specific municipalities and ethnicities.24 Furthermore, while there is a wide body of research on visual impairment in countries with predominantly Asian populations, these studies may not be representative of the unique health care challenges Asian Americans face regarding vision care. Glaucoma, in particular, is one ocular condition shown to be more prevalent in Asian Americans than non-Hispanic Caucasians.16 Given the treatable nature of glaucoma and irreversible visual impairment if untreated, early detection and appropriate management are crucial.25,26 Thus, this research study aims to fill the gap in research regarding glaucoma treatment in an underrepresented and at-risk population.

The TriNetX US Collaborative Network, with its vast database of information about patient demographics, diagnoses, medications, and procedures in a conglomerate patient population based in the United States, was well-suited for this study. Using this database, we could assess a large cohort of Asian Americans across the United States diagnosed with POAG and identify trends and patterns in their disease management. Treatment typically includes a combination of various treatment approaches depending on POAG severity—monotherapy with topical medications may initially be sufficient to treat mild POAG, whereas invasive procedures are generally reserved only for severe POAG.27 Because of differences in treatment approaches for different stages of POAG, this study investigated differences in treatment utilization for mild-to-moderate and severe POAG separately.

PSM was implemented to reduce confounding variables, such as risk factors and predisposition for glaucoma. Age, sex, obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and family history of glaucoma have all been well-established in the literature as risk factors for POAG, and thus, these variables were utilized for PSM.28, 29, 30 Table 2 and Table 3 reported a decrease in the statistical differences in these characteristics post-match for mild-moderate POAG and severe POAG cohorts, respectively. After propensity matching demographic characteristics and the aforementioned comorbidities, our findings reveal an important severity-dependent shift in how POAG is managed and treated in Asian American patients.

In mild-to-moderate POAG, Asian Americans were more likely to receive topical medications than matched non-Asian American counterparts, whereas all other treatment modalities had a comparable frequency of utilization, as shown in Table 4. This preference for pharmacotherapy may reflect the high accessibility and non-invasive nature of eyedrops, which aligns with documented trends in Asian American health care utilization, where patients may lack a consistent source of care or access to routine medical services, even after controlling for socioeconomic and cultural disparities.31, 32, 33 Socioeconomic factors faced by Asian Americans, including variations in vision and health insurance and cultural preferences, may also lead to a stronger baseline preference for a less resource-intensive, non-surgical approach such as topical medications.

Intriguingly, while Asian Americans with mild-to-moderate POAG utilized more topical medications, their utilization of procedural approaches—laser procedures, traditional surgeries, and MIGS—remained comparable to non-Asian American patients. Thus, these approaches are not necessarily competing factors, but rather complementary, where pharmacological approaches are more often used in combination with procedural approaches in Asian Americans compared with non-Asian Americans. In other words, despite factors that may make topical medications preferable initially in Asian Americans, a similar proportion of Asian American patients with mild-moderate POAG ultimately end up receiving treatment involving more invasive modalities.

This progression suggests either insufficient decrease in IOP in Asian Americans with topical medications or shifts in patient preferences with disease progression. Clinical trials do not report a consistent difference in the efficacy of topical medications between Asian Americans and other demographics,34,35 but these studies may not be fully reflective of topical medication usage in practice due to differences in patient adherence or application technique—considerations that can be exacerbated by socioeconomic factors and subsequently impact the extent of IOP reduction from these medications.5,27 Alternatively, Asian American patients could already be at a point where surgery is preferable, but may initially prefer topical medications more often than their non-Asian American counterparts due to a combination of socioeconomic factors or cultural beliefs, despite ultimately utilizing procedural approaches for long-term management.

In contrast, Asian Americans with severe POAG appeared to receive topical medications and laser treatments less often than their non-Asian American counterparts, whereas traditional surgery and MIGS had comparable levels of utilization. This pattern indicates a difference in clinician prescribing philosophies and treatment sequencing, where topical medications and laser procedures are not as commonly used in tandem with traditional surgery and MIGS for Asian Americans. Some literature suggests that cultural beliefs, such as fatalism regarding chronic disease, may lead patients to favor definitive surgical outcomes over complex, multi-drug regimens.36 For severe POAG treatment, this would translate as receiving definitive treatment without concurrent eyedrop usage. Furthermore, anatomical considerations, such as differences in trabecular meshwork pigmentation or lower baseline IOP in certain Asian phenotypes, may influence clinicians’ perceived efficacy of laser trabeculoplasty.37 Finally, the similar rates of surgical and MIGS utilization in severe POAG indicate that Asian American patients are not under-treated when compared with non-Asian Americans when these treatment modalities are clinically indicated, suggesting either comparable access or willingness to undergo more advanced care once POAG reaches the severe stage.

A primary strength of this study is the breadth of the patient population analyzed. Although Asian Americans with POAG represent a relatively small fraction of the millions of patients within the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, the resulting cohort of several thousand propensity-matched individuals significantly exceeds the sample sizes of prior ophthalmic research focused on this demographic. Consequently, this study provides a more geographically and institutionally diverse representation of Asian American health care utilization than previously possible. Furthermore, the rigorous application of PSM allowed us to account for key systemic confounders—such as age, comorbidities, and family history—to facilitate a statistically meaningful comparison between Asian American and non-Asian American cohorts.

Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective electronic medical record-based study, these findings identify significant associations in utilization but cannot definitively assign causality to specific social determinants of health or individual provider-level preferences. Second, the Asian American population was analyzed collectively in our study, as the available data did not allow for disaggregation into specific ethnicities, such as Chinese Americans or Indian Americans. As such, ethnicity-specific treatment patterns and cultural health beliefs could not be studied. Third, the database lacks granular clinical parameters such as specific IOP measurements or visual field mean deviation, which would provide deeper context for treatment escalations. Finally, the TriNetX database does not include socioeconomic variables such as income level or insurance type that could be confounding variables in this study. As such, future studies on this topic will explore other deidentified medical databases that offer comparable sample sizes to TriNetX while allowing for analyses pertaining to socioeconomic status.

Conclusion

This study identifies distinct glaucoma treatment trajectories for Asian Americans, characterized by an increased reliance on pharmacotherapy in early-stage disease and a relative underutilization of adjunctive medical and laser therapies in advanced stages when compared with non-Asian Americans. These findings suggest that current management paradigms may not fully account for the unique socioeconomics and cultural contexts of this rapidly growing demographic. Further investigation is required to determine how provider perceptions, patient adherence and beliefs, and health system barriers shape POAG treatment in Asian Americans.

The results serve as a critical foundation for optimizing equitable glaucoma care. To build upon these findings, the next phase of research must move beyond treating Asian Americans as a monolithic group, instead utilizing disaggregated data to identify ethnicity-specific clinical needs. Furthermore, the integration of socioeconomic factors, such as income level and insurance status, and longitudinal markers, such as IOP trends and visual field progression, will be essential to contextualizing real-world utilization patterns. Similarly, direct multi-cohort comparisons between Asian Americans and other specific racial and ethnic demographics, such as African American or Hispanic American populations, would help determine the relative extent of utilization gaps in glaucoma treatment. Additionally, cross-national investigations comparing domestic database cohorts to international registries could elucidate how geographic, environmental, and distinct systemic health care infrastructures alter these clinical pathways. Ultimately, these efforts are necessary to ensure that therapeutic advancements in glaucoma are distributed inclusively across a diverse and expanding patient population in the United States.
