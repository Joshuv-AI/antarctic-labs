Genomic and bioreactor evaluation of newly isolated cellulase-producing bacteria
Authors: Ida Romano, Marisa Amato, Lucia Amoroso, Paolo Dessì, Irene Giordano, Olimpia Pepe, Valeria Ventorino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cellulose is a renewable resource with broad biotechnological potential, and its hydrolysis by cellulases underpins applications such as biofuel production and biowaste valorization. This study combined isolation, screening, molecular identification, enzyme assays, genome sequencing, and bioreactor evaluation to characterize novel cellulase-producing bacteria. Preliminary screening identified and selected bacterial isolates by hydrolysis halo formation, with strains showing halos ≥ 20 mm selected for further quantitative assays. Streptomyces olivaceus C1_7A, Bacillus thuringiensis FBB7BB, and Bacillus licheniformis strains AT081C and AT082C showed the highest endoglucanase activities (from 0.11 to 0.26 U mL−1). Further testing on Arundo donax and Avicel led to the selection of B. thuringiensis FBB7BB and S. olivaceus C1_7A as the most promising strains for biotechnological application. Whole genome sequencing confirmed the presence of genes involved in polysaccharide degradation. Finally, lab-scale fermentation trials using 1–2% CMC showed that B. thuringiensis FBB7BB reached a maximum endo-1,4-β-glucanase activity of 0.30 ± 0.04 U mL−1 after 8 h at 37 °C, whereas S. olivaceus C1_7A achieved 0.36 ± 0.03 U mL−1 after 216 h at 30 °C.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57974-5.

Introduction

Over the past 200 years, fossil fuels have served as the primary energy source and chemical industry feedstock worldwide1. However, their depletion and associated environmental pollution call for more affordable, sustainable, and renewable alternatives1. In this context, biotechnological waste valorization is key to the transition toward green energy and bio-based chemicals.

Lignocellulosic biomass, including agricultural residues and forestry waste, represents a major renewable resource, with an estimated annual production of 1 × 1010 tons2. Its main component, cellulose -the most abundant organic polymer on Earth- is a structural element of the cell walls of green plants, algae, and oomycetes3. Several studies focused on cellulose valorization from energy crops such as Miscanthus, Panicum virgatum, Arundo donax, Populus nigra, and Eucalyptus camaldulensis, which can thrive on marginal lands unsuitable for food crops4,5. These crops offer an alternative to fossil fuels for the production of bio-based products6.

Cellulose is insoluble and highly resistant to enzymatic hydrolysis. Certain microorganisms, known as cellulolytic, can synthesize a broad range of multi-functional cellulolytic enzymes (cellulases) to break down cellulose into oligosaccharides called cellodextrins, or completely into glucose units3. Such microorganisms are widely distributed across diverse habitats, including extreme environments, soil, rumen, insect guts, marine and saline systems, sludge, bioreactors, compost, and hot springs2,7. These cellulolytic microorganisms are exceptionally diverse due to their functional and genetic adaptations2.

Cellulases are industrially significant enzymes, accounting for approximately 20% of the global enzyme market8. Their market demand is expected to rise soon, driven by the increasing interest in sustainable biofuel production8. As a result, intensification of cellulase production became a key research and development area.

Both fungi and bacteria have been exploited for their abilities to produce a wide variety of cellulases. Most emphasis has been placed on fungi, which are generally preferred over bacteria for cellulase production, given their ability to utilize a wide range of substrates and their superior penetration capabilities8, along with their production of notable amounts of cellulases which are secreted to the medium for easy extraction and purification9. However, few fungal strains are able to express all three cellulases necessary for efficient biomass hydrolysis: endoglucanase (endo-1, 4-β-D-glucanase, EG, EC 3.2.1.4), cellobiohydrolase or exoglucanase (exo-1, 4-β-D-glucanase, CBH, EC 3.2.1.91), and β-glucosidase (1, 4-β-D-glucosidase, BG, EC 3.2.1.21)10. Consequently, commercial cellulase production often relies on a consortium of fungal strains that collectively produce these three enzymes. This results in a cost-intensive process, primarily due to low end-product concentrations8. Although fungal cellulase production remains widely adopted, cellulase-producing bacteria have attracted increasing attention because of their strong adaptability and advantages for industrial applications. In fact, fungi present several limitations that have driven a shift toward cellulolytic bacteria, which generally exhibit: (1) higher growth rates; (2) glycoside hydrolases often organized into complex multi-enzyme systems, resulting in enhanced functionality and synergistic activity; and (3) the ability to colonize a broad range of environmental and industrial niches, enabling the development of cellulolytic strains with remarkable resistance to environmental stresses9,11. Moreover, bacterial enzymes often show high stability under harsh industrial conditions under extreme conditions such as high temperature, pH, or salinity. These characteristics make bacteria particularly attractive for industrial applications requiring robust and flexible biocatalysts7,10,12. Bacterial species including Thermomonospora fusca and several Bacillus strains are recognized for their high enzyme productions10. Cellulolytic activity was also observed in bacterial genera such as Pseudomonas, Cellulomonas, Streptomyces, and Actinomucor10. Although several cellulolytic microorganisms are well known and exhibit high enzymatic efficiency, substrate versatility, and stability, exploring novel bacterial isolates from diverse ecological niches remains crucial to reveal unexploited cellulase systems with enhanced catalytic performance and biotechnological potential.

For these reasons, increasing attention has been directed toward the exploration of novel bacterial cellulolytic strains, especially from underexplored ecological niches such as chestnut compost and bamboo forest undergrowth, in order to identify new enzymatic systems with enhanced catalytic performance and biotechnological potential. Chestnut was demonstrated to possess a lignocellulosic-adapted microbiota13, whereas bamboo forest litterfall has a highly specialized microbiota, which led to a faster decomposition than broadleaf forests14. Based on these considerations, this study aimed to identify and select promising bacterial strains for the development of efficient and scalable cellulase production systems.

The classical method for isolating cellulolytic bacteria involves selective media containing carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) as the sole carbon source for semi-quantitative screening on stained agar plates, followed by quantitative enzymatic assays7. Although widely used and validated, these methods rely on artificial soluble cellulose derivatives and may therefore identify “false positives”, namely strains capable of hydrolyzing amorphous substrates such as CMC but unable to efficiently degrade natural cellulose. As a result, these strains may appear cellulolytic in plate-based assays but fail to efficiently deconstruct lignocellulosic biomass under realistic conditions15. Consequently, CMC-based screening alone may overestimate cellulolytic potential. To address this limitation, genomic approaches can be integrated to identify genes encoding a broader spectrum of cellulolytic enzymes (e.g., endoglucanases, exoglucanases, and β-glucosidases), providing deeper insight into the actual degradation capabilities and regulatory mechanisms of the selected strains16.

This work aims to detect and isolate novel promising cellulolytic strains by using an integrated polyphasic approach. First, classical semi-quantitative and quantitative methods were employed to isolate bacterial strains that potentially produce cellulolytic enzymes. Then, the most interesting isolates were evaluated for their multi-enzymatic traits. To overcome the limitation of “false positives”, selected strains were tested on alternative cellulose sources. Furthermore, they were studied and compared in-depth through genome sequencing to link functional activity with genetic potential. Finally, considering the importance of scaling these processes to an industrial level, laboratory-scale cultures were performed to evaluate enzyme production at different CMC concentrations, providing a first assessment of process-level applicability.

Materials and methods

Preliminary screening of new cellulolytic bacteria

Isolation of cellulolytic bacteria from different sources

Potential cellulolytic microorganisms were isolated from mature chestnut compost and bamboo forest undergrowth (soil and plant biomass, 5–10 cm depth). Representative compost samples of 1 kg were taken from the external (about 5–10 cm depth) and internal part (at about 40 cm depth) of the compost pile (Campania region). Bamboo forest undergrowth samples were taken at 0–5 cm depth. Microbial isolates were obtained in solid media as described by Hankin and Anagnostakis17, with some modifications. Initial compost and undergrowth suspensions were prepared by adding 20 g (w/v) of sample to 180 mL of quarter-strength Ringer’s solution (Oxoid, Ltd, Oxford, UK) in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. After shaking, suitable dilutions were made in the same solution, which were used to inoculate solid growth media composed by 5 g L−1 carboxymethylcellulose (CMC; Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Steinheim, Germany), 1 g L−1 (NH4)NO3, 1 g L−1 yeast extract, 50 mL L−1 standard salt solution (K2HPO4 5.00 g L−1; MgSO4 2.50 g L−1; NaCl 2.50 g L−1; Fe2 (SO4)3 0.05 g L−1; MnSO4 0.05 g L−1), 1 mL L−1 trace elements solution (K2MoO4·5H2O 0.05 g L−1; Na2B4O₇·10H2O 0.05 g L−1; Co(NO3)2·6H2O 0.05 g L−1; MnSO4 0.05 g L−1; CdSO4 0.05 g L−1; ZnSO4·7H2O 0.05 g L−1; CuSO4·H2O 0.05 g L−1; FeCl3 0.10 g L−1), and 15 g L−1 bacteriological agar, at pH 7.0. The plates were incubated at 28 °C for 7 days. Afterwards, CMC-degrading colonies were detected by flooding the plates with filter-sterilized 0.1% (0.2 µm filtration prior application, to minimize contamination risks) Congo red solution for 30 min, followed by washing with 5 M NaCl18. All strains showing a clear halo around the colonies were considered positive; they were purified, isolated on CMC agar plates, and stored at 4 °C as slant cultures until their characterization.

Qualitative and semi-quantitative screening on solid media

To characterize enzyme-producing strains, a total of 48 isolates were subjected to a semi-quantitative screening. They included 24 isolates obtained from chestnut compost and 11 from bamboo biomass. Additionally, to expand the pool of potential enzyme producers, 13 isolates out of 366 previously obtained from different lignocellulose biomasses4, and belonging to the internal collection of the Department of Agricultural Sciences, were reactivated and analyzed. An evaluation of the endo-cellulase production was carried out by the agar spot method. Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to a turbidity corresponding to the 0.5 McFarland standard (approximately 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU mL−1) using Ringer’s solution (Sigma-Aldrich). The adjusted suspensions were then spotted onto agar medium in triplicate and incubated at 28 °C for four days. Following incubation, the plates were stained with 0.1% congo red solution as previously described. Halo diameters (mm) were recorded for primary screening. This was calculated as the difference between the degradation halo and the colony diameters on the CMC agar plates after staining. All isolates were tested in triplicate.

Phenotypic characterization of bacterial isolates

Morphological analysis of colonies of each bacterial strain was carried out, observing color, form (circular/irregular/rhizoid/filamentous), elevation (flat/raised/umbonate/convex/raised), and margin of colony (lobate/undulate/entire/filiform). The presence of the enzyme catalase was assessed by evaluating the immediate appearance of effervescence after dissolving a single colony in 3% hydrogen peroxide19. Gram-positive and Gram-negative microorganisms were distinguished by the KOH test as described by Halebian et al.20. Finally, cellular morphology was determined by observation in the optical microscope (Eclipse E200, Nikon).

Identification by 16S rRNA gene partial sequence

All the bacterial isolates were identified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. For this preliminary identification step, total genomic DNA of selected strains was extracted by boiling for 10 min and then used as a template for the PCR assay. The PCR mixture was prepared according to Marín et al.21, using the primers fD1 (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and rD1 (5′-AAGGAGGTGATCCAGCC-3′). The PCR conditions were performed as previously described22. PCR products were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Quiagen, Milan, Italy) according to the supplier’s recommendations, and sequenced by high-quality Sanger sequencing as previously reported23. The DNA sequences were compared to the GenBank nucleotide data library using the BLAST software of the National Center for Biotechnology Information website.

Quantitative endo-1,4-β-D-glucanase screening in batch culture by Azo-CMCase Assay

The 15 identified strains with halo diameters ≥ 20 mm were assessed for endo-1,4-β-D-glucanase production by quantitative assay. The strains were cultivated in 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks with cotton plugs and incubated on a rotary shaker (150 × g). The liquid medium used for endo-1,4-β-D-glucanase production analysis was prepared as described above, adding 1% CMC. Experiment duration and sampling times were determined based on the literature, where optimal production periods were identified as 10–15 h for Bacillus spp. and 5–6 days for Streptomyces spp.5,24. Endo-1,4-β-D-glucanase (cellulase) activity was quantified using the soluble chromogenic substrate Azo-CM-Cellulose (Megazyme, Bray, Ireland; assay kit S-ACMC), following the manufacturer’s standardized protocol. Briefly, the assay mixture consisted of 250 µL of AZO-CMC (0.2% w/v) in sodium acetate buffer (50 mM, pH 5.0) and 250 µL of enzyme and sodium acetate buffer. The mixtures were incubated at 50 °C for 5 min, then the reaction was stopped by cooling on ice for 10 min. Then, 1.25 mL of ethanol (96%) was added to the mixture, and samples were incubated at room temperature for 10 min, prior to centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 590 nm against a blank. Enzyme activity (U mL−1) was determined using a standard curve constructed with known amounts of reference cellulase (Trichoderma sp.) from Megazyme, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative screening was conducted in triplicate for each strain.

Quantitative screening in batch culture with different cellulose sources

The evaluation and quantification of endo-1,4-β-D-glucanase production from alternative cellulose substrates was conducted as previously described, but substituting the CMC (1%) with the same amount of Arundo donax powder or Avicel. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Multi-enzymatic evaluation of selected bacterial strains

Strains able to produce endo-1,4-β-D-glucanase with alternative carbon sources were subjected to an in-depth investigation for their multifunctional enzymatic potential. Bacterial cell suspensions (0.5 of McFarland Turbidity Standard) were spotted in triplicate on selective solid media for the detection of exocellulase, cellobiase, xylanase, pectinase, and ligninolytic activity by qualitative and semi-quantitative agar spot methods as previously described4. Briefly, pectinase activity was recorded as the diameter of the clearing or halo zone/colony using Pectin agar media4. Exocellulase activity was detected by observing the development of bacterial colonies after incubation on Avicel agar. Cellobiase activity was estimated by observing a clear zone around the colonies after incubation on cellobiose agar4. Xylanase activity was determined on Luria–Bertani (LB) agar medium (Oxoid, Milan, Italy) supplemented with 0.05% Remazol brilliant blue-R and a 0.5% solution of sonicated xylan (FALC Instruments, HK3300, Sigma-Aldrich)25. Finally, ligninolytic activity was estimated by cultivating isolates on plates containing the same medium used for endo-1,4-β-D-glucanase analysis, but deprived of CMC and supplemented with lignin (Lignin, alkali, Sigma, USA) (1%). In this case, halos could not be observed, so the presence of ligninolytic activity was evaluated by observing the development of bacterial colonies26. All tests were performed in biological triplicate.

Whole genome sequencing, annotation, and comparative analysis

Two strains capable of utilizing all the cellulose sources tested were selected for whole-genome sequencing. Total genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from pure colonies of S. olivaceus C1_7A and B. thuringiensis FBB7BB strains grown in CMC solid media for 24 h and 3 days, respectively. The pure culture of S. olivaceus C1_7A was frozen prior to DNA extraction to maximize yield. DNA was extracted using an UltraClean Microbial DNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA from B. thuringiensis FBB7BB was extracted according to the procedure described by Marmur27. Library preparation was performed using the Oxford Nanopore Technologies Native Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (SQK-NBD114.24). gDNA quality was assessed via agarose gel electrophoresis and Qubit (dsDNA HS Assay Kit). Sequencing was performed using an Oxford Nanopore GridION and Flow Cell R10 Version (FLO-MIN114). Raw nanopore sequencing reads were assessed for quality. Low-quality and short reads were removed using Filtlong v0.2.128 to retain only high-quality data for downstream analysis. The filtered reads were used for de novo assembly of bacterial genomes using Flye v2.9.329, with parameters optimized for bacterial assemblies. Contig polishing was performed using Medaka v1.830 to correct base-level errors and improve assembly accuracy. Whole-genome nucleotide sequences of S. olivaceus C1_7A and B. thuringiensis FBB7BB were used as input for genome annotation. Genome quality and taxonomic consistency were further evaluated using Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) analysis performed with JSpeciesWS (ANIb). Reference genomes of closely related taxa were selected from public databases, and pairwise comparisons were conducted using default parameters. Genome completeness was further evaluated using BUSCO (Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs) with default parameters on the Galaxy platform, employing the appropriate bacterial lineage dataset. The genome sequence data were uploaded to the Type (Strain) Genome Server (TYGS; https://tygs.dsmz.de), for a phylogenomic analysis based on whole-genome similarity to support the taxonomic assignment of B. thuringiensis FBB7BB and S. olivaceus C1_7A. Gene prediction and functional annotation were performed using Prokka within the Galaxy platform, starting from the genomic FASTA files. Prokka was used to predict protein-coding sequences and to generate the corresponding amino acid FASTA files. Predicted protein sequences were subsequently analyzed for the identification of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) using dbCAN. The protein FASTA files generated by Prokka were used as input for dbCAN, which assigns CAZy families based on hidden Markov model (HMM) profiles. Only proteins with at least one HMMER hit were retained for downstream analyses. CAZy annotations were classified into the main functional classes, including glycoside hydrolases (GH), glycosyltransferases (GT), carbohydrate esterases (CE), polysaccharide lyases (PL), auxiliary activities (AA), and carbohydrate-binding modules (CBM).

dbCAN results were imported into R and processed using the tidyverse package (2.0.0). CAZy family annotations were expanded to account for proteins containing multiple CAZy domains. For each CAZy family, enzyme counts were calculated separately for S. olivaceus C1_7A and B. thuringiensis FBB7BB. When multiple product annotations were associated with the same CAZy family, the most frequent Prokka-derived product annotation was used for visualization. Comparative analyses of CAZy repertoires were visualized using bar plots showing CAZy family abundance, with colors indicating the bacterial genus and facets corresponding to CAZy classes. All plots were generated using ggplot2 (4.0.0). Genome visualization was carried out using Proksee (https://proksee.ca).

Enzyme production trials in liquid media with different CMC concentrations

S. olivaceus C1_7A and B. thuringiensis FBB7BB strains were further evaluated for endo-1,4-β-D-glucanase production in a fermenter. Both strains were cultivated in Minibio Reactors (Applikon Biotechnology BV, The Netherlands) controlled by Lucullus PIMS software v. 24.1.0. Minibio Reactors were used due to their small working volume combined with scalable process control, enabling reliable lab-scale validation of the bioreactor systems. Bioreactors with a total volume of 500 mL were autoclaved at 121 °C and 100 kPa for 15 min and filled with 300 mL of liquid medium containing either 1 or 2% CMC (pH 7.0). Sensors for pH and DO2 (Dissolved Oxygen) were calibrated before sterilization. The temperature was set at 30 °C for S. olivaceus C1_7A, and at 37 °C for B. thuringiensis FBB7BB. Stirring was set at 100 rpm for both strains. Aeration was maintained at 50 mL min−1. The pH was initially adjusted to 7.0 and monitored throughout the fermentation without active control. Optical density (OD) was monitored throughout the experiment. To ensure comparable viable cell concentrations at inoculation, a 2% (v/v) bacterial cell suspension (3.39 × 106 ± 0.12 CFU mL−1) of B. thuringiensis FBB7BB, and a 3% (v/v) suspension (1.85 × 106 ± 0.21 CFU mL-1) of S. olivaceus C1_7A were used as inoculum. Cell concentrations were preliminarily determined by CFU counts on CMC media obtained through serial dilution and plating after defined incubation times for each strain. The enzyme production and bacterial growth were carried out for 24 h for B. thuringiensis FBB7BB and 240 h for S. olivaceus C1_7A according to bacterial growth rates. Samples were withdrawn every two hours for strain FBB7BB and once per day for strain C1_7A to enable the quantitative estimation of enzymatic (endo-1,4-β-glucanase) activity by Azo-CMCase assay. Samples collected at time 0 (T0) were used as the baseline reference for evaluating temporal changes in growth and enzyme production. All trials were performed in biological triplicate.

Statistical analysis

One-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the effects of incubation time and CMC concentration on endo-1,4-β-glucanase production. Tukey’s HSD post hoc test, was applied for pairwise comparisons of means at a significance level of P < 0.05 to determine differences in enzymatic activities among the isolated strains. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 13.0 (SPSS Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Isolation and characterization of potential enzymes-producing bacteria

A total of 48 bacterial isolates were screened for cellulolytic activity on CMC-containing agar plates stained with congo red solution. Only 8.3% of the isolates showed no enzymatic activity; 14.6% produced halos smaller than 10 mm, while 58.3% produced halos larger than 20 mm, suggesting their potential endoglucanase activity.

Multi-enzymatic traits of selected S. olivaceus C1_7A and B. thuringiensis FBB7BB strains

Based on their ability to produce endo-1,4-β-D-glucanase on alternative substrates, S. olivaceus C1_7A and B. thuringiensis FBB7BB were selected for multi-enzymatic activity characterization. B. thuringiensis FBB7BB displayed a broad enzymatic profile, with exocellulase, xylanase (10.00 ± 0.00 mm), cellobiase (4.00 ± 1.70 mm), and pectinase activity (1.00 ± 0.00 mm), whereas no ligninolytic activity was detected. S. olivaceus C1_7A showed exocellulase, xylanase (1.00 ± 0.00 mm), pectinase (4.33 ± 0.37 mm), and ligninolytic activity (+), but not detectable cellobiase activity.

Whole genome sequencing and annotation of putative genes involved in polysaccharide metabolism

Bacillus thuringiensis FBB7BB gDNA sequencing produced 3,081 reads for a total of 24.18 Mb of data, resulting in an approximate coverage of 5 × . The assembly yielded a total genome length of 4,144,134 bp distributed across 51 contigs, with an N50 value of 80,728 bp and a maximum contig length of 393,035 bp. The GC content was 35.37%. Genome annotation identified 3,555 coding sequences, with a coding density of 0.788 and an average gene length of 204.78 bp. Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) analysis confirmed the taxonomic assignment of the Bacillus thuringiensis FBB7BB genome, with values ranging from 95.32 to 96.89% across comparisons with several B. thuringiensis reference genomes (NBIN-866, BMB171, Bt407, and HD-771). BUSCO analysis indicated a genome completeness of 66.1%, with an additional 10.2% of BUSCOs detected in fragmented form and 23.7% missing. The very low proportion of duplicated BUSCOs (0.3%) supports the internal consistency of the draft assembly of B. thuringiensis FBB7BB. Sequencing of Streptomyces olivaceus C1_7A generated 112,086 reads totaling 519.01 Mb, corresponding to an average coverage depth of 48 × . The assembly resulted in a genome size of 10,926,188 bp across 9 contigs, with an N50 of 2,500,795 bp and a maximum contig length of 4,946,212 bp. The GC content was 63.01%. A total of 9,665 coding sequences were predicted, with a coding density of 0.876 and an average gene length of 330.63 bp. ANI analysis showed high similarity between the Streptomyces olivaceus C1_7A assembly and reference genomes, with values of 98.44% and 98.43% against S. olivaceus NRRL B-3009 and CGMCC 4.1369. BUSCO analysis indicated a near-complete genome for Streptomyces olivaceus C1_7A, with 99.7% completeness and no duplicated BUSCOs, supporting the high quality and internal consistency of the assembly. The annotation output led to the identification of 9965 genes for Streptomyces and 4890 genes for Bacillus (Supplementary Fig. 1A–B). Phylogenomic analysis further supported the taxonomic placement of strains FBB7BB and C1_7A within the B. thuringiensis and S. olivaceus clades, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2A–B).

CAZy annotations were classified into five functional classes: glycosyltransferases (GT), carbohydrate esterases (CE), polysaccharide lyases (PL), auxiliary activities (AA), and carbohydrate-binding modules (CBM). S. olivaceus C1_7A contained a total of 289 annotated CAZy enzymes, whereas B. thuringiensis FBB7BB possessed 72 (Fig. 1). Discussion

The search for novel cellulolytic enzymes is crucial to improve the profitability of biomass for biotechnological valorization7. This study confirms that bacteria isolated from diverse environments, including chestnut compost and bamboo forest undergrowth, possess cellulolytic capabilities, in agreement with previous findings reporting the occurrence of cellulolytic bacteria across habitats such as soil, sludge, bioreactors, compost, forest litter, hot springs, and animal guts2,4,5,7,16,24. Building on ecological diversity, the present research integrates classical screening, multi-enzymatic profiling, validation on alternative cellulose substrates, genomic analysis, and preliminary scale-up assays to identify and characterize novel cellulolytic bacterial strains with biotechnological potential.

Most isolates presented in this work belong to Actinobacteria and Firmicutes, which, along with Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes, are the most common phyla of bacterial strains capable of degrading crystalline cellulose31. Over 90% of the identified cellulase sequences derived from these four phyla, in accordance with the broad phylogenetic diversity of cellulase-producing bacteria reported in previous studies7,31. This taxonomic distribution reflects the ecological ubiquity of plant biomass but also highlights the evolutionary advantage conferred by bacterial adaptability in degrading lignocellulose across diverse environments.

Endo-1,4-β-D-glucanase activity was quantified for 15 identified strains that showed high enzymatic activity in semi-quantitative solid-medium assays. Among them, S. olivaceus C1_7A exhibited the highest enzymatic activity, followed by B. licheniformis AT081C and AT082C, and B. thuringiensis FBB7BB. Notably, strains such as S. sylvae AET2SB82P / APT5SB51A and Kosakonia pseudosacchari AT5SB64C, which formed large halos (≥ 20 mm), did not show detectable endo-1,4-β-glucanase activity through AZO-CMCase measurement. This suggests that halo formation may not always directly correspond with the bacterial production of cellulase, but may also be influenced by other factors, such as the breakdown of cellulose into glucose, which rely on complex enzymatic synergy16. These results also highlight the cellulolytic potential of Gram-positive genera such as Streptomyces and Bacillus, confirming previous studies and highlighting their role in biomass degradation through cellulase32. Streptomyces species are known for producing a wide range of extracellular enzymes and secondary metabolites, which contribute to their multifunctional role in sustainable agriculture and environmental applications33,34. These bacterial genera are well reported for secreting lytic enzymes such as cellulases and amylases33. Their ability to degrade complex polysaccharides, alongside their antimicrobial activity, makes them valuable candidates for integrated strategies in biowaste valorization33. Furthermore, members of the genus Bacillus are well-documented for their strong cellulolytic capacity, attributed to the diversity and abundance of glycoside hydrolases encoded in their genomes35,36.

To further evaluate the cellulolytic capability of selected strains, four isolates with activity ≥ 0.10 U mL−1 were tested for endo-1,4-β-D-glucanase production on Arundo donax and Avicel, a microcrystalline form of cellulose. Avicel was selected based on its highly ordered microcrystalline cellulose structure that allows to detect exoglucanases37,38. In fact, endoglucanases preferentially hydrolyze amorphous regions of cellulose -such as CMC-, while exoglucanases play a key role in the degradation of crystalline cellulose39. S. olivaceus C1_7A demonstrated superior enzymatic performance among the four isolates on both natural and refined cellulose substrates, confirming its versatile cellulolytic potential. B. thuringiensis FBB7BB displayed lower activity on both Arundo donax, and Avicel, while B. licheniformis strains AT081C and AT082C did not exhibit measurable enzyme production. These results suggest that some strains may have minimal or negligible capacity to degrade structurally diverse cellulosic substrates due to substrate-specific regulation or ineffective enzyme systems. Thus, semi-quantitative determination using specific kits7 may result in false positive strains, while genomic approaches are required to successfully detect and characterize in depth the cellulolytic microbial strains16.

The multi-enzymatic screening of S. olivaceus C1_7A and B. thuringiensis FBB7BB revealed their potential for the complete breakdown of lignocellulosic biomass, a crucial step in biotechnological applications such as biofuel production, composting, and sustainable soil management1,4,6. In detail, B. thuringiensis FBB7BB showed a broader enzymatic repertoire, exhibiting exocellulolytic activity, as well as β-cellobiase (4.00 ± 1.70 mm), xylanase (10.00 ± 0.00 mm), and pectinase (1.00 ± 0.00 mm) activities, suggesting its potential capability for deconstructing plant cell walls. In contrast, S. olivaceus C1_7A showed a different cellulolytic potential with the presence of exocellulolytic activity but no cellobiase activity, including pectinase activity and ligninolytic capacity. This indicates a different ecological strategy compared to B. thuringiensis FBB7BB, likely involving lignin modification to access recalcitrant polysaccharides. Furthermore, the detection of xylanase activity in both strains supports the hypothesis that the degradation pathways of hemicellulose and cellulose are metabolically co-regulated40.

The combination of endo- and exocellulolytic activities, along with accessory enzymes such as xylanases, pectinases and β-glucanase, highlights the synergistic enzyme systems of the strains under study. This enzymatic synergy is crucial for efficiently saccharifying complex plant biomass. For example, co-expressing cellulases and xylanases was shown to enhance biomass conversion efficiency4,35,36. Furthermore, genomic data confirmed the enzymatic profiles qualitatively observed in plate assays, supporting the presence of multi-enzymatic systems involved in lignocellulose degradation. In particular, the detection of GH families associated with xylanase, pectinase, and auxiliary activities was in agreement with the activities observed in agar-based screening. The activities observed in plate assays should be interpreted as indicative of functional potential rather than quantitative enzyme production, while genome-based analysis supports the genetic basis underlying this multi-enzymatic capability.

In detail, the comparative genomic analysis highlighted differences in genome architecture and carbohydrate metabolic potential between B. thuringiensis FBB7BB and S. olivaceus C1_7A (Supplementary Fig. 1A–B). The larger genome size, higher GC content, and nearly twofold greater number of predicted genes in S. olivaceus C1_7A align with the well-documented genomic complexity of Streptomyces species, known for their versatile metabolic capabilities and adaptation to diverse ecological niches41. However, the higher number of predicted genes does not necessarily correspond to increased enzyme production, as gene expression is strongly influenced by regulatory mechanisms and growth conditions.

The functional potential inferred from CAZy annotation is further supported by the presence of key carbohydrate-active enzymes in both genomes (Fig. 1). In B. thuringiensis FBB7BB, the CAZy repertoire includes glycoside hydrolases involved in starch and oligosaccharide degradation (e.g., GH13 family α-amylases, oligo-1,6-glucosidases, pullulanases, and trehalose-6-phosphate hydrolases), as well as enzymes targeting structural carbohydrates such as cellulose (GH5, GH8 endoglucanases) and chitin (GH18 chitinases, GH25 lysozymes)42,43. The detection of AA10 lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases and associated CBMs further supports the capacity for oxidative cleavage of recalcitrant polysaccharides, despite the relatively compact CAZy repertoire observed. In addition, multiple glycosyltransferases (e.g., GT2, GT4, GT28, GT51) linked to peptidoglycan biosynthesis, glycogen metabolism, and secondary metabolite modification indicate a strong investment in carbohydrate remodeling and storage rather than extensive extracellular degradation.

In contrast, S. olivaceus C1_7A exhibited an expanded and diverse CAZy repertoire (Fig. 1), including a broad range of glycoside hydrolases targeting cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin, and chitin (e.g., GH10, GH11, GH28, GH43, GH64, GH141), together with multiple polysaccharide lyases (PL1, PL7, PL12) and abundant carbohydrate-binding modules. The presence of several AA10 lytic monooxygenases and diverse CBMs highlights an advanced enzymatic system optimized for the degradation of complex plant-derived biomass41. Overall, the comparative genomic analysis suggests two distinct ecological and metabolic strategies for lignocellulose utilization: S. olivaceus C1_7A appears to function as a broad-spectrum polysaccharide degrader, whereas B. thuringiensis FBB7BB relies on a more compact and tightly regulated enzymatic system specialized for selective carbohydrate utilization, modification, and intracellular storage. Interestingly, despite these different genomic organizations and ecological strategies, both strains share key GH families involved in cellulose degradation. This convergence suggests that phylogenetically distant microorganisms exposed to similar selective pressures may independently evolve functionally analogous enzymatic solutions for efficient lignocellulose deconstruction, highlighting the existence of conserved and highly effective biochemical strategies for cellulose degradation44.

To investigate the effect of substrate concentration on cellulase production, endo-1,4-β-glucanase activity was evaluated in fermenter at different CMC concentrations and incubation times, as substrate availability influences both enzyme induction and potential inhibition phenomena. Enzymatic assays revealed that the endo-1,4-β-glucanase activity, and thus the overall enzyme yield of both B. thuringiensis FBB7BB and S. olivaceus C1_7A, widely depend on the initial CMC concentration and incubation time. Lower CMC concentrations (1%) resulted in higher catalytic efficiency, while higher concentrations (2%) likely caused substrate inhibition, leading to reduced enzyme production. Both B. thuringiensis FBB7BB and S. olivaceus C1_7A exhibited higher volumetric productivity at 1% CMC compared to 2%, indicating that lower substrate concentrations favor not only enzyme yield but also production kinetics. The reduced volumetric productivity observed at 2% CMC could be related to the increased medium viscosity and reduced mass transfer, which can hinder substrate accessibility and microbial activity. In addition, higher substrate concentrations may lead to substrate inhibition or metabolic imbalance, ultimately decreasing enzyme productivity45. In B. thuringiensis FBB7BB, a clear correspondence was observed between the peak in enzymatic activity and the maximum optical density measured (with 1% CMC), confirming that endo-1,4-β-glucanase production primarily occurred during the exponential growth phase, when metabolic activity was highest24. In contrast, no overlap between enzyme production and OD660 was observed with 2% CMC, probably due to a rapid pH shift. Chaiharn et al. (2013)46 observed that neutral and controlled pH (~ 7) is optimal for enzyme production by certain Bacillus strains. B. thuringiensis FBB7BB proved to be an efficient enzyme-producing bacterium reaching a high enzymatic activity (0.30 ± 0.04 U mL−1) within 8 h, compared to B. amyloliquefaciens B31C (0.20 U mL−1 in 15 h, 37 °C)24. The rapid enzyme production observed in this study is notable given that it is a wild-type strain tested under non-optimized culture conditions. In a related study, B. velezensis H1 reached maximum activity only after 72 h, despite being cultivated under optimized conditions47. Previous studies have shown that enhanced enzyme production is closely associated with the availability of organic nitrogen sources47. The medium used in this work contained low levels of organic nitrogen (e.g., 1 g L−1 yeast extract) compared to concentrations reported as optimal (e.g., up to 2.5 g L−1 yeast extract plus 5 g L−1 peptone). These findings confirm that B. thuringiensis FBB7BB possesses notable enzymatic characteristics for industrial applications requiring short production cycles. Further studies should focus on screening the operational conditions (e.g., by pH control) to reach higher productivity. Different incubation times applied to the two strains were selected based on their distinct growth kinetics, as reflected by the early peak in enzymatic activity observed for B. thuringiensis FBB7BB (8 h) compared to the delayed maximum production in S. olivaceus C1_7A (216 h).

S. olivaceus C1_7A showed an early induction of enzymatic activity with both 1 and 2% CMC, reaching concentrations of 0.23 ± 0.00 and 0.16 ± 0.00 U mL−1, respectively, after 48 h of trials. The highest production (0.36 ± 0.03 U mL−1) was achieved with 1% CMC, albeit after longer incubation times (216 h) than 2% trials. While the two different CMC concentrations initially led to similar enzyme concentrations, 1% CMC resulted in a higher enzyme production in the long term. The enzymatic activity reached by S. olivaceus C1_7A resulted in a level higher than other reported Streptomyces strains, such as S. albidoflavus (0.27 U mL−148) S. auranticus (0.21 U mL−149), and S. argenteolus (0.25 U mL−150). With both 1% and 2% CMC, the OD660 showed an increasing trend in the first 48 h, after which it remained relatively stable. This apparent uncoupling between bacterial growth and enzyme secretion may be explained by the filamentous morphology of Streptomyces, which tends to form dense mycelial aggregates or pellets in submerged cultures51. These structures cause sedimentation and interfere with light scattering, leading to biomass underestimation when using optical density. The prolonged enzymatic production observed may reflect a metabolically active biomass that remains viable and productive in the later growth stages. Accordingly, Pennacchio et al.5 reported high cellulase activity (~ 0.46 U mL−1) after 7–10 days of growth under optimal production conditions for Streptomyces sp. G12. In general, filamentous actinomycetes are capable of maintaining a secondary metabolism under nutrient-limiting or stationary-phase conditions34. The observed pH and dissolved oxygen dynamics reflected the metabolic activity of the strains throughout the whole monitoring trial. Furthermore, for S. olivaceus C1_7A, the pH increased progressively, exceeding 8.0, potentially due to the production of alkaline metabolites such as ammonia, derived from protein degradation, as previously described in the cellulolytic process in presence of organic nitrogen52,53. Finally, higher CMC concentrations (> 2%) were not further evaluated due to the increased medium viscosity, which limited oxygen transfer and mixing efficiency, as well as the observed reduction in enzymatic performance.

Conclusions

This study provides a detailed and integrated evaluation of two newly isolated cellulolytic wild-type bacterial strains, B. thuringiensis FBB7BB and S. olivaceus C1_7A, isolated from lignocellulose-rich environments. Both strains could degrade multiple cellulose substrates, including CMC, as well as more recalcitrant forms such as A. donax and Avicel. The evaluation of the enzymatic activity on different cellulose sources, along with genomic analysis and bioreactor validation, provides a clear overview of their potential applications in the biotech field. B. thuringiensis FBB7BB showed rapid enzymatic responses, while S. olivaceus C1_7A demonstrated sustained activity over time. These results suggest that their application is suitable for different process configurations. The reported enzymatic activities highlight the cellulolytic potential of the wild-type strains, although further studies to optimize and maximize enzyme production are needed to confirm their potential biotechnological application. Overall, this work emphasizes the importance of integrating strain discovery with functional and lab-scale validation to provide a realistic framework for selecting and applying cellulolytic bacteria in scalable lignocellulosic bioconversion systems.
