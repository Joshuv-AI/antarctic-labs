Systematic Design of Local Rules for Directing Emergent Structure in Bottom-Up Systems Preprint: APS/123-QED
Andrew Slezak, Varda F. Hagh

Abstract
Many biological systems collectively construct complex, adaptive, and functional architectures, where function emerges from bottom-up building processes rather than top-down planning or centralized control. However, general strategies for programming and controlling such emergent function in engineered systems remain largely unexplored. In this work, we present a systematic framework for designing local behavioral rule sets for simple builders such that, when adhered to, structures with targeted global properties emerge. Using a minimal model inspired by tent caterpillars, we study how simple agents equipped with limited sensing and no memory or global knowledge construct networked structures through local deposition of line segments. We base our framework on tuning local degrees of freedom in a complex system to alter global behavior. By identifying the degrees of freedom that influence a given property and specifying how they are tuned through local rules, we demonstrate that the corresponding global properties can be directed. We explore this through three geometric properties of the agents’ resulting networks, in particular area coverage, average line density, and front curvature. We show that agents can reliably achieve targeted values for these properties while maintaining low variability in the presence of stochasticity. These results establish a generalizable approach for programming emergence in decentralized systems and suggest new pathways for designing adaptive materials and autonomous construction strategies in complex, uncertain environments.

Here we investigate how the local rules governing the behavior of simple builders, referred to as agents, can be specified so that desired global properties emerge in the structures they build. In particular, we ask how agents with minimal sensing, no centralized control, and minimal knowledge of their environment can reliably produce such outcomes? Can agent behaviors be encoded so that they adapt naturally as construction modifies the environment without requiring explicit branching logic? Finally, what systematic approaches can be used to evaluate and refine the local rules that govern the behavior of these builders?

A central challenge arises in creating and applying such a framework due to the relationship between component-level degrees of freedom and emergent system-level properties. This mapping is rarely straightforward or deterministic due to inherent nonlinearities. Moreover, perturbations and noise introduce stochasticity into the construction process, which propagate through the system and lead to non-trivial variability in the resulting structures. As a consequence, even when the same local rules are applied to the same set of degrees of freedom, the resulting global outcomes may vary significantly. Conversely, different sets of local degrees of freedom may produce similar emergent properties through different rules. This many-to-many mapping between local degrees of freedom and global emergent properties, illustrated in Fig.

To address this challenge, we introduce a methodology that begins with heuristic local rules informed by domain knowledge and systematically refines them through statistical analysis of emergent properties across ensembles of realizations. Using this approach, we show that simple agent behaviors can be tuned to direct global properties of the resulting networks, including area coverage, line density, and front curvature. By directing these properties, we mean not only that they approach a specified target value, but also that ensembles of parallel realizations exhibit sufficiently low standard deviation about that target.

II
Methodology

In Fig.
3
we present our methodology for rule design in diagram form, which we use throughout this work to achieve our stated objectives. We begin by defining the tunable degrees of freedom and selecting a target property or set of properties, whose emergence we aim to direct. We then specify an initial set of governing rules along with associated input parameters. Although the space of possible rules and parameters is large, we constrain the design space by first considering a purely stochastic rule set to establish a baseline and identify what emerges in the absence of additional specificity. We then incorporate domain knowledge to define a tractable subset of candidate rules informed by this initial exploration. For example, because the structures formed by our agents are networks and the target properties are geometric, we select the spatial placement and lengths of edges as the primary degrees of freedom and initialize the system with a simple geometry-based rule set, as described later in this section.

Using the defined system, we simulate the building process via agent-based modeling under prescribed boundary conditions. We then evaluate the resulting structures over an ensemble of realizations to assess whether the desired properties emerge reliably.
If the emergent properties are sufficiently close to their target values, the rule design is considered achieved. If the property of interest emerges but its value lies outside the acceptable range, we refine the
input parameters
until the target value is achieved. If the system shows little to no progress toward the target properties or remains outside the acceptable range, we instead modify the rules and continue the iterative process until the desired behavior is obtained.

Figure 3:
Forward rule design diagram.

After identifying the tunable degrees of freedom, we select a set of heuristic rules and their input parameters to direct the property of interest. We then perform parallel simulations to evaluate whether this property emerges reliably. If it emerges and attains the target value, the design is complete. If it emerges but does not reach the target value, the input parameters are adjusted to direct it toward the desired value. If it does not emerge, or lies too far outside the desired range to be effectively directed, the rules are modified.

Selecting and modifying rules is inherently nontrivial, as it depends sensitively on the discrepancy between current and desired outcomes. To address this, we employ a heuristic rule design approach that preserves interpretability and maintains intuition about the relationship between local degrees of freedom, the governing rules for tuning them, and the resulting global properties. This approach also helps retain distinct and separable rule behaviors, whereas more automated methods may lead to increasingly interdependent and less interpretable outcomes.

Agent Design
. To test our proposed framework, we define a model for the agents. We assume that a building agent interacts with its local environment through sensing and action. In this work, sensing is restricted to distance measurements, which are used to inform decisions but are not themselves considered actions. We model agents as simple, point-like entities capable of moving within their environment and depositing line segments, with each action requiring a single time step. Each agent is assigned a fixed radius of vision
R_{\rm v}
, which defines the region of the environment within which it can perform measurements and execute actions. Within this radius, the agent may move to or build toward any detected point. After depositing a line segment, the agent’s position is updated to the endpoint of the line, as illustrated in Fig.
4
. Agents do not retain memory of previously observed points; instead, at each time step, they first sense (
i.e.
, measure) their local environment within
R_{\rm v}
, then select an action based on these measurements, and finally execute that action. Accordingly, an agent samples its local environment at each time step and selects either a
build
or
move
action. If no suitable build location is identified, the agent continues moving until it reaches a region where construction is possible or the allotted simulation time is exhausted.

Using this model for agents, three initial input parameters can be varied for any given rule to evaluate its effectiveness within a given geometry: the radius of vision
R_{\rm v}
, the building time
t
, and the number of agents
N_{\rm a}
. To characterize the effect of the radius of vision without anchoring it to a specific scale, we normalize it by the characteristic length scale
L
of the environment, such that
0<R_{\rm v}/L\leq 1
. We fix the number of agents at
2
, representing the minimal collective, and set the total number of time steps to
500
for all simulations unless otherwise stated.

Note the geometry of the environment is intractable to evaluate comprehensively due to the vast space of possible configurations, orientations, and structural elements, even when a characteristic length scale is specified and fixed. We therefore fix the environment geometry throughout this study and acknowledge that its coupling to emergent properties is not fully explored. Instead, our analysis focuses on selecting and refining rules that provide direct control over agent behavior and the resulting structures.

Behavioral Rule Sets
. With the initial parameters defined, we study how behavioral rule sets can be determined to direct agents to construct networks with target values for three geometric properties: the covered area ratio, average line density, and front curvature. Accordingly, we use geometry-based reasoning to formulate the rules within each set. A complete rule set consists of at least three components: a
build
rule, which determines the point to which an agent builds; a
movement
rule, which determines the point to which an agent moves; and a
decision
rule, which determines whether an agent moves or builds at a given time step. Together, these rules provide the minimal basis for describing agent actions during network construction and offer independent mechanisms for modifying agent behavior.

Schematics of the building process in one time step.

A) The agent begins the time step by discretizing the environment within its radius of vision into a set of points. It then selects a point from this list based on the rule set it follows, constructs a line to that point, and repositions to the endpoint of the line. B) In the next time step, the agent again discretizes its environment, including the newly deposited line, and repeats the process.

II.1
Random Build, Random Movement, Random Decision (RRR)

The purpose of this rule set is to establish what emerges from the simplest possible rules where no additional direction is provided to agents. It is in essence a baseline against which everything else can be compared. For this rule set, agents select a point uniformly at random from all detected points. The decision to move or build to that point is similarly chosen with uniform random probability.

II.2
Maximum Length Build, Random Movement, Random Decision (MRR)

Motivated by the results of the RRR rule set, we aim to understand if by simply modifying our build rule to always deposit maximum length segments we can direct agents to maximize the total area covered by the networks. Agents following this rule set build only to points within their radius of vision that are maximally distant from their current position; if multiple points satisfy this condition, one is selected uniformly at random. As before, both movement and the decision to build or move remain random.

II.3
Area-based Build, Random Movement, Area Target Decision (ARA)

Following our exploration of the RRR and MRR rule sets, we develop a new rule set that reliably maximizes covered area as quickly as possible using only local information. To achieve this, agents estimate local area coverage using their existing sensing capabilities and exclusively build in regions where the local area is lower than a prescribed target. Under this rule set, each agent is assigned a target local area ratio
A_{\rm t}
, a grid resolution
N_{\rm cc}
, and an exclusion radius
R_{\rm ex}
. At each time step, the agent discretizes the area within its radius of vision into a uniform Cartesian grid of square cells with characteristic length
\ell_{c}=R_{\rm v}/N_{\rm cc}
and computes the local area ratio
A_{\rm\ell}
, as described in the Supplemental Information. This value is then compared to the target
A_{\rm t}
to determine whether the agent moves or builds. If the local area ratio
A_{\rm\ell}
exceeds
A_{\rm t}
, the agent moves to a randomly selected point within its radius of vision until it identifies a region where the local area ratio falls below the target. Otherwise, the agent considers candidate line segments that connect its current position to points located within
R_{\rm v}
but outside the exclusion radius
R_{\rm ex}
. Among these candidates, it deposits the segment that produces the largest increase in local area coverage. The exclusion radius (
R_{\rm ex}=0.5R_{\rm v}
) is introduced to reduce computational cost by excluding short segments, which are less likely to increase area coverage significantly. If multiple candidate segments yield the same maximal increase in area coverage, the first in the list is selected.

II.4
Area-based Build, Maximum Distance Movement, Density Target Decision (AMD)

Building on the ARA rule set, which reliably produces networks with controllable area coverage, we introduce a modified rule set that preserves similar network structures while also enabling control over the average line density. To achieve this, agents are provided with an additional measure of local density, computed using their existing sensing capabilities.
Under this rule set, each agent is assigned a target local density
\rho_{\rm t}
, a grid resolution
N_{\rm cc}
, and an exclusion radius
R_{\rm ex}
. Agents deposit line segments that result in the largest increase in local area coverage and adopt a maximum-distance movement strategy to explore the surrounding space more efficiently. The decision to build or move is determined by comparing the measured local density
\rho_{\ell}
to the target value
\rho_{\rm t}
(see Supplemental Information for details on how
\rho_{\ell}
is computed from the number of line segments per unit area). If
\rho_{\ell}<\rho_{\rm t}
, the agent deposits a line segment of length
R_{\rm ex}\leq l\leq R_{\rm v}
that maximizes the increase in local area coverage. Otherwise, the agent moves maximally until it reaches a region where
\rho_{\ell}<\rho_{\rm t}
and construction is possible. As in ARA, when multiple candidate segments yield identical maximal increases in area coverage, the first in the list is selected.

III
Results

Using the four rule sets outlined above, we examine how three global geometric properties can be directed as line segments are arranged within a given environment. These properties include the ratio of total area covered by a network, the line density within the covered region, and the curvature of the emerging front after the building process is completed. While these properties are not unique, their coupling provides useful insight into how rule sets can be adjusted to produce multifunctional structures with additional desired characteristics. We also characterize the sensitivity of each rule set to initial conditions by randomly assigning agent positions and repeating each simulation
100
times for each parameter set. This allows us to quantify the variability of the emergent properties and assess the extent to which they can be directed using a given rule set and its associated parameters.

III.1
Area Coverage Ratio

We begin our exploration by analyzing the area covered by agents within a given geometry over a fixed number of time steps. A time step corresponds to a single update during which each agent observes its local environment and either builds or moves to a selected location.
This definition also sets an upper bound on the amount of building material that must be allocated to the agents. Assuming that each agent can construct at most one line segment per time step and that the maximum segment length is
R_{\rm v}
, the maximum required material length is
M_{\rm tot}=N_{\rm a}R_{\rm v}t
, where
N_{\rm a}
is the number of agents and
t
is the number of time steps.

In Figs.
5
A–D we show representative networks formed after
500
time steps with
R_{\rm v}=0.2L
under the four rule sets. These snapshots qualitatively show that the fraction of covered area varies significantly across rule sets, even when agents share the same radius of vision. To estimate the area covered by the deposited segments, we discretize the entire global domain into square pixels of a fixed size; then we classify a pixel as covered if any portion of a line segment passes through it. Pixel size for our analysis is set to
\ell_{\rm p}=4\times 10^{-3}L
, where
L
is the characteristic length scale of the environment. The total number of covered pixels,
N_{\rm p}
, is then used to approximate the covered area as
A_{\rm cov}\approx N_{\rm p}\ell_{\rm p}^{2}
.

Next, we compute the total available area
A_{\rm tot}
, which is defined by the area of the convex hull
2
of the initial geometry. The covered area ratio
A_{\rm cov}/A_{\rm tot}
is then calculated as a function of time and averaged over an ensemble of
100
simulations initialized with randomized agent positions. The results are shown in Figs.
5
E–H for varying
R_{\rm v}
across all four rule sets. This approximation systematically overestimates the covered area due to the discretization scheme, as line segments are represented on a finite grid and may occupy only a small fraction of a given pixel. Reducing this error requires decreasing
\ell_{\rm p}
, which increases computational cost. Therefore, the covered area is evaluated every
100
time steps, and the area ratio is bounded within
0<A_{\rm cov}/A_{\rm tot}\leq 1
.

Analyzing the baseline case (RRR) in Fig.
5
E, we observe that the results exhibit substantial variability except at small values of
R_{\rm v}
. The standard deviation is low for smaller values of
R_{\rm v}
(
0.02L
&
0.2L
) because small
R_{\rm v}
constrains agents’ ability to explore their surroundings at each time step. In these cases, agents primarily construct along a single scaffold branch for most of the simulation and only occasionally encounter regions where connections to other branches are possible. As a result, only agents that begin near, or subsequently reach, junctions in the geometry contribute to expanding the network beyond segments co-linear with the scaffold branches. This behavior is consistent with exploration-exploitation dynamics observed in active matter systems, where motion without directional bias limits spatial exploration and leads to repeated sampling of previously visited regions
37
;
33
;
36
.

To address this limitation, we introduce a bias into the rule set by replacing the random build rule with a maximum length construction rule (MRR). This modification increases the length of each deposited segment, enabling agents to reach new regions more often. As shown in Fig.
5
F, this change leads to a modest increase in the overall area coverage for
R_{\rm v}=0.2L
and a substantial increase for larger values of
R_{\rm v}
. For sufficiently large
R_{\rm v}\geq 0.6L
, maximizing area coverage becomes the dominant outcome, accompanied by a significant reduction in the standard deviation. This improvement arises because maximum length construction reduces the dependence on initial agent positions by facilitating access to previously unreachable regions. However, neither RRR nor MRR reliably converge to maximal area coverage across all parameter regimes, and these rule sets do not contain additional parameters that can be tuned to systematically achieve this objective.

We therefore introduce the ARA rule set, which incorporates a measure of local area coverage and a target value
A_{\rm t}
to guide construction. Under this rule, area coverage converges rapidly for most values of
R_{\rm v}
, often reaching
A_{\rm cov}/A_{\rm tot}=1
in fewer than
100
time steps, with minimal variability across parallel simulations.

For small values of
R_{\rm v}
, the coverage is not maximized and exhibits a significant standard deviation, but still exceeds the performance of the previous rule sets at comparable values of
R_{\rm v}
. However, as shown in Fig.
5
C, this rule leads to relatively dense overlap of deposited segments, resulting in inefficient material usage. If both area coverage and network density are to be directed, additional modifications are required.

III.2
Average Network Line Density

Building on our analysis of area coverage, we now examine how to direct the average network line density, defined as the number of deposited line segments per control volume (
e.g.
, pixel). Using the same grid discretization introduced earlier, we count the number of line segments passing through each pixel to obtain the local line density. With this definition, two complementary averaging procedures can be used to characterize the resulting networks across an ensemble of
100
parallel realizations.
The first averaging scheme captures how density is distributed in space, on average, for a given rule set and value of
R_{\rm v}
. For each pixel, we average its local line density over
100
realizations to obtain an ensemble-averaged spatial density field, visualized as heat maps in Figs.
8
A–D. These maps highlight the spatial heterogeneity in the constructed networks and serve as the basis for our subsequent analysis of front curvature.

The second averaging scheme provides a measure of how the mean network density varies across independent realizations. For each individual network, we first average the local line density over all nonzero pixels to obtain a single mean density value, and then average these values over the
100
realizations to compute
\overline{\rho}
. This average value is shown as a function of time steps in Figs.
8
E–H.

It is worth noting that the emergence of front curvature in networks composed entirely of linear segments is largely governed by the geometry of the boundary conditions. In our exploration, line segments are deposited within a scaffold defined by two branches that form a convex angle. This geometry constrains the directions in which agents can build and effectively defines a triangular domain for growth. As a result, the scaffold geometry imposes a position-dependent limit on the maximum attainable height. Since the front is defined as the maximum height at each horizontal location, these variations give rise to a curved profile.
For this reason, directing curvature alone is relatively straightforward, as many rule sets and parameter choices produce curved fronts rather than flat ones, provided that the domain is not fully covered. However, when the goal is to control curvature in conjunction with other properties, such as area coverage or density, the range of feasible solutions is restricted, and the choice of rule set and parameters must be made more carefully to achieve the desired outcome.

IV
Conclusion

In summary, we have explored how to systematically design behavioral rules for simple agents so that, when they follow these rules to manipulate and deposit building blocks locally, they construct structures whose global properties emerge in a controlled way. These properties arise not only from how building blocks connect to one another, but also from how agents interact with the evolving structure, their surrounding environment, and the decisions they make during deposition. Throughout this work, we have shown that these local behavioral rules can be designed and refined so that the emergent properties of the resulting structures reliably approach desired target values.
Our approach relies on simple heuristic rules grounded in domain knowledge. When the properties of interest are geometric, the rules can be built from equally simple geometric principles. Following this idea, we demonstrated how properties such as area coverage, average line density, and front curvature can be directed in network structures, when agents building them operate with minimal sensing and limited information about their environment.

What we have studied here represents only a small fraction of what is possible. In this work, we have primarily focused on how to design rules for individual agents and have considered only minimal forms of collective behavior. Yet it is in the presence of collective that the problem becomes richer. How should agents be coupled so that they do not just build alongside one another, but truly build together? What kinds of structures become possible when agents share information, coordinate their actions, or adapt to one another in real time? There remains a wide and largely unexplored space in understanding how local interactions between agents, and not just between agents and their environment, shape emergent structure.
More broadly, many fundamental questions remain open. For instance, the mapping between available degrees of freedom, the rules used to tune them, and the structures that ultimately emerge is still poorly understood. What is the minimal set of degrees of freedom required to reliably produce a desired emergent property? And how much information about the environment is truly necessary to direct emergence?

We will pursue these questions in future work. The results presented here point toward an alternative way of thinking about how structures may be designed and built in environments where centralized control or continuous human supervision is not possible. Rather than exerting top-down specification over outcomes, the goal in such settings would be to define proper constraints that guide the self-organization of simple agents toward building functional structures. We view this as a step forward in extending human capabilities for engineering in unfamiliar and uncertain environments.
