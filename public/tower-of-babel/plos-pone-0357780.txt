YOLO-CCG: An enhanced model for weed detection in complex cotton fields

Lingmei Wu, Liqiang Zhang, Rifeng Wang, Huannian Meng, Yonghua Pan, Linli Jiang, Xing Zhang

ABSTRACT
Accurate weed detection is essential for site-specific herbicide application in cotton production. However, variations in weed appearance, complex field backgrounds, uneven target distributions, and leaf occlusion can result in missed detections and false positives. To address these challenges, this study proposes YOLO-CCG, an improved YOLO11-based model for weed detection in cotton fields. First, the Coordinate Attention (CA) mechanism is introduced into the backbone to embed spatial coordinate information into channel attention, thereby strengthening target localization and the representation of discriminative weed features. Second, nearest-neighbor upsampling in the neck is replaced with the Content-Aware ReAssembly of Features (CARAFE) module, which performs content-aware feature reassembly to preserve fine-grained spatial information during feature fusion. Finally, GSConv and the VoVGSCSP lightweight module are introduced into the bottom-up path of the neck to replace the corresponding standard convolution and C3k2 blocks, reducing computational complexity without increasing the parameter count. Experimental results show that, compared with YOLO11s, YOLO-CCG achieves relative improvements of 0.4%, 2.2%, 0.8%, and 1.7% in precision, recall, mAP50, and mAP50–95, respectively. These results indicate that YOLO-CCG provides an accurate and computationally efficient solution for weed detection in complex cotton-field environments.

1. Introduction Cotton is one of the most important cash crops worldwide and a core raw material for the textile industry, occupying a critical position in global agricultural trade. However, weed infestation poses a substantial threat to cotton growth and yield [ 1 ]. Weeds compete with cotton seedlings for essential resources including light, water, and nutrients, provide habitats for pests and diseases, and accumulate weed seeds in the soil, all of which damage crop productivity across growing seasons [ 2 ]. Since cotton seedlings grow slowly in the early developmental stage and have weak competitiveness against weeds, ineffective or delayed weed control can cause considerable economic losses [ 3 ]. To address this practical demand, vision-based intelligent weed detection and targeted spraying technology has become a core research direction for precision cotton management, and a field robot system has been developed for seedling-stage cotton fields [ 4 ]. Accurate and efficient weed management is therefore essential for sustaining agricultural productivity and profitability, and remains a long-standing challenge in field crop production [ 5 ]. Conventional weed management strategies mainly include mechanical methods (e.g., mowing and plowing) and chemical herbicide application. Mechanical weeding is environmentally friendly and reduces herbicide dependence [ 6 ]. However, it can inflict damage on crop seedlings, particularly in densely planted fields, and often requires repeated operations due to weed regrowth [ 7 ]. Chemical control is widely adopted for its convenience and high efficiency, but overuse of herbicides has led to the emergence of resistant weed biotypes, raising growing concerns about environmental safety, food security, and human health. To address these issues, reducing herbicide usage and developing precision weeding technologies have become important trends in sustainable agriculture [ 8 ]. Precision spraying based on weed detection can effectively suppress weeds while minimizing chemical inputs, which has been proven to improve control efficiency, reduce production costs, and mitigate environmental pollution [ 9 ]. The innovative application of artificial intelligence (AI) in agriculture has catalyzed the emergence of smart agriculture as a transformative production model. As a key driver of traditional agriculture modernization, AI technology has not only reshaped core production processes but also become a critical enabler of sustainable agricultural development. Unlike experience-based traditional agriculture, smart agriculture establishes a synergistic system characterized by “precision management—intensive resource utilization—high-quality output” through deep integration of AI with agricultural production scenarios. This system significantly enhances production efficiency, lowers operational costs, and improves product quality through intelligent, full-process regulation [ 10 ]. This technological integration effectively addresses the inherent challenges of conventional agriculture—high resource consumption, low efficiency, and complex management—while providing scalable technical pathways for advancing global sustainable agriculture. Within the precision management framework of smart agriculture, accurate weed identification and control are crucial for safeguarding crop yields. Weeds compete aggressively with crops for light, nutrients, and water, making effective management strategies indispensable. Early technical explorations in weed recognition primarily relied on computer vision combined with conventional machine learning classifiers. For example, Zhao et al. [ 11 ] utilized Hu moment invariants to extract soybean leaf features, achieving 90.5% recognition accuracy with a nearest neighbor classifier. Lavania et al. [ 12 ] combined a 3D OTSU method with principal component analysis, improving inter-row weed detection accuracy to 91.67%. Bakhshipour et al. [ 13 ] attained 92.92% classification accuracy for four common weed species in sugar beet fields using artificial neural networks. Zhang et al. [ 14 ] achieved 94.51% weed recognition accuracy in wheat fields by integrating a modified GrabCut method, an adaptive fuzzy dynamic K-means algorithm, and sparse representation classification. These early approaches demonstrated the technical feasibility of weed recognition under their respective experimental conditions, laying a foundation for subsequent research. However, traditional computer vision methods typically identify weeds by segmenting crop images and extracting low-level features such as color, shape, and texture. Due to their multi-stage, manual design and limited generalization capacity, these methods struggle to adapt to species diversity, complex growth patterns, and variable lighting conditions encountered in field environments. As a result, they have proven inadequate for reliable weed detection under natural growing conditions. In recent years, rapid advances in deep learning, particularly the development of object detection algorithms such as the YOLO series, have significantly alleviated the limitations of conventional agricultural perception methods [ 15 ]. Unlike the traditional “manual feature extraction + classifier” pipeline, YOLO algorithms autonomously learn discriminative features directly from large volumes of field images, exhibiting strong adaptability across varied scenarios. Deep learning models have achieved remarkable results in key areas including crop image classification, object detection, and instance segmentation [ 16 ]. These technologies enable real-time monitoring of crop growth, facilitating timely interventions. For example, the integration of computer vision with deep learning in weed identification and automated spraying systems allows precise detection and targeted chemical application. This approach has been demonstrated to reduce pesticide use and labor costs while improving weeding efficiency, offering significant benefits for both agricultural productivity and environmental protection [ 17 , 18 ]. In this study, we conduct a comprehensive analysis of the YOLO11 algorithm and propose a series of tailored optimization strategies to improve weed detection performance in cotton fields. First, the Coordinate Attention (CA) mechanism [ 19 ] is incorporated into the backbone network. This module strengthens the model’s capacity to capture positional and morphological information by embedding spatial coordinate information into channel attention. Specifically, it decomposes channel attention into two separate one-dimensional feature encoding processes that aggregate features along the height and width directions, respectively. This enables the model to capture long-range dependencies along one spatial direction while preserving precise positional information along the other. The resulting feature maps are then integrated to produce direction-aware and position-sensitive attention weights, which enhance the representation of target regions. Second, the original upsampling operator in the neck network is replaced with the Content-Aware Reassembly of Features (CARAFE) module [ 20 ]. CARAFE consists of a content-aware kernel predictor and a feature reassembly module. Its key advantage lies in its ability to adaptively generate upsampling kernels based on input feature content, enabling more effective use of contextual information. This strategy not only improves the resolution of feature maps but also enhances the model’s ability to reconstruct fine details, particularly for small weeds in complex backgrounds. Finally, to reduce model complexity and improve feature learning efficiency, we introduce GSConv and VoVGSCSP into the bottom-up path of the neck [ 21 ]. Specifically, GSConv combines standard convolution, depthwise convolution, and channel shuffling to promote inter-channel information exchange. VoVGSCSP supports feature reuse and fusion in the corresponding C3k2 replacement blocks. Their integration reduces computational complexity without increasing the final parameter count. The main contributions of this study are as follows: We propose an improved model named YOLO-CCG for weed detection in cotton fields. The model strengthens feature representation and reduces computational complexity without increasing the number of parameters. The CA mechanism is integrated into the backbone, enabling the model to jointly model channel dependencies and spatial positional information. This helps the lightweight network retain positional cues with only a small computational overhead. In the neck network, nearest-neighbor upsampling is replaced with CARAFE, which predicts content-aware reassembly kernels and can preserve fine-grained local information during feature fusion. The bottom-up neck path incorporates GSConv and VoVGSCSP in place of the corresponding downsampling convolution and C3k2 blocks. This reduces computational redundancy while maintaining feature fusion capacity. 2. Related work 2.1. YOLO-based weed detection methods In recent years, the YOLO series has become one of the most widely adopted frameworks for agricultural weed detection because of its favorable balance between detection accuracy and inference speed. With the continuous evolution from YOLOv5 to YOLO12, numerous studies have introduced task-specific improvements to enhance weed detection performance under complex field conditions. Dang et al. [ 22 ] systematically evaluated seven YOLO-based detectors on the CottonWeedDet12 dataset using Monte Carlo cross-validation. Their results showed that mAP50 ranged from 88.14% to 95.22%, while mAP50-95 ranged from 68.18% to 89.72%, demonstrating the effectiveness of YOLO-based architectures for cotton weed detection. Similarly, Khan et al. [ 23 ] evaluated YOLOv8 with multi-scale feature fusion and an anchor-free detection strategy. The model achieved 96.1% mAP50 on the CottonWeedDet12 dataset with an inference latency of 6.3 ms per frame, reduced classification errors among visually similar weed species, and used a loss function combining Complete IoU (CIoU) and Distribution Focal Loss (DFL). These studies illustrate the performance range of YOLO-based detectors for cotton weed detection. To further improve weed detection performance, Zheng et al. [ 24 ] proposed YOLOv8-DMAS, which integrates a Dilation-wise Residual (DWR) module, a Multi-Scale module (MSBlock), and an additional small-object detection layer. Compared with YOLOv8s, the proposed model improved precision, recall, mAP50, and mAP50-95 by 1.7, 3.8, 2.1, and 3.7 percentage points, respectively. These studies demonstrate that enhancing feature extraction and multi-scale feature fusion is an effective strategy for improving weed detection performance in complex agricultural environments. 2.2. Attention mechanisms and feature enhancement strategies Attention mechanisms have been extensively adopted to improve the discrimination capability of object detectors in complex agricultural scenes. By emphasizing informative regions and suppressing irrelevant background information, attention modules can significantly enhance feature representation. Ren et al. [ 25 ] developed EY8-MFEM by incorporating Adaptive Local-Global Attention (ALGA), a Multi-scale Directional Perception Module (MDPM), and CARAFE, resulting in substantial improvements in F1-score and detection accuracy. Chen et al. [ 26 ] further proposed the YOLOv8-EGC-Fusion model, which integrates Efficient Group Convolution and Group Context Anchor Attention to balance detection accuracy and computational efficiency. Ma et al. [ 27 ] proposed YOLO-CWD by integrating an Efficient Channel-Spatial Attention (ECSA) module and a Power IoU (PIoU) loss function. Experimental results showed improvements in both detection accuracy and robustness under low-light and high-occlusion conditions. The above studies verify that attention mechanisms and feature reconstruction strategies can effectively improve feature discrimination and model robustness under complex agricultural backgrounds, which provides a technical reference for the feature optimization design of this study. 2.3. Lightweight detection models for agricultural applications Real-time agricultural applications often require deployment on resource-constrained edge devices, making lightweight model design an important research direction. Wei et al. [ 28 ] employed C3k2_Ghost modules and a Feature Refinement Module (FRM) to construct a lightweight detector for tomato detection tasks, achieving a favorable trade-off between accuracy and efficiency in complex agricultural environments. Wang et al. [ 29 ] proposed YOLO-Weed Nano by replacing the YOLOv8n backbone with DS_HGNetV2 and introducing a LiteDetect head. The resulting model reduced the parameter count to 1.09 M (a 63.8% reduction in parameter count) while achieving 93.1% mAP50 and an inference speed of 227.3 FPS. Hu et al. [ 30 ] developed GBM-YOLOv5 for cotton impurity detection by integrating GhostBottleneck and SoftPool modules, significantly reducing computational complexity while improving detection accuracy. Likewise, Karim et al. [ 31 ] introduced the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) and C3Ghost modules into a lightweight YOLOv8 framework, improving weed recognition performance while maintaining suitability for edge deployment, and further developed a graphical user interface to support practical field operation. For cross-crop scenarios, Li et al. [ 32 ] proposed YOLOv7-FWeed for soybean fields by introducing an improved activation function and a multi-head self-attention module. Recent studies show that lightweight convolution, feature reuse, and efficient feature fusion can reduce computational overhead while retaining competitive detection performance. These findings provide guidance for deploying weed detectors on agricultural robots and intelligent spraying platforms. 2.4. Research gap and motivation Although existing studies have achieved considerable progress in agricultural weed detection, several challenges remain. First, cotton field environments are characterized by significant weed diversity, complex background interference, and frequent leaf occlusion, which continue to affect detection accuracy. Second, many high-accuracy models rely on increasingly complex network architectures, resulting in higher computational costs and reduced deployment efficiency. Third, lightweight models often sacrifice feature representation capability to achieve computational savings, leading to performance degradation when detecting small or partially occluded weeds. Therefore, there remains a need for a detection framework that can simultaneously enhance feature representation, improve small-object perception, and maintain computational efficiency. To address these challenges, this study proposes YOLO-CCG, which integrates CA, CARAFE, GSConv, and VoVGSCSP modules to improve weed detection performance while preserving deployment feasibility in real-world agricultural applications. 3. Methods 3.1. YOLO11 model The YOLO11 series provides five model variants: n, s, m, l, and x, with different depth and width scaling factors to adapt to different application scenarios and hardware constraints. The architecture mainly consists of three core components: backbone, neck, and head, as shown in Fig 1 . 10.1371/journal.pone.0357780.g001 Fig 1 YOLO11 network structure diagram. The backbone is responsible for multi-scale feature extraction, capturing key visual attributes such as leaf shape, color, and texture, which form the basis for subsequent target classification and localization. The neck acts as an intermediate module that receives multi-level feature maps from the backbone and performs feature fusion. It adopts a top-down and bottom-up multi-level feature fusion strategy to optimize channel information and spatial resolution, generating more robust feature representations for the detection head. This process is critical for distinguishing weeds from cotton plants under complex field conditions. Finally, the head performs weed classification and localization based on the refined feature maps. It learns category-specific features from annotated data to predict bounding boxes and category probabilities of weed targets. Despite its strong general object detection performance, YOLO11s still faces specific challenges when applied to cotton weed detection. The high diversity of weed species and their morphological similarity to cotton seedlings (especially in color and leaf texture) increase the difficulty of accurate classification. In addition, variable field conditions such as fluctuating illumination and complex soil backgrounds further degrade detection reliability. Therefore, targeted optimizations are necessary to improve the accuracy and robustness of YOLO11s for this specific agricultural task. 3.2. Proposed model To enhance the performance of the YOLO11 algorithm for cotton weed detection, this study introduces several architectural modifications based on the YOLO11s framework. First, the CA module is inserted immediately after the Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fast (SPPF) module in the backbone to enhance the model’s ability to capture position-sensitive features and improve target localization accuracy. Second, the original nearest-neighbor interpolation upsampling operators in the neck are replaced with CARAFE modules, which adaptively reconstruct fine-grained local details and contextual information during upsampling. Finally, the standard convolutional layers and C3k2 modules in the neck are substituted with GSConv and VoVGSCSP modules, respectively. These replacements streamline the feature fusion and processing pipeline, achieving a balance between improved detection performance and reduced computational complexity. The overall architecture of the proposed model, termed YOLO-CCG, is depicted in Fig 2 . 10.1371/journal.pone.0357780.g002 Fig 2 Proposed YOLO-CCG model architecture. 3.2.1. Coordinate attention. In natural field environments, crop cultivation and weed growth often involve challenges such as overlapping foliage and spatial occlusion. These factors impose stringent demands on detection models, particularly regarding localization precision and the capability to accurately capture target shapes. Conventional approaches often enhance feature representation by increasing model parameters, which leads to a significant increase in computational complexity and can dilute the model’s focus on salient features. Attention mechanisms can improve model performance by suppressing background noise and highlighting key regions without substantially increasing computational overhead. Nonetheless, conventional attention modules, such as Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) and Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM), may compromise precise positional information when encoding channel weights and struggle to effectively capture long-range dependencies. To address these issues, we incorporate the CA mechanism into the YOLO11 framework. CA factorizes two-dimensional global pooling into two parallel one-dimensional feature encoding operations along the height and width dimensions. Together, these operations capture long-range dependencies along one spatial direction while retaining precise positional information along the other. The resulting attention maps are direction-aware and position-sensitive, helping the network delineate weed contours and localize weed instances in complex field scenes. The structure of CA is illustrated in Fig 3 . 10.1371/journal.pone.0357780.g003 Fig 3 Schematic of Coordinate Attention. Specifically, pooling along the width dimension for each height coordinate generates the height-oriented feature map z h ∈ R C × H , as shown in Eq (1) : z c h ( h ) = 1 W ∑ 0 ≤ i < W x c ( h , i ) (1) where z c h ( h ) represents the pooled output for the c -th channel at height h , and x c ( h , i ) denotes the feature value at spatial location ( h , i ) . Similarly, pooling along the height dimension for each width coordinate generates the width-oriented feature map z w ∈ R C × W , as shown in the following Eq (2) : z c w ( w ) = 1 H ∑ 0 ≤ j < H x c ( j , w ) (2) where z c w ( w ) represents the pooled output for the c -th channel at width w . Subsequently, the two feature maps z h and z w are concatenated along the spatial dimension and passed through a shared bottleneck transformation. This process can be formulated as shown in Eq (3) : f = δ ( F 1 ( [ z h , z w ] ) ) (3) where [ z h , z w ] denotes the concatenation, F 1 is a 1 × 1 convolutional transformation function, and δ is a non-linear activation function. The bottleneck channel dimension is C / r . Here, r is the reduction ratio. The resulting intermediate feature map f ∈ R C / r × ( H + W ) is then split along the spatial dimension into two tensors, f h ∈ R C / r × H and f w ∈ R C / r × W . Two additional 1 × 1 convolutional transformations F h and F w are applied to f h and f w , respectively, to restore the channel number to C . The corresponding height and width attention weights are then generated using the sigmoid activation function, as shown in Eqs (4) and (5): g h = σ ( F h ( f h ) ) (4) g w = σ ( F w ( f w ) ) (5) where σ is the sigmoid function, g h ∈ R C × H , g w ∈ R C × W . The final output Y is obtained by element-wise multiplying the input tensor X by the attention weights g h and g w , as shown in Eq (6) : y c ( i , j ) = x c ( i , j ) × g c h ( i ) × g c w ( j ) (6) where y c ( i , j ) is the value of the output feature map at spatial location ( i , j ) . By explicitly embedding coordinate information into channel attention, the CA module enhances the model’s ability to capture long-range dependencies while maintaining a lightweight structure. This significantly improves the perception of target positions and shapes, providing an effective solution for the accurate detection of weeds in natural scenes. 3.2.2. CARAFE. The original neck module of YOLO11 utilizes nearest-neighbor interpolation for upsampling. This method, which determines the upsampling kernel based solely on pixel spatial positions without considering the semantic content of the feature maps, suffers from a limited receptive field and fails to capture rich contextual information. To address this limitation, we replaced the standard upsampling method with the CARAFE operator. As illustrated in Fig 4 , the CARAFE algorithm consists of two core components: a kernel prediction module that generates adaptive upsampling kernels based on local content, and a content-aware reassembly module that performs the feature reassembly using these predicted kernels. 10.1371/journal.pone.0357780.g004 Fig 4 Schematic of CARAFE. Given an input feature map X of shape H × W × C and an upsampling ratio σ , where H , W and C denote height, width, and the number of channels, respectively, CARAFE first employs the kernel prediction module to generate the upsampling kernels and then utilizes the content-aware reassembly module to produce the output feature map X ′ of size σ H × σ W × C . In the kernel prediction module, the input feature map is first processed by a 1 × 1 convolution to compress the channel dimension from C to C m . Subsequently, a convolutional layer with a kernel size of k e n c o d e r × k e n c o d e r and a stride of 1 is applied, yielding a feature map with σ 2 k u p 2 channels, where k u p is the reassembly kernel size. This output is then reorganized to form the adaptive upsampling kernels with a shape of σ H × σ W × k u p 2 . These dynamically generated kernels are content-aware, enabling the capture of more precise contextual information. Finally, the kernels are normalized via a softmax function to ensure that the sum of the weights equals one. The content-aware reassembly module is responsible for the actual upsampling operation. For each target location l ′ , the module extracts a k u p × k u p neighborhood N ( X l , k u p ) centered at the corresponding source location l in the input feature map. The reassembly is achieved by performing a matrix multiplication between the unfolded neighborhood N ( X l , k u p ) and the normalized kernel W l ′ , ultimately generating the new, upsampled feature map X ′ of size σ H × σ W × C . Through its content-aware mechanism, CARAFE adaptively learns the optimal sampling strategy for local regions, thereby effectively extracting detailed texture and contextual semantic information. Compared to conventional upsampling methods, CARAFE benefits from a larger and more adaptive receptive field, producing feature maps that are richer in information and thus providing superior feature representations for subsequent detection tasks. 3.2.3. GSConv and VoVGSCSP. GSConv integrates the advantages of both standard convolution and depthwise convolution (DWConv) [ 21 , 33 ]. As illustrated in Fig 5 , the standard convolutional layer is first applied to the input feature map to produce C 2 / 2 channels. A depthwise convolution is subsequently applied to this intermediate feature map to generate another C 2 / 2 channels. The two feature maps are concatenated and channel-shuffled [ 34 ], producing an output with C 2 channels. Thus, GSConv combines the feature representation capability of standard convolution with the low computational cost of depthwise convolution. 10.1371/journal.pone.0357780.g005 Fig 5 Structure of GSConv. In the neck network, we adopt a Slim-Neck design by replacing the original convolutional modules and C3k2 modules with GSConv and VoVGSCSP modules, respectively, to improve feature fusion efficiency and detection accuracy. The structure of the GS bottleneck is shown in Fig 6(a) . The input is processed by two branches: one passes through two sequential GSConv modules, and the other is processed by a single standard convolution module. The outputs of these two branches are then added by element-wise summation to form the final output. 10.1371/journal.pone.0357780.g006 Fig 6 GS bottleneck and VoVGSCSP modules. Building upon the GS bottleneck, the VoVGSCSP module is introduced to replace the original C3k2 module in YOLO11, thereby enhancing feature extraction capacity. Its architecture is depicted in Fig 6(b) . The input is first processed by two parallel convolutional layers, each producing half the number of target output channels. One branch serves as a shortcut connection directly to the concatenation layer, while the other undergoes feature extraction through a GSBottleneck module. The features from both branches are then concatenated along the channel dimension and fused by a final convolutional layer. The integration of GSConv and VoVGSCSP modules not only reduces the model’s computational burden but also enhances its feature representation capacity and multi-scale perceptual ability for target regions. 4. Experiments and analysis To evaluate the performance of the proposed model, we first conducted ablation studies to quantify the contribution of each component and their combined effect. We then compared the proposed model with representative detectors using the same evaluation metrics. Finally, qualitative visualizations were examined to complement the quantitative results. 4.1. Dataset This study employed the public CottonWeedDet12 dataset [ 22 ] for all experiments. The dataset contains 5,648 RGB images with 9,370 annotated bounding boxes in total. These images were captured using smartphones or handheld digital cameras under natural lighting conditions between June and September 2021, covering diverse illumination scenarios, weed growth stages, weather conditions, and field locations. The dataset includes twelve distinct weed categories, with all images stored in JPEG format. For consistency, all images were uniformly resized to 640 × 640 pixels with aspect-ratio-preserving padding. To robustly evaluate the model’s performance and generalization capability, the dataset was randomly split into training, validation, and test sets with ratios of 75%, 15%, and 10%, respectively. The first set of experiments introduced the CA module into YOLO11s. Compared with the baseline model, the CA variant improved precision, recall, mAP50, and mAP50-95 by 1.7%, 0.1%, 0.2% and 0.7%, respectively. Meanwhile, the parameter count remained unchanged at 9.4 M, while GFLOPs increased slightly by 0.1 GFLOPs. These results indicate that the CA module can effectively enhance feature representation with negligible computational overhead. The second set of experiments replaced the original upsampling operation with the CARAFE module. Compared with the baseline model, the CARAFE variant improved precision, recall, mAP50, and mAP50-95 by 1.1%, 1.0%, 0.5% and 0.3%, respectively. However, the parameter count increased to 9.6 M and GFLOPs increased to 21.6 GFLOPs, resulting in a slight reduction in inference speed. This shows that CARAFE can improve feature reconstruction capability and enhance detection performance. The third set of experiments replaced the standard convolution and C3k2 modules in the neck network with GSConv and VoVGSCSP, respectively. Compared with the baseline model, the GSConv + VoVGSCSP variant improved precision, recall, mAP50, and mAP50-95 by 0.8%, 0.2%, 0.2%, and 0.5%, respectively. Meanwhile, the parameter count decreased from 9.4 M to 9.3 M and GFLOPs decreased from 21.3 GFLOPs to 20.2 GFLOPs. In addition, the inference speed increased significantly to 285.7 frames/s. These results indicate that GSConv and VoVGSCSP effectively reduce computational complexity while maintaining competitive detection accuracy. The fourth set of experiments combined the CA and CARAFE modules. Compared with the baseline model, the CA+CARAFE variant decreased precision by 0.7% and improved recall, mAP50, and mAP50-95 by 1.9%, 1.1%, and 1.0%, respectively. This result indicates that the combination of attention enhancement and content-aware upsampling can strengthen feature extraction and localization capability, although a slight trade-off in precision was observed. The final experiment corresponds to the proposed YOLO-CCG model, which integrates the CA, CARAFE, GSConv, and VoVGSCSP modules. Compared with the baseline model, YOLO-CCG improved precision, recall, mAP50, and mAP50-95 by 0.4%, 2.2%, 0.8%, and 1.7%, respectively. At the same time, the parameter count remained unchanged at 9.4 M, while GFLOPs decreased by 4.2%. Although the inference speed was 4.1% lower than that of the baseline model and lower than that of the GSConv-based lightweight model, YOLO-CCG achieved consistent improvements across all four accuracy metrics while maintaining a lightweight architecture. These results demonstrate that the complementary integration of CA, CARAFE, GSConv, and VoVGSCSP improves feature representation, feature fusion, and multi-scale detection capability, thereby enhancing overall detection performance. In practical field environments, weed targets often exhibit significant variations in size, complex background interference, and partial occlusion caused by cotton leaves. The Coordinate Attention module enhances feature representation by simultaneously modeling channel relationships and long-range positional information. This mechanism enables the network to focus more effectively on discriminative weed regions while suppressing irrelevant background information. As a result, the model is better able to distinguish weeds from visually similar crop leaves and surrounding vegetation. The CARAFE upsampling operator further improves feature reconstruction during multi-scale feature fusion. Unlike conventional interpolation-based upsampling methods, CARAFE generates content-aware kernels according to local semantic information, allowing fine-grained features to be preserved during feature propagation. Its inference speed of 204.1 FPS remains compatible with real-time processing on the evaluated platform. From an application perspective, agricultural intelligent spraying systems and field robots often operate on resource-constrained edge devices that require both real-time processing capability and high detection accuracy. The lightweight design of YOLO-CCG suggests potential for meeting these deployment requirements while maintaining robust weed detection performance under complex field conditions. Overall, YOLO-CCG achieves a favorable trade-off among detection accuracy, computational efficiency, and deployment feasibility, demonstrating strong potential for real-time cotton weed detection and precision agriculture applications. 4.6. Comparison of different attention mechanisms To quantitatively validate the advantages of the CA module for cotton weed detection, this study compared five common attention mechanisms: Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE), Efficient Multi-scale Attention (EMA), Efficient Channel Attention (ECA), Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM), and Global Attention Mechanism (GAM) [ 43 – 47 ]. SE compresses channel features through global average pooling and then learns channel weights via a fully connected layer to enhance critical channels. EMA splits features into groups and applies parallel 1 × 1 and 3 × 3 convolutions to achieve cross-dimension interaction and multi-scale aggregation at minimal computational cost. ECA employs one-dimensional convolutions to capture inter-channel dependencies, which reduces parameters and improves efficiency. CBAM is an attention mechanism that integrates spatial and channel attention. GAM significantly enhances the performance of deep neural networks in cross-dimensional interactions by preserving and strengthening both channel and spatial information. Conclusion To mitigate false positives and missed detections in complex cotton-field environments, this study proposes an improved YOLO11-based detector named YOLO-CCG. The model integrates Coordinate Attention, CARAFE, GSConv, and VoVGSCSP to strengthen feature representation while controlling computational complexity. Specifically, the CA module strengthens spatial and channel information modeling, enabling more accurate localization of weed targets. The CARAFE module uses content-aware reassembly during upsampling to retain fine-grained local information. Meanwhile, GSConv and VoVGSCSP reduce redundant computation in the corresponding bottom-up neck blocks while retaining feature fusion operations. Experimental results on the CottonWeedDet12 dataset demonstrate that YOLO-CCG achieves a precision of 94.8%, recall of 90.0%, mAP50 of 95.1%, and mAP50-95 of 90.0%. Compared with the baseline YOLO11s model, precision, recall, mAP50, and mAP50-95 are improved by 0.4%, 2.2%, 0.8%, and 1.7%, respectively, in relative terms. Meanwhile, the computational complexity is reduced from 21.3 GFLOPs to 20.4 GFLOPs while maintaining a lightweight architecture with only 9.4 M parameters. In addition, statistical analysis confirms that the improvements in precision, mAP50, and mAP50-95 are statistically significant (p < 0.05), demonstrating the effectiveness and reliability of the proposed architecture. Visualization results further reveal that YOLO-CCG can focus more accurately on weed regions and extract more discriminative features under complex backgrounds. Although the proposed model achieves favorable performance, several limitations remain. First, the inference speed decreases slightly compared with the baseline model due to the introduction of additional feature enhancement modules. Second, the current experiments are conducted on a single cotton weed dataset, which may limit the model’s generalization ability across different crops and field environments. Future work will focus on incorporating multimodal information such as infrared and hyperspectral imagery, developing adaptive attention mechanisms for dense weed scenarios, and applying model compression techniques including knowledge distillation and network pruning to facilitate deployment on resource-constrained agricultural edge devices. Furthermore, larger multi-crop weed datasets will be collected to improve model robustness and generalization performance in practical agricultural applications.
