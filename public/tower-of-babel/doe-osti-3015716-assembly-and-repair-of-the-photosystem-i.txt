Award Number: DE-SC0020119
Project Title: Assembly and Repair of the Photosystem II Reaction Center
Funding Organization: Office of Basic Energy Sciences
Principal Investigator: David J. Vinyard
Institution: Louisiana State University and A&M College
Project Period: 09/01/2019 – 08/31/2024
Report Type: Final Technical Report

Abstract
This project investigated the biochemical and biophysical mechanisms governing
assembly and repair of Photosystem II (PSII), the membrane protein complex
responsible for solar-driven water oxidation in oxygenic photosynthesis. The work
focused on three integrated areas: (1) protein–protein interactions that facilitate PSII
assembly in cyanobacterial biogenesis centers, (2) the chemical mechanism of photo-
assembly of the Mn4CaO5 oxygen-evolving complex (OEC), and (3) mechanisms that
target PSII reaction centers for degradation and repair in photosynthetic organisms.
Using electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy, protein biochemistry, molecular
genetics, quantitative mass spectrometry, and computational modeling, the project
demonstrated that proton release events limit early steps of OEC assembly and that
chloride and calcium ions facilitate Mn oxidation and intermediate stabilization.
Complementary studies identified chaperone recruitment mechanisms in cyanobacterial
PSII biogenesis centers and translation and protease factors involved in PSII turnover in
Chlamydomonas. Together, these results establish proton management and coordinated
protein quality control as central design principles in PSII assembly and repair and
provide mechanistic insight relevant to biological and artificial photosynthetic systems.

Project Goals and Scope
The major goals of this project were to determine the chemical and proton-transfer
mechanisms controlling Mn4CaO5 oxygen-evolving complex photo-assembly, identify
protein–protein interactions that support PSII assembly in cyanobacterial biogenesis
centers, and define molecular mechanisms governing PSII reaction center turnover and
repair. These goals were pursued through coordinated spectroscopic, biochemical,
genetic, and computational approaches.

Technical Accomplishments by Aim
Aim 1 – PSII Assembly Protein–Protein Interactions
This aim focused on identifying and characterizing protein–protein interactions that
facilitate assembly of PSII reaction centers in cyanobacterial biogenesis membranes.
Expression systems were established for PratA, DnaK isoforms, DnaJ, and related
chaperones. In vitro pull-down assays, mutagenesis, and truncation analysis
demonstrated that C-terminal regions of DnaK proteins contribute to binding specificity.
Mutant analysis in cyanobacterial strains showed reduced PSII activity and altered
membrane association of chaperones when PratA was disrupted. Fluorescent tagging
experiments revealed punctate localization patterns consistent with biogenesis center
recruitment. The work expanded to include DnaK–DnaJ interaction controls, providing
broader mechanistic insight into chaperone-assisted membrane protein assembly.

Aim 2 – OEC Photo-Assembly Mechanism
This aim defined the chemical and proton-coupled steps governing assembly of the
Mn4CaO5 oxygen-evolving complex. Dual-mode EPR spectroscopy, kinetic
measurements, and controlled biochemical assays demonstrated that early OEC
assembly steps are limited by proton release events associated with Mn oxidation.
Chloride was shown to facilitate these steps by tuning proton-transfer energetics, and
Ca2+ binding was required for efficient Mn(III) intermediate accumulation. Spectroscopic
detection of Mn intermediates under varying pH and anion conditions provided direct
mechanistic evidence linking proton release to rate-limiting steps. Mutational and
structural analyses further demonstrated that hydrogen-bond networks and channel
conformations near the OEC regulate proton transfer efficiency during both assembly
and catalytic stabilization.

Aim 3 – PSII Repair and Turnover
This aim investigated how PSII centers are targeted for degradation and repair. Stable
isotope labeling strategies and quantitative mass spectrometry methods were developed
to measure D1 turnover rates. A forward genetic screen and mutant analysis in
Chlamydomonas identified candidate proteases and translation factors involved in PSII
repair. Deg-family proteases were characterized through localization and genetic
approaches, and CRISPR-Cas9 mutants and complements were generated. A psbA
translation factor, HCF244, was functionally characterized in algae, demonstrating both
conserved and divergent features relative to plant systems. Experimental results were
integrated with numerical simulations to evaluate PSII turnover targeting models.

Scientific and Technical Impact
The project produced new mechanistic insight into proton-coupled metal cluster
assembly, PSII repair, and chaperone-assisted membrane protein biogenesis. Results

clarified rate-limiting steps in OEC assembly, defined ion and proton coupling effects,
and identified specific protein factors involved in assembly and repair pathways. These
findings inform the broader fields of bioenergetics, catalytic water oxidation, and artificial
photosynthesis design. Methodological advances in EPR spectroscopy and quantitative
turnover measurements also strengthened research infrastructure and analytical
capabilities.
