Trends in both Alzheimer’s disease and Diabetes mellitus related mortality among middle-aged and older adults in the United States, 1999 to 2023: a CDC WONDER database analysis
Authors: Aiman Javed, Shiva Kumar, Manahil Iftekhar, Hafiza Tooba Siddiqui, Ayesha Khan, Fatima Faisal, Muaz Ali, Fatima Sohail, Muhammad Hanif Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo analyze the temporal trends of both Alzheimer’s disease and Diabetes mellitus-related mortality in adults aged > 45years in the United States between 1999 and 2023, and to evaluate the changes in mortality patterns over time.BackgroundAlzheimer’s Disease and Diabetes Mellitus are two different diseases that have diverse underlying pathophysiology, but they often coexist, having common pathways. There is a high prevalence of concurrence between these two conditions, yet their combined mortality trend is underexplored.MethodsWe utilize mortality data from the CDC Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER). Individuals aged > 45 were included who had both Alzheimer’s disease (G30) and Diabetes mellitus(E10-14). Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) and crude mortality rates (CMRs) per 100,000 were calculated and were standardized to the 2000 U.S population. Joint point regression models were used to identify the temporal variations and to calculate Annual Percentage Change (APC) and Average Annual Percentage Change (AAPC) with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsOverall, a total of 224,082 deaths occurred in patients of both Alzheimer’s disease and Diabetes mellitus, in the age group ≥ 45 years, from 1999 to 2023. There is an upward trajectory noted from 2.82 in 1999 to 4.42 in 2023, with the highest incidence between 2017 and 2020, followed by a decline. Mortality rose in both sexes, with a persistently higher rate in females. The mortality rise from 1999 to 2023 in middle-aged people (45–64 years), and there was a rise in the trend of around 41% among adults ≥ 65 years. White individuals show higher deaths (78.9%), yet higher AAMR is observed in Black and Hispanic populations, showing racial disparities. Regionally, the West shows the highest AAMR, while non-metropolitan areas show higher mortality than metropolitan areas.ConclusionThe trend of mortality in individuals with both Alzheimer’s disease and Diabetes Mellitus has increased in the past two decades, but there is a sharp rise observed after 2020 that may show the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. These findings emphasized public health strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05058-2.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that results in memory problems, cognitive dysfunction, and loss of the ability to perform everyday activities. There is no cure for the disease, which primarily strikes those who are adults aged 65 years and older. Alzheimer’s disease AD resulted in more than 119,000 deaths in the United States, including being the seventh leading cause of death in America [1]. An aging population and greater coding of AD as a cause of death-on-death certificates explain the continued rise in AD deaths since 1999 [2, 3]. Overall and by sex, race, and state of residence area [4], age-adjusted rates were calculated per 100,000 population; increases in rates from 1999 to 2022 are presented. Diabetes mellitus, type 1 (T1DM) and type 2 (T2DM), are chronic metabolic disorders accompanied by prolonged elevated blood glucose, as well as consisting of prevalent microvascular and macrovascular complications. An estimated 38 million Americans, or one in nine adults, have diabetes [5]. Despite improved management and increased awareness, diabetes remains an important contributor to CVD mortality and premature mortality overall [6]. Diabetes mellitus was the eighth leading underlying cause of death in the United States in 2023, accounting for more than 95,000 death certificates [7]. Previous analyses show that, although DM mortality decreased significantly during the early 2000s, this trend may have stabilized or reversed in later years [8, 9].

The relationship between DM and AD can be localized more by the way that both have some common pathophysiologic mechanisms, including insulin resistance, vascular damage in the body, and inflammation (before they develop) [10]. Patients with diabetes are in much greater danger of developing AD and cognitive impairment, which is why these conditions must be addressed together in mortality surveillance [11]. Recent epidemiological studies suggested that DM increases the risk of dementia-related mortality if develops in midlife and persists for longer duration [12]. Time trends in AD and DM mortality over periods of time, where a common analytic design allowed for direct comparison, have rarely been compared. The CDC WONDER Multiple Cause of Death file for 1999–2023 presents age-adjusted, population-based rates by demographic characteristic. This study aims to quantify mortality trend involving both AD and DM among U.S. adults from ≥ 45 years older, with the objective of identifying temporal patterns, associations, and disparities to inform future prevention strategies, healthcare planning, and public health policy efforts in the United States.

Methodology

Study setting and population

This study utilizes data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s (CDC) Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER) website to examine trends in mortality related to coexisting Alzheimer’s disease and diabetes mellitus in all U.S. states from 1999 to 2023 [13]. The information was taken from the Multiple Cause of Death (MCOD) dataset, which aggregates data from U.S. resident death certificates.

Decedents aged more than 45 years were included if they had both Alzheimer’s disease and diabetes mellitus. The International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes are G30 for Alzheimer’s disease and E10-14 for diabetes mellitus [14, 15]. These codes have been validated and used in prior studies.

This study utilizes publicly available, de-identified data from CDC WONDER database and has no human interaction. According to Institutional policies for analysis of publicly available data, this study was considered exempt from Institutional Review Board (IRB) review. The study adhered to the Strengthening Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines to ensure transparency of the study [16].

Data abstraction

Data on mortality, including age, sex, race, state, census, urbanization, and location of death, were taken from 1999 to 2023. Medical facilities (inpatient, outpatient, emergency room, death on arrival), the decedent’s home, hospice facility, nursing home, or unknown.

The age group was divided into two categories: 45–64 and 65 years. Hispanic or Latino, Non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH Black, NH American Indian or Alaska Native, and NH Asian or Pacific Islander were the categories for race and ethnicity. The National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Urban-Rural Classification Scheme was used to classify urbanization, dividing nations into metropolitan and non-metropolitan categories [17]. The four geographic regions are categorized as South, Midwest, Northeast, and West.

Statistical analysis

The age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) and crude mortality rate (CMR) per 100,000 were computed for deaths involving both Alzheimer’s disease and Diabetes Mellitus. By dividing the number of fatalities by the entire population of that year, the crude mortality rate is calculated. 95% CIs were supplied for AAMRs, which were standardized to the 2000 U.S. standard population [18].

The Join point Regression Program was used to identify temporal trends. It fits a log-linear regression model that detects significant trend changes and calculates Average Annual Percentage Changes (AAPCs) and Annual Percentage Changes (APCs) with 95% confidence intervals [19]. A p-value of less than 0.05 indicates that a trend is statistically significant.

Results

Annual trends for Alzheimer’s disease mortality in patients with Diabetes mellitus

In individuals with both Alzheimer’s disease and Diabetes mellitus, the overall age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) rose from 2.82 (95% CI: 2.74 to 2.90) in 1999 to 4.42 (95% CI: 4.34 to 4.50) in 2023. A significant rise was observed in the trend between 1999 and 2023. From 1999 to 2005, there was a significant increase in mortality rate (APC: 6.00%; CI: 3.71 to 8.34 ; p < 0.05) than followed by an insignificant rise in trend from 2005 to 2017 (APC: -0.59%; CI: -1.31 to 0.12 ; p > 0.05) than, there was a significant increase in trend between 2017 and 2020 (APC: 10.87%; CI: 0.64 to 22.13 ; p < 0.05). Between 2020 and 2023, there was a significant decrease in the trend (APC: -5.18%; CI: -9.37 to -0.78; p < 0.05) (Tables 1 and 2, and Fig. 1).

Trends stratified by gender

Females consistently maintained higher Age-Adjusted Mortality Rates (AAMRs) across all years, with an overall AAMR of 4.01 compared to 3.86 among males.

In males, there was a significant increase in mortality trend from 1999 to 2005 (APC: 6.11%; CI: 2.52 to 9.83; p < 0.05), followed by an insignificant decrease in the trend from 2005 to 2015 (APC: -1.04%; CI: -2.57 to 0.51; p > 0.05). From 2015 to 2021, there was a gradual, significant increase in trend (APC: 6.08%; CI: 2.85 to 9.41; p > 0.05) followed by a steeper, insignificant decrease in trend from 2021 to 2025 (APC: -8.31%; CI: -19.80 to 4.82; p > 0.05).

In females, there was a significant increase in a trend from 1999 to 2005 (APC: 6.24%; CI: 3.90 to 8.63 ; p < 0.05), followed by a gradual, insignificant decrease in a trend from 2005 to 2017 (APC: -0.66%; CI: -1.40 to 0.08 ; p > 0.05). From 2017 to 2020, there was an insignificant increase in a trend (APC: 10.55%; CI: -0.17 to 22.43; p > 0.05) followed by a steeper, significant decline in a trend from 2020 to 2023 (APC: -5.12%; CI: -9.55 to -0.47; p < 0.05) (Table 2, Supplemental Tables 1, and Fig. 1).

Trends stratified by age

Most age groups were showing an increase in mortality. In adults aged 45–64 years, the crude death rate rose from 0.09 in 1999–2020 to 0.15 in 2021–2023. In contrast, adults aged ≥ 65 years experienced a slower growth, however crude death rate remain elevated from 19.94 in 1999–2020 to 21.32 in 2021–2023. Overall, mortality showed an increasing trend in middle-aged and older adults.

Among the age group 45–64 years (middle age group), there was a significant increase in mortality trend from 1999 to 2023 (APC: 1.58%; CI: 0.34 to 2.83; p < 0.05).

Among the age group ≥ 65 years (older adults), there was a significantly steeper incline in a trend from 1999 to 2005 (APC: 6.01%; CI: 3.74 to 8.32 ; p < 0.05) followed by a gradual decrease in a trend from 2005 to 2017 (APC: -0.59%; CI: -1.30 to 0.12 ; p > 0.05). From 2017 to 2020, there was a significantly steeper incline in the trend (APC: 10.82%; CI: 0.68 to 21.98; p < 0.05), followed by an insignificant decrease in the trend from 2020 to 2023 (APC: -5.16%; CI: -9.32 to -0.81; p < 0.05) (Table 2, Supplemental Tables 2, and Fig. 2).

Trends stratified by race

The Hispanic or Latino individuals accounted for 8.59% deaths, the vast majority of deaths occurred in non-Hispanic individuals (91.41%), with White individuals comprising the largest proportion (78.98%), followed by Black/African American (10.84%), Asian or Pacific Islander (2.88%), and American Indian or Alaska Native (0.45%).

Among Asian or Pacific Islander, there was a significant gradual increase in mortality trend from 1999 to 2010 (APC: 8.31%; CI: 4.97 to 11.75 ; p < 0.05) followed by an insignificant decrease in the trend from 2010 to 2013 (APC: -7.94%; CI: -31.21 to 23.19 ; p > 0.05). From 2013 to 2023, there was a significant gradual increase in a trend (APC: 3.33%; CI: 1.46 to 5.23; p < 0.05).

Among Black/African American, there was a significant increase in a mortality trend from 1999 to 2005 (APC: 7.68%; CI: 4.16 to 11.31 ; p < 0.05) followed by a significant decrease in a trend from 2005 to 2017 (APC: -1.69%; CI: -2.75 to -0.62 ; p < 0.05). From 2017 to 2020, there was an insignificant increase in trend (APC: 11.49%; CI: -3.92 to 29.39; p > 0.05) followed by a significant decrease in trend from 2020 to 2023 (APC: -8.38%; CI: -14.40 to -1.94; p < 0.05).

Among White individuals, there was a similar trend. From 1999 to 2005, there was a significant increase in the trend (APC: 6.12%; CI: 3.17 to 9.15; p < 0.05), followed by a significant gradual decrease in the trend from 2005 to 2015 (APC: -1.39%; CI: -2.70 to -0.06; p < 0.05). From 2015 to 2021, there was a significant increase in a trend (APC: 6.06%; CI: 3.07 to 9.14; p < 0.05) followed by an insignificant decrease in a trend from 2021 to 2023 (APC: -6.09%; CI: -16.44 to 5.54; p > 0.05).

Among Hispanic individuals, there was a progressive increase followed by a decrease in the trend. From 1999 to 2005, there was a significant increase in a trend (APC: 8.01%; CI: 4.40 to 11.75; p < 0.05) followed by an insignificant gradual increase in a trend from 2005 to 2017 (APC: 0.77%; CI: -0.08 to 1.64; p > 0.05). From 2017 to 2020, there was a significantly steeper incline in the trend (APC: 13.55%; CI: 3.20 to 24.94; p < 0.05), followed by a significant decrease in the mortality trend from 2020 to 2023 (APC: -7.73%; CI: -11.65 to -3.62; p < 0.05) (Table 2, Supplemental Tables 3, and Fig. 3). In American Indian or Alaska Native populations, the mortality data were unreliable.

Trends stratified by geographic region

The highest AAMR was observed in the West (4.66; 95% CI: 4.46–4.86), followed by the Midwest (4.28; 95% CI: 4.09–4.46), then the South (4.20; 95% CI: 4.06–4.35), followed by the Northeast (2.66; 95% CI: 2.50–2.81).

In the West region, there was a significant increase in a trend from 1999 to 2006 (APC: 6.68%; CI: 4.54 to 8.85; p < 0.05) followed by an insignificant gradual increase in a trend from 2006 to 2018 (APC: 0.59%; CI: -0.17 to 1.36; p > 0.05). From 2018 to 2021, there was an insignificant increase in trend (APC: 9.89%; CI: -0.04 to 20.82; p > 0.05) followed by a steeper and significant decrease in the trend from 2021 to 2023 (APC: -11.01%; CI: -19.02 to -2.19; p < 0.05).

Discussion

In this national, population-level analysis of CDC WONDER data (1999–2023), we document 224,082 deaths among U.S. adults aged ≥ 45 years with both diabetes mellitus and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), of whom women accounted for two-thirds of deaths (62.9%). The age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) rose from 2.82 to 4.42 per 100,000 over the study period, but the temporal trend was non-linear with phases of accelerating (1999–2005; 2017–2020) and an interim decline in 2020–2023. These overall characteristics are consistent with recent estimates having reported increasing AD-related mortality in individuals with diabetes and increasing population burdens of AD that also reflect period-specific changes and disruptions (e.g., pandemic effects) [4, 12, 20].

Women made up a disproportionately high proportion of deaths. This is consistent with known epidemiologic patterns in which women have higher AD prevalence and lifetime risk because of greater survival, sex-specific biology (e.g., menopausal-related changes, hormonal and immunologic differences), and slopes of cognitive decline differences. These mechanisms—and concomitant vascular comorbidity and competing cause of death sex differences in men (men’s preclinical cardiovascular mortality)—presumably are responsible for the observed excess female mortality among the AD + diabetes cohort [21].

Statistically significant mortality increases in 45–64-year-olds is concerning and consistent with findings that midlife diabetes hugely increases subsequent risk of dementia and dementia mortality; earlier age and longer duration of diabetes multiply neurodegenerative process through hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, ad vascular injury [12, 22]. Our findings support this interpretation, and midlife metabolic risk therefore is a critical time for prevention.

Recent data indicate a statistically significant rise in mortality among adults aged 45–64. This is in concert with epidemiologic evidence that midlife diabetes markedly increases later dementia risk and mortality. For instance, one large twin-cohort study estimated that diabetes doubles dementia risk, with the strongest effect when diabetes begins before age 65 [22]. Similarly, population data indicate that younger age at diabetes onset and longer diabetes duration accelerate neurodegeneration. Thus, one Australian study estimated that diabetic subjects developed dementia ~ 2.2 years earlier and died ~ 2.6 years sooner than nondiabetics; subjects with ≥ 15-year diabetes histories and early-onset dementia had nearly twice the mortality rate of nondiabetics (HR ≈ 1.9) [23]. Together, these findings support the idea that midlife metabolic health represent a critical window for prevention include screening for diabetes, glycemic control, lifestyle modifications and reducing the risk of vascular injury lowering the risk for dementia.

Alzheimer’s-related deaths remain highest among non‐Hispanic whites, but temporal patterns differ markedly by race/ethnicity. For example, whites have the largest burden of AD mortality, but Black and Hispanic populations have different growth rates. Such heterogeneity will depend on many factors to include genetic risks (e.g., APOE ε4 prevalence), comorbidities, and social determinants. Notably, vascular risk factors vary by group: hypertension and diabetes are more prevalent in some communities. For instance, hypertension has been cited as a major contributor to higher AD risk in Black compared with White populations. Access to care and diagnostic practices also vary neurology specialists commonly are lacking within rural and socioeconomically disadvantaged areas, which contributes to higher age-adjusted AD mortality in non‐metropolitan regions (where vascular disease also is more common and resources scarcer). In sum, differences in risk‐factor profiles, healthcare access, and socioeconomic context help explain the observed racial/ethnic and urban–rural disparities in dementia mortality. Regional variation and larger non-metropolitan AAMRs likely arise from unequal access to specialist neurology care, higher burden of vascular risk, and socioeconomic disparities [4, 24].

The fall in AAMR after 2020 can partly be explained by pandemic distortions: (1) competing mortality causes (COVID-19 pandemic) generating substitute primary cause assignment; (2) disruption in chronic-disease care and diagnostic determination; and (3) changes in healthcare use and death certification during the pandemic era. The COVID-19 pandemic distorted mortality patterns after 2020. Much of the apparent fall in AD-related mortality is owing to competing mortality and reporting changes. In the first pandemic year, the COVID-19 pandemic directly or indirectly accounted for many deaths that otherwise might have been attributed to chronic causes. For example, one state-level analysis estimated that dementia accounted for 16.4% of the additional deaths associated with the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 [25]. At the same time, healthcare disruptions - lockdowns, delayed care, testing shortages - along with changed death certification practices led to undercounting or misclassification of dementia deaths. These factors mean the post-2020 decline in AD mortality rates needs to be interpreted with caution; it likely reflects pandemic-related reporting and care, rather than a true improvement in dementia outcomes [25–27]. Caution is therefore required to interpret such a fall as an actual reduction in AD-related mortality.

Diabetes accelerates AD development through synergistic processes—vascular injury, inflammation, insulin resistance, and disordered cerebral glucose metabolism—thereby augmenting mortality hazard over additive impacts [7, 15, 28, 29]. In addition, increased survival from cardiovascular illness over recent decades may paradoxically raise the number exposed to AD mortality (a competing-risk/survivor effect) [28, 30, 31].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include large national sample size and extended study duration to permit join point analyses and subgroup stratification. Weaknesses include deaths-certificate coding dependency (potential misclassification), lack of individual-level data on diabetes duration, glycemic control, AD severity, and potential pandemic artifacts on cause assignment. These limitations limit causal inference and emphasize the need for linked clinical registry and EHR studies [24, 29].

Implications

The results highlight the need for midlife prevention and control of diabetes as part of risk-reduction strategies for dementia, sex-specific studies and care pathways, and targeted interventions to bridge geographic and racial disparities—especially in non-metropolitan and underserved populations. Prospective cohort studies that relate diabetes courses, AD biomarkers, and cause-of-death analyses will be critical to untangle causal pathways and inform targeted interventions [12, 22, 23].

Conclusions

Mortality associated with both Alzheimer’s disease and diabetes mellitus has increased over the time with disparities in sex, gender, race, and geographical distributions. The mortality rise post 2020 may reflect changes associated with the COVID-19 pandemic. These findings underscore the need for preventing strategies targeting diabetes and vascular risk factors.

However, Alzheimer cannot be fully prevented but effective diabetes prevention and management through glycemic control, smoking cessation, cardiovascular risk reduction, and other lifestyle modifications can reduce mortality related to dementia.
