Causal inference-integrated temporal graph convolutional networks for dynamic prediction and optimization of enterprise total factor productivity
Authors: Gaoyuan Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTotal factor productivity (TFP) serves as a critical indicator for measuring enterprise efficiency and technological progress. However, existing prediction methods often fail to distinguish genuine causal mechanisms from spurious correlations while neglecting inter-enterprise network dependencies. This study proposes a Causal-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (CT-GCN) that integrates causal inference techniques with temporal graph convolutional networks for dynamic TFP prediction and optimization. The framework employs the Levinsohn-Petrin method for TFP estimation, double machine learning for causal effect identification, and constructs enterprise relationship graphs capturing supply chain linkages, geographic proximity, and technological similarity. Using panel data from 12,847 Chinese manufacturing enterprises spanning 2008–2022, empirical results demonstrate that CT-GCN achieves substantial prediction improvements over baseline models, with RMSE reductions exceeding 19%. The causal analysis identifies R&D investment, digital transformation, and human capital as genuine productivity drivers, with significant treatment effect heterogeneity across industry sectors, firm sizes, and regions. An optimization decision mechanism translates these insights into differentiated strategic recommendations. This research contributes a novel methodology bridging causal reasoning and deep learning for economic forecasting applications.Keywords: Total factor productivity, Causal inference, Temporal graph convolutional network, Double machine learning, Enterprise prediction, Optimization decisionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The digital economy has fundamentally reshaped the operational landscape of modern enterprises, bringing both unprecedented opportunities and complex challenges to productivity enhancement. Total factor productivity (TFP), as a core indicator measuring the efficiency of resource allocation and technological progress, has attracted considerable scholarly attention in recent years1. Understanding how enterprises can accurately predict and optimize their TFP has become not merely an academic inquiry but a pressing practical concern for policymakers and business leaders alike. Against this backdrop, the development of robust predictive frameworks that capture the dynamic nature of productivity evolution emerges as a critical research priority.

Traditional approaches to TFP measurement have largely relied on parametric methods such as the Cobb-Douglas production function and its extensions, which, despite their theoretical elegance, often struggle to accommodate the heterogeneity and non-linearity inherent in real-world production processes2. Semi-parametric estimation techniques, including the Olley-Pakes and Levinsohn-Petrin methods, have partially addressed endogeneity concerns, yet these frameworks remain fundamentally static in their conceptualization of productivity dynamics3. More recently, scholars have turned to machine learning algorithms to capture complex patterns in productivity data, though such data-driven approaches frequently sacrifice interpretability for predictive accuracy4. This trade-off raises a fundamental question: can we design analytical tools that simultaneously achieve high predictive performance and transparent causal reasoning?

Causal inference methods offer a promising avenue for addressing this challenge. The potential outcomes framework and structural causal models have been increasingly applied to economic phenomena, enabling researchers to disentangle genuine causal relationships from mere statistical associations5. In the context of enterprise productivity, understanding the causal mechanisms through which factors such as R&D investment, human capital, and digital transformation affect TFP holds substantial policy implications. However, existing causal inference applications in productivity research have predominantly focused on cross-sectional or panel settings, with limited attention to the temporal dynamics that characterize real business environments6. The evolution of productivity is inherently a time-dependent process, and capturing this temporal dimension requires methodological innovations beyond conventional causal frameworks.

Graph neural networks have emerged as a powerful paradigm for modeling relational data structures, demonstrating remarkable success across diverse domains from social network analysis to molecular property prediction7. Within economic forecasting, graph-based methods have shown particular promise in capturing the spatial dependencies and interconnections among economic agents. Enterprises do not operate in isolation; rather, they are embedded in complex networks of supply chains, competitive relationships, and knowledge spillovers that shape their productivity trajectories8. Temporal graph convolutional networks (T-GCN) extend this capability by jointly modeling spatial relationships and temporal dynamics, making them well-suited for productivity prediction tasks where both dimensions matter9. Nevertheless, the application of such architectures to TFP forecasting remains nascent, and critical questions regarding their integration with causal reasoning frameworks have yet to be systematically addressed. It is worth situating this work against a parallel line of research in which deep models are paired with metaheuristic optimizers—such as the Grey Wolf Optimizer10 and the Whale Optimization Algorithm11—for hyperparameter search and feature selection; that paradigm tackles the optimization of model configurations rather than the identification of causal structure, and so it complements rather than substitutes for the approach we pursue here. A related strand of dynamic, graph-based prediction has also matured in adjacent technology-management settings, including patent transformation prediction12 and domain-adaptation methods for patent transfer prediction across heterogeneous regions13, which speak directly to the twin problems of time-varying features and distributional shift that any cross-context productivity model must eventually confront.

Several notable gaps persist in the current literature. First, while dynamic modeling of TFP has received growing attention, existing methods often treat temporal patterns as exogenous rather than endogenously determined by underlying causal mechanisms14. Second, the identification of causal relationships in productivity studies typically proceeds independently from predictive modeling, resulting in a disconnect between explanation and forecasting15. Third, although inter-enterprise spatial correlations have been recognized as important, their quantitative characterization within graph-structured representations for TFP analysis remains underdeveloped. These limitations collectively point to the need for an integrated analytical framework that bridges causal inference, temporal modeling, and network-based representations.

This study proposes a novel approach that fuses causal inference techniques with temporal graph convolutional networks to construct a dynamic prediction and optimization decision mechanism for enterprise TFP. The integration serves dual purposes: enhancing predictive accuracy through the incorporation of structural dependencies, while simultaneously providing interpretable causal pathways that inform policy interventions. By constructing enterprise relationship graphs based on industrial linkages and geographic proximity, we capture the spatial correlation structure that conventional methods overlook. The temporal dimension is addressed through sequential modeling components that track productivity evolution across multiple time horizons16.

The main contributions of this research are threefold. First, we develop a hybrid architecture that combines the representational power of graph convolutional networks with the inferential rigor of causal models, offering both predictive and explanatory capabilities. Second, we introduce a dynamic temporal encoding mechanism that captures the evolving nature of enterprise productivity and its determinants. Third, we design an optimization decision module that translates predictive insights into actionable recommendations for productivity enhancement. The technical roadmap proceeds from data preprocessing and graph construction through model training and validation to decision support system implementation. Through this comprehensive framework, we aim to advance both the theoretical understanding and practical application of TFP prediction in the digital economy era.

Theoretical foundation and methodological review

Measurement methods for enterprise total factor productivity

Total factor productivity represents the portion of output growth that cannot be attributed to the accumulation of measurable inputs such as labor and capital. From a theoretical standpoint, TFP captures technological progress, managerial efficiency, and organizational innovations that enhance the transformation of inputs into outputs. Causal inference theoretical framework

The fundamental challenge of causal inference lies in the impossibility of observing the same unit under both treatment and control conditions simultaneously. The Rubin causal model, often termed the potential outcomes framework, addresses this through a counterfactual conceptualization23. For each unit i, we define potential outcomes and corresponding to treatment and control states. The individual treatment effect is then expressed as:

Since only one potential outcome is ever realized, causal identification relies on constructing credible comparison groups. The average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) becomes the primary estimand when selection into treatment is non-random, which characterizes most observational studies in productivity research.

Quasi-experimental methods have developed various identification strategies to recover causal effects. Difference-in-differences exploits parallel trends assumptions between treated and control groups across time, while instrumental variables address endogeneity through exogenous variation uncorrelated with the error term24. Regression discontinuity designs capitalize on sharp cutoffs in treatment assignment, and synthetic control methods construct counterfactual trajectories through weighted combinations of control units25. Each approach carries distinct identifying assumptions whose validity depends heavily on the empirical context.

These methods hold considerable promise for productivity analysis. Identifying which factors genuinely drive TFP growth—as opposed to merely correlating with it—requires rigorous causal reasoning. Policy interventions such as R&D subsidies or digital transformation initiatives demand effect evaluations that distinguish genuine impacts from confounding influences28. By embedding causal inference within our predictive framework, we aim to ensure that model outputs support not only accurate forecasting but also actionable policy recommendations grounded in causal mechanisms.

None of this comes for free, and it would be misleading to present the causal machinery as if its conclusions were guaranteed. Every estimate below rests on identifying assumptions, and three deserve to be stated plainly. The first is conditional unconfoundedness: once the high-dimensional controls are partialled out, treatment assignment is taken to be as good as random. Hidden confounders—unobserved managerial quality being the obvious candidate—would violate this, and double machine learning cannot rescue an estimate from a confounder it never observes. The second is the validity of the instruments used for robustness, which requires both relevance and an exclusion restriction; weak or invalid instruments would bias the very checks meant to reassure the reader. The third, and in our setting the most stubborn, is the direction of causation. R&D investment, digital transformation, and human capital are plausibly shaped by past productivity as much as they shape future productivity, so reverse causality is a live concern. We confront these threats in Sect. 4 through lagged treatment specifications, pre-trend inspection, placebo tests on lagged treatments, and instrumental-variable estimation, while acknowledging that such defenses narrow, rather than fully close, the gap between association and cause.

Construction of the causal inference-integrated temporal graph convolutional prediction model

Data sources and variable design

The empirical analysis draws upon the China Industrial Enterprises Database (also known as the Annual Survey of Industrial Enterprises, ASIE), which is constructed and maintained by the National Bureau of Statistics of China (NBS). The database provides comprehensive firm-level information on manufacturing establishments above a designated size threshold (i.e., all state-owned enterprises and non-state-owned enterprises with annual main business revenue exceeding the stipulated threshold). The data were accessed through the NBS National Data portal (https://data.stats.gov.cn/). This database has been widely recognized for its reliability and coverage in productivity research35. Our sample spans the period from 2008 to 2022, encompassing 15 consecutive years that capture multiple business cycles and policy transitions. To ensure data quality, we applied standard cleaning procedures: excluding observations with negative total assets or employment, removing firms with fewer than eight employees, and eliminating entries with missing key financial indicators. The final balanced panel comprises 12,847 enterprises with complete records across all years. For transparency, it helps to trace how that figure was reached. The raw extract contained roughly 41,000 manufacturing firms and about 0.46 million firm-year records. Dropping observations with non-positive total assets, output, or employment removed close to 6,300 firms; the eight-employee floor and the requirement of non-missing capital, labor, intermediate-input, and output fields removed a further 9,500 or so; and the decision to retain only firms observed in every year of the window—necessary for the temporal graph model to receive an uninterrupted sequence per node—accounts for the remainder, yielding the 12,847 firms and 192,705 firm-year observations used throughout. One caveat should be flagged here rather than buried: the firm-level microdata are restricted-access and are not directly downloadable from the public portal. The aggregate series at the portal are open, but reproduction at the firm level requires authorization from NBS, so we provide processed, de-identified sample data and the full analysis code (see the Code availability statement) to support verification.

For TFP estimation via the LP method, output is measured by gross industrial output value deflated to constant prices using industry-specific producer price indices. Capital input is proxied by net fixed assets, also adjusted for inflation through the fixed asset investment price index. Labor input follows the conventional approach of using total employee counts, while intermediate inputs—serving as the productivity proxy—are calculated as total expenditure on raw materials, fuel, and power36. These measurement choices align with established practices in the Chinese industrial productivity literature.

Control variables encompass firm age, ownership structure, export intensity, and R&D investment ratio. For causal identification purposes, we construct instrumental variables based on historical policy shocks and regional tax incentives that plausibly satisfy the exclusion restriction37. The enterprise relationship graph integrates three distinct dimensions of inter-firm connectivity. Supply chain linkages are derived from input-output tables at the four-digit industry level, capturing upstream and downstream dependencies. Geographic proximity is quantified through inverse distance weighting among firms within the same prefecture-level city. Technological similarity, perhaps the most nuanced dimension, is computed using patent citation networks and industry classification overlap38. Because graph construction shapes everything the convolution layers subsequently learn, the precise recipe matters. Supply-chain edges are drawn from the national four-digit input-output table, with the edge weight between two firms set to the corresponding inter-sector transaction coefficient and then row-normalized so each firm’s outgoing weights sum to one. Geographic edges use inverse-distance weighting, , where d is the great-circle distance in kilometers between registered addresses, truncated to zero beyond a 150-km threshold to keep the graph sparse. Technological similarity is the cosine similarity between firms’ patent-class distribution vectors, refined by shared forward and backward citations; it is recomputed in five-year rolling windows, so this layer is explicitly time-varying. Firms without any patent record—a sizeable group—are not dropped but are connected solely through the supply-chain and geographic layers, with their technological-similarity row set to zero. The three normalized layers are combined as a weighted sum (0.5, 0.3, 0.2), and edges below a small cutoff are pruned. Two honest limitations attach to this design. First, using industry-level input-output coefficients to proxy firm-to-firm supply linkages introduces measurement error, since firms in the same four-digit sector differ substantially in their actual trading partners; in the absence of transaction-level data this aggregation is a pragmatic compromise rather than a faithful map. Second, the layer weights and the distance cutoff are choices, not facts. We therefore re-estimated the model under alternative graph specifications—equal layer weights, a 100-km cutoff, and a similarity-only graph—and found the prediction ranking and the sign of every causal effect unchanged, which we report as a sensitivity check rather than as proof of correctness.

Causal inference module design

The integration of causal reasoning into predictive modeling demands a carefully structured approach that separates effect identification from outcome prediction. We design a causal inference module grounded in the double machine learning (DML) framework, which provides a principled method for eliminating confounding bias while preserving the flexibility of modern machine learning algorithms39. Empirical analysis and results discussion

Comparative analysis of prediction performance

To rigorously evaluate our proposed CT-GCN model, we design a comprehensive experimental framework encompassing multiple baseline methods and evaluation metrics. The dataset is partitioned chronologically: observations from 2008 to 2018 serve as the training set, 2019–2020 constitute the validation set, and 2021–2022 form the held-out test set. This temporal splitting strategy prevents data leakage and simulates realistic forecasting scenarios where future information remains unavailable during model training47. To keep the comparison fair, every baseline saw the same chronological splits and the same input information wherever architecturally possible, and each received a comparable hyperparameter-search budget under identical early-stopping rules. Because neural models are sensitive to initialization and partitioning, we do not rest on a single fit: every reported figure is the mean over five runs with distinct random seeds, and the run-to-run standard deviation stayed below 0.01 RMSE for all deep models (for CT-GCN, 0.312 ± 0.006). Pairwise differences between CT-GCN and each baseline are significant at the 1% level under paired t-tests across seeds.

Causal effects and heterogeneity analysis

The double machine learning framework yields precise estimates of causal effects for key productivity determinants. After controlling for high-dimensional confounders through the two-stage residualization procedure, we obtain unbiased average treatment effects that reveal genuine causal relationships rather than spurious correlations. As Fig. 7 illustrates, R&D investment intensity exerts the strongest positive causal impact on TFP, with a point estimate of 0.287 (SE = 0.034; 95% CI [0.220, 0.354]; p < 0.001). Digital transformation, measured by the information-technology investment ratio, follows closely at 0.241 (SE = 0.031; 95% CI [0.180, 0.302]; p < 0.001). Human capital quality, proxied by the proportion of employees with tertiary education, demonstrates a moderate but statistically significant effect of 0.156 (SE = 0.041; 95% CI [0.076, 0.236]; p < 0.001)51. To make the magnitudes comparable, these are standardized effects, so a one-standard-deviation rise in R&D intensity is associated with roughly 0.29 standard deviations of additional log TFP—an effect almost twice that of human capital. Reporting the intervals rather than point estimates alone seems the honest course, since it lets the reader see how much, or how little, separates the three drivers.

The results in Table 5 demonstrate remarkable consistency across specifications. Switching the first-stage learner from gradient boosting to random forest produces nearly identical estimates. Trimming extreme observations has negligible impact. Crucially, the placebo test using lagged treatment variables yields near-zero effects, confirming that our identified relationships are genuinely causal rather than artifacts of temporal confounding54. Instrumental variable estimation, though slightly more conservative, corroborates the direction and approximate magnitude of all three effects. Two further checks address concerns about the estimator and about the direction of causation. Swapping the first-stage learner once more, from gradient boosting to a feed-forward neural network, again left the effects essentially unchanged, which suggests the findings are a property of the data rather than of any single nuisance model. And because productivity and its supposed drivers could in principle move together for reasons of reverse causality, we re-estimated the effects using one- and two-year lagged treatments and inspected pre-treatment trends; the lagged estimates remained positive and significant while pre-trends were flat, evidence consistent with a forward-running causal channel rather than firms simply investing more after a good year. We are careful not to overclaim here: instruments built on historical policy shocks and regional tax incentives could still influence productivity through channels other than the treatments, so the exclusion restriction is argued for, not proven, and the IV results are best read as one strand of corroboration among several.

How do these causal insights translate into improved prediction? The mechanism operates through two channels. First, causal structure constrains the adjacency matrix to reflect genuine influence pathways, filtering out misleading correlations that would otherwise introduce noise. Second, the attention weights derived from causal effect magnitudes focus model capacity on the most consequential features, enhancing both accuracy and interpretability. This dual pathway explains why removing the causal module in ablation experiments produces substantial performance degradation.

Optimization decision mechanism verification

Translating predictive insights and causal knowledge into actionable decision support constitutes the ultimate objective of our integrated framework. We construct an optimization decision mechanism that synthesizes the CT-GCN predictions with heterogeneous treatment effects to generate tailored recommendations for enterprise productivity enhancement. This mechanism moves beyond passive forecasting toward active intervention design, enabling firms and policymakers to evaluate potential strategies before implementation.

The decision framework operates through counterfactual scenario simulation. For each enterprise, we pose the question: what would happen to TFP if specific decision variables were modified while holding other factors constant? The causal estimates obtained through double machine learning provide the foundation for answering such hypothetical queries. We simulate interventions across three primary decision domains—resource allocation adjustments, technology investment intensification, and management practice improvements—and project the resulting TFP trajectories using our trained model.

The marginal contribution analysis reveals substantial variation across decision variables. Technology investment yields the highest average marginal return, with each percentage point increase in R&D intensity generating approximately 0.29 log points of TFP improvement. Digital infrastructure investment follows at 0.24 log points, while workforce skill upgrading contributes 0.16 log points. These rankings, however, mask important heterogeneity. For firms already at the technological frontier, further R&D investment encounters diminishing returns, whereas human capital investments become increasingly valuable. Conversely, technology-lagging enterprises benefit disproportionately from catching up through focused R&D efforts.

Based on these insights, we propose differentiated optimization strategies. High-technology enterprises operating near the innovation frontier should prioritize complementary investments in digital systems and skilled personnel to maximize absorptive capacity. Traditional manufacturing firms with substantial technological gaps should concentrate resources on targeted R&D projects with clear commercialization potential. Small and medium enterprises, constrained by limited resources, benefit most from participating in industry-university collaboration networks that provide access to external knowledge spillovers without requiring massive internal investment56.

Regional considerations further refine these recommendations. Firms located in eastern coastal innovation hubs can leverage dense knowledge networks and should emphasize collaborative innovation strategies. Those in inland regions, facing thinner innovation ecosystems, may find greater returns from internal capability building and selective technology acquisition. The optimal strategy thus depends critically on the interaction between firm characteristics and environmental context.

Several practical limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, our counterfactual simulations assume stable causal relationships, yet structural changes in the economic environment could alter treatment effects over time. Second, resource constraints impose feasibility boundaries that pure optimization ignores—recommending massive R&D increases provides little guidance to cash-strapped enterprises. Third, implementation challenges including organizational resistance and capability gaps may prevent firms from realizing simulated gains. Finally, the decision mechanism cannot account for competitive dynamics whereby simultaneous strategy adoption by multiple firms might erode individual returns.

Despite these constraints, the optimization framework offers valuable guidance by narrowing the decision space and highlighting promising intervention directions. Rather than providing definitive prescriptions, it functions as a decision support tool that combines data-driven insights with managerial judgment. In that spirit, every counterfactual projection should travel with the confidence interval of the underlying CATE rather than as a point estimate, so that a recommendation reads as a range of plausible gains under stated assumptions; pairing those intervals with explicit budget and implementation-cost constraints would turn the module from an optimizer into a more honest screening device. Future extensions could incorporate budget constraints, dynamic adjustment mechanisms, and game-theoretic considerations to enhance practical applicability.

Discussion

The empirical findings presented in the preceding sections carry substantial theoretical implications that extend beyond the immediate context of productivity prediction. At a fundamental level, our results demonstrate that incorporating causal structure into deep learning architectures produces more than incremental performance gains—it fundamentally changes how models learn from data. Traditional neural networks discover statistical regularities without distinguishing correlation from causation, leading to predictions that may fail when underlying data distributions shift57. By contrast, our CT-GCN architecture encodes invariant causal mechanisms that remain stable across environmental changes, explaining its superior performance at extended prediction horizons.

The fusion of causal inference with graph neural networks represents a methodological advance with implications reaching beyond TFP prediction. Conventional approaches treat these paradigms as separate tools: causal methods for explanation, machine learning for prediction. Our integrated framework challenges this dichotomy. The causal module does not merely provide interpretable outputs alongside black-box predictions; rather, causal knowledge actively shapes the learning process through constrained adjacency matrices and attention weights derived from treatment effects. This bidirectional integration—where causal insights improve predictions and predictive performance validates causal structures—points toward a new paradigm for economic modeling that we find quite promising.

Compared with purely data-driven alternatives, the proposed method offers notable advantages. First, the causal-aware adjacency matrix filters spurious correlations that might otherwise mislead the model, enhancing robustness against confounding. Second, heterogeneous treatment effect estimation provides actionable information that pure prediction models cannot supply. Third, the multi-scale temporal convolution captures productivity dynamics across different frequencies, addressing a limitation of single-scale approaches58. Nevertheless, room for improvement remains. The current implementation assumes that causal relationships remain stable throughout the sample period—an assumption that may prove restrictive during periods of structural economic transformation. Future work could incorporate time-varying causal discovery algorithms to relax this constraint.

The applicability boundaries of our framework merit careful consideration. Data requirements represent a primary constraint: constructing meaningful enterprise relationship graphs demands information on supply chain linkages, geographic locations, and technological profiles that may be unavailable in many contexts. The model performs optimally with large, balanced panels spanning substantial time periods; applications to sparse or short-horizon datasets would likely yield degraded performance. Economic environment also matters considerably. Our sample covers manufacturing enterprises in a rapidly developing economy characterized by intensive industrial upgrading and policy interventions. Whether the identified causal relationships and their magnitudes transfer to mature economies with slower productivity dynamics remains an open empirical question59. The same caution extends across sectors. Service industries, where output and intermediate inputs are harder to measure and where network ties run through information and contracting rather than physical supply chains, would likely require a re-specified production function and a re-built relationship graph before the framework could be applied with confidence. We would expect the qualitative architecture to carry over, but not the specific effect magnitudes: the relative importance of R&D, digital, and human-capital channels almost certainly shifts with the institutional and technological environment, so any cross-context use should re-estimate effects locally rather than import ours.

For enterprise management, our findings suggest several actionable insights. The heterogeneity analysis reveals that optimal productivity enhancement strategies depend critically on firm characteristics—there exists no universal prescription. Technology-intensive firms should prioritize complementary investments in digital infrastructure and human capital, while traditional manufacturers benefit most from focused R&D targeting specific technological gaps. The network effects captured by our model further imply that strategic positioning within industry relationship structures matters; firms embedded in knowledge-rich networks enjoy productivity spillovers that isolated competitors cannot access.

Policy implications emerge at multiple levels. Government industrial policy could benefit from the differential treatment effects we document across regions and sectors. Rather than uniform subsidies, targeted interventions directing resources toward firms with highest potential treatment effects would maximize aggregate productivity gains. The causal structure itself—showing R&D, digitalization, and human capital as genuine productivity drivers—validates existing policy emphases while providing quantitative guidance on relative priorities60.

Reflecting on challenges encountered during this research, three stand out. First, constructing reliable enterprise relationship graphs required extensive data integration across multiple sources, a process involving considerable uncertainty about edge weight specifications. We addressed this through sensitivity analyses confirming robustness to alternative graph construction choices. Second, the two-stage nature of our approach—first identifying causal structure, then embedding it into the neural network—creates potential for error propagation. Errors in causal discovery could mislead subsequent prediction. Our validation procedures, including placebo tests and instrumental variable checks, provide some reassurance, though perfect verification remains elusive. Third, computational demands of the full pipeline exceed those of simpler alternatives, potentially limiting adoption by resource-constrained practitioners. Developing efficient approximation algorithms could broaden accessibility without sacrificing core capabilities.

Conclusion

This study has developed an integrated prediction framework that combines causal inference techniques with temporal graph convolutional networks for dynamic forecasting and optimization of enterprise total factor productivity. The research journey began with recognizing a fundamental gap in existing approaches: conventional prediction models excel at pattern recognition but fail to distinguish genuine causal mechanisms from spurious correlations, while traditional causal methods provide interpretable effects but lack the predictive power demanded by practical applications. Bridging this divide required not merely combining two toolkits but designing an architecture where causal knowledge actively shapes the learning process.

The core findings of this investigation can be distilled into several key conclusions. First, the proposed CT-GCN model achieves substantial prediction accuracy improvements over baseline methods, with RMSE reductions exceeding 19% compared to the strongest alternatives. This performance advantage grows more pronounced at extended forecast horizons, suggesting that causal grounding enhances model robustness against distributional shifts. Second, the double machine learning framework successfully identifies R&D investment, digital transformation, and human capital as genuine causal drivers of productivity growth, with estimated effects that remain stable across multiple robustness specifications. Third, treatment effect heterogeneity proves substantial and systematic—firms in high-technology sectors, medium-sized enterprises, and coastal regions derive significantly larger productivity gains from identical interventions. Fourth, the optimization decision mechanism demonstrates practical value by enabling counterfactual scenario evaluation that translates model outputs into actionable strategic recommendations.

The contributions of this research span three interconnected dimensions. Methodologically, we introduce a novel architecture that embeds causal constraints within graph neural network adjacency matrices, creating a principled fusion that preserves the strengths of both paradigms. The causal attention mechanism further enhances interpretability by weighting features according to their estimated causal relevance. Theoretically, our work extends the productivity literature by demonstrating that inter-enterprise network structures carry predictive information that firm-level analyses alone cannot capture. The temporal evolution of causal effects documented here challenges static conceptualizations of productivity determinants. From a practical standpoint, the differentiated optimization strategies derived from heterogeneous treatment effects offer guidance tailored to specific firm contexts rather than one-size-fits-all prescriptions. For managers, the actionable message is concrete: diagnose where a firm sits—its sector, size, region, and ownership—before committing resources, since the same yuan of R&D buys very different productivity gains across these groups; for policymakers, the heterogeneity argues for targeted rather than uniform support, directing marginal subsidies toward the firms and channels where the estimated causal returns are largest.

Candid acknowledgment of limitations is essential. The current analysis draws exclusively from Chinese manufacturing enterprises, and whether our findings generalize to service sectors or alternative national contexts awaits future investigation. Data coverage constraints prevented incorporation of several potentially relevant variables, including intangible assets and organizational culture measures that likely influence productivity but resist quantification. The model’s computational demands may impede real-time deployment in operational settings where rapid prediction updates are required. Additionally, our causal discovery procedures assume acyclicity and time-invariance—assumptions that, while standard, may not hold universally.

Several promising avenues merit exploration in subsequent research. Multimodal data fusion incorporating textual information from annual reports, satellite imagery of facilities, and network data from patent citations could enrich the input feature space considerably. Online learning algorithms that update model parameters continuously as new observations arrive would enhance practical applicability in dynamic business environments. Cross-national comparative studies examining whether causal mechanisms identified here operate similarly in developed economies would test the external validity of our conclusions. Finally, extending the framework to incorporate strategic interactions among competing firms through game-theoretic formulations represents an intellectually exciting frontier that could substantially advance both theory and practice in productivity analysis.
