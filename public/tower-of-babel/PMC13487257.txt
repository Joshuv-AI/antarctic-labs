Ultra-wideband reflective polarization rotator achieving 104% measured bandwidth with high-efficiency multi-state polarization control
Authors: A. K. M. Baki, Md. Shahriar Hasan, Aalolika Roy Chowdhury, Md. Rasheduzzaman Al-Amin, Ali Ghadimi, Elham Baladi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article introduces a compact ultra-wideband reflective polarization rotator, covering 12.68–38.79 GHz, which offers notable linear-to-elliptical, linear, and circular polarization conversion. The metasurface comprises a semi-octa-circular copper layout on an F4BM220 substrate with a unit cell size of 5mm×5mm×2mm backed by a ground plane, configured to facilitate four strong resonances enabling highly precise polarization rotation within Ku, K, and Ka bands. Unlike traditional symmetric polarization rotators, the proposed hybrid octa-circular geometry incorporates a rectangular cutout that triggers Omega-type bianisotropy, extending the operational fractional bandwidth to 104%. Full-wave simulations exhibit a -10 dB rotational bandwidth (RBW) of 112.16% using Generalized Sheet Transition Conditions (GSTC) analysis, while experimental measurements of the fabricated 60× 60 unit cell array feature an RBW of 104.76%, demonstrating superior real-world performance. The rotator sustains polarization conversion efficiency exceeding 91% and exhibits stable functionality for oblique incidence up to 30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}. Concordance between theory and experimental observations is validated through CST-analysis, Bi-Mode Foster Equivalent Circuit analysis, and laboratory measurements. Ultra-wideband operation, compact size, strong resonances, high PCR, and multi-state polarization response highlight the novelty of the design and ensure suitability for radar imaging, sensing, satellite communications, and emerging 6G systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52580-x.

Introduction

The exponential growth of wireless applications, dense networks, evolving user demands, and emerging trends is expected to introduce major communication challenges by 2030, requiring new physical-layer paradigms for sixth-generation (6G) and beyond 6G (B6G) systems. Polarization mismatches, atmospheric absorption, multi-path fading, and shadowing further degrade channels across frequency bands. Polarization Conversion Metasurfaces (PCMs) can mitigate these impairments for 6G and B6G communications. Metasurfaces, often integrated with various structures, enable functionalities such as polarization diversity, beam steering, bandwidth expansion, miniaturization, performance enhancement, and reflect/transmit-array configurations1. These ultra-thin, two-dimensional structures efficiently manipulate electromagnetic (EM) waves, advancing 6G technologies2–4. PCMs have gained attention for imaging, sensing, and antennas5, but traditionally suffer from large size, narrow bandwidth, and low Polarization Conversion Ratio (PCR)2,6,7. Passive PCMs offer simpler designs, higher PCR, broader bandwidths, and lower losses, enhancing wireless system performance and energy efficiency.

A reflective metasurface is reported in4, achieving a 90% polarization conversion ratio (PCR) within 12.16-18.32 GHz. Another converter with 90% efficiency within 24.4-34.6 GHz is reported8. The proposed metasurface in9 performs cross-polarization conversion within a very wide frequency range of 13.4-33.6 GHz with high angular stability. Recently, the demand for multifunctional polarization converters has increased. A linear-cross and linear-circular polarization converter is presented in10, which has a compact structure but operates in five narrow bands. The design of11 also offers multi-mode conversion capability within C, X, and Ku bands, along with structural simplicity. The converter proposed in12 delivers very high conversion efficiency but functions within limited frequency ranges of C and X bands. Multi-functional metasurfaces for enhanced communication are also explored in13,14, while transmissive metasurfaces for signal processing and satellite communications are studied in15,16. A holographic polarization converter for Ku/K-band (14-25 GHz) with dual reflection/transmission functionality is reported in17, and another X-band dual-mode metasurface in18. Other advancements include a graphene-based linear-to-linear converter for THz indoor applications with 85% PCR19, a wideband 5G mmWave converter20, and an anomalous reflective metasurface for D-band non-line-of-sight (NLOS) communication. This research presents the development of a novel Semi Octa Circular Polarization Converter Metasurface (SOCPCM) operating within the frequency band of 12.68-38.79 GHz. It has the ability to generate multiple polarization states (linear to linear, circular, and elliptical), which enhances its significance for advanced multifaceted applications in modern communication systems. The originality of this work lies in the strategic engineering of omega-type bianisotropy27,28 within a nested, hybrid octa-circular unit cell. Omega-type bianisotropy introduces strong magnetoelectric coupling within magnetic and electric currents, which can be obtained by asymmetric metasurface designs to enhance the conversion efficiency29.

The existing reflective polarization rotators (RPRs) in the literature19,23,30 are typically based on simple geometric anisotropies like L-shaped or V-shaped patches. They are often constrained by an inherent bandwidth-to-thickness trade-off, resulting in the need for increased substrate thickness exceeding 0.12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _0$$\end{document} to achieve wideband performance. This leads to larger profiles, often using multi-layer stacked patches23 or 3D-printed metasurfaces31,32, which contribute to high fabrication costs and reduce the angular stability. Additionally, they are sensitive to alignment tolerances. The proposed design addresses these shortcomings. Its single-layer metallic pattern on a 2 mm thick F4BM220 substrate facilitates structural simplicity, and it’s fully compatible with cost-effective and standard PCB lithography. It utilizes a quad-resonant synthesis that merges four discrete physical mechanisms: capacitive-loaded magnetic loops, structural bianisotropy, higher-order electric modes, and TM-cavity resonances. Its ultra-low profile of only 0.08\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _0$$\end{document} at the lowest operating frequency (12.68 GHz) reflects a notable breakthrough in miniaturization, which sustains the operational stability and reduces surface-wave losses compared to the distinct multiband or narrowband properties of other prevailing anisotropic metasurfaces. Many of the previous converter designs depend on empirical parameter sweeping11,12,30, whereas the proposed SOCPCM is validated through GSTC polarizability extraction to interpret the mechanism of omega-type bianisotropy.

The designed SOCPCM can play a vital role in various domains, including satellite communications, radar technologies, and THz technologies. Evolution stages of the SOCPCM design

The SOCPCM unit cell is designed, covering the Ku, K, and Ka bands and targeting the highest possible -10 dB rotational bandwidth (RBW). The unit cell consists of two metallic layers implemented on an F4BM220 substrate, selected for its high-speed operation, featuring a low loss tangent (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tan \delta = 0.001$$\end{document}) and a low dielectric constant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon _r = 2.20$$\end{document}). The top and bottom layers are composed of copper with a thickness of 0.017 mm. The unit cell has undergone multiple iterations to enhance functionality and efficiency, where each stage is improved on the basis of the insights gained from the previous iteration, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

The development of the SOCPCM follows strategic approaches. It focuses on modal overlapping that is achieved by placing the four distinct resonances (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_1$$\end{document}–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_4$$\end{document}) to maintain the reflection phase difference (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \Phi$$\end{document}) within the required range. The use of nested geometries and asymmetric shapes triggers omega-type bianisotropy, which provides the necessary cross-coupling between magnetic and electric polarizabilities. Finally, efficiency of the design is maximized by balancing the inductive metallic patch with the capacitive grounded dielectric (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h \approx 0.08\lambda _0$$\end{document}) for precise impedance matching and minimal co-polarized reflection.

Analysis of the surface currents distribution

To better understand the polarization conversion mechanism, the surface current distribution of the unit cell is analyzed, particularly at some co-polarized dips where the PCR is very high. When an electromagnetic wave is incident on a metasurface, it induces surface currents, which play a crucial role in electric, magnetic, and electromagnetic polarizations. The distribution of the surface current density on the unit cell's top and bottom planes determines the nature of these polarizations (particularly at the near-field of the unit cell).Fig. 6Magnetic field distribution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_1, \; b_1, \; c_1, \; d_1$$\end{document}), electric field distribution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_2, \; b_2, \; c_2, \; d_2$$\end{document}) and surface current distribution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_3, \; b_3, \; c_3, \; d_3$$\end{document}) of top patch and ground plane at 13.50 GHz, 20.46 GHz, 31.39 GHz, and 37.90 GHz for y-polarized incident electric field.

Electric dipole resonance is established when the surface current distributions on the top patch and on the bottom plate are in phase (parallel), creating an electric dipole moment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{p} = q\mathbf{d}$$\end{document}, where q is the magnitude of charge and d is the displacement. This dipole moment induces electric field, expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf {dipole}} = -\frac{1}{4\pi \epsilon_0}\frac{\mathbf{p}}{r^3}$$\end{document}.

In contrast, magnetic behavior arises when the currents of the top patch and ground plane are out of phase (antiparallel), forming a circulating surface current that generates a magnetic dipole moment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{m} = I\mathbf{A}$$\end{document}, where I is the surface current density and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{A}$$\end{document} measures the area of the loop. This magnetic dipole induces a magnetic field given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf {dipole}} = -\frac{\mu _0}{4\pi }\frac{\mathbf{m}}{r^3}$$\end{document}.

The generation of electric and magnetic dipole moments and their corresponding induced fields play a crucial role in polarization conversion of incident EM waves. In Fig. 6(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {a_1}$$\end{document}) and (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {b_1}$$\end{document}), the direction of the produced magnetic dipole on the top metal patch indicates “into the page” while the direction is “out of the page” for the ground. This results in magnetic resonances at those frequencies (13.50 and 20.46 GHz). Meanwhile, in Fig. 6(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {c_1}$$\end{document}) and (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {d_1}$$\end{document}), the directions of the magnetic dipole are parallel for the top patch and ground plane, which are not responsible for magnetic resonances. According to Fig. 6(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {c_2}$$\end{document}) and (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {d_2}$$\end{document}), the induced electric dipole moment on the top metal patch and ground plane lies in the same direction (parallel), and it produces electric resonances at the corresponding frequencies (31.39 and 37.90 GHz). The local surface current distributions shown in Figs. 6(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {a_3, \, b_3, \, c_3,}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {d_3}$$\end{document}) correspond to local magnetic resonances at 13.50 GHz and 20.46 GHz and electric resonances at 31.39 GHz and 37.90 GHz. Generalized sheet transition conditions (GSTC) based analysis of the SOCPCM

This section presents a more comprehensive GSTC-based theoretical and numerical analysis of the proposed metasurface. GSTC-based analysis treats the metasurface as a zero-thickness “sheet” or a 2D boundary. Instead of simulating the internal physical structure, it uses a set of boundary conditions to mathematically relate the electric and magnetic fields on one side of the metasurface to the fields on the other side. The GSTC-based equations relate jumps in tangential electric and magnetic fields across a metasurface to the induced surface polarization currents. These conditions are derived by considering the effective properties (like surface currents or polarizabilities) of the metasurface. This investigation provides critical insights into the modeling of the proposed bianisotropic SOCPCM for wavefront control, non-reciprocity, and polarization engineering. It is noteworthy to discuss here that the ground plane suppresses magnetic coupling to free space at some frequency bands. The incident magnetic field is weak near the patch due to image currents from the ground. This suppresses magnetic field excitation at the far-field, even if loop currents exist on the metasurface. Though the SOCPCM supports a magnetic loop, the far-field pattern may resemble an electric dipole, as can be inferred from Fig. 8, due to boundary conditions and incidence angle (normal incidence supports electric excitation).

Physical mechanism of cross-polarization conversion and mathematical synthesis

Fabrication and testing of the proposed SOCPCM

Fig. 9(a) Top view of the fabricated SOCPCM DUT with 60 × 60 array (b) Illustration of measurement setup in an anechoic chamber.

(a) Top view of the fabricated SOCPCM DUT with 60 × 60 array (b) Illustration of measurement setup in an anechoic chamber.

An SOCPCM-based Device Under Test (DUT) is fabricated on an F4BM220 substrate, as shown in Fig. 9. The dimensions of the DUT are 12.40 × 12.40 in2, and it consists of 60 × 60 semi-octa-circular unit cells. To prevent oxidation, the surface of the DUT is coated with Electroless Nickel Immersion Gold (ENIG). The DUT is prototyped using a laser-cut stencil and silk bottom layer, with an additional 7.5 mm padding on each side. Eight mounting holes (each 3 mm in diameter) are placed at 2.8 mm from the board cutout. The thickness of the copper layer is 0.017 mm (0.5 oz). Other design parameters are depicted in Fig. 1(b)–(c).

The experiments are carried out in an anechoic chamber using a vector network analyzer (VNA) and three sets of frequency-specific horn antennas. ETS 3160-08 horn antennas, DBE-520-20 horn antennas, and another set of horn antennas are used for the 12.5-18 GHz band, 18-26.5 GHz band, and 26.5-40 GHz band, respectively, to cover the full frequency span of 12.68-38.79 GHz. A KEYSIGHT N5224B VNA is employed to measure the reflection coefficients, operating within the 10-43.5 GHz frequency range. Calibration is performed using the Anritsu K 3652 calibration kit. The measurement procedures for the three aforementioned frequency bands are presented in the subsequent discussion. It may be mentioned here that it is not possible to perform the measurement of the DUT under the full frequency range of 12.68-38.79 GHz in a single uninterrupted process due to the frequency limitations of the available Tx/Rx horn antennas.

SOCPCM is well-suited for implementation in numerous applications in the emerging 6G world. It sustains cross-polarization reflection coefficient, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {R_{xy}}$$\end{document} = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {R_{yx}}$$\end{document} ≈ 0 dB despite the variation in axial ratio. This characteristic facilitates electromagnetic interference (EMI) isolation and intelligent signal redirection in high-density networks, being a cost-efficient and PCB-integrable system. In modern wireless telecommunication and satellite communication, multipath fading and polarization mismatch, occurring in high-frequency environments, can be mitigated with the multi-mode conversion capability of SOCPCM. Moreover, this ability offers enhanced performance in Radar Cross Section (RCS) reduction applications relative to pure linear conversion by preventing the modern adaptive radars from easily identifying the cross-polarized components. This metasurface can also be integrated as a Reconfigurable Intelligent Surface (RIS) by utilizing its bianisotropic nature for active beamsteering.

Conclusion

In this paper, a compact ultra-wideband (UWB) reflective polarization rotator has been designed, fabricated, and experimentally validated across the 12.68-38.79 GHz band. The proposed Semi-Octa-Circular Polarization Converter Metasurface (SOCPCM) ensures remarkable cross-polarization conversion, yielding -10 dB rotational bandwidth of 112.16% from simulation using GSTC analysis and 104.76% from measurement of fabricated prototype, with a polarization conversion ratio over 91% for incident angles up to 30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}. The analyses presented through CST, Bi-Mode Foster Equivalent Circuit, Generalized Sheet Transition Conditions (GSTC) and laboratory measurements of the fabricated SOCPCM ensure its capability for cross-polarization conversion across broadband and oblique incidence, demonstrating close agreement between simulated and measured responses. The novelty of the SOCPCM is evidenced by its wide rotational bandwidth, high PCR, compact electrical size, multi-state polarization control, and structural simplicity. PCR reflects the effectiveness of converting the incident polarization state into the desired orthogonal polarization. Thus, the demonstrated high PCR of the proposed metasurface across a wide frequency band highlights the practical applicability of this design. It is well-suited for applications such as radio astronomy, radar systems, medical imaging, defense/ security, and weather monitoring, with its angular stability as well as multi-state polarization conversion ability. Furthermore, SOCPCM has the potential to enhance high-speed broadband connectivity, real-time video conferencing, HD streaming, and other advanced features in the emerging 6G era. The strong UWB performance of the reflective cross-polarized converter enables seamless integration into advanced systems. Future efforts will focus on incorporating beam-forming and beam-steering into the reflected beam to achieve precise spatial localization and received-power maximization, which are essential for next-generation UWB communication networks.
