Introduction

1.1.
Background

Understanding eigenfunctions of quantum Hamiltonians is central in both physics and mathematics, as they determine the time evolution and statistical behaviour of quantum systems. A guiding principle in quantum chaos is that the spectral properties of a quantum system reflect the dynamics of the associated classical flow. In particular, when the classical dynamics is ergodic, one expects delocalisation of eigenfunctions. This is formalised by the quantum ergodicity theorem of Shnirelman, Zelditch, and Colin de Verdière
[
92
,
100
,
34
]

[

,
10
]
, results towards quantum unique ergodicity
[

,

]
, and connections to the fractal uncertainty principle and spectral gaps
[

. We refer to
[

for comprehensive surveys.

Over the past decade, there has been substantial progress in discrete analogues of quantum chaos. In the setting of large graphs, the high energy limit is replaced by a large size limit, and quantum ergodicity has been established for sequences of regular graphs converging locally to trees
[

,

, building on earlier delocalisation results
[

. These results have been extended to non-regular graphs, Cayley graphs and to Schrödinger operators (including Anderson-type models) under assumptions on the limiting spectral measure
[

. Parallel developments in random matrix theory and random graphs, such as local semicircle laws and eigenvector delocalisation
[

,

]
.

In the continuous setting, the natural analogue of large graphs is given by sequences of hyperbolic surfaces whose genus tends to infinity and which converge to the hyperbolic plane in the Benjamini-Schramm sense. Quantum unique ergodicity for sequences of arithmetic surfaces in this setting was considered by Nelson

Despite these advances, most results in the continuous setting concern the Laplacian, corresponding to free motion. Introducing a potential leads to Schrödinger operators
H=-\Delta+V
, which break the symmetries underlying Selberg theory and spherical analysis. In contrast, in the discrete setting, methods based on Green’s functions and equidistribution properties of non-backtracking operators have led to quantum ergodicity and even quantum unique ergodicity for large graphs

Understanding Schrödinger operators

can also give insight into many-body quantum chaos, where fundamental problems such as the eigenstate thermalisation hypothesis

Assuming a uniform spectral gap for the sequence of surfaces, they proved Bose-Einstein condensation onto the constant mode in the dilute regime. At the mean-field level, this leads to an effective one-particle dynamics governed by a Schrödinger operator

Another related problem is that of Anderson localisation and delocalisation. The study of Anderson localisation was initiated by the pioneering work of Anderson that established exponential localisation of eigenfunctions in lattice models with strong disorder

from energy

within a window of width

can be made arbitrarily small, for sufficiently small

Large-scale quantum mixing builds on the ideas of large-scale quantum ergodicity. An analogue of this for the Wigner ensemble was proved by Cipolloni, Erdős, and Yau

1.2.
The main results and examples

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of compact connected hyperbolic surfaces. We will be interested in sequences of surfaces that satisfy the following conditions.

(BSC)
:

Benjamini-Schramm convergence:
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\mathbb{H}
in the Benjamini-Schramm topology. That is for any
R>0
, we have

\frac{|\{x\in X_{n}\ :\ \InjRad_{X_{n}}(x)\leq R\}|}{|X_{n}|}\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

(UND)
:

Uniform discreteness:
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
the injectivity radius
\InjRad_{X_{n}}
has uniform lower bound.

(EXP)
:

Expander property:
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a uniform lower bound for the spectral gap
\lambda_{1}(X_{n})
.

These conditions on the sequence of surfaces are the same in works
[
67
,
56
]
. In the first work, quantum ergodicity is established, and in the second quantum mixing. For both works, they consider any orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator. Our aim here is to generalise this to sequences of Schrödinger operators defined on each
X_{n}
. That is, operators of the form

H_{V_{n}}=-\Delta_{X_{n}}+V_{n},

where
-\Delta_{X_{n}}
is the positive Laplace-Beltrami operator and

is a potential. We will make the following assumption on the sequences of potentials.

(POT)
:

\{V_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of potentials on
(X_{n})
satisfying for some

C_{\min},C_{\max}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

C_{\min}\leq V_{n}\leq C_{\max}\qquad\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$}

and
\left\lVert V_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X_{n})}^{2}=o(g_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The property

can be achieved by fixing

is a fundamental domain of

below. Conditions such as

or other strong decay conditions like Kato-type conditions are typically assumed in e.g. Strichartz estimates and dispersivity properties of the wave- and Schödinger equations in

With these we can state the main theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of compact connected hyperbolic surfaces that satisfies
(BSC)
,
(UND)
, and
(EXP)
, and let
\{V_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of potentials that satisfies
(POT)
with the two numbers
C_{\min}
and
C_{\max}
. Consider for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the Schrödinger operator
H_{V_{n}}^{(n)}=-\Delta_{X_{n}}+V_{n}
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}^{(n)}\leq\lambda_{2}^{(n)}\leq\lambda_{3}^{(n)}\leq\lambda_{4}^{(n)}\cdots
and eigenvectors
\{\psi_{j}^{(n)}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. For all
n,j\in\mathbb{N}
set
\varrho_{j}^{(n)}=\sqrt{\lambda_{j}^{(n)}-\frac{1}{4}}
. Fix
\frac{1}{4}<a^{\prime}<a<b<b^{\prime}
such that
b-C_{\max}>a-\min(0,C_{\min})
and a sequence of spectral windows
I_{n}
such that
[a,b]\subset I_{n}\subset[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
. Then for any uniformly bounded sequence of measurable functions
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
we have the following three properties:

(1)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}\big(H_{V}^{(n)},I_{n}\big)}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}^{(n)}\in I_{n}}\Big|\langle a_{n}\psi_{j}^{(n)},\psi_{j}^{(n)}\rangle_{L^{2}(X)}-\frac{1}{\Vol(X_{n})}\int_{X_{n}}a_{n}(x)\,dx\Big|^{2}=0,

where
dx
is the volume form on
X_{n}
.

(2)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta(\varepsilon)>0
such that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}\big(H_{V}^{(n)},I_{n}\big)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq k:\lambda_{j}^{(n)},\lambda_{k}^{(n)}\in I_{n}\\
|\varrho_{j}^{(n)}-\varrho_{k}^{(n)}|<\delta(\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}\Big|\langle a_{n}\psi_{j}^{(n)},\psi_{k}^{(n)}\rangle_{L^{2}(X_{n})}\Big|^{2}<\varepsilon.

(3)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta(\varepsilon)>0
such that for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}\big(H_{V}^{(n)},I_{n}\big)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq k:\lambda_{j}^{(n)},\lambda_{k}^{(n)}\in I_{n}\\
|\varrho_{j}^{(n)}-\varrho_{k}^{(n)}-\tau|<\delta(\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}\Big|\langle a_{n}\psi_{j}^{(n)},\psi_{k}^{(n)}\rangle_{L^{2}(X_{n})}\Big|^{2}<\varepsilon.

In all three cases
\mathcal{N}(H_{V}^{(n)},I_{n})
is the number of eigenvalues of
H_{V}^{(n)}
in
I_{n}
counted with multiplicity.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\{X_{g}\}_{g\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random compact hyperbolic surfaces, where the distribution of

is given by the Weil-Petersson distribution on the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g}
for all
g\in\mathbb{N}
, where

\{V_{g}\}_{g\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of potentials that satisfies the condition
(POT)
with the two numbers
C_{\min}
and
C_{\max}
. Consider for each
g\in\mathbb{N}
the Schrödinger operator
H_{V_{g}}^{(g)}=-\Delta_{X_{g}}+V_{g}
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}^{(g)}\leq\lambda_{2}^{(g)}\leq\lambda_{3}^{(g)}\leq\lambda_{4}^{(g)}\cdots
and eigenvectors
\{\psi_{j}^{(g)}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. For all
g,j\in\mathbb{N}
set
\varrho_{j}^{(g)}=\sqrt{\lambda_{j}^{(g)}-\frac{1}{4}}
. Fix
\frac{1}{4}<a^{\prime}<a<b<b^{\prime}
such that

such that

A major difference between the deterministic setting and this random setting is that the properties

(UND)

(EXP)

In the following, we let

In particular, the potentials

(b)
Point cloud potentials.
Alternatively, we can also apply the result if the potential is a superposition over a point set that is sufficiently sparse. Suppose that
\mathcal{X}
is a locally finite set in
\mathbb{H}
such that for some
\delta>0
it holds
\operatorname{dist}(x_{i},x_{j})>2\delta
, for all
x_{i},x_{j}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
. Then, depending on the density of
\mathcal{X}
in
D_{n}
we have the following two cases.

Suppose first that
\#\mathcal{X}\cap D_{n}=o(g_{n})
and that
W\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H})
are such that
\mathrm{supp}(W)\subset B(i,\delta)
. Define the sequence
\{V_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of potentials given by

V_{n}(x)=\sum_{x_{j}\in\mathcal{X}\cap D_{n}}W(T_{x_{j}}(x)),

where
T_{x_{j}}
is the unique translation that maps
x_{j}
to

. Then this sequence of potentials will satisfy
(POT)
. Due to the assumptions, we have that the sequence is uniformly bounded. For the

-norm bound, we have

(1.1)

\left\lVert V_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X_{n})}^{2}\leq C\sum_{x_{j}\in\mathcal{X}\cap D_{n}}\int_{D_{n}}|W(T_{x_{j}}(x))|^{2}\,dx\leq C\sum_{x_{j}\in\mathcal{X}\cap D_{n}}\left\lVert W\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{H})}=o(g_{n}).

This verifies that
\left\lVert V_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X_{n})}^{2}=o(g_{n})
. Now, if we have instead that
\#\mathcal{X}\cap D_{n}=\mathcal{O}(g_{n})
and that
\{W_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a uniformly bounded sequence in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H})
with
\mathrm{supp}(W_{n})\subset B(i,\delta_{n})
with
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\delta_{n}=0
. Define the sequence
\{V_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of potentials given by

V_{n}(x)=\sum_{x_{j}\in\mathcal{X}\cap D_{n}}W_{n}(T_{x_{j}}(x)),

Then this sequence of potentials will satisfy
(POT)
. This is seen by an analogous argument to the one given above. These examples complement the low density limit or Boltzmann-Grad limit in the Euclidean case, where the linear Boltzmann equation can also be derived as the associated macroscopic transport equation; see, e.g.
[

[

,

]

If we allow the potentials to vanish in the limit, then we can relax the

This verifies that

This means that in weak coupling limit one obtain free motion as the macroscopic transport equation.

[0,R]
for some
R>0
. Under
(EXP)
, they proved that
Bose–Einstein condensation
occurs in this setting, meaning that if
\Psi_{0,n}
is the
ground state
, i.e. the state minimising
\langle\Psi,H_{N_{n}}\Psi\rangle
, then its one-particle density matrix

\gamma_{n}(x,x^{\prime})=\int_{X_{n}^{N_{n}-1}}\Psi_{0,n}(x,x_{2},\dots,x_{N_{n}})\overline{\Psi_{0,n}(x^{\prime},x_{2},\dots,x_{N_{n}})}\,dx_{2}\cdots dx_{N_{n}}

satisfies, for
\psi_{0}=\Vol(X_{n})^{-1/2}\mathbf{1}_{X_{n}}
and for all
\varepsilon>0
, that there exists
Y_{0}(\varepsilon)>0
such that

\langle\psi_{0},\gamma_{n}\psi_{0}\rangle\geq 1-\varepsilon,

as long as the diluteness parameter

\frac{N_{n}}{\Vol(X_{n})}\,\log\big((\tanh a)^{-1}\big)<Y_{0}(\varepsilon).

Here
a>0
is the
scattering length
determined by solving
f_{\infty}(r)=\log\!\big(\tanh(r/2)/\tanh(a/2)\big)
, where for
r>R
the function
f_{\infty}
is the radial solution of
-\Delta_{\mathbb{H}}f=0
outside the support of
V\subset[0,R]
, see Appendix A of
[
69
]
for its exact derivation. In the Hartree approximation of the ground state
\Psi_{0,n}
, one then restricts the minimisation problem for
\langle\Psi,H_{N_{n}}\Psi\rangle
to product states

\Psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{N_{n}}):=\varphi_{n}(x_{1})\cdots\varphi_{n}(x_{N_{n}})
,
\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(X_{n})}=1.

In this case

\langle\Psi,H_{N_{n}}\Psi\rangle=N_{n}\langle\varphi_{n},-\Delta\varphi_{n}\rangle+\frac{N_{n}(N_{n}-1)}{2}\int\int_{X_{n}\times X_{n}}V(d(x,y))|\varphi_{n}(x)|^{2}|\varphi_{n}(y)|^{2}\,dx\,dy,

so minimising
\langle\Psi,H_{N_{n}}\Psi\rangle
under the constraint
\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(X_{n})}=1
yields

(-\Delta+U_{\varphi_{n}})\varphi_{n}=\lambda\varphi_{n}
,
U_{\varphi_{n}}(x):=(N_{n}-1)\int_{X_{n}}V(d(x,y))|\varphi_{n}(y)|^{2}\,dy.

Thus we arrive at a single-body Schrödinger operator
H_{n}^{\mathrm{fr}}=-\Delta_{X_{n}}+U_{\varphi_{n}}
describing fluctuations around the condensate. Since for all
\varepsilon>0
we have
\langle\psi_{0},\gamma_{n}\psi_{0}\rangle\geq 1-\varepsilon
, the one-particle density matrix concentrates on the constant mode
\psi_{0}
, and hence
|\varphi_{n}(x)|^{2}
is approximately uniform. Evaluating the Hartree potential at
\varphi_{n}=\psi_{0}
gives

U_{\varphi_{n}}(x)=\frac{N_{n}-1}{\Vol(X_{n})}\int_{X_{n}}V(d(x,y))dy.

For points
x
with
\InjRad_{X_{n}}(x)>R
, the ball
B(x,R)
is isometric to a hyperbolic ball, and the integral is independent of
x
. Thus we can write

U_{\varphi_{n}}=c_{n}+W_{n},

where
c_{n}:=\frac{N_{n}-1}{\Vol(X_{n})}\int_{\mathbb{H}}V(d(i,z))\,dz

and
W_{n}
is supported on the thin part
\{x\in X_{n}:\InjRad_{X_{n}}(x)\leq R\}
. Thus by
(BSC)
,

\|W_{n}\|_{L^{2}(X_{n})}^{2}=o(g_{n}).

Since we are in thermodynamic limit
N_{n}\sim\Vol(X_{n})
, the shifts
0<c\leq c_{n}\leq c^{\prime}<\infty
for some universal constants
c,c^{\prime}
and as adding a constant only introduces a spectral shift,
H_{n}^{\mathrm{fr}}
has the same eigenfunctions in
I\subset(\frac{1}{4}+c,\infty)
from shifted windows
I_{n}:=I-c_{n}\subset(\frac{1}{4},\infty)
as

-\Delta_{X_{n}}+W_{n},

which satisfies
(POT)
. Therefore, Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
apply, and the eigenfunctions of the frozen Hartree operator are quantum mixing in any compact window
I\subset(\frac{1}{4}+c,\infty)
in the thermodynamic limit. In other words, in the dilute regime, the condensate produces an effective one-particle operator whose excitation modes are necessarily delocalised.

In the non-dilute regime of Bose gas, one expects the condensate profile
\varphi_{n}
to become non-constant, leading to a Hartree potential
U_{\varphi_{n}}
of macroscopic size, with

\|U_{\varphi_{n}}\|_{L^{2}(X_{n})}^{2}\asymp g_{n}.

In this case, our perturbative framework no longer applies. Extending quantum ergodicity to this thermodynamic regime remains an open problem. A natural conjectural direction is to replace the
L^{2}
-smallness assumption by a condition on the limiting lifted operators on
\mathbb{H}
, for instance, requiring absolute continuity of the spectrum of periodic Schrödinger operators on
\mathbb{H}
, which seems to be a challenging open problem.

Comparison to other results and outline of the method

In the works of Anantharaman and Sabri

(Green)

potentials in possible future extensions of our work. For us the role of asymptotically uncorrelated observables

and Matheus

If we wanted to also consider potentials

given by a periodic potential by setting small
\varepsilon
-bumps in an orbit
\Gamma z_{0}
of a Fuchsian group
\Gamma
, even the spectral theory of

is poorly understood. In the Anderson model on regular trees, Anantharaman and Sabri adapted on the work of Klein
[

on absolute continuity of the analogous result for Schrödinger operators on trees, and Klein’s proof heavily uses the tree-like nature of the graphs. In contrast, for

even the absolute continuity of the spectrum is not known, which is an interesting open problem. Heuristically, one of the key properties used is that if a branch is removed from a tree, the tree splits into two subtrees, which allows one to deduce crucial recursive properties for the Green’s function. Such properties are not true in the hyperbolic plane if one would attempt to build e.g. via microlocal analysis an approximate theory. For example one could develop non-backtracking random walk as in
[
12

in hyperbolic plane such as via cones of geodesic sprays or semiclassical quantisation of the geodesic flow, but small initial errors lead to overlaps in future paths and thus loops that do not appear in the tree language. Thus genuinely new ideas are be needed. Given the recent links of the topic to polynomial method and strong convergence

53

, adapting these approaches to surface groups as in
[

could be a potential future avenue such as approaching surfaces of variable curvature.

Let us now discuss the main strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.1
and how it compares to
[
23
]
on graphs and other works
[
67
,
56
]
on surfaces. In our proof, we use time averages of Heisenberg evolutions
P_{V_{n}}(t)aP_{V_{n}}(t)
in
[0,T]
for a large
T
to the observable
a
using the hyperbolic sine propagator

P_{V_{n}}(t)=\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{H_{V_{n}}-\tfrac{1}{4}})}{\sqrt{H_{V_{n}}-\tfrac{1}{4}}}

that solves the wave equation for
H_{V_{n}}-\tfrac{1}{4}
. This is in contrast to several earlier works
[
67
,
28
,
1
,
68
]
, where a ball averaging propagator was used. The reason for introducing the wave propagator is that it satisfies the Duhamel formula allowing us to extract
V
as a multiplication operator. In this way, we can reduce the quantum variance to a free part and an error part. We establish the convergence of the error using the
L^{2}
bound for the sequence of potentials
V_{n}
and the Benjamini-Schramm convergence. For the free part, as in the recent work of Hippi
[
56
]
, we can use the exponential mixing of the geodesic flow from the works of Ratner and Matheus
[
85
,
75
]
to obtain a uniform bound in the Benjamini-Schramm limit and then take time to infinity to obtain the convergence. Compared to
[
67
,
28
,
1
,
68
]
, the use of exponential mixing replaces the quantitative ergodic theorem of Nevo
[
83
]
. When comparing Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
with the unperturbed results in sequences of locally symmetric spaces in
[
67
,
1
,
84
,
56
]
one difference is that here we need to put any additional assumption on the windows
I_{n}
. This assumption on the window
I_{n}
appears when we estimate the number of eigenvalues of
H_{V_{n}}
in the window
I_{n}
. It is not immediately clear whether the assumption is just a product of the techniques used or whether it is a necessary assumption.

Compared to
[
23
]
instead of studying Heisenberg evolution at a fixed macroscopic
I=I_{n}
window directly, they bound the quantum variance over the macroscopic window by the quantum variance on narrow spectral windows of size
2\eta
, where
\eta>0
. The quantum variance in a narrow spectral window
[E-\eta,E+\eta]
can be rewritten as a trace involving the spectral projection in this narrow window. The spectral projection on the narrow window can then be approximated by
\eta\mathrm{Im}\frac{1}{H_{N}+(E+i\eta)}
, where
H_{N}
is the adjacency matrix of the underlying graph of size
N
. Hence, in this approach bounding the quantum variance becomes a question of bounding certain traces involving imaginary parts of resolvents of the operator and then taking
N
to infinity followed by taking
\eta
to zero. Although the two methods are different, one can note that interestingly, the sine propagator used here and the imaginary part of the resolvent used in
[
23
]
can be related by a Laplace transform. Hence, heuristically, one could think of
1/\eta
as taking the role of
T
in our approach. It would be an interesting future problem to try to translate their proof to surfaces.

Moreover, is natural to ask about sharpness and weaker forms of behaviour of the eigenstates. For example, if the potential grows sufficiently rapidly, one expects localisation phenomena (e.g. via Agmon estimates) such as when the limiting potential
V
would have a pure point spectrum. On the other hand, even if we had absolutely continuous spectrum in the limit, if the geometric assumptions fail, for instance if the uniform spectral gap go to zero rapidly enough, one expects quantum ergodicity to break down. Similarly, global negative curvature seems important, as the large-scale quantum mixing fails for the growing tori, see
[
56
]
. This is consistent with known counterexamples in graph models, see for instance recent examples of McKenzie
[
76
]
. At the present stage however, these constructions use crucially the graph structure and it is not clear how they would translate as examples to give sharpness to Theorem
1.1
. Finally, in a critical regime where
V
not too rapidly but without pure point spectrum, it could also be of interest to investigate
intermediate
quantum statistics such as quantum multifractality that have seen recent developments in the Euclidean setting; see e.g.
[
97
,
61
,
96
]
.

2.
Geometric preliminaries

2.1.
Hyperbolic surfaces

We will by
\mathbb{H}
denote the hyperbolic plane and we will identify
\mathbb{H}
with the upper half plane, that is

\mathbb{H}=\big\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,y>0\big\}

equipped with the Riemannian metric

ds^{2}=\frac{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}{y^{2}}.

For a point
z\in\mathbb{H}
and a tangent vector
v\in T_{z}\mathbb{H}
, we will also write
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{z}
for its norm with this metric.
We will write
d(z,z^{\prime})
for the distance between two points
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}
. Moreover, the hyperbolic volume is given by

dz=\frac{1}{y^{2}}dxdy.

The orientation-preserving isometry group of
\mathbb{H}
can be identified with the group
\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
, consisting of real
2\times 2
matrices of determinant 1 modulo
\pm\operatorname{id}
. The group
\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
acts on
\mathbb{H}
via Möbius transformations. That is, for
\gamma\in\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
with

(2.1)

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}

the element
\gamma z
for any
z\in\mathbb{H}
is given by

\gamma z=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.

The unit tangent bundle of
\mathbb{H}
is

T^{1}\mathbb{H}=\big\{(z,v)\in\mathbb{H}\times T_{z}\mathbb{H}\,|\,\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{z}=1\big\}.

The group
\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
also acts on
T^{1}\mathbb{H}
through the action

\gamma(z,v)=\Big(\tfrac{az+b}{cz+d},\tfrac{v}{(cz+d)^{2}}\Big),

for any
(z,v)\in T^{1}\mathbb{H}
and
\gamma\in\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
, where
\gamma
is again given by (
2.1
). It can be verified that the two spaces
\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
and
T^{1}\mathbb{H}
can be identified. This can be done, for example, by evaluating the action at the point
(i,i)
to show that
\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
is homeomorphic to
T^{1}\mathbb{H}
. This leads to the identification that a point
(z,v)\in T^{1}\mathbb{H}
is identified with
\gamma\in\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
such that
\gamma(i,i)=(z,v)
.

A compact hyperbolic hyperbolic surface can be seen as the quotient
X=\Gamma\setminus\mathbb{H}
of
\mathbb{H}
, where
\Gamma\in\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
is a discrete subgroup consisting solely of hyperbolic elements and the identity. That is, for all
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\operatorname{id}\}
we have
|\Tr[\gamma]|>2
. These groups are called Fuchsian groups. Given a point
z
in
\mathbb{H}
, we can associate the Dirichlet fundamental domain
D
to
X
defined by

D=D_{z}:=\big\{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}\,|\,d(z,z^{\prime})<d(z,\gamma z^{\prime}),\,\forall\,\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\operatorname{id}\}\big\}.

We will, if nothing else is assumed, suppose that the fundamental domain is generated around the point
i
in
\mathbb{H}
.

The injectivity radius of
X=\Gamma\setminus\mathbb{H}
at a point
z\in X
is given by

\InjRad_{X}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\operatorname{id}\}}d(z,\gamma z),

and the global injectivity radius of the surface
X
is

\InjRad_{X}=\inf_{z\in X}\InjRad_{X}(z).

In our analysis, we will need to split the surface into two parts, the one where the injectivity radius is large and the other where it is small: for
T>0
we will use the notation

X(>2T)\coloneqq\{x\in X\,|\,\InjRad_{X}(x)\geq 2T\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad X(\leq 2T)=X\setminus X(>2T).

We will use similar notation for the corresponding parts of the surface in the fundamental domain.

2.2.
Lattice counting

In the proof, we will need some a priori lattice counting estimates. This is contained in the following lemma, which is a slightly modified version of
[
74
, Lemma 5]
, and the proof is based on the same ideas. The difference is that we are giving explicit constants.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X=\Gamma\setminus\mathbb{H}
, where
\Gamma
is a strictly hyperbolic Fuchsian group. Then for any

0<r\leq\min\!\left(1,\frac{\InjRad_{X}}{2}\right)
and
t\geq 0
one has

\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}}\sharp\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid d(x,\gamma y)\leq t\}\leq\frac{e^{t+1}}{r^{2}}.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{H}
be two points in the hyperbolic plane.
Due to our assumptions on
r
, it follows that
B(\gamma y,r)\cap B(\gamma^{\prime}y,r)=\varnothing
for all
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
and

(2.2)

\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Gamma\\
d(x,\gamma y)\leq t\end{subarray}}B(\gamma y,r)\subset B(x,t+r).

Now we set

M_{xy}=\sharp\big\{\gamma\in\Gamma\,\big|\,d(x,\gamma y)\leq t\big\}.

Using (
2.2
) and that
\Vol(B(\gamma y,r))=4\pi\sinh^{2}(\frac{r}{2})
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
we have

4\pi e^{t+r}\geq 4\Vol(B(x,t+r))\geq M_{xy}16\pi\sinh^{2}(\frac{r}{2})\geq M_{xy}4\pi e^{r-1}r^{2}.

This implies the bound

M_{xy}\leq\frac{e^{t+1}}{r^{2}},

where we have used our assumptions on
r
. Since the upper bound is independent of
x
and
y
, this implies the stated estimate and concludes the proof.
∎

2.3.
Quantitative exponential mixing of the geodesic flow

The geodesic flow
\varphi_{t}
on
\mathbb{H}
is a one-parameter family of maps

\varphi_{t}:T^{1}\mathbb{H}\mapsto T^{1}\mathbb{H},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}

such that for any point
(z,v)\in T^{1}\mathbb{H}
the curve
\varphi_{t}(z,v)=(\eta(t),\eta^{\prime}(t))
is the unique geodesic parameterised by the arc length that satisfies
\eta(0)=z
and
\eta^{\prime}(0)=v
. Under the identification of
T^{1}\mathbb{H}
with
\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
we can parameterise the geodesic flow as a right multiplication in
\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
by the one parameter subgroup

\Bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}e^{t/2}&0\\
0&e^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\,\Bigg|\,t\in\mathbb{R}\Bigg\}.

Given a point
z
in
\mathbb{H}
, we can parameterise any other point
z^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{H}
using the geodesic flow. We do this in the usual way by fixing some direction so that it has angle 0 and then any point
z^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{H}
can be written as

z^{\prime}=\pi_{1}(\varphi_{r}(z,\theta)),

where
r=d(z,z^{\prime})
,
\pi_{1}
is the projection onto the first coordinate, and
\theta
is the angle depending on the fixed direction and
z^{\prime}
. Using this, we can define the polar coordinates in
\mathbb{H}
around
z
and the change of variables
z^{\prime}\mapsto(r,\theta)
transforms the metric and the volume element as follows

ds^{2}=dr^{2}+\sinh^{2}(r)d\theta^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad dz=\sinh(r)drd\theta.

Let us now fix a compact hyperbolic surface
X=\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{R})\setminus\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
is a lattice in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
. As a Riemannian surface
X
, we have the canonical unit-cotangent bundle
T^{1}X
consisting formally of pairs
x\in X
and directions
\theta\in S_{x}^{*}X
, which we can identify with the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Then the unit co-tangent bundle of
X
can be identified with
T^{1}X=\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\Gamma,
where also geodesic flow
\varphi_{t}:T^{1}X\to T^{1}X
is defined, and for which the unique absolutely continuous invariant measure
Liouville measure

dx\,d\xi
projects onto the hyperbolic volume measure
dx
on
\mathbb{H}
.

A key tool in the proof of Theorem
1.1
is a quantitative exponential mixing rates for the geodesic flow on compact hyperbolic surfaces
X
purely in terms of the spectral gap
\lambda_{1}(X)
. These were first proved by Ratner
[
85
]
who connected the spectral gap to the rate of exponential mixing of the geodesic flow via representation theoretic approach, but the quantitative bound we use here comes from the work of Matheus
[
75
]
, in particular that the multiplicative constant is independent of the surface. We will use it in the following form:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X
be a compact connected hyperbolic surface with geodesic flow
\varphi_{t}
and with
\lambda_{1}(X)
being the smallest eigenvalue of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
-\Delta_{X}
. Let
f,g\in L^{2}(X)
. Then for all
t\geq 0

\bigg|\big\langle f\circ\varphi_{t},g\big\rangle_{L^{2}(T^{1}X)}-\int_{T^{1}X}f\int_{T^{1}X}g\bigg|\leq 11\,e^{\beta(\lambda_{1}(X))}\,(1+t)\,e^{-\beta(\lambda_{1}(X))t}\,\|f\|_{2}\,\|g\|_{2},

where

\displaystyle\beta(\lambda)=\begin{cases}1-\sqrt{1-4\lambda},\qquad&\text{ if }\lambda\leq\frac{1}{4},\\
1,\qquad&\text{ if }\lambda>\frac{1}{4},\end{cases}

and where
f(x)=f(x,\theta)
and
g(x)=g(x,\theta)
are viewed as functions on
T^{1}X
, the unit tangent bundle.

Note that in this statement, the observables
f,g
are in
L^{2}(X)
and then lifted to
L^{2}(T^{1}X)
. This is why the error term only has
L^{2}
norms. There is a more general (and usual) form in which the observables are defined on phase space
T^{1}X
, and then the error term can be given in terms of Sobolev norms of the observables. This can probably be used to give a phase space version of our result along the lines of Abért-Bergeron-Le Masson
[
1
]
, but we do not pursue this in this work.

3.
Spectral theory of Schödinger operators

3.1.
Definition of operators, self-adjointness and large scale spectral bounds

Given a connected hyperbolic surface
X
we denote by
-\Delta_{X}
the standard Laplace-Beltrami operator acting in
L^{2}(X)
, defined in the usual way. This is a positive self-adjoint operator with domain
\mathcal{D}(-\Delta_{X})=H^{2}(X)
, the second order Sobolev space. By the Kato-Rellich theorem it follows that for all real valued
V\in L^{\infty}(X)
the Schrödinger operator

H_{V}=-\Delta_{X}+V,

is well defined, self-adjoint, and has domain
\mathcal{D}(H_{V})=H^{2}(X)
. For details, see, e.g.
[
87
]
. Moreover, we have that if the hyperbolic surface
X
is compact, then
-\Delta_{X}
and
H_{V}
for any real valued
V\in L^{\infty}(X)
have a purely discrete spectrum. For the case where
X=\mathbb{H}
we have that
-\Delta_{\mathbb{H}}
have purely absolute continuous spectrum in
[\frac{1}{4},\infty)
. Moreover, according to the Kato–Rellich theorem, for any real valued
V\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{H})
, the essential spectrum of
H_{V}
is
[\frac{1}{4},\infty)
. In particular, note that we do not a priori know that
[\frac{1}{4},\infty)
is an absolute continuous spectrum. Self-adjointness of the operators gives us access to the functional calculus for the operator, and hence for any measurable function
f
the (possible unbounded) operator
f(H_{V})
is well defined; see, e.g.,
[
86
]
. We will here be interested in three different functions of our operators. Two of them are defined in the following subsection. The third function corresponds to the spectral projections. For a given interval
I
we will use the notation
\Pi_{I}(H_{V})
to denote the spectral projection of
H_{V}
onto
I
. That is,

\Pi_{I}(H_{V})=\bm{1}_{I}(H_{V}).

In our setting, we are considering a sequence of surfaces that Benjamini-Schramm converges to the hyperbolic plane. For our approach here, it is important to know how the number of eigenvalues in a fixed window
I=[a,b]\subset(\frac{1}{4},\infty)
behaves under this limit. For the case of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on a sequence of compact connected hyperbolic surfaces that Benjamini-Schramm converges to the hyperbolic plane we have from
[
67
, Lemma 9.1]
the following large scale Weyl law result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of compact connected hyperbolic surfaces that satisfy the property
(BSC)
. For any compact interval
I\subset(\frac{1}{4},\infty)
, we have

(3.1)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathcal{N}(-\Delta_{X_{n}},I)}{\Vol(X_{n})}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bm{1}_{I}(\tfrac{1}{4}+r^{2})\tanh(\pi r)r\,dr,

where
\mathcal{N}(-\Delta_{X_{n}},I)
denotes the number of eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{X_{n}}
in the interval
I
.

Using this result, we have the following asymptotic lower bound for the number of eigenvalues for a sequence of Schrödinger operators in a sequence of compact intervals
I_{n}
containing a fixed spectral window
I
.

Lemma 3.2

(Lower bound for counting function)
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of compact connected hyperbolic surfaces that satisfy the property
(BSC)
and let
\{V_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of potentials satisfying
(POT)
. For any compact interval
I_{n}=[a_{n},b_{n}]\subset(\frac{1}{4},\infty)
containing a fixed
I=[a,b]\subset I_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
b-C_{\max}>a-\min(0,C_{\min})
, there exist a positive constant
c
and a natural number
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(3.2)

\mathcal{N}(H_{V_{n}},I_{n})\geq c\Vol(X_{n})\qquad\text{for all $n\geq N$.}

The constant
c
and
N
depends on the numbers
a
,
b
,
C_{\max}
, and
C_{\min}
.

Proof.

In this proof, we will let
\widetilde{C}_{\min}=\min(0,C_{\min})
. Due to our assumptions on the sequence of potentials, we have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

-\Delta_{X_{n}}+\widetilde{C}_{\min}\leq H_{V_{n}}\leq-\Delta_{X_{n}}+C_{\max}

in the sense of quadratic forms. By the min-max Theorem (see, e.g.
[
88
, Chapter XIII]
), this implies for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
that

\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{(-\infty,\alpha)}(-\Delta_{X_{n}}+C_{\max})\big]\leq\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{(-\infty,\alpha)}(H_{V_{n}})\big]\leq\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{(-\infty,\alpha)}(-\Delta_{X_{n}}+\widetilde{C}_{\min})\big].

The same inequalities are true if
\alpha
is included in the interval. Using these, we have that

\displaystyle\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{[a,b]}(H_{V_{n}})\big]={}

\displaystyle\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{(-\infty,b]}(H_{V_{n}})\big]-\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{(-\infty,a)}(H_{V_{n}})\big]

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{(-\infty,b]}(-\Delta_{X_{n}}+C_{\max})\big]-\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{(-\infty,a)}(-\Delta_{X_{n}}+\widetilde{C}_{\min})\big]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{(-\infty,b-C_{\max}]}(-\Delta_{X_{n}})\big]-\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{(-\infty,a-\widetilde{C}_{\min})}(-\Delta_{X_{n}})\big]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{[a-\widetilde{C}_{\min},b-C_{\max}]}(-\Delta_{X_{n}})\big].

We set
\tilde{I}=[a-\widetilde{C}_{\min},b-C_{\max}]
. Due to our assumptions, we have
\tilde{I}\subset(\frac{1}{4},\infty)
. Since
\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{\tilde{I}}(-\Delta_{X_{n}})\big]=\mathcal{N}(-\Delta_{X_{n}},\tilde{I})
it follows from Lemma
3.1
that there exists a positive constant
c
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{[a-\widetilde{C}_{\min},b-C_{\max}]}(-\Delta_{X_{n}})\big]\geq c\Vol(X_{n})\qquad\text{for all $n\geq N$,}

where the constant
c
and
N
depends on the numbers
a
,
b
,
C_{\max}
and
\widetilde{C}_{\min}
. Combining this with the estimate obtained above, we have the following

\Tr\big[\bm{1}_{[a,b]}(H_{V_{n}})\big]\geq c\Vol(X_{n})\qquad\text{for all $n\geq N$.}

This concludes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Propagators

As usual in these kinds of results we will introduce a propagator to access dynamical information. Let
X
be a connected hyperbolic surface, and let
V\in L^{\infty}(X)
be real valued and consider the Schrödinger operator
H_{V}=-\Delta+V
. Then the propagator we will use here is the following wave propagator

P_{V}(t)=\big(H_{V}-\tfrac{1}{4}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sin\big(t\sqrt{H_{V}-\tfrac{1}{4}}\big),

where the subtraction of
\frac{1}{4}
is a spectral shift, which is introduced since the spectrum of
-\Delta_{\mathbb{H}}
is
[\frac{1}{4},\infty)
. We can define these propagators using the functional calculus. One thing to note here is that
P_{V}(t)
is self-adjoint and bounded for all
t\geq 0
. To see this, let
h:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be given by

(3.3)

h(t,\lambda)=\big(\lambda-\tfrac{1}{4}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sin\big(t\sqrt{\lambda-\tfrac{1}{4}}\big).

That
h
is indeed real valued can be seen by taking
\lambda_{0}<\frac{1}{4}
and computing that

h(t,\lambda_{0})=\big(\tfrac{1}{4}-\lambda_{0}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sinh\big(t\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}-\lambda_{0}}\big).

Moreover, it is also easily seen that when restricted to
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times[\alpha,\infty)
, the function
h
is bounded, for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Since our operator
P_{V}(t)=h(t,H_{V})
and
H_{V}
is self-adjoint and semi-bounded from below, we see that
P_{V}(t)
is a self-adjoint bounded operator.

The intuition behind calling this a propagator is that for a suitable function
\varphi
, then the function
u(t,x)=P_{V}(t)\varphi
is a solution to the wave equation
\partial_{t}^{2}u=H_{V}u
with the initial conditions
u(0,x)=0
and
\partial_{t}u(t,x)|_{t=0}=\varphi(x)
.

It would be desirable to have good expressions for the kernel of the operator
P_{V}(t)
. However, this is not feasible when the potential
V
is just a general bounded function with real values. What we have instead is that the propagator
P_{V}(t)
satisfies a Duhamel formula. For the sake of completeness, we will include the formula as a lemma and also prove it.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
be a connected hyperbolic surface and
H_{V}=-\Delta_{X}+V
be a Schrödinger operator with
V\in L^{\infty}(X)
with the convention that
H_{0}=-\Delta_{X}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator. Consider the wave propagators

P_{V}(t)=h(t,H_{V})\qquad\text{and}\qquad P_{0}(t)=h(t,H_{0}),

where
h
is given by (
3.3
). Then for any
t>0
we have

(3.4)

P_{V}(t)=P_{0}(t)-Q_{V}(t),

where

(3.5)

Q_{V}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}P_{V}(t_{1})VP_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1}.

Proof.

To establish this formula, we will need the following axillary operator
S_{i}(t)
for
i\in\{0,V\}
given by

S_{i}(t)=\cos\big(t\sqrt{H_{i}-\tfrac{1}{4}}\big).

By functional calculus, this is again a well-defined operator. For these operators, we have the following relations

\partial_{t}P_{i}(t)=S_{i}(t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\partial_{t}S_{i}(t)=-H_{i}P_{i}(t),

where
i\in\{0,V\}
. Using these relations, we obtain from the fundamental theorem of calculus that

\displaystyle P_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}S_{i}(t_{1})\,dt_{1},\qquad\text{for $i\in\{0,V\}$}

and

\displaystyle S_{V}(t)

\displaystyle=S_{0}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}S_{V}(t_{1})H_{0}P_{0}(t-t_{1})-P_{V}(t_{1})H_{V}S_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1}.

where we have used
P_{i}(0)=0
and
S_{i}(0)=1
for
i\in\{0,V\}
. Combining these expressions yields the following identity

(3.6)

\displaystyle P_{V}(t)={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}S_{0}(t_{2})+\int_{0}^{t_{2}}S_{V}(t_{1})H_{0}P_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})-P_{V}(t_{1})H_{V}S_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})\,dt_{1}dt_{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle P_{0}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}S_{V}(t_{1})H_{0}P_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})-P_{V}(t_{1})H_{V}S_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})\,dt_{1}dt_{2}.

What remains to establish (
3.4
) is to prove that the double integral equals
-Q_{V}(t)
. To establish this, we first notice that by the fundamental theorem of calculus we also have the following

(3.7)

\displaystyle P_{V}(t)={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{t_{1}}\big[P_{V}(t_{1})S_{0}(t-t_{1})\big]\,dt_{1}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}S_{V}(t_{1})S_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1}+\int_{0}^{t}P_{V}(t_{1})H_{0}P_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1}.

Returning to the double integral in (
3.6
) we notice that only one of the operators in each term depends on the variable
t_{2}
. Hence, by integrating we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}S_{V}(t_{1})H_{0}P_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})-P_{V}(t_{1})H_{V}S_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})\,dt_{1}dt_{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}S_{V}(t_{1})\,dt_{1}-\int_{0}^{t}S_{V}(t_{1})S_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1}-\int_{0}^{t}P_{V}(t_{1})H_{V}P_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle P_{V}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}S_{V}(t_{1})S_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1}-\int_{0}^{t}P_{V}(t_{1})H_{V}P_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}P_{V}(t_{1})H_{0}P_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1}-\int_{0}^{t}P_{V}(t_{1})H_{V}P_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}P_{V}(t_{1})VP_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1},

where we have used (
3.7
). This establishes that the double integral is indeed equal to
-Q_{V}(t)
, and therefore we have established the Duhamel formula (
3.4
).
∎

With this formula, it will suffice for later estimates to have a good expression for the kernel of
P_{0}(t)
. This is the content of the following Lemma which is taken from
[
56
]
, where it is Proposition 6.1.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X=\Gamma\setminus\mathbb{H}
, where
\Gamma
be a strictly hyperbolic Fuchsian group, and let
-\Delta_{X}
be the Laplace-Beltrami operator. Set

P_{0}(t)=h(t,-\Delta_{X}).

Then the integral kernel of
P_{0}(t)
is given by

K^{\Gamma}_{t}(x,y)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}A(t,d(x,\gamma y))=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}\pi}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\frac{\bm{1}(t>d(x,\gamma y))}{\sqrt{\cosh(t)-\cosh(d(x,\gamma y))}}.

The function
A(t,r)
from the above lemma is the Abel kernel and we will refer to it by that name in what follows. For later applications, the following small lemma on integrals in time for the function
h(t,\lambda)
will be useful.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be given by (
3.3
) and let
I=[a,b]\subset(\frac{1}{4},\infty)
be a compact interval. Then there exists
T_{0}>0
depending on
a
and
b
such that

\inf_{\lambda\in I}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}h^{2}(t,\lambda_{0})\,dt\geq\frac{1}{3(b-\frac{1}{4})}\qquad\text{for all $T\geq T_{0}$}.

Proof.

The proof is a simple calculation, where we will use the identity
\sin^{2}(t\theta)=\frac{1-\cos(2t\theta)}{2}
. For
\lambda>\frac{1}{4}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}h^{2}(t,\lambda)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\sin^{2}(t\sqrt{\lambda-\frac{1}{4}})}{(\lambda-\frac{1}{4})}\,dt=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1-\cos\big(2t\sqrt{\lambda-\frac{1}{4}})}{2(\lambda-\frac{1}{4})}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(\lambda-\frac{1}{4})}-\frac{\sin\big(2T\sqrt{\lambda-\frac{1}{4}})}{2T(\lambda-\frac{1}{4})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\geq\frac{1}{2(\lambda-\frac{1}{4})}-\frac{1}{2T(\lambda-\frac{1}{4})^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

This inequity gives us the following lower bound

\inf_{\lambda\in I}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}h^{2}(t,\lambda_{0})\,dt\geq\frac{1}{2(b-\frac{1}{4})}-\frac{1}{2T(a-\frac{1}{4})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\geq\frac{1}{3(b-\frac{1}{4})}\qquad\text{for all $T\geq T_{0}$,}

where
T_{0}
depends on the values of
a
and
b
. This completes the proof.
∎

The propagator discussed so far is the one we will use to prove point (1) Theorem
1.1
. To prove points (2) and (3) in Theorem
1.1
we will need to slightly modify one of the propagators used. This is the same modified propagator used in
[
56
]
. For a given
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
we define the function
\tilde{h}_{\tau}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(3.8)

\tilde{h}_{\tau}(t,\lambda)=\cos(\tau t)h(t,\lambda)=\cos(\tau t)\big(\lambda-\tfrac{1}{4}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sin\big(t\sqrt{\lambda-\tfrac{1}{4}}\big).

As above, we then define the family of operators
\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)
by

\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)=\tilde{h}_{\tau}(t,H_{V})=\cos(\tau t)P_{V}(t).

From Lemma
3.4
we also obtain a kernel expression for
\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)
by simply multiplying the kernel of
P_{0}(t)
by
\cos(\tau t)
. Moreover, since
\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)=\cos(\tau t)P_{V}(t)
the operator
\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)
will also satisfy a Duhamel formula

(3.9)

\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)=\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)-\widetilde{Q}_{V}(t,\tau),

where
\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)=\tilde{h}_{\tau}(t,-\Delta_{X})
and

(3.10)

\widetilde{Q}_{V}(t,\tau)=\cos(\tau t)\int_{0}^{t}P_{V}(t_{1})VP_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1}.

We conclude this subsection with the following small lemma on integrals in time for products of functions
h(t,\lambda)
and
\tilde{h}_{\tau}(t,\lambda)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
and let the functions
h,\tilde{h}_{\tau}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be given by (
3.3
) and (
3.8
) respectively. Assume that
\frac{1}{4}<m<a,b<\infty
and that

\sqrt{a-\tfrac{1}{4}}-\sqrt{b-\tfrac{1}{4}}-\tau\in(-\delta,\delta),

where
\delta\in(0,\frac{2}{9}\sqrt{m-\frac{1}{4}})
. Then for
T=\frac{\pi}{2\delta}
it holds

\frac{1}{T}\Bigg|\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{h}_{\tau}(t,a)h(t,b)\,dt\Bigg|>8\pi\sqrt{a-\tfrac{1}{4}}\sqrt{b-\tfrac{1}{4}}.

A proof of this lemma can be found in
[
56
]
, where it is Proposition 4.1.

4.
Quantitative bounds on Hilbert-Schmidt norms

In this section, we establish a number of estimates on Hilbert-Schmidt norms needed in our proof of the two main results. However, before considering the Hilbert-Schmidt norms, we will need some a priori integral estimates. Then for the estimates on the Hilbert-Schmidt norms we will firstly prove estimates that enable localisation to the “thick” part of the surface. Then we will establish estimates for the terms in which the potential appears. Next, we will use exponential mixing as in
[
56
]
to estimate the main free term. Finally, we will combine all three estimates into a single quantitative estimate needed for our analysis.

4.1.
A priori integral bounds

The first integral bound is a slight modification of
[
56
, Proposition 7.2]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}
and
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then we have the following

\int_{\mathbb{H}}A(t,d(z,x))A(t^{\prime},d(z^{\prime},x))\,dx\leq\frac{\bm{1}_{[0,t+t^{\prime}]}(d(z,z^{\prime}))}{\sqrt{\sinh(\max(|t-t^{\prime}|,d(z,z^{\prime})))}},

where
A(\tau,r)
is the Abel kernel introduced in Lemma
3.4
.

The difference between this lemma and
[
56
, Proposition 7.2]
is only in the constant. This is because here we have the factor
(2\sqrt{2}\pi)^{-2}
on the left-hand side.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}
and
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then we have the following

\int_{\mathbb{H}}\frac{\bm{1}_{[0,t]}(d(x,z))\bm{1}_{[0,t^{\prime}]}(d(x,z^{\prime}))}{\sqrt{\sinh(d(x,z))}\sqrt{\sinh(d(x,z^{\prime}))}}\,dx\leq\bm{1}_{[0,t+t^{\prime}]}(d(z,z^{\prime}))4\pi\min(t,t^{\prime}).

Proof.

By symmetry, we may without loss of generality assume that
t^{\prime}\leq t
. Moreover, due to the presence of the two indicator functions, we see that the integral is zero if
d(z,z^{\prime})>t+t^{\prime}
.

Suppose that
z=z^{\prime}
. Then by switching to polar coordinates centred at z we obtain the following

(4.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}}\frac{\bm{1}(d(x,z)<t^{\prime})}{\sinh(d(x,z))}\,dx=2\pi\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}1\,dr=2\pi t^{\prime}.

Suppose that
0<d(z,z^{\prime})\leq 2t^{\prime}
. We first extend the area of integration to the entire ball
B(z^{\prime},t^{\prime})
and split this integral into two parts depending on whether
x
is closer to
z
or
z^{\prime}
.

(4.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}}\frac{\bm{1}(d(x,z)<t)\bm{1}(d(x,z^{\prime})<t^{\prime})}{\sqrt{\sinh(d(x,z))}\sqrt{\sinh(d(x,z^{\prime}))}}\,dx\leq{}

\displaystyle\int_{B(z^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sinh(d(x,z))}\sqrt{\sinh(d(x,z^{\prime}))}}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\int_{D_{1}}\frac{1}{\sinh(d(x,z))}\,dx+\int_{D_{2}}\frac{1}{\sinh(d(x,z^{\prime}))}\,dx,

where
B(z^{\prime},t^{\prime})
is the ball centered at
z^{\prime}
with radius
t^{\prime}
and the sets
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are given by

\displaystyle D_{1}=B(z^{\prime},t^{\prime})\cap\big\{x\in\mathbb{H}\,\big|\,d(x,z)<d(x,z^{\prime})\big\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad D_{2}=B(z^{\prime},t^{\prime})\cap\big\{x\in\mathbb{H}\,\big|\,d(x,z)\geq d(x,z^{\prime})\big\}.

For each of the two integrals on the right-hand side, we switch polar coordinates centred at
z
and
z^{\prime}
respectively and obtain

(4.3)

\displaystyle\int_{D_{1}}\frac{1}{\sinh(d(x,z))}\,dx+\int_{D_{2}}\frac{1}{\sinh(d(x,z^{\prime}))}\,dx\leq 4\pi\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}1dr=4\pi t^{\prime}

Suppose that
2t^{\prime}<d(z,z^{\prime})\leq t+t^{\prime}
. For this case, we find that if
x\in B(z^{\prime},t)
, then we have
d(x,z)\geq d(x,z^{\prime})
. Hence, if we switch to polar coordinates centred at
z^{\prime}
we find that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}}\frac{\bm{1}(d(x,z)<t)\bm{1}(d(x,z^{\prime})<t^{\prime})}{\sqrt{\sinh(d(x,z))}\sqrt{\sinh(d(x,z^{\prime}))}}\,dx\leq{}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\frac{\sinh(r)}{\sqrt{\sinh(R(r,\theta))\sinh(r)}}\,drd\theta

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle 2\pi\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}1\,dr=2\pi t^{\prime},

where
R(r,\theta)
is
d(x,z)
written in these polar coordinates. Combining the estimates in (
4.1
), (
4.2
), (
4.3
), and (
4.4
) we obtain the state estimate, and this concludes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Restriction to thick parts of the surface

Here, we establish that it will be sufficient to consider the propagated observable on the thick part of the surface. Recall the notation

X(>2T)\coloneqq\{x\in X\,|\,\InjRad_{X}(x)\geq 2T\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad X(\leq 2T)=X\setminus X(>2T),

for all
T>0
. Moreover, we will use similar notation for the fundamental domain of
X
. Before we prove the main result of this section, we will need an auxiliary lemma on Hilbert-Schmidt norm bounds of free propagation.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
X=\Gamma\setminus\mathbb{H}
, where
\Gamma
be a strictly hyperbolic Fuchsian group, and let
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
P_{0}(t)
and
\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)
be the free and modified free wave propagator associated with
-\Delta_{X}
. Then for all
a\in L^{\infty}(X)
,
0<r\leq\min(1,\frac{\InjRad_{X}}{2})
and all
T>0
we have for any
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
\max(t,t^{\prime})\leq T
the estimates

\big\lVert P_{0}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(\leq 2T)}P_{0}(t^{\prime})\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq\frac{8\pi Te^{4T+1}\lVert a\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X)}}{r^{2}}\Vol(X(\leq 2T)),

and

\big\lVert\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)a\bm{1}_{X(\leq 2T)}P_{0}(t^{\prime})\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq\frac{8\pi Te^{4T+1}\lVert a\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X)}}{r^{2}}\Vol(X(\leq 2T)).

Proof.

Firstly, we notice that since
\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)=\cos(\tau t)P_{0}(t)
we have

\big\lVert\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)a\bm{1}_{X(\leq 2T)}P_{0}(t^{\prime})\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq\big\lVert P_{0}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(\leq 2T)}P_{0}(t^{\prime})\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}.

Hence, we only need to establish the bound for
\big\lVert P_{0}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(\leq 2T)}P_{0}(t^{\prime})\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}
. Using that
P_{0}(t)
has an explicit kernel that is positive, we obtain

(4.5)

\displaystyle\big\lVert P_{0}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(\leq 2T)}P_{0}(t^{\prime})\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}={}

\displaystyle\int_{D}\int_{D}\Big|\sum_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma}\int_{X(\leq 2T)}A(t,d(x,\gamma_{1}w))a(w)A(t^{\prime},d(y,\gamma_{2}w))\,dw\Big|^{2}\,dxdy

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X)}\int_{D^{3}}\int_{D(\leq 2T)}\sum_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\in\Gamma}A(t,d(x,\gamma_{1}w))A(t^{\prime},d(y,\gamma_{2}w))

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\times A(t,d(x,\gamma_{3}w^{\prime}))A(t^{\prime},d(y,\gamma_{4}w^{\prime}))\,dwdw^{\prime}dxdy,

where we could also have restricted the integration domain for
w^{\prime}
to
D(\leq 2T)
. Using that the fundamental domain tiles the hyperbolic plane we obtain

(4.6)

\displaystyle\int_{D^{3}}\int_{D(\leq 2T)}\sum_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}\in\Gamma}A(t,d(x,\gamma_{1}w))A(t^{\prime},d(y,\gamma_{2}w))A(t,d(x,\gamma_{3}w^{\prime}))A(t^{\prime},d(y,\gamma_{4}w^{\prime}))\,dwdw^{\prime}dxdy

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{3}}\int_{D(\leq 2T)}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}A(t,d(x,w))A(t^{\prime},d(y,\gamma w))A(t,d(x,w^{\prime}))A(t^{\prime},d(y,w^{\prime}))\,dwdw^{\prime}dxdy.

Now, applying Lemma
4.1
to the integrals in
x
and
y
we obtain the following estimate

(4.7)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{3}}\int_{D(\leq 2T)}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}A(t,d(x,w))A(t^{\prime},d(y,\gamma w))A(t,d(x,w^{\prime}))A(t^{\prime},d(y,w^{\prime}))\,dwdw^{\prime}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}}\int_{D(\leq 2T)}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\frac{\bm{1}_{[0,t+t^{\prime}]}(d(\gamma w,w^{\prime}))\bm{1}_{[0,t+t^{\prime}]}(d(w,w^{\prime}))}{\sqrt{\sinh(d(w,\gamma w^{\prime}))\sinh(d(w,w^{\prime}))}}\,dwdw^{\prime}.

Then applying Lemma
4.2
to the integral in
w^{\prime}
and Lemma
2.1
to control the sum over
\Gamma
we obtain

(4.8)

\int_{\mathbb{H}}\int_{D(\leq 2T)}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\frac{\bm{1}_{[0,t+t^{\prime}]}(d(\gamma w,w^{\prime}))\bm{1}_{[0,t+t^{\prime}]}(d(w,w^{\prime}))}{\sqrt{\sinh(d(\gamma w,w^{\prime}))\sinh(d(w,w^{\prime}))}}\,dwdw^{\prime}\leq\frac{4\pi e^{2(t+t^{\prime})+1}(t+t^{\prime})}{r^{2}}\Vol(D(\leq 2T)).

By combining the estimates in (

)-(
4.8
), using the assumption that
\max(t,t^{\prime})\leq T
, and
\Vol(D(\leq 2T))=\Vol(X(\leq 2T))
, we obtain the stated estimate. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
X=\Gamma\setminus\mathbb{H}
, where
\Gamma
be a strictly hyperbolic Fuchsian group, and let

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{V}(t)aP_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle 2\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{V}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{C_{I}T^{7}e^{4T+1}\max(1,\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{4})\lVert a\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X)}}{r^{2}}\Vol(X(\leq 2T)),

and

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)aP_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle 2\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{C_{I}T^{7}e^{4T+1}\max(1,\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{4})\lVert a\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X)}}{r^{2}}\Vol(X(\leq 2T)).

In both cases, the constant

.

Proof.

The proof of the two bounds are analogous and we will only give a proof for the first bound. Since the sets

and

are disjoint, we have

(4.9)

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{V}(t)aP_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq{}

\displaystyle 2\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{V}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle+2\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{V}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(\leq 2T)}P_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}.

\tilde{a}=a\bm{1}_{X(\leq 2T)}

3.4
) for

\displaystyle\Bigg(\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}P_{0}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}+\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}Q_{V}^{*}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle+\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})Q_{V}(t)\tilde{a}P_{0}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}+\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})Q_{V}(t)\tilde{a}Q_{V}^{*}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\Bigg)^{2}.

4.3

(4.11)

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}P_{0}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq{}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\big\lVert P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}P_{0}(t)\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{8\pi}T^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{2T+\frac{1}{2}}\lVert a\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}}{r}\sqrt{\Vol(X(\leq 2T))}.

Recall that

Q_{V}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}P_{V}(t_{1})VP_{0}(t-t_{1})\,dt_{1},

we obtain for the second term the estimate

(4.12)

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}Q_{V}^{*}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}

4.3

4.3
to obtain the bound

(4.13)

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})Q_{V}(t)\tilde{a}Q_{V}^{*}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}_{I}^{2}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\frac{\sqrt{8\pi}T^{\frac{7}{2}}e^{2T+\frac{1}{2}}\lVert a\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}}{3r}\sqrt{\Vol(X(\leq 2T))},

where again
\tilde{C}_{I}=(\min(I)-\frac{1}{4})^{-1/2}
. By combining the estimates in (

)-(

(4.14)

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{V}(t)\tilde{a}P_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq C_{I}\frac{T^{7}e^{4T+1}\max(1,\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{4})\lVert a\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X)}}{r^{2}}\Vol(X(\leq 2T)),

. Finally, combining the estimates in (
4.9

4.3.
Bounds on terms involving the potential

In this subsection, we will state some quantitative bounds that will be used in the proof of the main result. First, we state an auxiliary bound. The second bound is the main estimate used to control the error terms.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
X=\Gamma\setminus\mathbb{H}
, where
\Gamma
is a strictly hyperbolic Fuchsian group, and let
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose
V\in L^{\infty}(X)
and let
P_{0}(t)
and
\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)
be the free and modified free wave propagator associated with
-\Delta_{X}

and

such that

\big\lVert P_{0}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{0}(t^{\prime})V\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq 4\pi(t+t^{\prime})\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}

and

\big\lVert\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{0}(t^{\prime})V\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq 4\pi(t+t^{\prime})\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, we have

\big\lVert\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{0}(t^{\prime})V\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq\big\lVert P_{0}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{0}(t^{\prime})V\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

since
\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)=\cos(\tau t)P_{0}(t)
. Hence, we will only prove the first bound.
Letting
K_{t}^{\Gamma}(x,y)
be the integral kernel of
P_{0}(t)

(4.15)

\displaystyle\big\lVert P_{0}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{0}(t^{\prime})V\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{D}\int_{D}\big|\int_{D(>2T)}\sum_{\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma}\sum_{\gamma_{1}\in\Gamma}A(t,d(x,\gamma_{1}w)a(w)A(t^{\prime},d(w,\gamma_{2}y))V(y)\,dw\big|^{2}\,dydx,

where we have used the form of the kernel
K^{\Gamma}_{t}(x,y)
as given in Lemma
3.4
.
Since the Abel kernel is positive, we see from taking the absolute value under the integral and performing
\gamma_{1}\mapsto\gamma_{1}^{-1}\gamma_{2}^{-1}

(4.16)

\displaystyle\int_{D}\int_{D}\big|\int_{D(>2T)}\sum_{\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma}\sum_{\gamma_{1}\in\Gamma}A(t,d(x,\gamma_{1}w)a(w)A(t^{\prime},d(w,\gamma_{2}y))V(y)\,dw\big|^{2}\,dydx

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\lVert a\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\int_{D}\int_{D}|V(y)|^{2}\big|\int_{\mathbb{H}(>2T)}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}A(t,d(\gamma x,w)A(t^{\prime},d(w,y))\,dw\big|^{2}\,dydx,

where
\mathbb{H}(>2T)=\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\gamma D(>2T)
. We then obtain that

(4.17)

\displaystyle\int_{D}\int_{D}|V(y)|^{2}\big|\int_{\mathbb{H}(>2T)}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}A(t,d(\gamma x,w)A(t^{\prime},d(w,y))\,dw\big|^{2}\,dydx

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{D}\int_{D}|V(y)|^{2}\int_{[\mathbb{H}(>2T)]^{2}}\sum_{\gamma,\tilde{\gamma}\in\Gamma}A(t,d(\gamma x,w)A(t^{\prime},d(w,y))A(t,d(\gamma x,\tilde{\gamma}\tilde{w})A(t^{\prime},d(\tilde{w},y))\,d\tilde{w}dwdydx

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}}\int_{D}|V(y)|^{2}\int_{[\mathbb{H}(>2T)]^{2}}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}A(t,d(x,w)A(t^{\prime},d(w,y))A(t,d(x,\gamma\tilde{w})A(t^{\prime},d(\tilde{w},y))\,d\tilde{w}dwdydx

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}}\int_{D}|V(y)|^{2}\int_{[\mathbb{H}(>2T)]^{2}}A(t,d(x,w)A(t^{\prime},d(w,y))A(t,d(x,\tilde{w})A(t^{\prime},d(\tilde{w},y))\,d\tilde{w}dwdydx,

\tilde{\gamma}\mapsto\tilde{\gamma}^{-1}\gamma
. Due to the properties of
A
, we have that
d(x,w),d(x,\gamma\tilde{w})<t
and
d(w,y),d(\tilde{w},y)<t^{\prime}

\frac{1}{2}\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\mathrm{id}\}}d(\tilde{w},\gamma\tilde{w})>2T\Rightarrow\forall\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\mathrm{id}\}:d(\tilde{w},\gamma\tilde{w})>4T.

Then by applying Lemma
4.1
to the integrals in
w

\tilde{w}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}\frac{\bm{1}_{[0,t+t^{\prime}]}(d(x,y))}{\sinh(\max(|t-t^{\prime}|,d(x,y)))}|V(y)\bm{1}_{D}(y)|^{2}\,dxdy

\displaystyle\leq 4\pi(t+t^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{H}}|V(y)\bm{1}_{D}(y)|^{2}\,dy

where in the second inequality, we have used Lemma
4.2
and the invariance over
\Gamma
of the volume measure. Combining the estimates in (
4.15
), (
4.16
), (
4.17
), and (
4.18
) we obtain the stated estimate, and this concludes the proof.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let

\Gamma

in both cases.

Proof.

The proof of both bounds are analogous, and we will only give a proof for the first bound. In the following, we let
\tilde{a}=a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}
. Using the Duhamel formula (
3.4
) we obtain the following estimate

(4.19)

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{V}(t)\tilde{a}P_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})(P_{0}(t)-Q_{V}(t))\tilde{a}(P_{0}(t)-Q_{V}^{*}(t))\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle 2\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}P_{0}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}+2\Big(\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}Q_{V}^{*}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle+\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})Q_{V}(t)\tilde{a}P_{0}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}+\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})Q_{V}(t))\tilde{a}Q_{V}^{*}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\Big)^{2}

\Pi_{I}(H_{V})
is a spectral projection, we have

(4.20)

\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}P_{0}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}P_{0}(t)\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}.

Q_{V}^{*}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}P_{0}(t-t_{1})VP_{V}(t_{1})\,dt_{1}

\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}Q_{V}^{*}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq{}\tilde{C}_{I}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\big\lVert P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}P_{0}(t-t_{1})V\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\,dt_{1}dt,

\tilde{C}_{I}=(\min(I))^{-1}

4.5
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\big\lVert P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}P_{0}(t-t_{1})V\big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\,dt_{1}dt\leq{}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}2\sqrt{\pi(2t-t_{1})}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}\,dt_{1}dt

\displaystyle 14\sqrt{\pi}\tilde{C}_{I}T^{\frac{3}{2}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}

Combining these two estimates, we get

(4.21)

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{0}(t)\tilde{a}Q_{V}^{*}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}+\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})Q_{V}(t)\tilde{a}P_{0}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle\leq 28\sqrt{\pi}\tilde{C}_{I}T^{\frac{3}{2}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}.

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})Q_{V}(t))\tilde{a}Q_{V}^{*}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}_{I}^{2}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\big\lVert P_{0}(t-t_{1})\tilde{a}P_{0}(t-t_{2})V\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\,dt_{1}dt_{2}dt,

\tilde{C}_{I}

4.5

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\big\lVert P_{0}(t-t_{1})\tilde{a}P_{0}(t-t_{2})V\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}\,dt_{1}dt_{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}2\sqrt{\pi(2t-t_{1}-t_{2})}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}e^{\frac{R_{V}}{2}}\,dt_{1}dt_{2}dt

Combining the estimates in (
4.19
)-(
4.22

Lemma 4.7

Let
\beta
be a positive constant smaller than 1 and let
0<t^{\prime}<t
. Then

F(t,t^{\prime},r)\leq\frac{\bm{1}_{[0,t+t^{\prime}](r)}}{\sinh(\max(t-t^{\prime},r))},

and moreover this implies that

\int_{0}^{2T}\sinh(r)(1+r)e^{-\beta r}F(t,t^{\prime},r)\,dr\leq\frac{4}{\beta^{2}}e^{-\frac{\beta}{4}(t-t^{\prime})}.

With this we are now ready to state and prove the main estimate of this subsection.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
X=\Gamma\setminus\mathbb{H}
, where
\Gamma
be a strictly hyperbolic Fuchsian group, and let
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
P_{0}(t)
and
\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)
be the free and modified free wave propagator associated with
-\Delta_{X}
. Moreover, set
\beta_{1}\coloneqq\beta(\lambda_{1})
, where
\lambda_{1}
is the first non-trivial eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{X}

\beta(x)=\begin{cases}1-\sqrt{1-4x},&\text{if $x\leq 1/4$}\\
1,&\text{if $x>1/4$}.\end{cases}

Then for all
T\geq 1
and
a\in L^{\infty}(X)
with zero average, we have the estimates

\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}P_{0}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{0}(t)\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq\frac{CT}{\beta_{1}^{3}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(X)}+4\pi T^{3}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\vol(X(\leq 2T)),

and

\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{0}(t)\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq\frac{CT}{\beta_{1}^{3}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(X)}+4\pi T^{3}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\vol(X(\leq 2T)),

where the constant
C=352e
.

Proof.

We will here give the proof for the second estimate. The first is obtained by an analogous argument that omits the factor
\cos(\tau t)
. Letting
K_{t}^{\Gamma}(x,y)
be the integral kernel of
P_{0}(t)
, we have

\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\widetilde{P}_{0}(t,\tau)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{0}(t)\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=\int_{D}\int_{D}\big|\int_{0}^{T}\cos(\tau t)\int_{D(>2T)}K_{t}^{\Gamma}(x,w)a(w)K_{t}^{\Gamma}(w,y)\,dwdt\big|^{2}\,dydx.

Recall that

K^{\Gamma}_{t}(x,y)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}A(t,d(x,\gamma y)).

By arguing as in the proof of Lemma
4.5
we obtain after expanding the square the following identity

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}P_{0}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{0}(t)\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{D}\int_{D}\big|\int_{0}^{T}\cos(\tau t)\int_{D(>2T)}\sum_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma}A(t,d(\gamma_{1}x,w))a(w)A(t,d((w,\gamma_{2}y))\,dwdt\big|^{2}\,dydx\Big|

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\cos(\tau t)\cos(\tau t^{\prime})\int_{D(>2T)}\int_{B(w,2T)\cap\mathbb{H}(>2T)}a(w)\overline{a(w^{\prime})}F(t,t^{\prime},d(w,w^{\prime}))dw^{\prime}dwdt^{\prime}dt\Big|,

where we view

\mathbb{H}
,
\mathbb{H}(>2T)=\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\gamma D(>2T)
, and the function

is as defined in (
4.23
). Moreover, the restriction of the integration domain of

follows from the support properties of the Abel kernels. We then notice that due to the support properties of

\displaystyle\int_{D(>2T)}\int_{B(w,2T)\cap\mathbb{H}(>2T)}a(w)\overline{a(w^{\prime})}F(t,t^{\prime},d(w,w^{\prime}))dxdydw^{\prime}dw

\displaystyle=\int_{D(>2T)}\int_{B(w,2T)\cap D(>2T)}a(w)\overline{a(w^{\prime})}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}F(t,t^{\prime},d(w,\gamma w^{\prime}))dxdydw^{\prime}dw

\displaystyle=\int_{D(>2T)}\int_{B(w,2T)\cap D(>2T)}a(w)\overline{a(w^{\prime})}F(t,t^{\prime},d(w,w^{\prime}))dxdydw^{\prime}dw,

where an argument analogous to the one used in the proof of Lemma

(4.24)

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}P_{0}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{0}(t)\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\cos(\tau t)\cos(\tau t^{\prime})\int_{D(>2T)}\int_{B(w,2T)\cap D(>2T)}a(w)\overline{a(w^{\prime})}F(t,t^{\prime},d(w,w^{\prime}))dw^{\prime}dwdt^{\prime}dt\Big|,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\cos(\tau t)\cos(\tau t^{\prime})\int_{D(>2T)}\int_{B(w,2T)\cap D(\leq 2T)}a(w)\overline{a(w^{\prime})}F(t,t^{\prime},d(w,w^{\prime}))\,dw^{\prime}dwdt^{\prime}dt

and

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\int_{D}\int_{B(w,2T)}a(w)\overline{a(w^{\prime})}F(t,t^{\prime},d(w,w^{\prime}))\,dw^{\prime}dw\Big|dt^{\prime}dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\int_{D(>2T)}\int_{B(w,2T)\cap D(\leq 2T)}a(w)\overline{a(w^{\prime})}F(t,t^{\prime},d(w,w^{\prime}))\,dw^{\prime}dw\Big|dt^{\prime}dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\int_{D(\leq 2T)}\int_{B(w,2T)}a(w)\overline{a(w^{\prime})}F(t,t^{\prime},d(w,w^{\prime}))\,dw^{\prime}dw\Big|dt^{\prime}dt

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}(a,T)+\mathcal{I}_{2}(a,T)+\mathcal{I}_{3}(a,T),

where we have used

. We will first estimate

and
\mathcal{I}_{3}(a,T)

to the integrals of the variables

and

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}(a,T)+\mathcal{I}_{3}(a,T)

where we first evaluated the integral in

by switching to polar coordinates centered at

and then the remaining integrals were evaluated.

we first notice that

is symmetric in

and

. Therefore, we obtain the following

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}(a,T)\leq 2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\Big|\int_{D}\int_{B(w,2T)}a(w)\overline{a(w^{\prime})}F(t,t^{\prime},d(w,w^{\prime}))\,dw^{\prime}dw\Big|dt^{\prime}dt

Next we write
w^{\prime}
in polar coordinates around

such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}(a,T)

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\Big|\int_{D}\int_{0}^{2T}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(w)\overline{a(\pi_{1}(\varphi_{r}(w,\theta))}\sinh(r)F(t,t^{\prime},r)\,d\theta drdw\Big|dt^{\prime}dt

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{2T}\sinh(r)F(t,t^{\prime},r)\big|\langle a,a\circ\varphi_{r}\rangle_{L^{2}(D\times\mathbb{S}^{1})}\big|drdt^{\prime}dt.

(4.28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}(a,T)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{88e^{\beta_{1}}}{\beta_{1}^{2}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(X)}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\beta_{1}}{4}(t-t^{\prime})}dt^{\prime}dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{CT}{\beta_{1}^{3}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(X)},

4.25

4.27

4.28

4.5.
Quantitive bound on the full Hilbert-Schmidt norm

We will here combine the previously obtained estimates into a single estimate for the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of main interest. This is the content of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
X=\Gamma\setminus\mathbb{H}
, where
\Gamma
be a strictly hyperbolic Fuchsian group, and let
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
.
For a

H_{V}=-\Delta_{X}+V
and for

the associated wave and modified wave propagator
P_{V}(t)
and
\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)
. Moreover, suppose that

\beta_{1}\coloneqq\beta(\lambda_{1})
, where
\lambda_{1}
is the first non-trivial eigenvalue of

and

\beta(x)=\begin{cases}1-\sqrt{1-4x},&\text{if $x\leq 1/4$}\\

with average zero,
0<r\leq\min(1,\frac{\InjRad_{X}}{2})
and all

we have

(4.29)

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{V}(t)aP_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{CT}{\beta_{1}^{3}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(X)}+16\pi T^{3}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\vol(X(\leq 2T))

\displaystyle+C_{I}T^{7}\max(1,\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{4})\lVert a\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X)}\left(\frac{e^{4T+\frac{1}{2}}\Vol(X(\leq 2T))}{r^{2}}+\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\right)

and

(4.30)

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)aP_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{CT}{\beta_{1}^{3}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(X)}+16\pi T^{3}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\vol(X(\leq 2T))

\displaystyle+C_{I}T^{7}\max(1,\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{4})\lVert a\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X)}\left(\frac{e^{4T+\frac{1}{2}}\Vol(X(\leq 2T))}{r^{2}}+\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\right),

where the constants are given by
C=1408e
and
C_{I}=\big(200\pi\max(1,(\min(I)-\frac{1}{4})^{-1})\big)^{2}
in both cases.

Proof.

Again, the proofs of the two estimates are analogous, hence we will only prove the first estimate. We start by applying Lemma
4.4
and obtain the bound

(4.31)

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{V}(t)aP_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle 2\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{V}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{C_{I,1}T^{7}e^{4T+1}\max(1,\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{4})\lVert a\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X)}}{r^{2}}\Vol(X(\leq 2T)),

where the constant
C_{I,1}=16\pi\max(1,(\min(I)-\frac{1}{4})^{-1})
. Next, we apply Lemma
4.6
to the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.31
). This gives us the estimate

(4.32)

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}\Pi_{I}(H_{V})P_{V}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{V}(t)\Pi_{I}(H_{V})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}P_{0}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{0}(t)\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}+C_{I,2}T^{7}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\max(1,\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2})\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}^{2},

where the constant

4.6
to the first term on the right-hand side of (

(4.33)

\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T}P_{0}(t)a\bm{1}_{X(>2T)}P_{0}(t)\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\leq\frac{\tilde{C}T}{\beta_{1}^{3}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(X)}+4\pi T^{3}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\vol(X(\leq 2T)),

where the constant
\tilde{C}=352e
. Combing the estimates from (

), (
4.32
), and (

h,\tilde{h}_{\tau}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be given by

h(t,\lambda)=\big(\lambda-\frac{1}{4}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sin\big(t\sqrt{\lambda-\tfrac{1}{4}}\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{h}_{\tau}(t,\lambda)=\cos(\tau t)\big(\lambda-\frac{1}{4}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sin\big(t\sqrt{\lambda-\tfrac{1}{4}}\big),

We get for
\lambda_{j}^{(n)},\lambda_{k}^{(n)}\in I_{n}
such that
|\varrho_{j}^{(n)}-\varrho_{k}^{(n)}-\tau|<\delta

\langle a_{n}\psi_{j}^{(n)},\psi_{k}^{(n)}\rangle_{L^{2}(X_{n})}=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{T_{\delta}}\int_{0}^{T_{\delta}}\tilde{h}_{\tau}(t,\lambda_{k}^{(n)})h(t,\lambda_{j}^{(n)})\,dt}\langle\frac{1}{T_{\delta}}\int_{0}^{T_{\delta}}\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)a_{n}P_{V}(t)\,dt\,\psi_{j}^{(n)},\psi_{k}^{(n)}\rangle_{L^{2}(X_{n})},

where we have used
h(t,H_{V})=P_{V}(t)
and
h_{\tau}(t,H_{V})=\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)
. From Lemma
3.6
we have

\inf_{\lambda_{j}^{(n)},\lambda_{k}^{(n)}\in I^{\prime}}\frac{1}{T_{\delta}}\Big|\int_{0}^{T_{\delta}}\tilde{h}_{\tau}(t,\lambda_{k}^{(n)})h(t,\lambda_{j}^{(n)})\,dt\Big|>8\pi\big(b^{\prime}-\tfrac{1}{4}\big).

Since
I_{n}\subset I^{\prime}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we obtain from the above estimate for all
\lambda_{j}^{(n)},\lambda_{k}^{(n)}\in I_{n}
such that
|\varrho_{j}^{(n)}-\varrho_{k}^{(n)}-\tau|<\delta
the estimate

\big|\langle a_{n}\psi_{j}^{(n)},\psi_{k}^{(n)}\rangle_{L^{2}(X_{n})}\big|^{2}\leq\frac{64\pi^{2}b^{\prime 2}}{T_{\delta}^{2}}\Big|\langle\int_{0}^{T_{\delta}}\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)a_{n}P_{V}(t)\,dt\,\psi_{j}^{(n)},\psi_{k}^{(n)}\rangle_{L^{2}(X_{n})}\Big|^{2}.

Using this estimate, we obtain with an analogous estimate to the one used in the proof of point (1) the following estimate

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq k:\lambda_{j}^{(n)},\lambda_{k}^{(n)}\in I_{n}\\
|\varrho_{j}^{(n)}-\varrho_{k}^{(n)}-\tau|<\delta(\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}\Big|\langle a_{n}\psi_{j}^{(n)},\psi_{k}^{(n)}\rangle_{L^{2}(X_{n})}\Big|^{2}\lesssim\frac{b^{\prime 2}}{T_{\delta}^{2}}\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T_{\delta}}\Pi_{I_{n}}(H_{V}^{(n)})\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)a_{n}P_{V}(t)\Pi_{I_{n}}(H_{V}^{(n)})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}.

Applying Lemma
4.9
to the Hilbert-Schmidt norm on the right-hand side, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\frac{b^{\prime}}{T_{\delta}^{2}}\Big\lVert\int_{0}^{T_{\delta}}\Pi_{I_{n}}(H_{V}^{(n)})\widetilde{P}_{V}(t,\tau)a_{n}P_{V}(t)\Pi_{I_{n}}(H_{V}^{(n)})\,dt\Big\rVert_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{C_{1}}{T_{\delta}\beta_{1}^{3}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(X)}+16\pi T^{3}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\vol(X(\leq 2T))

\displaystyle+C_{2}T_{\delta}^{5}\max(1,\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{4})\lVert a\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X)}\left(\frac{e^{4T_{\delta}+\frac{1}{2}}\Vol(X(\leq 2T_{\delta}))}{r^{2}}+\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}^{2}\right),

where
C_{1},C_{2}
depend only on
I,I^{\prime}
. Now, again arguing as in the proof of point (1) this gives us the following

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}\big(H_{V}^{(n)},I_{n}\big)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq k:\lambda_{j}^{(n)},\lambda_{k}^{(n)}\in I_{n}\\
|\varrho_{j}^{(n)}-\varrho_{k}^{(n)}-\tau|<\delta(\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}\Big|\langle a_{n}\psi_{j}^{(n)},\psi_{k}^{(n)}\rangle_{L^{2}(X_{n})}\Big|^{2}\leq\frac{2C_{1}\delta}{\pi\beta_{1}^{3}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X)},

where we have used
T_{\delta}=\frac{\pi}{2\delta}
. From this estimate, we see that by choosing
\delta
sufficiently small we obtain the desired estimate. This concludes the proof.
∎

6.
Proof of probabilistic main result

We do this just for a single fixed window
I=[a,b]
, the same argument holds for a sequence of windows
I_{g}
as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. In order to establish Theorem
1.2
, we will need the probabilistic versions of
(BSC)
,
(UND)
, and
(EXP)
. These are collected in the following Theorem.

Theorem 6.1
.

Suppose that
X
is a random hyperbolic surface with the distribution of
X
given by the Weil-Petersson distribution on the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g}
. Denote by

0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots

the eigenvalues of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
-\Delta_{X}
. Then with probability
1-o(1)
for large genus the following properties hold:

(1)

\InjRad_{X}\geq g^{-\frac{1}{24}}\log(g)^{\frac{9}{16}}.

(2)

\frac{\Vol(\{x\in X\,|\,\InjRad_{X}(x)<\frac{1}{6}\log(g)\})}{\Vol(X)}=\mathcal{O}\big(g^{-\frac{1}{4}}\big).

(3)

For any
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{4})

\lambda_{1}\geq\frac{1}{4}-\eta.

(4)

For any compact interval
I\subset(\frac{1}{4},\infty)

\Bigg|\frac{\mathcal{N}(-\Delta_{X},I)}{\Vol(X)}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bm{1}_{I}(\tfrac{1}{4}+r^{2})\tanh(\pi r)r\,dr\Bigg|=\mathcal{O}\bigg(\sqrt{\max(I)-\frac{1}{4}}-\sqrt{\min(I)-\frac{1}{4}}+\sqrt{\tfrac{\max(I)+1}{\log(g)}}\bigg).

Properties (1) and (2) in the above theorem are taken from
[
80
]
, property (4) is obtained similar to
[
68
, Theorem 6.2]
using the results in
[
80
]
, and property (3) is taken from
[
9
,
54
]
, although up to changing the constants, a weaker result would suffice for our purposes (e.g.
[
78
]
or
[
99
]
). The first three properties are those that replace assumptions
(BSC)
,
(UND)
, and
(EXP)
in this setting. Property (4) we will use to establish a lower bound on the number of eigenvalues in an interval
I
in this setting. This is the content of the following lemma.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
X
be a random hyperbolic surface with the distribution of
X
given by the Weil-Petersson distribution in the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g}
. Suppose that
V\in L^{\infty}(X)
and consider the Schrödinger operator
H_{V}=-\Delta_{X}+V
. Then with probability
1-o(1)
for large genus For any compact interval
I=[a,b]\subset(\frac{1}{4},\infty)
, such that
b-\max(V)>a-\min(0,\min(V))
, there exist a positive constant
c
and a natural number
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(6.1)

\mathcal{N}(H_{V_{n}},I)\geq c\Vol(X)-\mathcal{O}\bigg(\sqrt{\max(\tilde{I})-\frac{1}{4}}-\sqrt{\min(\tilde{I})-\frac{1}{4}}+\sqrt{\tfrac{\max(\tilde{I})+1}{\log(g)}}\bigg).

where
\tilde{I}=[a-\min(0,\min(V),b-\max(V)]
.

Proof.

By arguing as in the proof Lemma
3.2
we obtain the following inequality

\mathcal{N}(H_{V_{n}},I)\geq\mathcal{N}(-\Delta_{X},\tilde{I}),

where
\tilde{I}=[a-\min(0,\min(V),b-\max(V)]
. Then using Theorem
6.1
point
(4)
, we obtain the stated estimate, which concludes the proof.
∎

With this established, we can now give an outline for the proof of Theorem
1.2
that provides details on how to modify the arguments used in
[
68
]
to include potentials. Moreover, we will only give this outline of property (1) as the proof of properties (2) and (3) is obtained by analogous arguments.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We just need to find a sequence
\varepsilon_{g}\to 0
such that the probability of the event that a surface
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g}
satisfies for any
V
and orthognormal basis
\psi_{j}^{(g)}
on
X
:

\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}\big(H_{V_{g}}^{(g)},I_{g}\big)}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}^{(g)}\in I_{g}}\Big|\langle a_{g}\psi_{j}^{(g)},\psi_{j}^{(g)}\rangle_{L^{2}(X_{g})}-\frac{1}{\Vol(X_{g})}\int_{X_{g}}a_{g}(x)\,dx\Big|^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{g},

converges to
1
as
g\to\infty
. We may assume without the loss of generality that the average of
a_{g}
is zero for all
g\in\mathbb{N}
. By arguing as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
part (1) we obtain the following estimate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}\big(H_{V_{g}}^{(g)},I_{g}\big)}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}^{(g)}\in I_{g}}\Big|\langle a_{g}\psi_{j}^{(g)},\psi_{j}^{(g)}\rangle_{L^{2}(X_{g})}\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}\big(H_{V_{g}}^{(g)},I_{g}\big)}\Bigg(\frac{C}{\beta_{1}^{3}T}\left\lVert a_{g}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(X_{g})}+16\pi T\left\lVert a_{g}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\vol(X_{g}(\leq 2T))

\displaystyle+\tilde{C}_{I}T^{5}\max(1,\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X_{g})}^{4})\lVert a_{g}\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X_{g})}\left(\frac{e^{4T+\frac{1}{2}}\Vol(X_{g}(\leq 2T))}{r^{2}}+\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X_{g})}^{2}\right)\Bigg)

where
r\leq\min(0,\frac{\InjRad_{X_{g}}}{2})
and
\beta_{1}=1-\sqrt{1-4\lambda_{1}^{(g)}}
. By applying Theorem
6.1
and Lemma
6.2
we obtain with high probability and for large genus the upper bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}\big(H_{V_{g}}^{(g)},I_{g}\big)}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}^{(g)}\in I}\Big|\langle a_{g}\psi_{j}^{(g)},\psi_{j}^{(g)}\rangle_{L^{2}(X_{g})}\Big|^{2}\leq{}

\displaystyle C_{g}\Bigg[\frac{C_{1}\left\lVert a_{g}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}(X_{g})}}{(1-2\sqrt{\eta})^{3}T}+\frac{C_{2}T\left\lVert a_{g}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\vol(X_{g}(\leq 2T))}{\Vol(X_{g})}

\displaystyle+\tilde{C}_{I}\left(\frac{T^{5}e^{4T+\frac{1}{2}}g^{\frac{1}{12}}\Vol(X_{g}(\leq 2T))}{\log(g)^{\frac{9}{8}}\Vol(X_{g})}+\frac{T^{5}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X_{g})}^{2}}{\Vol(X_{g})}\right)\Bigg],

where the constants will contain the missing terms from the previous estimate with the exception of
C_{g}
. The constant
C_{g}
will satisfy that

C_{g}^{-1}=c-\frac{1}{\Vol(X)}\mathcal{O}\bigg(\sqrt{\max(\tilde{I})-\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\min(\tilde{I})-\frac{1}{4}}+\sqrt{\tfrac{\max(\tilde{I})+1}{\log(g)}}\bigg)

where
\tilde{I}=[a-\min(0,\min(V),b-\max(V)]
and
c
is some positive constant. Now by choosing
T=c\log(g)
for some small
c\in(0,1/6)
and then taking genus to infinity we see that we obtain the desired results with high probability for a suitable
\varepsilon_{g}\to 0
due to the properties (1)-(4) of Theorem
6.1
, see
[
68
]
for a more quantitative deduction in the zero potential case. As mentioned before the outline of proof, the proof of part (2) and (3) are analogous to the one given above.
∎
