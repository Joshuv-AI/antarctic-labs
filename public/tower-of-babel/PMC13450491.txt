Modernizing renal eligibility in oncology trials
Authors: Harold Nathan Tan, Sreedhar Mandayam, Prabir Roy-Chaudhury, Timothy A. Yap
Published: 2026

The selection of appropriate eligibility criteria is a cornerstone of early phase clinical trial design. The objective assessment of renal function is especially critical, as a significant proportion of investigational agents undergo renal elimination. Impaired renal function can result in systemic drug accumulation and dose-dependent toxicities. In early phase trials, where the safety profile of oncology drug products remains largely uncertain, satisfactory renal function plays a key role in minimizing the risk of toxicities. Therefore, ensuring acceptable renal function at trial entry is essential for minimizing patient risk and preserving the integrity of pharmacokinetic and safety data that guide further clinical development.

Renal eligibility criteria extend beyond determining study entry. It influences access to investigational therapies, the representativeness of enrolled populations, and the applicability of trial findings to routine clinical practice. This issue is particularly relevant across the Americas, where early-phase oncology trials are conducted in academic cancer centers, regional referral networks, community-based programs, and health systems with variable access to laboratory testing and nephrology expertise. A modern framework must protect participants and preserve pharmacologic fidelity, while remaining feasible across these different trial settings.

Historically, the Cockcroft–Gault (CG) equation has been widely used to estimate renal function in early phase trials.1 Its simplicity and longstanding adoption have supported standardization across studies. However, its limitations are increasingly relevant in contemporary cancer populations. CG was derived in a small historical cohort and assumes a fixed relation between serum creatinine, body weight, and muscle mass.2 That assumption is frequently violated in patients with cancer who often experience significant changes in body composition due to sarcopenia, cachexia, or treatment-related effects.

These limitations are particularly consequential in early phase trials, where accurate renal assessment is critical for determining eligibility and dose escalation. Underestimating renal impairment may result in excessive drug exposure and increased toxicity, whereas overestimating impairment may lead to inappropriate dose reduction, reduced efficacy, or exclusion of otherwise suitable patients from potentially beneficial clinical trials. Either error can distort pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic modeling and weaken real-world relevance.

Serum creatinine is influenced not only by renal function but also by muscle mass, nutritional status, fluid balance, age, and sex.3 Conversely, cystatin C is affected by inflammation, corticosteroid use, thyroid dysfunction, and adiposity.4 A modern strategy should acknowledge these limitations rather than assume one marker or cutoff is definitive.

No single marker provides a perfect estimate of GFR. The Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2024 guidelines and the American Society of Onco-Nephrology (ASON) now advocate for a more sophisticated approach: using validated equations that incorporate both serum creatinine and cystatin, specifically the CKD-EPIcr-cys equation.5,6 Creatinine remains inexpensive, widely available, and well established in clinical pharmacology, whereas cystatin C is produced at a relatively constant rate by all nucleated cells and is less affected by muscle mass, age, or sex, offering a more stable biomarker under certain conditions.7 Because their non-GFR determinants differ, combining the two markers can provide a more reliable and less biased estimate of GFR.

Measured GFR using exogenous filtration markers may be warranted in select cancer patients, particularly when estimated GFR falls near key thresholds for drug dosing or trial eligibility.5,6 Although these assessments are more resource-intensive, their greater precision may be important for agents with substantial renal clearance, narrow therapeutic windows, or anticipated nephrotoxicity. A pragmatic framework across the Americas would use contemporary race-free eGFR for routine screening, add dual-marker assessment when results are borderline or clinically discordant, and reserve measured GFR for high-consequence decisions. This approach improves confidence in renal assessment without making advanced testing a universal requirement or relying on historical creatinine thresholds that lack a pharmacologic rationale.

Feasibility must be embedded in protocol design. Access to advanced nephrology testing, including measured eGFR and cystatin assays, varies substantially across the Americas. Although these resources may be readily available at major academic centers in the United States and Canada, patients enrolled through regional centers in Latin America and the Caribbean may face additional costs, travel, and delays when specialized testing is required. Protocols should define a core assessment at every site, with prespecified triggers for confirmation. Where these tests are not available locally, central laboratories and referral networks should be incorporated into the trial infrastructure.

These operational considerations have direct implications for representativeness. Kidney dysfunction in patients with cancer commonly reflects the cumulative burden of aging, medical comorbidities, prior therapies, limited preventive care, and unequal access to specialized cancer services. Because these factors are unevenly distributed across geographic regions and health systems, restrictive or resource-dependent renal criteria may disproportionately exclude populations already underrepresented in early-phase research. Race should not be used as a biological adjustment in GFR estimation.8 Instead, race-free assessment methods should be coupled with transparent reporting of the renal function method used, the reasons for renal-related screen failure, and the characteristics of excluded patients. Such reporting would allow investigators to determine whether renal criteria are proportionately protecting participants or creating avoidable barriers to investigational therapies.

Modern oncology trials must consider tailoring eligibility criteria to reflect the mechanism of action, drug metabolism, and potential renal toxicity of the specific investigational new drug being tested. For example, agents primarily cleared via the renal route might require more stringent criteria or dose adjustments for patients with impaired kidney function. However, agents metabolized through non-renal pathways may permit broader eligibility when preclinical and early pharmacokinetic data support this approach. Stable chronic kidney disease should be distinguished from acute kidney injury or changing renal function, for which deferral is appropriate until stability is restored. Incorporating this mechanistic understanding at the design stage can support rational inclusion criteria that maximize patient safety without unduly restricting access to those with borderline renal impairment.

At the same time, it is important to recognize the inherent risks of enrolling patients with impaired renal function at early dose levels, especially in trials using rule-based designs where early toxicity events can disproportionately influence dose-escalation decisions. Rather than advocating for across-the-board inclusion, a more refined strategy would involve integrating accurate renal function assessment tools and tailoring eligibility to the drug's metabolic profile. Pre-specified renal function subgroups or expansion cohorts in later dose levels could allow for safer, staged evaluation of inclusion in patients with mild to moderate renal dysfunction.

While the primary objective of most phase I trials is to evaluate the safety of an investigational agent, renal eligibility criteria should not remain static as the drug advances through development. Real-world cancer populations more frequently exhibit varying degrees of renal impairment, reflecting the natural disease burden and effects of prior therapies. As trials progress into later phases, new pharmacokinetic, safety, and efficacy data may support a dynamic reassessment of renal function thresholds. The US Food and Drug Administration has encouraged the use of contemporary estimates or direct measures of GFR rather than absolute serum creatinine thresholds and has emphasized prospective consideration of organ dysfunction during development.9,10

Redefining renal function eligibility criteria in early phase oncology trials is essential to address the limitations of traditional creatinine-based estimates and to reflect advances in renal biomarker development. This evolution does not imply a loosening of safety standards, but rather a shift toward individualized, evidence-based assessments aligned with the pharmacologic profile of the investigational agent.
