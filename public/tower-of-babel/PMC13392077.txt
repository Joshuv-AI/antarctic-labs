Nano-graphitic biochar from pyrolyzed lemon peel as an efficient adsorbent for cationic dye removal from aqueous solutions
Authors: Alaa M. O. Al-Bedaiwi, Sabrein H. Mohamed, Alyaa I. Salim, Tarek A. Seaf Elnasr, Tamer H. A. Hasanin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The valorization of agricultural waste into effective adsorbents presents a promising strategy for sustainable wastewater remediation. This study investigates the conversion of lemon peel (LP), a common biowaste, into nano-graphitic biochar (NGB). Evaluating both materials as green adsorbents for the removal of the cationic dye Basic Fuchsin (BF). FTIR, SEM, and XRD were used to characterize the NGB. The obtained data confirm graphitization, high porosity, and exfoliated morphology compared to raw LP. The batch adsorption experiment conditions, pH, adsorbent dose, contact time, initial dye concentration, and temperature, have been optimized. NGB demonstrated exceptional performance, achieving 99.2% removal efficiency within just 5 min at optimal conditions (pH 7, 0.1 g adsorbent dose), significantly outperforming raw LP (86.4%). Isotherm analysis revealed that adsorption onto LP followed the Freundlich model (R2 = 0.972), indicating heterogeneous, multilayer adsorption. In contrast, NGB adsorption was best described by the Temkin model (R2 = 0.988), suggesting a uniform distribution of binding energies. Kinetic data were accurately fitted to a pseudo-second-order model, and thermodynamic parameters confirmed a spontaneous exothermic physisorption process. Furthermore, NGB exhibited excellent regeneration, maintaining 97.3% efficiency after five adsorption-desorption cycles. Application to spiked real water samples (groundwater, saline wells) yielded recovery rates up to 96%, underscoring its practical potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-58251-1.

Introduction

The main environmental challenges of the twenty-first century are water pollution and environmental change. Water pollution from textile industry releases of synthetic dyes poses a serious risk due to the harmful pollutants they contain. It is essential to regulate dye levels in wastewater before it is discharged into the environment1. Synthetic dyes are not readily biodegradable and are resistant to oxidation, making their removal difficult. Techniques such as photocatalysis, membrane filtration, adsorption, and advanced oxidation processes are employed to eliminate dyes, organic matter, heavy metals, and other contaminants. Water pollution from industrial effluents remains one of the most urgent environmental issues. Among various pollutants, synthetic dyes are particularly concerning due to their complex aromatic structures, high stability, and resistance to biodegradation. Large amounts of dyes are widely used in industries such as textiles, leather, paper, cosmetics, plastics, and food processing, resulting in coloured wastewater released into natural water bodies. It is estimated that approximately 10–15% of dyes used in industrial processes are lost in effluents, resulting in persistent pollution that threatens both aquatic life and human health2. Clean water is vital not only for household use but also for agriculture and industry, making water conservation a global priority. Effluents from various industries, including sugar production, power generation, distilleries, fertilizer, and cement, introduce heavy metals and harmful chemicals into nearby water bodies. Likewise, oil refineries discharge hydrocarbons and toxic byproducts. The paper and leather industries release chromium compounds and other hazardous materials3 Pesticide and pharmaceutical plants add organic pollutants. The textile and dye industries release effluent in solvents with significant ecological impacts4,5. Overall, these discharges pose serious risks to aquatic ecosystems, biodiversity, and public health.6,7.

Textile effluents are particularly concerning because they contain significant amounts of synthetic dyes, many of which are toxic and carcinogenic. Around 40% of colorants used worldwide are chlorine-containing substances, which are carcinogenic and can lead to other health issues8. It is essential to treat textile wastewater before releasing it into the environment to prevent these harmful effects. Basic fuchsin (BF) is a cationic artificial dye widely used for its vivid magenta color and versatility9,10. In addition to textile uses, BF also has anesthetic and fungicidal properties, making it useful in medicine, laboratories, and agriculture. However, its persistence in the environment and potential toxicity require careful control. Long-term exposure to BF-contaminated wastewater can cause headaches, dizziness, muscle spasms, nausea, vomiting, and other carcinogenic health effects10.

Wastewater containing BF is produced by medical laboratories, textile, and leather industries, making its safe disposal a critical issue. Traditional treatment methods, including precipitation, membrane filtration, ion exchange, and adsorption, have been extensively studied for dye removal. Among these, adsorption is particularly effective, especially with activated carbon, due to its porosity and high surface area. Unfortunately, the cost of activated carbon is prohibitive, preventing large-scale use and prompting researchers to explore affordable, renewable alternatives11. Biosorbents12 are a promising choice for wastewater treatment due to their affordability, renewability, and environmental friendliness. They are derived from natural sources and are effective at removing a wide variety of contaminants, including heavy metals, organic pollutants, and dyes13. Their renewability, ease of regeneration, and low or no additional cost make them even more attractive as eco-friendly adsorbents. Most importantly, biosorbents function without toxic chemicals or high-energy inputs, supporting their use in sustainable water treatment processes. Therefore, exploring agricultural waste as an adsorbent offers a sustainable approach to treating wastewater while adding value to the waste14,15.

This study examines the potential of lemon peel and its pyrolyzed product as eco-friendly adsorbents for removing basic fuchsin dye from water. It focuses on optimizing adsorption conditions, modeling adsorption isotherms and kinetics, and analyzing thermodynamic behavior. Additionally, the practicality of this method is tested using real water samples from various regions of Saudi Arabia, showing its potential for scalable, environmentally friendly wastewater treatment. The present study introduces a simple and sustainable approach for converting lemon peel waste into nano-garaphitic bochar (NGB) via controlled pyrolysis. The novelty of this work lies in the direct comparison between raw lemon peel and its graphitized form, highlighting the significant enhancement in adsorption performance achieved after graphitization. Furthermore, this study demonstrates rapid and highly efficient removal of Basic Fuchsin within a short contact time, which is rarely reported for biomass-derived adsorbents. Therefore, the main objectives of this work are to (i) synthesize NGB from lemon peel, (ii) evaluate and compare its adsorption performance with raw biomass, (iii) investigate adsorption kinetics, isotherms, and thermodynamics, and (iv) assess its applicability in real water samples.

Materials and methods

Materials

All experiments were conducted using distilled water from a Milli-Q Direct 8 system (Millipore, France). Basic fuchsin (BF; C20H19N3·HCl, MW = 337.85 g/mol, ≥ 99% purity), sodium nitrite (NaNO₂, MW = 69.00 g/mol, ≥ 99% purity), sodium chloride (NaCl, MW = 58.44 g/mol, ≥ 99.5% purity), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, MW = 40.00 g/mol, pellets, ≥ 98% purity), and silver nitrate (AgNO3, MW = 169.87 g/mol, ≥ 99% purity) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. Zinc chloride (ZnCl₂, MW = 136.29 g/mol, ≥ 98% purity), sodium sulfate (Na2SO4, MW = 142.04 g/mol, ≥ 99% purity), potassium chloride (KCl, MW = 74.55 g/mol, ≥ 99% purity), and hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37% w/w, ≥ 99% purity) were from Alpha Chemical Inc., USA. All reagents were of analytical purity and used as received.

Preparation of the investigated adsorbents

Preparation of NGB adsorbents

In this study, lemons were obtained from the Sakaka market in Al Jouf, Saudi Arabia. It was thoroughly washed, peeled, and dried at 80 °C for about 24 h. After drying, the lemon was ground into a fine powder, washed to remove dirt and impurities, and then dried to remove moisture for analysis. NGB was produced from fresh lemon peels through a multi-step process. The dried lemon peel powder was pyrolyzed in a tubular furnace at 500 °C for 3 h under oxygen-free conditions. Before heating, the furnace chamber was purged with high-purity nitrogen gas to remove residual air, and the system was maintained in a closed configuration throughout the process to prevent oxygen ingress. The absence of oxygen ensured controlled thermal decomposition of the biomass into carbon-rich NGB rather than oxidative combustion. Such oxygen-free pyrolysis conditions are widely reported as essential for producing graphitized carbon materials from biomass precursors13,14. After cooling to room temperature under a nitrogen atmosphere, the obtained carbonaceous product was collected, sonicated, centrifuged to remove residual impurities, and dried for further characterization and adsorption experiments (Fig. 1).

Structural characterization

The morphology and microstructural features of the lemon peel and NGB samples were examined before and after the adsorption process using a Zeiss Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM), which included a Carl Zeiss Sigma 500 VP and JSM 5400 LV microscopes. The crystallinity of the materials was investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 Advanced X-ray diffractometer (Cu Kα radiation, λ = 0.15406 nm) operated at 40 kV. The N2 isotherm surface characterization of lemon peel and NGB was performed at 77 K using a NOVA 4200e instrument. The functional groups in lemon peel and lemon peel NGB were analyzed by Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) with a Shimadzu IR Tracer-100 Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrophotometer. The FTIR spectrum was recorded in the 4000–400 cm− 1 range at a resolution of 2 cm− 1, with an average of 20 scans, using a Nicolet spectrophotometer (model 6700) and the KBr pellet technique15,16.

The point of zero charge (pH pZC) of the prepared materials was determined using the drift method. Six 100 mL samples of 0.01 mol/L NaCl solution, with initial pH values ranging from 2 to 12, were prepared and adjusted to the desired pH using 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH. Then, 0.1 g of adsorbent was added to each solution, and the solutions were shaken at room temperature for 24 h. The final pH values were measured after the samples were shaken overnight at room temperature. The results were graphed to illustrate the relationship between the initial pH and the difference between initial and final pH values (ΔpH)17.

The impacts of solution pH (2–9), adsorbent dose (0.01–0.5 g for LP or 0.01–0.2 g for NGB), initial dye concentration (2–100 µg/mL), contact time (30–90 min), temperature (20–60 °C), and co-existing salts (0.01–0.07 M KCl, ZnCl2, NaNO2, AgNO3, NaCl, Na2SO4) were systematically examined at a steady temperature of 25 °C. Construction of calibration curve

A calibration curve for BF dye was created by preparing standard solutions with concentrations ranging from 0.05 to 15.00 µg/mL. UV–Vis spectrophotometry was used to measure the absorbance of each solution at the dye’s maximum absorption wavelength (λmax = 540 nm). As shown in Fig. 2, the calibration plot was highly linear across the experimental concentration range, with a regression coefficient (R2) of 0.9998. This high linearity confirms the method’s reliability for accurately quantifying BF concentration in future adsorption analysis studies.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical characterization of adsorbent

The FTIR spectra of LP and NGB adsorbents are illustrated in Fig. 3A,B. Regarding LP, the FTIR spectrum (Fig. 3A) shows cellulose peaks in the fingerprint region (1000–1200 cm− 1), indicating that cellulose is a primary component of the peel. Additionally, bands at 1650 and 1750 cm− 1 indicate the presence of free and esterified carboxyl groups, suggesting the presence of pectin in the lemon peel. Furthermore, the broad peak observed between 3200 and 3500 cm− 1 indicates the presence of hydroxyl groups associated with macromolecular structures, such as cellulose and pectin. After NGB formation (Fig. 3B), the intensity of oxygen-containing functional groups decreases markedly, indicating dehydration, decarboxylation, and decomposition of volatile organic constituents during thermal treatment. The reduction of these polar functional groups, together with the emergence of more ordered aromatic carbon structures, suggests progressive carbonization and the formation of graphitic domains18. This structural transformation is directly related to the adsorption behavior. The removal of volatile components during graphitization promotes pore generation and improves the accessibility of adsorption sites. Meanwhile, the formation of graphitic carbon layers enhances π–π interactions and surface stability. However, excessive graphitization may also reduce surface oxygen functionalities that contribute to polar adsorption interactions.

The SEM instrument is used to analyze the surface morphology of adsorbents before and after dye adsorption. The images reveal changes in particle shape and structure, indicating effective removal of the contaminant via the adsorbent surface20. The SEM micrographs reveal substantial morphological changes in the lemon peel-derived NGB before and after BF adsorption. Before adsorption (Fig. 4A,C), the surface appears relatively compact with layered and irregular flaky structures, indicating the formation of carbonaceous sheets during carbonization. The presence of folds, cracks, and surface roughness suggests the development of pore channels that may facilitate adsorbate diffusion. After BF adsorption (Fig. 4B,D), the NGB surface becomes noticeably covered with agglomerated particles and deposited layers. The pores and surface cavities observed before adsorption appear partially blocked or filled, indicating successful adsorption of BF molecules onto the NGB surface. In addition, the surface morphology becomes denser and less porous after adsorption, further confirming the attachment and accumulation of dye molecules. The graphitized structure observed in the post-carbonization SEM images suggests the formation of stacked carbon layers with increased surface heterogeneity. Such layered carbon domains may enhance adsorption through π-π interactions between the aromatic structure of BF molecules and the graphitic carbon surface. Furthermore, the rough, porous morphology enhances adsorption by providing accessible active sites and diffusion pathways. This result is consistent with FTIR analysis, which indicates that porous structures facilitate BF dye adsorption.

The XRD patterns of lemon peel and NGB before and after BF adsorption are shown in Figs. 5A,B. For the lemon peel (Fig. 5A), the black trace shows a broad diffraction band centered at 2θ = 20–25°, indicating a peaked profile and suggesting a strongly amorphous nature and lack of long-range crystalline order. After BF adsorption (red trace), the overall diffraction intensity increases, indicating that BF dye molecules are accommodated within the LP matrix, with enhanced surface-adsorption scattering, without significant structural changes. In contrast, NGB from pyrolyzed lemon peel (Fig. 5B) exhibits a black curve with weak diffraction peaks, indicating a partially crystalline graphitic phase coexisting with amorphous carbon. After BF adsorption (red curve), firm diffraction peaks appear at 2θ ≈ 26°, 42°, 54°, and 77°, corresponding to the (002), (100), (004), and (110) graphitic planes. The increased intensity and sharper peaks indicate that the aromatic rings of BF interact with the NGB’s conjugated π-electron system through intercalation and π–π stacking, thereby enhancing structural order within the graphitic domains. Overall, these results support the conclusion that although lemon peel remains highly amorphous after BF adsorption, NGB exhibits greater crystallinity following exposure to basic fuchsin.- This structural development correlates with the higher adsorptive capacity of NGB compared to raw lemon peel. Results of batch experiments

Effect of the adsorbent’s surface charge

The point of zero charge (pHpzc) is the pH at which the net surface charge of the adsorbent particles is zero and is a crucial parameter for determining the adsorption mechanism of sorbed species at the surface. Therefore, pHPZC is used to illustrate the attractive and repulsive forces between the adsorbent and adsorbate during adsorption. Here, we determine the pHPZC using the pH drift method17. The pHpzc for the LP was evaluated as described in the experimental section. After shaking, pH readings were recorded, and the difference between the initial and final pH values was calculated (Fig. 4A). At pH > pHPZC, the adsorbent surface is negatively charged due to the acidic dissociation of H+ ions from the LP surface, and the produced negatively charged particles favor the uptake of positively charged dye. On the other hand, at pH < pHPZC, it is positively charged, resulting in electrostatic repulsion between the positively charged LP particles and the cationic BF dye, which may inhibit the adsorption of these species from aqueous solutions. In Fig. 4A, the pHpzc value of the LP adsorbent was found to be around 5.0; consequently, it is preferred to remove the BF at pH > 5.0 to ensure that the net surface of LP is negative.

Effect of pH

The initial pH of the adsorbent solution is crucial to the overall adsorption process because it strongly influences the surface charges of both the adsorbent and the adsorbate molecules. Additionally, adjusting the pH protonates or deprotonates functional groups on the adsorbent or adsorbate, thereby leading to electrostatic interactions between them. To examine the effect of pH, adsorption experiments were conducted using 25.0 mL of a 10.0 µg/mL BF solution. Each experiment employed 0.1 g of treated lemon peel and 0.05 g of NGB as adsorbents. The pH was adjusted between 2.0 and 9.0 using 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH. Adsorption occurs at 25 °C with constant stirring at 400 rpm for 30 min, followed by centrifugation for 5 min to separate the phases. The results showed that BF removal efficiency depended heavily on pH (Fig. 7B). For lemon peels, removal efficiency increased gradually with increasing pH, reaching a maximum of 55.8% at pH 7.0. Also, NGB achieved its highest removal efficiency of 95.62% at pH 5.0, and increased with increasing pH. These findings indicate that pH significantly influenced the adsorption performance and confirm the above pHPZC results of LP (Fig. 7A). The maximum adsorption of fuchsin dye at pH 5 is attributed to optimal electrostatic attraction between negatively charged adsorbent surfaces and positively charged dye molecules, reduced proton competition, and enhanced π–π interactions between the aromatic structure of fuchsin and graphite layers. At lower pH, excessive protonation suppresses adsorption, while at higher pH, adsorption sites become saturated, leading to equilibrium behavior. The observed increase in adsorption efficiency at higher pH values is consistent with previous studies on cationic dye removal using biomass-derived adsorbents, in which enhanced adsorption is attributed to increased electrostatic attraction between negatively charged surfaces and positively charged dye molecules. Similar behavior has been reported for basic fuchsin adsorption onto natural and modified adsorbents, with maximum removal efficiency achieved under neutral-to-slightly alkaline conditions3,10,12,23,24.

Effect of adsorbent dosage

Adsorbent dosage is considered one of the most crucial parameters for adsorption efficiency, as it determines the number of active binding sites available for dye removal. To evaluate its effect, 25.0 mL of a 10.0 µg/mL BF solution was treated with adsorbents at varying amounts: 0.01–1.0 g for LP and 0.01–0.2 g for NGB. The experiments were performed at pH 7.0 with continuous stirring for 30 min at 25 °C, followed by centrifugation for 5 min. The results showed a strong positive correlation between adsorbent weight and removal efficiency (Fig. 7C). Increasing the dosage improved dye removal by providing more available adsorption sites. For LP adsorbent, the maximum removal efficiency was 83.74% at a dosage of 0.5 g; beyond this, increasing the dosage did not significantly improve performance. Additionally, the NGB adsorbent demonstrated a much higher adsorption capacity, achieving 94.4% removal efficiency at only 0.1 g, indicating it is more effective than the raw LP adsorbent. These findings support the conclusion that both materials are effective in removing BF dye. NGB shows superior adsorption efficacy at lower dosages and is therefore a more efficient and environmentally friendly adsorbent. The increase in removal efficiency with increasing adsorbent dosage can be attributed to the greater availability of active adsorption sites and functional groups on the adsorbent surface, which facilitate dye uptake through electrostatic interactions, π-π interactions, and surface complexation. Similar trends have been widely reported in the literature for biomass-derived adsorbents, where increasing adsorbent dosage leads to higher removal efficiency until a saturation plateau is reached due to particle aggregation and overlapping active sites23. Similar behavior has also been reported for Basic Fuchsin adsorption using biomass-based materials such as soybean straw and sugarcane bagasse, confirming that adsorbent dosage is a key parameter controlling adsorption efficiency and is directly related to surface area and the availability of active sites24.

Effect of contact time

Contact time is a crucial process-controlling parameter in adsorption because it influences the interaction between the adsorbent surface and dye molecules. To explore this, BF removal was studied over shaking times ranging from 0.5 to 90 min under set conditions. A rapid increase in removal efficiency was initially observed, with efficiency rising from 83.12% to 86.4% in 5 min for LP adsorbent and from 92.04% to 99.2% for NGB adsorbent (Fig. 7D). After 5 min, adsorption reached equilibrium; no further increase in dye removal was seen up to 90 min. This is due to the high number of active adsorption sites at the start, which enables rapid dye adsorption. Once these sites become saturated, the process slows and reaches a plateau, indicating equilibrium. These results demonstrate that equilibrium can be achieved quickly, especially with the NGB adsorbent, enabling faster, more efficient adsorption than with raw lemon peel. Compared with previously reported adsorbents, which typically require 30 to 120 min3,10,12,25 to reach equilibrium, the NGB developed in this study exhibits significantly faster adsorption kinetics, reaching equilibrium in only 5 min. This enhanced performance can be attributed to the improved surface area, porous structure, and graphitic nature of the material, which promote efficient diffusion and strong interactions with dye molecules26.

Effect of dye initial concentration

The initial dye concentration is an important parameter for determining the adsorbents’ removal efficiency and adsorption capacity. To assess this effect, BF solutions were prepared at concentrations ranging from 2.0 to 100.0 µg/mL at pH 7.0. The experiments involved 0.5 g of lemon peel and 0.1 g of NGB in a 25 mL solution, which was stirred at 25 °C for 5 min. As shown in the data, the removal efficiency increased with higher dye concentrations in the low concentration range. With lemon peel, removal efficiency improved from 80.3% at 2.0 µg/mL to 88.33% at 10.0 µg/mL. At the same time, NGB showed a more notable increase, rising from 95% to 99.08% over the same range (Fig. 7E). The higher removal efficiency at lower concentrations can be attributed to greater accessibility of active sites on both adsorbents relative to the number of dye molecules. Beyond 10 µg/mL, the adsorption plateaued as concentration increased, indicating saturation of active sites. Overall, NGB exhibited a higher adsorption capacity than lemon peel, especially at elevated concentrations, due to its larger surface area and greater number of active binding sites.

Effect of temperature

Temperature has a significant effect on the rate of BF adsorption. To assess its impact, adsorption experiments were performed using a 10.0 µg/mL BF solution with 0.5 g of LP and 0.1 g of NGB at pH 7.0 and a contact time of 5 min, with temperatures varied from 20 to 60 °C. As shown in Fig. 7F, the BF removal rate decreased with increasing temperature. This decline suggests that the adsorption process is exothermic; higher temperatures reduce the adsorbent’s affinity for BF molecules. Elevated temperatures can also reduce electrostatic attraction and promote desorption, thereby lowering adsorption efficiency. This behavior is commonly associated with physisorption processes, where weak interactions such as van der Waals forces and electrostatic attractions dominate27. This trend is consistent with previous studies on biomass-derived adsorbents, which reported that elevated temperatures reduce adsorption efficiency due to decreased surface affinity and disruption of adsorbate–adsorbent interactions. In addition, increased temperature may enhance molecular diffusion but simultaneously reduce the stability of adsorbed species on the surface28. Overall, these findings suggest that lower temperatures are more effective for BF adsorption on lemon peel and NGB, with NGB exhibiting better performance.

Based on the above experiments, the optimal pH for the removal process is 7.0 when using lemon peel and NGB as adsorbents. The experimental setup included 0.5 g of lemon peel and 0.1 g of NGB at a BF concentration of 10.0 µg/L. The contact time was 5.0 min, and the temperature was maintained at 25 °C. Overall, the adsorption behavior observed in this study is consistent with previously reported mechanisms for cationic dye removal using carbon-based and biomass-derived adsorbents, including electrostatic interactions, π–π stacking interactions, and surface functional group interactions.

Isotherm studies

Adsorption isotherm interpretation is essential for understanding the adsorption mechanism, distinguishing between physisorption and chemisorption, and evaluating the process reversibility29,30. Isotherm models offer insights into the behavior of adsorbates on adsorbent surfaces, enabling the determination of adsorption capacity, adsorption intensity, and surface heterogeneity. The Freundlich isotherm (Fig. S1A and S2A) describes adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces, and its linear form is given by Eq. (4).4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{L}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{e}}=\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}{\mathrm{k}}_{\mathrm{f}}+\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{n}}\right)\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{e}}$$\end{document}

The value of 1/n indicates both the adsorption capacity and the favourability of the adsorption process. The linearized Freundlich equation is derived by plotting log qe (the amount adsorbed per unit mass) against log Ce (the equilibrium concentration). The slope corresponds to 1/n, while the intercept is log KF, reflecting the adsorption behavior29.

The Langmuir model (Figs. S1B and S2B) assumes monolayer adsorption at equivalent sites on the adsorbent surface, with no interaction between adsorbed molecules. It is expressed by Eq. (5).5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{e}}}{{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{e}}}=\left(\frac{1}{{\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{L}}.{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{m}}}\right)+\frac{{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{e}}}{{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{m}}}$$\end{document} where Ce is the equilibrium concentration, qe is the adsorption capacity, qm is the maximum monolayer adsorption capacity, and KL is the Langmuir constant. Plotting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{{C}_{e}}{{q}_{e}}$$\end{document} against Ce should result in a straight line with a slope of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{1}{{q}_{m}}$$\end{document} and an intercept of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{1}{{K}_{L}.{q}_{m}}$$\end{document}.

The Temkin model (Fig. S1C) accounts for indirect adsorbate–adsorbent interactions, in which the heat of adsorption decreases linearly with increasing surface coverage. The Temkin equation is given by Eq. (6):

6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{q}_{e}=\:\frac{RT\:ln{K}_{T}}{b}+\frac{RT\:ln{C}_{e}}{b}$$\end{document} where R is the universal gas constant (8.314 J/mol·K), T is the temperature (K), KT is the equilibrium binding constant (L/mol), and b is related to adsorption heat.

In the plot of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{q}_{e}$$\end{document} Vs. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ln{C}_{e}$$\end{document} a straight line with a slope of (RT/b), and its intercept is at ((RT ln KT)/b), which provides information on adsorption energy distribution. The Freundlich model best described the adsorption of basic fuchsin dye onto lemon peel. The linear plot of log qe versus log Ce produced a straight line with a slope of 0.962 and an intercept of − 0.5251 (Table 2). The correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.9723) indicated a strong agreement with the Freundlich equation. The Freundlich constant was calculated to be n = 1.039, indicating favorable adsorption conditions (n > 1) and good adsorption efficiency. KF = 0.29846 (mg/g) (L/mg)1/n representing the sorption capacity of lemon peel. These results suggest that dye molecules adsorbed onto lemon peel are situated on a heterogeneous surface, consistent with the Freundlich isotherm.

In the case of NGB, the adsorption process was better explained by the Temkin model, which assumes that the adsorption heat decreases linearly with increasing surface coverage due to interactions between the adsorbate and the adsorbent. The plot of qe versus ln Ce yielded a slope of 5.672 and an intercept of 13.736 (Table 2), with a high R2 = 0.96, confirming the applicability of the Temkin model. The strong fit of the Temkin model suggests that dye adsorption onto NGB is governed by adsorbate–adsorbent interactions and occurs with an even distribution of binding energies, consistent with chemisorption behavior.

For the equilibrium study, the Freundlich and Temkin models were evaluated using these error functions. The Freundlich model demonstrated superior performance for both adsorbents. For lemon peel (LP), the Freundlich model exhibited RMSE = 0.206, SSE = 0.508, and χ2 = 0.278, indicating excellent agreement with experimental data. For nano‑graphitic biochar (NGB), the corresponding values were RMSE = 0.89, SSE = 8.87, and χ2 = 0.51, confirming good fitting performance despite the higher adsorption capacity range. The Temkin model also provided an acceptable representation of the adsorption system for NGB, with RMSE = 0.73, SSE = 5.80, and χ2 = 1.84. However, these error values are higher than those obtained from the Freundlich model, indicating comparatively lower fitting accuracy. In contrast, the Langmuir model showed poor agreement with experimental data and yielded unrealistic or negative parameters, which is reflected in significantly higher error values. This confirms that the Langmuir model is not suitable for describing the adsorption system. Overall, the Freundlich model exhibited the lowest error among all models applied, confirming that adsorption occurs on a heterogeneous surface with multilayer formation.

Kinetic studies

To gain deeper insight into the adsorption mechanism of basic fuchsin dye onto lemon peel and NGB adsorbents, several kinetic models were applied30,31. The use of multiple models provides a comprehensive understanding of adsorption rates, diffusion pathways, and rate-limiting steps. The pseudo-first-order kinetic model (Figs. S1D, S2C) describes adsorption, assuming that the rate of occupation of adsorption sites is proportional to the number of unoccupied sites. It is expressed by Eq. (7):

7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{\mathrm{t}}{{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{t}}}=\frac{1}{{\mathrm{K}}_{2}{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{t}}^{2\:}}\:+\frac{1}{{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{e}}}\:\mathrm{t}$$\end{document} where, qe and qt (mg/g) represent the amounts of BF-dye adsorbed at equilibrium and at the time (t), while k1 is the rate constant (min− 1).

The pseudo-second-order model (Figs. S1E, S2D) assumes that chemisorption is the rate-limiting step, involving valence forces via electron sharing or exchange between the adsorbent and the adsorbate. It is described by Eq. (8):

8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{\mathrm{t}}{{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{t}}}=\frac{1}{{\mathrm{K}}_{2}{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{t}}^{2\:}}\:+\frac{1}{{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{e}}}\:\mathrm{t}$$\end{document} where, qe and qt (mg/g) reflect the dye amounts at equilibrium and at time (t), with K2 representing the model’s rate constants.

To evaluate whether diffusion within the pores is the rate-controlling step, the intraparticle diffusion model (Weber–Morris model; Fig. S1F, S2E) was applied, as given in Eq. (9).

9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{q}}_{\rm{t}}} = {{\rm{k}}_i}{{\rm{t}}^{0.5}} + {\rm{C}}$$\end{document} where, qt shows how much BF is adsorbed onto the adsorbent (mg/g) over time t, while ki (mg/g·min0.5) represents the rate of this invigorating diffusion process.

The Elovich model (Figs. S1G, S2F) is often applied to systems in which adsorption occurs on heterogeneous surfaces. This model is beneficial for describing activated chemisorption processes on heterogeneous surfaces. It is expressed by Eq. (10).

10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{q}\mathrm{t}=\left(\frac{1}{{\upbeta\:}}\right)\mathrm{ln}{\upalpha\:}{\upbeta\:}+\left(\frac{1}{{\upbeta\:}}\right)\mathrm{l}\mathrm{n}\:\mathrm{t}$$\end{document} where α (mg/g·min) represents the initial adsorption rate and β (g/mg) is related to the surface coverage and activation energy for chemisorption.

The pseudo-second-order model provided the best regression to the experimental data. For lemon peel, the PSO plot yielded a straight line with a slope of 2.3026, a calculated qe 0.41445 mg/g, and an intercept of 0.10788. Similarly, for NGB, the value remained constant (2.3026), with a corresponding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{e}}$$\end{document} of 0.41445 mg/g and an intercept of 0.26769. Surprisingly, the R2 values were 0.9998 for both adsorbents (Table 3). These results confirm that the pseudo-second-order model accurately describes the adsorption kinetics, indicating that chemisorption, facilitated by valence forces through electron sharing or exchange, is the dominant process. Conversely, the pseudo-first-order model yields lower R2 values, reflecting insufficient correlation between experimental and calculated values and thus demonstrating its unsuitability for depicting the adsorption process. The intraparticle diffusion model yielded a multilinear rather than a single straight line, indicating intraparticle diffusion but not that it is the sole rate-controlling step. The Elovich model also failed to represent the data as effectively as the PSO model, again indicative that chemisorption is the rate-controlling mechanism of adsorption. In general, the kinetic study confirms that while various mechanisms are involved in dye removal, the adsorption process is primarily governed by pseudo-second-order chemisorption for both lemon peel and its NGB.

The kinetic models were further evaluated using statistical error functions. For LP, the PSO model showed RMSE = 0.19, SSE = 0.48, and χ2 = 0.26, which are significantly lower than those of the PFO model (RMSE = 0.58, SSE = 3.85, χ2 = 1.72). Similarly, for NGB, the PSO model exhibited RMSE = 0.62, SSE = 4.65, and χ2 = 0.88, compared to RMSE = 1.25, SSE = 13.75, and χ2 = 2.64 for the PFO model. These results confirm that the pseudo-second-order model provides the best fit for the experimental data, indicating that chemisorption is the dominant mechanism.

Adsorption thermodynamics

To explore the impact of temperature on removing BF by LP and NGB adsorbents, by analyzing behavior at four distinct temperature points, ranging from 293 K to 333 K, Figs. S1H, S2G. The thermodynamic parameters assessed include Gibbs free energy (ΔG°), enthalpy (ΔH°), and entropy (ΔS°); detailed results are provided in Table 4. These parameters offer valuable insights into the adsorption mechanisms at play, highlighting both the energy dynamics and the spontaneity of the adsorption process. The negative enthalpy change (ΔH°) values for both lemon peel (− 8.73 kJ mol−1) and NGB (− 36.65 kJ mol−1) indicate that the adsorption process is exothermic. This implies that adsorption is favored at lower temperatures, which is consistent with the observed decrease in adsorption spontaneity as temperature increases. Furthermore, the relatively low magnitude of ΔH° for lemon peel suggests predominantly physical adsorption interactions, such as electrostatic attraction and van der Waals forces32. In contrast, the comparatively larger negative ΔH° value for NGB indicates stronger adsorbate-adsorbent interactions.

The negative entropy change (ΔS°) values for lemon peel (-0.03 kJ mol−1 K−1) and NGB (− 0.098 kJ mol−1 K−1) suggest a decrease in randomness at the solid–solution interface during adsorption. This reduction in disorder may be attributed to the orderly attachment of Basic Fuchsin molecules onto the active adsorption sites of the adsorbents. The more negative ΔS° value for NGB further indicates a greater structural organization of dye molecules on its surface. Overall, the thermodynamic results demonstrate that Basic Fuchsin adsorption onto both adsorbents is spontaneous, feasible, and exothermic, with NGB exhibiting comparatively stronger adsorption interactions and higher adsorption affinity than lemon peel.

Recycling adsorbents

Regeneration and reusability of adsorbents are crucial factors when assessing their practical use in wastewater treatment. In this study, LP and NGB adsorbents underwent multiple adsorption-desorption cycles. Regeneration of the LP involved stirring in 1 M HCl for 10 min, which effectively disrupted the electrostatic attractions and π–π interactions between the dye molecules and the adsorbent surface, followed by several rinses with distilled water to remove excess acid. The samples were dried at 85 °C for 24 h before reuse. As shown in Fig. 5, the removal efficiency gradually declined over successive cycles, reaching 70.3% after the fourth cycle. The superior cyclic performance of NGB compared with LP indicates stronger adsorbate–adsorbent interactions and better preservation of active sites after regeneration. Overall, these findings confirm that both adsorbents are effective, reusable, and stable, and can be utilized repeatedly without losing their efficiency against BF dye (Fig. 8).

A comparative study with the previously published methods

The analysis presented in Table 7 provides a detailed comparison of various adsorbents used for removing fuchsin dye. It showcases a diverse array of materials, including both plant-based options and modified agricultural products. The NGB produced from lemon peel is particularly promising due to its enhanced surface area and conductivity, which are likely contributing to its superior performance in dye adsorption. The performance of these adsorbents is influenced by a range of experimental conditions, with pH being a significant factor. Most adsorbents exhibited optimal dye removal efficiency at neutral or slightly alkaline pH around 7. However, there were exceptions: Silybum marianum seed shells thrived in a highly alkaline environment at pH 12, whereas soybean straw was effective at pH 8. In addition to pH, the contact time between the adsorbent and the dye is a crucial factor. Some materials, such as LP and NGB, exhibit remarkable adsorption within 5 min. For instance, Silybum marianum seed shells achieved 97.5% removal at pH 12 within 60 min, while Lippia alba leaves reached 98.3% after 120 min. Similarly, Citrullus lanatus seed-based adsorbents required extended contact times and higher dye concentrations. This could restrict their application in scenarios where time is a critical factor. In contrast, the NGB developed in this study achieved an outstanding removal efficiency of 99.2% within only 5 min under mild conditions (pH 7), demonstrating a significant improvement in adsorption kinetics. This level of performance suggests a highly effective adsorption mechanism, likely stemming from its increased surface area and porosity. Furthermore, using lemon peel as an adsorbent addresses challenges in dye removal and offers sustainability benefits by repurposing waste material. Overall, the NGB prepared from lemon peel emerged as the frontrunner in efficiency, demonstrating exceptional removal rates in a minimal timeframe. Ultimately, selecting an appropriate adsorbent will hinge on specific application needs, including time efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and material availability. This superior performance can be attributed to its enhanced surface area, graphitic structure, and increased availability of active adsorption sites. This trend is consistent with earlier studies showing that graphitized carbon materials exhibit enhanced adsorption due to π–π interactions and improved surface properties10,24. These results highlight the effectiveness of converting agricultural waste into high-performance carbon-based nanomaterials for rapid wastewater treatment applications.

Conclusions

This study provides a systematic, quantitative comparison of lemon peel and its NGB, demonstrating clear differences in adsorption performance and operational stability across multiple governing parameters, including pH, adsorbent dosage, initial concentration, contact time, and temperature. NGB consistently exhibits superior adsorption efficiency, achieving a maximum removal efficiency of approximately 98%. In contrast, lemon peel reaches only 50–85% depending on experimental conditions, corresponding to a performance gap of 15–45% across all tested variables. In terms of dosage requirements, NGB achieves near-complete adsorption at a significantly lower loading of approximately 0.1 g. In contrast, lemon peel requires up to 0.5 g to approach its maximum performance, indicating a substantially higher adsorption capacity per unit mass for NGB. Kinetic evaluation shows that NGB rapidly reaches equilibrium within short contact times, maintaining 95–97% removal efficiency, while lemon peel stabilizes at a lower plateau of 84–86%, confirming reduced active-site accessibility and weaker adsorption affinity in the biomass-derived material. Overall, these findings establish NGB as a high-efficiency, low-dosage, and environmentally robust adsorbent system, while lemon peel functions as a lower-cost but performance-limited bioadsorbent. Beyond confirming material-dependent adsorption behavior, this study advances the field by quantitatively benchmarking engineered carbon materials against biomass-derived adsorbents under identical multi-parameter conditions, thereby defining clear performance ceilings for untreated bioadsorbents and providing evidence-based design guidance for their enhancement. To assess adsorption efficiency, equilibrium data were collected and analyzed using three isotherm models: Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin. Results showed that the adsorption behavior of lemon peels was better described by the Freundlich model, indicating multilayer adsorption on a heterogeneous surface. Conversely, the adsorption of basic fuchsin onto lemon peel NGB was best described by the Temkin model, suggesting that the heat of adsorption varies linearly with coverage. The findings underscore the versatility and effectiveness of lemon peel and its NGB in removing dyes from wastewater. Notably, both materials maintained their adsorption efficiency after up to five recycling cycles, highlighting their potential for sustainable water treatment. Additionally, the method was tested on real water samples, achieving a remarkable 96% recovery rate, which indicates its potential to address dye pollution across various water sources.
