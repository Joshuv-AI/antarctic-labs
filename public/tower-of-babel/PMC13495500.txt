Evaluation of communication competence among undergraduate dental students in the pediatric dentistry clinic using 360-degree feedback: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Ahmed Adel Salama, Yasmin M Aboul-Ela, Hesham Marei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the field of dentistry, the dentist-patient relationship plays a pivotal role in treatment outcomes. Effective communication enhances patient satisfaction, ensures full understanding of treatment options, decreases patient anxiety, and improves adherence to dental instructions. The present study aimed to evaluate communication competence among fifth-year undergraduate dental students in the Pediatric Dentistry clinic at Gulf Medical University using a 360° evaluation approach.MethodsThis cross-sectional mixed-methods study enrolled 45 fifth-year dental students at Gulf Medical University, United Arab Emirates. A 10-item checklist assessed communication skills from four perspectives: self-assessment, peer evaluation, expert observation, and parent evaluation. The checklist underwent expert review by pediatric dentists and medical education professors to support content validity. Four focus group discussions (24 students) explored in-depth insights into dental students’ perceptions, level of self-awareness, and the challenges they encounter in developing communication competencies. Quantitative data were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient, and Spearman’s correlation. Qualitative data underwent thematic analysis.ResultsOperator self-evaluations were significantly underestimated compared to parent evaluations (treatment explanation: p = 0.032; cooperation: p = 0.007; post-operative instructions: p = 0.029) and peer evaluations (p = 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed between self-evaluations and expert evaluations. Generally, agreement and reliability across evaluators were poor to moderate, raising concerns regarding the consistency and validity of single-source evaluations. Focus groups identified key challenges including managing child anxiety, parental interference, language barriers, and emotional fatigue. Female students demonstrated greater rapport-building skills and self-awareness.ConclusionsThis study underscores the value of multi-source feedback in capturing the multifaceted nature of communication competence more comprehensively than single-source assessment. The findings provide a foundation for curriculum enhancements and point toward the need for longitudinal and multi-institutional research to better prepare students for patient-centered care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09686-7.Keywords: Communication competence, Dental education, Pediatric dentistry, Multi-source feedback, Self-assessment, Undergraduate

Introduction

The development of communication skills is increasingly recognized as a fundamental component of dental education. Beyond technical expertise and clinical knowledge, dentists need effective communication abilities to provide patient-centered care. The ability to communicate effectively with patients using active listening skills, gathering and sharing information accurately, sensitively handling patient emotions, building rapport, as well as demonstrating empathy, ethical awareness, and professionalism is crucial [1].

Health communication has increasingly shifted from an information-based model, in which treatment decisions are made with limited consideration of patients’ views, understanding, or emotional responses, toward a shared decision-making model that gives greater weight to patients’ comprehension and satisfaction with their treatment options [2, 3]. This patient-targeted approach requires possession of strong communication competencies where healthcare professionals are adequately trained and practice effective communication [4]. For dental and medical students, the transition from preclinical to clinical settings presents unique challenges, including anxiety and lack of confidence attributable to deficiencies in communication skills; thus, enhancing students’ communication skills is essential for building self-confidence and delivering competent dental care [5, 6].

Pediatric dentistry presents distinctive communication challenges that extend beyond typical adult-patient interactions. The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry underscores the role of effective communication as means of reducing children anxiety by engaging them in developmentally appropriate ways while simultaneously reassuring parents and facilitating treatment adherence [7]. Indeed, young children are particularly susceptible to dental anxiety, owing to the invasive nature of procedures, making behavior management strategies that rely heavily on effective communication essential [8].

The Tell-Show-Do (TSD) technique is one of these behavior management strategies, where children are first informed about procedures, shown demonstrations, and finally undergo treatment as explained [7]. In addition to behavior management, communication with pediatric patients often incorporates reflective listening that demonstrates genuine understanding and empathy, as well as praise to encourage positive reinforcement. Beyond rapport-building, these techniques also create a supportive environment that helps reduce fear and anxiety, ultimately facilitating more effective and compassionate treatment [9]. Empathy, defined as the ability to understand and share patients’ feelings, is crucial in pediatric settings [10]. Sarnet and colleagues reported that empathic communication approaches are significantly associated with treatment success in children aged 3–12 years [11].

Dental education accreditation bodies worldwide recognize communication as a core competency. Contemporary frameworks for dental education incorporate interpersonal and communication skills alongside clinical and technical competencies, recommending a range of teaching methods from, didactic instruction to simulated practice and clinical coaching [12].

Medical curricula are increasingly incorporating interactive learning approaches into communication skills training to provide students with practical opportunities to develop effective patient interaction skills. An example of such approaches includes small-group discussions that allow students to reflect on communication challenges, exchange perspectives, and practice in a supportive environment [13]. Direct observation, either of peers or of one’s own interactions through video recording, offers a unique opportunity for self-reflection and critical analysis of verbal and non-verbal behaviors [14]. Additionally, simulated patients provide students with realistic, yet controlled, clinical scenarios in which they can practice eliciting comprehensive patient histories, responding to emotional cues, and delivering complex information clearly and empathetically [15, 16].

Traditional methods of assessing communication skills in dental education often provide a limited perspective, failing to capture a comprehensive understanding of a student’s communicative competence in real-world clinical settings. Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) offer standardized evaluation but may not fully replicate genuine patient interactions, with students potentially performing differently under observation pressure [17]. Direct observation by faculty provides insights into live clinical settings, yet subjectivity and limited feedback are major shortcomings [18].

Self-assessment, a process by which students monitor and evaluate the quality of their thinking and behavior during learning, has emerged as both an assessment method and a learning activity that enables the identification of strengths and limitations, fostering lifelong learning capacity [19]. In dentistry, accurate self-assessment is essential for recognizing strengths, identifying areas for improvement, and maintaining high standards of clinical practice [20, 21].

The Commission on Dental Accreditation requires that graduates demonstrate the ability to self-assess, including developing professional competencies and demonstrating professional values associated with self-directed lifelong learning [22]. This reflective process not only promotes self-awareness but also nurtures empathy, active listening, and patient-centered care. Such competencies positively impact the quality of care and patient satisfaction [23].

The limitations of single-source assessment highlight a critical research gap: the need for more robust, multidimensional approaches that incorporate diverse viewpoints. Multi-source feedback (360-degree evaluation) addresses this gap by systematically collecting assessments from multiple stakeholders, including peers, supervisors, subordinates, and in healthcare contexts, patients and faculty [24]. This approach, integrating diverse perspectives, provides comprehensive evaluation, thereby reducing biases related to single-source assessment [25]. Lanning et al. in their research indicate that, when properly designed and integrated into educational frameworks with clear feedback delivery mechanisms, multi-source feedback serves as a powerful tool for individual and systemic performance enhancement [24].

In regions with rich cultural diversity such as the United Arab Emirates (UAE), healthcare professionals interact with patients from various linguistic and cultural backgrounds, making clear and empathetic communication crucial for building trust and ensuring patient comfort. This cultural diversity necessitates that dental students develop self-awareness regarding communication strengths and areas for improvement to address patients’ unique needs, clarify procedures, and alleviate anxiety.

Building upon existing evidence highlighting the value of multi-source feedback and the complementary roles of self-assessment and various external evaluations, the present study aimed to evaluate communication competence among fifth-year undergraduate dental students in the pediatric dentistry clinic at Gulf Medical University using a 360-degree evaluation design. The findings aim to inform targeted curriculum enhancements that strengthen communication skills and improve pediatric patient care outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional mixed-methods study was conducted at Thumbay Dental Hospital, College of Dentistry, Gulf Medical University, Ajman, UAE, over a period of 6–8 months. The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Gulf Medical University (Ref. no. IRB-COM-MHPE-STD-78-Jan-2025). This mixed-methods study was reported in accordance with the Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) checklist, with integration of quantitative and qualitative findings occurring at the interpretation stage to explain concordant and discordant patterns across methods.

Participants

The study enrolled 45 fifth-year dental students (18 males [40%]; 27 females [60%]) who voluntarily participated. All fifth-year students were eligible regardless of gender. For each student, one parent or guardian of a pediatric patient was enrolled to evaluate communication skills. Two faculty members conducted expert observations for all 45 students. Computer-based randomization was used for peer assignments. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Sample size calculation

Based on previous research by Ab Ghani et al. [25], sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software, adopting an effect size of 0.57 with alpha set at 0.05 and power at 95%, yielding an estimated minimum sample size of 41. A total of 45 students were enrolled.

Qualitative data collection

Focus group discussions explored students’ perceptions of the importance of communication skills, self-awareness levels, and challenges in developing these competencies. Participants were stratified into four groups based on their cumulative GPA (cGPA) to ensure representation of different academic performance levels and to capture a wider range of perspectives on communication challenges and self-awareness: (1) higher-scoring males, (2) higher-scoring females, (3) lower-scoring males, and (4) lower-scoring females, each comprising six students. Focus group durations ranged from 62 to 76 min. Two sessions per group ensured within-group theme saturation. After four focus groups involving 24 students total, data saturation was achieved with no new themes emerging.

Focus groups followed a structured format with predefined open-ended questions addressing four domains (Table 2).

Two English/Arabic-speaking moderators, FAIMER fellows, received training from experts in pediatric dentistry and health professions education to ensure consistent question delivery. Sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed using Zoom, with participants’ permission. Moderators also took written notes during sessions.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Numerical data were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD). Data were examined for normality using distribution visualization and Shapiro-Wilk test, revealing non-parametric distributions. Differences were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired non-parametric comparisons across evaluator sources. Reliability analyses employed Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC). Correlation analyses used Spearman’s rank-order correlation coefficient (rs). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R statistical software version 4.4.1 for Windows [26].

Qualitative analysis

A thorough line-by-line transcript analysis was conducted. Coding and thematic analysis were conducted manually using Microsoft Word. Data were grouped and categorized, followed by theme identification for each focus group discussion.

Results

Quantitative findings

Across all evaluator comparisons, inter-rater agreement ranged from poor to moderate. Operator-role self-scores were consistently underestimated relative to external evaluators (parents and peers), while assistant-role scores showed greater convergence across sources. Self and expert evaluations were closely aligned for both roles. Detailed pairwise comparisons are presented below.

Self and parent evaluations

As illustrated in Table 3, no significant differences were observed between self and parent scores for the assistant role. However, for the operator role, self-evaluation scores were significantly underestimated compared to parent evaluations (treatment explanation: p = 0.032; cooperation: p = 0.007; post-operative instructions: p = 0.029). Treatment explanation scores showed moderate positive correlation, while other correlations were weak. All reliability coefficients indicated poor agreement.

Self and expert evaluations

No significant differences were found between self and expert evaluation scores for either role (operator: p = 0.056; assistant: p = 0.068). All reliability and correlation coefficients were weak (operator CCC = 0.105, rs = 0.137; assistant CCC = -0.036, rs = -0.080), suggesting limited concordance despite similar average scores.

Self and peer evaluations

As depicted in Fig. 1, operator self-scores were significantly underestimated compared to peer scores (p = 0.001), with poor agreement (CCC = 0.262) but moderate positive correlation (rs = 0.461). Assistant scores showed no significant differences (p = 0.168), with moderate agreement (CCC = 0.517) and correlation (rs = 0.506).

Qualitative findings

Theme 1: challenges in communicating with pediatric patients

Participants across all focus groups agreed on the unique challenges of pediatric communication. Students implemented various approaches to manage fear and gain cooperation, most commonly the Tell-Show-Do technique and distractions. One male student noted: “Tell-show-do approach really works; kids nowadays, even as young as 4 years old, are smart, curious, and would like to feel respected and involved. Once we introduce them to what we are doing, show them our dental instruments and let them hold them, they start getting interested and cooperative.”

Digital era influences were recognized as useful tools: “In this digital era, kids are influenced and attached to gaming and using iPad and mobile phones. Just by giving the child a phone to watch his/her favourite YouTube cartoon or play his/her favourite game, cooperation is gained, and the treatment session is accomplished” (male student).

Female students demonstrated distinctive rapport-building approaches. One student stated: “I try to make a story out of the tooth condition and treatment plan and involve the child so I can easily gain his/her cooperation. I refer to the local anesthetic as ‘sleeping juice,’ the tooth decay as the bad enemy we need to conquer.” Another noted: “At the beginning of the session, I spend so much time talking to the child to gain his/her trust. I ask about his/her friends, hobbies, favorite toys, favorite cartoons, favorite game. Kids tend to get along and start talking; they feel we are their friends rather than doctors, afterwards they become very cooperative. I think they feel safe.”

Previous traumatic dental experiences emerged as significant barriers. One male student observed: “Sometimes children present to us already psychologically traumatized after visiting 2–3 dentists. Their traumatic experience usually puts an end to all our trials in communicating with the child. Their objective fear makes them completely uncooperative even with investing much time in talking and dealing with them.”

All participants emphasized patience as an essential skill. Notably, collaboration among team members appeared more evident among female students, who consistently used “we” and “my partner,” reflecting team harmony, a pattern less evident among males.

Theme 2: parental influence and extreme parental behaviors

Parental interference emerged as affecting children’s behavior negatively. One female student explained: “Some parents interfere with our treatment plan; they believe that as we are training, then we might hurt their child, or we are not confident of what we are doing.” Another recounted: “One time, the treatment session was going fine, I was able to gain the child’s trust, and I was trying my best to hide the local anesthetic, then the parent said, ‘alright it will only hurt a little bit!’ All efforts to keep the child calm and cooperative were lost!”

Students reported lacking experience to handle extreme parental behaviors, including cases where fathers physically disciplined crying children, situations that left students uncertain how to respond professionally.

Theme 3: language barriers and cultural diversity

During clinical training, students encounter families with limited English or Arabic proficiency, requiring adaptation strategies including simplified language, visual demonstrations, and occasionally seeking interpreter assistance. One male student reported using google translate service to clarify instructions in Arabic or Urdu for Arabic & Urdu-speaking parents. Another female student explained “Sometimes I had to ask for assistance from colleagues who are good with Arabic or Urdu to come and explain to the parent, children usually communicate easily in English.” Cultural differences in parenting styles and dental care expectations also necessitated sensitivity and flexibility.

Theme 4: emotional fatigue and psychological demands

Students, particularly females who reported investing considerable time in rapport-building, described the psychological burden of pediatric communication: “With all the manipulations, the diverse approaches we implement, and the time we spend talking and explaining even for simple procedures, we feel drained, with emotional and psychological fatigue. I think this is the challenge of pediatric dentistry”. This insight correlates with quantitative findings showing students’ tendency to underestimate their operator-role performance, potentially reflecting heightened self-criticism stemming from the emotional demands of patient care.

Theme 5: the need for enhanced training

Throughout discussions, most students agreed on and emphasized the need for direct observation with constructive feedback and additional training in communication skills. They highlighted feeling unprepared for challenging scenarios such as extreme parental behaviors, suggesting that incorporating standardized patients or role-plays could provide valuable opportunities to anticipate and effectively manage difficult situations. One female student clarified “I think we need to shadow our professors and instructors more, observe them dealing in different clinical scenarios or may be watch videos of difficult patient situations where we reflect on the difficulties in communication in specific situations.”

Discussion

The current study evaluated communication competence among fifth-year dental students in pediatric dentistry clinics using a 360-degree multi-source feedback approach, comparing self-assessments with evaluations from peers, clinical supervisors, and parents. The findings provide evidence to guide curriculum enhancements aimed at strengthening communication skills and improving pediatric patient care outcomes.

Communication skills are widely acknowledged as essential competencies in dental education, yet their structured development often remains outweighed by the strong focus on technical training. However, a growing body of evidence shows that patients achieve better health outcomes when healthcare professionals communicate effectively. Patient-centered care models therefore emphasize preparing oral health providers who not only perform procedures competently but also connect with patients through clear, compassionate communication. In this context, empathy becomes a key component of professional growth and effective clinical practice [27].

Understanding the gap between perceived and actual communication skills is vital for curriculum development in dental education. By assessing dental students’ self-awareness of their communication abilities, educators can design targeted interventions that enhance these skills, ultimately contributing to better patient outcomes and more effective dental practices.

In the present work, an interesting finding was students’ significant underestimation of their operator-role communication performance compared to parent and peer evaluations, while assistant-role scores showed no significant differences. This role-dependent pattern suggests that assuming primary responsibility for patient care heightens self-criticism and potential anxiety. Similar findings were reported in other studies [15, 28] where Cuevas-Nunez & colleagues, through comparing students’, faculty and standardized patient’s assessment, demonstrated that self-assessment accuracy varies depending on the level of responsibility and clinical complexity.

Conversely, student overestimation of performance is reported in other literature, this is attributed to high self-confidence exceeding actual abilities [20]. The present study’s findings may reflect the heightened stress associated with dental treatment procedures and exhaustion among students resulting from sustained academic demands as well as the psychological burden of managing pediatric patients, as reflected by focus group discussions. Female students’ reports of investing considerable time in rapport-building and the emotionally draining nature of pediatric communication provide context for this self-underestimation pattern.

The poor to moderate inter-rater agreement raises important questions about the consistency and validity of relying on single-source evaluations. These findings underscore the communication assessment’s subjective nature and emphasize the importance of employing multi-source feedback to capture a more comprehensive overview of students’ competence. However, given this poor to moderate agreement between evaluator groups, the current 360-degree approach may be more suitable as a formative feedback tool rather than a stand-alone summative assessment method. Indeed, this pattern of correlations across evaluator sources suggest that different aspects of communication are perceived and prioritized by different stakeholders.

In this context, focus group discussions provided rich, in-depth understanding of the quantitative findings. The identification and agreement on challenges such as managing child anxiety, parental interference, language barriers, and emotional fatigue, helps explain the tendency toward self-underestimation in operator roles where primary responsibility adds to psychological demands.

Qualitative findings revealed an interesting pattern regarding gender differences in communication approaches, where female students more frequently described rapport building strategies, collaborative language (“we,” “my partner”), and reflective awareness; however, these observations require confirmation through quantitative subgroup analysis.

Students’ reports of previous traumatic dental experiences as significant barriers, and their sense of being unprepared for extreme parental behaviors, highlight critical gaps in the current curriculum. These findings align with the recognition by Smith et al. that clinical exposure alone does not automatically translate into enhanced professional communication skills [13]. This underscores the importance of structured training that incorporates role-plays, standardized patients, and guided reflection on challenging scenarios, enabling students to develop the confidence and competence needed to navigate complex clinical interactions.

The findings support several curriculum enhancements. First, structured communication skills training programs, incorporating role-play, standardized patients, and video-recorded self-observation, should be integrated throughout clinical education where evidence demonstrates that such programs significantly improve practical communication abilities [13, 15].

Second, well-designed assessment rubrics should be developed to clarify evaluation criteria for students and faculty. Rubrics serve as valuable guides, provide clear examples illustrating different achievement levels, and add transparency [29]. By clearly illustrating criteria, rubrics facilitate meaningful feedback and encourage students to reflect on and evaluate their own performance.

Third, incorporating standardized patients or trained patient-instructors from diverse backgrounds would provide valuable controlled practice opportunities [29, 30], meanwhile exposing students to cross-cultural communication challenges, particularly important in multicultural contexts like the UAE.

Fourth, multi-source feedback should be systematically implemented as a formative assessment approach. Besides relying on faculty evaluation, integrating self-assessment, peer feedback, expert observation, and patient perspectives provides more comprehensive developmental guidance.

Finally, addressing the emotional and psychological dimensions of patient communication deserves specific attention. Training should acknowledge the affective burden of challenging patient interactions and provide strategies for maintaining professional composure while managing personal emotional responses.

Conclusion

This study highlights the value of 360-degree multi-source feedback in assessing communication competence among undergraduate dental students in pediatric dentistry. Students tended to underestimate their operator-role performance, aligning with expert assessments. Poor to moderate agreement between raters suggests that no single evaluator fully captures communication competence. Qualitative findings showed that child anxiety, parental interference, language barriers, and emotional fatigue influenced performance. Overall, the findings support incorporating structured multi-source assessment and targeted communication training into dental curricula.

The present work provides a foundation for curriculum enhancements aimed at enabling more reliable, valid, and meaningful assessments of communication skills. Future research should adopt longitudinal designs tracking competence development over time, employ more refined measurement tools such as Likert scales with validated rubrics, conduct quantitative subgroup analyses by gender and performance level, and extend to multi-institutional contexts to enhance generalizability. Incorporating standardized patient evaluations would further strengthen assessment comprehensiveness.
