Modeling of the electrostatic field generated by horizontal current sources in a sea-ice-covered four-layer medium
Authors: Xueyao Chen, Zhaolong Sun, Chaoli Jiang, Kui Zhu, Mengxiang Li, Runxiang Jiang, Wentie Yang, Kai Yan, Yuedou Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe distribution of the underwater electric field around a target in stratified media provides a crucial foundation for research on target detection and localization in marine environments. This paper proposed a four-layer analytical model (air–sea ice–seawater–seabed) to address the issue of electric field propagation in ocean environments involving sea ice. Starting from the Laplace equation for a steady electric field, the repeated image method was employed to introduce image current sources. Combined with the boundary conditions, an analytical solution for the electric field intensity within the seawater layer of the four-layer medium model was derived. Subsequently, a numerical model employing the finite volume method (FVM) was constructed according to the conditions of the experiment and compared with the analytical solutions under the same idealized four-layer conditions. Finally, a scaled-down experiment was designed to compare the measured electric field intensity values with the analytical solutions. The results indicated that the relative error between the measured electric field intensity values and the analytical solutions ranged from 1.17% to a maximum of 11.28%, thus validating the effectiveness of the analytical model and demonstrating its applicability in realistic environments. This work provides the theoretical and technical foundations for detecting underwater targets in the sea-ice-covered marine electromagnetic environment.Keywords: Underwater electric field, Horizontal static current source, Four-layer medium model, The repeated image methodSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Ocean sciences

Introduction

The physical fields of vessels include electric fields, acoustic fields, magnetic fields, and hydrostatic pressure fields, which serve as crucial target signals for the identification and localization of underwater detection systems. At present, a variety of advanced underwater multi-physical field detection and measurement systems have been developed internationally, including the Multi-Influence Range System (MIRS) developed by SAES in Spain, the underwater multi-influence module developed by the Polish Naval Academy, and the TECOTEC Magnetic and Acoustic Measurement Range System1–4. These studies further demonstrate the crucial role of multi-field detection in target recognition. Among these detection modalities, electric field detection offers advantages such as being unaffected by hydrometeorological conditions in the marine environment, having stable and reliable detection performance, and possessing strong identification capability. Consequently, research related to environmental and target electric field characteristics has garnered increasing attention in the field of marine environmental target detection and localization. Furthermore, underwater electric fields can be applied to monitoring the corrosion status of vessel hulls and evaluating the effectiveness of anti-corrosion measures, conducting geophysical surveys to obtain seabed geological structure information, facilitating the exploration of seabed oil and natural gas deposits, and locating sunken vessels to support rescue and salvage operations. These diverse application scenarios validate the practical value of underwater electric fields and highlight their wide-ranging utility in the field of marine engineering.

Given that marine medium exhibits good electrical conductivity and corrosiveness, dissimilar metals submerged in it form corrosion currents, thereby generating corrosion-related static electric fields (CRSE)5. For vessels, corrosion currents form a loop through the hull, seawater, and propellers, which in turn gives rise to CRSE6–8. Among the existing modeling methods for CRSE, the finite element method (FEM), boundary element method (BEM), and equivalent source method (ESM) are widely applied9–11. Due to its concise mathematical form and clear physical meaning, the ESM has become an important theoretical basis for constructing CRSE models12–14. This method demonstrates that actual sources with arbitrarily complex distributions can be characterized by the linear superposition of a finite set of equivalent electric dipoles or current sources, as shown in Fig. 1. This significantly simplifies electric field calculations and enables adaptation to different seawater electrolyte environments15,16.

Equivalent diagram of corrosion-related static electric field.

The equivalent source method (ESM) provides a crucial technical means for the inversion of electromagnetic fields from underwater targets. However, in practical underwater environments, when considering the electrostatic field distribution within underwater targets such as vessels and unmanned underwater vehicles (UUV), the issue of multiple layer media distribution should be taken into account17. Sommerfeld pioneered the investigation into the electric field generated by an electric dipole on conductor surfaces beneath a planar interface18. Building upon this foundation, Hoitham proposed an equivalent dipole pair model for the static electric field of vessels, thereby establishing the cornerstone for research on vessel electric field signatures19. Early research primarily concentrated on two-layer medium models. Bannister extended the theoretical framework towards practical scenarios by solving for the electromagnetic fields of horizontal electric dipoles in a two-layer medium, employing finitely conducting earth-image theory techniques20. Meanwhile, Schäfer demonstrated that the underwater potential is subject to the coupled effects of material polarization, measurement depth, and conductivity21. This finding underscores the necessity of accurately accounting for environmental factors in any predictive model.

With the advancement of marine detection requirements, the three-layer medium model of “air-seawater-seabed” in the shallow marine environment has progressively become the research focus due to its closer alignment with realistic environments. Cao and Tian utilized CST simulations to demonstrate that increasing the number of medium layers can extend electromagnetic propagation distances, thereby validating the necessity of the three-layer medium model22,23. Further research has refined this understanding. Wang identified low-frequency and interface effects as critical factors for long-distance transmission and quantified the influences of water depth and medium conductivity24,25. Feng extended investigations to complex seabed topography and its impact on field distribution26,27, while Wu systematically investigated the influence of the seabed medium in the stratified marine environment on the electromagnetic field in the air radiated from the extremely low-frequency horizontal electric dipole located in the seawater28. Significant methodological innovations have emerged for solving the three-layer medium model. These include Huang’s complex image theory with vector potential, Zhang’s conclusion on surface-wave-dominated long-distance propagation, Xu’s near-field approximation formula for ELF signals, Wang’s simplified lateral wave model, and Ren’s field decomposition method (separating secondary and background fields)29–33. Collectively, these approaches provide efficient pathways for analyzing the electric field distribution in the three-layer medium. It is noteworthy that a fundamental similarity exists between the propagation characteristics of extremely low-frequency electromagnetic waves and those of static current sources within a three-layer medium. The propagation behavior of both is predominantly governed by the stratified structure and interfacial effects of the three-layer medium. Key parameters, such as seawater depth and the electrical conductivity of each layer, exhibit a commonality in their modulating effects on the electric field distribution and attenuation patterns. Nevertheless, the three-layer medium model struggles to fully capture the complex stratified structures prevalent in actual marine environments. This limitation has spurred the development of four-layer medium models. Woloszyn proposed an analytical four-layer medium model comprising “air-seawater-seabed-non-conductive” layer, enhancing the accuracy of describing complex seabed structures34.

Affected by special temperature-salinity structures, dynamic distribution of ice-water mixtures, and periodic freezing-thawing processes of ice layers, the electromagnetic environment in sea areas with ice layers differs significantly from that in conventional sea areas35. The low temperature of ice layers inhibits the migration rate of ions in seawater, reduces the overall medium conductivity, and intensifies the electrochemical polarization effect at the interface between the vessel hull and seawater36. In addition, the freshwater input from sea ice melting and the deep high-salinity seawater form a vertical salinity gradient, driving the salt-gradient battery effect and generating an electrostatic field dominated by ion transport37. Transient salinity fluctuations caused by ice layer freezing-thawing also induce transient currents, which in turn interfere with the stability of vessel’s electric field. Lofgren and Weeks experimentally investigated the correlation mechanisms among sea ice salinity, ice growth rate, and environmental solute concentration38. Islam introduced deep learning techniques to achieve intelligent estimation of sea ice thickness based on dielectric constant inversion39. However, due to the pronounced heterogeneity and environmental sensitivity of sea ice in terms of its physical structure and chemical composition, it is seldom treated as an independent and critical layer in most current electromagnetic modeling studies of multilayered marine environments, thereby lacking dedicated modeling and systematic analysis.

This paper proposes an analytical model of the electric field generated by horizontal static current sources in a four-layer medium (air-sea ice-seawater-seabed), aiming at the accurate modeling of the underwater electric field of vessels in sea areas with a sea ice layer. Firstly, based on Laplace’s equation for steady electric fields, the electric potential and electric field intensity generated by a pair of horizontal static current sources in the seawater layer were derived for a two-layer medium model. The repeated image method was then introduced to satisfy the multilayer interface boundary conditions via successive mirror reflections, leading to an analytical expression for the electric field in the seawater layer. Subsequently, a finite-volume numerical model was established under experimental conditions and compared with the analytical solution under the same idealized four-layer scenario to verify its correctness. Finally, a scaled-down experiment was designed, and the measured electric field value was compared with the analytical results to evaluate the applicability of the analytical model in realistic environments.

Methods

Current sources or electric dipoles are the basic units for the inversion of underwater electromagnetic fields of targets such as vessels. Previous studies have shown that due to the differences in electrical conductivity between the air layer and seabed layer in the marine environment relative to seawater, the distribution of underwater electric fields in the marine environment is significantly affected by different media. As a special medium, sea ice also often has a non-negligible impact on the distribution of underwater electric fields in polar and other low-temperature sea areas40. Therefore, this paper takes the current source in the four-layer medium of sea-ice-covered areas (such as polar regions) as the basic research unit, as illustrated in Fig. 2, and focuses on analyzing the distribution of its underwater electrostatic field under the influence of the ice layer. Consider a horizontally stratified four-layer medium representing the polar marine environment. The domain is defined within a Cartesian coordinate system where the z-axis denotes depth. The individual layers are characterized by their respective macroscopic electrical conductivities (), which represent the air (), sea ice (), seawater (), and seabed (). The parallel planar interfaces between these domains are located at depths (air-sea ice, namely BC1), (sea ice-seawater, namely BC2), and (seawater-seabed, namely BC3). A horizontal current source pair, constituting a generalized dipole with positive current and negative current separated by a discrete distance 2a, is submerged strictly within the high-conductivity seawater layer (Layer 3) at an operational depth . However, it should be noted that in actual marine environments, sea ice is a complex composite consisting of pure ice, brine pockets, and air bubbles. Due to the intricate internal pore structures and the heterogeneous distribution of brine pockets among ice crystals, the electrical conductivity of the ice layer is non-uniformly distributed, with localized regions of higher conductivity. Such complexity is difficult to faithfully simulate under engineering and laboratory conditions. Therefore, this paper proposes a simplified four-layer medium model, in which the electrical conductivity of each layer is assumed to be homogeneous.

Experimental platform

Experiment environment

To validate the correctness of the analytical formulas derived in Methods and to specifically investigate the characteristics of the near-region electric field generated by a pair of horizontal current sources, a systematic model experiment was conducted in a low-temperature laboratory. The experimental setup was designed to simulate a four-layer medium environment consisting of “air–sea ice–seawater–seabed”. A fiberglass tank (dimensions: 3.2 m × 2 m × 1.6 m) was employed to reduce external electromagnetic interference and provide structural support for the stratified media. Both the electric current sources and the three-component electric field sensors were mounted on a low-dielectric, low-loss epoxy resin bracket to minimize the influence of auxiliary structures on the electromagnetic field distribution.

The initial depth of seawater was set to 1.39 m, and the ambient temperature was maintained at − 15 °C. The initial water temperature was 18 °C, which gradually decreased during the cooling process and eventually stabilized at − 2.9 °C. The freezing process consisted of two stages: it took 5 days from the completion of water filling until a 5 cm ice layer formed on the surface, and an additional 2 days for the ice to thicken from 5 cm to 8 cm. Throughout the freezing process, the electrical conductivity was not constant. To accurately determine the conductivity values, measurements were taken at multiple locations in the tank after the water temperature had stabilized, and the arithmetic mean was adopted as the final value. The resulting measurements indicated a sea ice conductivity of 0.02 S/m and a seawater conductivity of 2.2 S/m. The experimental platform is shown in Fig. 4. Prior to the commencement of the experiment, the three-component electric field sensor was connected and calibrated with the data acquisition unit, and the sampling frequency was set to 500 Hz to ensure adequate capture of dynamic variations in the electric field signals. During the formal experiment, the paired electric current source was connected to a bipolar power supply. To accurately separate the effective signal from environmental interference, background noise was first acquired continuously for 30 s under no-excitation conditions to assess the ambient and system inherent noise level. Subsequently, the bipolar power supply was activated to inject a steady direct current signal into the seawater medium, and the electric field response was recorded for no less than 30 s. Throughout the process, all experimental parameters and ambient conditions were meticulously documented to ensure data reproducibility and result reliability.

Equivalence scheme

In a traditional experimental configuration, the electric current source is typically held stationary, while the measurement process is accomplished by moving the electric field sensor. However, in the present experiment, the imperative to preserve the structural integrity of the ice sheet was a primary consideration. To prevent potential damage to the ice caused by sensor movement, and based on the principle of equivalence, the strategy of increasing the number of electric current sources was adopted to equivalently substitute for the physical translocation of the sensor. The experimental system features a fixed epoxy resin bracket as its core supporting component. Leveraging the exceptional insulating properties and mechanical stability of the epoxy resin material, this bracket ensures the spatial positions of both the current sources and the sensors remain constant throughout the measurement process, thereby effectively mitigating system errors introduced by mechanical movement.

Eleven horizontal electric current sources and twelve three-component electric field sensors were pre-deployed in the experimental setup. Through a pairwise combination strategy of adjacent current sources, an equivalent measurement configuration was achieved, effectively emulating a measurement system comprising a pair of current sources and twelve survey lines. Each survey line corresponds to a fixed spatial path. By sequentially switching the current source combinations along each line, ten discrete measurement points were established per line, thereby enabling systematic acquisition of the spatial distribution of electric field intensity within the seawater layer. This approach replaces the conventional practice of physically relocating electric field sensors, significantly enhancing operational feasibility and ensuring data reliability under sea-ice-covered conditions.

In the figures, the green spheres represent the preplaced horizontal electric current sources, while the blue and red spheres denote three-component electric field sensors. Data process

The data acquired by the three-component electric field sensor comprises two distinct phases: the first phase consists of background potential difference measurements recorded over 30 s while the DC excitation source was inactive, primarily representing ambient and system inherent noise; the second phase involves mixed potential difference signals, also lasting no less than 30 s after the DC excitation source was activated, which include both the target electric field response and superimposed background interference, as shown in the raw signal in Fig. 9. To accurately extract the effective potential difference signal generated by the DC excitation source, the average values of the potential difference data over the two 30-s intervals were first computed to suppress random noise and highlight the steady-state response. Subsequently, the difference between these two averages was taken. This differential operation eliminated common-mode background interference, thereby isolating the net change in potential difference attributable solely to the source excitation. The net potential difference value was then divided by the electrode spacing in the corresponding measurement direction to obtain the three orthogonal components of the electric field strength () at the measurement point. Finally, based on the electric field vector magnitude formula , the total electric field strength magnitude at each location was calculated. This value serves as the empirical basis for subsequent comparison and validation with theoretical results.

Comparison of numerical solution and analytical solution

To preliminarily verify the correctness of the analytical model, a four-layer numerical model with identical parameters was established based on the experimental conditions. The model employs the finite volume method (FVM) to solve Laplace’s equation on a Cartesian grid. The core principle of the FVM lies in discretizing the continuous computational domain into a series of non-overlapping control volumes that fully cover the domain. By integrating the conservation law over each control volume, a system of discrete algebraic equations is constructed to approximate the solution of the original equation. This method strictly ensures both local and global conservation, meaning that the net flux into any cell must equal the flux out of its adjacent cells. In other words, each discrete equation represents the rate of change of a physical quantity within a specific control volume being equal to the net flux entering through its boundaries plus any internal source terms42.

Discussion

Overall, the relative error in Plane 1 is slightly higher than that in the other three planes. Plane 1 lies immediately adjacent to the sea ice–seawater interface, and the observed discrepancy is primarily attributed to distortion in the electromagnetic field distribution caused by boundary effects at the sea ice–seawater interface. According to the electromagnetic theory, the core principle of the repeated image method is to simplify the electric field calculation by replacing the irregularly distributed induced charges at the interface with equivalent image charges. Therefore, as the actual charge approaches the interface, its distribution deviates from the idealized image equivalence, which results in the increasing modeling error. Moreover, the sea ice layer in the experiment is a crystalline composite containing inhomogeneities such as air bubbles, which results in a spatially non-uniform distribution of electrical conductivity. This inherent heterogeneity diverges from the homogeneous medium assumption adopted in the analytical model, which further exacerbates the error in the electric field distribution near the interface.

In Plane 3 and Plane 4, the relative errors observed along Survey Line 2, located along the x-axis, are generally higher than those recorded on Survey Line 1 and Survey Line 3. This is because Survey Line 2 is geometrically closest to the electric current source. Under these conditions, even minor deviations in the position, orientation, and excitation strength of the source will be amplified, which will result in noticeable variation in the field distribution and thus leading to large measurement errors.

Furthermore, as can be seen from most survey lines, the relative error decreases slightly when the excitation current increases from 30 to 50 mA. The results aforementioned indicate that the random measurement noise can be suppressed to some extent by increasing the signal intensity, which improves the signal-to-noise ratio. Moreover, the comparative results validate the high reliability of the experimental parameters adopted in this paper, which can provide strong signal support for subsequent electric field measurements in low-temperature marine environments.

In summary, under the four different cases involving varying ice thicknesses and current magnitudes, the relative errors across all survey lines were observed to range from a minimum of 1.17% to a maximum of 11.28%. The results aforementioned validate the effectiveness of the analytical solution model, which provides strong theoretical support for the rapid and accurate analysis of electric fields under sea-ice-covered maritime conditions.

The electrical conductivity differences between the sea ice and seawater layers significantly modulate the reflection coefficients at the interfaces, which consequently governs the spatial attenuation and field distribution patterns. This demonstrates that incorporating the ice layer as a discrete environmental characteristic is essential for a reliable analysis of electric signatures in polar regions. Consequently, the proposed model enables the rapid and accurate calculation of electric fields in ice-covered maritime conditions. The model performs significantly faster than traditional numerical methods such as FEM or BEM. It offers robust theoretical support for the underwater electric stealth design of ships and the evaluation of anti-corrosion measures.

Conclusion

To investigate the distribution characteristics of electric field strength in ice-covered marine environments, this paper presented a four-layer medium model comprising air, sea ice, seawater, and seabed. Based on electromagnetic theory, the repeated image method was employed to derive an analytical solution for the electric field distribution within this stratified structure. The accuracy of this analytical model was verified through a comparative analysis with a finite volume method (FVM) numerical model, yielding maximum and minimum relative deviations of 8.06% and 0.54%, respectively. Furthermore, laboratory experiments provided empirical validation, demonstrating maximum and minimum relative errors of 11.28% and 1.17% when compared to the analytical solutions.

Despite being characterized by computational efficiency, the current analytical model is limited to strictly parallel layers and controlled laboratory conditions, which may not fully capture the complexities of non-parallel interfaces or environments with significant 3D effects, such as irregular seabed topography, non-uniform sea ice thickness, and the heterogeneous distribution of ice conductivity in actual maritime environments. Additionally, while the laboratory experiments validated the model using scaled-down sea ice thicknesses (5–8 cm), the mathematical framework of the analytical model is theoretically applicable to a wide range of actual polar sea ice thicknesses (from several centimeters to several meters), provided the electrical conductivity can be approximated as a homogeneous effective value. In practical electrical prospecting applications, sophisticated filtering and rejection processing are required for multiple noise sources, including vessel corrosion electric fields, equipment leakage currents, and environmental noises from sea ice freezing-thawing, salinity gradients, marine organisms and seawater turbulence.

Future research will focus on developing a multi-current source model for vessel underwater electric signatures in ice-covered seas based on the proposed analytical model, analyzing the impacts of layer thickness and conductivity variations on electric field distribution, and identifying equivalent source parameters with measured data. By accurately reconstructing electric signatures under specific polar conditions, the validated model will directly contribute to real-time vessel hull corrosion monitoring and anti-corrosion effectiveness evaluation. Moreover, it provides a critical foundation for non-contact sea mine risk control, helping to protect vessels against underwater detection systems triggered by electric field intensity measurements in ice-covered regions.
