Molecular diversity and evolutionary dynamics of phage receptor binding proteins in Staphylococcus aureus: implications for phage therapy and resistance
Authors: C. S. VinodKumar, B. S. Prasad, A. Arunkumar, Ishfaq Nabi Najar, Aarti Singh, J. K. Veni Emilda, R. Venkatesh, Vikram Sivaji, V. L. Jayasimha, Suresh Basavaraj Arakera, Suneeta Kalasuramath, N. Ramesh, Balaji Veeraraghavan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phage–host interactions are defined by the molecular specificity of receptor-binding proteins (RBPs), which determine the efficacy of infection and the host range. The genetic and structural variability of bacterial receptor molecules is essential for designing effective phage therapeutics and mitigating resistance. This study investigated four receptor-binding proteins, tarS, tarL, tagO, and tagG, in multiple Staphylococcus aureus isolates. Single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis was performed to assess receptor diversity and evolutionary conservation. Molecular docking was conducted between these bacterial receptors and Staphylococcus phage tail and spike proteins (TPCARΦ38, TPCARΦ53, TSCARΦ53, and SPCARΦ58) to evaluate binding affinity and interaction specificity. SNP profiling revealed conserved and strain-specific mutations across the receptor genes, predominantly transition-type substitutions, without insertions or deletions. Docking simulations demonstrated strong receptor–phage interactions, with binding energies ranging from 8 to 14 kcal/mol. Despite underlying genetic variation, conserved docking hotspots suggested functional stability and evolutionary pressure to preserve key receptor-binding motifs essential for phage attachment. The findings indicate a coevolutionary balance between S. aureus receptor polymorphism and phage adaptation. Such receptor diversity may drive partial resistance while maintaining susceptibility to related phages. This molecular understanding of RBP evolution provides valuable insights for designing rational, broad-spectrum, or combination phage therapy strategies against S. aureus infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58647-z.

Introduction

Bacteriophages (phages) are a class of viruses that precisely target bacteria and cause their lysis, playing a vital role in regulating bacterial populations across diverse natural environments1. The preliminary step in the bactericidal activity of phage infection involves the recognition and binding to specific receptors located on the bacterial outer cell surface. These receptors, comprising proteins, carbohydrates, lipopolysaccharides, or teichoic acids, determine the phage host range and infection dynamics2. Understanding these receptor–phage interactions is therefore critical for advancing the application of phage therapy and designing targeted antibacterial approaches. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a major global pathogen responsible for severe infections, both in hospitals and the community3. The capability of MRSA to evade multiple antibiotics has intensified the search for alternative therapeutic options, presenting bacteriophage therapy as a viable alternative4. Nevertheless, the success of phage therapy relies on identifying and characterizing the bacterial receptors that facilitate phage adsorption and host cell entry. Most phages being investigated for therapeutic5 purposes are tailed dsDNA phages from the order Caudovirales, which have a tail organelle for translocating their genomic DNA into the host bacterium’s cytoplasm6. Specialised receptor-binding proteins (RBPs), mainly Tail fibers (TFs) or tail spikes (TSPs), originate from the tail and interact with particular ligands located on the bacterial host cell surface. However, in contrast, wall teichoic acid represents the main determinant controlling phage binding and recognition in Staphylococcus. Staphylococci phages appear to use a receptor, a peptidoglycan-linked WTA polymer with ribitol-phosphate (RboP) repeats7. TarS and TarM are the predominant glycosyltransferases catalyzing β-1,4-GlcNAc attachment observed in the majority of S. aureus strains. Current research has identified five key Staphylococcus aureus receptor-binding proteins: TarS, TarM, TarP, TarL, and TagO8. However, relatively little information is available about these receptors, and there is considerable scope for studying Staphylococcus aureus and its phage interactions at the molecular level. The present study identifies the bacteriophage receptors expressed on MRSA strains and defines their contribution in facilitating phage binding and infection. Concurrently, by examining these interactions, it becomes possible to discern how bacteria manipulate or obscure their receptors to generate resistance against phages. Such insights will not only enhance our understanding of the mechanisms of phage-host specificity but also help predict and overcome phage resistance, thereby improving the efficacy and sustainability of phage-based therapeutics.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

The study protocol (Reference No. 106/2024, dated 25 April 2024) was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Board (IERB), S. S. Institute of Medical Sciences and Research Centre, Karnataka. The Institutional Biosafety Committee of S. S. Institute of Medical Sciences and Research Centre, Karnataka, also approved the study protocol (IBC No. 03/2024, dated 26 April 2024). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrolment in the study in accordance with the International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) guidelines.

Collection and identification of clinical MRSA isolates

Five MRSA strains were isolated from the diabetic foot pus sample of patients admitted to SS Hospital, Davangere, Karnataka. The isolates were cultured on Blood Agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) of S. aureus were conducted using the automated VITEK 2 system in accordance with CLSI guidelines9. The bacterial isolates were preserved in glycerol stocks at minus 80 °C for further genomic analysis10.

Whole genome sequencing of MRSA

Genomic DNA was extracted using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quantity and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and Qubit fluorometer. Paired-end sequencing libraries (2 × 150 bp) were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep Kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform11. Raw reads were quality-checked using FastQC (v0.11.9)12 and trimmed with Trimmomatic (v0.39)13 to remove adapters and low-quality bases. Genome assembly was carried out using MetaSPAdes (v3.15.5)14, and the quality of assemblies was evaluated using QUAST (v5.2)15. Annotation of assembled genomes was performed with Prokka (v1.14.6)16.

Isolation and identification of bacteriophage for MRSA isolates

Bacteriophages specific to methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) were isolated from raw sewage collected from a municipal sewage treatment plant in Davangere, following the method described by Smith and Huggins17. Briefly, 50 mL of sewage water was collected in a sterile conical flask and treated with a few drops of chloroform. An equal volume of sterile nutrient broth was then added, followed by inoculation with 1 mL of a 24-h-old MRSA broth culture. The inoculated mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to facilitate phage enrichment. After incubation, the lysate was treated with a few drops of chloroform and gently agitated for approximately 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm pore-size Acrodisc membrane filter (PALL, Germany) to remove residual bacterial cells. The filtrate was subsequently subjected to plaque-forming unit (PFU) enumeration using the double-layer agar technique as described by Smith and Huggins17. Purified phage isolates were finally stored in SM buffer at minus 80 °C until further use.

In-vitro confirmation of bacteriophage activity on MRSA

A bacterial lawn was prepared on nutrient agar plates by evenly flooding the surface with 1.0 mL of a 24-h-old MRSA culture and allowing the excess inoculum to drain off. Using the spot assay method, 10 µL of bacteriophage lysate (3 × 105 PFU/mL) was carefully spotted onto the prepared bacterial lawn10. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, after which the presence and size of zones of inhibition were observed and recorded as evidence of phage lytic activity.

Electron microscopic study of phage

Transmission electron microscopy of the isolated bacteriophages was performed at NIMHANS, Bengaluru. The phage suspension was first passed through a 0.22 μm pore-size Acrodisc membrane filter (PALL, Germany) to eliminate residual host bacterial cells. The filtrate was washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.0). Subsequently, 10 µL of the purified bacteriophage preparation was placed onto carbon-coated copper grids and negatively stained using 2% uranyl acetate. Excess stain was removed with filter paper, and the grids were air-dried. The prepared specimens were then examined using a JEM-14,000 PLUS transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan)18.

Whole genome sequencing of bacteriophages

Whole-genome sequencing of five Staphylococcus phages was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. DNA was extracted, quality-checked12, and sequenced using paired-end reads (2 × 150 bp). Reads were processed and assembled with MetaViralSPAdes19, followed by functional annotation using Pharokka20 and taxonomic classification via the PhageScope database21.

Retrieval of publicly available Staphylococcusgenomes

Complete genome sequences of seven methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) strains were analyzed to investigate temporal genomic variations and the potential evolution of phage receptor-associated genes over time. WGS were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The selection was based on genome submissions spanning a 25-year period, from 2000 to 2025, ensuring representation from different time points to capture genomic diversity and evolutionary trends. Genomes were curated based on high assembly quality (≥ 95% completeness and ≤ 5% contamination) and the availability of relevant metadata, including source of isolation, year, and geographic origin. Representative genomes chosen were Staphylococcus aureus subsp. aureus MW2 (2002), Staphylococcus aureus subsp. aureus N315 (2003), Staphylococcus aureus subsp. aureus Mu50 (2005), Staphylococcus aureus 04-02981 (2010), Staphylococcus aureus ATCC BAA1680 (2015), Staphylococcus aureus CC1153-MRSA (2020), Staphylococcus aureus SW-MRSA58 (2025). These particular years were selected to provide even intervals across the study period and to include genomes with high-quality assembly and annotation status (at the complete genome level).

In-silico identification of phage receptors

Annotated genomes from both NCBI and clinical isolates were systematically screened for potential phage-binding receptors. Genes encoding key surface components, including wall teichoic acids (WTA), membrane transporters, and surface-associated adhesins, were identified using BLASTp (E-value < 1 × 10−5) against a curated database of experimentally validated phage receptors and the PHROG database22. The Protein domain and functional annotations were verified using InterProScan (v5.67) and Pfam (v36.0)23. Structural modelling of selected receptor proteins was performed using Phyre223 and AlphaFold Protein Structure Database24 to predict three-dimensional conformations and potential phage-interacting domains.

Comparative genomic and evolutionary analysis of Staphylococcus receptor genes

Multiple sequence alignments were generated using MAFFT (v7.525)25 to evaluate conservation of receptor genes across Staphylococcus lineages. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred in MEGA X using the Maximum Likelihood approach with 1000 bootstrap replicates26, and bootstrap values were indicated at the corresponding nodes. The Tamura-Nei substitution model, selected based on the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), was applied to infer evolutionary history. The analysis included 12 nucleotide sequences encompassing 1549 aligned positions. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms and non-synonymous substitutions were identified using SNP-sites and annotated with SnpSift27. SNP data were visualized using Snipit within the Galaxy platform28.

Phage–host protein interaction analysis

Molecular docking analyses were performed to examine interactions between Staphylococcus aureus receptor-binding proteins (TarS, TarL, TagO, and TagG) and structural proteins of Staphylococcus bacteriophages, such as tail proteins (TPCARΦ38 and TPCARΦ53), tail sheath protein (TSCARΦ53), and spike protein (SPCARΦ58). Both receptor binding proteins and structural proteins were subjected to structure prediction using AlphaFold24. The predicted models (ipTM/pTM = 0.92) were prepared and visualized in PyMOL (https://www.pymol.org/).

Protein-protein docking simulations were conducted using the MEGADOCK platform (v4.0) to predict binding interactions and docking accuracy. A rigid-body Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) based algorithm with an angular sampling step of 6° was applied, and docking scores were calculated based on shape complementarity, electrostatic interactions, and desolvation energy. For each protein pair, 10,800 docking poses were generated and ranked according to the MEGADOCK scoring function.

Interaction likelihood was assessed using the MEGADOCK PPI score (E), a dimensionless metric used to rank docking configurations rather than estimate binding free energy. Based on benchmark-derived thresholds, docking scores of E > 8, E > 10, and E > 12 correspond to approximately 10%, 50%, and 80% positive predictive values, respectively, indicating increasing confidence in the predicted protein–protein interactions.

Molecular dynamics simulation study

Molecular dynamics setup

All-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to investigate the structural stability and dynamic behavior of the protein in an explicit aqueous environment using GROMACS 2023.229. The protein was parameterized with the GROMOS 54A7 force field30, and solvated in a cubic box using the SPC water model31, maintaining a minimum distance of 1.0 nm from the box edges. System neutrality was achieved by adding appropriate Na⁺/Cl⁻ ions. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm until the convergence criterion was met, which was defined as a value below 1000 kJ·mol⁻¹·nm⁻¹. The system was equilibrated under NVT (100 ps, 300 K) using the V-rescale thermostat, followed by NPT (100 ps, 1 bar) using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat, with position restraints applied to the protein’s heavy atoms. Production MD simulations were carried out for 100 ns with a 2-fs time step under periodic boundary conditions. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method32, and covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm33. Trajectory data were saved every 10 ns for subsequent analysis.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to probe large-scale collective motions. PCA reduces complex correlated motions into a limited number of principal components (PCs) that describe dominant conformational dynamics34. The Cα positional covariance matrix was calculated using gmx_covar in GROMACS 2023.2 after least-squares fitting to the average structure to remove global translational and rotational motions. The covariance matrix was diagonalized using gmx_anaeig, yielding eigenvalues and eigenvectors representing the magnitude and direction of collective motions. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) captured the majority of conformational variance, and projecting the trajectories onto these components enabled visualization of the dominant dynamic behaviors.

Phylogenomic analysis

Whole-genome phylogenetic analysis of the isolated bacteriophages was performed using the VICTOR (Virus Classification and Tree Building Online Resource) platform. The analysis was conducted using the nucleotide sequence-based Genome-BLAST Distance Phylogeny (GBDP) method with the recommended D6 formula and trimming option. Pairwise intergenomic distances were calculated, and a balanced minimum evolution tree was inferred using FASTME. The phylogenomic tree was midpoint-rooted and annotated with taxonomic information, including family, genus, species assignment, GC content, and genome length. Reference genomes of closely related Staphylococcus phages retrieved from GenBank were included for comparative analysis.

Data validation and statistical analysis

Identified receptor genes were validated by cross-referencing with published literature and established phage-host interaction databases, including PHI-base and PhaRe35. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the distribution of putative phage receptor genes across NCBI-derived genomes and clinical MRSA isolates. Sequence conservation and variability were assessed using percent identity and pairwise distance matrices generated in MEGA X26, while nucleotide diversity (π) was calculated using Clustal Omega36. Data visualization and graphical analyses were performed in R (v4.3.1) employing the ggplot2 and circlize packages.

Results

Phenotypic identification and antimicrobial susceptibility analysis of bacterial isolates

The five clinical isolates (SSCARSSTI000033, SSCARSSTI000041, SSCARSSTI000043, SSCARSSTI000057, and SSCARSSTI000064) were confirmed as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) using the VITEK-2 automated identification system. All isolates were obtained from patients with diabetic foot infections, specifically from Wagner Grade III ulcers, reflecting advanced lesions with deep tissue involvement. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) demonstrated a pronounced multidrug-resistant profile, with resistance observed to penicillin, oxacillin, cefoxitin, ciprofloxacin, daptomycin, linezolid, tigecycline, doxycycline, co-trimoxazole, and levofloxacin. Despite this extensive resistance pattern, the isolates were sensitive to clindamycin and vancomycin, suggesting that these agents may still represent viable therapeutic options. Overall, the resistance profile and clinical origin of these MRSA strains underscore the significant treatment challenges associated with chronic, non-healing diabetic foot ulcers.

WGS Analysis for Staphylococcus aureus strains

Antimicrobial resistance determinants were identified using the CARD database37. The analysis revealed that the Staphylococcus aureus strains harbored multiple resistance genes, including tet (38), mepR, mepA, and mgrA, which are implicated in tetracycline resistance. In addition, fluoroquinolone resistance-associated genes such as norA, norC, vanT, arlS, and arlR were detected. The presence of the β-lactamase encoding gene blaZ further confirmed the strain as MRSA. Moreover, the fosB gene, responsible for fosfomycin resistance, and the lmrS gene, which mediates resistance to a broad spectrum of antimicrobials, including macrolides, aminoglycosides, oxazolidinones, phenicols, and diaminopyrimidines, were also identified (Fig. 1).

In addition, genes associated with virulence factors, adhesion, and immune evasion were also identified, providing a broader understanding of their pathogenic potential. The major virulence factors were related to capsules, the Type VII secretion system, adhesion proteins, hemolysin, staphopains, and staphylokinase, among others (Fig. 2).

Phage Identification, characterization, and WGS analysis

The bacteriophages isolated against the MRSA strains produced well-defined, circular, and clear plaques with an average diameter of approximately 3 mm (SD ± 0.3) (Fig. 4). The purified phages were designated CARΦ29, CARΦ38, CARΦ46, CARΦ53, and CARΦ58. Transmission electron microscopy revealed that these phages possessed a short tail (~ 30 nm; SD ± 1.4) and an icosahedral head (~ 53 nm; SD ± 2.3), consistent with class Caudoviricetes according to ICTV nomenclature (Fig. 5). Taxonomic analysis using the PhageScope classification tool further confirmed that the isolates are DNA viruses belonging to the class Caudoviricetes. Host specificity assays demonstrated that all five bacteriophages were 100% specific against all five clinical MRSA isolates tested in this study.

Whole-genome sequencing analysis confirmed that all five phages were strictly lytic and lacked genes associated with antibiotic resistance, toxin production, or lysogeny. Bacterial phage receptors and sequence alignment

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) analysis of NCBI-retrieved strains and clinical MRSA isolates identified a total of thirteen receptor-binding protein (RBP) associated genes, namely tarA, tarB, tarD, tarL, tarS, tarM, tarK, tarF, tarI-1, tarI-2, tagO, tagU-2, and tagG. These genes encode glycosyltransferases, polymerases, and other modifying enzymes that govern the structural heterogeneity of wall teichoic acids (WTAs), thereby influencing bacteriophage recognition and attachment (Fig. 6).

Comparative genomic analysis showed that most S. aureus genomes harbored all thirteen RBP-related genes. However, six strains of Staphylococcus aureus subsp. aureus MW2 (2002), N315 (2003), Mu50 (2005), SSCARSSTI000041, SSCARSSTI000043, and SSCARSSTI000057 lacked the tarM gene, suggesting potential alterations in WTA composition that may modulate phage susceptibility.

Given their high prevalence across isolates and well-established roles in phage adsorption reported in earlier studies, four key receptors (tarS, tarL, tagO, and tagG) were selected for sequence alignment and detailed analysis. The alignment revealed a high degree of sequence conservation with only minor point mutations (Fig. 7). SNP analysis and annotation

Analysis of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the tarS gene (1.7 kb) identified several substitution sites scattered throughout the coding sequence. Repeated SNPs were identified at positions 309, 342, 481, 819, 1398, 1495, 1594–1596, and 1647–1659 bp, indicating areas prone to mutation. The most common substitutions occurred at 309 bp (A→G), 342 bp (C→A), 481 bp (A→G), and 1495 bp (T→A), appearing in six to eight isolates, while other changes were less frequent. Transitions (AG, CT) were more prevalent than transversions, and no insertions or deletions were observed, indicating structural stability (Fig. 8a, Supplementary File 1). The tarL gene (1,515 bp) exhibited nine SNPs at positions 414, 540, 645, 894, 951, 1146, 1168, 1242, and 1515 bp. A T→C change at 645 bp was common among most isolates, indicating a stable polymorphic site, while the alteration at 1242 bp marked a secondary hotspot. Other SNPs were unique to specific isolates. The mutations were primarily transitions, with no insertions or deletions (indels) detected (Fig. 8c, Supplementary File 2). SNP analysis of the tagO gene (1056 bp) revealed substitutions at positions 507, 669, 721, 729, 828, 903, 943, 960, and 1032–1053 bp. Frequent SNPs at 1050 and 1053 bp were found in multiple historical and clinical isolates, indicating conserved polymorphic sites under selective pressure. Most variations consisted of transitions, with occasional transversions, and no insertions or deletions (indels) were observed. Overall, tagO showed moderate variability, especially towards the 3′ region, consistent with microevolution while maintaining its functional role (Fig. 8b, Supplementary File 3). The tagG gene showed limited variation, with eight SNPs at positions 87, 114, 144, 414, 657, 659, 772, and 804 bp. Common substitutions at 144 bp (A→T) and 772 bp (C→T) were shared across most isolates, while other changes were isolate-specific. Transition mutations were predominant, and the absence of indels confirmed the conservation of tagG, consistent with its essential role in wall teichoic acid transport (Fig. 8d, Supplementary File 4).

These substitutions were predominantly located within the coding regions of the analyzed genes and were identified as synonymous changes. Thus, no major structural alterations were observed, as supported by consistent structural and functional annotations across gene clusters. Functional classification analysis revealed no discernible effects, with a missense/silent substitution ratio of 0. Furthermore, no variant by effect was found in any of the genes or their corresponding protein structures. This observation was further supported by SnpEff annotation analysis, which revealed no functional annotation for the identified SNPs across all four receptor-binding protein (RBP) genes (tarS, tarL, tagO, and tagG). This lack of annotation suggests that the detected variants are located outside of regions critical for protein coding or regulation, and therefore are unlikely to exert any structural or functional impact on the encoded proteins. WGS of phage structural proteins

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) analysis of the five isolated bacteriophages enabled comprehensive annotation of genes encoding structural and host-interaction proteins. This analysis revealed that tail-associated structural components, specifically tail fiber proteins, tail sheath proteins, and spike proteins, function as the primary receptor-binding proteins responsible for host recognition and adsorption. These proteins are known to mediate the initial and highly specific interaction between bacteriophages and bacterial surface receptors, thereby determining host range and infection efficiency.

All five phage genomes consistently harbored genes encoding these structural proteins, indicating a conserved architecture among the isolated phages. In total, seventeen putative receptor-associated structural proteins were identified across the genomes. To prioritize proteins most likely involved in stable and biologically relevant phage–host interactions, proteins were further screened based on their predicted inter-protein TM-score (ipTM), a measure of confidence in protein structure and interaction predictions.

Based on a stringent selection criterion of ipTM > 8, four proteins were shortlisted for detailed interaction and functional analyses. These included two tail proteins, designated TPCARΦ38 and TPCARΦ53, one tail sheath protein designated TSCARΦ53, and a spike protein designated SPCARΦ58. The selection of these high-confidence proteins provided a robust framework for subsequent molecular docking and interaction studies aimed at elucidating the mechanistic basis of phage adsorption and host specificity in MRSA.

Phage–host protein interaction (docking) analysis

Molecular docking simulations were performed using the MEGADOK platform to predict protein–protein binding affinities and interaction precision. Docking analysis of tarS with phage tail proteins revealed strong binding affinities, with interaction energies ranging from 9.0 to 13.6 kcal/mol and prediction precision between 50% and 80%. Comparable interaction strengths were observed between tarL and phage tail proteins, with binding energies ranging from 8.5 to 14.9 kcal/mol at similar precision levels.

Furthermore, docking of tagO with the phage tail proteins revealed even higher binding energies, ranging from 8.0 to 14.1 kJ/mol, suggesting a potentially robust interaction interface. Likewise, tagG also showed significant binding affinities, with energies ranging from 9.0 to 12.7 and a prediction precision of 50–80%.

Overall, these docking results indicate strong, specific molecular interactions between S. aureus receptor-binding proteins and phage structural proteins, reinforcing their probable roles in phage adsorption and host recognition. Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

Backbone RMS deviations were analyzed for tarS–TPCAR38 and tarL–TPCAR53 complexes over a 100 ns MD simulation (Fig. 10a). The tarL–TPCAR53 complex exhibited an average RMSD of 0.51 ± 0.07 nm, with initial fluctuations observed during the first 25 ns, followed by stable convergence for the remainder of the simulation. In contrast, tarS–TPCAR38 exhibited a slightly higher RMSD of 0.54 ± 0.13 nm and displayed persistent fluctuations at ~ 25 ns intervals, indicating reduced structural stability. RMSF analysis revealed distinct residue-level flexibility patterns (Fig. 10b). The tarS–TPCAR38 complex showed higher flexibility with a mean RMSF of 0.33 ± 0.12 nm, whereas tarL–TPCAR53 exhibited lower fluctuations (0.19 ± 0.11 nm), suggesting stronger backbone stabilization upon ligand binding. Intermolecular hydrogen bonds were monitored using a 0.35 nm cutoff over the 100 ns trajectory (Fig. 10c). The tarL–TPCAR53 complex maintained a consistently higher number of hydrogen bonds, averaging ~ 12 H-bonds, reflecting stable and persistent interactions. In comparison, tarS–TPCAR38 formed slightly fewer hydrogen bonds (~ 11 H-bonds) with broader temporal fluctuations, indicating comparatively weaker and more transient intermolecular interactions.

The radius of gyration (Rg) was analyzed to assess compactness and structural stability over the 100 ns simulation (Fig. 10d). The tarS–TPCAR38 complex showed a higher average Rg of 2.99 ± 0.15 nm, indicating an expanded and flexible conformation with noticeable early fluctuations followed by transient compaction and expansion events. In contrast, the tarL–TPCAR53 complex maintained a lower and more stable Rg (2.70 ± 0.02 nm) throughout the simulation, reflecting a compact structure with minimal deviations. Overall, the lower Rg and reduced fluctuations of tarL–TPCAR53 indicate greater structural stability, while the higher Rg of tarS–TPCAR38 suggests enhanced flexibility, consistent with RMSF and hydrogen bond results. SASA analysis further supported differences in compactness between the complexes (Fig. 10e). The tarS–TPCAR38 complex exhibited a higher average SASA of 298.2 ± 4.99 nm2, indicating greater solvent exposure and dynamic surface rearrangements. Conversely, tarL–TPCAR53 showed a lower mean SASA of 289.3 ± 5.81 nm2 with smaller fluctuations, suggesting tighter packing and reduced solvent accessibility. These observations align with the Rg analysis, confirming higher flexibility of tarS–TPCAR38 and enhanced stability of tarL–TPCAR53. PCA revealed distinct conformational sampling patterns for the two complexes (Fig. 10f). The tarS–TPCAR38 complex sampled a broader conformational space (PC1: − 15 to + 25 nm, PC3: − 6 to + 6 nm), indicating large-scale collective motions and higher flexibility. In contrast, tarL–TPCAR53 displayed a more confined distribution (PC1: − 8 to + 8 nm, PC3: − 7 to + 7 nm), reflecting restricted motions and dynamic stability. Phylogenomic relationships of isolated phages

The VICTOR phylogenomic analysis revealed that the five isolated phages (CARPHAGE0029, CARPHAGE0038, CARPHAGE0046, CARPHAGE0053, and CARPHAGE0058) formed a distinct and well-defined cluster within the group of Staphylococcus phages. The isolates clustered closely together, indicating a high degree of genomic relatedness among them.

The phage cluster was positioned in close proximity to Staphylococcus phage Koomba kaat 1 (OP263969), suggesting a common evolutionary lineage. The isolates were clearly separated from other reference phages, including Staphylococcus phage SAP24 (PZ051232), vB_Sau_RP15_TrD7 (PX943855), SAP15 (PX872122), pelagios (PX842708), and vB_SauM_V1SA20/V1SA22 (ON814135/ON814136), indicating sufficient genomic divergence from these previously described phages.

The analyzed genomes exhibited GC contents of approximately 30% and genome sizes ranging from 120,855 bp to 139,470 bp, which are characteristics typical of Staphylococcus aureus phages belonging to the family Herelleviridae within the class Caudoviricetes.

Discussion

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) remains a significant global public health concern due to its extensive antimicrobial resistance and its capacity to cause severe skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs). In the present study, all five MRSA strains, isolated from diabetic foot infections, exhibited resistance to multiple antibiotics, including penicillin, oxacillin, cefoxitin, ciprofloxacin, daptomycin, linezolid, tigecycline, doxycycline, co-trimoxazole, and levofloxacin. The increasing prevalence of such multidrug-resistant MRSA strains has markedly limited the effectiveness of conventional therapeutic options, underscoring the urgent need for alternative treatment strategies.

Bacteriophage therapy, which provides highly specific and self-replicating antibacterial activity, has re-emerged as a promising approach for managing antibiotic-resistant infections. However, during the previous century, phage therapy received limited acceptance due to several critical limitations, including the lack of rigorously designed animal studies, insufficient characterization of bacteriophages, dependence on phages with narrow host ranges, contamination of phage preparations with bacterial toxins, the development of phage-resistant bacterial variants, and the absence of whole-genome sequencing to confirm the lack of toxin-encoding or lysogenic genes. Over the past two decades, substantial progress has been made through well-designed animal studies employing thoroughly characterized, toxin-free bacteriophages with broader host ranges. Nevertheless, studies elucidating phage–host interactions, particularly those defining the molecular determinants at the phage binding sites on bacterial hosts, remain limited. In this context, the present study sought to investigate potential mutations at phage adsorption sites on the host by analyzing four cell wall biosynthesis genes known to function as receptors for bacteriophage attachment. The current study analysed four Staphylococcus aureus cell wall biosynthesis genes (tarS, tarL, tagO, and tagG) that are important receptors for bacteriophage adsorption. The study analyzed 25 years of S. aureus whole-genome sequences from NCBI and clinical MRSA isolates from S. S. Hospital, Davangere. The study found limited but conserved genetic variation among these loci, underscoring their significance in bacterial physiology and phage-host interactions. Phage-host receptor diversity and evolution represent a dynamic and critical aspect of bacteriophage biology, directly influencing host range, therapeutic efficacy, and bacterial resistance mechanisms38. The ribitol-containing wall teichoic acid in Tetragenococcus halophilus has been identified as a key binding receptor for bacteriophage phiWJ7 10. In S. aureus, WTAs are crucial cell wall glycopolymers whose structural diversity and glycosylation patterns dictate susceptibility to phages39.

The genes linked to wall teichoic acid (WTA), including tarS and tarL, function as glycosyltransferases responsible for WTA modification, whereas tagO and tagG contribute to the construction of the WTA structural framework. Such modifications influence bacteriophage attachment due to alterations in surface electrical properties and receptor availability40. The modest degree of polymorphism observed in this investigation, especially within the terminal regions of tagO and tarS, suggests adaptive refinement rather than disruptive genetic diversification. Previous studies have reported comparable patterns of restricted variation, wherein receptor preservation maintains bacterial cell wall structural stability while allowing for limited diversity to evade the immune system41.

The predominance of transition mutations (A↔G, C↔T) relative to transversions aligns with the characteristic mutational patterns documented in bacterial housekeeping genes subjected to purifying selection pressure42. Here, Synonymous changes or changes that do not affect protein function are preferred over time43. This structural conservation is particularly evident in the receptor-binding proteins (RBPs), which must maintain a high affinity for host Wall Teichoic Acids (WTA) to ensure successful adsorption, a stabilizing trend also documented in staphylococcal Twort-like phages7. The absence of lysogenic or pathogenicity gene markers in the analyzed phages additionally demonstrates that these phages have established a restricted yet stable host specificity, with minimal potential for horizontal genetic transfer, while ensuring the maintenance of infection efficacy through successive generations44.

The phage adsorption frequently involves a two-step process comprising reversible attachment to primary receptors followed by irreversible binding to secondary receptors, ultimately leading to genome injection. Although the present study identified sequence diversity among RBPs, future functional studies involving adsorption assays, receptor mapping, and structural interaction analyses will be required to determine whether specific RBPs participate predominantly in reversible receptor recognition or irreversible attachment processes during phage infection.

Phylogenomic analysis using VICTOR demonstrated that all five phage isolates belong to a closely related evolutionary lineage and likely represent members of the same species complex or a highly related group of species. The tight clustering of CARPHAGE0029, CARPHAGE0038, CARPHAGE0046, CARPHAGE0053, and CARPHAGE0058 suggests conservation of core genomic architecture and functional genes involved in replication, host recognition, and lytic activity.

The closest relationship of the isolates with Staphylococcus phage Koomba kaat 1 indicates that these phages may belong to a lineage of virulent staphylococcal phages that have evolved from a common ancestor. However, the formation of an independent sub-cluster distinct from previously characterized phages suggests the presence of unique genomic features that may contribute to differences in host range, receptor recognition, or infection dynamics.

The observed genome sizes (120–139 kb) and low GC content (~ 30%) are consistent with those reported for therapeutic staphylococcal myophages, supporting their classification within the group of large lytic Staphylococcus phages. Such phages are often considered promising candidates for phage therapy because of their broad host range, strictly lytic lifestyle, and reduced likelihood of carrying undesirable lysogenic genes.

The phylogenomic distinctiveness of the CARPHAGE isolates highlights their potential novelty and supports further comparative genomic analyses to identify unique genes, receptor-binding proteins, and host adaptation mechanisms that may enhance their therapeutic applicability against multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infections.

Implications for phage therapy and resistance

Phage attachment to bacterial surfaces is the first and most important step in successful infection; thus, the molecular compatibility of phage receptor-binding proteins (RBPs) and host cell wall components is critical in determining host range and therapeutic efficacy45. Molecular docking analyses of S. aureus receptor proteins (tarS, tarL, tagO, tagG) and phage-derived RBPs (TPCARΦ38, TPCARΦ53, TSCARΦ53, and SPCARΦ58) revealed stable binding interactions with energy values between 8 and 14 kcal/mol, indicating the phage’s strong affinity for receptors. These findings reinforce prior research demonstrating that phage tail and spike proteins have gained sensitivity for conserved teichoic acid motifs46,47.

Given the restricted genetic diversity within receptor genes, mutations that confer phage resistance through receptor deletion or alteration may incur a high fitness cost due to their critical metabolic functions48. Phage therapy can benefit from targeting conserved and important receptors, which are less susceptible to fast bacterial escape mutations. Previous research has shown that mutations in tagO or tarS can provide resistance to certain siphoviruses, but also reduce cell wall integrity and pathogenicity49.

Consequently, the identified phages (CARΦ29, CARΦ38, CARΦ46, CARΦ53, and CARΦ58) targeting conserved WTA-related receptors are strong therapeutic prospects due to their persistent receptor-binding contacts and functional importance to bacterial physiology. Combining receptor mapping and genomic surveillance can enhance phage cocktail design, ensuring that treatment formulations are durable against emerging MRSA strains50.

Evolutionary perspective

S. aureus WTA gene conservation and phage adaptation form a co-evolutionary equilibrium. Phages use RBPs to exploit stable structural motifs in bacterial receptors, whereas bacteria have limited receptor mutability due to evolutionary restrictions. This dynamic equilibrium supports the “evolutionary arms balance” hypothesis, which proposes that host-phage systems maintain long-term coexistence through limited diversity rather than a continual arms race51. These findings can help forecast phage-host compatibility, devise stable therapeutic formulations, and prevent resistance in clinical settings.

To conclude, in the present study, the overall mutation landscape of tarS, tarL, tagO, and tagG demonstrates limited polymorphism, primarily arising from isolated point mutations with no evidence of insertions, deletions, or functionally disruptive changes, thereby indicating the genetic stability of these receptor-binding genes across the analyzed S. aureus isolates. This also indicates an evolutionary stability that is essential for the biosynthesis of wall teichoic acid and the recognition of phages. The phages characterized displayed pronounced, specific, and non-hazardous lytic properties, devoid of antibiotic resistance or toxin-encoding genes, thereby accentuating their potential therapeutic application against multidrug-resistant S. aureus strains.

Limitations

A limitation of the present study is that the phage-host interaction analysis is primarily supported by in silico molecular docking, and direct biochemical validation of individual receptor binding protein interactions was not performed. Although docking predictions were reinforced by concordant genomic evidence, phage adsorption efficiency, plaque morphology, and efficiency of plating data, quantitative binding assays such as surface plasmon resonance, pull-down assays, or validation using isogenic receptor-deficient mutants (e.g., ΔtarS) were beyond the scope of this study. Future investigations incorporating these approaches will be valuable to further refine binding kinetics and confirm receptor specificity at the molecular level. Nonetheless, the integrated genomic, structural, and phenotypic evidence presented here provides a robust framework supporting the biological relevance of the observed phage-host interactions.

The characterization of phage-resistant mutants and their genomic analysis would provide direct experimental validation of the in silico predictions regarding receptor-associated mutations. Further research involving the generation and whole-genome sequencing of phage-resistant S. aureus mutants will be valuable to experimentally validate the receptor-associated mutations predicted through comparative genomic analyses and to elucidate the genetic basis of phage resistance.
