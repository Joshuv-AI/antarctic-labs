A skin-conformal rigid-in-soft array-based imaging system
Authors: Xiaoyun Yuan, Jianing Zhang, Youhai Liu, Yichen Wang, Lu Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Array-based imaging systems offer unparalleled capabilities for mapping complex environments, but their inherent structural rigidity prevents intimate integration with dynamic, non-rigid hosts such as human skin and soft robots. While flexible visual sensor arrays offer compliance, fabrication constraints severely limit their pixel density and overall resolution. Here, we present SkinSight, a skin-conformal imaging system that overcomes this trade-off through a hybrid “rigid-in-soft” architecture. By integrating miniaturized imagers with stretchable interconnects, SkinSight achieves stable, high-resolution imaging under complex mechanical deformations. Furthermore, to resolve processing challenges induced by continuous deformation, we leverage large-scale pre-trained vision transformers for end-to-end geometric inference. This deformation-adaptive framework no longer relies on rigid structural priors, enabling robust, calibration-free 3D visual perception. We demonstrate seamless integration with human skin, transforming a human palm into a sensory interface capable of perceiving objects beyond the direct line of sight. By augmenting the perceptual capabilities of humans and robots, SkinSight demonstrates broad potential for next-generation wearables and embodied artificial intelligence.

Introduction

Array-based imaging systems excel at mapping complex environments, making them essential for modern platforms like smartphones and autonomous vehicles1–5. Their exceptional performance fundamentally relies on a spatially distributed architecture, which establishes the large effective aperture and extended baseline required for advanced visual tasks. However, maintaining this spatial distribution demands structural rigidity, thereby preventing integration with dynamic, non-rigid hosts such as humans and soft robots. While individual camera modules can undergo miniaturization, the overall array cannot. Shrinking the overall footprint inherently collapses the system into a single viewpoint, eliminating the core advantages of multi-aperture sensing. Consequently, the requirement to maintain a large physical span makes structural rigidity a fundamental barrier to their intimate integration with deformable hosts.

Driven by the need for conformal integration, recent research has explored intrinsically flexible vision sensors based on advanced fabrication techniques or novel materials, including serpentine6 or compressible copper wires7–9, nanowire composites10,11, kirigami-12 or origami-inspired13 silicon structures, and materials like quantum dots and perovskites14–17. However, these systems face severe fabrication bottlenecks. Mechanical and electrical limitations restrict the ability to shrink individual pixel sizes while simultaneously scaling up the total pixel count. Consequently, these arrays suffer from low pixel counts, rendering them inadequate for advanced visual tasks that typically demand megapixel-level fidelity. Conversely, while mature semiconductor technologies yield densely integrated and high-performance arrays, fabrication constraints inherently confine them to planar inflexible platforms. While unstructured camera arrays have been developed to introduce internal spatial adaptability, their design purpose differs fundamentally from conforming to dynamic hosts2–5,18. These systems merely reconfigure camera orientations within a rigid supporting body. This continued reliance on a rigid backbone prevents their intimate integration with non-rigid surfaces.

To overcome these limitations, we present SkinSight, a skin-conformal imaging system built upon a hybrid “rigid-in-soft” architecture. This design physically decouples optical sensing from mechanical strain by utilizing rigid components to protect high-density sensor arrays and a soft matrix to absorb overall deformation. A key advantage of this format is its ability to provide high-resolution vision without altering the host’s internal structure or compromising its inherent mechanical properties, thereby enabling a non-invasive sensory augmentation that is highly adaptable to diverse dynamic surfaces, from human skin to soft robotic platforms. Specifically, we integrate miniaturized commercial CMOS imagers as high-fidelity rigid nodes onto a flexible substrate. To stably transmit high-throughput image signals under continuous motion, we employ stretchable serpentine interconnects designed to accommodate complex deformations, such as bending, twisting, and stretching. Fundamentally, SkinSight functions as a skin-conformal unstructured camera array. By liberating this array architecture from traditional rigid backbones, our system effectively bridges the critical gap between high-resolution machine vision and mechanically compliant bio-integrated electronics.

While this rigid-in-soft architecture solves the physical integration problem, it introduces a major computational challenge. As the array conforms to dynamic hosts, the relative poses of individual cameras undergo continuous variations. To address this issue, we leverage recent breakthroughs in large-scale pretrained vision transformers to develop a deformation-adaptive computational framework. This approach enables the fusion of multi-perspective information without explicit spatial calibration, effectively liberating array-based imaging from the requirement of rigid structural priors. By enabling robust 3D visual perception from continuously varying inputs, SkinSight overcomes the fundamental limitations of static imaging geometries.

We demonstrate the practical utility of SkinSight through its seamless integration with complex dynamic hosts, including human skin and soft robots. In particular, attaching the system to the human palm transforms the hand into an active sensory interface, empowering users to perceive and reconstruct objects beyond their direct line of sight. By evolving array-based imaging from rigid platforms to flexible skin-conformal formats through AI-driven data fusion, SkinSight demonstrates broad potential for transformative applications ranging from next-generation wearables to embodied artificial intelligence.

Results

Architecture of the skin-conformal imaging system

Compared to existing pixel-level stretchable imagers, SkinSight (Fig. 1c) utilizes a hybrid “rigid-in-soft” architecture that shifts the fundamental sensing node from individual pixels to integrated imager modules. In this paradigm, the pixel matrix remains rigid within each imager, while the overall mechanical flexibility of the system is provided by the stretchable serpentine interconnects routing across the soft substrate. Each node is a CMOS sensor with a compact footprint of approximately 1× 11×1 mm² and a pixel count of 160 K. In comparison, individual pixels in existing stretchable imagers often span hundreds of micrometers due to fabrication limits, resulting in low total pixel counts (typically <1 K). Consequently, each node in SkinSight only occupies a rigid footprint comparable to only a small cluster of these pixels, imposing minimal mechanical constraints on the overall deformable substrate. This architecture also leads to a highly efficient signal readout strategy. Existing pixel-level stretchable imagers adopt row-column matrix data addressing, demanding a massive number of readout ports (Supplementary Fig. S4). In SkinSight, the CMOS sensor in each node inherently integrates pixel matrix addressing and data serialization, thus requiring only four essential ports (VDD, GND, CLK, and VOUT) per node to continuously transmit 160 K pixel signals. This significantly reduces the overall complexity, allowing the system to be flexibly configured with 1 to over 10 imager nodes to adapt to specific task requirements.

The physical integration of these nodes utilizes a layered structure designed for mechanical robustness (Fig. 1c). In this configuration, a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) film serves as the elastic substrate to fully encapsulate the stretchable interconnects. To ensure an unobstructed optical path, a 1× 11×1 mm2 opening is precisely patterned in the top PDMS layer to expose the CMOS sensor module. To further minimize the node profile, a thin lens is integrated onto each sensor module. These underlying interconnects comprise laser-patterned serpentine copper wires on a polyimide (PI) substrate. This specific geometry, coupled with the PDMS encapsulation, effectively distributes mechanical stress during deformation. As demonstrated in Fig. 1d, this integrated architecture ensures that the optical nodes remain functionally isolated from the strains of bending, twisting, and stretching. This architecture physically decouples the imaging core from macroscopic strain by encapsulating it within a zero-strain rigid island. Consequently, the intrinsic image quality remains fundamentally unaffected by any external dynamic deformation.

With optical imaging physically decoupled from macroscopic strain by the rigid-in-soft architecture, our characterizations focus on signal transmission reliability. Furthermore, because our encoding-decoding scheme intrinsically tolerates baseline electrical noise, the decoded image fidelity (pixel-level SNR) serves as the definitive metric for validating transmission robustness (see Supplementary Fig. S1).

To experimentally validate the system’s signal transmission stability, a single SkinSight node is evaluated using a custom-built cyclic loading test platform (Fig. 2e). The setup undergoes continuous dynamic deformation across three mechanical states: a compressed state (6.4 cm), the initial flat state (9.2 cm), and a stretched state (11.6 cm). Operating at a loading rate of one cycle per minute and capturing at 30 frames per second (fps), each mechanical cycle consists of 1800 frames. During this dynamic process, the node continuously captures a video stream of a uniform whiteboard (Supplementary Fig. S1). To quantify the SNR, we first extract and analyze the temporal intensity of randomly sampled pixels. Although imaging the uniform white board should yield constant pixel intensities, the relative spatial shifts induced by the macroscopic motion inevitably cause low-frequency baseline drifts in the raw signal. Therefore, a moving average filter with a window size of 200 frames (approx. 1/9 of a cycle) is applied to the temporal sequences (Fig. 2f). This isolates the signal drift (red dashed curve) without distorting its macroscopic envelope, allowing us to strictly extract the true intrinsic noise (the high-frequency residual, green curve) for the final SNR calculation (38.67 dB). Furthermore, fatigue life characterization (Fig. 2g) demonstrates that the interconnects remain stable under repeated mechanical stress. Every 100 cycles, the system pauses at a fixed position to capture 200 static frames for static SNR calculation. The result exhibits a remarkably stable SNR (42.83 dB) even after 500 stretch-compress cycles, confirming the capability of the interconnects to sustain image signal transmission under mechanical fatigue (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Movie 1). To further assess the electrical reliability, we continuously monitor the resistance variation of the interconnects throughout the cyclic loading process. The resistance remains within a negligible range (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Movie 2). These combined results conclusively validate the robust performance of SkinSight under repetitive mechanical deformations.

Deformation-adaptive reconstruction

To reconstruct the spatiotemporal environment from the highly deformable SkinSight system, we propose an asynchronous reconstruction paradigm driven by visual geometry foundation models23–27 (Fig. 3a). To handle the continuous topological deformations, our paradigm decouples the reconstruction process into an online reconstruction pathway and an asynchronous Gaussian optimization pathway, coupled with a rendering pathway for visual readout. The input undergoes temporal chunking via a sliding window, yielding overlapping chunks. For each chunk, we employ the π3 geometry foundation model28 to back-project 2D pixels into dense 3D point maps, and predict camera view poses without pre-calibrated camera poses. The relative rotation, translation, and scaling between adjacent chunks are analytically solved using a least-squares formulation based on the overlapping poses and point maps. While the online reconstruction pathway rapidly establishes the 3D point map, direct point-based representations and rendering lack fidelity. Therefore, the Gaussian optimization pathway concurrently performs 3D Gaussian splatting optimization in the backend using uniformly sampled frames29,30. We bypass feed-forward Gaussian architectures27, as their current methods lack the robustness required by SkinSight (Supplementary Fig. S8). Lastly, a real-time rendering process transforms these optimized geometric representations (point map or Gaussian) into the final rendered view for perceptual feedback.Fig. 3Deformation-adaptive reconstruction.a Schematic of the asynchronous computational architecture. As explicitly compared in Fig. 3b, while the online pathway successfully extracts dense and metrically accurate point maps, these point-based representations remain inherently discrete and visually fragmented. As highlighted by the zoomed-in crops, the asynchronous Gaussian optimization effectively addresses this limitation by filling the spatial voids inherent to the raw point map, restoring continuous surfaces and fine textural details. This transformation into a continuous, high-fidelity 3D representation significantly enhances intuitive visual immersion. Furthermore, our approach demonstrates robust superiority over existing baselines (see Supplementary Fig. S6, S7, and S8). Conventional pipelines like COLMAP31,32 are computationally slow and typically yield sparse, noisy point clouds. While recent geometry models like InstantSplat33 also utilize geometric foundation models for one-time global reconstruction, they function as static global estimators that lack the capacity for online updates, rendering them structurally unsuited for continuous video streams.

Since our method relies purely on visual streams from the uncalibrated array, the reconstructed geometry inherently contains a scale ambiguity, capturing only relative dimensions. To achieve absolute metric recovery, a universal physical reference is required. This metric scale factor can be obtained using auxiliary depth sensors, IMUs, or an object with known physical dimensions (see Methods). To quantitatively evaluate the dimensional accuracy, we reconstructed a complex desktop scene comprising various household objects (Fig. 3c). By anchoring the metric scale to the length of the red rectangular box, we extracted the physical dimensions of the remaining objects utilizing 3D bounding boxes from the reconstructed point maps. Crucially, this asynchronous paradigm is intrinsically capable of reconstructing dynamic environments containing moving targets. The online reconstruction pathway can dynamically prune obsolete chunks and recursively integrate newly observed chunks, ensuring the temporal coherence of the reconstructed scene. To rigorously validate this capability, we conducted an experiment tracking a target (a sunflower ornament) translating on a motorized conveyor belt (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Movie 3). Specifically, we tracked the target within the reconstructed scene to pinpoint its absolute 3D positions34. By applying a least-squares linear regression to the 3D spatial displacements over time, we directly quantified the target’s physical velocity. The visually derived average velocity is 1.669 cm/s, which exhibits a remarkable consistency with the programmed mechanical speed of 1.666 cm/s, achieving a highly accurate dynamic measurement.

Augmented vision via skin-conformal system integration

The SkinSight system establishes a versatile visual interface for human vision augmentation, transforming deformable surfaces into active sensory extensions. We demonstrated this paradigm by integrating the SkinSight onto a human palm to navigate narrow, occluded environments (Fig. 4a). In this scenario, both the human eye and the rigid headset camera are physically blocked by the opaque box, permitting only a severely restricted view of the internal space through a narrow window. Conversely, the flexible SkinSight-enabled hand can be inserted into the box through the narrow window, bypassing these physical obstructions to comprehensively capture the internal scene. To grant the user a functional see-through perception, we implement spatial registration between the SkinSight coordinates and the headset coordinate system, enabling the dynamic overlay of reconstructed entities directly onto the headset’s camera view (Fig. 4b). The resulting AR visualization explicitly validates the performance of our dual-pathway architecture. As demonstrated in the top row of Fig. 4d, the registered 3D point maps provide fast, geometrically accurate feedback that effectively localizes hidden objects within the user’s field-of-view. However, as further highlighted by the zoomed-in crops, these point-based representations suffer from visual fragmentation and blurring artifacts. The asynchronous Gaussian optimization (bottom row) directly addresses this by filling spatial gaps and restoring photorealistic surface textures. This transition from discrete points to continuous, high-fidelity Gaussian representations is essential for creating a naturalistic sense of visual immersion, ensuring that the augmented overlay is not only metrically precise but also perceptually intuitive. Crucially, both pathways achieve accurate spatial alignment with the physical objects (e.g., the red box) partially visible through the narrow window, confirming the reliability of our cross-platform coordinate registration. Supplementary Movie 4 demonstrates this functional see-through capability through a complete pipeline of coordinate alignment, reconstruction, and rendering.

Beyond human augmentation, SkinSight demonstrates significant utility in soft robotics, where rigid visual sensors are often mechanically incompatible. When integrated onto a soft gripper (Supplementary Fig. S11, S17, S18), the conformal SkinSight array maintains robust, blind-spot-free visual sensing, whereas a commercial RGB-depth camera suffers from severe occlusions. This comparison demonstrates that our flexible system offers significant advantages in constrained environments. In summary, by combining an ultra-flexible hardware design with AI-empowered reconstruction, SkinSight successfully transforms highly deformable surfaces into intelligent sensory interfaces. This versatile platform provides unprecedented visual perception capabilities for next-generation human-computer interaction and embodied artificial intelligence.

Discussion

We introduce SkinSight, a skin-conformal artificial vision system that combines high pixel resolution with mechanical flexibility. Unlike existing flexible vision systems that suffer from low total pixel counts, SkinSight achieves a substantial leap in resolution through a hybrid rigid-in-soft architecture. This massive increase in total pixel number (160 K pixels per node) enables the capture of high-resolution visual data necessary for advanced machine vision tasks. Furthermore, compared to existing unstructured camera arrays, SkinSight revolutionizes the physical form factor of visual sensors. By transitioning from bulky hardware to thin and wearable formats, the system empowers dynamic hosts, such as human hands, into sensory extensions to perform high-dimensional visual perception in severely restricted environments.

The practical deployment of SkinSight hinges on three key engineering characteristics: integration limits, fabrication yield, and failure modes. First, regarding integration limits, the system achieves good conformability through the moderate stretchability of the serpentine interconnects between the rigid sensor islands. This design prioritizes high pixel counts and massive data throughput while maintaining sufficient flexibility for dynamic hosts, offering a highly practical balance for advanced visual tasks. Second, in terms of fabrication yield, SkinSight leverages mature industrial processes, including flexible printed circuits (FPC) and automated surface-mount technology (SMT). This approach ensures highly reproducible fabrication, yielding robust electrical connectivity and reliable encapsulation without the inconsistency of manual lab-scale assembly. Third, concerning failure modes, the ultra-miniature size of the imagers inherently restricts the soldering area at the rigid-flex interface, making these connection points the primary mechanical weak link compared to the highly flexible serpentine wires. Our current encapsulation strategy effectively protects these connections to ensure stable data transmission. Crucially, as validated by our cyclic loading evaluations, the system maintains intact functionality without any observable solder joint delamination even after hundreds of stretch cycles (Fig. 2). Future optimizations of these specific interfaces will further enhance the system’s long-term resilience under extreme mechanical stress.

As summarized in Table 2, SkinSight combines the high-resolution capabilities of unstructured camera arrays with the mechanical flexibility of intrinsically flexible artificial vision. Unstructured camera arrays rely on bulky data readout interfaces and rigid assemblies, limiting their mechanical conformability. Conversely, intrinsically flexible vision systems excel in extreme deformation but suffer from severe resolution bottlenecks. This limitation stems from both fabrication and data readout constraints. Lab-scale micro-transfer printing techniques struggle with the yield and scalability required for massive pixel integration. Because these flexible systems typically employ direct row-column matrix addressing, scaling up the pixel count leads to a significant growth in the number of external wires for data readout (Supplementary Fig. S4). SkinSight circumvents these physical constraints through its rigid-in-soft architecture, by leveraging commercial CMOS sensors for in-node matrix addressing, which enables serial data transmission via 4-wire stretchable interconnects. Because each node operates independently without complex cross-wiring, the total node number of SkinSight is not physically restricted by the readout architecture, making the system modularly scalable to meet various application demands (Supplementary Fig. S5). At the system level, the massive data stream generated by the highly deformable array presents a fundamental computational challenge. To address this, SkinSight leverages recent breakthroughs in geometry foundation models for robust 3D reconstruction and Gaussian splatting for high-fidelity rendering feedback. These AI-driven advancements are crucial for extracting meaningful spatial information from the complex, unanchored data flows of our flexible sensors. As demonstrated by our human hand integration, this technology effectively transforms biological limbs into active sensory extensions, enabling users to comprehensively perceive and navigate environments that are visually inaccessible.

In summary, the flexibility of SkinSight is not merely a physical feature but a transformative enabler. By combining an ultra-flexible array-based imaging system with AI-empowered reconstruction, SkinSight successfully transforms highly deformable surfaces into intelligent visual interfaces. This evolution from passive measurement tools into active and conformal sensory systems establishes a robust foundation for the next generation of human-computer interaction and embodied artificial intelligence.

Methods

Stretchable interconnects fabrication and system integration

The fabrication and integration process involves three primary steps: designing and manufacturing the serpentine stretchable interconnects, soldering the imagers, and encapsulating the system with PDMS. The serpentine interconnects are fabricated using mature flexible printed circuit (FPC) technology. This layout comprises an 18-μm copper layer for power and signal transmission, patterned on a 12.5-μm polyimide substrate. Subsequently, the miniaturized imagers (OV6946, OmniVision Technologies) are soldered onto the designated contact pads at the interconnect terminals. Prior to PDMS bonding, a cavity is punched into the top PDMS film to accommodate the imager housing. The surfaces of both the serpentine interconnects and the PDMS films undergo oxygen plasma treatment to enhance interfacial adhesion. The top and bottom PDMS layers are then thermally bonded to the flexible circuit under pressure and cured at 80 °C for 6 hours. Finally, additional PDMS is cast around the imager base to reinforce local structural integrity (Supplementary Fig. S14). The image data from the miniaturized sensors is transmitted through the serpentine interconnects and processed by ADC chips (OV426, OmniVision Technologies).

The primary manufacturing bottleneck of the system involves reliably soldering the ultra-miniature imagers at the rigid-flex interface. This challenge is successfully overcome by designing customized alignment fixtures and employing precision automated SMT reflow soldering to standardize the connections. Beyond this critical integration step, the remaining fabrication procedures are highly straightforward and reproducible. The stretchable interconnects leverage mature industrial FPC processes to provide a highly reliable electrical baseline, and the final PDMS encapsulation is completed through a simple casting and bonding process. Consequently, the entire system assembly achieves exceptional reproducibility and a high overall yield without demanding highly complex laboratory techniques.

Electromechanical cyclic loading and optoelectronic stability characterization

The dynamic mechanical evaluations detailed in the Results section are conducted using a custom-built motorized linear stage platform (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S1). The platform is driven by a programmable stepper motor to ensure precise control over the applied strain and continuous displacement, operating at a rate of approximately one complete stretch-release-compress cycle per minute. For the dynamic signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) quantification, the SkinSight node captures the uniform white target under constant illumination at a frame rate of 30 frames per second (fps).

To isolate the intrinsic sensor noise from the macroscopic motion artifacts, data processing is performed using custom analytical scripts. The raw temporal intensity vector for each pixel is first extracted. The low-frequency baseline drift, which arises from the relative translation between the sensor and the target surface, is mathematically estimated by convolving the raw signal with a 200-frame moving average filter. This calculated baseline is then subtracted from the raw data to strictly isolate the high-frequency residual noise. The temporal SNR is subsequently computed as the ratio of the mean raw signal intensity to the standard deviation of this high-frequency residual noise.

For the fatigue life characterization, the automated protocol programs the linear stage to execute 500 continuous stretch-compress cycles. The system is designed to automatically pause the motion every 100 cycles, holding the substrate at the flat state to capture 200 static frames. Since the substrate remains completely stationary during these captures, motion-induced baseline drift is inherently eliminated. Consequently, the static SNR is directly computed using the raw mean and standard deviation of these frames without any moving average filtering.

Image restoration and semantic segmentation

For image restoration, we leverage established neural network architectures to accommodate specific application requirements. A lightweight U-Net is sufficient to provide fast inference for recovering high-quality, aberration-free images. Furthermore, a diffusion-based generative network35–37 can be optionally incorporated to further elevate the reconstruction quality. LoRA-based fine-tuning38 is adopted for parameter-efficient adaptation. The optimization process minimizes a joint objective comprising Mean Squared Error (MSE) and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS)39 losses, ensuring both pixel-wise metric accuracy and perceptual visual quality. Finally, for downstream scene understanding, semantic segmentation is performed utilizing the mature Grounded-DINO40 framework in a zero-shot manner, requiring no additional training or modifications. Further algorithmic details and extended evaluations are provided in Supplementary Note 2.
