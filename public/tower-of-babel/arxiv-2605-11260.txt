Curriculum Learning-Guided Progressive Distillation in Large Language Models
Jincheng Cao, Fanzhi Zeng, Leqi Liu, Aryan Mokhtari

Abstract
Knowledge distillation is a key technique for transferring the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) into smaller, more efficient student models. Existing distillation approaches often overlook two critical factors: the learning order of training data and the capacity mismatch between teacher and student models. This oversight limits distillation performance, as manifested by the counter-intuitive phenomenon where stronger teachers fail to produce better students. In this work, we propose Curriculum Learning–Guided Progressive Distillation (CLPD), a unified framework that explicitly accounts for both factors by aligning data difficulty with teacher strength. CLPD constructs an explicit curriculum by organizing training examples from easy to hard, while simultaneously applying an implicit curriculum over supervision signals by progressively scheduling teachers of increasing capacity. Our framework is modular and can be integrated into standard distillation algorithms with minimal overhead. Empirical results on the reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that CLPD consistently outperforms standard distillation, data ordering alone, and teacher scheduling alone across multiple settings. These findings highlight the importance of jointly considering data ordering and teacher capacity when distilling reasoning abilities into small language models.

Introduction

Knowledge distillation (KD)
12
, a widely used model compression paradigm in deep learning, addresses this challenge by transferring knowledge from a high-capacity teacher model to a smaller student model. As LLMs have advanced, KD has become a central strategy for building efficient small language models that preserve much of the teacher’s capability. This trend is evident in recent systems such as Llama 3.2
23
, Gemma-2
30
, and the Deepseek-R1 distilled models
5
, which explicitly rely on distillation to balance quality and efficiency.

In the LLM era, KD methods are often grouped into two broad families: white-box KD and black-box KD. White-box distillation refers to settings where the student has access to the teacher’s internal probability distributions or hidden states. Standard white-box methods align the teacher and student by matching their output distributions, often using Kullback–Leibler divergence (KLD)
26
;
8
or by aligning their intermediate representations
28
;
17
;
9
. Recent studies
8
;
1
in white-box distillation highlight the importance of on-policy learning, where the student learns from its own generated responses rather than solely imitating the teacher’s outputs. These studies show that performing reverse KLD on student-generated text promotes mode-seeking behavior and reduces exposure bias compared to
teacher-forced training. However, white-box access is often impractical when the teacher is a proprietary API model (e.g., GPT-5). In this scenario, only teacher-generated texts are accessible, defining the more challenging black-box distillation setting. Black-box KD transfers knowledge through the teacher’s outputs
19
and is widely adopted because many LLMs, such as GPT-4o
14
, Claude 3.5
2
, and Gemini 1.5
29
, are proprietary and expose only prediction interfaces.

Despite these advances, most schemes pay limited attention to how training data and supervision are organized during distillation. In practice, poorly matched combinations of loss functions and data selection strategies can hinder student learning, especially when there is a substantial capacity gap between teacher and student models. Most KD methods treat teacher choice as fixed and training examples as i.i.d., rather than coupling teacher capability with example difficulty. Only a small number of works, such as
35
;
22
, explicitly address aspects of this mismatch through modified supervision or curriculum-based training strategies during distillation.
Curriculum Learning (CL) exposes models to progressively more complex training examples and has been shown to improve optimization and generalization
34
;
32
. Recently, CL has been applied to LLM distillation
22
; however, existing approaches typically focus on ordering training data while keeping the teacher fixed, leaving the interaction between data difficulty and teacher capability unexplored.

One curiously persistent phenomenon is that a better teacher does not always yield a stronger student. Prior works
24
;
15
;
10
;
37
hypothesized that this arises from a capability gap
38
;
18
between teacher and student, and proposed progressive distillation, where the student is incrementally supervised by increasingly capable teachers. However, these approaches typically schedule teachers independently of the difficulty of training examples, leaving the interaction between teacher strength and data complexity largely unexplored.

Contributions.

In this paper, we argue that effective LLM distillation requires aligning what the student learns (data difficulty) with who teaches it (teacher capability). Inspired by human learning, where students first master fundamentals under simpler instruction before advancing to harder material, we propose Curriculum Learning–Guided Progressive Distillation (CLPD): an approach that gradually increases both training difficulty and teacher strength, reducing mismatch in the early stages while still providing high-quality guidance on the hardest examples.

Concretely, CLPD constructs a difficulty-ordered curriculum using CoT-derived complexity when available or student loss otherwise, and couples it with a teacher schedule that progresses from weaker to stronger models across curriculum stages. The method can be integrated seamlessly with the standard LLM distillation. Empirically, CLPD consistently outperforms strong distillation baselines as well as curriculum-only and progressive-only variants across different reasoning datasets. We complement these results with analyses that pinpoint when stronger teachers help and when they hurt, thereby clarifying the necessity of scheduling teachers in accordance with the data difficulty.

Background and Preliminaries

As our goal is to develop a plug-and-play framework for knowledge distillation, we first review common distillation objectives for LLMs, and then discuss two complementary factors that strongly affect distillation performance: the order in which training examples are presented and the capacity gap between teacher and student models.

2.1
Knowledge Distillation

Knowledge distillation was popularized by
12
, where the student model is trained to imitate the teacher’s token-level probability distribution. Let
p_{S}^{\theta}
denote the student distribution and
p_{T}
the teacher distribution. The standard supervised knowledge distillation loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SKD}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SKD}}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim X}\Bigl[\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{T}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,p_{S}^{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\right)\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim X}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{T}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\log\frac{p_{T}(y\mid x)}{p_{S}^{\theta}(y\mid x)}\right]\right],

where the outer expectation is taken over the input distribution and the inner expectation is taken over the teacher’s output distribution. This objective provides a dense training signal by leveraging the full token-level distribution of the teacher.
In contrast to this distribution-matching objective,
19
introduced sequence-level knowledge distillation (SeqKD), which trains the student to maximize the likelihood of high-probability sequences generated by the teacher:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SeqKD}}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim X}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{T}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[-\log p_{S}^{\theta}(y\mid x)\right]\right].

This can be viewed as supervised fine-tuning on teacher-generated outputs; in practice, the outputs
y
are typically obtained via greedy or beam decoding rather than sampling. Sequence-level distillation is one of the most widely used distillation methods for large language models, as it does not require access to teacher logits and is computationally efficient. Vanilla
baseline shown in the last row of Fig.

2.2
Learning Order and Curriculum Learning

A key challenge in KD for LLMs is the training–inference mismatch: student models are typically trained on fixed datasets that do not reflect the diverse distribution of inputs encountered at inference time
8
;
1
. To mitigate this issue, recent on-policy distillation methods incorporate student-generated outputs (SGOs) into training, allowing the student to learn from its own trajectories and reducing the gap between training and deployment. However, SGO usage introduces an inherent trade-off. While moderate SGO integration can improve generalization, excessive reliance on SGOs may degrade performance, as the distribution of noisy student-generated samples can deviate from the teacher’s training distribution and misguide the distillation process
21
;
20
.

An alternative approach to addressing training–inference mismatch is to structure the distillation data in a meaningful learning order, drawing inspiration from curriculum learning. In the context of LLMs, curriculum learning
32
organizes training examples from simple to complex, mirroring human learning processes and enabling more stable optimization. A curriculum may begin with shorter prompts, simpler reasoning chains, or high-quality low-noise data, and gradually progress to longer, more syntactically complex, or noisier samples. Difficulty can be quantified using metrics such as prompt length, model loss, teacher confidence, or reasoning depth. Recent studies
22
;
16
show that curriculum learning accelerates convergence, improves generalization, and enhances performance, particularly for instruction tuning and reasoning-intensive tasks, without increasing model size or dataset scale. However, curriculum learning mainly controls
which examples
the student sees and
when
, and does not address mismatches in the
source
or
difficulty
of supervision when the teacher is much stronger than the student. Applying curriculum learning with a fixed teacher corresponds to the
Curriculum Learning only
baseline shown in the third row of Fig.
1
.

2.3
Capacity Gap and Progressive Distillation

Given the capacity gap between teacher and student models, a stronger teacher does not necessarily yield a better student in knowledge distillation. When the teacher is too powerful, its supervision signal is often high-entropy or encodes nuanced multi-step reasoning that may exceed what a small student model can meaningfully absorb, leading to suboptimal dynamics and degraded student performance. Prior work
24
;
15
;
10
has shown that, for a fixed student capacity, employing a weaker but more student-aligned teacher can be more effective, as such teachers induce distributions and task structures that are easier for the student to mimic.

An additional complication is that the teacher–student relationship is not static. As the student learns, its representations and predictions evolve, so a teacher that is initially well matched may become inadequate later in training. This motivates
progressive distillation

25
;
39
, where supervision is staged by updating the teacher over time to maintain an appropriate difficulty level. The student thus learns first from simpler teachers to build core representations and later from stronger teachers as their capacity increases. A variety of strategies have been proposed for constructing intermediate teachers, including using intermediate checkpoints of a final teacher
25
, pruning or compressing a large teacher to obtain a spectrum of lighter-weight models
39
, or selecting pretrained models of increasing size from the same architecture family
24
. These approaches share the goal of keeping supervision commensurate with student capability over the course of training.

Scheduling multiple teachers over time while treating the training data stream as i.i.d. corresponds to the
Progressive Distillation only
baseline shown in the second row of Fig.
1
. However, existing progressive distillation frameworks typically schedule teachers as a function of training time or student progress, without explicitly accounting for the difficulty of individual training examples. As a result, they do not couple
teacher capability
with
example difficulty
: strong teachers may still be applied to easy instances early on, or weaker teachers to hard instances later in training, reintroducing supervision mismatch. This limitation motivates a unified approach that jointly schedules teachers and examples, aligning the strength of supervision with the difficulty of the data.

3
The Proposed Framework

We now present
Curriculum Learning–Guided Progressive Distillation
(CLPD), a plug-and-play framework for LLM distillation on reasoning tasks. The key premise is that distillation is governed by
two coupled axes
: (i)
which examples
the student learns from and
when
(learning order), and (ii)
who teaches
those examples (teacher capability). Existing approaches typically control one axis while treating the other as fixed, which can leave a persistent supervision mismatch. CLPD resolves this by
jointly
aligning
example difficulty
with
teacher strength
throughout training.

At a high level, CLPD proceeds in two stages. First, we construct an explicit curriculum by ranking training examples from easy to hard using a difficulty estimator. Second, we schedule multiple teachers along this curriculum so that weaker teachers supervise earlier, easier segments, and stronger teachers supervise later, harder segments. This coupling ensures that supervision remains within the student’s effective learning range: the student is not overwhelmed by overly complex signals early on, while still receiving high-quality guidance on the hardest examples. The overall framework is presented in Fig.
1
, and we describe each stage in detail below.

3.1
Curriculum Construction

CLPD begins by imposing structure on the training stream through an explicit easy-to-hard curriculum. The central objective is not simply to reorder data, but to control the
pace
at which reasoning complexity is introduced. By exposing the student to simpler instances first, the model can acquire stable and reusable reasoning primitives before being asked to imitate longer or noisier chains of thought. This prepares the student to benefit from stronger supervision later in training.

We estimate example difficulty using one of two strategies, depending on the supervision signals available. When demonstration chain-of-thought (CoT) solutions are provided, we use their structural complexity as a direct and interpretable proxy for difficulty. Specifically, we rank examples primarily by the number of reasoning steps in the demonstrated solution: problems requiring fewer steps are treated as easier, while those requiring longer reasoning chains are treated as harder. When multiple examples share the same number of steps, we break ties using the total CoT length. This yields a transparent curriculum that closely reflects the depth of reasoning required by each problem.

When CoT demonstrations are not available, we instead estimate difficulty using the student model itself. In this setting, we run the (possibly warm-started) student on each training example and record the loss of its generated solution, treating lower-loss examples as easier and higher-loss examples as harder. Although this approach incurs an additional forward pass over the dataset, it provides a model-driven notion of difficulty that adapts to the student’s current representational capacity and does not rely on expert annotations.

After computing difficulty scores
\{d_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
using either method, we sort the dataset into an ordered sequence
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{curr}}
such that
d_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq d_{(N)}
and train on the data in this easy-to-hard order. In the student-loss setting, we compute difficulty scores using the initial (or warm-started) student and keep the ordering fixed during distillation for simplicity; while the curriculum could be refreshed dynamically, we find that a one-shot ordering is sufficient in practice. Importantly, this curriculum governs
which examples
the student sees and
when
, but it does not specify
who
should supervise different parts of the curriculum. We address this missing dimension in the next subsection by introducing curriculum-aware teacher scheduling.

3.2
Teacher Scheduling

Curriculum learning determines
which examples
the student sees and
when
, but not
who
should supervise different parts of the curriculum. In LLM distillation, this missing dimension is often important: supervision from a very strong teacher can be overly complex early in training (e.g., high-entropy distributions or long reasoning chains), while supervision from a weak teacher can be insufficient on the hardest examples later on. As a result, using a single fixed teacher throughout training can create a persistent mismatch between supervision complexity and the student’s evolving competence, either overwhelming the student early or under-challenging it later.

CLPD addresses this issue by making teacher selection
curriculum-aware
. The key idea is to align
teacher capability
with
example difficulty
so that supervision stays within the student’s effective learning range throughout training. Concretely, suppose we have access to
n
candidate teacher models
{T_{j}}_{j=1}^{n}
spanning different capability levels. We first evaluate each teacher on a small validation subset for the target task and retain only those whose performance exceeds a threshold
\tau
, yielding a viable set
\mathcal{T}
of size
m
. This filtering step removes teachers that are too weak to provide reliable guidance.

Given the curriculum-ordered dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{curr}}
, we then impose a monotone alignment between data difficulty and teacher strength. We order the viable teachers from weak to strong according to the performance metric
\mathrm{Perf}(\cdot)
, and partition
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{curr}}
into
m
(the number of viable teachers) contiguous segments of increasing difficulty. Each segment is assigned to one teacher, so that the student is first trained on easier examples under weaker supervision, and gradually transitions to stronger teachers as it encounters harder examples later in the curriculum. Contiguous segmentation preserves the easy-to-hard progression and prevents mixing easy and hard supervision within the same stage, which can otherwise introduce instability.

This staged teacher scheduling has two complementary effects. Early in training, weaker teachers provide supervision that the student can imitate more easily.
Later in training, stronger teachers are introduced when the student is better prepared to learn more complex reasoning patterns, allowing the distillation process to exploit high-quality guidance on challenging examples.
By explicitly coupling teacher strength with example difficulty, CLPD goes beyond curriculum-only and progressive-only strategies and resolves the supervision mismatch that arises when either axis is controlled in isolation. The complete procedure is described in Algorithm

.

3.3
Further Intuition and Empirical Interpretation

We explain why CLPD is effective by linking its design to empirical behavior observed in our experiments. The central insight is that teacher optimality depends on
example difficulty
: a teacher that is effective on hard instances can be suboptimal on easy ones, and vice versa. CLPD instantiates this principle by jointly scheduling training examples and teachers, enforcing a monotone alignment in which supervision strength increases only as the student progresses to more difficult data.

For a fixed student model, we attribute a teacher’s effectiveness in improving student performance via distillation on a given subset of examples to two factors: (i)
task competence
on that subset, and (ii)
teacher—student alignment
, i.e., how well the student can model the teacher’s output distribution for those examples.
The ideal teacher model should strike a balance between the two. Strong teacher models typically have high competence, particularly on hard examples, but can be poorly aligned with the student and may provide complex supervision that a small student cannot reliably imitate early in training.
On the other hand, weaker teachers may be better aligned with the student but lack sufficient competence, yielding low-quality supervision that caps student performance.

We validate the above explanation by distilling the same student model with different teachers on easy and hard subsets of GSM8K
4
. Specifically, we use
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct
,
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct
, and
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
as teachers and
Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct
as the student. We perform standard distillation on either the top
20\%
easiest or top
20\%
hardest examples, and evaluate the resulting students on the full test set. To understand the underlying mechanism, we jointly measure (i)
competence
, quantified by the teacher’s accuracy on each subset, (ii)
alignment
, quantified by the teacher–student distribution gap via the student’s average negative log-probability on teacher-generated outputs, and (iii) the resulting student performance after distillation. The results are summarized in Table

.

4
Experiments

We evaluate CLPD on mathematical reasoning and commonsense reasoning tasks, focusing on two questions:
(i) whether aligning teacher capability with example difficulty improves over standard single-teacher distillation, and
(ii) whether the gains come from the
coupling
of curriculum learning and progressive distillation rather than either component alone.
We evaluate both within- and cross-family distillation under a fixed training budget, comparing CLPD with strong baselines under identical optimization and data conditions.

Our primary distillation framework is sequence-level knowledge distillation (SeqKD)
19
, where the student is trained on teacher-generated chain-of-thought (CoT) demonstrations.
Within this framework, we compare CLPD against three baselines:
(i) standard SeqKD with a single fixed teacher,
(ii) curriculum learning only, where training examples are ordered from easy to hard while the teacher remains fixed, and
(iii) progressive distillation only, where multiple teachers are scheduled over training while the data are treated as i.i.d.
These baselines allow us to isolate the contributions of data ordering, teacher scheduling, and their interaction.

4.1
Mathematical Reasoning

Setup.
We first evaluate CLPD on two standard math reasoning benchmarks. GSM8K
4

to rank the training data and partition it evenly into easy and hard subsets. Our experiments focus on improving the reasoning performance of relatively small language models, where distillation is most impactful. We consider student models from two model families: Qwen-2.5
36
and Llama-3.2
7
. For GSM8K, we use
Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct
and
Llama3.2-1B-Instruct
as students; for MATH, we use
Qwen2.5-3B
and
Llama3.2-3B
. As teacher models, we adopt
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct
and
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
for GSM8K, and
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
and
Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct
for MATH. We restrict our experiments to two teachers per task, as this setting already captures the key effects of progressive supervision and yields consistent improvements. In general, larger Llama models show weaker math reasoning than their Qwen counterparts. We therefore use Qwen models as teachers, while including both Qwen and Llama models as students to evaluate CLPD under both within-family and cross-family distillation.

Results.
Table
2
summarizes the performance of CLPD against standard distillation, curriculum learning, and progressive distillation. Across both Qwen and Llama setups on GSM8K and MATH, student models start far below their teachers, underscoring the difficulty of transferring reasoning capabilities.
Standard distillation
yields only limited gains, and stronger teachers do not consistently improve outcomes.
Curriculum learning
(easy-to-hard ordering) and
progressive distillation
(multi-teacher scheduling) each provide modest improvements, but remain insufficient when used alone. In contrast,

consistently achieves the best performance across all settings. By aligning data difficulty with teacher capability, it outperforms all baselines and remains robust across different curriculum construction strategies. These results provide evidence that neither data ordering nor teacher scheduling alone is sufficient; the strongest gains arise when the two are aligned.

Conclusion

We introduced Curriculum Learning–Guided Progressive Distillation (CLPD), a unified and plug-and-play framework that improves knowledge distillation by aligning data difficulty with teacher supervision strength. By jointly ordering training examples and progressively scheduling teachers, CLPD reduces supervision mismatch and enables more effective knowledge transfer without architectural changes or additional computational overhead. Across multiple reasoning benchmarks, CLPD consistently outperforms standard distillation, curriculum learning alone, and progressive distillation alone. More broadly, our findings suggest that many distillation failures attributed to teacher quality instead stem from misalignment between supervision strength and learning stage, and that explicitly coupling these factors is essential for transferring reasoning capabilities to smaller language models.

Appendix B
Static vs. Dynamic Curriculum Construction

In CLPD, we construct the curriculum using a static (one-shot) difficulty ordering computed prior to training and kept fixed throughout distillation. A natural question is whether incorporating adaptivity—by updating difficulty estimates as the student evolves—can further improve performance.

To explore this, we consider a simple two-stage re-ranking scheme. We first compute a global difficulty ordering using the loss of a pretrained (or warm-started) student model. During training, after the first stage is completed, we re-estimate difficulty using the updated student and re-rank the remaining portion of the data (i.e., the second half). This provides a lightweight approximation to a dynamic curriculum while preserving most of the original training pipeline.

Appendix C
CLPD and On-policy Distillation

CLPD shares a similar underlying motivation with on-policy distillation in that both aim to mitigate the distribution mismatch between the teacher-generated data and the student’s evolving prediction distribution. In on-policy distillation
[
8
,
1
]
, the student actively generates samples from its own policy and queries the teacher for supervision, thereby aligning training data with the student’s current capabilities. In contrast, CLPD achieves a related effect in a fully offline manner by progressively aligning data difficulty with teacher capability. A key aspect of CLPD is the explicit coupling between data difficulty and teacher scheduling: easier samples are paired with weaker teachers at early stages, while harder samples are introduced alongside stronger teachers as training progresses. This coordinated design ensures that the supervision signal remains well-matched to the student’s learning stage, reducing mismatch not only in the data distribution but also in the quality and complexity of teacher guidance. As a result, while on-policy distillation adapts the data distribution dynamically through student sampling, CLPD approximates this adaptation through a structured curriculum over pre-generated data. This perspective highlights CLPD as a stable and efficient alternative that captures key benefits of on-policy distillation without requiring costly online data generation or repeated teacher queries.

This connection also clarifies why CLPD is not directly applicable to on-policy distillation. The primary benefit of CLPD arises from its ability to reduce distribution mismatch in an offline setting through the coupling of data difficulty and teacher scheduling. In contrast, on-policy distillation inherently mitigates this mismatch by constructing training data from the student’s current policy, ensuring that supervision is already well-aligned with the student’s learning state. As a result, the key mechanism that CLPD is designed to address becomes largely redundant in the on-policy setting. Moreover, incorporating teacher scheduling into on-policy distillation is fundamentally different from the offline case. In on-policy settings, switching teachers effectively changes the token-level learning signal (or reward model) during training, rather than merely altering the data distribution. In the absence of the distribution mismatch, it is unclear which teacher provides a more appropriate token-level supervision signal for the student at a given stage. This makes the benefits of teacher scheduling less well-defined and potentially unstable in on-policy distillation.

We provide additional preliminary results exploring the connection between CLPD and on-policy distillation. We first compare CLPD in the standard off-policy distillation setting with on-policy distillation, and then investigate the effect of applying CLPD within the on-policy framework.

C.1
CLPD vs. On-policy Distillation

We first compare CLPD in the standard off-policy distillation setting with on-policy distillation. Both approaches aim to reduce the mismatch between the student’s learning state and the supervision it receives, but they achieve this through fundamentally different mechanisms. On-policy distillation dynamically generates training data from the student’s current policy and queries the teacher for supervision, ensuring that the data distribution is naturally aligned with the student. In contrast, CLPD operates in an offline setting and reduces distribution mismatch through a structured curriculum, achieved by coupling data difficulty with teacher scheduling.

Empirically, from Fig.
3
, we observe that CLPD substantially improves over standard off-policy distillation (SKD) and achieves performance that is competitive with on-policy distillation. This suggests that a substantial portion of the gains from on-policy distillation can be attributed to improved alignment between training data and the student’s capability. CLPD provides an efficient alternative by approximating this alignment through a carefully designed curriculum over pre-generated data, without requiring expensive online sampling or repeated teacher queries.

C.2
Applying CLPD to On-policy Distillation

We next investigate whether CLPD can further improve performance when applied on top of on-policy distillation. In this setting, the student generates data from its own policy, and the teacher provides supervision on these samples. As a result, the distribution mismatch that CLPD is designed to address is largely mitigated by construction. Nevertheless, we empirically evaluate whether CLPD can still provide additional benefits in this setting.

Fig.
3
also shows that incorporating CLPD into on-policy distillation does not improve performance and can even degrade it. This is consistent with the understanding that the primary benefit of CLPD lies in reducing distribution mismatch in offline settings. Moreover, introducing teacher scheduling in the on-policy setting alters the token-level supervision signal during training, effectively changing the reward model over time. In the absence of a distribution mismatch, it is unclear which teacher provides the most appropriate supervision at each stage. As illustrated in the figure, applying CLPD to the On-Policy Distillation (OPD) setting results in a performance decline. This suggests that for OPD, a single, strong teacher (a token-level reward model) is more effective than a progressive switching strategy.

These findings suggest that CLPD is most effective in offline distillation settings, where supervision mismatch is a primary challenge. In contrast, on-policy distillation already adapts supervision to the student’s evolving policy, leaving limited room for additional gains from curriculum-aware teacher scheduling.

Appendix D
Limitations

There are several limitations that warrant further investigation.
(i) CLPD relies on predefined teacher switching points to align data difficulty with teacher capability. While we adopt a simple and effective uniform partition, the optimal switching schedule may depend on the dataset, model pair, and task. Although our ablation studies suggest that CLPD is reasonably robust to partition choices, automatically adapting the switching schedule during training remains an open problem.
(ii) We primarily evaluate CLPD on mathematical and commonsense reasoning benchmarks, where example difficulty can be naturally characterized and aligned with teacher capability. Extending CLPD to broader domains and more heterogeneous tasks is an important direction for future work.
(iii) Our experiments mainly focus on standard offline sequence-level distillation. While we discuss the relationship between CLPD and on-policy distillation, the benefits of curriculum-aware teacher scheduling under other sophisticated distillation paradigms remain unclear. In particular, when the training distribution is already adapted to the student policy, the advantages of CLPD may diminish. Exploring these interactions more systematically is an interesting direction for future research.

E.2
Generated Chain-of-Thought Data

All sequence-level distillation experiments use teacher-generated chain-of-thought (CoT) demonstrations as the supervised training targets. To ensure a fair comparison, we first generate and store the teacher outputs offline, and then reuse the same generated data across all methods that use the same teacher. Thus, standard distillation, curriculum learning only, progressive distillation only, and CLPD differ only in the ordering of examples and/or the assignment of teachers, rather than in the underlying teacher generations.

Prompt format.

For each dataset, we use a fixed prompt template for all teacher models. For mathematical reasoning tasks, the teacher is prompted to solve the problem step by step and provide the final answer in a parsable format. For GSM8K, the final answer is required to be a numeric value. For MATH, the final answer is required to be enclosed in
\boxed{}
when applicable. For commonsense and science reasoning tasks, the teacher is prompted to explain its reasoning step by step and end with a clearly formatted final answer. For StrategyQA, the final answer is restricted to a binary answer, and for ARC-Challenge, the final answer is restricted to one of the candidate option letters. The same prompt template is used for all teachers within each dataset.

Teacher generation.

Teacher CoT demonstrations are generated using greedy decoding to reduce randomness and improve reproducibility. Specifically, we set the sampling temperature to zero and disable stochastic sampling. The maximum generation length is set according to the task complexity: 512 tokens for GSM8K and StrategyQA, 1024 tokens for MATH, and 256 tokens for ARC-Challenge. These limits match the evaluation-time decoding budget used in Appendix
E.4
. Each generated response is stored together with the original example identifier, question, answer label, teacher model name, and the parsed final answer when available. Note that including CoT generally improves performance across many reasoning tasks, including mathematical and commonsense reasoning
[
27
]
.

Data reuse across baselines.

For single-teacher baselines, all examples are supervised by the same pre-generated CoT outputs from the selected teacher. For progressive distillation and CLPD, each teacher first generates CoT demonstrations for the full training set. The difference between the two methods is how examples are assigned to teachers. Progressive distillation schedules teachers over training while treating the data stream as unordered, whereas CLPD assigns easier curriculum segments to weaker teachers and harder curriculum segments to stronger teachers. Therefore, the comparison isolates the effect of difficulty–capability alignment rather than differences in teacher decoding or prompt design.
