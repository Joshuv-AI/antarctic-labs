Time-Dependent PDE-Constrained Optimization via Weak-Form Latent Dynamics
April Tran, Terry Haut, David M. Bortz, Youngsoo Choi

Abstract
Optimization problems constrained by high-dimensional, time-dependent partial differential equations require repeated forward and sensitivity solves, making high-fidelity optimization computationally prohibitive in many-query design and control settings. We present a weak-form latent-space reduced-order modeling framework for accelerating gradient-based PDE-constrained optimization. The proposed approach builds on Weak-form Latent Space Dynamics Identification (WLaSDI), which compresses high-dimensional solution trajectories into a low-dimensional latent representation and identifies parametric latent dynamics using weak-form system identification. By avoiding explicit numerical differentiation of training trajectories, the weak-form improves robustness to noisy data and yields more reliable surrogate dynamics for optimization. We formulate the resulting reduced PDE-constrained optimization problem and derive both direct-sensitivity and adjoint-based gradient expressions for the learned latent dynamics, enabling scalable gradient evaluation with respect to design parameters. The framework is demonstrated on three time-dependent benchmark problems: thermal radiative transfer for optimal hohlraum design, the two-stream instability Vlasov–Poisson system, and the inviscid Burgers equation. Across these examples, WLaSDI produces accurate optimal designs, remains robust under noisy training data, and delivers substantial computational savings, including speedups of up to five orders of magnitude relative to full-order optimization. These results demonstrate that weak-form latent dynamics provide an efficient and noise-robust surrogate foundation for gradient-based optimization of complex time-dependent PDE systems.

Introduction

A practical digital twin for complex systems must support repeated solutions of design and control problems governed by high-fidelity physical models. In many applications, particularly in PDE-constrained optimization, this entails solving parametric, time-dependent partial differential equations (PDEs) within iterative optimization loops. The computational cost of repeatedly invoking high-fidelity PDE solvers, however, renders such many-query workflows prohibitively expensive. This fundamental challenge has motivated the development of surrogate-based optimization strategies aimed at reducing computational cost.

One common approach constructs surrogate models by interpolating selected quantities of interest (QoIs)

In essence, LaSDI projects the solution of a parametric PDE onto a low-dimensional parametric ODE governing the latent dynamics. By evolving this latent ODE in time and reconstructing the full-order state, LaSDI enables efficient and accurate simulation across the parameter domain at a substantially reduced computational cost.

A key advantage of LaSDI lies in its interpretable and physics-informed representation of the latent dynamics. 1.1
Problem Statement

Consider a parameterized, time-dependent dynamical system obtained from the spatial discretization of an underlying partial differential equation. The state of the system is represented by a finite-dimensional vector

\mathbf{u}:[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}},

which evolves according to the semi-discrete system

\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}\big(\mathbf{u}(t,\,\boldsymbol{\mu}),t,\,\boldsymbol{\mu}\big),\qquad\mathbf{u}(0,\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\mu}),

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{D}}}
denotes a finite-dimensional parameter vector,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}
is the spatially discretized operator and
\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
is the parameter-dependent initial condition.

Temporal discretization of Eq. (
1
) using a general time-integration scheme yields a sequence of algebraic equations defining the discrete state evolution. Let
t_{n}=n\Delta t
, with
\Delta t=\frac{T}{N}
and
n=0,1,\dots,N
, and denote the discrete state by

\mathbf{u}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}):=\mathbf{u}(t_{n},\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}}.

For notational clarity, we omit the explicit dependence of
\mathbf{u}_{n}
on the parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}
in what follows.
The fully discrete system can be expressed compactly in residual form as

\begin{aligned} &\mathbf{r}_{0}\big(\mathbf{u}_{0},\ \boldsymbol{\mu}\big)=\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\\
&\mathbf{r}_{n}\big(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{n},\ \boldsymbol{\mu}\big)\end{aligned}\begin{aligned} &=\mathbf{0}_{N_{\mathbf{u}}},\\
&=\mathbf{0}_{N_{\mathbf{u}}},\qquad n=1,2,\dots,N.\end{aligned}

(2)

where
\mathbf{r}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}}\times\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}}
denotes the discrete PDE residual at time step
n
. Note that
\mathbf{r}_{n}
depends on the chosen spatial discretization and time-integration scheme and may involve the full state history, allowing for implicit or multistep methods.

The focus of this paper is the following fully discrete PDE-constrained optimization problem:

\boxed{\begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf{u}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{N},\ \boldsymbol{\mu}}\quad&f(\mathbf{u}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mu})\\[2.15277pt]
\text{s.t.}\quad&\mathbf{r}_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{n},\ \boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathbf{0}_{N_{\mathbf{u}}},\qquad n=0,1,\ldots,N,\\
&\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{N},\ \boldsymbol{\mu})\leq\mathbf{0}_{N_{\mathbf{c}}}.\end{aligned}}

(3)

Here,
f
is a scalar-valued objective functional that depends on the discrete state trajectory and the parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{c}}}
represents a collection of inequality constraints. The optimization seeks parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu}
(and the corresponding state trajectory
\{\mathbf{u}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
satisfying the discrete PDE constraints in Eq. (
2
)) that minimize the objective while respecting the inequality constraints.

2
Background

2.1
LaSDI: Latent Space Dynamics Identification

In this section, we provide an overview of the Latent Space Dynamics Identification (LaSDI) framework
21
. LaSDI is a data-driven simulation methodology designed to accelerate the numerical solution of high-dimensional, parameterized partial differential equations (PDEs). The central idea of LaSDI is to project the governing parametric, semi-discrete system in Eq. (
1
) onto a low-dimensional latent space and to identify a parametric ODE that governs the evolution of the latent variables. In doing so, LaSDI effectively recasts the original problem from repeatedly solving a high-dimensional PDE to integrating a low-dimensional ODE.

We briefly recall the parametric system in Eq. (
1
) and its fully discrete numerical solution
\mathbf{u}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
for
n=0,1,\dots,N
. The discrete trajectory is collected as:

\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\mu}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbf{u}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mu})&\cdots&\mathbf{u}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\end{array}\right]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times N_{\mathbf{u}}}.

We now describe the LaSDI framework in its most general form, which consists of four key stages: data sampling, compression, latent space dynamics identification, and prediction.
A comprehensive overview of the method can be found in
7
. A schematic illustrating the workflow of the weak form extension, WLaSDI, is provided in Figure
1
.

Data Sampling

In this stage, a finite set of training parameters is selected from the parameter domain
\mathcal{D}
. For each selected parameter value, a high-fidelity numerical solution of Eq. (
1
) is computed to generate training trajectories. The selection of training parameters can follow either uniform sampling or an adaptive procedure; see
7
,
25
,
26
,
16
for details.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(2)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(K)}\right\}\subset\mathcal{D}
denote the set of training parameters. For each
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{S}
, the computed trajectories is denoted by
\mathbf{U}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}):=\mathbf{U}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times N_{\mathbf{u}}}
. All training trajectories are then concatenated to form the global training data matrix

\mathbf{U}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\left(\mathbf{U}^{(1)}\right)^{T}&\cdots&\left(\mathbf{U}^{(K)}\right)^{T}\end{array}\right]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)K\times N_{\mathbf{u}}}.

Compression

Given the collection of high-dimensional training trajectories, the next stage of LaSDI seeks to obtain a low-dimensional representation of the system state.
Let
\mathbf{z}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{z}}}
denote the latent variable associated with

\mathbf{u}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu})
, where
N_{\mathbf{z}}\ll N_{\mathbf{u}}
is the latent dimension. The mapping between the full-order and latent representations is defined through an encoder–decoder pair,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\text{en}}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{z}}},\qquad\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{z}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}}.

These operators may be constructed using Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) or autoencoder neural networks. For notational convenience, we define

\mathbf{z}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}):=\mathcal{G}_{\text{en}}(\mathbf{u}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}))
, and conversely,

\mathbf{u}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu})).

The full-time-discretized latent space trajectory
is then given by

\mathbf{Z}(\boldsymbol{\mu}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbf{z}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mu})&\cdots&\mathbf{z}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\end{array}\right]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times N_{\mathbf{z}}}.

Dynamics Identification

Within the LaSDI framework, the temporal evolution of the latent variable
\mathbf{z}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{z}}}
for an arbitrary
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is assumed to satisfy a parametric surrogate ODE of the form

\frac{d\mathbf{z}}{dt}\ (t,\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathbf{W}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\ \boldsymbol{\theta}\left(\mathbf{z}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu})\right),\qquad\mathbf{z}(0,\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathcal{G}_{\text{en}}(\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\mu})),

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbf{z}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}f_{1}(\mathbf{z})&\cdots&f_{J}(\mathbf{z})\end{array}\right]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{{J}}
denotes the user-specified library of candidate nonlinear functions
f_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{z}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The coefficient matrix
\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times N_{\mathbf{z}}}
encodes the parametric dependence of the latent dynamics.

In the offline phase of LaSDI, latent trajectories corresponding to the training parameters

\mathcal{S}

are used to identify the coefficient matrices governing the latent dynamics. Specifically, for each
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{S}
, the corresponding coefficient matrix
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}:=\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)})
is identified from the latent trajectory
\mathbf{Z}^{(k)}:=\mathbf{Z}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)})
; see section
2.2
for details
1
1

1

Rather than performing compression and dynamics identification sequentially, as described here, the autoencoder and latent space ODE can alternatively be trained simultaneously in an end-to-end manner; see
25
,
6
for details.
.
In the online phase,
Prediction
, given
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{D}
, the associated coefficient matrix
\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
is obtained by interpolating the precomputed training coefficients.

This formulation offers several advantages. First, it is highly flexible: the choice of the library
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is user-defined and can be tailored to incorporate structural or physical constraints in the latent space. Second, by interpolating coefficient matrices rather than latent trajectories, parametric generalization is achieved efficiently and robustly. Finally, the resulting model is interpretable, as the latent dynamics are represented explicitly as a parametric ODE rather than a black-box surrogate.

Prediction

Given an arbitrary parameter value
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{D}
, the corresponding coefficient matrix
\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
is obtained via interpolation of the precomputed coefficients
\left\{\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\right\}^{K}_{k=1}.
A range of interpolation strategies for this purpose is discussed in
A
.

Following Eq. (
4
), the latent initial condition is obtained by encoding the full-order initial condition,

\mathbf{z}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathbf{z}(0,\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathcal{G}_{\text{en}}(g(\boldsymbol{\mu})).

With
\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
and
\mathbf{z}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
determined, the full latent space trajectory
\mathbf{Z}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
is obtained by numerically solving the identified IVP.
Finally, the latent trajectory is decoded to recover the full-order solution
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
.

Weak-form Identification of Latent Space Dynamics

This section focuses on the identification of latent space dynamics within the LaSDI framework, specifically the coefficient matrix
\mathbf{W}
appearing in the surrogate latent ODE in the
Dynamics Identification
stage, using the weak-form. This approach is rooted in the
weak-form
of a differential equation, obtained by multiplying the governing equation by a smooth test function
\phi
and applying integration by parts to transfer derivatives from the state to
\phi

,
57

24
,

,
55
.
More broadly, one can consider
Weak-form Scientific Machine Learning
(WSciML) framework

as encompassing both equation discovery (WSINDy) and parameter estimation (WENDy, see below). WSciML builds on methods from sparse regression, total least squares, and maximum likelihood, and is supported by theory on asymptotic consistency

and optimal test-function design

\mathbf{z}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{z}}}
is assumed to satisfy the ODE

\frac{d\mathbf{z}}{dt}(t)=\mathbf{W}^{T}\boldsymbol{\theta}\left(\mathbf{z}(t)\right),\qquad\mathbf{z}(0)=\mathbf{z}_{0},

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
denotes a library of candidate functions, and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times N_{\mathbf{z}}}
is the unknown coefficient matrix.
Suppose the latent trajectory
\left\{\mathbf{z}_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{N}
, with
\mathbf{z}_{n}=\mathbf{z}(t_{n})
, are available at uniformly spaced time instances
t_{n}=n\Delta t
.
In the original LaSDI implementation,
\mathbf{W}
is identified using SINDy

, which solves the linear system

2

While SINDy is typically formulated as a sparse regression problem, sparsity is not enforced in the LaSDI setting, as the goal is to construct a surrogate model of the latent dynamics.

\dot{\mathbf{Z}}\approx\mathbf{\Theta}(\mathbf{Z})\mathbf{W},

where
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times N_{\mathbf{z}}}
and
\mathbf{\Theta}(\mathbf{Z})\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times J}
are defined as

\dot{\mathbf{Z}}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{\mathbf{z}}_{0}^{T}\\
\vdots\\
\dot{\mathbf{z}}_{N}^{T}\end{array}\right],\qquad\mathbf{\Theta}(\mathbf{Z}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{0})&\cdots&f_{J}(\mathbf{z}_{0})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{N})&\cdots&f_{J}(\mathbf{z}_{N})\\
\end{array}\right].

Computing
\dot{\mathbf{Z}}
directly requires numerical differentiation of the latent trajectories, typically via finite difference approximations, which can be inaccurate and highly sensitive to noise. To avoid explicit differentiation, WLaSDI instead adopts the weak-form for identifying the coefficient matrix
\mathbf{W}
. Multiplying both sides of Eq. (
5
) by a smooth, compactly supported test function
\phi(t)
and integrating by parts yields

-\int_{0}^{T}\dot{\phi}\mathbf{z}dt=\int_{0}^{T}\phi\mathbf{W}^{T}\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbf{z})dt.

(6)

Approximating these integrals using the trapezoidal rule leads to

-\boldsymbol{\dot{{\phi}}}^{T}\mathbf{Z}\approx\boldsymbol{\phi}^{T}\mathbf{\Theta(Z)}\mathbf{W},

where the row vectors are defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}^{T}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\phi(t_{0})&\cdots&\phi(t_{N})\end{array}\right]\mathcal{Q},\qquad\boldsymbol{\dot{{\phi}}}^{T}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\dot{\phi}(t_{0})&\cdots&\dot{\phi}(t_{N})\end{array}\right]\mathcal{Q},

with quadrature matrix
\mathcal{Q}=\text{diag}\left(\frac{\Delta t}{2},\Delta t,\cdots,\Delta t,\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times(N+1)}.
Using a collection of

\mathcal{M}:=\left\{\phi_{m}\right\}_{m=1}^{M}

\mathbf{W}
can be cast as the weak-form least squares problem:

\min_{\mathbf{W}}\left\lVert\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{G}\mathbf{W}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

(7)

where the matrices

\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N_{\mathbf{z}}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}:=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{\Theta}(\mathbf{Z}),\quad\mathbf{B}:=-\mathbf{\dot{\Phi}}\mathbf{Z},\qquad\textrm{with}\qquad\boldsymbol{\Phi}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}^{T}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\phi}_{M}^{T}\end{array}\right]^{T},\quad\mathbf{\dot{\Phi}}

\displaystyle:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\boldsymbol{\dot{\phi}}_{1}^{T}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\dot{\phi}}_{M}^{T}\end{array}\right]^{T}.

Accelerating Gradient-based PDE-Constrained Optimization with WLaSDI Framework

3.1
Gradient-based Optimization with Time-Dependent PDE Constraints

This section focuses on computing the gradients required for gradient-based optimization of the time-dependent PDE-constrained optimization problem defined in Eq. (
3
). For clarity of notation, we omit the explicit parametric dependence of the state vectors and write
\mathbf{u}_{n}\equiv\mathbf{u}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
.

Gradient-based optimization algorithms require evaluation of the first derivatives of the objective functional

f(\mathbf{u}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N},\ \boldsymbol{\mu})
and any constraint functions

\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N},\ \boldsymbol{\mu})
with respect to the parameter vector

\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{D}}}
. Without loss of generality, we consider the derivative of the objective function
f
with respect to an individual parameter component
\mu_{i}
,
\frac{df}{d\mu_{i}}
, for
i=1,\dots,N_{\mathcal{D}}
. Applying the chain rule yields

\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\mu_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mu})=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N},\ \boldsymbol{\mu})\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N},\ \boldsymbol{\mu}).

(8)

For a given objective function
f
, the derivatives
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}
and

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N_{\mathbf{u}}},n=0,1,\dots,N
are generally straightforward. The main challenge lies in evaluating the state sensitivities
\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}}
. To this end, we note that the discretized PDE residuals

\mathbf{r}_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{n},\ \boldsymbol{\mu})
, defined in Eq. (
2
), satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{r}_{n}}{d\mu_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}+\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{j}}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{j}}{\partial\mu_{i}}=\mathbf{0}_{N_{\mathbf{u}}},\qquad n=0,1,\dots,N,

(9)

where
\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}\times N_{\mathbf{u}}}.

Solving these relations forward in time yields the state sensitivities

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{0}}{\partial\mu_{i}}

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{0}}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{0}}\right]^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{0}}{\partial\mu_{i}},

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\right]^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{j}}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{j}}{\partial\mu_{i}}\right),\qquad n=1,2,\dots,N.

This procedure is referred to as the
direct
sensitivity approach, and the resulting sensitivities can be substituted into the gradient expression in Eq. (
8
).
Alternatively, the
adjoint
approach can be derived by introducing Lagrange multipliers
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}}
. Using Eq. (
9
), we write

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}^{T}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}+\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{j}}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{j}}{\partial\mu_{i}}\right).

(11)

Rearranging terms yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}=-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}^{T}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}+\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}-\sum_{j=n}^{N}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}^{T}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{j}}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\right)\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}.

By enforcing the adjoint equations such that the expressions in parentheses vanish,
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}-\sum_{j=n}^{N}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}^{T}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{j}}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}=\mathbf{0}^{T}_{N_{\mathbf{u}}},

we can solve for the Lagrange multipliers
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}}
backward in time by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{N}^{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{N}}\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{N}}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{N}}\right]^{-1},

(12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}^{T}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}-\sum_{j=n+1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}^{T}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{j}}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\right)\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\right]^{-1},\qquad n=(N-1),(N-2),\dots,0.

The gradient of the objective function
f
in Eq. (
8
) then becomes

\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\mu_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mu})=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N},\ \boldsymbol{\mu})-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}_{n}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}(\mathbf{u}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N},\ \boldsymbol{\mu}).

(13)

Remark 1
.

The direct sensitivity approach requires solving a linear system at each time step for every parameter component
\mu_{i}
, resulting in a total of
(N+1)N_{\mathcal{D}}
linear solves (Eq. (
10
)). Once these state sensitivities are computed, no additional solves are needed to evaluate gradients of the constraint functions
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{c}}}
.
In contrast, the adjoint approach eliminates the dependence on the parameter dimension by introducing the Lagrange multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}
associated with the PDE constraints at each time step. This requires solving
(N+1)
adjoint systems for the objective function and an additional
(N+1)
systems per constraint, for a total of
(N+1)(1+N_{\mathbf{c}})
linear solves (Eq. (
12
)). Consequently, the adjoint formulation is computationally advantageous when
1+N_{\mathbf{c}}<N_{\mathcal{D}}
.

Using the gradients derived above, the PDE-constrained optimization problem in Eq. (
3
) can be solved using standard nonlinear optimization algorithms, such as trust-region methods
18
, interior-point methods
69
, or Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP)
23
.
However, the treatment of the discretized PDE constraints is computationally intensive. In particular, evaluating the state sensitivities
\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}}
at each time step requires solving large-scale linear systems associated with the full-order model, resulting in significant computational cost. To address this challenge, we employ Weak-form Latent Space Dynamics Identification (WLaSDI) to accelerate the optimization procedure by enabling efficient surrogate simulation of the underlying PDE. The resulting reduced optimization framework is presented in Section
3.2
.

3.2
WLaSDI for PDE-Constrained Optimization

We begin by recalling the full-order model (FOM) describing the evolution of the state

\mathbf{u}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu})
, governed by the semi-discrete system in
Eq. (
1
).
Within the LaSDI framework (see Section
2
), the high-dimensional
state
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}}
is approximated by a low-dimensional latent
representation
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{z}}}
, with
N_{\mathbf{z}}\ll N_{\mathbf{u}}
, through
encoder–decoder maps
\mathcal{G}_{\text{en}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}
.
The evolution of the latent state
\mathbf{z}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu})
is governed by the learned surrogate
dynamics defined in Eq. (
4
).

Applying a time-integration scheme to the latent dynamical system yields a sequence of
reduced residuals
, denoted by
\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}
, which enforce the discrete-time evolution of the latent variables and serve as reduced-order analogues of the full-order residuals

\mathbf{r}_{n}
defined in Eq. (
2
).
For notational convenience, we write
\mathbf{z}_{n}\equiv\mathbf{z}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
.
The reduced residuals are given by

\begin{aligned} &\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{0}\big(\mathbf{z}_{0},\ \boldsymbol{\mu}\big)=\mathbf{z}_{0}-\mathcal{G}_{\text{en}}(\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\mu}))\\
&\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}\big(\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{n},\ \boldsymbol{\mu}\big)\end{aligned}\begin{aligned} &=\mathbf{0}_{N_{\mathbf{z}}},\\
&=\mathbf{0}_{N_{\mathbf{z}}},\qquad n=1,2,\dots,N.\end{aligned}

(14)

Consequently, the optimization in Eq. (
3
) is reformulated as

\boxed{\begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf{z}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{N},\ \boldsymbol{\mu}}\quad&f(\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{0}),\dots,\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{N}),\ \boldsymbol{\mu})\\[2.15277pt]
\text{s.t.}\quad&\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{n},\ \boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathbf{0}_{N_{\mathbf{z}}},\qquad n=0,1,\dots,N,\\
&\mathbf{c}(\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{0}),\dots,\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{N}),\ \boldsymbol{\mu})\leq\mathbf{0}_{N_{\mathbf{c}}}.\end{aligned}}

(15)

We now turn to the computation of gradients required for gradient-based optimization.
The derivation closely parallels the full-order case presented in
Section
3.1
, with the key distinction that
sensitivities are computed with respect to the latent variables.
Defining
\mathbf{\tilde{u}}_{n}:=\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}}
, the total derivative of the objective function with respect to parameter
\mu_{i}
is

\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\mu_{i}}(\mathbf{\tilde{u}}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{\tilde{u}}_{N},\ \boldsymbol{\mu})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}(\mathbf{\tilde{u}}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{\tilde{u}}_{N},\ \boldsymbol{\mu})\ \nabla\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu_{i}}(\mathbf{\tilde{u}}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{\tilde{u}}_{N},\ \boldsymbol{\mu}).

(16)

Here and throughout,

\nabla\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}\times N_{\mathbf{z}}}
denotes the Jacobian of the decoder map
\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}
evaluated at
\mathbf{z}_{n}
.
The latent sensitivities
\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}
are obtained by differentiating
\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{n},\ \boldsymbol{\mu}),

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{d\mu_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}+\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{j}}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{j}}{\partial\mu_{i}}=\mathbf{0}_{N_{\mathbf{z}}},\qquad n=0,1,\dots,N.

(17)

The direct approach follows Eq. (
10
)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{0}}{\partial\mu_{i}}

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{0}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{0}}\right]^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{0}}{\partial\mu_{i}},

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}\right]^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{j}}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{j}}{\partial\mu_{i}}\right),\qquad n=1,2,\dots,N.

For the adjoint approach, we introduce adjoint variables
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{z}}}
and follow the same procedure outlined in Eq. (
11
)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\ \nabla\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{n})\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}=-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}^{T}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}+\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\ \nabla\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{n})-\sum_{j=n}^{N}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}^{T}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{j}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}\right)\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}.

Choosing the adjoint variables to eliminate the dependence on

\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}
yields the backward-in-time adjoint equations

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{N}^{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{N}}\ \nabla\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{N})\ \left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{N}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{N}}\right]^{-1},

(19)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}^{T}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\ \nabla\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{n})-\sum_{j=n+1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}^{T}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{j}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}\right)\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}\right]^{-1},\qquad n=(N-1),(N-2),\dots,0.

Remark 2
.

The computational advantages of LaSDI become apparent when comparing the cost of gradient evaluation.
In both the direct and adjoint formulations, the full-order model requires
N+1
linear solves involving Jacobians

\frac{\partial\mathbf{r}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{u}}\times N_{\mathbf{u}}}
.
In contrast, the reduced-order formulation involves
N+1
linear solves with Jacobians

\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{z}}\times N_{\mathbf{z}}}
.
Since
N_{\mathbf{z}}\ll N_{\mathbf{u}}
, this reduction yields substantial savings in both memory usage and computational complexity.

4
Results

In this section, we evaluate the performance of WLaSDI surrogates for accelerating PDE-constrained optimization, with a focus on both accuracy and computational efficiency. We compare WLaSDI against optimization using full-order models (FOM) as well as alternative surrogate-based approaches, with particular attention to robustness under noisy training data. Specifically, we consider additive Gaussian noise applied to the snapshot data, with standard deviation
\sigma=\sigma_{NR}\left\lVert\mathbf{U}\right\rVert F
, where
\sigma_{NR}
denotes the noise ratio.

We demonstrate the performance of WLaSDI on three benchmark problems: the one-dimensional inviscid Burgers’ equation, the two-stream instability Vlasov-Poisson system, and thermal radiative transfer (TRT) for optimal hohlraum design. Across these examples, we consider both gradient-based and derivative-free optimization strategies, all implemented using
scipy.optimize

68
, and evaluate performance in terms of solution accuracy and wall-clock time.
Unless otherwise noted, all computations are performed on a single 2.0 GHz Intel Xeon CPU core of the LC Livermore Computing Dane system at LLNL, and reported runtimes are averaged over 10 runs.

4.1
Burgers’ Equation

4.1.1
High-Fidelity Simulation and WLaSDI Surrogates

We consider the one-dimensional inviscid Burgers’ equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+u\ \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=0,\qquad t\in[0,1],\qquad x\in[-10,10],

(20)

subject to periodic boundary conditions
u(x=10,t)=u(x=-10,t)
. The initial condition is parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\mu}=[a_{1},w_{1},a_{2},w_{2}]\in\mathcal{D}

(21)

representing two Gaussian pulses with amplitudes
a_{i}
and widths
w_{i}
. The parameter domain is

\mathcal{D}=[0.7,\,0.9]\times[0.9,\,1.1]\times[0.7,\,0.9]\times[0.9,\,1.1].

For numerical discretization, the spatial domain is approximated using a uniform grid with mesh spacing
\Delta x=0.02
, and spatial derivatives are computed using a first-order forward finite difference scheme. Time integration is performed with the backward Euler method using a time step
\Delta t=0.001
. Figure
2
shows the numerical solution of Eq. (
20
) for
\boldsymbol{\mu}=[0.7,\,1.1,\,0.9,\,0.9]
, with
40\%
Gaussian noise.

To construct the WLaSDI surrogates, we follow the training procedure described in Section

99.99\%

.
In addition to the noise-free setting, we consider noisy training data by introducing additive noise levels of

and
40\%
.

4.1.2
WLaSDI Performance in PDE-Constrained Optimization

In this section, we investigate the performance of the WLaSDI surrogate in a PDE-constrained optimization setting. The objective is twofold: (i) to assess the robustness and accuracy of WLaSDI relative to other surrogate-based approaches, and (ii) to evaluate its computational efficiency compared to full-order model (FOM) optimization.

To this end, we compare optimization using WLaSDI-based surrogates against both alternative surrogate methods and direct optimization using the FOM. In the noise-free setting, we specifically benchmark WLaSDI against FOM-based optimization and radial basis function (RBF) interpolation of the objective function. \displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\!\left[\frac{d\phi}{dx}\ u\right]=0,\quad t\in[0,5],\quad x\in[0,2\pi],\quad v\in[-7,7],

22

4.3
Thermal Radiative Transfer

Nuclear fusion has long been pursued as a potential source of clean and renewable energy. In indirect-drive fusion experiments, a small fuel capsule is placed inside a device called a
hohlraum
, where intense radiation is used to compress the capsule and initiate fusion reactions. For this process to succeed, the capsule must be heated as uniformly as possible; even small asymmetries can disrupt the compression and reduce performance. Achieving such uniform energy delivery is therefore a central and challenging design goal.

In this section, we formulate an optimization problem to identify temperature configurations that promote uniform heating of the fusion capsule. Solving this problem directly using the underlying high-fidelity model is computationally expensive. To address this challenge, we employ the WLaSDI surrogates to enable efficient exploration of the design space.

WLaSDI provides substantial computational acceleration. The total training time for the local WLaSDI surrogate models is

These substantial gains make the WLaSDI surrogate model well-suited for efficient and accurate design optimization of the hohlraum device detailed below.

4.3.3
Design Optimization of the Hohlraum

To achieve thermonuclear fusion, the fuel capsule located at the center of the hohlraum (see Fig.
7
) must be heated as uniformly as possible. In this work, we seek to promote uniform heating by adjusting the prescribed temperatures of the laser-induced wall hot spots.
Therefore, the quantity of interest is the total temporal variation in the temperature distribution along the surface of the capsule. Specifically, we measure the standard deviation of the capsule surface temperature at each time step and integrate this quantity over time.

The temperature along the capsule surface is obtained by linearly interpolating the Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) elements onto points along a circular contour centered at the origin with the prescribed capsule radius.
Let
\mathbf{u}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
denote the discrete temperature field at time step
t_{n}
, we obtain an interpolation operator

\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma}
, which maps the DG solution to temperature values along the capsule surface
\Gamma
,
where
\Gamma
denotes the circular boundary of the capsule.

Let
\sigma(\cdot)
denote the standard deviation of the interpolated surface temperature. To promote uniform heating, we define the objective function as the time-integrated standard deviation,

f(\mathbf{u}_{0},\cdots,\mathbf{u}_{N};\ \boldsymbol{\mu})=\Delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N}{}^{\prime\prime}\sigma\left(\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{n}\right)

(30)

where the double prime notation on the sum
\sum{}^{\prime\prime}
denotes the trapezoidal-weighted sum, i.e., the first and last terms are halved before summing.

Figure
10
illustrates the time evolution of
\sigma\left(\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{n}\right)
for two parameter choices:
\boldsymbol{\mu}=[0.3,\,0.15]
(blue) and
\boldsymbol{\mu}=[0.05,\,0.05]
(orange). The latter yields an almost negligible standard deviation, indicating nearly uniform temperature throughout the simulation. This behavior is expected, as the initial temperature is
0.05
keV and the hot-spot temperatures are set to the same value, resulting in minimal thermal gradients and thus little evolution. While this represents a trivial optimum of the objective, it is not physically interesting.

g(\mathbf{u}_{N};\ \boldsymbol{\mu})=\mu\left(\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{u}_{N}\right)-T_{\mathrm{min}}\geq 0.

We now seek to minimize the objective functional in Eq. (
30
), subject to the governing TRT equations (Eqs. (

Because the WLaSDI/LaSDI surrogate models consist of 25 local submodels (Fig.
9
), the resulting objective function is piecewise-defined and may be discontinuous along the boundaries between subregions. In this setting, global derivative-free optimization methods such as Differential Evolution
64
can be applied directly. However, gradient-based methods are typically much more computationally efficient, although the piecewise structure of the surrogate means that the objective is discontinuous at subregion interfaces.
One natural strategy is to perform the optimization independently on each submodel and then combine the results. This approach is highly parallelizable and allows the optimization tasks to be distributed across multiple processors. Alternatively, one may perform optimization directly on the full surrogate model. In this case, the objective is piecewise defined and may be nondifferentiable at the boundaries between subregions. A common remedy would be to smooth the objective to obtain a continuous optimization problem. In this case, however, the discontinuities occur only along subregion boundaries, which form a set of measure zero in the parameter space. Consequently, the gradient is well defined
almost everywhere
, allowing gradient-based optimization methods to be applied effectively in practice.

Overall, the results demonstrate that WLaSDI surrogate models provide an effective and computationally efficient framework for PDE-constrained optimization. In the TRT hohlraum design problem considered here, WLaSDI reduces the computational cost by approximately five orders of magnitude compared to optimization with the full-order model. This efficiency is further enhanced by constructing the surrogate as a collection of local submodels, which enables reliable and accurate optimization even when gradient-based methods are employed.
At the same time, the weak-form exhibits strong robustness to noisy training data, with WLaSDI consistently producing more reliable optimization results than LaSDI in the presence of noise.

The optimal hohlraum design example highlights the flexibility of WLaSDI for optimization.
We begin with an optimization problem that minimizes the variation of the capsule surface temperature, and subsequently introduce an additional constraint to enforce physically meaningful result. More broadly, WLaSDI readily accommodates further modifications to the objective or constraints, e.g., enforcing a target heating level within a prescribed time, demonstrating its adaptability.

This underscores a key advantage of WLaSDI over surrogate approaches that approximate only a scalar QoI (e.g., radial basis function interpolation).
By reconstructing the full solution field, WLaSDI enables the evaluation of arbitrary QoIs without retraining. This flexibility is particularly valuable in PDE-constrained optimization, where objectives and constraints may evolve over time.
In contrast, QoI-specific surrogate models must be retrained whenever a new QoI is introduced, and it can be computationally expensive, especially if the FOMs were not retained.
By learning the full field, WLaSDI provides a reusable surrogate capable of supporting multiple downstream optimization tasks.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that WLaSDI enables significantly more efficient optimization than using full-order models, while maintaining greater robustness and accuracy than other surrogate-based approaches.
This positions WLaSDI as a powerful and versatile tool for accelerating PDE-constrained optimization.

5
Conclusion

Large-scale optimization problems constrained by high-dimensional, time-dependent partial differential equations (PDEs) are computationally prohibitive, as each optimization step typically requires repeated solutions of the governing equations. The Weak-form Latent Space Dynamics Identification (WLaSDI) framework
66
, a data-driven, projection-based reduced-order modeling (ROM) approach, mitigates this problem by enabling robust and efficient surrogate simulations within optimization loops.

As an extension of Latent Space Dynamics Identification (LaSDI)
21
, WLaSDI projects the high-dimensional PDE onto a low-dimensional latent space, where the latent evolution is represented by a parametric ordinary differential equation (ODE). The latent dynamics are identified using Weak-form Estimation of Nonlinear Dynamics (WENDy)
8
, which enables robust and computationally efficient simulation of high-dimensional dynamics, even with noisy training data.

In this work, we apply WLaSDI to accelerate PDE-constrained optimization. By substituting the full-order model (FOM) with a WLaSDI surrogate, governed by a low-dimensional latent ODE and coupled to the physical space through encoder–decoder mappings, the optimization no longer requires repeated high-fidelity solves. Instead, it relies entirely on the efficient solution of the latent ODE system. We present the complete formulation of this approach and derive adjoint-based sensitivities to enable efficient gradient evaluation through the latent ODE dynamics.

We demonstrate the performance of WLaSDI surrogates on several benchmark problems, including the optimal design of a hohlraum device in inertial confinement fusion governed by a thermal radiative transfer system, as well as inverse parameter recovery for the Vlasov-Poisson and Burgers’ equations. We compare WLaSDI against direct optimization using full-order models (FOMs) and alternative surrogate-based approaches.
WLaSDI achieves up to five orders of magnitude speedup relative to FOM-based optimization, while maintaining high accuracy and robustness. In particular, it improves accuracy by up to two orders of magnitude compared to other surrogate methods, even when trained on noisy data.

Despite the strong performance demonstrated in this work, the reliability of the optimization process ultimately depends on the accuracy of the WLaSDI surrogates. It is therefore crucial to understand, quantify, and control the uncertainty from the surrogates. Without a rigorous treatment of uncertainties, such surrogates cannot be reliably applied.

Another important direction for future work is the development of multi-fidelity optimization strategies that combine high-fidelity (HF) FOMs with low-fidelity (LF) WLaSDI surrogates. In this setting, WLaSDI can be used for rapid exploration, while the FOMs are invoked adaptively when higher accuracy is needed and to refine the surrogate. Systematically coupling LF and HF models within the optimization loop can improve robustness and reliability in complex problems.

Appendix A
Interpolation of Coefficients

Within the LaSDI framework, latent dynamics are governed by an ODE whose coefficient matrix may depend on the physical parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{D}
. Given training data
\mathcal{S}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
, the goal of this section is to construct an operator

\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{\Xi}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}\;\middle|\;\{\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K},\mathcal{S}\right),

that provides the coefficient matrix at arbitrary parameter values, together with its parameter sensitivities when needed.

Broadly, the approaches considered here fall into two categories:
(i)
global formulations
, which identify a single parameter-independent coefficient matrix, possibly with implicit parameterization, and
(ii)
explicit interpolation methods
, which construct
\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
directly from parameter-dependent coefficients learned at the training points.
We briefly outline these strategies below; full details are provided in
7
.

A.1
Global Coefficient

The goal of this section is to identify a
global
latent space ODE representation of the form,

\frac{d{\mathbf{z}}}{dt}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathbf{W}^{T}\boldsymbol{\theta}(\mathbf{z}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu})),

where
\mathbf{W}
is a parameter-independent coefficient matrix to be learned.
Here, we seek a parametric latent space ODE in which parameter variability is encoded only through the initial condition of the latent dynamics, while the governing coefficient matrix
\mathbf{W}
remains global and shared across all trajectories.

To this end, we concatenate all training latent trajectories into a single data matrix,

\mathbf{Z}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{(1)}\right)^{T}&\cdots&\left(\mathbf{Z}^{(K)}\right)^{T}\end{array}\right]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)K\times N_{\mathbf{z}}}.

Similarly, we construct the global feature matrix by concatenating the library evaluations
\mathbf{\Theta}^{(k)}:=\mathbf{\Theta}(\mathbf{Z}^{(k)})
,

\mathbf{\Theta}(\mathbf{Z}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\left(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(1)}\right)^{T}&\cdots&\left(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(K)}\right)^{T}\end{array}\right]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)K\times J}.

Given the aggregated latent data
\mathbf{Z}
and feature matrix
\mathbf{\Theta}(\mathbf{Z})
, the parameter independent coefficient matrix
\mathbf{W}
is given by
\mathbf{W}=\text{WENDy}(\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{\Theta}(\mathbf{Z}))
. Because
\mathbf{W}
is parameter-independent, all parameter sensitivities vanish:
\frac{\partial\mathbf{W}}{\partial\mu_{i}}=\mathbf{0}
.

A.2
Implicit Parameterization

While the use of a global coefficient matrix, as described in
A.1
, is appealing, as it eliminates the need for an explicit interpolation stage and reduces computational cost, it may fail to capture inherent parameter dependence in the latent space dynamics for certain problems. To address this limitation, we introduce an implicit parameterization of the latent dynamics by augmenting the latent state with the parameter vector itself. Specifically, we define the augmentation function
\mathbf{v}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{z}}}\times\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{(N_{\mathbf{z}}+N_{\mathcal{D}})}
, given by

\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{z}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu}),\boldsymbol{\mu}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{z}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu})\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{(N_{\mathbf{z}}+N_{\mathcal{D}})}.

Rather than modeling the dynamics directly in terms of
\mathbf{z}
, we seek a latent ODE for the augmented variable

\mathbf{v}
,

\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathbf{W}^{T}\boldsymbol{\theta}\left(\mathbf{v}(t,\boldsymbol{\mu})\right),

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\cdot)
is now evaluated on the augmented latent state. The same procedure outlined in
A.1
is then applied, but with
\mathbf{v}
replacing
\mathbf{z}
. In particular, we concatenate the augmented latent trajectories to form
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)K\times(N_{\mathbf{z}}+N_{\mathcal{D}})}
, and construct the corresponding feature matrix
\mathbf{\Theta}(\mathbf{V})\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)K\times J}
. The coefficient matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times(N_{\mathbf{z}}+N_{\mathcal{D}})}
is subsequently identified via
\mathbf{W}=\text{WENDy}(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{\Theta}(\mathbf{V}))
. This formulation yields a single global model with constant coefficients, while implicitly encoding parameter dependence through the augmented state. As a result, the latent dynamics can adapt to variations in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
without requiring parameter-dependent coefficients or post hoc interpolation. Notably,
\frac{\partial\mathbf{W}}{\partial\mu_{i}}=\mathbf{0}
.

A.3
Radial Basis Function

We next consider explicit interpolation of parameter-dependent coefficients using radial basis functions (RBFs). Independent coefficient matrices

\{\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are first identified at the training parameters.
To enable interpolation, we first vectorize each coefficient matrix
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times N_{\mathbf{z}}}
into a vector
\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{JN_{\mathbf{z}}}
. For a parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{D}
, the interpolated coefficient vector is expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(k)}\phi\left(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}\right\rVert_{2}\right)

(33)

where
\phi
denotes a chosen radial basis function, such as a Gaussian or multiquadric kernel. The interpolation weights
\left\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(k)}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{JN_{\mathbf{z}}}
are determined by enforcing exact reconstruction of the training coefficients, i.e., by solving Eq. (
33
) at the training parameters
\left\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}\right\}
. Let
r^{(k)}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}\right\rVert_{2}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{w}}{\partial\mu_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(k)}\phi^{\prime}\left(r^{(k)}\right)\left(\frac{\mu_{i}-\mu^{(k)}_{i}}{r^{(k)}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{JN_{\mathbf{z}}}.

The resulting gradient vector is reshaped back into matrix form to yield
\frac{d\mathbf{W}}{d\mu_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times N_{\mathbf{z}}}
.

A.4
Convex Interpolation via Mahalanobis Distance

We consider a convex interpolation strategy based on Mahalanobis distance in parameter space. For a query parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and training parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, define

r^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\bigl((\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)})^{\top}\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)})\bigr)^{1/2},

where
\mathbf{S}
is the empirical covariance matrix of the training parameters.
The coefficient matrix is interpolated as a convex combination of the training coefficients,

\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\,\mathbf{W}^{(k)},\qquad\beta^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\frac{r^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\mu})^{-2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}r^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\mu})^{-2}},

which ensures
\sum_{k}\beta^{(k)}=1
.
Parameter sensitivities follow directly from differentiation of the weights,

\frac{\partial\mathbf{W}}{\partial\mu_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu_{i}}\left[\beta^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\right]\,\mathbf{W}^{(k)},

with

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu_{i}}\left[r^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\mu})^{-2}\right]=-2\frac{\left(\mathbf{S}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(j)}\right)\right)_{i}}{r^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{\mu})^{4}}.

A.5
Gaussian Process Regression Interpolation

We next consider the Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) approach for interpolating the entries of the coefficient matrix
\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
. First introduced in GPLaSDI
6
, GPR provides a probabilistic model that yields both a mean prediction and an uncertainty estimate at each parameter value.

Each entry of
\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
is interpolated independently using a Gaussian process prior. Without loss of generality, let
w(\boldsymbol{\mu})
denote one such entry, and let
\overline{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
denote its predictive mean. The mean function is expressed as

\displaystyle\overline{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha^{(k)}k\left(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha^{(k)}\gamma\text{exp}\left(-\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}\right).

Here,
k\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
is the covariance kernel,
\gamma
denotes the kernel amplitude, and
\lambda>0
is the characteristic length scale. The coefficients
\left\{\alpha^{(k)}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
are determined by solving the associated Gaussian process regression system for each of
\overline{w}^{(k)}
.
The derivative of the predictive mean with respect to the
i
-th parameter component is then

\displaystyle\frac{d\overline{w}}{d\mu_{i}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha^{(k)}\frac{\partial k}{\partial\mu_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha^{(k)}\frac{\gamma}{\lambda^{2}}\text{exp}\left(-\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}\right)(\mu^{(k)}_{i}-\mu_{i}).

The resulting partial derivatives are then reshaped and assembled across all entries to yield the full Jacobian
\frac{\partial\mathbf{W}}{\partial\mu_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
.

Direct and Adjoint Sensitivities

The gradient of the surrogate objective in Eq. (
16

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{0}}{\partial\mu_{i}}

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{0}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{0}}\right]^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{0}}{\partial\mu_{i}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}\right]^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{\partial\mu_{i}}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n-1}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n-1}}{\partial\mu_{i}}\right),\qquad n=1,\dots,N.

For adjoint method with adjoint variables
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathbf{z}}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{N}^{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{N}}\nabla\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{N})\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{N}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{N}}\right]^{-1},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}^{T}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{n}}\nabla\mathcal{G}_{\text{de}}(\mathbf{z}_{n})-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n+1}^{T}\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n+1}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}\right)\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{r}}_{n}}{\partial\mathbf{z}_{n}}\right]^{-1},\qquad n=N-1,\dots,0.
