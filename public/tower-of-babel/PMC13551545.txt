Providing Mental Health and Substance Use Services to People Released From Custody During the COVID‐19 Pandemic: A Qualitative Study of Service Providers
Authors: Lucas M Seuren, Arthur McLuhan, Madison Ford, Nikoleta Curcin, Angela Mashford‐Pringle, Patricia O'Campo, Flora I Matheson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis paper explores the perspectives of mental health and substance use providers who supported people released from custody during the pandemic in Ontario, Canada, with a focus on service disruptions, adaptations, and experiences. We conducted 20 in‐depth qualitative interviews and analysed these using Applied Thematic Analysis. Pandemic public health responses limited the ability of providers to support people released from custody. Policies designed to mitigate viral spread were not tailored to the mental health and substance use context, leading to disruptions in service delivery. This had domino effects for service users and providers. Criminal‐legal‐involved populations had to isolate and at the same time providers could not reach out to them, leading to increased risks for substance use, overdose, and suicide. Providers in turn felt pressure to help people released from custody, when policy made it exceedingly difficult for them to do so. Still, providers did find silver linings through opportunities to work together and provide better person‐centred care. For future pandemic preparedness, decision‐makers must consider the unintentional fallout that blanket policies can have on underserved communities and mitigate against unintended consequences. Future research should engage underserved communities to understand their needs during emergencies.Keywords: COVID‐19, criminal‐legal, frontline healthcare, mental health and substance use services, qualitative research, re‐entry

Introduction

During the COVID‐19 pandemic, governments implemented various public health measures to stop the spread of the virus, including ‘unprecedented border closures, stay‐at‐home and quarantine initiatives, and closures of schools, private businesses and organisations that were deemed non‐essential’ [1, p. 4; 2, 3]. The reduction of populations living in congregant settings was also designed to limit the spread of the virus, including the rapid release of people from custody, as public health experts identified prisons as high‐risk sites for transmitting the virus [4, 5].

An unintended, but not unexpected consequence of the restrictions was significant disruption to mental health and substance use services [1, 6]. Such disruptions created a landscape of resource scarcity for people released from custody in their most critical time of need. Especially high rates of mental illness and substance use disorders [7, 8, 9, 10] often intersect with great material hardship post‐release [11], including poverty and homelessness, increasing the risk of re‐contact with the criminal‐legal‐system [12]. Reentry—the transition from custody to community—is also a high‐risk period for self‐harm, suicide, and fatal overdose [10, 13, 14, 15], particularly in the first weeks post‐release [15]. Given the burden of mental health and substance use concerns among people released from custody, timely access to mental health and substance use services post‐release is considered an important pathway to improving health outcomes [16].

People released from custody during the pandemic encountered a reentry context marked by pandemic‐related fears, masking mandates, physical distancing measures, and lockdowns, with the potential to exacerbate mental health and substance use challenges [17]. They also faced fewer resources and more barriers to care while experiencing exacerbated challenges to successful community reentry [18, 19, 20]. For example, people released from custody reported many services shifted to virtual delivery or telemedicine, and while that could facilitate access for some (e.g., removing transportation or childcare barriers), for many it created an additional barrier as they lacked the technology or virtual literacy to use telemedicine effectively [20, 21, 22]. Furthermore, disruptions to release planning and preparation services resulted in people feeling unprepared for release and reentry, and communication breakdowns at service providers made it hard for clients to connect to services upon release [20].

Mental health and substance use service providers recognized the increased strain of the pandemic on people released from custody [19, 22, 23] and were creative in finding ways to deliver services in this complex context. They used personal networks to fill gaps in resources (e.g., facemasks, hygiene products) and ongoing community check‐ins via phone or in‐person with social distancing protocols [23, 24]. Providers pointed to lockdown periods as especially difficult contexts of service access.

As providers adapted their services to the risks and restrictions of the pandemic environment, many also grappled with their own mental health challenges [20, 25]. Frontline staff reported elevated mental anguish and vicarious trauma in their work with clients [26, 27]. The increased needs of their clients and the constraint of funding and resources further strained their mental wellbeing [26]. Providers also struggled to adapt to disruptions to external service provisions, further increasing their frustration and stress [26] thus increasing their own need for psychological support [27, 28].

This paper adds to the emerging picture of service disruptions by exploring the perspectives of mental health and substance use providers who supported people released from custody during the pandemic in Ontario, Canada. We focused on providers' perspectives on the impact of the pandemic and public health response on client health and ability to access care, changes to service delivery, strategies to evolve services over the pandemic, and providers' own mental health and substance use challenges.

Methods

Design

We conducted a qualitative study of service providers' perspectives on the impact of the public health response using Applied Thematic Analysis [29]. We conducted qualitative interviews with providers who worked directly with people released from custody during the pandemic in Ontario, Canada. Interviews and recruitment were conducted between May and August 2021. We used a community‐based participatory research approach, defined as a ‘systematic inquiry, with the collaboration of those affected by the issue being studied, for the purposes of education and taking action or effecting social change’ [30, p. 1927]. We worked collaboratively throughout the study—from grant design to dissemination—with a network of community‐based service providers who support people involved with the criminal‐legal system. They supported recruitment and data collection, and once preliminary thematic analysis was complete, we presented the qualitative findings to them in a series of meetings. These discussions provided deeper interpretation of the themes and began the process of etching out the recommendations for pandemic preparedness [31].

The Unity Health Toronto Research Ethics Board [REB # 21‐007] provided approval for the study which followed the principles of the Tri‐Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans [32].

Setting

Approximately two million Ontarians report or seek care for either mental illness and/or addictions yearly; both are leading causes of disability in Canada [33]. These services are primarily delivered through community agencies, primary care and speciality physicians' offices, and hospitals, but research shows significant unmet need and several barriers to care related to the accessibility, affordability, acceptability, and timeliness of services [33]. The pandemic compounded these concerns, with more than a third of adults in Ontario reporting somewhat worse or much worse mental health during than prior to the pandemic [34] and widespread disruptions to mental health and substance use services [35].

The prevalence of mental health and substance use is high in the Ontario population of persons detained in carceral settings with rates in one study ranging from 11% for psychosis to 34% for drug use and 34% for depression [36]. A recent scoping review suggests that mental health services are in a dismal state in Ontario correctional facilities with limited access to psychiatrists, use of segregation, difficulty with timely access to medication, inconsistencies in opioid agonist practices across jails, lack of privacy during mental health care delivery, barriers to care when correctional officers (who lack training on mental health) act as gatekeepers between people imprisoned and care providers [37].

The majority of people incarcerated in Ontario correctional facilities are white (51.7%) and male (85.6%). However, Indigenous and Black communities are overrepresented in these correctional facilities. Black communities represent 13.7% of those incarcerated but only 5.5% of the Ontario population and Indigenous communities represent 18.3% of those incarcerated, but only 2.9% of the Ontario population [38].

Canada has a two‐tiered carceral system. The federal system is responsible for persons sentenced to two or more years. The provincial and territorial systems, such as the one in Ontario, is responsible for persons sentenced to less than 2 years, individuals awaiting bail, trial or sentencing, as well as those on immigration hold. For this project, we interviewed service providers who supported people who were sentenced both federally and provincially.

Throughout the COVID‐19 pandemic, the public health policies governing the operation of mental health and substance use providers in Ontario evolved through multiple phases of lockdowns and emergency orders. While these orders were in effect, only ‘essential services’ were allowed to remain open. This included ‘workers who support food, shelter, and social services, addictions treatment and outreach, supervised consumption sites and other necessities of life for economically disadvantaged or otherwise needy individuals, such as those residing in shelters or children in care’. Employers in these services had to implement practices and procedures to protect the health and safety of employees, and employees were expected to work remotely where feasible [39, Annex A].

Participants and Recruitment

Individuals were eligible to participate if they worked in organisations that provided mental health and/or substance use services for people recently released from custody. We recruited through our longstanding partners and their networks who represented multi‐sector organisations with extensive experience responding to the complex needs of criminal‐legal‐system involved communities such as mental illness, brain injuries, and homelessness, including community and reentry service agencies, the Ministry of the Solicitor General, brain injury organisations, people with lived experience, and mutual support groups. We reached out to these contacts formally for the purpose of recruitment via email, sending them a study flyer to post onsite and an information sheet to distribute to their clients. Potential participants could then self‐select by contacting the study team.

At the beginning of the interview, via phone or Zoom, participants provided either written or verbal informed consent. Before commencing each interview, the person obtaining consent confirmed the participant had read and understood the consent form. If necessary, the person obtaining consent would read and discuss the content of the consent form. Once the participant had verbally consented or signed the consent form, the person obtaining the consent electronically signed the consent form. At the end of each interview, we asked participants to share study information with anyone in their circle of contacts who might also be interested in participating in the study.

Team Training and Interview Process

People with lived expertise were integral members of the research team. Senior members of the team provided training in research ethics, data collection and analysis, with a focus on qualitative interviewing. Training included shadowing senior team members during initial interviews to build expertise and confidence. All interviews, whether with a peer researcher or not, were conducted by two people, enabling one person to provide technical support while the other was able to focus solely on the interview. Debriefing sessions were used to provide learning opportunities for further interviews, identify key insights while memories were still fresh, and provide peer support after listening to difficult or painful stories [40].

Data Collection

In collaboration with persons with lived expertise and community partners, we developed a qualitative semi‐structured interview guide (Supporting Information S1) focused on the impact of the pandemic on client health, access to care, mental health and substance use service delivery, and providers' mental health and substance use challenges and sociocultural identities in relation to their work. Interviews were conducted through Zoom for Healthcare, a secure, web‐based application approved for healthcare and research purposes. Audio files of the interviews were transcribed verbatim and de‐identified by an external transcription service that was approved by the institutional ethics board.

Data Analysis

Data was reviewed and analysed using Applied Thematic Analysis [29] to examine the multidimensional (emotional, social and health) effects of COVID‐19‐related changes in people's lives. We identified modifiable processes and circumstances to assist in developing recommendations for policy change and programme modification.

We read the transcripts multiple times to become familiar with the data before doing open coding. We used a mix of inductive and deductive coding. We started with an open coding approach using two sensitising concepts as these were our key interests [41]: service disruptions and service adaptations. Each interview was coded by a single team member (L.S., A.M., M.F., F.M.). Following this initial open coding phase, the lead author systematised the coding system by reading all transcripts and initial codes and creating a definitive codebook. This codebook was approved by all team members and subsequently applied to all interviews. We developed initial themes using these codes and used member reflection [31] with community partners, some of whose staff were participants in the study, to determine whether the results were relevant to and resonated with them. Through these meetings and further reflection on the data, we developed the final themes for our analysis. Rigour was achieved through regular team meetings in which the codebook and themes were discussed, and all team members approved of the final themes (see Table 1). SP_XX denotes the participant ID for each quote.

Results

We conducted 20 semi‐structured qualitative interviews with a diversity of service providers (see Table 2 for sample characteristics). Interviews ranged between 44 and 150 min. The early COVID‐19 public health response created considerable problems for the ability of service providers to support people released from custody. They found that the policies that were implemented to mitigate the spread of the virus were not tailored to the mental health and substance use context thus leading to a disruption in service delivery. This had knock‐on effects both for potential service users and providers themselves. Criminal‐legal‐involved populations had to isolate at the same time as service providers could not reach out to them, which led to increased risks for substance use, overdose, and even suicide. Providers in turn felt the additional and growing pressure to help people released from custody, when policy made it exceedingly difficult for them to do so. In trying to adapt to these challenges, providers did find silver linings, creating and embracing opportunities to work together and provide better person‐centred care. Some of these practices were seen to offer opportunities for improving services in a post‐pandemic world. In the rest of this section, we address these themes in more detail.

Public Health Policy Not Tailored to Mental Health and Substance Use Context

The public health response in Ontario focused on minimising the spread of COVID‐19. While infection control policies affected everyone, they were particularly disruptive to the lives of people already facing complex social and health needs. Participants viewed the government's response as incongruous with the lived experience of underserved communities; it did not consider how the measures would affect the communities they served, particularly the lives and service needs of people released from custody during the pandemic.

Policy for the Privileged

A central critique within provider responses was that public health policies were designed for people who had resources–potentially because they were designed by people who had resources. For example, stay‐at‐home‐orders assume that people have stable housing, income and employment that will allow them to isolate from others or purchase personal protective equipment to stay safe; however a substantial number of people released from custody were precariously housed or unhoused and not able to isolate or socially distance. Initial restrictions such as the closure of libraries and affordable dine‐in restaurants (e.g., Tim Hortons, McDonald's) deprived people released from custody with vital lifelines to community supports, and subsequent closure of outdoor recreational spaces was highly challenging for those with precarious housing or who were unhoused. Even for those with housing, it was not necessarily suited to extended periods of isolation (e.g., small spaces, poor maintenance, no access to a yard or balcony). Later restrictions such as mask mandates were similarly not suitable for vulnerable communities, particularly people affected by mental health conditions. Throughout the pandemic, there was little to no consideration for how people without resources could adhere to policies. One service provider felt public health policy was a matter of privilege:

It felt like decisions were made to protect like the people like us, who have like homes and transportation and they weren't really looking at like how that impacts people with like perhaps less privilege or more vulnerability.

[SP_18]

Policy Disrupting Services

The public health response created problems not only for people released from custody, but also those who provided them support. Following the implementation of public health restrictions, participants were confronted by regulations from their organisations that were designed to comply with these restrictions without considering the impact they would have on their ability to deliver services to people released from custody. Participants felt that policies within their organisations were implemented top‐down by people who did not work in the institutions and with underserved communities. Because of this, providers quickly started to look for ‘loopholes to the things that were set up for COVID’ [SP_18] to support their clients. They also felt frustrated with policies that kept them safe but not their clients and that policies should have been more inclusive, placing equal emphasis on the safety of both providers and clients. Participants were further frustrated by administrative barriers to service provision, such as the need to apply for special permission from management to see clients:

I would have to get special permission from my manager who would have to check with the director to say if I could meet someone. I said I'll stay 10 feet away, I just, they need to see me, they need to know somebody is there for them so sometimes they would say no, I wasn't allowed to go out because the risk for myself was too high. They didn't want to put us at risk and while I appreciate them looking out for our safety they, the consideration given to the clients' safety wasn't there but that's what I was looking for.

[SP_07]

Participants perceived that government and organizational policies were based on tacit assumptions about what services were essential, ‘I think we have to recognise that those [mental health and substance use services] are essential. They can't, we cannot close’ [SP_02]. These policies restricted their ability to provide support and highlighted to them that even though they were formally deemed essential and could continue service delivery, the practical challenges (such as remote work) implied the government deemed these services non‐essential, even though they served ‘the groups and agencies that are hardest hit by situations like this’ [SP_02].

Another key concern was that policies were not sensitive to the cultural needs and histories of criminal‐legal populations, especially people from Indigenous communities. An Indigenous Elder who provides one‐on‐one counselling to men in prison and continues to provide guidance to some men post‐release reported a disruption to the continuum of care for Indigenous clients during the pandemic. They were cut off from in‐person cultural support inside the prison prior to discharge and continuing in the community post‐release.

Widespread lockdowns and service suspensions meant that upon release Indigenous clients faced a community reentry context with few to no community‐based services, including Indigenous‐focused services such as Friendship Centres, which provide a range of cultural and spiritual services and activities for the Indigenous community, including, for example, smudging, sharing circles, and sweat lodge ceremonies:

I couldn't send them [Indigenous men released from custody] to my normal resources. You couldn't go to the Friendship Centres. They were closed. Everything was closed…. I can't send you to the services that you need at the Friendship Centre where there's counselling and there's a men's group and there's spiritual stuff and there's sweats happening and none of that's happening…. This whole thing has really impacted the continuum of care and aftercare services huge, huge… When you get released from prison and the Friendship Centre is locked out, you don't have that option. That's a significant impact. You got nowhere to go. No support. So, you're walking around, you're more vulnerable to the old way of life.

(SP 10)

In the absence of those services for Indigenous clients, this Elder did what they could to fill the gap and continue to support clients who are ‘on road [that is, on their post‐release journey]’ (SP 10), even though it is outside the scope of their formal institutional role. Still, most of this work was ‘on the telephone. It's not the same [as in person guidance]’ (SP 10).

People Being Set Up to Fail

Participants stressed that the impact of the public health response on service access and delivery was setting people released from custody up to fail. Proscriptions against social gatherings and in‐person service delivery, combined with closure of public spaces such as affordable dine‐in restaurants and public recreational areas (e.g., parks, beaches) meant that individuals found themselves alone and isolated from community support, families, and friends. The service landscape was drastically changed overnight—some services closed, others had restricted hours, and many had become virtual. In short, people leaving custody needed greater supports at a time when far fewer options were available.

Isolation and Substance Use

Participants felt that the fear of COVID‐19 and the barriers to accessing services increased the emotional and psychological distress of people released from custody, which contributed to increased substance use, overdose, and suicide rates among clients. Particularly during the early stages of the pandemic, participants noticed a lot of fear among their clients, not only for their own health, but for the health of their friends and family. Forced isolation deprived clients of their social support networks and services. When people left custody they returned to a world when they could no longer re‐establish their social networks—according to one participant this left clients feeling unexpectedly like they were back in jail, again isolated, alone, and locked down:

We tried our best to connect with people. I think they also felt really lost ‘cause they felt like it was like jail again’. We had a few clients say they felt like they were in jail again when they got out. They were looking forward to being able to get out and be in the community and suddenly they had to find somewhere and lock themselves down again.

[SP_06]

Substance use among clients was a concern for the participants, who felt that COVID‐19 and the public health response were directly related to greater rates of use. They noted that deprived of potential support networks and services, clients seemed to use substances to cope with the fear and isolation they experienced with their release and in the days after release. Isolation also played a role in overdoses among their clients, who did not have a support network to enable them to use safely, emphasising again how the public health response did not take into account the needs of these communities.

I think the problem like a huge part of the addictions issues and problems is, like it's rooted in isolation and I think that's only gotten worse with the pandemic. People have been forced to isolate if they were using with a friend or they're kind of forced to use alone.

[SP_14]

Invisible Means Unavailable

With the implementation of social distancing regulation, providers were no longer able to connect with clients prior to release to help them prepare for community re‐entry, a second mechanism through which people were set up to fail. Before the pandemic, providers could connect with clients in correctional facilities to build rapport and to introduce them to services that would be available in the community after their release. With social distancing protocols, providers were no longer allowed to visit correctional facilities, which meant both became invisible to the other. Providers noted significant drop‐off in client engagement at the time of their release, which they attributed to this imposed invisibility:

It's almost like they've gotten used to the fact that we aren't as accessible as we were before. So, yeah, I think that was a major challenge … I had like significantly less client engagement and I attribute that to like not being able to make face‐to‐face connections in the jail.

[SP_19]

Service Barriers Everywhere

The public health response posed barriers not only to service visibility, but also to service accessibility for people released from custody. Providers noted that the government's public health response deprived clients of walk‐in options and other established practices for accessing services: ‘Nothing really substitutes the in‐person stuff and that was the biggest change for everybody and it was a pretty drastic change for our clients too who are used to being able to come and just open the doors and come for service whenever they need it’ [SP_05]. At the same time, service users were contending with a complex set of post‐release challenges and constraints that were made all the more difficult with pandemic regulations. For example, requirements of community supervision meant that people abide by a set of conditions of release (e.g., curfews, appointments with probation officers, abstinence from substance use, restrictions on accessing internet‐enabled devices). However, the pandemic made it more challenging to manage these conditions, invariably leading to breaches and putting people at risk of re‐arrest: ‘…so we did have a few people where probation breach was almost an unavoidable situation because they couldn't get the services they needed to kind of maintain outside of an institution’ [SP_11].

The restrictions on in‐person interactions, whether social or professional, forced a shift to remote forms of communication, particularly video and other virtual services. While these were appropriate for those with access to the necessary technology (e.g., smartphones, reliable internet), environment (e.g., private space), and digital skills, people released from incarceration were less able to make use of this new virtual world. According to participants, clients often lacked access to technology and to public spaces where they might access computers or free internet:

So, the challenge that remains is clients having access to a cell phone or some type of technology. Again, they were not able to go to the public library or our social planning office or any of those other hubs in the community to use a computer so they were equally disconnected as, from us as we were from them.

[SP_13]

Without the ability to build connections with clients prior to release and with the only ‘safe’ recourse to public health policy being virtual services as opposed to going out into the community or offering walk‐in options, participants noted a reduction in the number of criminal‐legal‐involved clients attending the agency when they stopped providing walk‐in services:

So, we had a significant reduction in people who were actually accessing us who had been involved with justice at that point because a lot of those people would have come through our walk‐ins and so they didn’t have a place to walk‐in anymore and so we weren’t seeing them as often.

[SP_11]

Stretching Staff to Breaking Point

The pandemic and the impact of the public health response also created a challenging situation for service providers. With the increasing needs of their clients, they felt pressure and often wanted to work on‐site (e.g., office, hospital) where that was allowed, but they also experienced fear of infection, potentially putting themselves and their family at risk. This was particularly acute when little was known about COVID‐19 (e.g., health and mortality risks, who was vulnerable, method of spread). Providers worried about their own health and the health of their clients released from custody, who increasingly risked overdose and suicide.

Health‐Related Fear

The many uncertainties around COVID‐19 created an atmosphere of fear. Providers were expected and wanted to support clients released from custody, and they initially did so with limited access to personal protective equipment or knowledge of how to properly use it. Participants noted that the fear and anxiety around these uncertainties added to the strain on their own mental health.

I remember that quite vividly like March 2020 [when the World Health Organisation designated COVID‐19 a pandemic] being terrifying and being absolutely probably at a 10 out of 10 in terms of my own anxiety and being more emotional and being more tearful at that time in my anxiety.

[SP_17]

Feeling Powerless

Participants felt demoralised by the restrictions placed on the type and amount of support they could provide. They were acutely aware of the vulnerability of clients leaving custody at the time of the crisis, and while they wanted to help, there was often little they could do. One provider stated that, ‘in my heart I try very hard for my clients, I want to support them any way I can’ [SP_07]. When they could not offer services, even though this was a result of government regulations, they took it personally:

I had a client who passed away from an overdose and I sort of took that on for a bit like had I only found him a Safe Bed you know if I had just phoned back one more time to see if there was a spot that day maybe he wouldn't have been where he was to get the drugs you know so a lot of guilt anxiety you know questioning myself whether I was doing a good job you know so that's how that affected me.

[SP_07]

Although the system was failing them, providers still felt personally responsible to their clients. Because many support programs for housing or mental health and substance use could not operate as usual during the early stages of the pandemic, providers could offer their clients released from custody little support. They were confronted by the immense needs of this population every day, but there was often nothing they could do:

I had clients coming to my office bawling their eyes out, desperate for detox programming and I, there was nothing I could do (…) the substance use services for me was a complete write‐off this whole time and women really suffered from it. And I was powerless. There was nothing I could do.

[SP_21]

Frustration With Restrictions

The correctional system implemented government policies in ways that limited providers' abilities to support clients released from custody. Participants commented that correctional facilities did not consider or coordinate with community‐based providers on how best to maintain or facilitate service delivery while complying with provincial rules and restrictions. This led to a situation where clients were unaware of the support that might be available to them upon release and providers unaware of client release times and types of support they needed at time of release.

Providers reported that they were frustrated with this lack of commitment from correctional facilities, which made it impossible for them to do their job and made it all the harder for people released from custody to be safe and access supports at discharge. One provider felt clients were left to fend for themselves, when discharge plans could have been adapted in a way that would comply with COVID regulations and allow for support for clients in need:

So this is why I signed up for this [study] cause it’s been, that was just terrifying and really alarming, you know, clients were released from custody, we had a number of them and there was no plan. They were told to like attend a clinic. They didn’t call us. They didn’t share anything. They didn’t get consent to send us records.

[SP_20]

No Time to Recover

The amplified needs and distress experienced by clients in the criminal‐legal‐system coupled with the increased demands placed on providers to deliver services left them little time to process their own needs, health and safety. This created a flashpoint for providers experiencing trauma and burnout themselves.

…you know I think we really got a taste and very quickly, of what burnout looks like because it happened so fast, like the switch happened so quickly that I think it was more detectable than when burnout sort of happens over time. That sort of like incremental frog already in the cold water as it warms up. It was like into the boiling pot, crisis mode.

[SP_15]

Providers often had to witness the impact of the COVID‐19 restrictions on their clients. With increased overdoses and suicides, providers routinely faced devastating loss and vicarious trauma. One provider reported that 16 clients and an employee died during the pandemic:

…as of today we lost 16 people that were known to [service agency] and one employee during this time. One person to suicide and the rest to overdose so we haven't even caught our breath because at least once a month we are grieving someone.

[SP_13]

Those providers who could still meet with clients in‐person (e.g., those working in hospitals) had to consider the risk of infection, as well as the risk of transmitting the virus to friends and family members. Some found ways to manage: one participant changed clothes in their garage and showered before entering the house, but that was not an option for everyone. For other providers, the only course of action was to forego seeing their friends and family. This social isolation deprived them of critical support and exacerbated their emotional and psychological burden.

Finding ‘Silver Linings’

As the pandemic progressed and service providers were coming to grips with the specific restrictions and their impact on people released from custody, they started to develop practices that would allow them to mitigate against disruptions. In fact, some ways in which they redesigned service delivery entailed removing long‐existing barriers and improving coordination between providers, thereby facilitating person‐centred care and organisational changes that could be maintained beyond the pandemic.

Removing Barriers

Public health restrictions created multiple barriers to services post‐release. This meant that individuals released from custody were often unaware what services were available, and providers were often unable to do outreach, missing a timely intervention point critical to successful reentry: ‘If you don't catch people when they're wanting to change, you don't catch them at all’ [SP_11].

While providers struggled with these constraints, in order to do the best in a difficult situation, providers started to make efforts to reduce pre‐existing barriers and improve access to support. For example, tailoring service delivery meant being able to better meet the needs of clients in the pandemic environment (e.g., providing flexible options for clients to access services):

So, we tried to remove some of the barriers just to get some appointments for anyone who would have accessed us through a walk‐in. So, we just said you can call reception and reception was given free range to just book them right into counselling openings wherever there was one so it used to be there was a certain section that was reserved in our schedules for the walk‐ins and so we removed that and said anywhere if someone calls it’s walk‐in related, they just go into our schedule so that was one system strategy that we tried to use to remove some barriers.

[SP_11]

Although virtual services could be a barrier for some clients; for others it was found to lower barriers to access, such as travel costs and the anxiety of being in a group setting. For participants, this allowed greater flexibility to provide services in‐office or remotely—even from home. For a service provider based in rural Ontario virtual care had its advantages as clients no longer had to travel hours for service which for many had been an insurmountable barrier. Another provider mentioned a hospitalised client who was able to access services virtually.

Working Together Works

For the most part, the mental health and substance use service landscape is siloed, lacking coordination of care. This situation is perpetuated by competition for limited funding. However, as providers noted, the impact public health restrictions were having on their ability to provide services and thereby on the lives of people released from custody, they recognized a need to work together. Providers made an effort to bridge silos within the sector during the pandemic, making for better coordination of care for people released from custody.

For the first time in my duration of the social service sector I truly saw people working together in a way that did was not competitive, was not about who's going to have these funding outcomes. It was really about how are we going to get food and basic necessities to these folks.

[SP_02]

Services began planning together to support multiple clients rather than clients within agencies. The goal was to coordinate care for clients across agencies with mutual plans of action:

I’ve had more instances where there has actually been more, rather than just sharing information there’s been sort of plan‐making between me and other providers working with people within the justice system where it’s sort of like okay, this is the plan, this is what we’re thinking we’re doing, how do we manage this thing; right? So, there’s been a lot more collaborative planning rather than just maintenance conversations.

[SP_11]

Participants felt that overcoming the siloed nature of community support service was not only necessary during the pandemic, but in a way was a positive change that was made possible by the pandemic, one that could be carried forward.

Discussion

This paper explored provider perspectives on the delivery of mental health and substance use services for people released from custody during the COVID‐19 pandemic. From the perspectives of providers, pandemic public health policies failed to consider the needs of their clients, limiting their ability to access ‘essential’ services. While the intent of the public health response was to keep everyone safe by reducing spread of infection, in practice, it further isolated and made invisible an already underserved community. Providers experienced frustration with the prioritisation of staff safety over the urgent needs of clients, challenging their ability to provide even a minimum standard of care. Other pandemic‐related studies of service provision of underserved populations also point to this difficulty balancing client‐staff safety and providing adequate care to clients [27, 42, 43]. In contrast, studies show that mental health providers serving general populations ‘maintained or increased quality of care, therapeutic relationships, and communication with their patients’ [44, p. 6]. These findings align with the view of many participants in our study felt that policy was created for the privileged—that is, those with the knowledge, skills, and resources (e.g., digital literacy, computer and internet access, stable housing, financial resources, and social support) required to comply with and adapt to pandemic policies while still being able to access essential mental health and substance use care. This could be attributed to the one‐size‐fits‐all policy implemented using a top down approach by those without firsthand experience with underserved communities which created undue administrative burden for agencies serving these communities [26, 45].

Participants felt that closure of drop‐in/walk‐in services significantly hindered provider‐client interaction [26]. In the post‐data collection consultations with our community partners, we heard that the systemic deficiencies noted in the study findings are enduring historical challenges in the criminal‐legal system. The pandemic intensified already existing inequities. For example, providers often noted how isolation and lack of social interaction contributed to the worsening of substance use disorders [45, 46]. This is consistent with previous research which reported a 54% decrease in attendance in substance use treatment among people in the criminal‐legal‐system [47]. Furthermore, study participants were unable to meet with clients in person, both inside and outside of jails, detentions centres and prisons, to build trusting and safe relationships. This challenge to build trust and form connections with clients contributes to low engagement in therapy and loss to follow‐up after release, particularly in a virtual service delivery environment [48, 49].

Clients experienced practical barriers to accessing virtual services. With the majority of services shifting to virtual care, equitable access to services remains an important concern [45]. A reoccurring theme in our interviews and other studies was the inability to access necessary technology, the lack of technological literacy, and the difficulty in finding adequate spaces (e.g., comfortable, private, safe) to attend virtual appointments [45, 46, 48, 49]. It was difficult for people released from custody to follow conditions of release prior to the onset of the pandemic; however, the increased mental health and substance use needs paired with the barriers to service made it almost impossible.

Providers noted their own mental and physical health concerns that emerged during the pandemic. Consistent with previous research, providers were under immense stress related to feelings of fear, powerlessness, frustration, and grief [26, 42, 45, 46]. Coupled with this many felt frustration due to ‘ambiguous guidelines, insufficient response efforts and, in some cases, inaction entirely from the government [which] undercut [their ability] to make informed, safe and practical decisions regarding service provision’ [26, p. 7], also evident in our study findings. Also noted was frustration with the restrictions in the correctional facilities that prevented participants from meeting with clients pre‐release to establish rapport and begin to create connections to community services for clients. The psychological burden of grieving client deaths also created their own trauma and mental health challenges [26, 50, 51] which permeated their personal lives, making it difficult to keep work and home life separate. One study reported an unmet need of 79.5% for mental health and substance use care among direct service providers in Canada during the pandemic [51, 52]. Beyond providing access, providers must be afforded the necessary time to seek support, as participants also report not having time due to other commitments [51]. With insufficient funding and lack of other types of resources, there is a significant potential for burnout among those who provide mental health and substance use services during a public health crisis, threatening the sustainability of these organisations [52, 53].

From the ashes of the crisis emerged service innovations that offer opportunities for future pandemic preparedness. The shift to virtual care was consistent across most social service organisations [45]. Though virtual care created added inequities for some, it did enable providers to transcend barriers to care related to travel, planning and anxiety for other clients. Provider perspectives of virtual services align with recent literature in which providers express support for the continuation of virtual services, as they improve access to services and flexibility in scheduling for both providers and clients [45, 49]. Innovations in collaboration also emerged with a deeper focus on client‐centred care and coordination of client care plans across sister organisations [46]. This aligns with wraparound models of care that are comprehensive, holistic, and person‐centred.

The findings speak to the resiliency of providers to care for clients in a hostile service environment [51, 52]. While agencies and providers create innovation under duress, governments should not place the responsibility on them alone to ensure equitable service delivery during pandemics; rather the burden of equitable care for underserved communities lies with policymakers. Lessons learnt during the pandemic by agencies and providers who supported people released from custody should be translated into pandemic preparedness policies to ensure our health, mental health, substance use, and criminal‐legal‐system sectors work in unison to support rather than further marginalise these communities.

Many of the disruptions discussed by participants concerned the wider landscape of mental health and substance use services in Ontario. Some of these findings may extend to service provision to other underserved communities, many of whom overlap and intersect with criminal‐legal‐involved populations, including people living in congregant settings, people experiencing poverty and homelessness, people experiencing high rates of mental health and substance use concerns, and people experiencing high rates of chronic health conditions and co‐morbidities. However, the disruptions to release planning and support services had a particular impact on criminal‐legal populations who were released into a society where they were not even aware of support systems (virtual or otherwise) and who were at particular risk of re‐incarceration.

Strengths and Limitations

The data were collected between May and August 2021, a time during the pandemic when services were still highly disrupted and staff and clients alike were adapting to a changing and hostile service landscape. This may have hindered our ability to recruit agencies and providers who were particularly burdened by the public health measures. Relying on self‐selection may also have biased our sample, for example, providers who had particularly difficult experiences during the pandemic may have been more motivated to participate. Using team discussions and member reflection provided credibility to our analysis. We did not conduct intercoder reliability to assess the rigour of the coding system. This could have potentially provided further opportunities for refinement of the codebook.

This paper only provides insights from providers. The perspectives of people released from custody during the pandemic are absent and are important to consider for policy development. The data, however, are rich and offer insights at ground zero for staff who provided care to particularly underserved communities. A particular strength of this study is its community‐based participatory research approach. We had input from partners on design/methodology (recruiting during a pandemic, developing data collection tools), and analysis/data interpretation. For the latter, we held meetings with our partners to review findings and hear their feedback. The qualitative interviews provide rich and detailed stories about the challenges and triumphs of working through the pandemic and the ‘silver linings’ that emerged in supporting clients. One of the key insights from providers was that the issues reflected in the data were not new. The failures in the system laid bare during the pandemic existed before and were exacerbated during the pandemic. They stressed it imperative that we highlight this message in publications from this study.

Conclusion

Several key messages emerge from the findings and our consultations with our community partners. First, for future pandemic preparedness, decision makers must consider the unintentional fallout that blanket policies can have on underserved communities and mitigate against unintended consequences. Solution‐focused strategies to support underserved communities will encompass shared decision‐making between health, shelter and housing, transportation, income support, and the criminal‐legal system—and especially the affected communities—with a focus on racial, ethnic, cultural, gender and sexual inclusion.

Second, resiliency is key to ensure ‘the system’ can survive the weight of a pandemic. The findings of this study showed that the resiliency of the staff was tested but many found ways to cope and work around restrictions to provide care and support for clients released during the pandemic into an adverse service environment. Applying the lessons learnt from the mental health and substance use service sector and their staff to future pandemic preparedness planning will enable effective decision‐making to ensure the health and safety of underserved communities and the human and social service systems that support them. The wellbeing of our communities relies on overall resilience of the system, which depends on the resilience of each of its components [54].

Third, the historical governmental underfunding of care and mishandling of matters related to people released from custody was exemplified during the pandemic. Their situation of crisis was intensified during the pandemic. Lack of access to mental health and substance use treatment, lack of access to shelter, unmet yet persistent health needs and chronic lack of funding in both the community service and criminal‐legal‐system to meet the needs of people released from custody is an historical failure of governments globally. Pandemic planning must reflect policies that consider all communities and ensure their health and wellbeing in times of crisis and in times of stability. Enhancing funding to bolster health, shelter and housing, other infrastructure and alternatives to custody will be important to transform our society and support pandemic preparedness.

Data Availability Statement

This study reports on qualitative data which by nature is difficult to fully anonymize. Participants were not asked to agree to their data being available for other studies, as they work with people who experience significant social and health burdens that are highly stigmatized, including criminalization, homelessness, and poverty. Requesting that they agree to share their data more publicly would have deterred some from taking part in the study and would have the potential to significantly alter the data obtained. Ethical approval for this research study was granted by Unity Health Toronto Research Ethics Board [REB 21‐007] on the basis that participants’ data was only accessible by the research team.
