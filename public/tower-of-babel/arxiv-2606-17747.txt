Abstract
. The problem of computing the volume of tubes in riemannian manifolds goes back to Weyl and Hotelling. Here we find explicit Taylor series for the volume of a tube in a Lie group equipped with a bi-invariant metric. The coefficients are smooth valuations, given by the convolution powers of the surface area valuation. We show that the tube coefficients can be naturally described as the unique valuations given by universal formulas through the formalism of differential graded Lie and Gerstenhaber algebras; in fact, they are generated by the gauge action on the Maurer–Cartan cone in the free differential graded Lie algebra on one generator. Moreover, we introduce a new convolution product on the corresponding free Gerstenhaber algebra which is compatible with the convolution of valuations and differential forms. To complete the picture, we show that a Lie group—not necessarily connected—admits a smooth bi-invariant valuation, beyond the Euler characteristic and the Haar measure, if and only if it admits a bi-invariant riemannian metric.

Introduction and main results

In 1939, extending previous work of Hotelling
[
25
]
, Weyl

Computing tube formulas in more general settings proved to be challenging, with few cases settled to date. In real space forms, tube formulas were worked out in works of Allendoerfer

Valuations on smooth manifolds were introduced by Alesker in the early 2000s, as a natural extension to smooth manifolds of the notion of valuation in convex geometry

The space of smooth valuations on

Alesker has introduced a natural product on

It was observed by Alesker that Weyl’s result implies the existence on a riemannian manifold

For any compact submanifold with corners

The so-called Lipschitz–Killing space

Furthermore, Fu–Wannerer

consists of exactly those valuations that are given by universal formulas in terms of the riemannian structure, more precisely using the Cartan apparatus of solder/connection/curvature forms.
Similar distinguished subspaces of valuations were also found in Kähler manifolds

11
]
and contact manifolds

In this paper we prove a Lie group counterpart of these results. Let
G
be a Lie group. The subspace
\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)^{G\times G}\subset\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)
of bi-invariant valuations admits a natural convolution product, introduced by Alesker and the first named author in the compact case and extended to the general case by the authors in
[

Assume
G
is equipped with a bi-invariant metric. We will show that the tube coefficients in

are given by universal formulas in terms of the Lie structure and the metric, and span a convolution subalgebra of
\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)^{G\times G}
.

While not every Lie group admits a bi-invariant metric, this is in fact the case for any Lie group admitting interesting smooth bi-invariant valuations, as our first theorem shows.

Theorem A
.

A Lie group
G
of dimension at least 2 admits a smooth bi-invariant valuation outside of
\Span\{\chi,\vol_{G}\}
if and only if
G
admits a bi-invariant riemannian metric.

In
[
10
, Theorem B]
we proved this under the additional assumption that
G
is connected. The general case established here relies on some deep results from group theory.

Next we look for an abstract space that will act as a space of universal formulas, generating valuations on arbitrary Lie groups equipped with a bi-invariant metric. Consider the free differential graded Lie algebra
\mathcal{A}
on one generator
\xi
, namely,
\mathcal{A}=\langle\xi,\gamma\rangle
with
\deg\xi=0
, and
\gamma=d\xi
. The corresponding graded exterior algebra
\mathcal{G}=\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
is the free differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra on one generator
\xi
. It comes equipped with two gradings, the degree
\deg
inherited from
\mathcal{A}
, and the wedge degree
\deg_{w}
, which we combine into
\Deg x=\deg x-\deg_{w}x
. It also has two differential operators:
d
which is inherited from
\mathcal{A}
, and the Koszul boundary operator
\partial
, which we combine into
Dx=dx+(-1)^{\Deg x+1}\partial x
. Finally, there are two products on
\mathcal{G}
: the Schouten–Nijenhuis extension of the Lie bracket on
\mathcal{A}
, and the wedge product. We will later show that these two products can also be naturally combined into one convolution product rendering
(\mathcal{G},\ast,\Deg,D)
a differential graded algebra.

For any Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
we have a realization map
\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}:\mathcal{G}\to\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}:=\Omega(\mathfrak{g},\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g})
, mapping
\xi
to the identity map
\id\in C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
, and
\gamma
to the tautological
1
-form
d(\id)\in\Omega^{1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
. Later
\mathfrak{g}
will be the Lie algebra of a Lie group equipped with a bi-invariant metric, and we will be considering a closely related spherical realization map
\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}^{S}
mapping
\mathcal{G}
to real-valued forms on the sphere bundle of
G
.

We define the space of abstract tube coefficients

\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}=\{\omega\in\mathcal{G}:\Deg\omega=-1,D\omega\in\mathcal{H}\},

where
\mathcal{H}
is spanned by all wedge products not having
\xi
as a factor. The degree restriction simply translates under the realization map to the requirement that a tube coefficient is an
(n-1)
-form on the sphere bundle, while the requirement
D\omega\in\mathcal{H}
effectively means that its de Rham differential is a vertical form (see definitions in Section
7
); up to a measure, any smooth valuation can be given by such an
(n-1)
-form.
We define also the closely associated space
\mathcal{D}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}=\{\tau\in\mathcal{H}:\Deg\tau=0,D\tau=0\}
. While a-priori it is only clear that
D(\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C})\subset\mathcal{D}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}
, we will see that in fact
\mathcal{D}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}
coincides with
D(\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C})
up to constants.

In
[
10
]
we defined a convolution product on
\Omega_{\ori}(SG)^{G\times G}\otimes\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
, where
\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
is the one-dimensional space of dual densities on the Lie algebra of
G
, which corresponds to a convolution product on
\Omega(\mathbb{P}_{+}(\mathfrak{g}^{*}),\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g})^{G\times G}
. One can similarly define a convolution on
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}=\Omega(\mathfrak{g},\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g})
, see Proposition
5.2
. We prove that the Gerstenhaber algebra
\mathcal{G}
admits a convolution product which corresponds to those various convolutions under the respective realization maps.

Theorem B
.

There exists a unique convolution product
*
on
\mathcal{G}
such that for every Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, the realization map
\Re:(\mathcal{G},\ast)\to(\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}},\ast)
is a morphism of algebras. It is associative, satisfies
\Deg(\tau\ast\zeta)=\Deg(\tau)+\Deg(\zeta)
and the Leibniz rule

D(\tau*\zeta)=D\tau*\zeta+(-1)^{\Deg\tau}\tau*D\zeta.

Moreover,
\mathcal{D}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}\subset(\mathcal{G},*)
is a convolution subalgebra.

We then find an explicit basis for
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}
, exploiting the formalism of Maurer–Cartan elements and the gauge action in the differential graded Lie algebra
\mathcal{A}
.
Define

\displaystyle\zeta_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle:=e^{\epsilon\xi}*0=-\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\epsilon^{i+1}}{(i+1)!}(\ad\xi)^{i}\gamma\in\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]],

\displaystyle\rho_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\varrho_{m}\epsilon^{m}:=\widehat{\exp}(-\zeta_{\epsilon})\in\mathcal{G}[[\epsilon]],

where the last exponent is in the sense of formal power series using the wedge product.

Theorem C
.

The space
\mathcal{TC}
of abstract tube coefficients is given by
\xi\wedge\mathcal{DTC}
.
\mathcal{DTC}
is spanned by
\varrho_{m}
,
m\geq 0
. Furthermore,
\varrho_{m}=\frac{1}{m!}\varrho_{1}^{\ast m}
.

Theorems

and
C
together tell us that on every Lie group
G
equipped with a bi-invariant metric, there is a distinguished subalgebra
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}(G)
of the convolution algebra of valuations on
G
that are constructed using an appropriate realization map from the algebra
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}
of abstract tube coefficients. Thus, for each such
G
we have a natural homomorphism of algebras
\mathbb{C}[t]\to\left(\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)^{G\times G}\otimes\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*}),*\right)
. This exhibits some formal resemblance with the valuation-theoretic version of Weyl’s principle
[
20
]
, which says that for each riemannian manifold, there is a natural homomorphism of algebras
\mathbb{C}[t]\to\left(\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(M),\cdot\right)
, mapping

to (a certain normalization of) the intrinsic volume
\mu_{k}
. In this case, the formal model of differential forms on the sphere bundle is based on riemannian data (connection and curvature forms), while in our case it is based on Lie algebraic data, combined with the choice of a bi-invariant metric.

We show that, when
G
is semisimple, the convolution algebra
\mathcal{TC}(G)
is infinite-dimensional, unless its Lie algebra is
\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
. In particular,
\mathcal{TC}(G)
is in general distinct from the Lipschitz–Killing subspace. An important exception is the case
G=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where the two spaces are not only isomorphic as vector spaces, but, through the Alesker–Fourier transform
[
5
,
18
]
, as algebras.

Finally we elucidate the geometric meaning of
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}
. Consider a Lie group
G
equipped with a bi-invariant metric. First we have a map
\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}^{S}
that to an element
\omega\in\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}
associates an
(n-1)
-form on the sphere bundle of
G
. It is given by the composition of the map
\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}
above with the restriction to the sphere bundle and with the Hodge star operator. Finally, integration over the normal cycle of
\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}^{S}
defines a valuation
\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}^{V}(\omega)\in\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)\otimes\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
.

Denote by
\upsilon_{m}\in\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)\otimes\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
,
m\geq 1
, the valuation given by

\upsilon_{m}=\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}^{V}(\omega_{m})=\int_{\nc(\bullet)}\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}^{S}\omega_{m},

where
\omega_{m}:=\frac{1}{m}\xi\wedge\varrho_{m-1}
. Denote by
\upsilon_{0}:=\vol\otimes\vol^{*}
the unit element in
\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)\otimes\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
.

Theorem D
.

Let
G
be a Lie group equipped with a bi-invariant metric. Then for all
Z\in\mathcal{P}(G)
and
\epsilon\ll 1
,

\vol(Z_{\epsilon})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\upsilon_{k}(Z)\epsilon^{k}.

Thus the image
\mathcal{TC}(G)\subset\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)\otimes\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
of
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}
under the realization map is spanned by the tube volume coefficients.

We will give two different proofs of this theorem. The first is more abstract and uses the free differential Gerstenhaber algebra described above together with recent results on tubes in riemannian manifolds due to Solanes and Trillo. The second approach is more geometric and direct, but gives less insight on the universal nature of the tube coefficients.

While we assume that our bi-invariant metric is riemannian, it is straightforward to accommodate the case of a bi-invariant Finsler metric by adjusting the definition of the realization map
\Re^{S}_{\mathfrak{g}}
.

2.
Differential graded Lie algebras and Gerstenhaber algebras

In this section we build an abstract framework which will later be used to construct a canonical space of invariant valuations on a Lie group. More precisely, we construct explicitly the free Gerstenhaber algebra generated by a graded vector space. Its existence also follows from general category-theoretic considerations, however, the explicit construction will be useful. In particular, it allows us to construct the Koszul boundary operator
\partial
on this algebra which will eventually be used to define an abstract analog of the space of closed differential forms on the sphere bundle of a Lie group. We will show that this space corresponds, via the exponential map, to the Maurer–Cartan elements in a differential graded Lie algebra. Finally, to construct the Maurer–Cartan elements, we use the gauge action, a procedure well known in general deformation theory.

Our main reference is
[
27
]
. We will consider the following categories:

•

\mathbf{GV}
: graded real vector spaces.

•

\mathbf{DGV}
: differential graded vector spaces.

•

\mathbf{DBGV}
: differential bigraded vector spaces.

•

\mathbf{GLA}
: graded Lie algebras.

•

\mathbf{DGLA}
: differential graded Lie algebras.

•

\mathbf{DBGLA}
: differential bigraded Lie algebras.

•

\mathbf{DGGA}
: differential graded Gerstenhaber algebras.

•

\mathbf{GUAA}
: graded unitary associative algebras.

In all cases the morphisms are the obvious ones, i.e., those preserving all structures like degrees, differentials, brackets etc.

2.1.
Graded vector spaces and differential graded vector spaces

Definition 2.1
.

(i)

A

Definition 2.6

-morphism, we can forget the differential and look at this as a graded vector space morphism. By the universal property of the free graded Lie algebra, we obtain a unique
\mathbf{GLA}
-morphism
\tilde{\phi}:\mathcal{A}\to C
.

We claim that it is also a
\mathbf{DGLA}
-morphism, i.e. that
\tilde{\phi}d_{\mathcal{A}}x=d_{C}\tilde{\phi}x
for all
x\in\mathcal{A}
. We may assume
x=n\rho(v_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{n})
. We then use induction on
n
, noting that the case of
n=1
holds by assumption. Denoting
w=(n-1)\rho(v_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes v_{n})
, we have
x=[v_{1},w]
and so by the induction assumption

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}d_{\mathcal{A}}x

\displaystyle=\tilde{\phi}[d_{V}v_{1},w]+(-1)^{\overline{v}_{1}}\tilde{\phi}[v_{1},d_{\mathcal{A}}w]

\displaystyle=[\tilde{\phi}d_{V}v_{1},\tilde{\phi}w]+(-1)^{\overline{v}_{1}}[\tilde{\phi}v_{1},\tilde{\phi}d_{\mathcal{A}}w]

\displaystyle=[d_{C}\tilde{\phi}v_{1},\tilde{\phi}w]+(-1)^{\overline{v}_{1}}[\tilde{\phi}v_{1},d_{C}\tilde{\phi}w]

\displaystyle=d_{C}[\tilde{\phi}v_{1},\tilde{\phi}w]

\displaystyle=d_{C}\tilde{\phi}[v_{1},w]

\displaystyle=d_{C}\tilde{\phi}x.

This finishes the proof.
∎

2.4.
Graded exterior powers

We collect some basic facts about graded exterior powers and refer to
[
27
, Section 10.1]
for more details.

Let
V
be a graded vector space, where we write
\deg v
for the degree of a homogeneous element.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
V
be a graded vector space. Then there exists a graded unitary associative algebra
(\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}V,\wedge,\deg_{w})
that is unique up to a unique
\mathbf{GUAA}
-morphism and a
\mathbf{GV}
-morphism
\iota:V\to\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}V
such that the following universal property is satisfied:
For every graded unitary associative algebra
G
and every
\mathbf{GV}
-morphism
\phi:V\to G
such that

\phi(v_{1})\phi(v_{2})=(-1)^{\deg v_{1}\deg v_{2}+1}\phi(v_{2})\phi(v_{1}),

there exists a unique
\mathbf{GUAA}
-morphism
\tilde{\phi}:\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}V\to G
such that the following diagram commutes

\textstyle{\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\tilde{\phi}}
\textstyle{V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota}
\scriptstyle{\phi}
\textstyle{G}

Proof.

The proof is the usual one: Take
T(V)=\bigoplus_{k}V^{\otimes k}
the tensor algebra of
V
and factor out by the two-sided ideal
I
in
T(V)
generated by all elements
v_{1}\otimes v_{2}+(-1)^{\deg v_{1}\deg v_{2}}v_{2}\otimes v_{1}
, where
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
are homogeneous elements. Clearly
I
is homogeneous with respect to the natural grading on
T(V)
, and hence the quotient inherits a grading denoted
\deg_{w}
.
∎

We will call
\deg_{w}
the
wedge degree
. When convenient we put
\widehat{X}=\deg_{w}X
.

Proposition 2.10
.

The grading
\deg
of
V
extends to a grading on
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}V
by imposing additivity under the wedge product. For

X,Y\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}V
bi-homogeneous it then holds that

X\wedge Y=(-1)^{\deg X\deg Y+\deg_{w}X\deg_{w}Y}Y\wedge X.

(4)

Proof.

We define a grading
\deg
on
T(V)
such that for all homogeneous elements
v_{1},\ldots,v_{l}\in V
,

\deg(v_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{l})=\sum_{i}\deg v_{i}.

The ideal
I
is homogeneous, and hence the quotient
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}V
inherits a grading
\deg
. The displayed equation then follows by induction on the wedge degree of
X
and
Y
.
∎

We will need another description of
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}V
as a subset of
T(V)
instead of quotient. For homogeneous elements
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
and a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, the Koszul sign
\varepsilon(\pi)
is the signature of the restriction of
\pi
to the indices such that
v_{i}
has odd degree. The antisymmetric Koszul sign is defined by
\chi(\pi):=\varepsilon(\pi)\sgn(\pi)
.

We define the linear operators
p_{k}:V^{\otimes k}\to V^{\otimes k}
by
p_{0}=\id
,

\displaystyle p_{k}(v_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{k})=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\chi(\pi)v_{\pi_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{\pi_{k}},

(5)

and set
p:=\bigoplus_{k}p_{k}:T(V)\to T(V)
.
Then one can check that
p
is a projection with kernel
I
, from which it follows that
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}V\cong\mathrm{Image}(p)\subset T(V)
.

2.5.
Differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra

Definition 2.11
.

A
differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra (DGGA)
is a quadruple
(\mathcal{G},d,[\bullet,\bullet],\wedge)
such that
(\mathcal{G},d,[\bullet,\bullet])
is a differential bigraded Lie algebra,
(\mathcal{G},\wedge)
is a bigraded unitary associative algebra and the two structures are compatible in the sense that for
X,Y,Z\in\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle d(X\wedge Y)

\displaystyle=dX\wedge Y+(-1)^{\deg X}X\wedge dY

(6)

\displaystyle[X\wedge Y,Z]

\displaystyle=X\wedge[Y,Z]+(-1)^{\deg X\deg Y+\deg_{w}X\deg_{w}Y}Y\wedge[X,Z].

(7)

We remark that from the antisymmetry of the Lie bracket and (
7
), it follows that

[X,Y\wedge Z]=[X,Y]\wedge Z+(-1)^{\deg Y\deg Z+\deg_{w}Y\deg_{w}Z}[X,Z]\wedge Y.

(8)

Proposition 2.12
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a DGLA, there is a unique structure of a DGGA on
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
such that the inclusion
\mathcal{A}\to\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
is a
\mathbf{DBGLA}
-morphism.
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
is called the
free differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra
generated by
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

The grading of
\mathcal{A}
induces a grading
\deg
on
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
. The second grading
\deg_{w}
comes from the natural grading
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}=\bigoplus_{k}\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathcal{A}
.

We have to construct the differential
d
and the Lie bracket
[\bullet,\bullet]
on
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
. Uniqueness of both is clear from (
6
), (
7
) and (
8
).

To construct
d
, note that the map
d:T(\mathcal{A})\to T(\mathcal{A})
defined in (
3
) satisfies
dI\subset I
and hence induces a map
d:\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}\to\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
. Equation (
6
) is then obvious.

The graded Schouten–Nijenhuis extension of the Lie bracket (see
[
14
, Equation (II.14)]
) is given by

\displaystyle[v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{k},w_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge w_{l}]:=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\epsilon_{i,j}v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\widecheck{v}_{i}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{k}\wedge[v_{i},w_{j}]\wedge w_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\widecheck{w}_{j}\wedge\ldots\wedge w_{l},

where
\widecheck{v}_{i}
means that
v_{i}
is omitted, and

\epsilon_{i,j}:=(-1)^{i+j+\deg v_{i}\sum_{a=i+1}^{k}\deg v_{a}+\deg w_{j}\sum_{b=1}^{j-1}\deg w_{b}}.

The bihomogeneity of the bracket is obvious and the bigraded skew-symmetry is an easy computation. We have to prove the bigraded Jacobi identity and the bigraded Leibniz rule (see Definitions
2.6
and
2.7
). Our proof of these equations is adapted from
[
27
, Lemma 9.1.4]
.

For
X\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
, let us write
\ad_{X}:=[X,\bullet]
. One can easily verify that
\ad_{X}
is a graded derivation in the sense that

\ad_{X}(Y\wedge Z)=\ad_{X}(Y)\wedge Z+(-1)^{\deg X\deg Y+\deg_{w}Y(\deg_{w}X-1)}Y\wedge\ad_{X}Z.

(9)

Using the antisymmetry of the bracket, this implies eq. (
7
).

With the Leibniz rule (
6
), we see that the operator

A_{X}:=d\circ\ad_{X}+(-1)^{\deg X+1}\ad_{X}\circ d-\ad_{dX}

satisfies

A_{X}(Y\wedge Z)=A_{X}Y\wedge Z\pm Y\wedge A_{X}Z,

(10)

where the sign depends on the bidegrees of
X,Y
. We want to show that
A_{X}=0
, which is precisely the bigraded Leibniz rule. Suppose first that
X
and
Y
are both of wedge degree
1
, i.e. elements of
\mathcal{A}
. Then the equation
A_{X}Y=0
boils down to the graded Leibniz rule (
2
) satisfied in the DGLA
\mathcal{A}
. By (
10
) we conclude that
A_{X}Y=0
for
X\in\mathcal{A}
and
Y\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
. Using the skew-symmetry of the bracket, we see that
A_{X}Y=\pm A_{Y}X
, where the sign depends on the bidegrees of
X,Y
. We thus have
A_{Y}X=0
for all
Y\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A},X\in\mathcal{A}
. By (
10
), we even have
A_{Y}X=0
for all
X,Y\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
, which we wanted to prove.

Let us next prove the bigraded Jacobi identity (see Definition
2.6
). With

T(X,Y):=\ad_{X}\circ\ad_{Y}+(-1)^{\overline{X}\overline{Y}+(\widehat{X}-1)(\widehat{Y}-1)+1}\ad_{Y}\circ\ad_{X}-\ad_{\ad_{X}Y}

we want to prove that
T(X,Y)=0
for all
X,Y\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
. Repeated application of (
9
) shows that
T(X,Y)
satisfies the equation

T(X,Y)(Z\wedge W)=T(X,Y)Z\wedge W\pm Z\wedge T(X,Y)W.

(11)

where the sign depends on the bidegrees of
X,Y,Z
.
If
X,Y,Z\in\mathcal{A}
, then
T(X,Y)Z=0
by the graded Jacobi identity and the graded skew-symmetry (Definition
2.3
) in the graded Lie algebra
\mathcal{A}
. By (
11
) it follows that
T(X,Y)Z=0
for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{A},Z\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
. An easy computation shows that

T(X,Y)Z=\pm T(Y,Z)X=\pm T(Z,X)Y,

where the sign depends on the bidegrees of
X,Y,Z
. It follows from what we already have shown that
T(Y,Z)X=0
for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{A},Z\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
. By (
11
) this even holds for all
X\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
. Hence
T(Z,X)Y=0
for all
Z,X\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
and
Y\in\mathcal{A}
, and finally for all
Y\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
by (
11
).
∎

For
\mathcal{G}:=\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
, we write
\mathcal{G}^{j}=\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{j}\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{G}_{k}=\{x\in\mathcal{G}:\deg x=k\}
.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
G
be a differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra and
\mathcal{A}
a differential graded Lie algebra, considered as a bigraded vector space with

. Let

be a

-morphism

.

Proof.

By the universal property of the graded exterior power (Proposition

), there is a unique
\mathbf{GUAA}
-morphism
\tilde{\phi}
extending

. The Lie bracket on
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
and the Lie bracket on

both satisfy eqs. (
4
) and (

), hence
\tilde{\phi}
is compatible with the Lie brackets. Similarly, the differentials on
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
and

), hence
\tilde{\phi}

\tilde{\phi}

-morphism.
∎

2.6.
The Koszul boundary operator

Definition 2.15
.

Given a DGLA
\mathcal{A}
and the associated DGGA
\mathcal{G}=\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
, the
Koszul boundary operator

\partial:\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}\to\mathcal{G}^{\bullet-1}
is defined inductively on the wedge degree as follows: For
v\in\mathcal{A}
and
X\in\mathcal{G}
we set
\partial(v)=0
and

\partial(v\wedge X)=[v,X]-v\wedge\partial X.

(12)

Note that
\partial
decreases
\deg_{w}
by
1
while preserving
\deg
. Moreover,

\partial(\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{m}\mathcal{A}_{1})\subset\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{m-2}\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}.

Proposition 2.16
.

It holds for
X,Y\in\mathcal{G}
that

\partial(X\wedge Y)=\partial X\wedge Y+(-1)^{\deg_{w}X-1}[X,Y]+(-1)^{\deg_{w}X}X\wedge\partial Y.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
\deg_{w}X+\deg_{w}Y
. The statement is clear for
\deg_{w}X=\deg_{w}Y=1
. We may assume by linearity that
X,Y
are simple wedge products. As both sides of the identity are easily seen to be supersymmetric in
X,Y
, it suffices to consider the case
X=u\wedge X^{\prime}
with
u\in\mathcal{A}
.

The verification is now straightforward. We have by the induction hypothesis

\displaystyle\partial(X\wedge Y)

\displaystyle=\partial(u\wedge X^{\prime}\wedge Y)

\displaystyle=[u,X^{\prime}\wedge Y]-u\wedge\partial(X^{\prime}\wedge Y)

\displaystyle=[u,X^{\prime}]\wedge Y+(-1)^{\overline{X^{\prime}}\overline{Y}+\widehat{X^{\prime}}\widehat{Y}}[u,Y]\wedge X^{\prime}

\displaystyle-u\wedge\partial X^{\prime}\wedge Y+(-1)^{\widehat{X^{\prime}}}u\wedge[X^{\prime},Y]+(-1)^{\widehat{X^{\prime}}+1}u\wedge X^{\prime}\wedge\partial Y.

This should be shown to equal

\displaystyle\partial(u\wedge X^{\prime})\wedge Y+(-1)^{\widehat{X}-1}[u\wedge X^{\prime},Y]+(-1)^{\widehat{X}}u\wedge X^{\prime}\wedge\partial Y

\displaystyle=[u,X^{\prime}]\wedge Y-u\wedge\partial X^{\prime}\wedge Y

\displaystyle+(-1)^{\widehat{X^{\prime}}}u\wedge[X^{\prime},Y]+(-1)^{\overline{X^{\prime}}\overline{u}}X^{\prime}\wedge[u,Y]

\displaystyle+(-1)^{\widehat{X}}u\wedge X^{\prime}\wedge\partial Y,

which is easily seen to hold.
∎

Proposition 2.17
.

The Koszul boundary operator is a derivation for the Lie bracket on
\mathcal{G}
:

\partial[X,Y]=[\partial X,Y]+(-1)^{\deg_{w}X+1}[X,\partial Y],\quad X,Y\in\mathcal{G}.

Proof.

We do induction on
\deg_{w}X+\deg_{w}Y
. The assertion is clear for
\deg_{w}X=\deg_{w}Y=1
. We may assume by linearity that
X,Y
are simple wedge products. As the identity is easily seen to be supersymmetric in
X,Y
, we may assume
\deg_{w}X\leq\deg_{w}Y
and
Y=v\wedge Y^{\prime}
with
v\in\mathcal{A}
.

The verification is now straightforward. We have by Proposition
2.16
and the induction hypothesis

\displaystyle\partial[X,Y]

\displaystyle=\partial([X,v]\wedge Y^{\prime}+(-1)^{\overline{v}\overline{Y^{\prime}}+\widehat{Y^{\prime}}}[X,Y^{\prime}]\wedge v)

\displaystyle=[\partial X,v]\wedge Y^{\prime}+(-1)^{\widehat{X}+1}[[X,v],Y^{\prime}]+(-1)^{\widehat{X}}[X,v]\wedge\partial Y^{\prime}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{\overline{v}\overline{X}+\widehat{X}+1}[v,[X,Y^{\prime}]]+(-1)^{\overline{v}\overline{X}+\widehat{X}}v\wedge\partial[X,Y^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=[\partial X,v]\wedge Y^{\prime}+(-1)^{\widehat{X}+1}[[X,v],Y^{\prime}]+(-1)^{\widehat{X}}[X,v]\wedge\partial Y^{\prime}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{\overline{v}\overline{X}+\widehat{X}+1}[v,[X,Y^{\prime}]]+(-1)^{\overline{v}\overline{X}+\widehat{X}}v\wedge[\partial X,Y^{\prime}]+(-1)^{\overline{v}\overline{X}+1}v\wedge[X,\partial Y^{\prime}].

We ought to show this equals

\displaystyle[\partial X,v\wedge Y^{\prime}]+(-1)^{\widehat{X}+1}[X,\partial(v\wedge Y^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=[\partial X,v\wedge Y^{\prime}]+(-1)^{\widehat{X}+1}[X,[v,Y^{\prime}]-v\wedge\partial Y^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=[\partial X,v]\wedge Y^{\prime}+(-1)^{\overline{v}\overline{Y^{\prime}}+\widehat{Y^{\prime}}}[\partial X,Y^{\prime}]\wedge v

\displaystyle+(-1)^{\widehat{X}+1}[X,[v,Y^{\prime}]]+(-1)^{\widehat{X}}[X,v]\wedge\partial Y^{\prime}+(-1)^{\widehat{X}+\overline{v}\overline{Y^{\prime}}+\widehat{Y^{\prime}}+1}[X,\partial Y^{\prime}]\wedge v,

which easily follows from the supersymmetry relations and the Jacobi identity.
This completes the induction and the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.18
.

It holds that
\partial^{2}=0
on
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

If
\partial^{2}X=\partial^{2}Y=0
, it follows by Propositions
2.17
and
2.16
that also
\partial^{2}(X\wedge Y)=0
. The claim follows by induction on wedge degree.
∎

2.7.
Maurer–Cartan elements and the gauge action

Definition 2.19
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a differential graded Lie algebra. An element
a\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
such that

da+\frac{1}{2}[a,a]=0

(13)

is called a
Maurer–Cartan element
, and
\mathrm{MC}(\mathcal{A})
denotes the cone of such elements.

If
\mathcal{A}
is nilpotent, there is an action, called
gauge action
, of
\exp(\mathcal{A}_{0})
on
\mathcal{A}_{1}
that leaves
\mathrm{MC}(\mathcal{A})
invariant
[
27
, Lemma 6.3.4]
. Since we will be dealing with a differential graded Lie algebra that is not nilpotent, we work with a slight variation of the gauge action as follows.

Let
\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]]
be the space of formal power series with coefficients in
\mathcal{A}
. This is again a differential graded Lie algebra, where
\epsilon
is of degree
0
and where

d\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}\epsilon^{i}:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}da_{i}\epsilon^{i},\quad\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}\epsilon^{i},\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}b_{j}\epsilon^{j}\right]:=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}[a_{i},b_{j}]\epsilon^{i+j}.

A sequence of
\sum_{i}a_{i}^{(k)}\epsilon^{i}
of elements in
\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]]
converges to
\sum_{i}a_{i}\epsilon^{i}
if for each
i
,
a_{i}^{(k)}
converges to
a_{i}
in the discrete topology. We write
\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]]_{>0}\subset\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]]
for the elements without absolute term.

Definition 2.20
.

The
gauge action
of
\exp(\mathcal{A}_{0}[[\epsilon]]_{>0})
on
\mathcal{A}_{1}[[\epsilon]]
is defined by the converging series

e^{a}*x:=x+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\ad(a)^{n}}{(n+1)!}([a,x]-da),\quad a\in\mathcal{A}_{0}[[\epsilon]]_{>0},x\in\mathcal{A}_{1}[[\epsilon]].

Observe that this is well defined, as for each fixed power of
\epsilon
there are only finitely many summands.

Proposition 2.21
.

The set
\mathrm{MC}(\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]])
of Maurer–Cartan elements is invariant under the gauge action. In particular, since

0\in\mathrm{MC}(\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]])
, we have
e^{a}*0\in\mathrm{MC}(\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]])
for every
a\in\mathcal{A}_{0}[[\epsilon]]_{>0}
.

Proof.

Following
[
27
, Section 6.3]
, we construct a new DGLA
\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]]^{\prime}:=\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]]\oplus\mathbb{R}\cdot d
, where
d
is a formal symbol of degree
1
. The Lie bracket is defined by

[\tau+vd,\zeta+wd]^{\prime}:=[\tau,\zeta]+vd\zeta-(-1)^{\deg\tau}wd\tau,\quad\tau,\zeta\in\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]],v,w\in\mathbb{R}.

The differential is defined by

d^{\prime}(\tau+vd)=[d,\tau+vd]=d\tau.

One easily checks that
(\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]]^{\prime},[\bullet,\bullet]^{\prime},d^{\prime})
is a DGLA. The gauge action can be rewritten in
\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]]^{\prime}
as

e^{a}*x=e^{\ad a}(x+d)-d.

Let
\tau^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]]^{\prime}
. By the graded Jacobi identity, we have for each
a\in\mathcal{A}_{0}[[\epsilon]]_{>0}

\ad(a)[\tau^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime}]^{\prime}=[\ad(a)\tau^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime}]^{\prime}+[\tau^{\prime},\ad(a)\zeta^{\prime}]^{\prime}.

It follows inductively that

\ad(a)^{n}[\tau^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime}]^{\prime}=\sum_{k+l=n}\binom{n}{k}[\ad(a)^{k}\tau^{\prime},\ad(a)^{l}\zeta^{\prime}]^{\prime}

and hence

e^{\ad a}[\tau^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime}]^{\prime}=[e^{\ad a}\tau^{\prime},e^{\ad a}\zeta^{\prime}]^{\prime}.

Let
\tau\in\mathcal{A}_{1}[[\epsilon]]
. Then
\tau
is a Maurer–Cartan element if and only if
[\tau+d,\tau+d]^{\prime}=0
. In this case,

[e^{a}*\tau+d,e^{a}*\tau+d]^{\prime}=[e^{\ad(a)}(\tau+d),e^{\ad(a)}(\tau+d)]^{\prime}=e^{\ad(a)}[\tau+d,\tau+d]^{\prime}=0,

which means that
e^{a}*\tau
is a Maurer–Cartan element.
∎

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a DGLA and
\mathcal{G}
the DGGA generated by
\mathcal{A}
. All operations on
\mathcal{G}
extend to the space of formal power series
\mathcal{G}[[\epsilon]]
by (multi)-linearity in
\epsilon
, making the latter into a DGGA. Convergence in
\mathcal{G}[[\epsilon]]
is defined as for
\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]]
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1}[[\epsilon]]_{>0}\subset\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]]
denote the space of elements of degree
1
with no absolute term. Note that elements in this space pairwise commute. We define the exponential map by the converging series

\widehat{\exp}:\mathcal{A}_{1}[[\epsilon]]_{>0}\to\mathcal{G}[[\epsilon]],\zeta\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^{\wedge n}}{n!}.

Proposition 2.22
.

Let
\zeta\in\mathcal{A}_{1}[[\epsilon]]_{>0}
. Then
-\zeta\in\mathrm{MC}(\mathcal{A}[[\epsilon]])
if and only if
\widehat{\exp}(\zeta)\in\ker(d-\partial)
.

Proof.

We have
d\widehat{\exp}(\zeta)=d\zeta\wedge\widehat{\exp}(\zeta)
and

\partial\widehat{\exp}(\zeta)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\partial(\zeta^{j})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}{j\choose 2}[\zeta,\zeta]\wedge\zeta^{j-2}=\frac{1}{2}[\zeta,\zeta]\wedge\widehat{\exp}(\zeta).

Thus
\widehat{\exp}(\zeta)\in\ker(d-\partial)
if and only if
d\zeta=\frac{1}{2}[\zeta,\zeta]
, that is
-\zeta
is Maurer–Cartan.
∎

3.
The free differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra on one generator

In the previous section we considered the free differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra generated by a graded vector space. We will now apply this general construction to a vector space generated by one element. In the resulting Gerstenhaber algebra we identify a certain subspace which we denote
\mathcal{DTC}
. Its importance will become clear in Section
7
, where its elements will yield closed and vertical
n
-forms on the sphere bundle of an
n
-dimensional Lie group endowed with a bi-invariant riemannian metric and hence, via the Rumin differential, smooth valuations on the group.

The main result of this section is an explicit description of the space
\mathcal{DTC}
. Namely, using the gauge-action construction of Maurer–Cartan elements, we identify a sequence of elements and using some combinatorial arguments we show that it in fact spans the whole space.

Definition 3.4
.

We write

3.3.
Higher Jacobi identities

As a consequence of the graded Jacobi identity satisfied in
\mathcal{A}
, there are higher Jacobi identities among iterated Lie brackets. A systematic study of such identities is contained in
[
1
]
. We only need a special case that we prove directly here.

Recall that we constructed
\mathcal{A}
as a graded Lie subalgebra of the tensor algebra
T(V)
, where
V=\mathbb{R}\cdot\xi\oplus\mathbb{R}\cdot\gamma
, see the proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Lemma 3.8
.

It holds in
T(V)
that

[\xi^{b}\gamma\xi^{a}\gamma]=\sum_{0\leq i+j\leq a+b}c_{i,j}(a,b)\xi^{i}\gamma\xi^{j}\gamma\xi^{a+b-i-j},

where

c_{i,j}(a,b)=(-1)^{i+a+b}{a\choose j}\left((-1)^{j}{b\choose i}+{b\choose i+j-a}\right).

In terms of generating functions, we have

\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}(a,b)x^{i}y^{j}=(x-1)^{b}\big((y-1)^{a}+(x-y)^{a}\big).

(20)

Proof.

By induction on
a
, one proves

[\xi^{a}\gamma]=\sum_{i=0}^{a}(-1)^{i+a}\binom{a}{i}\xi^{i}\gamma\xi^{a-i},

from which the case
b=0
of the lemma follows. The general case follows by induction over
b
.
∎

Let
B_{n}
denote the Bernoulli numbers, that is

\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{n}}{n!}x^{n}.

Thus
B_{0}=1
,
B_{1}=-1/2
,
B_{2}=1/6
, and
B_{2k+1}=0
for
k\geq 1
.

We set

\beta_{b}(m)=-\frac{2(2^{b+1}-1)}{b+1}{m\choose b}B_{b+1}.

A generating function for
\beta_{b}(m)
is

\sum_{b\leq m}\beta_{b}(m)x^{b}\frac{y^{m-b}}{m!}=\frac{2e^{y}}{e^{x}+1}.

(21)

Proposition 3.9
.

(i)

For all odd
m\geq 1
we have the relation

\sum_{b=0}^{m}\beta_{b}(m)[\xi^{b}\gamma\xi^{m-b}\gamma]=0,

(22)

or equivalently

[\gamma\xi^{m}\gamma]=\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{m+1}{2}}{m\choose 2j-1}\frac{4^{j}-1}{j}B_{2j}[\xi^{2j-1}\gamma\xi^{m-(2j-1)}\gamma].

(23)

(ii)

The iterated Lie brackets
[\xi^{m-2k}\gamma\xi^{2k}\gamma]
,
0\leq k\leq\frac{m}{2}
, form a basis of

\mathrm{Span}\{[\xi^{m-j}\gamma\xi^{j}\gamma]\}_{j\geq 0}
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
3.8
and (
20
), (
22
) is equivalent to

\sum_{b=0}^{m}\beta_{b}(m)(x-1)^{b}\big((y-1)^{m-b}+(x-y)^{m-b}\big)=0

for all odd
m
, which is equivalent to saying that the formal power series

\sum_{b\leq m}\frac{1}{m!}\beta_{b}(m)(x-1)^{b}\big((y-1)^{m-b}+(x-y)^{m-b}\big)t^{m}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y][[t]]

is even. By (
21
), this power series is given by

2\frac{e^{(y-1)t}+e^{(x-y)t}}{e^{(x-1)t}+1},

and it is easy to check that it is indeed even. This proves (
22
). The non-zero terms in (
22
) are for
b=0
and
b=2j-1
odd, and writing it out gives (
23
).

Let us now prove (ii). From (
23
) it follows that the displayed elements form a generating system. It remains to show that they are linearly independent. We prove this by induction over
m
, the cases
m=0,1
being trivial. Suppose that
m\geq 2
and that we have a linear relation

\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor}c_{k}[\xi^{m-2k}\gamma\xi^{2k}\gamma]=0.

This is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor}c_{k}(x-1)^{m-2k}((y-1)^{2k}+(x-y)^{2k})=0.

(24)

Taking two partial derivatives with respect to
y
gives

\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{m-2}{2}\right\rfloor}c_{k+1}(2k+2)(2k+1)(x-1)^{(m-2)-2k}((y-1)^{2k}+(x-y)^{2k})=0.

By induction,
c_{k+1}=0
for
k=0,\dots,\lfloor\tfrac{m-2}{2}\rfloor
, and so it follows from (
24
) that also
c_{0}=0
.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

For each
m\geq 0
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{m}[\xi^{m-k}\gamma\xi^{k}\gamma]\neq 0.

Proof.

Otherwise we would obtain as above that
\sum_{b=0}^{m}(x-1)^{b}((y-1)^{m-b}+(x-y)^{m-b})=0
, but the coefficient of
x^{m}y^{0}
is
m+2>0
.
∎

3.4.
Cohomology

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\omega\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{m}\mathcal{A}_{1}

Proof.

Denote
\Lambda_{j}(k):=\{\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{j}\geq 0,\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{j}=k\}

Since

If

Next assume

\omega=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{m}(n-m)}c_{\lambda}[\xi^{\lambda_{1}}\gamma]\wedge\dots\wedge[\xi^{\lambda_{m}}\gamma].

Then

\displaystyle d\omega

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j+1}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{m}(n-m):\lambda_{j}>0}c_{\lambda}\bigwedge_{t=1}^{j-1}[\xi^{\lambda_{t}}\gamma]\wedge\sum_{k=0}^{\lambda_{j}-1}[\xi^{\lambda_{j}-1-k}\gamma\xi^{k}\gamma]\wedge\bigwedge_{t=j+1}^{m}[\xi^{\lambda_{t}}\gamma]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\quad\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{m}(n-m):\lambda_{j}>0}c_{\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{\lambda_{j}-1}[\xi^{\lambda_{j}-1-k}\gamma\xi^{k}\gamma]\wedge\bigwedge_{1\leq t\leq m,t\neq j}[\xi^{\lambda_{t}}\gamma].

For
\mu=i_{j}(\lambda):=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{j-1},\lambda_{j+1},\dots,\lambda_{m})

and
\lambda_{j}=n-m-|\mu|
. For any fixed
\mu
with

we find, by Lemma
3.10

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Lambda_{m}(n-m)\\
i_{j}(\lambda)=\mu\end{subarray}}c_{\lambda}=0.

Assume that

Proof of Theorem

Any

4.
Realization map

Given a finite-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, the space of differential forms
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}:=\Omega(\mathfrak{g},\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g})
is a DGGA. The universal property of the free DGGA
\mathcal{G}
gives rise to a map
\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}:\mathcal{G}\to\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}
which we call the realization map. The main result of this section is the following injectivity property: The intersection of the kernels of the different realization maps
\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}
, as
\mathfrak{g}
runs over all finite-dimensional Lie algebras, is trivial. In Sections
5
and
6
, we will use this result to establish some statements about the structure of
\mathcal{G}
that appear hard to prove by direct arguments.

The proof proceeds in two steps. In the first step, we use a realization map to some space
\Omega_{U}
of differential forms on a vector space
U
with values in the exterior algebra of the free Lie algebra it generates, and show its injectivity. To do so, we use the explicit constructions of the free DGLA and the graded wedge product in terms of tensor algebras and Dynkin projectors from Section
2
. The second step is a standard truncation argument to reduce from the infinite-dimensional free Lie algebra to a finite-dimensional Lie algebra.

4.1.
Double forms on
\mathfrak{g}

All differential forms will be considered with complex coefficients. Thus we will write
\Omega(X,V)
for the differential forms on
X
with values in
\mathbb{C}\otimes V
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra. Define

\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}:=\Omega(\mathfrak{g},\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g})=\Gamma(\mathfrak{g},\mathbb{C}\otimes\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\otimes\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g}).

We write
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathrm{eqv}}
for the subspace consisting of all forms invariant under the group of automorphisms of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Since
\mathfrak{g}
acts on itself by the adjoint action, an element
x\in\mathfrak{g}
induces a vector field
x^{\sharp}
on
\mathfrak{g}
. Explicitly we have

x^{\sharp}|_{\xi}=[x,\xi],\quad\xi\in\mathfrak{g}.

We define the space of
\ad
-invariant elements

\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}=\{\tau\in\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}:\mathfrak{L}_{x^{\sharp}}\tau=\ad_{x}\tau,\quad x\in\mathfrak{g}\}.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra of a Lie group
G
, we also define the subspace
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{G}
of
\Ad G
-equivariant elements, i.e. elements
\tau
for which

\Ad_{g^{-1}}^{*}\otimes\Ad_{g}\tau=\tau

(26)

for all
g\in G
, where
\Ad_{g^{-1}}^{*}
acts on the form part of
\tau
and
\Ad_{g}
acts on
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g}
. Evidently
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathrm{eqv}}\subset\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{G}\subset\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
. If
G
is connected and simply connected and
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra of
G
, then the last inclusion is an equality.

Lemma 4.2
.

\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}
is in a natural way a differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra and
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathrm{eqv}},\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{G},\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
are differential graded Gerstenhaber subalgebras.

Proof.

There is the obvious bigrading
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}=\bigoplus_{d,k}\Omega^{d}(\mathfrak{g},\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathfrak{g})
. For a homogeneous element
\rho\in\Omega^{d}(\mathfrak{g},\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathfrak{g})
we set
\deg\rho=d
and
\deg_{w}\rho=k
.

The product is the usual double wedge product, namely
(\tau_{1}\otimes X_{1})\wedge(\tau_{2}\otimes X_{2})=(\tau_{1}\wedge\tau_{2})\otimes(X_{1}\wedge X_{2})
, where
\tau_{i}
are forms on
\mathfrak{g}
and
X_{i}\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g}
. The differential is defined by
d(\tau\otimes X)=(d\tau)\otimes X
. The Lie bracket is defined by
[\tau_{1}\otimes X_{1},\tau_{2}\otimes X_{2}]=(\tau_{1}\wedge\tau_{2})\otimes[X_{1},X_{2}]
, where the bracket on the right hand side means the Schouten–Nijenhuis bracket in
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g}
. The verification of the axioms of a differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra is then straightforward. It is clear that the wedge product and the Lie bracket commute with automorphisms of the Lie algebra, rendering the subspaces in the statement subalgebras.
∎

Definition 4.3
.

4.2.
Injectivity of the realization map

Let
\mathfrak{g}:=\mathfrak{g}_{f}(U)
be the free (ungraded) Lie algebra generated by a vector space
U
.

This is an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra whenever
\dim U>1
. Since it is the direct sum of the finite-dimensional spaces

, see Section
2
, we can use the colimit topology on
\mathfrak{g}
. It induces a topology on each space
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathfrak{g}
. A function

is called weakly smooth if for every
\eta
in the topological dual space to
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathfrak{g}
, the scalar-valued function
\langle\eta,f\rangle:U\to\mathbb{R}
is smooth. Similarly we can define weakly smooth differential forms on
U
with values in
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathfrak{g}
.

We let

\Omega_{U}:=\Omega(U,\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g})=\bigoplus_{d,k}\Omega^{d}(U,\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathfrak{g})

be the space of weakly smooth
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{\mathrm{fin}}g
-valued forms on
U
.

The wedge product, the Lie bracket and the differential are defined in the natural way, turning
\Omega_{U}
into a differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra.

By Theorem
2.14
we can extend the map
\xi\mapsto\mathrm{id}\in C^{\infty}(U,U)\subset\Omega^{0}(U,\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g})
to a
\mathbf{DGGA}
-morphism

.

Recall that
\mathcal{G}^{k}=\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathcal{A}
, and
\mathcal{G}_{d}\subset\mathcal{G}
is the space of degree
d
elements. Denote
\mathcal{G}^{k}_{d}=\mathcal{G}_{d}\cap\mathcal{G}^{k}
.

When no confusion can arise, we will write simply

instead of
\Re_{U}\tau|_{X}(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})
.

For instance,

([\xi,\gamma]\wedge\gamma\wedge\xi)(X;Y_{1},Y_{2})=[X,Y_{1}]\wedge Y_{2}\wedge X-[X,Y_{2}]\wedge Y_{1}\wedge X\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{3}\mathfrak{g}

We now proceed to establish the claimed injectivity of the realization map, starting with a special case.

Proposition 4.7
.

\mathfrak{g}
be the free algebra generated by a vector space

. Let

(27)

with

We prove by induction that for every

(28)

this is (

). Suppose that we have shown (

) for some

.
We apply

to this equation. Using the antisymmetry of the

N+1
. For a basis
(X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})
of
U
we have

\Re_{\mathfrak{h}}\tau|_{X}(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})=\pi_{U}(\Re_{U}\tau|_{X}(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})).

Since the Lie degree of
\Re_{U}\tau|_{X}(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})
is not larger than
N
, the claim follows from Propositions
4.5
and
4.7
.
∎

5.
Convolution of double forms on a Lie algebra

We mimic the construction from
[
10
]
to construct a convolution product on
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}
. It can be thought of as a deformation of the usual wedge product. The main technical result of the section is that this product satisfies a Leibniz rule with respect to the operator
D
.

5.1.
Defining convolution.

For a real finite dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, we consider
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}=\Omega(\mathfrak{g},\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g})
as in Definition
4.1
, and the subspace
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
of
\ad
-invariant elements.

The following construction is taken, with some adaptions, from
[
10
, Section 4]
.
Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
a basis of
\mathfrak{g}
and
e_{1}^{*},\ldots,e_{n}^{*}\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
the dual basis. Recall that for an element
x\in\mathfrak{g}
,
x^{\sharp}
denotes the induced vector field on
\mathfrak{g}
. Explicitly,
x^{\sharp}|_{\xi}=[x,\xi]
.

Definition 5.1
.

For
\tau,\zeta\in\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
and
r\geq 0
we set

\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)=\sum_{|K|=r}\iota_{e_{K}^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{K}^{*}}\zeta,

where the sum runs over all sets
K=\{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\}\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
and we define
\iota_{e_{K}^{\sharp}}=\iota_{e^{\sharp}_{k_{1}}}\circ\cdots\circ\iota_{e^{\sharp}_{i_{r}}}
and similarly for
\iota_{e^{*}_{K}}
.

The definition of
\hat{S}_{r}
depends neither on the ordering of the elements in
K
nor on the choice of a basis. Note that
\deg\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\wedge\zeta)=\deg\tau+\deg\zeta-r,\deg_{w}\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\wedge\zeta)=\deg_{w}\tau+\deg_{w}\zeta-r
. Clearly
\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)=0
if
r>\deg\tau
or
r>\deg_{w}\zeta
.

Proposition 5.2
.

The
convolution product

\tau*\zeta\in\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
defined on homogeneous elements
\tau,\zeta\in\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
with
k_{1}=\deg\tau,l_{1}=\deg_{w}\tau,k_{2}=\deg\zeta,l_{2}=\deg_{w}\zeta
by

\tau*\zeta=\sum_{r}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta),

where

\displaystyle\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}=(-1)^{(k_{2}+l_{2})(l_{1}+r)+rk_{1}},

is well defined.

Proof.

We have to check that the definition makes sense. Letting
G
be the simply connected Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, we have
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{G}=\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
so it remains to check that
\tau*\zeta\in\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{G}
whenever
\tau,\zeta\in\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{G}
.

By (
26
) we have to show

\Ad_{g^{-1}}^{*}\otimes\Ad_{g}\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)=\hat{S}_{r}(\tilde{\tau}\otimes\tilde{\zeta}),\quad r\geq 0.

To this end, observe first that for any index subset
K
we have

\displaystyle\Ad_{g^{-1}}^{*}\circ\iota_{e_{K}^{\sharp}}=\iota_{(\Ad_{g}e_{K})^{\sharp}}\circ(\Ad_{g^{-1}}^{*})^{*},

\displaystyle\Ad_{g}\circ\iota_{e_{K}^{*}}=\iota_{(\Ad_{g}e_{K})^{*}}\circ\Ad_{g}.

Then the claim follows easily from the invariance of
\tau
and
\zeta
and the fact that the definition of
\hat{S}_{r}
is independent of the basis of
\mathfrak{g}
.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

The convolution product on
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
is associative.

Proof.

The proof is verbatim the same as in
[
10
, Proposition 4.4]
.
∎

5.2.
Leibniz rule

Now we will show that the convolution in
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
satisfies a version of the Leibniz rule. As in Section
3
we set
\Deg X=\deg X-\deg_{w}X
and define the operator
D:\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}\to\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
by

D\tau=d\tau+(-1)^{\Deg\tau+1}\partial\tau.

Proposition 5.4
.

For any
\tau,\zeta\in\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
one has

D(\tau*\zeta)=D\tau*\zeta+(-1)^{\Deg\tau}\tau*D\zeta.

First we will prove a technical lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

For
\tau,\zeta\in\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}d\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)&=(-1)^{r}\hat{S}_{r}(d\tau\otimes\zeta)+(-1)^{\deg\tau+r}\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes d\zeta)\\
&\quad+(-1)^{\deg_{w}\tau+1}\hat{S}_{r-1}(\partial\tau\otimes\zeta)+(-1)^{r}\hat{S}_{r-1}(\tau\otimes\partial\zeta)\\
&\quad+(-1)^{\deg_{w}\tau}\partial\hat{S}_{r-1}(\tau\otimes\zeta).\end{split}

(30)

Proof.

We set
k_{1}:=\deg\tau
,
l_{1}:=\deg_{w}\tau
,
k_{2}:=\deg\zeta
, and
l_{2}:=\deg_{w}\zeta
. Define maps
[\bullet],\{\bullet\}:\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}\otimes\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}\to\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ad}
by

\displaystyle[\tau\otimes\zeta]

\displaystyle:=\sum_{a,b=1}^{n}[e_{a},e_{b}]\wedge\iota_{e_{a}^{*}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{b}^{*}}\zeta,

\displaystyle\{\tau\otimes\zeta\}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{a,b=1}^{n}\iota_{[e_{a},e_{b}]^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{a}^{*}}\iota_{e_{b}^{*}}\zeta.

We claim that

\displaystyle d\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{r}\hat{S}_{r}(d\tau\otimes\zeta)+(-1)^{k_{1}+r}\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes d\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad-[S_{r-1}(\tau\otimes\zeta)]-\frac{1}{2}\{S_{r-2}(\tau\otimes\zeta)\},

(31)

\displaystyle\partial\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)

\displaystyle=\hat{S}_{r}(\partial\tau\otimes\zeta)+(-1)^{l_{1}+r}\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\partial\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+(-1)^{l_{1}+1}[{S_{r}}(\tau\otimes\zeta)]+\frac{(-1)^{l_{1}+1}}{2}\{S_{r-1}(\tau\otimes\zeta)\}.

(32)

To prove the claim, we rewrite
\hat{S}_{r}
as

\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)=\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{|I|=r}\iota_{e_{I}^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{I}^{*}}\zeta,

where
I
runs over all
r
-tuples.

To prove the first equation, we compute

d\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)=\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{|I|=r}d\iota_{e_{I}^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{I}^{*}}\zeta+(-1)^{k_{1}+r}\iota_{e_{I}^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{I}^{*}}d\zeta.

The second summand is
(-1)^{k_{2}+r}\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes d\zeta)
.

Consider now the first summand. Using the formula
[\mathcal{L}_{X},\iota_{Y}]=\iota_{[X,Y]}
and induction on
r
, we find that

\displaystyle d\iota_{e_{i_{1}}^{\sharp}}\cdots\iota_{e_{i_{r}}^{\sharp}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{r}\iota_{e_{i_{1}}^{\sharp}}\cdots\iota_{e_{i_{r}}^{\sharp}}d

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{r}(-1)^{j+1}\iota_{e_{i_{1}}^{\sharp}}\cdots\widehat{\iota_{e_{i_{j}}^{\sharp}}}\cdots\iota_{e_{i_{r}}^{\sharp}}\mathcal{L}_{e_{i_{j}}^{\sharp}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq r}(-1)^{r+j_{1}+j_{2}}\iota_{e_{i_{1}}^{\sharp}}\cdots\widehat{\iota_{e_{i_{j_{1}}}^{\sharp}}}\cdots\widehat{\iota_{e_{i_{j_{2}}}^{\sharp}}}\cdots\iota_{e_{i_{r}}^{\sharp}}\iota_{[e_{i_{j_{1}}}^{\sharp},e_{i_{j_{2}}}^{\sharp}]}.

Replacing this into our formula for
d\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)
, we get three more terms, the first of which is
(-1)^{r}\hat{S}_{r}(d\tau\otimes\zeta)
.

For the second term, we use that
\tau
is
{\ad}
-invariant, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{e_{j}^{\sharp}}\tau=\ad_{e_{j}}\tau
.

We write
I^{\prime}=(i_{2},\ldots,i_{r})
and
I^{\prime\prime}=(i_{3},\ldots,i_{r})
. For
j=1
we obtain the term

\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\sum_{I^{\prime}}\ad_{e_{i_{1}}}\iota_{e_{I^{\prime}}^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{i_{1}}^{*}}\iota_{e_{I^{\prime}}^{*}}\zeta=-\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{I^{\prime}}[\iota_{e_{I^{\prime}}^{\sharp}}\tau\otimes\iota_{e_{I^{\prime}}^{*}}\zeta]=-\frac{1}{r}[S_{r-1}(\tau\otimes\zeta)].

For other values of
j
, we obtain the same sum, after some change of indices.

The last term only appears if
r\geq 2
. We first take
j_{1}=1,j_{2}=2
. Then we obtain a term

-\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}=1}^{n}\sum_{I^{\prime\prime}}\iota_{[e_{i_{1}}^{\sharp},e_{i_{2}}^{\sharp}]}\iota_{e_{I^{\prime\prime}}^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{i_{1}}^{*}}\iota_{e_{i_{2}}^{*}}\iota_{e_{I^{\prime\prime}}^{*}}\zeta=-\frac{1}{r(r-1)}\{S_{r-2}(\tau\otimes\zeta)\}.

For other values of
j_{1}<j_{2}
, we obtain the same term, after some change of indices. Since there are
\frac{r(r-1)}{2}
such pairs, we obtain (
31
).

For the boundary operator we compute

\displaystyle r!\partial\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b}\sum_{|I|=r}[e_{a},e_{b}]\wedge\iota_{e_{b}^{*}}\iota_{e_{a}^{*}}(\iota_{e_{I}^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{I}^{*}}\zeta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|I|=r}\iota_{e_{I}^{\sharp}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b}[e_{a},e_{b}]\wedge\iota_{e_{b}^{*}}\iota_{e_{a}^{*}}\tau\right)\wedge\iota_{e_{I}^{*}}\zeta

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b,|I|=r}[e_{a},e_{b}]\wedge(-1)^{l_{1}+1}\iota_{e_{a}^{*}}\iota_{e_{I}^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{b}^{*}}i_{e_{I}^{*}}\zeta

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b,|I|=r}[e_{a},e_{b}](-1)^{l_{1}}\iota_{e_{b}^{*}}\iota_{e_{I}^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{a}^{*}}\iota_{e_{I}^{*}}\zeta

\displaystyle\quad+(-1)^{l_{1}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b,|I|=r}\iota_{e_{I}^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge[e_{a},e_{b}]\wedge\iota_{e_{I}^{*}}\iota_{e_{b}^{*}}\iota_{e_{a}^{*}}\zeta

\displaystyle=r!\hat{S}_{r}(\partial\tau\otimes\zeta)+(-1)^{l_{1}+1}r![S_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)]

\displaystyle\quad+(-1)^{l_{1}+r}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b,|I|=r}\iota_{e_{I}^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{I}^{*}}([e_{a},e_{b}]\wedge\iota_{e_{b}^{*}}\iota_{e_{a}^{*}}\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(-1)^{l_{1}+r+1}r}{2}\sum_{a,b,i_{r},|I^{\prime}|=r-1}\iota_{e_{I^{\prime}}^{\sharp}}\iota_{e_{i_{r}}^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{i_{r}}^{*}}[e_{a},e_{b}]\cdot\iota_{e_{I^{\prime}}^{*}}\iota_{e_{b}^{*}}\iota_{e_{a}^{*}}\zeta

\displaystyle=r!\hat{S}_{r}(\partial\tilde{\tau}\otimes\zeta)+(-1)^{l_{1}+1}r![S_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)]+(-1)^{l_{1}+r}r!\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\partial\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(-1)^{l_{1}+r+1}r}{2}\sum_{a,b,|I^{\prime}|=r-1}\iota_{e_{I^{\prime}}^{\sharp}}\iota_{[e_{a},e_{b}]^{\sharp}}\tau\wedge\iota_{e_{I^{\prime}}^{*}}\iota_{e_{b}^{*}}\iota_{e_{a}^{*}}\zeta

\displaystyle=r!\hat{S}_{r}(\partial\tau\otimes\zeta)+(-1)^{l_{1}+1}r![S_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)]+(-1)^{l_{1}+r}r!\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\partial\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(-1)^{l_{1}+1}r}{2}(r-1)!\{S_{r-1}(\tau\otimes\zeta)\}.

This proves (
32
). Combining (
31
) and (
32
) we obtain (
30
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.4
.

From (
30
) we infer that

\displaystyle D(\tau*\zeta)

\displaystyle=d(\tau*\zeta)+(-1)^{k_{1}+l_{1}+k_{2}+l_{2}+1}\partial(\tau*\zeta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}(-1)^{r}\hat{S}_{r}(d\tau\otimes\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{r}(-1)^{k_{1}+r}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes d\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{r}(-1)^{l_{1}}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}\partial\hat{S}_{r-1}(\tau\otimes\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{r}(-1)^{l_{1}+1}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}\hat{S}_{r-1}(\partial\tau\otimes\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{r}(-1)^{r}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}\hat{S}_{r-1}(\tau\otimes\partial\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{r}(-1)^{k_{1}+l_{1}+k_{2}+l_{2}+1}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}\partial\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}((-1)^{l_{1}}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}+(-1)^{k_{1}+l_{1}+k_{2}+l_{2}+1}\epsilon^{r+1}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}})\partial\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{r}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1}+1,l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}\hat{S}_{r}(d\tau\otimes\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+(-1)^{k_{1}+l_{1}+1}\sum_{r}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1}-1,k_{2},l_{2}}\hat{S}_{r}(\partial\tau\otimes\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+(-1)^{k_{1}+l_{1}}\sum_{r}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2}+1,l_{2}}\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes d\zeta)

\displaystyle\quad+(-1)^{k_{1}+l_{1}+k_{2}+l_{2}+1}\sum_{r}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}-1}\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\partial\zeta)

\displaystyle=\left(d\tau+(-1)^{\Deg\tau+1}\partial\tau\right)*\zeta+(-1)^{\Deg\tau}\tau*\left(d\zeta+(-1)^{\Deg\zeta+1}\partial\zeta\right)

\displaystyle=D\tau*\zeta+(-1)^{\Deg\tau}*D\zeta.

∎

Let us finish this section with a remark. If we put a factor
t^{r}
in front of
\hat{S}_{r}
in Definition
5.1
, we obtain a
1
-parameter family of products
*_{t},t\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying a Leibniz rule with respect to the operators
D_{t}=d+t\partial
. The case
t=0
corresponds, up to a sign, to the usual wedge product. We can thus think of
(\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathrm{ad}},*_{t},D_{t})
as a deformation of the differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra
(\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathrm{ad}},\wedge,d)
.

6.
Abstract convolution

We construct a convolution product on the free differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra generated by one element. The approach from Section
5
does not work directly, since we no longer have a finite basis. Instead we will rewrite the operations
\hat{S}_{r}
in a form that does not use any basis. The idea is that we choose
r
factors from the second form and plug them into some
\gamma
-terms of the first form. To make this work requires some technical care. It is not obvious, and appears hard to prove directly, that the convolution product obtained in this way satisfies associativity and the Leibniz rule. Instead, we show that the operators
\hat{S}_{r}
and hence the convolution are compatible with the realization map and use the injectivity result from Section
4
and the associativity and Leibniz rule on the space
(\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}},*)
from Section
5
to deduce the corresponding statements for
(\mathcal{G},*)
.

We then show that the space
\mathcal{DTC}
is closed under convolution and, using the explicit description of this space from Section
3
, compute its algebra structure. Finally we define the space
\mathcal{TC}\subset\mathcal{G}
of abstract tube coefficients, and introduce a slight modification of the convolution product on
\mathcal{G}
, which we call valuation convolution and denote by
\vee
, turning
\mathcal{TC}
into an algebra.

6.1.
Statement of the theorem

Recall the DGV
V=\mathbb{R}\cdot\xi\oplus\mathbb{R}\cdot\gamma
, generating the DGLA
\mathcal{A}
and DGGA
\mathcal{G}=\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}
.

The differential graded Gerstenhaber algebra
\mathcal{G}
is a universal model for
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g}
-valued differential forms on
\mathfrak{g}
, in the sense that for every Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
we have the realization map
\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}:\mathcal{G}\to\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}
. This map is a morphism of DGGAs. However, on
\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}
there is also the convolution product, defined in Section
5
. Our next theorem shows that the convolution product can be defined on the universal model as well.

Theorem 6.1
.

There exists a unique convolution product
*
on
\mathcal{G}
such that for every Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, the realization map
\Re:(\mathcal{G},\ast)\to(\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}},\ast)
is a morphism of algebras. It is associative, satisfies
\Deg(\tau\ast\zeta)=\Deg(\tau)+\Deg(\zeta)
and the Leibniz rule:

D(\tau*\zeta)=D\tau*\zeta+(-1)^{\Deg\tau}\tau*D\zeta.

The uniqueness of the convolution product follows immediately from the injectivity of the realization maps, see Theorem
4.8
. The construction is more involved and is the topic of this section.

6.2.
Contraction

Let
T(V)
be the tensor algebra of
V
, and
T(V)_{k}
be the component of degree
k
(i.e. containing precisely
k
copies of
V_{1}=\Span\{\gamma\}
). Recalling that
\mathcal{A}\subset T(V)
, we have
\mathcal{A}_{k}=T(V)_{k}\cap\mathcal{A}
. The Dynkin projector

\displaystyle\rho:T(V)

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{1}{n}[a_{1},[a_{2},\ldots,[a_{n-1},a_{n}]]]

induces a map

\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\rho:\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}T(V)\to\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathcal{A},

and thus also a map

\overline{\rho}:=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\rho:\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}T(V)\to\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{G}.

Define the linear map

r:\mathcal{A}^{\otimes k}\otimes T(T(V))_{k}\to T(T(V))

by

r:\left(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{k}\right)\otimes P(\gamma[k],\xi[n-k])\mapsto P(a_{1},\dots,a_{k},\xi[n-k]).

Here
P(\gamma[k],\xi[n-k])
is an arbitrary element in
T(T(V))
which has degree
k
, that is
\gamma
appears
k
times in each monomial summand of
P
, and we replace the
i
-th
\gamma
in each monomial with
a_{i}
, for all
i=1,\dots,k
.

Let
p:T(T(V))\to\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}T(V)\subset T(T(V))
be the antisymmetrization, see (
5
). We then define the map
\iota
as the following composition:

\iota:\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{k}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{A}^{\otimes k}\otimes T(T(V))_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r}}{{\to}}T(T(V))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\to}}\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}T(V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bar{\rho}}}{{\to}}\mathcal{G}.

We extend this to a map

\iota:\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{l}\to\mathcal{G}

for all
l
by putting for
b\in\mathcal{G}_{l}

\iota_{a_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge a_{k}}b:=\begin{cases}0&l<k\\
\iota_{a_{1}\wedge\ldots a_{k}\wedge\gamma\wedge\ldots\wedge\gamma}b&l\geq k.\end{cases}

Extending by linearity, we obtain the
contraction map

\iota:\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}
, for all
k
.

6.3.
The maps
S_{r}

For
r\geq 0
, define the disintegration function
f_{r}:\mathcal{G}\to\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{r}\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{G}
as the unique linear map
f_{r}:\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{\bullet}\mathcal{A}\to\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{r}\mathcal{A}\otimes\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{\bullet-r}\mathcal{A}
satisfying

\displaystyle f_{r}(x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{n})=\frac{1}{r!(n-r)!}\cdot

\displaystyle\cdot\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}(-1)^{\sgn\sigma+\chi(\sigma)}x_{\sigma 1}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{\sigma r}\otimes x_{\sigma(r+1)}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{\sigma n},

where

\chi(\sigma)=\sum_{\scriptsize\begin{array}[]{cc}i<j\\
\sigma i>\sigma j\end{array}}\deg x_{i}\deg x_{j}.

Note that
f_{r}
is well-defined by eq. (
4
). We will need the factor switching map

\beta_{r}:\mathcal{G}\otimes\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{r}\mathcal{A}\to\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{r}\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{G}

which on pairs of
\deg
-homogeneous elements is given by

\beta_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)=(-1)^{(\deg\tau-r)\deg\zeta}\zeta\otimes\tau.

The reason behind the sign in
\beta_{r}
will become evident in the course of the proof of Proposition
6.3
. Essentially, both factors will correspond to differential forms, with
\tau
corresponding to a form of degree
\deg\tau-r
following the substitution of
r
vectors into the realization of
\tau
, while
\zeta
corresponds to a form of degree
\deg\zeta
.

For
r\geq 0
we define the operators
S_{r}:\mathcal{G}\otimes\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}\otimes\mathcal{G}
by

\textstyle{\mathcal{G}\otimes\mathcal{G}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{Id}\otimes f_{r}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{G}\otimes\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{r}\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{G}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{Id}\otimes\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{r}\ad_{-\xi}\otimes\mathrm{Id}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{G}\otimes\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{r}\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{G}}

6.4.
Convolution

Definition 6.2
.

The convolution of two homogeneous elements
\tau,\zeta\in\mathcal{G}
with
\deg\tau=k_{1},\deg_{w}\tau=l_{1},\deg\zeta=k_{2},\deg_{w}\zeta=l_{2}
is defined by

\tau\ast\zeta:=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta),

where

\epsilon^{r}_{k_{1},l_{1},k_{2},l_{2}}=(-1)^{(k_{2}+l_{2})(l_{1}+r)+rk_{1}}.

We now verify that our two definitions of convolution are compatible through the realization map.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\tau,\zeta\in\mathcal{G}
and
\mathfrak{g}
a finite dimensional Lie algebra. Then

\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}(\tau*\zeta)=\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}\tau*\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}\zeta.

For the proof we will need the following general construction. Assume
\alpha
is a
k
-form on
\mathfrak{g}
, and
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}
are
\mathfrak{g}
-valued forms on
\mathfrak{g}
, of degrees
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}
, respectively. Setting
b:=b_{1}+\dots+b_{k}
, we define the composition form

\omega=\alpha(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})

to be the
b
-form on
\mathfrak{g}
given by

\displaystyle\omega(v_{1},\dots,v_{b}):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{b}}(-1)^{\sgn\sigma}\alpha(\beta_{1}(v_{\sigma 1},\dots,v_{\sigma b_{1}}),\dots,\beta_{k}(v_{\sigma(b-b_{k}+1)},\dots,v_{\sigma b})).

Proof.

It suffices to prove that for
\tau,\zeta\in\mathcal{G}
we have

\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}\hat{S}_{r}(\tau\otimes\zeta)=\hat{S}_{r}(\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}\tau\otimes\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}\zeta).

(33)

We may further assume
\zeta=\zeta_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\zeta_{m}
,
\zeta_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
. Put
\widetilde{\tau}=\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}\tau
,
\widetilde{\zeta}=\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}\zeta
. Consider the summand of
\widehat{S}_{r}(\widetilde{\tau}\otimes\widetilde{\zeta})
given by

\iota_{e_{1}^{\sharp}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{r}^{\sharp}}\widetilde{\tau}\wedge\iota_{e_{1}^{*}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{r}^{*}}\widetilde{\zeta},

where
e_{i}^{\sharp}=[e_{i},\xi]
.

Applying
\iota_{e_{1}^{*}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{r}^{*}}
to
\widetilde{\zeta}
, one has summands corresponding to all choices of
r
wedge factors of
\widetilde{\zeta}
. Consider the summands corresponding to
\widetilde{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\zeta}_{r}
. Those are

\displaystyle S=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{r}}(-1)^{\sgn\sigma}\iota_{e_{1}^{\sharp}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{r}^{\sharp}}\widetilde{\tau}\wedge e_{1}^{*}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{\sigma 1})\wedge\dots\wedge e_{r}^{*}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{\sigma r})\wedge\widetilde{\zeta}_{r+1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{\zeta}_{n}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{r}}(-1)^{\sgn\sigma}e_{1}^{*}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{\sigma 1})\wedge\dots\wedge e_{r}^{*}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{\sigma r})\wedge\iota_{e_{1}^{\sharp}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{r}^{\sharp}}\widetilde{\tau}\wedge\widetilde{\zeta}_{r+1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{\zeta}_{m},

where
\epsilon=(-1)^{(\deg\widetilde{\tau}-r)\sum_{j=1}^{r}\deg\widetilde{\zeta}_{j}}
. By definition,

\iota_{e_{1}^{\sharp}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{r}^{\sharp}}\widetilde{\tau}=\widetilde{\tau}([e_{1},\xi],\dots,[e_{r},\xi],\bullet[\deg\widetilde{\tau}-r]).

Now
e_{j}^{*}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{\sigma j})
is a
\mathbb{C}
-valued form, and so
e_{j}^{*}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{\sigma j})[e_{j},\xi]=[e_{j}^{*}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{\sigma j})e_{j},\xi]
is a
\mathfrak{g}
-valued form on
\mathfrak{g}
. We may thus present
S
as a sum of composition forms wedged by the remaining
\widetilde{\zeta}_{j}
:

\displaystyle S=

\displaystyle\epsilon\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{r}}(-1)^{\sgn\sigma}\widetilde{\tau}([e_{1}^{*}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{\sigma 1})e_{1},\xi],\dots,[e_{r}^{*}(\widetilde{\zeta}_{\sigma r})e_{r},\xi],\bullet[\deg\widetilde{\tau}-r])\wedge\widetilde{\zeta}_{r+1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{\zeta}_{m}.

Now summing over all
r
-tuples of basis elements, by the anti-symmetry of
\tau
we may also sum over
\sigma
and thus obtain the form

S=\epsilon\widetilde{\tau}([\widetilde{\zeta}_{1},\xi],\dots,[\widetilde{\zeta}_{r},\xi],\bullet[\deg\widetilde{\tau}-r])\wedge\widetilde{\zeta}_{r+1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{\zeta}_{m},

which transparently corresponds under the realization map to the abstract convolution, namely to the summands with the disintegration function splitting off the first
k
wedge factors of
\zeta
.

As both sides of eq. (
33
) transform by the same sign under permutations of the
\zeta_{j}
factors, this completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Associativity of the convolution follows at once from Proposition
5.3
and Theorem
4.8
. That
\Deg
is additive is immediate from definitions. Since the realization map is a
\mathbf{DGGA}
-morphism, it commutes with
D
and then the Leibniz rule follows from Proposition

and Theorem

.
∎

Corollary 6.4
.

The subspaces
\mathcal{Z}
,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{D}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}
are closed under convolution.

Proof.

For
\mathcal{Z}
, this follows at once from the Leibniz law for convolution. Inspecting the contraction map, it is clear that
\iota((\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{H})\otimes\mathcal{H})\subset\mathcal{H}
, and consequently
\mathcal{H}
is a convolution subalgebra. For
\mathcal{D}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}
this now follows by the additivity of

.
∎

Proposition 6.5
.

It holds that

\varrho_{m}*\gamma=(m+1)\varrho_{m+1}.

Proof.

We have by definition

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{1}(\zeta_{\epsilon}\otimes\gamma)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(i+1)!}\hat{S}_{1}([\xi^{i}\gamma]\otimes\gamma)\epsilon^{i+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(i+1)!}[\xi^{i+1}\gamma]\epsilon^{i+1}

\displaystyle=-[\xi,\zeta_{\epsilon}].

For
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
one checks that

\hat{S}_{1}((\tau_{1}\wedge\tau_{2})\otimes\gamma)=(-1)^{\deg\tau_{2}}\hat{S}_{1}(\tau_{1}\otimes\gamma)\wedge\tau_{2}+(-1)^{\deg\tau_{1}}\tau_{1}\wedge\hat{S}_{1}(\tau_{2}\otimes\gamma).

By induction we deduce that

\hat{S}_{1}(\zeta_{\epsilon}^{i}\otimes\gamma)=(-1)^{i-1}i\zeta_{\epsilon}^{i-1}\wedge\hat{S}_{1}(\zeta_{\epsilon}\otimes\gamma).

The definition of the convolution product gives, using
\deg\zeta_{\epsilon}^{i}=i
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\exp}(-\zeta_{\epsilon})*\gamma

\displaystyle=\widehat{\exp}(-\zeta_{\epsilon})\wedge\gamma+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i!}\hat{S}_{1}((-\zeta_{\epsilon})^{i}\otimes\gamma)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\exp}(-\zeta_{\epsilon})\wedge\gamma+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i-1}}{(i-1)!}\zeta_{\epsilon}^{i-1}\wedge\hat{S}_{1}(\zeta_{\epsilon}\otimes\gamma)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\exp}(-\zeta_{\epsilon})\wedge\gamma-\widehat{\exp}(-\zeta_{\epsilon})\wedge[\xi,\zeta_{\epsilon}]

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\widehat{\exp}(-\zeta_{\epsilon}),

where in the last line we used eq. (
19
). Comparing the coefficient of
\epsilon^{m}
on both sides gives the claimed equality
\varrho_{m}*\gamma=(m+1)\varrho_{m+1}
.
∎

Corollary 6.6
.

It holds that
\varrho_{m}=\frac{1}{m!}\varrho_{1}^{\ast m}
and
\varrho_{m}\ast\varrho_{n}={m+n\choose n}\varrho_{m+n}
.

Proof.

Since the convolution product on
\mathcal{G}
is associative,
\varrho_{1}^{\ast m}
is well defined.
From Proposition
6.5
we deduce that
\varrho_{m}\ast\varrho_{1}=(m+1)\varrho_{m+1}
for all

.
It follows by induction on

\varrho_{m}=\frac{1}{m!}\varrho_{1}^{\ast m}
. The second equality is an immediate consequence. The last statement follows from Theorem
3.7
.
∎

6.5.
The abstract valuation convolution

Definition 6.7
.

The valuation convolution on
\mathcal{G}
is given by

\kappa_{1}\vee\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{1}\ast D\kappa_{2}.

Lemma 6.8
.

The valuation convolution is associative.

Proof.

We use Theorem
6.1
. By the Leibniz rule for convolution,

\kappa_{1}\vee(\kappa_{2}\vee\kappa_{3})=\kappa_{1}\ast D(\kappa_{2}\ast D\kappa_{3})=\kappa_{1}\ast(D\kappa_{2}\ast D\kappa_{3}),

while

(\kappa_{1}\vee\kappa_{2})\vee\kappa_{3}=(\kappa_{1}\ast D\kappa_{2})\ast D\kappa_{3}

by definition. Equality now follows from associativity of convolution.
∎

Lemma 6.9
.

For all
\tau\in\mathcal{H}
,
D\tau\in\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

For
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
it holds that
\deg[x,y]\geq 1
when
\deg x,\deg y\geq 1
, in particular
\partial(\mathcal{H})\subset\mathcal{H}
. It is also clear that
d(\mathcal{H})\subset\mathcal{H}
, implying the statement.
∎

We identify a subspace of
\mathcal{G}
which under the realization map
\Re^{S}_{\mathfrak{g}}
from Section
7
below gives rise to
(n-1)
-forms over the sphere bundle of a Lie group equipped with a bi-invariant metric, with the property that their differential is a vertical form.

Definition 6.10
.

The space of
abstract tube coefficients
is

\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}=\{\kappa\in\mathcal{G}(-1):D\kappa\in\mathcal{H}\}.

There is a simple relation between the spaces
\mathcal{TC}
and
\mathcal{DTC}
as follows.

Proposition 6.11
.

It holds that
\mathcal{DTC}=D(\mathcal{TC})\oplus\mathbb{C}
, while
\mathcal{TC}=\xi\wedge\mathcal{DTC}
.

Proof.

Evidently
D(\mathcal{TC})\oplus\mathbb{C}\subset\mathcal{DTC}
. The reverse inclusion
\mathcal{DTC}\subset D(\mathcal{TC})\oplus\mathbb{C}
follows from Theorem
3.7
and Proposition
3.3
.

If
\tau\in\mathcal{DTC}
then by the Leibniz rule for the convolution and Corollary
6.4
,

D(\xi\wedge\tau)=(-1)^{\Deg\tau}D(\xi\ast\tau)=(-1)^{\Deg\tau}\gamma\ast\tau\in\mathcal{DTC}\subset\mathcal{H},

and

, hence
\xi\wedge\mathcal{DTC}\subset\mathcal{TC}
.

On the other hand, let
\kappa\in\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}
. Since

we can write it as
\kappa=\xi\wedge\tau
for some

. Since
\xi\wedge\xi=0
, we may assume

. Then

\xi\wedge D\tau=(-1)^{\Deg D\tau}\xi\ast D\tau=D(\xi\wedge\tau)+(-1)^{\Deg\tau+1}\gamma\ast\tau\in\mathcal{H},

∎

Corollary 6.12
.

The elements
\omega_{j}=\frac{1}{j}\xi\wedge\varrho_{j-1}

\mathcal{TC}
.

Proof.

This follows at once from Theorem

.
∎

Proposition 6.13
.

The space
\mathcal{TC}
is closed under the valuation convolution.

Proof.

Take
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in\mathcal{TC}
. By the Leibniz rule for convolution and Corollary

∎

7.
The tube coefficient subalgebra

In this section we verify that the abstract tube coefficients
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{C}
correspond to the tube coefficient valuations over any Lie group equipped with a bi-invariant metric, thus proving Theorem
D
. We will provide two proofs of this theorem. The first one is indirect and uses the abstract machinery developed so far. It shows that the tube coefficients are very natural objects from the Lie algebraic point of view. The second proof is more geometric and direct.

7.1.
Smooth valuations

We begin with a rapid overview of smooth valuations, recalling key definitions and notation and referring to
[
10
, Section 2]
for details.

To an
n
-dimensional vector space
V
, we associate three different one-dimensional spaces: the determinant
\det V=\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{n}V
, the space of densities (Lebesgue measures)
\Dens(V)
, and the orientation line
\ori(V)
. They are related by

\Dens(V)\cong\det V^{*}\otimes\ori(V).

If
M
is a smooth manifold, we let
\pi:\mathbb{P}_{M}\to M
be its cosphere bundle with fiber
\mathbb{P}_{+}(T^{*}M)
. If
M
is riemannian, we let
SM
be the unit sphere bundle. Obviously
SM\cong\mathbb{P}_{M}
.
We write
\Omega_{\ori}(X)
for the complex-valued differential forms on
X
twisted by
\ori(M)
, where
X
could be
\mathbb{P}_{M}
or
M
. A form on
\mathbb{P}_{M}
is vertical if its restriction to any contact hyperplane vanishes.

The space of smooth valuations on a smooth
n
-dimensional manifold
M
is denoted by
\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(M)
. There is a surjection
\Omega_{\ori}^{n}(M)\times\Omega_{\ori}^{n-1}(\mathbb{P}_{M})\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(M),(\mu,\omega)\mapsto[[\mu,\omega]]
,
Explicitly,
[[\mu,\omega]]
denotes the valuation

Z\mapsto\int_{Z}\mu+\int_{\nc(Z)}\omega,

where
\nc(Z)
is the normal cycle of
Z
.

There is a also an injection
\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(M)\hookrightarrow C^{\infty}(M)\times\Omega^{n}_{\ori}(\mathbb{P}_{M})
, which to the valuation
\phi=[[\mu,\omega]]
puts in correspondence the function
f(x)=\phi(\{x\})
and the
n
-form
\tau=D\omega+\pi^{*}\mu
, where
D
is the Rumin operator (not to be confused with the operator
D
on
\mathcal{G}
that is central to the present paper). The unique valuation that is mapped to the pair
(f,\tau)
is denoted by
\{f,\tau\}
.

7.2.
Valuations on Lie groups

The convolution of valuations on a compact Lie group is defined as

\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)\times\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)\to\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G),(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\mapsto\phi_{1}*\phi_{2}:=m_{*}(\phi_{1}\boxtimes\phi_{2}),

where
m:G\times G\to G
is the multiplication map and
\boxtimes
denotes the exterior product. Since the exterior product of two smooth valuations is a generalized valuation, one has to check that the push-forward under
m
is defined, and that the result is smooth again. This was shown in
[
6
]
.

In
[
10
]
, we have defined the convolution of bi-invariant valuations on an arbitrary Lie group.
More precisely, there is a convolution product

\ast:\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)^{G\times G}\otimes\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*})\times\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)^{G\times G}\otimes\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*})\to\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)^{G\times G}\otimes\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*}),

which coincides with the compact case once a probability Haar measure is used to trivialize
\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
.

7.3.
Forms on Lie groups

We henceforth let
G
be an
n
-dimensional Lie group equipped with a bi-invariant riemannian metric. As in
[
10
, Section 3]
, we identify the left-invariant forms on

with
\Omega(S(\mathfrak{g}),\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g}^{*})
using left translations.

The Hodge star on
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g}
induces an isomorphism

\ast:\Omega(S(\mathfrak{g}),\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g})\to\Omega(S(\mathfrak{g}),\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g}^{*})\otimes\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{n}\mathfrak{g}=\Omega(SG)^{L_{G}}\otimes\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{n}\mathfrak{g}=\Omega_{\ori}(SG)^{L_{G}}\otimes\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*}).

We arrive at the identification

\Omega_{\ori}(SG)^{L_{G}}\otimes\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*})=\Omega(S(\mathfrak{g}),\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g}).

(34)

For
\tau\in\Omega(S(\mathfrak{g}),\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g})

denote its degree as a differential form, while its wedge degree

corresponds to the grading on the exterior algebra
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}\mathfrak{g}
. Define also
D\tau=d\tau+(-1)^{\deg\tau-\deg_{w}\tau+1}\partial\tau
.
The following fact appears in
[
10

.

Proposition 7.1
.

For
\zeta\in\Omega_{\ori}(SG)^{L_{G}}\otimes\Dens(\mathfrak{g}^{*})

Corollary 7.2
.

The convolution product on

We have a surjective map from

5.4
.
∎

7.5.
Geometric proof of the tube formula

Proof of Theorem
D
.

Define the function

F_{\epsilon}(t):=\frac{e^{\epsilon t}-1}{t}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{i}\epsilon^{i+1}}{(i+1)!}.

The definition of
\zeta_{\epsilon}
can be rewritten as

\zeta_{\epsilon}=-F_{\epsilon}(\ad_{\xi})\gamma.

For a Lie group
G
and
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, the differential
d\exp|_{\xi}:\mathfrak{g}=T_{\xi}\mathfrak{g}\to T_{\exp(\xi)}G
is given by
dR_{\exp\xi}\circ F_{1}(\ad\xi)

[
15
, Theorem 1.5.3]
. Since
\gamma
is mapped to the identity map under the realization map
\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}
, we have

\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}\zeta_{\epsilon}=-F_{\epsilon}(\ad_{\xi})\in\Omega^{1}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})\subset\Omega_{\mathfrak{g}}.

Consider the map

\exp_{\epsilon}:SG=G\times S(\mathfrak{g})\to G,(g,\xi)\mapsto\exp_{g}\epsilon dL_{g}(\xi)=g\exp(\epsilon\xi),g\in G,\xi\in S(\mathfrak{g}).

Here we have used that the riemannian exponential map is
G
-equivariant and that it coincides with the Lie group exponential map at the neutral element.

Fix
(g,\xi)
and set
q:=\exp(\epsilon\xi)\in G
.
We set

\Phi:=dL_{(gq)^{-1}}\circ d\exp_{\epsilon}|_{g,\xi}\circ(dL_{g}\oplus\mathrm{id}):\mathfrak{g}\oplus T_{\xi}S(\mathfrak{g})\to\mathfrak{g}.

One computes that

\Phi(X,Y)=\Ad_{q^{-1}}(X+F_{\epsilon}(\ad\xi)Y).

Take a positive orthonormal basis
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
of
\mathfrak{g}
and denote
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n}\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
the dual basis. The volume form of
G
at the neutral element is given by
\vol_{G}=\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}
. Since it is
\Ad(G)
-invariant, it follows that

\Phi^{*}\vol_{G}=\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}_{i=1}^{n}(\pi^{*}\tau_{i}+F_{\epsilon}(\ad\xi)^{*}\tau_{i})\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{n}(\mathfrak{g}^{*}\oplus T_{\xi}^{*}S(\mathfrak{g})).

We can identify
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{n}(\mathfrak{g}^{*}\oplus T_{\xi}^{*}S(\mathfrak{g}))=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}T_{\xi}^{*}S(\mathfrak{g})\otimes\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{n-k}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, see
[
10
, Equation (13)]
.

For a subset
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
in increasing order, we let
I^{c}
be the complement, ordered increasingly, and
\sigma(I)
the sign of the permutation
(I,I^{c})
. Using the notation
\tau_{I}=\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}_{l=1}^{k}\tau_{i_{l}}\in\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
we can write

\Phi^{*}\vol_{G}=\sum_{I}\sigma(I)F_{\epsilon}(\ad\xi)^{*}\tau_{I}\otimes\tau_{I^{c}},

where the sum runs over all possible
I
.

We now apply the inverse of the Hodge star
\ast:\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{k}\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{n}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\to\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{n-k}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, where we identify
\mbox{\Large$\wedge$}^{n}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\cong\mathbb{C}
using the volume form. This gives

\displaystyle\ast^{-1}\Phi^{*}\vol_{G}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}F_{\epsilon}(\ad\xi)^{*}\tau_{I}\otimes X_{I}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{\epsilon}(\ad\xi)^{*}\tau_{i}\otimes X_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp F_{\epsilon}(\ad\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{res}\circ\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}(\rho_{\epsilon}),

and therefore

\exp_{\epsilon}^{*}\vol_{G}=\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}^{S}(\rho_{\epsilon}).

Let
Z\in\mathcal{P}(G)
. Then
Z
is of positive reach. For
0<\epsilon<\mathrm{reach}(Z)
, the boundary
\partial B_{\epsilon}Z
is given by
(\exp_{\epsilon})_{*}\nc(Z)
. By the Gauss lemma, the unit vector
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}=T_{\exp_{\epsilon}(g,\xi)}G
is orthogonal to
\partial Z_{\epsilon}
. Hence the volume form of
\partial Z_{\epsilon}
is
\vol_{\partial Z_{\epsilon}}=\iota_{\xi}\vol_{G}
.

Since
\xi=\Phi(\xi,0)
, we have

\ast^{-1}\Phi^{*}\iota_{\xi}\vol_{G}=\ast^{-1}\iota_{(\xi,0)}\Phi^{*}\vol_{G}=\xi\wedge\ast^{-1}\Phi^{*}\vol_{G}=\mathrm{res}\circ\Re_{g}(\xi\wedge\rho_{\epsilon})

and thus

\exp_{\epsilon}^{*}\vol_{\partial Z_{\epsilon}}=\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}^{S}(\xi\wedge\rho_{\epsilon}).

We conclude that

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\vol(Z_{\epsilon})=\vol(\partial Z_{\epsilon})=\int_{\nc(Z)}\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}^{S}(\xi\wedge\rho_{\epsilon})=\Re_{\mathfrak{g}}^{V}(\xi\wedge\rho_{\epsilon})(Z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\upsilon_{k}(Z)\epsilon^{k-1},

and hence

\vol(Z_{\epsilon})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\upsilon_{k}(Z)\epsilon^{k}.

∎

7.6.
Example

Let us work out explicitly a particular case, namely

with its round metric. Note that on each tangent space to the sphere

,

. The series for

Let us compute

is a point. We need the component of degree

and wedge degree

in

. We have

8.
Existence of invariant valuations

In this final section we strengthen Theorem B from
[
10
]
. There we have shown that a
connected
Lie group that admits a smooth bi-invariant valuation outside the span of the Euler characteristic and the Haar measure, must also admit a bi-invariant metric. As it turns out, this holds for general Lie groups.

Next, we showed in
[
10
]
that if there is a bi-invariant metric on
G
, then the space
\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)^{G\times G}
of smooth bi-invariant valuations is typically infinite-dimensional. For compact semisimple groups

we give here a more explicit argument, showing that the subspace
\mathcal{TC}(G)\subset\mathcal{V}^{\infty}(G)^{G\times G}
is infinite-dimensional.

8.1.

The following two lemmas appeared in

Remark 8.10

We observe that in fact we prove something stronger. For contracting or expanding matrices

Theorem 8.11
.

Let

be any subgroup, and

-invariant metric on

In other words, the stabilizer of a nonzero

with

in

is compact.

Proof.

We may replace

by its closure in

, so that it is a Lie subgroup by Cartan’s theorem. By Theorem

, all

are elliptic type. It then follows by a theorem of Fresnel-van der Put

As an immediate corollary we finally prove Theorem
A
.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

If a bi-invariant metric exists, its first intrinsic volume is a bi-invariant valuation as desired.

In the other direction, let

be a bi-invariant valuation. Substracting multiples of
\chi
and

, we may suppose

,

at

-invariant. By Theorem

-invariant metric on

∎

8.2.
Semisimple groups

In
[
10
, Theorem B]
we have shown by abstract arguments that for compact connected Lie groups, the space of bi-invariant smooth valuations is infinite-dimensional except for the groups
S^{3}
and
\mathrm{SO}(3)
. Here we show that for semisimple Lie groups with identity component other than
S^{3},\mathrm{SO}(3)
, already the tube coefficients span an infinite-dimensional space.

Proposition 8.12
.

Let
G
be a compact semisimple Lie group equipped with a bi-invariant riemannian metric. Then
\mathcal{TC}(G)
is infinite dimensional unless
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Consider the
n
-forms of
\mathcal{TC}(G)
. The subspace of bi-degree
(n-1,1)
consists, after the Hodge star, of the forms
[\xi^{k},\gamma]
,
k\geq 0
. We will verify those span an infinite-dimensional space unless
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
.

If the span is finite dimensional, there is an identity of the form

\sum_{j=0}^{N}c_{j}\ad_{\xi}^{j}=0,

which holds on
\xi^{\perp}
, for all
\xi
.

Let
u,v
span an invariant subspace of
\ad_{\xi}
with
\ad_{\xi}u=\lambda v
,
\ad_{\xi}v=-\lambda u
,
\lambda\neq 0
. Putting
\beta=-\lambda^{2}
and plugging
u
, it must hold that

\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}c_{2k}\beta^{k}=0,\quad\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor(N-1)/2\rfloor}c_{2k+1}\beta^{k}=0.

If we can find
\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1
different such values
\beta
, Vandermonde’s determinant then shows
c_{j}=0
for all
j
.

Assume that a root
\alpha
of
\mathfrak{g}
and
\xi\in S(\mathfrak{h})
satisfy
\alpha(\xi)\neq 0
. Let
0\neq u+iv,u,v\in\mathfrak{g}
be in the root space
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
. Noting that
\alpha(\xi)\in\mathbb{R}i
, the equation
\ad_{\xi}(u+iv)=\alpha(\xi)(u+iv)
implies that
(u,v)
is a pair as above with
\lambda=i\alpha(\xi)
and
\beta=-|\alpha(\xi)|^{2}
.

If the Cartan algebra
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}
is of dimension at least
2
, we may take any root
\alpha
of
\mathfrak{g}
and find
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in S(\mathfrak{h})
such that
\alpha(\xi^{\prime})=0\neq\alpha(\xi)
. Connecting
\xi,\xi^{\prime}
by a curve in
S(\mathfrak{h})
, the continuity of
\alpha
along this curve provides us with infinitely many different values
\beta
as desired.

It follows that
\dim\mathfrak{h}=1
, which implies
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
.
∎
