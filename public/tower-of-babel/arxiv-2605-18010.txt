Functionalization via Structure Completion and Motion Rectification Journal: TOG
Mingrui Zhao, Sai Raj Kishore Perla, Kai Wang, Sauradip Nag, Duc Anh Nguyen, Jiayi Peng, Ruiqi Wang, Angel X. Chang, Manolis Savva, Ali Mahdavi-Amiri, Hao Zhang

Abstract
. Acquisition and creation of 3D assets have been largely view- or appearance-driven. As a result, existing digital 3D models often lack the requisite structural components to function as intended, such as joints, supports, interiors, or interaction elements. At the same time, even human-annotated motions are frequently error-prone, leading to physically implausible behavior. We introduce object functionalization , a novel task aimed at transforming visually plausible but non-functional 3D models into functional and physically operable ones. We formulate functionalization as a graph completion problem over a new functional graph representation, where labeled nodes represent object parts, labeled edges encode functional and contact relations, and movable nodes carry motion attributes, so that structural functional deficiencies manifest as missing nodes or incorrect edges. We develop a neural Graph Functionalizer (GraFu) to complete an incomplete graph representing a non-functional 3D object. The completed graph then drives a geometry realization stage that instantiates predicted connectors and structural elements in 3D, with the compelling side effect of rectifying erroneous human-annotated and predicted motions. To support training and evaluation, focusing on furniture as a rich and challenging target category, we introduce FurFun-233, a dataset of 233 paired non-functional and functionalized furniture models. On PartNet-Mobility (“zero-shot”) and HSSD test sets, our method matches state-of-the-art methods in motion prediction accuracy while substantially improving functionality in terms of collision and connectivity.

1.
Introduction

We live in a 3D world, surrounded by 3D objects that we constantly act on and interact with to carry out our daily activities. These activities define how we function as humans, while the same also applies to the robots we design to assist us. Correspondingly, the 3D objects have been constructed with their intended functionalities to afford the actions of humans or robotic agents alike. For example, all cabinets must have interior shelving and tops for storage and support, drawers have handles or grooves so that they can be pulled, doors have hinges to swing open, while a swivel chair must have a full cylinder assembly to stabilize and rotate its seat.

In reality, however, very few, if any, of the existing 3D assets fully possess the necessary
functional
components such as joints, handles, tracks, or other constituents of a part assembly for them to work. The majority of daily objects such as furniture and appliances do not have interior structures, and worse yet, a great many of them contain self collisions, under- or over-segmented parts, misalignments, etc., which prevent realization of functional motions. Even when the assets were manually created by 3D artists
(
Collins et al. 2022
;
Deitke et al. 2024
;
Chang et al. 2015
)
, the modeling emphasis has predominantly been placed on their
appearance
,
rarely their interior structures, and certainly
not their functionalities.

Besides these geometric and topological functional deficiencies, accuracy is another issue. For example, the majority (80%+) of
human-annotated
articulations for the storage furnitures in PartNet-Mobility
(
Xiang et al. 2020
)
contain errors in the motion ranges or axes which may cause part collision during articulation. We argue that all of these deficiencies and inaccuracies for existing 3D assets need to be corrected, since the main purpose of reconstructing or generating a 3D object is
not
to merely look at it, but to
use
it,
interact with
it, perhaps to eventually
manufacture
it.

In this paper, we pose the novel problem of object
functionalization
, whose goal is to convert a non-functional 3D object into one that works as intended, by fixing its
functional deficiencies through structural and geometric transformations.

Given the immense diversity of object functionalities, a single unified solution capable of resolving
all
forms of functional deficiencies would be overly ambitious.
As a first step, we focus on a tractable and well-defined subset: structural functional deficiencies stemming from
incomplete object structures
, as summarized in Table
1
. Accordingly, our functionalization solution involves adding parts or connectors and adjusting part relationships, while addressing the challenge of devising a unified approach across both the dynamic and static settings.

To this end, we formulate object functionalization as a
graph completion
problem, which offers a unified treatment of both dynamic and static deficiencies.
Specifically, we parse an input 3D model, with part segmentation, into a new
functional graph representation
, where every mesh part becomes a labeled node,
every functional or contact relation becomes a typed edge (e.g., contact, hinge, rail, etc.), while movable nodes are further annotated with motion attributes. Under this representation, structural functional deficiencies manifest uniformly as missing nodes and/or incorrect edges, regardless of whether they are dynamic (e.g., missing hinges for revolute motion between a door and its frame and missing rails for prismatic motion) or static (e.g., a missing countertop node).

We develop a neural model called a
graph functionalizer
(GraFu), which takes an incomplete graph representing a non-functional object and transforms it into a complete graph representing its functional counterpart. Technically, GraFu is a graph-to-graph translation network comprising a node encoder, a Graph Transformer encoder operating on a fully-connected topology, and a DETR-style (i.e., DEtection TRansformers
(
Carion et al. 2020
)
) decoder that predicts new structural nodes, functional edges (hinge, rail, attached), and motion attributes for movable parts.

Graph completion with GraFu is only the first step, which is dedicated to structural (i.e., topological) functionalization without fully resolving geometric or motion precisions. In the second stage, the completed graph drives
geometric realization
, where predicted connectors are instantiated and snapped into place, template meshes are inserted for new structural nodes, and the full pipeline is wrapped in a Blender add-on for interactive review and adjustment.

A compelling side effect of such a geometric functionalization is that by grounding part motion in explicit mechanical structures rather than virtual motion axes, any errors in the initial motion annotations, as those in PartNet-Mobility
(
Xiang et al. 2020
)
, can be
rectified
to produce physically realistic, collision-free motions; see Fig.
1
.

To support training GraFu and evaluation, we introduce
FurFun
-233, for Furniture Functionalization, a dataset of 233 furniture models with paired non-functional and functionalized counterparts, sourced from ShapeNet
(
Chang et al. 2015
)
, Objaverse
(
Deitke et al. 2023
)
, ABO
(
Collins et al. 2022
)
, HSSD
(
Khanna* et al. 2023
)
, and artist creations. Each model is pre-screened to ensure it contains interior structures, annotated with per-part semantic labels, and paired with a ground-truth functional graph. Functionalized counterparts include manually inserted mechanical components drawn from an expanded gallery of 9 connector types (7 hinges and 2 rails). While our formulation is general, we focus on furniture in this paper, as it represents one of the richest and most challenging categories of functional objects, encompassing both dynamic deficiencies (hinges, rails) and static ones (shelves, handles, countertops).

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

A functional graph representation encompassing part and connector/edge labeling and motion attributes.

•

A neural graph functionalizer for structure completion.

•

A two-stage functionalization pipeline that follows topological completion with geometry realization which can rectify predicted motion attributes via mechanical grounding.

•

A high-quality dataset
FurFun
-233 to support training and evaluation of object functionalization models.

We evaluate our method for its motion prediction using GraFu by comparing against state-of-the-art baselines, Particulate
(
Li et al. 2025
)
and SINGAPO
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
. On the Storage Furniture subset of PartNet-Mobility (PN-M), GraFu matches their motion prediction accuracy while geometric realization substantially improves physical plausibility in terms of collision resolution and part connectivity. Our method also exhibits strong generalizability with this test since its training set does not contain any PN-M models. On HSSD, our method outperforms both baselines across all three metrics. We further demonstrate robustness via an ablation study in which input node labels are progressively corrupted up to 100%, showing that GraFu maintains competitive motion prediction and near-perfect connectivity even under severe label noise.

2.
Related Work

With rapid advances in 3D generative and spatial AI, robotics, and the pursuit of world models, the value of high-quality 3D assets has received increasing attention. Take single-view 3D reconstruction as an example, recent works such as CLAY
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, Trellis
(
Xiang et al. 2025
)
, and Hi3DGen
(
Ye et al. 2025
)
unanimously highlight the importance of
high-quality
3D training data. However, achieving this only through careful data selection and curation is insufficient. Our work seeks to bring object functionalization as a new means of quality upgrades for existing 3D assets.

Broadly speaking, object functionalization falls into the category of shape modeling and editing, a well-studied topic in computer graphics with a variety
of criteria or objectives such as symmetry
(
Mitra et al. 2007
)
, stackability
(
Li et al. 2012
)
, balance
(
Prévost et al. 2013
)
, strength
(
Lu et al. 2014
)
, foldability
(
Li et al. 2015
)
, detail preservation
(
Botsch and Sorkine 2008
)
, structure preservation
(
Mitra et al. 2013
)
, and sketch conformation
(
Olsen et al. 2009
)
, among others.

Umentani et al. 2015
focus on interactive design of static plank-based furniture under frictional and nail-joint constraints, offering real-time guidance to a human designer. It does not address motion-enabling components such as hinges or rails, nor does it automate the functionalization of existing 3D assets.
To our knowledge, no prior works targeted functionalizing 3D objects via shape completion or geometric transformation.

Shape Completion and Assembly.

Shape completion has been extensively studied under different settings and representations from classical hole-filling algorithms
(
Liepa 2003
;
Tekumalla and Cohen 2004
;
Ju 2004
;
Kazhdan et al. 2006
;
Zhao et al. 2007
;
Kazhdan and Hoppe 2013
;
Centin et al. 2015
)
to more recent deep learning approaches
(
Yan et al. 2022
;
Arora et al. 2022
;
Tesema et al. 2023
)
.
However, most of these techniques try to complete a portion of the shape that is missing due to occlusion or flaws in the scanning process as opposed to our technique that recovers a missing functional part necessary for interaction, movement, or function.

Shape assembly methods are more directly related to our approach, as they typically focus on predicting how parts and joints should be connected. For example,
JoinABLe
(
Willis et al. 2022
)
is a learning-based assembly method which connects pairs of CAD parts by predicting the correct parametric joints, operating directly on boundary representations without class labels or human guidance. It also
contributed the Fusion 360 Gallery assembly dataset.

Schulz et al. 2014
propose a data-driven interactive system to help users design 3D models that are
fabricable
by composing parts from a database of expert-created templates. It automatically aligns, parameterizes, and connects parts, with proper constraints and connectors even including screws and corner braces, so that the resulting models can be manufactured. Such a “fabricabilization" process bears resemblance to one of our functionalization options, namely, hinge/track insertion, but without motion rectification. Generally, while fabricability and functionality are related, they are distinctive modeling goals. Operationally, our work is less about shape assembly but more on structure completion and shape transformation.

In other related work
Lin et al. 2017
reconstruct kinematic assemblies from raw 3D scans by inferring part segmentation and joint parameters, and
Mitra et al. 2010
analyze and visualize mechanical motion relationships. More recently, Kinematic Kitbashing
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
assembles reusable parts into a prescribed kinematic graph by optimizing per-part transforms under an exemplar-based vector distance field energy aggregated across each joint’s motion range. However, all of these methods presuppose a complete assembly as input, whereas our work addresses inferring and inserting
missing
functional geometry to enable articulated motion.

Motion Prediction and Generation.

Since many object functions are realized through part motions and human-object interactions, works that predict, reconstruct, and/or generate part articulations
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
are relevant to functionalization, but their goal is not to functionalize an object by altering its shape or part structure. Recent data-driven methods predict part articulations directly from geometry or images
(
Liu et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Le et al. 2024
)
, but while effective at inferring
where
and
how
parts move, they do not address
why
a motion may be physically implausible or how to correct it. Our rule-based geometric fixing stage is thus complementary: as shown in our experiments, applying hinge and rail insertion on top of predicted motion axes consistently reduces collisions, demonstrating that learned articulation and explicit mechanical grounding are mutually reinforcing.

ArtLLM
(
Wang et al. 2026
)
autoregressively predicts part layouts and joint structures from point clouds using a multimodal large language model, generating high-fidelity articulated assets; while it advances joint inference and part geometry synthesis, it does not address functional deficiencies in existing assets or insert physical connectors to correct implausible motions.
In this spirit, annotated
(
Xiang et al. 2020
)
or generated (e.g.,
(
Li et al. 2025
;
Vora et al. 2025
;
Qiu et al. 2025
)
) part motions can serve to initialize motion parameters in our dynamic functionalization setting, which are then improved through functionalization. Motion prediction, and the ensuing simulation, has recently been employed for functional anomaly
detection

(
Bhunia et al. 2025
)
by comparing observed (possibly abnormal) part articulations with anticipated (normal) ones. But the detected anomalies are not corrected.

Interior Modeling.

Object interiors are often exposed under part motion, e.g., when opening a cabinet door or drawer. As a result, recent works on geometry and interaction reconstruction such as DRAWER
(
Xia et al. 2025
)
and S2O
(
Iliash et al. 2024
)
contain modules which complete drawer/cabinet interiors that were hidden from the input videos or missing from the input 3D objects,
using a category-specific template approach similar to ours.

For representation learning, aside from volumetric representations such as NeRF
(
Mildenhall et al. 2020
)
and 3DGS
(
Kerbl et al. 2023
)
, object interiors can also be modeled by multi-slice images
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
and variants of signed distance functions (SDFs) such as 3-poled SDF
(
Chen et al. 2022
)
.
But all of these works have targeted reconstruction or generation tasks, e.g., from images or point clouds, not functionalization, for which we simply employ polygonal meshes.
Furniture interiors, along with part articulation, can be acquired in conjunction through active 3D reconstruction
(
Yan et al. 2023
)
, where a robot interacts with a physical
piece (e.g., opening/closing a drawer) while scanning the geometry.

Fixing Malfunctions.

To our knowledge, the closest work to ours is
(
Hong et al. 2022
)
, which also resolves functional deficiencies via a discrete set of fixes detected by a physics simulator. However, it focuses solely on motion malfunctions, whereas our work covers both dynamic and static deficiencies. More fundamentally, our motion rectification is not simulation-driven but a natural consequence of inserting proper mechanical connectors, which allows us to correct even the erroneous human annotations in PartNet-Mobility, which usually serves as ground truth. For static deficiencies, no prior work directly addresses the full scope we tackle: works such as
(
Hržica et al. 2022
)
and
(
Badami et al. 2017
)
address the related but distinct problems of storage space detection and furniture segmentation without modifying geometry, while PhysX-Anything
(
Cao et al. 2025
)
and PhysX-3D
(
Cao et al. 2026
)
generate simulation-ready assets from scratch rather than functionalizing existing ones. Procedural tools such as the IKEA kitchen planner similarly operate under rigid predefined templates and do not reason about functional deficiencies in arbitrary 3D assets.

3.
Method

We address the problem of
functionalization
—transforming non-functional 3D objects, particularly furniture, into physically plausible, functional counterparts.

Our approach consists of two main stages: a) The input 3D model is first converted into a graph representation that captures part-level relationships within the object. In this graph, functional deficiencies, missing nodes, or incorrect edges may arise due to imperfections in the input model.
A novel
Gra
ph
Fu
nctionalizer (
GraFu
) module then operates on this non-functional graph to predict the missing functional nodes which can be static nodes (i.e, nodes associated with stationary parts) or dynamic nodes (i.e, nodes corresponding to moving parts), correct relational edges, and estimate motion axes for dynamic nodes, thereby producing a complete functional graph. b) The 3D geometry is subsequently updated by cross-referencing the predicted graph with the original input: a suite of
geometric post-fix
algorithms directly embeds hinge and rail mechanisms into the 3D model for newly introduced revolute and prismatic edges, respectively, while template or bounding-box geometries are instantiated for any newly added static nodes. To support practical usability, we additionally provide a Blender add-on that allows users to interactively inspect and refine the output at each stage of the pipeline.

In the following, we discuss each of these components in detail.

The overall pipeline of our method is illustrated in
Figure

2
.

3.1.
Functional Graph Parsing

For a segmented 3D model, we construct a functional graph
G=(V,E)
, where
V
denotes the set of annotated part nodes and
E
denotes the set of inter-part contact relationships.
Each node
v\in V
encodes three attributes: the 3D bounding box of the part, its geometric representation, and a semantic label. Part labels are assigned from a fixed vocabulary:
{top/side/back/bottom panels, face frame, shelf, divider, bar, leg, door, drawer, handle, misc}
. This label set covers the vast majority of structural geometries encountered in practice; parts that do not fit any named category, such as stretchers, are assigned to the
misc
label. Each edge
e\in E
belongs to one of four types:
{contact, hinge, rail, attached}
. A
contact
edge encodes static structural connectivity, representing joints analogous to glued or nailed panels in physical assemblies.
Hinge
and
rail
edges capture revolute and prismatic kinematic relationships between movable parts and their fixed mounting anchors. Handles are connected to their parent door or drawer via an
attached
edge. For dynamic nodes, a motion axis
M
is additionally defined in the local coordinate frame of the part. For revolute joints, the motion axis is selected from the bounding box edges of the part, where a positive sign denotes counter-clockwise rotation and a negative sign denotes the opposite. For prismatic joints, the motion axis is assigned as one of the canonical
\pm\{x,y,z\}
directions.

3.2.
GraFu Network

GraFu
is a graph-to-graph translation network that transforms a non-functional graph
G_{u}
into a functional graph
G_{f}
by predicting missing structural nodes, inferring functional edges (
hinge
,
rail
,
attached
), and estimating motion attributes for dynamic nodes. The architecture comprises three sequential components: a) a
Node Encoder
that extracts per-node feature representations, b) a
Graph Encoder
that models relational dependencies across nodes, and c) a
Graph Decoder
that produces the functionalized output graph
G_{f}
. The complete pipeline is illustrated in
Figure

2
.

Node Encoder.

The node encoder maps each node
v\in V
in the non-functional graph
G_{u}
to a fixed-dimensional latent embedding. The
part category
, represented as a one-hot vector, is projected into a latent embedding of dimension
d_{\text{cat}}=128
via a lightweight MLP, capturing the semantic identity of each node. The 3D geometry of each part is encoded using a lightweight variant of PointNet++
(
Qi et al. 2017
)
, which maps the per-part point cloud to a latent embedding of dimension
d_{\text{pc}}=64
. Additionally, the centroid of the part bounding box is encoded by a separate lightweight MLP into a latent of dimension
d_{\text{geo}}=32
. The three embeddings are concatenated to yield a unified per-node latent representation
z_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{224}
for each node
v\in V
. By design, the category embedding
d_{\text{cat}}
constitutes the dominant component of
z_{v}
, since part category is the most informative attribute for describing each node.

Graph encoder.

To model inter-node relationships in the non-functional graph, we employ a lightweight Graph Transformer that operates over a
fully-connected
graph topology, where all nodes attend to one another and absent edges are assigned
null
-type edge labels. Edge features are constructed by encoding the edge connectivity type as a one-hot vector, augmented with an 8-dimensional geometric descriptor
[\,\lVert r\rVert,\,\lVert g\rVert,\,\hat{r},\,g_{\text{axis}}\,]
that captures the spatial relationship between part bounding boxes. Here,
\hat{r}
denotes the signed unit vector along the centroid-to-centroid direction, and
g_{\text{axis}}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}
represents the per-axis surface gap between oriented bounding boxes (set to zero where boxes overlap). The connectivity encoding and geometric descriptor are jointly projected into a 128-dimensional edge feature embedding
z_{e}
for each node pair using an MLP. At each Graph Transformer layer,
z_{e}
is incorporated into the attention computation as a learned edge bias:

(1)

\text{Attn}(z^{Q}_{v},z^{K}_{v},z^{V}_{v})=(\frac{z^{Q}_{v}.(z^{K}_{v})^{T}}{\sqrt{d}}+z_{e}).z^{V}_{v},

where
z^{Q}_{v}
,
z^{K}_{v}
and
z^{V}_{v}
are Query/Key/Value derived from the node embeddings
z_{v}
. This formulation enables the model to jointly reason over both node attributes and inter-part relational structure, such as which parts are connected via hinge or rail joints, thereby improving its capacity to predict a functionally coherent output graph.

Graph Decoder.

To decode the learned latent representations into a functional graph, we adopt a DETR
(
Carion et al. 2020
)
-style Transformer Decoder as our Graph Decoder. Rather than using object queries as in the original DETR formulation, we introduce
node slots
that serve as learned queries over graph nodes. Graph Encoder features are passed to the Graph Decoder via cross-attention. We initialize
N

anchor slots
, one per node in the input graph, and set a global maximum slot count of
M
. To allow the decoder to predict nodes beyond those present in the non-functional input, we introduce
K
additional
free query slots
alongside the
N
anchor slots. These free queries are tasked with recovering missing structural nodes required to make the graph functional; unused slots are zero-padded and treated as null nodes. To provide free queries with global graph context, we pool the Graph Encoder output and add its projected summary as an initialization to each free query embedding. Each decoder slot independently predicts: (i) node validity, (ii) part category, (iii) bounding box, and (iv) centroid. For every pair of nodes, the decoder additionally classifies the connecting edge into one of
\{
rail
,
hinge
,
attached
,
contact
,
null
\}
. When a
hinge
or
rail
edge is predicted between dynamic nodes, the motion axis can be estimated by aligning the edge on the target geometry. The first three edge types are directed, encoding a parent-child relation between parts, while
contact
and
null
edges are treated as bidirectional.

Training.

To train
GraFu
, we require paired (non-functional, functional) graph data. To address the scarcity of such supervision, we introduce the FurFun-233 dataset, which provides ground-truth functional graphs. Non-functional counterparts
G_{u}
are synthesized from each functional graph
G
via a set of structure-corrupting augmentations, including random edge collapse, node and edge removal, node and edge label masking, and geometric flips. The resulting paired data constitutes the training corpus for
GraFu
. The whole network is trained using a combination of an anchor loss, node and edge reconstruction losses, and a graph completion loss, all computed over the synthesized training pairs. Full details of the loss formulations, ablation studies, and augmentation strategies are provided in the supplementary material.

3.3.
Geometric Fixing

Figure 3.
Various application conditions for Contact-face snapping, Flushing-face snapping, and Perpendicular-face snapping hinges under counter-clockwise rotation motion.

Given the predicted graph and input mesh, Stage 2 realizes the prediction in 3D via dynamic and static geometric fixes. For every predicted hinge or rail edge, the corresponding mechanical geometry is instantiated and integrated into the mesh, with dynamic nodes inheriting the embedded motion animations — mirroring how real-life furniture motion is governed by physical joints rather than virtual axes. For every predicted new node, a template mesh is instantiated and surfaced through a Blender API for the user to visualize, tune, accept, or deny.

Hinge Insertion.

Inserting actual hinge geometries into the static mesh offers multiple benefits. First, it ensures physical motion plausibility by keeping all parts connected throughout motion, with the range naturally constrained by the hinge. Second, the hinge carries its own local revolution system, snapping it between the movable and static parts rectifies the predicted motion axis, making the revolute motion geometrically grounded and physically consistent.

Physically plausible revolute motion requires
bilateral surface contact
. This means that the joint must simultaneously contact both the static frame and movable part at geometrically compatible locations. We identify three hinge types based on surface arrangement (Fig.
3
): C-hinge for two contacting faces, F-hinge for flushing faces, and P-hinge for perpendicular interior faces. Together these three types empirically cover all furniture revolute joints, with C-hinge being the most prevalent in practice.

For hinges, we apply a template-based snapping algorithm using a gallery of the three hinge types, each annotated with static/dynamic snap planes and a rotation axis. Given a predicted hinge edge and its two nodes, we detect the presence of contacting, flushing, or interior perpendicular faces to determine the appropriate hinge type, after which a local geometric solver finds the optimal hinge placement.

Rail Insertion.

Rail insertion follows a similar template-based approach, with each template pre-annotated with static and dynamic snap planes. The rail is oriented to the predicted sliding axis, snapped to the movable part’s main body, and scaled to its length. Since most 3D assets lack mechanical compatibility, gaps between the rail and mounting panel are bridged with an additional supporting block. The inserted rail constrains motion range proportionally to the part size and ensures the movable part remains physically connected throughout animation.

Interactive Correction.

The above algorithms are wrapped as Blender operators so that the user can switch between different template geometries and tune the motion range, speed, scale and placement of these templates through Blender API. In addition to functional edge instantiation, our GraFu also predicts new nodes that complement the model structure, such as additional handles, top panel (which is the most commonly missed item in PartNet-Mobility dataset), shelves and dividers. We develop respective algorithms for each of the categories, where users can choose to accept or deny the introduction of new nodes or interactively tune the position and geometry of them in Blender. The detailed algorithm and Blender API are presented in the Supplementary Material.

Generalizability.

Note that both baselines were previously trained on PN-M, whereas our method is “zero-shot" on this dataset, since our training data does not contain any ShapeNet, and hence PN-M, models. This suggests that a comparison on PN-M is also a test of generalizability for our method.

Metrics.

Because PN-M contains substantial model duplication with differing motion annotations, we report two motion-correctness measures:
GT-aligned

Reasonable

For collision, we densely sample 50 frames spanning each part’s rest-to-full-extent motion and count frames in which the movable and static parts intersect; the per-object rate is averaged over all movable parts. Connectivity is measured by the distance between the movable and static parts along the motion: if this distance exceeds 5 mm (models are normalized to a unit cube), the part is deemed detached, and we report the fraction of movable parts that remain connected.

Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work

We present object functionalization, a novel 3D modeling task to convert visually plausible but non-functional 3D assets into objects that operate as intended. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic attempt to correct major functional deficiencies in both dynamic and static settings. Our functionalization pipeline operates in two stages, by disentangling relation/topology prediction, via a graph completion neural network (GraFu), and geometry realization driven by the predicted graph which can help refine the predicted motion parameters (e.g., for collision avoidance).

While effective, our approach is still limited on several fronts. First, it can only functionalize parts that have been segmented, which would not cover, for example, the top drawer in Figure
7
. This example also reveals that our current method can only add whole parts, not partial structures, such as the missing panels of a drawer. Second, our method does not handle geometric deficiencies that compromise physical validity at the modeling or mesh level (e.g., multiple doors in one segment or shell-only models). Last but not least, our current focuses has been on a limited set of motion types (e.g., no chained motions) and a narrow set of object categories (i.e., furniture) as dictated by the training set we constructed. Figure
4
shows several failure cases that reveal additional limitations.

Despite these limitations, we believe that our first attempt at addressing such an important modeling problem opens the door for further research. Enriching our
FurFun
dataset and extending our framework to accommodate broader functional mechanisms and additional object categories would increase applicability. Beyond furniture, functionalization has potential applications in fabrication, simulation, and embodied AI, where functional correctness is critical. We hope this work encourages the community to move beyond appearance-driven 3D assets and toward models that are not only visually plausible but also physically usable and functional.

Loss Function Formulation and Ablation

Given a training pair
(G_{u},G)
where
G_{u}
is the non-functional input graph
and
G
is the ground-truth functional graph, GraFu decodes a slot graph

\hat{G}=\{\hat{s}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
with
M
output slots. The first
N
slots
are
anchored
to the input nodes and the next
K
are
free

queries; the remaining
M-N-K
slots are zero-padded (and supervised to be
null). We optimize a weighted sum of per-slot, per-pair, and motion losses,
where the per-pair terms are evaluated after Hungarian matching between the
predicted slot set and the GT node set.

Set assignment.

Free slots are matched to unmatched GT nodes using the Hungarian assignment

\sigma=\arg\min_{\sigma}\sum_{i}\;[\,\lambda_{\mathrm{cls}}\,\mathrm{CE}(\hat{m}_{\sigma(i)},m_{i})+\lambda_{\mathrm{pos}}\,\|\hat{c}_{\sigma(i)}-c_{i}\|_{1}\,]
,
where
m_{i},c_{i}
are the GT category and centroid. Anchored slots are

not
matched: they keep their input-node correspondence so the input
identity is preserved across the decoder.

Per-slot losses.

For every slot
\hat{s}_{i}
with ground-truth match index
\sigma(i)
, we
group six per-slot terms into a single node loss:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{node}}=\;

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{exist}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{free\_exist}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{count}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mat}}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cent}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bbox}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{anchor}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{anchor\_bbox}}.

The six common terms supervise existence (binary cross-entropy on the
existence logit; with a free-slot-only variant

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{free\_exist}}
), expected slot count

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{count}}=\bigl|\sum_{i\in\mathrm{free}}\sigma(\hat{e}_{i})-N_{\mathrm{free}}^{\mathrm{gt}}\bigr|

(here
\sigma
is the sigmoid and
N_{\mathrm{free}}^{\mathrm{gt}}
is the
count of GT nodes not covered by any anchored slot), part category
(categorical cross-entropy), centroid
\ell_{1}
, and bbox half-extent

\ell_{1}
. Anchored slots receive two additional pose terms

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{anchor}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{anchor\_bbox}}

that pull each anchored prediction back onto its input-node pose,
preventing encoder drift from corrupting the identity transform on
existing parts.

Per-pair losses.

For every ordered pair
(i,j)
of real slots, the decoder emits an edge-type
distribution
\hat{r}_{ij}
over five classes

\{\textsc{contact},\textsc{hinge},\textsc{rail},\textsc{attached},\textsc{null}\}

:

(2)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}=\mathrm{CE}\bigl(\hat{r}_{ij},\;r_{ij}\bigr).

Two auxiliary directed heads enforce the parent–child semantics of

attached
and
hinge
edges. For each handle slot, the

soft-parent
head selects exactly one parent (door or drawer)
among the predicted dynamic slots, and is trained with cross-entropy
against the GT parent, yielding
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{parent}}
. The

hinge-target
head is its analogue on the door side: for each
door slot it picks exactly one static mounting panel, yielding

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hinge\_target}}
.

Motion losses.

For each (door, panel) hinge edge and each (drawer, body) rail edge, three
per-pair heads predict pose-invariant joint attributes:

(3)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{motion}}=\mathrm{CE}\bigl(\hat{f}^{(4)}_{ij},\,f^{(4)}_{ij}\bigr)+\mathrm{CE}\bigl(\hat{\epsilon}_{ij},\,\epsilon_{ij}\bigr)+\mathrm{CE}\bigl(\hat{a}^{(6)}_{ij},\,a^{(6)}_{ij}\bigr),

where
\hat{f}^{(4)}
is the
4
-way
hinge-border
(one of the four
non-thin panel-face edges of the door),
\hat{\epsilon}
is the
2
-way

axis sign
along the orthogonal non-thin axis, and
\hat{a}^{(6)}
is
the
6
-way signed rail axis for drawers.

Total loss.

The full training objective is a weighted sum over the five groups defined
above:

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{node}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{node}}+w_{\mathrm{edge}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}+w_{\mathrm{parent}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{parent}}

\displaystyle+w_{\mathrm{ht}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hinge\_target}}+w_{\mathrm{motion}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{motion}}.

We use equal group weights
w_{\mathrm{node}}=w_{\mathrm{edge}}=w_{\mathrm{parent}}=w_{\mathrm{ht}}=w_{\mathrm{motion}}=1
. Within

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{node}}
, the per-term sub-weights

\displaystyle\{w_{\mathrm{exist}},w_{\mathrm{free\_exist}},w_{\mathrm{count}},w_{\mathrm{mat}},w_{\mathrm{cent}},w_{\mathrm{bbox}},w_{\mathrm{anchor}},w_{\mathrm{anchor\_bbox}}\}

\displaystyle=\{1,\;3,\;1,\;1,\;1,\;0.5,\;0.3,\;0.3\}

gave the best validation loss.

Setup.

We probe the contribution of each supervisory signal by holding out one
loss component (or a tightly coupled pair, when two losses jointly
supervise the same quantity) on top of the winning configuration and
retraining from scratch under identical optimisation settings
(
85
epochs, cosine-annealed learning rate with
T_{\max}{=}450
, same
train/val/test split as the main model). The six variants we evaluate
are:

•

-w_{\mathrm{ht}}
: removes
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hinge\_target}}
.
This loss trains the directed head that, for each door, picks exactly
one static mounting panel; removing it disables the door-to-panel
selection signal.

•

-w_{\mathrm{parent}}
: removes
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{parent}}
.
This loss trains the directed head that, for each handle, picks exactly

•

\ell_{1}
inside

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{node}}

\ell_{1}

•

The un-ablated baseline is the best overall configuration. Looking across

Data Augmentation

Beyond the natural
(G_{u},G)
pairs obtained from FurFun-233 (where

G_{u}
is the rest-state contact-only graph and
G
the functional
counterpart), we synthesize additional input variants by corrupting
the functional graph
G
via one of
thirteen
structure-corrupting
strategies plus a random geometric flip. Per training step, a strategy
is sampled from a weighted categorical distribution (Table
5
);
the sampled corruption is then applied to
G
to produce a synthetic

G_{u}^{\mathrm{aug}}
, while the supervision target remains the
original
G
. With
\alpha=1.0
augmentation ratio, every real

(G_{u},G)
pair is paired with one synthetic

(G_{u}^{\mathrm{aug}},G)
pair in each epoch, doubling the effective
paired set and exposing the model to a wider distribution of input
defects than the natural test split would.

Category drops.

Five strategies remove all nodes of a specific structural category
(handles, top panels, bottom panels, doors, drawers). Each forces the
free-slot existence and material heads to recover the missing category
from the surrounding contact graph and the global pooled summary fed
into the free queries. A sixth strategy removes two or three of the
above categories simultaneously, training the decoder to handle
compound completion where multiple structural roles are missing at
once. A seventh strategy randomly removes a handful of arbitrary nodes
regardless of category.

Label-masking corruptions.

Two strategies anonymize the input labels: one replaces every node’s category with the
unknown
token and collapses all edges to contact edges, and another does the
same while keeping all surviving edge types. Both train the material head
to recover part categories from geometry and topology alone, even when
the input graph carries no semantic labels.

Edge-preserving partial-functional drops.

Three strategies preserve a partial functional edge set on the input,
training the model on
partial-functional inputs
—useful for
cascaded inference, where prior iterations have already labelled some
edges. They are:

•

drop a random fraction of edges but keep the functional type of
every surviving edge;

•

drop a few nodes while keeping all surviving edge types intact;

•

drop
1
–
4
random non-door / non-drawer nodes and retain every
remaining hinge / rail / attached edge unchanged;

Geometric flip.

Independently of the corruption strategy, with probability
p=0.5
we
apply a random axis-aligned mirror across
X
or
Y
(never
Z
, so
“up” is preserved). Coordinates and bbox extents are negated on the
mirrored axis, point-cloud samples are flipped, and the face-encoded
motion labels are remapped through a fixed face-permutation table so
that the GT joint axis stays geometrically consistent. The mirror
doubles the effective data without enlarging the dataset on disk and
acts as a chirality regulariser, since cabinet motion is invariant
under left/right reflection but face-indexed labels are not.

Curriculum.

The fraction of input nodes/edges removed by a given strategy follows
a per-sample uniform draw from a curriculum interval that anneals
during training:
(0.30,0.50)
early (forcing aggressive recovery
and strong free-slot “fire” priors), interpolated linearly to

(0.10,0.20)
late (matching the test distribution, which is
typically only mildly incomplete). A per-graph cap of

\#\,\mathrm{free\_slots}{=}16
ensures every removed node has a
slot available to be recovered into. The curriculum progress

t\in[0,1]
is updated from
train.py
as

t={\mathrm{epoch}}/{\mathrm{total\_epochs}}
.

Appendix D
Geometric Realization Algorithms

Figure 8.
Mechanical part annotation. Row 1: interior mount hinge with non-linear motion; Row 2: exterior mount hinge with linear revolute motion. Cyan and gold indicate geometry and snap plane for static and dynamic parts, respectively. Row 3: Sliding rail. Magenta and lime indicate two snapping planes.

Revolute Motion: Hinge Attachment

We observe that physically plausible revolute motion requires
bilateral surface contact
—the mechanical joint must simultaneously contact both the static frame and the movable part at geometrically compatible locations.
Specifically, given a static frame
\mathcal{S}
, a movable part
\mathcal{P}
, and a rotation axis
\mathbf{a}=

(\mathbf{o},\mathbf{d})
with origin
o\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and axis
d\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we seek the optimal placement of a hinge
\mathcal{H}
that best approximates the intended revolute motion while achieving the highest snapping quality with respect to two contact entities.
Rather than inferring mechanical parameters from data, we use
hinge templates
collected from the internet, and annotate
snap planes

\{\Pi_{s},\Pi_{d}\}
for static and dynamic contact, a
rotation axis indicator
, and
handedness anchors
for orientation disambiguation, as shown in Figure
8
.
This template-based formulation transforms the continuous placement problem into a discrete selection followed by geometric fitting.

We begin by determining the pose of the hinge template to be inserted.
We initialize the hinge template at
\mathbf{o}
and rotate it to align with
\mathbf{d}
via a minimal rotation.
We further ensure that the hinge’s static plate is closer to the static mesh part and vice versa, flipping the hinge as needed.

After pose initialization, we then snap the hinge template to the static mesh and the movable part.
We formulate this as a search-and-snap process.
For each snap plane
\Pi
with normal
\mathbf{n}_{\Pi}
, we cast rays towards the target mesh and collect hit points
\{(\mathbf{h}_{i},t_{i})\}
where
t_{i}
is the distance along
\mathbf{n}_{\Pi}
.
To handle multi-layer geometry (e.g., cabinet panels with two-sided walls), we cluster hits by proximity and select the appropriate layer based on the hinge mounting type.
The interior mounting hinge snaps to the inner layer of the static frame and the exterior mounting hinge snaps to the outer layer of the static frame, while both of them snap to the inner layer of the movable parts.
The snap displacement is then
\Delta=\mathbf{n}_{\Pi}\cdot(\bar{t}+\delta_{\text{offset}})
where
\bar{t}
is the distance to the chosen surface and
\delta_{\text{offset}}
adds a heuristic offset that accounts for snap-plane thickness.

Finally, we align the edge of the hinge with the existing geometry.
This is handled implicitly for interior-mounted hinges (P-Hinge) during the snapping process, against the side-panels.
For exterior-mounted hinges (C-Hinge and F-Hinge), we conduct an additional
tangential alignment
step to ensure the hinge edge coincides with the door edge along the rotation axis:
let
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{n}_{\Pi}\times\mathbf{d}
be the co-border direction perpendicular to both the snap normal and rotation axis. We project bounding extents onto
\mathbf{u}
and align outer edges:

(5)

\Delta_{\text{co-border}}=\mathbf{u}\left(\max_{\mathbf{v}\in\partial\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}-\max_{\mathbf{v}\in\partial\Pi_{d}}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}\right)

This ensures the hinge pin axis passes through the geometric edge of the door, producing visually correct exterior hardware placement.

Prismatic Motion: Sliding Rail Attachment

Prismatic joints present distinct challenges: the mechanical component must span the full travel distance while avoiding collision with non-sliding geometry such as drawer fronts.
We introduce a body-region detection algorithm that automatically identifies the functional sliding extent.
Specifically, given a drawer
\mathcal{D}
with sliding axis
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and a cabinet frame
\mathcal{S}
, we seek to place paired rails
\{\mathcal{R}_{L},\mathcal{R}_{R}\}
satisfying:

(6)

\min_{\mathcal{R}_{L},\mathcal{R}_{R}}\mathcal{E}_{\text{contact}}(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{S})+\lambda\mathcal{E}_{\text{symmetry}}(\mathcal{R}_{L},\mathcal{R}_{R})

subject to mechanically governed motion over the translation range
[0,d_{\max}]
.

To do so, we begin by detecting the
container body
(the functional storage volume) and the
pulling board
(the front panel extending beyond the body) of the drawer—rails should span only the body region to avoid mechanical interference.
We do so via a geometric discontinuity analysis.
Sampling points along the sliding axis at the drawer’s lateral edge, we compute the local surface profile
z(t)
where
t
parameterizes position along
\mathbf{s}
. The body-board boundary
t^{*}
is identified as the first significant discontinuity:

(7)

t^{*}=\min\left\{t:|z(t)-z(t-\delta)|>\tau_{z}\;\lor\;\|\mathbf{p}(t)-\mathbf{p}(t-\delta)\|>\tau_{p}\right\}

where
\tau_{z}
and
\tau_{p}
are geometric thresholds. The rail is then scaled to match the detected body length
\ell_{\text{body}}=t^{*}-t_{\min}
.

We then snap the drawer rail to the exterior of the drawer body.
We employ directional surface search with expected normal priors:

(8)

\mathbf{n}_{\text{bottom}}=+\hat{\mathbf{z}},\quad\mathbf{n}_{\text{side}}=\pm(\mathbf{s}\times\hat{\mathbf{z}})

The sign of
\mathbf{n}_{\text{side}}
depends on left/right rail placement. Ray-surface intersection identifies contact points, with candidates filtered to exclude the pulling board region (
t>t^{*}
).

We then correct the orientation of the rail templates to ensure correct contact topology.
We handle two cases: (1) the support plane (drawer bottom contact) should be
below
the static plane (drawer wall contact), and (2) the support plane should be
closer
to the drawer centerline than the static plane.
We correct these via axis-aligned reflections:

(9)

\mathbf{S}_{\text{correct}}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}),\quad\sigma_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}

As drawers require bilateral rail support, we only compute the left rail placement and generate the right rail via reflection across the drawer’s sagittal plane:

(10)

\mathbf{M}_{\text{mirror}}=\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{n}_{\perp}\mathbf{n}_{\perp}^{\top}

where
\mathbf{n}_{\perp}=\mathbf{s}\times\hat{\mathbf{z}}
is the perpendicular direction. This guarantees perfect geometric symmetry and simplifies the optimization to a single-rail problem.

Finally, we align the rail’s dynamic end (the drawer-attached component) with the detected body-board boundary
t^{*}
. This prevents the rail from protruding into the pulling board region, which would cause collision during drawer closure:

(11)

\Delta_{\text{cutoff}}=\mathbf{s}\cdot\left(t^{*}-\max_{\mathbf{v}\in\partial\mathcal{R}_{d}}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{s}\right)

where
\mathcal{R}_{d}
denotes the rail’s dynamic component.

Adding Countertops

We frequently observe base-cabinets in PartNet-Mobility
(
Xiang et al. 2020
)
that are modeled without an explicit countertop. This is often reasonable when the cabinet is intended to be placed beneath a shared counter surface or paired with appliances (
e.g.
, a stove). However, when the asset is considered in isolation, the missing top surface makes it unusable as a standalone cabinet. We therefore treat
missing countertops
as a static malfunction and repair them as part of our functionalization pipeline.

To check if an object has a countertop, from a top view, we look at how much space it covers on the floor and how much of that space is covered near the top of the object.
If a large part of the floor area is also covered near the top, we say a countertop exists.

Algorithmically, we project the mesh onto the ground plane and create two binary occupancy maps:
\mathbf{O}_{all}
for the entire mesh and
\mathbf{O}_{top}
for geometry near the maximum height.
We then compute the coverage ratio
c=\texttt{area(}\mathbf{O}_{top}\texttt{)}/\texttt{area(}\mathbf{O}_{all}\texttt{)}
. If
c
exceeds a threshold
\alpha
, we declare a countertop present; otherwise, it is considered missing.

When a countertop is missing, we generate one by first estimating a planar outline and then extruding it into a slab. The outline is obtained by slicing the furniture near the top, fitting an axis-aligned rectangle to the rim points (with optional overhang), and extruding it by a user-defined thickness to form the final panel.

Fixing Leg Misalignment

Human-made furniture often has symmetric parts, which naturally keeps geometric consistency, such as having legs of equal length. Automatically generated models usually lack these constraints and, although they may look plausible, can fail basic functional checks, for example due to
leg misalignment
that makes them unstable.
To detect leg misalignment, we first need to identify leg regions. We do that by first rendering the object from several views and using a text-guided segmentation model (SAM3)
(
Carion et al. 2025
)
with the prompt
“legs”
. These regions are mapped back onto the 3D mesh and combined across views to keep only parts consistently labeled as legs. We remove small isolated regions, group the remaining geometry into individual legs using mesh connectivity and connected components information, and compare their bottom heights to check leg alignment.

We repair leg misalignment by resizing each leg group so that all leg bottoms reach a common target height. Let
h_{z}
denote the bottom height of leg group
z
in the canonical coordinate frame. We choose a target height
h^{\star}
from the set of existing bottom heights to minimize the total adjustment:

(12)

h^{\star}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{h\in\{h_{1},\dots,h_{N}\}}\sum_{z=1}^{N}\left|h-h_{z}\right|.

This selection favors a target that reduces the overall amount of resizing while keeping the target consistent with one of the original legs. Given
h^{\star}
, we scale each leg group either (i) isotropically, or (ii) only along the
up
axis (user-controlled), to obtain a final model whose leg bottoms are coplanar.

Please note that our pipeline works for furniture with clear, separable legs, but is less suitable for pedestal bases or fused leg designs.

Interior Generation

While the previous components address exterior defects,
interior structure
is an equally important aspect of functionalization that is often missing or incomplete in 3D furniture assets. Although not applicable to categories such as chairs or tables, interior layout largely defines the functionality of
cabinet-like furniture (e.g., wardrobes). Missing interiors are prevalent in both human-made repositories
(
Deitke et al. 2023
;
Xiang et al. 2020
)
and automatically generated models
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
.

Here
, we discuss generating
shelves
and
dividers
for cabinet-like furniture, and
utilize
S2O
(
Iliash et al. 2024
)
for generating drawer boxes.

We start by slicing the furniture down the middle to get a 2D cross-section. Empty compartments are extracted as connected components and then extruded into 3D axis-aligned boxes using the surrounding panels, forming initial interior candidates.
We then refine the candidate spaces by removing occupied areas (like drawers) and partitioning the remaining space, horizontally for shelves, vertically for dividers. We filter by size and support, then generate panels of user-set thickness, inserting them at adjustable intervals.

This approach supports generating interiors from scratch as well as augmenting existing interiors, while providing direct control over orientation, number of panels, spacing, and thickness. Although we do not propose an explicit interior
detector
, the same candidate-compartment extraction can be used to infer whether an asset contains meaningful interior free space. The primary limitation is that the representation relies on axis-aligned rectangular compartments; consequently, irregularly shaped interiors are not
directly
modeled by this procedure.

Adding Interaction Elements

Our final static functionalization module augments furniture models with
interaction elements
—geometry that enables a user to grasp and operate movable parts such as drawers and cabinet doors. We consider two complementary types.

Additive
elements correspond to externally attached handles that protrude from the drawer/door surface.

Subtractive
elements correspond to
recessed
grips, where a portion of the drawer/door geometry is carved to form a finger groove (with no external attachment).
In practice, additive handles are generally easier to detect from appearance, whereas recessed grips can be visually similar to decorative grooves or other surface ornamentation.
Here, we focus on detecting
additive
handles. Using the same render–segment–back-project pipeline as for legs (Sec.
D
) with SAM3
(
Carion et al. 2025
)
, we sample views along a front-facing arc and prompt SAM3 with
“handle”
to get per-view masks, which are then back-projected and combined on the mesh.

When
attaching
interaction elements, our pipeline handles both additive handles and recessed grips.
The main steps are choosing a placement region and setting handle orientation and size.

For drawers, additive handles go at the front center, and recessed grips are carved on a chosen side.
For doors, we use the motion axis to find the hinge side, placing handles on the opposite side, additive at mid-height with an offset, recessed carved into the panel thickness.
For additive handles, we add a small
snap plane
to define the mounting position. During attachment, the handle is oriented toward the target, rays are cast from the snap plane to the surface, and the handle is moved until it sits flush. The handle is also scaled to match the part. For recessed grips, we subdivide the target face and deform vertices into a U-shaped groove, with profile (sharpness, depth, size) controllable by users.

We compare our geometric realization algorithms against BlenderMCP, a tool that connects a vision-language model (VLM) to Blender.
We use Claude 4.5 Sonnet as the VLM, and prompt it to functionalize the object under a range of instructions with different amounts of detail.
