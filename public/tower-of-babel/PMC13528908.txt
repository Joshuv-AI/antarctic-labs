No effect of rhythmic visual stimulation on experimental pain perception
Authors: Nicolas Roy, Coralie Deslauriers, Thaliane Côté-Cazes, Audrey Etcheverry, Michel-Pierre Coll
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.Across 2 experiments, rhythmic visual stimulation elicited frequency-specific brain responses but did not alter thermal, laser, or electrical pain perception, regardless of frequency or duration.

1. Introduction

Pain arises from dynamic brain network coordination, likely mediated by oscillatory activity across specific frequency bands.29,51 In several studies, suppression of alpha (8-13 Hz) rhythms has been observed during phasic and tonic painful stimulation.24,39,46 Conversely, beta (13-30 Hz) activity typically increases during painful stimuli, particularly during tonic pain,11,40 while gamma-band (>30 Hz) oscillations correlate with stimulus intensity7,42,62 and can predict subjective pain perception.19,23,66,68 However, most findings remain correlational, leaving the precise role of these bands in generating pain unclear.20

To investigate this association, studies have directly modulated human brain oscillations using noninvasive techniques such as transcranial alternating current stimulation and neurofeedback. While alpha-band transcranial alternating current stimulation applied to sensorimotor areas was linked to a reduction in pain perception in some contexts,5,34 other studies targeting alpha and gamma frequencies over these same regions found no significant impact on pain perception37 or reported mixed results.14 Alpha-band neurofeedback also yields inconsistent findings: While some report pain modulation,47 a recent large pre-registered study found no effect on subjective pain.21

By contrast, studies using rhythmic sensory stimulation31 have yielded more promising results. Specifically, rhythmic visual stimulation (RVS) induces neural responses aligning with external input timing, termed neural entrainment.26,43,44 Here, entrainment was operationalized as a frequency-specific increase in electroencephalography (EEG) spectral power at the RVS frequency and its harmonics, measured from EEG, while acknowledging that this measure cannot fully separate entrainment of intrinsic oscillations from steady-state evoked responses (see Discussion). Rhythmic visual stimulation at alpha frequencies has been shown to be associated with reductions in laser-induced pain perception and evoked cortical responses compared with control conditions.12,13 Similar alpha-band effects have also been observed in chronic pain populations5,35,67 and a recent meta-analysis of 9 studies reported significant, but heterogeneous, analgesic effects of rhythmic sensory stimulation across both experimental and clinical pain.36 Yet, despite these findings, the overall evidence base for RVS-induced analgesia remains limited. It is still unclear whether the analgesic effects are confined to alpha-band stimulation and whether they reflect direct entrainment of pain-related oscillations or more general, nonspecific influences on pain perception.

To address these gaps, we conducted 2 within-participant experiments assessing the robustness and generalizability of RVS-induced analgesia. Experiment 1 tested whether short blocks of RVS at various frequencies were associated with changes in ongoing thermal pain and its associated brain oscillatory activity. Experiment 2 used longer stimulation durations and phasic pain stimuli to align with the methodology of Ecsy et al. (2017) and assess the replicability of their findings. We hypothesized that alpha-band RVS would be associated with lower pain ratings and reduced alpha suppression during pain, while also exploring the effects of stimulation in the beta and gamma bands. Although RVS was associated with increased power in the targeted frequency bands, our results provide evidence against a modulatory effect of RVS on acute pain perception or the oscillatory neural response to pain.

2. Methods

2.1. Experiment 1

2.1.1. Participants

Forty-one participants aged 18 to 40 years completed experiment 1 (21 women; Mage = 27.02 years, SDage = 6.25, 18-40 years old), after excluding one participant with substantial missing data due to technical difficulties (approximately 50% of the data set). Our initial sample size target was 70 participants, which would provide approximately 80% power to detect effect sizes of Cohen dz = 0.35 using a 2-tailed paired t test with α = 0.05. However, interim analyses revealed evidence against the hypothesized effects on pain perception (see below), prompting us to terminate data collection at 41 participants. Most participants identified as White (75.6%), followed by Chinese (7.3%), South Asian (4.9%), Latino/a (4.9%), Arab (2.4%), Black (2.4%), and West Asian (2.4%). Most participants had completed a university degree (75.6%), including bachelor's, master's, or doctoral degrees, while 24.4% had completed college or technical education. Participants were excluded from recruitment if they reported chronic pain, neurological, or psychiatric disorders, or current use of medication affecting attention or alertness. Individuals with epilepsy or a family history of epilepsy were also excluded due to the potential risks associated with RVS. Data for this experiment were collected in Quebec City, Canada between October 2023 and December 2024. All procedures were approved by the ethics committee of the CIUSSS de la Capitale-Nationale (project #2024-2922). All participants provided informed consent and received CAD 35 for their participation.

2.1.2. Rhythmic visual stimulation

We used a modified headrest support (SR-HDR, SR research, Canada) equipped with 4 white LED lights positioned at eye level around each eye. The LEDs were controlled using an Arduino microcontroller, allowing them to flicker at predetermined frequencies. RVS stimulations followed a square-wave pattern with a 50% duty cycle, which has been shown to promote neural entrainment.60 We administered RVS in 3 frequencies: alpha at 10 Hz (50 milliseconds on/50 milliseconds off), beta at 18 Hz (28 milliseconds on/28 milliseconds off), and gamma at 42 Hz (12 milliseconds on/12 milliseconds off). The control RVS condition involved a random and inconsistent rhythm, with LED on/off durations drawn randomly from a uniform distribution ranging from 10 to 100 milliseconds. The arrhythmic control condition was designed to match the rhythmic stimulation in overall visual input and luminance, while removing periodic temporal structure, thereby allowing isolation of frequency-specific effects of rhythmic stimulation. Stimulation frequencies were selected based on prior research showing that RVS within these frequency bands is associated with changes in perception and behavior,1,13,22 and because these frequencies are known to be involved in pain experience.29,51 Rhythmic visual stimulation was delivered with eyes closed, consistent with previous studies12,13 and to ensure participant comfort. Flicker perceptibility was assessed informally by the experimenters during piloting and through participant debriefing, with no participants reporting an inability to perceive flickers in the stimulation. However, because perceptibility was not systematically quantified across all frequencies and participants, differences in perceived flicker strength across the 10, 18, and 42 Hz conditions cannot be ruled out.

Luminance of the RVS stimulation was measured using a handheld digital lux meter (URCERI, model MT-912, China) placed in front of the LEDs. Based on prior work,1,2 the stimulation luminance levels were set to 1000 lux in experiment 1 and in the electrical condition of experiment 2, and to 250 lux in the laser condition. This change in luminance reflects an update to the experimental protocol to increase luminance after the laser condition data had already been collected. Supplementary analyses show that this discrepancy did not lead to significant differences between the laser and electrical conditions in experiment 2 (see supplemental digital content, Materials S3, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C529).

2.1.3. Thermal nociceptive stimulation

Painful thermal stimulation was chosen because of its ability to elicit clear and sustained oscillatory activity related to pain41 and was delivered using a TCS-II thermode and the T09 probe (QST Lab, France). The heat stimulation was applied to 4 different equidistant locations on the nondominant forearm. The locations were counterbalanced across experimental conditions to avoid sensitization or habituation. The stimulation temperatures were determined during a calibration session using a staircase procedure.64 Starting from a baseline temperature of 35°C, the procedure began with the lowest possible stimulation intensity of 40°C and increased in 1°C increments up to a maximum of 50°C, until participants could no longer tolerate the next stimulation. The duration of each stimulation was 6.5 seconds, with 0.75 seconds ramps and a 5-second plateau. After each stimulation, participants rated their perceived pain using a verbal scale ranging from 0 (No pain) to 10 (Worst imaginable pain), and were asked whether they could tolerate the next stimulation. This procedure was repeated 3 times to obtain reliable estimates of both participants' pain detection and tolerance thresholds and to minimize potential biases related to anxiety or initial apprehension during the first staircase.

After the staircase procedure, 14 equidistant thermal stimulations selected between each participant's detection and tolerance thresholds were administered in a random order. For each participant, first- to third-degree polynomial regressions were applied to model the relationship between stimulation intensity and pain ratings. The best-fitting function describing the association was chosen. The calibration allowed for the selection of temperature levels corresponding to low and high pain (40% and 70% of the maximum rating, respectively). On average, the low pain temperature was set at 44.81°C (SD = 2.04, range: 40.3-48.4°C) and the high pain temperature was set at 46.68°C (SD = 1.79, range: 41.4-49.5°C).

2.1.4. Electroencephalography recordings

Brain electrical activity was recorded using a 64-channel EEG cap (BrainProducts Acticap system, Germany). Signals were amplified by either a BrainProducts ActiChamp (N = 8) or an EGI NA300 (N = 33), with the latter using a BrainProducts Acticap adapter and ControlBox. This hardware change occurred due to an equipment upgrade during collection. The signals remain comparable as the same electrodes were used. All recordings were referenced to FCz and grounded at FPz. Electrode impedances were maintained below 10 kΩ throughout the experiment, with Brain Vision SuperVisc electrolyte gel reapplied as needed during experimental breaks. Data were sampled at 500 or 1000 Hz and filtered online with a low-pass filter at 250 or 280 Hz depending on the amplifier used.

2.1.5. Procedure

Participants were seated in a sound-attenuated shielded booth for thermal pain calibration, followed by the EEG cap installation. Participants were informed that the experimental task would consist of 10 blocks and last approximately 1 hour and 15 minutes in total. They were told that, during each trial, they would receive a flashing light while their eyes were closed and that a painful thermal stimulus would occur at some point during the light presentation. No additional information was provided regarding the timing, predictability, or other details of the task. Throughout the experiment, participants kept their eyes closed and their chin positioned on a headrest to which the LED lights were mounted, ensuring consistent light delivery and minimizing head movement artifacts in the EEG recordings.

The experiment was controlled using PsychoPy v2021.2.3.45 The time course of an experimental trial is illustrated in Figure 1A. Participants first viewed a fixation cross for 2 to 2.5 seconds, followed by 13 seconds of RVS. A painful thermal stimulus was delivered between 5 and 6.5 seconds after RVS onset. The thermode baseline temperature was maintained at 35°C. After each trial, participants rated their perceived pain intensity using a verbal numeric scale ranging from 0 (“No pain”) to 10 (“Worst imaginable pain”).

Trial structure for experiment 1 and design for experiment 2. (A) Schematic representation of a single trial in experiment 1. Each trial began with a cross presented for 2 to 2.5 seconds (intertrial interval; ITI), followed by 5 to 6.5 seconds of rhythmic visual stimulation (RVS) alone. A painful thermal stimulus (6.5 seconds total duration) was then delivered during ongoing RVS, with onset occurring between 5 and 6.5 seconds after RVS initiation. After each trial, participants rated their perceived pain intensity on a verbal numeric scale from 0 (“No pain”) to 10 (“Worst imaginable pain”). (B) Experimental design for experiment 2. Participants completed a baseline block with 30 pain stimuli, followed by 2 counterbalanced entrainment conditions. Each entrainment condition consisted of 10 minutes of RVS at either 1 or 10 Hz, immediately followed by 30 pain stimuli.

The experimental task included 8 stimulation conditions created by crossing 4 RVS frequencies (10, 18, 42 Hz, and control) with 2 pain intensity levels (moderate and high). Each participant received 160 pain stimuli, with 20 trials per condition, presented in a pseudo-randomized order in blocks of 32 trials. A 60-second break was provided after the 16th trial within each block and between blocks. During each 32-trial block, the 8 stimulation conditions were presented once at each of the 4 locations on the nondominant forearm. The pseudo-randomization algorithm constrained the trial order such that no RVS frequency or pain intensity level could appear more than 3 times consecutively.

Before the experiment began, participants completed the French-Canadian adapted state subscale of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-Y17) to assess their current anxiety levels. At the end of the experiment, they completed the STAI-Y trait subscale and the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS59). These questionnaires were administered as part of standard laboratory procedures but were not analyzed in this study.

2.1.6. Data processing and feature extraction

All analyses were conducted using custom Python (version 3.12.7) scripts. Specifically, EEG data were processed using MNE-Python (version 1.10.2)18,32 and the MNE-BIDS pipeline (version 1.9).4 The full list of packages used to analyze and plot the data is provided in the publicly accessible code repository.

2.1.6.1. Electroencephalography preprocessing

We converted the raw data to BIDS format.49 Bad EEG channels were first identified using a two-step process: visual inspection followed by automated detection with the pyprep package.6 The automated procedure flagged channels as bad if they showed consistently low correlation (r < 0.4) with other channels or if their z-scored amplitude deviated substantially from the median channel amplitude (z > 5). This led to the identification of an average of 0.73 bad channels per participant (SD = 1.43, range = 0-7). We then preprocessed the data using the MNE-BIDS pipeline (https://mne.tools/mne-bids-pipeline). The line noise at 60 Hz was first suppressed using the ZapLine algorithm.9 The data were bandpass filtered between 1 and 100 Hz, then resampled at 500 Hz if acquired at 1000 Hz and re-referenced to the average of all channels. Epochs from −2 to 6 seconds centered around the onset of the plateau of the thermal stimulation were then created. Epochs containing large artifacts were removed before independent component analysis (ICA) using a peak-to-peak threshold of 300 µV. Independent components were automatically labelled using the mne_icalabel package.33,50 Components labelled as muscle activity, eye blinks, heartbeat, line noise, or channel noise with a high confidence (>0.7 probability) were removed from the recordings. This led to the removal of an average of 8.17 components (SD = 2.71, range: 4-15). Remaining noisy trials were rejected if their maximum peak-to-peak amplitude exceeded 150 µV. This led to the removal of 1.28 epochs with a remaining number of 18.71 epochs on average (SD = 1.99, range: 10-20) for each of the 8 Pain (2) × RVS (4) conditions. Rejected channels were interpolated using spline interpolation before group analyses.

Finally, to standardize spatial representation across participants, we accounted for the fact that stimulation was applied to the nondominant arm, resulting in right-arm stimulation for left-handed participants (n = 7) and left-arm stimulation for right-handed participants (n = 34). To enable group-level analysis, we mirrored the electrode montage according to the midline for left-handed participants so that all data would represent the stimulated side on the right. This approach assumes spatial symmetry in pain-related neural responses across hemispheres, although we acknowledge this may not fully account for potential hemispheric asymmetries in pain processing or differences in brain organization between handedness groups. We report additional EEG analyses excluding left-handed participants in supplemental digital content (see Material S1, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C529), which confirmed that our main findings were not driven by this methodological choice.

2.1.6.2. Frequency spectrum and signal-to-noise ratio

To determine whether stimulation was associated with an increase in activity within the expected frequency bands, we computed the power spectral density for each epoch using the multitaper method after zero-padding each 8-second epoch to 10 seconds to obtain a 0.1 Hz frequency resolution. To compare power in each frequency band independently of the attenuation due to the 1/f slope, we computed the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) by dividing the power in each 0.1 Hz frequency bin by the average power in the 10 neighboring bins (±0.5 Hz), excluding the 2 immediately neighboring frequency bins. This measure indicates the power in each frequency bin relative to the neighboring bins. Higher SNR was expected for stimulated frequencies. Signal-to-noise ratio for each epoch was finally averaged within each RVS stimulation condition.

2.1.6.3. Time-frequency decomposition

To assess oscillatory responses to pain stimulation and their potential association with RVS, we obtained time-frequency representations (TFR) for each condition from −2 to 6 seconds relative to the onset of the thermal plateau. We used a Morlet wavelet transform with frequencies from 1 to 80 Hz (1 Hz steps) and cycles that increased logarithmically from 3 to 30 across the frequency range. Time-frequency data were downsampled to 250 Hz temporal resolution and averaged across trials within each of the 8 conditions of our 4 (RVS: 10, 18, 42 Hz, Control) × 2 (Intensity: High, Low) design. Data were baseline-corrected using the log ratio calculated from the prestimulus period (−1.75 to −0.75 seconds before the pain plateau to avoid including the rising phase of the temperature in the baseline) and cropped to the end of the thermal plateau at 5 seconds.

2.1.7. Statistical analyses

2.1.7.1. Statistical approach

All statistical testing was performed using the MNE-Python (version 1.10.2)18,32 and pingouin (version 0.5.5) packages63 in Python. Assumptions for parametric tests were confirmed by visually inspecting the distribution of the models' residuals to ensure normality, the absence of extreme outliers, and similar variance across groups for the between-subjects factor in experiment 2. All diagnostic plots are provided in supplemental digital content (see Materials S4, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C529). For repeated-measures ANOVA, the sphericity assumption was tested using the Mauchly test and a Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied to adjust the degrees of freedom when necessary. No missing data were observed in the behavioral measures, as response completeness was verified for all participants. For EEG analyses, only trials excluded during preprocessing as described above were missing from the analyses. Although the statistical analyses were guided by our experimental design, experiments 1 and 2 were not formally pre-registered.

Frequentist analyses were used as the primary inferential framework. To further characterize potential effects and quantify the strength of evidence for both the null and alternative hypotheses, we conducted additional Bayesian pairwise t-tests. In all Bayesian analyses, we used the default prior distribution (ie, Cauchy distribution centered at 0 with a scale parameter of √2/2) and interpreted Bayes factors (BF10) >1, >3, and >10 as anecdotal, substantial, and strong evidence for the alternative hypothesis, respectively. Reciprocal values (<1, <1/3, and <1/10) were interpreted as corresponding levels of evidence for the null hypothesis.53 In cases of divergence between the 2 frameworks, the frequentist results were prioritized, while the Bayesian results provided complementary interpretative context. The above analytical approach was applied uniformly across all reported analyses in experiments 1 and 2.

2.1.7.2. Pain ratings

Pain ratings were averaged across the 2 Pain Intensity (low, high) × 4 RVS Frequency (10, 18, 42 Hz, control) conditions and compared using a 2-way repeated-measures ANOVA. We additionally performed Bayesian pairwise t-tests comparing each rhythmic condition (10, 18, 42 Hz) to the arrhythmic control condition (control) on the pain ratings. For these analyses, pain ratings served as the dependent variable, whereas Pain Intensity (low, high) and RVS Frequency were included as within-subjects independent variables.

2.1.7.3. Electroencephalography analyses in regions of interest

Rhythmic visual stimulation Frequency and Pain Intensity were entered as within-subjects independent variables in the EEG analyses, with baseline-corrected oscillatory power as the dependent measure. Power was averaged across canonical frequency bands (alpha: 8-13 Hz, beta: 13-30 Hz, and gamma: 30-80 Hz48) over centro-parietal channels (Cz, C1, C2, C3, C4, C5, C6, CPz, CP1, CP2, CP3, CP4, CP5, CP6) during the thermal plateau (0-5 seconds). We compared Pain Intensity conditions using Bonferroni-corrected paired t-tests, and RVS Frequency conditions using a repeated-measures ANOVA. To maximize the signal-to-noise ratio and isolate the main effects of each independent variable, data were collapsed across the nontarget dimension. Specifically, for analyses of RVS Frequency effects, data were averaged across both pain intensity levels; conversely, for analyses of Pain Intensity effects, data were averaged across all RVS conditions.

2.1.7.4. Electroencephalography analyses with cluster-based permutation tests

To complement the regions of interest analyses and evaluate the data without a priori spatial or spectral constraints, we performed cluster-based permutation tests (2000 permutations). The main effect of Pain Intensity (low vs high) was evaluated using a permutation paired t test, while the main effect of RVS Frequency was evaluated using a permutation repeated-measures ANOVA (F-test). Clustering was performed across all frequencies, channels, and time points. Clusters were formed using a cluster entering threshold of P < 0.05, and we used the “hat” correction with sigma = 0.001 to account for lower signal and noise in some frequency bands.52

2.2. Experiment 2

2.2.1. Participants

A new sample of participants, who did not take part in experiment 1, was recruited. Fifty healthy participants, aged between 18 and 40 years, completed experiment 2. One participant was removed due to technical issues during data collection making the data unusable. The final sample of 49 individuals included 28 women (Mage = 23.57 years, SDage = 3.90, range: 18-37 years old). This sample size exceeded that of the original study (n = 32, visual group; Ecsy et al., 2017) and provided >99% power to detect an effect size comparable with the original findings (Cohen dz = 1.01 for the difference in pain ratings between 10 and 1 Hz conditions), as well as 80% power to detect a more plausible moderate effect size (dz = 0.41), using a two-tailed paired t test with α = 0.05. Most participants identified as White (62.5%), followed by Arab (12.5%), Black (10.4%), Latino (6.2%), Chinese (4.2%), Indigenous (First Nations/Métis/Inuk; 2.1%), and Southeast Asian (2.1%). Regarding education level, more than half of the participants had a university degree (56.2%), while 35.4% had completed college or technical education, and 8.3% had a secondary or vocational level of education. Exclusion criteria were identical to experiment 1, and all procedures were approved by the same institutional review board (# Project 2023-2741). Data for this experiment were collected in Quebec City, Canada, between January 2023 and December 2024.

2.2.2. Rhythmic visual stimulation

We used the same LEDs as in experiment 1. However, since there were no EEG recordings, we mounted the LEDs in a modified Virtual Reality headset to enhance participant comfort during the longer stimulation duration and to replicate the procedures of Ecsy et al. (2017). Stimulation followed a square wave pattern with a 50% duty cycle at either 1 Hz (500 milliseconds on/500 milliseconds off) or 10 Hz (50 milliseconds on/50 milliseconds off).

2.2.3. Laser and electrical nociceptive stimulation

Nineteen participants received laser heat stimuli, while 30 received painful electrical shocks. We initially used laser stimulation to closely replicate Ecsy et al. (2017). Owing to equipment issues partway through data collection, we switched to electrical stimulation, which similarly delivers brief phasic noxious input and is widely used in experimental pain research. At the same time, we increased the RVS luminance from 250 to 1000 lux to match the conditions of experiment 1. The change in stimulation modality is therefore confounded with the change in luminance in the present design; however, as shown in supplemental digital content (see Figure S4, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C529), the pattern of results was comparable across both subgroups, and including stimulation type as a factor in the analyses did not alter the findings. Given the absence of differences between subgroups, we report all analyses pooled across stimulation types.

Laser stimuli were applied to the nondominant inner forearm using a Nd-YAP laser stimulator (Stimul 1340, Deka). The laser was moved between each stimulation across a 32-point grid covering approximately 4 by 8 cm to avoid habituation, sensitization, and skin damage. The laser intensity was calibrated at the beginning of the experiment for each participant using the same staircase procedure as in experiment 1. Five-millisecond laser pulses with a 7-mm beam diameter were delivered at 15-second intervals. After each stimulus, participants rated their perceived pain using a visual analog scale ranging from 0 (“No pain”) to 100 (“Worst imaginable pain”). The scale was presented on a computer using PsychoPy v2021.2.3.45 Participants used a response box to move a cursor along the scale. The procedure began with the lowest laser intensity of 0.5 J, which was incrementally increased by 0.25 J up to a maximum of 4.75 J, until participants could no longer tolerate the next stimulation. This procedure was repeated 3 times. Then, 14 laser stimulations of varying intensities were administered to determine an intensity corresponding to 70% of the participant's maximum reported pain. On average, a laser intensity of 3.63 J was used (SD = 0.92 J, range: 1.75-4.75, n = 19).

Electrical stimuli were delivered using a biphasic constant-current electrical stimulator (DS8R, Digitimer, United Kingdom) and train generator (DG2A, Digitimer, United Kingdom), configured to deliver five 2-millisecond pulses at 200 Hz per trial (total stimulation duration ≈ 25 milliseconds). Two 8-mm cup electrodes filled with electroconductive gel were placed 2 cm apart on the dorsum of the nondominant hand. The calibration procedure was identical to the one above: starting at a baseline intensity of 1 mA with 1 mA increments, and selecting intensities corresponding to 70% of the participant's maximum reported pain. The mean intensity used was 11.15 mA (SD = 8.58, range: 2.5-40 mA, n = 30).

2.2.4. Procedure

The design of the experiment is illustrated in Figure 1B. Participants were seated in a sound-attenuated booth for the whole experiment and completed the same questionnaires as experiment 1. They were informed that the experimental task would consist of 3 blocks and last approximately 45 minutes in total. Participants were instructed to rate the perceived intensity of each painful stimulus using a visual analog scale ranging from “No pain” to “Worst imaginable pain” during the 3 blocks, and to be exposed to a 10-min RVS session with their eyes closed between each block. No additional information was provided about the nature or purpose of the RVS. The RVS conditions at 1 or 10 Hz were counterbalanced between participants. In each block, participants received 30 electrical stimuli of identical intensity at 15-s intervals.

2.2.5. Statistical analyses

Pain ratings, as the dependent variable, were analyzed using a mixed-design ANOVA with Condition (within-subjects: baseline, 1, 10 Hz) and Order (between-subjects: 1 Hz first vs 10 Hz first) as the independent variables. As in experiment 1, these frequentist analyses were complemented with Bayesian t-tests following the previously described approach.

3. Results

3.1. Experiment 1

3.1.1. Manipulation checks

We first confirmed that the expected frequency responses were observable in the EEG signal for each stimulation frequency. Figure 2A shows the SNR across frequency bins for each stimulation condition at the centro-parietal regions of interest, revealing clear peaks at the stimulated frequency and its harmonics in all rhythmic conditions. By contrast, the control condition exhibited no discernible SNR peaks other than the endogenous alpha peak typical of the EEG spectrum. Topographic SNR maps further demonstrated elevated SNR values at occipital electrode sites specifically for the stimulated frequencies, with no such increase observed at nonstimulated frequencies.

In a second manipulation check, we confirmed that changes in thermal stimulation intensity were associated with expected changes in oscillatory activity. As shown in Figure 2B, compared with low pain, high pain stimulation was associated with greater alpha-to-beta suppression relative to baseline and enhanced gamma activity at centro-parietal sites, consistent with previous findings.46,58 Statistical tests of power averaged across the 5-second thermal plateau and centro-parietal channels (Figs. 2C, D) revealed significant effects of Pain Intensity across all canonical frequency bands. Bonferroni-corrected paired samples t-tests indicated that high pain intensity, compared with low pain, was associated with decreased theta power (t(40) = −3.96, P < 0.001, Cohen d = 0.68, BF10 = 86.87), decreased alpha power (t(40) = −3.61, P < 0.001, Cohen d = 0.60, BF10 = 35.63), decreased beta power (t(40) = −2.96, P = 0.005, Cohen d = 0.42, BF10 = 7.10), and increased gamma power (t(40) = 3.09, P = 0.004, Cohen d = 0.50, BF10 = 9.73). Cluster-based permutation tests also revealed a similar pattern, with 2 significant large clusters indicating alpha-to-beta suppression contralateral to the stimulation site and a frontal gamma increase (see supplemental digital content, Fig. S1, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C529).

3.1.2. Behavioral results

Having confirmed that RVS was associated with the expected frequency-specific responses in the EEG signal, we next assessed the effect of stimulation frequency on pain perception. As illustrated in Figure 3A, participants rated high-intensity stimulations as significantly more painful than low-intensity stimulations (F[1, 40] = 224.70, P < 0.001, η2P = 0.85). However, there was no significant main effect of RVS Frequency on pain ratings (F[1.98, 79.11] = 3.04, P = 0.054, η2P = 0.07), nor was there a significant interaction between RVS condition and stimulation intensity (F[2.72, 108.63] = 2.12, P = 0.11, η2P = 0.05).

Bayesian analyses revealed anecdotal to substantial evidence for small decreases in pain ratings in the 10 Hz condition (Hedges g = −0.08, BF10 = 5.43) and 42 Hz condition (Hedges g = −0.11, BF10 = 2.87) compared with the control condition (Fig. 3B). However, the 18 Hz condition showed no meaningful difference from control (Hedges g = −0.03, BF10 = 0.34). Direct comparisons between the 3 rhythmic conditions provided evidence against meaningful differences (BF10 = 0.18 to 0.65). Given the negligible effect sizes and lack of statistical significance in the primary analysis, these results suggest that brief RVS has no meaningful effect on acute thermal pain perception.

3.1.3. Electroencephalography results

The EEG results (Figs. 3C–E) mirrored the behavioral findings, revealing no clear evidence for differences in pain-related oscillatory responses across RVS conditions. Repeated-measures ANOVAs did not reveal significant differences between RVS conditions at centro-parietal sites in the theta (F[3, 120] = 2.01, P = 0.121, η2P = 0.020), alpha (F[3, 120] = 0.59, P = 0.608, η2P = 0.005), beta (F[3, 120] = 1.98, P = 0.130, η2P = 0.008), or gamma (F[2.43, 97.10] = 2.66, P = 0.064, η2P = 0.019) bands. However, cluster-based permutation tests revealed one significant cluster around the 18-Hz condition compared with the control condition, primarily located at occipital sites within the 18-Hz frequency band. This likely reflects differences in the stimulated frequency rather than changes in pain processing (see supplemental digital content, Fig. S2, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C529). The other permutation tests between the 10- and 42-Hz conditions and the control condition revealed no significant clusters.

3.1.4. Experiment 1 summary

The absence of RVS effects on experimental pain perception in this experiment differs from previous findings.13 However, methodological factors may account for this discrepancy. Notably, we used RVS with brief stimulation durations. Although short entrainment durations have been shown to be associated with changes in cognition in certain contexts,3,54 previous studies investigating visual entrainment effects on pain used substantially longer RVS durations, typically lasting several minutes.5,13,35

To assess whether the null effect of RVS on pain perception was attributable to these methodological differences, we conducted a partial replication of Ecsy et al. (2017). Specifically, in this second experiment, participants received 10 minutes of RVS at 10 Hz or control 1 Hz before rating the intensity of 30 painful laser or shock stimuli. Although the original study tested RVS at multiple frequencies, we selected 10-Hz and the 1-Hz control condition since 10 Hz showed the largest differential effect on pain perception in this study.

3.2.1. Experiment 2 summary

Despite using a larger sample size and closely replicating the methodology of Ecsy et al. (2017), including extended 10-minute stimulation periods and similar frequency parameters, we found no evidence that alpha-frequency RVS was associated with changes in pain perception. Pain ratings following both 1-Hz and 10-Hz entrainment did not differ significantly from baseline, nor did the 2 frequencies differ from each other. Bayesian analyses provided strong evidence in favor of the null hypothesis when comparing 1- to 10-Hz conditions, suggesting that the absence of a differential effect was not simply due to insufficient statistical power.

4. General discussion

We assessed the effect of rhythmic visual stimulation (RVS) on pain perception using 2 complementary experimental approaches across different pain modalities. In the first experiment, short-duration RVS presented concurrently with thermal pain stimulation did not modulate pain ratings despite showing evoked brain responses in the stimulated frequency bands. In the second experiment, longer duration RVS (10-minute stimulation periods preceding pain delivery) similarly failed to influence pain perception after laser or electrical stimulation. These null findings across both thermal, laser, and electrical pain modalities raise questions about the robustness and generalizability of previously reported effects of visual entrainment on pain perception. They also highlight the need for further investigation into the boundary conditions under which such effects may emerge.

Our findings contrast with previous works suggesting moderate to large effects of alpha rhythm stimulation on experimental12,13 and clinical pain perception.36 However, there is considerable variation in the reported effect sizes of sensory entrainment on pain perception across the literature, and some other studies have similarly failed to observe meaningful effects (see Maddison et al., 2023 for a review). Our null findings also align with recent evidence demonstrating that neither transcranial alternating current stimulation37 nor neurofeedback21 robustly modulates experimental pain perception or pain-related evoked potentials. Altogether, our findings add to a growing body of mixed evidence and suggest that the pain-modulatory effects of RVS may be less strong or more context-dependent than initially proposed.13 This warrants careful consideration of the mechanisms by which RVS can influence pain processing and the specific experimental parameters required to achieve reliable efficacy.

First, although our results show that RVS reliably elicited frequency-specific neural activity, it remains uncertain whether these responses represent genuine entrainment of intrinsic oscillations or merely transient, stimulus-driven effects. Rhythmic sensory stimulation has been shown to synchronize neural activity with the temporal structure of the external stimuli.26,43,44 However, increases in spectral power at the stimulation frequency may also reflect steady-state evoked responses rather than true entrainment of endogenous oscillations, as frequency-specific activity can arise from stimulus-driven responses without necessarily indicating modulation of intrinsic oscillatory dynamics.27,43,61 Importantly, the current analyses do not allow us to dissociate steady-state evoked responses from genuine entrainment of endogenous oscillations. Future studies attempting to demonstrate the influence of RVS on pain perception should employ experimental designs capable of disentangling these mechanisms. For instance, investigating whether the frequency-specific activity outlasts the stimulation offset could help determine whether RVS induces lasting changes in intrinsic pain networks rather than purely transient sensory responses.

Even assuming genuine entrainment of endogenous oscillations occurred, it is uncertain whether this modulation reached pain-relevant functional networks. While previous animal studies demonstrate that RVS can propagate beyond the primary visual cortex into regions involved in pain processing such as the primary somatosensory and prefrontal cortices,1,51 our human EEG recordings yielded no evidence that RVS modulated oscillatory responses to painful stimulation. Instead, the largest frequency responses were localized to occipital sites, and pain-evoked neural oscillations remained unchanged. This suggests that modulation of endogenous oscillations by RVS may not propagate beyond sensory cortex to directly influence nociceptive processing, and may instead act on broader cognitive functions such as attention, expectancy, or executive control.56 If so, behavioural effects would likely emerge only in contexts that place demands on these processes, conditions that our paradigm, focused on the perception of brief and predictable noxious stimuli, may not have engaged. However, there is evidence that these cognitive processes are frequently disrupted in chronic pain and associated with atypical oscillatory patterns such as elevated resting alpha power and reduced peak alpha frequency.15,25,56 For this reason, it could still be worth investigating whether RVS has any effect in chronic pain populations, where these cognitive and oscillatory disruptions are already present and may interact with rhythmic sensory modulation in ways that are not observable in healthy volunteers. Whether acute experimental paradigms in healthy volunteers can meaningfully capture these dynamics remains an open question. Experimental models involving longer-lasting or tonic pain may offer a more sensitive context in which to detect such effects.8

Several methodological factors may contribute to variability across RVS pain studies. The first is the choice of control condition. Previous studies have used a range of baselines, including the absence of sensory input, rhythmic stimulation in a frequency band not expected to influence the target behaviour, and arrhythmic stimulation within the same modality.10,65 In this study, we used an arrhythmic control condition that held visual input constant while manipulating temporal structure. This approach isolates frequency-specific neural effects but introduces differences in temporal predictability that may influence attention, salience, arousal, or expectancy, factors known to shape pain perception.28,57 As a result, we cannot fully rule out that frequency-specific effects of RVS on pain perception were obscured by concurrent differences in arousal or attentional engagement between rhythmic and arrhythmic conditions. The current design partially mitigated this concern by including multiple rhythmic frequencies (10, 18, and 42 Hz), allowing comparisons across frequency-specific conditions while holding visual input constant. Notably, 10-Hz stimulation has previously been associated with reduced pain perception relative to 1, 8, and 12 Hz,12,13 and the absence of such frequency-specific differences in the present study provides a frequency-controlled comparison that does not depend on the choice of arrhythmic baseline. Future RVS pain studies would benefit from combining multiple control conditions within the same design and from collecting measures of attention, arousal, or expectancy that could help disentangle frequency-specific effects from broader changes in cognitive state.

A second methodological factor concerns stimulation parameters, which are rarely reported in RVS pain studies. Beyond the stimulation frequency itself, parameters such as luminance, waveform, duty cycle, and viewing conditions (eg, eyes open vs closed) can all influence the magnitude of neural responses and may shape the behavioural effects of RVS. In this study, luminance levels were selected based on prior work1,2 and set to either 250 or 1000 lux across conditions. Supplementary analyses showed no intensity-related differences in RVS effects on pain ratings, suggesting that luminance within this range was unlikely to be a limiting factor for behavioural effects. Furthermore, RVS was delivered with eyes closed, following previous studies,12,13 primarily for participant comfort. This choice may have elevated baseline alpha activity,30 which could in principle reduce the relative magnitude of stimulation-driven alpha responses or alter their functional impact. As such, this stimulation setting represents a potential limitation when comparing the current findings with studies conducted under eyes-open conditions. Systematic reporting of these parameters in future work would facilitate cross-study comparisons and help determine which stimulation features are critical for pain-relevant neural and behavioural effects.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. While we performed Bayesian analyses to assess support for the null hypothesis, our failure to reject the null hypothesis could reflect insufficient statistical power. However, this would suggest effect sizes substantially smaller than those reported in previous studies, raising questions about the practical significance of any such effects. We selected 10 Hz as our alpha-band stimulation frequency based on prior evidence of its effectiveness.13 However, converging evidence suggests that individual variations in resting alpha peak frequency are related to experimental pain perception.15,16,38 Future studies should assess the effectiveness of personalizing RVS frequency based on each participant's individual alpha frequency, as this approach may reveal effects that are blurred when using a fixed stimulation frequency across all individuals. Finally, our analyses were not preregistered. However, they closely followed the logic of the experimental designs and previous studies, and we openly shared all data and code to allow others to assess the robustness of our findings to alternative analytical choices.

In conclusion, across 2 experiments using 3 pain modalities, rhythmic visual stimulation did not modulate experimental pain perception in healthy volunteers, despite eliciting robust frequency-specific neural responses. These null findings suggest that, under the conditions tested, RVS may not be a reliable approach for probing the functional role of oscillatory brain rhythms in pain processing. Whether this reflects a genuine absence of effect, a mismatch between the cognitive processes engaged by RVS and those recruited by brief experimental pain, or methodological factors that limit the sensitivity of current paradigms remains to be determined. Future work employing rigorous control conditions, transparent reporting of stimulation parameters, and pain models that more strongly engage attentional and affective processes is needed to establish whether RVS can meaningfully influence pain perception and, if so, under what conditions.
