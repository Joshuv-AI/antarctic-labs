Stochastic nonlocal traffic flow models with Markovian noise
Timo Böhme, Simone Göttlich, Andreas Neuenkirch

Abstract
We extend the stochastic nonlocal traffic flow model from [ 5 ] to more general random perturbations, including Markovian noise derived from a discretized Jacobi-type stochastic differential equation (SDE). Invoking a deterministic stability estimate, we show that the arising random weak entropy solutions are measurable, ensuring that quantities such as the expectation are well-defined. We show that the proposed Jacobi-type noise is of particular interest as it ensures interpretability, preserves boundedness, and significantly alters the stochastic realizations compared to the previous white noise approach. Moreover, we introduce a local solution operator which provides information on the local effect of the noise and utilize it to derive a mean-value hyperbolic nonlocal PDE, which serves as a proxy for the mean value of the exact solution. The quality of this proxy and the impact of the noise process are analyzed in several simulation studies.

Introduction

In the search for mathematical models describing traffic flow, hyperbolic conservation laws have proven particularly effective over the past decades. Describing traffic as a macroscopic quantity, they are computationally efficient and allow for the simulation of large-scale networks. For a comprehensive overview, we refer to
[
6
,
32
]
and to
[
16
,
17
]
for the mentioned network extensions.
Although the aforementioned classical models assume that driving behavior depends on local quantities around each driver, nonlocal traffic flow models have become an active area of research.
As exemplarily (further) developed in
[
4
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
15
,
19
,
23
]
,
these models extend local approaches by integrating traffic conditions (far-) ahead into the behavior.
In particular in the context of connected autonomous vehicles, this allows for the inclusion of not only local data but also remote information covering the entire road downstream.
From a modeling perspective, the use of such data enables the description of anticipatory drivers and smart vehicles that adjust their speed early in response to distant congestion, thus saving time and resources while stabilizing the traffic.

It is well known that driving behavior and traffic dynamics are subject to stochastic influences, which can be interpreted in two primary ways.
First, stochasticity may stem directly from agents, representing intrinsic randomness. For instance, human drivers do not have fixed reactions to specific traffic conditions, but rather follow a probabilistic response distribution within certain limits. Similarly, autonomous vehicles, which can react deterministically, are subject to measurement noise.
Second, from a modeling perspective, stochasticity can be understood as a placeholder for incomplete information regarding unknown variables.
In this context, even if the underlying traffic dynamics are deterministic, the modeling simplifications necessitate aggregating unobserved effects into a stochastic term.
Although some stochastic extensions exist for local macroscopic models, see e.g.,
[
20
,
26
,
33
]
,
stochastic extensions to nonlocal models remain sparse.
To address this gap, in
[
5
]
, we proposed a traffic flow model incorporating stochastic nonlocal velocities. The so-called sNV model belongs to the broader class of stochastic conservation laws with random fluxes, see e.g.,
[
18
,
27
,
28
,
30
]
.
Its base is a nonlocal model presented in
[
15
]
that uses downstream velocities but is evaluated using a noisy response function.
For this framework, in
[
5
]
, we established theoretical results regarding the pathwise existence and uniqueness of weak entropy solutions and provided a time-dependent white-noise process satisfying the necessary regularity requirements.

The overall aim of this paper is to extend the initial framework, providing additional theoretical foundations and significantly expanding the numerical possibilities.
We proceed in three directions, which collectively yield a generalized and more capable sNV model.
First, we address open theoretical gaps from a stochastic perspective, including the stability of solutions and critically the existence of the expectation.
Second, we expand the model to incorporate autocorrelated Markovian noise, introducing transformed SDE-type noise of Jacobi nature.
This not only allows for a direct physical interpretation but leads to significantly stronger perturbations, as we demonstrate.
Third, motivated by these strong perturbations, we derive a mean-value hyperbolic PDE based on a local solution operator to describe expected traffic conditions.
We support this with numerical Monte Carlo results that demonstrate convergence in the characteristic space. Thereby we simultaneously contribute to the fields of both stochastic conservation laws and nonlocal traffic flow modeling.

1
The class of sNV models and their properties

1.1
A deterministic nonlocal velocity model (NV)

The deterministic nonlocal velocity model, originally introduced in
[
15
]
, is given by the scalar conservation law

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}(\rho(W_{\eta}*v(\rho)))=0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\;t>0,

(NV)

where the convolution is

\displaystyle(W_{\eta}*v(\rho))(t,x):=\int_{x}^{x+\eta}W_{\eta}(y-x)v(\rho(t,y))\,dy,\quad\eta>0,

and the Cauchy problem is equipped with initial conditions of the form

\displaystyle\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x)\in(L^{1}\cap\text{BV})(\mathbb{R};[0,\rho^{max}])

(I)

for
\rho^{max}>0
given. The assumptions on the kernel function are as follows:

(A)

Given the look-ahead distance
\eta>0
, we assume that

W_{\eta}\in C^{1}([0,\eta];\mathbb{R}^{+})\;\;\text{with}\;\;W_{\eta}^{\prime}\leq 0,\quad\int_{0}^{\eta}W_{\eta}(x)\,dx=W_{0},\quad\lim_{\eta\to\infty}W_{\eta}(0)=0.

Regarding the velocity function, the following assumptions need to be made:

(B)

Given
\rho^{max}>0
, we assume for the velocity
v(\rho)
that

v\in C^{2}([0,\rho^{max}];\mathbb{R}^{+})\;\;\text{with}\;\;v^{\prime}\leq 0,\quad v(0)=v^{max}>0.

The solution concept for (
NV
) are weak entropy solutions as of Kružkov
[
24
]
.

Definition 1.1

(Nonlocal weak entropy solution)
.

A function
\rho\in C([0,T];L^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
with
\rho(t,\cdot)\in\text{BV}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
is a weak entropy
solution to (
NV
) with (
I
), i.e., to the Cauchy problem, if

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\rho-c|\partial_{t}\phi+\text{sign}(\rho-c)(f(t,x,\rho)-f(t,x,c))\partial_{x}\phi-\text{sign}(\rho-c)\partial_{x}f(t,x,c)\phi\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\rho_{0}(x)-c|\phi(0,x)\,dx\geq 0

holds for all non-negative test-functions
\phi\in C_{0}^{1}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{+})
and any constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
This reduces to
[
4
, Def. 1]
for bounded
\rho
and special choices of
\phi
and
c

[
14
, 2.12]
.

Under assumptions (A) and (B) it has been shown in
[
14
]
that equation (
NV
) with initial condition (
I
) admits a unique entropy solution. This result has been extended in
[
5
]
to a randomly perturbed velocity function.

1.2
A stochastic nonlocal velocity model (sNV)

As a first step towards combining a nonlocal velocity model with stochastic perturbations, we introduced a stochastic NV model in
[
5
]
, given by the conservation law:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}\Bigl(\rho\bigl(W_{\eta}*v_{\epsilon}(\rho,t)\bigr)\Bigr)=0.

(sNV)

Here, the convolution is as above and stochasticity is introduced through a time-dependent velocity function

\displaystyle v_{\epsilon}(\rho,t)=\max\{0,v(\rho)+\epsilon(t)\},

where
\epsilon(t)
denotes a time-dependent error term, which is given by

\displaystyle\epsilon(t)(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{R_{T}}\epsilon^{k}(\omega)\chi_{[t^{k},t^{k+1})}(t),\qquad t\in[0,T],\,\,\omega\in\Omega,

where
t^{k}=k\delta_{R}
,
R_{T}=\lfloor T/\delta_{R}\rfloor

and the random variables
\epsilon^{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,R_{T}
, are defined in a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, and are independent as well as uniformly distributed on
[-\tau,\tau]
for some
\tau\in(0,v^{max}]
.
One of the main results of
[
5
]
is that
for such a random perturbation the (sNV) model admits for every
\omega\in\Omega
a unique entropy solution.
However, a revision of the proof shows that the independence assumption and the distribution assumption were motivated by modeling reasons and were not required for this result. Thus, Theorem 5.8 of
[
5
]
extends directly to random perturbations of the following type:

(C)

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete probability space. The time-dependent error is given

\displaystyle\epsilon(t)(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{R_{T}}\epsilon^{k}(\omega)\chi_{[t^{k},t^{k+1})}(t),\qquad t\in[0,T],\,\,\omega\in\Omega,

(1)

where
\epsilon^{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,R_{T}
, are random variables on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, which are uniformly bounded by some
\tau\in(0,v^{max}]
, that is

\sup_{k=1,\ldots,R_{T}}|\epsilon^{k}(\omega)|\leq\tau,\qquad\omega\in\Omega.

Under Assumption (C) we have
0\leq v_{\epsilon}(\rho,t)\leq 2v^{max}
and thus a deterministic maximal velocity.

Remark 1.2
.

The assumption
\tau\leq v^{max}
is a modeling assumption, which ensures that the noise does not overwhelm the system. Most of the mathematical analysis of the SNV model given below is valid also under the global assumption
\tau<\infty
.

Remark 1.3
.

While nonlocal models are known to provide more realistic driver behavior, the velocity-based formulation positions the model within the framework of scalar conservation laws with stochastic fluxes.
This enables the approximation scheme we develop in Section
1.6
to leverage the existence of an expected flux function.
Consider instead a stochastic nonlocal density model,
where we similarly perturb the nonlocal quantity, i.e., the density of the Blandin and Goatin model
[
4
]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}\Bigl(\rho v\bigl(W_{\eta}*\min\{\max\{\rho+\epsilon,0\},\rho^{\max}\}\bigr)\Bigr)=0.

(sND)

Here, evaluating the expected flux depends entirely on the input to the deterministic flux function, which requires knowledge of
\mathbb{E}[\rho]
throughout the nonlocal horizon.
As we describe in Section
1.6
, this information is not directly available.

1.3
Existence, uniqueness and stability of the sNV model

Theorem 1.4

(Existence, uniqueness and properties of (sNV))
.

Let
\rho_{0}
as in (
I
) and assume that assumptions (A), (B) and (C) hold.
Then, for
any
T>0
and any fixed
\omega\in\Omega
a weak entropy solution
\rho(t,x)(\omega)
, in the sense of Def.
1.1
, to
the Cauchy Problem of (
sNV
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\partial_{x}f_{\epsilon}(t,x,\rho(t,x))=0,&(t,x)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R},\\
\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

with
f_{\epsilon}(t,x,\rho)=\rho\bigl(W_{\eta}*v_{\epsilon}(\rho,\cdot)\bigr)(t,x)
, exists and is unique.
Further, it holds for all
\omega\in\Omega
:

(1)

Maximum principle: for any
t\in[0,T]
we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\rho_{0}(x)\leq\rho(t,x)(\omega)\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\rho_{0}(x)\leq\rho^{max}.

(2)

L^{1}
-conservation: for any
t\in[0,T]
we have

\displaystyle{\lVert(\rho(t,\cdot))(\omega)\rVert}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}={\lVert\rho_{0}\rVert}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

(3)

TV bounds: for any
T>0
we have

\displaystyle\text{TV}(\rho(T,\cdot)(\omega);\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigl(T{C}_{1}\Bigr)\text{TV}(\rho_{0};\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle\text{TV}(\rho(\cdot,\cdot)(\omega);\mathbb{R}\times[0,T])

\displaystyle\leq T\exp\bigl(TC_{1}\bigr)C_{2}\text{TV}(\rho_{0};\mathbb{R}),

where
C_{1}=C_{1}(W_{\eta},v,\rho^{max},\tau)>0
and
C_{2}=C_{2}(W_{\eta},v,\rho^{max},\tau)>0
are constants, which only depend on
W_{\eta}
,

,
\rho^{max}
and
\tau
.

As noted above, this result can be derived along the same lines as Theorem 5.8 in
[
5
]
. This proof is carried out using a Godunov-type approximation
(\rho_{j}^{n})_{n=0,\ldots,N_{T},j\in\mathbb{Z}}
. While the
quantities
\rho_{j}^{n}
are

-
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
measurable, i.e., they are well-defined random variables, the weak entropy solution
\rho
is obtained using a sub-sequence argument and Helly’s theorem for fixed
\omega\in\Omega
. Thus, from this approach, it remains unclear whether quantities as

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[\rho(t,x)^{p}]\phi(x)dx,\qquad t\in[0,T],

with
p\geq 0
and
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{+})

or
\mathbb{E}[\rho(t,x)^{p}],\;t\in[0,T],\,x\in\mathbb{R}
,
with

are well defined.
The key to solving these technical issues is the following deterministic stability result assuming given noise realizations.

Lemma 1.5

(Stability estimate)
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}:[0,T]\rightarrow\infty
be given by

\gamma_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{R_{T}}\gamma_{i}^{k}\chi_{[t^{k},t^{k+1})}(t),\qquad t\in[0,T],

where
\gamma_{i}^{k}\in[-\tau,\tau]
,
k=1,\ldots R_{T}
,
i=1,2
, with
\tau\in(0,v^{max}]
. Let
\rho_{1}^{0},\rho_{2}^{0}
be as in (
I
) and assume that assumptions (A) and (B) hold.
Finally, let
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
be
the respective weak solutions to (
sNV
) in the sense of Def.
1.1
, i.e., they are the solutions to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{1}(t,x)+\partial_{x}\bigl(\rho_{1}(t,x)V_{{\gamma_{1}}}(t,x)\bigr)=0,

\displaystyle V_{{\gamma_{1}}}=W_{\eta}*v_{{\gamma_{1}}}(\rho_{1},t),

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(0,x)=\rho_{1}^{0}(x),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{2}(t,x)+\partial_{x}\bigl(\rho_{2}(t,x)V_{{\gamma_{2}}}(t,x)\bigr)=0,

\displaystyle V_{{\gamma_{2}}}=W_{\eta}*v_{{\gamma_{2}}}(\rho_{2},t),

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(0,x)=\rho_{2}^{0}(x).

Then, we have

\displaystyle{\left\lVert\rho_{1}(t,\cdot)-\rho_{2}(t,\cdot)\right\rVert}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\exp(KT\|v^{\prime}\|_{\infty})\left({\left\lVert\rho_{1}^{0}-\rho_{2}^{0}\right\rVert}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+K\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(t)\right\rvert\,dt\right)

with

K=W_{\eta}(0)\left(2\|\rho_{1}^{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+\exp(TC_{1})TV(\rho_{0};\mathbb{R})\right)+\|W^{\prime}_{\eta}\|_{\infty}\|\rho_{1}^{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}.

Since
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are arbitrary but deterministic, to establish the above result, we can proceed similarly to classical uniqueness proofs with the additional aspect of differing velocity functions. This is captured by the Lipschitz estimate in both variables:

\displaystyle\left\lvert v_{\gamma_{1}}(\rho_{1})-v_{\gamma_{2}}(\rho_{2})\right\rvert\leq\lVert v^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\left\lvert\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}\right\rvert,

which carries through the subsequent estimates. As major parts of the proof follow classical techniques, see e.g.,
[
4
,
7
,
15
]
, the full derivation is provided in Appendix
A
.

1.4
Measurability of solutions

We now transition from deterministic perturbations to random noise with corresponding solutions, presenting the first of our three key contributions.
Similarly, as described in
[
3
, 2.2]
and
[
28
, 3.11]
, we establish the measurability of solutions to (
sNV
) using the stability result above.

Lemma 1.6

(Measurability of solutions)
.

Let assumptions (A), (B) and (C) hold, and let
\rho
be the weak entropy solution from Theorem
1.4
. Moreover, let
t\in[0,T]
.
Then, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
there exist random variables

\rho^{*}(t,x):(\Omega,\mathcal{F})\rightarrow(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))

such that

\mathbb{\rho}^{*}(t,x)(\omega)=\rho(t,x)(\omega)

for almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and almost all
\omega\in\Omega
. In other words,
\rho^{*}(t,\cdot)(\cdot)
is a measurable modification of
\rho(t,\cdot)(\cdot)
.

Since the underlying probability space is complete, i.e., the sigma-algebra
\mathcal{F}
contains all
\mathbb{P}
-zero sets, the same holds for the product space

(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega,\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\otimes\mathcal{F},\lambda\otimes\mathbb{P}).

Thus, for the entropy solution for any fixed
t\in[0,T]
,
the mappings

\rho(t,\cdot)(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

are
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\otimes\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
measurable.
This yields now that quantities as

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)\mathbb{E}[\rho(t,x)^{p}]dx

with
p>0
and
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{+})

or

\mathbb{E}[\rho(t,x)^{p}],

with
p>0
are well-defined for all
t\in[0,T]
and almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

(1)

Assume now that the process
\epsilon
takes only finitely many values. Thus, the set of possible values of
\epsilon
can be defined as

\displaystyle\{\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{L}\},\quad L\in\mathbb{N},

with
\gamma_{i}
as in Lemma
1.5
.
This allows us to assign for
i\in I=\{1,...,L\}
the pre-image subsets

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}:=\{\omega\in\Omega:\epsilon(\cdot)(\omega)=\gamma_{i}\}\in\mathcal{F},\quad i\in I.

For each
\omega\in\Omega_{i}
we obtain the same entropy solution

\rho(t,\cdot)(\omega)
that we will denote by
\rho_{\gamma_{i}}(t,\cdot)
. Since

\rho_{\gamma_{i}}(t,\cdot)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
we have that

A_{i}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\rho_{\gamma_{i}}(t,x)\leq\alpha\}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})

for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. We have

\displaystyle\{(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Omega:\rho(t,x)(\omega)\leq\alpha\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i\in I}\{(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Omega_{i}:\rho(t,x)(\omega)\leq\alpha\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i\in I}\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\rho_{\gamma_{i}}(t,x)\leq\alpha\}\times\Omega_{i}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}\times\Omega_{i}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\otimes\mathcal{F}

due to the finiteness of
I
.
Thus, for a finite-dimensional error process, the weak entropy solution

\rho(t,\cdot)(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

is
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\otimes\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
measurable for any
t\in[0,T]
.

(2)
For the general case, we use an approximation argument:

(2a)
Since the random variables
\epsilon^{k}
are uniformly bounded by
\tau
, using quantization, see, e.g., Theorem 5.2(b) in
[
29
]
, we can find discrete random variables:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{(N)}^{1},\,...\,,\epsilon_{(N)}^{R_{T}},

which take at most
N\in\mathbb{N}
values and are uniformly bounded by
\tau
,
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{k=1,..,R_{T}}\lvert\epsilon_{(N)}^{k}-\epsilon^{k}\rvert^{2}\right]\leq C(\tau,R_{T})^{2}N^{-2}

for a constant
C(\tau,R_{T})>0
.
For the corresponding error process

\displaystyle\epsilon_{(N)}(t;\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{R_{T}}e^{k}_{(N)}(\omega)\chi_{[t_{k},t_{k+1})}(t)

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\epsilon_{(N)}(t)-\epsilon(t)\rvert\,dt\leq T\sqrt{C(\tau,R_{T})}\cdot N^{-1}

using the Lyapunov inequality.
In particular, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\epsilon_{(N+M)}(t)-\epsilon_{(N)}(t)\rvert\,dt\leq T\sqrt{C(\tau,R_{T})}\cdot N^{-1}

(2)

for all
N,M\in\mathbb{N}
.

(2b)
Denote now by
\rho_{(N)}
the unique weak entropy solution corresponding to
\epsilon_{(N)}
. Let
t\in[0,T]
. Since the constant
K
in Lemma
1.5
is deterministic, equation (
2

\mathbb{E}\|\rho_{(N+M)}(t;\cdot)-\rho_{(N)}(t;\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq K\exp(KT\|v^{\prime}\|_{\infty})T\sqrt{C(\tau,R_{T})}\cdot N^{-1}

and therefore
\rho_{(N)}(t,\cdot)(\cdot)
is a Cauchy-sequence in

.
By completeness there exists a measurable
\rho^{*}(t,\cdot)(\cdot)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times\Omega)
such that

\mathbb{E}\|\rho_{(N)}(t;\cdot)-\rho^{*}(t;\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\rightarrow 0,\qquad N\rightarrow\infty,

as well
as

\displaystyle\|\rho_{(N_{\ell})}(t,\cdot)(\omega)-\rho^{*}(t,\cdot)(\omega)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\rightarrow 0,\qquad\ell\rightarrow\infty,

(3)

for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
for a sub-sequence
(\rho_{(N_{\ell})}(t,\cdot)(\cdot))_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
of

(2c)
Using the discrete structure of

\displaystyle\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\max_{k=1,..,R_{T}}\lvert\epsilon_{(N)}^{k}-\epsilon^{k}\rvert\geq\psi\Bigr)

\displaystyle\max_{k=1,..,R_{T}}\lvert\epsilon^{k}_{(N)}(\omega)-\epsilon^{k}(\omega)\rvert\rightarrow 0,\;N\rightarrow\infty,

for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
.
By the boundedness of the noise terms and the dominated convergence theorem, it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\epsilon_{(N)}(t)(\omega)-\epsilon(t)(\omega)\rvert\,dt\rightarrow 0,\qquad N\rightarrow\infty,

for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
.
Lemma
1.5
and the previous estimate now imply that

\displaystyle\|\rho_{(N_{\ell})}(t;\cdot)(\omega)-\rho(t;\cdot)(\omega)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\rightarrow 0,\qquad\ell\rightarrow\infty,

(4)

for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
.
Equations (

) and (
4

\displaystyle\|\rho^{*}(t,\cdot)(\omega)-\rho(t,\cdot)(\omega)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\|\rho^{*}(t,\cdot)(\omega)-\rho_{(N_{\ell})}(t,\cdot)(\omega)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\|\rho(t,\cdot)(\omega)-\rho_{(N_{\ell})}(t,\cdot)(\omega)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=0

for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
.
Consequently, we must have that

\rho^{*}(t,x)(\omega)=\rho(t,x)(\omega)

for almost all

and almost all
\omega\in\Omega
.

again implies that

\displaystyle\lVert\rho_{{(N),1}}(t,\cdot;\omega)-\rho_{{(N),2}}(t,\cdot;\omega)\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq K\exp(KT\|v^{\prime}\|)\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\epsilon_{(N),1}(t)-\epsilon_{(N),2}(t)\rvert\,dt\rightarrow 0

for
N\rightarrow\infty
.

Remark 1.8
.

As already mentioned, we employed in
[
5
]
a Godunov-type approximation
\rho^{\Delta x}(t,\cdot)
, which converges for fixed
t\in[0,T]
under a CFL-condition along a suitable sub-sequence in
L^{1}_{\textrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
to
\rho(t,\cdot)
. Using dominated convergence, the same holds true now for the convergence of
\mathbb{E}[\rho^{\Delta x}(t,\cdot)^{p}]
to
\mathbb{E}[\rho(t,\cdot)^{p}]
in
L^{1}_{\textrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
for any
p\in\mathbb{R}
.
For more details on the Godunov scheme see Section
2
.

1.5
Local solution operator

Now let us consider the parametrized deterministic NV model

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\xi+\partial_{x}\Bigl(\xi\bigl(W_{\eta}*v_{a}(\xi,t)\bigr)\Bigr)=0

with initial value and modified velocity function:

\displaystyle\xi(0,x)=\xi_{0}(x),\qquad v_{a}(\xi,t)=\max\{0,v(\xi)+a\},

where
|a|\leq v_{max}
. Under assumptions (A), (B) and (
I
), this equation has a unique weak entropy solution
\xi^{a}
and we can define
the operators

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{t}^{a}\bigl[\xi_{0}\bigr](\cdot):=\xi^{a}(t,\cdot),\qquad t\in[0,T].

(5)

In our stochastic model we have piecewise constant noise in time, such that we can use these operators also to describe the evolution of our stochastic NV-model.
Using the time-grid from (
1
) and setting

\gamma_{k}=\gamma(k\delta_{R}),\quad k=0,\ldots,R_{T},

we have

\rho(t_{k}+\delta t,\cdot)=\mathcal{S}_{\delta t}^{\gamma_{k}}\bigl[\rho(t_{k},\cdot)\bigr](\cdot),\quad\delta t\in[0,\delta_{R}],\,\,k=0,1,\ldots R_{T}-1.

Thus, locally the evaluation of
\rho
can be described by the solution operators
\mathcal{S}_{\delta t}^{a}
with
\delta t\leq\delta_{R}
, which we therefore call
local solution operators
.
Lemma
1.5
shows that these operators are locally Lipschitz in
\xi
and
a
. We have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}_{t}^{a_{1}}\bigl[\xi_{1}\bigr]-\mathcal{S}_{t}^{a_{2}}\bigl[\xi_{2}\bigr]\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\exp(Kt\|v^{\prime}\|_{\infty})\left({\left\lVert\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\right\rVert}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+Kt|a_{1}-a_{2}|\right),

with

K=W_{\eta}(0)\left(2\|\xi_{1}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+\exp(tC_{1})TV(\xi_{1};\mathbb{R})\right)+\|W^{\prime}_{\eta}\|_{\infty}\|\xi_{1}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}.

1.6
Mean velocity function and mean value proxy

If we want to estimate quantities as the expected density

\mathbb{E}[\rho(t,x)]
,
this can be done using the Godunov-scheme and the standard Monte-Carlo approach, i.e., we sample
M
i.i.d. copies of
\rho^{\Delta x}(t,x)
and average these, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\rho(t,x)]\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\rho^{\Delta x,(k)}(t,x),

where the superscript
(k)
denotes the i.i.d. copies.
Since this approach is computationally demanding, one might look for other approximations of the mean value.

In general, the nonlinear coupling between
\rho
and
\epsilon
prevents the direct reconstruction of the expected density
\mathbb{E}[\rho(t,x)]
from the statistical properties of
\epsilon
alone.
In particular, the stochastic flux

\displaystyle f_{\epsilon}(t,x,\rho)=\rho\bigl(W_{\eta}*v_{\epsilon}(\rho,\cdot)\bigr)(t,x)

depends on
\rho
in a nonlocal manner, which not only prevents us from obtaining a closed form equation for
\mathbb{E}[\rho(t,x)]
, but makes numerical evaluation using, e.g., collocation methods highly expensive.
In terms of kinetic theory this issue is often referred to as the Closure-Problem. Whereas for example in
[
1
,
25
]
minimization concepts are used to obtain closed equations in an approximate but analytical way,

[
11
]
identifies expected drift-parameters by observing conditional trajectories on short time-intervals, leading to empirical results on the governing expectation law.
In our case, we leverage the fact that stochasticity in the sNV model is introduced solely through the flux, whereas the PDE mechanics remain deterministic.
In detail, for a given constant realization of the noise
a\in[-\tau,\tau]
the density evolution is fully described by
\mathcal{S}_{t}^{a}
as of (
5
).
Hence, if the distribution
\nu_{t}
of
\epsilon(t)
is known, a natural candidate for the mean density is obtained by averaging these deterministic evolutions starting from the known initial value:

\displaystyle\tilde{m}(t,x):=\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\mathcal{S}_{t}^{a}[\rho_{0}](x)\,\nu_{t}(da),

However, this is not directly computable as it requires knowledge of the solution operator for every
a
. Moreover, this equality can not be calculated for arbitrary
t\in(0,T]
. In general, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[\rho(t_{k}+\delta t,\cdot)\ |\,\rho(t_{k},\cdot)\big]=\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\mathcal{S}_{t}^{a}[\rho(t_{k},\cdot)](x)\,\nu_{t}(da),\quad\delta t\in[0,\delta_{R}],

only if
\epsilon(t)
is independent from
\rho(t_{k},\cdot)
for
t\in(t_{k},t_{k}+\delta_{R}]
and does not lead to an accessible expression for the mean density due to the non-linear nature of the local solution operator.

Instead, we analyze the deterministic proxy
\bar{m}(t,x)
given by the NV model:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{m}+\partial_{x}\Bigl(\bar{m}\bigl(W_{\eta}*\bar{v}(\bar{m},t)\bigr)\Bigr)=0,

(EsNV)

with initial condition
\bar{m}(0,x):=\rho_{0}(x)
, and the expected velocity
\bar{v}
is defined by

\displaystyle\bar{v}(\bar{m},t):=\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}v_{a}(\bar{m},t)\nu_{t}(da),

(6)

where
\nu_{t}
denotes the distribution of
\epsilon(t)
. For many error processes,
\bar{v}
can be computed explicitly, see below.
Essentially, we are decoupling the noisy propagation from the perturbed densities and are only propagating
one
expected density, using an averaged velocity function. This can also be understood as using the approximation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\rho(t,x){V}_{\epsilon}(t,x;\rho(t,\cdot))\bigr]\approx\mathbb{E}\left[\rho(t,x)\right]\mathbb{E}\bigl[{V}_{\epsilon}(t,x;\mathbb{E}[\rho(t,\cdot)])\bigr].

(7)

Remark 1.9
.

As mentioned in Remark
1.3
, we note that a similarly perturbed model like (
sND
) does not exhibit a separable error contribution as captured in (
7
). This prevents the calculation and use of an expected velocity, which is an advantage of the formulation (
sNV
).

We will study the validity of the approximation aforementioned in our computations carried out for a high-noise-autocorrelated error process in Section
3
.
Before introducing such a specific class of processes and related numerical schemes in Section
1.7
, we illustrate the approximation capabilities of (
EsNV
) by comparing its characteristics with those of (
sNV
).

1.7
Markov-type error processes

Compared to the initial white‑noise approach of
[
5
]
, we build on the relaxed assumption (C), which now allows for auto‑correlated noise increments in (
1
). As our numerical evaluations in Section
3
will demonstrate, the use of such increments can generate substantially stronger fluctuations in the resulting densities while also supporting the physical interpretation.

Markov chains are natural candidates for our error processes. A suitable definition of Markov chains for our purposes as a dynamical system can be extracted from Proposition 11.6 in
[
22
]
and page 10 of
[
13
]
, which states that any Markov process
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with values on
(S,\mathbb{B}(S))
where
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),
can be written as a dynamical system

X_{k+1}=f_{k}(X_{k},U_{k+1}),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where the maps
f_{k}:S\times S^{\prime}\rightarrow S
are measurable and
X_{0},U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{k},\ldots
are independent random variables
with
U_{k}
taking values in a measurable space
(S^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
, allowing for an acceptance-rejection sampling, which we leverage below.
Using this definition, the transition probabilities of the Markov chain satisfy

\mathbb{P}(X_{k+1}\in B|X_{k}=x)=\mathbb{P}(f_{k}(x,U_{k+1})\in B)

for any
x\in S
,
B\in\mathcal{B}(S)
.
Naturally, we will work with
S=[-\tau,\tau]
in the following.

Definition 1.12

(Admissible Markovian error process)
.

Let
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a Markov chain with values in
(S,\mathbb{B}(S))
and
X_{0}=0
. Then, the process

\displaystyle\epsilon(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{R_{T}}X_{k}\chi_{[t^{k},t^{k+1})}(t),\qquad t\in[0,T],

where
t^{k}=k\delta_{R}
and
R_{T}=\lfloor T/\delta_{R}\rfloor<\infty
, is called an admissible Markovian error process.

The white noise of
[
5
]
reads in the above setting as

\displaystyle X_{k+1}=f_{k}(X_{k},U_{k+1})=U_{k+1},\qquad X_{0}=0,\;\;U_{1},\ldots,U_{k+1}\sim\mathcal{U}((-\tau,\tau)),

(9)

which directly gives the time-independent expression of the expected velocity, based on the corresponding admissible Markovian error process and definition of
v_{a}
:

\displaystyle\bar{v}(\bar{m},t)=\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}v_{a}(\bar{m},t)\nu_{t}(da)=\frac{1}{4\tau}\Bigl(\bigl(\tau+v(\bar{m})\bigr)^{2}-\max\{0,v(\bar{m})-\tau\}^{2}\Bigr).

(10)

Compared to this, the Markovian increments allow for modeling temporal correlation structures, e.g., by using a (particular) Jacobi-process.
So consider the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\displaystyle d\epsilon(t)=-\alpha\,\epsilon(t)\,dt+\sigma\sqrt{\bigl(\epsilon(t)+\tau)(\tau-\epsilon(t)\bigr)}\,dW_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],\quad\epsilon(0)=0,

(JP)

with a standard Wiener process
W=(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, a symmetric bound
0<\tau\leq v^{\max}
and noise parameters
\alpha,\sigma>0
, controlling the mean reversion and volatility. This process is a symmetric version of the classical Wright-Fisher diffusion process as considered, for example, in
[
12
]
or
[
2
]
.
We have

\mathbb{E}[\epsilon(t)]=0,\qquad t\geq 0

and

\mathbb{E}[\epsilon(t)^{2}]=\frac{\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}{2\alpha+\sigma^{2}}\bigl(1-\exp(-(2\alpha+\sigma^{2})t)\bigr),\qquad t\geq 0,

See, e.g.,
[
12
]
, also for expressions of higher moments. Moreover, the sample paths of the Jacobi process remain in
[-\tau,\tau]
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(\epsilon(t)\in[-\tau,\tau],\,t\in[0,T]\bigr)=1.

(11)

We emphasize that uniform boundedness is a key property of the Jacobi process, which is generally not given for classical diffusion models such as the (generalized) Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.
Although exact simulation of the Jacobi process is feasible, see e.g.,
[
21
]
, it is computationally quite expensive.
However, one can use an acceptance-rejection Euler scheme for its simulation on a grid

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}=T
.
Set
X_{0}=0
and

\displaystyle X_{k+1}=\Psi\!\left(X_{k}(1-\alpha(t_{k+1}-t_{k}))+\sigma\sqrt{(X_{k}+\tau)(\tau-X_{k})}(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_{k}})\right)

(12)

for
k=0,\ldots,N-1
,
where
\Psi
denotes the acceptance-rejection operator, defined for a random variable
Z
by

\displaystyle\Psi(Z)=\begin{cases}Z,&|Z|\leq\tau,\\
\Psi(Z^{\prime}),&|Z|>\tau,\end{cases}

with
Z^{\prime}
an independent copy of
Z
.
This correction is only required due to the numerical grid
t_{k+1}-t_{k}>0
as the continuous Jacobi process itself remains in
[-\tau,\tau]
in the sense of (
11
).

Remark 1.13
.

In contrast to a projection approach as, e.g.,

\displaystyle\Pi(z)=\max\{-\tau,\min\{z,\tau\}\},\qquad z\in\mathbb{R},

the resampling scheme does not create point masses the boundaries.

Remark 1.14
.

Another benefit of the Jacobi process is that its distribution
\nu_{t}
can be characterized through the evolution of its density
f_{t}^{\epsilon}(a)
, which follows a Fokker-Planck evolution (see e.g.,
[
31
]
) which is the solution to the PDE:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f^{\epsilon}_{t}(a)=\alpha\partial_{a}(af^{\epsilon}_{t}(a))+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\partial_{aa}^{2}\Bigl((\tau^{2}-a^{2})f^{\epsilon}_{t}(a)\Bigr),\quad f_{0}^{\epsilon}(a)=1\chi_{\{0\}}(a).

(FP)

Hence,
\bar{v}
can be explicitly derived as

\displaystyle\bar{v}(\bar{m},t)=\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}v_{a}(\bar{m},t)f^{\epsilon}_{t}(a)\ da.

(13)

However, as we outline in Section
2.1
this remains a theoretical property, as our implementation needs to account not only for the acceptance-rejection sampling of the process itself but also for the piecewise constant error structure of
\epsilon(t)
as of Assumption (C).

2
Numerical discretization

For all subsequent evaluations, we assume a time mesh given by
t^{n}=n\Delta t
for
n=0,\dots,N_{T}
, with
N_{T}:=\left\lfloor T/\Delta t\right\rfloor
.
Moreover, we make the simplification that the time-dependent error (
1
) evolves on a finer (generally unknown)
\delta_{R}
-grid than our numerical observations.
That is,
\delta_{R}\leq\Delta t
. This allows us to neglect additional correlations between consecutive observations introduced by the piecewise constant nature of the error.
For further rationale, we refer to Section 3.1 of
[
5
]
, but note that this is equivalent to assuming that decreasing
\Delta t
improves not only the approximation of the conservation law, but also the representation of the error term until
\Delta t<\delta_{R}
is obtained. However, this is purely of a technical nature.

2.1
Noise sampling and evaluation

Since we require only
N_{T}
evaluations of
\epsilon(t)
(as in Definition
1.12
), we can directly sample from the generating Markov chain by setting the time increments equal:

\displaystyle\epsilon^{n}:=\epsilon(t^{n})=X_{k_{n}},\quad\text{where }k_{n}=\big\lfloor n\Delta t/\delta_{R}\big\rfloor.

(14)

For simplicity, we take
\delta_{R}=\Delta t
in our numerical schemes, so that we can ease the notation by setting
k_{n}=n
.
If we require a finer noise grid as, for example, in convergence analysis of
\Delta t
, we can always generate the error process with
\delta_{R}<\Delta t
and then evaluate it at the coarse grid points.

In the white-noise case, the sampling of
\epsilon(t)
is straightforward by drawing at least
N_{T}
i.i.d. realizations according to (
9
), thus constructing an admissible (though time-independent) process as in Definition
1.12
.
In addition,
\bar{v}
is given by (
10
).

However, using the Jacobi process as a generator requires a more careful implementation. We have already established that the increments of the admissible Markovian error process are drawn using the acceptance‑rejection approach from (
12
).
Hence, we are left with just identifying

\displaystyle t_{n+1}-t_{n}=\Delta t\quad\text{and}\quad W_{t_{n+1}}-W_{t_{n}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Delta t).

To compute
\bar{v}(\bar{m},t)
as in (
6
), we need access to the distribution
\nu_{t}
of
\epsilon(t)
. Although the continuous process satisfies (
FP
), the construction of
\epsilon(t)
requires a separate approximation of its density at every time step.
To do so, we fix a simulation grid of the probability space with cell centers
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}\subset[-\tau,\tau]
and integration weights
\{w_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
, corresponding to the represented cell-width.
We denote by
\hat{f}_{n}
the numerical density of
\epsilon(t^{n})
and set the initial density
\hat{f}_{0}
to approximate the cell centered around zero with mass one.
From there, we evolve
\hat{f}_{n}
on the same time grid as before using forward equations with respect to the update scheme (
12
).
More precisely, given any realization
\epsilon(t^{n})=a_{j}
, the Markov property together with the Euler-Maruyama implementation allows us to assume a conditionally Gaussian distribution for the proposal step of (
12
).
Thus, for each possible grid-realization
a_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
, we compute

\displaystyle\mu_{j}

\displaystyle=a_{j}(1-\alpha\Delta t),\qquad\sigma_{j}^{2}=\sigma^{2}(\tau^{2}-a_{j}^{2})\Delta t,

allowing us to calculate the density after the acceptance-rejection update as

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{n+1}(a_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\hat{f}_{n}(a_{i})\,\frac{\phi(a_{j};\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})}{\sum_{k=1}^{M}\phi(a_{k};\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})w_{k}}\,w_{i},\qquad a_{j}\in\mathcal{A},

where
\phi(\cdot;\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})
denotes a standard Gaussian Kernel.
Note, that the denominator is necessary to redistribute the mass of the unbounded kernel to
[-\tau,\tau]
, thus respecting the acceptance-rejection approach as of (
12
).
This then allows us to calculate
\bar{v}(\bar{m},t)
via quadrature of (
13
), such that:

\displaystyle\bar{v}(\rho,t^{n})\approx\sum_{j=1}^{M}\max\!\bigl(0,v(\rho)+a_{j}\bigr)\hat{f}_{n}(a_{j})w_{j}.

(15)

Finally, we apply the Godunov scheme for (
EsNV
), which we outline after two short remarks.

Remark 2.1
.

•

•

\mathcal{A}
, which clusters points near the boundaries
\pm\tau
, since
\hat{f}_{n}
can exhibit rapid gradients there. Although a rigorous analysis of the approximation error is outside the scope of this work, we found that
M=601
points provide density profiles that match those obtained from high-sample Monte Carlo comparisons, while keeping the runtime short through parallel computation, see Figure

for an example.

2.2
Numerical scheme for the sNV model

For an in-depth description of the numerical scheme alongside convergence proofs, we refer to
[
5
]
, but repeat its central points, while slightly expanding the notation with respect to
\epsilon(t)
sampled as described before.
As typical for Godunov type schemes, we assume an equidistant spatial grid with cell centers
x_{j}
, cell interfaces
x_{j-1/2}
and cell length
\Delta x=x_{j+1/2}-x_{j-1/2}\;\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Let, as usual,
\rho_{j}^{n}:=\rho(t^{n},x_{j})
and define the piecewise constant function

\displaystyle\rho^{\Delta x}(t,x)=\rho_{j}^{n}\text{ for }(t,x)\in[t^{n},t^{n+1})\times[x_{j-1/2},x_{j+1/2}).

(16)

Then, the initial deterministic density
\rho_{0}
is discretized by the cell averages with respect to (
16
).
In each time step, the Riemann problems arising at the discontinuities between the numerical densities
\rho^{n}_{j},\;j\in\mathbb{Z}
are then solved exactly until the first shocks collide. Thus, the update of the cell densities is calculated as

\displaystyle\rho_{j}^{n+1}=\rho_{j}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(F^{n}_{j+1/2}(\rho_{j}^{n})-F^{n}_{j-1/2}(\rho_{j-1}^{n})\right),

where the numerical flux
F^{n}_{j+1/2}
is based on the solution to the Riemann problems at the cell interfaces and the actual flux.
Defining

\displaystyle v_{\epsilon}^{n}(\rho_{j}^{n})

\displaystyle:=v_{\epsilon}(\rho_{j}^{n},t^{n})=\max\{0,v(\rho_{j}^{n})+\epsilon^{n}\},

where
\epsilon^{n}
is sampled as in (
14
) according to its generating Markov chain, which may be white noise (
9
), a Jacobi process (
12
) or any other admissible generator. We employ the stochastic upwind flux

\displaystyle F_{j+1/2}^{n}(\rho_{j}^{n})=\rho_{j}^{n}V_{\epsilon,j}^{n},\;\;\text{with}\;\;V_{\epsilon,j}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\eta}-1}\gamma_{k}v_{\epsilon}^{n}(\rho^{n}_{j+k+1}),\quad N_{\eta}=\left\lfloor\eta/\Delta x\right\rfloor,

given the kernel evaluation

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{k\Delta x}^{(k+1)\Delta x}W_{\eta}(x)\,dx,\quad k=0,\dots,N_{\eta}-1.

Thus, our stochastic time step update reads

\displaystyle\rho_{j}^{n+1}=\rho_{j}^{n}-\lambda\left(\rho_{j}^{n}V_{\epsilon,j}^{n}-\rho_{j-1}^{n}V_{\epsilon,j-1}^{n}\right),\quad\lambda:=\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}.

(17)

As described in
[
5
, Rem. 4.1]
, the stochastic CFL condition
for (
sNV
)

\displaystyle\lambda\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}\lVert v^{\prime}\rVert\rho^{\max}+\lVert v_{\epsilon}\rVert(\omega)}:=C_{\text{CFL}}^{\max},

(18)

can be deterministically bounded with the help of the error terms bound
\tau
as

\displaystyle C_{\text{CFL}}^{\text{det}}:=\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}\lVert v^{\prime}\rVert\rho^{\max}+v^{\max}+\tau}\leq C_{\text{CFL}}^{\max}.

(19)

2.3
Numerical scheme for the EsNV model

The numerical scheme for our expectation model (
EsNV
) follows directly from the scheme for the sNV model, by replacing the stochastic velocity
v_{\epsilon}^{n}
with the expected deterministic velocity
\bar{v}^{n}(\cdot):=\bar{v}(\cdot,t^{n})
, calculated by
(
10
) for white noise or (
15
) for the Jacobi process.
The corresponding numerical flux is

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{j+1/2}^{n}(\bar{m}_{j}^{n})=\bar{m}^{n}\bar{V}_{j}^{n},\;\;\text{with}\;\;\bar{V}_{j}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\eta}-1}\gamma_{k}\bar{v}^{n}(\bar{m}^{n}_{j+k+1}),\quad N_{\eta}=\left\lfloor\eta/\Delta x\right\rfloor,

where
\bar{m}_{j}^{n}
approximates
\bar{m}(t^{n},x_{j})
with
\bar{m}_{j}^{0}=\rho_{0}(x_{j})
discretized as before.
The update is then

\displaystyle\bar{m}_{j}^{n+1}=\bar{m}_{j}^{n}-\lambda\left(\bar{m}_{j}^{n}\bar{V}_{j}^{n}-\bar{m}_{j-1}^{n}\bar{V}_{j-1}^{n}\right),\quad\lambda=\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}.

The CFL condition coincides with the deterministic bound in (
18
), i.e.,
\lambda\leq C_{\text{CFL}}^{\text{det}}
.

We now focus on the numerical investigation of the two primary extensions of the initial model of

The aim of this part is twofold.
First, in Section

Second, motivated by these strong perturbations and the need for a mean-value estimation, Section

4
Conclusion and outlook

The presented work strengthens the initial framework of the sNV model by consolidating its theoretical foundations and significantly expanding its modeling capabilities, notably through the derivation and validation of a mean-value proxy.
The key contributions of this paper are threefold, building on one another.
First, we set the theoretical foundation by proving the measurability of the random weak entropy solutions, thereby ensuring the existence of a well-defined expectation.
Then, we took a modeling perspective and expanded the framework to allow for Markovian noise.
Specifically, we introduced a suitable noise process of Jacobi type that bridges the three-dimensional gap between required regularity, modeling flexibility, and physical interpretation.
Inspired by the stronger perturbations of this approach and the knowledge of the existence of an expectation, we introduced a local solution operator to capture the effect of the noise, leading us to a deterministic mean-value hyperbolic PDE.
Lastly, we highlighted the smoothing of standard Monte Carlo simulations when faced with low-regularity densities. To address this, we presented an alternative perspective that uses a characteristic Monte Carlo average, which allowed us to validate the use of the proxy model.

For future research, we plan to benchmark the modeling capabilities of the sNV model against real-world data.
In this context, the calibration of the stochastic parameters, the application of the expectation proxy, and the influence of the non-local parameter are of particular interest. Especially, exploring how varying the non-local look-ahead range impacts the formation and propagation of traffic waves under uncertainty will provide deeper insights into the role of stochastic driver anticipation.
