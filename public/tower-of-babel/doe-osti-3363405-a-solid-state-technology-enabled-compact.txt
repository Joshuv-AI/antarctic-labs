 Executive Summary
The goal of this program was to develop a solid-state transformer (SST) capable of supplying
800 V DC to electric vehicle charging dispensers when connected to a 13.2 kV rms line-line, 3-
phase AC grid input. The power level as tested was 1.3 MW.
The SST has the following key features:
 • Medium voltage AC grid input (12.47-13.8 kV rms line-line)
 • Liquid cooling system, enabling maintenance-free outdoor operation
 • Utilization of readily available 1200 V Silicon Carbide (SiC) power electronics technology
 • Loop thermosyphon system for electrically isolated liquid cooling of medium voltage
 power electronics
 • Daisy chain communication network for easy scalability of controls to different
 configurations
The SST was successfully demonstrated at Eaton’s Thomas A. Edison Technical Center in
Franksville, Wisconsin, where it was connected to the grid at 12 kV AC rms line-line and ran at
full power up to 1.3 MW with 98.6% peak efficiency. The SST was also used to charge 2 electric
vehicles simultaneously (a Ford Mach-E and a Tesla Model Y) demonstrating its application for
charging infrastructure. This SST, with its 800 V DC output, is also highly relevant as a power
supply for datacenter power delivery applications.

 Project Results
 1. Approach

 A. Objectives
This project proposes an innovative approach to develop and demonstrate dramatic reductions
in the cost of direct current (DC) fast charging equipment. The project seeks to reduce
equipment, real estate, and installation costs for electric vehicle supply equipment and provide
faster charging times than current technology, which is expensive to purchase and install and
charging times are slow. The project directly addresses these issues in research and
demonstration of the technology in a test lab and in a demonstration charging electric vehicles.
The objectives of this research effort are as follows:
 • Develop compact electric vehicle (EV) charging infrastructure directly connected to
 medium voltage distribution networks at 12.47-13.8kV
 • Integrate a stationary storage system on the DC bus to minimize negative impacts on
 the grid [simulation only]
 • Integrate multiple chargers at the facility with a safe, distributed DC architecture
 • Reduce onsite commissioning time with factory commissioning
 • Reduce installation complexity
 • Develop a site controller that manages the vehicle charging, onsite distributed energy
 resources (DERs), and utility interconnection requirements [simulation only]
 • Design with an integrated liquid cooling system to ensure long life under high utilization

 B. Impact
The increase in electrical vehicle adoption and the associated demands for high power DC fast
chargers need careful design of the electrical infrastructure addressing the DC distribution to the
chargers as well as the utility connection. Several DC fast charging infrastructure needs have been
identified, such as fleet charging, highway corridor charging, and transit station charging. The
electrical infrastructure and charging demand can be several MW with demand variation that
could be in hundreds of kW within the hour. There is also the potential to have many smaller
sized chargers connected to the DC output. This project addresses these market needs, and the
impact of the solution developed here will be:
 • Compact design for DC fast charging stations will minimize installation time, enable
 maximum utilization, manage grid impact, and provide safety of charging dispensers.
 • Site/fleet management along with integrated onsite DER to minimize cost of ownership.
 • Demonstration to highlight ease of interconnection and installation.
 • Factory pre-configuration and commissioning to reduce delays in installation.

 2. Hardware Design

 A. System Design

In this project, a medium voltage solid state transformer (SST) was designed to interface the
medium voltage alternating current (AC) distribution grid to a direct current (DC) output. The
system was constructed using a multi-level cascaded H-bridge topology, an approach that is
well known in scientific literature. Each level of the SST consists of an active single-phase AC-DC
rectifier on the AC side. The output of this active front end (AFE) supplies a bi-directional DC-DC
converter. The high-frequency transformer in the DC-DC converter provides isolation between
the input and the output. The bidirectional DC-DC converter is configured as a dual active
bridge (DAB). A representative single-phase schematic of an n-level Cascaded H-Bridge is shown
in Figure 1. On the AC side, the SST is connected to the grid through a 3-phase grid inductor. All
DC outputs are connected in parallel for a lower voltage, higher current DC output.

 Figure 1. High level schematic of a multi-level cascaded H-bridge converter

The full system consists of 9 medium voltage cells connected in series on the AC side from line
to neutral on each phase. Within each cell there are 2 H-bridges connected in series, for a total
of 18 H-bridges in series per phase on the AC side.

A rendering of the entire SST assembly designed in this project in its enclosure is shown in
Figure 2. This system can be integrated onto a skid allowing for the placement of a single
component at the installation site, with only the 15kV-class input and DC output connections to
make. This skid was not implemented for this test regimen due to test and validation site access
constraints. On the AC side, the SST derives its own control power and provides its own grid

filter and isolation. On the DC output side, the SST provides for isolation and protection of the
direct current connections to the dispensers or other loads in the system.

 C. High Frequency Transformer
The team developed a high frequency transformer for the DC-DC converter to provide the
required isolation in the system. The transformer was designed for 25 kW of power with an 800
V input and 800 V output with a switching frequency in the range of 20-30 kHz.

A rendering of the transformer design is shown in Figure 5. This transformer requires 15kV-class
isolation between the primary winding and the core and lower voltage winding, both of which
are ground referenced. The transformer is filled with potting material to accomplish this isolation.

The transformer is designed with a semi-conductive shield around the medium voltage winding
to manage the field gradient between the winding and the core. With the semi-conductive shield
around the primary winding, the electric field gradient between the primary winding and the

dielectric strength capability of the potting material.

 D. Medium Voltage Front End
The front-end design assumes that the system is downstream of protection switchgear or fuses
sufficient to protect the branch circuit. Therefore, a visible disconnect, protection breaker (or
fuses), and over-voltage protection are assumed to be present upstream, and are not included
in this design. These components may be added to the SST assembly in the future if needed.
Within the front end, the input is connected to a 15 kV class contactor, then to a 3-phase
inductor, then to the SST cells. Also included are control power transformers to provide
208V/120V 3 phase AC control power, potential transformers used for pre-charge, and voltage
sensors for control.
The components in the main power path were designed to have at least 115 A rms current
capability. While the SST was demonstrated at 1.3 MW, the front-end design can supply a
potential future 2.7 MW design. All front end power components connected to the 15 kV class
input have a 95 kV BIL impulse rating.
Inductor
The grid inductor was a 3-phase inductor designed to provide 5 mH of inductance with 15 kV
class isolation and a 95 kV BIL rating. The rated current level with natural convection cooling
was 115 A rms.
Control Power Transformers
The control power transformers primary windings are rated for 13.2 kV line-line on the primary
side and output 120V on the secondary. Three of them connected in a wye configuration on the
secondary side can provide 120/208V 3 phase power for control loads in the SST and future
control power loads in the DC distribution system or site. This power is distributed through a
load center to the control loads in the system. The high voltage windings are connected
through a fuse on each terminal.

                                                 Page 10 of 35

Potential Transformers
The potential transformers (PTs) are rated for 7620 Volts line-neutral on the primary side and
120V on the secondary side. They are capable of 1000 VA continuous power and come with
their own protection fuses on the primary side.
The pre-charge scheme uses the impedance of the PTs. When the PTs are energized from the
low voltage side the voltage is stepped up to the medium voltage side, and the high impedance
of the PT, which is greater than 400 Ω, allows the capacitors of the series connected AC-DC
converters to charge slowly through the body diodes of the power electronics. Testing showed
that the input current during charging is low, minimizing inrush and stress on the grid, and the
capacitors charge as expected.
The scheme works by first closing the pre-charge contactor, which allows current to flow
through the PTs, charging the capacitors in the SST. Once the capacitors are charged to the line-
rectified voltage through the PTs, the pre-charge contact opens, and the main contact closes,
thereby directly connecting the SST to the grid via the grid inductor.
AC Voltage Sensors
The three voltage sensors are connected from each phase to ground on the grid side of the
inductor to measure the grid voltage. The sensors have a voltage divider ratio of 1400:1 and are
rated for 95kV BIL

 E. Output Protection
On the DC output side, the main components are the DC current sensor, the DC voltage sensor,
and the DC bus discharge system and the output protection system.
The DC protection scheme is a combination of a fuse for protection, and a DC switch for
isolation. Both components are sized to accommodate a 150kW dispenser unit and interrupt
high capacitive discharge fault currents. Fuse sizing is dependent on the available short circuit
current of the system and may vary depending on system configuration and requirements.

 F. Current Loop
The series connected H-bridge cells on the AC side of the SST require isolated 24V DC control
power. The isolation barrier needs to be capable of 15 kV-class isolation. A custom current loop
type solution provides this isolation. The system structure of the current source based auxiliary
power supply is shown in Figure 10. The sending unit (BU) and the receiving units (DU) were
validated in the configuration required for the SST with one sending unit powering 9 receiving
units. This combination is replicated in each of the power electronics cabinets to power all the
medium voltage cells in the system that require high voltage isolation for their control power.

 H. Packaging
The SST is comprised of one utility interface cabinet and three SST cabinets. The team designed
a steel platform for all the cabinets to be mounted to, allowing for a single structure that can be
lifted and set in place to achieve reduced installation time and labor. To accommodate sub-
assembly and system testing and test lab dimensional constraints, this integrated mounting
platform was not built, and the demonstration took place with the cabinets resting on
individual bases, allowing for easy movement and installation at indoor test facilities without
crane access.
The SST hardware enclosure was constructed of aluminum powder coated enclosure panels
with stainless steel hardware and was designed for a NEMA 3R environmental rating. The
system is water cooled with no air vents to the outside of the enclosure and no filters to
maintain.
Figure 12 shows a photo of the individual cabinets physically separated and with doors
removed during subsystem validation testing.

 Figure 12. SST cabinets and utility connection cabinet in the lab

Each SST cabinet uses fiberglass and other insulating components to achieve the required voltage
isolation for the medium voltage components. The enclosure uses embedded liquid cold plates
for cooling major power dissipating components, along with manifolds for distribution of the
coolant to each of the cells. As such, no other air ventilation is required. A temperature-controlled
heater was installed for condensation control.

 I. Cooling
To eliminate the need for costly filter maintenance, the system is cooled with a liquid cooling
system. The thermal design of the system consists of a water/glycol solution that is provided by
a heat exchanger and pump, which in turn provides cooling to each of the MV cells, LV cells and
transformers. This system uses manifolds in each SST cabinet to disperse the pumped coolant
                                               Page 13 of 35

from the main heat exchanger to each of the individual components. Flow control and metering
is implemented on the manifold to adjust flow and pressure for cooling throughout all the
components.
Loop Thermosyphon
The loop thermosyphon is a key component to isolate the medium voltage power electronics
from the ground-referenced water/glycol cooling loop. A rendering of the loop thermosyphon
system designed for electrically isolated cooling of the medium voltage cells is shown in Figure
13. The system is designed for the ground referenced water/glycol solution to be pumped
through the condenser, with the power electronics cold plate electrically isolated from the
condenser by the dielectric refrigerant in the thermosyphon.

 3. Firmware and Control Design

 A. Communications and Embedded Design
The control system for the SST utilizes an EtherCAT communications scheme handling
communication and synchronization between all the cells and the central controller in the
system.
EtherCAT Communication Overview
The EtherCAT system runs custom EtherCAT firmware. The system topology shown in Figure 16
features a central controller connected to the local controllers in a daisy-chain configuration.
This simplifies cabling, reduces installation and maintenance costs, and supports scalability by
allowing for easy addition of new nodes.

 Figure 16. EtherCAT system design

Both the central controller and the local controllers utilize embedded microcontrollers, which
have an integrated EtherCAT slave controller. The integrated controller hardware accelerates
EtherCAT protocol handling and offloads communication tasks from the processor, allowing for
deterministic performance. The controllers run an EtherCAT slave stack firmware stack, which
supports a distributed clock feature and ensures reliable integration with the master controller.
For connectivity, the system uses fiber optic cables instead of copper cables, providing for
electrical isolation between cells. Fiber optic cables offer several advantages, including
eliminating electromagnetic interference and providing excellent noise immunity.
Synchronization between the central and local controllers is achieved using the EtherCAT
distributed clock mechanism. The distributed clock compensates for propagation delays in the
daisy-chain configuration, delivering excellent synchronization accuracy. This is essential for
applications where timing jitter could degrade operation and performance.
Central and Local Controller Design
                                            Page 17 of 35

The central and local controller firmware was optimized to enhance its efficiency and
functionality. Key features include a parallel state machine within the control interrupt service
routine (ISR) to manage the scheduler, system state machine interrupt generation, and a
human machine interface (HMI) functionality. Additionally, the system was restructured into
parallel state machines for fault handling and system control. A slower housekeeping task was
added to handle status control and input/output operations.
Fault Detection:
The system has several fault detection capabilities to ensure robust operation. Basic fault
checks include overvoltage, overcurrent, and gate drive ready and gate drive fault signals. More
advanced fault checks include checks for the local controller firmware version, field
programmable gate array (FPGA) version, and hardware version. EtherCAT faults are monitored
for communication loss, duplicate cell IDs, and invalid cell IDs. The system also detects phase-
lock-loop lock loss. Fiberoptic faults are addressed by monitoring for serial communication data
loss. The resulting protection system generates all faults and disables the system based on the
type of faults. The overview data block diagram is shown in Figure 17. The diagram categorizes
faults into three groups. The first group is based on measured values such as voltage, current,
and temperature. The second group of faults is related to communication issues. The third
group of faults is determined by validating the correct firmware version number.

 Figure 17. Data block diagram of the system showing fault details

 The AFE voltage signal has a waveform that more closely resembles a sinusoidal signal than a

command with an overshoot of less than 2%. As a result of these tests, the control loops of the
PI regulators in the system were verified and ready for full system validation and testing.

 C. Energy Management Simulations
The team performed energy management simulations to determine how the SST prototype
would perform as part of a larger DC-coupled EV charging site that included many more
dispensers as well as optional DC resources such as solar or battery storage coupled to the DC
bus. Even though the larger DC system was not constructed as part of this project, these
simulations show how the system can behave as part of a larger DC charging and energy
management system. Three potential use cases were developed based on an electric refuse
truck charging scenario and were simulated to determine how a site controller in a future
system could reduce the cost of energy while still maintaining the necessary state of charge on
the fleet vehicles.

Charging Site Controller
The primary objective of the charging site controller is to manage the charging power and
energy demands of an EV fleet in an intelligent fashion, ensuring that it meets the set control
goals from both the fleet owner and the utility perspectives. It utilizes Fleet DNA data
[https://www.nrel.gov/transportation/fleettest-fleet-dna.html], site design parameters, utility-
imposed requirements, and real-time measurements as inputs.
To achieve the control objectives, the site controller determines setpoints for EV charger ports,
manages the on-site battery storage system (either sourcing or sinking power), curtails the PV
system if necessary, and imports power from the grid. The following control objectives are
considered for site energy management and control in this work:
 • Charge the EV fleet to their desired state of charge within the available dwell time.
 • Ensure power import from the grid remains below the utility interconnection
 contractual agreement limit.
 • Maintain power import ramp up and down from the grid within ramp-rate limits at the
 point of common coupling.
 • With DERs (PV and battery storage): minimize PV curtailment and keep the battery
 energy storage charged for the next day operation.
 • Reduce charging energy costs and demand charge related costs.
The site controller is designed to ensure all fleet EVs are charged to their desired state of
charge (SOC) within their dwell time, guaranteeing that all fleet EVs receive sufficient charge to
complete their next scheduled trip without running out of power.
Dwell time refers to the period between an EV’s arrival and departure from the site. The
prioritized charging method focuses on (a) the time of plugging an EVs into an available charger
at the site, and (b) limiting the charge rate to an EV based on its dwell time and energy needs.
The site controller can manage when an EV is charged and the EV charging rate by considering
ToU rates and demand charges from the utility. The site controller reduces monthly demand
charges through the smart operation of fleet charging and managing the battery storage. The
algorithm incorporates utility requirements as limits, treating them as hard constraints that
must not be violated. This approach ensures the reliability and quality of the electric power
supply to the station and neighboring loads on the same feeder.

Key Performance Indicators
The installation time key performance indicator (KPI) was verified during the assembly of the
SST at the test site. The SST was commissioned in 3 days, even though it was not mounted onto
a skid, meaning that each cabinet had to be installed individually. The skid mounting was not

The efficiency was calculated with the system running from the maximum 12 kV l-l voltage
input that the configurable transformers could output. The input power was determined by
integrating a 1 second sample of the instantaneous power calculations of each phase. The
output power was calculated by multiplying the voltage and current readings internal to the
load bank. The system control power and cooling system pumping losses were also included in
this calculation.

 C. Electric Vehicle Charging Demonstration
For the EV charging demonstration, the SST was connected to the 12 kV l-l source at the input
and connected to the two 150kW Tritium dispensers at the output. Due to limitations of the

During the EV charging test, two different EVs were connected, a Ford Mustang Mach E and a

charge rates. The Mach E peak charge rate was limited due to the higher state of charge when

 5. Conclusions
In this project, a solid-state transformer with a 15 kV-class input and an 800V DC output was
designed, built, tested, and demonstrated for the purpose of high-power charging of electric
vehicles.
The design and demonstration proved several key objectives of the project, including:
 • Direct connection of the power electronics to the utility grid, eliminating the need for a
 traditional 60 Hz step-down transformer.
 • The ability to integrate and factory commission the charging infrastructure assembly,
 allowing for reduced installation time compared to the baseline.
 • DC distribution to multiple dispensers, eliminating unnecessary AC-DC conversions
 within the site.
 • Improved efficiency over the baseline state of the art.
 • Integrated liquid cooling system allowing for maintenance free operation in an outdoor
 environment.

The system included several technological improvements over existing hardware and
technology demonstrations, including:
 • Scalable, EtherCAT-based, communications and synchronization architecture to the
 individual cells.
 • A passive loop thermosyphon system for electrically isolated liquid cooling.
 • Improved control algorithms for managing the series and parallel connected cells as a
 single converter.
